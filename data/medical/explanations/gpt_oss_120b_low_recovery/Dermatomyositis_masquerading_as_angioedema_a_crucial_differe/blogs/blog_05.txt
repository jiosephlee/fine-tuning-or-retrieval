Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# Dysphagia in Dermatomyositis: From Subclinical to Airway Threat  

---  

## Why this case grabbed my attention  

When a 40‑something man walks into the ED with a “bloated” neck and no fever, the reflex is to think Ludwig’s angina, a dental infection, or—if the CT looks “fluffy”—idiopathic angioedema.  In the case report by Chan et al., the neck swelling turned out to be a **pseudo‑angioedema** caused by **dermatomyositis (DM)**.  What tipped the team off was *not* the imaging but the **combination of subtle skin clues (peri‑ungual telangiectasias, a “holster” rash) and a massive CK of >16 000 U/L**.  

The downstream consequence? A **severe dysphagia** that threatened the airway, required nasogastric feeding, and delayed discharge for weeks.  This illustrates a broader teaching point: **myositis‑related swallowing dysfunction can evolve silently and then become an emergent airway problem if missed**.  

Below we’ll walk through how to spot the early red flags, what investigations are most useful, and how to manage the swallowing crisis while your immunosuppression kicks in.  

---

## The pathophysiology in a nutshell  

Dermatomyositis is a complement‑mediated microangiopathy that targets the capillaries of skin and muscle.  In the **pharyngeal and laryngeal musculature**, the same perimysial inflammation leads to:

* **Fiber necrosis & regeneration** → loss of contractile strength.  
* **Peri‑vascular complement deposition (C5b‑9)** → edema of the sub‑muscular tissue.  
* **Reduced coordination** of the swallow because the intrinsic muscles of the pharynx are disproportionately affected.  

When the inflammation is intense (as in anti‑SAE1 positive disease), you can get **subcutaneous edema** that mimics angioedema and **massive swelling** that narrows the airway.  

---

## Practical bedside approach – “Swallow Screen in the ED”

1. **Ask the right questions** – “Do you feel food ‘stuck’ in your throat?”, “Do you cough during meals?”, “Any change in voice after eating?”  
2. **Quick physical exam** – look for:  
   * Peri‑ungual erythema or nailfold capillary changes.  
   * Rash over the face, chest, or proximal limbs (even subtle heliotrope or shawl‑sign variants).  
   * Neck swelling that is **soft, non‑tender, non‑fluctuant** – think edema rather than cellulitis.  
   * **Weakness of hip flexors or other proximal muscles** (strength 4/5 or less).  

3. **Rapid CK check** – a CK >1 000 U/L in the setting of neck swelling should prompt myositis work‑up.  

If any of the above are positive, **don’t settle for “idiopathic angioedema”**—activate the rheumatology/immunology consult early.

---

## Imaging and functional studies – what to order and when  

| Modality | What it tells you | Timing/Indication |
|----------|-------------------|--------------------|
| **CT neck (contrast‑free)** | Excludes abscess, shows diffuse strap‑muscle edema. | First‑line when airway compromise feared. |
| **Videofluoroscopic Swallow Study (VFSS)** | Characterises pharyngeal residue, penetration, aspiration; quantifies severity. | As soon as the patient can tolerate oral contrast (usually within 24‑48 h). |
| **Flexible Nasendoscopy** | Direct view of secretions, vocal cord motion, supraglottic edema. | Bedside, especially useful for ENT/ICU teams. |
| **MRI of the neck / torso** | Shows active myositis (STIR/FS hyperintensity) – helps with disease activity monitoring. | If diagnosis still uncertain or to guide biopsies. |
| **FDG‑PET** | Detects systemic muscle inflammation, screens for occult malignancy (important in DM). | After initial work‑up, before long‑term immunosuppression. |
| **Pulmonary function tests** | Baseline for ILD, restrictive pattern from neck/respiratory muscle weakness. | Routine in DM, especially anti‑SAE1 positivity. |

The case example used **VFSS**, which revealed severe pharyngeal dysphagia with pyriform fossa pooling and penetration of thin liquids—classic for myositis‑related swallowing impairment.  

---

## The role of the speech‑language pathologist (SLP) – don’t wait  

* **Early assessment (within 24 h of admission)** can identify safe diet modifications and teach compensatory maneuvers (chin‑tuck, supraglottic swallow).  
* **Serial VFSS** tracks recovery as immunotherapy takes effect; it also guides the timing of oral diet re‑introduction.  
* **Swallow‑related quality‑of‑life tools** (e.g., MDADI) help quantify impact and need for rehabilitation.  

In the reported case, the patient required an NG tube for **2 months** while the steroids, IVIG, rituximab, and mycophenolate were doing their work.  Prompt SLP involvement meant that once muscle strength returned, the transition to oral intake was smooth and without aspiration events.

---

## Nutritional support – NG tube vs PEG  

| Situation | Preferred route | Rationale |
|-----------|----------------|-----------|
| Acute, reversible dysphagia (<6 weeks) with expected rapid improvement after immunotherapy | **Nasogastric (NG) tube** | Less invasive, easy to place, can be removed once swallowing recovers. |
| Persistent dysphagia >6–8 weeks, poor oral intake, or high aspiration risk despite therapy | **Percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG)** | Provides long‑term enteral nutrition, reduces risk of tube dislodgement, better patient comfort. |
| Contraindications to NG (e.g., severe facial edema, esophageal varices) | **PEG** or **jejunostomy** | Bypass obstructed or high‑risk anatomy. |

The case illustrates that **NG feeding can bridge the gap** while aggressive immunosuppression works, but a low threshold for PEG is warranted if the dysphagia does not improve after 6–8 weeks.  

---

## Immunotherapy – the driver of swallow recovery  

The **key to reversing dysphagia** is rapid control of the underlying inflammatory process:

1. **High‑dose IV methylprednisolone** (1 g × 3 days) – hits the muscle inflammation hard.  
2. **IVIG** – especially useful in anti‑SAE1 disease, where complement-mediated attack is prominent.  
3. **Steroid‑sparing agents** – mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, or methotrexate (avoid methotrexate if ILD risk).  
4. **Early taper** – once CK normalises and strength returns, start tapering steroids to avoid long‑term toxicity.  

In the case, CK fell from 16 184 U/L to 37 U/L within 3 weeks, and hip flexor strength returned to normal.  Swallowing function lagged behind muscle strength, underscoring that **functional recovery can trail biochemical improvement**.  

---

## Take‑home pearls  

- **Never accept “idiopathic angioedema”** in a patient with neck swelling and any hint of proximal muscle weakness or skin changes.  
- **CK >1 000 U/L** in this context is a red flag for inflammatory myopathy; order it early.  
- **VFSS is the gold‑standard** for quantifying dysphagia severity and guiding diet progression.  
- **Early SLP involvement** reduces aspiration risk and expedites safe oral intake.  
- **NG tube is usually sufficient** for the short‑term nutritional bridge; move to PEG if dysphagia persists beyond 6 weeks.  
- **Aggressive immunosuppression** (steroids + IVIG ± rituximab/MMF) can reverse muscle inflammation and, consequently, swallowing dysfunction—often within weeks.  
- **Screen for malignancy** in all DM patients, especially those with anti‑SAE1 antibodies, dysphagia, and male sex.  

---

## Bottom line  

Dermatomyositis is a master of disguise; it can masquerade as angioedema, deep neck infection, or a simple “muscle ache.”  The **airway‑threatening dysphagia** seen in the Chan et al. case is a stark reminder that **myositis‑related swallowing dysfunction is a medical emergency** when it progresses.  A systematic bedside screen, rapid CK measurement, early VFSS, and prompt immunotherapy—paired with skilled speech‑language pathology—turn a potential airway catastrophe into a recoverable episode.  

**Next time you see a neck “swelling” with subtle skin signs, think DM. Your early suspicion could spare a patient weeks of NG feeding and the looming risk of aspiration.**
