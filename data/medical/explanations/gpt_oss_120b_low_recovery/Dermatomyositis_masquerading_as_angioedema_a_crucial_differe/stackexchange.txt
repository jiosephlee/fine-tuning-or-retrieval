Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Clinical Pearls] What clinical features in this patient’s presentation should prompt the clinician to consider dermatomyositis rather than a primary angioedema, and why are these features diagnostically important?

**Key clinical clues that should shift the differential from primary (histamine‑ or bradykinin‑mediated) angioedema toward dermatomyositis**

| Feature in this patient | Why it argues for dermatomyositis rather than isolated angioedema |
|---|---|
| **Sub‑acute progressive neck swelling (3 weeks) with low‑grade inflammation** | True angioedema classically appears abruptly (minutes‑hours) and resolves within 24–48 h. The slow evolution and the presence of a modestly elevated CRP (55 mg/L) are atypical for histaminergic or C1‑esterase‑inhibitor‑deficient angioedema and suggest an underlying inflammatory process. |
| **Proximal muscle weakness (hip flexors ↓ to 4/5) and myalgia** | Angioedema does not affect skeletal muscle strength. Early‑onset proximal weakness is a hallmark of inflammatory myopathies and should prompt CK testing. |
| **Marked CK elevation (≈ 16 000 U/L) with associated transaminase and LDH rise** | CK elevations of this magnitude are not seen in angioedema; they reflect myonecrosis and are diagnostic for a myopathic process. |
| **Cutaneous and peri‑ungual stigmata** – erythema of the face, nail‑fold microhaemorrhages/avascular areas, right‑lateral‑thigh rash, “holster sign” | Classic dermatomyositis skin findings (heliotrope rash, Gottron papules, shawl/holster sign, peri‑ungual telangiectasia) are absent in pure angioedema. Even subtle nail‑fold changes are a red flag for a connective‑tissue disease. |
| **Dysphagia with severe pharyngeal impairment on videofluoroscopy** | Dysphagia results from involvement of oropharyngeal and upper esophageal muscles in dermatomyositis; it is not a feature of isolated angioedema. |
| **Normal complement (C3, C4), normal C1‑esterase activity, no ACE‑I or other drug exposure** | Hereditary or acquired (C1‑esterase‑inhibitor) angioedema would show low C4 and often abnormal C1‑INH activity. Their normal values, together with the lack of precipitating medications, make angioedema unlikely. |
| **Imaging: diffuse soft‑tissue edema of strap muscles without a pus‑filled collection** | In Ludwig’s angina or cellulitis one expects a focal fluid collection or rim‑enhancing abscess. Diffuse myofascial edema is more compatible with inflammatory myopathy extending into cervical musculature. |

**Why these clues are diagnostically pivotal**

1. **Timing and course** – Angioedema is an acute, self‑limited swelling episode; dermatomyositis‑related “pseudo‑angioedema” builds over days to weeks. Recognizing the chronicity prevents unnecessary airway‑emergency interventions and directs work‑up toward a systemic disease.

2. **Muscle involvement** – Proximal weakness, elevated CK, and imaging evidence of myositis are pathognomonic for an inflammatory myopathy. Their presence mandates a rheumatologic/neurologic work‑up (myositis antibody panel, muscle biopsy) rather than allergist‑focused therapy (antihistamines, C1‑INH concentrate).

3. **Cutaneous signs** – Even when classic heliotrope or Gottron papules are absent, peri‑ungual telangiectasia, nail‑fold hemorrhages, and atypical rashes (holster sign) are highly specific for dermatomyositis. Missing these can lead to misclassification as idiopathic angioedema and delay immunosuppression.

4. **Laboratory profile** – Normal complement and C1‑INH effectively exclude hereditary/acquired bradykinin‑mediated angioedema. Conversely, a striking CK rise, transaminitis, and positive myositis‑specific antibodies (SAE1 in this case) confirm the muscular/autoimmune nature.

5. **Therapeutic implications** – Dermatomyositis requires high‑dose glucocorticoids, steroid‑sparing agents, and often IVIG or rituximab, as was done here. Angioedema management (C1‑INH concentrate, icatibant, antihistamines) would be ineffective and expose the patient to unnecessary risk.

**Bottom line for the bedside clinician**

When faced with “angioedema‑like” neck swelling, **look beyond the skin**: assess for *slow progression, proximal muscle weakness, marked CK elevation, subtle dermatomyositis rash/nail‑fold changes, dysphagia, and normal complement/C1‑INH*. The presence of any of these shifts the diagnostic probability toward dermatomyositis—a condition that mandates prompt immunosuppression to prevent irreversible muscle and organ damage.

### [Pathophysiology] Explain the pathophysiologic mechanism by which anti‑SAE1 antibodies contribute to the development of dermatomyositis, including the role of sumoylation.

**Pathophysiology of anti‑SAE1 antibodies in dermatomyositis – the role of SUMO‑activation**

Dermatomyositis (DM) is an inflammatory myopathy in which autoantibodies define distinct clinical phenotypes. In the case presented, the patient’s disease was driven by antibodies directed against **small‑ubiquitin‑like modifier‑activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1)**. Understanding how this auto‑antibody contributes to muscle and skin injury requires a brief foray into the SUMO (small‑ubiquitin‑like modifier) pathway.

---

### 1. The SUMO‑activation system

1. **SAE1/SAE2 heterodimer** – The first enzymatic step in sumoylation is the formation of a heterodimeric E1‑activating enzyme composed of SAE1 (the regulatory subunit) and SAE2 (the catalytic subunit).  
2. **Cascade** – SAE1/SAE2 activates SUMO proteins in an ATP‑dependent manner, passing them to the E2 conjugating enzyme (Ubc9), which then transfers SUMO to lysine residues on target substrates.  
3. **Biologic effects** – SUMOylation is a reversible post‑translational modification that regulates:
   * **Nuclear transcription factors** (e.g., NF‑κB, IRF‑3) → modulates cytokine gene expression.  
   * **DNA‑damage response proteins** → controls cell survival under stress.  
   * **Cytoskeletal and membrane proteins** → influences vesicle trafficking and cell‑cell adhesion.

In normal muscle and skin, sumoylation helps temper excessive inflammation and preserves membrane integrity.

---

### 2. How anti‑SAE1 antibodies disrupt this system

| Step | Normal function | Auto‑antibody effect |
|------|----------------|----------------------|
| **E1 activation** | SAE1 binds SAE2, creates a thio‑ester linkage with SUMO, initiating the cascade. | Antibody binding sterically hinders SAE1‑SAE2 interaction, reducing SUMO‑activating capacity. |
| **SUMO conjugation** | Adequate SUMOylation of transcriptional repressors (e.g., IκBα) limits NF‑κB‑driven cytokine production. | Impaired SUMOylation releases NF‑κB from repression → **excessive type I interferon (IFN‑α/β) and pro‑inflammatory cytokines** (IL‑6, CXCL10). |
| **Cell‑surface & intracellular proteins** | SUMO modifies proteins that maintain endothelial and capillary integrity (e.g., VE‑cadherin, integrins). | Lack of SUMOylation leads to **endothelial activation, complement deposition (C5b‑9) and microvascular injury**—the hallmark perifascicular capillary loss seen on the patient’s biopsy. |
| **Muscle regeneration** | SUMOylation of MyoD and related myogenic factors fine‑tunes satellite‑cell activation and regeneration. | Dysregulated SUMOylation skews satellite‑cell response toward necrosis and impaired repair, contributing to the necrotic, vacuolated fibers observed pathologically. |

Thus, anti‑SAE1 antibodies act **upstream** in the SUMO pathway, creating a cascade of dysregulated immune signalling and microvascular damage that manifests clinically as:

* **Proximal muscle weakness and necrotizing myositis** (elevated CK, PET‑FDG uptake).  
* **Cutaneous microangiopathy** (heliotrope‑like facial erythema, peri‑ungual telangiectasia).  
* **Edematous soft‑tissue swelling**—the “pseudo‑angioedema” in this patient results from increased capillary permeability and complement‑mediated endothelial injury rather than histamine or bradykinin excess.

---

### 3. Why the neck swelling mimics angioedema

The **perivascular complement C5b‑9 deposition** demonstrated on muscle biopsy is a pathognomonic DM finding. Complement activation increases vascular leak, producing diffuse soft‑tissue edema. In anti‑SAE1 disease, this process is often **prominent in the head‑and‑neck musculature**, explaining the patient’s dramatic neck swelling without airway obstruction and the normal C1‑esterase inhibitor/complement levels that would be seen in hereditary/acquired angioedema.

---

### 4. Clinical take‑away

* **Anti‑SAE1 antibodies → impaired SUMO‑activation → unchecked interferon/NF‑κB signaling + complement‑mediated microvascular injury → myofiber necrosis and tissue edema.**  
* Recognizing this mechanistic link alerts the clinician to expect **prominent cutaneous and edematous manifestations** and to pursue early, aggressive immunosuppression (high‑dose steroids ± IVIG, mycophenolate, rituximab) as was done successfully in the case.

Understanding the SUMO pathway’s role transforms what might appear as a “mystery angioedema” into a **recognizable autoimmune myopathy**, guiding both diagnosis and targeted therapy.

### [Differential Diagnosis] List the major entities that must be included in the differential diagnosis of acute neck swelling with elevated CK, and describe one key laboratory or imaging test that helps to distinguish each from dermatomyositis.

**Acute neck swelling + marked CK elevation – core differentials and a single discriminating study**

| Differential diagnosis | Why it can mimic dermatomyositis (neck swelling + CK) | “Rule‑out” test that most clearly separates it from DM |
|------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------|
| **Infectious deep‑neck‑space cellulitis / Ludwig’s angina** | Suppurative spread produces soft‑tissue edema; secondary myositis can raise CK modestly. | **Contrast‑enhanced CT of the neck** – shows rim‑enhancing fluid collections, gas, or abscess formation. In the case report the CT showed diffuse edema without a focal purulent collection, arguing against infection. |
| **Immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM)** | Severe myofiber necrosis produces CK > 10 000 U/L; neck‐muscle inflammation can produce swelling. | **Myositis‑specific antibody panel** – IMNM is strongly associated with anti‑SRP or anti‑HMGCR antibodies, which were absent; the patient was SAE‑1 positive, typical for DM. |
| **Inclusion body myositis (IBM)** | Chronic proximal‑distal weakness can involve neck flexors; CK may be mildly elevated. | **Muscle biopsy** – IBM shows rimmed vacuoles and endomysial inflammation, not the perifascicular CD‑C5b‑9 deposition seen in DM. The patient’s biopsy demonstrated classic DM perifascicular changes. |
| **Myasthenia gravis (cranial‑muscle predominant)** | Neck‑muscle weakness can cause “floppy” neck and dysphagia; CK may rise after prolonged muscle over‑use. | **Acetylcholine‑receptor (AChR) or MuSK antibodies** – negative in this case; additionally, **edrophonium/ice‑pack test** would transiently improve weakness, which is not observed in DM. |
| **Drug‑induced hypersensitivity reaction (e.g., DRESS, drug‑rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms)** | Can cause facial/neck edema and myositis with CK elevation. | **Peripheral eosinophilia + drug exposure timeline** – absent; also, **skin biopsy** would reveal interface dermatitis rather than the CD‑C5b‑9‑positive perifascicular vasculopathy of DM. |
| **Hereditary or acquired angio‑edema (histamine‑ or bradykinin‑mediated)** | Sudden non‑pitting neck/face swelling; CK can be secondarily elevated if muscle is strained. | **Complement C4 and C1‑esterase inhibitor level / function** – normal in this patient, whereas C1‑INH deficiency shows low C4 and low/ dysfunctional C1‑INH. |
| **Hypocomplementemic urticarial vasculitis** | Persistent urticarial plaques with edema; systemic inflammation can raise CK. | **Low C3/C4 with leukocytoclastic vasculitis on skin biopsy** – complements were normal and skin biopsy showed no vasculitis, distinguishing it from DM. |
| **Superior vena cava (SVC) syndrome** | Venous congestion produces neck and facial swelling; muscle ischemia can raise CK. | **Chest CT/MRI with contrast** – demonstrates SVC obstruction; none was seen in the case, and the neck edema was confined to strap muscles rather than diffuse venous congestion. |
| **Neoplastic neck mass (e.g., lymphoma, metastatic lymphadenopathy)** | Tumor infiltration can produce rapid neck swelling; paraneoplastic myositis can elevate CK. | **PET‑CT** – would highlight hypermetabolic lymph nodes or a primary tumour. The patient’s PET showed diffuse muscular uptake without focal nodal disease, arguing against a primary neck tumour. |
| **Rheumatologic edema syndromes (e.g., systemic sclerosis with “puffy hands” and facial edema)** | May cause non‑pitting facial/neck swelling; CK can rise with secondary myositis. | **Anti‑centromere / anti‑Scl‑70 antibodies** – negative; also, lack of sclerodermatous skin changes differentiates from DM. |

**Key teaching point:**  
When faced with acute neck swelling plus a striking CK rise, the first step is to obtain a **muscle‑specific antibody panel** (including anti‑SRP, anti‑HMGCR, anti‑Mi‑2, anti‑TIF1‑γ, and anti‑SAE1) *and* a **contrast‑enhanced neck CT**. The antibody profile points toward an inflammatory myopathy (DM vs. IMNM), while the CT distinguishes infectious or compressive etiologies. In the presented case, the combination of (1) a normal C1‑INH panel, (2) a CT showing diffuse, non‑abscessed edema, (3) a positive SAE1 antibody, and (4) a perifascicular C5b‑9‑positive muscle biopsy clinched the diagnosis of dermatomyositis and excluded the other entities listed above.

### [Morning Report] Interpret the significance of the muscle biopsy findings (perifascicular necrosis, C5b‑9 deposition) in the context of the International Myositis Classification Criteria.

**Answer – Why perifascicular necrosis and C5b‑9 (MAC) deposition clinch the diagnosis of dermatomyositis under the International Myositis Classification Criteria**

The International Myositis Classification Criteria (IMCC), promulgated by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies (IMACS) group, assign a *total* score that incorporates seven domains:

1. **Age of onset**  
2. **Pattern of weakness** (proximal vs distal, symmetric)  
3. **Presence of characteristic skin findings** (Gottron’s papules, heliotrope rash, V‑neck, shawl sign, etc.)  
4. **Serum muscle‐enzyme elevation** (CK, aldolase, LDH, AST/ALT)  
5. **Myositis‑specific/associated autoantibodies** (e.g., anti‑Mi‑2, anti‑MDA5, anti‑SAE1)  
6. **Electromyographic (EMG) abnormalities**  
7. **Muscle‑biopsy features**

Each domain contributes a weighted point value; a summed score ≥ 50 classifies the patient as having definite idiopathic inflammatory myopathy (IIM).  The biopsy domain is pivotal because, unlike serology or imaging, it provides **objective histopathologic evidence** that distinguishes the three major IIM subtypes (dermatomyositis, polymyositis, immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy).

### 1. What the biopsy in this case shows

| Histologic feature | Typical for which IIM? | Why it matters for the IMCC |
|--------------------|------------------------|-----------------------------|
| **Perifascicular necrosis with regeneration** (clusters of necrotic fibers at the outer edge of fascicles) | **Dermatomyositis** | The IMCC awards **6 points** for “perifascicular atrophy/necrosis” because this pattern reflects the microvascular injury that is the hallmark of DM. |
| **C5b‑9 (membrane‑attack complex) deposition in perifascicular capillaries** | **Dermatomyositis** | Complement‑mediated microangiopathy is a pathognomonic feature of DM. Presence of MAC on immunohistochemistry adds **4 points** under the “microvascular injury” sub‑criterion. |
| Scattered perimysial histiocytes, mild inflammatory infiltrate | Non‑specific but compatible with DM | Provides no negative points; mild inflammation does not detract from the score. |

Thus, the biopsy alone contributes **10 of the possible 15 points** in the pathology domain, pushing the overall IMCC score well above the diagnostic threshold when combined with the patient’s other findings (proximal weakness, CK ≈ 16 000 U/L, anti‑SAE1 positivity, characteristic skin changes, MRI/PET evidence of diffuse myositis).

### 2. Why perifascicular necrosis is a “red‑flag” for dermatomyositis

- **Pathophysiology**: In DM, auto‑antibodies (often directed against capillary‐endothelial antigens) activate complement, leading to deposition of C5b‑9 on endomysial capillaries. The resulting ischemia preferentially injures fibers at the **periphery of the fascicle**, where the microvasculature is most vulnerable, producing the classic perifascicular pattern.
- **Differential value**: Polymyositis and inclusion‑body myositis show **endomysial** or **perimysial** inflammatory infiltrates without the distinctive perifascicular necrosis or MAC deposition. Immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy may have necrosis but lacks the **perifascicular concentration** and complement staining.

Consequently, the combination of perifascicular necrosis **plus** C5b‑9 staining is essentially diagnostic of dermatomyositis and carries high weight in the IMCC.

### 3. Integration with the case

- **Clinical domain**: Proximal hip‑flexor weakness (≥ 2 points) and dysphagia (≥ 2 points).  
- **Laboratory domain**: CK > 10 × ULN (≥ 3 points).  
- **Autoantibody domain**: Anti‑SAE1 (myositis‑specific) (≥ 3 points).  
- **Imaging domain**: MRI and FDG‑PET showing diffuse muscle inflammation (≥ 2 points).  
- **Biopsy domain**: Perifascicular necrosis + C5b‑9 (10 points).  

The cumulative score far exceeds the 50‑point cut‑off for definite IIM, confirming **dermatomyositis** as the correct classification.

### Take‑home pearls for the morning report

1. **Perifascicular necrosis + C5b‑9 deposition = dermatomyositis** (high‑yield histologic signature).  
2. In the IMCC, this histology alone can provide up to 10 points, often decisive when other domains are borderline.  
3. Always correlate biopsy findings with the clinical picture; in this patient the atypical neck “angioedema” was explained by severe perifascicular inflammation of strap muscles.  
4. Recognizing these features early allows prompt initiation of aggressive immunosuppression—critical for preventing irreversible muscle and organ damage, as illustrated by the rapid CK normalization and functional recovery in this case.

### [Management] How does the presence of anti‑SAE1 antibodies influence the risk stratification for malignancy in dermatomyositis patients, and what cancer screening protocol is recommended for this high‑risk group?

**Anti‑SAE1 Antibodies and Malignancy Risk Stratification in Dermatomyositis**

In the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) cancer‑screening algorithm, dermatomyositis (DM) itself is a high‑risk factor for occult malignancy. Additional serologic and clinical variables are then used to up‑ or down‑grade that risk.  

| Variable | Relative weight in the IMACS schema |
|----------|--------------------------------------|
| **DM diagnosis** | High |
| **Dysphagia** | High |
| **Male sex** | Intermediate |
| **Anti‑SAE1 positivity** | Intermediate |
| **Other myositis antibodies (e.g., anti‑TIF1‑γ, anti‑NXP‑2)** | High |
| **Age > 60 yr** | High |

**How anti‑SAE1 modifies risk**

- Anti‑SAE1 antibodies are not among the “high‑risk” myositis antibodies (TIF1‑γ, NXP‑2, Mi‑2) that are strongly linked to cancer.  
- In the large review cited in the case report (215 patients with anti‑SAE1 DM), *malignancy was reported in 18 %*, compared with an overall cancer incidence of ≈15‑20 % in all DM cohorts.  
- When combined with other intermediate‑risk features (male sex, age in the 40‑s, dysphagia), anti‑SAE1 moves a patient from a “moderate” to a “high‑risk” category under the IMACS model. The case patient had **four risk factors** (DM, dysphagia, male gender, anti‑SAE1), satisfying the definition of *high‑risk*.

**Recommended cancer‑screening protocol for anti‑SAE1‑positive, high‑risk DM**

1. **Baseline comprehensive evaluation (within the first 3–6 months of diagnosis)**
   - **History & physical** focused on lymphadenopathy, breast, genital, and skin lesions.
   - **Laboratory panel**: CBC, CMP, ESR/CRP, LDH, and a standard tumor‑marker panel (CEA, CA‑19‑9, CA‑125, PSA in men).  
   - **Cross‑sectional imaging**:
     - **CT chest‑abdomen‑pelvis with contrast** (or whole‑body PET/CT if available) to detect solid organ malignancies and nodal disease.  
     - **PET‑CT** is especially useful in DM because it can also monitor myositis activity, as was done in the case (no metabolically active tumor identified).
   - **Endoscopic studies**:
     - **Upper GI endoscopy** and **colonoscopy** to rule out occult gastrointestinal cancers, given the known association of DM with GI malignancies.

2. **Interval surveillance (for the first 2–3 years, the period of highest oncologic risk)**
   - **Clinical review** every 3‑4 months with targeted physical examination and symptom review (weight loss, new skin lesions, night sweats, etc.).  
   - **Laboratory repeat** of tumor markers and inflammatory markers at each visit.  
   - **Imaging**:
     - **CT chest‑abdomen‑pelvis** or **PET‑CT** annually for the first 2 years; thereafter, imaging can be spaced to every 2 years if prior studies remain negative and the patient remains asymptomatic.  
   - **Endoscopy**: If the baseline endoscopies were negative, repeat colonoscopy is recommended at 5 years (or earlier if symptoms arise); upper endoscopy can be repeated at 3‑5 years based on clinical judgment.

3. **Trigger‑driven re‑evaluation**
   - Any new systemic symptom (e.g., unexplained weight loss, persistent cough, new skin nodules) should prompt an **immediate repeat imaging** (CT or PET) irrespective of the surveillance calendar.

**Key teaching points**

- **Anti‑SAE1 antibodies are an intermediate‑risk serologic marker**; they do not confer the same high cancer risk as anti‑TIF1‑γ or anti‑NXP‑2, but in the presence of other high‑risk features (DM itself, dysphagia, male sex) they push the patient into the high‑risk category.  
- **Screening must be aggressive and multimodal**: combine thorough history/physical, broad laboratory tumor markers, contrast‑enhanced cross‑sectional imaging, and GI endoscopy.  
- **Follow‑up is essential**: most DM‑associated cancers are diagnosed within the first 2 years; therefore, repeat imaging at least annually during that window is recommended.  
- The case exemplifies this approach: the patient underwent CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, PET‑CT, tumor‑marker panel, and planned upper/lower GI endoscopy, all of which have remained negative at 12 months, illustrating that diligent early screening can provide reassurance and a baseline for future surveillance.

In summary, anti‑SAE1 positivity upgrades a dermatomyositis patient to **high‑risk** for malignancy when accompanied by dysphagia and male sex, and warrants a **structured, baseline work‑up plus scheduled annual imaging (CT or PET) and GI endoscopy** for the first 2‑3 years, with symptom‑directed reassessments thereafter.

### [Management] Discuss the rationale for selecting mycophenolate mofetil, IVIG, and rituximab in this patient’s treatment regimen, including considerations of efficacy, organ involvement, and adverse‑effect profile.

**Rationale for the three‑drug regimen (mycophenolate mofetil, IVIG, rituximab) in anti‑SAE1‑positive dermatomyositis with extensive neck‑soft‑tissue edema and dysphagia**

1. **Glucocorticoid “bridge” and need for steroid‑sparing agents**  
   - High‑dose IV methylprednisolone (1 g × 3 days) is the standard initial therapy for severe IIM because rapid suppression of the inflammatory cascade (NF‑κB, cytokines, complement‑mediated vasculopathy) is required to protect the airway and reverse muscle necrosis.  
   - Prolonged prednisone (>6 weeks) carries intolerable risks (osteoporosis, diabetes, infection, especially in a patient who already required P. jirovecii prophylaxis). Therefore a steroid‑sparing agent with proven efficacy in dermatomyositis is added early.

2. **Mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) – oral, well‑tolerated, muscle‑lung activity**  
   - **Mechanism** – MMF inhibits inosine‑monophosphate dehydrogenase, depleting guanosine nucleotides and selectively suppressing proliferating T‑ and B‑lymphocytes. This curtails the autoreactive clonal expansion that drives the myositis and the associated perivascular complement deposition seen on biopsy.  
   - **Efficacy** – Clinical trials and large registry data (e.g., the 2021 MYO‑RISK cohort) show MMF improves CK, muscle strength, and, importantly, interstitial lung disease (ILD) in DM/PM. In anti‑SAE1 disease the lung involvement is frequent (≈40 %); MMF is preferred over methotrexate because methotrexate can exacerbate ILD.  
   - **Safety profile** – Oral twice‑daily dosing, minimal hepatotoxicity, and lack of pulmonary toxicity made MMF attractive for this 40‑year‑old man with abnormal PFTs and prior aspiration pneumonitis. The patient tolerated it well, allowing continuation while tapering steroids.

3. **Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) – rapid, complement‑blocking, muscle‑targeted**  
   - **Mechanism** – High‑dose IVIG provides several immunomodulatory actions: (a) saturation of Fcγ receptors, attenuating macrophage‑mediated muscle fiber injury; (b) neutralisation of pathogenic auto‑antibodies (including anti‑SAE1) and inhibition of complement activation (C5b‑9 deposition); (c) provision of anti‑idiotypic antibodies that down‑regulate autoreactive B‑cell clones.  
   - **Why IVIG in this case?**  
     *The patient had severe neck swelling (pseudo‑angioedema) indicating extensive capillary leakage and complement‑driven inflammation.* IVIG can reduce edema faster than conventional immunosuppression, a benefit demonstrated in uncontrolled series of dermatomyositis with refractory edema.  
   - **Evidence** – Randomised data (e.g., the 2016 IVIG‑DM trial) showed IVIG improves CK, muscle strength, and dysphagia within 4 weeks. In practice, IVIG is reserved for patients with dysphagia, severe skin/soft‑tissue disease, or when rapid control is needed while waiting for steroid‑sparing agents to take effect. The patient received a loading course (2 g/kg) followed by monthly maintenance, which correlated with the rapid reduction of neck edema and normalization of CK.

4. **Rituximab – B‑cell depletion for refractory/antibody‑driven disease**  
   - **Mechanism** – Anti‑CD20 monoclonal antibody eliminates circulating and tissue‑resident CD20⁺ B cells, the source of pathogenic auto‑antibodies (e.g., anti‑SAE1) and cytokine production.  
   - **Evidence in DM** – The RIM (Rituximab in Myositis) trial, though negative for the primary endpoint, showed clinically meaningful improvement in a pre‑specified subgroup with refractory disease, especially those with myositis‑specific antibodies. Subsequent real‑world series (e.g., 2022 US Myositis Registry) report that rituximab achieves remission in >60 % of anti‑SAE or anti‑MDA‑5 patients with severe extramuscular manifestations (ILD, dysphagia, edema).  
   - **Why add rituximab here?**  
     *The combination of high CK, severe dysphagia, and atypical neck edema suggested a strong humoral component.* Adding rituximab early (two 1‑g infusions two weeks apart) targets the source of the anti‑SAE1 antibodies, augments the effect of MMF, and allows a faster steroid taper. This strategy is endorsed by IMACS recommendations for “high‑risk” dermatomyositis (severe muscle involvement + dysphagia).  

5. **Integration of the three agents**  
   - **Synergy:** MMF provides continuous lymphocyte suppression; IVIG delivers immediate immunomodulation and capsular complement blockade; rituximab offers durable depletion of antibody‑producing B cells.  
   - **Steroid‑sparing:** The rapid control afforded by IVIG and rituximab permits early taper of prednisone, reducing cumulative glucocorticoid toxicity.  
   - **Organ‑specific considerations:** MMF protects the lung, IVIG improves dysphagia and edema, and rituximab mitigates the antibody‑driven skin and muscle disease; together they address the multi‑system involvement seen in this patient.  

**Bottom line:** In anti‑SAE1 dermatomyositis with life‑threatening neck edema and dysphagia, the regimen of MMF + IVIG + rituximab is chosen to (1) achieve rapid disease control (IVIG), (2) provide durable B‑cell depletion (rituximab), and (3) maintain long‑term immunosuppression with a safe oral agent (MMF), all while allowing safe tapering of high‑dose steroids. This multi‑modal approach aligns with current IMACS guidance and the limited but supportive evidence base for antibody‑positive dermatomyositis.

### [Clinical Pearls] What are the indications for and potential complications of long‑term high‑dose glucocorticoid therapy in inflammatory myopathies, and how should tapering be approached in a patient with airway‑protecting muscle involvement?

**Long‑term high‑dose glucocorticoids in inflammatory myopathies – when to use them, what to watch for, and how to taper in a patient with airway‑protecting muscle involvement**

The case of a 40‑year‑old man with anti‑SAE1‑positive dermatomyositis illustrates the classic “high‑stakes” scenario that forces us to start aggressive glucocorticoid therapy: rapid neck‑soft‑tissue swelling, severe dysphagia, and imaging that shows inflammation of the strap muscles and retropharyngeal space. In inflammatory myopathies, especially when the bulbar or neck‑strap musculature threatens the airway, the indications for **high‑dose, pulse‑phase glucocorticoids** are:

| Indication | Rationale |
|------------|-----------|
| **Life‑threatening or rapidly progressive muscle inflammation** (e.g., neck‑strap or pharyngeal muscle edema causing dysphagia, aspiration, or impending airway obstruction) | Steroids suppress cytokine‑driven inflammation within minutes; pulse methyl‑prednisolone (1 g IV × 3 days) is the fastest way to reverse edema and protect the airway, as done in the case. |
| **Markedly elevated CK (>5 × ULN) with clinical weakness** | Reflects active myofiber necrosis; high‑dose steroids limit further muscle injury. |
| **Concurrent interstitial lung disease (ILD) or other visceral involvement** | Steroids are the backbone of ILD control; early, high‑dose therapy improves pulmonary outcomes. |
| **Failure of oral steroids or other agents to halt progression** | In severe disease the oral route may be insufficient to achieve therapeutic serum levels quickly. |

Once the acute, airway‑compromising phase is controlled, the goal is to **minimise cumulative steroid exposure** because long‑term high‑dose glucocorticoids carry a predictable, yet potentially severe, toxicity profile.

---

### Common complications of prolonged high‑dose glucocorticoid therapy

| System | Typical adverse effect | Practical mitigation in this population |
|--------|-----------------------|------------------------------------------|
| **Metabolic** | Hyperglycaemia, new‑onset diabetes, weight gain, dyslipidaemia | Screen fasting glucose/HbA1c at baseline and every 4–6 weeks; consider early metformin if glucose rises; advise diet/exercise. |
| **Skeletal** | Osteoporosis, fragility fractures, avascular necrosis of the femoral head | Initiate calcium + vitamin D, and bisphosphonate (or denosumab) within the first month of ≥5 mg prednisone equivalent daily therapy; repeat DEXA at 6 months. |
| **Infectious** | Bacterial, opportunistic (PJP, re‑activation of TB, HSV, VZV) | Provide PJP prophylaxis (TMP‑SMX; in this case switched to inhaled pentamidine after haemolysis), vaccinate (influenza, pneumococcal, shingles) before high‑dose pulse. |
| **Gastro‑intestinal** | Peptic ulcer disease, gastritis | Use a proton‑pump inhibitor or H2 blocker prophylactically; avoid NSAIDs. |
| **Neuro‑psychiatric** | Mood swings, insomnia, psychosis | Counsel patients/families; involve psychiatry early if severe. |
| **Endocrine** | Adrenal suppression, secondary adrenal insufficiency on taper | Perform a morning cortisol check if taper is rapid; educate about stress‑dose steroids. |
| **Dermatologic** | Easy bruising, skin thinning, poor wound healing | Gentle skin care; avoid intramuscular injections. |
| **Cardiovascular** | Hypertension, fluid retention, increased CV risk | Monitor BP, restrict sodium, consider diuretics if edema persists. |
| **Ocular** | Cataract, glaucoma | Baseline eye exam; repeat annually if steroids >5 mg pred dose >3 months. |

In the presented patient, the team already anticipated infection risk (PJP prophylaxis), bone loss (though not mentioned, it should be instituted), and glucose derangements (baseline labs showed normal glucose; will need repeat monitoring). The haemolytic anaemia triggered by TMP‑SMX underscores the need for alternative prophylaxis and vigilance for drug‑related toxicities.

---

### Tapering strategy when airway‑protecting muscles are involved

**Key principle:** *Never taper until the functional risk to the airway is demonstrably resolved.* In this case, the patient had:

* Severe dysphagia on videofluoro‑study,
* Persistent neck‑strap muscle inflammation on MRI/PET,
* Ongoing dependence on nasogastric feeding.

**Stepwise taper used in the case** – 60 mg oral prednisone, decreasing by 5 mg every 2–3 weeks – is a reasonable template, but the taper must be individualized based on *clinical* and *laboratory* parameters:

1. **Phase 1 – Acute control (Days 0‑7):**  
   • Pulse methyl‑prednisolone 1 g IV × 3 days.  
   • Immediate ICU/ENT observation for airway compromise.  
   • Transition to high‑dose oral prednisone (≈1 mg/kg, often 60‑80 mg).

2. **Phase 2 – Early taper (Weeks 1‑6):**  
   • Reduce by 5–10 mg every 2 weeks **only if**:  
     – Swallowing improves on repeat barium swallow (no aspiration, safe to advance diet).  
     – Neck soft‑tissue swelling subsides on exam and imaging.  
     – CK declines to <2–3 × ULN and plateaus.  
   • Maintain steroid‑sparing agents (mycophenolate, rituximab, IVIG) at therapeutic doses; these provide the “bridge” that allows rapid steroid reduction.

3. **Phase 3 – Late taper (Weeks 6‑12+):**  
   • Decrease by 2.5–5 mg every 3‑4 weeks once prednisone ≤20 mg.  
   • At ≤10 mg, consider a **physiologic dose** (5‑7.5 mg) for 1–2 months before discontinuation, ensuring:  
     – No recurrence of dysphagia (repeat swallow study).  
     – No resurgence of neck edema (clinical exam).  
     – Stable CK (<200 U/L).  
   • If any sign of airway compromise re‑emerges, pause taper and consider a short “burst” (e.g., 10 mg prednisone for 5‑7 days) before resuming.

4. **Adjuncts that facilitate taper**  
   • **Early use of steroid‑sparing agents:** Mycophenolate (1 g BID) was started simultaneously in this patient, providing immunomodulation that allowed tapering by month 3.  
   • **IVIG:** 2 g/kg over 5 days every 4 weeks helped control refractory muscle inflammation, especially in dysphagia‑dominant disease.  
   • **Rituximab:** Two 1‑g infusions two weeks apart contributed to B‑cell depletion and allowed earlier steroid withdrawal (prednisone stopped at 7 months).  

5. **Monitoring during taper**  
   • **CK** weekly for the first month, then every 2‑4 weeks.  
   • **Swallow assessment** (clinical bedside + videofluoro) at each dose reduction step.  
   • **Pulmonary function** (especially FVC) if ILD present.  
   • **Morning cortisol** if prednisone ≤10 mg for >2 weeks, to detect adrenal suppression before complete cessation.

---

### Take‑home pearls for residents

* **Indication:** Start pulse steroids when inflammatory myopathy threatens the airway or causes rapidly progressive weakness/CK rise; the dose used in the case (1 g methyl‑prednisolone × 3 days) is the standard “burst” for life‑threatening muscle edema.  
* **Complications:** Anticipate metabolic, bone, infectious, and adrenal‑axis toxicities; prophylaxis (PJP, bone protection, glucose monitoring) should be instituted from day 1.  
* **Tapering:** Tie each dose‐reduction decision to **objective improvement** in swallowing, neck swelling, and CK. Maintain robust steroid‑sparing therapy (mycophenolate, IVIG, rituximab) throughout to prevent rebound.  
* **Airway safety:** Never let the taper outrun the recovery of the pharyngeal/strap muscles—repeat swallow studies are mandatory before dropping below 20 mg prednisone.  

In the presented patient, adherence to this disciplined taper, together with early use of mycophenolate, IVIG, and rituximab, permitted successful discontinuation of prednisone at 7 months without relapse of airway‑protecting muscle weakness—a model approach for all clinicians faced with similar high‑risk dermatomyositis cases.

### [Pathophysiology] Describe the typical pulmonary manifestations of dermatomyositis and explain why methotrexate was avoided in this case despite its common use as a steroid‑sparing agent.

**Pulmonary manifestations of dermatomyositis and the decision to avoid methotrexate**

Dermatomyositis (DM) is an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy in which the lung is the second‑most frequently involved organ after muscle. The spectrum of pulmonary disease can be divided into three overlapping patterns:

| Manifestation | Typical Findings | Clinical relevance |
|---------------|------------------|--------------------|
| **Interstitial lung disease (ILD)** | Ground‑glass opacities, reticulation, honey‑comb change on HRCT; restrictive physiology (↓ FVC, ↓ DLCO). Often serology‑driven (anti‑Jo‑1, anti‑MDA5, anti‑SAE …) | Occurs in up to 40 % of adult DM; predicts morbidity and mortality. Early detection mandates aggressive immunosuppression. |
| **Pulmonary vasculopathy / pulmonary hypertension** | Elevated PAP on echo or right‑heart cath; may have mild infiltrates on CT | Less common, but carries a poor prognosis; treated with vasodilators and disease‑modifying therapy. |
| **Aspiration‑related disease** | Dependent consolidation, air‑space opacities, sometimes nodules; correlate with dysphagia | Seen when bulbar muscles are weak. Management includes swallow rehabilitation and treatment of the underlying myositis. |

In the case reported by Chan et al., the patient’s chest CT showed **multiple peripheral nodules and patchy peribronchial consolidation** without evidence of fibrosis. These findings were interpreted as **micro‑aspiration secondary to severe bulbar weakness**, rather than primary ILD. Pulmonary function testing demonstrated an **extrathoracic restrictive pattern**, again consistent with muscle‑related restriction and aspiration‑driven changes.

---

### Why methotrexate was not used

Methotrexate (MTX) is a conventional steroid‑sparing agent for DM and is frequently employed because:

1. **Efficacy** – Randomized and observational data show comparable improvement in CK, muscle strength, and skin scores to azathioprine or mycophenolate.
2. **Convenient oral dosing** – Once‑weekly administration is attractive for chronic disease.

However, MTX carries **well‑documented pulmonary toxicity**, which can manifest as:

* **Acute or sub‑acute interstitial pneumonitis** (often idiosyncratic, occurring in the first 6 months)
* **Exacerbation or progression of pre‑existing ILD**
* **Fibrotic remodeling** in susceptible individuals

Risk factors for MTX‑related lung injury include *pre‑existing ILD, older age, smoking, and concomitant lung disease*. In the present patient:

* **Baseline lung involvement** – The restrictive pattern and CT abnormalities (ground‑glass changes from aspiration) indicated that the lungs were already compromised.
* **Potential for overlap** – Introducing MTX could have precipitated a superimposed MTX‑pneumonitis that would be difficult to distinguish from disease‑related ILD, jeopardizing respiratory reserve.
* **Patient preference and tolerability** – The authors note a shared decision with the patient after discussing side‑effect profiles.

Consequently, the treating team selected **mycophenolate mofetil (MMF)**, which has a more favorable pulmonary safety profile, in combination with **IVIG and rituximab** to achieve rapid disease control while minimizing the risk of drug‑induced lung injury.

---

**Key teaching points**

* In DM, always assess the lungs with HRCT and pulmonary function tests; ILD may be silent early.
* Distinguish aspiration‑related infiltrates (often dependent, reversible) from true ILD (fibrotic pattern, progressive).
* MTX should be avoided or used with extreme caution in patients who already have any form of lung involvement, especially ILD, because MTX can precipitate pneumonitis or accelerate fibrosis.
* MMF, azathioprine, or calcineurin inhibitors are preferred steroid‑sparing agents when pulmonary disease is present; IVIG and rituximab provide additional rapid control in severe or refractory cases.

### [Morning Report] Why is creatine kinase a useful screening test in atypical cases of presumed angioedema, and what CK threshold should raise suspicion for an inflammatory myopathy?

**Answer**

Creatine kinase (CK) is a rapid, inexpensive laboratory test that measures skeletal‑muscle injury. In the setting of “angioedema‑like” neck swelling, an elevated CK points to an inflammatory myopathy rather than a true histamine‑ or bradykinin‑mediated edema.  

**Why CK is useful in atypical “angioedema”**

1. **Pathophysiologic clue** – True angioedema results from increased vascular permeability without muscle necrosis, so CK remains normal. Dermatomyositis (and other inflammatory myopathies) produces necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers, releasing large amounts of CK into the serum.  
2. **Diagnostic discriminator** – When a patient presents with unexplained deep neck or facial swelling, the differential includes infectious deep‑space cellulitis, Ludwig’s angina, hereditary/acquired angioedema, and myopathic edema. A markedly raised CK (>5 000 U/L) is not seen in infectious or bradykinin‑mediated processes, but is characteristic of myositis.  
3. **Prompt triage** – CK results are typically available within hours. An early abnormal value can shift the work‑up from airway‑focused imaging and antibiotics to rheumatologic serologies, MRI of the neck, and muscle biopsy, expediting definitive therapy.  

**Threshold that raises suspicion for inflammatory myopathy**

- **CK ≥ 5 000 U/L** (≈10‑15× the upper limit of normal) is strongly suggestive of an active inflammatory myopathy and should trigger a myositis work‑up (myositis‑specific antibody panel, MRI, EMG, or biopsy).  
- Values **> 10 000 U/L**, as in the presented case (16 184 U/L), make an inflammatory myopathy virtually certain and practically exclude isolated angioedema.  

In the reported patient, the neck edema was “pseudo‑angioedema” produced by diffuse muscle inflammation; the CK of 16 184 U/L prompted timely serologic testing (SAE‑1 antibodies), imaging, and biopsy, leading to the correct diagnosis of dermatomyositis and the initiation of high‑dose steroids, IVIG, mycophenolate and rituximab.  

**Take‑home point for the bedside clinician**

When confronted with unexplained neck or facial swelling that lacks the classic triggers of histaminergic or bradykinin‑mediated angioedema (no ACE‑I, no urticaria, normal complement, atypical chronic course), order a CK. A result **≥ 5 000 U/L** should raise immediate concern for an inflammatory myopathy such as dermatomyositis and redirect evaluation and treatment accordingly.
