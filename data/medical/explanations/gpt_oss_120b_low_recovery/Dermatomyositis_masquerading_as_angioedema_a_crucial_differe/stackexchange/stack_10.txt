Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Clinical Pearls] How does dysphagia in dermatomyositis arise pathophysiologically, and what monitoring and supportive measures should be instituted during acute management?

**Pathophysiology of dysphagia in dermatomyositis**

In dermatomyositis the immune‐mediated attack is directed at the microvasculature of skeletal muscle (perifascicular capillary loss, complement C5b‑9 deposition) and at the muscle fibres themselves.  The resulting myositis produces inflammation, edema, and necrosis in the cranio‑pharyngeal musculature—namely the suprahyoid, pharyngeal constrictors, and upper esophageal sphincter.  When these muscles become inflamed they lose contractile strength and generate excess interstitial fluid, which together impair bolus transport and airway protection.  The clinical consequence is a **mechanical dysphagia** that may be apparent on videofluoroscopic swallow studies as:

* prolonged pharyngeal transit,
* residue in the pyriform sinuses,
* penetration or aspiration of thin liquids,
* reduced elevation of the larynx.

Because the disease process is inflammatory rather than neuropathic, the dysphagia often improves rapidly with effective immunosuppression, as illustrated in the reported case (resolution after high‑dose steroids, IVIG, mycophenolate and rituximab).

**Monitoring and supportive measures during the acute phase**

1. **Airway vigilance**  
   * Perform a bedside swallow assessment and obtain a video‑fluoroscopic swallow study early; any signs of aspiration (cough, hypoxia, or radiographic infiltrates) should prompt consideration of supplemental oxygen, bronchoscopy, or ICU monitoring.  
   * In severe neck‑muscle involvement (as in the case), keep a low threshold for ENT or anesthesia consultation in case of airway compromise.

2. **Nutritional support**  
   * Insert a nasogastric tube (NGT) when the patient cannot maintain safe oral intake, as was done here.  
   * If dysphagia persists beyond 2–3 weeks or if the NGT is poorly tolerated, transition to a percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) to avoid aspiration pneumonia and to allow continuation of oral medications.

3. **Laboratory and biochemical surveillance**  
   * Serial CK, AST/ALT, LDH, and inflammatory markers (CRP, ESR) track muscle inflammation and response to therapy.  
   * Monitor complete blood count, renal function, and drug‑specific toxicities (e.g., mycophenolate – CBC, LFTs; rituximab – immunoglobulins, hepatitis B screen).

4. **Infection prophylaxis**  
   * High‑dose steroids plus additional immunosuppressants confer a high risk of opportunistic infection.  Provide Pneumocystis jirovecii prophylaxis (TMP‑SMX, or nebulised pentamidine if intolerance) and consider antiviral prophylaxis if B‑cell depletion (rituximab) is used.

5. **Physical therapy and speech‑language therapy**  
   * Early involvement of PT and SLP helps maintain residual muscle strength, teaches safe swallowing techniques, and facilitates weaning from tube feeding.

6. **Medication administration route**  
   * Ensure all oral agents (e.g., mycophenolate, prednisone) are given via the NGT until swallowing is sufficient, confirming compatibility with tube feeding formulas.

7. **Serial imaging and functional testing**  
   * Repeat videofluoroscopic swallow studies every 2–4 weeks to document improvement and decide on tube removal.  
   * Pulmonary function tests and chest imaging should be repeated to assess for aspiration‑related lung injury and interstitial lung disease, both of which can be aggravated by dysphagia.

**Take‑home point**

Dysphagia in dermatomyositis results from inflammatory edema and weakness of the pharyngeal and upper esophageal muscles.  During the acute phase the clinician must (1) protect the airway, (2) secure nutrition via NGT/PEG, (3) monitor disease activity and treatment toxicity with serial labs, (4) provide infection prophylaxis, and (5) engage multidisciplinary rehab services.  Prompt, aggressive immunosuppression—as demonstrated in this patient—leads to rapid reversal of the muscular inflammation and eventual resolution of dysphagia.
