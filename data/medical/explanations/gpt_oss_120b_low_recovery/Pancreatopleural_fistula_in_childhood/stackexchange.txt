Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

### [Clinical Pearls] What pleural fluid laboratory finding is considered pathognomonic for a pancreatopleural fistula, and why is the type of amylase important in differentiating it from other causes of amylase‑rich effusions?

**Pathognomonic pleural‑fluid finding** – A pleural‑fluid amylase concentration ≥ 1 000 IU/L (often several thousand, as in this child’s 6 118 U/L) is considered diagnostic for a pancreatopleural fistula (PPF).  

**Why the amylase type matters** – Amylase exists in two iso‑enzymes: **pancreatic (P‑type)** and **salivary (S‑type)**. In PPF the fluid contains **pancreatic‑type amylase**, reflecting direct drainage of pancreatic secretions through the fistulous tract into the pleural space. By contrast, other amylase‑rich effusions—such as those from esophageal rupture (Boerhaave’s syndrome), malignant lung or pancreatic tumors, or tuberculosis—show a predominance of **salivary‑type amylase** (or a mixed pattern) because the excess enzyme derives from leakage of upper‑digestive or bronchial secretions rather than pancreatic juice.  

**Clinical reasoning illustrated by the case**  

1. **Recurrent massive, blood‑stained effusions** prompted thoracentesis; routine studies were nondiagnostic.  
2. **Serum and pleural fluid amylase and lipase were markedly elevated** (pleural amylase ≈ 6 000 U/L), far above the 1 000 U/L threshold.  
3. **Iso‑enzyme typing (if performed) would have confirmed pancreatic‑type amylase**, separating PPF from tuberculosis (mildly elevated ADA), malignancy, or esophageal perforation—each of which can raise total amylase but usually with a salivary profile.  
4. **Imaging (CT, MRCP)** then visualized the fistulous track from the pancreatic tail through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity, completing the diagnosis.

Thus, in any patient with an unexplained, recurrent, hemorrhagic pleural effusion, a pleural‑fluid amylase ≥ 1 000 IU/L—preferably characterized as pancreatic‑type—is the hallmark of a pancreatopleural fistula and should prompt cross‑sectional imaging of the pancreas and thoraco‑abdominal junction. Early recognition avoids unnecessary thoracic interventions and directs therapy toward bowel rest, somatostatin analogues, and definitive pancreatic drainage.

### [Morning Report] In a child presenting with recurrent unilateral, blood‑stained pleural effusions, what key historical element should prompt consideration of a pancreatic origin rather than primary thoracic disease?

**Key historical clue:** A recent episode of *blunt abdominal trauma* (in this child, a bicycle‑handle injury about one month before the first pleural effusion).  

**Why this matters**

1. **Pathophysiology** – Traumatic disruption of the pancreatic duct or a peripancreatic pseudocyst allows pancreatic secretions to leak posteriorly into the retro‑peritoneum. From there the fluid follows the path of least resistance—usually the esophageal or aortic hiatus—into the mediastinum and finally ruptures through the pleura, producing a large, often hemorrhagic pleural effusion.  

2. **Clinical presentation** – The child’s dominant problem is respiratory (recurrent unilateral, blood‑stained pleural effusions, chest pain, dyspnoea). Abdominal symptoms are often absent or mild, so the thoracic picture masks the pancreatic source. When the history reveals a recent blunt abdominal injury, the diagnostic focus should shift from primary lung disease (infection, TB, malignancy) to a pancreatopleural fistula.  

3. **Diagnostic implications** – Once trauma is recognized, the next steps are:
   - Measure amylase/lipase in serum **and** pleural fluid (levels >1 000 U/L are highly suggestive of a pancreatic fistula).
   - Obtain cross‑sectional imaging that includes the pancreas (CT or MRCP). In the reported case, CT demonstrated a hypodense collection tracking from the para‑esophageal region to the pancreatic tail, and MRCP confirmed a fistulous tract.  

4. **Management consequence** – Early suspicion based on the trauma history avoids unnecessary pulmonary interventions (e.g., empiric anti‑TB therapy, repeated thoracenteses) and directs therapy toward pancreatic rest (NPO, TPN), somatostatin analogues (octreotide), and drainage of the thoracic collection. In the case described, this approach led to cessation of the effusion within 10 days.  

**Take‑home point for the morning report:**  
When a child presents with recurrent unilateral, blood‑stained pleural effusion, *always ask about recent abdominal trauma* (or any episode that could injure the pancreas). This single historical element should prompt measurement of pleural fluid amylase and targeted abdominal imaging, steering you toward the diagnosis of a pancreatopleural fistula rather than a primary thoracic disease.

### [Pathophysiology] Describe the anatomic pathway by which pancreatic secretions from a posterior pancreatic duct disruption can reach the pleural cavity, and explain why the effusion is often right‑sided.

**Pathway of a posterior pancreatic‑duct disruption to the pleural cavity**

1. **Location of the injury** – When trauma (or a ruptured pseudocyst) breaches the **posterior aspect of the pancreatic duct** in the body or tail, pancreatic juice is released directly into the **retro‑peritoneal space** because the pancreas lies largely retroperitoneally.

2. **Retro‑peritoneal tracking** – From the site of disruption the fluid follows the path of least resistance along **loose fascial planes**. It climbs superiorly in the **posterior mediastinum** because the retro‑peritoneal tissue is continuous with the mediastinal connective tissue through the **diaphragmatic hiatuses**.

3. **Hiatus of communication** – The two diaphragmatic apertures that most readily allow upward propagation are:
   - **Esophageal (oesophageal) hiatus** – a muscular opening at T10 that permits the esophagus and vagal trunks to pass. It is relatively wide and lies slightly to the **right of the midline**.
   - **Aortic hiatus** – at T12, posterior to the diaphragm, also providing a conduit for the aorta and thoracic duct.  
   In most reported pediatric PPFs, as in this boy, the fluid preferentially uses the **esophageal hiatus** (MRCP showed a tubular collection extending through this hiatus).

4. **Mediastinal/pleural entry** – Once the fluid reaches the **right pleural space** through the hiatus, it breaches the parietal pleura (often where the pleura is thinnest adjacent to the mediastinum) and accumulates as a **right‑sided pleural effusion**. Because the fluid is rich in pancreatic enzymes, it quickly becomes **hemorrhagic and exudative**, producing the dark‑red thoracentesis described.

**Why the effusion is most often right‑sided**

- **Anatomic asymmetry of the hiatuses** – The esophageal hiatus is located **right‑ward** of the vertebral column; the aortic hiatus lies slightly left of midline but is more posterior and less directly connected to the pleural cavity. Fluid that ascends through the esophageal hiatus therefore reaches the **right pleural cavity first**.
- **Retro‑peritoneal fascial planes** – The right retro‑peritoneal space is less compartmentalised by the posterior parietal peritoneum than the left, allowing easier upward migration on the right side.
- **Liver “shield” effect** – The liver buttresses the right hemidiaphragm, making direct trans‑diaphragmatic erosion less likely; instead the fluid uses the pre‑existing hiatal route, which deposits it on the right.
- **Clinical observation** – In the series cited in the report, 68 % of pancreatic‑pleural fistulas were right‑dominant, matching the described anatomic route.

Thus, a posterior duct disruption dumps pancreatic secretions into the retro‑peritoneum, the fluid ascends through the right‑ward esophageal (or occasionally aortic) hiatus, traverses the mediastinum, and breaches the right pleura, producing the characteristic right‑sided, enzyme‑rich pleural effusion seen in this child.

### [Differential Diagnosis] List the imaging modalities used to diagnose pancreatopleural fistula and compare their sensitivity for directly visualizing the fistulous tract.

**Imaging work‑up of a pancreatopleural fistula (PPF): What studies actually show the fistulous tract?**  

| Modality | What it depicts | Reported sensitivity for *direct* fistula visualization* | Practical role in the case |
|----------|----------------|-----------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------|
| **Contrast‑enhanced CT (abdomen‑chest)** | Pancreatic parenchyma, pseudocysts, retro‑peritoneal fluid collections, pleural effusion; can suggest a tract when a low‑density “string” of fluid extends from pancreas to pleura. | **33 %–47 %** – the tract is seen only when the fluid track is large enough to be resolved on axial images. | In the boy, CT demonstrated a multiloculated hypodense collection that *tracked* from the para‑esophageal region to the pancreatic tail, raising suspicion for a PPF, but it did not directly delineate a patent fistulous channel. |
| **Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography (MRCP)** | T2‑weighted pancreatic ductal anatomy and fluid‑filled tracts; excellent for demonstrating communication between a pancreatic duct/pseudocyst and the pleural cavity. | **70 %–80 %** – the fistula is directly visualized in the majority of cases because the high‑contrast fluid on MRCP outlines the narrow ductal‑to‑pleural connection. | MRCP in this patient *confirmed* bilateral (right > left) pancreatopleural fistulas, showing a tubular cystic collection extending through the esophageal hiatus into the pleural space. |
| **Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography (ERCP)** | Direct opacification of the pancreatic duct and any abnormal side‑branches or leaks; allows therapeutic stenting. | **38 %–78 %** (reported in pediatric series). When the leak is active, contrast extravasation into the pleural cavity can be captured, giving the highest anatomic fidelity, but the rate varies with ductal anatomy and technical factors. | ERCP is the *gold‑standard* for therapeutic planning (duct stenting, sphincterotomy) and, when performed, can demonstrate the fistulous communication. In this case it was not required because MRCP already provided a clear anatomic map. |
| **Ultrasound (abdominal or thoracic)** | Detects pancreatic fluid collections, pleural effusion; can guide drainage. | **<20 %** for fistula visualization – limited by acoustic windows and inability to track retro‑diaphragmatic pathways. | Used only to confirm the large pleural effusion and guide thoracentesis; did not contribute to fistula identification. |
| **Fluoroscopic fistulography (percutaneous injection of contrast into a collection)** | Directly outlines the tract when contrast is poured into a known pseudocyst or retro‑peritoneal fluid. | **Variable, 40 %–70 %**; highly operator dependent and rarely performed nowadays. | Not employed in this case. |

\*Sensitivity figures are drawn from the series cited in the case report’s discussion and from contemporary reviews of pediatric PPF imaging. They reflect the proportion of studies that **directly visualized the communicating channel**, not merely indirect signs (e.g., a fluid collection abutting the diaphragm).

**Key teaching points**

1. **CT is often the first cross‑sectional study** because it is readily available and can suggest a PPF by showing a fluid track from the pancreas to the pleural space, but its ability to *directly* depict the narrow fistulous lumen is limited (≈ 1/3 of cases).  
2. **MRCP is the most sensitive non‑invasive test** for direct fistula visualization (≈ 75 %); the high‑contrast fluid column on heavily T2‑weighted sequences makes a thin duct‑to‑pleura connection conspicuous. In our boy, MRCP clinched the diagnosis.  
3. **ERCP remains the reference standard** when therapeutic intervention (ductal stenting) is planned; it can demonstrate a leak in up to three‑quarters of pediatric patients, but its invasive nature and the need for expertise limit its use as a pure diagnostic tool.  

**Bottom line:** When a pancreatopleural fistula is suspected, start with a contrast‑enhanced CT to look for a retro‑diaphragmatic fluid track, then obtain an MRCP for definitive non‑invasive delineation of the fistulous tract. Reserve ERCP for cases that will benefit from endoscopic therapy or when MRCP is equivocal.

### [Management] What are the first‑line conservative management steps for a pediatric pancreatopleural fistula, and what is the expected timeline to assess response before escalating therapy?

**First‑line conservative management of a pediatric pancreatopleural fistula (PPF)**  

1. **Bowel rest (nil per os)** – eliminates the stimulus for pancreatic exocrine secretion.  
2. **Total parenteral nutrition (TPN)** – provides caloric support while the gut is rested and prevents catabolism.  
3. **Somatostatin analogue (octreotide)** – given intravenously at 10‑40 µg kg⁻¹ day⁻¹ (the case used 30 µg kg⁻¹ day⁻¹). Octreotide suppresses pancreatic enzyme output, reducing the volume of fluid leaking through the fistula.  
4. **Pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT)** – supplies the necessary digestive enzymes to the gut once oral intake is resumed, but does not stimulate pancreatic secretion.  
5. **Adequate thoracic drainage** – placement of an intercostal chest tube (or repeated thoracenteses) to evacuate the pleural collection and allow assessment of output.  
6. **Supportive care** – supplemental oxygen, analgesia, and monitoring of fluid‑electrolyte balance.  

These steps comprise the “medical” arm of treatment and are appropriate when imaging shows a fistulous tract without an obstructive pancreatic duct lesion that would mandate immediate endoscopic or surgical intervention.

**Expected timeline for response before escalation**

- **Initial observation period:** 7‑10 days of strict NPO + TPN + octreotide while the chest tube remains in place.  
- **Clinical endpoint:** a progressive decline in chest‑tube drainage to < 20‑30 mL day⁻¹ (or complete cessation) and radiographic resolution/partial improvement of the pleural effusion. In the reported child, drainage fell to zero by day 10, at which point the tube was removed.  

If after **10‑14 days** (or sooner if the output remains > 100 mL day⁻¹, the patient remains symptomatic, or imaging shows persistent large collections) there is no meaningful reduction in drainage, the next step is **endoscopic therapy** (ERCP with pancreatic duct stenting or balloon dilation). Persistent high output beyond **3‑4 weeks** of optimal medical therapy is the usual trigger for surgical consideration (distal pancreatectomy, cystojejunostomy, etc.).

**Take‑home point:** In children with PPF, start with bowel rest, TPN, octreotide, PERT, and effective pleural drainage. Expect a demonstrable fall in chest‑tube output within the first 10 days; failure to achieve this within 2 weeks warrants escalation to endoscopic or surgical management. This algorithm reproduced the successful outcome in the presented case, where the fistula closed after 10 days of conservative therapy and the child was discharged asymptomatic.

### [Management] When conservative therapy fails in a child with PPF, what endoscopic intervention is preferred, what is its mechanism of action, and what are the typical success rates reported in pediatric series?

**Answer – Endoscopic trans‑papillary stenting for refractory pediatric pancreatopleural fistula**

When a child with a pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) does not respond to the initial 10‑ to 14‑day course of bowel rest, total parenteral nutrition, octreotide and thoracic drainage, the next step that most authors recommend is **endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) with placement of a trans‑papillary pancreatic duct stent**.

**Why this technique is preferred**

1. **Pathophysiologic rationale**  
   - The fistula persists because pancreatic secretions continue to leak through a disrupted duct or a pseudocyst into the retro‑peritoneum and up through the diaphragmatic hiatus.  
   - A plastic stent across the major papilla into the main pancreatic duct creates a low‑pressure “drainage conduit” that diverts the bulk of exocrine flow downstream into the duodenum, thereby **reducing intraductal pressure** at the site of disruption.  
   - Lower downstream pressure allows the ruptured ductal wall to approximate and heal, while the existing fistulous tract gradually fibroses and closes.

2. **Technical advantages in children**  
   - ERCP is now routinely performed in pediatric centers with adult‑size scopes and dedicated pediatric accessories.  
   - The procedure is minimally invasive, avoids a laparotomy, and can be repeated if needed.  
   - Stent dwell times of **4–6 weeks** are usually sufficient; the stent is then removed endoscopically.

**Reported success in pediatric series**

| Series (children) | Number of pts | Endoscopic strategy | Primary closure rate |
|-------------------|--------------|---------------------|----------------------|
| Yang et al., 2021 (25 pts) | 25 | ERCP ± pancreatic duct stent | 48 % fistula closure within 2–3 wks; overall success ≈ 100 % when stent placed |
| Uchiyama et al., mixed age (including children) | — | Trans‑papillary stent | Long‑term success > 90 % |
| Surgical series (for comparison) | 15 | Distal pancreatectomy, Puestow, etc. | 93 % immediate success (but higher morbidity) |
| Overall pediatric literature (≤ 47 reported PPF) | — | Endoscopic stent after failed medical therapy | **~48 % rapid closure**, and when a stent could be placed the **overall technical success approaches 100 %** (i.e., the duct is successfully cannulated and stented). |

Thus, in the setting of failed conservative therapy, **ERCP‑guided trans‑papillary pancreatic duct stenting** is the preferred endoscopic intervention. Its mechanism—decompression of the pancreatic ductal system and diversion of secretions—produces fistula closure in roughly half of children within a few weeks, and when the duct can be successfully accessed the procedural success rate is essentially 100 %. If stenting is not feasible (e.g., severe distal duct obstruction or inaccessible fistula), surgical options are considered thereafter.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Identify three non‑pancreatic conditions that can also produce pleural fluid with markedly elevated amylase, and indicate one clinical or laboratory clue that helps distinguish each from a PPF.

**Non‑pancreatic causes of a markedly amylase‑rich pleural effusion (≥1 000 U/L) and the key distinguishing feature for each**

| Condition | Why amylase is high in the pleura | One practical clue that steers you away from a pancreatopleural fistula |
|-----------|-----------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Esophageal perforation (Boerhaave’s syndrome)** | Leakage of saliva‑rich fluid (salivary‑type amylase) into the mediastinum and pleural space after a full‑thickness tear. | **Acute onset after forceful vomiting or retching** – the patient typically reports a sudden, severe chest/upper‑abdominal pain, often with subcutaneous emphysema. A contrast esophagogram (water‑soluble) will demonstrate extravasation, whereas pancreatic imaging is normal. |
| **Malignancy (lung adenocarcinoma, lymphoma, metastatic pancreatic or ovarian carcinoma)** | Tumor cells can produce amylase (often salivary iso‑enzyme) or cause malignant pleural effusion that becomes secondarily enzyme‑rich. | **Pleural fluid cytology showing malignant cells or a markedly lymphocyte‑predominant exudate with high LDH**. In addition, systemic features such as weight loss, night sweats, or a radiographic mass on chest CT point toward malignancy rather than a retro‑peritoneal pancreatic source. |
| **Tuberculous pleuritis** | Mycobacterial infection can stimulate pleural mesothelial cells to release amylase (usually salivary type) and also raise ADA. | **Pleural fluid ADA > 40 U/L together with a positive interferon‑γ release assay or GeneXpert for MTB**, and a clinical picture of fever, night sweats, and a known TB exposure. The effusion is typically lymphocyte‑rich, and imaging shows pleural thickening without any pancreatic pathology. |

**Teaching point:**  
When you encounter an exudative pleural effusion with a **very high amylase level**, the first step is to determine the **iso‑enzyme pattern** (pancreatic vs. salivary). Pancreatic‑type amylase (P‑amylase) virtually confirms a pancreatopleural fistula, whereas salivary‑type amylase suggests one of the above extra‑pancreatic entities. Correlate the amylase result with the clinical context (vomiting, TB exposure, systemic malignancy signs) and targeted imaging (esophagogram, chest CT, abdominal/pancreatic MRI) to avoid mis‑diagnosis, as illustrated in the presented child whose trauma‑related pancreatic injury was initially obscured by a misleading TB work‑up.

### [Pathophysiology] Discuss the role of genetic testing in pediatric chronic pancreatitis and its relevance to the development of pancreatopleural fistula.

**Answer – The role of genetic testing in paediatric chronic pancreatitis and its link to pancreatopleural fistula (PPF)**  

1. **Why we test for genes in a child with pancreatitis**  
   • In children, chronic pancreatitis (CP) is *rare* and, unlike adults, is *almost never* due to alcohol. The most frequent etiologies are **hereditary** (≈ 30 % of paediatric CP) and **anatomic ductal abnormalities**. The main genes repeatedly implicated are **PRSS1, SPINK1, CFTR** and **CTRC**.  
   • Identification of a pathogenic variant has three practical consequences:  
   – **Prognosis** – certain mutations (e.g., PRSS1 R122H) are associated with early‐onset, severe, recurrent disease and a higher likelihood of complications such as pseudocyst formation.  
   – **Management** – knowledge of a genetic basis prompts early, aggressive measures (enzyme replacement, dietary modification, avoidance of pancreatic‐toxic drugs) and often justifies early referral for endoscopic or surgical drainage before irreversible ductal damage occurs.  
   – **Family counseling** – autosomal‑dominant mutations (PRSS1, CFTR) merit cascade testing of first‑degree relatives and counseling about recurrence risk, which can influence organ‑donor eligibility, surveillance, and future reproductive decisions.  

2. **Genetics and the development of a pancreatopleural fistula**  
   • A PPF results from a **rupture of a disrupted pancreatic duct or a pseudocyst** that tracks retro‑peritoneally through the aortic or oesophageal hiatus into the pleural cavity. The *driving force* is a persistent high intraductal pressure.  
   • In hereditary pancreatitis, **mutations that increase intra‑ductal pressure** (PRSS1 gain‑of‑function, SPINK1 loss‑of‑inhibition) predispose to **recurrent ductal leaks** and to formation of large fluid collections that can erode the diaphragm. Consequently, patients with these mutations are *more likely* to develop rare extra‑abdominal fistulae, including PPF.  
   • The case report under discussion illustrates a *trauma‑initiated* PPF, but the authors note that “idiopathic cases continue to appear which may be due to probable under‑recognised genetic or ductal variants.” In the 47 paediatric PPF cases reported to date, roughly one‑third have an underlying **genetic mutation** (the same proportion seen in paediatric CP overall). Thus, even when a precipitant such as blunt injury is identified, a **genetic predisposition** may have lowered the threshold for ductal rupture.  

3. **Practical approach for the clinician**  
   • **Step 1 – Recognise chronic pancreatitis**: recurrent abdominal pain, elevated serum amylase/lipase, imaging showing ductal dilation or pseudocyst. In a child with unexplained pleural effusion and markedly high pleural amylase, think “PPF secondary to CP.”  
   • **Step 2 – Order a targeted gene panel** (PRSS1, SPINK1, CFTR, CTRC). This can be done after the acute episode once the child is clinically stable; results are available within weeks and do not delay emergent management of the fistula.  
   • **Step 3 – Integrate results**:  
     – **Positive pathogenic variant** → counsel family, consider earlier endoscopic stenting or surgical drainage to prevent further leaks, institute pancreatic enzyme replacement, and avoid pancreatic‑stimulating foods.  
     – **Negative panel** → pursue evaluation for anatomic anomalies (MRCP/ERCP) and consider the trauma as the primary driver, as in the presented case.  

4. **Take‑home message for trainees**  
   - **Genetic testing is not optional** in paediatric chronic pancreatitis; it is a cornerstone of the diagnostic work‑up.  
   - **A positive mutation explains why a relatively modest insult (e.g., bicycle‑handle trauma) can produce a dramatic complication such as a pancreatopleural fistula** by having already compromised ductal integrity.  
   - **Even when trauma is the obvious trigger, a genetic evaluation should be performed** because the presence of a hereditary mutation will alter long‑term management and surveillance for recurrent pancreatic complications, including PPF.  

By incorporating genetic testing into the assessment of every child with recurrent or chronic pancreatitis, we not only refine the immediate therapeutic plan (medical vs endoscopic vs surgical) but also anticipate and potentially prevent rare sequelae such as pancreatopleural fistula.

### [Morning Report] Why was antitubercular therapy initiated in this patient, and what specific test results ultimately led to its discontinuation?

**Answer**

The child was started on antitubercular therapy (ATT) because, on the second admission, the pleural fluid analysis showed a **mildly elevated adenosine deaminase (ADA)** and there was a **known household contact with pulmonary tuberculosis** (grandfather treated for “Koch’s”). In practice, an exudative pleural effusion with ADA > 35 U/L in a patient with a credible TB exposure raises a high pre‑test probability of tuberculous pleuritis, and many clinicians will begin empiric ATT while awaiting confirmatory tests. In this case the team followed that algorithm and instituted a full four‑drug ATT regimen after the first two thoracenteses yielded dark‑red, blood‑stained fluid.

The decision to discontinue ATT was made after **definitive evidence of a pancreatic source of the effusion** was obtained:

1. **Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP)** demonstrated a tubular cystic collection in the distal pancreas extending through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity – the classic imaging appearance of a **pancreatopleural fistula (PPF)**.  
2. **Pleural fluid biochemistry** revealed **markedly elevated amylase (6 118 U/L) and lipase (7 211 U/L)**, levels far above the threshold (>1 000 U/L) that is considered pathognomonic for PPF.  
3. **Microbiologic testing** for Mycobacterium tuberculosis (GeneXpert) on the pleural fluid was negative, further lowering the likelihood of tuberculous pleuritis.

When the imaging and the biochemical profile clearly identified pancreatic secretions as the source of the recurrent hemorrhagic effusions, the working diagnosis shifted from tuberculous pleuritis to a post‑traumatic PPF. At that point the ATT was stopped, and the patient was treated with bowel rest, total parenteral nutrition, octreotide, and pleural drainage, leading to resolution of the fistula.

**Key teaching points**

- **Elevated pleural ADA** in the setting of TB exposure is a useful screening tool but is not diagnostic; confirmatory microbiology or alternative explanations must be sought before committing to prolonged ATT.  
- **Pleural fluid amylase > 1 000 U/L** is highly specific for a pancreatic origin (PPF, pancreaticopleural fistula, or pancreatic cancer) and should prompt abdominal imaging, especially when the clinical picture includes recurrent, bloody effusions and a history of abdominal trauma.  
- **MRCP** is the non‑invasive modality of choice to visualize the fistulous tract and guide management; once demonstrated, ATT should be discontinued unless concurrent TB is proven.  

Thus, in this case ATT was initiated on the basis of an elevated ADA and TB contact, and it was stopped when MRCP and pleural amylase/lipase levels unequivocally established a pancreatopleural fistula as the cause of the effusions.
