Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

### [Morning Report] Why was antitubercular therapy initiated in this patient, and what specific test results ultimately led to its discontinuation?

**Answer**

The child was started on antitubercular therapy (ATT) because, on the second admission, the pleural fluid analysis showed a **mildly elevated adenosine deaminase (ADA)** and there was a **known household contact with pulmonary tuberculosis** (grandfather treated for “Koch’s”). In practice, an exudative pleural effusion with ADA > 35 U/L in a patient with a credible TB exposure raises a high pre‑test probability of tuberculous pleuritis, and many clinicians will begin empiric ATT while awaiting confirmatory tests. In this case the team followed that algorithm and instituted a full four‑drug ATT regimen after the first two thoracenteses yielded dark‑red, blood‑stained fluid.

The decision to discontinue ATT was made after **definitive evidence of a pancreatic source of the effusion** was obtained:

1. **Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP)** demonstrated a tubular cystic collection in the distal pancreas extending through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity – the classic imaging appearance of a **pancreatopleural fistula (PPF)**.  
2. **Pleural fluid biochemistry** revealed **markedly elevated amylase (6 118 U/L) and lipase (7 211 U/L)**, levels far above the threshold (>1 000 U/L) that is considered pathognomonic for PPF.  
3. **Microbiologic testing** for Mycobacterium tuberculosis (GeneXpert) on the pleural fluid was negative, further lowering the likelihood of tuberculous pleuritis.

When the imaging and the biochemical profile clearly identified pancreatic secretions as the source of the recurrent hemorrhagic effusions, the working diagnosis shifted from tuberculous pleuritis to a post‑traumatic PPF. At that point the ATT was stopped, and the patient was treated with bowel rest, total parenteral nutrition, octreotide, and pleural drainage, leading to resolution of the fistula.

**Key teaching points**

- **Elevated pleural ADA** in the setting of TB exposure is a useful screening tool but is not diagnostic; confirmatory microbiology or alternative explanations must be sought before committing to prolonged ATT.  
- **Pleural fluid amylase > 1 000 U/L** is highly specific for a pancreatic origin (PPF, pancreaticopleural fistula, or pancreatic cancer) and should prompt abdominal imaging, especially when the clinical picture includes recurrent, bloody effusions and a history of abdominal trauma.  
- **MRCP** is the non‑invasive modality of choice to visualize the fistulous tract and guide management; once demonstrated, ATT should be discontinued unless concurrent TB is proven.  

Thus, in this case ATT was initiated on the basis of an elevated ADA and TB contact, and it was stopped when MRCP and pleural amylase/lipase levels unequivocally established a pancreatopleural fistula as the cause of the effusions.
