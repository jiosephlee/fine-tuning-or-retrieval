Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

Chapter 4: ## Therapeutic Management and Clinical Course  

The patient’s management required a balanced approach that addressed three overlapping problems: a presumed acute inflammatory process of the central nervous system, the systemic inflammatory disease of Behçet’s disease (BD), and the electrolyte disturbance caused by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH).  The therapeutic plan was therefore built in stages, each guided by evolving diagnostic information and careful monitoring of clinical and laboratory parameters.

### Empiric antimicrobial therapy  

On presentation the combination of fever, headache, and a modest lymphocytic pleocytosis in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) raised the specter of a bacterial central nervous system infection.  Because bacterial meningitis can progress rapidly and carries a high mortality, empiric broad‑spectrum antibiotics were initiated promptly after obtaining blood cultures.  Cefepime (2 g intravenously every 12 h) was chosen because of its excellent coverage of common gram‑negative and gram‑positive pathogens, including *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, and its ability to achieve therapeutic concentrations in the CSF when the meninges are inflamed.  

Within 48 h the CSF profile remained non‑purulent (protein 0.97 g/L, lymphocyte predominance, no organisms on Gram stain), and cultures remained sterile.  In the absence of any focal infectious source on imaging, and with a negative work‑up for typical bacterial pathogens, the antimicrobial was de‑escalated on day 3.  Cefepime was discontinued without a switch to narrower agents, reflecting a judgment that an infectious etiology was unlikely and that further antibiotic exposure would confer only toxicity risk.

### Antiviral coverage and its withdrawal  

Because viral meningoencephalitis—especially herpes simplex virus (HSV) and varicella‑zoster virus (VZV)—remains a treatable cause of acute meningitis, intravenous acyclovir (10 mg/kg every 8 h) was added on day 3 when the patient’s headache and vertigo worsened.  Acyclovir was continued until definitive viral studies (CSF PCR for HSV‑1/2, VZV, and cryptococcal antigen) returned negative on day 5.  Given the lack of clinical or laboratory evidence of viral infection, acyclovir was stopped on day 5, avoiding unnecessary nephrotoxicity and further drug burden.

### Initiation of colchicine  

With the exclusion of infection and the emergence of genital ulceration, the diagnostic focus shifted to Behçet’s disease.  Colchicine (0.6 mg orally twice daily) was commenced on day 8 as the first disease‑specific therapy.  Colchicine binds to tubulin, preventing microtubule polymerisation, which in turn blocks neutrophil chemotaxis, degranulation, and the release of pro‑inflammatory cytokines such as IL‑1β and IL‑6.  These actions are particularly relevant to BD, in which neutrophil hyper‑reactivity and IL‑6–driven inflammation underlie both mucocutaneous lesions and neuro‑inflammatory spikes.  The dosing schedule (0.6 mg BID) is the standard adult regimen for BD‑related mucocutaneous disease and is well tolerated in elderly patients; renal and hepatic function were monitored because colchicine clearance is reduced in organ dysfunction.

### Supportive care for SIADH  

The patient’s serum sodium of 124 mmol/L, low serum osmolality (267 mOsm/kg), inappropriately concentrated urine (900 mOsm/kg) and elevated urine sodium (88 mmol/L) fulfilled the classic criteria for SIADH.  Because the hyponatraemia was profound yet neurologically stable, the first‑line therapy was fluid restriction.  A restriction target of <800 mL/day was imposed, consistent with guidelines that recommend limiting free water intake to 0.5–1 L/day in moderate‑to‑severe SIADH.  

Serum sodium was measured every 6 h for the first 48 h, then daily, to detect over‑correction (>10 mmol/L in the first 24 h) which can precipitate osmotic demyelination.  No hypertonic saline bolus or vasopressin‑2‑receptor antagonist (e.g., tolvaptan) was required because the sodium rose gradually (by ≈4 mmol/L per day) and the patient remained euvolemic without signs of cerebral edema.  The fluid‑restriction protocol was continued until the ADH level fell to 1.8 pg/mL on day 16 and serum sodium normalized at 136 mmol/L.

### Adjunctive topical therapy for mucocutaneous lesions  

While systemic colchicine targeted the underlying inflammatory cascade, local measures were employed to expedite wound healing and prevent secondary infection.  Oral aphthous ulcers received a topical triamcinolone acetonide 0.1 % paste applied 3–4 times daily; the low‑dose steroid provided anti‑inflammatory relief without systemic absorption.  Genital ulcers and facial folliculitis‑like lesions were dressed with silver sulfadiazine cream, chosen for its broad antimicrobial coverage and mild anti‑inflammatory effect.  These topical agents were continued for two weeks, at which point the lesions had resurfaced and were no longer symptomatic.

The sequence above illustrates that once colchicine was introduced, systemic inflammation subsided rapidly, which was mirrored by a fall in CRP, normalization of CK (indicating resolution of rhabdomyolysis), and disappearance of neurologic complaints.  Importantly, the correction of hyponatraemia tracked the decline in ADH, supporting the concept that neuro‑inflammation was the driver of SIADH rather than an extrinsic drug effect.

### Rationale for avoiding corticosteroids and other immunosuppressants  

Current guidelines for acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease recommend high‑dose intravenous glucocorticoids (e.g., methylprednisolone 1 g/day for 3–5 days) as first‑line therapy, often followed by a tapering oral course or adjunctive immunosuppressants such as azathioprine or cyclophosphamide for severe parenchymal disease.  In this patient, several considerations favored a corticosteroid‑sparing strategy:

1. **Mild neurologic phenotype** – The CSF revealed only modest pleocytosis and protein elevation, and the MRI was normal.  There were no focal deficits, mass effect, or brain‑stem involvement, all of which are predictors of a more aggressive disease course that usually necessitates steroids.

2. **Rapid clinical response to colchicine** – Within 48 h of colchicine initiation the patient’s fever subsided, CRP fell sharply, and neurological symptoms improved, suggesting that neutrophil‑driven inflammation was the dominant pathogenic mechanism and could be controlled without steroids.

3. **Comorbidity profile** – The patient had uncontrolled type‑2 diabetes mellitus.  High‑dose glucocorticoids would have markedly worsened glycaemic control, increasing the risk of hyperglycaemic emergencies and infection.  Avoiding steroids thus reduced iatrogenic morbidity.

4. **Risk of steroid‑induced SIADH** – Glucocorticoids can worsen water retention and potentiate ADH activity; in a patient already manifesting SIADH, steroid use might have delayed hyponatraemia correction.

5. **Absence of refractory disease** – After 10 days of colchicine, the patient’s inflammatory markers and sodium were normalizing, obviating the need for escalation to more potent immunosuppression.

Collectively, these factors justified proceeding with colchicine monotherapy and close supportive care, reserving corticosteroids for a potential flare that did not materialize.  The patient’s uneventful 4‑year follow‑up, without additional immunosuppressive agents, underscores that in selected cases of acute neuro‑BD with limited CNS involvement, colchicine may provide sufficient disease control while sparing the patient from steroid‑related adverse effects.
