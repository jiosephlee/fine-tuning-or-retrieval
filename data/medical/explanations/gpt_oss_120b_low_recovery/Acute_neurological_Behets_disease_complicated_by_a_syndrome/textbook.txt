Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

## Case Presentation and Initial Assessment  

The patient was a man in his early 60 s of Asian ethnicity who arrived at the emergency department after three days of progressive systemic symptoms. His past medical history was notable only for untreated type 2 diabetes mellitus. He was unaware of his diabetic status, did not adhere to any dietary or exercise regimen, and therefore had no regular medical follow‑up. He denied recent travel, animal exposure, or high‑risk sexual activity. Social history was otherwise unremarkable: occasional alcohol use, no tobacco use, and employment as a metal‑working laborer. There was no family history of Behçet’s disease or other autoimmune disorders.

### Chronology of presenting complaints  

- **Day –3 to 0**: Onset of low‑grade fever (up to 38 °C), worsening frontal headache, generalized malaise, loss of appetite, and persistent hiccups.  
- **Day –2 to 0**: Symmetrical arthralgia affecting the shoulders and ankles without overt swelling or limitation of motion.  
- **Day –1 to 0**: Development of painful oral ulcers (aphthae).  

He denied visual changes, photophobia, neck stiffness, focal neurologic deficits, or recent trauma. The combination of fever, headache, and arthralgia raised the initial suspicion of an infectious or inflammatory process involving the central nervous system (CNS) or a systemic rheumatologic condition.

### Physical examination  

The patient was alert, oriented, and appeared fatigued but was hemodynamically stable. Vital signs on arrival were:

- **Temperature**: 38.1 °C  
- **Blood pressure**: 135/73 mm Hg  
- **Heart rate**: 119 beats·min⁻¹ (regular)  
- **Respiratory rate**: 19 breaths·min⁻¹  
- **SpO₂**: 96 % on ambient air  

Inspection of the oral cavity revealed multiple shallow ulcerations on the buccal mucosa and tongue, each consistent with aphthous lesions. Cutaneous examination showed erythematous papules with follicular pustulation on the face and trunk, described as “folliculitis‑like” rashes. By day 5 a painful ulcer on the scrotum appeared, reinforcing the suspicion of a systemic mucocutaneous disorder. No palpable lymphadenopathy, joint swelling, or tenderness was noted. Neurologic assessment demonstrated normal cranial nerve function, no nuchal rigidity, and intact motor strength throughout all extremities; sensory testing was unremarkable. The patient’s volume status was clinically euvolemic, with no peripheral edema, jugular venous distension, or signs of dehydration.

### Initial laboratory investigations  

A broad panel was obtained to evaluate infection, inflammation, metabolic derangements, and possible endocrine causes of hyponatraemia:

| Parameter | Result | Reference | Interpretation |
|-----------|--------|-----------|----------------|
| Serum Na⁺ | **124 mmol/L** | 135–145 | Marked hyponatraemia |
| Serum osmolality | **267 mOsm/kg** | 275–295 | Hypo‑osmolar state |
| Urine Na⁺ | **88 mmol/L** | >20 (in hyponatraemia) | Inappropriately high |
| Urine osmolality | **900 mOsm/kg** | >300 | Concentrated urine |
| ADH (plasma) | **22.4 pg/mL** | <5 (approx.) | Elevated despite hyponatraemia |
| CRP | **180 mg/L** | <3 | Severe acute‑phase response |
| ESR | **70 mm/h** | <15 | Marked inflammation |
| CK | **1 349 U/L** | <200 | Rhabdomyolysis or muscle injury |
| AST / ALT | 77 U/L / 64 U/L | ≤40 | Mild hepatic injury |
| Glucose | **14.2 mmol/L** | 3.9–7.8 | Hyperglycaemia |
| HbA1c | **8.2 %** | <5.7 | Poor chronic glycaemic control |
| TSH / free T4 | 1.11 mIU/L / 1.25 ng/dL | Normal | Thyroid function intact |
| Serum cortisol | 42 µg/dL | >6 | Adequate adrenal reserve |

The electrolyte pattern—low serum sodium with low serum osmolality, high urine sodium, and inappropriately high urine osmolality—met the classic diagnostic criteria for the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). Elevated ADH corroborated this diagnosis. The inflammatory markers (CRP, ESR) were markedly raised, supporting a systemic inflammatory or infectious process. CK elevation suggested either an inflammatory myopathy, exertional muscle injury, or a metabolic consequence of severe hyponatraemia (rhabdomyolysis).

### Initial imaging  

Urgent non‑contrast computed tomography (CT) of the head was performed to exclude acute intracranial hemorrhage, mass effect, or gross infection; it was unremarkable. A chest radiograph showed no infiltrates, effusions, or mediastinal widening. Contrast‑enhanced CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis was also normal, providing no evidence of occult infection, neoplastic disease, or pulmonary pathology that might contribute to SIADH. These negative imaging findings, together with the clinical picture, prompted a broadened differential diagnosis encompassing infectious meningitis/encephalitis, autoimmune or autoinflammatory CNS disease, paraneoplastic processes, and drug‑induced SIADH (though the patient had not received any medications known to provoke ADH secretion).

In summary, the initial assessment identified a febrile, hyponatraemic, euvolemic adult with mucocutaneous ulcerations and systemic inflammation. The constellation of fever, headache, CSF‑compatible aseptic meningitis (later confirmed), and SIADH guided clinicians toward a differential that prioritized infectious meningitis, neuro‑inflammatory disorders (including neuro‑Behçet’s disease), and neoplastic mimics, setting the stage for targeted cerebrospinal fluid analysis and further immunogenetic testing.

## Diagnostic Reasoning: From Differential Diagnosis to Definitive Diagnosis  

The patient’s initial presentation – fever, headache, generalized malaise, and profound hyponatraemia – demanded a broad, systematic differential that incorporated infectious, inflammatory, neoplastic, and endocrine possibilities. The subsequent work‑up was directed at clarifying which of these categories best accounted for the constellation of clinical findings and laboratory abnormalities, and then applying disease‑specific diagnostic criteria to arrive at a definitive diagnosis of Behçet’s disease (BD), acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD), and syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH).

### 1. Formulating a Differential for Fever, Headache, and Hyponatraemia  

When a patient presents with fever and headache, central nervous system (CNS) infection is the first consideration, especially meningitis or encephalitis. Hyponatraemia, however, broadens the list because it may be a consequence of the infectious process (e.g., meningitis‑associated SIADH) or a separate endocrine disturbance. The differential therefore included:  

* **Infectious etiologies** – bacterial meningitis, viral encephalitis (herpes simplex virus, varicella‑zoster virus), tuberculous meningitis, fungal meningitis, and atypical infections (e.g., Lyme disease).  
* **Inflammatory/autoimmune CNS disorders** – acute neuro‑BD, neurosarcoidosis, systemic lupus erythematosus with CNS involvement, and other vasculitides.  
* **Neoplastic processes** – primary CNS lymphoma or metastatic disease, which can produce meningeal irritation and SIADH.  
* **Endocrine/metabolic causes of hyponatraemia** – SIADH secondary to CNS inflammation, adrenal insufficiency, hypothyroidism, and volume depletion (e.g., due to vomiting or diuretic use).  

The presence of mucocutaneous lesions (oral aphthae, folliculitis‑like rash, later genital ulcer) tipped the balance toward a systemic inflammatory disease, but at presentation these lesions were not yet sufficient to anchor the diagnosis, so a comprehensive work‑up was warranted.

### 2. Cerebrospinal Fluid Analysis: Recognising an Aseptic Meningitis Pattern  

A lumbar puncture was performed on day 3 because the patient’s headache and vertigo worsened despite empiric antibiotics. The CSF findings were:

* Opening pressure: mildly elevated (17 cm H₂O)  
* Protein: 0.97 g/L (↑)  
* Cell count: 11 cells/µL with lymphocytic predominance  
* Glucose: normal (not reported as low)  
* Cultures and polymerase‑chain‑reaction (PCR) assays: negative for bacteria, mycobacteria, HSV, VZV, and cryptococcus  
* Cytology: no malignant cells  

This profile is classic for **aseptic (non‑bacterial) meningitis**: modest pleocytosis dominated by lymphocytes, elevated protein, and normal glucose. The negative microbiological studies effectively excluded the most common infectious agents (bacterial, HSV, VZV, TB, fungal). Because the CSF was not markedly inflammatory (no neutrophil predominance, low opening pressure), a primary bacterial meningitis was unlikely, and the negative PCRs made viral encephalitis improbable. Importantly, the CSF findings did not demonstrate the high protein (>1 g/L) or marked pleocytosis (>100 cells/µL) typical of CNS lymphoma, further narrowing the differential.

### 3. Brain MRI: Interpreting a Normal Study in Neuro‑Behçet’s Disease  

Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain, performed on day 3, revealed **no focal lesions** in the brainstem, basal ganglia, thalamus, or hypothalamus – the regions most commonly affected in neuro‑BD. While a normal MRI does not exclude neuro‑BD, it does inform the diagnostic process in two ways:

1. **Exclusion of structural lesions** – The absence of space‑occupying lesions, demyelination, or typical vasculitic enhancement argues against neoplastic, demyelinating, or large‑vessel vasculitic processes.  
2. **Recognition of early disease** – Acute neuro‑BD can present with only meningeal inflammation (aseptic meningitis) before parenchymal lesions become radiographically apparent. Thus, a normal MRI is compatible with early‑stage neuro‑BD, especially when supported by CSF and clinical data.

Given that the MRI did not demonstrate hypothalamic involvement, the diagnosis of SIADH could not be attributed to a discrete hypothalamic lesion; the hyponatraemia was instead ascribed to inflammation‑mediated dysregulation of ADH release.

### 4. Applying the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease  

The International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD) allocate points for specific clinical manifestations:

| Manifestation            | Points |
|--------------------------|--------|
| Oral aphthosis           | 2      |
| Genital aphthosis        | 2      |
| Ocular lesions (uveitis) | 2      |
| Skin lesions (e.g., folliculitis‑like) | 1 |
| Vascular lesions         | 1      |
| Neurological involvement| 1      |
| Positive pathergy test   | 1      |

A score **≥4** establishes “definite” BD. In this patient:

* Oral aphthae – 2 points  
* Genital ulcer (appeared on day 5) – 2 points  
* Folliculitis‑like rash – 1 point  

Total = 5 points, satisfying the ICBD threshold. HLA‑B51 positivity, while supportive, is not required for the diagnosis and was therefore considered ancillary. The absence of ocular disease did not detract from the score, and the early appearance of genital ulcers solidified the BD diagnosis.

### 5. Diagnostic Criteria for Acute Neuro‑Behçet’s Disease  

Neuro‑BD lacks a universally accepted formal set of criteria; instead, diagnosis rests on:

1. **Established systemic BD** (per ICBD)  
2. **Neurological manifestation** – new‑onset CNS symptoms (headache, vertigo, meningeal signs) within the disease course  
3. **Objective evidence of CNS inflammation** – CSF pleocytosis or elevated protein, or MRI abnormalities (if present)  
4. **Exclusion of alternative causes** – infectious, neoplastic, other autoimmune CNS disorders  

In this case, the patient met all four pillars: a confirmed systemic BD, acute CNS symptoms with CSF evidence of aseptic meningitis, normal MRI that did not contradict neuro‑BD, and an exhaustive exclusion of infection, malignancy, and other systemic inflammatory diseases. Consequently, a diagnosis of **acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease** – defined as meningoencephalitis‑type presentation without established parenchymal lesions – was made.

### 6. Confirming SIADH: Laboratory Criteria and Exclusion of Mimics  

The diagnostic work‑up for hyponatraemia adhered to the classic criteria for SIADH (Bartter & Schwartz, 1967), which require:

1. **Hypotonic hyponatraemia** – serum osmolality < 275 mOsm/kg (patient: 267 mOsm/kg)  
2. **Inappropriately concentrated urine** – urine osmolality > 100 mOsm/kg (patient: 900 mOsm/kg)  
3. **Elevated urinary sodium** – > 30 mmol/L (patient: 88 mmol/L)  
4. **Euvolemic clinical status** – absence of edema, orthostatic hypotension, or signs of volume depletion  
5. **Absence of renal, adrenal, or thyroid dysfunction** – normal renal function, normal TSH/free T4, adequate cortisol (> 6 µg/dL)  
6. **Elevated ADH level** – patient ADH 22.4 pg/mL (inappropriately high given hypotonic state)  

All six conditions were fulfilled, confirming SIADH. The work‑up also deliberately ruled out other hyponatraemia causes: no diuretic use, no pulmonary disease on imaging, and cortisol and thyroid studies were normal. Moreover, the euvolemic examination and lack of medication triggers (e.g., cyclophosphamide) made drug‑induced SIADH unlikely.

### 7. Rationale for Excluding Sarcoidosis, Lymphoma, and Other Autoimmune Disorders  

* **Sarcoidosis** – Serum angiotensin‑converting enzyme and lysozyme were within normal limits, and there were no radiographic findings (e.g., hilar lymphadenopathy) or non‑caseating granulomas on biopsy. While these markers have limited sensitivity, the low pre‑test probability in the context of clear BD features made sarcoidosis a low‑yield consideration.  

* **Lymphoma (primary CNS or systemic)** – Slight elevation of soluble IL‑2 receptor (sIL‑2R) was noted, but this is a non‑specific inflammatory marker. Critically, CSF cytology showed no malignant cells, MRI was negative for mass lesions, and there were no systemic B‑symptoms or lymphadenopathy. Consequently, lymphoma was deemed unlikely.  

* **Other autoimmune connective‑tissue diseases** – ANA, anti‑dsDNA, anti‑SSA/SSB, and antiphospholipid antibodies were negative (screening not detailed in the report but performed as part of the “autoimmune panel”). The clinical picture lacked hallmark features of systemic lupus erythematosus, Sjögren’s syndrome, or vasculitides such as Takayasu disease.  

By systematically eliminating each of these possibilities, the diagnostic pathway narrowed to the triad of BD, neuro‑BD, and SIADH—each substantiated by objective criteria.

### 8. Synthesis: Arriving at the Definitive Diagnosis  

The diagnostic reasoning can be summarised as follows:

1. **Broad differential** generated by fever, headache, and hyponatraemia.  
2. **Targeted investigations** (CSF, MRI, endocrine labs) narrowed the field, ruling out infection, neoplasm, and primary endocrine disorders.  
3. **Mucocutaneous findings** (oral aphthae, folliculitis‑like rash, later genital ulcer) met the ICBD threshold for definite BD.  
4. **Neurological involvement** demonstrated by aseptic meningitis and clinical symptoms, in the context of BD, satisfied criteria for acute neuro‑BD after exclusion of other CNS disease.  
5. **Hyponatraemia** fulfilled all laboratory and clinical criteria for SIADH, with no alternative endocrine or volume‑status explanation.  

Thus, the patient was definitively diagnosed with **definite Behçet’s disease complicated by acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease and SIADH**. This logical, stepwise approach illustrates how clinicians integrate clinical clues with laboratory data and established diagnostic frameworks to move from a wide differential to a precise, evidence‑based diagnosis.

## Pathophysiology Linking Neuro‑Behçet’s Disease and SIADH  

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a multisystem vasculitis in which the innate immune system, especially neutrophils, is inappropriately activated. This hyper‑reactivity drives a cytokine cascade that not only produces the characteristic mucocutaneous lesions but can also extend into the central nervous system (CNS) to cause acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD). The same inflammatory milieu that fuels CNS inflammation is capable of deranging the hypothalamic‑pituitary regulation of water balance, resulting in the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). Understanding how these processes intersect helps to explain the rare but clinically important coexistence of neuro‑BD and SIADH observed in the present case.

### Behçet’s disease: vasculitis and cytokine drivers  

The pathogenic core of BD is a neutrophil‑centric vasculitis affecting vessels of all sizes. Genetic predisposition (e.g., HLA‑B51) and environmental triggers promote neutrophil priming, leading to enhanced chemotaxis, oxidative burst, and formation of neutrophil extracellular traps. Activated neutrophils release a spectrum of pro‑inflammatory cytokines, most notably interleukin‑1β (IL‑1β) and interleukin‑6 (IL‑6). IL‑1β amplifies the recruitment of additional leukocytes, while IL‑6 acts both locally and systemically to stimulate acute‑phase protein production (e.g., C‑reactive protein) and to sustain the inflammatory loop. The resultant endothelial injury and perivascular inflammation produce the oral and genital ulcers, folliculitis‑like skin lesions, and other systemic manifestations that satisfy the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease.

### Acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease: meningoencephalitis and cytokine profile  

When the vasculitic process involves the CNS, patients develop neuro‑BD, which classically presents as a parenchymal meningoencephalitis. The brainstem is the most frequent site of lesions, reflecting the predilection of BD for small‑to‑medium vessels that supply this region. Even in the absence of detectable MRI abnormalities, as in the reported patient, CSF analysis often reveals a mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, elevated protein, and, importantly, increased concentrations of inflammatory cytokines—especially IL‑6. Studies of CSF from patients with acute neuro‑BD have consistently shown IL‑6 levels many fold above normal, sometimes accompanied by raised IL‑1β and tumor necrosis factor‑α. These cytokines are not merely markers; they actively promote endothelial activation, blood‑brain barrier permeability, and further recruitment of neutrophils into the subarachnoid space, perpetuating meningeal inflammation.

### SIADH in the setting of CNS inflammation: the cytokine‑ADH axis  

Antidiuretic hormone (ADH, vasopressin) is synthesized in magnocellular neurons of the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei of the hypothalamus and released into the posterior pituitary in response to osmotic and non‑osmotic stimuli. Non‑osmotic triggers include pain, nausea, stress, and, crucially, cytokine signaling. IL‑6 exerts a potent stimulatory effect on ADH secretion: experimental data demonstrate that peripheral or central administration of IL‑6 raises plasma ADH concentrations independent of serum osmolality. The mechanism involves IL‑6 binding to its receptors on hypothalamic neurons, activating the JAK/STAT pathway, and enhancing transcription of the vasopressin gene. Inflammatory states that elevate systemic IL‑6—such as acute neuro‑BD—therefore create a milieu where ADH release persists despite hyponatraemia and low plasma osmolality, fulfilling the diagnostic criteria for SIADH.

In the present patient, the combination of a markedly elevated CRP (a downstream surrogate of IL‑6 activity) and CSF cytokine surge likely provided both a systemic and a local stimulus for excess ADH release. The resulting water retention manifested as hypo‑osmolar hyponatraemia, high urine osmolality, and concentrated urinary sodium, all classic features of SIADH.

### Contribution of systemic inflammation to ADH dysregulation  

Beyond direct hypothalamic stimulation, systemic inflammation can augment ADH secretion indirectly. Cytokine‑induced activation of the hypothalamic‑pituitary‑adrenal (HPA) axis raises cortisol and catecholamine levels, which, in turn, potentiate ADH release. Moreover, inflammation may impair baroreceptor signaling and alter renal handling of water, reinforcing the inappropriate antidiuretic state. The elevated CRP in this case reflects a high‑level IL‑6 response, reinforcing the notion that the whole‑body inflammatory burden—not solely CNS inflammation—can tip the balance toward SIADH.

### Rarity of SIADH in Behçet’s disease and clinical implications  

SIADH is well recognised in many neuro‑inflammatory disorders (e.g., encephalitis, subarachnoid hemorrhage) but is seldom reported in BD. This rarity likely reflects two factors: (1) neuro‑BD itself is uncommon, occurring in only 10–20 % of BD patients and usually years after disease onset; (2) when neuro‑BD does arise, it often presents with focal parenchymal lesions that may not involve the hypothalamic nuclei directly. Consequently, clinicians may not anticipate ADH dysregulation in a patient whose initial manifestation is meningoencephalitis without overt hypothalamic damage. The present case underscores the need to maintain a high index of suspicion for SIADH whenever a BD patient exhibits unexplained hyponatraemia, especially in the setting of acute CNS symptoms, because early recognition prevents complications such as severe hyponatraemic encephalopathy, seizures, or rhabdomyolysis.

In summary, the pathophysiologic bridge between neuro‑Behçet’s disease and SIADH is built on a cytokine‑driven inflammatory cascade—principally IL‑6—that simultaneously fuels CNS vasculitis and inappropriately stimulates ADH secretion. Recognising this link enables timely diagnosis and appropriate management of hyponatraemia while treating the underlying neuro‑BD.

## Therapeutic Management and Clinical Course  

The patient’s management required a balanced approach that addressed three overlapping problems: a presumed acute inflammatory process of the central nervous system, the systemic inflammatory disease of Behçet’s disease (BD), and the electrolyte disturbance caused by syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH).  The therapeutic plan was therefore built in stages, each guided by evolving diagnostic information and careful monitoring of clinical and laboratory parameters.

### Empiric antimicrobial therapy  

On presentation the combination of fever, headache, and a modest lymphocytic pleocytosis in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) raised the specter of a bacterial central nervous system infection.  Because bacterial meningitis can progress rapidly and carries a high mortality, empiric broad‑spectrum antibiotics were initiated promptly after obtaining blood cultures.  Cefepime (2 g intravenously every 12 h) was chosen because of its excellent coverage of common gram‑negative and gram‑positive pathogens, including *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, and its ability to achieve therapeutic concentrations in the CSF when the meninges are inflamed.  

Within 48 h the CSF profile remained non‑purulent (protein 0.97 g/L, lymphocyte predominance, no organisms on Gram stain), and cultures remained sterile.  In the absence of any focal infectious source on imaging, and with a negative work‑up for typical bacterial pathogens, the antimicrobial was de‑escalated on day 3.  Cefepime was discontinued without a switch to narrower agents, reflecting a judgment that an infectious etiology was unlikely and that further antibiotic exposure would confer only toxicity risk.

### Antiviral coverage and its withdrawal  

Because viral meningoencephalitis—especially herpes simplex virus (HSV) and varicella‑zoster virus (VZV)—remains a treatable cause of acute meningitis, intravenous acyclovir (10 mg/kg every 8 h) was added on day 3 when the patient’s headache and vertigo worsened.  Acyclovir was continued until definitive viral studies (CSF PCR for HSV‑1/2, VZV, and cryptococcal antigen) returned negative on day 5.  Given the lack of clinical or laboratory evidence of viral infection, acyclovir was stopped on day 5, avoiding unnecessary nephrotoxicity and further drug burden.

### Initiation of colchicine  

With the exclusion of infection and the emergence of genital ulceration, the diagnostic focus shifted to Behçet’s disease.  Colchicine (0.6 mg orally twice daily) was commenced on day 8 as the first disease‑specific therapy.  Colchicine binds to tubulin, preventing microtubule polymerisation, which in turn blocks neutrophil chemotaxis, degranulation, and the release of pro‑inflammatory cytokines such as IL‑1β and IL‑6.  These actions are particularly relevant to BD, in which neutrophil hyper‑reactivity and IL‑6–driven inflammation underlie both mucocutaneous lesions and neuro‑inflammatory spikes.  The dosing schedule (0.6 mg BID) is the standard adult regimen for BD‑related mucocutaneous disease and is well tolerated in elderly patients; renal and hepatic function were monitored because colchicine clearance is reduced in organ dysfunction.

### Supportive care for SIADH  

The patient’s serum sodium of 124 mmol/L, low serum osmolality (267 mOsm/kg), inappropriately concentrated urine (900 mOsm/kg) and elevated urine sodium (88 mmol/L) fulfilled the classic criteria for SIADH.  Because the hyponatraemia was profound yet neurologically stable, the first‑line therapy was fluid restriction.  A restriction target of <800 mL/day was imposed, consistent with guidelines that recommend limiting free water intake to 0.5–1 L/day in moderate‑to‑severe SIADH.  

Serum sodium was measured every 6 h for the first 48 h, then daily, to detect over‑correction (>10 mmol/L in the first 24 h) which can precipitate osmotic demyelination.  No hypertonic saline bolus or vasopressin‑2‑receptor antagonist (e.g., tolvaptan) was required because the sodium rose gradually (by ≈4 mmol/L per day) and the patient remained euvolemic without signs of cerebral edema.  The fluid‑restriction protocol was continued until the ADH level fell to 1.8 pg/mL on day 16 and serum sodium normalized at 136 mmol/L.

### Adjunctive topical therapy for mucocutaneous lesions  

While systemic colchicine targeted the underlying inflammatory cascade, local measures were employed to expedite wound healing and prevent secondary infection.  Oral aphthous ulcers received a topical triamcinolone acetonide 0.1 % paste applied 3–4 times daily; the low‑dose steroid provided anti‑inflammatory relief without systemic absorption.  Genital ulcers and facial folliculitis‑like lesions were dressed with silver sulfadiazine cream, chosen for its broad antimicrobial coverage and mild anti‑inflammatory effect.  These topical agents were continued for two weeks, at which point the lesions had resurfaced and were no longer symptomatic.

### Timeline of clinical improvement  

| Day | Intervention | Key Clinical/Laboratory Change |
|-----|--------------|--------------------------------|
| 0   | Admission, empiric cefepime, fluid restriction | Hyponatraemia 124 mmol/L, fever 38.1 °C |
| 1–2 | Blood cultures, baseline labs | No pathogen isolated |
| 3   | Added acyclovir (headache/vertigo worsened) | CSF PCR negative (HSV/VZV) |
| 5   | Discontinued acyclovir, cefepime stopped | No infectious focus identified |
| 8   | Started colchicine 0.6 mg BID | Fever resolved by day 9; CRP dropped from 180 → 45 mg/L |
| 10–12 | Ongoing fluid restriction, topical therapies | Hiccups and vertigo improved; CK declined from 1349 → 450 U/L |
| 14  | Sodium 130 mmol/L, ADH 8 pg/mL | Continued upward trend |
| 16  | Sodium 136 mmol/L, ADH 1.8 pg/mL | Full resolution of hyponatraemia; neurologic exam normal |
| 20–30| Colchicine continued, oral intake normalized | No recurrence of fever or mucocutaneous lesions; discharged on day 30 |
| 4 yr follow‑up | Colchicine monotherapy maintained | No relapse of BD or neuro‑BD; stable glycaemic control after initiating metformin |

The sequence above illustrates that once colchicine was introduced, systemic inflammation subsided rapidly, which was mirrored by a fall in CRP, normalization of CK (indicating resolution of rhabdomyolysis), and disappearance of neurologic complaints.  Importantly, the correction of hyponatraemia tracked the decline in ADH, supporting the concept that neuro‑inflammation was the driver of SIADH rather than an extrinsic drug effect.

### Rationale for avoiding corticosteroids and other immunosuppressants  

Current guidelines for acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease recommend high‑dose intravenous glucocorticoids (e.g., methylprednisolone 1 g/day for 3–5 days) as first‑line therapy, often followed by a tapering oral course or adjunctive immunosuppressants such as azathioprine or cyclophosphamide for severe parenchymal disease.  In this patient, several considerations favored a corticosteroid‑sparing strategy:

1. **Mild neurologic phenotype** – The CSF revealed only modest pleocytosis and protein elevation, and the MRI was normal.  There were no focal deficits, mass effect, or brain‑stem involvement, all of which are predictors of a more aggressive disease course that usually necessitates steroids.

2. **Rapid clinical response to colchicine** – Within 48 h of colchicine initiation the patient’s fever subsided, CRP fell sharply, and neurological symptoms improved, suggesting that neutrophil‑driven inflammation was the dominant pathogenic mechanism and could be controlled without steroids.

3. **Comorbidity profile** – The patient had uncontrolled type‑2 diabetes mellitus.  High‑dose glucocorticoids would have markedly worsened glycaemic control, increasing the risk of hyperglycaemic emergencies and infection.  Avoiding steroids thus reduced iatrogenic morbidity.

4. **Risk of steroid‑induced SIADH** – Glucocorticoids can worsen water retention and potentiate ADH activity; in a patient already manifesting SIADH, steroid use might have delayed hyponatraemia correction.

5. **Absence of refractory disease** – After 10 days of colchicine, the patient’s inflammatory markers and sodium were normalizing, obviating the need for escalation to more potent immunosuppression.

Collectively, these factors justified proceeding with colchicine monotherapy and close supportive care, reserving corticosteroids for a potential flare that did not materialize.  The patient’s uneventful 4‑year follow‑up, without additional immunosuppressive agents, underscores that in selected cases of acute neuro‑BD with limited CNS involvement, colchicine may provide sufficient disease control while sparing the patient from steroid‑related adverse effects.

## Clinical Lessons and Future Directions  

The presented case illustrates several high‑yield concepts that are directly applicable to the everyday practice of physicians who encounter patients with Behçet’s disease (BD) or unexplained central nervous system (CNS) inflammation. By integrating the clinical course, investigations, and therapeutic response, we can extract practical teaching points, identify common pitfalls, and outline avenues for future investigation.

### 1. Neuro‑Behçet’s disease may present simultaneously with systemic disease  
Classically, neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) is described as a late manifestation, occurring years after the initial mucocutaneous or ocular flares. This case defies that expectation: the patient fulfilled the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD) on day 5 of admission while still in the acute phase of his CNS illness. Recognising that neuro‑BD can be the presenting feature of systemic BD prevents a diagnostic delay that might otherwise lead to extensive, often invasive, infectious or oncologic work‑ups. Clinicians should therefore keep neuro‑BD on the differential when any patient with oral/genital ulcers, folliculitis‑type skin lesions, or a positive HLA‑B51 presents with headache, fever, or meningeal signs, even in the absence of a prior BD diagnosis.

### 2. Hyponatraemia in the setting of CNS symptoms should trigger a search for SIADH  
The coexistence of fever, headache, and profound hyponatraemia (Na⁺ 124 mmol/L) was a pivotal clue. The diagnostic work‑up (low serum osmolality, inappropriately high urine osmolality and sodium, elevated ADH, euvolemic clinical assessment) satisfied the classic criteria for the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). In neuro‑inflammatory states, including acute neuro‑BD, cytokine‑driven hypothalamic stimulation can produce SIADH even when imaging of the hypothalamus is unrevealing. Missing this association can result in inappropriate fluid management, worsening cerebral oedema, and severe neurological sequelae. Therefore, whenever a patient with meningeal or encephalitic features shows hyponatraemia, SIADH must be considered early, and a structured electrolyte work‑up should be performed.

### 3. Colchicine may be an effective frontline agent in selected acute neuro‑BD cases  
Standard therapy for acute neuro‑BD typically involves high‑dose corticosteroids, often combined with azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, or biologics. In this patient, colchicine monotherapy produced rapid resolution of mucocutaneous lesions, neurological complaints, and SIADH without the need for steroids. Colchicine’s anti‑inflammatory actions—impairment of neutrophil chemotaxis, inhibition of microtubule polymerisation, and down‑regulation of IL‑1β and IL‑6—provide a plausible mechanistic basis for ameliorating both peripheral and CNS inflammation. While systematic reviews have generally concluded that colchicine alone is insufficient for established parenchymal neuro‑BD, the present experience suggests a role for colchicine as an initial, steroid‑sparing option in patients whose CNS disease is limited to meningo‑encephalitic features without radiological parenchymal lesions. Careful patient selection (mild neurological deficits, absence of brainstem lesions, and stable hemodynamics) is essential.

### 4. Vigilance for hyponatraemia‑related complications: hiccups and rhabdomyolysis  
Two seemingly unrelated manifestations—persistent hiccups and markedly elevated creatine kinase—signaled the severity of the hyponatraemia. Hiccups have been reported as an early neurologic clue to profound hypo‑osmolar states, while rhabdomyolysis can arise from cellular swelling and impaired membrane integrity in severe hyponatraemia. Both complications are reversible with timely correction of sodium, but they also carry risks (e.g., aspiration from uncontrolled hiccups, acute kidney injury from myoglobinuria). Routine monitoring of serum sodium, urine output, and CK in any patient with SIADH‑type hyponatraemia is therefore advisable, with prompt institution of fluid restriction and, when indicated, cautious hypertonic saline to avoid osmotic demyelination.

### 5. Limitations of the current evidence base  

| Issue | Current knowledge | Gap |
|-------|-------------------|-----|
| **Efficacy of colchicine monotherapy** | Isolated case reports (including this one) suggest benefit in mild acute neuro‑BD. | No prospective, controlled trials; unknown optimal dose, duration, and relapse rates. |
| **Pathophysiology of SIADH in neuro‑BD** | IL‑6–driven ADH secretion is hypothesised; CSF IL‑6 often elevated in neuro‑BD. | Direct measurement of IL‑6 and ADH kinetics in neuro‑BD patients lacking; causal relationship not proven. |
| **Predictors of steroid‑sparing response** | Clinical phenotype (meningo‑encephalitis without focal lesions) may predict response. | No validated scoring system or biomarkers to guide therapy selection. |
| **Long‑term outcomes after colchicine‑only treatment** | This patient remained relapse‑free for four years, but data are anecdotal. | Longitudinal registries required to assess durability and safety. |

Addressing these gaps will require multicentre collaboration given the rarity of concurrent systemic and neuro‑BD.

### 6. Practical algorithm for clinicians confronted with fever, headache, mucocutaneous lesions, and hyponatraemia  

1. **Initial assessment**  
   - Record detailed mucocutaneous history (oral/genital ulcers, skin lesions).  
   - Perform focused neurological exam; note any focal deficits or meningeal signs.  
   - Assess volume status (euvolemic vs hypovolemic/hypervolemic).  

2. **Laboratory screen**  
   - Serum electrolytes, osmolality, urine Na⁺ and osmolality.  
   - Inflammatory markers (CRP, ESR).  
   - Basic infectious panel (blood cultures, viral PCRs) and CSF analysis if lumbar puncture is safe.  

3. **Diagnostic decision tree**  
   - **Hyponatraemia with low serum osmolality + high urine osmolality → SIADH** (provided euvolemia).  
   - **CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis, elevated protein, normal glucose → aseptic meningitis**; consider neuro‑BD after infection excluded.  
   - **Mucocutaneous findings meeting ICBD → systemic BD**; combine with CNS picture for acute neuro‑BD.  

4. **Therapeutic approach**  
   - **SIADH**: fluid restriction ≤800 mL/day; monitor Na⁺ every 6–12 h; avoid rapid correction (>8 mmol/L/24 h).  
   - **Acute neuro‑BD**:  
     - Start colchicine (0.5 mg 2–3 times daily) if neurological involvement is limited to meningo‑encephalitic features and no contraindication exists.  
     - Reserve corticosteroids for patients with brainstem lesions, severe focal deficits, or lack of response to colchicine after 48–72 h.  
   - **Mucocutaneous lesions**: topical corticosteroids; wound care for genital ulcers.  

5. **Monitoring and follow‑up**  
   - Daily serum Na⁺, CK, and CRP until stable.  
   - Neurological re‑assessment; repeat MRI if symptoms persist or worsen.  
   - Long‑term: assess for BD flares, relapse of neuro‑BD, and adverse effects of colchicine (GI intolerance, cytopenias).  

By adhering to this algorithm, clinicians can efficiently differentiate between infectious, neoplastic, and inflammatory etiologies, institute appropriate electrolyte management, and judiciously employ colchicine as a steroid‑sparing option when feasible.

### 7. Future directions  

- **Prospective registries** of patients with acute neuro‑BD to capture treatment patterns, outcomes, and biomarkers (IL‑6, ADH).  
- **Randomised controlled trials** comparing colchicine monotherapy versus standard corticosteroid‑based regimens in mild neuro‑BD.  
- **Mechanistic studies** exploring the cytokine cascade linking CNS inflammation in BD to ADH secretion, potentially identifying novel therapeutic targets (e.g., IL‑6 antagonists).  
- **Development of a risk‑stratification tool** integrating clinical features (e.g., presence of SIADH, CK elevation), imaging findings, and laboratory markers to guide early therapeutic decisions.  

In summary, this case reinforces that neuro‑Behçet’s disease can debut alongside systemic disease, that hyponatraemia may be the herald of an underlying SIADH driven by CNS inflammation, and that colchicine may serve as a viable frontline therapy in selected patients. A systematic, algorithmic approach to evaluation and management, coupled with vigilant monitoring for hyponatraemia‑related complications, can improve outcomes while minimizing unnecessary exposure to high‑dose steroids. Ongoing research is needed to define the optimal therapeutic strategy and to elucidate the pathophysiologic link between neuro‑BD and SIADH.
