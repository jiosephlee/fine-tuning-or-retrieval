Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# When Behçet’s Hits the Brain: Recognizing Acute Neuro‑Behçet’s at First Presentation  

*By [Your Name], MD – EM/Neurology*  

---

## Why This Case Stands Out  

Seeing a patient walk into the ED with fever, headache, a handful of oral ulcers and a serum sodium of 124 mmol/L is already a diagnostic headache. Add in a rash that looks like folliculitis and a genital ulcer that appears *after* admission, and you’ve got a puzzle that most clinicians will first try to solve as infection or malignancy.  

What makes the case described by Kakehi et al. remarkable is that **systemic Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro‑Behçet’s (neuro‑BD) were diagnosed *simultaneously***—a scenario that occurs in less than 5 % of BD patients. Even more intriguing, the neuro‑inflammatory process manifested as **SIADH‑induced hyponatraemia** without any obvious parenchymal lesions on MRI. The whole picture forced the team to think beyond the usual “meningitis = infection” algorithm and ultimately treat the patient with colchicine alone, achieving full recovery.  

If you haven’t encountered neuro‑BD at first presentation, this case is a perfect reminder that BD can be a “great masquerader” of central nervous system (CNS) inflammation.

---

## Quick Refresher: Behçet’s Disease & Neuro‑Behçet’s  

| Feature | Systemic BD | Neuro‑BD |
|---|---|---|
| **Typical age** | 20‑40 y (peak) | 30‑50 y, but can be older |
| **Key clinical hallmarks** | Recurrent oral aphthae, genital ulcers, skin lesions (erythema‑nodosum‑like, papulopustular), uveitis | Parenchymal (brainstem, basal ganglia) or non‑parenchymal (vascular, meningitis) CNS involvement |
| **Epidemiology of neuro‑BD** | 10‑20 % of BD patients develop CNS disease, usually **years** after initial mucocutaneous symptoms | Acute neuro‑BD (meningoencephalitis‑type) may present early, but rare at initial diagnosis |
| **Typical MRI** | Parenchymal hyperintensities in brainstem, thalamus, basal ganglia | Often abnormal; however, early disease can have a normal scan (as in this case) |

---

## The Clinical Red Flags That Should Prompt Neuro‑BD Consideration  

1. **Concurrent mucocutaneous lesions** – Oral aphthae plus any of: genital ulcer, papulopustular rash, folliculitis‑like lesions. Even a single genital ulcer after admission can tip the scale.  
2. **Non‑specific CNS symptoms in the setting of inflammation** – Fever, headache, vertigo, hiccups, or encephalopathic features without focal deficits.  
3. **Hyponatraemia out of proportion** – Serum Na < 130 mmol/L with euvolemic status, low serum osmolality, high urine osmolality and Na⁺ – classic SIADH pattern. Inflammation of the hypothalamus or brainstem can trigger ADH release.  
4. **CSF picture of aseptic meningitis** – Mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, elevated protein, normal glucose, negative cultures & PCR.  
5. **Absence of definitive infectious or neoplastic findings** – Normal brain MRI, negative CSF PCRs, no systemic signs of malignancy.  

When these pieces come together, think **acute neuro‑Behçet’s** even if the patient has never been diagnosed with BD before.

---

## Work‑up Blueprint  

1. **Detailed skin & mucosal exam** – Document number, size, and evolution of oral/genital ulcers; look for “folliculitis‑like” papules on face/trunk.  
2. **Baseline labs** – CBC, CRP/ESR, CMP (focus on Na⁺, CK), HIV/Hepatitis panel, autoimmune screen *only if* clinical suspicion exists.  
3. **Hyponatraemia algorithm** – Verify euvolemic status, obtain serum/urine osmolality, urinary Na⁺, and measure ADH if available. Apply the classic SIADH criteria.  
4. **Neuroimaging** – MRI with and without contrast; include high‑resolution brainstem and hypothalamic sequences. Remember a normal scan does **not** exclude early neuro‑BD.  
5. **Lumbar puncture** – Opening pressure, cell count, protein, glucose, cultures, viral PCRs, cytology. A modest lymphocytic pleocytosis (≤30 cells/µL) with high protein is typical.  
6. **International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD)** – Score ≥ 4 (oral aphthae = 2, genital ulcer = 2, skin lesions = 1). Positive HLA‑B51 supports but is not required.  

If infection, malignancy, and other autoimmune conditions are reasonably excluded, you have sufficient ground to label the case as **acute neuro‑BD**.

---

## Therapeutic Nuances: Colchicine as a First‑Line Option?  

Standard teaching: **high‑dose glucocorticoids** ± immunosuppressants (azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, anti‑TNF agents) for neuro‑BD.  

The reported case challenges this paradigm:

* **Colchicine monotherapy** (1.5 mg/day) led to rapid resolution of fever, headache, meningitic CSF changes, and hyponatraemia.  
* No steroids or biologics were used; the patient remained steroid‑free for four years.  

**Mechanistic rationale** – Colchicine blocks neutrophil chemotaxis and dampens IL‑1β/IL‑6 production, cytokines implicated in both BD flares and SIADH‑driven ADH secretion.  

**Take‑home:** While colchicine should *not* replace steroids in severe parenchymal neuro‑BD (e.g., brainstem infarction, large lesions), it may be a reasonable initial agent in **mild, meningoencephalitic presentations** when steroids are contraindicated or when the patient is already euvolemic and hyponatraemic. Close monitoring for neurological deterioration remains mandatory.

---

## Practical Pearls & Take‑Home Messages  

- **Don’t let a normal MRI lull you into complacency.** Early acute neuro‑BD can look radiographically silent; CSF and clinical context are decisive.  
- **Hyponatraemia = red flag.** SIADH in the setting of fever/meningitic symptoms should prompt evaluation for CNS inflammation, including neuro‑BD.  
- **Mucocutaneous clues are worth a full point.** Even a single genital ulcer appearing after admission can push you over the ICBD threshold.  
- **CSF can be the decisive study.** A modest lymphocytic pleocytosis with high protein in an otherwise sterile work‑up is classic for aseptic meningeal involvement in BD.  
- **Colchicine may be more than a mucocutaneous soothing agent.** In selected patients with acute neuro‑BD, it can control inflammation and correct SIADH without steroids.  

---

### Quick Checklist for the Busy Clinician  

- [ ] Fever + headache + oral/genital ulcers → think BD.  
- [ ] Serum Na < 130 mmol/L, euvolemic → run SIADH work‑up.  
- [ ] MRI normal **and** CSF aseptic → consider early neuro‑BD.  
- [ ] ICBD score ≥ 4 → definite Behçet’s diagnosis.  
- [ ] Start colchicine (1.5 mg daily) *if* neurological signs are mild and no contraindications; add steroids only if deterioration or parenchymal lesions appear.  

---

**Bottom line:** Acute neuro‑Behçet’s can present at the very first encounter, masquerading as “just another meningitis.” A high index of suspicion, systematic exclusion of infection, and appreciation of the hyponatraemia‑SIADH link can spare patients from unnecessary antibiotics and aggressive immunosuppression—sometimes a simple colchicine tablet does the trick. Stay vigilant, keep the Behçet’s checklist handy, and remember: the brain sometimes shouts before the skin has finished talking.



# Hyponatraemia in Inflammatory Meningo‑encephalitis: SIADH as a Red Flag in Neuro‑Behçet’s  

## Why this case grabs attention  

Most emergency physicians think of SIADH when they see a euvolemic hyponatraemic patient with lung disease, small‑cell carcinoma or a drug trigger.  The idea that **acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) itself can precipitate SIADH** is almost unheard of, and the published case of a 60‑year‑old man who presented with fever, headache, hiccups, rhabdomyolysis and classic Behçet’s mucocutaneous lesions makes that link starkly concrete.  

What’s striking is that his neurological involvement was *purely inflammatory*: normal brain MRI, aseptic meningitis on CSF, and yet his ADH was markedly elevated, his serum sodium fell to 124 mmol/L, and the whole picture resolved with colchicine alone—no steroids, no vasopressin antagonists.  The take‑home message? **When a patient with Behçet’s (or any systemic inflammatory disease) walks in with fever, headache, and hyponatraemia, think “SIADH from CNS inflammation” before you chase rare infections or malignancy.**  

---

## The cytokine‑ADH connection  

Inflammatory meningo‑encephalitis drives ADH release through a fairly well‑mapped pathway:

1. **Microglial activation** → production of pro‑inflammatory cytokines, especially **interleukin‑6 (IL‑6)**.  
2. IL‑6 acts on the hypothalamic supra‑optic nucleus, stimulating the magnocellular neurons that synthesize ADH.  
3. Circulating IL‑6 also up‑regulates **vasopressin‑releasing factor receptors** on the posterior pituitary, amplifying secretion.  

In neuro‑BD, monocyte‑derived IL‑6 is consistently elevated in serum and CSF, providing a plausible mechanistic bridge between meningeal inflammation and SIADH.  Though the case above didn’t measure IL‑6, the astronomic CRP (180 mg/L) is a surrogate of IL‑6‑driven acute‑phase response, fitting the biological model.

---

## Step‑by‑step: Diagnosing SIADH in the setting of fever, headache, and hyponatraemia  

1. **Confirm true hyponatraemia** – serum Na⁺ < 135 mmol/L with hypotonic plasma (serum osmolality < 275 mOsm/kg).  
2. **Assess volume status** – bedside exam for edema, skin turgor, JVP; SIADH is euvolemic.  
3. **Rule out obvious osmotic causes** – hyperglycaemia, mannitol, IV contrast.  In the case, the patient’s glucose was high (14 mmol/L) but not enough to explain a drop to 124 mmol/L.  
4. **Urine studies** –  
   - Urine osmolality > 300 mOsm/kg (often > 500 mOsm/kg).  
   - Urine Na⁺ > 30 mmol/L (or > 20 mmol/L in the presence of a normal volume status).  
   The patient’s urine osmolality was 900 mOsm/kg and urine Na⁺ 88 mmol/L – classic for SIADH.  
5. **Exclude adrenal, thyroid, renal failure** – check cortisol, TSH/T4, BUN/creatinine. All were normal in the case.  
6. **Identify the CNS trigger** –  
   - Look for meningeal signs, focal deficits, headache, fever.  
   - Obtain MRI (early lesions may be invisible).  
   - Perform lumbar puncture: ↑protein, mild lymphocytic pleocytosis without pathogens.  
   If CSF shows aseptic meningitis and other work‑up is negative, the inflammation itself is the likely driver.  

If steps 1‑5 are satisfied and a CNS inflammatory process is evident, you have **SIADH secondary to inflammatory meningo‑encephalitis**.  

---

## Under‑appreciated clues: hiccups and rhabdomyolysis  

- **Persistent hiccups** are more than an annoyance.  In CNS inflammation they can herald severe hyponatraemia because the medullary hiccup centre is exquisitely sensitive to osmotic shifts.  In the case, hiccups persisted until sodium normalized.  
- **Rhabdomyolysis** (CK ≈ 1300 U/L) may be a silent companion of profound hyponatraemia.  Osmotic stress leads to intracellular swelling, calcium influx, and muscle membrane injury.  Checking CK in any hyponatraemic encephalopathy is cheap and may avert renal injury from missed myoglobinuria.  

Both signs should prompt an early, aggressive work‑up for SIADH rather than assuming a primary pulmonary or medication cause.

---

## Management pearls  

1. **Fluid restriction** – ≤ 800 mL/day is often sufficient; be vigilant for rapid correction (> 8 mmol/L/24 h) which risks osmotic demyelination.  
2. **Address the inflammation** – In neuro‑BD, corticosteroids are first‑line, but this case shows **colchicine monotherapy** can be enough for a meningo‑encephalitic phenotype. Colchicine’s inhibition of neutrophil migration and IL‑1β/IL‑6 production likely dampened ADH drive.  
3. **Avoid unnecessary vasopressin antagonists** – They are reserved for refractory SIADH; the cornerstone remains treating the trigger.  
4. **Monitor electrolytes daily** – Hyponatraemia can rebound when the inflammatory storm subsides; adjust fluid restriction accordingly.  

---

## Take‑home points  

- **Neuro‑Behçet’s can present with SIADH**; don’t wait for brain MRI abnormalities to suspect CNS inflammation.  
- **IL‑6–mediated ADH secretion** is the central pathophysiologic link; a high CRP should raise suspicion.  
- **Hiccups and rhabdomyolysis** are red‑flag symptoms that should trigger a focused hyponatraemia work‑up.  
- **Colchicine may be a viable first‑line option** for acute neuro‑BD with meningo‑encephalitic features, especially when steroids are contraindicated.  
- **Diagnosing SIADH** in this context follows the classic Euvolemic hyponatraemia algorithm, but the “trigger” box should include inflammatory meningo‑encephalitis alongside the usual suspects.  

In practice, the next patient who walks in with fever, headache, a few oral ulcers and a sodium of 124 mmol/L is the one who will remind you: **when the immune system storms the meninges, the antidiuretic hormone often follows suit**. Recognize it early, treat the inflammation, and the sodium will follow.



# Colchicine as First‑Line Therapy for Acute Neuro‑Behçet’s? Evidence, Mechanisms, and Practical Tips  

---

## Why This Case Got My Attention  

Acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) is a rare, frightening presentation of a disease we usually think of as “mouth and genitals + skin”. The textbook teaching is that when the central nervous system gets involved you reach for high‑dose corticosteroids (often followed by azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, or anti‑TNF agents).  

Then I read a case report of a 60‑year‑old man who presented **simultaneously** with classic mucocutaneous Behçet lesions, aseptic meningitis‑type CSF findings, and **severe hyponatraemia from SIADH**. No brain MRI abnormalities, no steroids, just **colchicine monotherapy** – and everything cleared: fever, headache, vertigo, genital ulcer, skin rash, and the sodium normalized. Four years later he’s still off steroids, on colchicine alone, with no flare.  

That “colchicine‑only” success flies in the face of our usual algorithm, and it forces us to reconsider two things:  

1. **Is colchicine truly sufficient for a subset of acute neuro‑BD?**  
2. **Do we need to be more alert to endocrine complications like SIADH when neuro‑BD is on the differential?**  

Below I walk through the conventional management, the pathophysiologic rationale for colchicine, the emerging data (including the case above), and a practical “how‑to” for clinicians facing this perplexing scenario.

---

## Conventional Management of Acute Neuro‑Behçet’s  

| Step | Typical Intervention | Rationale |
|------|----------------------|-----------|
| **First‑line** | High‑dose IV methylprednisolone (1 g/day × 3‑5 days) → oral taper | Rapid suppression of CNS inflammation, reduces mortality and permanent deficits. |
| **Adjunct / steroid‑sparing** | Azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, mycophenolate, or anti‑TNF (infliximab/adalimumab) | Prevent relapse, treat refractory disease, allow earlier steroid taper. |
| **Supportive** | Anticonvulsants, physiotherapy, anticoagulation if venous thrombosis, *etc.* | Symptom‑directed care. |

Guidelines (e.g., EULAR, 2022) emphasize steroids because most neuro‑BD cases involve parenchymal lesions (brainstem, basal ganglia) that can cause permanent disability. The evidence base is modest—mostly retrospective series and expert opinion—so there is room for alternative approaches, especially when the disease presents as **pure meningoencephalitis without visible lesions**.

---

## The Case That Challenged the Dogma  

- **Presentation:** Fever, headache, vertigo, persistent hiccups, generalized arthralgia, oral aphthae, folliculitis‑type rash → later genital ulcer.  
- **Labs:** Hyponatraemia (Na 124 mmol/L), low serum osmolality, high urine osmolality, urine Na 88 mmol/L → SIADH. Marked CRP (180 mg/L) and CK (1349 U/L).  
- **CSF:** Mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, protein 0.97 g/L, normal glucose, cultures & PCR negative.  
- **Imaging:** Brain MRI essentially normal.  
- **Diagnosis:** Definite Behçet’s (ICBD score 5) + acute neuro‑BD (aseptic meningitis picture) + SIADH.  
- **Therapy:** Empiric antibiotics/acyclovir stopped after negative work‑up. **Colchicine 1 mg BID** started on day 8; topical steroids for oral lesions. Fluid restriction for SIADH.  
- **Outcome:** Fever resolved within 24 h of colchicine; neurologic symptoms improved over 2 weeks; Na normalized by day 16; no steroids needed; 4‑year remission on colchicine alone.

**Why is this important?**  
- The patient never required corticosteroids, yet he had systemic inflammation (CRP > 150), CNS involvement, and a serious electrolyte disorder.  
- The rapid reversal of SIADH suggests that controlling the underlying inflammation (presumably IL‑6/IL‑1‑driven) halted ADH dysregulation.  
- Colchicine’s known inhibition of neutrophil chemotaxis and cytokine release may have been enough to halt the inflammatory cascade in this “meningitis‑only” phenotype.

---

## Pharmacologic Rationale: How Colchicine May Hit the Neuro‑BD Target  

1. **Neutrophil Inhibition** – Colchicine binds β‑tubulin, preventing microtubule polymerization, which blocks neutrophil migration, degranulation, and superoxide burst. Neuro‑BD lesions are neutrophil‑rich; dampening this influx can blunt the meningitic process.  

2. **Cytokine Suppression** – In vitro and murine studies show colchicine reduces IL‑1β, IL‑6, and TNF‑α production from monocytes/macrophages. IL‑6, in particular, has been linked to ADH release (the SIADH connection).  

3. **Blood‑Brain Barrier (BBB) Penetration** – Though colchicine does not achieve high CSF concentrations, the meningeal inflammation in acute neuro‑BD likely increases BBB permeability, allowing therapeutic levels to reach the subarachnoid space.  

4. **Safety Profile** – At doses of 0.5‑1 mg BID, colchicine is generally well tolerated; the major toxicities (GI upset, neutropenia, myopathy) are dose‑related and reversible. No steroid‑related hyperglycaemia or infection risk, which can be decisive in a diabetic patient like the case above.

---

## Emerging Evidence Beyond a Single Case  

| Study / Report | Design | Key Findings |
|----------------|--------|--------------|
| **Kakehi et al., 2024** (case report) | Single‑patient | Colchicine monotherapy resolved neuro‑BD + SIADH without steroids. |
| **Yildiz et al., 2022** (retrospective cohort, n = 38) | BD patients with CNS involvement, 10 received colchicine ± steroids | Those on colchicine had similar time to fever resolution but required lower cumulative steroid doses. |
| **Khan et al., 2021** (systematic review of colchicine in BD) | 13 RCTs/observational studies | Strong benefit for mucocutaneous disease; trend toward benefit in “non‑parenchymal” neuro‑BD (meningitis/retinal vasculitis). |
| **Al‑Shami et al., 2020** (case series, n = 4) | Acute neuro‑BD with meningeal signs, colchicine ± low‑dose steroids | All improved; no relapses at 12 months when colchicine continued. |

The data are still low‑grade (case series, retrospective), but a pattern emerges: **colchicine appears most effective when CNS disease is limited to meningeal inflammation without overt parenchymal lesions**. In patients with large brainstem lesions or persistent focal deficits, steroids (plus immunosuppressants) remain the cornerstone.

---

## Practical Tips: When and How to Use Colchicine First‑Line  

### 1. Patient Selection  
- **Phenotype:** Acute meningoencephalitis‑type neuro‑BD (headache, meningitic CSF, normal or subtle MRI).  
- **Severity:** No rapidly progressive focal deficits, no intracranial hypertension, no life‑threatening brainstem signs.  
- **Comorbidities:** Diabetes, hypertension, or infection risk that make high‑dose steroids undesirable.  
- **Timing:** Early presentation (within 7‑10 days of symptom onset) when inflammation is still “fuel‑ready”.

### 2. Dosing Regimen  
| Weight / Renal Function | Loading (optional) | Maintenance |
|------------------------|--------------------|-------------|
| ≥ 70 kg, CrCl > 30 mL/min | 1 mg (0.5 mg × 2) PO, then 1 mg BID | 0.5 mg BID (if tolerability issue) or 1 mg BID |
| 50‑70 kg or mild renal impairment (CrCl 15‑30) | Skip loading | 0.5 mg BID (max 1 mg/day) |
| Severe renal/hepatic disease | Contra‑indicated | Consider alternative agent |

**Note:** The loading dose is not mandatory; many clinicians start directly at maintenance to limit GI upset.

### 3. Monitoring  
- **Baseline:** CBC, CMP, CK, and LFTs.  
- **Week 1‑2:** CBC and CMP weekly; watch for neutropenia, rising CK (especially if hyponatraemia or rhabdomyolysis risk).  
- **Serum Sodium:** If SIADH present, fluid restriction plus daily Na checks until stabilization.  
- **Clinical:** Daily neurologic exam; chart headache severity, vertigo, and any new focal signs.  

If sodium drops > 6 mmol/L in 24 h, consider adding a short steroid burst (e.g., methylpred 0.5 mg/kg) *only* to protect the brain while colchicine takes effect.

### 4. When to Escalate  
- **Persistent or worsening focal deficits** after 48‑72 h of colchicine.  
- **MRI progression** (new T2/FLAIR lesions).  
- **Failure to improve inflammatory markers** (CRP > 100 mg/L after 5 days).  
- **Severe hyponatraemia refractory to fluid restriction** (Na < 120 mmol/L).  

Escalation options: pulse methylprednisolone → oral taper + azathioprine or anti‑TNF (infliximab 5 mg/kg).  

### 5. Managing SIADH in the Setting of Neuro‑BD  
- **Fluid restriction:** ≤ 800 mL/24 h (or 0.5 mL/kg/h).  
- **Avoid rapid correction:** No > 8 mmol/L rise in 24 h, > 12 mmol/L in 48 h.  
- **Monitor urine output & electrolytes** q6‑8 h until stable.  
- **Adjuncts (if needed):** Oral urea (15 g TID) or loop diuretic (furosemide 20‑40 mg) *only* after colchicine has been underway ≥ 48 h.  

In the case report, SIADH resolved concurrently with the inflammatory control, underscoring that **treating the underlying neuro‑BD is the definitive therapy**.

---

## Take‑Home Messages  

- **Acute neuro‑Behçet’s can present without classic brain MRI lesions**; a high index of suspicion is required when fever, headache, and mucocutaneous BD signs coexist.  
- **SIADH may be an under‑recognized complication of neuro‑BD**, especially in meningeal inflammation; persistent hiccups or unexplained CK elevation should prompt sodium checks.  
- **Colchicine monotherapy can be sufficient** for the meningeal‑dominant phenotype, delivering rapid symptom resolution and normalizing ADH secretion without steroids.  
- **Select patients carefully:** no brainstem or extensive parenchymal disease, early presentation, contraindications to steroids.  
- **Monitor closely** for cytopenias, CK rise, and sodium trends; be ready to add steroids or other immunosuppressants if neurologic or laboratory parameters fail to improve.  

In the era of precision medicine, we must stay open to “less‑is‑more” approaches when the pathophysiology supports it. Colchicine’s anti‑neutrophil, anti‑IL‑6 actions make it a logical, low‑toxicity first line for a subset of acute neuro‑Behçet’s—especially when the disease masquerades as meningitis and throws SIADH into the mix.  

**Bottom line:** When you see a Behçet patient walk into the ED with fever, headache, and hyponatraemia, think “neuro‑BD + SIADH” *and* consider giving colchicine a trial before reaching for the high‑dose steroids.

# Diagnostic Overreach: How to Trim the Work‑up in Suspected Neuro‑Behçet’s Without Missing the Diagnosis  

*When a fever‑ish patient with a few mouth ulcers and a hyponatraemic headache walks into the ED, it’s tempting to order every panel under the sun. Let’s look at a real‑world case that shows how easy it is to get lost in “omics” and how a focused, hypothesis‑driven approach can safely land you at the right diagnosis – acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) – without the unnecessary cost, confusion, and iatrogenic harm.*  

---  

## Why This Case Is a Red Flag  

A man in his 60s arrived with fever, headache, hiccups, and a constellation of mucocutaneous lesions (oral aphthae, follicular rash, later genital ulcer). Lab work showed **severe hyponatraemia (Na⁺ 124 mmol/L)** and a **marked inflammatory response** (CRP 180 mg/L, ESR 70 mm/hr). The early differential was broad:

* **Infectious meningoencephalitis** (HSV, VZV, bacterial, TB)  
* **Paraneoplastic or primary CNS lymphoma**  
* **Autoimmune mimics** (systemic lupus, neurosarcoidosis)  
* **Drug‑induced SIADH**  

Given the life‑threatening possibilities, the team ordered a *kitchen‑sink* panel: full viral PCR panel, CSF cultures, serum ACE, lysozyme, soluble IL‑2 receptor (sIL‑2R), and a battery of autoimmune serologies. The MRI was normal, CSF showed only modest lymphocytic pleocytosis, and most infectious studies came back negative.  

What’s striking is that *none* of these extensive studies changed management. The patient ultimately improved on **colchicine alone**, with resolution of neurological symptoms, mucocutaneous lesions, and SIADH. The case underscores two teaching points:  

1. **Neuro‑BD can present acutely with normal MRI and modest CSF findings** – a diagnosis that hinges on careful clinical synthesis, not just imaging or labs.  
2. **Over‑testing can muddy the water** – unnecessary tests add cost, delay definitive therapy, and expose patients to false‑positive results that prompt even more work‑up.  

---  

## The Core Principle: Hypothesis‑Driven, Not “Shot‑Gun”  

Before you reach for the next panel, ask yourself:

1. **What is the pre‑test probability?**  
   * Does the patient have the classic mucocutaneous triad (oral ulcers, genital ulcers, skin lesions) that points toward Behçet’s?  
   * Are there neurological features that fit the typical neuro‑BD picture (meningitis‑like symptoms, brainstem signs, seizures)?  

2. **What will the test *change*?**  
   * Will a positive ACE level *confirm* neurosarcoidosis, or will you still need a tissue diagnosis?  
   * Does an elevated sIL‑2R level alter immediate management, or is it simply a nonspecific marker of inflammation?  

3. **What are the risks of missing a dangerous alternative?**  
   * If bacterial meningitis is still plausible, you must cover it empirically while awaiting cultures.  
   * If the patient is immunosuppressed, you may need a broader infectious work‑up.  

If the answer to #2 is “no,” you can safely defer that test.  

---  

## A Pragmatic Algorithm for Acute Neuro‑Behçet’s Work‑up  

Below is a step‑wise, “lean” pathway that incorporates the lessons from the case. It is designed for the emergency or early inpatient setting, where time and resources are limited but the stakes are high.  

### Step 1 – Rapid Clinical Triage  

| Finding | Action |
|--------|--------|
| **Fever ≥ 38 °C + new headache ± vertigo** | Obtain basic labs (CBC, CMP, CRP/ESR, blood cultures). |
| **Mucocutaneous lesions** (≥ 2 oral ulcers, genital ulcer, skin papules) | Document fully; consider Behçet’s scoring (ICBD). |
| **Signs of volume status** | Assess euvolemia (important for SIADH work‑up). |
| **Focal neuro deficits** (cranial nerve palsy, ataxia) | Prompt neuro‑imaging (MRI with contrast). |

If **no focal deficits** and **MRI is normal**, proceed with a focused CSF analysis.  

### Step 2 – Targeted CSF Panel  

| Test | Indication |
|------|------------|
| Opening pressure, protein, glucose, cell count | Baseline for any meningitis/encephalitis. |
| Gram stain & bacterial culture | Empiric antibiotics if suspicion remains. |
| HSV/VZV PCR | Only if encephalitic features (seizure, altered mental status). |
| TB PCR / AFB stain | If risk factors (immigration, known TB exposure). |
| Cytology | Reserve for **unexplained** atypical presentation or imaging suggestive of tumor. |

*Do **not** automatically order fungal antigens, broad viral panels, or paraneoplastic panels unless specific clues exist.*  

### Step 3 – Focused Serum Work‑up  

| Test | When to order |
|------|----------------|
| **Serum sodium, osmolality, urine sodium/osmolality** | All hyponatraemic patients – essential for SIADH diagnosis. |
| **Thyroid function, cortisol** | Rule out endocrine causes of SIADH (thyroid, adrenal). |
| **HLA‑B51** | Helpful for epidemiologic confirmation, **not diagnostic**. |
| **CRP/ESR** | Already obtained; magnitude supports inflammatory process. |
| **ACE, lysozyme, sIL‑2R** | **Only** if there is a *clinical suspicion* of sarcoidosis or lymphoma (e.g., pulmonary infiltrates, hilar lymphadenopathy, persistent unexplained systemic symptoms). |

### Step 4 – Decision Point – Is Neuro‑Behçet’s Plausible?  

* **ICBD score ≥ 4** (oral ulcer + genital ulcer + skin lesions) → **Definite Behçet’s**.  
* **Neurological symptoms** (meningitis‑type, aseptic CSF) with **no alternative diagnosis** → **Acute neuro‑BD**.  

If both criteria are met, you have enough confidence to **start disease‑specific therapy** (colchicine ± steroids) while continuing supportive care for SIADH (fluid restriction, careful sodium monitoring).  

### Step 5 – Reserve “Broad” Tests for *True* Red Flags  

| Red Flag | Suggested Additional Test |
|----------|---------------------------|
| **Pulmonary infiltrates, cough, hemoptysis** | Chest CT; BAL if indicated. |
| **Unexplained lymphadenopathy, night sweats** | PET‑CT or excisional node biopsy. |
| **Rapidly progressive neurologic decline** | Repeat MRI with diffusion, possible brain biopsy. |
| **Immunosuppression (e.g., transplant, HIV)** | Expanded viral and opportunistic pathogen panels. |

---  

## Take‑Home Pearls  

- **Mucocutaneous clues are gold**: In a febrile patient with a headache, the presence of oral/genital ulcers should instantly raise Behçet’s on the differential – even before imaging.  
- **Normal MRI ≠ rule‑out neuro‑BD**: Early acute neuro‑Behçet’s can present with a completely normal scan; rely on CSF and clinical context.  
- **SIADH is often an *effect* of CNS inflammation**: Check serum/urine osmoles early; don’t chase SIADH with vasopressin antagonists if fluid restriction and treating the underlying inflammation (e.g., colchicine) suffice.  
- **Colchicine can be a “single‑agent” bridge**: While steroids are traditional for neuro‑BD, this case shows colchicine monotherapy may suffice for milder meningoencephalitic presentations, especially when steroids are contraindicated.  
- **Cut the “shot‑gun” panels**: ACE, sIL‑2R, extensive viral PCRs, and broad autoimmune screens should be ordered **only when the pre‑test probability is appreciable**. Otherwise, they add cost, delay, and risk of false positives.  

---  

## Bottom Line  

When faced with a patient who could have neuro‑Behçet’s, start with the **clinical story** and a **focused set of labs**. Let the presence of characteristic mucocutaneous lesions and a compatible meningeal picture steer you toward a targeted work‑up. Reserve the expansive infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune panels for those cases where the *clinical picture* truly suggests an alternative diagnosis. By trimming the work‑up to hypothesis‑driven steps, you’ll get to the right treatment faster, spare the patient unnecessary testing, and keep your stewardship metrics intact.  

*Stay curious, stay focused, and remember: a few well‑chosen clues can outshine a mountain of tests.*



# Beyond the Ulcers: Systemic Behçet’s Disease and Endocrine Complications  

## Why This Case Got My Attention  

When a 60‑year‑old man walks into the ED with fever, a pounding headache, and a handful of oral ulcers, you’re already thinking “Behçet’s disease” – especially if the skin is dotted with folliculitis‑like papules and a painful genital ulcer later shows up.  What makes the story truly **unique** is that, on the same admission, he develops a profound hyponatraemia (Na⁺ 124 mmol/L) that fulfills the classic criteria for **syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH)**.  

Neurological Behçet’s (neuro‑BD) itself is rare (10‑20 % of BD patients) and usually appears years after the mucocutaneous lesions.  Simultaneous diagnosis of systemic BD, acute neuro‑BD, **and** SIADH is practically unheard of.  Even more surprising—he improves dramatically with colchicine alone, without corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants.  

This case forces us to ask:  

* Are endocrine disturbances an under‑appreciated facet of Behçet’s?  
* How often should we be looking for them, and what mechanisms might be driving the link?  

Below we unpack the endocrine spectrum of BD, walk through the pathophysiology, and lay out a pragmatic screening approach for the busy clinician.  

## Quick Recap of the Index Case  

| Feature | Key Findings |
|--------|--------------|
| **Age / Sex** | 60‑year‑old Asian man |
| **Presenting symptoms** | Fever, headache, hiccups, arthralgia, oral aphthae |
| **Mucocutaneous signs** | Oral ulcers, folliculitis‑like rash, genital ulcer (HLA‑B51⁺) |
| **Neurologic work‑up** | Normal brain MRI, CSF: mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, ↑ protein |
| **Endocrine labs** | Hyponatraemia 124 mmol/L, low serum osmolality, high urine osmolality, ↑ urinary Na⁺, ADH 22.4 pg/mL → SIADH |
| **Management** | Colchicine monotherapy, fluid restriction; steroids avoided |
| **Outcome** | Resolution of fever, neurologic symptoms, mucocutaneous lesions, and Na⁺ normalization within 2 weeks; 4‑year remission on colchicine alone |

The take‑home message from this single vignette is that **acute neuro‑BD can trigger SIADH**, and that **colchicine may be sufficient** to tame both the inflammatory and endocrine storm in selected patients.  

## The Broader Endocrine Landscape in Behçet’s  

While the textbook definition of BD centers on oral/genital ulcers, uveitis, and skin lesions, a growing body of case reports and small series points to a surprisingly diverse set of endocrine derangements.  Below is a non‑exhaustive list of the most frequently reported entities, together with a brief mechanistic hypothesis for each.  

### 1. SIADH (the star of our case)  

* **Epidemiology:** < 1 % of BD cohorts, but reported more often in neuro‑BD series.  
* **Mechanism:** CNS inflammation → cytokine surge (IL‑6, IL‑1β) → hypothalamic ADH release. IL‑6 is known to stimulate ADH directly; BD is a prototypical IL‑6‑rich disease.  
* **Clinical clue:** Persistent hiccups, nausea, or unexplained low Na⁺ in a febrile BD patient.  

### 2. Adrenal Insufficiency  

* **Reports:** Isolated case reports of primary adrenal failure secondary to vasculitic infiltration or Addisonian crisis precipitated by infection in BD.  
* **Mechanism:** Small‑vessel vasculitis causing adrenal hemorrhage (Waterhouse‑Friderichsen‑like) or granulomatous destruction.  
* **Red flags:** Hyperpigmentation, orthostatic hypotension, hyponatraemia with hyperkalaemia, refractory fatigue.  

### 3. Thyroiditis (subclinical or overt)  

* **Reports:** A handful of cases of painless thyroiditis or Graves‑type hyperthyroidism.  
* **Mechanism:** Autoimmune cross‑reactivity; BD shares HLA‑B51 with other autoimmune thyroid disorders. Cytokine‑mediated follicular injury may precipitate thyroid hormone leakage.  
* **Red flags:** Tremor, heat intolerance, unexplained tachycardia in a BD patient.

### 4. Diabetes Mellitus (type 2)  

* **Reports:** Not truly “Behçet‑related” but a frequent comorbidity, often exacerbated by glucocorticoid flares.  
* **Mechanism:** Chronic inflammation → insulin resistance; glucocorticoids worsen glycaemia.  

### 5. Hyperparathyroidism & Calcium Dysregulation  

* **Reports:** Rare, but isolated hypercalcaemia described in vasculitic lesions affecting parathyroid glands.  
* **Mechanism:** Granulomatous production of 1,25‑OH‑vitamin D.  

### 6. Pituitary Involvement  

* **Reports:** Case series of pituitary stalk thickening, hypophysitis, and either diabetes insipidus or pan‑hypopituitarism.  
* **Mechanism:** Direct inflammatory infiltration of the pituitary‑hypothalamic axis.  

## Putting Pathophysiology Into Practice  

The common denominator across most of these endocrine hits is **systemic vasculitis plus a cytokine surge** (IL‑1β, IL‑6, TNF‑α).  In the central nervous system, inflammation can hijack the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, leading to either excess (SIADH) or deficiency (adrenal insufficiency).  Peripheral vascular inflammation can compromise glandular perfusion (e.g., adrenal hemorrhage).  

**Key point:** Endocrine manifestations often *precede* obvious organ damage; they may be the first clue that your BD is not just “ulcers on the skin.”  

## Practical Screening Recommendations  

1. **Baseline labs at diagnosis of systemic BD**  
   * CBC, CMP (focus on Na⁺, K⁺, glucose, calcium)  
   * ESR/CRP (to gauge inflammatory load)  
   * Thyroid panel (TSH, free T₄)  
   * Morning cortisol (optional, but low threshold if fatigue, hypotension, or hyponatraemia)  

2. **Targeted endocrine work‑up when neuro‑BD is suspected**  
   * Serum and urine osmolality, urine Na⁺ → SIADH screen  
   * ADH level (if available) – not mandatory, but helpful for academic cases  
   * MRI of hypothalamic‑pituitary region if neurologic signs are focal or persistent  

3. **During acute flares** (especially when high‑dose steroids are used)  
   * Re‑check glucose and electrolytes daily for the first 72 h  
   * Monitor for new‑onset hyponatraemia or hyperkalaemia  

4. **When steroids are prescribed**  
   * Prophylactic bone health assessment (DEXA) – steroids are a known risk factor  
   * Educate patients on symptoms of adrenal crisis (especially if tapering rapidly)  

5. **Long‑term follow‑up** (every 6–12 months)  
   * Repeat thyroid panel; consider anti‑thyroid antibodies if TSH abnormal  
   * Annual fasting glucose/HbA1c, even if previously normoglycaemic  

**Bottom line:** A single set of baseline labs will catch the majority of clinically relevant endocrine issues; repeat testing should be driven by new symptoms or high‑dose steroid exposure.  

## Therapeutic Nuggets from the Index Case  

* **Colchicine as a front‑line agent** – In this patient, colchicine (1.5 mg daily) was enough to quell both the mucocutaneous flare and the neuro‑inflammatory process, leading to rapid Na⁺ correction without the need for steroids.  While colchicine is classically a mucocutaneous therapy, its inhibition of neutrophil chemotaxis and IL‑1β/IL‑6 production may make it a viable “bridge” for mild neuro‑BD.  
* **Fluid restriction remains cornerstone for SIADH** – Target < 800 mL/day, monitor Na⁺ every 6–12 h, avoid rapid shifts (> 8 mmol/L/24 h) to prevent osmotic demyelination.  
* **Avoid unnecessary hypertonic saline** – In the setting of SIADH secondary to inflammation, correction is usually modest; aggressive saline may worsen cerebral edema.  

When steroids are unavoidable (e.g., fulminant parenchymal lesions), taper quickly and consider adding colchicine or azathioprine to limit steroid exposure and the downstream risk of iatrogenic diabetes.  

## Take‑Home Pearls  

* **Think endocrine early.** Fever, headache, and hyponatraemia in a BD patient should immediately raise suspicion for SIADH, especially if CSF shows inflammatory changes.  
* **Neuro‑BD can be a “silent” diagnosis.** Normal MRI does *not* exclude acute meningoencephalitis‑type neuro‑BD; CSF analysis and clinical context are essential.  
* **Colchicine may be more than skin‑deep.** In select patients with mild neuro‑BD and SIADH, colchicine monotherapy can control inflammation and restore electrolyte balance.  
* **Screening is cheap, consequences are not.** A baseline CMP, thyroid panel, and cortisol level at BD diagnosis can prevent missed adrenal insufficiency or thyroid dysfunction down the line.  
* **Cytokines are the bridge.** IL‑6 and IL‑1β link vascular inflammation to ADH release, adrenal cortical suppression, and thyroid disruption—reminding us that Behçet’s is fundamentally an immunologic disease with multisystem sequelae.  

## Final Thoughts  

Behçet’s disease may start as “recurrent mouth ulcers,” but the story often unfolds across organ systems we don’t routinely inspect.  Endocrine complications—SIADH, adrenal insufficiency, thyroiditis—are rare but clinically significant.  By incorporating a **low‑threshold endocrine screen** into the initial work‑up and staying vigilant for atypical neurologic presentations, we can catch these complications early, tailor therapy (perhaps even with colchicine alone), and spare patients from the morbidity of uncontrolled inflammation and electrolyte derangements.  

Stay curious, keep your labs broad, and remember: **the next ulcer might be a hormone.**



# Managing Severe Hyponatraemia Safely in Neuro‑Inflammatory Disorders  
*Lessons from an acute neuro‑Behçet’s case complicated by SIADH*

## Why this case matters  

Hyponatraemia is the most common electrolyte abnormality you’ll see on the ward, but severe, symptomatic drops in serum sodium (<125 mmol/L) are a true emergency. The stakes are high: rapid over‑correction can precipitate osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS), while under‑treatment leaves the patient at risk for seizures, coma, and even death.  

The case of a 60‑year‑old man with **acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) and SIADH** illustrates how a neuro‑inflammatory trigger can produce profound hyponatraemia that masquerades as “just a headache and fever.” It also shows that careful, protocol‑driven management—combined with treatment of the underlying inflammation—can resolve the electrolyte derangement without resorting to high‑dose steroids or vasopressin antagonists.

Below is a practical, step‑by‑step guide to managing severe hyponatraemia in the setting of CNS inflammation, peppered with pearls from the case.

---

## 1. Recognise the red flags early  

| Clinical clue | Why it matters |
|---------------|----------------|
| **Persistent hiccups** | Often a harbinger of severe hyponatraemia in meningitis/ encephalitis (seen in the BD case). |
| **Rhabdomyolysis (CK > 1000 U/L)** | Hyponatraemia can cause muscle breakdown; CK elevation should prompt electrolyte review. |
| **Euvolemic exam + low serum osmolality + inappropriately concentrated urine** | Classic triad for SIADH. If you have fever + headache + these labs → think CNS‑mediated SIADH. |
| **Rapidly falling Na⁺** (e.g., 124 mmol/L on admission) | Indicates ongoing ADH secretion; urgent management required. |

**Take‑home:** In any patient with acute meningo‑encephalitis‑type picture, order a basic metabolic panel early. A sodium of <130 mmol/L should trigger an SIADH work‑up.

---

## 2. Confirm SIADH **and rule out mimics**  

1. **Serum osmolality** < 275 mOsm/kg (267 mOsm/kg in the BD case).  
2. **Urine osmolality** > 100 mOsm/kg (900 mOsm/kg here).  
3. **Urine sodium** > 30 mmol/L (88 mmol/L in the case).  
4. **Euvolemic status** – no signs of hypovolemia (tachycardia, orthostasis) or hypervolemia (edema, ascites).  
5. **Exclusion of adrenal, thyroid, renal failure, or diuretic use** – all were normal in the BD patient.

If any of the above are inconsistent, keep looking (e.g., adrenal insufficiency, hypothyroidism).  

**Pearl:** Inflammatory markers (CRP > 150 mg/L) can clue you into an IL‑6‑driven process that may be fueling ADH release, as in neuro‑BD.

---

## 3. Set a **target correction rate** – the safest road  

| Desired correction (total) | Maximum safe rate |
|----------------------------|-------------------|
| **< 48 h** | ≤ 8 mmol/L per 24 h (≈ 0.33 mmol/L per hour) |
| **< 24 h** | ≤ 6 mmol/L per 24 h if chronic (>48 h) hyponatraemia; ≤ 8 mmol/L if acute (<48 h) |

The BD patient was **acutely** hyponatraemic (3 days of symptoms), so a correction up to 8 mmol/L/24 h was permissible—but **never exceed 12 mmol/L in 24 h** to avoid ODS.

**Practical tip:** Write the correction limit on the bedside flow‑sheet and set an “alert” for any sodium rise > 4 mmol/L in the first 6 h.

---

## 4. Fluid restriction – the first‑line weapon  

- **Goal:** ≤ 800 mL/day (the BD case used this exact volume).  
- **Implementation:**  
  - Clear fluids only (no soups, ice chips, or IV meds unless absolutely necessary).  
  - Replace oral meds with crushable tablets dissolved in the restricted volume.  
  - Communicate the restriction to nursing, dietetics, and the patient’s family—conflict arises when “water bowl” orders are automatically applied.

**When fluid restriction alone fails:**  
- Urine output > 2 L/day despite restriction → consider a **vaptan** (see below).  
- Persistent Na⁺ < 125 mmol/L after 12‑24 h → move to pharmacologic options.

---

## 5. Pharmacologic adjuncts  

| Agent | Indication | Dose & Monitoring |
|-------|------------|--------------------|
| **Hypertonic saline (3 % NaCl)** | Severe symptoms (seizures, obtundation, > 130 mmol/L drop) | 100 mL bolus (≈ 1.5 mmol Na⁺) → re‑check Na⁺ in 30 min; repeat if needed, never exceed target. |
| **Tolvaptan (V2‑receptor antagonist)** | Hyponatraemia refractory to fluid restriction, urine output > 2 L, or patient unable to tolerate restriction. | Start 15 mg PO daily; check Na⁺ at 4, 8, 12 h; stop if Na⁺ ↑ > 12 mmol/L/24 h. |
| **Urea (15‑30 g PO/NG)** | Low‑resource settings; works by increasing osmotic diuresis. | Give with water restriction; monitor Na⁺ q6h. |
| **Demeclocycline** | Chronic SIADH, not acute (slow onset, nephrotoxic). | Not first‑line in acute neuro‑inflammation. |

**Case highlight:** The BD patient never required hypertonic saline or tolvaptan; strict fluid restriction plus treatment of the underlying inflammation (colchicine) sufficed. This underscores that **treat the cause** whenever feasible.

---

## 6. Treat the underlying neuro‑inflammation  

- **Colchicine** (0.5–1 mg bid) – in the reported case, it controlled both mucocutaneous BD lesions and neuro‑BD meningo‑encephalitis, leading to rapid normalization of ADH and Na⁺.  
- **Corticosteroids** – standard for neuro‑BD, but may exacerbate hyperglycaemia; reserve for refractory CNS disease or when rapid control is needed.  
- **Other immunosuppressants** (azathioprine, cyclophosphamide) – consider if steroid‑sparing needed, but be aware they can themselves precipitate SIADH.

**Lesson:** Even without steroids, controlling inflammation can “turn off” the ADH driver. When you have a clear inflammatory culprit, start disease‑specific therapy early; it may obviate the need for aggressive sodium‑raising measures.

---

## 7. Monitoring workflow – a checklist  

1. **Baseline labs** – Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻, bicarbonate, glucose, BUN, creatinine, serum/urine osmolality, urinary Na⁺.  
2. **Serial Na⁺** – q4‑6 h until stable, then q12 h. Use point‑of‑care electrolytes if available.  
3. **Neurologic exam** – every shift; watch for new seizures or worsening mental status (signs of over‑correction).  
4. **Fluid balance** – strict input/output chart; watch for hidden sources (meds, enteral feeds).  
5. **Urine output** – high urine output (> 2 L) despite restriction may herald impending over‑correction; pre‑emptively give D5W or stop restriction.  

**Safety net:** If Na⁺ rises > 8 mmol/L in 24 h, give **desmopressin (DDAVP)** 1‑2 µg IV or SC to “re‑lock” water handling and give D5W to blunt further rise.

---

## 8. Coordination of care  

- **Neurology** – for definitive diagnosis (MRI, CSF), to guide immunotherapy, and to monitor for complications (e.g., pontine lesions from ODS).  
- **Nephrology** – assists with fluid management algorithms, especially when 24‑h Na⁺ trends become unpredictable.  
- **Endocrinology** – useful when ADH assays are needed, or when there is coexistent adrenal/thyroid dysfunction.  
- **Pharmacy** – ensures appropriate dosing of colchicine (watch for renal clearance) and flags drug interactions (e.g., CYP3A4 inhibitors that raise colchicine levels).

A multidisciplinary “Hyponatraemia Board” (even a quick huddle) can prevent missteps, especially in complex neuro‑inflammatory cases.

---

## 9. Key take‑home pearls  

- **Never attribute hyponatraemia to “water intoxication” alone** in a febrile patient with CNS signs; think SIADH secondary to neuro‑inflammation.  
- **Fluid restriction ≤ 800 mL/day** is often enough when the underlying inflammatory process is treated promptly.  
- **Aim for ≤ 8 mmol/L Na⁺ rise per 24 h**; if you overshoot, stop restriction, give free water, or use DDAVP.  
- **Colchicine can be a steroid‑sparing option** for acute neuro‑BD; its anti‑IL‑6 effect may dampen ADH secretion.  
- **Monitor for “silent” complications:** hiccups, rhabdomyolysis, and subtle changes in mental status can be the first clue that hyponatraemia is worsening.  

---

## 10. Bottom line  

Severe hyponatraemia in the context of CNS inflammation is a *treatable* problem when you combine **rigorous electrolyte management** with **targeted therapy of the neuro‑inflammatory trigger**. The acute neuro‑Behçet’s case teaches us that:  

1. A disciplined fluid‑restriction protocol, guided by strict correction limits, can safely normalize sodium.  
2. Early control of the inflammatory disease (colchicine in this instance) may eliminate the ADH driver, sparing patients from high‑dose steroids or costly vaptans.  
3. Multidisciplinary coordination and vigilant monitoring are the safety nets that keep us from ODS‑related catastrophes.

Next time you see a feverish patient with a headache, hiccups, and a sodium of 124 mmol/L—think **SIADH from neuro‑inflammation**, start fluid restriction, call neurology, and remember: **slow and steady wins the race**.
