Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Clinical Pearls] What are the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD) and how did this patient meet the threshold for a definite diagnosis?

**International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD) – Quick Reference**

| Clinical manifestation | Points* |
|------------------------|---------|
| Oral aphthosis (≥ 1 ulcer) | 2 |
| Genital ulceration (≥ 1 ulcer) | 2 |
| Skin lesions (papulopustular, erythema nodosum‑like, acne‑iform, folliculitis‑like) | 1 |
| Ocular involvement (anterior/posterior uveitis, retinal vasculitis) | 2 |
| Vascular involvement (venous thrombosis, arterial aneurysm/occlusion) | 1 |
| Neurologic involvement (meningoencephalitis, parenchymal CNS lesion, peripheral neuropathy) | 1 |
| Positive pathergy test* | 1 (optional, not required for diagnosis) |

*The ICBD uses a ≥ 4‑point threshold for **definite Behçet’s disease**. The pathergy test is optional and not required for the score; a patient can reach the threshold without it.

---

### How the patient satisfied the ICBD and crossed the diagnostic cut‑off

1. **Oral aphthae** – documented at presentation (multiple painful aphthous ulcers).  
   *Score: 2 points.*

2. **Genital ulcer** – a painful scrotal ulcer appeared on hospital day 5, characteristic of genital aphthosis in BD.  
   *Score: 2 points.*

3. **Skin lesions** – folliculitis‑like papules on the face and trunk (Fig 1A‑C), a recognized cutaneous manifestation of BD.  
   *Score: 1 point.*

   - No ocular, vascular, or pathergy findings were reported, and the neurologic involvement (aseptic meningitis) was present but already accounted for by the mucocutaneous criteria. Even without counting the neurologic point the sum is **5 points**.

4. **Total ICBD score = 2 (oral) + 2 (genital) + 1 (skin) = **5 points**.  
   Since the predefined threshold for definite disease is **≥ 4 points**, the patient meets the ICBD definition of Behçet’s disease.

---

### Teaching points

- **ICBD are purely clinical**; laboratory or HLA typing (e.g., HLA‑B51) does not influence the score. In this case, HLA‑B51 was positive, reinforcing suspicion but not required for the diagnosis.
- **Neurologic involvement can contribute** 1 point, but a diagnosis can be made without it when mucocutaneous findings already reach the threshold. This is particularly useful early in the disease when CNS imaging may be normal, as occurred here.
- **Pathergy testing is optional**; many centers do not perform it routinely, and a diagnosis should not be delayed awaiting the result.
- **Early recognition** of a ≥ 4‑point score enables timely treatment (colchicine in this case) and prompts evaluation for organ‑specific complications such as SIADH.

Thus, by applying the ICBD point system, the patient’s oral aphthae (2), genital ulcer (2), and folliculitis‑like skin lesions (1) summated to 5 points, comfortably surpassing the ≥ 4‑point cut‑off for definite Behçet’s disease.

### [Morning Report] In a patient with fever, headache, hyponatraemia and aseptic meningitis, outline the stepwise differential diagnosis and the key investigations that help distinguish infectious meningitis from acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease.

**Acute fever‑headache‑hyponatraemia with aseptic meningitis – how do we separate an infectious meningitis from acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD)?**  

Below is a step‑wise diagnostic algorithm that can be used at the bedside, illustrated with the case of the 60‑year‑old man who presented with fever, headache, marked hyponatraemia (Na⁺ 124 mmol/L) and a lymphocytic CSF pleocytosis.

---

### 1.  First‑line “rule‑out” for infection (the “infectious‑meningitis” branch)

| Question / Finding | What it tells you | Where it fits in the algorithm |
|--------------------|-------------------|--------------------------------|
| **Rapid onset, high fever, neck stiffness?** | Classic bacterial meningitis (usually >38.5 °C, stiff neck, altered mental status). | If present → start empiric ceftriaxone + vancomycin ± ampicillin, obtain urgent CSF. |
| **CSF opening pressure > 25 cm H₂O, neutrophil predominance, low glucose, high protein?** | Bacterial meningitis (or early tuberculous meningitis). | Positive → treat as bacterial; consider adjunctive dexamethasone. |
| **CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis, normal/ mildly low glucose, protein ↑ modestly?** | Viral or aseptic meningitis, tuberculous meningitis, early neuro‑BD. | Proceed to targeted viral PCR and TB work‑up. |
| **Viral PCR (HSV‑1/2, VZV, enteroviruses, EBV, CMV) negative?** | Decreases likelihood of common viral encephalitis. | In the case, all viral studies were negative. |
| **CSF acid‑fast stain, TB PCR, culture negative?** | Makes tuberculous meningitis unlikely (but remember that TB may be smear‑negative early). | In the case, TB tests were negative. |
| **Blood cultures, urine cultures, chest X‑ray, ENT exam – no focus of infection** | Low pre‑test probability for bacterial sepsis. | Reinforces moving away from infection. |

**Take‑home point:** Once the CSF shows a *lymphocytic* picture, normal glucose and negative bacterial/viral/amoebic studies, infectious meningitis becomes a diagnosis of exclusion.

---

### 2.  Evaluate for **non‑infectious inflammatory meningitis** – the “neuro‑Behçet” branch

| Clinical clue | Why it matters | How it guides testing |
|---------------|----------------|-----------------------|
| **Mucocutaneous lesions (oral aphthae, genital ulcers, folliculitis‑like rash)** | Pathognomonic for Behçet’s disease (BD). | Apply the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD). In the case, oral aphthae (2 pts) + genital ulcers (2 pts) + skin lesions (1 pt) = 5 pts → definite BD. |
| **HLA‑B51 positivity** | Supports BD epidemiologically but not required for diagnosis. | Helpful background but does not change immediate management. |
| **Absence of focal neurologic deficits or parenchymal lesions on MRI** | Acute neuro‑BD can present as “meningoencephalitis‑like” syndrome with a *normal* MRI early on. | Normal brain MRI does **not** exclude neuro‑BD; repeat imaging if symptoms persist. |
| **CSF profile: mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (≤30 cells/µL), protein ↑ modestly, glucose normal** | Typical for acute neuro‑BD meningoencephalitis. | Correlate with systemic BD features. |
| **Elevated inflammatory markers (CRP > 100 mg/L, ESR > 50 mm/hr)** | Reflects systemic vasculitis; also explains IL‑6‑driven SIADH. | No specific cutoff, but helps differentiate from viral meningitis (usually CRP modest). |
| **SIADH (eu‑volemic hyponatraemia, low serum osmolality, urine osmolality >300 mOsm/kg, urine Na⁺ >30 mmol/L, ADH ↑)** | CNS inflammation can trigger ADH release; SIADH is a recognized complication of neuro‑BD. | Confirms a neuro‑inflammatory process; does not distinguish infection vs. BD alone, but supports an inflammatory etiology. |

**Key investigations that tip the balance toward neuro‑BD**

1. **Detailed mucocutaneous exam** – look for *genital ulcers* (high specificity) and *typical skin lesions* (pseudofolliculitis, erythema nodosum‑like).  
2. **ICBD scoring** – ≥4 points = definite BD.  
3. **Brain MRI with contrast** – while early neuro‑BD may be normal, look for characteristic brainstem, basal ganglia or thalamic hyperintensities (if present, strongly supportive).  
4. **CSF analysis** – lymphocytic pleocytosis, protein 0.5‑1.0 g/L, normal glucose, *negative cultures/PCR*.  
5. **Serum IL‑6 (if available)** – markedly elevated IL‑6 favors a cytokine‑driven inflammatory process and can explain SIADH. (Not done in the case but conceptually useful.)  
6. **Exclusion labs** – ACE (sarcoidosis), sIL‑2R (lymphoma), ANA, ANCA, anti‑NMDAR antibodies – performed only if clinical suspicion exists; all were negative in the case.  

---

### 3.  Putting it together – a practical flow‑chart for the presenting triad

```
Fever + Headache + Hyponatraemia
          |
   → Obtain serum electrolytes, osmolality, urine studies (SIADH screen)
          |
   → Perform lumbar puncture (CSF cell count, protein, glucose, cultures,
                              viral PCR, TB PCR)
          |
   ├─ If CSF neutrophilic, low glucose → Bacterial meningitis → treat
   └─ If CSF lymphocytic, cultures/PCR negative → Proceed to non‑infectious work‑up
          |
   → Detailed skin/mucosal exam + ICBD scoring
          |
   ├─ Meets ICBD criteria → Diagnose systemic BD
   │    → Add CSF + MRI → If compatible = acute neuro‑BD
   └─ Does NOT meet BD criteria → Consider other aseptic meningitis causes
          (autoimmune, sarcoid, lymphoma, drug‑induced SIADH)
```

---

### 4.  Take‑home pearls for the morning report

* **Infectious meningitis is the first consideration**, but a *lymphocytic* CSF with negative PCR/cultures should prompt a search for an inflammatory etiology.  
* **Behçet’s disease can present neurologically at the first encounter**; look carefully for oral/genital ulcers and characteristic skin lesions.  
* **A normal brain MRI does not exclude acute neuro‑BD**; the early meningo‑encephalitic phase may be radiologically silent.  
* **SIADH is a valuable physiologic clue**—CNS inflammation (viral, tuberculous, or neuro‑BD) can cause ADH release; hyponatraemia often precedes or accompanies the neurologic picture.  
* **CSF analysis remains the cornerstone**: modest lymphocytic pleocytosis (≤30 cells/µL) with protein up‑trend and sterile cultures leans toward neuro‑BD once systemic BD is established.  
* **Management implications**: If neuro‑BD is diagnosed, immunosuppression (corticosteroids ± colchicine, azathioprine, or cyclophosphamide) is indicated; however, this case shows that colchicine alone can suffice in a mild, self‑limited presentation.  

By following this step‑wise approach, you can efficiently distinguish infectious meningitis from acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease in patients who present with the striking triad of fever, headache, hyponatraemia and aseptic meningitis.

### [Pathophysiology] Explain the pathophysiologic mechanisms by which central nervous system inflammation can lead to syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH), and discuss the role of interleukin‑6 in this process.

**Pathophysiologic link between CNS inflammation and SIADH – the role of IL‑6**

The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) is defined by euvolemic hyponatraemia with inappropriately concentrated urine and elevated plasma ADH despite low plasma osmolality.  In the setting of acute central nervous system (CNS) inflammation, the “inappropriateness” of ADH release is driven by two related mechanisms:

1. **Direct stimulation of the hypothalamic‑posterior pituitary axis**  
   • Inflammatory cells, cytokines and vasoactive mediators infiltrate or activate the perivascular space surrounding the supra‑optic and paraventricular nuclei, the sites where ADH‑producing magnocellular neurons reside.  
   • Activation of these neurons increases the synthesis and axonal transport of pre‑pro‑vasopressin, leading to heightened secretion into the systemic circulation.

2. **Cytokine‑mediated augmentation of ADH synthesis and release**  
   • Among the cytokines released during CNS inflammation, interleukin‑6 (IL‑6) is the most consistently implicated in SIADH.  IL‑6 binds to its receptor on magnocellular neurons and on adjacent astrocytes, triggering intracellular signaling (JAK/STAT3) that up‑regulates vasopressin gene transcription and promotes exocytosis of stored ADH.  
   • IL‑6 also acts peripherally by sensitising the renal collecting‑duct V2 receptors, augmenting water reabsorption even when ADH levels fall, thereby amplifying the hyponatraemic effect.

**Evidence from the reported case**

* The patient presented with acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) manifested as aseptic meningitis.  CSF showed lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein—classic hallmarks of CNS inflammation that would expose the hypothalamic nuclei to inflammatory mediators.  
* Serum CRP was markedly elevated (180 mg/L).  CRP is an acute‑phase reactant whose synthesis is driven by IL‑6; thus the high CRP serves as a surrogate marker for an IL‑6‑rich inflammatory milieu.  
* ADH measured during the hyponatraemic phase was **inappropriately high** (22.4 pg/mL) despite hypo‑osmolar plasma, fulfilling the biochemical criteria for SIADH.  
* The hyponatraemia resolved in parallel with clinical remission after colchicine therapy.  Colchicine attenuates neutrophil activation and suppresses IL‑1β and IL‑6 production; the temporal association suggests that dampening IL‑6‑driven inflammation reduced ADH secretion and restored water homeostasis.

**Putting it together**

In acute neuro‑BD, the inflammatory cascade (activated monocytes, neutrophils, and Th17 cells) leads to excess IL‑6 production both systemically and within the CSF.  IL‑6 then:

1. Directly stimulates hypothalamic magnocellular neurons → ↑ ADH synthesis/secretion.  
2. Enhances renal responsiveness to ADH → ↑ water reabsorption.  

The net effect is a euvolemic hyponatraemia that meets the diagnostic thresholds for SIADH.  When the underlying inflammation subsides—here achieved with colchicine‑mediated IL‑6 suppression—ADH levels fall (from 22.4 pg/mL to 1.8 pg/mL) and serum sodium normalises.

**Clinical take‑home points**

* **CNS inflammation should always be on the differential for SIADH**—especially when hyponatraemia accompanies fever, headache, or meningitic signs.  
* **IL‑6 is the key cytokine linking inflammation to ADH dysregulation**; elevated CRP can be a bedside clue to an IL‑6‑driven process.  
* **Targeted anti‑inflammatory therapy that reduces IL‑6 (e.g., colchicine, glucocorticoids, IL‑6 antagonists)** can correct SIADH without the need for specific anti‑diuretic agents.  
* Monitoring ADH or surrogate markers (CRP, IL‑6 when available) can help gauge response and guide safe fluid‑restriction strategies.

Thus, the case illustrates a classic pathophysiologic cascade: acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease → CNS IL‑6 surge → hypothalamic ADH excess → SIADH → hyponatraemia, all of which reversed when IL‑6–mediated inflammation was controlled.

### [Differential Diagnosis] What clinical and laboratory criteria are required to diagnose SIADH, and which alternative causes of hyponatraemia must be excluded in this case?

**SIADH – diagnostic checklist and “must‑exclude” differentials (as illustrated by the Behçet case)**  

| **Criterion** (required) | **What to demonstrate in this patient** |
|---------------------------|------------------------------------------|
| **1. Hyponatraemia with hypo‑osmolar plasma** | Serum Na⁺ = 124 mmol/L; plasma osmolality = 267 mOsm/kg ( < 275 mOsm/kg). |
| **2. Inappropriately concentrated urine** | Urine osmolality = 900 mOsm/kg ( > 100 mOsm/kg despite low plasma osmolality). |
| **3. Elevated urine sodium** | Urine Na⁺ = 88 mmol/L ( > 30‑40 mmol/L in the setting of euvolaemia). |

All seven are met → **SIADH is diagnosed** in this case.

---

### Take‑home pearls for the bedside clinician

1. **SIADH is a diagnosis of exclusion** – a stepwise approach (hyponatraemia → hypo‑osmolar → inappropriately concentrated urine → euvolaemia → rule‑out endocrine, renal, medication, and neoplastic causes) reliably isolates SIADH even in complex inflammatory states such as acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease.

2. **The Behçet case illustrates two classic pitfalls**  
   - *Attribution of hyponatraemia to diabetes mellitus* – hyperglycaemia can cause a translocational hyponatraemia, but correction for glucose still left a serum Na⁺ ≈ 124 mmol/L, confirming true hypotonic hyponatraemia.  
   - *Assuming infection as the sole cause of low sodium* – CNS infections can trigger SIADH, but extensive CSF work‑up was negative; the inflammatory milieu of neuro‑BD itself was the more plausible stimulus (likely IL‑6–driven ADH release).

3. **Management hinges on confirming euvolaemia** – aggressive fluid restriction (≤ 800 mL/day in this patient) and careful sodium monitoring are sufficient when the underlying inflammatory trigger (neuro‑BD) is treated (colchicine in this case). Avoid rapid correction (> 8–10 mmol/L/24 h) to prevent osmotic demyelination.

4. **Always re‑evaluate the “exclusions” if the clinical picture changes** – emergence of new pulmonary findings, new medications, or a rising creatinine should prompt repeat assessment for alternative causes of hyponatraemia.

In summary, the patient met all validated SIADH criteria and had every competing etiology reasonably excluded, allowing the team to attribute the hyponatraemia to SIADH secondary to acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease. This systematic approach should be applied to all patients presenting with unexplained hypo‑osmolar hyponatraemia.

### [Clinical Pearls] Compare the typical radiologic findings of parenchymal neuro‑Behçet’s disease with the imaging pattern seen in this patient and explain why a normal MRI does not rule out acute neuro‑BD.

**Clinical Pearl – Imaging in Acute Parenchymal Neuro‑Behçet’s Disease**

**Typical radiologic pattern**  
Parenchymal neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) most often produces focal, T2/FLAIR hyper‑intense lesions in the brainstem (especially the pons, midbrain, and medulla), basal ganglia, thalamus, and occasionally the cerebral white matter. On contrast‑enhanced studies these lesions may show faint or rim‑like enhancement, and chronic disease can leave residual gliosis or atrophy. Diffusion‑weighted imaging can demonstrate restricted diffusion when the inflammation is acute, and susceptibility sequences may reveal small hemorrhagic foci that are characteristic of BD–related vasculitis.

**What we saw in this case**  
The 60‑year‑old man presented with fever, headache, vertigo, and aseptic meningitis‑type CSF changes, yet his brain MRI was completely normal—no brain‑stem hyper‑intensities, no hypothalamic or diencephalic signal alteration, and no contrast enhancement.  

**Why a normal MRI does not exclude acute neuro‑BD**

1. **Early meningo‑encephalitic phase** – In the first days to weeks, inflammation may be confined to the leptomeninges and subarachnoid space, producing meningeal irritation (headache, fever) and CSF pleocytosis without parenchymal involvement. MRI is insensitive to pure meningitis; the meninges are thin and may not show enhancement unless the blood‑brain barrier is markedly disrupted.

2. **Transient or microscopic lesions** – Small perivascular inflammatory infiltrates can be below the spatial resolution of routine 1.5‑T MRI. Even high‑resolution 3‑T scans can miss lesions that are microscopic or only present as subtle edema, particularly when the patient is treated early (empiric antibiotics, acyclovir) or when the inflammatory burden is modest.

3. **Heterogeneous disease spectrum** – Approximately 20 % of acute neuro‑BD cases present as isolated meningo‑encephalitis without detectable parenchymal lesions. The International Study Group criteria for neuro‑BD explicitly state that radiologic abnormalities are supportive but not required; the diagnosis rests on compatible clinical picture together with CSF evidence of inflammation and exclusion of infection, malignancy, or other autoimmune disease.

4. **Dynamic evolution** – Lesions often appear **after** the acute meningeal phase – serial imaging performed weeks later may reveal the classic brain‑stem hyper‑intensities. A single early MRI, therefore, can be falsely reassuring.

**Take‑home message for trainees**

- **Do not rely on a normal MRI to rule out acute neuro‑BD** when the clinical constellation (fever, headache, mucocutaneous ulcers, CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis, elevated protein, hyponatraemia from SIADH) is suggestive.  
- **CSF analysis** and a careful assessment of systemic Behçet features remain the cornerstone of diagnosis in the early stage.  
- **Repeat imaging** after 2–3 weeks may be needed if neurological symptoms persist or evolve.  
- Recognize that neuro‑BD can manifest solely as aseptic meningitis, a pattern exemplified by the present case, and that timely treatment (here colchicine) can lead to rapid clinical resolution even in the absence of radiologic evidence.

Thus, while typical neuro‑BD shows brain‑stem T2/FLAIR lesions, a normal MRI—as in this patient—does not exclude acute neuro‑BD because early meningeal inflammation may be radiographically occult. Clinical judgment, CSF findings, and exclusion of other etiologies are paramount.

### [Management] Discuss the standard first‑line therapeutic approach for acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease, including the role of corticosteroids versus colchicine, and justify why colchicine monotherapy was reasonable in this scenario.

**First‑line therapy for acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD)**  
The usual initial treatment of acute neuro‑BD is high‑dose systemic glucocorticoids (e.g., methylprednisolone 1 g IV daily for 3–5 days, followed by an oral taper). Steroids rapidly suppress the meningo‑encephalitic inflammation that characterises the “parenchymal” form of neuro‑BD and are supported by the majority of case series and expert consensus. In patients who fail to respond to steroids or who have relapsing disease, additional immunosuppressants (azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, mycophenolate mofetil, or biologics such as anti‑TNF agents) are added.

**Why colchicine is not the standard first‑line agent**  
Colchicine is the cornerstone for mucocutaneous lesions, erythema nodosum‑like lesions and, to a lesser extent, arthropathy in Behçet’s disease. Its anti‑inflammatory actions (inhibition of neutrophil chemotaxis, blockade of microtubule polymerisation, reduction of IL‑1β and IL‑6 release) are useful for superficial manifestations but, in systematic reviews, colchicine has **not** shown efficacy in controlling acute CNS inflammation. Consequently, guidelines place colchicine in a “supportive” role, usually combined with steroids or other immunosuppressants, not as monotherapy for neuro‑BD.

**Rationale for colchicine monotherapy in the reported case**  
In the case of the 60‑year‑old man with simultaneous systemic BD and acute neuro‑BD complicated by SIADH, several practical considerations justified the exclusive use of colchicine:

1. **Mild CNS phenotype** – The patient presented with headache, vertigo, and aseptic meningitis on CSF analysis but had no focal neurodeficits, parenchymal lesions on MRI, or rapid clinical deterioration. In such “meningo‑encephalitic” presentations, spontaneous resolution can occur, especially when the inflammatory burden is modest.

2. **Absence of contraindications to steroids but also lack of an urgent indication** – While steroids are effective, they carry risks (hyperglycaemia, infection, psychiatric effects). This patient already had poorly controlled diabetes, making high‑dose glucocorticoids potentially hazardous. The treating team therefore elected a steroid‑sparing approach.

3. **Evidence of systemic inflammation responsive to colchicine** – The patient’s fever, CRP (180 mg/L), and mucocutaneous lesions improved promptly after colchicine initiation, implying that the drug was adequately dampening the underlying IL‑6‑driven inflammation that also likely contributed to the SIADH.

4. **Temporal association of clinical improvement with colchicine** – Within 24 h of starting colchicine, fever resolved; over the next two weeks the headache, vertigo, and hyponatraemia normalized, and no additional immunosuppression was required. Although spontaneous remission cannot be excluded, the clear chronological link supports a therapeutic effect.

5. **Safety and tolerability** – Colchicine was well tolerated, required only oral administration, and avoided the complications of high‑dose steroids (worsening hyperglycaemia, risk of infection in a hospitalised patient).

**Bottom line for clinical practice**  

- **Standard first‑line** for acute neuro‑BD: high‑dose systemic glucocorticoids, with early addition of a steroid‑sparing immunosuppressant for refractory or relapsing disease.  
- **Colchicine** remains a valuable adjunct for mucocutaneous disease and may be considered as sole therapy **only** when the neuro‑BD presentation is mild, steroids are contraindicated, and the patient shows rapid clinical response, as illustrated by this case.  
- In all patients, close monitoring of neurological status, serum sodium (for SIADH), and adverse effects of any immunomodulatory therapy is essential.

Thus, while corticosteroids are the conventional first‑line agents for acute neuro‑BD, colchicine monotherapy was a reasonable, individualized choice in this patient because of a mild CNS presentation, concomitant diabetes, and an observed rapid clinical response.

### [Morning Report] Identify the potential complications of severe hyponatraemia in the setting of SIADH and explain why rhabdomyolysis and persistent hiccups can be presenting clues.

**Potential complications of severe hyponatraemia in SIADH (as illustrated in the Behçet‑related case)**  

1. **Cerebral oedema and neurologic deterioration** – Rapid fall in serum Na⁺ (<125 mmol/L) causes water shift into the intracellular compartment, especially brain cells. Patients can develop headache, confusion, seizures, or coma. In the reported patient the persistent hiccups and worsening vertigo were early neurologic warnings that the hyponatraemia was clinically significant.

2. **Rhabdomyolysis** – Severe hypotonicity impairs Na⁺‑dependent muscle cell homeostasis, leading to intracellular swelling, membrane disruption and leakage of creatine kinase (CK) and myoglobin. The case showed a CK of 1 349 U/L, confirming muscle injury that resolved as the sodium level normalized. Rhabdomyolysis can precipitate acute kidney injury if not recognised promptly.

3. **Cardiopulmonary effects** – Hyponatraemia reduces extracellular osmolarity, which can impair cardiac contractility and predispose to pulmonary oedema, particularly when concurrent volume‑status alterations exist. The patient remained euvolemic, but careful fluid monitoring was essential to avoid over‑correction‑related osmotic demyelination.

4. **Osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS)** – Although ODS is a risk of *rapid* correction, it is a feared complication in any severe hyponatraemia because the brain adapts to chronic low Na⁺. The treating team limited fluid intake to <800 mL/day and avoided hypertonic saline, thereby preventing a rapid rise in Na⁺ and the possibility of ODS.

---

### Why **persistent hiccups** and **rhabdomyolysis** are useful clues

| Symptom | Pathophysiologic link to hyponatraemia | Clinical relevance in the case |
|---------|----------------------------------------|--------------------------------|
| **Persistent hiccups** | Hiccups arise from irritation of the phrenic‑nerve‑mediated respiratory centre in the medulla. Severe hyponatraemia causes subtle brainstem oedema, which can stimulate the hiccup reflex. Reported in meningitis‑related SIADH, hiccups may be the first or only sign of profound hypo‑osmolarity. | The patient’s three‑day history of “persistent hiccups” preceded the neurological work‑up and, together with headache and vertigo, pointed toward a central electrolyte disturbance rather than a primary gastrointestinal cause. |
| **Rhabdomyolysis (elevated CK)** | Low extracellular Na⁺ diminishes the Na⁺/K⁺‑ATPase gradient, leading to intracellular Na⁺ accumulation, calcium overload, and muscle cell lysis. It is a well‑described, though under‑appreciated, complication of Na⁺ <125 mmol/L. | CK was markedly raised (1 349 U/L) on admission, prompting evaluation for muscle injury. Recognition that severe hyponatraemia can cause rhabdomyolysis guided the team to monitor renal function and avoid nephrotoxic agents while treating the electrolyte abnormality. |

**Take‑home teaching points**

- In any patient with SIADH, **always assess for neurologic signs** (confusion, seizures, hiccups) and **muscle enzyme elevation**; both may herald severe hyponatraemia even when the patient appears clinically stable.
- **Fluid restriction** and **slow, controlled correction** (≤8 mmol/L in 24 h) are mandatory to prevent ODS while allowing the underlying inflammation (neuro‑Behçet’s) to resolve.
- Resolution of the hyponatraemia, hiccups, and CK elevation after colchicine therapy in this case underscores that treating the primary inflammatory driver (neuro‑BD) is as important as the electrolyte management.

### [Management] How would you monitor and safely correct hyponatraemia in a patient with SIADH secondary to neuro‑inflammation, and what are the risks of overly rapid sodium correction?

**Monitoring and correcting hyponatraemia in SIADH secondary to neuro‑Behçet’s disease**

1. **Initial assessment and goals**  
   * Confirm true hypotonic hyponatraemia – serum Na < 135 mmol/L, serum osmolality < 275 mOsm/kg, inappropriately concentrated urine (> 100 mOsm/kg) with urine Na > 30 mmol/L, as was demonstrated in the case (Na 124 mmol/L, serum osmolality 267 mOsm/kg, urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg, urine Na 88 mmol/L).  
   * Establish volume status (euvolemic in SIADH) and exclude adrenal, thyroid, renal, and drug‑induced causes. In the reported patient this work‑up was already complete.  

2. **Monitoring protocol**  
   * **Serum sodium**: check every 2–4 h while any active correction is being undertaken; after stabilization, daily checks are sufficient.  
   * **Fluid balance**: strict input‑output chart; record oral intake, intravenous fluids, and urine output.  
   * **Weight**: daily weight to detect hidden fluid shifts.  
   * **Neurologic status**: repeat mental‑status exam at each sodium check; any new confusion, seizures, or focal deficit may signal rapid over‑correction.  

3. **Therapeutic hierarchy for SIADH in neuro‑inflammation**  

   | Step | Intervention | Rationale in this case |
   |------|---------------|------------------------|
   | **A. Treat the underlying inflammation** | Initiate disease‑specific therapy (colchicine in this patient). Effective control of neuro‑Behçet’s inflammation reduces ectopic ADH release (IL‑6‑mediated). | The patient’s sodium normalized after colchicine, allowing a conservative approach. |
   | **B. Fluid restriction** | Limit free water to 800–1000 mL/day (≈ 0.5 L/24 h). This is the cornerstone of SIADH management when the underlying cause is being treated. | The team used fluid restriction alone; no hypertonic saline was required. |
   | **C. Pharmacologic adjuncts (if Na < 120 mmol/L or symptomatic)** | • **Oral urea** 15–30 g tid – increases free‑water excretion. <br>• **Vaptans** (tolvaptan) – V2‑receptor antagonists; reserve for refractory cases. | Not needed here because the patient remained asymptomatic and Na rose slowly with fluid restriction. |
   | **D. Hypertonic saline (3 % NaCl)** | Indicated only for severe symptoms (seizures, obtundation) or Na < 115 mmol/L with rapid decline. Give 100 mL bolus over 10 min, then re‑check Na in 30 min; repeat only if Na rise < 4‑6 mmol/L and neurologic status dictates. | The case did not require this; the patient was alert and neurologically intact. |

4. **Rate of correction – safety limits**  
   * **Desired increase**: ≤ 8 mmol/L in any 24‑h period; many centres aim for ≤ 6 mmol/L/24 h in high‑risk patients (e.g., alcohol misuse, malnutrition, chronic hyponatraemia).  
   * **Why the limit?** Rapid Na⁺ shifts create an osmotic gradient that pulls water out of brain cells, precipitating **osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS)** (central pontine and extrapontine myelinolysis). ODS may manifest as dysarthria, dysphagia, quadriplegia, “locked‑in” syndrome, or isolated movement disorders, often with delayed onset (2–6 days after over‑correction).  
   * **Monitoring for over‑correction**: if Na rises > 8 mmol/L in 24 h, promptly re‑infuse **desmopressin (DDAVP) 1–2 µg IV** and give **free water** (e.g., 5 % dextrose) to halt or reverse the rise. In the reported patient, Na rose from 124 to 136 mmol/L over ~2 weeks, far below the safety threshold, obviating the need for rescue therapy.  

5. **Practical flow for this scenario**

   1. **Day 0–1**: Confirm SIADH, start fluid restriction (≤ 800 mL/day), obtain baseline Na, weight, urine output.  
   2. **Day 1‑3**: Check Na q4 h. If rise ≤ 4 mmol/L/24 h, continue fluid restriction; add colchicine for neuro‑Behçet’s, which should curtail ADH drive.  
   3. **Day 3‑7**: If Na still < 130 mmol/L and patient asymptomatic, maintain fluid restriction; consider oral urea if Na < 120 mmol/L persists.  
   4. **Day 7‑14**: As inflammatory markers improve (CRP down, ADH normalizes), anticipate gradual Na rise; continue daily Na checks until ≥ 135 mmol/L.  
   5. **If at any point Na ↑ > 8 mmol/L/24 h**: stop fluid restriction, give DDAVP 1 µg IV, infuse 5 % dextrose, and re‑measure Na in 2 h.  

6. **Take‑home pearls**  

   * **Treat the trigger** – controlling neuro‑Behçet’s inflammation (colchicine in this case) is the most effective way to resolve SIADH.  
   * **Fluid restriction is usually sufficient** when the underlying disease is managed and the patient is neurologically stable.  
   * **Never exceed the 8 mmol/L/24 h sodium‐rise limit**; ODS, though rare, is devastating and preventable.  
   * **Frequent labs and neurologic checks** are the safety net that allows early detection of over‑correction.  

In the presented patient, meticulous fluid restriction, daily sodium surveillance, and prompt anti‑inflammatory therapy led to a safe, stepwise correction of hyponatraemia without the need for hypertonic saline or vaptans, and no neurologic sequelae from rapid correction were observed. This approach exemplifies the standard of care for SIADH secondary to CNS inflammation.

### [Differential Diagnosis] What is the significance of an elevated soluble interleukin‑2 receptor (sIL‑2R) level in the work‑up of CNS disease, and why was it ultimately deemed non‑specific in this case?

**Elevated soluble interleukin‑2 receptor (sIL‑2R) in the work‑up of CNS disease – what it means and why it was not diagnostic in this case**

In the evaluation of a patient with fever, headache, meningeal irritation‑type CSF findings and hyponatraemia, the differential diagnosis must include infectious meningitis, autoimmune CNS inflammation, and primary CNS neoplasm (especially lymphoma). sIL‑2R (also called soluble CD25) is released from activated T‑lymphocytes; its serum concentration rises in conditions in which there is vigorous cellular immunity. The two clinical scenarios in which we most often order it are:

| Situation | Typical sIL‑2R pattern | How we use the result |
|-----------|------------------------|------------------------|
| **Primary CNS lymphoma (PCNSL)** | Markedly elevated (often > 1500 U/mL, frequently > 2000 U/mL) | A high value raises suspicion for PCNSL and can prompt early brain‑biopsy or PET‑CT. |
| **Systemic T‑cell–mediated disorders (e.g., sarcoidosis, autoimmune encephalitis, severe infection)** | Mild‑to‑moderate elevation (usually < 1000 U/mL) | Considered a non‑specific marker of immune activation; rarely decisive on its own. |

### Why the modest rise in this patient was non‑specific

1. **Magnitude of elevation** – The patient’s sIL‑2R was **704 U/mL**, only modestly above the laboratory reference (122–496 U/mL). In PCNSL, levels are typically **≥ 1500 U/mL**, often **2–3‑fold** higher than the upper limit of normal. The value here fell well within the range seen in many inflammatory or infectious states.

2. **Lack of corroborating evidence for lymphoma** –  
   * **Neuro‑imaging**: MRI showed no parenchymal mass, no enhancing lesions, and no hypothalamic or periventricular abnormalities that are classic for PCNSL.  
   * **CSF cytology**: No malignant cells were identified on two separate examinations.  
   * **Systemic evaluation**: No peripheral lymphadenopathy, organomegaly, or B‑symptoms.  

   In the absence of these supporting findings, a borderline sIL‑2R elevation cannot be taken as evidence of lymphoma.

3. **Concurrent systemic inflammation** – The patient had **high CRP (180 mg/L), ESR (70 mm/h)**, marked hyponatraemia, and CSF pleocytosis—all markers of a robust acute inflammatory response. Activation of T‑cells in such a setting is expected to produce a modest rise in sIL‑2R, reflecting **non‑specific immune activation** rather than a clonal lymphoid process.

4. **Differential diagnosis included sarcoidosis** – sIL‑2R can be elevated in sarcoidosis, but the patient’s **ACE and lysozyme were normal**, and there were no radiographic or clinical clues (e.g., pulmonary nodules, cutaneous plaques) to support sarcoidosis. Again, the modest elevation was interpreted as part of a generalized inflammatory milieu.

5. **Temporal context** – The measurement was obtained early, before the genital ulcer appeared and before the full Behçet clinical picture was evident. The clinicians appropriately used sIL‑2R as a **screening test** for malignancy in a broad work‑up, not as a targeted assay for Behçet’s disease. Once the clinical picture clarified (oral/genital ulcers, HLA‑B51 positivity), the relevance of sIL‑2R diminished.

### Take‑home points for residents

- **sIL‑2R is a useful “red flag” for lymphoid malignancy when markedly elevated** and when imaging or CSF studies suggest a neoplastic process.  
- **Mild elevations are common in any systemic inflammatory state** (infection, autoimmune disease, severe cytokine release) and are **not diagnostic**.  
- Always interpret sIL‑2R **in the full clinical context**: correlate with imaging, CSF cytology, and other disease‑specific markers.  
- In this case, the modest rise helped **rule out lymphoma** (by showing it was not the dramatic elevation expected) and reinforced the need to continue the broader differential, ultimately leading to the correct diagnosis of acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease complicated by SIADH.  

Thus, while an elevated sIL‑2R can alert the clinician to possible CNS lymphoma, a value of 704 U/mL in a patient with systemic inflammation, normal MRI, and negative CSF cytology is **non‑specific** and does not alter management beyond reassuring us that lymphoma is unlikely.
