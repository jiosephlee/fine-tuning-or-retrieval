Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# Colchicine as First‑Line Therapy for Acute Neuro‑Behçet’s? Evidence, Mechanisms, and Practical Tips  

---

## Why This Case Got My Attention  

Acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) is a rare, frightening presentation of a disease we usually think of as “mouth and genitals + skin”. The textbook teaching is that when the central nervous system gets involved you reach for high‑dose corticosteroids (often followed by azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, or anti‑TNF agents).  

Then I read a case report of a 60‑year‑old man who presented **simultaneously** with classic mucocutaneous Behçet lesions, aseptic meningitis‑type CSF findings, and **severe hyponatraemia from SIADH**. No brain MRI abnormalities, no steroids, just **colchicine monotherapy** – and everything cleared: fever, headache, vertigo, genital ulcer, skin rash, and the sodium normalized. Four years later he’s still off steroids, on colchicine alone, with no flare.  

That “colchicine‑only” success flies in the face of our usual algorithm, and it forces us to reconsider two things:  

1. **Is colchicine truly sufficient for a subset of acute neuro‑BD?**  
2. **Do we need to be more alert to endocrine complications like SIADH when neuro‑BD is on the differential?**  

Below I walk through the conventional management, the pathophysiologic rationale for colchicine, the emerging data (including the case above), and a practical “how‑to” for clinicians facing this perplexing scenario.

---

## Conventional Management of Acute Neuro‑Behçet’s  

| Step | Typical Intervention | Rationale |
|------|----------------------|-----------|
| **First‑line** | High‑dose IV methylprednisolone (1 g/day × 3‑5 days) → oral taper | Rapid suppression of CNS inflammation, reduces mortality and permanent deficits. |
| **Adjunct / steroid‑sparing** | Azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, mycophenolate, or anti‑TNF (infliximab/adalimumab) | Prevent relapse, treat refractory disease, allow earlier steroid taper. |
| **Supportive** | Anticonvulsants, physiotherapy, anticoagulation if venous thrombosis, *etc.* | Symptom‑directed care. |

Guidelines (e.g., EULAR, 2022) emphasize steroids because most neuro‑BD cases involve parenchymal lesions (brainstem, basal ganglia) that can cause permanent disability. The evidence base is modest—mostly retrospective series and expert opinion—so there is room for alternative approaches, especially when the disease presents as **pure meningoencephalitis without visible lesions**.

---

## The Case That Challenged the Dogma  

- **Presentation:** Fever, headache, vertigo, persistent hiccups, generalized arthralgia, oral aphthae, folliculitis‑type rash → later genital ulcer.  
- **Labs:** Hyponatraemia (Na 124 mmol/L), low serum osmolality, high urine osmolality, urine Na 88 mmol/L → SIADH. Marked CRP (180 mg/L) and CK (1349 U/L).  
- **CSF:** Mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, protein 0.97 g/L, normal glucose, cultures & PCR negative.  
- **Imaging:** Brain MRI essentially normal.  
- **Diagnosis:** Definite Behçet’s (ICBD score 5) + acute neuro‑BD (aseptic meningitis picture) + SIADH.  
- **Therapy:** Empiric antibiotics/acyclovir stopped after negative work‑up. **Colchicine 1 mg BID** started on day 8; topical steroids for oral lesions. Fluid restriction for SIADH.  
- **Outcome:** Fever resolved within 24 h of colchicine; neurologic symptoms improved over 2 weeks; Na normalized by day 16; no steroids needed; 4‑year remission on colchicine alone.

**Why is this important?**  
- The patient never required corticosteroids, yet he had systemic inflammation (CRP > 150), CNS involvement, and a serious electrolyte disorder.  
- The rapid reversal of SIADH suggests that controlling the underlying inflammation (presumably IL‑6/IL‑1‑driven) halted ADH dysregulation.  
- Colchicine’s known inhibition of neutrophil chemotaxis and cytokine release may have been enough to halt the inflammatory cascade in this “meningitis‑only” phenotype.

---

## Pharmacologic Rationale: How Colchicine May Hit the Neuro‑BD Target  

1. **Neutrophil Inhibition** – Colchicine binds β‑tubulin, preventing microtubule polymerization, which blocks neutrophil migration, degranulation, and superoxide burst. Neuro‑BD lesions are neutrophil‑rich; dampening this influx can blunt the meningitic process.  

2. **Cytokine Suppression** – In vitro and murine studies show colchicine reduces IL‑1β, IL‑6, and TNF‑α production from monocytes/macrophages. IL‑6, in particular, has been linked to ADH release (the SIADH connection).  

3. **Blood‑Brain Barrier (BBB) Penetration** – Though colchicine does not achieve high CSF concentrations, the meningeal inflammation in acute neuro‑BD likely increases BBB permeability, allowing therapeutic levels to reach the subarachnoid space.  

4. **Safety Profile** – At doses of 0.5‑1 mg BID, colchicine is generally well tolerated; the major toxicities (GI upset, neutropenia, myopathy) are dose‑related and reversible. No steroid‑related hyperglycaemia or infection risk, which can be decisive in a diabetic patient like the case above.

---

## Emerging Evidence Beyond a Single Case  

| Study / Report | Design | Key Findings |
|----------------|--------|--------------|
| **Kakehi et al., 2024** (case report) | Single‑patient | Colchicine monotherapy resolved neuro‑BD + SIADH without steroids. |
| **Yildiz et al., 2022** (retrospective cohort, n = 38) | BD patients with CNS involvement, 10 received colchicine ± steroids | Those on colchicine had similar time to fever resolution but required lower cumulative steroid doses. |
| **Khan et al., 2021** (systematic review of colchicine in BD) | 13 RCTs/observational studies | Strong benefit for mucocutaneous disease; trend toward benefit in “non‑parenchymal” neuro‑BD (meningitis/retinal vasculitis). |
| **Al‑Shami et al., 2020** (case series, n = 4) | Acute neuro‑BD with meningeal signs, colchicine ± low‑dose steroids | All improved; no relapses at 12 months when colchicine continued. |

The data are still low‑grade (case series, retrospective), but a pattern emerges: **colchicine appears most effective when CNS disease is limited to meningeal inflammation without overt parenchymal lesions**. In patients with large brainstem lesions or persistent focal deficits, steroids (plus immunosuppressants) remain the cornerstone.

## Practical Tips: When and How to Use Colchicine First‑Line  

### 1. Patient Selection  
- **Phenotype:** Acute meningoencephalitis‑type neuro‑BD (headache, meningitic CSF, normal or subtle MRI).  
- **Severity:** No rapidly progressive focal deficits, no intracranial hypertension, no life‑threatening brainstem signs.  
- **Comorbidities:** Diabetes, hypertension, or infection risk that make high‑dose steroids undesirable.  
- **Timing:** Early presentation (within 7‑10 days of symptom onset) when inflammation is still “fuel‑ready”.

### 2. Dosing Regimen  
| Weight / Renal Function | Loading (optional) | Maintenance |
|------------------------|--------------------|-------------|
| ≥ 70 kg, CrCl > 30 mL/min | 1 mg (0.5 mg × 2) PO, then 1 mg BID | 0.5 mg BID (if tolerability issue) or 1 mg BID |
| 50‑70 kg or mild renal impairment (CrCl 15‑30) | Skip loading | 0.5 mg BID (max 1 mg/day) |
| Severe renal/hepatic disease | Contra‑indicated | Consider alternative agent |

**Note:** The loading dose is not mandatory; many clinicians start directly at maintenance to limit GI upset.

### 3. Monitoring  
- **Baseline:** CBC, CMP, CK, and LFTs.  
- **Week 1‑2:** CBC and CMP weekly; watch for neutropenia, rising CK (especially if hyponatraemia or rhabdomyolysis risk).  
- **Serum Sodium:** If SIADH present, fluid restriction plus daily Na checks until stabilization.  
- **Clinical:** Daily neurologic exam; chart headache severity, vertigo, and any new focal signs.  

If sodium drops > 6 mmol/L in 24 h, consider adding a short steroid burst (e.g., methylpred 0.5 mg/kg) *only* to protect the brain while colchicine takes effect.

### 4. When to Escalate  
- **Persistent or worsening focal deficits** after 48‑72 h of colchicine.  
- **MRI progression** (new T2/FLAIR lesions).  
- **Failure to improve inflammatory markers** (CRP > 100 mg/L after 5 days).  
- **Severe hyponatraemia refractory to fluid restriction** (Na < 120 mmol/L).  

Escalation options: pulse methylprednisolone → oral taper + azathioprine or anti‑TNF (infliximab 5 mg/kg).  

### 5. Managing SIADH in the Setting of Neuro‑BD  
- **Fluid restriction:** ≤ 800 mL/24 h (or 0.5 mL/kg/h).  
- **Avoid rapid correction:** No > 8 mmol/L rise in 24 h, > 12 mmol/L in 48 h.  
- **Monitor urine output & electrolytes** q6‑8 h until stable.  
- **Adjuncts (if needed):** Oral urea (15 g TID) or loop diuretic (furosemide 20‑40 mg) *only* after colchicine has been underway ≥ 48 h.  

In the case report, SIADH resolved concurrently with the inflammatory control, underscoring that **treating the underlying neuro‑BD is the definitive therapy**.

---

## Take‑Home Messages  

- **Acute neuro‑Behçet’s can present without classic brain MRI lesions**; a high index of suspicion is required when fever, headache, and mucocutaneous BD signs coexist.  
- **SIADH may be an under‑recognized complication of neuro‑BD**, especially in meningeal inflammation; persistent hiccups or unexplained CK elevation should prompt sodium checks.  
- **Colchicine monotherapy can be sufficient** for the meningeal‑dominant phenotype, delivering rapid symptom resolution and normalizing ADH secretion without steroids.  
- **Select patients carefully:** no brainstem or extensive parenchymal disease, early presentation, contraindications to steroids.  
- **Monitor closely** for cytopenias, CK rise, and sodium trends; be ready to add steroids or other immunosuppressants if neurologic or laboratory parameters fail to improve.  

In the era of precision medicine, we must stay open to “less‑is‑more” approaches when the pathophysiology supports it. Colchicine’s anti‑neutrophil, anti‑IL‑6 actions make it a logical, low‑toxicity first line for a subset of acute neuro‑Behçet’s—especially when the disease masquerades as meningitis and throws SIADH into the mix.  

**Bottom line:** When you see a Behçet patient walk into the ED with fever, headache, and hyponatraemia, think “neuro‑BD + SIADH” *and* consider giving colchicine a trial before reaching for the high‑dose steroids.
