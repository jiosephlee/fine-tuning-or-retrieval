Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# Hyponatraemia in Inflammatory Meningo‑encephalitis: SIADH as a Red Flag in Neuro‑Behçet’s  

## Why this case grabs attention  

Most emergency physicians think of SIADH when they see a euvolemic hyponatraemic patient with lung disease, small‑cell carcinoma or a drug trigger.  The idea that **acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) itself can precipitate SIADH** is almost unheard of, and the published case of a 60‑year‑old man who presented with fever, headache, hiccups, rhabdomyolysis and classic Behçet’s mucocutaneous lesions makes that link starkly concrete.  

What’s striking is that his neurological involvement was *purely inflammatory*: normal brain MRI, aseptic meningitis on CSF, and yet his ADH was markedly elevated, his serum sodium fell to 124 mmol/L, and the whole picture resolved with colchicine alone—no steroids, no vasopressin antagonists.  The take‑home message? **When a patient with Behçet’s (or any systemic inflammatory disease) walks in with fever, headache, and hyponatraemia, think “SIADH from CNS inflammation” before you chase rare infections or malignancy.**  

---

## The cytokine‑ADH connection  

Inflammatory meningo‑encephalitis drives ADH release through a fairly well‑mapped pathway:

1. **Microglial activation** → production of pro‑inflammatory cytokines, especially **interleukin‑6 (IL‑6)**.  
2. IL‑6 acts on the hypothalamic supra‑optic nucleus, stimulating the magnocellular neurons that synthesize ADH.  
3. Circulating IL‑6 also up‑regulates **vasopressin‑releasing factor receptors** on the posterior pituitary, amplifying secretion.  

In neuro‑BD, monocyte‑derived IL‑6 is consistently elevated in serum and CSF, providing a plausible mechanistic bridge between meningeal inflammation and SIADH.  Though the case above didn’t measure IL‑6, the astronomic CRP (180 mg/L) is a surrogate of IL‑6‑driven acute‑phase response, fitting the biological model.

---

## Step‑by‑step: Diagnosing SIADH in the setting of fever, headache, and hyponatraemia  

1. **Confirm true hyponatraemia** – serum Na⁺ < 135 mmol/L with hypotonic plasma (serum osmolality < 275 mOsm/kg).  
2. **Assess volume status** – bedside exam for edema, skin turgor, JVP; SIADH is euvolemic.  
3. **Rule out obvious osmotic causes** – hyperglycaemia, mannitol, IV contrast.  In the case, the patient’s glucose was high (14 mmol/L) but not enough to explain a drop to 124 mmol/L.  
4. **Urine studies** –  
   - Urine osmolality > 300 mOsm/kg (often > 500 mOsm/kg).  
   - Urine Na⁺ > 30 mmol/L (or > 20 mmol/L in the presence of a normal volume status).  
   The patient’s urine osmolality was 900 mOsm/kg and urine Na⁺ 88 mmol/L – classic for SIADH.  
5. **Exclude adrenal, thyroid, renal failure** – check cortisol, TSH/T4, BUN/creatinine. All were normal in the case.  
6. **Identify the CNS trigger** –  
   - Look for meningeal signs, focal deficits, headache, fever.  
   - Obtain MRI (early lesions may be invisible).  
   - Perform lumbar puncture: ↑protein, mild lymphocytic pleocytosis without pathogens.  
   If CSF shows aseptic meningitis and other work‑up is negative, the inflammation itself is the likely driver.  

If steps 1‑5 are satisfied and a CNS inflammatory process is evident, you have **SIADH secondary to inflammatory meningo‑encephalitis**.  

---

## Under‑appreciated clues: hiccups and rhabdomyolysis  

- **Persistent hiccups** are more than an annoyance.  In CNS inflammation they can herald severe hyponatraemia because the medullary hiccup centre is exquisitely sensitive to osmotic shifts.  In the case, hiccups persisted until sodium normalized.  
- **Rhabdomyolysis** (CK ≈ 1300 U/L) may be a silent companion of profound hyponatraemia.  Osmotic stress leads to intracellular swelling, calcium influx, and muscle membrane injury.  Checking CK in any hyponatraemic encephalopathy is cheap and may avert renal injury from missed myoglobinuria.  

Both signs should prompt an early, aggressive work‑up for SIADH rather than assuming a primary pulmonary or medication cause.

---

## Management pearls  

1. **Fluid restriction** – ≤ 800 mL/day is often sufficient; be vigilant for rapid correction (> 8 mmol/L/24 h) which risks osmotic demyelination.  
2. **Address the inflammation** – In neuro‑BD, corticosteroids are first‑line, but this case shows **colchicine monotherapy** can be enough for a meningo‑encephalitic phenotype. Colchicine’s inhibition of neutrophil migration and IL‑1β/IL‑6 production likely dampened ADH drive.  
3. **Avoid unnecessary vasopressin antagonists** – They are reserved for refractory SIADH; the cornerstone remains treating the trigger.  
4. **Monitor electrolytes daily** – Hyponatraemia can rebound when the inflammatory storm subsides; adjust fluid restriction accordingly.  

---

## Take‑home points  

- **Neuro‑Behçet’s can present with SIADH**; don’t wait for brain MRI abnormalities to suspect CNS inflammation.  
- **IL‑6–mediated ADH secretion** is the central pathophysiologic link; a high CRP should raise suspicion.  
- **Hiccups and rhabdomyolysis** are red‑flag symptoms that should trigger a focused hyponatraemia work‑up.  
- **Colchicine may be a viable first‑line option** for acute neuro‑BD with meningo‑encephalitic features, especially when steroids are contraindicated.  
- **Diagnosing SIADH** in this context follows the classic Euvolemic hyponatraemia algorithm, but the “trigger” box should include inflammatory meningo‑encephalitis alongside the usual suspects.  

In practice, the next patient who walks in with fever, headache, a few oral ulcers and a sodium of 124 mmol/L is the one who will remind you: **when the immune system storms the meninges, the antidiuretic hormone often follows suit**. Recognize it early, treat the inflammation, and the sodium will follow.
