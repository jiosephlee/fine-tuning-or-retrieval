Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and Initial Post‑operative Assessment  

The patient was a man in his seventies with a two‑year history of exertional dyspnoea attributable to progressive aortic valve disease and longstanding atrial fibrillation for which he was taking apixaban 5 mg twice daily.  Aside from age‑related physiologic changes, his comorbid profile was limited; he had no known renal or hepatic impairment, no prior thrombotic events, and no history of heparin exposure in the months preceding admission.  The chronic oral anticoagulant was continued through a pre‑operative diagnostic coronary angiogram because the interventionalist elected a trans‑radial approach and administered a reduced intraprocedural heparin dose (2 000 U of unfractionated heparin, UFH) solely to prevent catheter‑related thrombosis.  This brief exposure to UFH would later prove to be the immunologic priming event for his subsequent autoimmune heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT).

### Intra‑operative management  

One week after the catheterisation the patient was taken to the operating room for definitive cardiac surgery.  Full‑dose UFH (45 000 U) was given after heparinisation of the systemic circulation and the activated clotting time (ACT) was maintained within the target range for cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB).  The operation comprised three components: (1) aortic valve replacement with a 27‑mm bioprosthetic valve, (2) replacement of the ascending aorta using a 30‑mm Gelweave graft, and (3) a single coronary artery bypass graft to the obtuse marginal branch using a saphenous vein conduit together with left atrial appendage excision.  The bypass run was uneventful, blood loss was minimal, and the patient was weaned off pump with stable haemodynamics and no need for intra‑operative transfusion.

### Early postoperative laboratory trends  

Baseline laboratory values obtained before surgery showed a platelet count of 210 × 10⁹/L (reference 150–400 × 10⁹/L), haemoglobin 141 g/L, normal white‑cell count, and a routine coagulation panel within normal limits.  On postoperative day 1 (POD 1) the platelet count fell to 155 × 10⁹/L, a reduction of roughly 25 % that is commonly observed after cardiac surgery owing to hemodilution, consumption on the CPB circuit, and postoperative inflammatory activation.  However, from POD 1 through POD 5 the count fell in a stepwise fashion to 50 × 10⁹/L, representing a >55 % decline from baseline and crossing the clinical threshold that alarms for a pathologic process (a fall >30 % or an absolute count <150 × 10⁹/L after cardiac surgery warrants evaluation for HIT, sepsis, or consumptive coagulopathy).  Concomitantly, the haemoglobin remained stable around 100 g/L, reflecting expected postoperative anaemia without ongoing bleeding, while the white‑cell count and differential were unremarkable, arguing against an infectious trigger.

The coagulation profile on POD 5 was notable for a normal prothrombin time/INR (1.1) and activated partial thromboplastin time (34 s), but an elevated fibrinogen level of 6.59 g/L (reference 2.0–3.9 g/L).  Elevated fibrinogen is typical in the early postoperative period as an acute‑phase reactant and, in isolation, does not suggest consumptive coagulopathy.  Likewise, renal and hepatic chemistries were within normal limits, and haptoglobin was paradoxically elevated, further excluding haemolysis.

### Thromboprophylaxis and its modification  

Standard postoperative venous‑thrombo‑embolism prophylaxis with low‑molecular‑weight heparin (LMWH) was initiated on POD 1 (dalteparin 5 000 U subcutaneously) and increased to 7 500 U on POD 2‑4.  When the platelet count reached 50 × 10⁹/L on POD 5, the LMWH dose was held and replaced with a single prophylactic dose of fondaparinux (2.5 mg subcutaneously) at 18:00 h, reflecting the clinician’s concern for heparin‑related thrombocytopenia while still providing some antithrombotic coverage.

### Neurologic assessment and imaging  

The patient’s neurologic examination was initially limited to the presence of postoperative delirium, a common cognitive complication after cardiac surgery.  No focal deficits were observed.  On POD 6, however, the delirium worsened and the platelet count dropped further to 26 × 10⁹/L (repeat 24 × 10⁹/L).  The combination of rapidly falling platelets and a changing mental status prompted an urgent non‑contrast CT scan of the brain.  Imaging revealed an acute infarct in the left parietal lobe measuring 3.2 × 1.8 cm, extending to the cortical surface.  The infarct explained the neuro‑cognitive decline and provided an early, concrete manifestation of the hyper‑coagulable state that can accompany HIT.

### Interpretation of the initial work‑up  

Taken together, the patient’s baseline risk factors (advanced age, recent UFH exposure, major cardiac surgery with CPB) set the stage for immune sensitisation to platelet factor‑4/heparin complexes.  The progressive platelet decline beginning on POD 1, exceeding the expected postoperative nadir and falling below 100 × 10⁹/L, signalled a pathological thrombocytopenia.  The abrupt temporal relationship between the platelet fall, the new cerebral infarct, and the absence of infection, bleeding, or other drug‑induced causes sharpened the clinical suspicion for HIT, specifically an atypical, heparin‑independent (“autoimmune”) form, given the early onset (before the usual POD 5–10 window) after a brief pre‑operative UFH exposure.  These observations laid the groundwork for targeted laboratory testing and the subsequent therapeutic decisions described in later sections of the report.
