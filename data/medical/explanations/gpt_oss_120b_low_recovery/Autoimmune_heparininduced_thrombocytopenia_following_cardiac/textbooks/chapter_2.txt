Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

Chapter 2: ## Differential Diagnosis and Diagnostic Reasoning  

Post‑operative thrombocytopenia is a frequent laboratory abnormality after cardiac surgery, but its etiology can range from benign, self‑limited processes to life‑threatening immune‑mediated disorders. A structured, algorithmic approach—anchored on the timing of the platelet fall, the depth of the nadir, and the presence or absence of associated clinical complications—helps the clinician move efficiently from common to rare causes and focus on the investigations that will confirm or exclude each possibility.  

### Algorithmic work‑up of postoperative thrombocytopenia  

1. **Timing** – Platelet counts typically dip modestly (≈30 % of baseline) within the first 24–48 h after cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) because of hemodilution, consumption on the bypass circuit, and peri‑operative blood loss. A secondary, more delayed decline occurring **after day 3–5** raises suspicion for an immune‑mediated process such as heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) or drug‑dependent immune thrombocytopenia.  
2. **Severity** – Mild reductions (150–100 × 10⁹/L) are often physiologic. Counts that fall below 100 × 10⁹/L, especially when they reach <50 × 10⁹/L, merit urgent evaluation for a pathogenic cause.  
3. **Associated findings** – New thrombosis, bleeding, schistocytes, or a rapid fall (>50 % from baseline) are red flags that point toward a consumptive or immune mechanism rather than simple dilution.  

Applying this algorithm to the patient, the platelet trajectory was:  

- **Pre‑op:** 210 × 10⁹/L (baseline).  
- **POD 1:** 155 × 10⁹/L (expected early drop).  
- **POD 5–6:** Progressive decline to 50 → 26 × 10⁹/L, then a nadir of 13 × 10⁹/L, well beyond the expected postoperative pattern and accompanied by an acute cerebral infarct.  

These data placed the case squarely in the “delayed, severe, and complicated” quadrant, prompting a systematic exclusion of more common causes.  

### Common postoperative etiologies and reasons for exclusion  

| Etiology | Typical presentation | Why it was excluded in this patient |
|----------|----------------------|--------------------------------------|
| **Hemodilution & CPB‑related consumption** | Platelet nadir on POD 1–2, modest (~30 % drop), recovery by POD 4–5 | Decline continued beyond POD 2, reached profound thrombocytopenia (13 × 10⁹/L) |
| **Surgical blood loss/ongoing hemorrhage** | Associated falling hemoglobin, overt bleeding, need for transfusion | Hemoglobin stable (≈97 g/L) after POD 1, no surgical site bleeding, no transfusions given |
| **Infection/sepsis** | Fever, leukocytosis, positive cultures, rise in inflammatory markers | Afebrile, normal WBC, negative cultures, normal CRP (not reported but not elevated) |
| **Cardiogenic shock/critical illness** | Hypotension, organ hypoperfusion, lactate elevation | Hemodynamically stable, normal lactate, good cardiac output off‑pump |
| **Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) secondary to surgery** | Low fibrinogen, prolonged PT/aPTT, elevated D‑dimer, schistocytes | Fibrinogen **elevated** early post‑op, PT/aPTT normal; schistocytes appeared only later, coinciding with HIT‑related consumptive coagulopathy |

Because none of these frequent explanations fit the timing, severity, or associated laboratory picture, the team turned to rarer entities.  

### Rare causes evaluated  

1. **Post‑transfusion purpura (PTP)** – Requires prior transfusion and typically manifests 5–10 days after the transfused blood product. The patient received **no blood products**, making PTP untenable.  
2. **Thrombotic microangiopathies (TMAs)** – Include thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) and atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome (aHUS). Both present with schistocytes, markedly elevated LDH, and often renal dysfunction. Early labs showed normal haptoglobin, absence of schistocytes on the POD 5 film, and normal renal indices, arguing against a TMA at that stage. Schistocytes that later appeared were interpreted as secondary to severe HIT‑related consumptive coagulopathy rather than primary TMA.  
3. **Drug‑induced immune thrombocytopenia** – Usually follows exposure to a sensitizing drug with a latency of 5–10 days. The patient’s postoperative drugs (vancomycin, cefazolin, tranexamic acid, furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, midodrine) were either administered only peri‑operatively or started after the platelet fall had already begun, making a drug‑immune mechanism improbable.  

Having ruled out both common and rare non‑heparin‑related causes, the investigative focus shifted to **heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia**.  

### Heparin exposure history and its diagnostic impact  

Heparin exposure can be categorized as **intra‑operative UFH** (45 000 U given for CPB) and **pre‑operative UFH** (2 000 U administered during a diagnostic cardiac catheterisation a week earlier). Classic HIT after cardiac surgery usually manifests **after postoperative heparin prophylaxis** (typically LMWH) and appears between POD 5–10. In this case, platelet decline began **before POD 5**, an atypical window that raised suspicion for a sensitizing event **preceding surgery**. The modest UFH dose during catheterisation likely primed the immune system, generating PF4/heparin antibodies that were subsequently boosted by the massive intra‑operative UFH exposure. Awareness of this timeline was crucial in prioritizing HIT testing early, despite the “too early” appearance for classic postoperative HIT.  

### Interpretation of the PF4‑ELISA  

The PF4/polyanion enzyme‑linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) detects IgG (and sometimes IgA/IgM) antibodies that bind PF4/heparin complexes. Results are expressed as optical density (OD).  

- **OD < 0.4** – Negative.  
- **OD 0.4–1.0** – Weakly positive; clinical relevance variable.  
- **OD > 1.0** – Strongly positive, strongly associated with platelet‑activating antibodies.  

The patient’s sample returned an OD of **2.2**, far above the positivity threshold and comparable to values seen in confirmed HIT. Such a high OD substantially raises pre‑test probability, especially when coupled with the clinical picture (timing, severe drop, new thrombus). Nonetheless, ELISA alone cannot distinguish classic HIT from autoimmune HIT (aHIT) because both generate PF4‑reactive antibodies.  

### Serotonin‑Release Assay (SRA): mechanics and patterns  

The SRA is a functional assay that measures serotonin released from donor platelets after incubation with patient serum and varying concentrations of heparin.  

- **Typical HIT pattern:** Minimal serotonin release at 0 U/mL (no heparin), **high release** at therapeutic heparin concentrations (0.1–0.3 U/mL), and **inhibition** at supratherapeutic concentrations (≥100 U/mL).  
- **Autoimmune HIT (aHIT) pattern:** **Heparin‑independent activation** (high release at 0 U/mL) together with the classic heparin‑dependent peak; sometimes the high‑dose heparin inhibition is incomplete.  

The assay also incorporates IV.3, a monoclonal antibody that blocks FcγRIIa (the platelet receptor mediating HIT antibody activation). In classic HIT, IV.3 **abolishes** serotonin release at therapeutic heparin concentrations, confirming FcγRIIa dependence.  

### Patient’s SRA profile and its implication  

| Heparin concentration | % Serotonin release (patient serum) | Expected in classic HIT | Observed behavior |
|-----------------------|--------------------------------------|--------------------------|-------------------|
| 0 U/mL                | 96 %                                 | <20 % (no activation)    | **Heparin‑independent activation** (aHIT hallmark) |
| 0.1 U/mL              | 97 %                                 | 80–100 % (strong)        | Consistent with HIT |
| 0.3 U/mL              | 99 %                                 | 80–100 % (strong)        | Consistent with HIT |
| 100 U/mL              | 0 %                                  | 0 % (inhibition)         | Classic high‑dose inhibition present |
| 0.1 U/mL + IV.3      | 76 %                                 | <20 % (blocked)          | **Partial inhibition** – atypical |

The **key observations** are:  

1. **Heparin‑independent activation** (96 % at 0 U/mL) unequivocally places the antibodies in the aHIT spectrum.  
2. **Partial blockade by IV.3** (still 76 % release) indicates that a proportion of the platelet activation is **FcγRIIa‑independent** or that the antibodies have exceptionally high affinity, overwhelming the blocking capacity of IV.3. This pattern has not been previously described and explains why the reporting laboratory initially labeled the result “indeterminate.”  

Together, these findings satisfy the functional criteria for **autoimmune HIT**: (i) strong ELISA positivity, (ii) platelet activation without heparin, (iii) incomplete inhibition by FcγRIIa blockade, and (iv) clinical evidence of HIT (thrombocytopenia plus new arterial thrombosis).  

### Diagnostic criteria for autoimmune HIT and case fulfilment  

Consensus diagnostic frameworks for aHIT integrate clinical scoring (e.g., the 4‑T score), timing, ELISA OD, and functional assay patterns. The essential elements are:  

1. **Clinical suspicion** – Platelet fall ≥50 % from baseline, nadir <150 × 10⁹/L, new thrombotic event, and a timing compatible with heparin exposure (including pre‑operative exposure). The patient met all.  
2. **Strong PF4‑ELISA** – OD > 1.0. The patient’s OD = 2.2.  
3. **SRA evidence of heparin‑independent activation** – >20 % release at 0 U/mL heparin. The patient showed 96 %.  
4. **Partial or absent inhibition by IV.3** – reinforcing an FcγRIIa‑independent component, as seen here (76 % release despite IV.3).  

Thus, the case fulfills the full set of criteria for autoimmune HIT.  

### Synthesis of the reasoning pathway  

1. **Initial platelet trend** prompted a standard postoperative thrombocytopenia work‑up.  
2. **Exclusion of common causes** (hemodilution, bleeding, infection, DIC) based on laboratory and clinical stability.  
3. **Consideration of rare mimics** (PTP, TMAs, drug‑immune thrombocytopenia) found to be implausible given exposure history and peripheral smear.  
4. **Heparin exposure chronology** highlighted a pre‑operative UFH dose as a plausible sensitizing event, shifting suspicion to HIT despite an “early” onset.  
5. **Strong PF4‑ELISA** supported the presence of anti‑PF4/heparin antibodies.  
6. **SRA with atypical profile** clarified that the antibodies were **heparin‑independent** and **partially resistant to FcγRIIa blockade**, confirming **autoimmune HIT**.  

By systematically traversing this algorithm—from generic postoperative platelet kinetics to nuanced functional assay interpretation—the clinicians arrived at the correct, albeit uncommon, diagnosis and could tailor therapy (argatroban → fondaparinux, high‑dose IVIG) to address the unique pathophysiology of autoimmune HIT.
