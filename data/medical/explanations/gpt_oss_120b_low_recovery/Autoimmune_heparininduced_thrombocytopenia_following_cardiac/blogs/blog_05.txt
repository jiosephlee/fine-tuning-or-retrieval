Title: Blog about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

# Thrombotic Stroke in the Post‑Operative Cardiothoracic Patient: When HIT Is the Hidden Culprit  

## Why This Case Is Worth Your Attention  

A 70‑year‑old man walked out of the operating room after an uneventful aortic valve replacement, ascending‑aorta graft, and single‑vessel bypass – only to develop a new cerebral infarct on postoperative day 6. The trigger? A rare but devastating form of heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) that behaved like an autoimmune disorder, striking before the usual “day 5‑10” window and driving a massive platelet plunge to 13 × 10⁹/L.  

What makes this story a teaching moment is not just the stroke itself, but the constellation of clues that should have raised suspicion for HIT **earlier**:  
* an early, steep platelet decline that began **before** the typical postoperative HIT timeframe,  
* a delirium that worsened out of proportion to the surgery,  
* an arterial thrombus (stroke) in a patient without obvious atherosclerotic risk factors, and  
* a prior exposure to a tiny dose of unfractionated heparin during a pre‑operative cardiac catheterisation.  

If you recognize this pattern, you can intervene before the patient pays the price of a massive cerebral infarct, massive bleeding, or even death.

## The Pathophysiology Bite‑Size  

Classic HIT is an IgG‑mediated immune response against platelet factor‑4 (PF4)‑heparin complexes. The immune complex cross‑links platelet FcγIIa receptors → platelet activation → thrombocytopenia *and* a pro‑thrombotic state.  

**Autoimmune HIT (aHIT)** is a variant wherein the antibodies can activate platelets **without heparin**. These antibodies tend to be more potent, cause deeper platelet nadirs, and are less readily inhibited by FcγIIa blockade (as shown in the serotonin‑release assay of this case). The clinical picture often includes early onset, severe thrombocytopenia, and arterial events such as stroke or limb ischemia.

## Practical Checklist – When to Think HIT in the Cardiac Surgery ICU  

1. **Platelet kinetics** – A drop >30 % from baseline that starts **before POD 5**, especially if the fall continues beyond the typical postoperative nadir of 70–100 × 10⁹/L.  
2. **Neurologic red flags** – New delirium, focal deficits, or unexplained changes in mental status should prompt neuro‑imaging, even in the absence of classic cardio‑embolic sources.  
3. **Timing of heparin exposure** – Any intra‑procedural UF heparin (even tiny doses for coronary angiography) within the prior week can prime the immune system.  
4. **Thrombosis without classic risk** – Arterial occlusion (stroke, limb ischemia) or unusual venous thrombosis in the early postoperative period.  

If **≥2** of these are present, order a PF4‑ELISA (high‑OD) **and** a functional assay (SRA or heparin‑induced platelet activation test) without delay.

## Interpreting the Lab – The SRA Gotchas  

The serotonin‑release assay is the gold‑standard functional test, but aHIT can produce atypical patterns:  

* **Strong release at 0 U/mL heparin** – hallmark of heparin‑independent activity.  
* **Partial inhibition by IV.3 (FcγIIa blocker)** – suggests antibodies so avid they can overcome the blockade, as in this case.  

When you see “indeterminate” or “partial inhibition,” don’t dismiss it. Correlate with the clinical picture; aHIT is a clinical diagnosis confirmed by laboratory nuance.

## Immediate Management – Stroke Meets HIT  

### 1. Stop all heparin products  
Drop UFH, LMWH, and heparin‑flushes. Switch to a non‑heparin anticoagulant *immediately* – the choice depends on renal/hepatic function, bleeding risk, and drug availability.

| Drug | Dosing | Key points |
|------|--------|------------|
| **Argatroban** | 0.5–1 µg/kg/min IV, target aPTT 1.5–3× baseline | Short half‑life, safe in renal failure, easy titration |
| **Bivalirudin** | 0.15–0.25 µg/kg/min ± bolus, target aPTT | Useful if liver dysfunction limits argatroban |
| **Fondaparinux** (off‑label therapeutic) | 5–10 mg SC daily (adjust for weight/renal) | Once‑daily, no routine monitoring, but slower onset; give AT concentrate if AT low |
| **Direct oral Xa inhibitors** (e.g., apixaban) | Not recommended in acute HIT stroke until platelet recovery | May be used for long‑term after platelet normalization |

In this case, argatroban was started first to allow rapid cessation of platelet activation while the stroke evolved, then transitioned to fondaparinux when the platelet count began to climb.

### 2. Consider high‑dose IVIG (1 g/kg daily for 2–3 days)  
IVIG can saturate Fcγ receptors and blunt platelet activation, especially in aHIT. The response may be blunted if the antibody burden is massive (as evidenced by the modest platelet rise in our patient), but it remains a low‑risk adjunct.

### 3. Neuro‑protective and stroke‑specific care  

* **Blood pressure** – Maintain MAP ≥ 80 mmHg (or higher if large penumbra is suspected) but avoid hypertensive spikes that could worsen hemorrhagic conversion.  
* **Antiplatelet therapy** – Generally deferred until platelet count > 50 × 10⁹/L and HIT antibodies wane; otherwise, the risk of bleeding outweighs benefit.  
* **Thrombolysis** – Contraindicated while on therapeutic anticoagulation for HIT and with platelet counts < 100 × 10⁹/L. In selected cases where HIT is recognized *after* tPA, consider immediate switch to non‑heparin anticoagulant and monitor closely.  

### 4. Monitor the coagulation milieu  

Serial fibrinogen, D‑dimer, and peripheral smear give a real‑time picture of HIT‑related consumptive coagulopathy. Falling fibrinogen and rising D‑dimer, as seen in this patient while on argatroban, signal ongoing microvascular thrombosis and may prompt a switch to a more potent anti‑Xa agent (fondaparinux in this case).

## Follow‑Up – When Does the Storm Pass?  

* **Platelet recovery** – Usually within 7–10 days of stopping heparin, but aHIT can linger for weeks. In this case, normal counts returned only after POD 62.  
* **Serology** – HIT ELISA may remain positive for months; functional assays (SRA) often evolve from heparin‑independent to typical heparin‑dependent patterns as the immune response matures.  
* **Long‑term anticoagulation** – Once platelets are stable (>150 × 10⁹/L) and HIT antibodies are waning, transition to a non‑heparin oral anticoagulant (e.g., apixaban) for at least 3 months, then reassess with repeat SRA/ELISA if clinically indicated.

## Take‑Home Messages  

- **Early, steep platelet drops** in cardiac surgery patients demand a HIT work‑up *even before* POD 5, especially when a pre‑operative UFH dose was given.  
- **Arterial thrombosis** (stroke, limb ischemia) in the postoperative period is a red flag for autoimmune HIT; the classic “venous” phenotype is only part of the story.  
- **Atypical SRA patterns** (serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin, partial FcγIIa blockade) are diagnostic clues—not failures.  
- **Stop all heparin**, start a non‑heparin anticoagulant (argatroban or fondaparinux), and consider high‑dose IVIG for severe aHIT.  
- **Balance stroke care** (BP targets, neuro‑imaging) with the need to avoid hemorrhagic complications from anticoagulation in a thrombocytopenic patient.  

Recognizing the subtle triad of early platelet fall, delirium, and unexpected arterial clot can dramatically change outcomes for post‑cardiothoracic patients. Keep HIT on your differential; the consequences of missing it are, quite literally, life‑threatening.
