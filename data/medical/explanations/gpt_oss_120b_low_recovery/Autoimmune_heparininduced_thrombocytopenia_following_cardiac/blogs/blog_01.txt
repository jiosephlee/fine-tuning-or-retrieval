Title: Blog about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

# When HIT Strikes Early: Pre‑operative Heparin Exposure and Post‑Cardiac Surgery Thrombocytopenia  

## Why This Case Grabs Your Attention  

Heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) is usually thought of as a “day‑5‑to‑day‑10” complication after cardiac surgery.  In reality, a tiny bolus of unfractionated heparin (UFH) given *days* before the operation can set the stage for a fulminant, autoimmune‑type HIT that presents **before** the classic window.  The report by St Bernard et al. illustrates exactly that: a 70‑year‑old man who received only 2 000 U of UFH during a diagnostic coronary angiogram went on to develop a devastating platelet‑fall (nadir 13 × 10⁹/L), an ischemic stroke, and a serologic pattern of autoimmune HIT that was resistant to the usual Fc‑receptor blockade on the serotonin‑release assay (SRA).  

The lesson? Don’t dismiss early postoperative thrombocytopenia as “just post‑CPB dilution” or “infection‑related” just because the drop occurs before POD 5. A brief pre‑operative heparin exposure can make the immune system primed, and the massive UFH dose used for cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) can then trigger an *auto‑reactive* antibody response.

## Core Concepts & Pathophysiology  

1. **Classic HIT vs. Autoimmune HIT (aHIT)**  
   * Classic HIT: IgG anti‑PF4/heparin antibodies that activate platelets only in the presence of therapeutic heparin concentrations (0.1–0.4 U/mL).  
   * aHIT: Antibodies that can activate platelets *without* heparin, sometimes even at high heparin concentrations; they often bind PF4 directly or form larger immune complexes. This results in more severe thrombocytopenia and a higher thrombotic burden.  

2. **Priming Effect of Small Heparin Doses**  
   * Even a single, low‑dose UFH exposure (e.g., 2 000 U during a cath lab procedure) can sensitize the immune system.  
   * When the patient later receives the massive UFH dose required for CPB (≈45 000 U), the pre‑sensitized B‑cells generate high‑titer, heparin‑independent antibodies.  

3. **Laboratory Signature of aHIT**  
   * **ELISA:** Very high optical density (≥2.0).  
   * **SRA:**  
     - Strong serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin (heparin‑independent).  
     - Partial (or no) inhibition by Fc‑γRIIA blocking antibody (IV.3).  
   * This pattern may be flagged as “indeterminate” by labs unfamiliar with aHIT, risking a missed diagnosis.  

## How to Spot Early‑Onset HIT in the Post‑Cardiac Surgery Patient  

| Clinical clue | Why it matters |
|----------------|----------------|
| **Platelet drop that starts on POD 1‑2 and continues** (instead of the usual nadir around POD 3‑4 from CPB dilution) | Suggests an immune‑mediated process rather than simple consumption. |
| **Platelet nadir <50 × 10⁹/L, especially <20 × 10⁹/L** | Severe HIT, often aHIT. Classic HIT rarely drives counts that low. |
| **New or worsening neurological findings, unexplained hypoxia, or limb ischemia** | HIT is a *pro‑thrombotic* disorder; thromboses can appear early, even before the platelet count bottoms out. |
| **Recent UFH exposure within 7–10 days (cath lab, VTE prophylaxis, intra‑arterial line flushes)** | Provides the immunologic priming window. |
| **Absence of other explanations (no active bleeding, sepsis, drug‑induced thrombocytopenia, TTP)** | Excludes common mimics. |

When at least two of these points line up, **order HIT testing immediately**, *even if you are still before POD 5*.  

## Practical Diagnostic Algorithm  

1. **Daily platelet count** (ideally on POD 0–7).  
2. **Threshold to act:**  
   * ≥30 % drop from baseline **or** absolute count <150 × 10⁹/L → calculate 4 T score.  
   * If the 4 T score is ≥4 *and* the drop starts before POD 5, **send PF4 ELISA and SRA** (or a rapid functional assay). |
3. **Interpretation of ELISA**  
   * OD ≥ 1.0: high pre‑test probability; proceed to functional assay regardless of timing. |
4. **Functional assay nuances**  
   * Ask the lab specifically whether they performed SRA with and without heparin, and whether they added IV.3.  
   * If the result is “indeterminate” but there is strong ELISA positivity and the clinical picture fits, **treat as HIT** until proven otherwise. |
5. **Management while awaiting results**  
   * **Stop all heparin** (including LMWH prophylaxis).  
   * **Start a non‑heparin anticoagulant**: argatroban (IV, short half‑life) is preferred in the immediate postoperative period, especially if bleeding risk is high. Fondaparinux is an alternative once the patient is stable. |
6. **Adjunctive therapy**  
   * High‑dose IVIG (1 g/kg daily × 2–3 days) can blunt platelet activation, but response may be blunted in severe aHIT.  
   * Monitor fibrinogen, d‑dimer, and peripheral smear for consumptive coagulopathy. |
7. **Re‑check antibodies**  
   * Serial SRA/ELISA at 2‑week intervals can demonstrate evolution from aHIT (heparin‑independent) to classic HIT or seroreversion. |

## Therapeutic Pearls from the Case  

* **Argatroban first, fondaparinux later** – Argatroban’s short half‑life provides a safety net if the patient develops a bleed after a stroke. Once the hypercoagulable state appears controlled (rising fibrinogen, falling d‑dimer), transitioning to once‑daily fondaparinux simplifies care.  
* **Antithrombin supplementation** – When using fondaparinux in the early postoperative period, AT levels can be low; a single 1 000 U AT concentrate dose can ensure adequate drug activity.  
* **IVIG dosing** – In severe aHIT, three consecutive 100 g doses may still yield only modest platelet recovery. Expect a slower rise; do not abandon anticoagulation waiting for a rapid platelet bounce.  
* **Long‑term anticoagulation** – Once platelets normalize and SRA reverts to a heparin‑dependent pattern, you can safely resume a DOAC (e.g., apixaban) for chronic indications.  

## Take‑Home Messages  

- **Any UFQ heparin exposure in the week preceding cardiac surgery can prime HIT**, making early postoperative thrombocytopenia a red flag, not a benign postoperative dip.  
- **Don’t wait for the classic POD 5‑10 window**; order HIT testing as soon as the platelet trajectory looks abnormal, especially if the count falls below 100 × 10⁹/L or if thrombosis appears.  
- **A strong PF4 ELISA with an “indeterminate” SRA should not be dismissed**—consider aHIT, where platelet activation occurs without heparin and may resist Fc‑receptor blockade.  
- **Start a non‑heparin anticoagulant promptly**; argatroban is the work‑horse in the immediate postoperative period, with fondaparinux or DOACs later when the patient is stable.  
- **High‑dose IVIG can help, but response may be muted in severe aHIT**; keep an eye on fibrinogen, d‑dimer, and smear for consumptive signs.  
- **Serial antibody testing is useful** to track the transition from aHIT to classic HIT or seroreversion, guiding when it is safe to re‑introduce heparin‑based prophylaxis.  

**Bottom line:** A tiny UFH bolus in the cath lab can set off a cascade that culminates in catastrophic, early‑onset HIT after cardiac surgery. Recognize the pattern, test early, and initiate appropriate non‑heparin anticoagulation—your patient’s brain (and platelet count) will thank you.
