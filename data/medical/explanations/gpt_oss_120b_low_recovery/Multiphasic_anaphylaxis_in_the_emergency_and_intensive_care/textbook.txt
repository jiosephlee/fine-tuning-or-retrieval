Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Emergency Management  

The patient was a woman in her early 60 years, weighing 104 kg, with a past medical history notable for type‑2 diabetes, hypertension, chronic plaque psoriasis, and major depressive disorder. Her regular medications included metformin, gliclazide, hydrochlorothiazide, omeprazole and sertraline. Importantly, she reported a remote mild β‑lactam allergy (adolescent facial swelling after amoxicillin) and a pronounced local reaction to a wasp sting, but no prior systemic reactions. These historical clues placed her at modest baseline risk for drug‑induced anaphylaxis but did not predict the severity of the ensuing event.

### Exposure‑to‑symptom timeline  

Five days before presentation, the patient’s general practitioner prescribed clindamycin for erysipelas. After five days of clindamycin she developed an abdominal rash, interpreted as a delayed maculopapular reaction, and the antibiotic was changed to clarithromycin 500 mg orally—her first exposure to this macrolide. One hour after swallowing the first clarithromycin dose, she began to experience pruritus, flushing, profuse sweating, a sense of throat swelling, and increasing agitation. Over the next hour the symptoms progressed, prompting her to seek care; she arrived at the emergency department (ED) approximately two hours after drug ingestion and one hour after symptom onset.

### Recognition of anaphylaxis  

Anaphylaxis is a clinical diagnosis defined by the abrupt onset of multisystem involvement. The most widely used criteria (World Allergy Organization, 2020) require either:

1. **Acute onset (minutes to ≤ 1 hour) of involvement of the skin‑plus‑mucosal tissue** (e.g., urticaria, angio‑edema, flushing) **and at least one of the following:** respiratory compromise (dyspnoea, wheeze, hypoxaemia), reduced blood pressure, or persistent gastrointestinal symptoms; **or**  
2. **Two or more of the following occurring rapidly after exposure to a likely trigger:** (a) skin‑mucosal signs, (b) respiratory compromise, (c) reduced systolic blood pressure < 90 mm Hg or > 30 % drop from baseline, (d) persistent gastrointestinal symptoms.

In this case the patient exhibited rapid‑onset generalized urticaria and flushing, profound hypotension (74/50 mm Hg), hypoxaemia (SpO₂ 88 % on room air), tachypnoea (22 breaths min⁻¹), and marked agitation—fulfilling the diagnostic criteria for severe anaphylaxis with circulatory shock.

### Initial vital signs and physical examination  

- **Blood pressure:** 74/50 mm Hg (systolic < 90 mm Hg)  
- **Heart rate:** not explicitly recorded but likely tachycardic given shock state  
- **Respiratory rate:** 22 breaths min⁻¹, with SpO₂ 88 % on ambient air  
- **Temperature:** not reported  
- **Skin:** diffuse erythema, urticaria covering the entire body, facial swelling (no obvious tongue or oropharyngeal edema)  
- **Airway:** patient reported sensation of throat swelling, yet no visible tongue or laryngeal edema on inspection; no stridor noted  
- **Cardiopulmonary exam:** normal heart sounds, clear lung fields on auscultation  
- **Other:** glucose 12.7 mmol/L (elevated but not critical), sinus rhythm on ECG.

These findings combined to a picture of anaphylactic shock—hypotension, hypoxaemia, and cutaneous manifestations in the setting of a clear temporal relationship to a new drug exposure.

### First‑line pharmacologic treatment  

Current anaphylaxis guidelines (e.g., EAACI 2022, WAO 2020) mandate **immediate intramuscular (IM) epinephrine** as the cornerstone of therapy, administered in the mid‑anterolateral thigh. The recommended dose is **0.01 mg kg⁻¹** of a 1 mg mL⁻¹ solution, up to a maximum of **0.5 mg** per injection. For most adults (> 50 kg), a 0.5‑mg dose is advised. However, many commercially available auto‑injectors for adult use contain **0.3 mg** (e.g., EpiPen®), a dose originally intended for children and adolescents. In this case the ED team used the **0.3‑mg auto‑injector** because it was immediately accessible, and rapid administration trumps delayed dosing in the “golden minute” of anaphylaxis. While a 0.3‑mg dose may be sub‑optimal for a 104‑kg adult, it nonetheless delivers a therapeutic concentration of epinephrine capable of reversing vasodilation, bronchoconstriction, and mucosal edema.

Concomitant therapies administered according to local protocol included:

- **Clemastine 2 mg IV (slow infusion):** a second‑generation H1‑antihistamine; used to ameliorate cutaneous pruritus and urticaria but does **not** address the life‑threatening airway or hemodynamic components.
- **Supplemental oxygen:** 15 L min⁻¹ via a non‑rebreather mask to counteract hypoxaemia while awaiting circulatory stabilization.
- **Fluid resuscitation:** 500 mL isotonic saline (0.9 % NaCl) infused rapidly to augment intravascular volume and support blood pressure.

The decision to forgo immediate intravenous epinephrine infusion was consistent with guideline recommendations, which reserve IV epinephrine for patients who **do not respond** to at least two IM doses or who are in refractory shock under close cardiac monitoring.

### Rationale for the 0.3 mg auto‑injector versus weight‑based dosing  

- **Speed of delivery:** Auto‑injectors require only seconds to activate, eliminating preparation delays that could be fatal in the ED’s chaotic initial phase.
- **Staff familiarity and safety:** Many clinicians, especially those not regularly managing anaphylaxis, are more comfortable using a pre‑filled device than drawing up a weight‑based dose, reducing the risk of dosing errors or hesitation.
- **Availability:** In the described institution the 0.3‑mg auto‑injector was the only ready‑to‑use device; a 0.5‑mg formulation would have required pharmacy retrieval and preparation, potentially delaying therapy.
- **Clinical pragmatism:** While guidelines state 0.5 mg is optimal for adults > 50 kg, real‑world practice acknowledges that a rapid 0.3‑mg dose is still life‑saving and can be repeated promptly if the response is inadequate.

Thus, the initial management adhered to the core principle of “early epinephrine, as soon as possible, using the most readily available method”.

### Immediate response and assessment of therapeutic effect  

Within minutes of IM epinephrine administration, the patient’s **blood pressure rose toward normal** and the **facial swelling and widespread erythema began to subside**. Oxygen saturation improved sufficiently to allow removal of the non‑rebreather mask, indicating reversal of the hypoxaemic component. The rapid improvement confirmed a **positive response** to the first epinephrine dose, satisfying the immediate assessment criteria:

1. **Hemodynamic stabilization** (systolic > 90 mm Hg or return toward baseline).  
2. **Improvement in respiratory status** (SpO₂ > 94 % on room air, reduced work of breathing).  
3. **Resolution or marked reduction of cutaneous signs** (decreased urticaria, facial edema).  

Standard practice dictates that **re‑assessment occurs every 5–10 minutes** after the initial dose. If hypotension, hypoxaemia, or cutaneous signs persist, **repeat IM epinephrine** (0.3 mg or 0.5 mg) should be administered at 5‑minute intervals, up to a cumulative dose of 0.5 mg per hour, while preparing for possible escalation to IV infusion under intensive monitoring.

In this case, the initial response was deemed adequate, prompting admission to an acute internal medicine ward for observation. Subsequent recurrences (described in later sections) highlighted the importance of sustained vigilance even after an apparently successful first‑line intervention.

## Pathophysiology of Anaphylaxis and Underlying Mast Cell Disorders  

Anaphylaxis is the clinical manifestation of a rapid, systemic release of pre‑formed and newly synthesised mediators from activated mast cells and, to a lesser extent, basophils. The cascade that leads to this abrupt degranulation can be broadly divided into IgE‑mediated and non‑IgE–mediated pathways, both of which are relevant to drug‑induced reactions such as the one described in the case of clarithromycin‑triggered multiphasic anaphylaxis.

### IgE‑mediated versus non‑IgE pathways in drug‑induced anaphylaxis  

In the classical IgE‑mediated mechanism, a sensitising exposure to a drug (or a metabolite that acts as a hapten) leads to the production of drug‑specific IgE antibodies that bind with high affinity to the FcεRI receptors on mast cells and basophils. Upon re‑exposure, the drug (or hapten‑protein conjugate) cross‑links surface‑bound IgE, initiating a signalling cascade that culminates in rapid degranulation. This pathway explains the patient’s first anaphylactic event after a single oral dose of clarithromycin, despite a prior uneventful exposure to other macrolides; the drug likely acted as a hapten, binding to serum proteins and forming a neo‑antigen recognized by pre‑existing IgE generated during earlier, unrelated sensitisations (e.g., the remote amoxicillin reaction).

Non‑IgE mechanisms do not rely on specific antibodies. Certain drugs can directly activate mast cells through receptors such as Mas‑related G‑protein‑coupled receptor X2 (MRGPRX2) or via complement activation (C3a, C5a). These pathways produce a clinical picture indistinguishable from IgE‑mediated anaphylaxis but lack a detectable specific IgE. Clarithromycin is not a classic MRGPRX2 agonist, yet macrolides have been reported to provoke pseudo‑allergic reactions in susceptible individuals, suggesting that a small proportion of drug‑induced anaphylaxis may involve direct mast cell activation.

### Mediators released by mast cells and their clinical effects  

When mast cells degranulate, they liberate a repertoire of vasoactive and inflammatory mediators that drive the signs and symptoms observed in anaphylaxis:

| Mediator | Primary source | Key clinical actions |
|----------|----------------|----------------------|
| **Histamine** | Pre‑formed granule content | Vasodilation, increased capillary permeability (hypotension, edema), bronchoconstriction, urticaria, pruritus, gastric acid secretion |
| **Tryptase** | Granules (serine protease) | Proteolysis of extracellular matrix, activation of complement, marker of mast cell activation; elevated serum levels peak 1–2 h after symptom onset |
| **Leukotriene C4, D4, E4** | Synthesised de novo | Sustained bronchoconstriction, increased vascular permeability, mucus production; contribute to late‑phase symptoms |
| **Prostaglandin D2** | Synthesised de novo | Vasodilation, bronchoconstriction, platelet aggregation; may perpetuate prolonged airway obstruction |
| **Platelet‑activating factor (PAF)** | Synthesised by mast cells, basophils, monocytes | Potent vasodilator, increases vascular leak, can cause shock; higher PAF levels have been linked to severe and fatal anaphylaxis |
| **Cytokines (IL‑4, IL‑5, IL‑13, TNF‑α)** | De novo synthesis | Promote recruitment of eosinophils and other inflammatory cells, contribute to the biphasic “late” phase |

The early, rapid symptoms (hypotension, urticaria, flushing) are driven mainly by histamine and PAF, whereas the later phase—often manifesting as recurrent wheeze, rash, or gastrointestinal upset—correlates with leukotrienes, prostaglandins, and cytokines. In the presented patient, the first episode featured profound hypoxaemia and hypotension, reflecting a dominant histamine/PAF surge, while subsequent episodes were milder, indicating a shift toward the slower‑acting mediators.

### Systemic mastocytosis: definition and WHO criteria  

Systemic mastocytosis (SM) is a clonal mast cell disorder characterised by an abnormal accumulation of neoplastic mast cells in extracutaneous tissues (bone marrow, liver, spleen, gastrointestinal tract, etc.). The 2016 WHO classification defines SM by the presence of **one major criterion** *or* **three minor criteria**:

| **Major criterion** | Multifocal, dense infiltrates of mast cells (≥15 cells per aggregate) in bone marrow or other extracutaneous organ biopsies. |
|----------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Minor criteria**  | 1. **Morphology**: ≥25 % of mast cells in infiltrate are atypical (spindle‑shaped) or >25 % of total marrow mast cells are atypical.<br>2. **Immunophenotype**: Aberrant expression of CD2 and/or CD25 on mast cells.<br>3. **Molecular**: Presence of KIT D816V mutation in peripheral blood, bone marrow, or tissue.<br>4. **Serum tryptase**: Baseline level >20 ng/mL (in the absence of an associated myeloid neoplasm). |

In the case report, the patient fulfilled two minor criteria (elevated baseline tryptase and presumed KIT mutation not directly documented) but lacked tissue confirmation of the major criterion, leaving the diagnosis “probable SM” rather than definitive.

### Impact of increased mast cell burden on severity, recurrence, and tryptase dynamics  

A higher mast cell load translates into a larger pool of cells capable of releasing mediators upon activation. Consequently:

1. **Severity** – More mast cells → larger histamine and PAF releases → deeper hypotension and bronchospasm, as observed in the first anaphylactic episode.
2. **Recurrence (biphasic/multiphasic course)** – Mast cell disease can prolong the activation cascade. After the initial degranulation, residual activated mast cells may continue to synthesize and release newly formed mediators (leukotrienes, prostaglandins, cytokines), giving rise to late‑phase symptoms without further allergen exposure.
3. **Tryptase kinetics** – Baseline tryptase is chronically elevated in SM (>20 ng/mL). During an acute reaction, tryptase spikes above baseline (often 1.5–2 times higher) and then declines over 24–48 h. In this patient, the first measured sample (taken after the third episode) already showed a markedly raised value, confirming mast cell activation superimposed on a pre‑existing elevation.

Thus, the presence of SM provides a mechanistic explanation for both the intensity of the initial shock and the propensity for repeated, albeit attenuated, episodes.

### Clarithromycin and mast cell activation  

Macrolide antibiotics, including clarithromycin, are not classic IgE‑mediated allergens, yet case reports describe immediate hypersensitivity reactions. Two plausible mechanisms are relevant:

* **Hapten formation** – Clarithromycin can bind covalently to self‑proteins, creating a novel antigenic epitope that is recognised by drug‑specific IgE. The patient’s prior exposure to β‑lactams and a remote amoxicillin reaction suggests a predisposition to developing drug‑specific IgE.
* **Direct mast cell activation** – Some macrolides have been shown in vitro to trigger degranulation through non‑IgE pathways (e.g., via MRGPRX2). Although clarithromycin is a weaker activator than some other agents, in a host with an expanded mast cell compartment (as in SM) even modest direct activation may be amplified.

Both mechanisms likely contributed synergistically to the severe reaction after a single oral dose.

### Risk factors for biphasic/multiphasic anaphylaxis  

Evidence from registry and cohort studies identifies several clinical variables that increase the likelihood of a second wave of symptoms:

| Risk factor | Pathophysiologic rationale |
|-------------|-----------------------------|
| **Delay in epinephrine administration** | Prolonged mast cell activation before vasoconstriction allows greater mediator accumulation, facilitating a late‑phase response. |
| **Severe index reaction** (hypotension, hypoxaemia, need for >1 epinephrine dose) | Reflects a larger mast cell reservoir being triggered; more mediator load predisposes to a persistent inflammatory milieu. |
| **Multiple epinephrine doses** | Indicates refractory or severe disease, often seen in SM, and signals a high burden of released mediators. |
| **Underlying mast cell disorder (SM, mast cell activation syndrome)** | Provides a larger, hyper‑reactive mast cell pool that can sustain mediator release beyond the initial trigger. |
| **High baseline tryptase** | Correlates with mast cell burden; patients with elevated baseline levels have more severe and prolonged reactions. |

In the presented case, all four risk factors were present: a delayed presentation (symptoms began 1 h after drug intake, patient sought care another hour later), profound hypotension and hypoxaemia, requirement for four epinephrine administrations, and laboratory evidence of an increased mast cell burden suggestive of systemic mastocytosis. These elements collectively explain the multiphasic pattern observed.

---

Understanding the immunologic cascade—from IgE‑mediated or direct mast cell activation through the release of a complex mediator milieu—and recognizing how disorders such as systemic mastocytosis amplify each step, equips clinicians to anticipate a more protracted course, to interpret tryptase measurements accurately, and to tailor observation periods and therapeutic strategies for patients at high risk of biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis.

## Diagnostic Work‑up and Reasoning: From Acute Phase to Follow‑up  

The diagnostic pathway for this patient illustrates how the acute management of anaphylaxis dovetails with a systematic search for underlying mechanisms that might explain an unusually prolonged, multiphasic course. The clinicians balanced the urgency of lifesaving treatment with the need to collect time‑sensitive laboratory specimens, to arrange targeted imaging, and to decide whether formal allergy testing or a mast cell disorder work‑up was warranted.

### Guideline‑recommended timing of serum tryptase

Serum tryptase, a protease released almost exclusively from mast cells, is the most widely available biomarker of mast cell activation. Current anaphylaxis guidelines (e.g., World Allergy Organization, EAACI) recommend a minimum of three samples:

1. **Acute sample** – as soon as possible after the onset of symptoms, but without delaying epinephrine administration. This captures the peak of the mast‑cell release.
2. **Peak sample** – 1–4 hours after symptom onset (most protocols specify 1–2 h, still acceptable up to 4 h). Tryptase levels usually reach a maximum in this window.
3. **Baseline sample** – ≥24 hours after complete clinical resolution, when tryptase has returned to the individual’s steady‑state level.

A rise of >2 ng/mL plus a 20 % increase over baseline is considered a significant elevation, indicating systemic mast‑cell activation. Persistently elevated baseline tryptase (>11.4 ng/mL) raises suspicion for an underlying clonal mast cell disorder such as systemic mastocytosis.

### Interpretation of the case’s tryptase values

In the presented patient, the first tryptase measurement was drawn **1 hour after the third anaphylactic episode** (i.e., during the “peak” window) and a second sample was taken **27 hours after the last recurrence**, approximating a baseline value. Although the exact numerical results are not reproduced here, the narrative states that both values were elevated and that the baseline remained high on follow‑up. This pattern fulfills the biochemical criterion for mast‑cell activation (acute rise) and, because the baseline did not normalize, suggests a **constitutively increased mast‑cell burden**. In the context of multiphasic anaphylaxis, such a profile is especially informative: it signals that each clinical relapse was driven by genuine de‑granulation rather than lingering effects of a single massive release.

### Bone‑marrow biopsy and WHO criteria for systemic mastocytosis

Systemic mastocytosis (SM) is diagnosed by the 2016 WHO criteria, which require **one major + one minor** or **three minor** criteria. The major criterion (multifocal dense infiltrates of mast cells in bone‑marrow or other extracutaneous tissue) necessitates an adequate marrow core for histologic assessment. In this patient, a bone‑marrow biopsy was performed, but **material was insufficient** to evaluate the major criterion. Two minor criteria were met:

* **Elevated serum tryptase** (>20 ng/mL) – already demonstrated.
* **Expression of CD2 and/or CD25 on mast cells** – identified on flow cytometry of the limited sample.

Because the major criterion could not be assessed, the patient does **not meet formal WHO diagnostic thresholds** for SM, yet the constellation of findings (persistent tryptase elevation, minor criteria, hepatomegaly) yields a **high pre‑test probability** for a clonal mast‑cell disorder. Clinicians therefore labeled the situation as “probable SM” and arranged longitudinal follow‑up, anticipating that repeat marrow sampling or molecular testing (e.g., KIT D816V mutation) might clarify the diagnosis later.

### Role and limitation of skin testing or drug provocation

Standard allergy work‑up after an anaphylactic episode includes skin prick or intradermal testing and, when safe, graded drug provocation. In this case, the trigger (clarithromycin) was a **new, single exposure** with an immediate severe reaction. Performing skin testing with a macrolide carries a **high risk of inducing another systemic reaction**, especially in a patient who already demonstrated a fulminant response and who may have an underlying mast‑cell disease. Moreover, the **diagnostic yield of macrolide skin testing is low**, as standardized extracts are rarely available. Consequently, the team **deferred any in‑vivo testing**, relying instead on thorough history, avoidance of the implicated drug, and provision of an epinephrine auto‑injector for future exposure protection.

### Distinguishing true biphasic recurrence from persistent/refractory anaphylaxis

A critical diagnostic nuance is whether each subsequent episode represented a **new biphasic reaction** (symptom-free interval followed by re‑emergence) or **persistent anaphylaxis** with incomplete resolution after the first epinephrine dose. The patient’s clinical course supports true biphasic episodes:

* **Clear intervals of symptom resolution** (normotension, normal SpO₂, no cutaneous signs) lasting several minutes to hours before the next flare.
* **Repeated need for additional epinephrine** despite prior adequate dosing, indicating that the prior dose had resolved the preceding episode rather than merely blunted a continuously evolving one.
* **Gradual attenuation of severity** across relapses, a pattern more consistent with separate de‑granulation events than with refractory, uncontrolled shock.

Recognizing true biphasic recurrences guided the decision to **continue vigilant monitoring** and to consider adjunctive measures (corticosteroids, potential continuous epinephrine infusion) rather than escalating to aggressive rescue therapies appropriate for refractory anaphylaxis.

### Adjunct investigations supporting mastocytosis suspicion

Beyond tryptase and marrow evaluation, the team pursued **non‑specific but supportive studies**:

* **Abdominal ultrasound** revealed **hepatomegaly**, a frequent extra‑cutaneous manifestation of systemic mastocytosis secondary to mast‑cell infiltration or fibrosis.
* **Neuropsychiatric assessment** highlighted **depression and memory complaints**, which are reported more often in SM patients, possibly reflecting central mast‑cell mediator effects.

These findings do not confirm SM on their own, but they **strengthen the clinical suspicion** and justify close follow‑up with hematology‑oncology specialists, repeat tryptase monitoring, and possibly targeted molecular testing.

### Synthesis of the diagnostic reasoning

The diagnostic algorithm proceeded as follows:

1. **Acute phase** – focus on life‑saving epinephrine; obtain the first tryptase sample promptly (within the 1‑hour window) without delaying treatment.
2. **Early hospital course** – recognize multiple distinct anaphylactic recurrences; document timing, severity, and response to each epinephrine dose.
3. **Second tryptase** – drawn after the last episode (≈27 h) to approximate baseline; elevated result prompted consideration of a mast‑cell disorder.
4. **Targeted work‑up** – bone‑marrow biopsy (limited sample), flow cytometry, serum tryptase trend, abdominal imaging, and neuro‑psychiatric review.
5. **Allergy testing decision** – withheld skin testing/ drug challenge due to safety concerns and low incremental diagnostic value.
6. **Long‑term plan** – discharge with epinephrine auto‑injector, antihistamine prophylaxis, and scheduled follow‑up for repeat tryptase measurement and possible repeat marrow or KIT mutation analysis.

By integrating **timely biomarker sampling**, **structured clinical observation**, and **focused ancillary investigations**, the team arrived at a plausible explanation for the patient’s multiphasic anaphylaxis—probable systemic mastocytosis—while avoiding unnecessary and potentially hazardous allergy testing. This approach exemplifies how clinicians can move from acute emergency management to a nuanced, evidence‑based diagnostic work‑up in complex anaphylaxis cases.

## Management of Multiphasic Anaphylaxis: Strategies, Controversies, and Decision Points  

Anaphylaxis classically presents as a single, rapidly evolving episode, but a proportion of patients experience a **biphasic** or **multiphasic** course, in which symptom resolution is followed by one or more recurrences without re‑exposure to the trigger. Understanding the temporal pattern, recognizing the signs of each relapse, and applying evidence‑based therapeutic choices are essential for preventing deterioration and for allocating appropriate resources (ward versus intensive‑care unit). The present case of a woman in her 60 s with clarithromycin‑induced anaphylaxis illustrates the practical challenges of managing repeated reactions and highlights the points at which clinicians must decide between competing management options.

### Biphasic versus Multiphasic Anaphylaxis  

- **Biphasic anaphylaxis** refers to a single recurrence of symptoms after the initial episode has resolved. Most recurrences occur within the first 6–12 hours, although reports document a range of 0.5 hours to 78 hours.  
- **Multiphasic anaphylaxis** describes two or more distinct recurrences after the index reaction. In the current report the patient experienced **four** episodes within a 12‑hour window, fitting the definition of multiphasic disease. The term implicitly conveys that each episode is separated by a symptom‑free interval and that the patient has returned to a baseline state (e.g., normotensive, non‑hypoxic) before the next flare.  

Recognition of this pattern is critical because management algorithms for a single episode do not fully address the risk of further decompensation, the potential need for higher‑dose epinephrine, or the decision to intensify monitoring.

### Recurrence Review in the Presented Patient  

| Episode | Timing (post‑clarithromycin) | Epinephrine dose & route | Clinical response | Trend in severity |
|--------|------------------------------|--------------------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| 1 (ED) | 1 h | 0.3 mg IM (auto‑injector) | Immediate rise in BP, resolution of flushing, oxygen weaned | Severe: hypotension 74/50 mm Hg, SpO₂ 88 % |
| 2 (ward) | ~4 h | 0.3 mg IM (auto‑injector) | BP restored, skin findings improved | Moderate: dyspnoea, cyanosis, repeat hypotension |
| 3 (ICU) | ~7 h | 0.5 mg IM (prepared syringe) | Prompt hemodynamic stabilization | Mild‑moderate: dyspnoea, urticaria, pruritus |
| 4 (ICU) | ~11 h | 0.5 mg IM (prepared syringe) | No further escalation required | Mild: rash recurrence, no hypotension |

The dosing escalation from 0.3 mg to 0.5 mg reflects a pragmatic response to the patient’s weight (104 kg) and the diminishing efficacy of the lower‐dose auto‑injector. Moreover, the *clinical trajectory*—from profound circulatory collapse to progressively milder cutaneous and respiratory symptoms—suggests that each subsequent epinephrine dose achieved sufficient catecholaminergic effect while the underlying mast‑cell activation may have waned.

### Repeat Intramuscular Epinephrine versus Intravenous Infusion  

#### Evidence for Repeat IM Dosing  

Guidelines (e.g., World Allergy Organization, European Academy of Allergy and Clinical Immunology) recommend **prompt intramuscular (IM) epinephrine** as first‑line therapy, with repeat IM doses every 5–15 minutes for persistent or recurrent symptoms. The evidence supporting this approach comes primarily from observational data and pharmacologic reasoning: IM delivery into the vastus lateralis achieves rapid peak plasma concentrations while minimizing cardiovascular toxicity. In the present case, the patient’s hemodynamics responded swiftly to each repeat IM dose, supporting the adequacy of this route for episodic recurrences.

#### When IV Epinephrine is Considered  

Intravenous (IV) epinephrine infusion is reserved for **refractory anaphylaxis**—defined as ongoing hypotension, bronchospasm, or airway compromise despite at least two appropriate IM doses. The literature consists of case series and small cohort studies demonstrating faster hemodynamic recovery but also a higher incidence of arrhythmias and extravasation injuries, especially when administered as a rapid bolus rather than a titrated infusion. In multiphasic scenarios, the decision hinges on whether the relapse appears *refractory* (i.e., no appreciable response to IM epinephrine) or simply *recurrent* (adequate response after repeat IM dosing).  

In this patient, each recurrence was terminated with a single IM dose, obviating the need for IV infusion. However, the **ICU team considered a prophylactic IV infusion** after the second relapse because of the uncertainty surrounding future episodes. The risk‑benefit analysis (potential for severe hypotension versus adverse cardiac effects) ultimately favored continued observation with ready‑to‑administer IM epinephrine rather than a continuous infusion.

#### Practical Take‑Home Points  

- **Repeat IM epinephrine** remains the cornerstone for each new anaphylactic episode, even when episodes are multiple.  
- **IV infusion** should be reserved for true refractory reactions (no response after ≥2 IM doses) or for patients with life‑threatening airway obstruction where immediate, titratable catecholamine support is needed.  
- When choosing the IM dose, weight‑based calculations (0.01 mg/kg, max 0.5 mg) are preferred; many auto‑injectors contain only 0.3 mg, which may underdose patients >50 kg. Consider using a pre‑drawn 0.5 mg syringe in such individuals, as was done after the second recurrence.

### Corticosteroids: Historical Rationale and Contemporary Controversy  

**Historical use** of systemic corticosteroids in anaphylaxis arose from the belief that they could blunt late‑phase mediator release and thus prevent biphasic reactions. Empiric protocols often included a single dose of 1 mg/kg prednisone (or equivalent) administered intravenously after the initial stabilization.

**Recent evidence** has challenged this practice. A 2017 systematic review of 22 studies found **no reduction** in biphasic reaction rates with corticosteroid use, and a 2019 cohort demonstrated **increased ICU admission** among patients who received pre‑hospital steroids, likely reflecting confounding by severity. Consequently, most contemporary guidelines (e.g., WAO 2020, UK Resuscitation Council) advise *against routine corticosteroid administration* for the sole purpose of preventing a biphasic episode, although steroids remain acceptable for co‑existing asthma exacerbations or other indications.

**Application to the case**  
- After the first recurrence, the patient received **30 mg prednisone IV daily for five days** and a **single 8 mg dose of dexamethasone** because the local protocol mandated steroids for biphasic anaphylaxis.  
- Given the lack of robust benefit and the potential for hyperglycemia (especially relevant in a diabetic patient), the steroid course may have been unnecessary.  
- The clinical course—progressively milder symptoms despite continued steroid exposure—does not allow a causal inference, but the case underscores the need to **individualize steroid use**, reserving it for patients with a clear alternate indication (e.g., severe asthma, known mast‑cell disease with organ involvement).

### Observation Period and Level of Care  

#### Duration of Monitoring  

- **Traditional recommendation**: 4–6 hours of observation after symptom resolution.  
- **Risk‑stratified models** (e.g., UK 2021 guideline) suggest extending observation to **12 hours** for patients with severe initial presentations, multiple epinephrine doses, or delayed epinephrine administration.  
- In the present case, the patient experienced **four episodes within 12 hours**, justifying an **ICU stay** for at least 24 hours until no further relapse occurred.

#### Ward versus ICU Placement  

Factors favoring ICU admission include:  
1. **Hemodynamic instability** despite fluid resuscitation.  
2. **Repeated need for epinephrine** (≥2 doses).  
3. **High‑risk comorbidities** (obesity, diabetes, potential mast‑cell disorder).  
4. **Uncertainty about airway protection** (recurrent hypoxaemia, cyanosis).  

Conversely, once the patient remains **stable for ≥4 hours** after the last episode, with **no further epinephrine requirement** and **normalized vitals**, transfer to a monitored ward is reasonable. The case illustrates a stepwise escalation—from acute internal ward to ICU after the second relapse—guided by the **clinical trajectory** rather than a rigid time‑based rule.

### Practical Algorithm for Clinicians Facing a Second or Third Reaction  

1. **Rapid Assessment** – Re‑measure vitals, airway patency, skin findings, and capillary glucose.  
2. **Administer IM Epinephrine** – Use weight‑adjusted 0.5 mg for patients >50 kg; repeat every 5–10 minutes if symptoms persist.  
3. **Supportive Measures** – Oxygen, high‑flow IV crystalloids, antihistamines, and bronchodilators as indicated.  
4. **Consider IV Epinephrine** only if:  
   - No response after ≥2 IM doses, **or**  
   - Ongoing severe hypotension/bronchospasm requiring continuous catecholamine support.  
5. **Consult Allergy/Immunology Early** – To evaluate for underlying mast‑cell disease (serum tryptase, bone‑marrow biopsy) and to advise on long‑term prophylaxis.  
6. **Re‑evaluate Corticosteroid Need** – Reserve for concomitant asthma, COPD exacerbation, or documented adrenal insufficiency.  
7. **Determine Monitoring Level** – ICU for refractory or repeatedly relapsing cases; otherwise, a high‑dependency or telemetry‑equipped ward with 12‑hour observation.  
8. **Document Tryptase Timings** – Obtain a sample as soon as possible after each episode (ideally within 1–2 hours) and a baseline ≥24 hours after full resolution.

### Patient‑Centered Discharge Planning  

- **Epinephrine Auto‑Injector Prescription** – Provide a **0.5 mg device** (or a weight‑adjusted single‑dose syringe) together with a written **emergency action plan**.  
- **Antihistamine Maintenance** – Initiate a non‑sedating H₁‑antagonist (e.g., levocetirizine 5 mg daily) to reduce chronic urticaria and possibly blunt mild mast‑cell activation.  
- **Education** – Emphasize avoidance of macrolides (clarithromycin) and any future β‑lactam cross‑reactivity, review the signs of anaphylaxis, and demonstrate proper auto‑injector usage.  
- **Follow‑Up** – Arrange outpatient allergology review for **serum tryptase**, possible **bone‑marrow evaluation**, and **risk stratification for future anaphylaxis** (including consideration of mast‑cell stabilizers such as ketotifen if systemic mastocytosis is later confirmed).  
- **Documentation** – Ensure the electronic health record clearly flags the drug allergy and the need for epinephrine on every future encounter.

### Summary  

Multiphasic anaphylaxis challenges clinicians to move beyond the single‑dose epinephrine protocol and to tailor monitoring and therapeutic intensity to a dynamic clinical picture. In the described patient, **repeat IM epinephrine at an appropriate weight‑based dose** successfully terminated each episode, while **IV infusion was avoided** because each relapse remained responsive. The routine use of **systemic corticosteroids** is not supported by current evidence and should be limited to specific co‑existing conditions. A **risk‑stratified observation period**—often extending to 12 hours or longer for severe or recurrent cases—and judicious ICU admission can safeguard against missed deterioration. Early allergy specialist involvement, precise tryptase sampling, and comprehensive discharge planning (adequate auto‑injector, patient education, and specialist follow‑up) complete the management continuum for patients experiencing multiphasic anaphylaxis.

## Clinical Lessons, Future Directions, and System‑Level Recommendations  

The case of a 60‑year‑old woman with multiphasic anaphylaxis to clarithromycin illustrates several principles that trainees must internalise when confronting severe allergic reactions, and it also exposes important gaps in our current evidence base and practice guidelines. The following discussion synthesises the most salient teaching points, highlights where existing recommendations fall short, and outlines concrete system‑level actions that could improve patient safety and resource utilisation for complex anaphylaxis.

### Key Learning Points  

1. **Prompt intramuscular epinephrine remains the cornerstone of therapy.**  
   In the presented case the first dose of 0.3 mg epinephrine administered via auto‑injector produced rapid haemodynamic recovery, underscoring the life‑saving effect of early catecholamine support. Nevertheless, the patient required four separate epinephrine administrations within 12 hours, reminding us that the initial response does not guarantee a sustained cure. Clinicians must therefore maintain a low threshold for repeat dosing whenever signs of circulatory collapse, severe bronchospasm, or cutaneous flare reappear.  

2. **Awareness of under‑dosing in obese adults.**  
   The recommended adult dose of 0.01 mg kg⁻¹ (maximum 0.5 mg) often exceeds the 0.3 mg contained in many commercially available auto‑injectors. Our patient weighed 104 kg, for whom the calculated dose would be 1 mg (capped at 0.5 mg). Using a 0.3 mg device therefore delivers only 60 % of the guideline‑based amount. Recent retrospective data reveal a higher need for rescue dosing and escalation to intensive care when the lower‑dose device is used. Trainees should recognise that, when feasible, a 0.5 mg autoinjector or a manually prepared 0.5 mg intramuscular dose is preferred for patients >50 kg.  

3. **Repeated, systematic reassessment is essential.**  
   Anaphylaxis can evolve rapidly; vital signs, airway patency, and skin findings must be reassessed at least every 5 minutes during the acute phase and hourly thereafter until a stable plateau is reached. In this case, each relapse was identified promptly because nursing staff performed structured checks. A systematic assessment framework (e.g., ABC‑D checklist) reduces the risk of missing a nascent biphasic or multiphasic event.  

### Mast Cell Disorders as Hidden Risk Factors  

Systemic mastocytosis dramatically amplifies mast‑cell mediator release, predisposing patients to severe and prolonged anaphylaxis even when the inciting allergen is identified and withdrawn. The case patient displayed two WHO minor criteria (elevated baseline tryptase, hepatomegaly) and persistently high tryptase during relapse, suggesting an underlying mast‑cell disease that fell short of a formal diagnosis because a marrow biopsy was limited.  

For trainees, the practical implication is to consider a mast‑cell disorder whenever an anaphylactic reaction is unusually severe, recalcitrant, or multipl

e‑phasic, especially in the context of:
- Baseline tryptase > 11.4 ng/mL,
- Unexplained flushing, pruritus, or gastrointestinal symptoms preceding the reaction,
- A personal or family history of urticaria pigmentosa, unexplained anaphylaxis, or osteolytic lesions.

When such clues arise, early consultation with allergy‑immunology and measurement of serial tryptase levels should be pursued, and patients may benefit from mast‑cell stabilisers (e.g., ketotifen) and lifelong high‑dose epinephrine prescriptions.  

### Critical Appraisal of Current Guidelines  

1. **Monitoring duration.**  
   Existing recommendations range from a minimum 4‑hour observation after symptom resolution to more generous 12‑hour windows for high‑risk patients. The heterogeneity stems from limited data on the timing of biphasic recurrences, which in this series often occurred within 6–12 hours. The present case supports extending observation to at least 12 hours for patients who required >1 epinephrine dose, have comorbid mast‑cell disease, or present with hypotension.  

2. **Corticosteroid use.**  
   Historically, systemic steroids were administered to “prevent” biphasic reactions. Contemporary systematic reviews, however, demonstrate no reduction in biphasic incidence and highlight potential harms (hyperglycaemia, infection, delayed wound healing). In the case, steroids were given after the first relapse per local protocol, yet the subsequent episodes displayed a clear trend toward decreasing severity, suggesting that the steroids may have been superfluous. Current best practice favours omission of routine steroids; they may be reserved for patients with concurrent asthma exacerbations or other steroid‑responsive comorbidities.  

### Proposed Risk‑Stratification Tool  

A pragmatic bedside score can assist clinicians in deciding observation intensity and the need for prophylactic measures. The following points may be assigned (≥ 4 points indicating high risk):  

| Variable | Points |
|----------|--------|
| Age > 65 y | 1 |
| Body weight > 90 kg (potential under‑dosing) | 1 |
| Initial systolic BP < 90 mm Hg or SpO₂ < 92 % | 1 |
| ≥ 2 epinephrine doses required in first 2 h | 1 |
| Known or suspected mast‑cell disorder (baseline tryptase > 11 ng/mL) | 2 |
| Delay > 15 min from symptom onset to first epinephrine | 1 |

Patients scoring ≥ 4 could be transferred directly to a monitored unit (step‑down or ICU), observed for ≥ 12 h, and considered for a low‑dose continuous IV epinephrine infusion if recurrent episodes appear imminent.  

### Value of Case Registries  

Multiphasic anaphylaxis is intrinsically rare, making randomized trials infeasible. A prospective, multicentre registry that captures detailed phenotypic data (trigger, dosing, comorbidities, tryptase kinetics, treatment timelines, outcomes) would provide the high‑quality observational evidence needed to refine risk scores, validate observation periods, and examine the real‑world effectiveness of steroids versus no‑steroid strategies. Participation could be incentivised through integration with existing emergency medicine and allergy networks, and data governance should ensure patient confidentiality while allowing granular analysis.  

### Priority Research Areas  

| Topic | Rationale |
|-------|-----------|
| **Epinephrine dosing in obese adults** | Determine pharmacodynamic thresholds and optimal auto‑injector dosing (0.3 mg vs 0.5 mg). |
| **Prophylactic IV epinephrine infusion** | Assess safety and efficacy for patients with ≥ 3 recurrences despite IM dosing. |
| **Steroid efficacy for biphasic prevention** | Large‑scale pragmatic trials to resolve ongoing controversy. |
| **Mastocytosis‑specific protocols** | Evaluate adjunctive agents (ketotifen, cromolyn) and epinephrine dosing algorithms in confirmed systemic mastocytosis. |
| **Predictive biomarkers (tryptase kinetics, basal eosinophils)** | Identify early laboratory signals that forecast multiphasic courses. |

Funding agencies and professional societies should prioritize collaborative grant mechanisms to address these questions, recognizing that the cumulative burden of rare but high‑impact events justifies dedicated investigation.  

### Practical Checklist for Multiphasic Anaphylaxis  

1. **Initial Management**  
   - • Assess ABCs; obtain vitals and SpO₂.  
   - • Administer IM epinephrine 0.01 mg kg⁻¹ (max 0.5 mg); prefer 0.5 mg device for patients > 50 kg.  
   - • Position patient supine with legs elevated; give high‑flow O₂.  
   - • Start isotonic crystalloid bolus (15–20 mL kg⁻¹).  

2. **Adjuncts**  
   - • H1‑antihistamine (e.g., clemastine 2 mg IV).  
   - • Consider H2‑antihistamine (e.g., ranitidine) if severe cutaneous symptoms.  
   - • **Do not** give routine systemic steroids unless specific indication (asthma, COPD exacerbation).  

3. **Monitoring & Re‑assessment**  
   - • Re‑check vitals every 5 min for the first 30 min, then hourly.  
   - • Document any recurrence of hypotension, hypoxaemia, wheeze, urticaria, or angio‑edema.  

4. **Risk Stratification** (apply score above).  

5. **Escalation**  
   - • If ≥ 2 epinephrine doses needed or high‑risk score: transfer to monitored setting.  
   - • Consider continuous IV epinephrine infusion (starting 0.01 µg kg⁻¹ min⁻¹) if recurrent episodes within 30 min despite IM dosing.  

6. **Diagnostic Work‑up**  
   - • Draw tryptase ASAP (ideally within 1 h of onset), repeat at 1–4 h and ≥ 24 h for baseline.  
   - • Order baseline serum tryptase if not previously known; screen for mastocytosis if persistently elevated.  

7. **Discharge Planning**  
   - • Prescribe 0.5 mg epinephrine auto‑injector (or appropriate weight‑based device).  
   - • Provide written action plan, allergy alert bracelet, and education on trigger avoidance.  
   - • Arrange follow‑up with allergy/immunology within 2 weeks; consider referral for bone‑marrow evaluation if tryptase > 20 ng/mL or other minor criteria present.  

By internalising these lessons, critically evaluating guideline limitations, and advocating for system‑level improvements such as registries and targeted research, trainees will be better equipped to manage the rare but formidable challenge of multiphasic anaphylaxis safely and efficiently.
