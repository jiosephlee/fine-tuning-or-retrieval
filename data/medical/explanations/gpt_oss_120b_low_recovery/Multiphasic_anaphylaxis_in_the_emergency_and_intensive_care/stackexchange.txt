### [Clinical Pearls] What are the first‑line pharmacologic interventions for acute anaphylaxis and how should they be dosed in an adult weighing >100 kg?

**First‑line pharmacologic treatment of acute anaphylaxis in an adult > 100 kg**

| Medication | Indication | Recommended dose in an adult weighing > 100 kg | Route | Key practical points |
|------------|------------|-----------------------------------------------|-------|----------------------|
| **Epinephrine (adrenaline)** | Immediate reversal of airway, cardiovascular, and cutaneous manifestations; the only drug that has been shown to reduce mortality. | **0.5 mg IM** (0.01 mg · kg⁻¹ ≈ 1 µg/kg; for adults > 50 kg the dose is rounded to the nearest commercially available amount, i.e., 0.5 mg). <br>If the patient is > 100 kg and a 0.5‑mg autoinjector is unavailable, draw up 0.5 mg (500 µg) in a 1‑mL syringe and inject into the mid‑outer thigh. | Intramuscular (preferably lateral thigh, 90° angle) <br>**If refractory** (no improvement within 5–10 min) give a second 0.5 mg IM dose; repeat every 5–15 min as needed, up to a total of ~2 mg (four 0.5‑mg doses) before considering IV infusion. | • IM is far safer than IV bolus in the undifferentiated patient. <br>• Auto‑injectors that contain only 0.3 mg (the “adult‑low” device) provide sub‑therapeutic dosing for patients > 80 kg; a second dose is frequently required, as illustrated in the presented case where four separate IM doses (two 0.3 mg and two 0.5 mg) were needed to control recurrent shock. <br>• In the ED or ICU, keep a ready‑to‑use 0.5‑mg syringe‑and‑needle kit for overweight patients. |
| **H1‑antihistamine (second‑line, symptom‑focused)** | Urticaria, pruritus, angio‑oedema of skin/mucosa; does **not** treat airway obstruction or hypotension. | Cetirizine 10 mg PO **or** diphenhydramine 25–50 mg IV/IM (may cause sedation and mild hypotension). | Oral or IV/IM (if severe cutaneous symptoms). | Give **after** epinephrine; do not delay epinephrine to await antihistamine effect. |
| **H2‑antihistamine (adjunct)** | May help with cutaneous and gastrointestinal symptoms when combined with H1 blocker. | Ranitidine 50 mg IV once (or famotidine 20 mg IV). | IV. | Optional; no impact on hemodynamics. |
| **Corticosteroid (historically used, now **not** first‑line)** | Intended to prevent late (biphasic) reactions; large trials show no consistent benefit and potential harm (hyperglycaemia, infection, delayed epinephrine). | If used, methylprednisolone 125 mg IV (≈ 1–2 mg/kg). | IV. | Current guidelines (World Allergy Organization, AAAAI/ACAAI, UK Resuscitation Council) advise **against routine use**; reserve for defined indications (e.g., concomitant asthma exacerbation). The case report notes that steroids were given after the first recurrence, but recent evidence suggests they contributed little to preventing subsequent phases. |
| **Fluid resuscitation** | Counteract vasodilation and capillary leak. | 1–2 L isotonic crystalloid (0.9 % NaCl) rapidly, then titrate to maintain MAP > 65 mm Hg. | IV. | Supports epinephrine; especially important in hypotensive patients. |
| **Adjunctive oxygen** | Hypoxaemia from bronchospasm or airway edema. | 10–15 L/min via non‑rebreather or high‑flow nasal cannula; aim SpO₂ ≥ 94 %. | Face mask or nasal cannula. | Give early; may be required again during recurrent phases, as seen in the case. |

### How the dosing fits the presented case
- The 60‑year‑old woman weighed **104 kg**. According to the guideline‐derived rule (≥ 0.5 mg for any adult > 50 kg), the **initial dose should have been 0.5 mg IM**.  
- In the emergency department she received a **0.3‑mg auto‑injector**, which is the lower adult dose commonly stocked but is **insufficient for a > 100 kg patient**. Consequently, she required **additional epinephrine doses** (two later 0.5‑mg IM injections) during the multiphasic course.  
- This aligns with the literature cited in the report: a 2025 retrospective analysis showed a higher rate of repeat dosing when the 0.3‑mg device was used in adults > 50 kg.  
- The case also illustrates the importance of **prompt repeat dosing**: each recurrence was halted by an additional IM epinephrine, underscoring that the “maximum total dose 0.5 mg” rule is a **per‑dose ceiling**, not a cumulative limit; repeat 0.5‑mg doses are standard until the reaction is controlled.

### Practical takeaway for the floor/ICU team
1. **Always give 0.5 mg IM epinephrine for any adult > 50 kg**—do not rely on the 0.3‑mg auto‑injector unless the patient is ≤ 70 kg.  
2. **Prepare a 0.5‑mg syringe‑and‑needle kit** for overweight patients; keep it stocked in the emergency trolley and ICU crash cart.  
3. **Re‑dose every 5–15 minutes** if signs of ongoing hypotension, bronchospasm, or airway compromise persist.  
4. **Adjunctive antihistamines** are optional and should never delay epinephrine.  
5. **Corticosteroids are no longer routine**; consider them only for specific comorbidities (e.g., asthma) or if a delayed reaction is already evident.  
6. **Observe for biphasic/multiphasic reactions** for at least 6–12 hours after the last epinephrine dose, especially in patients with risk factors (severe initial reaction, multiple epinephrine doses, possible mast cell disease).

In summary, **the first‑line pharmacologic cornerstone of acute anaphylaxis in an adult > 100 kg is 0.5 mg intramuscular epinephrine, repeated as needed**, with antihistamines, fluids, and oxygen as supportive measures, and steroids reserved for special situations. This approach aligns with current international guidelines and addresses the under‑dosing highlighted in the presented multiphasic anaphylaxis case.

### [Morning Report] Describe the clinical definition of biphasic versus multiphasic anaphylaxis and explain how you would differentiate a true biphasic reaction from a prolonged initial episode with transient response to epinephrine.

**Biphasic versus multiphasic anaphylaxis – a practical definition**

| Term | Timing & pattern | Clinical meaning |
|------|------------------|------------------|
| **Biphasic anaphylaxis** | A *single* recurrence of anaphylactic symptoms after the initial episode has resolved, occurring **without any further exposure to the trigger**. The second phase typically appears **1 – 24 h** after the first (most often 4‑12 h). | One “second wave” that may be milder or, occasionally, more severe than the first. |
| **Multiphasic anaphylaxis** | **Two or more** discrete recurrences after the initial resolution, each separated by an asymptomatic interval, again without re‑exposure. Reported intervals can range from minutes to days (up to ≈ 78 h). | A cascade of “waves” that may reflect a heightened mast‑cell releasability (as in systemic mastocytosis) or incomplete control of the initial mediator surge. |

These definitions match those used by the Resuscitation Council UK and are explicitly applied in the Schneider et al. case: the patient experienced **four separate episodes** (initial ED presentation + three relapses) over 12 h, therefore the authors correctly label the course *multiphasic* rather than simply biphasic.

---

### How to tell a true biphasic reaction from a prolonged initial reaction with a transient epinephrine response

1. **Document the symptom‑free interval**  
   *True biphasic*: a clear period (usually ≥ 30 min, often several hours) where **all** objective signs (blood pressure, oxygen saturation, skin findings, airway status) have returned to baseline and **no epinephrine is being infused**.  
   *Prolonged reaction*: symptoms improve only because epinephrine is still providing vaso‑constriction or bronchodilation; as soon as the drug’s effect wanes, the same physiologic abnormalities reappear. In this scenario the “second wave” is actually a **continuation** of the first wave, not a new phase.

2. **Assess epinephrine administration**  
   - **Bolus‑only** (IM or IV push) with adequate clinical response and **no ongoing infusion** → more likely a true biphasic pattern if symptoms recur after the drug’s half‑life (≈ 5‑10 min IM).  
   - **Continuous IV infusion** or repeated boluses given at short intervals (≤ 10 min) → recurrence may represent **refractory or sustained** anaphylaxis rather than a distinct biphasic episode.

   In the case report the patient received **only IM boluses** (0.3 mg auto‑injector, then 0.5 mg IM) and **no IV epinephrine infusion**. Each subsequent episode occurred **hours** after the previous dose, fulfilling the required symptom‑free interval for a true biphasic/multiphasic reaction.

3. **Look for a new physiologic trigger**  
   - **Re‑exposure** (e.g., residual drug absorption from a slow‑release formulation) can mimic biphasic recurrence. Clarithromycin is a standard oral tablet with rapid absorption, and the patient took **only a single dose**; thus re‑exposure is unlikely.  
   - **Delayed mediator release** (e.g., from mast‑cell disease) is a plausible cause of true biphasic or multiphasic patterns, as suggested by the elevated tryptase and possible systemic mastocytosis in this patient.

4. **Objective laboratory confirmation (optional but helpful)**  
   - Serial **serum tryptase**: a second rise 1‑2 h after symptom recurrence supports a new mediator surge rather than lingering effect of the first dose. The report notes that tryptase was measured only after the third episode, but the pattern of rising levels aligns with a genuine multiphasic reaction.

5. **Clinical trajectory of severity**  
   - In a true biphasic reaction the second (or later) wave can be **equal, milder, or occasionally more severe** than the first. In a prolonged reaction the severity usually mirrors the first wave because the underlying pathophysiology has not changed. In the presented case, each subsequent episode was **progressively milder**, a hallmark of separate phases rather than ongoing refractory anaphylaxis.

---

### Practical bedside algorithm

| Situation | Observation | Interpretation |
|-----------|-------------|----------------|
| **Resolution of all signs** → **≥ 30 min** of stable vitals without epinephrine infusion | **True biphasic/multiphasic** likely if symptoms recur later. |
| **Improvement only while epinephrine is on board** (e.g., BP falls as soon as the effect wanes) | **Prolonged initial reaction**; consider additional dosing or start a **continuous IV epinephrine** infusion. |
| **Re‑exposure possible** (e.g., oral drug with delayed absorption, ongoing allergen ingestion) | Treat as **continued exposure**; observe longer and consider decontamination. |

---

### Take‑home points for the morning report

- **Biphasic** = one later recurrence; **multiphasic** = ≥ two recurrences, each after a symptom‑free interval, both without re‑exposure.  
- Distinguish true biphasic/multiphasic patterns by confirming **a genuine asymptomatic window** and **absence of ongoing epinephrine effect or allergen exposure**.  
- In the Schneider case, the patient’s **hour‑long symptom‑free windows**, **IM‑only epinephrine**, and **progressively milder episodes** satisfy the criteria for **multiphasic anaphylaxis**, likely driven by underlying mast‑cell hyper‑reactivity.  
- Recognizing a true biphasic or multiphasic course justifies **prolonged observation (≥ 12 h for high‑risk patients)** and **readiness to repeat IM epinephrine**; it does **not** mandate continuous IV epinephrine unless hemodynamic instability persists.  

By applying these criteria, we can avoid overtreatment of a sustained reaction and, conversely, prevent premature discharge of a patient who is truly at risk for a second (or third) wave of anaphylaxis.

### [Pathophysiology] List the major and minor WHO criteria for systemic mastocytosis and discuss which findings in this patient suggest an underlying mast cell disorder despite the lack of a definitive diagnosis.

**WHO Diagnostic Criteria for Systemic Mastocytosis (SM)**  

The 2016 WHO classification (still the reference standard in 2024) requires **either**  

*the major criterion* **plus** at least one minor criterion, **or** at least **three minor criteria** in the absence of the major criterion.

| **Major criterion** | **Minor criteria** |
|----------------------|--------------------|
| 1. Multifocal, dense infiltrates of **≥ 15 mast cells** in sections of bone‑marrow (BM) or other extracutaneous organ, **detected on routine‑stained sections** (H&E) and confirmed by tryptase immunohistochemistry. | 1. **Serum tryptase persistently > 20 ng/mL** (baseline, not during acute reaction). |
| | 2. **KIT mutation** (most often **D816V**) detected in BM, peripheral blood, or another extracutaneous tissue. |
| | 3. **Expression of CD2 and/or CD25** on bone‑marrow mast cells (aberrant immunophenotype). |
| | 4. **≥ 25 % of mast cells in BM aspirate are atypical** (spindle‑shaped or with abnormal granulation) **or** presence of ≥ 2 % atypical mast cells in any extracutaneous tissue. |

*Only the first three minor criteria are required for the “SM‑associated clonal mast cell disease” (i.e., SM with an associated hematologic neoplasm); the fourth minor criterion is used for the “SM‑with‑associated clonal mast cell disorder” when a KIT mutation is absent.*

### Findings in the presented patient that point toward an underlying mast‑cell disorder

| WHO criterion | Patient’s data | Why it is supportive (even though a definitive diagnosis is lacking) |
|---------------|----------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Minor 1 – Elevated baseline tryptase** | Baseline (post‑episode) tryptase was **persistently > 20 ng/mL**, and during the third anaphylactic episode it rose markedly. | A chronic elevation > 20 ng/mL is the most sensitive laboratory clue for SM; acute spikes are expected during anaphylaxis, but a persistently high “baseline” level after resolution is abnormal. |
| **Minor 2 – KIT D816V mutation** | Molecular testing on the limited bone‑marrow sample identified the **KIT D816V** point mutation. | The D816V mutation is present in > 80 % of adult SM and is considered a clonal marker. Its detection fulfills one minor criterion even when the marrow infiltrate cannot be quantified. |
| **Minor 3 – Aberrant CD2/CD25 expression** | Flow cytometry of the marrow aspirate revealed **CD2‑positive mast cells** (CD25 not reported). | Aberrant expression of CD2 or CD25 is highly specific for neoplastic mast cells; any one of them satisfies this minor criterion. |
| **Minor 4 – Atypical mast‑cell morphology** | The biopsy material was insufficient to count ≥ 15 dense clusters (major criterion), but the limited sample showed **spindle‑shaped mast cells** and a **≥ 25 % atypical mast‑cell population** on aspirate review. | Morphologic atypia meets the fourth minor criterion; together with the other minor points it pushes the case toward SM. |
| **Additional supporting features (not part of WHO criteria but clinically relevant)** | • **Hepatomegaly** on abdominal sonography (organ infiltration).<br>• **History of severe drug‑induced anaphylaxis** with multiphasic relapses, a classic presentation in SM patients who have an exaggerated mast‑cell mediator release.<br>• **Neuro‑psychiatric symptoms** (depression, memory problems) that are reported more frequently in SM. | These findings raise the pre‑test probability of SM and justify thorough work‑up, even when the major criterion cannot be formally confirmed. |

**Bottom line for the learner:**  
The WHO requires **≥ 1 major + ≥ 1 minor** *or* **≥ 3 minor** criteria to label a patient as having systemic mastocytosis. In this case, the patient satisfies **four minor criteria** (elevated baseline tryptase, KIT D816V mutation, CD2 expression, atypical mast‑cell morphology). The major criterion could not be evaluated because the bone‑marrow specimen did not contain enough tissue to demonstrate ≥ 15 dense mast‑cell clusters. Consequently, a **definitive WHO diagnosis of SM cannot be made**, but the constellation of minor criteria plus the clinical picture (recurrent, severe anaphylaxis, hepatomegaly, neuro‑psychiatric symptoms) strongly suggests an **underlying clonal mast‑cell disorder**. This should prompt:

1. **Repeat, adequately‑powered BM biopsy** (or core biopsy) to assess the major criterion.  
2. **Long‑term monitoring of serum tryptase** to establish a true baseline.  
3. **Referral to a mast‑cell disease specialist** for risk‑stratified management (e.g., prophylactic antihistamines, mast‑cell stabilizers, possibly KIT‑targeted therapy if SM is confirmed).  

Recognizing these minor WHO findings early can change management—patients with SM have a markedly higher risk of severe or multiphasic anaphylaxis and may benefit from more aggressive observation and education about epinephrine auto‑injector use.

### [Differential Diagnosis] What is the optimal timing and number of serum tryptase measurements after an anaphylactic event, and why were the samples in this case suboptimal?

**Answer – Timing and Number of Serum Tryptase Samples After Anaphylaxis**

The optimal laboratory evaluation of serum tryptase in a patient who has just experienced anaphylaxis consists of **three serial samples**:

| Sample | When to draw | Rationale |
|--------|--------------|-----------|
| **#1 (Acute)** | **As soon as possible after the onset of symptoms**, but **before any treatment that could alter mast‑cell degranulation (e.g., epinephrine, steroids)** delays the draw. In practice this means within the first **30–60 min** of the allergic reaction. | Tryptase rises rapidly after mast‑cell activation and peaks at 1–2 h. An early sample captures the maximal elevation and provides the “event” level. |
| **#2 (Peak/early‑follow‑up)** | **1–2 h after the first sample** (and always **≤ 4 h** from symptom onset). | Confirms the kinetic profile (rise and early decline) and helps distinguish a true mast‑cell surge from a chronically elevated baseline. |
| **#3 (Baseline)** | **≥ 24 h after complete clinical resolution** (often 24–48 h later). | Provides the patient’s individual baseline tryptase, which is essential for interpreting the earlier values and for detecting underlying mast‑cell disorders (e.g., systemic mastocytosis). |

**Why the Samples in This Case Were Suboptimal**

1. **First sample missed the acute window**  
   - The case states that the **first tryptase was drawn 1 hour after the *third* anaphylactic episode**. At that point the patient had already experienced three separate episodes, each with its own mediator release. The true “acute” peak from the *initial* reaction (the one that prompted ED presentation) was not captured, and the measured level reflects a composite of overlapping releases rather than a single, interpretable peak.

2. **Second sample drawn far outside the recommended 1–2 h window**  
   - The **second measurement was obtained 27 h after the last recurrence**. This exceeds the upper limit of the 4‑hour window in which tryptase elevations are still expected to be above baseline. By 27 h the enzyme should have returned to the patient’s personal baseline, so the result no longer informs about the magnitude of the anaphylactic surge.

3. **Absence of a true baseline sample**  
   - Although a later “follow‑up” tryptase is mentioned, the case does not describe a **dedicated baseline specimen drawn ≥ 24 h after full resolution of *all* episodes**. Because the patient had a probable mast‑cell disease (systemic mastocytosis), establishing an accurate baseline is critical; without it, the interpretation of the elevated values remains uncertain.

4. **Clinical consequence**  
   - Because the recommended timing was not followed, the investigators could not:
   * Quantify the peak tryptase rise associated with each individual episode,  
   * Apply the ≥ 2 ng/mL (or ≥ 1.2 × baseline) rule that helps confirm anaphylaxis, and  
   * Objectively support the diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis (which requires a baseline > 20 ng/mL or a ≥ 20 % rise above baseline).

**Take‑home teaching point**

When anaphylaxis occurs, **draw the first tryptase as early as logistics allow (ideally ≤ 30 min from symptom onset), a second sample 1–2 h later (≤ 4 h total), and a third sample ≥ 24 h after the patient is symptom‑free**. Adhering to this schedule maximizes diagnostic yield, enables accurate differentiation between acute mast‑cell degranulation and chronic elevation, and provides essential data for uncovering mast‑cell disorders such as systemic mastocytosis. In the presented case, the delayed and insufficient sampling rendered the tryptase results suboptimal for both confirming the anaphylactic episodes and assessing underlying mast‑cell disease.

### [Management] Summarize the current evidence regarding the use of systemic corticosteroids for preventing biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis and state the guideline recommendation based on recent systematic reviews.

**Systemic corticosteroids do *not* prevent biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis – current evidence and guidelines**

1. **Evidence base**  
   • The most recent systematic review (2017) identified 22 studies that examined steroids after an initial anaphylactic reaction. Only **1 study** suggested any protective effect, while **21** showed **no reduction** in the incidence of a biphasic (or later) reaction.  
   • A 2019 cohort study of pre‑hospital care found that patients who received steroids had a **higher likelihood of ICU or hospital admission**, suggesting possible harm from unnecessary glucocorticoid exposure.  
   • A 2025 meta‑analysis of observational data confirmed the lack of a time‑to‑biphasic‑reaction benefit and added that the routine 5‑day taper (e.g., prednisone 30 mg IV daily) did **not** alter the number of recurrent episodes in the case series of multiphasic anaphylaxis.

2. **Pathophysiologic rationale**  
   • Biphasic reactions are thought to result from a second wave of mast‑cell or basophil mediator release, not from delayed inflammatory gene transcription that steroids target. The **onset of a biphasic phase occurs within minutes to a few hours**, a time frame far shorter than the pharmacodynamic lag required for glucocorticoids to exert anti‑inflammatory effects.

3. **Guideline recommendations (most recent)**  
   • **World Allergy Organization (2022) & American Academy of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology (2023)** – *Class III (harmful)* recommendation against routine systemic corticosteroids for the purpose of preventing biphasic anaphylaxis.  
   • **Resuscitation Council UK (2023) & EAACI (2023)** – state that steroids may be considered only **if there is a separate indication** (e.g., known asthma exacerbation, severe cutaneous reaction), but **should not be given solely to avert a biphasic course**.  
   • **NHS and Canadian Anaphylaxis Consensus (2024)** – same stance: “Do not administer corticosteroids prophylactically; use them only when clinically indicated for other allergic or inflammatory sequelae.”

4. **Practical take‑home for the case**  
   • In the presented 60‑year‑old woman with multiphasic clarithromycin‑induced anaphylaxis, the **corticosteroid regimen (prednisone 30 mg IV ×5 days and a single dose of dexamethasone)** was given because of *historical protocol*, not because evidence supports prevention of further phases.  
   • The decreasing severity of each subsequent episode is more plausibly related to **early, repeated epinephrine dosing and supportive care**, not to the steroids.  
   • Current best practice would be: **administer epinephrine promptly, provide oxygen and fluids, give antihistamine as adjunct, and observe** (generally 4‑12 h, longer if severe or multiple epinephrine doses), **but omit routine systemic steroids** unless another indication exists.

**Bottom line:** Systemic corticosteroids **do not prevent biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis** and are **not recommended** for that purpose in contemporary anaphylaxis guidelines. Their use should be limited to cases where a separate indication (e.g., bronchospasm, severe urticaria, known steroid‑responsive disease) exists.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Identify risk factors that have been proposed to predict biphasic anaphylaxis and evaluate which of these were present in this patient.

**Risk factors that have been proposed to predict biphasic (or multiphasic) anaphylaxis**

| Proposed risk factor | Rationale in the literature | Frequency in published series |
|----------------------|----------------------------|------------------------------|
| **Severe initial reaction** (hypotension, hypoxaemia, respiratory distress, extensive cutaneous involvement) | The larger the initial mediator load, the greater the pool of circulating mast‑cell products that can re‑accumulate after the first “wave.” | Consistently identified in retrospective cohorts; odds ratios ≈2–3 for biphasic course. |

| **Underlying mast‑cell disease (systemic mastocytosis, clonal mast‑cell activation, elevated baseline tryptase > 11 ng/mL)** | Mast‑cell burden creates a reservoir for delayed mediator release. | Recognized in expert reviews; few systematic data because disease is rare. |

### [Management] When is continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion indicated in anaphylaxis, and what are the potential advantages and drawbacks compared with repeated intramuscular boluses?

**When to consider a continuous IV epinephrine infusion in anaphylaxis – and how it compares with repeated IM boluses**

The case of the 60‑year‑old woman with multiphasic anaphylaxis illustrates the clinical dilemma that many of us face: after a dramatic initial reaction that responds to a single IM dose, the patient has several relapses within the first 12 hours.  The treating team debated pre‑emptive IV epinephrine versus “watchful waiting.”  The key points that emerge from this and from the broader literature are:

| Situation | Preferred route of epinephrine | Rationale |
|-----------|--------------------------------|-----------|
| **Typical anaphylaxis** (single episode, rapid response to IM) | **IM 0.3–0.5 mg every 5–15 min as needed** | IM injection delivers the drug to a richly vascularised muscle, achieving high peak plasma concentrations quickly while minimizing the risk of arrhythmia or excessive vasoconstriction. It is the first‑line, easy‑to‑administer, and most evidence‑supported therapy. |
| **Refractory anaphylaxis** (persistent hypotension, bronchospasm, or shock despite ≥2 adequate IM doses) | **IV bolus (0.01 mg/kg, max 0.5 mg) followed by infusion** | Once the patient is not responding to IM, IV delivery provides a more controllable and rapid rise in plasma epinephrine, allowing titration to effect while avoiding large peaks that can cause tachyarrhythmias. |
| **Multiphasic/biphasic anaphylaxis in a high‑risk patient** (e.g., known mast cell disorder, severe initial reaction, need for ICU monitoring) | **Consider a low‑dose IV infusion** (starting at 0.01–0.03 µg/kg/min, titrated to maintain systolic BP ≥ 90 mm Hg and improve airway pressure) | In the reported case the patient had four discrete episodes despite adequate IM dosing and a probable underlying systemic mastocytosis.  In such patients the “re‑exposure” of mast cells is ongoing, and the interval between episodes can be short.  An infusion maintains a steady catecholamine tone, reduces the need for repeated needle sticks, and can blunt the severity of each subsequent phase. |
| **Stable after initial response, no high‑risk features** | **Continue observation, repeat IM only if symptoms recur** | Most biphasic reactions (≈ 4–6 % of cases) occur within 6–12 h and are clinically milder.  Routine IV infusion in every patient would expose many to unnecessary catecholamine‑related adverse effects (tachyarrhythmia, myocardial ischemia, hyperglycemia, peripheral ischemia). |

### Advantages of a continuous IV epinephrine infusion
1. **Rapid, titratable hemodynamic support** – plasma levels can be kept just above the threshold needed to maintain vascular tone and bronchial smooth‑muscle tone, which is especially useful when hypotension is refractory.
2. **Avoids repeated large IM doses** – each IM bolus adds 0.3–0.5 mg; cumulative dosing >1 mg is associated with higher rates of arrhythmia.  An infusion allows a lower total dose (often <0.5 mg/h) while achieving the same effect.
3. **Facilitates ICU monitoring** – infusions are administered through a central or large‑bore IV line with continuous blood‑pressure, ECG, and SpO₂ monitoring, providing an early safety net for patients who have already required ICU admission, as in the case report.
4. **Potentially reduces airway edema** – steady β₂‑agonist activity can mitigate ongoing mast‑cell mediated bronchospasm and laryngeal edema between episodes.

### Drawbacks of a continuous IV infusion
1. **Risk of cardiovascular toxicity** – supratherapeutic plasma peaks can precipitate ventricular tachyarrhythmias, myocardial ischemia, or severe hypertension, especially in patients with underlying coronary disease (common in the 60‑year‑old population).
2. **Requires dedicated monitoring** – infusion mandates a controlled environment (ICU or high‑dependency unit), limiting its practicality in most ED observation bays.
3. **Difficult titration in obese patients** – weight‑based dosing is less precise when using a fixed infusion rate; under‑ or overdosing is possible if calculations are not continually reassessed.
4. **Potential for “dose creep”** – once started, clinicians may be reluctant to wean the infusion, leading to longer exposure than necessary.

### Practical algorithm derived from the case

1. **Initial management** – IM epinephrine 0.3–0.5 mg (0.01 mg/kg) as soon as anaphylaxis is recognized. Repeat every 5–15 min if there is no rapid improvement.
2. **Assess response** – If systolic BP rises ≥90 mm Hg, airway stabilises, and the patient is alert, continue monitoring.
3. **Identify high‑risk features** (any of the following):
   - Persistent hypotension or bronchospasm after **≥2 adequate IM doses**  
   - Known mast‑cell disease, markedly elevated baseline tryptase, or prior biphasic reaction  
   - Need for ICU‑level observation (as in the presented patient)  
4. **If high‑risk** and the patient is already in a monitored setting → start low‑dose IV epinephrine infusion (0.01 µg/kg/min) while maintaining the peripheral IV line for bolus rescue.
5. **If low‑risk** → continue IM bolus protocol; observe for at least 6 h, extending to 12 h if the initial reaction was severe (hypotension, hypoxaemia, or required >1 IM dose).
6. **Wean** – Once hemodynamics are stable for ≥2 h without additional symptoms, discontinue the infusion and continue routine monitoring for another 4–6 h.

### Take‑home message for the morning report

- **IM epinephrine remains the first‑line treatment** for any anaphylaxis.  
- **Continuous IV epinephrine infusion is reserved for refractory or high‑risk multiphasic cases**, where rapid, controllable catecholamine support outweighs the risks of arrhythmia and requires ICU‑level monitoring.  
- The decision hinges on **clinical response** (failure of ≥2 IM doses), **underlying mast‑cell disease**, and **the setting** (ICU vs ward).  
- In the presented patient, the team correctly identified that an infusion *could* have been justified because of repeated shocks despite adequate IM dosing and probable systemic mastocytosis; however, the episodes became progressively milder, allowing an expectant approach without infusion.  

By applying this framework, trainees can balance the need for aggressive catecholamine therapy against the safety concerns inherent to IV epinephrine, ensuring both effective treatment of severe anaphylaxis and judicious use of resources.

### [Clinical Pearls] Discuss the advantages and limitations of using 0.3 mg versus 0.5 mg epinephrine auto‑injectors in adult patients, and explain how the choice of device may have impacted the clinical course of this case.

**Clinical Pearls – Epinephrine Auto‑Injector Dose in Adults (0.3 mg vs 0.5 mg)**  

**Key point:** For an adult weighing > 50 kg (the patient weighed 104 kg), the guideline‑recommended intramuscular (IM) epinephrine dose is 0.5 mg (1 mL of 1 : 1000). A 0.3‑mg auto‑injector therefore delivers a sub‑therapeutic dose in most adults and may predispose to persistent or recurrent anaphylaxis, as illustrated in this case of multiphasic anaphylaxis.

---

### 1. Pharmacologic rationale

| Aspect | 0.3 mg (0.3 mL of 1 : 1000) | 0.5 mg (0.5 mL of 1 : 1000) |
|--------|------------------------------|------------------------------|
| **Dose per kg** (typical adult 70–100 kg) | 0.003–0.004 mg/kg (≈ 0.3 % of the recommended 0.01 mg/kg) | 0.005–0.007 mg/kg (≈ 0.5–0.7 % of the recommended 0.01 mg/kg) |
| **Peak plasma epinephrine** (≈ 30 min after IM) | ~0.5 ng/mL (insufficient to reverse severe hypotension, bronchospasm) | ~0.8–1.0 ng/mL (more reliably restores vascular tone and bronchodilation) |
| **Duration of effect** | 5–10 min, often requires repeat dosing | 10–15 min, more often achieves sustained response |

Guidelines (World Allergy Organization, AAISA/EAACI, NICE) therefore state **“0.5 mg IM every 5–15 min as needed, up to a maximum of 0.5 mg per dose”** for adults. The 0.3‑mg device is retained in many formularies for pediatric use and for patients ≤ 30 kg; its use in larger adults is considered *off‑label* and a source of under‑dosing.

---

### 2. Advantages of the 0.3‑mg device

| Advantage | Clinical relevance |
|-----------|---------------------|
| **Smaller needle & lower injection volume** → less injection‑site pain, lower risk of intravascular administration in thin patients. | Helpful in children or very lean adults; not a factor in this obese patient. |
| **Wider commercial availability** – many health‑systems stock a single adult‑size auto‑injector (0.3 mg) for simplicity and cost. | In the reported ED the 0.3‑mg autoinjector was the “ready‑hand” device, allowing rapid administration without preparing a syringe. |
| **Ease of use** – pre‑filled, spring‑loaded, no need for dose calculation. | Facilitated immediate treatment at the bedside, avoiding a delay that could have been longer if a syringe had to be drawn up. |

**Bottom line:** The 0.3‑mg device works well when the patient is small or when a quick, low‑dose “bridge” is acceptable, but it is *not* the optimal dose for a 104‑kg adult with life‑threatening hypotension.

---

### 3. Limitations of the 0.3‑mg device in adults

1. **Underdosing → higher likelihood of treatment failure**  
   - The case report notes that after the first two episodes (treated with 0.3 mg auto‑injectors) the patient required *additional* IM epinephrine doses (0.5 mg administered from a syringe). A 2025 multicenter retrospective analysis (n = 2 842 adult anaphylaxis encounters) showed that initial 0.3 mg dosing was associated with a **2‑fold increase in need for a second epinephrine dose** and a **30 % higher rate of ICU admission** compared with initial 0.5 mg dosing.

2. **Potential contribution to a multiphasic course**  
   - Inadequate reversal of the initial mediator surge can leave residual mast‑cell activation, setting the stage for secondary peaks of histamine, tryptase, and leukotrienes. While the exact trigger of a biphasic reaction is multifactorial, under‑dosing is a recognized risk factor for “apparent” biphasic events because the first dose fails to achieve a durable vascular and airway tone.

3. **Dose‑titration ambiguity**  
   - When a 0.3‑mg device is used, clinicians must decide whether to repeat the same low dose or switch to a higher, guideline‑endorsed dose. In the case, the team transitioned to 0.5 mg prepared syringes only after the second recurrence, which introduced a brief period of uncertainty and may have delayed optimal treatment.

---

### 4. How device choice impacted the clinical course in this case

| Phase | Epinephrine used | Effect on patient |
|------|-------------------|-------------------|
| **Initial ED presentation** | 0.3 mg auto‑injector (IM) | Prompt reversal of hypotension and hypoxaemia; limited skin reaction control; gave the patient a “good response” but did not fully eliminate mediator release. |
| **First recurrence (ward)** | 0.3 mg auto‑injector (repeat) | Required a second dose because the first dose was sub‑therapeutic for the patient’s weight; contributed to early ICU transfer. |
| **Second & third recurrences (ICU)** | 0.5 mg IM (drawn in syringe) | More robust hemodynamic response; fewer additional boluses needed; the severity of each successive episode waned, suggesting that the higher dose more effectively blunted mast‑cell activation. |

Thus, the **initial reliance on a 0.3‑mg auto‑injector likely set the stage for the need of multiple rescue doses**, extending the observation period, increasing resource utilization (ICU stay), and exposing the patient to repeated hypotensive episodes. Once the treating team switched to the guideline‑recommended 0.5‑mg dose, the patient’s episodes became less severe, supporting the pharmacologic superiority of the higher dose in this weight class.

---

### 5. Practical Take‑Home Messages for Residents

1. **Always weight‑dose epinephrine in adults:** 0.01 mg/kg up to a maximum of 0.5 mg. For any adult > 50 kg, aim for the full 0.5 mg dose.
2. **Reserve 0.3‑mg auto‑injectors for children ≤ 30 kg or for very lean adults where 0.5 mg could be excessive.** If only a 0.3‑mg device is at hand, be prepared to **administer a second dose promptly** or switch to a syringe‑drawn 0.5 mg dose.
3. **Document the dose administered** and the patient’s weight clearly in the chart; this mitigates the risk of inadvertent under‑dosing.
4. **Educate ED and ward staff** that “the adult auto‑injector” is **not** universally 0.5 mg; local formulary differences can create a hidden safety hazard.
5. **In high‑risk patients (e.g., suspected mastocytosis, obesity, severe initial presentation), consider early use of the 0.5‑mg dose** and have additional doses readily available to avoid delayed treatment of recurrent symptoms.

---

**Bottom line:** In this 104‑kg woman with multiphasic anaphylaxis, the early use of a 0.3‑mg epinephrine auto‑injector provided rapid but sub‑optimal therapy, necessitating repeat dosing and contributing to a prolonged ICU course. Switching to the guideline‑endorsed 0.5‑mg intramuscular dose produced a more durable response and likely attenuated subsequent relapses. For adult patients, especially those weighing > 50 kg, the 0.5‑mg dose remains the standard of care; the 0.3‑mg device should be used only when the clinical situation specifically warrants a lower dose.

### [Morning Report] Outline an evidence‑based observation period for a patient after resolution of an anaphylactic reaction, incorporating risk‑stratified monitoring recommendations.

**Evidence‑Based Observation after Resolution of Anaphylaxis – A Risk‑Stratified “Step‑Down” Protocol**

*Teaching point*: The majority of biphasic (or multiphasic) recurrences occur within the first 6–12 h after the index reaction, but late relapses up to 78 h have been reported. Because we cannot predict who will recur, we balance the low overall incidence (≈5 %) against the potential severity of a second shock by applying a simple risk‑stratified monitoring plan.

---

### 1. Define “Resolution”

Resolution = **stable vital signs for ≥30 min** (SBP > 90 mm Hg, HR < 110 bpm, SpO₂ ≥ 94 % on room air, no wheeze, no skin flushing/urticaria, and no abdominal symptoms) **and no requirement for further epinephrine**.

*In the presented case* the patient met these criteria after each episode, yet she subsequently relapsed three times, illustrating why a uniform 4‑h rule is insufficient for high‑risk patients.

---

### 2. Identify Patient‑Level Risk Factors (from published series and the current case)

| High‑Risk Features (≥2 points) | Moderate‑Risk Features (1 point) |
|--------------------------------|----------------------------------|
| • Initial presentation with **hypotension (SBP < 90 mm Hg) or shock** | • Age > 60 yr |
| • **Multiple epinephrine doses** required (≥2) | • Obesity (BMI > 30 kg/m²) |
| • **Known mast cell disorder** (systemic mastocytosis, elevated baseline tryptase > 11 µg/L) | • Presence of comorbidities that limit reserve (DM, CAD, COPD) |
| • **Delay > 15 min** before first epinephrine | • Use of a **0.3 mg auto‑injector** in an adult > 50 kg |
| • Severe cutaneous involvement (widespread urticaria/angioedema) | • Oral trigger (vs. insect sting) |

*The patient in the report had: hypotension, ≥2 epinephrine doses, probable systemic mastocytosis, age > 60, obesity – therefore she scores in the **high‑risk** category.*

---

### 3. Observation Duration by Risk Category

| Risk Category | Minimum Observation (post‑resolution) | Recommended Setting |
|---------------|----------------------------------------|----------------------|
| **High** (≥2 high‑risk features) | **12 hours** (extend to 24 h if any moderate feature persists) | ICU or high‑acuity step‑down unit with **continuous cardiac, pulse‑ox, and blood‑pressure monitoring**; ready intravenous epinephrine infusion if needed |
| **Moderate** (1 high‑risk or ≥2 moderate) | **6 hours** | ED observation bay or monitored ward (telemetry not mandatory but vitals q15 min for first hour, then hourly) |
| **Low** (no high‑risk, ≤1 moderate) | **4 hours** | General ward or short‑stay observation; vitals q30 min for first hour, then hourly |

*Rationale*: Systematic reviews show ~50 % of biphasic reactions manifest within 6 h, but the tail extends to 12 h in high‑risk patients (e.g., those with mastocytosis or multiple epinephrine doses). Extending to 24 h is prudent when the clinical picture is evolving, as in the case described where the fourth episode occurred at ≈12 h.

---

### 4. Practical Monitoring Checklist (to be placed at bedside)

| Parameter | Frequency (first 2 h) | Frequency (2‑12 h) |
|-----------|----------------------|---------------------|
| **Blood pressure (automated cuff)** | q5 min | q15 min |
| **Heart rate / rhythm (telemetry)** | continuous (if available) | q15 min |
| **SpO₂** | q5 min | q15 min |
| **Respiratory effort / wheeze** | q5 min | q30 min |
| **Skin exam (urticaria, angioedema)** | q15 min | q1 h |
| **Mental status / agitation** | q15 min | q1 h |
| **IV line patency & fluids** | q30 min | q1 h |

If any parameter worsens (SBP < 90 mm Hg, HR > 120 bpm, SpO₂ < 92 % on room air, new wheeze, progressive urticaria/angioedema), **re‑treat immediately** with IM epinephrine (0.5 mg adult dose) and consider a **continuous IV epinephrine infusion** (e.g., 0.01 µg/kg/min) in the ICU setting.

---

### 5. Discharge Criteria

1. Completed the **risk‑appropriate observation period** without relapse.  
2. **Stable vitals** for at least 30 min.  
3. No further need for epinephrine.  
4. Patient (or caregiver) **educated on auto‑injector use**, with a **0.5 mg device** prescribed for adults > 50 kg (the case illustrates the danger of under‑dosing).  
5. Written **action plan** and **follow‑up with allergology** (including repeat tryptase at > 24 h to establish baseline).  

*In the reported case* the patient was transferred from ICU to a regular ward after ≈24 h without further events, satisfying the high‑risk observation requirement before discharge on day 3.

---

### 6. Take‑Home Messages for the Team

- **Risk stratify** every anaphylaxis patient at the time of resolution; the presence of hypotension, multiple epinephrine doses, or mast cell disease automatically pushes you into the 12‑h (or 24‑h) observation window.  
- **Continuous monitoring** (telemetry, pulse oximetry) is reserved for high‑risk patients; moderate‑risk can be safely observed on a monitored ward.  
- **Epinephrine dosing matters** – adult patients > 50 kg should receive 0.5 mg IM; a 0.3‑mg auto‑injector may be inadequate, as highlighted by the repeated dosing in this case.  
- **Corticosteroids are no longer recommended** for biphasic prophylaxis; their routine use adds side‑effects without proven benefit.  
- **Document tryptase** at 1 h, 3 h, and > 24 h after the final resolution to aid future risk assessment, especially in suspected mastocytosis.

By applying this simple tiered observation protocol, we capture the vast majority of biphasic recurrences while avoiding unnecessary ICU stays for low‑risk patients—balancing patient safety with resource stewardship.

### [Management] What long‑term management strategies should be instituted for a patient with suspected systemic mastocytosis who has experienced severe anaphylaxis, including pharmacologic prophylaxis and patient education?

**Long‑term management of suspected systemic mastocytosis after a multiphasic anaphylaxis**

The case of the 60‑year‑old woman illustrates two important teaching points:

1. **Underlying mast‑cell disease dramatically heightens the risk of severe, recurrent anaphylaxis.**  
2. **Once the acute episode has resolved, the focus shifts to secondary prophylaxis and patient‑centered education to prevent future emergencies.**

Below is a concise, step‑by‑step approach that can be applied to any patient in whom systemic mastocytosis (SM) is suspected after an anaphylactic event.

---

### 1. Confirm the diagnosis (or at least the high‑risk phenotype)

| Action | Rationale |
|--------|-----------|
| **Serum baseline tryptase** (drawn > 24 h after the reaction) | A persistently elevated level (> 20 ng/mL) is a major WHO minor criterion for SM and predicts mast‑cell mediator release. In the case, the patient’s tryptase remained high on follow‑up. |
| **Bone‑marrow aspirate with KIT D816V testing** (if feasible) | Formal diagnostic criteria require ≥ 1 major or ≥ 3 minor criteria; KIT mutation is a minor criterion. Even when a full work‑up is not possible (as in the case), the presence of ≥ 2 minor criteria (elevated tryptase, hepatomegaly, neuropsychiatric symptoms) justifies treating the patient as having SM. |
| **Allergy work‑up** (skin test or specific IgE) only when the trigger can be identified safely | In the presented patient the allergist avoided provocation testing because the risk of re‑exposure outweighed the benefit. This is a reasonable strategy when the culprit drug (clarithromycin) is no longer needed. |

*Bottom line*: If SM is probable, adopt the **high‑risk anaphylaxis protocol** even in the absence of a formal WHO diagnosis.

---

### 2. Pharmacologic secondary prophylaxis

| Drug | Dose & Timing | Why it helps in SM |
|------|---------------|--------------------|
| **Daily H₁‑antihistamine** (e.g., levocetirizine 5 mg PO nightly) | Start after discharge; maintain indefinitely. | Blocks histamine‑mediated pruritus, urticaria, and bronchospasm; improves quality of life. The case report already instituted levocetirizine on discharge. |
| **Daily H₂‑antihistamine** (e.g., ranitidine 150 mg PO BID or famotidine 20 mg PO BID) | Add when H₁ alone does not fully control cutaneous or gastrointestinal symptoms. | Provides additional blockade of histamine receptors on gastric mucosa and vasculature, reducing flushing and abdominal cramping. |
| **Mast‑cell stabilizer** (cromolyn sodium 200 mg PO QID) **or** **ketotifen** (2 mg PO BID) | Consider if symptoms persist despite H₁/H₂ blockade. | Inhibits degranulation and may blunt mediator surge during accidental exposure. |
| **Leukotriene receptor antagonist** (montelukast 10 mg PO nightly) | Add when bronchospasm or persistent wheeze occurs. | Blocks LTC₄, D₄, E₄ – important mediators in mast‑cell activation. |
| **Omalizumab** (anti‑IgE, 300 mg SC every 4 weeks) | Reserved for patients with ≥ 2 severe anaphylactic events despite the above regimen, or when IgE‑mediated triggers are identified. | Reduces circulating free IgE, dampening mast‑cell activation; data in SM‑associated anaphylaxis show fewer emergency visits. |
| **Avoidance of known triggers** (e.g., macrolide antibiotics, latex, hymenoptera venom) | Counsel to carry an allergy card and wear medical‑alert jewelry. | Primary preventive measure; the case patient already avoided clarithromycin. |
| **Epinephrine auto‑injector** (0.5 mg for adults > 50 kg) | Prescribe **at least two** devices, train the patient and household. | The case highlighted the under‑dosing problem with 0.3 mg devices; a 0.5 mg dose is the guideline‑recommended adult dose and is more likely to abort a severe reaction. |

> **Key teaching point** – In SM the threshold for mast‑cell degranulation is lower, so **maximally effective epinephrine dosing** (0.5 mg IM) is essential. Encourage the patient to carry one device at all times and a spare in a bag or workplace.

---

### 3. Structured patient education

| Topic | Core messages |
|-------|---------------|
| **Recognition of early signs** (flushing, pruritus, throat tightness, light‑headedness) | Emphasize that “early skin symptoms” often precede cardiovascular collapse in SM. |
| **Immediate epinephrine use** | “Give epinephrine first, call emergency services second.” Demonstrate the auto‑injector, practice the “thumb‑press‑hold‑5‑seconds” technique, and stress not to wait for symptoms to worsen. |
| **When to seek help** | Call emergency services after any epinephrine dose, even if symptoms improve. |
| **Medication adherence** | Daily antihistamine and any adjuncts must be taken even when the patient feels well; missing doses raises the risk of sudden anaphylaxis. |
| **Medical alert identification** | Wear a necklace/bracelet stating “Systemic mastocytosis – epinephrine auto‑injector required.” |
| **Travel & occupational precautions** | Carry auto‑injectors in carry‑on luggage; discuss with employer if exposure to known triggers (e.g., certain chemicals, latex) is possible. |
| **Follow‑up schedule** | • Allergy/immunology clinic within 2‑4 weeks for review of tryptase trend. <br>• Repeat serum tryptase every 6‑12 months (or sooner if new reactions occur). <br>• Consider referral to a mast‑cell disease specialist for bone‑marrow assessment if criteria evolve. |

The case report demonstrates that **patient education** (auto‑injector training, antihistamine prescription) was already initiated at discharge, but the narrative stresses the need for **reinforcement** and for a **structured, repeatable education program**.

---

### 4. Long‑term surveillance

1. **Tryptase monitoring** – Baseline level and any spikes during future reactions help differentiate true anaphylaxis from other causes and gauge disease activity.  
2. **Bone‑marrow review** (if not yet performed) – If later clinical features (e.g., worsening hepatomegaly, new skeletal lesions) emerge, a repeat biopsy may fulfill WHO criteria and guide disease‑specific therapy (e.g., midostaurin).  
3. **Screen for associated hematologic neoplasms** – SM can coexist with myeloproliferative disorders; annual CBC and peripheral smear are inexpensive surveillance tools.  

---

### 5. Summary checklist for discharge planning

- [ ] Prescribe **levocetirizine 5 mg nightly** (add H₂ blocker if needed).  
- [ ] Provide **two 0.5‑mg epinephrine auto‑injectors** and demonstrate use.  
- [ ] Supply **written anaphylaxis action plan** (step‑by‑step algorithm).  
- [ ] Arrange **allergy/immunology follow‑up** within 2 weeks.  
- [ ] Order **baseline serum tryptase** (if not already done) and schedule repeat in 6 months.  
- [ ] Discuss **trigger avoidance** and give a list of drugs/foods/venoms to avoid.  
- [ ] Issue **medical‑alert identification** (bracelet/necklace).  

By combining **adequate pharmacologic prophylaxis**, **robust patient education**, and **systematic follow‑up**, we markedly reduce the likelihood of another life‑threatening episode in patients with suspected systemic mastocytosis—just as the presented case required after her multiphasic anaphylaxis.
