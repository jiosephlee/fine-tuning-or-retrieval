Chapter 4: ## Management of Multiphasic Anaphylaxis: Strategies, Controversies, and Decision Points  

Anaphylaxis classically presents as a single, rapidly evolving episode, but a proportion of patients experience a **biphasic** or **multiphasic** course, in which symptom resolution is followed by one or more recurrences without re‑exposure to the trigger. Understanding the temporal pattern, recognizing the signs of each relapse, and applying evidence‑based therapeutic choices are essential for preventing deterioration and for allocating appropriate resources (ward versus intensive‑care unit). The present case of a woman in her 60 s with clarithromycin‑induced anaphylaxis illustrates the practical challenges of managing repeated reactions and highlights the points at which clinicians must decide between competing management options.

### Biphasic versus Multiphasic Anaphylaxis  

- **Biphasic anaphylaxis** refers to a single recurrence of symptoms after the initial episode has resolved. Most recurrences occur within the first 6–12 hours, although reports document a range of 0.5 hours to 78 hours.  
- **Multiphasic anaphylaxis** describes two or more distinct recurrences after the index reaction. In the current report the patient experienced **four** episodes within a 12‑hour window, fitting the definition of multiphasic disease. The term implicitly conveys that each episode is separated by a symptom‑free interval and that the patient has returned to a baseline state (e.g., normotensive, non‑hypoxic) before the next flare.  

Recognition of this pattern is critical because management algorithms for a single episode do not fully address the risk of further decompensation, the potential need for higher‑dose epinephrine, or the decision to intensify monitoring.

### Recurrence Review in the Presented Patient  

| Episode | Timing (post‑clarithromycin) | Epinephrine dose & route | Clinical response | Trend in severity |
|--------|------------------------------|--------------------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| 1 (ED) | 1 h | 0.3 mg IM (auto‑injector) | Immediate rise in BP, resolution of flushing, oxygen weaned | Severe: hypotension 74/50 mm Hg, SpO₂ 88 % |
| 2 (ward) | ~4 h | 0.3 mg IM (auto‑injector) | BP restored, skin findings improved | Moderate: dyspnoea, cyanosis, repeat hypotension |
| 3 (ICU) | ~7 h | 0.5 mg IM (prepared syringe) | Prompt hemodynamic stabilization | Mild‑moderate: dyspnoea, urticaria, pruritus |
| 4 (ICU) | ~11 h | 0.5 mg IM (prepared syringe) | No further escalation required | Mild: rash recurrence, no hypotension |

The dosing escalation from 0.3 mg to 0.5 mg reflects a pragmatic response to the patient’s weight (104 kg) and the diminishing efficacy of the lower‐dose auto‑injector. Moreover, the *clinical trajectory*—from profound circulatory collapse to progressively milder cutaneous and respiratory symptoms—suggests that each subsequent epinephrine dose achieved sufficient catecholaminergic effect while the underlying mast‑cell activation may have waned.

### Repeat Intramuscular Epinephrine versus Intravenous Infusion  

#### Evidence for Repeat IM Dosing  

Guidelines (e.g., World Allergy Organization, European Academy of Allergy and Clinical Immunology) recommend **prompt intramuscular (IM) epinephrine** as first‑line therapy, with repeat IM doses every 5–15 minutes for persistent or recurrent symptoms. The evidence supporting this approach comes primarily from observational data and pharmacologic reasoning: IM delivery into the vastus lateralis achieves rapid peak plasma concentrations while minimizing cardiovascular toxicity. In the present case, the patient’s hemodynamics responded swiftly to each repeat IM dose, supporting the adequacy of this route for episodic recurrences.

#### When IV Epinephrine is Considered  

Intravenous (IV) epinephrine infusion is reserved for **refractory anaphylaxis**—defined as ongoing hypotension, bronchospasm, or airway compromise despite at least two appropriate IM doses. The literature consists of case series and small cohort studies demonstrating faster hemodynamic recovery but also a higher incidence of arrhythmias and extravasation injuries, especially when administered as a rapid bolus rather than a titrated infusion. In multiphasic scenarios, the decision hinges on whether the relapse appears *refractory* (i.e., no appreciable response to IM epinephrine) or simply *recurrent* (adequate response after repeat IM dosing).  

In this patient, each recurrence was terminated with a single IM dose, obviating the need for IV infusion. However, the **ICU team considered a prophylactic IV infusion** after the second relapse because of the uncertainty surrounding future episodes. The risk‑benefit analysis (potential for severe hypotension versus adverse cardiac effects) ultimately favored continued observation with ready‑to‑administer IM epinephrine rather than a continuous infusion.

#### Practical Take‑Home Points  

- **Repeat IM epinephrine** remains the cornerstone for each new anaphylactic episode, even when episodes are multiple.  
- **IV infusion** should be reserved for true refractory reactions (no response after ≥2 IM doses) or for patients with life‑threatening airway obstruction where immediate, titratable catecholamine support is needed.  
- When choosing the IM dose, weight‑based calculations (0.01 mg/kg, max 0.5 mg) are preferred; many auto‑injectors contain only 0.3 mg, which may underdose patients >50 kg. Consider using a pre‑drawn 0.5 mg syringe in such individuals, as was done after the second recurrence.

### Corticosteroids: Historical Rationale and Contemporary Controversy  

**Historical use** of systemic corticosteroids in anaphylaxis arose from the belief that they could blunt late‑phase mediator release and thus prevent biphasic reactions. Empiric protocols often included a single dose of 1 mg/kg prednisone (or equivalent) administered intravenously after the initial stabilization.

**Recent evidence** has challenged this practice. A 2017 systematic review of 22 studies found **no reduction** in biphasic reaction rates with corticosteroid use, and a 2019 cohort demonstrated **increased ICU admission** among patients who received pre‑hospital steroids, likely reflecting confounding by severity. Consequently, most contemporary guidelines (e.g., WAO 2020, UK Resuscitation Council) advise *against routine corticosteroid administration* for the sole purpose of preventing a biphasic episode, although steroids remain acceptable for co‑existing asthma exacerbations or other indications.

**Application to the case**  
- After the first recurrence, the patient received **30 mg prednisone IV daily for five days** and a **single 8 mg dose of dexamethasone** because the local protocol mandated steroids for biphasic anaphylaxis.  
- Given the lack of robust benefit and the potential for hyperglycemia (especially relevant in a diabetic patient), the steroid course may have been unnecessary.  
- The clinical course—progressively milder symptoms despite continued steroid exposure—does not allow a causal inference, but the case underscores the need to **individualize steroid use**, reserving it for patients with a clear alternate indication (e.g., severe asthma, known mast‑cell disease with organ involvement).

### Observation Period and Level of Care  

#### Duration of Monitoring  

- **Traditional recommendation**: 4–6 hours of observation after symptom resolution.  
- **Risk‑stratified models** (e.g., UK 2021 guideline) suggest extending observation to **12 hours** for patients with severe initial presentations, multiple epinephrine doses, or delayed epinephrine administration.  
- In the present case, the patient experienced **four episodes within 12 hours**, justifying an **ICU stay** for at least 24 hours until no further relapse occurred.

#### Ward versus ICU Placement  

Factors favoring ICU admission include:  
1. **Hemodynamic instability** despite fluid resuscitation.  
2. **Repeated need for epinephrine** (≥2 doses).  
3. **High‑risk comorbidities** (obesity, diabetes, potential mast‑cell disorder).  
4. **Uncertainty about airway protection** (recurrent hypoxaemia, cyanosis).  

Conversely, once the patient remains **stable for ≥4 hours** after the last episode, with **no further epinephrine requirement** and **normalized vitals**, transfer to a monitored ward is reasonable. The case illustrates a stepwise escalation—from acute internal ward to ICU after the second relapse—guided by the **clinical trajectory** rather than a rigid time‑based rule.

### Practical Algorithm for Clinicians Facing a Second or Third Reaction  

1. **Rapid Assessment** – Re‑measure vitals, airway patency, skin findings, and capillary glucose.  
2. **Administer IM Epinephrine** – Use weight‑adjusted 0.5 mg for patients >50 kg; repeat every 5–10 minutes if symptoms persist.  
3. **Supportive Measures** – Oxygen, high‑flow IV crystalloids, antihistamines, and bronchodilators as indicated.  
4. **Consider IV Epinephrine** only if:  
   - No response after ≥2 IM doses, **or**  
   - Ongoing severe hypotension/bronchospasm requiring continuous catecholamine support.  
5. **Consult Allergy/Immunology Early** – To evaluate for underlying mast‑cell disease (serum tryptase, bone‑marrow biopsy) and to advise on long‑term prophylaxis.  
6. **Re‑evaluate Corticosteroid Need** – Reserve for concomitant asthma, COPD exacerbation, or documented adrenal insufficiency.  
7. **Determine Monitoring Level** – ICU for refractory or repeatedly relapsing cases; otherwise, a high‑dependency or telemetry‑equipped ward with 12‑hour observation.  
8. **Document Tryptase Timings** – Obtain a sample as soon as possible after each episode (ideally within 1–2 hours) and a baseline ≥24 hours after full resolution.

### Patient‑Centered Discharge Planning  

- **Epinephrine Auto‑Injector Prescription** – Provide a **0.5 mg device** (or a weight‑adjusted single‑dose syringe) together with a written **emergency action plan**.  
- **Antihistamine Maintenance** – Initiate a non‑sedating H₁‑antagonist (e.g., levocetirizine 5 mg daily) to reduce chronic urticaria and possibly blunt mild mast‑cell activation.  
- **Education** – Emphasize avoidance of macrolides (clarithromycin) and any future β‑lactam cross‑reactivity, review the signs of anaphylaxis, and demonstrate proper auto‑injector usage.  
- **Follow‑Up** – Arrange outpatient allergology review for **serum tryptase**, possible **bone‑marrow evaluation**, and **risk stratification for future anaphylaxis** (including consideration of mast‑cell stabilizers such as ketotifen if systemic mastocytosis is later confirmed).  
- **Documentation** – Ensure the electronic health record clearly flags the drug allergy and the need for epinephrine on every future encounter.

### Summary  

Multiphasic anaphylaxis challenges clinicians to move beyond the single‑dose epinephrine protocol and to tailor monitoring and therapeutic intensity to a dynamic clinical picture. In the described patient, **repeat IM epinephrine at an appropriate weight‑based dose** successfully terminated each episode, while **IV infusion was avoided** because each relapse remained responsive. The routine use of **systemic corticosteroids** is not supported by current evidence and should be limited to specific co‑existing conditions. A **risk‑stratified observation period**—often extending to 12 hours or longer for severe or recurrent cases—and judicious ICU admission can safeguard against missed deterioration. Early allergy specialist involvement, precise tryptase sampling, and comprehensive discharge planning (adequate auto‑injector, patient education, and specialist follow‑up) complete the management continuum for patients experiencing multiphasic anaphylaxis.
