Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Differential Diagnosis] Identify risk factors that have been proposed to predict biphasic anaphylaxis and evaluate which of these were present in this patient.

**Risk factors that have been proposed to predict biphasic (or multiphasic) anaphylaxis**

| Proposed risk factor | Rationale in the literature | Frequency in published series |
|----------------------|----------------------------|------------------------------|
| **Severe initial reaction** (hypotension, hypoxaemia, respiratory distress, extensive cutaneous involvement) | The larger the initial mediator load, the greater the pool of circulating mast‑cell products that can re‑accumulate after the first “wave.” | Consistently identified in retrospective cohorts; odds ratios ≈2–3 for biphasic course. |
| **Delayed administration of epinephrine** (≥ 15 min from symptom onset) | Early epinephrine limits further mast‑cell degranulation; delay allows a second surge of mediators. | One of the strongest predictors in several case‑control studies. |
| **Multiple epinephrine doses required during the first phase** | Necessity for ≥ 2 doses suggests incomplete control of mediator release and a higher residual burden. | Frequently reported; some series show > 50 % of biphasic cases needed ≥ 2 doses. |
| **Older age (> 50 yr)** | Age‑related increases in comorbidities and altered pharmacokinetics may blunt the initial response. | Weakly associated; often present because most anaphylaxis cohorts are older. |
| **Obesity (BMI > 30 kg/m²)** | Larger distribution volume may reduce effective epinephrine concentration; also associated with higher baseline tryptase. | Observational signal; not yet validated. |
| **Underlying mast‑cell disease (systemic mastocytosis, clonal mast‑cell activation, elevated baseline tryptase > 11 ng/mL)** | Mast‑cell burden creates a reservoir for delayed mediator release. | Recognized in expert reviews; few systematic data because disease is rare. |
| **Previous biphasic reaction** | Personal history suggests an individual susceptibility. | Strong predictor when present, but rarity limits its utility. |
| **Allergen type with prolonged absorption** (e.g., oral antibiotics, latex, intramuscular depot formulations) | Ongoing antigen exposure can fuel a second wave after the initial clearance. | Described mainly in case series; not a universal rule. |
| **Concomitant β‑blocker therapy** | Blocks adrenergic rescue and may permit prolonged mediator activity. | Sparse data; more relevant for refractory rather than biphasic anaphylaxis. |
| **Use of corticosteroids in the first phase** (historical hypothesis) | Thought to blunt the late phase, but modern data show no protective effect; its absence therefore is not a risk factor. | Now considered a non‑predictor. |

*The above list reflects the most commonly cited variables in the last decade of anaphylaxis research. No single factor reliably predicts a biphasic course; instead, risk appears cumulative.*

### Which of these risk factors were present in the patient described in the case report?

| Risk factor | Presence in the patient | Comment |
|------------|------------------------|---------|
| **Severe initial reaction** | **Yes** – hypotension (74/50 mm Hg), hypoxaemia (SpO₂ 88 %), generalized urticaria, facial edema, marked agitation. | Meets the definition of severe anaphylaxis; aligns with the strongest predictor. |
| **Delayed epinephrine administration** | **No** – epinephrine given promptly in the ED (auto‑injector) within minutes of presentation. | Timing was appropriate; this factor does not contribute to her biphasic course. |
| **Multiple epinephrine doses in the first phase** | **Yes** – two 0.3 mg auto‑injector doses were required during the initial ED visit, and additional 0.5 mg IM doses were needed for later relapses. | The need for ≥ 2 doses reflects incomplete early control and is a recognized risk. |
| **Older age** | **Yes** – woman in her 60 s. | Age alone is a weak predictor; it adds modestly to risk. |
| **Obesity (BMI ≈ 35 kg/m²)** | **Yes** – weight 104 kg, height not given, but likely BMI > 30. | Possible contribution through altered drug distribution and higher baseline tryptase. |
| **Underlying mast‑cell disease** | **Probable** – markedly elevated baseline and peak tryptase; bone‑marrow biopsy showed two WHO minor criteria for systemic mastocytosis. | This is the most biologically plausible driver of a multiphasic pattern; the literature cites mast‑cell disorders as a strong, though infrequently studied, risk factor. |
| **Previous biphasic reaction** | **No** – first documented anaphylaxis; earlier drug reactions were limited to mild urticaria or local sting reaction. | Not a contributor. |
| **Allergen with prolonged absorption** | **Potentially** – oral clarithromycin (a single 500 mg dose) is rapidly absorbed; no evidence of slow‑release formulation. | Unlikely to have contributed. |
| **β‑blocker use** | **No** – patient’s antihypertensive regimen was hydrochlorothiazide, not a β‑blocker. | Not applicable. |
| **Corticosteroid use in the first phase** | **Yes**, but as the discussion notes, corticosteroids have not been shown to prevent biphasic reactions and therefore do not constitute a risk factor. | Their use was conventional but not predictive. |

**Summary:**  
The patient exhibited several of the most consistently reported predictors of biphasic/multiphasic anaphylaxis: a severe initial presentation, the need for multiple epinephrine doses early on, advanced age, obesity, and—most importantly—a high probability of systemic mast cell disease (elevated baseline tryptase, minor WHO criteria). The only classic “negative” factor was timely epinephrine administration, which was appropriate. The constellation of severe initial mediator release plus an underlying mast‑cell proliferation most plausibly explains why she experienced four distinct anaphylactic episodes within 12 hours despite optimal acute therapy. Recognizing these risk factors in real time can guide clinicians to adopt prolonged observation (≥ 12–24 h in high‑risk patients) and consider adjunctive strategies (e.g., inpatient monitoring, avoidance of under‑dosed auto‑injectors, early hematology/allergy referral) in future similar cases.
