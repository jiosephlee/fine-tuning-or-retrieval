Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Differential Diagnosis] What is the optimal timing and number of serum tryptase measurements after an anaphylactic event, and why were the samples in this case suboptimal?

**Answer – Timing and Number of Serum Tryptase Samples After Anaphylaxis**

The optimal laboratory evaluation of serum tryptase in a patient who has just experienced anaphylaxis consists of **three serial samples**:

| Sample | When to draw | Rationale |
|--------|--------------|-----------|
| **#1 (Acute)** | **As soon as possible after the onset of symptoms**, but **before any treatment that could alter mast‑cell degranulation (e.g., epinephrine, steroids)** delays the draw. In practice this means within the first **30–60 min** of the allergic reaction. | Tryptase rises rapidly after mast‑cell activation and peaks at 1–2 h. An early sample captures the maximal elevation and provides the “event” level. |
| **#2 (Peak/early‑follow‑up)** | **1–2 h after the first sample** (and always **≤ 4 h** from symptom onset). | Confirms the kinetic profile (rise and early decline) and helps distinguish a true mast‑cell surge from a chronically elevated baseline. |
| **#3 (Baseline)** | **≥ 24 h after complete clinical resolution** (often 24–48 h later). | Provides the patient’s individual baseline tryptase, which is essential for interpreting the earlier values and for detecting underlying mast‑cell disorders (e.g., systemic mastocytosis). |

**Why the Samples in This Case Were Suboptimal**

1. **First sample missed the acute window**  
   - The case states that the **first tryptase was drawn 1 hour after the *third* anaphylactic episode**. At that point the patient had already experienced three separate episodes, each with its own mediator release. The true “acute” peak from the *initial* reaction (the one that prompted ED presentation) was not captured, and the measured level reflects a composite of overlapping releases rather than a single, interpretable peak.

2. **Second sample drawn far outside the recommended 1–2 h window**  
   - The **second measurement was obtained 27 h after the last recurrence**. This exceeds the upper limit of the 4‑hour window in which tryptase elevations are still expected to be above baseline. By 27 h the enzyme should have returned to the patient’s personal baseline, so the result no longer informs about the magnitude of the anaphylactic surge.

3. **Absence of a true baseline sample**  
   - Although a later “follow‑up” tryptase is mentioned, the case does not describe a **dedicated baseline specimen drawn ≥ 24 h after full resolution of *all* episodes**. Because the patient had a probable mast‑cell disease (systemic mastocytosis), establishing an accurate baseline is critical; without it, the interpretation of the elevated values remains uncertain.

4. **Clinical consequence**  
   - Because the recommended timing was not followed, the investigators could not:
   * Quantify the peak tryptase rise associated with each individual episode,  
   * Apply the ≥ 2 ng/mL (or ≥ 1.2 × baseline) rule that helps confirm anaphylaxis, and  
   * Objectively support the diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis (which requires a baseline > 20 ng/mL or a ≥ 20 % rise above baseline).

**Take‑home teaching point**

When anaphylaxis occurs, **draw the first tryptase as early as logistics allow (ideally ≤ 30 min from symptom onset), a second sample 1–2 h later (≤ 4 h total), and a third sample ≥ 24 h after the patient is symptom‑free**. Adhering to this schedule maximizes diagnostic yield, enables accurate differentiation between acute mast‑cell degranulation and chronic elevation, and provides essential data for uncovering mast‑cell disorders such as systemic mastocytosis. In the presented case, the delayed and insufficient sampling rendered the tryptase results suboptimal for both confirming the anaphylactic episodes and assessing underlying mast‑cell disease.
