Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

Chapter 2: ## Differential Diagnosis and Diagnostic Reasoning  

The patient’s presentation was striking for a constellation of seemingly unrelated abnormalities—pancytopenia, severe vitamin B12 deficiency, hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying, osteoporosis, peripheral neuropathy, and subtle mucocutaneous changes. When faced with such a multisystem picture in a young adult, a structured differential diagnosis is essential. Below we walk through the major diagnostic categories, the specific findings that support or refute each, and the logical steps that ultimately directed attention to a telomere biology disorder (TBD).

### 1. Differential for Pancytopenia in a Young Adult  

| Category | Typical Features | Alignment with Patient | Why It Was Excluded |
|----------|------------------|------------------------|---------------------|
| **Nutritional deficiencies** (B12, folate, copper, iron) | Macro‑ or megaloblastic anemia, neutropenia, thrombocytopenia; often high MCV; low reticulocyte count; hypersegmented neutrophils | Severe B12 and folate deficiency; dimorphic smear, hypersegmentation, Cabot rings; low reticulocytes | Cytopenias improved only partially after aggressive vitamin replacement; persistent hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis cannot be explained by deficiency alone. |
| **Autoimmune cytopenias** (autoimmune hemolytic anemia, immune thrombocytopenia, aplastic anemia) | Positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT), brisk reticulocytosis if hemolysis; autoimmune serologies; marrow hypocellularity | DAT positive for IgG (2+); elevated LDH, indirect hyperbilirubinaemia | Reticulocyte response was blunted (0.39 %); bone marrow showed trilineage hematopoiesis without hypocellularity or aplasia; autoimmune serologies negative; therefore immune‑mediated destruction was unlikely to be the sole cause. |
| **Marrow failure syndromes** (aplastic anemia, myelodysplastic syndrome, inherited BMFS) | Pancytopenia with hypocellular marrow, dysplastic changes, cytogenetic abnormalities | Bone marrow cellularity preserved; no dysplasia or clonal cytogenetic lesions | Lack of hypocellularity and dysplasia argues against primary marrow failure at this stage, although a progressive BMFS could still emerge later. |
| **Infiltrative or malignant marrow disease** (leukemia, lymphoma, metastatic carcinoma, storage disease) | Abnormal blasts, infiltrates, abnormal histology, organomegaly | PET–CT showed heterogeneous FDG uptake in bone marrow but no focal lesions; marrow biopsy negative for malignancy | No morphologic or immunophenotypic evidence of malignancy; PET uptake likely reflects reactive changes rather than neoplastic infiltration. |
| **Infectious causes** (viral—EBV, HIV, hepatitis; bacterial; parasitic) | Often accompanied by systemic symptoms, serologic positivity, abnormal liver enzymes | EBV IgG positive (past infection); HIV, hepatitis B/C, CMV negative; inflammatory markers elevated but non‑specific | No active infection identified; chronic infectious hepatitis would usually produce a different serologic pattern. |
| **Genetic disorders** (congenital dyserythropoietic anemia, Fanconi anemia, TBDs) | Early‑onset cytopenias, associated congenital anomalies, telomere shortening | Multi‑organ involvement (fibrosis, premature ageing signs) plus family‑history‑negative presentation | After excluding acquired etiologies, a hereditary syndrome became the most plausible explanation, prompting targeted genetic testing. |

### 2. Ineffective Erythropoiesis versus Hemolysis  

The peripheral smear showed dimorphic red cells, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils—features classic for ineffective erythropoiesis caused by severe B12 deficiency. Laboratory markers (elevated LDH, indirect bilirubin) suggested some degree of hemolysis, yet the reticulocyte count remained markedly low, indicating that the marrow could not mount an appropriate compensatory response. A positive DAT raised the possibility of immune‑mediated hemolysis, but the absence of a robust reticulocyte rise and the presence of marrow erythroid hyperplasia favored ineffective erythropoiesis as the dominant mechanism. Consequently, B12 replacement was instituted, but the persistence of pancytopenia beyond correction signaled an additional, non‑nutritional driver.

### 3. Hepatic Fibrosis Without Classic Etiology  

Imaging (ultrasound, transient elastography) demonstrated cirrhosis (liver stiffness 18.1 kPa) in a patient without:

* **Alcohol excess** – history denied heavy drinking.
* **Viral hepatitis** – serologies for HBV, HCV, and HIV were negative.
* **Autoimmune hepatitis** – ANA, SMA, LKM‑1 antibodies absent.
* **Metabolic liver disease** – no evidence of steatohepatitis beyond mild steatosis; iron studies did not meet criteria for hemochromatosis; Wilson disease work‑up not indicated.

The absence of these common causes, together with the patient’s premature ageing phenotype, directed attention toward a systemic fibrotic process driven by telomere dysfunction, as has been reported in several TBD cohorts.

### 4. Pulmonary Fibrosis in a Young Adult  

High‑resolution CT revealed diffuse ground‑glass haziness, subpleural atelectasis, and fibrotic changes. In a person in her 20s, the differential includes:

* **Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF)** – typically presents after age 50; rare before 30.
* **Connective‑tissue‑disease–associated interstitial lung disease** – ANA, rheumatoid factor, anti‑CCP, ENA panel negative; no clinical stigmata of systemic sclerosis, lupus, or rheumatoid arthritis.
* **Environmental/occupational exposures** – no occupational dust, smoking, or drug exposures reported.
* **Genetic interstitial lung disease** – mutations in surfactant proteins, telomerase components, or PARN can cause early‑onset fibrosis.

Given the exclusion of autoimmune, environmental, and typical idiopathic causes, a hereditary interstitial lung disease—particularly a telomere‑related form—became the leading consideration.

### 5. Synthesising Multi‑System Involvement  

The coexistence of marrow dysfunction, hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying, osteoporosis, peripheral neuropathy, and mild mucocutaneous abnormalities (knuckle hyperpigmentation, bald tongue) hints at a **syndromic disorder affecting highly proliferative tissues**. This pattern is characteristic of telomere biology disorders, where defective telomere maintenance limits the replicative capacity of stem cells in bone marrow, epithelium, and mesenchyme. The lack of the classic dyskeratosis congenita triad (nail dystrophy, reticular skin pigmentation, oral leukoplakia) does **not** exclude a TBD, as many patients present with only a subset of features or with organ‑specific disease.

### 6. Rationale for Escalating to Genetic Testing  

When a thorough evaluation—biochemical, serologic, radiologic, and histologic—fails to uncover an acquired cause, the diagnostic algorithm for unexplained pancytopenia and multisystem fibrosis recommends **early genetic investigation** for several reasons:

1. **Timeliness** – Early identification of a TBD influences surveillance (e.g., pulmonary function testing, liver imaging) and informs transplant eligibility.
2. **Family counseling** – Determines recurrence risk and guides testing of relatives.
3. **Therapeutic implications** – Certain agents (androgens, telomerase activators) are being explored in telomere‑related disease; inappropriate immunosuppression can be avoided.
4. **Prognostication** – Telomere length measurement and genotype correlate with disease trajectory.

Thus, after excluding infectious, autoimmune, metabolic, and infiltrative etiologies, the clinical team pursued **clinical exome sequencing**.

### 7. Role of Clinical Exome Sequencing in Unexplained Multisystem Disease  

Whole‑exome or clinical‑exome sequencing interrogates all coding regions of the genome, enabling detection of both known and novel pathogenic variants. In the context of this case, exome sequencing identified a **monoallelic missense variant in PARN (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg)**. Although classified as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS) per ACMG criteria, several points supported its relevance:

* **Location in a critical domain** (R3H domain) with no benign variation reported.
* **Absence from population databases**, indicating rarity.
* **In silico predictions** of deleterious impact (four of five tools concordant).
* **Phenotypic concordance** with previously described PARN‑related TBDs (pulmonary fibrosis, bone‑marrow failure, hepatic fibrosis).

The identification of this variant provided a unifying genetic explanation for the patient’s multi‑organ pathology and justified ongoing monitoring for progressive marrow failure or fibrotic complications. Moreover, the result reinforced the principle that **targeted genetic testing should follow a systematic exclusion of common acquired causes**, especially in young adults with atypical, multisystem disease.

---  

In summary, the differential diagnosis evolved from common acquired conditions to a rare hereditary syndrome through meticulous evaluation of laboratory and imaging data, careful interpretation of the pattern of ineffective erythropoiesis versus hemolysis, and exclusion of infectious, autoimmune, metabolic, and infiltrative processes. The persistent, multisystem nature of the disease ultimately directed the clinicians toward genetic testing, where exome sequencing revealed a PARN variant, solidifying the diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder. This systematic approach exemplifies how clinicians should integrate organ‑specific findings into a broader syndromic framework, especially when standard work‑ups are unrevealing.
