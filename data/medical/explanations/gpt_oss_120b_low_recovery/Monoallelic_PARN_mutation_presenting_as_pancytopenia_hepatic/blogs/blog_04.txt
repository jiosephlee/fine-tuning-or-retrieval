Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# Genetic Testing in the Era of Overlapping Fibrotic Diseases: When to Order a Telomere Panel  

## Why This Case Stands Out  

If you’ve ever stared at a young adult with pancytopenia, premature greying, and both liver and lung fibrosis and thought, “That can’t be just B12 deficiency,” you’re not alone. The case of a woman in her late‑20s described by Soni et al. is a perfect illustration of how telomere biology disorders (TBDs) can masquerade as common nutritional or autoimmune problems, only to reveal a germline PARN missense variant when a broad genetic work‑up is finally performed. What makes it a teaching moment is not just the rare diagnosis, but the decision‑making pathway that led to ordering a clinical exome rather than a targeted test—something most clinicians face daily in the “fibrosis‑of‑any‑organ” clinic.

## The Clinical Crossroads: Fibrosis, Cytopenias, and Premature Ageing  

Patients with TBDs often present with a mixture of:

* **Bone‑marrow failure** – pancytopenia, macro‑ or micro‑cytosis, low reticulocytes.  
* **Pulmonary fibrosis** – usually “idiopathic” in a 30‑40 yr old, with subpleural reticulations.  
* **Hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis** – surprisingly common when you actually look for it.  
* **Premature ageing signs** – greying hair, nail changes, osteoporosis, neuro‑degeneration.  

When any two of these organ systems are involved, especially in a patient under 40, the threshold for a genetic evaluation should be very low. In the index case, even after aggressive B12 repletion and correction of nutritional deficits, the patient’s systemic disease progressed, prompting the team to look beyond the usual suspects.

## Choosing the Right Test: Targeted Panel vs. Clinical Exome vs. Telomere Length  

| Test | What it detects | Turn‑around time | Cost (US) | When to use |
|------|----------------|-------------------|-----------|------------|
| **Targeted telomere gene panel** (e.g., TERT, TERC, RTEL1, PARN, DKC1) | Pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants in known TBD genes | 2‑4 weeks | $500‑$1,200 | Classic DC phenotype, strong family history, or a single organ involvement (e.g., IPF) with suspicion of TBD |
| **Clinical (or “soft”) exome** (broad sequencing of ~5 000 genes) | All coding variants, including TBD genes and unexpected hits | 4‑6 weeks | $1,200‑$2,500 | Multisystem disease, atypical presentation, or when a differential includes other rare monogenic disorders (e.g., metabolic, immunodeficiency). |
| **Telomere length assay** (flow‑FISH or qPCR) | Functional read‑out of telomere attrition | 1‑2 weeks | $300‑$700 | As a screening adjunct when genetics are pending, or to support a VUS; not diagnostic on its own. |

**Key take‑aways**

* **Start simple, but be ready to broaden.** If the picture screams “DC” (mucocutaneous triad, early marrow failure), a targeted panel is cost‑effective and fast.  
* **When the phenotype is “mixed”** (lung + liver + marrow, or when classical skin findings are missing), a clinical exome is more likely to capture atypical or novel TBD genes (like the PARN Cys205Arg variant).  
* **Telomere length measurement is a useful “stress test.”** A markedly short telomere (< 1st percentile for age) supports a TBD diagnosis and can help re‑classify a VUS, but a normal length does NOT exclude a telomere disorder (especially early in disease).  

## Interpreting Variants of Uncertain Significance (VUS)  

The PARN missense variant in the case report was labelled a VUS—yet the clinicians proceeded with a TBD diagnosis. How?  

1. **In silico weight** – Multiple algorithms (SIFT, PolyPhen, MutationTaster) predicted deleterious effect, and the variant lies in the highly conserved R3H domain.  
2. **Population rarity** – Absent from gnomAD and other large databases.  
3. **Phenotypic match** – The patient’s multi‑organ fibrosis aligned with the reported spectrum of mono‑allelic PARN disease.  
4. **Functional clues** – Short telomere length (if measured) or a family member with a similar phenotype would push the VUS toward “likely pathogenic.”  

When faced with a VUS, use **the ACMG/AMP criteria** as a framework, but remember that **clinical context can tip the balance**. In practice, you can:

* Order telomere length testing to provide functional data.  
* Look for **segregation** – test first‑degree relatives; de novo findings are highly supportive.  
* Re‑evaluate the variant after 6–12 months as databases (ClinVar, gnomAD) are updated.  

## Counseling and Ethical Considerations  

Genetic counselling is not an after‑thought. Patients with TBDs face:

* **Variable penetrance** – A mono‑allelic PARN mutation may cause fibrosis in one organ and be silent in another family member.  
* **Reproductive implications** – Autosomal dominant inheritance with a 50 % transmission risk, yet many carriers never develop disease.  
* **Surveillance plans** – Regular pulmonary function tests, liver elastography, and hematologic monitoring are mandatory.  

Provide a **clear roadmap**:  

1. Discuss the uncertainty surrounding VUS and the possibility of re‑classification.  
2. Outline a **surveillance schedule** tailored to organ involvement.  
3. Explain **treatment options** (supportive care, lung/ liver transplant, potential hematopoietic stem cell transplant, investigational telomere‑lengthening agents).  
4. Address **psychosocial impacts** – living with a “genetic predisposition” can be anxiety‑provoking, especially for young adults.  

## Putting It All Together – A Practical Algorithm  

1. **Identify red flags** – Multisystem fibrosis, early marrow failure, premature ageing signs, especially < 50 yr.  
2. **Baseline work‑up** – Exclude common causes (autoimmune, viral, alcohol, drug exposures). Obtain imaging and basic labs.  
3. **Order telomere length** (if available) **and start a targeted panel** if the phenotype is typical.  
4. **If panel is negative or phenotype atypical**, step up to a clinical exome.  
5. **Interpret results** – VUS → functional assays (telomere length, family testing).  
6. **Counsel and initiate organ‑specific surveillance**.  
7. **Re‑assess** – As the disease evolves or as new genetic data appear, revisit the classification.  

## Take‑Home Nuggets  

- **Multisystem fibrosis in a young adult should trigger a telomere‑disorder work‑up** even in the absence of classic DC skin findings.  
- **Targeted panels are cost‑effective for classic phenotypes**; **clinical exomes capture atypical presentations** like the PARN‑associated case.  
- **Telomere length assays serve as functional adjuncts**; they can help re‑classify VUS and guide management.  
- **Interpret VUS in context**: combine in silico predictions, rarity, phenotypic concordance, and—if possible—family segregation and telomere length data.  
- **Genetic counseling is essential**—explain inheritance, surveillance, and uncertainty, and provide psychosocial support.  

The bottom line: when fibrosis, cytopenias, and signs of premature ageing congregate in a single patient, the genetic roadmap should be set early. A timely, appropriately scoped genetic test can spare months of fruitless work‑ups, direct surveillance, and open doors to targeted therapies or transplant referrals—ultimately changing the trajectory of what would otherwise be a relentlessly progressive disease.
