Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# When Bone Marrow Failure Isn’t Just B12 Deficiency: Red Flags for Telomere Biology Disorders  

## Why This Case Stands Out  
A 20‑something woman walked into a tertiary centre with five years of fatigue, weight loss and a laundry list of “old‑person” findings—premature greying, osteoporosis, liver + lung fibrosis, and pancytopenia.  The obvious culprit? Severe B12 deficiency.  Replace the vitamin and – *boom* – the anemia should improve.  In reality, despite aggressive cobalamin repletion her cytopenias, hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis, and premature ageing signs persisted.  

The hook here is that a **monoallelic PARN missense variant**—a gene that sits at the heart of telomere maintenance—provided the unifying explanation.  This is a reminder that **telomere biology disorders (TBDs)** can masquerade as “pure” nutritional or autoimmune marrow failure, especially when the classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita (DC) is missing.  

## The Hematology Clues That Should Ring Alarm Bells  

1. **Ineffective erythropoiesis out of proportion to deficiency**  
   * Low reticulocyte count (0.39 %) despite severe anemia.  
   * Peripheral smear: dimorphic red cells, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, hypersegmented neutrophils—patterns that hint at defective DNA synthesis *and* marrow stress.  

2. **Bone‑marrow morphology not fully explained by B12**  
   * Trilineage hematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia, iron overload, but **no dysplasia or infiltrative disease**.  
   * Normal cytogenetics—ruling out clonal processes.  

3. **Positive DAT without classic hemolysis**  
   * IgG 2+ DAT could mislead toward autoimmune hemolytic anemia, yet the low retic response and marrow picture point to **production failure**.  

4. **Multisystem “old‑age” phenotype in a young adult**  
   * Premature greying, knuckle hyperpigmentation, bald tongue, scoliosis, osteoporosis, hepatosplenomegaly, and interstitial lung disease.  
   * When these roll together, think **premature‑aging syndrome** rather than isolated nutrient deficiency.  

These red flags should trigger a **genetic work‑up** even after the vitamin deficiency is corrected.  

## Telomere Biology Disorders 101  

- **Telomeres** protect chromosome ends; each cell division shortens them.  
- **Telomerase** (TERT, TERC) rebuilds telomeres; mutations in any component cause **premature attrition**.  
- The clinical spectrum is broad: from classic DC (mucocutaneous triad + bone‑marrow failure) to isolated **idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF)**, hepatic fibrosis, or isolated cytopenias.  

**PARN** is a 3′‑exoribonuclease that processes the TERC RNA tail, a key step for functional telomerase.  Monoallelic (heterozygous) PARN variants have been linked mainly to IPF and bone‑marrow failure, while biallelic loss leads to severe DC variants (HH syndrome).  

## Take‑Home Messages  

- **Low retic count + dimorphic smear = think beyond B12**  
  Ineffective erythropoiesis that doesn’t bounce back after repletion should raise suspicion for marrow‑intrinsic disease.  

- **Multisystem fibrosis in a young adult is a red flag**  
  Hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis together, especially when accompanied by premature ageing signs, point toward a telomere defect.  

- **Absence of classic DC mucocutaneous signs does NOT exclude TBD**  
  Many patients present with isolated organ involvement; the triad is the tip of the iceberg, not the whole iceberg.  

- **Genetic testing early can change management**  
  Identifying a TBD informs surveillance (regular pulmonary, hepatic, and hematologic monitoring), guides family counseling, and opens doors to targeted therapies (androgen therapy, HSCT) when disease progresses.  

- **Monitor marrow trends even after nutritional correction**  
  A patient may have “double hit” – a reversible B12 deficiency superimposed on an underlying TBD that compromises stem‑cell reserve.  

## Practical Approach in the Emergency/Clinic  

1. **Correct the obvious first** – replace B12, folate, vitamin D, treat electrolyte derangements.  
2. **Re‑check labs 2–4 weeks later** – if pancytopenia persists or reticulocyte response remains blunted, dig deeper.  
3. **Review the broader picture** – skin, hair, nails, skeletal abnormalities, organomegaly, pulmonary symptoms.  
4. **Order targeted investigations**  
   - Flow‑FISH for telomere length (if available).  
   - Comprehensive genomic panel for TBD genes (TERT, TERC, PARN, RTEL1, DKC1, etc.).  
   - Imaging for fibrosis (fibro‑scan, high‑resolution CT).  
5. **Engage genetics early** – even a variant of uncertain significance (VUS) can be clinically actionable when the phenotype matches.  

## Bottom Line  

Bone‑marrow failure is rarely *just* a nutrient deficiency. When the marrow is unresponsive, the smear is atypical, and the patient bears the hallmarks of premature ageing—**think telomere biology disorder**. Early recognition translates into tailored surveillance, appropriate counseling, and, in selected cases, life‑saving interventions like hematopoietic stem‑cell transplantation.  

Stay vigilant, keep the telomere toolbox handy, and remember: **the marrow often whispers before the rest of the body screams**.
