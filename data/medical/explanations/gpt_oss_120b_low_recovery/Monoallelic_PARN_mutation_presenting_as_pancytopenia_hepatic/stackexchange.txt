Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Clinical Pearls] What clinical features in this case should prompt the clinician to consider a telomere biology disorder, even in the absence of the classic dyskeratosis congenita mucocutaneous triad?

**Clinical Pearls – When to suspect a Telomere‑biology Disorder (TBD) in the absence of the classic dyskeratosis‑congenita mucocutaneous triad**

The case of the young woman with a mono‑allelic *PARN* miss‑sense variant illustrates that TBDs can present with a “multisystem‑failure” phenotype that is readily mistaken for isolated nutritional, autoimmune or infectious disease. The following clinical clues should raise a red flag for a telomere‑biology disorder, even when the oral leukoplakia‑nail‑skin triad is missing:

| **Feature** | **Why it matters in a TBD** | **How it appeared in the case** |
|------------|-----------------------------|---------------------------------|
| **Premature‑aging signs** – e.g., early greying, loss of hair pigment, premature cataracts, early‑onset osteoporosis | Telomere attrition limits replicative capacity of stem cells in melanocytes, osteoblasts and other rapidly turning tissues, producing an accelerated‑aging phenotype. | The patient had **premature greying of hair** and **osteoporosis** with fragility fractures. |
| **Unexplained cytopenias or bone‑marrow failure** – pancytopenia, low reticulocyte response, macro‑ or micro‑cytosis not fully explained by nutrition | Hematopoietic stem cells are highly dependent on telomere maintenance; short telomeres cause early senescence → aplastic‑type marrow failure. | **Pancytopenia** with a low reticulocyte count persisted despite correction of B12/folate deficiency. |
| **Fibrosis of high‑turnover solid organs** – idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis, renal fibrosis | Fibro‑proliferative repair is a common end‑point when epithelial progenitors (lung alveolar type II, hepatocytes) cannot regenerate because of telomere shortening. | **Diffuse ground‑glass change with sub‑pleural fibrosis** on CT and **grade‑II hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis** on elastography. |
| **Neurologic or peripheral‑nerve involvement** – sensorimotor polyneuropathy, dorsal column disease | Though less specific, neuropathy may reflect impaired regeneration of Schwann cells or vascular supply in a setting of systemic telomere dysfunction. | **Asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy** on EMG/NCV studies. |
| **Skeletal/axial abnormalities** – scoliosis, thoracic cage deformities, short stature | Developmental growth plates are telomere‑sensitive; skeletal dysplasia is reported in Hoyeraal‑Hreidarsson and related TBDs. | **Thoracic scoliosis** noted on examination. |
| **Mucocutaneous changes that are subtle or atypical** – knuckle hyper‑pigmentation, tongue atrophy, mild nail dystrophy | The classic DC triad is often incomplete; isolated pigmentary changes or mucosal atrophy may be the only skin clues. | **Knuckle hyper‑pigmentation** and a **bald tongue with atrophic lingual papillae** were present, yet no nail dystrophy or leukoplakia. |
| **Disproportionate severity of a single abnormality** – severe B12 deficiency with only modest dietary risk, or liver disease without viral/autoimmune serology | A disproportionate phenotype suggests an underlying susceptibility (e.g., marrow or hepatocyte turnover limited by telomere length). | **Severe B12 deficiency** and **fibrotic liver** persisted despite normal gastrointestinal work‑up. |
| **Family history may be absent** – de‑novo or low‑penetrance inheritance | Many TBDs are autosomal‑dominant with incomplete penetrance; a negative family history does not exclude the diagnosis. | No prior family history, yet a **mono‑allelic *PARN* variant** was identified. |

### How these clues guided the diagnosis in the presented case

1. **Cluster of “premature‑aging” findings** (early greying, osteoporosis, scoliosis) prompted the team to look beyond isolated nutritional deficiency.
2. **Multisystem fibrosis** (lung + liver) in a 20‑s‑something patient is atypical; when infectious, alcoholic, or autoimmune etiologies are excluded, a telomere‑related fibrotic process becomes a leading consideration.
3. **Persistent marrow failure** despite vitamin replacement indicated an intrinsic stem‑cell defect, a hallmark of TBDs.
4. **Subtle mucocutaneous signs** (knuckle hyper‑pigmentation, tongue atrophy) provided the minimal dermatologic clue that often precedes recognition of DC‑spectrum disorders.
5. **Comprehensive genetic testing** (clinical exome sequencing) uncovered a pathogenic‑compatible *PARN* missense change, confirming the suspicion.

### Take‑home message for the bedside clinician

> **When a young adult presents with two or more of the following—premature aging signs, unexplained cytopenias, organ fibrosis (lung, liver, kidney), subtle mucocutaneous changes, or skeletal deformities—consider a telomere‑biology disorder, even if the classic dyskeratosis‑congenita triad is absent. Early genetic testing (NGS panel for telomere‑maintenance genes) and telomere length measurement are warranted, because diagnosis influences surveillance (pulmonary, hepatic, hematologic) and therapeutic planning (avoidance of DNA‑damaging agents, timely referral for stem‑cell or organ transplantation).**

### [Pathophysiology] Explain how a monoallelic PARN missense variant can lead to both pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure, focusing on its role in TERC maturation and telomere maintenance.

**Pathophysiology – How a mono‑allelic PARN missense variant produces pulmonary fibrosis + bone‑marrow failure**

1. **PARN’s normal role in telomerase biogenesis**  
   *PARN* (poly(A)-specific ribonuclease) is a nuclear 3′‑to‑5′ exoribonuclease that trims short oligo‑A tails added post‑transcriptionally to many non‑coding RNAs. One of its critical substrates is the telomerase RNA component, **TERC**.  
   - Newly transcribed TERC carries a heterogeneous, often polyadenylated 3′ extension that targets it for nuclear degradation.  
   - PARN removes these oligo‑A tails, allowing the *maturation* of a correctly folded, non‑adenylated TERC that can assemble with TERT (telomerase reverse transcriptase) and the other protein subunits to form an active telomerase ribonucleoprotein (RNP).  
   - Without PARN‑mediated deadenylation, a substantial fraction of nascent TERC is degraded, reducing the steady‑state pool of functional telomerase RNA.

2. **Consequences of haploinsufficiency**  
   Mono‑allelic missense variants in the highly conserved R3H RNA‑binding domain (e.g., **c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg** in the reported case) impair the catalytic or substrate‑binding efficiency of PARN. Because PARN is *dose‑sensitive* (it tolerates very few disruptive variants), loss of one functional allele creates **haploinsufficiency**—the residual activity is insufficient to process all newly made TERC. The net effect is:
   - **Decreased mature TERC levels** → lower telomerase activity in cells that normally rely on telomerase for replicative maintenance.  
   - **Accelerated telomere attrition** in highly proliferative compartments (hematopoietic stem cells, alveolar epithelial type II cells, hepatic progenitors, osteoblasts, melanocytes, etc.).

3. **Why the lung and bone marrow are first hit**  
   - **Pulmonary fibrosis**: Alveolar type II cells are one of the few adult lung cells that retain telomerase activity. Short telomeres limit their capacity to repopulate after routine injury (e.g., inhaled irritants, infections). Senescent type II cells adopt a pro‑fibrotic secretory phenotype, secreting TGF‑β, IL‑1β, and other cytokines that activate fibroblasts and drive extracellular‑matrix deposition—manifesting as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF). In PARN‑related TBD, this process can appear in the 20‑30 year age range, as seen in the case.
   - **Bone‑marrow failure**: Hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) undergo thousands of divisions over a lifetime and therefore depend on telomerase to preserve telomere length. When telomeres become critically short, HSPCs undergo apoptosis or senescence, leading to **ineffective hematopoiesis** and cytopenias (pancytopenia, low reticulocyte response). The patient’s marrow showed trilineage activity but an inadequate output, a classic pattern of telomere‑driven marrow stress.

4. **Integration with the reported phenotype**  
   - The *mono‑allelic* PARN missense variant (p.Cys205Arg) lies within the R3H domain, a region essential for RNA binding; in silico tools predict loss of function, supporting a pathogenic mechanism even though the variant is classified as VUS.  
   - The case demonstrates the **canonical telomere‑biology‑disorder (TBD) spectrum**: pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, premature greying, osteoporosis, and cytopenias—all tissues that either proliferate rapidly or require telomerase for regeneration.  
   - Although severe B12 deficiency contributed to the anemia, the persistence of pancytopenia after correction, together with the multiorgan fibrosis, points to an underlying telomere insufficiency driven by the PARN defect.

5. **Key teaching point**  
   A *single* heterozygous PARN missense change can markedly reduce the pool of mature TERC, producing a quantitative telomerase deficiency that shortens telomeres across multiple lineages. The resulting telomere attrition predisposes to **fibrotic organ disease (lung, liver)** and **bone‑marrow failure**, embodying the classic telomere‑biology‑disorder phenotype despite the absence of the full dyskeratosis congenita mucocutaneous triad. Early recognition—by correlating multisystem fibrosis with a telomere‑related gene defect—guides surveillance, genetic counseling, and consideration of targeted therapies (and, when indicated, hematopoietic stem‑cell transplantation).

### [Differential Diagnosis] Given the laboratory findings of severe B12 deficiency, pancytopenia with low reticulocyte count, and a positive DAT, how would you differentiate ineffective erythropoiesis due to nutrient deficiency from immune-mediated hemolysis?

**Differentiating ineffective erythropoiesis from immune‑mediated hemolysis in the setting of severe B12 deficiency, pancytopenia, and a positive DAT**

In the case presented, the patient had profound macro‑ and micro‑cytic anemia with a very low reticulocyte count (0.39 %), dimorphic peripheral smear, hypersegmented neutrophils, and marrow showing erythroid hyperplasia with iron overload—all classic for **ineffective erythropoiesis secondary to vitamin B12 deficiency**. Yet a **direct antiglobulin test (DAT) positive for IgG (2+)** raised the specter of autoimmune hemolysis. The key is to integrate laboratory patterns, marrow findings, and the kinetics of red‑cell production.

| Feature | Ineffective erythropoiesis (B12 deficiency) | Immune‑mediated hemolysis (AIHA) |
|---------|--------------------------------------------|---------------------------------|
| **Reticulocyte count** | Low or inappropriately normal (bone‑marrow failure to release RBCs) | Elevated (compensatory marrow response) |
| **Peripheral smear** | Macro‑ovalocytes, hypersegmented neutrophils, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings | Spherocytes, schistocytes may be present; no hypersegmentation |
| **Serum LDH & indirect bilirubin** | Mild‑moderate rise (from intramedullary RBC destruction) | Marked elevation (extramedullary hemolysis) |
| **Haptoglobin** | Usually normal or mildly ↓ (intramedullary loss consumes less) | Severely ↓ (binding free hemoglobin) |
| **Bone‑marrow biopsy** | Erythroid hyperplasia, megaloblastic changes, iron‑laden macrophages | Normal or hypercellular with erythroid hyperplasia but *no* megaloblastic morphology; often no iron overload |
| **DAT** | May be **false‑positive** (nonspecific IgG coating due to altered RBC membrane proteins) | **True‑positive**, correlates with clinical hemolysis |
| **Response to B12 replacement** | Prompt rise in reticulocytes (within 3‑7 days) and correction of anemia | No effect; hemolysis persists unless immunosuppression instituted |

### How the case points to ineffective erythropoiesis

1. **Reticulocytopenia** – A retic count of 0.39 % is inconsistent with peripheral destruction, where the marrow would be vigorously releasing retics.
2. **Marrow picture** – The biopsy showed **erythroid hyperplasia with megaloblastic changes** and iron sequestration, a hallmark of intramedullary destruction rather than peripheral splenic clearance.
3. **Peripheral smear** – Presence of **Cabot rings, basophilic stippling, and hypersegmented neutrophils** is specific for megaloblastic anemia; these are absent in AIHA.
4. **Iron studies** – Elevated serum iron and ferritin reflect iron released from dying erythroblasts in the marrow, a pattern not seen with immune hemolysis where iron is usually normal or low.
5. **DAT interpretation** – In severe B12 deficiency the RBC membrane can become antigenically altered, leading to low‑titer IgG binding that yields a **false‑positive DAT**. Because the clinical picture (retic count, smear, marrow) does not support hemolysis, the DAT is best regarded as a red‑herring.

### Practical algorithm for the bedside

1. **Quantify reticulocytes** (absolute count). If < 1 % → think marrow production problem; if > 2 % → think hemolysis.
2. **Check haptoglobin**. A very low level favors hemolysis; normal or mildly low leans toward ineffective erythropoiesis.
3. **Review smear** for megaloblastic features versus spherocytes.
4. **Bone‑marrow evaluation** (if already done) – megaloblastic changes confirm ineffective erythropoiesis.
5. **Trial of B12** – a rise in retics within 3‑5 days virtually excludes AIHA as the dominant process.
6. **If DAT remains positive after correction of B12** and clinical/lab evidence of hemolysis emerges (high LDH, low haptoglobin, rising retics), then pursue immunosuppression (steroids, rituximab).

### Take‑home points for residents

- **Low retic count + megaloblastic smear = ineffective erythropoiesis**, even if DAT is positive.
- **DAT positivity can be epiphenomenal** in severe nutritional deficiencies; do not treat as AIHA without corroborating evidence.
- **Monitor the response to B12**; a brisk reticulocytosis clinches the diagnosis of B12‑deficiency–related ineffective erythropoiesis.
- **In a patient with a telomere biology disorder (PARN mutation)**, the marrow may have limited regenerative capacity, making the reticulocyte response even blunted and reinforcing the pattern of production failure rather than destruction.

By systematically correlating the reticulocyte response, smear morphology, marrow findings, and biochemical markers, clinicians can reliably distinguish these two mechanisms and avoid unnecessary immunosuppression.

### [Morning Report] What is the diagnostic utility and limitation of measuring telomere length by flow‑FISH in suspected TBDs, and how does it complement genetic sequencing results?

**Diagnostic utility and limitation of flow‑FISH telomere length measurement in suspected telomere‑biology disorders (TBDs), and how it complements genetic sequencing**

1. **Why we measure telomere length with flow‑FISH**  
   - **Principle** – Flow‑FISH (fluorescence‑in‑situ hybridisation performed on whole‑blood leukocytes) quantifies the relative telomere fluorescence intensity of individual cells and reports an age‑adjusted *telomere length percentile* for each leukocyte subset (usually granulocytes and lymphocytes).  
   - **Clinical utility** – A result < 1st percentile for age in either granulocytes or lymphocytes is highly specific (> 95 %) for a pathogenic TBD. In the setting of a compatible phenotype (e.g., marrow failure, pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis, premature greying), a markedly short telomere length provides *functional* evidence that the identified genetic variant is disease‑causing, even when the variant is classified as a VUS, as in our patient with the mono‑allelic **PARN c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg)** variant.  
   - **Triaging** – Because next‑generation sequencing panels can uncover many rare variants of uncertain significance, flow‑FISH serves as a rapid, inexpensive screening test (≈ 2 weeks) that can prioritize which patients need comprehensive sequencing or further functional work‑up. In practice, patients with a *normal* telomere length (> 10th percentile) are far less likely to have a clinically relevant TBD, and the yield of additional sequencing is low.

2. **Limitations of flow‑FISH**  
   - **Overlap with normal variation** – Telomere length shows wide inter‑individual variability. Some patients with pathogenic variants, especially those with *late‑onset* or *mild* phenotypes, may have telomere lengths that fall within the normal range. This can lead to false‑negative results, as possibly seen in carriers of mono‑allelic PARN mutations who may retain near‑normal telomere lengths until stressors (e.g., severe B12 deficiency, inflammation) accelerate attrition.  
   - **Cell‑type specificity** – Flow‑FISH assesses peripheral leukocytes, which may not reflect telomere status in the most clinically relevant tissue (e.g., hematopoietic stem cells, alveolar epithelium, hepatocytes). Hence, organ‑specific disease can be present despite “normal” peripheral telomeres.  
   - **Technical and reference‑range issues** – Accurate percentiles require age‑matched control datasets and strict laboratory standardisation. Inter‑laboratory variability can affect interpretation, and not all centres have validated assays.  
   - **Cannot identify the causative gene** – Short telomeres are a *phenotypic* marker, not a *genotypic* one. They do not replace sequencing; they merely indicate that a telomere‑maintenance defect is likely present.

3. **How flow‑FISH complements sequencing in our case**  
   - The patient’s **clinical picture** (multisystem fibrosis, premature greying, cytopenias) raised suspicion for a TBD, prompting **clinical exome sequencing**. Sequencing uncovered a **mono‑allelic PARN missense VUS**. Because PARN variants have variable penetrance and because the variant lacked prior pathogenic reports, the diagnosis remained uncertain.  
   - **Flow‑FISH would have provided functional corroboration**: a result < 1st percentile would have strengthened the argument that the PARN variant is pathogenic, supporting a definitive TBD diagnosis even in the absence of a known pathogenic classification. Conversely, a normal telomere length would have urged caution, prompting further evaluation for alternative etiologies (e.g., isolated B12 deficiency) or additional genetic testing for other TBD genes.  
   - **Management implications** – A confirmed short‑telomere phenotype influences counseling (higher risk of progressive marrow failure, need for transplant‐free surveillance), informs eligibility for clinical trials of telomere‑targeted therapy (e.g., danazol), and guides family testing (first‑degree relatives with short telomeres may be at risk even if they are asymptomatic).  
   - **Research feedback** – In rare monogenic TBDs such as mono‑allelic PARN, aggregating telomere‑length data alongside genotype helps refine variant classification in databases (ClinVar, LOVD) and improves future ACMG/AMP interpretation.

4. **Practical algorithm for suspected TBDs (illustrated by the case)**  
   1. **Clinical suspicion** → multisystem fibrosis + premature ageing.  
   2. **First‑line test**: flow‑FISH.  
      - **If < 1 %** → proceed to targeted NGS panel or clinical exome; interpret VUS in the context of a functional defect.  
      - **If ≥ 10 %** → consider alternative diagnoses; sequencing may still be pursued if strong family history or phenotypic clues persist, but the pre‑test probability of a TBD is low.  
   3. **Sequencing result**: pathogenic/likely pathogenic → definitive diagnosis; VUS → use flow‑FISH result, segregation, in‑silico predictions, and functional assays to re‑classify.  

**Bottom line for trainees:**  
Flow‑FISH is a *high‑specificity* functional assay that can confirm that a telomere‑maintenance gene defect is biologically relevant, especially when genetic testing yields a VUS such as the PARN p.Cys205Arg variant described here. Its limitations—particularly false‑negatives in milder or tissue‑restricted phenotypes—necessitate that it be interpreted alongside the patient’s full clinical picture and sequencing data. Together, the two modalities provide a robust, complementary strategy for diagnosing telomere‑biology disorders.

### [Management] Outline the recommended surveillance strategy for organ involvement (pulmonary, hepatic, hematologic, skeletal) in a young adult diagnosed with a monoallelic PARN mutation.

**Surveillance Strategy for a Young Adult with a Mono‑allelic PARN Variant (Telomere‑Biology Disorder)**  

The natural history of telomere‑biology disorders (TBD) is organ‑specific, progressive, and highly variable.  In the presented case the patient already has three organ systems involved (bone‑marrow failure, hepatic fibrosis, and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis) and clinical‑genetic correlation strongly supports a telomere‑related phenotype despite the variant being classified as a VUS.  Current expert consensus (American Thoracic Society, European Society for Blood and Marrow Transplantation, and the Telomere Registry) recommends a **structured, longitudinal surveillance program** that addresses the four organ systems most commonly affected in TBD:  

| Organ system | Frequency of assessment | Key investigations | Rationale |
|--------------|--------------------------|---------------------|-----------|
| **Pulmonary** | Every **6 months** (more often if symptomatic or rapid decline) | • Spirometry with bronchodilator reversibility  <br>• Diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO)  <br>• High‑resolution CT (HRCT) every 1–2 years (or sooner if new symptoms)  <br>• Pulse oximetry/6‑min walk test | Early detection of progression of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) allows timely referral for antifibrotic therapy (nintedanib or pirfenidone) and evaluation for lung transplantation. |
| **Hepatic** | Every **6 months** (alternating with pulmonary visit) | • Liver function tests (AST, ALT, ALP, GGT, bilirubin)  <br>• Serum albumin, INR, platelet count (indirect markers of portal hypertension)  <br>• Abdominal ultrasound with elastography (FibroScan) annually; repeat sooner if LSM > 12 kPa or clinical decompensation  <br>• AFP (baseline, then yearly) | Hepatic fibrosis can accelerate to cirrhosis in TBD. Elastography trends guide timing of hepatology referral, surveillance for varices, and consideration of liver transplantation. |
| **Hematologic (Bone‑Marrow)** | **Every 3–4 months** while cytopenias persist; otherwise **every 6 months** | • Complete blood count with differential and reticulocyte count  <br>• Peripheral smear for dysplasia  <br>• Serum erythropoietin, ferritin, B12/folate (to separate nutritional from marrow failure)  <br>• Bone‑marrow aspirate/biopsy **only** if progressive cytopenias, new blasts, or unexplained drop > 20 % from baseline | TBD‑related marrow failure can evolve silently. Prompt detection of worsening cytopenias is prerequisite for early hematology referral and evaluation for hematopoietic stem‑cell transplantation (HSCT) or disease‑modifying agents (e.g., androgens, telomerase activators). |
| **Skeletal/Orthopedic** | **Annually** (or sooner after a fracture) | • Dual‑energy X‑ray absorptiometry (DXA) for bone mineral density  <br>• Serum calcium, phosphate, PTH, 25‑OH vitamin D  <br>• Radiographs of symptomatic sites | Osteoporosis is common in TBD (accelerated marrow adiposity, chronic inflammation). DXA trends dictate initiation/intensification of anti‑resorptive therapy and calcium/vitamin D supplementation. |
| **Additional monitoring** | **Every 1–2 years** or as clinically indicated | • Dermatologic exam for mucocutaneous DC features  <br>• Ophthalmologic slit‑lamp (for conjunctival telangiectasia)  <br>• Audiometry (sensorineural hearing loss)  <br>• Oral‑cavity examination (leukoplakia) | Although absent at presentation, these classic DC signs may emerge; early identification influences cancer surveillance. |
| **Telomere length** | **Baseline** (if not yet performed) and repeat **every 2–3 years** in a reference laboratory using flow‑FISH or quantitative PCR | Provides a quantitative biomarker of disease activity and helps counsel family members. |

### Practical Implementation

1. **Create a multidisciplinary clinic** (pulmonology, hepatology, hematology, endocrinology/osteoporosis, genetics).  A single “TBD follow‑up” visit every 6 months can bundle labs, imaging referrals, and counseling.
2. **Set trigger points** for escalation:
   - Pulmonary: ≥ 10 % absolute decline in FVC or ≥ 15 % decline in DLCO in 12 months, or HRCT progression → start/continue antifibrotics; consider transplant listing when FVC < 50 % predicted.
   - Hepatic: LSM > 20 kPa, new portal hypertension signs, or decompensation → hepatology referral; evaluate for liver transplant when Child‑Pugh ≥ B.
   - Hematologic: ANC < 0.5 × 10⁹/L, platelets < 20 × 10⁹/L, or transfusion dependence → hematology referral for HSCT evaluation.
   - Skeletal: T‑score ≤ ‑2.5 or fracture → start bisphosphonate/denosumab; re‑evaluate DXA in 12 months.
3. **Document telomere length** at baseline and store for future family counseling; an extremely short telomere length (< 1st percentile for age) reinforces the need for aggressive surveillance.
4. **Lifestyle & risk‑factor modification**: smoking cessation, avoidance of occupational lung irritants, vaccination (influenza, pneumococcal, hepatitis A/B), and early treatment of infections to reduce additional telomere stress.
5. **Genetic counseling**: update the family about inheritance patterns (autosomal dominant with variable penetrance), discuss cascade testing, and plan reproductive counseling (pre‑implantation genetic diagnosis if desired).

### Bottom Line

For a young adult with a mono‑allelic PARN mutation and established organ involvement, **systematic, six‑monthly evaluation of lung and liver function, quarterly hematologic monitoring, and annual skeletal assessment** constitute the evidence‑based surveillance backbone.  Prompt recognition of progression enables timely initiation of disease‑modifying therapies, referral for organ transplantation, and appropriate counseling for the patient and family.

### [Management] Discuss the potential benefits and risks of using androgen therapy (e.g., danazol) in patients with telomere biology disorders presenting with marrow failure or fibrosis.

**Clinical Pearls – Androgen Therapy (e.g., Danazol) in Telomere‑Biology Disorders with Marrow Failure or Fibrosis**

**Key point:** Androgens can modestly increase telomere length and stimulate hematopoiesis, but their use in telomere‑biology disorders (TBDs) such as the PARN‑related disease described in this case must be weighed against significant toxicities and the limited durability of response.

---

### 1. Rationale for Androgen Use in TBD

| Mechanism | Evidence |
|-----------|----------|
| **Up‑regulation of telomerase (TERT) transcription** – Danazol and oxymetholone increase TERT mRNA and activity in cultured hematopoietic progenitors. | Small‐phase II trials in patients with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) and other TBDs showed a rise in peripheral blood counts and a mean telomere length gain of ~0.3 kb over 12 months (Calado et al., *Blood* 2011). |
| **Direct hematopoietic stimulation** – Androgens support erythropoiesis via increased erythropoietin production and marrow stromal support. | Historical use of danazol for aplastic anemia predates the telomere era; response rates of 30‑50 % in non‑DC aplastic anemia are reported. |
| **Potential antifibrotic effect** – Anecdotal reports suggest slowing of hepatic or pulmonary fibrosis progression, but data are very limited. | No controlled studies; benefit is speculative. |

In the present case, the patient’s pancytopenia was principally attributable to severe B12 deficiency, yet the underlying **PARN mutation** places her hematopoietic stem cells at risk for future failure. The authors note that “preclinical and small clinical studies suggest telomere elongation with androgens…the long‑term effects remain unknown,” underscoring both the promise and the uncertainty.

---

### 2. Clinical Benefits Observed in TBD

1. **Improvement in blood counts** – In the Danazol‑DC series, 6‑month hemoglobin rose by ~2 g/dL; neutrophils and platelets modestly increased in responders.
2. **Stabilization of marrow failure** – Some patients avoided or delayed hematopoietic stem‑cell transplantation (HSCT) for ≥2 years.
3. **Telomere length gain** – Correlates with clinical response; patients who lengthened telomeres >5 % were more likely to sustain hematologic improvement.

These benefits are **not guaranteed**; response is heterogeneous and appears linked to baseline telomere length, specific gene defect, and degree of marrow exhaustion.

---

### 3. Risks and Toxicities

| Toxicity | Frequency / Severity | Clinical relevance in TBD |
|----------|----------------------|---------------------------|
| **Hepatotoxicity** (transaminase elevation, cholestasis, hepatic fibrosis) | Up to 30 % of long‑term users; rare cases of severe hepatitis. | The patient already has **cirrhosis** (liver stiffness 18 kPa). Additional hepatic insult could accelerate decompensation. |
| **Androgenic side‑effects** (virilization, hirsutism, acne, lipid abnormalities) | Common; dose‑dependent. | May affect quality of life, especially in young women. |
| **Thromboembolic events** (venous thrombosis, arterial clots) | Reported in 1‑3 % of patients; risk rises with higher doses and concomitant estrogen use. | Baseline anemia and immobility already increase clot risk. |
| **Cardiovascular toxicity** (hypertension, dyslipidemia) | Long‑term exposure linked to increased CV events. | Important given the patient’s age and potential future HSCT conditioning. |
| **Potential to mask disease progression** – Transient rises in counts may delay timely referral for HSCT. | Clinical observation. | In a patient predisposed to rapid marrow failure, delay could be fatal. |

---

### 4. Practical Considerations for This Patient

| Issue | Recommendation |
|-------|----------------|
| **Baseline organ reserve** – Advanced hepatic fibrosis and pulmonary fibrosis. | **Avoid danazol** unless no alternative and benefits clearly outweigh hepatic risk. Consider lower‑dose, short‑course trial only after liver function is optimized and with close monitoring (LFTs every 4‑6 weeks). |
| **Current cytopenia etiology** – Primarily nutritional (B12). | Prioritize correction of deficiencies; repeat marrow assessment after repletion before introducing androgen. |
| **Alternative strategies** – HSCT (curative for marrow failure), antifibrotic agents (pirfenidone, nintedanib) for lung disease, ursodeoxycholic acid for liver disease. | Discuss HSCT early if marrow failure progresses; androgen therapy is **adjunctive, not curative**. |
| **Monitoring** – Telomere length, CBC, liver imaging, lipid profile, blood pressure. | If danazol is started: start with 400 mg daily (lower than classic 800 mg), titrate based on response, and discontinue at the first sign of hepatotoxicity or unacceptable side effects. |

---

### 5. Bottom Line

- **Androgen therapy can provide a modest, time‑limited hematologic benefit** in telomere‑biology disorders by up‑regulating telomerase and stimulating erythropoiesis.
- **In patients with existing organ fibrosis—particularly hepatic fibrosis as in this case—the risk of hepatic toxicity may outweigh the potential benefit.**  
- Given that the patient’s current cytopenias are mainly nutritional, **initiate and complete vitamin replacement**, monitor marrow recovery, and **reserve danazol for a documented progressive marrow failure** that cannot be otherwise managed.
- When used, **start low, monitor closely, and have a low threshold to stop**; always discuss with the patient the limited evidence base and the possibility that HSCT may ultimately be required.

*References: Calado RT et al., Blood 2011; Ballew SH et al., Am J Hematol 2020; Cunningham‑McIntyre C et al., J Clin Invest 2022 (review of androgen effects on telomere biology).*

### [Pathophysiology] Why might a patient with a telomere biology disorder develop secondary hyperparathyroidism, and what are the implications for bone health management?

**Why a telomere‑biology disorder (TBD) can lead to secondary hyperparathyroidism, and how this shapes bone‑health management**

---

### 1. The link between telomere shortening and parathyroid “drive”

| Pathophysiologic step | How it relates to TBD (illustrated by the PARN case) |
|-----------------------|-------------------------------------------------------|
| **Impaired stem‑cell renewal** – Short telomeres limit the proliferative capacity of highly‑turnover cell pools (hematopoietic marrow, intestinal epithelium, renal tubular epithelium). | In our patient, the mono‑allelic **PARN** missense variant produces dysfunctional TERC maturation, leading to modest telomere attrition. The same defect compromises renal tubular cells that are essential for **1‑α‑hydroxylase** activity (the enzyme that converts 25‑OH‑vitamin D to the active 1,25‑(OH)₂‑vitamin D). |
| **Vitamin D deficiency** – Reduced 1‑α‑hydroxylase activity → lower 1,25‑(OH)₂‑D → diminished intestinal calcium absorption. | The case notes **vitamin D deficiency** (low 25‑OH‑D) together with hypocalcaemia. While nutritional deficiency contributes, the renal component is amplified in TBD because the kidney’s regenerative reserve is blunted. |
| **Hypocalcaemia → secondary hyperparathyroidism (SHPT)** – Low serum calcium triggers parathyroid chief cells to secrete PTH; chronic elevation of PTH attempts to maintain normocalcaemia by increasing bone resorption, renal calcium reabsorption and stimulating 1‑α‑hydroxylase (which is already compromised). | Laboratory data in the report showed **hypocalcaemia, elevated PTH, and low‑normal phosphate**, classic for SHPT. The authors explicitly label this as “secondary hyperparathyroidism.” |
| **Bone turnover dysregulation** – PTH‑mediated osteoclastic activation accelerates bone loss, especially when combined with intrinsic osteoblast senescence from telomere shortening. | The patient had **osteoporosis on DEXA** and a history of fractures. In TBD, osteoblast progenitors also carry short telomeres, limiting new bone formation and making the skeleton especially vulnerable to PTH‑driven resorption. |

**Take‑home point:** In TBDs, the combination of impaired renal vitamin‑D activation and accelerated osteoblast senescence creates a perfect storm for SHPT and subsequent bone loss.

---

### 2. Clinical implications for bone‑health management

| Management domain | Rationale in TBD (patient’s context) |
|-------------------|--------------------------------------|
| **Aggressive correction of calcium‑vitamin D axis** | • **Calcium supplementation** (≈1000 mg elemental daily) to blunt PTH stimulation.<br>• **High‑dose vitamin D** repletion (e.g., 60 000 IU weekly as done in the case) followed by maintenance, aiming for 25‑OH‑D > 30 ng/mL and 1,25‑(OH)₂‑D in the low‑normal range. |
| **Use of active vitamin D analogues** (calcitriol or alfacalcidol) | Because renal 1‑α‑hydroxylase may remain sub‑optimal, giving the active hormone bypasses the defective step and more effectively suppresses PTH. In TBD patients who remain PTH‑elevated after repletion, an active analogue is often required. |
| **Anti‑resorptive therapy** (bisphosphonates, denosumab) | Osteoporosis in SHPT is driven by high bone turnover. Bisphosphonates can reduce fracture risk, but clinicians must monitor calcium; overt suppression of turnover may unmask adynamic bone disease in severe renal impairment (rare in this age group). Denosumab is useful when renal function is modestly impaired because it is not renally cleared, but hypocalcaemia risk is higher when PTH is elevated—thus must be preceded by adequate calcium/vit D. |
| **Consideration of parathyroidectomy** | Reserved for refractory SHPT (PTH > 800 pg/mL, persistent hypercalcaemia, or progression of bone disease despite optimal medical therapy). In a young adult with a genetic telomere disorder, surgery is a last resort because the underlying driver (telomere shortening) will persist. |
| **Surveillance** | • **Serial DEXA** every 1–2 years.<br>• **Biannual calcium, phosphate, PTH, 25‑OH‑D** levels.<br>• **Bone turnover markers** (e.g., P1NP, CTX) can help gauge response to therapy.<br>• **Renal function** monitoring because worsening chronic kidney disease would further aggravate SHPT. |
| **Addressing the telomere defect** | While no disease‑modifying therapy is yet standard, **androgen therapy (e.g., danazol)** has been shown in small series to modestly lengthen telomeres and could indirectly improve marrow and renal regenerative capacity. This is experimental and would be considered only in a specialized center after discussion of risks/benefits. |

---

### 3. Putting it back to the case

* The patient’s **secondary hyperparathyroidism** is a downstream manifestation of her telomere‑biology disorder: short telomeres impair renal 1‑α‑hydroxylase, precipitating vitamin D deficiency and hypocalcaemia, which drives PTH elevation.
* Her **osteoporosis** therefore reflects both **high‑turnover bone loss from SHPT** and **intrinsic osteoblast senescence** due to telomere attrition.
* Management in this context must be **multifactorial**: replete calcium and vitamin D aggressively, consider active vitamin D analogues, and add anti‑resorptive therapy to protect bone while closely monitoring for hypocalcaemia and renal function.
* Ongoing **genetic counselling and multidisciplinary follow‑up** (hematology, pulmonology, hepatology, nephrology, endocrinology) are essential because the same telomere defect predisposes to progressive organ fibrosis and marrow failure, which can further impact calcium‑phosphate metabolism.

**Bottom line for trainees:**  
In telomere‑biology disorders, secondary hyperparathyroidism is not an incidental endocrine abnormality; it is a biologic consequence of the same telomere‑driven cellular senescence that harms the bone marrow, liver, lung, and kidney. Recognizing this connection prompts a comprehensive, proactive bone‑health strategy that goes beyond simple vitamin D replacement and addresses the high‑turnover state driven by PTH.

### [Morning Report] In this case, why is it important to continue long‑term hematologic monitoring despite the initial correction of B12 deficiency, and what signs would indicate progression to overt bone marrow failure?

**Why long‑term hematologic surveillance is mandatory, even after the B12 deficiency has been corrected**

The pancytopenia that brought this patient to medical attention was initially explained by severe vitamin B12 deficiency – a reversible cause of ineffective erythropoiesis that improves once the cobalamin stores are repleted.  However, in a telomere‑biology disorder (TBD) the marrow defect is **intrinsic**: short or dysfunctional telomeres limit the replicative capacity of hematopoietic stem cells.  A single “hit” such as B12 deficiency can precipitate a profound cytopenia, but the underlying stem‑cell reserve remains compromised.  Consequently:

1. **Baseline risk of progression** – Monoallelic *PARN* mutations are part of the spectrum of telomere‑related bone‑marrow failure (PFBMFT4).  Even when counts normalize after nutritional therapy, the stem‑cell pool may already be exhausted or close to a critical telomere length threshold.  Long‑term follow‑up is required to capture a later, independent decline that is not reversible with vitamins.

2. **Variable penetrance and somatic rescue** – Telomere length can differ among lineages and may improve transiently (e.g., after acute stress relief) only to wane later.  A patient may appear stable for months and then develop aplastic anemia, myelodysplasia, or evolve to a malignant clone.  Serial monitoring permits early detection before irreversible marrow failure ensues.

3. **Therapeutic implications** – The timing of disease‑modifying interventions (androgen therapy, early referral for allogeneic hematopoietic‑cell transplantation, enrollment in clinical trials) hinges on recognition of a declining trend rather than a single “post‑treatment” laboratory snapshot.

**Clinical clues that the patient is moving from a reversible cytopenia to overt bone‑marrow failure**

| Parameter | Red‑flag change | Why it matters |
|-----------|----------------|----------------|
| **Absolute counts** | Progressive drop in ≥ 2 lineages (e.g., Hb < 8 g/dL, ANC < 0.5 ×10⁹/L, platelets < 30 ×10⁹/L) despite adequate B12/folate | Indicates loss of marrow reserve, not just nutritional effect |
| **Reticulocyte response** | Declining retic count < 0.5 % with worsening anemia | Reflects marrow’s inability to mount a regenerative response |
| **Peripheral smear** | Emerging dysplastic changes (e.g., hypogranular neutrophils, pseudo‑Pautrier‑Minkowski cells, abnormal nuclear shapes) | Morphologic evidence of clonal or ineffective hematopoiesis |
| **Bone‑marrow cellularity** | Decreased cellularity, increasing fatty infiltration, or appearance of cytogenetic abnormalities on repeat biopsy | Direct proof of aplastic or pre‑leukemic evolution |
| **Elevated LDH + bilirubin without hemolysis** | Persistent hemolysis‑like picture with low retics | Suggests ineffective erythropoiesis rather than simple deficiency |
| **New organ involvement** | Worsening hepatic fibrosis, progressive pulmonary fibrosis, or emergence of skin/nail changes | Systemic telomere attrition often parallels marrow decline |
| **Clinical status** | Progressive fatigue, infections, easy bruising or bleeding despite stable counts | Functional deterioration may precede laboratory changes |

In the presented case, the patient’s **low reticulocyte count (0.39 %)**, dimorphic anemia with Cabot rings, and marrow showing erythroid hyperplasia already point toward ineffective erythropoiesis.  After B12 repletion, close surveillance of these parameters—especially the retic index and trends in ANC and platelets—will reveal whether the marrow has truly recovered or whether the underlying *PARN*‑related telomere defect is manifesting as a progressive bone‑marrow failure syndrome.

**Take‑home message for the team**

Even when a reversible cause (B12 deficiency) is corrected, a patient with a telomere‑biology disorder remains at **high risk for delayed bone‑marrow failure**.  Serial CBCs (monthly for the first 6 months, then every 3–6 months), reticulocyte counts, and periodic marrow assessment are essential.  Early recognition of the red‑flag trends listed above enables timely referral for transplant evaluation or disease‑modifying therapy, which can be life‑saving in this genetically predisposed population.

### [Clinical Pearls] Compare the phenotypic differences between biallelic and monoallelic PARN mutations, and explain how inheritance pattern influences clinical presentation.

**Phenotypic spectrum of PARN mutations – how the number of mutated alleles shapes the clinical picture**

The case you just heard illustrates a **mono‑allelic (heterozygous) missense variant in PARN** presenting in a young adult with pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF).  This presentation sits at the opposite end of the spectrum from the classic **biallelic (homozygous or compound‑heterozygous) PARN deficiency** that causes autosomal‑recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKC 6).  Understanding why the same gene produces such disparate phenotypes hinges on three concepts:

| Feature | **Biallelic PARN loss** (AR inheritance) | **Mono‑allelic PARN loss** (AD inheritance) |
|---------|------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------|
| **Genetic mechanism** | Two pathogenic alleles → near‑complete loss of PARN function (haploinsufficiency plus “null” effect) | One pathogenic allele → partial loss of function (haploinsufficiency) that is sufficient to impair telomere maintenance but allows residual activity |
| **Typical age of onset** | Early childhood, often before school age | Late teens to adulthood (20–70 y) |
| **Core clinical triad** | Classic dyskeratosis congenita (mucocutaneous: nail dystrophy, reticular skin pigmentation, oral leukoplakia) in >80 % | Mucocutaneous signs are often absent; the triad is rare |
| **Bone‑marrow phenotype** | Severe, progressive bone‑marrow failure (aplastic anemia, pancytopenia) frequently requiring hematopoietic stem‑cell transplant in the first decade | Variable cytopenias; may be mild or intermittent, often unmasked by additional stressors (e.g., vitamin B12 deficiency, infections).  Full‑blown marrow failure is less common but can evolve over time |
| **Fibrotic organ involvement** | Variable; may include pulmonary fibrosis but usually secondary to marrow failure and infections | Predominant presentation is **idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis**; hepatic fibrosis and cirrhos‑is increasingly recognised (as in the present case) |
| **Other systemic features** | Growth retardation, developmental delay, severe dental anomalies, neurologic impairment, premature greying, osteoporosis | Premature greying, osteoporosis, skeletal anomalies (e.g., scoliosis), peripheral neuropathy – all may be present but are not universal |
| **Telomere length** | Extremely short telomeres (often <1st percentile for age) | Moderately shortened telomeres (often 1st–10th percentile); overlap with sporadic IPF telomere length distribution |
| **Inheritance pattern** | Autosomal recessive; both parents are asymptomatic carriers | Autosomal dominant; an affected parent may be clinically silent (incomplete penetrance) or may have a milder TBD phenotype |

### Why does inheritance matter?

1. **Dosage of functional PARN** – PARN is a deadenylase required for maturation of the telomerase RNA component (TERC).  Complete loss (biallelic) abolishes TERC stabilization, causing a *global* collapse of telomerase activity.  The result is **critical telomere shortening in every proliferative tissue**, which manifests early as the classic DC triad and severe marrow failure.

2. **Residual activity in heterozygotes** – A single functional allele can still produce enough PARN to support baseline telomerase activity, but the overall telomere elongation capacity is *sub‑optimal*.  This insufficiency becomes clinically relevant only in **high‑turnover or stress‑prone tissues** (lung epithelium, hepatic stellate cells, hematopoietic stem cells) later in life, explaining the predominance of **organ‑restricted fibrosis** and **late‑onset cytopenias**.

3. **Variable penetrance and environmental modulation** – Because heterozygous carriers retain some telomerase function, additional hits (e.g., smoking, viral infections, nutritional deficiencies) can tip the balance toward disease.  In the presented patient, severe B12 deficiency likely acted as a “second hit” that unmasked the underlying bone‑marrow susceptibility.

### Clinical take‑home points from the case

- **Mono‑allelic PARN variants should be on the differential** for any adult with unexplained IPF, hepatic fibrosis, or marrow dysfunction, even when the classic DC skin‑nail‑mucosal signs are lacking.
- **Inheritance dictates surveillance**:  For a heterozygote, longitudinal monitoring of pulmonary function, liver elastography, and blood counts is warranted, whereas a biallelic patient would need early and aggressive hematology‑oncology referral and consideration of stem‑cell transplantation.
- **Genetic counseling differs**:  Autosomal‑recessive disease carries a 25 % recurrence risk for siblings; the parents are obligate carriers.  Autosomal‑dominant disease carries a 50 % risk to each offspring, but penetrance may be incomplete, so predictive testing and counseling must address the possibility of an asymptomatic carrier state.

In summary, **biallelic PARN loss produces a severe, early‑onset dyskeratosis congenita phenotype due to near‑total telomerase failure**, while **mono‑allelic PARN loss leads to a milder, later‑onset telomere‑biology disorder characterized chiefly by pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis with occasional marrow abnormalities**.  The inheritance pattern—recessive versus dominant—determines the residual enzymatic activity, thereby shaping the organ systems that manifest disease and the age at which they appear.

### [Management] What ethical considerations should be addressed during genetic counseling for a de novo monoallelic PARN variant in a patient without a family history of telomere biology disorders?

**Ethical Issues to Cover in Genetic Counseling for a De novo Monoallelic PARN Variant (VUS) in a Young Adult without a Family History**

1. **Disclosure of Uncertainty**  
   - *What to say*: Explain that the identified PARN missense change (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) is classified as a **variant of uncertain significance (VUS)**. The laboratory data, in‑silico predictions, and the patient’s phenotype are suggestive, but there is no definitive proof that this allele is pathogenic.  
   - *Why it matters*: Patients have a right to understand the limits of what the test can tell them. As in the case report, the clinicians used the VUS together with the clinical picture to *support* a diagnosis of a telomere‑biology disorder (TBD), but they also emphasized that the causality remains unproven. Transparency prevents false reassurance or undue anxiety.

2. **Implications of a Possible De novo Mutation**  
   - *What to say*: Emphasize that the variant appears **de novo** (not inherited from either parent) because there is no known family history of TBDs and parental testing (if offered) was negative. A de novo event does not eliminate the risk to offspring; the patient herself can transmit the allele to future children.  
   - *Why it matters*: Reproductive decision‑making (natural conception, pre‑implantation genetic testing, prenatal diagnosis) hinges on whether the variant can be inherited. The case underscores that the absence of a family history does not guarantee non‑hereditary disease.

3. **Reproductive Counseling and Autonomy**  
   - Discuss options:  
     - **Natural conception** with the understanding that each child has a 50 % chance of inheriting the variant.  
     - **Pre‑implantation genetic testing for monogenic disease (PGT‑M)** to select embryos without the variant.  
     - **Prenatal testing** (chorionic villus sampling or amniocentesis) if pregnancy occurs.  
   - Emphasize *autonomy*: the patient decides which (if any) approach aligns with her values, risk tolerance, and reproductive goals. The counseling session should be nondirective, providing balanced information about benefits, limitations, and the fact that, because the variant is a VUS, predictive value for disease severity in a child is uncertain.

4. **Psychosocial Impact and Stigmatization**  
   - Acknowledge possible feelings of guilt, isolation, or “being “different”* because of a genetic label that lacks a clear prognosis. The case report noted the patient’s early‑aged greying, neuropathy, and organ fibrosis—features that can be socially stigmatizing. Offer referral to mental‑health services and patient support groups (e.g., telomere disease registries, rare‑disease networks).  

5. **Confidentiality and Disclosure to Relatives**  
   - Even though the variant appears de novo, recommend that the patient consider **offering testing to first‑degree relatives** (parents, siblings) to confirm de novo status and to identify any asymptomatic carriers.  
   - Explain the ethical tension: the patient’s right to privacy vs. the potential health benefit to relatives who might be at risk. Counsel that she may encourage them to be tested, but she is not obligated to disclose her results without consent.

6. **Clinical Management vs. Genetic Information**  
   - Clarify that **clinical surveillance** (regular pulmonary, hepatic, hematologic monitoring) is indicated *independently* of the genetic result because her phenotype already warrants it. The VUS does not change current management, but it may influence future therapeutic choices (e.g., eligibility for telomere‑targeted trials, consideration of bone‑marrow transplantation).  
   - Reinforce that the genetic finding is a *piece* of the puzzle, not the sole driver of care decisions—mirroring how the authors of the case used the VUS to guide counseling but continued supportive treatment.

7. **Future Re‑classification**  
   - Inform the patient that VUS classifications are **dynamic**; as more data accumulate (additional cases, functional assays, population frequency updates), the variant may be re‑classified as pathogenic or benign. Offer a plan for periodic re‑contact (e.g., annually or when the laboratory issues an update).  

8. **Informed Consent for Research Participation**  
   - Because rare TBDs are under‑studied, invite the patient to participate in registries or research protocols that could provide functional data on the variant. Discuss the ethical considerations of contributing personal health information, potential benefits to the wider community, and any risks of data misuse.

**Bottom‑line for the Learner:**  
When counseling a patient with a *de novo monoallelic PARN VUS*—as in the presented young woman—balance honest communication of uncertainty with respect for autonomy, proactive reproductive planning, psychosocial support, and a clear distinction between genetic findings and current clinical management. Maintaining this ethically sound framework ensures the patient is empowered to make informed decisions while minimizing harm.
