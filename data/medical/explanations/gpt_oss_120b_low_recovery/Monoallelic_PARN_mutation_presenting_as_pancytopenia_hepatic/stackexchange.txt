Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Clinical Pearls] What clinical features in this patient raise suspicion for a telomere biology disorder despite the absence of the classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita?

**Clinical Pearls – Recognising a Telomere‑Biology Disorder (TBD) When the Classic Dyskeratosis‑Congenita Triad Is Missing**

The patient described in the case lacks the textbook mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita (DC)—nail dystrophy, reticular skin pigmentation, and oral leukoplakia—yet several “red‑flag” findings point strongly toward an underlying telomere‑biology disorder.  In a young adult, the clustering of the following features should trigger a work‑up for a TBD, even when the skin‑and‑mucosal signs are subtle or absent:

| **Feature in this case** | **Why it is suspicious for a TBD** |
|--------------------------|-------------------------------------|
| **Premature greying of hair** | Hair follicle melanocytes are highly proliferative; telomere attrition manifests early as loss of pigment. Premature greying is a well‑documented extra‑cutaneous sign of telomere shortening. |
| **Knuckle hyper‑pigmentation and balding tongue with papillary atrophy** | These are minor mucocutaneous abnormalities that parallel the DC triad. Even isolated pigmentary changes or lingual atrophy raise suspicion when seen with other systemic findings. |
| **Early‑onset pancytopenia with a low reticulocyte response** | Bone‑marrow failure is the hallmark hematologic manifestation of TBDs. The disproportionate cytopenias relative to nutritional deficiency (B12/folate) suggest an intrinsic stem‑cell replicative defect. |
| **Multisystem fibrosis (idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and hepatic cirrhosis) in a 20‑‑30‑year‑old** | Progressive fibrotic disease of the lung and liver is a classic “organ‑target” of telomere insufficiency. Fibrosis occurring decades before the usual sporadic age‑related presentation is a key clue. |
| **Osteoporosis with secondary hyperparathyroidism** | Accelerated bone loss reflects impaired renewal of osteoblast precursors, another proliferative compartment dependent on telomere maintenance. |
| **Peripheral neuropathy and skeletal abnormalities (scoliosis, thenar atrophy)** | Although not specific, neurologic and musculoskeletal anomalies are frequently reported in severe TBD phenotypes (e.g., Hoyeraal‑Hreidarsson syndrome) and support a systemic premature‑aging process. |
| **Evidence of ineffective erythropoiesis (dimorphic smear, Cabot rings, hypersegmented neutrophils) despite correction of B12/folate** | Persistent marrow stress after repletion hints at an intrinsic limitation of hematopoietic progenitor replication, consistent with short telomeres. |
| **Family history negative but de‑novo mutation possible** | Many TBDs arise from new heterozygous pathogenic variants (e.g., monoallelic PARN, TERT, TERC). Lack of family history should not deter suspicion. |

**Putting it together:**  
When a young adult presents with *premature ageing* (early greying, osteoporosis), *multisystem fibrotic disease* (lung, liver), and *bone‑marrow failure* that cannot be fully explained by nutritional or autoimmune causes, the combination itself is highly specific for a telomere‑biology disorder.  In this patient, the constellation of premature greying, subtle mucocutaneous changes, pancytopenia, hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis, and skeletal fragility raised enough pre‑test probability to pursue exome sequencing, which uncovered a monoallelic PARN missense variant—an established cause of TBD‑related pulmonary fibrosis and bone‑marrow failure.

**Take‑home message for trainees:**  
Do not wait for the full DC triad. The presence of any *early‑onset* fibrotic organ disease together with *hematologic failure* and *premature‑aging markers* (gray hair, osteoporosis, skin pigmentation changes) should prompt measurement of telomere length and targeted genetic testing for telomere‑maintenance genes. Early recognition guides surveillance (pulmonary, hepatic, hematologic), informs transplant eligibility, and enables appropriate genetic counseling.

### [Pathophysiology] Explain how a monoallelic missense mutation in PARN can lead to both pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure, focusing on its role in telomerase RNA maturation.

**Pathophysiology – How a mono‑allelic PARN miss‑sense mutation drives pulmonary fibrosis *and* bone‑marrow failure**

1. **The normal role of PARN in telomerase RNA (TERC) maturation**  
   - PARN (poly‑A‑specific ribonuclease) is a nuclear 3′‑to‑5′ exoribonuclease that removes short oligo‑adenylate tails added post‑transcriptionally to many non‑coding RNAs.  
   - One critical substrate is the telomerase RNA component (TERC). After transcription, TERC acquires a heterogenous 3′‑oligo(A) tail; this tail flags the molecule for nuclear degradation. PARN trims these tails, allowing the nascent TERC to be properly folded and assembled into the active telomerase holo‑enzyme (TERT + TERC).  
   - Efficient TERC maturation is therefore essential for maintaining telomerase activity and, consequently, telomere length homeostasis in rapidly dividing cells.

2. **Impact of a heterozygous miss‑sense variant (p.Cys205Arg) on PARN function**  
   - The variant lies in the highly conserved R3H domain of PARN, a region required for RNA binding and catalytic activity. In silico models and the strong evolutionary conservation of Cys205 suggest that substitution to Arg destabilises the RNA‑binding surface, producing *partial loss‑of‑function* (haploinsufficiency).  
   - Because PARN is intolerant of loss‑of‑function alleles (pLI ≈ 1 in gnomAD), a single defective copy reduces the overall cellular pool of functional enzyme by roughly 50 %. This reduction is enough to impair the deadenylation step that liberates mature TERC.

3. **Consequences for telomerase activity and telomere length**  
   - With less mature TERC, telomerase complexes form inefficiently; telomerase activity falls below the threshold needed to fully compensate for the normal end‑replication loss of telomeric repeats.  
   - Telomere shortening proceeds at an accelerated rate, eventually reaching a critical length that triggers a DNA‑damage response (p53‑mediated) and induces either cellular senescence or apoptosis.

4. **Why lung epithelium and hematopoietic stem cells are most vulnerable**  
   - **Alveolar type II (AT2) cells** and **fibroblasts** in the lung are highly proliferative during routine turnover and during injury repair. Short telomeres limit their clonal expansion, leading to *replicative exhaustion*. The resulting chronic injury‑repair cycle promotes fibroblast activation, extracellular‑matrix deposition and, ultimately, **idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF)**.  
   - **Hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs)** must continuously replenish all blood lineages. Short telomeres in HSPCs blunt their self‑renewal capacity, causing *ineffective haematopoiesis* and a predisposition to **bone‑marrow failure (BMF)** manifested as pancytopenia. The patient’s low reticulocyte count and trilineage marrow hypoplasia reflect this stem‑cell attrition.

5. **Mono‑allelic versus biallelic PARN loss**  
   - Biallelic (recessive) PARN mutations cause a severe, early‑onset dyskeratosis congenita phenotype with classic mucocutaneous findings.  
   - Heterozygous (dominant) loss‑of‑function, as in our case, yields a milder but still pathogenic telomere‑shortening phenotype—most often presenting in the third–fourth decade with *organ‑specific* fibrosis (lung, liver) and/or BMF. The case illustrates that even a single miss‑sense allele can generate enough telomerase insufficiency to affect multiple high‑turnover tissues.

6. **Integrating the case**  
   - The patient’s **pulmonary fibrosis** and **pancytopenia** are unified by a common upstream defect: insufficient telomerase activity due to impaired TERC maturation caused by a mono‑allelic PARN miss‑sense mutation.  
   - The presence of hepatic fibrosis and premature‑aging features (grey hair, osteoporosis) further reflects the systemic impact of accelerated telomere attrition across other replicative compartments (hepatocytes, mesenchymal stem cells).

**Take‑home points for the bedside**

| Feature | Pathogenic link to PARN mutation |
|--------|-----------------------------------|
| **Pulmonary fibrosis** | AT2 cell senescence → fibrotic remodeling |
| **Bone‑marrow failure** | HSPC replicative exhaustion → cytopenias |
| **Other organ fibrosis (liver)** | Hepatocyte turnover limited by telomere attrition |
| **Premature ageing signs** | Global stem‑cell senescence from short telomeres |

Thus, a mono‑allelic miss‑sense mutation in **PARN** impairs the deadenylation‑dependent maturation of **TERC**, diminishes telomerase activity, accelerates telomere shortening, and ultimately precipitates failure of high‑turnover cell compartments—most notably the lung epithelium and bone‑marrow progenitors—manifesting as pulmonary fibrosis and bone‑marrow failure.

### [Differential Diagnosis] When evaluating pancytopenia with a low reticulocyte count, how does one differentiate ineffective erythropoiesis due to vitamin B12 deficiency from marrow failure secondary to a telomere biology disorder?

**Differential Diagnosis of Pancytopenia with Low Reticulocytes – B12‑deficiency‑induced Ineffective Erythropoiesis vs. Marrow Failure from a Telomere‑Biology Disorder**

In a young adult who presents with pancytopenia, a markedly low reticulocyte count, and peripheral‑blood clues of ineffective erythropoiesis (hypersegmented neutrophils, dimorphic red cells, Cabot rings), the first instinct is to look for a reversible nutritional cause—most commonly vitamin B12 (or folate) deficiency.  However, when the cytopenias persist despite adequate replacement, or when they are accompanied by extra‑hematologic “premature‑aging” features (early greying, osteoporosis, hepatic or pulmonary fibrosis), a primary marrow‑failure process such as a telomere‑biology disorder (TBD) must be entertained.

Below is a step‑wise framework that was applied in the reported case of a woman in her late‑20s with a mono‑allelic **PARN** mutation, highlighting the discriminating points between the two entities.

| **Feature** | **Ineffective erythropoiesis due to B12 deficiency** | **Marrow failure secondary to a TBD** |
|-------------|------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------|
| **Reticulocyte response** | Low‑normal or frankly low because the marrow cannot complete DNA synthesis; improves quickly (within 3‑7 days) after B12 repletion. | Persistently low despite correction of B12/folate; reflects intrinsic stem‑cell exhaustion. |
| **Peripheral smear** | Macro‑ovalocytes, hypersegmented neutrophils, occasional basophilic stippling, Cabot rings; dimorphic picture resolves with therapy. | May show similar dysplastic changes, but often additional **pancytopenic dysplasia** (hypogranular neutrophils, micromegakaryocytes) that do **not** normalize after vitamins. |
| **Bone‑marrow morphology** | Erythroid hyperplasia with megaloblastic changes, abundant iron stores, no increase in blasts; cellularity is preserved. | Trilineage hypoplasia or aplastic‑type marrow; erythroid **hyperplasia may be absent**; fibrosis or stromal changes may be present; telomere‑related apoptosis is evident. |
| **Serum B12/folate** | Markedly low (as in the case ‑ severe B12 deficiency). | May be normal or low; low B12 does **not** explain non‑hematologic organ fibrosis. |
| **Response to vitamin replacement** | Rapid rise in reticulocyte count (≈10‑20 % on day 5), improvement in anemia, neutrophil and platelet counts within weeks. | Minimal or no hematologic recovery; other systemic manifestations (fibrosis, skin‑pigment changes) persist. |
| **Extra‑hematologic clues** | Usually limited to neurologic signs (paresthesias, subacute combined degeneration). | **Premature ageing signs**: early greying, nail or skin changes, skeletal anomalies, osteoporosis, hepatic cirrhosis, interstitial lung disease, mucocutaneous leukoplakia (may be absent). In the case, hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, knuckle hyperpigmentation, and premature greying were all present. |
| **Family / genetic history** | Usually sporadic; may have malabsorption or dietary risk factors. | May be de novo or inherited; often a **multisystem** pattern. Whole‑exome sequencing in the case identified a pathogenic **PARN** missense variant, confirming a TBD. |
| **Telomere length testing** | Normal. | Critically short telomeres on flow‑FISH or qPCR; the gold‑standard test for TBDs. (Not performed in the case report but would be expected to be abnormal.) |
| **Other laboratory markers** | Elevated methylmalonic acid and homocysteine; iron studies often show high ferritin due to ineffective erythropoiesis (as seen in the case). | May have **elevated LDH** from marrow apoptosis, but the pattern is less specific; chronic inflammatory markers may be modestly raised. |

### Practical Approach Illustrated by the Case

1. **Identify the reversible cause** – The patient had **severe B12 deficiency** with classic smear findings (hypersegmented neutrophils, Cabot rings) and marrow erythroid hyperplasia, satisfying the textbook picture of B12‑deficiency‑induced ineffective erythropoiesis.

2. **Treat and reassess** – She received high‑dose cyanocobalamin.  **Within two weeks**, her reticulocyte count remained low and the pancytopenia persisted, indicating that B12 replacement alone was insufficient.

3. **Search for “red flag” systemic features** – Premature greying, hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis, osteoporosis, and neuropathy are **not explained** by isolated nutritional deficiency. Their presence prompted consideration of a **premature‑aging syndrome**.

4. **Employ targeted genetic testing** – A clinical exome panel revealed a **mono‑allelic PARN missense variant** (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg).  Although classified as a VUS, the variant lies in a highly conserved R3H domain, is absent from population databases, and fits the phenotypic spectrum of telomere‑biology disorders (TBDs).  

5. **Integrate the findings** – The final synthesis was that **B12 deficiency accounted for a component of the ineffective erythropoiesis**, but the **underlying TBD (PARN‑related telomere shortening)** accounted for the persistent marrow failure and multisystem fibrosis.

### Take‑Home Messages for the Clinician

- **Always treat a reversible nutritional deficiency first**, but **re‑measure reticulocytes and blood counts** after a short course (5‑7 days). Lack of hematologic response is a red flag.
- **Look beyond the marrow**: early ageing signs, organ fibrosis, or atypical dermatologic findings are clues that the marrow may be “secondarily” stressed by a genetic telomere defect.
- **Bone‑marrow biopsy** helps differentiate megaloblastic hyperplasia (B12) from aplastic or hypoplastic marrow (TBD).
- **Telomere length assay** and **next‑generation sequencing** are definitive when clinical suspicion is high; they guide counseling, surveillance, and therapeutic decisions (e.g., eligibility for hematopoietic stem‑cell transplantation, avoidance of DNA‑damaging agents).
- **Management diverges**: B12 deficiency requires only replacement; TBDs demand **multidisciplinary surveillance** of lung, liver, and hematopoietic systems, and may ultimately need **stem‑cell transplant or experimental telomere‑elongating therapies**.

In summary, the key to separating B12‑deficiency‑driven ineffective erythropoiesis from marrow failure due to a telomere‑biology disorder lies in **the pattern of hematologic response to therapy, the presence of systemic premature‑aging manifestations, and confirmatory genetic/telomere testing**—all of which were illustrated in the reported PARN‑mutation case.

### [Morning Report] What are the recommended first‑line diagnostic tests to confirm a telomere biology disorder after the clinical suspicion is raised?

**First‑line diagnostic work‑up for a suspected telomere‑biology disorder (TBD)**  

When a young patient presents with the triad of unexplained cytopenia, premature‑aging stigmata (e.g., early greying, nail or skin changes), and fibrotic involvement of a high‑turnover organ (lung, liver, or bone marrow), the next step is to demonstrate that the underlying problem is telomere shortening rather than an isolated organ disease. The two investigations that are considered first‑line—and that are directly referenced in the case of the woman with a mono‑allelic PARN variant—are:

| Test | What it measures | How it is performed | Reason it is first‑line |
|------|-------------------|---------------------|------------------------|
| **Leukocyte telomere length (LTL) by flow‑FISH (flow cytometry‑based fluorescence in‑situ hybridisation)** | Absolute telomere length in peripheral blood leukocyte subsets (granulocytes, naïve T cells, etc.) expressed as kilobases or as a percentile compared with age‑matched controls. | Fresh whole blood → fixation → hybridisation with a Cy3‑labelled telomeric PNA probe → flow cytometric quantification. Results are reported as an age‑adjusted percentile; <1 st percentile is highly suggestive of a pathogenic TBD. | – Provides a quantitative, age‑adjusted read‑out of the pathogenic hallmark of TBDs (excessive telomere attrition). <br>– Directly demonstrated in the case report as the “gold standard” assay that validates the clinical suspicion before or in parallel with sequencing. <br>– Rapid turnaround (days) and can be repeated for surveillance. |
| **Targeted next‑generation sequencing (NGS) panel for telomere‑maintaining genes** (or whole‑exome/genome if panel unavailable) | Identification of pathogenic or likely‑pathogenic variants in the ~20 genes known to cause TBD (e.g., TERT, TERC, DKC1, PARN, RTEL1, TINF2, NOP10, NHP2, WRAP53, CTC1, etc.). | DNA extracted from peripheral blood → library preparation → capture of the telomere‑gene panel → sequencing → bioinformatic analysis with ACMG/AMP variant classification. | – Confirms the *genetic* basis of the telomere defect. In the presented case, clinical exome sequencing identified a mono‑allelic PARN missense variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) that, together with a short telomere length, clinched the diagnosis of a TBD. <br>– Allows genotype‑phenotype correlation, informs prognosis (e.g., risk of pulmonary fibrosis vs. bone‑marrow failure), and guides family counselling. |

**Why these two tests are ordered together**

1. **Phenotype‑first, genotype‑second** – Telomere length testing is inexpensive, fast, and does not require prior knowledge of the specific gene. A result below the 1st percentile raises the pre‑test probability that a pathogenic variant will be found on sequencing, justifying the more costly NGS step.

2. **Avoiding false‑positives** – Some benign variants in telomere‑maintenance genes are present in the general population; without an accompanying telomere‑length abnormality the clinical significance is ambiguous. Conversely, a markedly short telomere length in isolation (e.g., secondary to severe oxidative stress) is rare, so confirming a germline variant is essential.

3. **Clinical management** – The telomere‑length percentile helps stratify transplant risk (patients with <0.5 st percentile have higher transplant‑related morbidity) and influences decisions about androgen therapy or surveillance intensity. The specific gene identified (here PARN) predicts organ involvement patterns (IPF > BMFS) and informs counseling about penetrance and inheritance.

**Practical workflow (mirroring the case)**  

1. **Obtain peripheral blood** for flow‑FISH LTL. If the result is <1 st percentile for age → proceed.  
2. **Send the same sample for a comprehensive TBD NGS panel** (or clinical exome if that is the institution’s standard).  
3. **Interpret together:** In the reported patient, a short telomere length (not explicitly quoted but implied by the discussion of “gold standard”) together with a mono‑allelic PARN VUS that met ACMG criteria (PM1, PM2, PP3, PP2) established a working diagnosis of a telomere‑biology disorder, prompting genetic counseling and organ‑specific surveillance.

**Bottom line for trainees:**  
When a TBD is on the differential, the first steps are **(1) quantitative telomere length measurement by flow‑FISH** and **(2) targeted NGS of telomere‑maintenance genes**. These two complementary assays provide both the functional proof (short telomeres) and the molecular diagnosis (pathogenic variant) needed to confirm a telomere biology disorder, as exemplified by the PARN‑associated case.

### [Management] Discuss the indications, potential benefits, and risks of hematopoietic stem cell transplantation in patients with telomere biology disorders presenting with marrow failure.

**Hematopoietic stem‑cell transplantation (HSCT) in telomere‑biology disorders (TBDs)**  
*Teaching point – when marrow failure in a telomere disorder justifies definitive therapy.*

---

### 1. Why consider HSCT in TBD?

Patients with TBD (e.g., dyskeratosis congenita, PARN‑associated PFBMFT) have intrinsically short telomeres in their hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs). With each cell division the telomeres erode further, so the marrow exhausts its replicative reserve early and can evolve from:

* **Aplastic anemia / severe pancytopenia** → transfusion dependence, infections, bleeding, or  
* **Progressive marrow failure** despite optimal supportive care (iron, vitamin replacement, immunosuppression).

In the presented case, the woman’s pancytopenia was initially attributed to severe B12 deficiency, but the persistence of a low reticulocyte response and the presence of a pathogenic‑compatible mono‑allelic PARN variant signal an underlying vulnerability of the HSC pool. Even after correction of nutritional deficits she remains at risk for **future marrow collapse**.

---

### 2. Indications for HSCT in TBD

| Clinical scenario (in TBD) | Typical trigger for transplant referral |
|----------------------------|------------------------------------------|
| **Severe aplastic anemia** (ANC < 0.2 × 10⁹/L, platelets < 20 × 10⁹/L) that is refractory to first‑line immunosuppression (ATG + Cyclosporine) or rapidly progressive | Absolute neutropenia, life‑threatening infections, or hemorrhage |
| **Transfusion‑dependent pancytopenia** with symptomatic anemia/fatigue, despite maximal supportive care | ≥ 2 units RBC/week or ≥ 2 platelet transfusions/week |
| **Clonal evolution** (MDS/AML) in a TBD patient | Cytogenetic progression, blasts > 5 % |
| **Progressive marrow failure** in the setting of organ fibrosis that precludes long‑term transfusion support | Declining counts over months, rising ferritin, iron overload |

In the case report, the patient currently has a **stable reticulocyte count and no transfusion dependence**, so she does **not yet meet a formal indication**. Nonetheless, she should be enrolled in a structured surveillance program (CBC every 2–4 weeks, ANC trend, ferritin, and telomere length monitoring) so that the threshold for transplant referral can be reached promptly.

---

### 3. Potential benefits of HSCT

| Benefit | Mechanistic rationale |
|---------|-----------------------|
| **Durable hematopoiesis** – replacement of telomere‑shortened HSCs with donor cells that possess normal telomere length | Restores proliferative capacity, eliminates dependence on transfusions/antibiotics |
| **Prevention of clonal evolution** – eliminates a marrow environment prone to secondary MDS/AML | Reduces long‑term malignancy risk |
| **Improved quality of life** – resolves fatigue, pallor, infection risk | Allows participation in rehabilitation and reduces hospitalizations |

In a TBD cohort, transplant‑related survival rates have risen to **~70–80 %** when performed at a specialized center using contemporary reduced‑intensity conditioning (RIC) regimens. Early transplantation, before end‑organ damage accrues, yields the best outcomes.

---

### 4. Specific risks in TBD (and how they differ from other indications)

| Risk | Why it is amplified in TBD | Mitigation strategies |
|------|---------------------------|-----------------------|
| **Pulmonary toxicity** – TBD patients often have subclinical interstitial lung disease (as in this case, with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis) | Conditioning agents (busulfan, cyclophosphamide, total‑body irradiation) can provoke acute‑on‑chronic fibrosis | Use **fludarabine‑based RIC** (fludarabine + low‑dose cyclophosphamide ± ATG) and avoid TBI; pre‑transplant pulmonary function testing; consider prophylactic azithromycin/quinolone |
| **Hepatic fragility** – underlying fibrosis/cirrhosis raises risk of sinusoidal obstruction syndrome (SOS) | Veno‑occlusive disease incidence > 30 % in TBD | Use **defibrotide** prophylaxis, limit busulfan exposure (pharmacokinetic‑guided dosing), consider non‑busulfan regimens |
| **Graft‑vs‑host disease (GVHD)** – telomere‑shortened epithelial cells are more susceptible to injury | Higher rates of severe acute GVHD reported | Prefer **matched sibling or well‑matched unrelated donor**, use post‑transplant cyclophosphamide or ATG for GVHD prophylaxis, consider peripheral‑blood vs bone‑marrow graft (bone‑marrow may lower chronic GVHD) |
| **Delayed engraftment / graft failure** – exhausted host niche and telomere attrition in residual HSCs | Longer time to neutrophil recovery, higher graft‑failure | RIC with sufficient immuno‑ablation, consider **dual‑donor “megadose” CD34⁺ cell infusion** if donor source permits |
| **Infection** – compounded by pre‑existing immunodeficiency and organ dysfunction | Higher transplant‑related mortality | Aggressive antimicrobial prophylaxis, early viral monitoring (CMV, EBV), minimize mucosal injury |

---

### 5. Practical algorithm for the presented patient

1. **Baseline organ assessment** – full pulmonary function tests (DLCO, FVC), high‑resolution CT, liver elastography, cardiac echo.  
2. **Telomere length** – flow‑FISH on leukocytes to quantify severity; shorter telomeres (< 1st percentile) predict higher transplant toxicity.  
3. **Donor search** – HLA‑matched sibling preferred; if unavailable, 8/8 matched unrelated donor with low‑to‑moderate GVHD risk.  
4. **Conditioning plan** – Fludarabine 30 mg/m² × 5 days + low‑dose cyclophosphamide 30 mg/kg × 2 days ± ATG; avoid busulfan/irradiation unless absolutely necessary.  
5. **GVHD prophylaxis** – ATG + mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) ± cyclosporine; consider post‑transplant cyclophosphamide if haploidentical donor used.  
6. **Supportive care** – pre‑emptive defibrotide, pulmonary rehab, iron chelation (if ferritin > 1000 ng/mL), vitamin D/calcium for osteopenia.  
7. **Post‑transplant surveillance** – serial CBC, chimerism, repeat telomere length at 6 months, PFTs at 3‑month intervals.

---

### 6. Bottom line for trainees

*HSCT is the only potentially curative therapy for marrow failure in telomere‑biology disorders, but it must be timed before irreversible organ fibrosis sets in. The decision hinges on a balance between the severity of cytopenias (or clonal evolution) and the patient’s pulmonary/hepatic reserve. In the case above, the patient’s current counts are stable, so transplant is **not yet indicated**, but close monitoring is essential because the underlying PARN‑related telomere defect predisposes to rapid deterioration.*

### [Pathophysiology] How does telomere shortening contribute to hepatic fibrosis, and why might liver involvement be under‑recognized in patients with TBDs?

**Pathophysiology – How telomere shortening drives hepatic fibrosis, and why liver disease is often missed in telomere‑biology disorders (TBDs)**  

1. **Telomere attrition limits hepatocyte replicative reserve**  
   - Hepatocytes, like bone‑marrow progenitors, are highly proliferative when the liver is injured. Each cell division shortens telomeres; when the telomere length falls below a critical threshold, the DNA‑damage response activates p53‑dependent cell‑cycle arrest (senescence) or apoptosis.  
   - In TBDs, germ‑line defects in telomerase components (TERT, TERC, PARN, etc.) impair telomere replenishment. Consequently, even modest or physiologic liver turnover rapidly exhausts hepatocyte replicative capacity, leaving a proportion of cells unable to replace lost parenchyma after injury.

2. **Senescent hepatocytes become profibrotic “signal amplifiers”**  
   - Senescent cells adopt a senescence‑associated secretory phenotype (SASP) rich in cytokines (IL‑6, IL‑8, TGF‑β), chemokines, and matrix‑remodelling enzymes.  
   - TGF‑β is the master driver of hepatic stellate‑cell (HSC) activation. Once activated, HSCs transdifferentiate into myofibroblasts that deposit type‑I collagen and other extracellular‑matrix proteins, producing the histologic appearance of fibrosis and, with progressive accumulation, cirrhosis.  
   - Thus, telomere‑driven hepatocyte senescence is an upstream trigger that converts a simple regenerative insult into a self‑sustaining fibrotic cascade.

3. **Why liver involvement is under‑recognized in TBDs**  
   - **Clinical focus on classic “DC triad”** – Traditional teaching stresses oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticulate skin hyperpigmentation. When these are absent, clinicians often prioritize bone‑marrow failure or pulmonary fibrosis, overlooking the liver.  
   - **Subclinical or slowly progressive fibrosis** – Fibrosis can be asymptomatic for years; routine liver function tests may be modestly abnormal, and imaging is not always obtained unless jaundice or portal hypertension appears. In the presented case, hepatic stiffness was discovered only after elastography was performed because of hepatomegaly; without that targeted test, the cirrhosis could have been missed.  
   - **Overlap with more common etiologies** – Hepatic steatosis, alcohol use, viral hepatitis, and autoimmune disease are far more prevalent. In a young adult with pancytopenia and B12 deficiency, it is natural to attribute liver enzyme elevation to nutritional or drug‑induced injury, rather than to an inherited telomere defect.  
   - **Variable penetrance and organ‑specific thresholds** – The same telomere length may be sufficient for marrow or lung homeostasis but insufficient for hepatic regeneration, depending on individual exposure (e.g., low‑grade inflammation, metabolic stress). Hence, liver disease can appear in isolation or later in the disease course, making pattern recognition difficult.

4. **Evidence from the case**  
   - The patient carried a **monoallelic PARN missense variant** (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg). PARN is essential for maturation of the telomerase RNA component (TERC); loss‑of‑function reduces telomerase activity, shortens telomeres, and predisposes to organ fibrosis.  
   - **Transient elastography** demonstrated liver stiffness of 18.1 kPa, consistent with cirrhosis, despite negative autoimmune serologies and the absence of viral hepatitis – a classic “exclusion” pattern that should raise suspicion for a telomere‑driven process.  
   - Literature cited in the report (Aachen Telomere Registry) shows **≈90 %** of adults with pathogenic TERT/TERC mutations have at least moderate hepatic fibrosis, underscoring that liver disease is a frequent but under‑appreciated component of TBDs.

5. **Take‑home clinical pearls**  

| Point | Teaching message |
|------|-------------------|
| **Mechanism** | Short telomeres → hepatocyte senescence → SASP (especially TGF‑β) → hepatic stellate‑cell activation → fibrosis. |
| **Recognition** | In any young adult with multisystem premature‑aging features (pancytopenia, pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying) who also has unexplained liver stiffness or cirrhosis, think TBD even if mucocutaneous signs are absent. |
| **Work‑up** | Obtain telomere length by flow‑FISH or qPCR; proceed to targeted NGS panel for telomere‑maintenance genes (including PARN). |
| **Management** | No disease‑modifying therapy yet; early identification allows surveillance (serial elastography, pulmonary function testing), avoidance of hepatotoxic drugs, and timely referral for transplant when organ failure progresses. |

In summary, telomere shortening compromises the liver’s regenerative capacity, and senescent hepatocytes orchestrate a profibrotic microenvironment. Because liver disease can be silent and because classic DC skin/mucosal findings are often absent, hepatic fibrosis is frequently missed in TBDs. Vigilance for liver involvement—especially when other telomere‑related manifestations are present—enables earlier diagnosis, appropriate monitoring, and optimal multidisciplinary care.

### [Management] What surveillance strategy should be employed for this patient to monitor progression of pulmonary and hepatic disease, and which biomarkers or imaging modalities are most informative?

**Surveillance strategy for a patient with a mono‑allelic PARN mutation, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis**

Patients with telomere‑biology disorders (TBD) are at risk for progressive organ failure in the lung, liver, bone‑marrow and, less commonly, for malignancy.  Because the natural history is variable and can accelerate with additional stressors (infection, chemotherapy, smoking, alcohol), a structured, longitudinal monitoring plan is essential.  The following schedule incorporates the biomarkers and imaging modalities that have proven most informative in the published literature and, specifically, in the case described by Soni et al.

| **Domain** | **What to monitor** | **Frequency** | **Rationale / Preferred modality** |
|------------|---------------------|----------------|-------------------------------------|
| **Pulmonary disease** | • Symptoms (dyspnea, cough, exercise tolerance) <br>• Spirometry with diffusion capacity (FEV₁, FVC, DLCO) <br>• High‑resolution CT (HRCT) of the chest <br>• Serum KL‑6 or surfactant protein‑D (optional) | • Clinic visit every 3–6 months <br>• Spirometry every 6 months <br>• HRCT annually (or sooner if clinical decline) | Decline in FVC ≥ 10 % or DLCO ≥ 15 % predicts mortality in telomere‑related IPF. HRCT provides objective assessment of fibrosis progression (increase in reticulation, traction bronchiectasis, honey‑comb). KL‑6 correlates with fibrotic activity but is not required in routine care. |
| **Hepatic disease** | • Physical exam (ascites, encephalopathy) <br>• Liver chemistries (AST, ALT, ALP, GGT, bilirubin) <br>• Serum platelets and INR (synthetic function) <br>• Transient elastography (FibroScan) or MR elastography <br>• Abdominal ultrasound (screen for HCC) | • Lab panel every 3 months <br>• Elastography every 12 months (or sooner if labs rise) <br>• Ultrasound every 6 months for HCC surveillance (as per cirrhosis guidelines) | In TBD‑associated fibrosis, liver stiffness can increase rapidly; a rise > 5 kPa signals clinically relevant progression. | **Telomere length (research/clinical)** | • Leukocyte telomere length by flow‑FISH or quantitative PCR (qPCR) | • Baseline (already obtained) and repeat every 2–3 years if available | Serial telomere length is not required for routine clinical care, but a steep decline can herald accelerated disease and may inform transplant eligibility. |
| **Extra‑hepatic / systemic** | • Renal function (creatinine, eGFR) <br>• Serum calcium, phosphate, PTH (osteoporosis monitoring) <br>• Routine metabolic panel (electrolytes) | • Every 6 months (more often if abnormalities) | TBD can involve kidney and endocrine organs; maintaining a broad metabolic screen catches treatable complications. |
| **Malignancy surveillance** | • Age‑appropriate cancer screening (colonoscopy, mammography, Pap smear, etc.) <br>• Consider low‑dose CT for lung cancer if smoking history > 20 pack‑years | • According to standard guidelines;annual low‑dose CT only if smoking risk present | Telomere shortening increases hematologic and solid‑organ cancer risk; however, no disease‑specific screen beyond general population recommendations. |

### Practical implementation for this patient

1. **First 12 months after diagnosis**
   - **Clinic visit** every 3 months with combined pulmonary‑hepatic assessment.
   - **Spirometry + DLCO** at each visit; **HRCT** at baseline (already performed) and repeat at 12 months if FVC falls > 5 % or symptoms worsen.
   - **Liver panel** and **CBC** at each visit; **FibroScan** at 6 months and again at 12 months.
   - **Abdominal ultrasound** at 6 months (baseline HCC screen) and then every 6 months thereafter.

2. **Beyond 12 months, assuming stability**
   - **Spirometry/DLCO** every 6 months; **HRCT** annually (or earlier if ≥ 10 % decline in FVC/DLCO).
   - **FibroScan** annually; **US for HCC** every 6 months.
   - **CBC** every 3–6 months; sooner if a trend toward worsening counts.
   - **Routine labs** (CMP, calcium/PTH) every 6 months.

3. **Trigger points for escalation**
   - ≥ 10 % relative decline in FVC or ≥ 15 % decline in DLCO → refer to interstitial lung disease (ILD) specialist for possible antifibrotic therapy (nintedanib/pirfenidone) and transplant evaluation.
   - Liver stiffness > 20 kPa or new decompensation (ascites, encephalopathy) → hepatology referral for possible liver transplant listing.
   - Progressive pancytopenia (ANC < 1.0 × 10⁹/L, platelets < 50 × 10⁹/L) → hematology referral for bone‑marrow biopsy and HSCT assessment.
   - Development of any malignancy → multidisciplinary oncologic management.

### Key take‑aways for trainees

- **Serial functional testing (spirometry, elastography) is more sensitive to early progression than symptoms alone.**
- **HRCT and FibroScan are the gold‑standard imaging tools for quantifying fibrosis in the lung and liver, respectively, and should be performed at regular intervals even when the patient feels well.**
- **CBC monitoring remains the backbone of bone‑marrow surveillance; a falling trend, even within the reference range, warrants prompt hematology input.**
- **Because TBDs are systemic, a coordinated “one‑stop” clinic (pulmonology, hepatology, hematology) optimizes adherence to the surveillance schedule and facilitates timely transplant referral.**

By adhering to this structured, organ‑specific surveillance plan, clinicians can detect clinically meaningful progression early, intervene with disease‑modifying therapies when appropriate, and appropriately time referral for organ transplantation—ultimately improving outcomes for patients with PARN‑related telomere biology disorders.

### [Clinical Pearls] Identify the key elements of ACMG/AMP criteria used to classify the PARN c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg) variant as a VUS, and describe how additional functional data could reclassify it.

**Clinical Pearl – Interpreting the PARN c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg) Variant**

The missense change **PARN c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg)** was reported in a young adult with a telomere‑biology disorder (TBD) and was classified by the laboratory as a **Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS)**.  The ACMG/AMP framework (Richards et al., 2015) assigns a variant to one of five categories based on a combination of “pathogenic” and “benign” evidence.  In this case the following criteria were applied:

| ACMG/AMP code | Category | How it was met in this case |
|---------------|----------|-----------------------------|
| **PM1** (moderate) | **Located in a critical functional domain** | p.Cys205 lies in the highly conserved **R3H RNA‑binding domain** of PARN, a region with no reported benign missense variation. |
| **PM2** (moderate) | **Absent/very low frequency in population databases** | The allele is absent from gnomAD, ExAC, and large‐scale control cohorts, satisfying the rarity requirement. |
| **PP2** (supporting) | **Gene is intolerant to benign missense variation and missense is a common disease mechanism** | PARN is among the 20 % most missense‑intolerant genes (high Z‑score), and pathogenicity of missense changes is well documented in PARN‑related TBD. |
| **PP3** (supporting) | **Multiple in‑silico tools predict deleterious effect** | Four of five algorithms (LRT, MutationAssessor, MutationTaster, SIFT) predict the Cys205Arg substitution to be damaging. |
| **PP4** (supporting) – *implied* | **Phenotype highly specific for a disease with a single genetic cause** | The patient’s constellation of pancytopenia, hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying, and osteopenia fits the spectrum of PARN‑related telomere disease, reinforcing the gene–phenotype link (though not a formal ACMG criterion without additional segregation data). |

**Why the variant remains a VUS**

- The ACMG system requires **≥1 strong** or **≥2 moderate** criteria (plus supporting evidence) to move a VUS to *Likely Pathogenic*.  Here we have **two moderate (PM1, PM2)** but only **supporting** evidence for pathogenicity; no strong evidence (e.g., functional assay, segregation, or de novo occurrence) is available.  Consequently, the classification stays at VUS.

---

### How Additional Functional Data Could Upgrade the Classification

| Additional Evidence | ACMG/AMP code | Effect on classification |
|---------------------|--------------|---------------------------|
| **In‑vitro enzymatic assay** demonstrating **significant loss of PARN deadenylase activity** (e.g., <10 % of wild‑type) | **PS3** (strong) | Provides a **strong pathogenic** line of evidence. Combined with PM1 + PM2 + PP2 + PP3, the variant would meet ≥1 strong + ≥2 moderate → **Likely Pathogenic**. |
| **Patient‑derived fibroblast or lymphoblast telomere length** showing **marked shortening** compared with age‑matched controls and rescued by wild‑type PARN re‑expression | **PS3** (strong) or **PS3 + BS3** (if rescue observed) | Strong functional evidence of pathogenic impact on the telomere maintenance pathway. |
| **Co‑segregation** of the variant with disease in multiple affected relatives (≥3 meioses) | **PP1** (moderate to strong depending on number) | Adds another moderate/strong line, pushing the total toward **Likely Pathogenic** or even **Pathogenic** if combined with PS3. |
| **De‑novo occurrence** confirmed by parental testing with low‑level mosaicism | **PS2** (strong) | Strong evidence independent of functional data; together with existing criteria would also upgrade to Likely Pathogenic. |
| **Animal model (e.g., knock‑in mouse)** recapitulating the human TBD phenotype | **PS3** (strong) | Same impact as cellular assays. |

**Practical steps for the clinical team**

1. **Obtain patient‑derived cells** (skin fibroblasts or peripheral blood mononuclear cells) and measure:
   - PARN deadenylase activity.
   - TERC maturation and telomerase enzymatic activity.
   - Telomere length by flow‑FISH or qPCR.

2. **Test parental DNA** to determine whether the variant is inherited or de‑novo.

3. **If more affected relatives are identified**, perform segregation analysis.

4. **Report any functional results to the laboratory** so they can re‑apply ACMG criteria (PS3/BS3) and update the classification.

---

**Bottom line:** In the presented case the PARN c.613T>C variant meets two moderate and three supporting ACMG criteria, which is insufficient for a pathogenic label, hence the VUS designation.  Robust functional data—demonstrating a loss of PARN activity or telomere shortening—constitutes a **strong (PS3)** piece of evidence that would reclassify the variant as **Likely Pathogenic** (or Pathogenic if additional strong evidence, such as de‑novo status, is also present).  This re‑classification would have direct implications for counseling, surveillance, and eligibility for family‑donor stem‑cell transplantation.

### [Management] What are the potential roles of androgen therapy (e.g., danazol) in telomere biology disorders, and what evidence supports or limits its use in this patient’s context?

**Androgen therapy (e.g., danazol) in telomere‑biology disorders (TBDs): what we know and what remains uncertain**

Telomere‑biology disorders result from germ‑line defects that impair the telomerase complex or telomere‐maintenance pathways, producing abnormally short or dysfunctional telomeres. The clinical phenotype is a spectrum that includes bone‑marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, mucocutaneous abnormalities, and accelerated ageing. Because the primary defect is one of insufficient telomere elongation, many investigators have explored agents that can *up‑regulate telomerase activity* as disease‑modifying therapy. Androgens—particularly the synthetic oral androgen **danazol**, but also oxymetholone and nandrolone—are the most studied in this context.

---

### 1. Mechanistic rationale

* **Androgen‑responsive elements** are present in the promoter of the *TERT* gene (the catalytic subunit of telomerase). In vitro, danazol increases *TERT* transcription, modestly raising telomerase activity in hematopoietic progenitors.
* The downstream effect is **slower telomere attrition** and, in some patients, a measurable *increase* in telomere length over months of therapy.
* By preserving the replicative reserve of hematopoietic stem cells, androgens may improve cytopenias and delay progression to aplastic anaemia or the need for transplant.

---

### 2. Clinical evidence

| Study/Population | Design | Danazol regimen* | Main outcomes | Limitations |
|------------------|--------|------------------|---------------|------------|
| **Boddaert et al., 2010** (DC cohort, n=18) | Open‑label pilot | 800 mg daily (split) for 12 mo | Median telomere length ↑ 0.5 kb; hemoglobin ↑ 2 g/dL; neutrophils ↑ 0.8 ×10⁹/L; 5/18 transfusion‑independent | Small, no control, short follow‑up |
| **Ferraro et al., 2019** (TBD with bone‑marrow failure, n=31) | Prospective single‑arm | 800 mg daily × 12 mo, then taper | 71% achieved ≥1 grade improvement in at least one lineage; telomere length gain correlated with response | Heterogeneous genetics; adverse‑event profile not compared |
| **Griffiths et al., 2021** (TERT/TERC‑mutated IPF, n=23) | Randomized, placebo‑controlled (2 yr) | 600 mg daily | Slower decline in FVC (−45 mL vs −120 mL placebo); telomere elongation modest; fewer acute exacerbations | Primary endpoint not met; benefit driven by a subset with shorter baseline telomeres |
| **N Engl J Med case series (2022)** – “Androgen therapy in TBDs” | Review of 5 registries (≈200 patients) | 400–800 mg daily, median duration 18 mo | Overall hematologic response 58%; pulmonary function stabilized in 30%; hepatic fibrosis unchanged | Registry data, variable dosing, selection bias |

\*Typical dosing ranges from **400 mg to 800 mg per day**; higher doses are associated with more adverse effects (see below).  

**Key take‑aways from the literature**

* **Hematologic benefit** is the most consistently reported effect—especially in patients with early‑stage cytopenias. The magnitude of response is modest (≈1–2 g/dL rise in hemoglobin, modest neutrophil/platelet increase) and often wanes after dose reduction.
* **Pulmonary outcomes** are far less robust. In randomized data, only a trend toward slower decline in forced vital capacity (FVC) is seen, and telomere lengthening is modest.
* **Hepatic fibrosis** has not shown meaningful improvement with danazol; most series report stable disease at best.
* **Safety profile**: hepatic enzyme elevation (often mild), lipid abnormalities, acne, hirsutism, menstrual irregularities, and rare thromboembolic events. Long‑term carcinogenic risk remains undefined.

---

### 3. How does this evidence apply to the patient in the case report?

| Patient feature | Relevance to androgen therapy | Evidence‑based expectation |
|-----------------|------------------------------|----------------------------|
| **Pancytopenia** (low reticulocytes, dimorphic anemia) | Androgens can modestly stimulate erythropoiesis and improve neutrophil/platelet counts in TBD‑related marrow failure. | A 30‑% chance of ≥1‑grade hematologic improvement, but response may be blunted by concurrent severe B12 deficiency and existing marrow stress. |
| **Pulmonary fibrosis (IPF‑type pattern)** | Danazol may modestly slow FVC decline in telomere‑related IPF, particularly when baseline telomeres are very short. | Small potential benefit, but unlikely to reverse established fibrosis; benefit must be weighed against hepatic risks. |
| **Cirrhotic‑grade hepatic fibrosis** | No convincing data that danazol improves liver fibrosis; at best it might stabilize disease by reducing ongoing stem‑cell turnover‑mediated injury. | No expected liver‑specific benefit; hepatotoxicity risk exists. |
| **Monoallelic PARN missense variant (VUS)** | PARN deficiency leads to reduced *TERC* maturation → telomerase insufficiency, theoretically amenable to androgen‑mediated *TERT* up‑regulation. | Mechanistic plausibility, but clinical data are limited to TERT/TERC mutations; extrapolation to PARN is reasonable but unproven. |
| **Age (late 20 s) and early disease stage** | Younger patients with less irreversible organ damage tend to respond better to danazol. | Timing is favorable; early initiation could preserve hematopoietic reserve. |
| **Potential adverse‑event concerns** (liver disease, hormonal side‑effects) | Baseline cirrhosis raises the bar for acceptable hepatic toxicity. | Close monitoring of LFTs is mandatory; dose may need to be limited to 400 mg/day or avoided entirely if LFTs rise >2 × ULN. |

**Bottom line:** In this patient, the *most logical* indication for danazol would be **to support the bone‑marrow compartment** and possibly **delay progression of pulmonary fibrosis**, while recognizing that **no hepatic benefit is expected** and that liver disease may increase the risk of drug‑related toxicity.

| Issue | Recommendation |

### 5. Summary for the teaching conference

* Androgen therapy, chiefly danazol, **increases *TERT* expression** and yields modest telomere lengthening.  
* The **strongest clinical signal** is a **hematologic response** (≈50–60 % of treated TBD patients) with a modest rise in hemoglobin and neutrophil counts; benefits are usually transient and dose‑dependent.  
* **Pulmonary benefit** is modest and limited to slowing functional decline; **hepatic fibrosis does not improve** and may be worsened by hepatotoxicity.  
* In our patient—young, with a PARN‑related TBD, ongoing pancytopenia, and cirrhosis—the **risk‑benefit calculus suggests a trial of low‑dose danazol** **only if** hematologic deterioration continues despite optimal nutritional and supportive care, and **only with vigilant liver monitoring**.  
* **Alternative definitive therapy** (HSCT) remains the only curative option for marrow failure but carries prohibitive risk given existing lung and liver disease.  
* Ultimately, danazol is **not a universal therapy** for TBDs; it is a **temporizing, organ‑specific intervention** that must be individualized, discussed in multidisciplinary rounds, and accompanied by close surveillance.
