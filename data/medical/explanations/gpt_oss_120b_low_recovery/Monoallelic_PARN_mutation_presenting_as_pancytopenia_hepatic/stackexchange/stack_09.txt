Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Morning Report] In a young adult with unexplained multisystem disease, outline an algorithmic approach to prioritize genetic testing versus more common acquired etiologies.

**Morning‑Report Pearls – When a young adult presents with a multisystem “mystery” syndrome, how do we decide whether to hunt for a rare germ‑line mutation or to exhaust the usual acquired work‑up?**  

Below is an **step‑wise algorithm** derived from the presented case of a 20‑‑‑30‑year‑old woman with pancytopenia, hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying, osteoporosis and neuropathy.  The same framework can be applied to any young adult with unexplained “multisystem” disease.

---  

### 1. **Start with a focused, systematic “common‑cause” screen**  
| Domain | First‑line tests (order as clinically appropriate) | Rationale | “Red‑flag” that pushes you beyond the common work‑up |
|--------|----------------------------------------------------|-----------|---------------------------------------------------|
| **Hematology** | CBC with differential, reticulocyte count, peripheral smear, iron studies, B12/folate, haptoglobin, LDH, DAT, marrow aspirate/biopsy if cytopenias persist | Rules out nutritional, immune‑mediated, infiltrative or clonal marrow disease | Persistent cytopenia **with low retic** despite correcting B12/folate; marrow showing ineffective erythropoiesis without dysplasia |
| **Liver** | LFT panel, viral hepatitis (HBV, HCV, HAV, HEV), autoimmune hepatitis panel (ANA, SMA, LKM‑1, IgG), Wilson & hemochromatosis screens, abdominal US/CT, elastography | Common causes of fibrosis in young adults are viral, autoimmune, metabolic | **Negative serologies** + **significant fibrosis** on elastography without steatosis or steatohepatitis |
| **Lung** | Pulmonary function tests, HRCT, bronchoscopy if indicated | Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis is rare <40 y; look for sarcoid, hypersensitivity pneumonitis, connective‑tissue disease | **Usual interstitial pneumonia pattern** on HRCT with **no connective‑tissue antibodies** |
| **Endocrine/Metabolic** | Calcium‑phosphate, PTH, vitamin D, thyroid panel, cortisol, glycemia | Excludes secondary hyperparathyroidism, endocrine amatosis | **Osteoporosis + secondary hyperparathyroidism** in a young adult |
| **Neurologic** | NCS/EMG, serum B12, copper, zinc | Evaluates peripheral neuropathy | **Axonal polyneuropathy** that cannot be explained by diabetes or toxin exposure |
| **Infectious** | HIV, EBV, CMV, parvovirus B19 PCR/serology, TB screen | Chronic infection can cause pancytopenia, hepatic injury, lung infiltrates | **All infectious work‑up negative** |

**If every tier above returns negative or the abnormalities are **disproportionate** to the identified abnormality (e.g., fibrosis far out of proportion to B12 deficiency), move to step 2.**  

---  

### 2. **Re‑evaluate the “phenotypic constellation” for a syndromic pattern**  

| Feature | Why it matters in a genetic context | Typical genetic groups |
|---------|--------------------------------------|------------------------|
| Premature greying / mucocutaneous changes (even subtle) | Classic clue for telomere‑biology disorders (TBD) | Dyskeratosis congenita spectrum |
| Early‑onset organ fibrosis (lung, liver, skin) | Fibrosis is a hallmark of telomere shortening | TBD, hereditary pulmonary fibrosis |
| Bone‑marrow failure (pancytopenia, low retic) | Hematopoietic stem‑cell exhaustion is the “first hit” in many TBDs | DC, PFBMFT (bone‑marrow‑failure telomere syndrome) |
| Short stature, skeletal abnormalities, scoliosis | Reflect impaired proliferative capacity of mesenchymal cells | Hoyeraal‑Hreidarsson, Revesz, other TBDs |
| Neuropathy, osteoporosis | May accompany telomere dysfunction but are non‑specific; their presence adds to “multisystem” burden | TBD, mitochondrial disorders, lysosomal storage – but the rest of the picture (fibrosis, marrow) leans toward TBD |

**When ≥ 2‑3 of these are present, a telomere‑biology disorder should be high on the differential, even if the classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita is absent – exactly as in the case report.**  

---  

### 3. **Prioritize genetic testing – but do it in a **targeted, step‑wise** fashion**  

| Step | Test | When to order | How results guide next steps |
|------|------|---------------|------------------------------|
| **A. Telomere length assay** (flow‑FISH on leukocytes) | First when TBD is suspected | Quick screen; severe shortening (< 1 st percentile) strongly supports a telomere disorder and justifies a broader gene panel. | Short telomeres → proceed to targeted TBD panel; normal telomeres → consider other inherited syndromes (mitochondrial, storage). |
| **B. Targeted next‑generation sequencing (NGS) panel** for TBD genes (TERT, TERC, DKC1, RTEL1, PARN, TINF2, NOP10, NHP2, ACD, CTC1, etc.) | After abnormal telomere length *or* if clinical suspicion is very high despite unavailable telomere testing | Detects pathogenic/likely‑pathogenic variants and VUS; may reveal mono‑allelic versus biallelic hits. | Pathogenic variant → diagnosis, cascade testing, transplant eligibility assessment. VUS → consider functional studies, segregation analysis, repeat testing. |
| **C. Broad clinical exome / genome** | If targeted panel is negative but phenotype remains unexplained, or if the patient has atypical features suggesting a different inheritance pattern. | Captures rare genes outside the TBD list (e.g., mitochondrial DNA, lysosomal genes). | May uncover an alternate diagnosis; if still VUS, multidisciplinary review is mandatory. |
| **D. Segregation / parental testing** | When a VUS (e.g., the PARN c.613T>C in the case) is found | Determines de‑novo status, compound heterozygosity, or familial segregation. | De‑novo VUS in a highly constrained gene (PARN) raises the likelihood of pathogenicity, prompting close clinical surveillance. |

**Key teaching point:** *Genetic testing is not “ordered for fun” – it should be driven by a phenotype that cannot be explained by common pathology.* In the case report, the combination of marrow failure, hepatic & pulmonary fibrosis, and premature aging pushed the team directly to **clinical exome sequencing**, which uncovered a mono‑allelic PARN missense variant (VUS) that fit the emerging phenotype of a telomere‑biology disorder.

---  

### 4. **Integrate the genetic result with clinical management**  

1. **Confirm the diagnosis** – If the VUS is in a gene with strong phenotype correlation (PARN, TERT, etc.) and telomere length is markedly reduced, treat as a **probable TBD**.  
2. **Counseling & family testing** – Even a VUS in a highly intolerant gene warrants discussion of inheritance (often autosomal dominant with incomplete penetrance). Offer testing to first‑degree relatives.  
3. **Surveillance strategy** –  
   * **Hematology:** serial CBC, retic, bone‑marrow assessment if counts drop.  
   * **Pulmonary:** baseline PFTs, HRCT every 12–24 mo.  
   * **Hepatic:** elastography or FibroScan annually; avoid hepatotoxins.  
   * **Oncologic:** low threshold for cancer screening (skin, head‑neck, GI) because TBDs increase malignancy risk.  
4. **Therapeutic implications** –  
   * **Stem‑cell transplantation** may be curative for marrow failure but carries high risk in the setting of organ fibrosis; decision hinges on disease trajectory.  
   * **Androgen therapy (danazol, nandrolone)** can modestly lengthen telomeres; consider in progressive cytopenia when transplant is not immediate.  
   * **Organ‑specific care** (antifibrotic agents for IPF, antifibrotic liver management).  

---  

### 5. **When to stop the algorithm**  

- **All investigations negative, phenotype not clustering** → consider multidisciplinary review; rare metabolic, mitochondrial, or immunodeficiency disorders may need specialized testing.  
- **Patient preference** – In the absence of actionable findings, shared decision‑making may lead to a conservative approach with symptom‑directed care.

---
