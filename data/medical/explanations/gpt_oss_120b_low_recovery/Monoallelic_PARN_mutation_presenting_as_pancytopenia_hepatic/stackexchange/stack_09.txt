Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Clinical Pearls] Compare the phenotypic differences between biallelic and monoallelic PARN mutations, and explain how inheritance pattern influences clinical presentation.

**Phenotypic spectrum of PARN mutations – how the number of mutated alleles shapes the clinical picture**

The case you just heard illustrates a **mono‑allelic (heterozygous) missense variant in PARN** presenting in a young adult with pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF).  This presentation sits at the opposite end of the spectrum from the classic **biallelic (homozygous or compound‑heterozygous) PARN deficiency** that causes autosomal‑recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKC 6).  Understanding why the same gene produces such disparate phenotypes hinges on three concepts:

| Feature | **Biallelic PARN loss** (AR inheritance) | **Mono‑allelic PARN loss** (AD inheritance) |
|---------|------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------|
| **Genetic mechanism** | Two pathogenic alleles → near‑complete loss of PARN function (haploinsufficiency plus “null” effect) | One pathogenic allele → partial loss of function (haploinsufficiency) that is sufficient to impair telomere maintenance but allows residual activity |
| **Typical age of onset** | Early childhood, often before school age | Late teens to adulthood (20–70 y) |
| **Core clinical triad** | Classic dyskeratosis congenita (mucocutaneous: nail dystrophy, reticular skin pigmentation, oral leukoplakia) in >80 % | Mucocutaneous signs are often absent; the triad is rare |
| **Bone‑marrow phenotype** | Severe, progressive bone‑marrow failure (aplastic anemia, pancytopenia) frequently requiring hematopoietic stem‑cell transplant in the first decade | Variable cytopenias; may be mild or intermittent, often unmasked by additional stressors (e.g., vitamin B12 deficiency, infections).  Full‑blown marrow failure is less common but can evolve over time |
| **Fibrotic organ involvement** | Variable; may include pulmonary fibrosis but usually secondary to marrow failure and infections | Predominant presentation is **idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis**; hepatic fibrosis and cirrhos‑is increasingly recognised (as in the present case) |
| **Other systemic features** | Growth retardation, developmental delay, severe dental anomalies, neurologic impairment, premature greying, osteoporosis | Premature greying, osteoporosis, skeletal anomalies (e.g., scoliosis), peripheral neuropathy – all may be present but are not universal |
| **Telomere length** | Extremely short telomeres (often <1st percentile for age) | Moderately shortened telomeres (often 1st–10th percentile); overlap with sporadic IPF telomere length distribution |
| **Inheritance pattern** | Autosomal recessive; both parents are asymptomatic carriers | Autosomal dominant; an affected parent may be clinically silent (incomplete penetrance) or may have a milder TBD phenotype |

### Why does inheritance matter?

1. **Dosage of functional PARN** – PARN is a deadenylase required for maturation of the telomerase RNA component (TERC).  Complete loss (biallelic) abolishes TERC stabilization, causing a *global* collapse of telomerase activity.  The result is **critical telomere shortening in every proliferative tissue**, which manifests early as the classic DC triad and severe marrow failure.

2. **Residual activity in heterozygotes** – A single functional allele can still produce enough PARN to support baseline telomerase activity, but the overall telomere elongation capacity is *sub‑optimal*.  This insufficiency becomes clinically relevant only in **high‑turnover or stress‑prone tissues** (lung epithelium, hepatic stellate cells, hematopoietic stem cells) later in life, explaining the predominance of **organ‑restricted fibrosis** and **late‑onset cytopenias**.

3. **Variable penetrance and environmental modulation** – Because heterozygous carriers retain some telomerase function, additional hits (e.g., smoking, viral infections, nutritional deficiencies) can tip the balance toward disease.  In the presented patient, severe B12 deficiency likely acted as a “second hit” that unmasked the underlying bone‑marrow susceptibility.

### Clinical take‑home points from the case

- **Mono‑allelic PARN variants should be on the differential** for any adult with unexplained IPF, hepatic fibrosis, or marrow dysfunction, even when the classic DC skin‑nail‑mucosal signs are lacking.
- **Inheritance dictates surveillance**:  For a heterozygote, longitudinal monitoring of pulmonary function, liver elastography, and blood counts is warranted, whereas a biallelic patient would need early and aggressive hematology‑oncology referral and consideration of stem‑cell transplantation.
- **Genetic counseling differs**:  Autosomal‑recessive disease carries a 25 % recurrence risk for siblings; the parents are obligate carriers.  Autosomal‑dominant disease carries a 50 % risk to each offspring, but penetrance may be incomplete, so predictive testing and counseling must address the possibility of an asymptomatic carrier state.

In summary, **biallelic PARN loss produces a severe, early‑onset dyskeratosis congenita phenotype due to near‑total telomerase failure**, while **mono‑allelic PARN loss leads to a milder, later‑onset telomere‑biology disorder characterized chiefly by pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis with occasional marrow abnormalities**.  The inheritance pattern—recessive versus dominant—determines the residual enzymatic activity, thereby shaping the organ systems that manifest disease and the age at which they appear.
