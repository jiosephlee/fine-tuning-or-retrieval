Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Clinical Pearls] What clinical features in this case should prompt the clinician to consider a telomere biology disorder, even in the absence of the classic dyskeratosis congenita mucocutaneous triad?

**Clinical Pearls – When to suspect a Telomere‑biology Disorder (TBD) in the absence of the classic dyskeratosis‑congenita mucocutaneous triad**

The case of the young woman with a mono‑allelic *PARN* miss‑sense variant illustrates that TBDs can present with a “multisystem‑failure” phenotype that is readily mistaken for isolated nutritional, autoimmune or infectious disease. The following clinical clues should raise a red flag for a telomere‑biology disorder, even when the oral leukoplakia‑nail‑skin triad is missing:

| **Feature** | **Why it matters in a TBD** | **How it appeared in the case** |
|------------|-----------------------------|---------------------------------|
| **Premature‑aging signs** – e.g., early greying, loss of hair pigment, premature cataracts, early‑onset osteoporosis | Telomere attrition limits replicative capacity of stem cells in melanocytes, osteoblasts and other rapidly turning tissues, producing an accelerated‑aging phenotype. | The patient had **premature greying of hair** and **osteoporosis** with fragility fractures. |
| **Unexplained cytopenias or bone‑marrow failure** – pancytopenia, low reticulocyte response, macro‑ or micro‑cytosis not fully explained by nutrition | Hematopoietic stem cells are highly dependent on telomere maintenance; short telomeres cause early senescence → aplastic‑type marrow failure. | **Pancytopenia** with a low reticulocyte count persisted despite correction of B12/folate deficiency. |
| **Fibrosis of high‑turnover solid organs** – idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis, renal fibrosis | Fibro‑proliferative repair is a common end‑point when epithelial progenitors (lung alveolar type II, hepatocytes) cannot regenerate because of telomere shortening. | **Diffuse ground‑glass change with sub‑pleural fibrosis** on CT and **grade‑II hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis** on elastography. |
| **Neurologic or peripheral‑nerve involvement** – sensorimotor polyneuropathy, dorsal column disease | Though less specific, neuropathy may reflect impaired regeneration of Schwann cells or vascular supply in a setting of systemic telomere dysfunction. | **Asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy** on EMG/NCV studies. |
| **Skeletal/axial abnormalities** – scoliosis, thoracic cage deformities, short stature | Developmental growth plates are telomere‑sensitive; skeletal dysplasia is reported in Hoyeraal‑Hreidarsson and related TBDs. | **Thoracic scoliosis** noted on examination. |
| **Mucocutaneous changes that are subtle or atypical** – knuckle hyper‑pigmentation, tongue atrophy, mild nail dystrophy | The classic DC triad is often incomplete; isolated pigmentary changes or mucosal atrophy may be the only skin clues. | **Knuckle hyper‑pigmentation** and a **bald tongue with atrophic lingual papillae** were present, yet no nail dystrophy or leukoplakia. |
| **Disproportionate severity of a single abnormality** – severe B12 deficiency with only modest dietary risk, or liver disease without viral/autoimmune serology | A disproportionate phenotype suggests an underlying susceptibility (e.g., marrow or hepatocyte turnover limited by telomere length). | **Severe B12 deficiency** and **fibrotic liver** persisted despite normal gastrointestinal work‑up. |
| **Family history may be absent** – de‑novo or low‑penetrance inheritance | Many TBDs are autosomal‑dominant with incomplete penetrance; a negative family history does not exclude the diagnosis. | No prior family history, yet a **mono‑allelic *PARN* variant** was identified. |

### How these clues guided the diagnosis in the presented case

1. **Cluster of “premature‑aging” findings** (early greying, osteoporosis, scoliosis) prompted the team to look beyond isolated nutritional deficiency.
2. **Multisystem fibrosis** (lung + liver) in a 20‑s‑something patient is atypical; when infectious, alcoholic, or autoimmune etiologies are excluded, a telomere‑related fibrotic process becomes a leading consideration.
3. **Persistent marrow failure** despite vitamin replacement indicated an intrinsic stem‑cell defect, a hallmark of TBDs.
4. **Subtle mucocutaneous signs** (knuckle hyper‑pigmentation, tongue atrophy) provided the minimal dermatologic clue that often precedes recognition of DC‑spectrum disorders.
5. **Comprehensive genetic testing** (clinical exome sequencing) uncovered a pathogenic‑compatible *PARN* missense change, confirming the suspicion.

### Take‑home message for the bedside clinician

> **When a young adult presents with two or more of the following—premature aging signs, unexplained cytopenias, organ fibrosis (lung, liver, kidney), subtle mucocutaneous changes, or skeletal deformities—consider a telomere‑biology disorder, even if the classic dyskeratosis‑congenita triad is absent. Early genetic testing (NGS panel for telomere‑maintenance genes) and telomere length measurement are warranted, because diagnosis influences surveillance (pulmonary, hepatic, hematologic) and therapeutic planning (avoidance of DNA‑damaging agents, timely referral for stem‑cell or organ transplantation).**
