Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Pathophysiology] How does telomere shortening contribute to hepatic fibrosis, and why might liver involvement be under‑recognized in patients with TBDs?

**Pathophysiology – How telomere shortening drives hepatic fibrosis, and why liver disease is often missed in telomere‑biology disorders (TBDs)**  

1. **Telomere attrition limits hepatocyte replicative reserve**  
   - Hepatocytes, like bone‑marrow progenitors, are highly proliferative when the liver is injured. Each cell division shortens telomeres; when the telomere length falls below a critical threshold, the DNA‑damage response activates p53‑dependent cell‑cycle arrest (senescence) or apoptosis.  
   - In TBDs, germ‑line defects in telomerase components (TERT, TERC, PARN, etc.) impair telomere replenishment. Consequently, even modest or physiologic liver turnover rapidly exhausts hepatocyte replicative capacity, leaving a proportion of cells unable to replace lost parenchyma after injury.

2. **Senescent hepatocytes become profibrotic “signal amplifiers”**  
   - Senescent cells adopt a senescence‑associated secretory phenotype (SASP) rich in cytokines (IL‑6, IL‑8, TGF‑β), chemokines, and matrix‑remodelling enzymes.  
   - TGF‑β is the master driver of hepatic stellate‑cell (HSC) activation. Once activated, HSCs transdifferentiate into myofibroblasts that deposit type‑I collagen and other extracellular‑matrix proteins, producing the histologic appearance of fibrosis and, with progressive accumulation, cirrhosis.  
   - Thus, telomere‑driven hepatocyte senescence is an upstream trigger that converts a simple regenerative insult into a self‑sustaining fibrotic cascade.

3. **Why liver involvement is under‑recognized in TBDs**  
   - **Clinical focus on classic “DC triad”** – Traditional teaching stresses oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticulate skin hyperpigmentation. When these are absent, clinicians often prioritize bone‑marrow failure or pulmonary fibrosis, overlooking the liver.  
   - **Subclinical or slowly progressive fibrosis** – Fibrosis can be asymptomatic for years; routine liver function tests may be modestly abnormal, and imaging is not always obtained unless jaundice or portal hypertension appears. In the presented case, hepatic stiffness was discovered only after elastography was performed because of hepatomegaly; without that targeted test, the cirrhosis could have been missed.  
   - **Overlap with more common etiologies** – Hepatic steatosis, alcohol use, viral hepatitis, and autoimmune disease are far more prevalent. In a young adult with pancytopenia and B12 deficiency, it is natural to attribute liver enzyme elevation to nutritional or drug‑induced injury, rather than to an inherited telomere defect.  
   - **Variable penetrance and organ‑specific thresholds** – The same telomere length may be sufficient for marrow or lung homeostasis but insufficient for hepatic regeneration, depending on individual exposure (e.g., low‑grade inflammation, metabolic stress). Hence, liver disease can appear in isolation or later in the disease course, making pattern recognition difficult.

4. **Evidence from the case**  
   - The patient carried a **monoallelic PARN missense variant** (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg). PARN is essential for maturation of the telomerase RNA component (TERC); loss‑of‑function reduces telomerase activity, shortens telomeres, and predisposes to organ fibrosis.  
   - **Transient elastography** demonstrated liver stiffness of 18.1 kPa, consistent with cirrhosis, despite negative autoimmune serologies and the absence of viral hepatitis – a classic “exclusion” pattern that should raise suspicion for a telomere‑driven process.  
   - Literature cited in the report (Aachen Telomere Registry) shows **≈90 %** of adults with pathogenic TERT/TERC mutations have at least moderate hepatic fibrosis, underscoring that liver disease is a frequent but under‑appreciated component of TBDs.

5. **Take‑home clinical pearls**  

| Point | Teaching message |
|------|-------------------|
| **Mechanism** | Short telomeres → hepatocyte senescence → SASP (especially TGF‑β) → hepatic stellate‑cell activation → fibrosis. |
| **Recognition** | In any young adult with multisystem premature‑aging features (pancytopenia, pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying) who also has unexplained liver stiffness or cirrhosis, think TBD even if mucocutaneous signs are absent. |
| **Work‑up** | Obtain telomere length by flow‑FISH or qPCR; proceed to targeted NGS panel for telomere‑maintenance genes (including PARN). |
| **Management** | No disease‑modifying therapy yet; early identification allows surveillance (serial elastography, pulmonary function testing), avoidance of hepatotoxic drugs, and timely referral for transplant when organ failure progresses. |

In summary, telomere shortening compromises the liver’s regenerative capacity, and senescent hepatocytes orchestrate a profibrotic microenvironment. Because liver disease can be silent and because classic DC skin/mucosal findings are often absent, hepatic fibrosis is frequently missed in TBDs. Vigilance for liver involvement—especially when other telomere‑related manifestations are present—enables earlier diagnosis, appropriate monitoring, and optimal multidisciplinary care.
