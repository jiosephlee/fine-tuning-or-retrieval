Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Management] What ethical considerations should be addressed during genetic counseling for a de novo monoallelic PARN variant in a patient without a family history of telomere biology disorders?

**Ethical Issues to Cover in Genetic Counseling for a De novo Monoallelic PARN Variant (VUS) in a Young Adult without a Family History**

1. **Disclosure of Uncertainty**  
   - *What to say*: Explain that the identified PARN missense change (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) is classified as a **variant of uncertain significance (VUS)**. The laboratory data, in‑silico predictions, and the patient’s phenotype are suggestive, but there is no definitive proof that this allele is pathogenic.  
   - *Why it matters*: Patients have a right to understand the limits of what the test can tell them. As in the case report, the clinicians used the VUS together with the clinical picture to *support* a diagnosis of a telomere‑biology disorder (TBD), but they also emphasized that the causality remains unproven. Transparency prevents false reassurance or undue anxiety.

2. **Implications of a Possible De novo Mutation**  
   - *What to say*: Emphasize that the variant appears **de novo** (not inherited from either parent) because there is no known family history of TBDs and parental testing (if offered) was negative. A de novo event does not eliminate the risk to offspring; the patient herself can transmit the allele to future children.  
   - *Why it matters*: Reproductive decision‑making (natural conception, pre‑implantation genetic testing, prenatal diagnosis) hinges on whether the variant can be inherited. The case underscores that the absence of a family history does not guarantee non‑hereditary disease.

3. **Reproductive Counseling and Autonomy**  
   - Discuss options:  
     - **Natural conception** with the understanding that each child has a 50 % chance of inheriting the variant.  
     - **Pre‑implantation genetic testing for monogenic disease (PGT‑M)** to select embryos without the variant.  
     - **Prenatal testing** (chorionic villus sampling or amniocentesis) if pregnancy occurs.  
   - Emphasize *autonomy*: the patient decides which (if any) approach aligns with her values, risk tolerance, and reproductive goals. The counseling session should be nondirective, providing balanced information about benefits, limitations, and the fact that, because the variant is a VUS, predictive value for disease severity in a child is uncertain.

4. **Psychosocial Impact and Stigmatization**  
   - Acknowledge possible feelings of guilt, isolation, or “being “different”* because of a genetic label that lacks a clear prognosis. The case report noted the patient’s early‑aged greying, neuropathy, and organ fibrosis—features that can be socially stigmatizing. Offer referral to mental‑health services and patient support groups (e.g., telomere disease registries, rare‑disease networks).  

5. **Confidentiality and Disclosure to Relatives**  
   - Even though the variant appears de novo, recommend that the patient consider **offering testing to first‑degree relatives** (parents, siblings) to confirm de novo status and to identify any asymptomatic carriers.  
   - Explain the ethical tension: the patient’s right to privacy vs. the potential health benefit to relatives who might be at risk. Counsel that she may encourage them to be tested, but she is not obligated to disclose her results without consent.

6. **Clinical Management vs. Genetic Information**  
   - Clarify that **clinical surveillance** (regular pulmonary, hepatic, hematologic monitoring) is indicated *independently* of the genetic result because her phenotype already warrants it. The VUS does not change current management, but it may influence future therapeutic choices (e.g., eligibility for telomere‑targeted trials, consideration of bone‑marrow transplantation).  
   - Reinforce that the genetic finding is a *piece* of the puzzle, not the sole driver of care decisions—mirroring how the authors of the case used the VUS to guide counseling but continued supportive treatment.

7. **Future Re‑classification**  
   - Inform the patient that VUS classifications are **dynamic**; as more data accumulate (additional cases, functional assays, population frequency updates), the variant may be re‑classified as pathogenic or benign. Offer a plan for periodic re‑contact (e.g., annually or when the laboratory issues an update).  

8. **Informed Consent for Research Participation**  
   - Because rare TBDs are under‑studied, invite the patient to participate in registries or research protocols that could provide functional data on the variant. Discuss the ethical considerations of contributing personal health information, potential benefits to the wider community, and any risks of data misuse.

**Bottom‑line for the Learner:**  
When counseling a patient with a *de novo monoallelic PARN VUS*—as in the presented young woman—balance honest communication of uncertainty with respect for autonomy, proactive reproductive planning, psychosocial support, and a clear distinction between genetic findings and current clinical management. Maintaining this ethically sound framework ensures the patient is empowered to make informed decisions while minimizing harm.
