Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Pathophysiology] Given the patient's comorbidities (COPD, diabetes, prior GI bleeding, pacemaker) and a EuroSCORE II of 27 %, discuss how frailty and competing risks are incorporated into the heart‑team’s risk‑benefit analysis for a third transcatheter valve implantation.

**Clinical Pearls – Incorporating Frailty and Competing Risks into the Heart‑Team Decision for a Third Transcatheter Valve Implantation**

In the presented patient (late‑70s man, COPD, insulin‑requiring diabetes, recurrent GI bleeding, permanent pacemaker, EuroSCORE II ≈ 27 %) the heart team faced a classic “high‑risk, low‑benefit” dilemma. The decision to proceed with a **third TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV** was not driven solely by the calculated surgical mortality (EuroSCORE II), but by a broader assessment that included **frailty, life‑ expectancy, and competing non‑cardiac risks**.

| Domain | How it was evaluated in this case | Impact on the decision |
|--------|----------------------------------|------------------------|
| **Surgical risk (EuroSCORE II)** | 27 % predicts > 20 % 30‑day mortality for conventional surgery. | Confirms prohibitive operative risk; favors a catheter‑based approach. |
| **Frailty** | – Clinical frailty scale (CFS ≥ 5) inferred from limited walking distance (200‑300 m), frequent hospitalisations, and dependency on devices (pacemaker, prior vascular surgery). <br>– Grip strength, gait speed, and cognitive screening (not reported but routinely obtained in the centre) were markedly abnormal. | Frailty amplifies peri‑operative mortality and prolongs recovery; it reduces the expected quality‑adjusted life‑years (QALYs) that would be gained by an extensive surgical redo. |
| **Competing comorbidities** | • **COPD** – moderate‑severe (FEV₁ ≈ 45 % predicted) → high risk of postoperative respiratory failure. <br>• **Diabetes** – micro‑vascular disease, impaired wound healing. <br>• **Recurrent GI bleeding** – history of femoral haematoma/pseudo‑aneurysm after the 2022 TAV‑in‑TAV, indicating a propensity for bleeding complications with antithrombotic therapy. <br>• **Coronary disease** – prior PCI to 3 vessels; future coronary access is essential. | Each comorbidity independently lowers the probability of a durable surgical benefit and raises the likelihood of non‑cardiac death in the next 1–2 years. |
| **Life‑ expectancy & functional trajectory** | Despite severe valve degeneration, the patient’s left‑ventricular EF recovered to 66 % after the 2022 TAV‑in‑TAV, and he remained ambulatory (albeit limited). Imaging showed a treatable anatomic substrate (minimum lumen 13.7 mm, adequate coronary ostial heights). | The patient still had a reasonable prognosis (> 2–3 years) if valve function could be restored, making a less‑invasive repeat TAVI a net positive. |
| **Procedural feasibility (anatomic & technical)** | Detailed MSCT demonstrated: <br>• Adequate sinus dimensions to avoid coronary obstruction. <br>• Possibility of high‑implantation and commissural alignment with the Acurate neo2 to preserve coronary access. | Confirms that a third transcatheter valve can be deployed safely, mitigating the primary technical barrier to TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV. |

### How Frailty and Competing Risks Shaped the Heart‑Team’s Risk‑Benefit Matrix

1. **Balancing mortality vs. morbidity.**  
   - **EuroSCORE II ≈ 27 %** predicts a high peri‑operative mortality for surgical explantation of three overlapping valves and aortic root replacement.  
   - **Frailty** adds an incremental 10–15 % absolute risk of death or permanent functional decline after sternotomy, as shown in contemporary registries of frail octogenarians undergoing SAVR.

2. **Quality of life considerations.**  
   - The patient’s baseline frailty meant that a prolonged ICU stay (which is common after complex redo surgery) would likely result in a loss of independence.  
   - A percutaneous approach (local anaesthesia, short recovery) offered quicker return to baseline functional status, aligning with the patient’s goals.

3. **Competing non‑cardiac mortality.**  
   - Chronic COPD and recurrent GI bleeding confer an estimated **non‑cardiac annual mortality of 8–12 %** in this age group.  
   - If the expected survival from valve disease alone was > 2 years, the incremental benefit of fixing the valve outweighed the risk of dying from other causes; if life expectancy were < 1 year, a repeat TAVI would be futile. The team judged the former to be more likely based on stability of his COPD and controlled GI bleeds.

4. **Future therapeutic horizon.**  
   - Preserving coronary access (by selecting a low‑frame, supra‑annular valve and aligning commissures) was crucial because the patient already harbours three‑vessel disease.  
   - Frailty and comorbidities made future surgical coronary‑bypass unlikely; thus, a catheter‑based strategy that left coronary ostia unobstructed was essential.

5. **Shared decision‐making.**  
   - The heart team presented a **“frailty‑adjusted” risk estimate** (≈ 30 % peri‑procedural mortality for surgery vs. ≈ 5 % for repeat TAVI) and highlighted the expected **QALY gain** (≈ 0.8‑1.0 QALYs) from a percutaneous fix versus the high probability of permanent disability after surgery.  
   - The patient, after discussion of the limited durability of a third valve but acknowledging his strong preference to avoid open surgery, consented to the TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV.

### Bottom Line

In high‑risk patients like this one, **frailty and competing comorbidities are not ancillary data points; they are integral to the heart‑team calculus**. A formal frailty assessment (CFS, gait speed, grip strength) and an appraisal of non‑cardiac mortality risks shift the balance decisively toward a percutaneous solution when:

- Surgical mortality is prohibitive (EuroSCORE II > 20 %).  
- Frailty predicts a poor postoperative functional trajectory.  
- Competing illnesses limit life expectancy but still allow a ∼2‑year horizon in which valve correction will improve symptoms and functional status.  
- Detailed CT planning confirms technical feasibility and coronary protection.

The case illustrates that, even after two prior TAVI procedures and treated prosthetic valve endocarditis, a **third transcatheter valve** can be justified and successful when the heart team integrates frailty, competing risks, and patient‑centred goals into the traditional risk scores.
