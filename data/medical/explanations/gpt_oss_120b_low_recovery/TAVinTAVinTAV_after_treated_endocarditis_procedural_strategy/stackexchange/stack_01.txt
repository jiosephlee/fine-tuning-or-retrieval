Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Clinical Pearls] What are the key anatomic measurements obtained from pre‑procedural multislice CT that determine the feasibility of a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV procedure and how do they influence the risk of coronary obstruction?

**Clinical Pearls – Pre‑procedural CT Planning for a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV**

When a third transcatheter valve must be placed inside two previously implanted devices, multidetector CT (MDCT) is the sole imaging modality that can answer three fundamental questions:

1. **Will the new valve expand adequately (i.e., is there enough residual lumen)?**  
2. **Will the coronary ostia remain patent after the new frame and its “neo‑skirt” are deployed?**  
3. **Can future coronary access be preserved?**

The case of the 70‑year‑old gentleman who received a Lotus → Evolut → Acurate neo2 sequence illustrates how these measurements are obtained and interpreted.

| **CT‑derived measurement** | **Why it matters for a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV** | **Critical cut‑offs (approximate, based on current consensus and the present case)** |
|----------------------------|-------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Annular perimeter / minimum inner diameter of the most‐proximal stent (the “landing zone”)** | Determines the size of the third valve that can be safely implanted without excessive oversizing that would crack the previous frame or cause underexpansion. | Minimum inner diameter ≥ 13 mm (the Evolut frame measured 13.7 mm in the case). Oversizing ≤ 20 % of this lumen is generally accepted. |
| **Sinus of Valsalva (SOV) height and diameter** | The SOV provides the “cushion” for the leaflets of the new valve and for the neo‑skirt that forms when the frame expands. A shallow sinus predisposes to coronary cover. | SOV height ≥ 10 mm and mean SOV diameter ≥ 23 mm are protective; in the case the SOV was sufficient to accommodate the Acurate neo2 supra‑annular leaflets. |
| **Coronary ostial height (distance from annular plane to ostium)** | Low ostia (<10 mm) are at greatest risk of being blocked by the displaced leaflets or by the neo‑skirt of the new valve. | Height ≥ 12 mm is considered low‑risk. The patient’s ostia were >12 mm, permitting implantation without coronary protection. |
| **Valve‑to‑coronary (V‑C) distance** – measured from the outer edge of the existing frame (Evolut) to each coronary ostium | Directly predicts the likelihood that the third valve’s frame or its skirt will encroach on the coronaries. | V‑C distance ≥ 4–5 mm after simulated implantation is the practical safety margin. In the case, CT modelling showed >5 mm after positioning the Acurate neo2 at the Evolut leaflets level. |
| **Frame‑to‑frame interaction (overlap depth and crossing angle)** | Overlapping frames can create a “double‑skirt” that reduces the effective orifice and can trap the ostia. Understanding the geometry guides the choice of a low‑profile valve and the optimal implantation depth. | The Evolut’s supra‑annular frame and the Lotus’s intra‑annular frame created a stacked configuration; CT demonstrated that a high implantation of the Acurate neo2 (aligned with Evolut leaflets) would keep the neo‑skirt above the coronary ostia. |
| **Projected neo‑skirt height** (derived from the selected valve’s design and simulated deployment) | The neo‑skirt is the portion of the frame that will sit just distal to the annulus; if it lies below the coronaries, obstruction can occur. | For self‑expanding supra‑annular devices (Acurate neo2), the neo‑skirt is ≈ 7 mm. Planning ensured that this skirt would be above the measured V‑C distance. |
| **Coronary access angle and “cell” availability** (virtual cannulation analysis) | Even if the ostia are not occluded, the mesh of three overlapping frames may prevent catheter passage. CT can identify a “clear cell” at the expected fluoroscopic working angle. | In the case, CT identified a cell in the Evolut frame that remained visible after the Acurate deployment, guiding the decision to forego prophylactic coronary wiring. |

### How These Measurements Influence Coronary Obstruction Risk

1. **Low ostial height + shallow sinus** → high probability that the displaced leaflets of the third valve will seal the coronary orifice (“coronary obstruction”).  
2. **Insufficient V‑C distance** → the neo‑skirt will physically cover the ostium.  
3. **Small residual lumen** → excessive oversizing is needed, which pushes the frame outward and can “pinch” the ostia.  
4. **Unfavourable frame overlap** (e.g., two intra‑annular frames beneath a supra‑annular one) → creates a narrow conduit for blood, increasing the chance of obstruction and making future coronary catheterisation difficult.

In the presented patient, CT showed:
- A minimum Evolut lumen of 13.7 mm (adequate for a 23 mm Acurate neo2).  
- Coronary ostial heights >12 mm and V‑C distances >5 mm after simulated high implantation.  
- A generous sinus of Valsalva that accommodated the supra‑annular leaflets without impinging on the coronaries.  

These findings allowed the heart team to proceed **without coronary protection** and to plan a **high, commissure‑aligned implantation** that preserved both coronary flow and future access.

### Take‑home Messages

- **Never rely on fluoroscopy alone**; a thin‑slice MDCT with 3‑D reconstructions is mandatory for any repeat TAVR, especially a third valve.  
- **Measure the residual inner diameter of the most proximal frame** – if < 13 mm, consider valve fracture, BASILICA, or surgical rescue.  
- **Always calculate coronary ostial height and V‑C distance after simulated implantation**; a safety margin of ≥ 4–5 mm is the current benchmark.  
- **Plan the implantation depth to keep the neo‑skirt above the coronaries**; high implantation aligned with the leaflets of the immediate prior valve is often the safest strategy.  
- **Assess future coronary access** by virtual cannulation; if no clear cell remains, be prepared for coronary protection or alternative revascularisation techniques.

By systematically applying these CT‑derived anatomic criteria, the feasibility of a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV can be reliably judged, and the risk of catastrophic coronary obstruction can be minimized—as demonstrated by the successful triple‑valve intervention in this case.
