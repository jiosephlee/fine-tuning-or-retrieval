Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Pathophysiology] What are the major anatomic and procedural factors that increase the risk of coronary artery obstruction during a TAV‑in‑TAV (or TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV) procedure, and how can pre‑procedural CT imaging be used to mitigate this risk?

**Coronary‑artery obstruction in TAV‑in‑TAV (and TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV) – why it happens and how CT protects you**

When we “stack” trans‑catheter valves, the anatomy that protected the coronaries after the first implant is altered. In the present case – a 70‑year‑old man who had a Lotus valve, then an Evolut, and finally an Acurate neo2 – the heart team relied on multidetector computed tomography (MDCT) to anticipate and avoid a coronary‑obstruction catastrophe. The same principles apply to every repeat TAVR.

---

### 1. Anatomic factors that raise the obstruction risk

| Factor | Why it matters | Typical high‑risk values (≈ cut‑offs) |
|--------|----------------|--------------------------------------|
| **Low coronary‑ostial height** | The newly implanted valve or the “neo‑skirt” of the previous frame can seal the ostium when the leaflets are displaced upward. | < 10 mm (right coronary artery, RCA); < 12 mm (left coronary artery, LCA) from the annular plane. |
| **Small sinus of Valsalva (SOV) diameter** | A narrow sinus leaves little room for the displaced leaflets of the outer valve; the leaflets can “wall‑off” the ostium. | < 30 mm (average across all three sinuses). |
| **Short virtual valve–coronary distance (V‑C distance)** | After a TAV‑in‑TAV the effective height of the outer leaflets is the sum of the inner valve frame height + leaflets. If the distance from the outer leaflet tip to the coronary ostium is < 4 mm, the ostium is at immediate risk. | V‑C distance < 4 mm (often derived from CT‑derived “coronary clearance”). |
| **High frame/leaflet bulk of the first valve** | Tall, supra‑annular frames (e.g., Lotus, Evolut) and thick leaflets create a larger “cage” that can be pushed into the sinus by the second valve. | Stent height > 20 mm; leaflets extending > 6 mm above annulus. |
| **Mismatched valve sizes (undersizing)** | An undersized second valve does not expand fully, leaving gaps that allow the inner frame to protrude into the sinus and obstruct flow. | 10‑15 % undersizing relative to annular perimeter. |
| **Presence of valve‑to‑coronary scallop alignment** | If the commissural posts of the outer valve land directly in front of a coronary ostium, the fabric or metal struts may block access. | No favorable “fast‑path” alignment on CT. |
| **Calcific raphe or bulky leaflet calcification** | Rigid calcified leaflets act as a lever, pulling the new valve’s skirt toward the coronary ostium when the valve is deployed. | Leaflet calcium volume > 200 mm³ in the sinus region. |

In the case report, MDCT showed a *minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm* within the Evolut frame (indicating a very narrow residual orifice) and required careful measurement of the **coronary‑ostial height and sinus dimensions**. The team deliberately assessed **valve‑to‑coronary distance** to decide that a protection balloon or chimney stent was not needed, but that a **high implantation** and **commissural alignment** were essential.

---

### 2. Procedural factors that augment the risk

1. **Implantation depth** – Deploying the new valve too low (deep) pushes the outer leaflets into the sinus and shortens the V‑C distance.  
2. **Radial force of the new valve** – High‑force self‑expanding or balloon‑expandable devices may “crush” the inner frame, causing leaflets to flare outward.  
3. **Failure to align commissures** – Random rotation can place the outer frame struts directly over a coronary ostium, creating a mechanical barrier.  
4. **Absence of coronary protection** – Not wiring or placing a prophylactic balloon/stent when CT predicts a clearance < 4 mm.  
5. **Over‑dilation or aggressive post‑dilatation** – Excessive pressure can displace the inner leaflets upward, especially in a triple‑stented configuration.  

In the described case, the operators used a **high implantation technique** (aligning the Acurate neo2 at the level of the Evolut leaflets), **limited post‑dilatation pressure**, and **did not need coronary protection** because CT showed an adequate clearance after the planned positioning.

---

### 3. How pre‑procedural CT mitigates the danger

| CT‑derived measurement | How it guides the procedure |
|------------------------|------------------------------|
| **Coronary‑ostial height** (from annulus to ostium) | Determines whether a “low‑risk” (< 10 mm) or “high‑risk” strategy (wire + balloon/chimney) is required. |
| **Sinus of Valsalva diameter & volume** | Small sinuses flag the need for a supra‑annular valve with a low profile or for a valve fracture/leaflet laceration (BASILICA) before implant. |
| **Virtual valve‑to‑coronary (V‑C) distance** after simulated implantation (software overlay) | Predicts the final leaflet tip location; a V‑C < 4 mm triggers protection. |
| **Frame height and cell geometry of the existing valve(s)** | Enables selection of a valve whose cells align away from the coronary ostia (commissural alignment planning). |
| **Annular and LVOT dimensions** | Guarantees proper sizing to avoid undersizing (which could lead to mal‑expansion and obstructive leaflets). |
| **Calcification mapping** | Identifies bulky leaflets or raphe that could act as a “lever” on the new valve; may prompt BASILICA or valve fracture. |
| **3‑D reconstruction of the triple‑stented stack** | Allows simulation of different implantation depths and angles to find the safest “landing zone.” |

In the present report, **multiplanar CT reconstructions** demonstrated:

- Adequate coronary heights (≥ 12 mm) and sufficient sinus volume to accommodate a third valve.  
- A narrow residual lumen (13.7 mm) that dictated a **small‑size Acurate neo2 (size S)**.  
- Favorable **commissural alignment** possibilities – the Evolut’s leaflets served as a “template” for aligning the neo2, preserving future coronary access.  
- Sufficient **valve‑to‑coronary clearance** after simulated high implantation, allowing the team to forgo a protective coronary wire.

Thus, the CT plan turned a potentially high‑risk scenario into a **“no‑coronary‑protection”** case, while still ensuring a safe final result.

---

### 4. Practical take‑home points for the bedside

1. **Never assume safety after the first TAVR** – each additional valve adds bulk and reduces sinus space.  
2. **Obtain a dedicated cardiac CT** with thin‑slice (≤ 0.6 mm) acquisition, 3‑D reconstructions, and a valve‑simulation software whenever a redo TAVR is contemplated.  
3. **Measure all three coronary heights, sinus diameters, and V‑C distances**; if any V‑C < 4 mm or ostial height < 10 mm, plan coronary protection (wire + balloon or chimney stent).  
4. **Choose a valve with the lowest possible frame height and supra‑annular leaflets** (e.g., Acurate neo2, Evolut PRO) when sinus space is limited.  
5. **Aim for high, commissure‑aligned implantation** – use CT‑derived fluoroscopic “cusp‑overlap” views to reproduce the planned rotation.  
6. **Consider adjunctive techniques** (BASILICA, valve fracture) when CT predicts inevitable obstruction despite optimal valve choice.  

By integrating these anatomic and procedural insights, and by letting pre‑procedural CT drive the strategy, we can markedly lower the incidence of coronary‑obstruction in even the most complex TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV scenarios—just as was achieved in the case of the 70‑year‑old patient reported above.
