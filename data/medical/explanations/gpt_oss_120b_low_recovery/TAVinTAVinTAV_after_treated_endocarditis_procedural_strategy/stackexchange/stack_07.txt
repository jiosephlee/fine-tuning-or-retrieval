Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Differential Diagnosis] What are the relative advantages and disadvantages of using a self‑expanding supra‑annular valve (e.g., Acurate neo2) versus a balloon‑expandable valve for a third‑in‑series TAV implantation in a narrow annulus with prior overlapping stent frames?

**Answer – Self‑expanding supra‑annular (Acurate neo2) versus balloon‑expandable valve for a “TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV” in a narrow annulus with prior overlapping frames**

In the case presented, the heart team chose a self‑expanding, supra‑annular Acurate neo2 for the third transcatheter valve because the anatomy (13.7 mm minimum lumen in the Evolut frame) and the need to preserve coronary access made its design advantages outweigh its limitations. The same considerations apply to any repeat TAV in a narrow annulus that already contains two stent frames.

| **Feature** | **Self‑expanding supra‑annular (Acurate neo2)** | **Balloon‑expandable (e.g., Sapien 3/Ultra)** |
|-------------|-----------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------|
| **Leaflet position** | Supra‑annular – leaflets sit above the frame, giving a larger effective orifice area (EOA) in small annuli. In the case, this helped achieve an indexed AVA of 0.4 cm²/m² after implantation, which later improved to 1.1 cm². | Intra‑annular – leaflets are confined within the stent, which can worsen patient‑prosthesis mismatch (PPM) when the landing zone is already narrowed by two previous frames. |
| **Radial force** | Low‑to‑moderate, continuous outward force; can gently expand the previously implanted frame (no intentional frame fracture needed). Post‑dilatation in the case further enlarged the Evolut frame and lowered the mean gradient from 67 mm Hg immediately after deployment to 27 mm Hg at 1 month. | High initial radial force during balloon inflation; may achieve fuller expansion of a rigid prior frame but also carries a higher risk of annular rupture or coronary obstruction when the landing zone is heavily scarred. |
| **Frame height / profile** | Shorter nitinol frame; less mesh above the commissures, which is helpful for coronary re‑access in a triple‑stented root. The case authors purposely aligned the Acurate commissures with the Evolut leaflets to maximise coronary clearance. | Taller stainless‑steel or cobalt‑chromium frame; more struts sit above the coronary ostia, increasing the likelihood of future coronary access difficulty after three layers of metal. |
| **Deployability** | Re‑positionable and recapturable before full release – valuable when the landing zone is uncertain. In the reported procedure the valve was aligned on fluoroscopic landmarks derived from CT, allowing precise high implantation. | Once inflated, the valve cannot be repositioned; any mal‑position must be managed with secondary devices (e.g., valve‑in‑valve, valve‑fracture). |
| **Risk of coronary obstruction** | Supra‑annular design and low frame height reduce the “neo‑skirt” that can cover the ostia; CT showed adequate valve‑to‑coronary distance, and no coronary protection was required. | Balloon‑expandable frames often have a larger external skirt that may protrude into the sinuses, heightening the risk of obstruction when previous frames already reduce sinus volume. |
| **Hemodynamic performance in small annuli** | Generally superior mean gradients and larger indexed EOA because the orifice is not constrained by the stent. In this patient, the final mean gradient settled at 26 mm Hg (acceptable for a 70‑year‑old) despite three overlapping prostheses. | Tend to produce higher gradients in small annuli, especially when the effective orifice is limited by the inner frame of earlier valves. |
| **Potential for valve‑to‑valve interaction** | The nitinol frame can conform around the previous stent, limiting interface stress and decreasing the chance of frame fracture. | Rigid frame can press against prior metal, increasing the risk of fracture or conduction disturbances, particularly in a previously Lotus (mechanically fixed) followed by Evolut (self‑expanding). |
| **Conduction system disturbance** | Lower radial force → lower incidence of new‑onset left‑bundle branch block or need for permanent pacing (important in a patient already pacemaker‑dependent). | Higher radial force → higher rates of new conduction abnormalities; less relevant here but a consideration for future cases. |

### Practical take‑aways for a third‑in‑series TAV in a narrow annulus

1. **Effective orifice area is paramount** – With each additional frame the residual lumen shrinks; a supra‑annular valve maximises the EOA and mitigates severe PPM, which was evident in the case (AVA increased from 0.5 cm² to 1.1 cm² after the Acurate implant).

2. **Coronary access preservation** – Aligning the commissures of a short, low‑profile self‑expanding valve with the leaflets of the preceding valve reduces the cumulative mesh burden above the coronaries. This was a pre‑procedural CT‑guided strategy that prevented the need for coronary protection.

3. **Re‑positionability** – The ability to recapture and fine‑tune depth before release is especially valuable when the landing zone is defined by two overlapping frames whose exact inner diameter may be difficult to gauge.

4. **Radial force balance** – In a heavily scarred sinus, a moderate, continuous outward force (self‑expanding) is often safer than the high‑pressure burst of a balloon‑expandable device, which can cause annular rupture or disrupt the integrity of prior frames.

5. **Future re‑intervention** – If another valve will eventually be needed, the shorter height of the Acurate leaves more “vertical room” for subsequent devices and for coronary catheters. Balloon‑expandable frames accrue more metal height, potentially sealing off the sinus after the third layer.

### When a balloon‑expandable valve might still be preferred

- **Very calcified annulus with a rigid, narrow landing zone** where maximal immediate radial force is needed to fracture a severely stenotic prior frame.
- **Anatomy with high coronary ostia and generous sinus dimensions**, making obstruction unlikely; the higher frame may then be acceptable.
- **Operator preference or institutional experience** with specific balloon‑expandable devices that have proven low gradients in similar “valve‑in‑valve” scenarios (e.g., the newer Sapien 3 Ultra with a lower skirt profile).

### Bottom line

For a third TAV implanted into an already narrow annulus that already houses two overlapping stent frames, a self‑expanding supra‑annular valve such as the Acurate neo2 offers:

- Larger post‑implant EOA and lower gradients,
- Lower profile and better coronary clearance,
- Re‑positionability for optimal depth,
- Safer radial force profile in a heavily scarred root.

The primary disadvantage is the lower immediate radial force, which may limit the ability to fracture a severely calcified prior frame—something that can be mitigated by high‑pressure post‑dilatation, as was done in the case. Balloon‑expandable valves retain a role when maximal expansion force is required or when the anatomy is otherwise favorable.
