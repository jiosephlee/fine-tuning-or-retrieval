Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Management] Discuss the future considerations for coronary re‑access after multiple TAV‑in‑TAV procedures, including the impact of commissural alignment and frame design on the feasibility of subsequent percutaneous coronary interventions.

**Future coronary re‑access after serial TAV‑in‑TAV procedures – what the clinician must keep in mind**

The case of a 70‑year‑old man who has now received three transcatheter valves (Lotus → Evolut → Acurate neo2) illustrates the practical limits of “lifetime” aortic valve therapy. Each additional frame adds metal, reduces the sinus‑of‑Valsalva volume, and can compromise the geometry that permits a catheter to reach the coronary ostia. When we plan a second, third, or even a fourth TAV we must ask three questions that will determine whether future percutaneous coronary interventions (PCI) remain feasible:

| Question | Why it matters | How the case addressed it |
|----------|----------------|---------------------------|
| **1. What is the coronary‑to‑annular distance after the new valve is implanted?** | The *neo‑skirt* (the portion of the valve frame that lies below the coronary ostia) must be ≥ 4 mm to avoid immediate obstruction and to leave a “gate” for catheters. | Pre‑procedural CT measured coronary heights (≈13 mm) and sinus dimensions; the Acurate neo2 was deliberately positioned high, aligning its commissures with the Evolut leaflets so that the neo‑skirt sat **above** the coronary ostia. |
| **2. Is the frame design compatible with coronary re‑access?** | Low‑profile, supra‑annular devices (e.g., Acurate neo2, Evolut R/PRO) have shorter stent cells and fewer “meshes” obstructing the sinus compared with taller, intra‑annular valves (e.g., Lotus). | The third valve was chosen for its low frame height and for the fact that its “crown” can be aligned with the previous frame’s commissures, creating a predictable “window” through the overlapping mesh. |
| **3. Was commissural alignment achieved?** | When the commissures of each successive valve are coaxial, the stent struts line up, leaving a larger, straight path to the coronaries. Mis‑alignment creates a “cobweb” of wires that can block catheters even if the ostial height is adequate. | CT‑based 3‑D modelling was used to pre‑set the delivery catheter rotation; fluoroscopic landmarks confirmed alignment of the Acurate neo2 commissures with those of the Evolut valve. |

### Key take‑aways for future planning

1. **Start the conversation at the index TAV.**  
   - For patients < 80 yr or with known coronary disease, favor a valve that permits future coronary access (supra‑annular, low frame, commissure‑aligned).  
   - Record the intended commissural orientation and implant depth on the procedural report; this becomes the reference for any redo.

2. **Use high‑resolution CT for every redo.**  
   - Measure: (a) coronary ostial height, (b) sinus of Valsalva diameter, (c) distance from the outermost frame of the *most distal* valve to the ostia, and (d) the projected “cell” geometry at the intended landing zone.  
   - In this case, the minimal lumen of the Evolut frame (13.7 mm) and the sinus dimensions gave a safe margin for a high Acurate neo2 implantation.

3. **Select a valve that maximizes effective orifice area (EOA) while minimizing additional metal.**  
   - Acurate neo2 offers supra‑annular leaflets (larger EOA) and a relatively short frame, reducing the cumulative “metal burden.”  
   - Avoid adding a tall intra‑annular valve unless the sinus is markedly oversized; otherwise you risk severe prosthesis‑patient mismatch and complete loss of coronary access.

4. **Plan for a high implantation depth.**  
   - Deploy the new valve at the level of the prior valve leaflets rather than at the native annulus. This “seal‑against‑leaflet” technique preserves sinus space and limits the creation of a new neo‑skirt below the coronaries.  
   - The case report demonstrates that a high Acurate neo2 deployment prevented immediate coronary obstruction and left a clear corridor for future catheters.

5. **Consider adjunctive techniques when the sinus is marginal.**  
   - **BASILICA** (leaflet laceration) or **valve‐fracture** can enlarge the effective orifice and create a larger gap, but their safety in triple‑stented anatomy is still unproven.  
   - In the present patient, careful sizing and high implantation obviated the need for such maneuvers.

6. **Document the “coronary clearance” metric.**  
   - After each TAV, record the measured valve‑to‑coronary distance on the angiogram and store the 3‑D CT reconstruction. This objective data will guide the heart team when the next degeneration occurs.

### Practical algorithm for a patient who may need a fourth TAV

1. **CT‑based feasibility** → If coronary‑to‑frame distance ≥ 4 mm after virtual implantation of the next valve, proceed with a low‑profile, commissure‑aligned device.  
2. **If distance < 4 mm** →  
   - Discuss coronary protection (balloon/guidewire) during the procedure, or  
   - Consider **BASILICA** of the at‑risk leaflet, or  
   - Evaluate surgical options despite high risk (root replacement) because percutaneous access may be impossible.  
3. **If frame height would exceed sinus dimensions**, select a **short‑frame valve** (e.g., Evolut PRO+, Acurate neo2) and accept a higher implantation depth, even if it means a modest residual gradient.

### Bottom line

The case of TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV after treated endocarditis teaches that **coronary re‑access is a finite resource**. Each additional valve consumes sinus space and adds mesh; without deliberate planning—high implantation, low‑profile frames, and commissural alignment—we risk closing the corridor permanently. By incorporating CT‑guided measurements, choosing the appropriate valve geometry, and aligning commissures at every step, we preserve the ability to treat future coronary disease percutaneously, thereby extending the “lifetime” of transcatheter aortic valve therapy.
