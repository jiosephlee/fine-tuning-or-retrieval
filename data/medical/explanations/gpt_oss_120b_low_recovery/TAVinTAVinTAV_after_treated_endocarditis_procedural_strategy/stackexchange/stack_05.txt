Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Management] What is patient‑prosthesis mismatch (PPM), how is it quantified after TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV, and what procedural strategies can be employed to minimize severe PPM in narrow annuli?

**Patient‑prosthesis mismatch (PPM) in the setting of repeat transcatheter aortic valve implantation**

---

### What is PPM?

Patient‑prosthesis mismatch occurs when, after valve replacement, the *effective* orifice area (EOA) of the implanted prosthesis is too small for the patient’s body surface area (BSA). The result is a trans‑valvular gradient that is higher than expected for a normally functioning valve and can limit forward flow, provoke left‑ventricular hypertrophy, and adversely affect long‑term survival.

* **Severe PPM** is conventionally defined as an indexed EOA (iEOA) ≤ 0.65 cm²/m² (or ≤ 0.55 cm²/m² in the presence of left‑ventricular dysfunction).  
* **Moderate PPM**: iEOA 0.65–0.85 cm²/m².  

These thresholds, derived from surgical cohorts, are now applied to transcatheter valves because the same haemodynamic principle holds.

---

### How is PPM quantified after a **TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV**?

1. **Echocardiographic measurement** – The continuity equation remains the gold‑standard:  

   \[
   \text{EOA}= \frac{{\text{LVOT area} \times \text{LVOT VTI}}}{{\text{Aortic valve VTI}}}
   \]

   In the presented case, post‑procedure transthoracic echo (TTE) at 7 days showed an EOA of **0.9 cm²**; with a BSA of ~1.73 m² the iEOA was **0.52 cm²/m²**, fulfilling the definition of **severe PPM**.  

2. **Indexed Doppler‑velocity index (DVI)** – A DVI < 0.25 strongly suggests severe mismatch; the case reported a DVI of **0.24** immediately after the third valve.  

3. **Computed‑tomography (CT)‑derived effective orifice area** – Modern multidetector CT (MDCT) can reconstruct the neo‑sinus and directly measure the projected flow area. In complex “stacked” valve configurations, CT is especially valuable because it visualizes the cumulative luminal narrowing caused by overlapping stent frames. In this patient, pre‑procedural CT demonstrated a **minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm** within the Evolut frame—a critical predictor of residual EOA after a third valve.

4. **Trend over time** – Early gradients may be supraphysiologic because the new valve must expand against the rigid prior frames. Serial echocardiography (as in this case) showed a rapid decline in mean gradient from 67 mm Hg (day 0) to 27 mm Hg at 1 month, with a modest increase in AVA to 1.1 cm². The final iEOA of ~0.64 cm²/m² sits at the border of severe/moderate PPM, illustrating that “balloon post‑dilatation” and frame remodeling can improve the effective area.

---

### Procedural strategies to **minimise severe PPM** when the annulus is narrow (as in this patient)

| Strategy | Rationale | How it was applied (or could be applied) in the case |
|----------|-----------|------------------------------------------------------|
| **1. Choose a supra‑annular, low‑profile valve** | Supra‑annular leaflets sit **above** the stent frame, delivering a larger EOA for a given annular diameter. Low frame height preserves sinus space and coronary clearance. | The **Acurate neo2 (size S)** was selected for its supra‑annular leaflet position and relatively short frame, maximizing the residual orifice despite a 13.7‑mm lumen inside the Evolut. |
| **2. High (or “leaflet‑aligned”) implantation** | Deploying the new valve so its inflow edge coincides with the previous prosthesis leaflets pushes the leaflets of the older valve outward, preventing them from folding inward and further narrowing the neo‑orifice. | The team deliberately positioned the Acurate neo2 **at the level of the Evolut leaflets** (high implantation), which helped keep the Evolut leaflets open and limited additional reduction of the EOA. |
| **3. Commissural alignment** | Aligning the new valve’s commissures with the previous valve’s leaflets distributes the metal struts away from the central flow path, preserving the central cross‑sectional area and facilitating future coronary access. | Pre‑procedural CT modelling guided deployment so that the Acurate neo2’s commissures matched the Evolut leaflets, reducing “mesh‑over‑mesh” impingement and maintaining central flow. |
| **4. Aggressive post‑dilatation (high‑pressure balloon)** | Radial force applied after deployment can fracture the inner frame of the previous valve (bioprosthetic valve fracture) or at least expand the composite stent construct, enlarging the neo‑orifice. | A **20 mm × 40 mm balloon** was inflated under rapid pacing, yielding a 40 % reduction in mean gradient within the first month. (No intentional valve fracture was performed because the Evolut self‑expanding frame is less amenable to fracture, but high‑pressure dilation still achieved useful expansion.) |
| **5. Pre‑procedural CT‑based sizing and virtual valve implantation** | 3‑D reconstruction predicts the final neo‑sinus geometry, allowing the operator to select the smallest valve that will still achieve acceptable EOA and avoid coronary obstruction. | CT measured the **minimum lumen diameter (13.7 mm)** and guided the choice of a **size‑S Acurate neo2** (nominal inflow diameter ≈ 19 mm) that could be safely expanded without compromising coronaries. |
| **6. Consider “valve‑in‑valve fracture” when feasible** | In surgical bioprostheses, high‑pressure balloons can fracture the rigid frame, markedly increasing the final EOA. Newer self‑expanding TAVR frames are less fracture‑prone, but emerging low‑profile balloon technologies may permit selective frame expansion. | Not performed in this case because the Evolut frame is self‑expanding and less amenable to fracture; however, the authors discuss it as a future option if severe PPM recurs. |
| **7. Alternative catheter‑based leaflet modification (BASILICA, LAMPOON)** | Intentional laceration of the existing leaflets creates a larger central opening and reduces the risk of coronary obstruction, indirectly mitigating PPM. | The report mentions BASILICA as a theoretical rescue if coronary obstruction had been imminent, but it was not needed because pre‑procedural imaging showed adequate coronary clearance. |

---

### Take‑home points for the morning report

1. **PPM is a haemodynamic mismatch, not a procedural complication.** In repeat TAVR, the cumulative stent material can dramatically shrink the residual orifice; therefore, routine calculation of indexed EOA (or DVI) after each intervention is mandatory.

2. **CT is indispensable** for quantifying the *real* lumen available before a repeat valve is placed. In this case, the 13.7 mm minimal diameter signaled a high risk of severe PPM and dictated the need for a supra‑annular, low‑profile device and high implantation.

3. **Procedural planning must be proactive.** Selecting a valve with supra‑annular leaflets, aligning commissures, implanting high, and performing post‑dilatation are the main levers to preserve the effective orifice area in narrow annuli, especially when a third valve is being added.

4. **Early haemodynamic improvement may be modest**, but serial echocardiography often reveals a “gradient wash‑out” as the composite stent complex remodels. In this patient, the mean gradient fell from 67 mm Hg to 26 mm Hg over one year, with the iEOA improving from severe to borderline moderate PPM.

5. **Future‑proofing matters.** Even after a successful third TAVR, the cumulative frame burden will limit any further redo‑TAVR. The case underscores the importance of “lifetime management” discussions at the first TAVR—particularly in patients with small annuli or anticipated long survival.

**Bottom line:** In a narrow annulus with stacked transcatheter prostheses, severe PPM is expected unless the operator deliberately chooses a supra‑annular, low‑profile valve, plans a high‑leaflet‑aligned implantation, optimises post‑dilatation, and employs CT‑driven sizing. Meticulous haemodynamic assessment after each stage confirms whether the strategy succeeded and guides follow‑up.
