Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis Meets TAVR: When Is a Redo TAVR Safe?

## Why This Case Stands Out  

Imagine a 78‑year‑old gentleman who has already survived **two transcatheter aortic valve implantations (TAVIs)**, a **pulse‑purulent femoral pseudo‑aneurysm**, and a **treated prosthetic‑valve endocarditis (PVE)** caused by *Enterococcus faecalis*. Nine years after his first Lotus valve, his aortic valve is now a tower of three overlapping frames – a **TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV** construct. Yet, despite the anatomic nightmare, he is doing fine one year after the third percutaneous valve replacement.  

This scenario forces us to confront a question that is still being scripted in the guidelines: **When is it reasonable to go back to TAVR after a prosthetic valve infection?** The answer is not a simple “yes or no.” It depends on a checklist of infection control, imaging, timing, and the ever‑shifting balance between surgical risk and transcatheter feasibility. Below we walk through the pearls that the case of Merkulov et al. illustrates, and then expand to the broader evidence base.

## The Infection‑Control Checklist  

Redo TAVR after PVE is only conceivable when the infection is truly **quiescent**. The following elements should be confirmed before the Heart Team green‑lights a transcatheter redo:

1. **Completion of a full antimicrobial course** (usually 6 weeks of IV therapy for *Enterococcus* or *Staphylococcus* prosthetic infections).  
2. **Negative blood cultures** taken at least 48 h apart after therapy.  
3. **Absence of vegetations or other active lesions** on **trans‑esophageal echocardiography (TEE)**.  
4. **No perivalvular abscess, pseudo‑aneurysm, or fistula** on multimodality imaging (CT/MRI).  
5. **Stable inflammatory markers** (CRP, ESR) trending toward normal.  

In the reported case, the patient completed a 6‑week ampicillin‑based regimen, had three consecutive negative cultures, and repeat TEE/CT showed clean leaflets with no residual infection. This satisfied the infection‑control gate.

### Timing Matters  

The **optimal interval** between finishing antibiotics and attempting a redo TAVR is not set in stone, but most series report a **minimum of 4–6 weeks**. In practice, several factors influence the decision:

* **Clinical stability:** The patient must be euvolemic, with controlled heart failure symptoms.  
* **Hemodynamic urgency:** If valve gradients are life‑threatening, earlier intervention may be justified after confirming infection control.  
* **Imaging readiness:** A high‑resolution gated CT is required to map out the existing frames and coronary ostia; this may dictate scheduling.  

The case patient was re‑intervened **≈ 5 months** after completing antibiotics, giving ample time for tissue healing and for the multidisciplinary team to acquire and analyse the CT data.

## Imaging: The Bedrock of a Safe Redo  

When you already have two frames in place, the **effective orifice area (EOA)** shrinks dramatically, and the risk of **coronary obstruction** spikes. Multiphase, ECG‑gated **multislice CT (MSCT)** is non‑negotiable for:

* Measuring **coronary ostial height** and **sinus of Valsalva dimensions**.  
* Assessing **frame‐to‑coronary distance** after each prior valve – the “virtual valve‐to‑coronary” (VTC) metric must stay > 4 mm for safety.  
* Planning **implant depth** to align commissures and avoid the “neo‑skirt” covering the coronary ostia.  
* Simulating **post‑dilatation** to predict how much the inner frames will expand.  

In the described patient, the CT showed a **minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm** inside the Evolut frame – borderline for a 23 mm self‑expanding valve but acceptable for a **supra‑annular Acurate neo2** with a low frame profile. The team deliberately aligned the new valve’s commissures with the Evolut leaflets, preserving a corridor for future coronary access.

## Choosing Between Surgery and Redo TAVR  

### When Surgery Remains the Gold Standard  

* **Uncontrolled infection** (persistent bacteremia, abscesses).  
* **Extensive annular or root destruction** requiring reconstruction.  
* **Concomitant cardiac pathology** (mitral valve disease, LVOT obstruction) that needs open repair.  

### When Redo TAVR Wins the Day  

* **Prohibitive surgical risk** (EuroSCORE II > 25 % or frailty > 4).  
* **Favorable anatomy** after CT – adequate coronary clearance, enough sinus space, and a viable landing zone.  
* **Completed infection treatment** with clean imaging.  

The patient’s EuroSCORE II of **27 %**, prior extensive PCI, COPD, chronic GI bleeding, and the presence of two already firmly integrated frames shifted the risk‑benefit ratio heavily toward a percutaneous approach.

## Key Technical Pearls from the Triple‑Valve Case  

* **High implantation (“leaflet‑to‑leaflet” positioning)** – by nesting the new valve’s inflow at the level of the prior Evolut leaflets, the team maximized residual EOA and minimized the neo‑skirt obstructing the coronaries.  
* **Commissural alignment** – Acurate neo2’s design supports oriented deployment; aligning the new frame’s commissures with those of the older valve preserves a “coronary window”.  
* **Post‑dilatation strategy** – a 20/40 mm balloon under rapid pacing expanded both the new valve and helped radially open the inner Evolut frame, reducing the post‑procedure mean gradient from 67 mm Hg (immediate) to 27 mm Hg at one month.  
* **Coronary protection not needed** – pre‑procedural CT predicted > 6 mm clearance; intra‑procedural aortograms confirmed perfusion, so no “chimney” stents or BASILICA were required.  

These tactics collectively transformed a potentially catastrophic triple‑stent scenario into a durable solution.

## Take‑Home Messages  

- **Redo TAVR after prosthetic‑valve endocarditis is possible**, but only after stringent infection clearance (negative cultures, clean TEE/CT, normalized inflammatory markers).  
- **Multimodality CT is indispensable**; it guides valve choice, implantation depth, commissural alignment, and predicts coronary obstruction risk.  
- **High implantation and commissural alignment are the twin pillars** of a successful TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV procedure, especially in narrow sinuses.  
- **Surgical risk outweighing anatomic complexity tips the scales** toward a percutaneous redo, even in octogenarians with multiple prior interventions.  
- **Long‑term surveillance matters** – yearly echocardiography remains crucial as the residual effective orifice area may drift over time, and coronary access may become increasingly challenging.  

In the rapidly expanding world of structural heart disease, we are learning to think **“lifetime management”** from day one. The case of a triple‑layered TAVR after treated endocarditis reminds us that with meticulous imaging, a disciplined infection work‑up, and a tailored implantation strategy, a redo TAVR can be both safe and durable—even in the most tangled anatomies.  

**Bottom line:** When infection is truly gone, the aortic root is anatomically permissive, and surgical risk is prohibitive, a redo TAVR is not just a salvage maneuver; it can be a rational, evidence‑based choice that delivers years of symptom‑free life. Stay vigilant, keep your CT scanner calibrated, and let the Heart Team discuss all the “what‑ifs” before you push that third valve across the aortic annulus.
