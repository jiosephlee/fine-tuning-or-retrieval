Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# Vascular Complications in Serial TAVR: Lessons from a Post‑Procedural Femoral Pseudo‑aneurysm  

## Why this case grabbed my attention  

When we talk about TAVR, the focus is usually on valve sizing, coronary protection, or the dreaded patient‑prosthesis mismatch. Rarely do we pause to think about the “road” the device takes to get there. In this 70‑something gentleman the story took an unexpected turn after his **second TAVR** (a TAV‑in‑TAV with a 23 mm Evolut): a pulsatile femoral hematoma evolved into a **pseudo‑aneurysm** that required open vascular surgery.  

That one complication forced a complete rethink of his access strategy for the **third TAVR** (TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV) and underscores a principle that will become increasingly important as more patients live long enough to need multiple transcatheter interventions: **cumulative vascular trauma matters**.  

## The anatomy of the problem – what went wrong?  

- **First TAVR (2014) – 27 mm Lotus via right femoral** – uneventful.  
- **Second TAVR (May 2022) – 23 mm Evolut via left femoral** – after valve deployment the patient developed a rapidly expanding groin hematoma. Duplex confirmed a femoral pseudo‑aneurysm, ultimately managed with surgical repair.  
- **Third TAVR (June 2024) – left radial & left femoral** – the team deliberately avoided the previously compromised limb, opting for a radial approach for imaging and a contralateral (right) femoral puncture for device delivery.  

Key take‑aways from the pseudo‑aneurysm episode:  

1. **Sheath size matters** – The Evolut required a 14‑F sheath; the earlier Lotus had used a 22‑F system. Larger bore sizes increase the risk of arterial wall injury, especially in calcified, tortuous femoral arteries.  
2. **Pre‑procedural vessel assessment** – A comprehensive CT angiogram of the ilio‑femoral tree is essential, not only to choose the delivery route but also to document pre‑existing wall disease that may predispose to complications on repeat access.  
3. **Closure device selection** – In this case a Perclose ProGlide system was used. While generally safe, the combination of large sheath, heavy calcification, and cumulative punctures can exceed the device’s sealing capacity, leading to delayed bleeding and pseudo‑aneurysm formation.  

## Planning the next access – what we learned  

### 1. **Never assume the same side is “safe”**  

Even if a groin looks fine on the day of the procedure, prior punctures (or surgical repair) create scar tissue and loss of elasticity. In a patient slated for a third TAVR, the heart team should **re‑image both sides** and treat the previously used limb as a “high‑risk” access site unless a compelling reason exists to reuse it.  

### 2. **Consider alternative access early**  

- **Trans‑axillary (subclavian)** – Provides a relatively straight path for self‑expanding valves, avoids the heavily diseased femoral arteries, and can be closed percutaneously with a plug or suture device.  
- **Trans‑carotid** – Offers a short, low‑profile route, especially attractive now that low‑profile devices (e.g., Evolut Navitor, Acurate neo2) can be delivered through a 14‑F sheath.  
- **Trans‑radial for imaging (as done here)** – Using the radial artery for coronary/angiographic runs saves femoral “real estate” for the large delivery system.  

In this case the team used **left radial for angiography** and **right femoral for valve delivery**, effectively giving the injured left side a “rest period”.  

### 3. **Use the smallest possible sheath**  

Modern valves can now be implanted through 14‑F or even 12‑F systems (e.g., Lotus Edge, Navitor). For a patient with a history of femoral complications, **down‑sizing the sheath** can dramatically reduce the risk of further arterial injury.  

### 4. **Tailor closure strategy to the vessel**  

- **Pre‑closure with two Perclose devices** works well in healthy, non‑calcified femoral arteries but may fail in the presence of severe calcification or prior scar.  
- **Plug‑based closure (e.g., AngioSeal, MANTA for large‑bore)** may be preferable when the arterial wall is fragile. The MANTA device, designed for 18‑22 F access, offers a “zip‑per” like sealing that can be more reliable in heavily pre‑treated vessels.  
- **Hybrid approach** – In some cases, a surgical cut‑down with a felt‑reinforced closure stitch is the safest option, especially when the puncture site is near a bifurcation or when multiple prior closures have been placed.  

### 5. **Plan for bailout**  

Even with meticulous planning, complications can happen. A **low threshold for surgical consultation** and **immediate availability of a vascular surgeon** in the cath lab are critical. In the index case, the pseudo‑aneurysm was recognized early, but the decision to proceed to open repair meant having a ready OR team.  

## The bigger picture – cumulative vascular trauma  

As TAVR penetrates lower‑risk and younger populations, we are moving from a “single‑shot” mindset to a **lifetime management strategy**. Each access episode leaves a “footprint”:  

- **Intimal injury → neointimal hyperplasia → stenosis**  
- **Adventitial scarring → loss of compliance → higher puncture force needed**  
- **Calcific plaque destabilization → dissection or pseudo‑aneurysm**  

When you add a third or fourth procedure, those footprints can convert a “clean” vessel into a hostile corridor, limiting future options.  

**Practical steps for the heart team:**  

- **Baseline CT of the entire aorto‑iliac tree at the first TAVR** – store the data; it becomes the reference for all future re‑interventions.  
- **Map out a “vascular road map”** – mark which side was used, sheath size, closure device, and any complications.  
- **Future‑proofing** – When possible, use the side with the most favorable anatomy for the first procedure, leaving the contralateral side as a “reserve” for later.  
- **Regular vascular follow‑up** – Duplex ultrasound at 1 month and again at 6 months can detect early stenosis or pseudo‑aneurysm formation before a repeat TAVR is planned.  

## Take‑home pearls  

- **Large‑bore femoral access is the Achilles’ heel of serial TAVR**; a pseudo‑aneurysm after the second valve is a red flag that the femoral vessel may no longer be reliable.  
- **Pre‑procedural CT angiography is non‑negotiable** for every repeat TAVR; it informs sheath size, predicts coronary obstruction risk, and reveals subtle wall disease that may predispose to vascular complications.  
- **Choose the smallest feasible sheath and the most appropriate closure device**; when calcification or prior scarring is present, consider plug‑based or surgical closure rather than purely suture‑based systems.  
- **Always have a bailout plan** – a vascular surgeon on standby, a hybrid OR nearby, and a clear algorithm for converting a percutaneous approach to an open repair.  
- **Think longitudinally** – document the access site, complications, and closure method after each procedure. This “vascular ledger” guides future access decisions and can spare the patient from repeat groin catastrophes.  

In the era of “TAVR‑in‑TAVR‑in‑TAVR”, the humble femoral artery can become the limiting factor. By respecting the cumulative trauma it endures and tailoring our access strategy accordingly, we keep the pathway clear for the next life‑saving valve deployment.
