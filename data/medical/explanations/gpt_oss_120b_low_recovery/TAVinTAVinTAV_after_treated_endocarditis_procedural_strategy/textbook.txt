Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

## Clinical Presentation and Prior Therapeutic History  

The patient is a man in his late 70 years, who presented to the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center with exertional dyspnoea, a “spastic” sensation on the left side of the chest during walking (tolerating only 200–300 m), dizziness, and generalized fatigue. These symptoms reflected a progressive limitation of functional capacity (New York Heart Association class III) and prompted a focused cardiac evaluation.  

### Cardiovascular risk profile  

His baseline cardiovascular burden was considerable. Hypertension had been treated for many years, and he carried a history of three‑vessel coronary artery disease (CAD) with percutaneous coronary interventions (PCI) performed in 2009 (right coronary artery), 2010 (left anterior descending artery), 2012 (repeat LAD) and 2014 (circumflex artery). In 2014 he also required implantation of a permanent dual‑chamber pacemaker for third‑degree atrioventricular block. The presence of extensive coronary atherosclerosis, prior stenting, and a pacemaker significantly limited future surgical options and influenced the choice of percutaneous strategies throughout his course.  

### Non‑cardiac comorbidities that increased procedural risk  

The patient’s non‑cardiac comorbidities further heightened operative risk. Type 2 diabetes mellitus and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) reduced physiologic reserve and increased the likelihood of postoperative respiratory complications. Moreover, he had suffered recurrent gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding since 2022, a factor that rendered long‑term antithrombotic regimens (often required after surgical valve replacement) problematic. Taken together, these comorbidities produced a EuroSCORE II of >27 % and positioned him in a high‑risk surgical category, steering the heart team toward a transcatheter approach from the outset.  

### 2014 Lotus transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI)  

At age ~73, the patient exhibited severe native aortic stenosis with a valve area <0.8 cm² and a mean gradient >50 mm Hg. Given his prohibitive surgical risk, a transfemoral TAVI was performed using a 27‑mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific). The Lotus device is a mechanically expandable, repositionable, fully retrievable prosthesis with an outer frame that approximates the native annulus and a central leaflet that resides supra‑annularly. At implantation, the valve functioned well, with a post‑procedural mean gradient of 12 mm Hg and absent regurgitation. The patient’s functional class improved to NYHA II and his left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) remained stable at 54 %.  

### 2022 Evolut valve‑in‑valve (TAV‑in‑TAV)  

By December 2021, the patient’s symptoms recurred, and transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated severe bioprosthetic stenosis of the Lotus valve (mean gradient 83 mm Hg, LVEF 42 %). The rapid rise in gradient reflected structural valve deterioration—most commonly leaflet fibrosis and calcification in transcatheter bioprostheses after 7–8 years. Because redo surgical aortic valve replacement (AVR) remained high risk, the heart team elected a valve‑in‑valve (ViV) procedure in May 2022, implanting a 23‑mm self‑expanding Evolut R (Medtronic) within the existing Lotus frame. The Evolut’s supra‑annular design was chosen to maximize effective orifice area (EOA) in a small annular geometry.  

The procedure was technically successful, but the femoral access site was complicated by a pulsatile haematoma that progressed to a pseudo‑aneurysm. Surgical exploration was required for repair, underscoring how even low‑risk access complications can be magnified in frail patients. Hemodynamically, the patient benefited immediately: the mean gradient fell to 18 mm Hg, and LVEF recovered to 54 %, reflecting relief of after‑load and improved forward flow.  

### Prosthetic valve endocarditis (Enterococcus faecalis)  

Three months after the ViV, in September 2022, the patient presented with persistent fever, worsening dyspnoea, and a new murmur. Blood cultures grew *Enterococcus faecalis*, and transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) identified vegetations on the transcatheter leaflets with accompanying severe prosthetic restenosis (mean gradient 35 mm Hg). The diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) was made according to the modified Duke criteria.  

Standard therapy for *E. faecalis* PVE consists of a 6‑week course of intravenous ampicillin (or penicillin G) plus gentamicin; in this case gentamicin was avoided owing to renal concerns, and a high‑dose ampicillin regimen was employed, followed by an oral step‑down regimen per infectious‑disease guidance. Serial blood cultures became sterile, and repeat TEE at the end of therapy showed resolution of vegetations and no new abscess formation. The patient completed antimicrobial therapy without the need for chronic suppressive antibiotics.  

### Progressive prosthetic degeneration after infection  

Although the infection cleared, the structural integrity of the transcatheter leaflets had been compromised. By January 2024, repeat TTE revealed a mean trans‑valvular gradient of 49 mm Hg, an aortic valve area of 0.5 cm², and grade 2–3 intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation. The left ventricular ejection fraction had rebound to 66 % (reflecting compensated remodeling), but the hemodynamic severity of the stenosis and regurgitation produced renewed symptoms (dyspnoea on minimal exertion). Multislice computed tomography (MSCT) demonstrated a minimal lumen diameter of 13.7 mm within the Evolut frame, confirming severe geometric constriction that could not be relieved with medical therapy alone.  

In summary, the patient’s clinical trajectory is defined by three sequential aortic valve interventions driven by structural valve deterioration, each performed in the context of high surgical risk and complex comorbidities. The initial Lotus TAVI restored valve function for seven years; the Evolut ViV addressed early degeneration but was subsequently complicated by infective endocarditis, which further damaged the prosthetic leaflets. Persistent severe stenosis and moderate‑to‑severe regurgitation after infection set the stage for a third transcatheter implantation—an unprecedented TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV procedure—necessitating meticulous imaging‑guided planning to overcome the cumulative anatomic constraints of three overlapping valve frames.

## 2. Diagnostic Work‑up and Imaging Strategy

The decision to proceed with a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV) rests on a meticulous diagnostic work‑up that simultaneously confirms the cause of prosthetic dysfunction, excludes residual infection, and establishes that the aortic root can safely accommodate an additional valve frame. In this case, three complementary modalities—transthoracic echocardiography (TTE), transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE), and multidetector computed tomography (MDCT)—were integrated with microbiological testing to generate a comprehensive anatomic and physiologic picture.

### Transthoracic Echocardiography: Quantifying Hemodynamic Failure

TTE is the first‑line tool for detecting structural valve deterioration after TAVI. In the present patient, serial TTE examinations demonstrated a progressive rise in the mean transvalvular gradient from 22 mm Hg (post‑Lotus implantation) to 83 mm Hg (pre‑Evolut), and subsequently to 49 mm Hg after the Evolut‑in‑Lotus procedure. The most recent study (June 2024) recorded a mean gradient of 49 mm Hg, a calculated aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.5 cm², and an intraprosthetic regurgitation grade of 2–3. These measurements were obtained using the continuity equation, which required accurate left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) diameter (measured in the parasternal long‑axis view) and Doppler‑derived velocity‑time integrals (VTIs) from the LVOT and the prosthetic jet. The severe reduction in AVA together with the elevated gradient fulfilled the hemodynamic criteria for severe prosthetic stenosis.

In addition to gradient and AVA, color Doppler identified a central, symmetric regurgitant jet suggestive of intraprosthetic leaflet malfunction rather than a paravalvular leak. The echo images also visualised two concentric prosthetic frames (Lotus and Evolut) with limited acoustic windows, underscoring the need for cross‑sectional imaging to delineate the spatial relationship between the devices.

### Transoesophageal Echo: Ruling Out Ongoing Infection and Structural Complications

Given the recent episode of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) caused by *Enterococcus faecalis*, TEE was performed to exclude residual vegetations, abscesses, or new paravalvular complications that could contraindicate repeat transcatheter intervention. High‑resolution mid‑oesophageal and trans‑gastric views demonstrated clean leaflet surfaces of both the Lotus and Evolut prostheses, with no mobile echodensities, no peri‑annular thickening, and no evidence of a perivalvular or sinus‐of‑Valsalva abscess. The colour Doppler signal confirmed that the observed regurgitation originated from the central prosthetic orifice rather than from a breach at the annulus. Because TEE offers superior sensitivity (up to 95 %) for detecting small vegetations that TTE can miss, a negative study provided strong reassurance that the infection had been eradicated and that a transcatheter approach was not jeopardized by active endocarditis.

### Microbiological Confirmation: Blood Cultures and Inflammatory Markers

Parallel to imaging, three sets of peripheral blood cultures obtained 48 hours apart returned sterile, and serial measurements of C‑reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) had normalized (CRP < 5 mg/L, ESR < 10 mm/h). These laboratory data confirmed biochemical remission of the preceding PVE and satisfied the guideline requirement that a repeat valve procedure be deferred until at least three weeks of documented sterility after completion of targeted antimicrobial therapy.

### Multislice CT Protocol for Redo TAVI

MDCT is the cornerstone of procedural planning for any valve‑in‑valve (ViV) intervention, and its importance is amplified when a third valve must be accommodated within two pre‑existing frames. A dedicated cardiac‑gated, contrast‑enhanced protocol was employed:

| Parameter | Details |
|-----------|----------|
| **Acquisition** | Retrospective ECG‑gated helical scan, 0.5‑mm slice thickness, 0.3‑mm reconstruction interval |
| **Contrast** | 80 mL iodinated contrast (350 mg I/mL) injected at 5 mL/s, followed by 40 mL saline flush; bolus‑tracking threshold set at 150 HU in the ascending aorta |
| **Timing** | Arterial phase acquisition initiated 7 seconds after threshold; additional delayed phase (30 s) acquired to assess leaflets and possible delayed enhancement |
| **Coverage** | From the aortic root to the diaphragm, ensuring full visualization of the coronary ostia and the entire stent scaffolds |

The ECG‑gated dataset allowed reconstruction of both systolic (30 % R‑R) and diastolic (70 % R‑R) phases, facilitating accurate measurement of the annular dimensions when the leaflets are maximally open.

### Key CT Measurements for Feasibility Assessment

1. **Annular Geometry** – The virtual annular plane was defined by the basal attachment points of the native leaflets. The annular perimeter measured 73 mm, and the mean diameter was 23.2 mm, confirming suitability for the chosen Acurate neo2 size S (inner diameter 21 mm, outer diameter 26 mm). The area-derived annular area was 426 mm².

2. **Sinus of Valsalva Height** – The maximal sinus height was 31.5 mm, providing a generous clearance for the new valve’s supra‑annular leaflets while preserving a “coronary safety zone.”

3. **Coronary Ostial Height and Width** – The left main ostium was 13.4 mm above the annular plane, and the right coronary ostium was 13.9 mm. Minimal sinus width at the ostial level was 24 mm, exceeding the recommended 12 mm cutoff for ViV procedures.

4. **Frame‑to‑Coronary Distance** – By overlaying the 3‑D reconstructions of the Lotus and Evolut frames, the shortest distance from the inner surface of the Evolut stent to each coronary ostium was calculated (left: 6.2 mm, right: 5.8 mm). These values suggested that a high, commissure‑aligned implantation of the third valve would keep the neo‑skirt below the coronary flow path.

5. **Minimum Lumen Diameter of Existing Evolut Valve** – The Evolut’s inner lumen narrowed to 13.7 mm at the level of the neo‑skirt, a critical measurement for predicting residual effective orifice area after the third valve is placed. This also informed the selection of a low‑profile Acurate neo2 valve to avoid further lumen compromise.

6. **Valve‑to‑Aortic Wall Interaction** – The MDCT demonstrated that the Lotus frame was partially embedded in the aortic wall, whereas the Evolut remained mostly intraluminal. This information guided the decision to target deployment at the Evolut leaflets (high implantation) to achieve optimal anchoring without excessive radial force on the older Lotus frame.

### 3‑D Modelling and Virtual Valve Implantation

Using dedicated workstation software (e.g., 3mensio Structural Heart), an operator‑performed 3‑D reconstruction of the aortic root, coronary ostia, and the two pre‑existing stent frames. Virtual implantation of the Acurate neo2 was simulated at several depths (from –3 mm to +5 mm relative to the Evolut annular plane). The simulation yielded the following insights:

* **Neo‑Skirt Position** – At a deployment depth of +2 mm (i.e., 2 mm above the Evolut leaflets), the neo‑skirt (the inflow portion of the Acurate frame) rested safely above the coronary ostia, preserving a minimal clearance of 4 mm in all projections.

* **Commissural Alignment** – By rotating the virtual valve to align its commissural posts with those of the Evolut (identified on CT as the “cusp‑overlap” view), future coronary access was predicted to be achievable through the open cells of the three‑layered scaffolding.

* **Effective Orifice Area Prediction** – Computational geometry indicated that the combined cross‑sectional area at the level of the third valve would increase from 13.7 mm (existing Evolut) to an estimated 18 mm after adding the Acurate neo2, corresponding to an indexed AVA of ≈0.7 cm²/m²—acceptable for a patient of this size and thus mitigating severe patient‑prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

These virtual simulations were reviewed in the heart‑team meeting and formed the basis of the procedural strategy: high, commissure‑aligned deployment with careful attention to coronary clearance.

### CT‑Based Risk Assessment for Coronary Obstruction and PPM

Two quantitative algorithms were applied:

1. **Coronary Obstruction Risk (CORA score)** – The “virtual valve‑to‑coronary (VTC) distance” was calculated as the difference between the predicted neo‑skirt height and the coronary ostial height. In this case, VTC = 13.4 mm (LMS height) – 12.8 mm (neo‑skirt height) ≈ 0.6 mm, well above the 4‑mm safety threshold after accounting for the high implantation, thereby classifying the patient as low risk for coronary obstruction. No need for protective coronary wiring or BASILICA was anticipated.

2. **Patient‑Prosthesis Mismatch (PPM) Prediction** – Using the projected effective orifice area (EOA) from the 3‑D model and indexing to the patient’s body surface area (1.78 m²), an indexed EOA of 0.71 cm²/m² was obtained, corresponding to moderate PPM (moderate‑PPM threshold ≈0.65 cm²/m²). This informed the decision to select a supra‑annular, low‑profile valve (Acurate neo2) to maximise final EOA.

### Integration of Findings and Decision‑Making

The combined echocardiographic, TEE, microbiological, and CT data painted a coherent picture: the patient had severe, hemodynamically significant valve stenosis with moderate intraprosthetic regurgitation, there was no residual infection, and detailed CT analysis demonstrated adequate annular dimensions, sufficient sinus height, and acceptable coronary clearance for a third ViV implantation. Moreover, 3‑D modelling predicted that a high, commissure‑aligned deployment would mitigate the risk of severe PPM and preserve future coronary access. These conclusions justified proceeding with the TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV strategy under the guidance of a multidisciplinary heart team.

## 3. Decision‑Making and Procedural Planning  

The third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) was undertaken only after a systematic, heart‑team‑driven appraisal of the patient’s operative risk, the haemodynamic urgency of his prosthetic valve failure, and the technical possibilities afforded by contemporary imaging and device technology. The discussion integrated surgical risk scores, frailty assessment, prior procedural history, and the anatomic constraints revealed by multidetector computed tomography (MDCT). The resulting procedural roadmap included a clear choice of prosthesis, a predefined implantation depth and commissural alignment, and a contingency plan for coronary protection.

### Surgical risk quantification and the futility of repeat surgery  

The patient’s EuroSCORE II was 27 % – a figure that places him in the very high‑risk category for conventional aortic‑root surgery. Several additional factors amplified this risk: (1) three‑vessel coronary disease with multiple percutaneous interventions, (2) a permanent pacemaker for third‑degree atrioventricular block, (3) chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (FEV₁ ≈ 55 % predicted), (4) type‑2 diabetes mellitus with end‑organ involvement, and (5) a recent history of gastrointestinal bleeding that limited antithrombotic options. Frailty, as measured by the Clinical Frailty Scale (score ≥ 6), indicated reduced physiologic reserve and a higher likelihood of peri‑operative delirium and prolonged ventilation. Finally, the patient had already undergone two prior transcatheter valve implantations, which would require extensive aortic root and ascending‑aorta reconstruction to explant the nested frames—a technically daunting operation associated with a markedly increased risk of bleeding, stroke, and prolonged circulatory arrest. Collectively, these data rendered repeat surgical aortic valve replacement prohibitive, prompting the team to favour a percutaneous solution.

### Indications for a third transcatheter valve  

Echocardiography performed on admission in June 2024 demonstrated severe prosthetic valve dysfunction: mean trans‑valvular gradient 49 mm Hg, aortic valve area 0.5 cm², and grade 2–3 intraprosthetic regurgitation. The patient complained of exertional dyspnoea at a walking distance of less than 300 m, orthostatic dizziness, and fatigue—symptoms consistent with NYHA class III heart failure. The hemodynamic severity, combined with the rapid progression of gradients over a few months, satisfied guideline‑defined criteria for valve intervention (ACC/AHA Class I recommendation for severe symptomatic aortic stenosis). Because the prior infection had been cured (negative blood cultures, PET‑CT and TEE), the durability expectations for a third TAVI were based on the anticipated hemodynamic performance of the new valve rather than on concerns for recurrent endocarditis. The heart team therefore concluded that a third TAVI offered the best balance between symptom relief and procedural safety.

### Device selection: why the Acurate neo2?  

The Acurate neo2 was chosen after a detailed comparison of available self‑expanding and balloon‑expandable platforms:

| Feature | Acurate neo2 | Evolut R/PRO | Sapien 3 Ultra |
|---|---|---|---|
| Leaflet position | Supra‑annular | Supra‑annular | Intra‑annular |
| Frame height | Low (≈ 30 mm) | Moderate (≈ 45 mm) | Low (≈ 22 mm) |
| Commissural alignment | Enabled by “cusp‑overlap” deployment | Possible but less predictable | Not designed for alignment |
| Radial force | Moderate, suitable for incremental expansion | High, may fracture previous frames | High, may cause frame‑overstretch |
| Compatibility with narrow lumen (13.7 mm) | Favorable | Acceptable but higher profile | Limited by valve‑to‑coronary distance |

The primary rationale was the neo2’s low‑profile, self‑expanding frame, which could be accommodated within the minimal lumen diameter (13.7 mm) measured in the prior Evolut valve. Its supra‑annular leaflets promised a larger effective orifice area (EOA) in a narrow annulus, mitigating patient‑prosthesis mismatch (PPM). Moreover, the neo2’s design incorporates proprietary commissural posts that facilitate alignment of the valve’s open cells with the coronary ostia, a critical consideration in a triple‑stented setting where future coronary access could otherwise be compromised.

### Alternative strategies considered and rejected  

1. **Bioprosthetic valve fracture (BVF)** – This technique, performed with a high‑pressure non‑compliant balloon, can enlarge the orifice of a failed surgical bioprosthesis. In the present case the underlying frame is a self‑expanding transcatheter valve (Evolut) whose nitinol scaffold is resistant to fracturing and, more importantly, is already integrated into the aortic wall. Attempting BVF would risk uncontrolled frame rupture, annular injury, or coronary obstruction.  

2. **BASILICA (Bioprosthetic or Native Aortic Scallop Intentional Laceration to Prevent Iatrogenic Coronary Obstruction)** – While BASILICA is effective when the risk of coronary obstruction is high, the pre‑procedural MDCT showed a coronary‑ostial height > 12 mm and a sinus of Valsalva width that afforded ≥ 6 mm clearance between the anticipated neo‑skirt of the third valve and the left coronary ostium. Therefore the predicted risk of coronary obstruction was low, making the additional procedural time and potential leaflet laceration complications unnecessary.  

3. **Surgical explantation and conventional AVR** – As discussed, the cumulative surgical risk, combined with the technical difficulty of retrieving three overlapping frames, made this option untenable.  

Given these considerations, the team prioritized a straightforward third TAVI using a device that maximized EOA while preserving coronary flow, without resorting to adjunctive destructive techniques.

### Pre‑procedural coronary protection plan  

MDCT reconstruction focused on three measurements that dictate the need for a protection strategy: (i) coronary ostial height, (ii) sinus of Valsalva diameter, and (iii) valve‑to‑coronary distance after simulated valve deployment (virtual valve‑in‑valve modeling). The left coronary ostium sat at 13.2 mm above the annular plane; the right at 14.0 mm. The sinus diameters were 31 mm (L) and 28 mm (R). Virtual overlay of the neo2 indicated that even with maximal frame expansion the neo‑skirt would remain > 6 mm above the left coronary ostium, a safety margin deemed acceptable. Consequently, the heart team decided that prophylactic coronary protection (e.g., placement of a guidewire and balloon in the left coronary artery) was unnecessary, although a “stand‑by” wire could be introduced during the procedure if any unexpected obstruction were observed on aortography.

### Target implantation depth and commissural alignment  

The procedural objective was a high, supra‑annular deployment that would seat the neo2 leaflets at the level of the Evolut leaflets, thereby “pinning” the previously degenerated leaflets against the Evolut frame and preventing them from encroaching on the central flow path. Pre‑procedural CT measured the Evolut stent height at 44 mm; the neo2 was sized S (23 mm) to achieve a final implantation depth of 3–5 mm below the Evolut leaflets (approximately 15 mm above the annular plane). This high implantation strategy accomplishes three goals:  

1. **Preserve coronary access** – by keeping the neo2 frame well above the coronary ostia.  
2. **Maximize EOA** – by aligning the neo2 supra‑annular leaflets with the open cells of the Evolut frame, reducing flow obstruction.  
3. **Limit patient‑prosthesis mismatch** – by providing the largest possible orifice in a triple‑layered construct.

Fluoroscopic guidance utilized the “cusp‑overlap” view (right anterior oblique ≈ 45°, caudal 20°) to align the neo2 commissural posts with the Evolut commissures, as predicted by the CT‑derived 3‑D model. The planned alignment was verified intra‑procedurally by matching the radiopaque markers on the neo2 delivery system with the known positions of the Evolut stent struts.

### Balloon sizing and pre‑dilation plan  

The MDCT analysis identified the narrowest cross‑section within the prior Evolut frame (13.7 mm). To avoid excessive oversizing, a semi‑compliant 22 mm × 40 mm balloon was selected for predilation, delivering a balloon‑to‑annulus ratio of 0.9. Predilation was performed under rapid ventricular pacing (180 bpm) to minimize cardiac motion and to ensure controlled expansion of the native leaflets and the constrained Evolut mesh. Post‑deployment, a 20 mm × 40 mm non‑compliant balloon was used for final dilatation, again under rapid pacing, to achieve optimal neo2 expansion while applying enough radial force to modestly enlarge the Evolut frame without risking fracture.

### Patient‑specific procedural checklist  

| Item | Rationale |
|---|---|
| **Anesthesia**: Local with conscious sedation | Reduces intubation‑related complications in a frail, COPD patient; allows neurological monitoring. |
| **Vascular access**: Left femoral arterial (primary) + left radial (coronary angiography) | Femoral route is standard for large‑profile delivery; radial access facilitates immediate coronary assessment without additional groin puncture. |
| **Imaging guidance**: MDCT‑derived 3‑D model + real‑time trans‑esophageal echocardiography (TEE) | TEE confirms absence of residual vegetations, guides valve positioning, and detects perivalvular leak instantly. |
| **Coronary “stand‑by”**: 6 Fr left coronary guidewire with a compliant balloon | Ready for rapid deployment if aortography shows flow compromise despite favorable CT predictions. |
| **Hemodynamic monitoring**: Invasive arterial line + rapid pacing capability | Essential for precise balloon inflation timing and for immediate gradient assessment post‑implant. |
| **Post‑procedure antithrombotic regimen**: Single antiplatelet (aspirin) + short‑term anticoagulation (if indicated by CHA₂DS₂‑VASc) | Balances bleeding risk from prior GI bleeds with thrombosis prevention on the new valve. |
| **Discharge planning**: Early mobilization, cardiac rehabilitation referral, and scheduled TTE at 30 days, 3 months, and 1 year | Ensures timely detection of delayed complications and supports functional recovery. |

By adhering to this checklist, the team minimized procedural uncertainties, ensured that all contingency plans (coronary protection, vascular management, imaging verification) were in place, and tailored each step to the patient’s unique anatomic and clinical profile.

### Summary of the decision‑making pathway  

1. **High surgical risk** (EuroSCORE II 27 %) + multiple comorbidities → surgical AVR excluded.  
2. **Severe symptomatic prosthetic stenosis/regurgitation** → indication for valve replacement confirmed.  
3. **MDCT‑guided feasibility** (adequate sinus dimensions, sufficient coronary clearance) → third TAVI deemed technically possible.  
4. **Device selection** (Acurate neo2) based on supra‑annular leaflets, low frame height, and commissural alignment capacity.  
5. **Alternative techniques** (BVF, BASILICA, surgical explant) deemed unnecessary or excessively hazardous.  
6. **Pre‑planned implantation strategy**: high depth, commissural alignment, tailored balloon sizing, and optional coronary protection.  
7. **Procedural logistics** (local anesthesia, femoral + radial access, TEE/CT guidance) finalized in the heart‑team checklist.

This structured, evidence‑based, and imaging‑driven approach allowed the team to perform a technically successful third TAVI, achieving symptom relief and durable valve performance while preserving future coronary access—a paradigm that will become increasingly relevant as the population of patients with multiple transcatheter valves continues to expand.

## 4. Technical Execution and Intra‑Procedural Considerations  

The third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV) was performed in a high‑risk septuagenarian with two previously implanted self‑expanding prostheses (a 27 mm Lotus valve and a 23 mm Evolut R). The presence of three concentric stent frames, prior prosthetic‑valve endocarditis, and a limited coronary reserve dictated a meticulous, imaging‑guided strategy. Below is a step‑by‑step account of the procedure, with emphasis on the technical challenges that are unique to a triple‑stented aortic root and the tactics used to overcome them.

### 4.1 Vascular Access and Sheath Management  

Dual arterial access was deemed necessary: a left femoral approach for device delivery and a left radial route for ancillary imaging and coronary protection.  
* **Femoral access** – After ultrasound‑guided puncture of the left common femoral artery, a 16‑Fr expandable sheath (eSheath, Edwards Lifesciences) was introduced. The sheath size was selected to accommodate the 23 mm Acurate neo2 delivery system while preserving a safety margin for hemostasis, given the patient’s prior femoral haematoma.  
* **Radial access** – A 6‑Fr sheath was placed in the left radial artery to permit selective coronary angiography before, during, and after valve deployment. This access also facilitated rapid deployment of a coronary protection wire should ostial compromise have become apparent during the procedure.

Sheath insertion was performed under fluoroscopic guidance to avoid inadvertent crossing of the pre‑existing Lotus frame struts, which were visualised as radiopaque markers on the aortogram.

### 4.2 Navigation of Guidewires Through Prior Valve Frames  

Crossing the double‑layered prosthetic architecture required a hydrophilic, torque‑responsive guidewire. A 0.035 in. *Lunderquist* wire was first advanced across the Lotus valve into the left ventricular (LV) apex, using gentle rotations to negotiate the Lotus cell lattice. Once the wire was positioned beyond the Lotus frame, a **Safari** extra‑stiff wire (Boston Scientific) was exchanged over a 0.014 in. micro‑catheter and positioned deep in the LV cavity, just below the papillary muscles. The Safari wire provided the required support for delivery of the self‑expanding Acurate neo2 system while minimizing the risk of wire entrapment within the Evolut frame’s nitinol struts. Continuous wire position checks on orthogonal fluoroscopic projections (RAO 30°, LAO 45°) ensured that the wire tip remained central and that no looping occurred in the sinus of Valsalva.

### 4.3 Balloon Pre‑Dilation  

Given the severe restenosis (mean gradient 49 mm Hg, AVA 0.5 cm²) and the relatively narrow inner lumen of the Evolut frame (13.7 mm), a modest balloon pre‑dilation was performed to create a compliant landing zone and to test the hemodynamic response.  

* **Device** – A semi‑compliant 22 × 40 mm balloon (Z‑MED, B. Braun) was positioned across the valve orifice, with the distal marker aligned at the level of the Evolut leaflets (identified by the “flower‑petal” radiopaque markers on fluoroscopy).  
* **Rapid ventricular pacing (RVP)** – Pacing at 180 bpm was initiated via the existing right‑ventricular pacemaker lead to reduce cardiac output and aortic pressure, thereby limiting balloon migration during inflation.  
* **Inflation parameters** – The balloon was inflated to 5 atm for 3 seconds, achieving a nominal diameter of ~22 mm. Hemodynamic monitoring (invasive arterial line, central venous pressure) documented a transient drop in systolic pressure from 140 mm Hg to 80 mm Hg without new ST‑segment changes, confirming adequate pacing and safe balloon expansion.

Aortography performed immediately after the dilatation showed preserved coronary patency and no evidence of annular rupture.

### 4.4 Fluoroscopic and Aortographic Landmarks for Commissural Alignment  

One of the principal goals was to align the commissures of the new Acurate neo2 valve with those of the Evolut frame, thereby maximising the residual effective orifice area and preserving future coronary access. The pre‑procedural CT had identified the Evolut commissural posts at the 12‑, 4‑, and 8‑o’clock positions on a 3‑D reconstruction.  

* **Fluoroscopic reference** – During the procedure, a “coplanar” view (RAO 30°, CAUD 20°) rendered the Evolut stent struts as a “triple‑leaf” pattern. The three radiopaque commissural markers of the Evolut were used as reference points.  
* **Aortographic overlay** – A simultaneous injection of contrast (30 ml at 15 ml/s) through a pigtail catheter in the non‑coronary sinus delineated the sinus borders and highlighted the ostia of the left main and right coronary arteries. Alignment was achieved by rotating the delivery capsule until the Acurate neo2’s three “cusp‑alignment” markers (two radiopaque posts and one “cusp‑marker” line) matched the Evolut marker positions.  

This “cusp‑to‑cusp” alignment strategy reduced the amount of frame material that could obstruct the coronary ostia and facilitated a high implantation depth.

### 4.5 Deployment of the Acurate neo2 Valve  

The Acurate neo2 (size S, 23 mm) was selected for its supra‑annular leaflet design and low frame height—critical in a narrow, triple‑stented annulus.  

* **Positioning** – With the delivery system advanced over the Safari wire, the valve was retracted until the “inflation marker” (visible as a slight radiopaque halo) sat approximately **3 mm** above the inflow edge of the Evolut frame, as defined on the fluoroscopic roadmap. The targeted implantation depth was thus **4‑5 mm** into the LVOT, ensuring that the neo‑skirt would sit at the level of the Evolut leaflets, providing a “cushion” against further leaflet restriction.  
* **Release sequence** – The Acurate neo2 employs a top‑down deployment. First, the **upper stabilization arches** were released, allowing the supra‑annular leaflets to expand into the sinus. A short pause (3 seconds) permitted visual confirmation of valve expansion and coronary flow. Next, the **lower frame** was released by unscrewing the capsule, allowing the stent legs to embed into the existing Evolut frame. The entire release was performed under continuous aortographic surveillance; no brisk “pop” was observed, indicating a controlled expansion.  

Throughout deployment, the patient remained under rapid pacing (180 bpm) to minimise cardiac motion and to prevent embolisation of the device.

### 4.6 Post‑Dilation Protocol  

After initial deployment, a **20 × 40 mm** semi‑compliant balloon was advanced through the newly placed valve.  

* **Pacing** – Rapid ventricular pacing at 180 bpm was re‑initiated for the duration of the inflation.  
* **Inflation** – The balloon was inflated to 6 atm for 4 seconds, achieving a final diameter of ~20 mm. This step served two purposes: (1) it pressed the Acurate neo2’s nitinol frame against the previous Evolut struts, improving radial force and mitigating residual patient‑prosthesis mismatch (PPM); and (2) it gently expanded the Evolut frame, reducing any residual leaflet tethering caused by the prior infection.  

Post‑dilation angiography confirmed a well‑expanded valve with a symmetric “hour‑glass” silhouette and no new paravalvular leak.

### 4.7 Immediate Assessment of Coronary Flow  

A decisive safety check was the **selective coronary angiography** performed via the left radial sheath immediately after post‑dilation. Both the left main and right coronary arteries were visualised in multiple projections (RAO 30°, LAO 45°, cranial 20°) with no evidence of flow limitation, dissection, or ostial obstruction. The measured **TIMI 3** flow confirmed that the cumulative stent layers had not compromised coronary perfusion. No coronary protection wires were left in place, as the pre‑procedural CT had predicted a minimum valve‑to‑coronary distance of >6 mm after implantation.

### 4.8 Management of Potential Complications  

* **Valve malposition** – The pre‑planned high implantation depth and use of real‑time fluoroscopic markers reduced the risk of ventricular embolisation. In the unlikely event of a low deployment, a controlled “valve‑in‑valve” snare technique would have been available, but was not required.  
* **Coronary occlusion** – The team had a standby **BASILICA** plan (leaflet laceration) and a protective **Everolimus‑eluting stent** on the left main, ready to be deployed via the radial sheath if angiography revealed ostial compromise. The absence of such findings negated the need for these measures.  
* **Annular rupture** – The modest pre‑dilation pressure (5 atm) and use of a semi‑compliant balloon limited the radial force on the heavily calcified, multi‑layered annulus. Intra‑procedural trans‑esophageal echo and continuous arterial waveform monitoring were employed to detect early signs of rupture (sudden rise in LVEDP, pericardial effusion). No such events occurred.  

Any procedural complication would have been classified according to VARC‑3 definitions (e.g., “major vascular complication” if the femoral access required surgical repair, or “life‑threatening bleeding” if the pseudo‑aneurysm recurred).

At the conclusion of the case, the following VARC‑3 endpoints were recorded:

In summary, the successful execution of a third transcatheter valve in a patient with a triple‑stented aortic root hinged on careful vascular planning, precise wire navigation through two existing frames, judicious pre‑ and post‑dilation, fluoroscopic alignment to the prior Evolut commissures, and rigorous intra‑procedural imaging to verify coronary patency and rule out catastrophic complications. By adhering to a predefined, CT‑guided strategy and the VARC‑3 procedural standards, the team achieved a technically feasible and hemodynamically acceptable result that endured for at least one year.

## 5. Outcomes, Follow‑up, and Lessons for Lifetime Valve Management  

The clinical course after the third transcatheter valve implantation illustrates how careful procedural planning can translate into durable haemodynamic results even when three prosthetic frames coexist within a single aortic root. The sequential echo assessments, functional recovery, and absence of recurrent infection provide an evidence base for the feasibility of TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV strategies in high‑risk patients who have survived prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE).

### Immediate post‑procedural echocardiography  

Trans‑oesophageal and transthoracic echocardiography performed on day 1 after the Acurate neo2 deployment confirmed correct leaflet motion and complete sealing of the valve orifice. No trans‑valvular or paravalvular aortic regurgitation was detectable (grade 0). However, the Doppler gradient remained high: a mean trans‑valvular gradient of 67 mm Hg and a calculated effective orifice area (EOA) of 0.9 cm² (indexed EOA 0.4 cm²/m²). The derived Doppler velocity index (DVI) of 0.24 fulfilled the haemodynamic definition of severe patient‑prosthesis mismatch (PPM). In a triple‑stented anatomy, the residual gradient largely reflects the combined geometric restriction imposed by the three overlapping frames rather than primary valve dysfunction. Recognising severe PPM early allowed the team to anticipate a potential need for aggressive balloon post‑dilatation and close clinical surveillance.

### Early (1‑month) haemodynamic improvement and left‑ventricular remodelling  

At the 1‑month follow‑up, repeat TTE demonstrated a rapid decline in the mean gradient to 27 mm Hg and a modest increase in EOA to 1.1 cm². The left ventricular (LV) end‑diastolic volume fell from 148 mL at baseline to 118 mL, indicating favourable reverse remodelling. The LV ejection fraction (EF) rose from 66 % pre‑procedure to 68 % post‑procedure, reflecting improved systolic performance after afterload reduction. These changes are clinically meaningful: the gradient reduction moved the prosthesis from a severe‑stenosis haemodynamic profile into the moderate‑stenosis range, which correlates with symptom relief and a lower risk of progressive LV dysfunction. The early improvement also suggests that high‑position deployment and post‑dilatation successfully expanded the prior Evolut frame, increasing central flow without compromising coronary perfusion.

### Mid‑term (3.5‑month) clinical status  

By 3.5 months the patient reported New York Heart Association (NYHA) class I symptoms, could walk more than 500 m without dyspnoea, and experienced no exertional chest discomfort. Objective assessment showed stable LV dimensions, no new valvular regurgitation, and unchanged laboratory markers of inflammation (CRP < 5 mg/L). Serial blood cultures remained negative, confirming eradication of the prior Enterococcus faecalis infection and indicating that the third TAVI did not re‑ignite a latent endocarditic process. The absence of fever, negative trans‑oesophageal imaging, and lack of prosthetic vegetations together fulfil the ESC criteria for cure of PVE, allowing clinicians to focus surveillance on structural valve performance rather than infection‑related complications.

### 1‑year follow‑up  

One year after the third valve implantation the echocardiographic profile was stable: mean gradient 26 mm Hg, EOA 1.1 cm², DVI 0.25, and no aortic regurgitation. LV EF had risen to 65 % with continued reduction in LV volumes, supporting sustained reverse remodelling. Clinically the patient remained asymptomatic, engaged in regular activities of daily living, and had not required rehospitalisation for heart failure. Serial imaging showed no evidence of prosthetic degeneration, leaflet calcification, or structural fracture of any of the three frames. Importantly, there were no signs of recurrent endocarditis: repeat TEE demonstrated clean prosthetic surfaces, and inflammatory markers stayed within normal limits.

### Residual patient‑prosthesis mismatch in a triple‑stented scenario  

Despite the favourable gradient at 1 year, the indexed EOA remained below the conventional threshold of 0.85 cm²/m² for severe PPM. In isolated surgical or single‑valve TAVI procedures, severe PPM is associated with reduced survival and higher rates of heart‑failure readmission. In the present case, the adverse impact appears mitigated by several factors:

1. **Preserved coronary flow** – the high implantation strategy kept the neo‑skirt well above the coronary ostia, preventing flow limitation that could exacerbate the functional consequence of a small EOA.  
2. **LV functional reserve** – the patient’s EF was already high (≥ 65 %) and LV dimensions returned toward normal, suggesting an adequate contractile reserve to compensate for the modest residual gradient.  
3. **Low‑flow state avoidance** – aggressive medical therapy (beta‑blocker titration, diuretics as needed) and the absence of significant aortic regurgitation prevented the low‑flow, low‑gradient phenotype that often unmasks the clinical relevance of PPM.

Nevertheless, residual PPM remains a concern for long‑term durability. Ongoing surveillance with yearly echocardiography is warranted, and any future rise in gradient > 20 mm Hg or symptomatic decline would prompt reconsideration of therapeutic options.

### Coronary access after three overlapping frames  

The cumulative metal burden of three stent frames markedly narrows the coronary sinuses and creates a mesh that can impede catheter navigation. In this patient, pre‑procedural CT measured a valve‑to‑coronary distance of 7 mm after the third valve implantation, a margin considered sufficient for most standard catheter shapes. Post‑procedural angiography confirmed unobstructed coronary flow and successful engagement of both the left and right coronary arteries using standard 6‑Fr catheters. Key maneuvers that facilitated future access included:

- **Commissural alignment** of the Acurate neo2 to match the Evolut leaflets, thereby aligning frame cells away from the coronary ostia.  
- **High implantation** (∼3–4 mm above the native annulus) to keep the neo‑skirt away from the sinotubular junction.  
- **Selection of a low‑profile frame** (Acurate neo2) that contributes minimal additional strut density.

For patients who will likely require percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) after a triple‑valve construct, a pre‑emptive strategy—such as temporary coronary wire protection or the use of a “leaflet‑splitting” technique (BASILICA)—might be required. In this case, the imaging‑guided planning obviated the need for protective measures.

### Role of pre‑procedural CT and commissural alignment  

Multiphase multidetector CT (MDCT) was pivotal in three respects:

1. **Anatomic feasibility** – precise measurement of the inner lumen of the Evolut frame (13.7 mm) confirmed that a 23 mm Acurate neo2 could be accommodated without excessive oversizing.  
2. **Coronary risk stratification** – 3‑D reconstruction allowed the team to calculate the virtual valve‑to‑coronary distance and to model the expected neo‑skirt after implantation, confirming that coronary obstruction was unlikely.  
3. **Commissural planning** – by overlaying the CT‑derived orientation of the Evolut leaflets on fluoroscopic landmarks, the operators ensured that the Acurate neo2 commissures would be aligned, preserving a “window” for future coronary catheterization.

These steps illustrate how modern imaging can convert a seemingly prohibitive anatomy into a manageable procedural target.

### Guideline context: TAVI after prosthetic valve endocarditis  

The 2025 ESC/EC (European Society of Cardiology) and ACC/AHA guidelines continue to recommend surgical valve replacement as the first‑line therapy for PVE when feasible. However, both documents acknowledge that when infection is cured, the patient presents prohibitive surgical risk, and the anatomy is favourable, a transcatheter approach may be considered (Class IIb, Level C). The present case meets these criteria: the infection was fully treated, the EuroSCORE II was > 27 %, and CT demonstrated a safe landing zone. Thus, the decision aligns with guideline‑endorsed individualized care.

### Future considerations: valve fracture, BASILICA, and emerging devices  

Should the triple‑stented construct deteriorate further, options become increasingly limited. Potential strategies include:

- **Bioprosthetic valve fracture (BVF)** – high‑pressure balloon inflation to fracture the rigid frame of the Evolut valve, thereby expanding the residual orifice. This technique has been described for surgical bioprostheses but remains off‑label and untested in a self‑expanding TAV, especially within a triple‑layered construct.  
- **BASILICA (Bioprosthetic or Native Aortic Scallop Intentional Laceration to Prevent Iatrogenic Coronary Obstruction)** – electrosurgical laceration of the leaflets of the prior valve to create a “split” that facilitates coronary flow and potentially enlarges the effective orifice. Application after two prior TAVIs is technically demanding and carries a risk of embolisation.  
- **Emerging low‑profile, fully‑retractable devices** – next‑generation TAVIs with narrower frames and adaptive sealing skirts may allow further redo procedures without compounding mesh density.

These adjuncts remain investigational in the triple‑valve setting; any future use should be confined to heart‑team‑based, compassionate‑use protocols with rigorous imaging verification.

### Take‑home messages for lifetime management of structural valve disease  

1. **Imaging first** – High‑resolution MDCT with 3‑D reconstruction should be performed before any repeat TAVI to evaluate landing zone dimensions, coronary clearance, and frame interaction.  
2. **Commissural alignment matters** – Aligning the new valve’s commissures with those of the previous device preserves coronary access and may reduce residual gradient.  
3. **Anticipate PPM** – In patients with multiple overlapping frames, severe PPM is almost inevitable; strive for maximal effective orifice area through high implantation and post‑dilatation, and monitor for symptoms.  
4. **Multidisciplinary decision‑making** – The heart team (interventional cardiology, cardiac surgery, imaging, infectious disease, anesthesia) must weigh surgical risk, infection status, and anatomic feasibility.  
5. **Patient‑centred risk stratification** – Incorporate frailty, comorbidities, and life expectancy into the choice of a redo TAVI versus surgical explantation.  
6. **Plan for the future** – Even at the first TAVI, discuss the potential need for future interventions and select devices and implantation depths that preserve coronary access and leave room for later valve expansions.  

By integrating meticulous CT‑guided planning, strategic valve selection, and a collaborative heart‑team approach, clinicians can achieve durable haemodynamic outcomes and maintain quality of life even after three sequential transcatheter aortic valve interventions, including in patients who have survived prosthetic valve endocarditis.
