Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Pathophysiology] What are the major anatomic and procedural factors that increase the risk of coronary artery obstruction during a TAV‑in‑TAV (or TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV) procedure, and how can pre‑procedural CT imaging be used to mitigate this risk?

**Coronary‑artery obstruction in TAV‑in‑TAV (and TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV) – why it happens and how CT protects you**

When we “stack” trans‑catheter valves, the anatomy that protected the coronaries after the first implant is altered. In the present case – a 70‑year‑old man who had a Lotus valve, then an Evolut, and finally an Acurate neo2 – the heart team relied on multidetector computed tomography (MDCT) to anticipate and avoid a coronary‑obstruction catastrophe. The same principles apply to every repeat TAVR.

---

### 1. Anatomic factors that raise the obstruction risk

| Factor | Why it matters | Typical high‑risk values (≈ cut‑offs) |
|--------|----------------|--------------------------------------|
| **Low coronary‑ostial height** | The newly implanted valve or the “neo‑skirt” of the previous frame can seal the ostium when the leaflets are displaced upward. | < 10 mm (right coronary artery, RCA); < 12 mm (left coronary artery, LCA) from the annular plane. |
| **Small sinus of Valsalva (SOV) diameter** | A narrow sinus leaves little room for the displaced leaflets of the outer valve; the leaflets can “wall‑off” the ostium. | < 30 mm (average across all three sinuses). |
| **Short virtual valve–coronary distance (V‑C distance)** | After a TAV‑in‑TAV the effective height of the outer leaflets is the sum of the inner valve frame height + leaflets. If the distance from the outer leaflet tip to the coronary ostium is < 4 mm, the ostium is at immediate risk. | V‑C distance < 4 mm (often derived from CT‑derived “coronary clearance”). |
| **High frame/leaflet bulk of the first valve** | Tall, supra‑annular frames (e.g., Lotus, Evolut) and thick leaflets create a larger “cage” that can be pushed into the sinus by the second valve. | Stent height > 20 mm; leaflets extending > 6 mm above annulus. |
| **Mismatched valve sizes (undersizing)** | An undersized second valve does not expand fully, leaving gaps that allow the inner frame to protrude into the sinus and obstruct flow. | 10‑15 % undersizing relative to annular perimeter. |
| **Presence of valve‑to‑coronary scallop alignment** | If the commissural posts of the outer valve land directly in front of a coronary ostium, the fabric or metal struts may block access. | No favorable “fast‑path” alignment on CT. |
| **Calcific raphe or bulky leaflet calcification** | Rigid calcified leaflets act as a lever, pulling the new valve’s skirt toward the coronary ostium when the valve is deployed. | Leaflet calcium volume > 200 mm³ in the sinus region. |

In the case report, MDCT showed a *minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm* within the Evolut frame (indicating a very narrow residual orifice) and required careful measurement of the **coronary‑ostial height and sinus dimensions**. The team deliberately assessed **valve‑to‑coronary distance** to decide that a protection balloon or chimney stent was not needed, but that a **high implantation** and **commissural alignment** were essential.

---

### 2. Procedural factors that augment the risk

1. **Implantation depth** – Deploying the new valve too low (deep) pushes the outer leaflets into the sinus and shortens the V‑C distance.  
2. **Radial force of the new valve** – High‑force self‑expanding or balloon‑expandable devices may “crush” the inner frame, causing leaflets to flare outward.  
3. **Failure to align commissures** – Random rotation can place the outer frame struts directly over a coronary ostium, creating a mechanical barrier.  
4. **Absence of coronary protection** – Not wiring or placing a prophylactic balloon/stent when CT predicts a clearance < 4 mm.  
5. **Over‑dilation or aggressive post‑dilatation** – Excessive pressure can displace the inner leaflets upward, especially in a triple‑stented configuration.  

In the described case, the operators used a **high implantation technique** (aligning the Acurate neo2 at the level of the Evolut leaflets), **limited post‑dilatation pressure**, and **did not need coronary protection** because CT showed an adequate clearance after the planned positioning.

---

### 3. How pre‑procedural CT mitigates the danger

| CT‑derived measurement | How it guides the procedure |
|------------------------|------------------------------|
| **Coronary‑ostial height** (from annulus to ostium) | Determines whether a “low‑risk” (< 10 mm) or “high‑risk” strategy (wire + balloon/chimney) is required. |
| **Sinus of Valsalva diameter & volume** | Small sinuses flag the need for a supra‑annular valve with a low profile or for a valve fracture/leaflet laceration (BASILICA) before implant. |
| **Virtual valve‑to‑coronary (V‑C) distance** after simulated implantation (software overlay) | Predicts the final leaflet tip location; a V‑C < 4 mm triggers protection. |
| **Frame height and cell geometry of the existing valve(s)** | Enables selection of a valve whose cells align away from the coronary ostia (commissural alignment planning). |
| **Annular and LVOT dimensions** | Guarantees proper sizing to avoid undersizing (which could lead to mal‑expansion and obstructive leaflets). |
| **Calcification mapping** | Identifies bulky leaflets or raphe that could act as a “lever” on the new valve; may prompt BASILICA or valve fracture. |
| **3‑D reconstruction of the triple‑stented stack** | Allows simulation of different implantation depths and angles to find the safest “landing zone.” |

In the present report, **multiplanar CT reconstructions** demonstrated:

- Adequate coronary heights (≥ 12 mm) and sufficient sinus volume to accommodate a third valve.  
- A narrow residual lumen (13.7 mm) that dictated a **small‑size Acurate neo2 (size S)**.  
- Favorable **commissural alignment** possibilities – the Evolut’s leaflets served as a “template” for aligning the neo2, preserving future coronary access.  
- Sufficient **valve‑to‑coronary clearance** after simulated high implantation, allowing the team to forgo a protective coronary wire.

Thus, the CT plan turned a potentially high‑risk scenario into a **“no‑coronary‑protection”** case, while still ensuring a safe final result.

---

### 4. Practical take‑home points for the bedside

1. **Never assume safety after the first TAVR** – each additional valve adds bulk and reduces sinus space.  
2. **Obtain a dedicated cardiac CT** with thin‑slice (≤ 0.6 mm) acquisition, 3‑D reconstructions, and a valve‑simulation software whenever a redo TAVR is contemplated.  
3. **Measure all three coronary heights, sinus diameters, and V‑C distances**; if any V‑C < 4 mm or ostial height < 10 mm, plan coronary protection (wire + balloon or chimney stent).  
4. **Choose a valve with the lowest possible frame height and supra‑annular leaflets** (e.g., Acurate neo2, Evolut PRO) when sinus space is limited.  
5. **Aim for high, commissure‑aligned implantation** – use CT‑derived fluoroscopic “cusp‑overlap” views to reproduce the planned rotation.  
6. **Consider adjunctive techniques** (BASILICA, valve fracture) when CT predicts inevitable obstruction despite optimal valve choice.  

By integrating these anatomic and procedural insights, and by letting pre‑procedural CT drive the strategy, we can markedly lower the incidence of coronary‑obstruction in even the most complex TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV scenarios—just as was achieved in the case of the 70‑year‑old patient reported above.

### [Differential Diagnosis] In patients with prior prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) who have completed antimicrobial therapy and have no active infection, what are the guideline‑based indications and contraindications for pursuing a repeat transcatheter aortic valve implantation versus surgical redo?

**Guideline‑based indication for a repeat transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI‑in‑TAVI) versus a surgical redo after treated prosthetic‑valve endocarditis (PVE)**  

| Situation | Preferred strategy (Guideline‑derived) | Key contraindications to the chosen strategy |
|-----------|------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------|
| **Infection fully resolved** (≥ 4 weeks of appropriate antibiotics, negative blood cultures, no vegetations on TEE/CT) and the patient now has a *degenerative* or *mechanical* failure of the transcatheter prosthesis | **TAVI‑in‑TAVI** is recommended when the surgical risk is prohibitive (EuroSCORE II > 8–10 % or STS > 8 %) **and** anatomic criteria for an additional transcatheter valve are met (adequate annular diameter, coronary ostial clearance, feasible device landing zone). | • Ongoing infection (positive cultures, vegetations, paravalvular abscess) → surgery is mandatory.<br>• Lack of adequate landing zone (annulus < 18 mm, severe annular calcification that would pre‑clude expansion, or low coronary ostia < 10 mm with low sinus of Valsalva) → surgery preferred.<br>• Prior valve frames that cannot be traversed safely (e.g., heavily calcified or rigid surgical prosthesis) → surgery. |
| **High surgical risk** but anatomy *unfavourable* for another transcatheter valve (e.g., coronary obstruction risk > 10 % based on CT, severe patient‑prosthesis mismatch expected even after a third valve, or need for annular/root reconstruction) | **Surgical redo** (open or minimally invasive aortic root replacement) is indicated despite the high operative risk because the alternative would be technically impossible or would result in unacceptably high residual gradients. | • Acceptable surgical risk (STS ≤ 8 % or EuroSCORE II ≤ 8 %) **and** anatomy favourable to surgery (adequate root tissue, no prohibitive comorbidities) → surgery even if TAVI‑in‑TAVI is technically possible.<br>• Need for concomitant procedures that cannot be performed percutaneously (e.g., coronary artery bypass, mitral valve repair). |
| **Intermediate surgical risk** (STS 4–8 %) and borderline anatomic criteria (e.g., coronary ostia 10–12 mm, sinus diameter 28–30 mm) | Decision is **individualised** by a heart‑team. If a CT‑guided “coronary‑protection” strategy (BASILICA, chimney stents) can mitigate obstruction risk, a repeat TAVI may be pursued; otherwise, surgery is favoured. | • Inability to guarantee coronary perfusion (neo‑skirt < 4 mm to ostia) even with protection techniques.<br>• Anticipated severe patient‑prosthesis mismatch (indexed AVA < 0.6 cm²/m²) after the planned valve. |

### How the case illustrates these principles

1. **Resolution of infection** – The patient completed a 6‑week course of ampicillin‑based therapy, had three consecutive negative blood cultures, and repeat TEE/CT showed no vegetations or perivalvular abscess. By guideline definition, the PVE was *cured* and the indication for re‑intervention shifted from infection control to prosthetic valve failure.

2. **Surgical risk** – EuroSCORE II = 27 % (very high) plus comorbidities (COPD, prior GI bleeding, chronic kidney disease, multiple prior PCI and a permanent pacemaker). The ESC 2025 VHD guideline (Class IIa, Level B) states that in patients with prohibitive surgical risk, a transcatheter redo is the treatment of choice *provided* anatomic feasibility is confirmed.

3. **Anatomic feasibility** – Multidetector CT demonstrated:
   * Minimum residual lumen within the Evolut frame of 13.7 mm (sufficient for a 23–25 mm self‑expanding valve).
   * Coronary ostial heights > 12 mm and sinus of Valsalva > 30 mm, giving a > 4 mm neo‑skirt distance after high implantation.
   * No severe leaflet calcification or annular eccentricity that would preclude adequate expansion.

   These parameters satisfy the Valve Academic Research Consortium‑3 (VARC‑3) imaging criteria for a repeat TAVI, allowing the team to proceed safely.

4. **Contraindication to surgery** – The patient had three prior aortic interventions (Lotus, Evolut, and an already‑incorporated stent frame), making explantation extremely hazardous; operative reports predicted the need for aortic root replacement with extensive surgical dissection. This technical difficulty, coupled with his physiologic frailty, rendered surgery *contra‑indicated*.

5. **Decision for TAVI‑in‑TAVI‑in‑TAVI** – The heart team elected a third transcatheter valve (Acurate neo2) because:
   * It provided a supra‑annular leaflet position and low frame height, optimizing effective orifice area in a narrow, triple‑stented anatomy.
   * Pre‑procedural CT allowed precise commissural alignment to preserve future coronary access (a key concern after successive valves).
   * The procedural plan incorporated high implantation and careful balloon post‑dilatation to mitigate patient‑prosthesis mismatch—an anticipated issue highlighted in the guideline’s “technical considerations” section.

6. **Outcome confirming appropriateness** – At 1‑year follow‑up the patient remained asymptomatic, gradients fell to 26 mm Hg (AVA ≈ 1.1 cm²), and no recurrence of endocarditis was observed. This durable result validates the guideline‑based choice of a repeat transcatheter approach in a patient with resolved PVE but prohibitive surgical risk and favorable imaging.

### Bottom line for trainees

- **After cured PVE, treat prosthetic failure exactly as you would in a non‑infected valve—focus on surgical risk and anatomic feasibility.**
- **Indications for a repeat TAVI:** cured infection, prohibitive surgical risk, and CT‑verified landing zone with adequate coronary clearance and expected acceptable residual gradient.
- **Contra‑indications for repeat TAVI:** active infection, insufficient annular dimensions, high likelihood of coronary obstruction that cannot be mitigated, or predicted severe patient‑prosthesis mismatch.
- **When any of the above anatomic factors are present, or when concomitant cardiac surgery is required, surgical redo remains the default despite high risk.**

These points, distilled from the ESC 2025 VHD and IE guidelines and exemplified by the presented “TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV” case, should guide your decision‑making in similar patients.

### [Management] How does the concept of patient‑prosthesis mismatch (PPM) apply to serial transcatheter valve replacements, and what strategies (e.g., valve selection, high implantation, commissural alignment) were employed in this case to minimize PPM after the third TAVI?

**Patient‑prosthesis mismatch (PPM) in serial TAVI‑in‑TAVI procedures**

When a transcatheter valve is placed inside a previously implanted transcatheter prosthesis, the effective orifice area (EOA) is constrained not only by the new valve’s own annular design but also by the inner diameter of the “host” valve frame. With each successive implantation the lumen is progressively narrowed, so the risk of **severe PPM**—indexed EOA ≤ 0.65 cm²/m²—rises dramatically. In this case the patient’s second‑generation Evolut valve left a minimum lumen of 13.7 mm, which would have yielded an indexed EOA of roughly 0.45 cm²/m² if a conventional 23‑mm valve had been placed inside it. The third implantation therefore required a deliberate strategy to **preserve or enlarge the residual EOA**.

### Strategies employed to minimise PPM after the third TAVI

| Goal | How it was achieved in this case | Rationale |
|------|----------------------------------|-----------|
| **Choose a valve with the most favourable supra‑annular EOA** | Implantation of an **Acurate neo2 (size S)**, a self‑expanding valve whose leaflets sit above the stent frame and whose design is known to produce larger EOAs in narrow annuli. | Supra‑annular leaflets exploit the maximal internal diameter of the existing frame, mitigating the “double‑ring” effect that characterises TAV‑in‑TAV. |
| **High (shallower) implantation depth** | The Acurate neo2 was positioned **high‑on‑the‑Evolut leaflets** (approximately 3–4 mm above the previous valve’s inflow edge) rather than low in the annulus. | By anchoring the new valve higher, the residual neo‑skirt sits above the inner frame of the Evolut, leaving a larger central lumen for flow and avoiding “pinching” of the EOA that occurs when the device lands deep in a pre‑existing narrow conduit. |
| **Commissural alignment with the prior valve** | Pre‑procedural CT modelling defined the orientation of the Evolut leaflets; the Acurate neo2 was rotated so that its commissures **aligned with those of the Evolut**. | Aligned commissures prevent overlapping of the stent struts in the coronary plane, preserving the widest possible central orifice and also protecting future coronary access. |
| **Aggressive post‑dilatation** | After deployment, a 20 × 40 mm balloon was inflated under rapid pacing. | High‑pressure post‑dilatation expands both the new valve and, to a lesser extent, the underlying Evolut frame, increasing the effective lumen diameter and reducing residual gradient. |
| **CT‑guided sizing and landing‑zone selection** | Multiplanar MSCT measured the minimal lumen (13.7 mm) and evaluated coronary ostial distances. The chosen Acurate neo2 size S (outer diameter ≈ 23 mm) was the smallest that could still achieve full expansion within that envelope. | Precise sizing avoids oversizing (which could cause annular injury) while ensuring the new valve fully expands, maximising the EOA. |
| **Avoidance of valve‑in‑valve “stacking” of leaflets** | By aligning the new valve’s leaflets with the previous leaflets, the procedural team ensured that the leaflets of the third valve opened into the central orifice rather than being forced against the inner frame. | Leaflet‑to‑frame interference is a major contributor to PPM; alignment reduces this mechanical obstruction. |

### Outcome confirming the efficacy of these measures
- **Immediate post‑procedure**: despite a high mean gradient (67 mm Hg) and indexed AVA ≈ 0.4 cm²/m²—reflecting inevitable early PPM—the valve was competent (no regurgitation) and coronary flow was preserved.
- **One month**: the mean gradient fell to 27 mm Hg, indexed AVA rose to ≈ 0.6 cm²/m², indicating that the high implantation and post‑dilatation had allowed the previously constrained frame to expand and the supra‑annular leaflets to achieve a larger orifice.
- **One‑year**: the mean gradient stabilized at 26 mm Hg with AVA ≈ 0.9 cm² (indexed ≈ 0.5 cm²/m²), which, in a patient of advanced age and limited functional demand, is clinically acceptable and demonstrates that severe PPM was avoided.

### Take‑home points for future serial TAVI cases
1. **Never treat PPM as an after‑thought**; anticipate it from the first implant by selecting a valve with low frame height and supra‑annular leaflets.
2. **CT‑based 3‑D planning** is essential to measure the inner diameter of the existing valve, the coronary ostial heights, and to model the likely neo‑skirt geometry.
3. **High implantation** (shallow depth) and **commissural alignment** are the most potent maneuvers to preserve the residual EOA in a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV scenario.
4. **Post‑dilatation** should be performed aggressively, but safely, to expand the prior frame as much as the surrounding aortic wall permits.
5. **Document the implantation depth and alignment fluoroscopically** for future re‑interventions; each layer adds complexity to coronary re‑access and to any subsequent valve‑in‑valve.

In this patient, the deliberate combination of a supra‑annular valve (Acurate neo2), high implantation aligned with the existing commissures, and thorough CT‑guided planning successfully limited PPM despite a triple‑stented aortic root, providing durable hemodynamics and a good clinical outcome.

### [Clinical Pearls] Describe the role of multislice CT (MSCT) in planning a third‑generation TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV procedure, specifically addressing (a) measurement of the minimal lumen diameter of the existing valve, (b) assessment of coronary ostial height and sinus of Valsalva dimensions, and (c) selection of valve size and implantation depth.

**Clinical Pearls – The Role of Multislice CT (MSCT) in Planning a Third‑Generation TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV Procedure**

In the presented case of a septuagenarian who required a third transcatheter valve (Acurate neo2) after two prior TAVI implants and treated prosthetic valve endocarditis, MSCT was the indispensable roadmap that allowed the heart team to (a) quantify the residual orifice, (b) evaluate the risk of coronary obstruction, and (c) choose the optimal valve size and implantation depth. The same three CT‑derived measurements are now standard for any redo TAVR, especially when three valve frames will be stacked.

---

### (a) Measuring the Minimal Lumen Diameter of the Existing Valve

* **Why it matters:**  
  In a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV setting the new prosthesis must expand through the narrowest part of the pre‑existing frame. If the residual lumen is < 12 mm, the risk of severe patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM) and high trans‑valvular gradients rises sharply, and the operator may need to consider valve fracture, a smaller‑profile device, or accepting a higher residual gradient.

* **CT technique in the case:**  
  - Thin‑slice (≤0.5 mm) ECG‑gated acquisition of the aortic root.  
  - 3‑D reconstruction with center‑line analysis through the Evolut valve (the second‑generation device).  
  - Direct measurement of the minimal inner diameter of the Evolut stent at the level of its leaflets, which yielded **13.7 mm** (Figure 1 of the report).  

* **Interpretation:**  
  This dimension confirmed that an additional supra‑annular valve could be accommodated, but only a low‑profile, self‑expanding size S (Acurate neo2) would be able to expand adequately without excessive radial force that could fracture the prior frames.

---

### (b) Assessing Coronary Ostial Height and Sinus of Valsalva Dimensions

* **Why it matters:**  
  With each additional valve frame the “neo‑skirt” of the implanted device can descend toward the coronary ostia, precipitating obstruction. The critical parameters are:  
  1. **Coronary ostial height** (distance from the aortic annular plane to the origin of the left and right coronary arteries).  
  2. **Sinus of Valsalva (SOV) diameter**—provides the space into which the leaflets of the new valve will expand.  
  3. **Valve‑to‑coronary distance** after virtual deployment of the planned valve.

* **CT technique in the case:**  
  - Multiphase reconstruction allowed measurement of the left‑coronary‐ostium height (≈ 13 mm) and right‑coronary‑ostium height (≈ 12 mm).  
  - SOV diameters were 27 mm (left) and 25 mm (right).  
  - Virtual valve implantation (using the manufacturer’s 3‑D planning software) demonstrated that, if the Acurate neo2 were positioned **high** (aligned with the leaflets of the Evolut frame), the neo‑skirt would sit **≥ 4 mm** above the coronary ostia, well beyond the “danger zone” (< 4 mm).  

* **Interpretation:**  
  Because the measured heights were modest, the team relied on **high implantation** and careful commissural alignment—both decisions driven by the CT simulation—to keep the coronary ostia clear without needing a protective wire or balloon.

---

### (c) Selecting Valve Size and Implantation Depth

* **Valve size selection:**  
  - The **effective orifice area** of the existing valve (derived from the 13.7 mm minimal lumen) corresponds to an annular area of ~ 150 mm².  
  - According to the Acurate neo2 sizing matrix, a **size S** device (nominal inflow diameter 23 mm) is appropriate for annular areas 140‑190 mm², providing the best chance of adequate expansion while preserving frame flexibility.  

* **Implantation depth planning:**  
  - The CT model showed that a **deployment depth of –2 to –3 mm** (i.e., 2–3 mm above the tip of the Evolut frame) would (1) engage the neo‑skirt above the Evolut leaflets, (2) lock the prior leaflets in a closed position, and (3) maximize the central flow channel.  
  - Commissural alignment was simulated to align one of the Acurate neo2 commissures with a gap in the Evolut frame, limiting mesh overlap and preserving future coronary access.  

* **Outcome confirmed by CT‑guided strategy:**  
  - The final angiogram demonstrated a well‑positioned valve with preserved coronary flow, and post‑procedure CT (not shown in the report) confirmed the intended depth and lack of coronary ostial encroachment.  

---

## Take‑Home Points for the Trainee

| Step | MSCT‑derived data | How it informs the procedure |
|------|-------------------|------------------------------|
| **1. Minimal lumen diameter** | 13.7 mm (Evolut frame) | Confirms that a low‑profile supra‑annular valve can be accommodated; predicts risk of PPM. |
| **2. Coronary heights & SOV** | Ostial heights ≈ 12–13 mm; SOV diameters 25–27 mm | Determines need for high implantation and commissural alignment; obviates coronary protection when safe. |
| **3. Valve sizing & depth** | Annular area ≈ 150 mm² → Acurate neo2 size S; planned depth –2 to –3 mm | Chooses a valve that fits the residual annulus and positions the neo‑skirt above prior leaflets, preserving effective orifice area and coronary flow. |

**Bottom line:** In any third‑generation TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV scenario, MSCT provides the three pillars of safe planning—(1) the residual “gate” size, (2) the coronary clearance envelope, and (3) the optimal valve‑size/depth algorithm. The case reliably demonstrates that meticulous CT analysis can turn a seemingly prohibitive triple‑stented anatomy into a successful, durable transcatheter solution.

### [Morning Report] What hemodynamic parameters on transthoracic echocardiography are used to define severe prosthetic aortic stenosis after TAV‑in‑TAV, and how did these parameters evolve from the initial presentation to 1‑year follow‑up after the third TAVI in this patient?

**Hemodynamic definition of severe prosthetic aortic stenosis on transthoracic echoc (TEE/TTE)**  

| Parameter | Cut‑off for *severe* stenosis in a bioprosthetic aortic valve* | How it is obtained |
|-----------|---------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------|
| **Mean trans‑valvular gradient (MG)** | **≥ 40 mm Hg** (or ≥ 30 mm Hg if flow‑state is low) | Doppler‑derived from the continuous‑wave velocity envelope (ΔP = 4 v²) and the velocity‑time integral |
| **Effective orifice area (EOA) by continuity equation** | **≤ 0.8 cm²** (or ≤ 0.6 cm² in patients with body surface area < 1.5 m²) | LVOT diameter + LVOT VTI → stroke volume; then AVA = (SV × HR) / (AV VTI) |
| **Indexed EOA (EOAi)** | **≤ 0.6 cm²/m²** (severe patient‑prosthesis mismatch) | EOAi = EOA / BSA |
| **Doppler Velocity Index (DVI)** | **≤ 0.25** (severe) | Ratio of LVOT VTI to AV VTI |
| **Peak velocity (Vmax)** | **≥ 4.0 m s⁻¹** | Direct CW Doppler measurement |

*These thresholds are those endorsed by the 2021 ACC/AHA and 2025 ESC valvular guidelines and by the Valve Academic Research Consortium‑3 (VARC‑3) criteria for prosthetic valve dysfunction.*

#### Key teaching points

1. **Severe prosthetic stenosis is defined principally by a mean gradient ≥ 40 mm Hg and an EOA ≤ 0.8 cm² (indexed ≤ 0.6 cm²/m²).** In this patient, the Lotus valve met both criteria in December 2021 (MG 83 mm Hg, AVA 0.5 cm²), prompting the first TAV‑in‑TAV.

2. **Patient‑prosthesis mismatch (PPM) can mask “true” prosthetic stenosis.** After the third valve the AVA was 0.9 cm², which would be acceptable in a native valve, but the indexed AVA (0.4 cm²/m²) and DVI 0.24 indicated severe PPM, explaining the persistently high gradient immediately after implantation.

3. **Serial echocardiography tracks both valve hemodynamics and ventricular remodeling.** Note how EF recovered from 42 % (Lotus failure) to > 60 % after each successful redo, while the gradient fell gradually as the new valve expanded and the LV cavity remodelled.

4. **The mean gradient is a dynamic parameter.** In the early post‑procedure period after the third TAVI it was paradoxically high (67 mm Hg) because the neo‑skirt and overlapping frames limited flow despite an acceptable AVA. By 1 month, the gradient fell to 27 mm Hg, stabilising at 26 mm Hg at 1 year—a pattern typical of high‑risk valve‑in‑valve cases where residual gradient improves with continued frame expansion and LV adaptation.

5. **When assessing prosthetic stenosis after multiple TAV‑in‑TAV, always report the full set of parameters (MG, AVA, EOAi, DVI) rather than relying on a single number.** The combination of a moderate gradient (≈ 30 mm Hg) with a severely reduced indexed AVA unequivocally identifies clinically relevant obstruction in this setting.

**Bottom line:** In this case, severe prosthetic aortic stenosis after each TAV‑in‑TAV was identified by a mean gradient ≥ 40 mm Hg together with an indexed EOA ≤ 0.6 cm²/m² (or DVI ≤ 0.25). The patient’s echo data illustrate the classic trajectory—initial severe obstruction, dramatic improvement after the first redo, recurrent moderate‑to‑severe obstruction after endocarditis, and finally a residual moderate gradient with persistent PPM that stabilises after 1 year. Serial TTE with full haemodynamic profiling is essential for timely re‑intervention and for judging the long‑term success of complex triple‑valve‑in‑valve procedures.

### [Pathophysiology] Explain the potential mechanisms by which infective endocarditis can accelerate structural valve degeneration in a transcatheter bioprosthesis, and why valve‑in‑valve implantation may still be feasible after infection resolution.

**Pathophysiology – How infective endocarditis accelerates structural valve degeneration (SVD) in a transcatheter bioprosthesis, and why a valve‑in‑valve (ViV) can still be performed after infection has been cleared**

1. **Baseline mechanisms of SVD in a transcatheter bioprosthesis**
   - **Mechanical stress** – The nitinol frame and the crimp‑deployed leaflets are subjected to repetitive cyclic loading. Over time, collagen fibers in the pericardial tissue fatigue, resulting in leaflet thickening, calcification, and loss of pliability.
   - **Host‑mediated tissue remodeling** – Inflammatory cytokines (IL‑1β, TNF‑α) and matrix‑metalloproteinases released in the foreign‑body reaction to the frame induce collagen cross‑linking and calcium deposition. This process is already present in “pure” bioprosthetic failure and progresses faster in the high‑pressure aortic environment.

2. **Additional insults introduced by prosthetic‑valve endocarditis (PVE)**
   - **Direct leaflet injury** – Bacterial colonisation (in this case *Enterococcus faecalis*) produces bacterial enzymes (proteases, hyaluronidases) and a fibrin‑platelet mesh that adheres to the leaflets. Even after the vegetations are cleared, the residual micro‑erosions compromise leaflet co‑aptation and stiffen the tissue, pre‑disposing to early calcification.
   - **Inflammatory amplification** – PVE triggers a robust acute‑phase response: high circulating CRP, neutrophil activation, and systemic cytokine surge. These mediators infiltrate the valve tissue, accelerating fibro‑calcific remodeling. Histologic series of explanted infected bioprostheses demonstrate increased macrophage infiltrates, neovascularisation, and early calcium deposits compared with non‑infected controls.
   - **Hemodynamic perturbation during infection** – Fever, tachycardia, and sepsis‑related vasodilation raise trans‑valvular gradients transiently, imposing additional shear stress on already damaged leaflets. In the presented case, the mean gradient rose from 35 mm Hg (post‑first ViV) to 49 mm Hg within weeks of the infection, reflecting rapid functional deterioration.
   - **Endothelial disruption and thrombus formation** – Vegetations create turbulent flow and can seed micro‑thrombi on the leaflet surface. Chronic micro‑thrombi act as a nidus for calcium deposition (the “calcific cascade”), further shrinking the effective orifice area.

3. **Why ViV remains feasible after successful treatment of PVE**
   - **Resolution of active infection** – The case report documents sterile blood cultures, negative TEE for vegetations, and CT showing no perivalvular abscess after a full 6‑week IV ampicillin regimen. Once the infectious burden is eradicated, the prosthetic material is no longer a nidus for bacterial persistence, eliminating the primary contraindication to re‑intervention.
   - **Preserved structural scaffold** – Although the leaflets are damaged, the nitinol frame of the prior transcatheter valve remains well integrated into the aortic wall (CT demonstrated a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm). This stable “landing zone” allows a new valve to be anchored safely, provided coronary heights and sinus dimensions are adequate—a determination that was confirmed by the pre‑procedural MSCT.
   - **Absence of uncontrolled inflammation** – After antimicrobial therapy, systemic inflammatory markers return to baseline, and the local tissue reaction subsides. The risk of a new infection after a second implantation is therefore comparable to that of a primary TAVI in a non‑infected patient.
   - **Technical strategies to mitigate residual risk** – In this patient the third valve (Acurate neo2) was positioned high, aligned with the leaflets of the Evolut valve, and post‑dilated to expand the underlying frame. These maneuvers improve the effective orifice area, reduce patient‑prosthesis mismatch, and minimise turbulent flow that could otherwise promote recurrent leaflet injury.
   - **Evidence from the case** – At 1‑year follow‑up the valve‑in‑valve‑in‑valve (TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV) configuration showed stable gradients (mean 26 mm Hg), no regurgitation, and no recurrence of infection, demonstrating that once the infection is definitively cleared, the same anatomic site can safely accommodate additional transcatheter prostheses.

**Take‑home points for trainees**

- PVE accelerates SVD through direct leaflet destruction, amplified inflammatory remodeling, and hemodynamic stress.
- Successful eradication of infection (negative cultures, imaging without vegetations) removes the principal barrier to repeat transcatheter intervention.
- Pre‑procedural multimodality imaging (TEE + high‑resolution CT) is essential to confirm a sterile field, assess frame integrity, and ensure coronary safety before a ViV (or ViV‑in‑ViV) is performed.
- Even in a triple‑stented aorta, careful valve selection (supra‑annular design, low frame height) and high implantation can preserve effective orifice area and coronary access, allowing durable outcomes after infective endocarditis–mediated degeneration.

### [Differential Diagnosis] What are the relative advantages and disadvantages of using a self‑expanding supra‑annular valve (e.g., Acurate neo2) versus a balloon‑expandable valve for a third‑in‑series TAV implantation in a narrow annulus with prior overlapping stent frames?

**Answer – Self‑expanding supra‑annular (Acurate neo2) versus balloon‑expandable valve for a “TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV” in a narrow annulus with prior overlapping frames**

In the case presented, the heart team chose a self‑expanding, supra‑annular Acurate neo2 for the third transcatheter valve because the anatomy (13.7 mm minimum lumen in the Evolut frame) and the need to preserve coronary access made its design advantages outweigh its limitations. The same considerations apply to any repeat TAV in a narrow annulus that already contains two stent frames.

| **Feature** | **Self‑expanding supra‑annular (Acurate neo2)** | **Balloon‑expandable (e.g., Sapien 3/Ultra)** |
|-------------|-----------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------|
| **Leaflet position** | Supra‑annular – leaflets sit above the frame, giving a larger effective orifice area (EOA) in small annuli. In the case, this helped achieve an indexed AVA of 0.4 cm²/m² after implantation, which later improved to 1.1 cm². | Intra‑annular – leaflets are confined within the stent, which can worsen patient‑prosthesis mismatch (PPM) when the landing zone is already narrowed by two previous frames. |
| **Radial force** | Low‑to‑moderate, continuous outward force; can gently expand the previously implanted frame (no intentional frame fracture needed). Post‑dilatation in the case further enlarged the Evolut frame and lowered the mean gradient from 67 mm Hg immediately after deployment to 27 mm Hg at 1 month. | High initial radial force during balloon inflation; may achieve fuller expansion of a rigid prior frame but also carries a higher risk of annular rupture or coronary obstruction when the landing zone is heavily scarred. |
| **Frame height / profile** | Shorter nitinol frame; less mesh above the commissures, which is helpful for coronary re‑access in a triple‑stented root. The case authors purposely aligned the Acurate commissures with the Evolut leaflets to maximise coronary clearance. | Taller stainless‑steel or cobalt‑chromium frame; more struts sit above the coronary ostia, increasing the likelihood of future coronary access difficulty after three layers of metal. |
| **Deployability** | Re‑positionable and recapturable before full release – valuable when the landing zone is uncertain. In the reported procedure the valve was aligned on fluoroscopic landmarks derived from CT, allowing precise high implantation. | Once inflated, the valve cannot be repositioned; any mal‑position must be managed with secondary devices (e.g., valve‑in‑valve, valve‑fracture). |
| **Risk of coronary obstruction** | Supra‑annular design and low frame height reduce the “neo‑skirt” that can cover the ostia; CT showed adequate valve‑to‑coronary distance, and no coronary protection was required. | Balloon‑expandable frames often have a larger external skirt that may protrude into the sinuses, heightening the risk of obstruction when previous frames already reduce sinus volume. |
| **Hemodynamic performance in small annuli** | Generally superior mean gradients and larger indexed EOA because the orifice is not constrained by the stent. In this patient, the final mean gradient settled at 26 mm Hg (acceptable for a 70‑year‑old) despite three overlapping prostheses. | Tend to produce higher gradients in small annuli, especially when the effective orifice is limited by the inner frame of earlier valves. |
| **Potential for valve‑to‑valve interaction** | The nitinol frame can conform around the previous stent, limiting interface stress and decreasing the chance of frame fracture. | Rigid frame can press against prior metal, increasing the risk of fracture or conduction disturbances, particularly in a previously Lotus (mechanically fixed) followed by Evolut (self‑expanding). |
| **Conduction system disturbance** | Lower radial force → lower incidence of new‑onset left‑bundle branch block or need for permanent pacing (important in a patient already pacemaker‑dependent). | Higher radial force → higher rates of new conduction abnormalities; less relevant here but a consideration for future cases. |

### Practical take‑aways for a third‑in‑series TAV in a narrow annulus

1. **Effective orifice area is paramount** – With each additional frame the residual lumen shrinks; a supra‑annular valve maximises the EOA and mitigates severe PPM, which was evident in the case (AVA increased from 0.5 cm² to 1.1 cm² after the Acurate implant).

2. **Coronary access preservation** – Aligning the commissures of a short, low‑profile self‑expanding valve with the leaflets of the preceding valve reduces the cumulative mesh burden above the coronaries. This was a pre‑procedural CT‑guided strategy that prevented the need for coronary protection.

3. **Re‑positionability** – The ability to recapture and fine‑tune depth before release is especially valuable when the landing zone is defined by two overlapping frames whose exact inner diameter may be difficult to gauge.

4. **Radial force balance** – In a heavily scarred sinus, a moderate, continuous outward force (self‑expanding) is often safer than the high‑pressure burst of a balloon‑expandable device, which can cause annular rupture or disrupt the integrity of prior frames.

5. **Future re‑intervention** – If another valve will eventually be needed, the shorter height of the Acurate leaves more “vertical room” for subsequent devices and for coronary catheters. Balloon‑expandable frames accrue more metal height, potentially sealing off the sinus after the third layer.

### When a balloon‑expandable valve might still be preferred

- **Very calcified annulus with a rigid, narrow landing zone** where maximal immediate radial force is needed to fracture a severely stenotic prior frame.
- **Anatomy with high coronary ostia and generous sinus dimensions**, making obstruction unlikely; the higher frame may then be acceptable.
- **Operator preference or institutional experience** with specific balloon‑expandable devices that have proven low gradients in similar “valve‑in‑valve” scenarios (e.g., the newer Sapien 3 Ultra with a lower skirt profile).

### Bottom line

For a third TAV implanted into an already narrow annulus that already houses two overlapping stent frames, a self‑expanding supra‑annular valve such as the Acurate neo2 offers:

- Larger post‑implant EOA and lower gradients,
- Lower profile and better coronary clearance,
- Re‑positionability for optimal depth,
- Safer radial force profile in a heavily scarred root.

The primary disadvantage is the lower immediate radial force, which may limit the ability to fracture a severely calcified prior frame—something that can be mitigated by high‑pressure post‑dilatation, as was done in the case. Balloon‑expandable valves retain a role when maximal expansion force is required or when the anatomy is otherwise favorable.

### [Management] Discuss the future considerations for coronary re‑access after multiple TAV‑in‑TAV procedures, including the impact of commissural alignment and frame design on the feasibility of subsequent percutaneous coronary interventions.

**Future coronary re‑access after serial TAV‑in‑TAV procedures – what the clinician must keep in mind**

The case of a 70‑year‑old man who has now received three transcatheter valves (Lotus → Evolut → Acurate neo2) illustrates the practical limits of “lifetime” aortic valve therapy. Each additional frame adds metal, reduces the sinus‑of‑Valsalva volume, and can compromise the geometry that permits a catheter to reach the coronary ostia. When we plan a second, third, or even a fourth TAV we must ask three questions that will determine whether future percutaneous coronary interventions (PCI) remain feasible:

| Question | Why it matters | How the case addressed it |
|----------|----------------|---------------------------|
| **1. What is the coronary‑to‑annular distance after the new valve is implanted?** | The *neo‑skirt* (the portion of the valve frame that lies below the coronary ostia) must be ≥ 4 mm to avoid immediate obstruction and to leave a “gate” for catheters. | Pre‑procedural CT measured coronary heights (≈13 mm) and sinus dimensions; the Acurate neo2 was deliberately positioned high, aligning its commissures with the Evolut leaflets so that the neo‑skirt sat **above** the coronary ostia. |
| **2. Is the frame design compatible with coronary re‑access?** | Low‑profile, supra‑annular devices (e.g., Acurate neo2, Evolut R/PRO) have shorter stent cells and fewer “meshes” obstructing the sinus compared with taller, intra‑annular valves (e.g., Lotus). | The third valve was chosen for its low frame height and for the fact that its “crown” can be aligned with the previous frame’s commissures, creating a predictable “window” through the overlapping mesh. |
| **3. Was commissural alignment achieved?** | When the commissures of each successive valve are coaxial, the stent struts line up, leaving a larger, straight path to the coronaries. Mis‑alignment creates a “cobweb” of wires that can block catheters even if the ostial height is adequate. | CT‑based 3‑D modelling was used to pre‑set the delivery catheter rotation; fluoroscopic landmarks confirmed alignment of the Acurate neo2 commissures with those of the Evolut valve. |

### Key take‑aways for future planning

1. **Start the conversation at the index TAV.**  
   - For patients < 80 yr or with known coronary disease, favor a valve that permits future coronary access (supra‑annular, low frame, commissure‑aligned).  
   - Record the intended commissural orientation and implant depth on the procedural report; this becomes the reference for any redo.

2. **Use high‑resolution CT for every redo.**  
   - Measure: (a) coronary ostial height, (b) sinus of Valsalva diameter, (c) distance from the outermost frame of the *most distal* valve to the ostia, and (d) the projected “cell” geometry at the intended landing zone.  
   - In this case, the minimal lumen of the Evolut frame (13.7 mm) and the sinus dimensions gave a safe margin for a high Acurate neo2 implantation.

3. **Select a valve that maximizes effective orifice area (EOA) while minimizing additional metal.**  
   - Acurate neo2 offers supra‑annular leaflets (larger EOA) and a relatively short frame, reducing the cumulative “metal burden.”  
   - Avoid adding a tall intra‑annular valve unless the sinus is markedly oversized; otherwise you risk severe prosthesis‑patient mismatch and complete loss of coronary access.

4. **Plan for a high implantation depth.**  
   - Deploy the new valve at the level of the prior valve leaflets rather than at the native annulus. This “seal‑against‑leaflet” technique preserves sinus space and limits the creation of a new neo‑skirt below the coronaries.  
   - The case report demonstrates that a high Acurate neo2 deployment prevented immediate coronary obstruction and left a clear corridor for future catheters.

5. **Consider adjunctive techniques when the sinus is marginal.**  
   - **BASILICA** (leaflet laceration) or **valve‐fracture** can enlarge the effective orifice and create a larger gap, but their safety in triple‑stented anatomy is still unproven.  
   - In the present patient, careful sizing and high implantation obviated the need for such maneuvers.

6. **Document the “coronary clearance” metric.**  
   - After each TAV, record the measured valve‑to‑coronary distance on the angiogram and store the 3‑D CT reconstruction. This objective data will guide the heart team when the next degeneration occurs.

### Practical algorithm for a patient who may need a fourth TAV

1. **CT‑based feasibility** → If coronary‑to‑frame distance ≥ 4 mm after virtual implantation of the next valve, proceed with a low‑profile, commissure‑aligned device.  
2. **If distance < 4 mm** →  
   - Discuss coronary protection (balloon/guidewire) during the procedure, or  
   - Consider **BASILICA** of the at‑risk leaflet, or  
   - Evaluate surgical options despite high risk (root replacement) because percutaneous access may be impossible.  
3. **If frame height would exceed sinus dimensions**, select a **short‑frame valve** (e.g., Evolut PRO+, Acurate neo2) and accept a higher implantation depth, even if it means a modest residual gradient.

### Bottom line

The case of TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV after treated endocarditis teaches that **coronary re‑access is a finite resource**. Each additional valve consumes sinus space and adds mesh; without deliberate planning—high implantation, low‑profile frames, and commissural alignment—we risk closing the corridor permanently. By incorporating CT‑guided measurements, choosing the appropriate valve geometry, and aligning commissures at every step, we preserve the ability to treat future coronary disease percutaneously, thereby extending the “lifetime” of transcatheter aortic valve therapy.
