# When Three Valves Aren’t Enough: Managing TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV Failures  

*Deep dive into the technical and anatomic challenges of a third‑generation transcatheter valve implantation.*

---  

## Why This Case Is Worth Your Attention  

A 70‑plus‑year‑old gentleman has already survived **two transcatheter aortic valve implantations (TAVIs)** and a bout of **prosthetic valve endocarditis**. By 2024 he needed **a third TAVI** – a *TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV* procedure that most of us have only seen in a handful of case reports.  

What makes it compelling isn’t just the “triple‑valve” factoid; it’s the **real‑world problem set** we’ll all face as TAVI expands:

* **Structural valve deterioration (SVD)** is no longer a curiosity – it’s becoming a predictable part of the patient’s life‑time journey.  
* **Endocarditis** adds a layer of infection‑control complexity that many operators are uncomfortable navigating with a percutaneous approach.  
* **Anatomical constraints** – overlapping frames, limited sinus dimensions, low coronary ostia – turn a routine valve deployment into a high‑stakes puzzle.  

The authors’ success (asymptomatic at 1 year, good gradients) offers a practical template for future “third‑generation” redo cases. Let’s unpack the pearls.

---

## Timeline in a Nutshell  

| Year | Event |
|------|-------|
| **2014** | 27 mm **Lotus** (first TAVI) for severe native AS. |
| **2022** (May) | **TAV‑in‑TAV**: 23 mm **Evolut** inside Lotus (complicated by femoral pseudo‑aneurysm). |
| **2022** (Sept) | Prosthetic valve endocarditis (Enterococcus faecalis); 6 weeks IV ampicillin → cure. |
| **2024** (June) | **TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV**: **Acurate neo2** (size S) implanted inside Evolut/Lotus. |
| **2025** (June) | 1‑year follow‑up: NYHA I, mean gradient ≈ 26 mm Hg, AVA ≈ 1.1 cm², no infection. |

---

## Core Clinical Pearls  

### 1. **Never Let Infection Status Be a Blanket Contra‑Indication**  
* Once blood cultures are negative, TEE shows no vegetations, and CRP normalizes, it is reasonable to consider a redo TAVI if surgical risk is prohibitive.  

### 2. **CT‑Guided Road‑Mapping Is Non‑Negotiable**  
* Measure **coronary ostial height**, **sinus of Valsalva diameter**, and **frame‑to‑coronary distance** after each implanted valve.  
* Re‑create the **virtual landing zone** for the next device; in this case a minimum 13.7 mm lumen in the Evolut frame dictated the need for a low‑profile, supra‑annular valve.  

### 3. **Choose the Valve That Plays Nicely With What’s Already There**  
* **Acurate neo2**: low frame height, supra‑annular leaflets, built‑in commissural alignment markers → preserves coronary access and maximises effective orifice area (EOA).  
* Avoid heavily post‑dilated or tall self‑expanding devices that could bury the coronaries under another “skirt.”  

### 4. **High (or “leaflet‑aligned”) Implantation Can Rescue the Effective Orifice Area**  
* Deploy the new valve **at the level of the prior prosthesis leaflets** (not too deep). This pins the previous leaflets outward, reduces “neo‑skirt” obstruction, and improves downstream flow.  

### 5. **Don’t Forget the “Minor” Details: Commissural Alignment, Post‑Dilatation, and Frame Fracture**  
* Align the neo2 commissures with the Evolut leaflets → opens a corridor for future coronary work.  
* A modest post‑dilatation (20 mm × 40 mm) under rapid pacing helped expand the embedded Evolut frame, lowering the mean gradient from 67 mm Hg immediately post‑implant to 27 mm Hg at 1 month.  
* **Valve fracture** was not needed here but remains an option if gradients stay prohibitive.  

### 6. **Multidisciplinary Heart‑Team Decision‑Making Is the Bedrock**  
* Infectious disease clearance, cardiac imaging, interventional cardiology, cardiac surgery, and anesthesia all contributed. This mitigates the “off‑label” nature of a third TAVI.  

### 7. **Plan for Future Interventions From Day 1**  
* Even if the patient is “cured,” a triple‑stented root can become a **coronary access nightmare** later.  
* Document the commissural orientation, keep a 3‑D CT archive, and discuss potential BASILICA or valve‑in‑valve‑in‑valve strategies should coronary disease progress.  

---

## Procedural Blueprint (Step‑by‑Step)

1. **Pre‑Procedure Imaging**  
   * **MSCT** with thin‑slice reconstructions → assess sinus size, frame overlap, and coronary clearance.  
   * **TEE** to reconfirm infection eradication and rule out paravalvular leak.  

2. **Access Strategy**  
   * Left radial for coronary protection (if needed) and left femoral for valve delivery.  
   * Use a **Safari** or similar stiff wire positioned in the LV.  

3. **Balloon Pre‑Dilate** (22 mm × 40 mm) under rapid pacing (≈180 bpm) to open the stiff Evolut frame and gauge coronary flow.  

4. **Deploy Acurate neo2 (Size S)**  
   * Align neo2 commissures with Evolut leaflets using CT‑derived fluoroscopic landmarks.  
   * Target a **high implantation** just above the Evolut leaflets.  

5. **Post‑Dilatation** (20 mm × 40 mm) under rapid pacing to fine‑tune expansion and improve gradients.  

6. **Immediate Assessment**  
   * Aortography → confirm no coronary obstruction, no perivalvular leak.  
   * Hemodynamics → note initial gradient (expected high), then re‑evaluate after post‑dilatation.  

7. **Post‑Procedural Surveillance**  
   * TTE before discharge → baseline gradients, AVA, regurgitation.  
   * Follow‑up at 1 month, 3 months, and 1 year: watch for gradient normalization, LV remodeling, and infection recurrence.  

---

## Take‑Home Messages (Bullet List)

- **Serial TAVIs are feasible** if infection is cleared, surgical risk is prohibitive, and CT shows adequate coronary clearance.  
- **Pre‑procedural CT is the cornerstone** – precise measurements of lumen diameter, sinus dimensions, and valve‑to‑coronary distances guide valve choice and implantation depth.  
- **Select a low‑profile, supra‑annular valve** (e.g., Acurate neo2) to maximize effective orifice area in a narrow, multilayered annulus.  
- **High, commissure‑aligned implantation** reduces neo‑skirt obstruction, preserves coronary access, and improves hemodynamics.  
- **Post‑dilatation can dramatically lower gradients** by expanding the previously implanted frame; consider it routinely in triple‑stented anatomy.  
- **Multidisciplinary heart‑team review** ensures that infection status, anatomical feasibility, and patient preferences are all reconciled before an off‑label redo.  
- **Future‑proof your plan** – document commissural orientation and keep 3‑D imaging on file; anticipate potential coronary interventions or another valve‑in‑valve down the line.  

---

## Final Thoughts  

As TAVI moves from “high‑risk only” to a **lifetime management strategy**, we must start thinking like architects rather than just surgeons. Every valve we implant is a **structural element** that will influence the next iteration. The case of a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV after treated endocarditis shows that with meticulous CT planning, judicious valve selection, and a heart‑team approach, even the most tangled aortic roots can be successfully navigated.

Keep your imaging stack updated, respect the anatomy, and don’t shy away from a third valve when the patient’s physiology, not the novelty of the procedure, dictates it. The future will likely bring **fourth‑generation** or hybrid solutions, but the lessons from this case—*measure twice, align wisely, and protect the coronaries*—will remain timeless.  

Stay sharp, stay open‑access, and happy valve‑ing!



# Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis Meets TAVR: When Is a Redo TAVR Safe?

## Why This Case Stands Out  

Imagine a 78‑year‑old gentleman who has already survived **two transcatheter aortic valve implantations (TAVIs)**, a **pulse‑purulent femoral pseudo‑aneurysm**, and a **treated prosthetic‑valve endocarditis (PVE)** caused by *Enterococcus faecalis*. Nine years after his first Lotus valve, his aortic valve is now a tower of three overlapping frames – a **TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV** construct. Yet, despite the anatomic nightmare, he is doing fine one year after the third percutaneous valve replacement.  

This scenario forces us to confront a question that is still being scripted in the guidelines: **When is it reasonable to go back to TAVR after a prosthetic valve infection?** The answer is not a simple “yes or no.” It depends on a checklist of infection control, imaging, timing, and the ever‑shifting balance between surgical risk and transcatheter feasibility. Below we walk through the pearls that the case of Merkulov et al. illustrates, and then expand to the broader evidence base.

## The Infection‑Control Checklist  

Redo TAVR after PVE is only conceivable when the infection is truly **quiescent**. The following elements should be confirmed before the Heart Team green‑lights a transcatheter redo:

1. **Completion of a full antimicrobial course** (usually 6 weeks of IV therapy for *Enterococcus* or *Staphylococcus* prosthetic infections).  
2. **Negative blood cultures** taken at least 48 h apart after therapy.  
3. **Absence of vegetations or other active lesions** on **trans‑esophageal echocardiography (TEE)**.  
4. **No perivalvular abscess, pseudo‑aneurysm, or fistula** on multimodality imaging (CT/MRI).  
5. **Stable inflammatory markers** (CRP, ESR) trending toward normal.  

In the reported case, the patient completed a 6‑week ampicillin‑based regimen, had three consecutive negative cultures, and repeat TEE/CT showed clean leaflets with no residual infection. This satisfied the infection‑control gate.

### Timing Matters  

The **optimal interval** between finishing antibiotics and attempting a redo TAVR is not set in stone, but most series report a **minimum of 4–6 weeks**. In practice, several factors influence the decision:

* **Clinical stability:** The patient must be euvolemic, with controlled heart failure symptoms.  
* **Hemodynamic urgency:** If valve gradients are life‑threatening, earlier intervention may be justified after confirming infection control.  
* **Imaging readiness:** A high‑resolution gated CT is required to map out the existing frames and coronary ostia; this may dictate scheduling.  

The case patient was re‑intervened **≈ 5 months** after completing antibiotics, giving ample time for tissue healing and for the multidisciplinary team to acquire and analyse the CT data.

## Imaging: The Bedrock of a Safe Redo  

When you already have two frames in place, the **effective orifice area (EOA)** shrinks dramatically, and the risk of **coronary obstruction** spikes. Multiphase, ECG‑gated **multislice CT (MSCT)** is non‑negotiable for:

* Measuring **coronary ostial height** and **sinus of Valsalva dimensions**.  
* Assessing **frame‐to‑coronary distance** after each prior valve – the “virtual valve‐to‑coronary” (VTC) metric must stay > 4 mm for safety.  
* Planning **implant depth** to align commissures and avoid the “neo‑skirt” covering the coronary ostia.  
* Simulating **post‑dilatation** to predict how much the inner frames will expand.  

In the described patient, the CT showed a **minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm** inside the Evolut frame – borderline for a 23 mm self‑expanding valve but acceptable for a **supra‑annular Acurate neo2** with a low frame profile. The team deliberately aligned the new valve’s commissures with the Evolut leaflets, preserving a corridor for future coronary access.

## Choosing Between Surgery and Redo TAVR  

### When Surgery Remains the Gold Standard  

* **Uncontrolled infection** (persistent bacteremia, abscesses).  
* **Extensive annular or root destruction** requiring reconstruction.  
* **Concomitant cardiac pathology** (mitral valve disease, LVOT obstruction) that needs open repair.  

### When Redo TAVR Wins the Day  

* **Prohibitive surgical risk** (EuroSCORE II > 25 % or frailty > 4).  
* **Favorable anatomy** after CT – adequate coronary clearance, enough sinus space, and a viable landing zone.  
* **Completed infection treatment** with clean imaging.  

The patient’s EuroSCORE II of **27 %**, prior extensive PCI, COPD, chronic GI bleeding, and the presence of two already firmly integrated frames shifted the risk‑benefit ratio heavily toward a percutaneous approach.

## Key Technical Pearls from the Triple‑Valve Case  

* **High implantation (“leaflet‑to‑leaflet” positioning)** – by nesting the new valve’s inflow at the level of the prior Evolut leaflets, the team maximized residual EOA and minimized the neo‑skirt obstructing the coronaries.  
* **Commissural alignment** – Acurate neo2’s design supports oriented deployment; aligning the new frame’s commissures with those of the older valve preserves a “coronary window”.  
* **Post‑dilatation strategy** – a 20/40 mm balloon under rapid pacing expanded both the new valve and helped radially open the inner Evolut frame, reducing the post‑procedure mean gradient from 67 mm Hg (immediate) to 27 mm Hg at one month.  
* **Coronary protection not needed** – pre‑procedural CT predicted > 6 mm clearance; intra‑procedural aortograms confirmed perfusion, so no “chimney” stents or BASILICA were required.  

These tactics collectively transformed a potentially catastrophic triple‑stent scenario into a durable solution.

## Take‑Home Messages  

- **Redo TAVR after prosthetic‑valve endocarditis is possible**, but only after stringent infection clearance (negative cultures, clean TEE/CT, normalized inflammatory markers).  
- **Multimodality CT is indispensable**; it guides valve choice, implantation depth, commissural alignment, and predicts coronary obstruction risk.  
- **High implantation and commissural alignment are the twin pillars** of a successful TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV procedure, especially in narrow sinuses.  
- **Surgical risk outweighing anatomic complexity tips the scales** toward a percutaneous redo, even in octogenarians with multiple prior interventions.  
- **Long‑term surveillance matters** – yearly echocardiography remains crucial as the residual effective orifice area may drift over time, and coronary access may become increasingly challenging.  

In the rapidly expanding world of structural heart disease, we are learning to think **“lifetime management”** from day one. The case of a triple‑layered TAVR after treated endocarditis reminds us that with meticulous imaging, a disciplined infection work‑up, and a tailored implantation strategy, a redo TAVR can be both safe and durable—even in the most tangled anatomies.  

**Bottom line:** When infection is truly gone, the aortic root is anatomically permissive, and surgical risk is prohibitive, a redo TAVR is not just a salvage maneuver; it can be a rational, evidence‑based choice that delivers years of symptom‑free life. Stay vigilant, keep your CT scanner calibrated, and let the Heart Team discuss all the “what‑ifs” before you push that third valve across the aortic annulus.



# Lifetime Planning for Structural Valve Disease: From First TAVR to the Third  

## Why this case matters  

When we first started doing TAVR, the mantra was “if the patient is high‑risk, we can safely avoid open surgery.” Fast forward a decade and the same patient who received a transcatheter aortic valve in 2014 may now be sitting in our clinic needing a *third* transcatheter valve – a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV. The report by Merkulov et al. describes exactly that scenario: an octogenarian with a Lotus valve, a subsequent Evolut valve, prosthetic‑valve endocarditis, and finally an Acurate neo2 placed three years later.  

What makes it compelling isn’t just the technical feat of cramming a fourth frame into a tiny aortic root. It’s the lesson that **every TAVR we implant should be thought of as the first step in a multi‑decade, multi‑procedure journey**. If we ignore “valve‑in‑valve stewardship” today, we may trap tomorrow’s patients in a maze of overlapping stents, impossible coronary re‑access, and prohibitive patient‑prosthesis mismatch (PPM).  

## The big picture: valve‑in‑valve stewardship  

1. **Know the likely lifespan of your first valve** – modern THVs last 10‑15 years on average, but durability is highly device‑specific. In younger or lower‑risk patients, you must assume at least one redo will be needed.  

2. **Choose a platform that “plays well” with others** – supra‑annular designs (e.g., Evolut, Acurate neo2) give a larger effective orifice area (EOA) after multiple layers, while intra‑annular devices (e.g., Sapien 3) add bulk to the annulus and raise the risk of severe PPM.  

3. **Plan the “future landing zone” at the index procedure** – use multidetector CT to measure:  
   * Annular dimensions, sinus of Valsalva, sinotubular junction  
   * Coronary ostial heights and distance to the valve frame (VA‑C distance)  
   * The “virtual valve‑to‑coronary” distance after implantation of a given THV size  

   Those numbers become the reference for any later ViV (valve‑in‑valve) or ViViV (valve‑in‑valve‑in‑valve) procedure.  

4. **Commissural alignment is not optional** – aligning the THV commissures with native leaflets (or with a prior THV) creates “windows” for coronary access that persist through successive implants. In the case above, the team deliberately aligned the Acurate neo2 leaflets with the Evolut frame to preserve coronary flow.  

5. **Document the strategy** – write the “valve trajectory” into the patient’s chart (and into the heart‑team’s shared decision‑making note). Include: projected valve size for the next redo, anticipated valve type, and any caveats (e.g., low coronary ostia, small sinus).   

## Imaging pearls – the CT that guides a third TAVR  

The key to the success in this case was a meticulously performed multislice CT (MSCT) that answered three questions:  

* **Is there enough residual lumen?** The Evolut frame measured a minimum lumen of 13.7 mm – enough to accommodate a 23 mm Acurate neo2 but borderline for larger devices.  

* **Will the coronaries stay open?** The coronary ostia were >12 mm from the valve‑to‑coronary distance after simulated implantation, meaning a coronary protection strategy (e.g., guidewire, balloon) was not mandatory.  

* **What implantation depth will optimise hemodynamics?** By aligning the new valve’s inflow at the level of the Evolut leaflets, the operators obtained a higher effective orifice area and avoided excessive “neo‑skirt” that could have impinged the coronary ostia.  

A practical tip: use dedicated 3‑D planning software (e.g., 3mensio, Materialise) to virtually “stack” the planned valve on the existing frames. The visualized overlap lets you judge the risk of coronary obstruction, frame crowding, and PPM before you ever touch the cath lab.  

## Procedural strategies that matter  

* **High implantation** – deploying the third valve slightly higher than usual keeps the neo‑skirt away from the coronaries and leaves room for future devices.  

* **Balloon post‑dilatation** – a controlled, high‑pressure post‑dilatation can “fracture” the prior valve’s frame enough to improve EOA without the need for full‑frame fracture (which carries higher rupture risk).  

* **Self‑expanding versus balloon‑expandable** – in a narrow annulus already occupied by two frames, a low‑profile self‑expanding valve (Acurate neo2, Evolut) is often easier to deliver and can conform better to the irregular geometry.  

* **Coronary protection when in doubt** – keep a side‑arm wire in the left coronary and be ready to balloon‑inflate a protective balloon if the aortography shows any compromise.  

## Take‑home pearls (bullet form for quick recall)  

- **Think long‑term at the first TAVR** – anticipate at least one redo, especially in patients <80 years or with low surgical risk.  
- **Prefer supra‑annular THVs when future ViV is likely** – larger post‑implant EOA translates into less PPM after multiple layers.  
- **Mandate pre‑procedural CT for every redo** – map annular dimensions, coronary heights, sinus size, and simulate the next valve.  
- **Aim for commissural alignment** – use fluoroscopic landmarks or 3‑D CT overlay to line up the new valve’s commissures with the previous device.  
- **Plan the landing zone depth** – a slightly higher implantation preserves coronary flow and leaves a “buffer” for later procedures.  
- **Document a “valve trajectory” in the chart** – include projected valve size, expected coronary clearance, and preferred platform for the next step.  
- **Accept that PPM will be a reality after ≥2 ViV** – mitigate it with larger supra‑annular valves, high implantation, and aggressive post‑dilatation.  
- **Coronary access will become harder** – if the patient has significant CAD, prioritize coronary‑friendly THVs (e.g., Evolut PRO+, Acurate neo) and consider BASILICA or valve‑fracture only when absolutely necessary.  

## Looking ahead – what does this mean for our practice?  

The “lifetime management” mindset is shifting from a reactive to a proactive discipline. In the same way we now screen for coronary artery disease before TAVR, we must screen for **future valve‑in‑valve feasibility** before we place the first valve.  

* **Multidisciplinary heart teams** should include an imaging specialist who can generate the 3‑D “stacked‑valve” simulation.  
* **Device manufacturers** might consider offering “future‑friendly” platforms with lower frame bulk, wider commissural windows, and built‑in markers for alignment.  
* **Registries** need to capture not just 30‑day outcomes but also planned re‑intervention strategy, valve types used at each step, and long‑term coronary access success.  

In the case presented, the patient went from a 27 mm Lotus (a “first‑generation” valve now notorious for its bulky frame) to a 23 mm Evolut and finally a size‑S Acurate neo2. The team leveraged CT‑guided planning, high implantation, and careful commissural alignment to achieve a functional result that held up at one year.  

If we apply these stewardship principles now, the next generation of patients may avoid the “triple‑valve” conundrum altogether, or at least navigate it with a clearer roadmap.  

**Bottom line:** every TAVR is the start of a marathon, not a sprint. Plan the route, choose the right shoes, and keep an eye on the finish line that may be many years away.



# Coronary Access After Multiple TAVR Layers: Imaging, Techniques, and Future Options  

## Why This Case Stands Out  

When you hear “TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV,” the first thought is usually *“how on earth do you keep the coronaries open?”* The recent case from the Chazov Center shows exactly that dilemma. A septuagenarian who already had two transcatheter aortic valve implants—and even survived prosthetic‑valve endocarditis—required a third valve. The team succeeded, but they had to **engineer coronary patency** from the ground up. That’s the sort of scenario that is moving from “once‑in‑a‑blue‑moon” to “keep‑your‑eye‑on‑this‑for‑the‑next‑decade” as TAVR expands into younger, lower‑risk patients who will inevitably outlive their first device.

The take‑home message? **If you’re going to stack valves, you must stack the planning.** Imaging, commissural alignment, and a toolbox of adjunctive techniques become the lifelines that keep the coronary ostia reachable for everything from routine angiography to emergent PCI.

## Imaging the Coronary Landscape Before the Third Valve  

A meticulous CT work‑up is non‑negotiable when you’re contemplating a third TAVR. In the reported case, the team used multi‑slice CT (MSCT) to draw a 3‑D map that answered three pivotal questions:

1. **Coronary Ostial Height** – The distance from the annular plane to the left and right coronary ostia. The rule of thumb remains > 10 mm to avoid obstruction, but with stacked frames you need a *buffer* that accounts for the “neo‑skirt” (the frame‑leaflet complex of the newly implanted valve) plus the residual frame of the previous valve(s).

2. **Sinus of Valsalva Dimensions** – The width of the sinus determines whether there’s enough “room” for the displaced leaflets of the older valve to move aside. A narrow sinus (< 30 mm) is a red flag; you may need to consider coronary protection (balloon‑catheter or wire) or an alternative strategy.

3. **Valve‑to‑Coronary Distance (VTC)** – This is measured from the projected outer edge of the upcoming valve frame to the coronary ostia after deployment. A VTC < 4 mm is associated with a high obstruction risk. In the triple‑layered anatomy, you have to model *each* frame’s impact on VTC, which the team did by overlaying the proposed Acurate neo2 onto the existing Evolut and Lotus scaffolds.

Beyond these static measurements, looking at **commissural orientation** on the CT helps you decide how to orient the new valve’s commissures relative to the coronaries. Modern 3‑D workstations can simulate valve rotation; a few degrees of tweak can shift the “neo‑skirt” away from the ostia dramatically.

### Practical Imaging Checklist  

- **Annular sizing**: still the foundation, but now you’re also sizing the *effective* landing zone after accounting for previous frames.  
- **Coronary height & sinus width**: record both absolute values and the *projected* values after the new valve.  
- **Frame overlap zone**: identify where the prior valve’s cells intersect the coronary ostia; this is where BASILICA or valve fracture might be needed.  
- **Commissural alignment plan**: decide on the target rotation (commonly 0°, 30°, or 60°) that lines the new valve’s commissures away from the ostia.  
- **Coronary protection plan**: if VTC < 4 mm, place a wire or balloon in the at‑risk coronary before deployment.

## Procedural Strategies to Preserve Coronary Access  

### 1. Commissural Alignment  

The Acurate neo2 used in the case is designed for **top‑down alignment**; its “crown” sits just above the annulus, and the three commissural posts can be rotated to line up with the native leaflets. By aligning the new valve’s commissures with the *Evolut* leaflets (instead of the Lotus frame), the operators created a wider central orifice and a more favorable “neo‑skirt” geometry.  

**Key point:** When you know you’ll need future coronary access, **aim for a commissural orientation that points the frame’s cells away from the coronaries**. For self‑expanding valves (Evolut, Acurate), this is achieved by rotating the delivery system under fluoroscopy using the three radiopaque markers.

### 2. High Implantation Technique  

Deploying the third valve **higher** in the aortic root (just above the leaflets of the second valve) serves two purposes:

- It pushes the new valve’s sealing skirt *above* the coronary ostia, reducing the chance that displaced leaflets will occlude them.  
- It preserves a “valve‑to‑coronary” clearance for future catheters.  

In the case report, the team pre‑dilated and post‑dilated under rapid pacing but monitored aortography to confirm that coronaries remained patent throughout.

### 3. Coronary Protection (Balloon/Wire)  

If pre‑procedural CT predicts a tight VTC, you can **pre‑emptively wire the at‑risk coronary** and have a low‑profile balloon ready for rapid inflation (“chimney” technique). Even if you never need to deploy the balloon, the wire itself provides a safety rail for immediate PCI if obstruction occurs.

### 4. BASILICA (Bioprosthetic or Native Aortic Scallop Intentional Laceration to Prevent Iatrogenic Coronary Artery Obstruction)  

BASILICA is most often discussed in valve‑in‑valve scenarios where the leaflets of the surgical prosthesis may obstruct flow. In a triple‑layered setup, the **leaflets of the second‑generation transcatheter valve** (Evolut) become the culprits. Lacerating them before deploying the third valve can create a gap for blood (and catheters) to pass.  

**Practical tip:**  
- Use a 0.014" guidewire looped around the target leaflet, electrify with a RF generator, and cut the leaflet in a controlled fashion.  
- Confirm correct orientation on CT to avoid lacerating the wrong scallop.  

### 5. Valve Fracture  

While usually reserved for surgical bioprostheses (e.g., valve‑in‑valve with a small surgical prosthetic), **bioprosthetic valve fracture (BVF)** with high‑pressure non‑compliant balloons can also expand the metallic frame of a self‑expanding valve if it’s made of a fracture‑prone alloy (e.g., some early‑generation Medtronic devices).  

In the presented case, the team didn’t fracture the Evolut frame, but a high‑pressure post‑dilation likely *expanded* the stent enough to improve the effective orifice area without causing structural failure.

### 6. Dedicated Coronary‑Friendly Devices  

Manufacturers are responding to the “coronary access problem” with new designs:

- **Lotus Edge** (discontinued) and the newer **JenaValve** feature open cells at the sinuses to facilitate coronary cannulation.  
- **Myval** and **Viva** have lower frame heights and larger cell sizes.  

If you anticipate the need for multiple re‑interventions, **choose a valve platform that leaves the sinuses as open as possible** from the start.

## Future Options When Access Becomes Impossible  

Even with the best planning, the cumulative mesh can eventually block coronary catheters. Here’s what the horizon holds:

| Technique | When to Consider | What You Need |
|-----------|------------------|---------------|
| **Retrograde Coronary Access via the Aortic Root** | Extremely narrowed sinus, no antegrade route | Specialized catheters (e.g., Turnpike, Ikari) and 3‑D roadmap |
| **Trans‑septal “Coronary Bypass” (Trans‑caval)** | No percutaneous route | Surgical hybrid deployment of a graft from the femoral vein to the coronary ostium (experimental) |
| **Coronary Stent “Scaffold” Pre‑Implant** | Planned TAVR‑in‑TAVR in tight sinuses | Implant a coronary stent across the ostium into the aortic root before valve deployment to keep a channel open |
| **Laser‑Assisted Frame Modification** | Resistant frames causing obstruction | High‑energy laser catheters to cut frame struts (still experimental) |
| **Surgical Redo with Root Replacement** | All percutaneous options exhausted | Conventional surgery, often requiring a full root/ascending aorta replacement|

Most of these remain in the exploratory phase, but they underline that **coronary access is a lifetime issue**, not just a peri‑procedural concern.

## Take‑Home Points  

- **Pre‑procedure CT is your roadmap.** Measure coronary height, sinus width, and valve‑to‑coronary distance for *each* existing frame; model the planned valve’s impact before you step into the cath lab.  
- **Commissural alignment isn’t optional**—it’s a cornerstone of preserving future coronary access, especially in self‑expanding devices.  
- **High implantation and careful device selection** (supra‑annular leaflets, low frame height, large cells) can give you the extra millimetres needed to keep the coronaries patent.  
- **Have a protection plan** (wire/balloon, BASILICA, BVF) ready for any VTC < 4 mm scenario.  
- **Think ahead**: if the patient has significant coronary disease, plan the initial TAVR with a “future‑access‑friendly” valve to avoid being trapped in a mesh cage later.  

The Chazov case proves that a third TAVR is technically achievable, but it also serves as a reminder that every additional layer makes coronary re‑access exponentially harder. By integrating detailed imaging, intentional commissural alignment, and a versatile toolbox of adjunctive techniques, we can turn a potential catastrophic obstruction into a routine, survivable procedure.  

**Bottom line:** When you stack TAVR valves, *stack* your planning—especially the coronary planning. 🚀



# Patient‑Prosthesis Mismatch in Redo TAVR: Hemodynamics, Device Choice, and Post‑Dilatation Tactics  

*Why a third transcatheter valve can still leave you with a “high‑gradient” valve – and how to avoid it.*

---

## Why this case grabbed my attention  

When you read about a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV, you expect a technical stunt that looks impressive on the cath‐lab screen, but you also expect the hemodynamics to be terrible – three metal cages crammed into a 13 mm lumen sounds like a recipe for a mean gradient > 60 mm Hg and severe patient‑prosthesis mismatch (PPM).  

The case from Merkulov et al. flips the script: after a third TAVR the patient’s gradient **spiked to 67 mm Hg** right after implantation, but with a focused strategy (high implantation, commissural alignment, aggressive post‑dilatation) it fell to **27 mm Hg** within a month and stayed there at one‑year follow‑up (mean gradient ≈ 26 mm Hg, AVA ≈ 1.1 cm²).  

That dramatic early drop – and the fact that they achieved a durable result in a 70‑something with prior prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) – is a perfect learning laboratory for anyone who is going to face redo TAVR in an aging, high‑risk cohort.

---

## The hemodynamic puzzle of redo TAVR  

1. **Effective orifice area (EOA) is a moving target.**  
   - Each additional frame adds radial stiffness, reducing the final EOA. In the reported patient the “minimum lumen diameter” of the Evolut frame was only 13.7 mm, predicting a very small residual EOA.  
   - The **indexed AVA** after the third valve was 0.4 cm²/m² – classic severe PPM.

2. **Gradient is not a static number.**  
   - Immediately after deployment the mean gradient was 67 mm Hg, but this reflected incomplete expansion of the newly placed Acurate neo2 and residual constraint from the inner Evolut frame.  
   - Rapid ventricular pacing (RVP) post‑dilatation, high‑pressure balloon inflations, and “frame‑relaxation” over the first weeks can all lower the gradient dramatically, as seen by the 27 mm Hg drop at 1 month.

3. **Leaflet motion matters.**  
   - The third valve was deliberately positioned *high* (aligned with the Evolut leaflets). This forced the inner leaflets to stay open during systole, limiting the “neo‑skirt” that would otherwise subtract from the effective flow area.

---

## Device selection: no “one‑size‑fits‑all”  

| Feature | Why it mattered in this case | Practical tip |
|---|---|---|
| **Supra‑annular leaflets (Acurate neo2)** | Leaves the leaflet orifice above the stacked frames, preserving EOA in a tight annulus. | When CT shows a post‑TAV lumen < 14 mm, favor a supra‑annular valve (neo2, Evolut PRO+) over intra‑annular devices. |
| **Low frame height & commissural alignment** | Reduces the cumulative mesh burden over the coronary ostia, and makes future coronary access feasible. | Use pre‑procedural 3‑D CT modelling (or dedicated software) to plan the fluoroscopic “cusp‑overlap” view and rotate the delivery system for optimal alignment. |
| **Self‑expanding vs. balloon‑expandable** | Self‑expanding valves apply continuous outward force, which can “re‑shape” a prior frame without high‐pressure rupture. | In a triple‑stented anatomy, a self‑expanding valve (neo2, Evolut) typically offers safer incremental expansion than a balloon‑expandable valve that may need excessive pressures to fracture the inner frame. |
| **Valve size** | A “size S” neo2 (23 mm) matched the measured annular perimeter (≈ 77 mm) while still leaving room for post‑dilatation. | Always confirm that the selected valve can be over‑expanded 10–15 % without compromising frame integrity – this is the safety margin for aggressive post‑dilatation. |

---

## Post‑dilatation tactics that rescued the gradient  

1. **Pre‑dilatation to “condition” the old frame** – a 22 mm balloon under RVP was used to break the tight adherence of the Evolut mesh to the aortic wall, creating a “cushion” for the new valve.  

2. **High‑pressure post‑dilatation (20 mm × 40 mm)** – performed after the neo2 was deployed, this step did three things:  
   - Expanded the neo2 to its nominal diameter, minimizing the “pin‑ching” effect of the inner frame.  
   - Further opened the Evolut stent struts, improving leaflet excursion.  
   - Helped the neo2 skirt sit *above* the prior leaflets, preserving coronary flow.

3. **Rapid ventricular pacing during each inflation** – keeps the LV outflow tract still, reduces the risk of eccentric expansion and protects the coronary ostia.

4. **Real‑time hemodynamic monitoring** – aortic root pressure tracing on the console revealed a drop from ~ 70 mm Hg to < 30 mm Hg after each inflation; keep an eye on the pressure­gradient curve; if it plateaus, consider a second, slightly larger balloon (e.g., 22 mm) but stop before you see a “pop” in the frame (sign of fracture).  

**Bottom line:** aggressive, well‑timed post‑dilatation can rescue a seemingly hopeless gradient, but it must be guided by CT‑derived coronary clearance and a clear “stop‑point” to avoid coronary obstruction.

---

## Practical algorithm for redo TAVR with high PPM risk  

1. **Screening** – Any patient with prior TAVR (or surgical valve) presenting with mean gradient > 40 mm Hg or indexed AVA < 0.6 cm²/m² → consider redo TAVR.  

2. **Imaging work‑up** –  
   - **Multidetector CT**: measure annular perimeter, minimum lumen diameter of existing frame, coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, valve‑to‑coronary distance.  
   - **3‑D reconstruction** to simulate valve‑in‑valve (ViV) geometry, predict neo‑skirt height.  

3. **Risk stratification** –  
   - **Coronary obstruction risk**: If valve‑to‑coronary distance < 4 mm after simulated ViV, plan for coronary protection (wire + balloon) or BASILICA.  
   - **PPM risk**: Calculate projected indexed AVA for each candidate valve (use valve‐specific sizing tables).  

4. **Device selection** – Prioritize supra‑annular, low‑frame, commissure‑aligned valves. If the annulus is < 14 mm, avoid intra‑annular balloons unless you plan a deliberate valve fracture.  

5. **Procedural strategy** –  
   - **Cusp‑overlap view** for high implantation.  
   - **Pre‑dilatation** of the old frame (size 1–2 mm below the intended valve).  
   - **Deployment** with targeted depth (aligned with leaflets of previous valve).  
   - **Post‑dilatation**: start with 1 mm undersized balloon, then up‑size if gradient remains > 30 mm Hg.  

6. **Intra‑procedural check** – Aortography + coronary angiography after each inflation to confirm flow; use rapid pacing for each balloon run.  

7. **Post‑procedure surveillance** – Echo at 24 h, 30 days, 6 months, then yearly. Watch for late PPM evolution; if mean gradient stays > 30 mm Hg, consider balloon‑mediated valve fracture (if the inner frame is balloon‑expandable) or medical optimization.

---

## Clinical pearls & take‑home messages  

- **PPM after third‑generation TAVR is common, but not inevitable.** High‑implantation, commissural alignment, and aggressive post‑dilatation can transform a mean gradient of 67 mm Hg into a durable 26 mm Hg.  
- **CT‐guided planning is non‑negotiable.** It tells you whether the coronary ostia will survive a triple‑stent stack and how much radial reserve you have for post‑dilatation.  
- **Device choice matters more than ever.** Supra‑annular, low‑profile frames (neo2, Evolut) give you the best chance at preserving an effective orifice area in a narrowed landing zone.  
- **Don’t be afraid to “push” the balloon.** A controlled high‑pressure post‑dilatation can expand both the new valve and the old frame, improving leaflet motion without causing obstruction—provided you’ve confirmed coronary clearance.  
- **Think ahead to the next‑generation.** Every frame you add reduces future coronary access and the feasibility of subsequent ViV. Aim for optimal commissural alignment now; it may be the difference between a feasible future PCI and a dead‑end.  

---

### Bottom line  

Redo TAVR is moving from “last resort” to a realistic option for many octogenarians with structural valve deterioration, even after a prior episode of prosthetic valve endocarditis. The key to avoiding severe residual gradients—and the dreaded patient‑prosthesis mismatch—is a **CT‑first, device‑smart, high‑implantation, aggressive post‑dilatation** approach. Master these steps now, and the next triple‑stented patient you see will likely walk out of the cath lab with a functional valve and a smile.



# Vascular Complications in Serial TAVR: Lessons from a Post‑Procedural Femoral Pseudo‑aneurysm  

## Why this case grabbed my attention  

When we talk about TAVR, the focus is usually on valve sizing, coronary protection, or the dreaded patient‑prosthesis mismatch. Rarely do we pause to think about the “road” the device takes to get there. In this 70‑something gentleman the story took an unexpected turn after his **second TAVR** (a TAV‑in‑TAV with a 23 mm Evolut): a pulsatile femoral hematoma evolved into a **pseudo‑aneurysm** that required open vascular surgery.  

That one complication forced a complete rethink of his access strategy for the **third TAVR** (TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV) and underscores a principle that will become increasingly important as more patients live long enough to need multiple transcatheter interventions: **cumulative vascular trauma matters**.  

## The anatomy of the problem – what went wrong?  

- **First TAVR (2014) – 27 mm Lotus via right femoral** – uneventful.  
- **Second TAVR (May 2022) – 23 mm Evolut via left femoral** – after valve deployment the patient developed a rapidly expanding groin hematoma. Duplex confirmed a femoral pseudo‑aneurysm, ultimately managed with surgical repair.  
- **Third TAVR (June 2024) – left radial & left femoral** – the team deliberately avoided the previously compromised limb, opting for a radial approach for imaging and a contralateral (right) femoral puncture for device delivery.  

Key take‑aways from the pseudo‑aneurysm episode:  

1. **Sheath size matters** – The Evolut required a 14‑F sheath; the earlier Lotus had used a 22‑F system. Larger bore sizes increase the risk of arterial wall injury, especially in calcified, tortuous femoral arteries.  
2. **Pre‑procedural vessel assessment** – A comprehensive CT angiogram of the ilio‑femoral tree is essential, not only to choose the delivery route but also to document pre‑existing wall disease that may predispose to complications on repeat access.  
3. **Closure device selection** – In this case a Perclose ProGlide system was used. While generally safe, the combination of large sheath, heavy calcification, and cumulative punctures can exceed the device’s sealing capacity, leading to delayed bleeding and pseudo‑aneurysm formation.  

## Planning the next access – what we learned  

### 1. **Never assume the same side is “safe”**  

Even if a groin looks fine on the day of the procedure, prior punctures (or surgical repair) create scar tissue and loss of elasticity. In a patient slated for a third TAVR, the heart team should **re‑image both sides** and treat the previously used limb as a “high‑risk” access site unless a compelling reason exists to reuse it.  

### 2. **Consider alternative access early**  

- **Trans‑axillary (subclavian)** – Provides a relatively straight path for self‑expanding valves, avoids the heavily diseased femoral arteries, and can be closed percutaneously with a plug or suture device.  
- **Trans‑carotid** – Offers a short, low‑profile route, especially attractive now that low‑profile devices (e.g., Evolut Navitor, Acurate neo2) can be delivered through a 14‑F sheath.  
- **Trans‑radial for imaging (as done here)** – Using the radial artery for coronary/angiographic runs saves femoral “real estate” for the large delivery system.  

In this case the team used **left radial for angiography** and **right femoral for valve delivery**, effectively giving the injured left side a “rest period”.  

### 3. **Use the smallest possible sheath**  

Modern valves can now be implanted through 14‑F or even 12‑F systems (e.g., Lotus Edge, Navitor). For a patient with a history of femoral complications, **down‑sizing the sheath** can dramatically reduce the risk of further arterial injury.  

### 4. **Tailor closure strategy to the vessel**  

- **Pre‑closure with two Perclose devices** works well in healthy, non‑calcified femoral arteries but may fail in the presence of severe calcification or prior scar.  
- **Plug‑based closure (e.g., AngioSeal, MANTA for large‑bore)** may be preferable when the arterial wall is fragile. The MANTA device, designed for 18‑22 F access, offers a “zip‑per” like sealing that can be more reliable in heavily pre‑treated vessels.  
- **Hybrid approach** – In some cases, a surgical cut‑down with a felt‑reinforced closure stitch is the safest option, especially when the puncture site is near a bifurcation or when multiple prior closures have been placed.  

### 5. **Plan for bailout**  

Even with meticulous planning, complications can happen. A **low threshold for surgical consultation** and **immediate availability of a vascular surgeon** in the cath lab are critical. In the index case, the pseudo‑aneurysm was recognized early, but the decision to proceed to open repair meant having a ready OR team.  

## The bigger picture – cumulative vascular trauma  

As TAVR penetrates lower‑risk and younger populations, we are moving from a “single‑shot” mindset to a **lifetime management strategy**. Each access episode leaves a “footprint”:  

- **Intimal injury → neointimal hyperplasia → stenosis**  
- **Adventitial scarring → loss of compliance → higher puncture force needed**  
- **Calcific plaque destabilization → dissection or pseudo‑aneurysm**  

When you add a third or fourth procedure, those footprints can convert a “clean” vessel into a hostile corridor, limiting future options.  

**Practical steps for the heart team:**  

- **Baseline CT of the entire aorto‑iliac tree at the first TAVR** – store the data; it becomes the reference for all future re‑interventions.  
- **Map out a “vascular road map”** – mark which side was used, sheath size, closure device, and any complications.  
- **Future‑proofing** – When possible, use the side with the most favorable anatomy for the first procedure, leaving the contralateral side as a “reserve” for later.  
- **Regular vascular follow‑up** – Duplex ultrasound at 1 month and again at 6 months can detect early stenosis or pseudo‑aneurysm formation before a repeat TAVR is planned.  

## Take‑home pearls  

- **Large‑bore femoral access is the Achilles’ heel of serial TAVR**; a pseudo‑aneurysm after the second valve is a red flag that the femoral vessel may no longer be reliable.  
- **Pre‑procedural CT angiography is non‑negotiable** for every repeat TAVR; it informs sheath size, predicts coronary obstruction risk, and reveals subtle wall disease that may predispose to vascular complications.  
- **Choose the smallest feasible sheath and the most appropriate closure device**; when calcification or prior scarring is present, consider plug‑based or surgical closure rather than purely suture‑based systems.  
- **Always have a bailout plan** – a vascular surgeon on standby, a hybrid OR nearby, and a clear algorithm for converting a percutaneous approach to an open repair.  
- **Think longitudinally** – document the access site, complications, and closure method after each procedure. This “vascular ledger” guides future access decisions and can spare the patient from repeat groin catastrophes.  

In the era of “TAVR‑in‑TAVR‑in‑TAVR”, the humble femoral artery can become the limiting factor. By respecting the cumulative trauma it endures and tailoring our access strategy accordingly, we keep the pathway clear for the next life‑saving valve deployment.
