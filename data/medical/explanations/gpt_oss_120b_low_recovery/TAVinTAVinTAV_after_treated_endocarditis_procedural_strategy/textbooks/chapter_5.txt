Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

Chapter 5: ## 5. Outcomes, Follow‑up, and Lessons for Lifetime Valve Management  

The clinical course after the third transcatheter valve implantation illustrates how careful procedural planning can translate into durable haemodynamic results even when three prosthetic frames coexist within a single aortic root. The sequential echo assessments, functional recovery, and absence of recurrent infection provide an evidence base for the feasibility of TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV strategies in high‑risk patients who have survived prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE).

### Immediate post‑procedural echocardiography  

Trans‑oesophageal and transthoracic echocardiography performed on day 1 after the Acurate neo2 deployment confirmed correct leaflet motion and complete sealing of the valve orifice. No trans‑valvular or paravalvular aortic regurgitation was detectable (grade 0). However, the Doppler gradient remained high: a mean trans‑valvular gradient of 67 mm Hg and a calculated effective orifice area (EOA) of 0.9 cm² (indexed EOA 0.4 cm²/m²). The derived Doppler velocity index (DVI) of 0.24 fulfilled the haemodynamic definition of severe patient‑prosthesis mismatch (PPM). In a triple‑stented anatomy, the residual gradient largely reflects the combined geometric restriction imposed by the three overlapping frames rather than primary valve dysfunction. Recognising severe PPM early allowed the team to anticipate a potential need for aggressive balloon post‑dilatation and close clinical surveillance.

### Early (1‑month) haemodynamic improvement and left‑ventricular remodelling  

At the 1‑month follow‑up, repeat TTE demonstrated a rapid decline in the mean gradient to 27 mm Hg and a modest increase in EOA to 1.1 cm². The left ventricular (LV) end‑diastolic volume fell from 148 mL at baseline to 118 mL, indicating favourable reverse remodelling. The LV ejection fraction (EF) rose from 66 % pre‑procedure to 68 % post‑procedure, reflecting improved systolic performance after afterload reduction. These changes are clinically meaningful: the gradient reduction moved the prosthesis from a severe‑stenosis haemodynamic profile into the moderate‑stenosis range, which correlates with symptom relief and a lower risk of progressive LV dysfunction. The early improvement also suggests that high‑position deployment and post‑dilatation successfully expanded the prior Evolut frame, increasing central flow without compromising coronary perfusion.

### Mid‑term (3.5‑month) clinical status  

By 3.5 months the patient reported New York Heart Association (NYHA) class I symptoms, could walk more than 500 m without dyspnoea, and experienced no exertional chest discomfort. Objective assessment showed stable LV dimensions, no new valvular regurgitation, and unchanged laboratory markers of inflammation (CRP < 5 mg/L). Serial blood cultures remained negative, confirming eradication of the prior Enterococcus faecalis infection and indicating that the third TAVI did not re‑ignite a latent endocarditic process. The absence of fever, negative trans‑oesophageal imaging, and lack of prosthetic vegetations together fulfil the ESC criteria for cure of PVE, allowing clinicians to focus surveillance on structural valve performance rather than infection‑related complications.

### 1‑year follow‑up  

One year after the third valve implantation the echocardiographic profile was stable: mean gradient 26 mm Hg, EOA 1.1 cm², DVI 0.25, and no aortic regurgitation. LV EF had risen to 65 % with continued reduction in LV volumes, supporting sustained reverse remodelling. Clinically the patient remained asymptomatic, engaged in regular activities of daily living, and had not required rehospitalisation for heart failure. Serial imaging showed no evidence of prosthetic degeneration, leaflet calcification, or structural fracture of any of the three frames. Importantly, there were no signs of recurrent endocarditis: repeat TEE demonstrated clean prosthetic surfaces, and inflammatory markers stayed within normal limits.

### Residual patient‑prosthesis mismatch in a triple‑stented scenario  

Despite the favourable gradient at 1 year, the indexed EOA remained below the conventional threshold of 0.85 cm²/m² for severe PPM. In isolated surgical or single‑valve TAVI procedures, severe PPM is associated with reduced survival and higher rates of heart‑failure readmission. In the present case, the adverse impact appears mitigated by several factors:

1. **Preserved coronary flow** – the high implantation strategy kept the neo‑skirt well above the coronary ostia, preventing flow limitation that could exacerbate the functional consequence of a small EOA.  
2. **LV functional reserve** – the patient’s EF was already high (≥ 65 %) and LV dimensions returned toward normal, suggesting an adequate contractile reserve to compensate for the modest residual gradient.  
3. **Low‑flow state avoidance** – aggressive medical therapy (beta‑blocker titration, diuretics as needed) and the absence of significant aortic regurgitation prevented the low‑flow, low‑gradient phenotype that often unmasks the clinical relevance of PPM.

Nevertheless, residual PPM remains a concern for long‑term durability. Ongoing surveillance with yearly echocardiography is warranted, and any future rise in gradient > 20 mm Hg or symptomatic decline would prompt reconsideration of therapeutic options.

### Coronary access after three overlapping frames  

The cumulative metal burden of three stent frames markedly narrows the coronary sinuses and creates a mesh that can impede catheter navigation. In this patient, pre‑procedural CT measured a valve‑to‑coronary distance of 7 mm after the third valve implantation, a margin considered sufficient for most standard catheter shapes. Post‑procedural angiography confirmed unobstructed coronary flow and successful engagement of both the left and right coronary arteries using standard 6‑Fr catheters. Key maneuvers that facilitated future access included:

- **Commissural alignment** of the Acurate neo2 to match the Evolut leaflets, thereby aligning frame cells away from the coronary ostia.  
- **High implantation** (∼3–4 mm above the native annulus) to keep the neo‑skirt away from the sinotubular junction.  
- **Selection of a low‑profile frame** (Acurate neo2) that contributes minimal additional strut density.

For patients who will likely require percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) after a triple‑valve construct, a pre‑emptive strategy—such as temporary coronary wire protection or the use of a “leaflet‑splitting” technique (BASILICA)—might be required. In this case, the imaging‑guided planning obviated the need for protective measures.

### Role of pre‑procedural CT and commissural alignment  

Multiphase multidetector CT (MDCT) was pivotal in three respects:

1. **Anatomic feasibility** – precise measurement of the inner lumen of the Evolut frame (13.7 mm) confirmed that a 23 mm Acurate neo2 could be accommodated without excessive oversizing.  
2. **Coronary risk stratification** – 3‑D reconstruction allowed the team to calculate the virtual valve‑to‑coronary distance and to model the expected neo‑skirt after implantation, confirming that coronary obstruction was unlikely.  
3. **Commissural planning** – by overlaying the CT‑derived orientation of the Evolut leaflets on fluoroscopic landmarks, the operators ensured that the Acurate neo2 commissures would be aligned, preserving a “window” for future coronary catheterization.

These steps illustrate how modern imaging can convert a seemingly prohibitive anatomy into a manageable procedural target.

### Guideline context: TAVI after prosthetic valve endocarditis  

The 2025 ESC/EC (European Society of Cardiology) and ACC/AHA guidelines continue to recommend surgical valve replacement as the first‑line therapy for PVE when feasible. However, both documents acknowledge that when infection is cured, the patient presents prohibitive surgical risk, and the anatomy is favourable, a transcatheter approach may be considered (Class IIb, Level C). The present case meets these criteria: the infection was fully treated, the EuroSCORE II was > 27 %, and CT demonstrated a safe landing zone. Thus, the decision aligns with guideline‑endorsed individualized care.

### Future considerations: valve fracture, BASILICA, and emerging devices  

Should the triple‑stented construct deteriorate further, options become increasingly limited. Potential strategies include:

- **Bioprosthetic valve fracture (BVF)** – high‑pressure balloon inflation to fracture the rigid frame of the Evolut valve, thereby expanding the residual orifice. This technique has been described for surgical bioprostheses but remains off‑label and untested in a self‑expanding TAV, especially within a triple‑layered construct.  
- **BASILICA (Bioprosthetic or Native Aortic Scallop Intentional Laceration to Prevent Iatrogenic Coronary Obstruction)** – electrosurgical laceration of the leaflets of the prior valve to create a “split” that facilitates coronary flow and potentially enlarges the effective orifice. Application after two prior TAVIs is technically demanding and carries a risk of embolisation.  
- **Emerging low‑profile, fully‑retractable devices** – next‑generation TAVIs with narrower frames and adaptive sealing skirts may allow further redo procedures without compounding mesh density.

These adjuncts remain investigational in the triple‑valve setting; any future use should be confined to heart‑team‑based, compassionate‑use protocols with rigorous imaging verification.

### Take‑home messages for lifetime management of structural valve disease  

1. **Imaging first** – High‑resolution MDCT with 3‑D reconstruction should be performed before any repeat TAVI to evaluate landing zone dimensions, coronary clearance, and frame interaction.  
2. **Commissural alignment matters** – Aligning the new valve’s commissures with those of the previous device preserves coronary access and may reduce residual gradient.  
3. **Anticipate PPM** – In patients with multiple overlapping frames, severe PPM is almost inevitable; strive for maximal effective orifice area through high implantation and post‑dilatation, and monitor for symptoms.  
4. **Multidisciplinary decision‑making** – The heart team (interventional cardiology, cardiac surgery, imaging, infectious disease, anesthesia) must weigh surgical risk, infection status, and anatomic feasibility.  
5. **Patient‑centred risk stratification** – Incorporate frailty, comorbidities, and life expectancy into the choice of a redo TAVI versus surgical explantation.  
6. **Plan for the future** – Even at the first TAVI, discuss the potential need for future interventions and select devices and implantation depths that preserve coronary access and leave room for later valve expansions.  

By integrating meticulous CT‑guided planning, strategic valve selection, and a collaborative heart‑team approach, clinicians can achieve durable haemodynamic outcomes and maintain quality of life even after three sequential transcatheter aortic valve interventions, including in patients who have survived prosthetic valve endocarditis.
