Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Morning Report] When evaluating a patient with unexplained CK elevation and proximal weakness, what is the stepwise diagnostic work‑up—including laboratory, imaging, and biopsy—to arrive at a diagnosis of IMNM?

**Morning Report Pearls – Working up Unexplained CK Elevation + Proximal Weakness to Diagnose Immune‑Mediated Necrotising Myopathy (IMNM)**  

*Case in point*: A woman in her 60 s developed progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia and a CK that rose from ~300 U/L (post‑semaglutide admission) to > 3 900 U/L over 4 months. Extensive serology was negative, MRI showed symmetric proximal muscle edema, and a thigh biopsy disclosed necrotic fibers with sparse CD3⁺ T‑cells, abundant CD68⁺ macrophages and MAC deposition – the histologic hallmark of IMNM.  

Below is a **step‑wise diagnostic algorithm** that you can apply to any patient with a similar clinical picture. The sequence follows the principle of “least invasive first, most definitive last,” while incorporating the investigations that proved decisive in the presented case.

---

### 1. Initial Clinical Assessment  
| Step | What to do | Why it matters |
|------|------------|----------------|
| **History** | • Onset, tempo, and distribution of weakness (proximal > distal).  <br>• Associated symptoms: dysphagia, dysphonia, myalgias, rash, fever.  <br>• Medication/exposure review (statins, niacin, supplements, GLP‑1 agonists, immune‑checkpoint inhibitors).  <br>• Family history of muscular disease. | Distinguishes inflammatory myopathy from toxic, metabolic, or hereditary causes. In the case, a single semaglutide dose preceded the cascade, prompting a drug‑induced work‑up. |
| **Physical exam** | • Manual muscle testing (MRC scale) – focus on hip flexors, shoulder abductors.  <br>• Look for skin findings (heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules) → dermatomyositis.  <br>• Assess bulbar function (swallowing, voice). | Confirms a proximal pattern and helps rule out inclusion‑body myositis or other neuromuscular disorders. |

### 2. Baseline Laboratory Panel  
| Test | Interpretation in IMNM |
|------|------------------------|
| **CK (total)** | Markedly elevated (often > 5 × ULN). In our patient CK peaked at 3 992 U/L. |
| **Aldolase** | Often parallels CK; useful if hemolysis or myocardial injury confounds CK. |
| **AST/ALT** | May be modestly raised due to muscle leakage; differentiate from liver disease (check GGT, ALP). |
| **CBC, CMP, ESR/CRP** | Rule out systemic inflammation or metabolic derangements; ESR/CRP modestly ↑ (22 mm/hr, 22.8 mg/L) in the case. |
| **Renal function & urinalysis** | Exclude rhabdomyolysis‑induced AKI; the case had transient AKI with modest CK rise initially. |
| **Serum myoglobin** (optional) | Helpful if rhabdomyolysis suspected. |

**Key point:** A CK that continues to rise weeks after drug exposure, rather than falling rapidly, raises suspicion for an immune‑mediated process.

### 3. Autoantibody Serology – Myositis Panel  
Order a **comprehensive myositis antibody panel** (e.g., line‑immunoassay or multiplex bead assay) that includes:

- **Anti‑HMGCR** (strongly linked to statin‑associated IMNM)  
- **Anti‑SRP** (classic IMNM)  
- **Anti‑Mi‑2, Jo‑1, PL‑7, PL‑12, EJ, OJ, MDA‑5, TIF1‑γ, NXP‑2, cN‑1A** (other myositis specific antibodies)  
- **ANA, ENA, anti‑SSA/SSB** (to capture overlap syndromes)

*Interpretation*: A positive HMGCR or SRP essentially confirms IMNM. **Negative antibodies do not exclude IMNM**—≈ 30 % are seronegative, as seen in the case (all autoantibodies negative). Persistently negative serology should prompt early muscle imaging/biopsy.

### 4. Muscle Imaging  
**MRI of the symptomatic region** (often thighs or pelvis) performed with STIR or fat‑suppressed T2 sequences.

- **Findings suggestive of IMNM**: Diffuse, symmetric, proximal muscle edema without focal fatty infiltration; modest subcutaneous edema may be present.  
- **Utility**: Guides biopsy site (most inflamed, still viable tissue) and helps rule out structural lesions (e.g., tumors, compartment syndrome).  

In the reported patient, MRI showed symmetric edema of the vastus lateralis and quadriceps, corroborating a proximal inflammatory process.

### 5. Exclusion of Mimics  
Before committing to a biopsy, eliminate common alternatives:

| Condition | Quick “rule‑out” tests |
|-----------|------------------------|
| **Statin‑ or drug‑induced toxic myopathy** | Review medication list, see if CK normalizes after drug withdrawal (usually within 2–4 weeks). |
| **Rhabdomyolysis** | CK > 10 000 U/L, myoglobinuria, acute kidney injury – CK falls rapidly with hydration. |
| **Endocrine myopathies** (hypothyroidism, Cushing) | TSH, cortisol, ACTH. |
| **Metabolic myopathies** (glycogen storage, fatty acid oxidation) | Resting CK modest, exercise‑induced spikes; consider forearm ischemic test or genetics. |
| **Neurological disorders** (motor neuron disease, neuropathy) | EMG/NCS – shows neurogenic pattern vs. myopathic. |
| **Infectious myositis** | Viral serologies (HIV, HTLV, hepatitis), ESR/CRP markedly high, fever. |

In the case, thyroid function was normal, viral serologies were negative, and the CK trajectory did not fit a simple toxic rhabdomyolysis course.

### 6. Electromyography (EMG)  
**Optional but helpful** when diagnosis remains uncertain.

- **Myopathic pattern**: Small, short‑duration, polyphasic motor units, early recruitment.  
- **Fibrillation potentials and positive sharp waves** may be present in active necrotising disease.  

A myopathic EMG supports an inflammatory myopathy and can help exclude neurogenic disorders.

### 7. Muscle Biopsy – The Definitive Test  
**Indications**: Persistent CK elevation > 5 × ULN, proximal weakness, negative serology, or need to differentiate IMNM from other inflammatory myopathies.

**Technical pearls**:

1. **Site selection** – Guided by MRI (most edematous yet non‑fat‑replaced muscle).  
2. **Specimen handling** – Obtain both frozen sections (for enzyme studies) and formalin‑fixed paraffin‑embedded tissue (for immunohistochemistry).  
3. **Core findings in IMNM**:  
   - **Scattered necrotic and regenerating fibers** with abundant macrophages (CD68⁺).  
   - **Sparse CD3⁺ T‑cells**, often perivascular.  
   - **Membrane attack complex (C5b‑9) deposition** on sarcolemma of necrotic and non‑necrotic fibers.  
   - **Minimal endomysial inflammatory infiltrate** (distinguishes from polymyositis).  

The patient's thigh biopsy showed exactly those features—necrosis with macrophage‑dominant infiltrate, MAC deposition, and only rare T‑cells—establishing the diagnosis of IMNM.

### 8. Ancillary Cardiac Evaluation (if indicated)  
Because IMNM can be associated with myocardial inflammation (as in the case’s mild cardiac MRI changes), a **baseline ECG, troponin, and echocardiogram** are reasonable. Persistent cardiac involvement may dictate additional immunosuppression.

---

## Putting It All Together – Practical Algorithm

1. **Proximal weakness + CK↑** → Basic labs + medication review.  
2. **CK > 5 × ULN, progressive, no rapid fall after drug stop** → Order comprehensive myositis antibody panel.  
3. **If antibodies positive** → Diagnose specific IMNM (HMGCR or SRP) → start immunosuppression.  
4. **If antibodies negative** → Obtain MRI of affected muscle → target biopsy.  
5. **Biopsy confirms necrosis with macrophage predominance & MAC** → Diagnosis of seronegative IMNM.  
6. **Initiate therapy** (high‑dose steroids ± IVIG; consider steroid‑sparing agents if refractory).  
7. **Follow CK, strength, and repeat imaging/clinical assessment** to gauge response.

**Take‑home message for the team:**  
- **Never rely on CK alone**; a rising CK over weeks despite drug withdrawal is a red flag for autoimmune necrotising myopathy.  
- **Autoantibody panels are essential but not definitive**; up to one‑third of IMNM patients are seronegative, as illustrated.  
- **MRI guides the biopsy**
