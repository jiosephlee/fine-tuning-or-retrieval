Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Clinical Pearls] How does the WHO‑Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) causality assessment classify the relationship between semaglutide exposure and the development of IMNM, and what criteria support a ‘probable’ categorization?

**Answer – WHO‑Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) causality assessment in this case**

The WHO‑UMC system classifies drug‑event relationships into *certain, probable/likely, possible, unlikely, conditional/unclassified* and *unassess­able*.  For a case to be judged **“probable/likely”** the following three core criteria must be satisfied:

| WHO‑UMC criterion | What it requires | How the semaglutide‑IMNM case meets it |
|-------------------|------------------|----------------------------------------|
| **Temporal relationship** | Onset of the adverse event must follow a plausible latency after drug exposure and be consistent with the known pharmacology. | The patient received a single 0.25 mg sub‑cutaneous dose of semaglutide and within hours developed nausea/vomiting and modest CK elevation (337 U/L). Over the next ≈ 4 months she manifested a progressive, proximal‑dominant weakness with a **12‑fold rise in CK (peak ≈ 4 000 U/L)** – a latency compatible with an immune‑mediated process triggered by the drug. |
| **Alternative explanations** | The event should not be better explained by concurrent disease, other drugs, or a spontaneous occurrence. | The patient denied statin or other myotoxic drug use, had no excessive exercise, no alcohol/illicit‑drug exposure, and extensive autoimmune serology was negative (including anti‑SRP, anti‑HMGCR, Jo‑1, etc.). Liver disease was chronic and did not account for the CK surge; cardiac work‑up was non‑specific. Thus, other known precipitants of IMNM were effectively excluded. |
| **De‑challenge (withdrawal) response** | Improvement after stopping the suspect drug (or after treatment that includes drug withdrawal) supports causality; re‑challenge is not required for “probable”. | Semaglutide was never repeated. After the acute illness the patient was treated with steroids + IVIG and, crucially, **remained off semaglutide**. CK normalized and the patient returned to baseline function within months, a pattern that is atypical for idiopathic IMNM (which usually needs prolonged immunosuppression) but mirrors drug‑induced IMNM that resolves after drug cessation. |
| **Re‑challenge (optional)** | A positive re‑challenge would shift the rating to “certain”, but a negative or absent re‑challenge does not preclude “probable”. | Re‑exposure to semaglutide would be unethical given the severity of the reaction; therefore the criterion is simply not met, which is permissible for a “probable” rating. |
| **Consistency with published data** (supportive, not mandatory) | Similar reports or known pharmacologic mechanisms lend plausibility. | Although this is the first published semaglutide‑associated IMNM, prior reports of GLP‑1‑RA‑related rhabdomyolysis and statin‑triggered HMGCR‑positive IMNM provide biologic plausibility that a GLP‑1 agonist can act as an immune trigger. |

**Putting it together:**  
- **Time‑link** (dose → symptoms → delayed autoimmune myopathy) ✔  
- **Exclusion of other causes** (no statins, negative myositis panel, no other systemic disease) ✔  
- **Improvement after drug withdrawal (plus short immunosuppression)** ✔  
- **Re‑challenge not performed (ethical impossibility)** ✔ (acceptable for “probable”)  

Because all three indispensable WHO‑UMC criteria for *probable/likely* are fulfilled, the authors appropriately label the relationship between semaglutide and the patient’s immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy as **probable causality**.  This classification acknowledges a strong, though not definitive, link, and it signals clinicians to report similar events for further pharmacovigilance.
