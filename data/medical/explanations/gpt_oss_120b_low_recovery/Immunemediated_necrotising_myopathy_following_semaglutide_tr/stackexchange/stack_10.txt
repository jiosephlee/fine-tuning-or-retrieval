Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Management] What are the considerations for tapering and discontinuing corticosteroids in drug‑induced IMNM, and why was rapid weaning possible in this case?

**Answer – Tapering and Discontinuing Corticosteroids in Drug‑Induced IMNM**

In immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) the default strategy is prolonged high‑dose glucocorticoid therapy (often 1 mg/kg prednisone) followed by slow tapering over many months, because the underlying autoimmune process tends to persist. However, when the necrotising myopathy is *drug‑induced*—as in the semaglutide case we just discussed—several practical considerations allow a much more rapid withdrawal:

| Consideration | Why it matters in drug‑induced IMNM | How it was applied in the case |
|---------------|--------------------------------------|--------------------------------|
| **Clear temporal relationship and drug withdrawal** | Removal of the inciting antigen eliminates the immune stimulus that perpetuates muscle injury. | The patient stopped semaglutide after the single dose; no further exposure occurred. |
| **Limited auto‑antibody repertoire** | Seronegative disease (negative HMGCR, SRP, Jo‑1, etc.) argues against an entrenched, auto‑antibody‑driven process that would relapse on taper. | Comprehensive myositis panel was negative; only a low‑titer SS‑B was present, which is not pathogenic in IMNM. |
| **Robust early response to immunomodulation** | A dramatic fall in CK and rapid clinical recovery suggest that the inflammatory cascade is still reversible and not self‑sustaining. | CK dropped from >3 900 U/L to normal within 2 weeks after IV methylprednisolone + IVIG; strength returned to baseline. |
| **Short duration of immunosuppression needed** | In drug‑induced cases, once the trigger is gone, the immune system often “resets” after a brief course of steroids/IVIG, obviating the need for maintenance therapy. | Only one pulse steroid course (5 days) followed by oral prednisone 1 mg/kg; IVIG was given once (2 g/kg). |
| **Absence of ongoing organ involvement** | Persistent cardiac, pulmonary or cutaneous disease would mandate slower taper; here those systems normalized (echo, cardiac MRI, CT). | Cardiac MRI abnormalities resolved; no interstitial lung disease; no rash or synovitis. |
| **Risk‑benefit balance** | Prolonged high‑dose steroids carry considerable adverse effects (infection, hyperglycaemia, bone loss). When the disease appears self‑limited, the harms of a long taper outweigh the benefits. | The patient experienced a severe septic shock unrelated to steroids after discharge; limiting steroid exposure was prudent. |
| **Monitoring of CK and functional status** | CK is a reliable surrogate for disease activity in IMNM; stable, normal CK permits a faster taper. | Serial CK measurements remained normal after the initial decline, guiding the 2‑month taper. |

**Why rapid weaning was possible in this patient**

1. **Drug‑related trigger removed:** Semaglutide was given only once and then discontinued. Without ongoing antigenic stimulation, the immune response could wane quickly.
2. **Seronegative, non‑persistent autoimmunity:** The lack of HMGCR or SRP antibodies (and the absence of other myositis‐specific antibodies) suggested a transient, drug‑mediated process rather than chronic idiopathic IMNM, which typically requires months of immunosuppression.
3. **Excellent early biochemical and clinical response:** CK fell from nearly 4 000 U/L to the normal range within 2 weeks; the patient regained the ability to stand, sit and swallow, indicating that muscle necrosis had been halted.
4. **No evidence of residual systemic inflammation:** Inflammatory markers (CRP, ESR) normalized, cardiac imaging cleared, and there was no interstitial lung disease—features that usually mandate a slower taper.
5. **Safety concerns:** The patient subsequently suffered a severe pseudomonal urinary‑tract infection; minimizing steroid exposure reduced the risk of further infections.

**Practical take‑home points for clinicians**

- **Confirm drug causality** (temporal association, exclusion of other triggers, improvement on withdrawal) before planning a short taper.
- **Obtain a baseline and serial CK**; a sustained normalization is the most objective gauge that the disease is quiescent.
- **Use a brief pulse of IV methylprednisolone** (e.g., 1 g/day × 3–5 days) followed by a moderate oral dose (≈1 mg/kg) for 2–4 weeks, then taper **by 10–20 % per week** if CK remains normal and strength is stable.
- **Add a single course of IVIG** when weakness is severe, as it can accelerate recovery and may reduce the required steroid dose.
- **Monitor for relapse** during and after taper (CK, strength, dysphagia); any rise should prompt a slower taper or restart of immunotherapy.
- **Educate the patient** to avoid the offending agent (all GLP‑1 receptor agonists in this case) and to report any recurrent muscle symptoms promptly.

In summary, drug‑induced IMNM behaves like a “self‑limited” autoimmune process once the offending medication is removed. The combination of seronegativity, a brisk CK response, and lack of ongoing systemic disease permits a rapid corticosteroid taper—often within 2 months—as demonstrated in this semaglutide‑associated case.
