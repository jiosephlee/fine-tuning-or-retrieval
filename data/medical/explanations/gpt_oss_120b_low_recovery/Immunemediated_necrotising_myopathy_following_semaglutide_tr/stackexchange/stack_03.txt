Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Management] Outline the recommended initial immunosuppressive regimen for severe IMNM and explain why intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was added in this patient’s management.

**Initial immunosuppressive regimen for severe immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM)**  

1. **High‑dose glucocorticoids** – First‑line therapy is pulse intravenous methyl‑prednisolone (≈1 g daily) for 3–5 days, followed by oral prednisone 1 mg/kg/day (or equivalent) with a rapid taper once clinical response and creatine‑kinase (CK) trends are favorable.  
2. **Early steroid‑sparing agents** – In practice, a second‑line agent (methotrexate, azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil, or tacrolimus) is added within the first few weeks to permit earlier taper of steroids and to reduce long‑term glucocorticoid toxicity. The choice is guided by comorbidities, patient age, and tolerability.  

These steps constitute the “standard” initial regimen for severe IMNM, which is characterised by rapid CK rise, profound proximal weakness, and, often, bulbar involvement (dysphagia/dysphonia). Early aggressive immunosuppression is essential because untreated disease can lead to irreversible muscle fiber loss and persistent disability.

**Why intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was added in this patient**  

- **Persistent, severe weakness despite steroids:** After 5 days of 1 g/day methyl‑prednisolone the patient still had marked proximal weakness and bulbar dysfunction. In IMNM, steroid monotherapy often yields a delayed kinetic; persistent functional deficit after the pulse phase is an indication for adjunct therapy.  
- **High CK burden and rapid rise:** CK peaked at ≈4 000 U/L, reflecting active necrosis. IVIG has demonstrated rapid CK reduction in antibody‑mediated necrotising myopathies, likely via Fc‑receptor blockade, complement inhibition, and neutralisation of pathogenic autoantibodies (even when seronegative, as in this case).  
- **Seronegative disease with a potential drug‑trigger:** The patient was negative for the classic IMNM antibodies (anti‑HMGCR, anti‑SRP). In drug‑associated or seronegative IMNM, clinical experience suggests a higher response rate to IVIG than to steroids alone, probably because the pathogenic immune effector mechanism is more humoral than cellular.  
- **Safety and rapid onset:** IVIG can be administered over 4 days (2 g/kg total) and often produces clinical improvement within 1–2 weeks, as was observed (CK fell markedly, dysphagia improved). This was especially valuable while the patient was recovering from a recent septic shock and needed a temporising measure that did not further immunosuppress her.  

**Take‑away for trainees:**  
- Start severe IMNM with high‑dose intravenous glucocorticoids, then transition to oral prednisone and add a steroid‑sparing agent early.  
- If weakness remains pronounced after the initial steroid pulse, or if CK is markedly elevated, add IVIG (2 g/kg divided over 4–5 days).  
- In seronegative or drug‑triggered IMNM, IVIG can be a decisive adjunct that shortens the course of high‑dose steroids and facilitates early taper, as illustrated in the semaglutide‑associated case where a single course of steroids + IVIG led to full functional recovery and allowed cessation of immunosuppression within months.
