Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Differential Diagnosis] Given the patient’s prior history of NAFLD and elevated transaminases, how can clinicians differentiate hepatic versus muscular sources of AST/ALT abnormalities?

**Answer – How to Tell Hepatic from Muscular AST/ALT Elevation in a Patient With NAFLD Who Later Develops Myopathy**

When a patient with known non‑alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) presents with a rise in aminotransferases, the first instinct is to attribute the abnormality to the liver. However, AST and ALT are not liver‑specific; skeletal muscle injury can raise both enzymes, and in the setting of an immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) they may be misleading. The case of the 60‑year‑old woman who developed IMNM after a single dose of semaglutide illustrates the systematic approach we use to separate hepatic from muscular sources.

---

### 1.  Look at the Pattern of Enzyme Elevation

| Feature | Suggests Liver | Suggests Muscle |
|---------|----------------|-----------------|
| **AST : ALT ratio** | >1 (AST predominates) | Often ≤1 (ALT may be equal or higher) |
| **Magnitude** | Mild‑moderate (≤5 × ULN) in NAFLD; >5 × ULN raises concern for acute injury | CK‑driven rises can push AST/ALT into the hundreds‑to‑thousands, but usually proportional to CK |
| **Concurrent enzymes** | ↑Alkaline phosphatase, GGT, bilirubin in cholestasis; synthetic dysfunction (↓albumin, ↑PT/INR) in severe hepatitis | Normal bilirubin, ALP, GGT; isolated transaminase rise |

In the case, the patient’s chronic NAFLD had produced a **steady, mild 2‑fold elevation of ALT > AST** with normal bilirubin, ALP and synthetic function—classic for steatohepatitis. When she later presented with IMNM, her AST (191 U/L) and ALT (165 U/L) were **only modestly above baseline** despite a CK of 3 992 U/L, suggesting that the bulk of the transaminase rise remained hepatic.

---

### 2.  Correlate With a Muscle‑Specific Marker – Creatine Kinase (CK)

CK is the most sensitive laboratory test for skeletal‑muscle injury.  

* **CK > 5 × ULN** in the presence of any transaminase rise strongly points to a muscular component.  
* **Trend comparison:** In pure liver disease CK stays normal; in myopathy CK mirrors the clinical course, falling rapidly with treatment or muscle recovery.

In the reported patient, CK rose from a low‑normal 337 U/L during the initial semaglutide reaction to **3992 U/L** (≈20 × ULN) four months later, temporally linked to the progression of weakness. The parallel drop of CK to normal after IVIG and steroids confirmed that the muscle injury, not the liver, was driving the enzyme abnormalities at that stage.

---

### 3.  Use the AST/ALT : CK Ratio (the “AST/CK index”)

A practical bedside heuristic is the **AST/CK ratio**:

\[
\text{AST/CK index} = \frac{\text{AST (U/L)}}{\text{CK (U/L)}}
\]

* **< 0.01** → Strongly suggests muscle origin (AST is tiny relative to a huge CK).  
* **≥ 0.03** → More compatible with hepatic injury.

In our patient during the IMNM flare:

\[
\frac{191\;U/L}{3992\;U/L} \approx 0.048
\]

This value is **borderline** but, together with the massive CK elevation and clinical myopathy, supports a **muscle contribution** to the transaminase rise. Conversely, three years earlier, CK was unremarkable and the ratio would have been > 0.1, consistent with liver‑derived AST/ALT.

---

### 4.  Assess Clinical Context and Ancillary Testing

| Clinical clue | Hepatic | Muscular |
|---------------|----------|----------|
| **Symptoms** | Right‑upper‑quadrant pain, jaundice, pruritus | Proximal weakness, myalgias, dysphagia, dark urine |
| **Physical exam** | Hepatomegaly, stigmata of chronic liver disease | Muscle tenderness, atrophy, decreased strength |
| **Imaging** | Ultrasound/CT showing steatosis, fibrosis | MRI showing muscle edema (as in the thigh MRIs) |
| **Liver biopsy** | Steatosis, ballooning, lobular inflammation | Normal hepatic architecture; muscle biopsy shows necrosis (as obtained) |

The case report demonstrated **macrovesicular steatosis on liver biopsy** (unchanged from prior) and **muscle biopsy with necrotising fibers and CD68+ macrophages**, confirming two distinct disease processes.

---

### 5.  Practical Algorithm for the Bedside

1. **Identify the presence of muscle symptoms** (weakness, dysphagia, myalgias).  
2. **Order CK** (and possibly aldolase). If CK is normal → hepatic source is most likely.  
3. **Calculate AST/CK index**; a low index points to muscle.  
4. **Review other liver tests** (bilirubin, ALP, GGT, INR). Isolated AST/ALT rise with normal others leans muscle.  
5. **Repeat labs after addressing the obvious cause** (e.g., stop offending drug, hydrate). A rapid fall in CK with persistent ALT suggests residual liver disease; parallel fall in both points to a single source.  
6. **Consider imaging/biopsy** if the diagnosis remains ambiguous.

---

### Bottom Line for This Patient

- **Baseline elevation** of ALT > AST (≈2 × ULN) with normal CK and liver‑specific abnormalities → **NAFLD‑related hepatic injury**.  
- **New, marked CK rise** to > 20 × ULN, concurrent proximal weakness, and MRI/biopsy evidence of necrotising myopathy → **muscle‑derived contribution** to AST/ALT.  
- **Persistent mild ALT/AST after CK normalises** reflects the underlying steatohepatitis, not ongoing muscle damage.

By systematically correlating enzyme patterns, CK, clinical features, and imaging/biopsy data, clinicians can confidently distinguish hepatic from muscular transaminase elevations, even in complex cases where both processes coexist. This approach prevents misattribution of a flare to the liver, prompts timely recognition of life‑threatening myopathies, and guides appropriate treatment—exactly what was required in the semaglutide‑associated IMNM case.
