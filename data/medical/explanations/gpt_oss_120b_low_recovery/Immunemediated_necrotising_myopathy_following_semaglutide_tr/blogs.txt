# When a Diabetes Drug Turns Autoimmune: GLP‑1 Agonists as Triggers of Immune‑Mediated Necrotising Myopathy  

*By [Your Name], MD – FOAM‑style commentary*  

---

## Why This Case Stands Out  

A 60‑year‑old woman with obesity and non‑alcoholic fatty liver disease received a **single 0.25 mg dose of semaglutide** (the GLP‑1 receptor agonist that’s taken by millions worldwide). Within hours she vomited, became dehydrated, and was admitted with acute renal failure. Four months later she developed **progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, dysphonia, and a CK that climbed to ~4 000 U/L**. Muscle biopsy showed necrotising fibres with a modest inflammatory infiltrate—classic for **immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM)**.  

What makes it striking is that:  

1. **Only one dose** of semaglutide preceded a **delayed, autoimmune‑type** myopathy rather than an acute toxic rhabdomyolysis.  
2. The patient was seronegative for the usual IMNM autoantibodies (anti‑HMGCR, anti‑SRP) and still responded to a short course of steroids + IVIG.  
3. After stopping the drug and a brief immunosuppressive regimen, she recovered completely and remains off steroids 7 months later.  

In the era of exploding GLP‑1 use for diabetes and obesity, this is the **first report of semaglutide‑associated IMNM**, hinting that the drug class may act as an environmental trigger for an otherwise rare autoimmune muscle disease.

---

## Quick Refresher: Drug‑Induced Autoimmune Myopathies  

| Feature | Statin‑associated HMGCR myopathy | Other drug‑induced IMNM (e.g., zidovudine, checkpoint inhibitors) | GLP‑1‑agonist‑associated IMNM (proposed) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Latency** | Weeks–months after exposure; may persist after drug stop | Variable; often weeks–months | Possible delayed onset (months) after a single dose |
| **CK pattern** | Very high (often >10 000 U/L) | Moderate–high | Moderate rise (2000‑4000 U/L) |
| **Autoantibody** | Anti‑HMGCR (often positive) | May be anti‑SRP, anti‑Mi‑2, or seronegative | Frequently seronegative (as in case) |
| **Biopsy** | Necrotic fibres, scant lymphocytes, MAC deposition | Similar necrosis with inflammatory infiltrate | Necrosis + macrophage infiltrates, rare CD3⁺ T cells |
| **Response to withdrawal** | Partial; many need immunosuppression | Variable; often requires therapy | Good response after drug cessation + short steroids/IVIG |

The common thread is that **drug exposure initiates an immune response against muscle antigens**, leading to a necrotising picture that does not resolve simply by stopping the culprit. Immunosuppression is often required, but the duration may be shorter than in truly idiopathic IMNM.

---

## Pathophysiologic Plausibility  

- **GLP‑1 receptors are expressed on skeletal muscle** and modulate protein synthesis pathways. Chronic activation can shift the balance toward **muscle protein catabolism**, which may expose cryptic antigens.
- **Immune modulation**: GLP‑1 agonists have anti‑inflammatory effects in the vasculature, but paradoxically can **alter T‑cell subsets** and **macrophage activation**. In a susceptible host, this could tip toward autoimmunity.
- **Molecular mimicry**: The peptide sequence of the GLP‑1 analogue might share homology with muscle‑specific proteins, priming an adaptive response.
- **Genetic predisposition**: The patient’s **low‑titer SS‑B positivity** hints at an underlying subclinical autoimmunity that could be unmasked by drug exposure.

While speculative, these mechanisms echo what we’ve seen with **statin‑induced HMGCR myopathy**—the drug modifies the target (HMGCR) and the immune system then attacks the altered self.

---

## Pharmacovigilance Signals  

- **FAERS (FDA Adverse Event Reporting System)** shows a modest number of “myopathy” and “rhabdomyolysis” reports linked to semaglutide and other GLP‑1 agonists, but **none yet flagged as IMNM**.  
- **EU pharmacovigilance** has a recent safety communication about **muscle mass loss** with GLP‑1 therapy, but not necrotising myopathy.  
- The **tirzepatide rhabdomyolysis case** (CK > 57 000 U/L) underscores that the class can cause **direct toxic myopathy**, making clinicians more alert to any CK rise in patients on these agents.

Given the **exponential rise in usage**, even rare events will appear more frequently in absolute numbers. A systematic review of post‑marketing databases may uncover a pattern that is currently buried in noise.

---

## Practical Framework: When a GLP‑1 Agonist Patient Presents with CK Elevation & Weakness  

1. **History & Timing**  
   - Ask specifically about **initiation date, dose, and any dose changes** of GLP‑1 agents.  
   - Note any **acute GI reaction** (nausea/vomiting) that could cause hypovolemia and a confounding CK rise.  

2. **Initial Labs**  
   - CK, AST/ALT (to differentiate hepatic vs muscular transaminases), electrolytes, renal function.  
   - **Autoantibody panel**: anti‑HMGCR, anti‑SRP, a broader myositis panel, ANA, ENA.  

3. **Imaging & EMG**  
   - MRI of proximal muscles for edema.  
   - EMG can help differentiate inflammatory vs neurogenic processes.  

4. **Biopsy (if CK > 1000 U/L or progressive weakness)**  
   - Look for necrosis, macrophage infiltration, MAC (C5b‑9) deposition, and scant lymphocytes—hallmarks of IMNM.  

5. **Causality Assessment** (adapted from WHO‑UMC criteria)  
   - **Temporal relationship**: onset within weeks‑months of drug exposure.  
   - **De‑challenge**: improvement after drug discontinuation ± immunosuppression.  
   - **Alternative explanations**: rule out statins, supplements, strenuous exercise, infections.  
   - **Re‑challenge**: generally unethical in severe myopathy, but note if incidental re‑exposure occurs.  

6. **Management Decision Tree**  

   - **If CK < 1000 U/L, mild symptoms** → observe, hydrate, stop GLP‑1 agent, repeat CK in 1‑2 weeks.  
   - **CK > 1000 U/L, progressive weakness** → start **high‑dose steroids** (e.g., IV methylpred 1 g × 3‑5 days) and consider **IVIG** if severe or refractory.  
   - **Seropositive** (HMGCR/SRP) → treat as **drug‑associated IMNM** (shorter immunosuppression may suffice).  
   - **Seronegative** → watch closely; many respond to a **finite course** of steroids + IVIG (as in the case).  

7. **Follow‑up**  
   - Serial CK every 2‑4 weeks until normal.  
   - Functional assessment (MRC grading, timed‑up‑and‑go).  
   - Cardiac evaluation if troponin or MRI suggests myocarditis.  

---

## Clinical Pearls  

- **A single dose of semaglutide can be the spark** for a delayed autoimmune myopathy—don’t be reassured by a lack of chronic exposure.  
- **CK trends matter**: a modest rise that climbs over months is more suggestive of IMNM than acute drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis.  
- **Muscle biopsy remains the gold standard** when serologies are negative and the clinical picture is atypical.  
- **IVIG can dramatically accelerate recovery** in severe GLP‑1‑associated IMNM, often allowing early steroid taper.  
- **Cardiac involvement is possible** (myocardial edema on MRI) and should be screened for, especially if troponin is modestly elevated.  

---

## Take‑Home Messages  

- **GLP‑1 receptor agonists, while generally safe, can act as environmental triggers for immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy**, even after a single exposure.  
- **Maintain a low threshold for CK testing** in patients on GLP‑1 agents who develop unexplained weakness, dysphagia, or dysphonia.  
- **Apply a systematic causality framework** (temporal relationship, de‑challenge, exclusion of other causes) to decide whether the drug is implicated.  
- **Early muscle biopsy and immunosuppression (steroids ± IVIG) can lead to full recovery** and may allow a brief treatment course, unlike idiopathic IMNM which often needs long‑term therapy.  
- **Pharmacovigilance matters**: report any myopathy cases to FDA/EMA databases to help define the true incidence and risk factors.  

---

### Bottom Line  

As GLP‑1 agonists become the cornerstone of modern obesity and diabetes care, clinicians must be vigilant for **unusual muscle presentations**. Recognizing the pattern of drug‑associated IMNM can spare patients from prolonged disability and guide safe prescribing—ensuring that the “sweet” benefits of semaglutide don’t come with an unwanted **autoimmune bite**.



# Beyond Rhabdomyysis: Distinguishing Toxic Myopathy from Immune‑Mediated Necrotising Myopathy  

## Why this case grabbed my attention  

The surge of GLP‑1 receptor agonists (semaglutide, tirzepatide, etc.) has transformed diabetes and obesity management, but it has also introduced new, uncharted adverse‑effect territory.  A 60‑year‑old woman who received a **single 0.25 mg dose of semaglutide** developed acute GI distress, renal failure, and a modest CK rise (≈ 300 U/L).  Two months later she was struggling to rise from a chair, swallowing liquids, and her CK had exploded to almost 4 000 U/L.  A muscle biopsy confirmed **immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM)**—a diagnosis that would have been missed if the team had assumed a classic rhabdomyolysis picture.  

The contrast between a toxic, self‑limited rhabdomyolysis and a true autoimmune myopathy is subtle but critical.  Mislabeling an IMNM as “just” drug‑induced muscle injury delays steroids/IVIG and may lead to permanent disability.  Let’s walk through the pearls that separate these entities, using the semaglutide case as a concrete example.

## Toxic (classic) rhabdomyolysis – the textbook pattern  

| Feature | Typical Presentation |
|---|---|
| **Trigger** | Direct myocyte injury (statins, illicit drugs, crush injury, severe electrolyte shifts). |
| **CK kinetics** | Sky‑high CK (often > 10 000 U/L) that peaks within 24–72 h and falls rapidly once the offending agent is stopped and aggressive hydration is instituted. |
| **Symptoms** | Severe muscle pain/pressure, dark urine, possible compartment syndrome. Systemic signs stem from volume depletion or electrolyte derangements. |
| **Renal impact** | Acute tubular necrosis from myoglobin → oliguria, rising creatinine; often improves with fluids. |
| **Biopsy** | Diffuse myofibre necrosis with little or no inflammatory infiltrate; macrophages present for clean‑up but no organized immune response. |
| **Treatment** | Prompt volume resuscitation, urine alkalinisation (if indicated), avoidance of the inciting drug. No immunosuppression required. |
| **Outcome** | CK normalises within days‑weeks; muscle strength returns assuming no secondary complications. |

Key takeaway: **CK is massive and falls quickly** once you address the toxin and volume status.

## Immune‑Mediated Necrotising Myopathy (IMNM) – the “autoimmune” mimicker  

| Feature | Typical Presentation |
|---|---|
| **Trigger** | Often idiopathic; can be statin‑associated (HMGCR antibodies) or, as emerging reports suggest, linked to newer agents (GLP‑1 agonists, immune checkpoint inhibitors). |
| **CK kinetics** | Very high CK (often > 5 000 U/L) that **rises subacutely** over weeks‑months and **does not normalize** with hydration alone. |
| **Symptoms** | Progressive proximal weakness (shoulder/hip girdle), dysphagia, dysphonia; pain may be mild or absent; systemic features (rash, fever) often lacking. |
| **Renal impact** | Usually mild; kidney injury is more a consequence of severe CK rise rather than direct toxic effect. |
| **Biopsy** | **Necrotic and regenerating fibres** with prominent **macrophage infiltrates (CD68+),** complement deposition (MAC), and occasional scarce CD3+ T‑cells. Lymphocytic infiltrates are **paucicellular** compared with polymyositis. |
| **Serologies** | Often positive for **anti‑HMGCR** or **anti‑SRP**; ~ 30 % seronegative, requiring reliance on histology and clinical course. |
| **Treatment** | High‑dose steroids ± IVIG; early immunosuppression dramatically shortens disease course. |
| **Outcome** | Responds to steroids/IVIG; if untreated, progresses to severe disability. Relapse can occur if the trigger is not removed. |

Key takeaway: **CK stays high despite fluids, and muscle weakness progresses; biopsy shows an immune‑driven necrosis pattern.**

## The semaglutide case in a nutshell  

1. **Temporal clues** – She had **one dose** of semaglutide, developed nausea/vomiting within hours, and was hospitalized for AKI. CK was modest (337 U/L) at that time—**not the massive CK surge of classic rhabdo**.  
2. **Delayed sub‑acute progression** – Four months later, CK surged to **≈ 4 000 U/L** and she manifested **proximal weakness, dysphagia, dysphonia**—classic IMNM features, not just muscle soreness.  
3. **Biopsy evidence** – Muscle tissue showed necrotic fibres with **macrophage infiltration, MAC deposition**, and only rare T‑cells → **immune‑mediated necrosis**, not pure toxic injury.  
4. **Serology** – Negative for the usual myositis antibodies (HMGCR, SRP, Jo‑1), placing her in the **seronegative IMNM** subgroup.  
5. **Response to therapy** – Aggressive IV methylprednisolone followed by oral prednisone and a **single course of IVIG** produced rapid CK drop and functional recovery, **without prolonged immunosuppression**.  
6. **Drug withdrawal** – No further exposure to semaglutide; the disease did not recur, supporting a **drug‑associated trigger**.

## Practical pearls for the front‑line clinician  

- **Don’t let CK magnitude alone dictate the diagnosis.** A modest rise early after a drug exposure can hide a later autoimmune process. Track CK trends over weeks, not just the initial value.  
- **Look for progressive proximal weakness and bulbar symptoms.** These are red flags for IMNM, even if the initial presentation seemed like a typical drug reaction.  
- **Hydration is necessary but not sufficient.** If CK fails to decline after adequate volume resuscitation, consider early rheumatology input and muscle imaging/biopsy.  
- **Order a comprehensive myositis panel** (including HMGCR, SRP, Mi‑2, MDA‑5, TIF1‑γ, NXP‑2, etc.) **even when the drug culprit is suspected**; a negative panel does not exclude IMNM.  
- **Muscle MRI can guide biopsy** – edema patterns in proximal thigh muscles are highly suggestive of active myositis and help pinpoint the best site for histology.  
- **Early immunosuppression saves function.** A short pulse of steroids plus IVIG, as in this case, can reverse the disease process quickly; delayed therapy risks irreversible muscle loss.  
- **Document drug exposure meticulously** and report to pharmacovigilance databases. Emerging agents like GLP‑1 analogues are still being mapped for rare immune adverse events.  

## How to approach a patient with suspected drug‑related myopathy  

1. **Initial assessment** – Detailed medication history (including recent start, dose, and any previous tolerability). Evaluate for classic rhabdo triggers (trauma, extreme exertion, illicit drugs).  
2. **Laboratories** – CK, renal panel, electrolytes, urinalysis (myoglobin), inflammatory markers (ESR, CRP). If CK > 5 000 U/L or CK fails to fall after 48‑72 h of hydration, move to step 3.  
3. **Imaging/biopsy** – MRI of affected muscle groups; schedule a **muscle biopsy** if CK remains elevated or weakness progresses. Request immunohistochemistry for MAC, CD68, CD3.  
4. **Serology** – Myositis antibody panel; remember seronegatives constitute ~1/3 of IMNM.  
5. **Management decision tree**  
   - **CK falling + symptoms improving →** continue hydration, monitor.  
   - **CK plateauing / rising + progressive weakness →** start high‑dose steroids (e.g., methylpred 1 g IV × 3‑5 days) and consider IVIG (2 g/kg).  
   - **Identify and discontinue offending drug** – even if the drug is not a classic myotoxin, removal may help prevent ongoing immune stimulation.  

## Take‑home messages  

- **Toxic rhabdomyolysis and IMNM can share a drug trigger but diverge dramatically in CK kinetics, symptom evolution, and pathology.**  
- **A single modest CK rise after a new medication does not rule out a later autoimmune myopathy; close follow‑up is essential.**  
- **Muscle biopsy remains the gold standard for distinguishing immune‑mediated necrosis from pure toxin‑induced injury.**  
- **Early immunosuppression (steroids ± IVIG) is the cornerstone of IMNM treatment and can lead to rapid, durable recovery if instituted promptly.**  
- **Clinicians prescribing GLP‑1 receptor agonists should be aware of this rare but serious potential adverse effect and educate patients to seek care for unexplained weakness or dysphagia.**  

In the era of ever‑expanding pharmacologic toolkits, vigilance for atypical immune adverse events is as important as mastering the classic side‑effect profiles. Recognizing the subtle cues that separate rhabdomyolysis from IMNM will spare patients from permanent disability and keep us ahead of the curve in drug safety.



# Seronegative IMNM: Diagnostic Pitfalls and the Role of Muscle Biopsy in the Era of Expanded Auto‑antibody Panels  

*By [Your Name], MD – FOAM enthusiast*  

---

## Why This Case is a Head‑turner  

When you think of immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM), the first things that pop up are the classic “anti‑HMGCR” or “anti‑SRP” antibodies. The textbook algorithm: **CK > 1 000 U/L → auto‑antibody panel → positive → diagnosis.**  

Enter a 60‑year‑old woman who, after a single dose of semaglutide, develops progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, and a CK that climbs from a modest 337 U/L to almost 4 000 U/L over four months. The auto‑antibody work‑up is a **sponge‑sized negative** – ANA, anti‑SRP, anti‑HMGCR, myositis panel, everything is negative. Yet her muscle biopsy tells a different story: necrotic fibers, macrophage infiltrates, complement (MAC) deposition, and only a few CD3⁺ T‑cells.  

In an era where panels of myositis‑specific antibodies are becoming ever more expansive, this case reminds us that **seronegativity does not equal “no disease.”** It also forces us to confront when to stop chasing labs and move on to tissue diagnosis.  

---

## The Clinical Puzzle of Seronegative IMNM  

**What makes seronegative IMNM tricky?**  

1. **Overlap with other myopathies** – Elevated CK, proximal weakness, and MRI edema can be seen in toxic myopathy, polymyositis, steroid‑induced myopathy, or even severe deconditioning.  
2. **Atypical auto‑antibody patterns** – Up to **⅓ of IMNM patients are truly seronegative**, meaning no HMGCR, SRP, or even myositis‑specific antibodies. A low‑titer SS‑B (as in our patient) is often an incidental finding and should not distract from the primary pathology.  
3. **Temporal ambiguity** – In drug‑related cases (GLP‑1‑RA, statins, etc.) the timing of symptom onset can be vague, raising the question: “Is this a direct toxic effect or an autoimmune response?”  

**Key clinical clues that should raise suspicion for IMNM despite a negative panel:**  

- **Rapidly progressive proximal weakness** with bulbar involvement (dysphagia, dysphonia).  
- **CK trajectory**: a sustained rise over weeks to months, not the abrupt peak‑and‑fall typical of pure rhabdomyolysis.  
- **MRI findings**: symmetric, proximal muscle edema with sparing of fascia and a “stippled” pattern on T2/STIR.  
- **Lack of clear alternative cause**: no recent strenuous exercise, no statin exposure, no obvious inflammatory disease.  

If you have two or more of these, it’s time to think “muscle biopsy now.”

---

## Muscle Biopsy: The Definitive Arbiter  

### What to Look For  

| Histologic Feature | Why It Matters |
|--------------------|----------------|
| **Scattered necrotic fibers with regeneration** | Hallmark of necrotising process; not seen in pure inflammatory myositis. |
| **Macrophage (CD68⁺) infiltrates** | Indicates ongoing phagocytosis of necrotic fibers; supports immune‑mediated injury. |
| **Sparse CD3⁺ T‑cells** | IMNM is characteristically *pauci‑lymphocytic* – a stark contrast to polymyositis (dense endomysial CD8⁺ infiltrate). |
| **Membrane Attack Complex (C5b‑9) staining** | Direct evidence of complement‑mediated fiber injury – a “smoking gun” for autoimmune necrosis. |
| **Fiber type grouping or atrophy?** | May suggest chronicity; not required for diagnosis but helps stage disease. |

In our patient, the biopsy showed **necrotic/regenerating fibers, CD68⁺ macrophages, and MAC deposition**, with only occasional CD3⁺ cells. This pattern is textbook IMNM and essentially clinches the diagnosis regardless of serology.

### When to Biopsy  

- **CK > 1 000 U/L** *and* **proximal weakness** that is not explained by a clear toxic exposure.  
- **Negative myositis panel** (including extended panels for HMGCR, SRP, Mi‑2, MDA‑5, etc.).  
- **MRI** shows **diffuse, symmetric muscle edema** without focal injury.  
- **Clinical course** suggests an autoimmune process (progressive, not self‑limited).  

If you’re still on the fence after these criteria, remember that **biopsy is low‑risk** (usually outpatient, local anesthesia) and can prevent months of diagnostic odyssey.

---

## Integrating Imaging, Labs, and Histology  

1. **Start with the bedside:** thorough neuromuscular exam, CK trend, and review of meds.  
2. **MRI as the gatekeeper:** A STIR‑hyperintense, symmetric pattern in proximal muscles (quadriceps, hip flexors) steers you away from focal injuries or compartment syndromes. Lack of fatty infiltration supports an acute process.  
3. **Serology comes next:** Run the broad myositis panel. If negative, still consider IMNM if the clinical picture fits.  
4. **Biopsy to seal the deal:** The histopathology will answer the “immune” vs “toxic” question definitively.  

In the semaglutide case, the **MRI showed symmetric edema**, the **auto‑antibodies were negative**, and the **biopsy confirmed IMNM** – allowing the team to start targeted immunosuppression (high‑dose steroids + IVIG) and avoid unnecessary prolonged investigations.

---

## Treatment Pearls (When Seronegative Doesn’t Mean “Untreatable”)  

- **Initial high‑dose steroids:** 1 mg/kg prednisone or IV methylprednisolone pulses (1 g/day for 3–5 days).  
- **Adjunct IVIG:** 2 g/kg divided over 4–5 days can be decisive, especially when the disease is fulminant or steroid‑refractory.  
- **Early taper** is possible in drug‑induced IMNM once CK normalises and strength improves – as shown by our patient who was off prednisone within two months.  
- **Avoid the culprit:** Discontinue GLP‑1‑RA (or any suspected drug). Re‑challenge is usually unethical.  

**Take‑home:** Seronegative IMNM often responds to a short, aggressive immunosuppressive course if caught early. Delay can lead to chronic weakness and unnecessary exposure to high‑dose steroids for months.

---

## Bottom Line – Key Take‑Home Messages  

- **Seronegative IMNM is real** and accounts for a substantial minority of necrotising myopathies.  
- **Don’t let a negative antibody panel lull you into complacency**; the clinical picture and imaging are equally, if not more, important.  
- **Muscle biopsy is the gold‑standard** when the diagnosis is in doubt – look for necrosis, macrophages, scant lymphocytes, and complement deposition.  
- **Rapid, targeted immunosuppression** (steroids ± IVIG) can restore function and allow early taper in drug‑associated cases.  
- **Pharmacovigilance matters** – as GLP‑1‑RAs become ubiquitous, clinicians must stay alert to rare but serious muscle toxicities that may masquerade as seronegative IMNM.

# Immunosuppression Strategies for Acute Immune‑Mediated Necrotising Myopathy (IMNM): Steroids, IVIG, and the Art of Short‑Course Therapy  

## Why This Case Is Worth Your Attention  

When a 60‑year‑old woman received a *single* 0.25 mg dose of semaglutide and, months later, presented with a CK nearing 4 000 U/L, profound proximal weakness, dysphagia and dysphonia, most of us would instinctively think “statin‑induced necrotising myopathy” or “severe rhabdomyolysis.” Instead, the biopsy showed classic IMNM – necrotic fibers, scant lymphocytes, macrophage infiltrates, and membrane‑attack‑complex deposition.  

What makes it a teaching moment is that **her disease resolved after a brief pulse of methylprednisolone followed by a short oral prednisone taper and a single 2 g/kg course of IVIG**. She never needed long‑term immunosuppression, and she has stayed off steroids and IVIG for > 7 months with normal CK and functional recovery.  

In an era where IMNM is often managed with aggressive, multi‑drug regimens (high‑dose steroids, steroid‑sparing agents, repeated IVIG), this case reminds us that a *targeted, time‑limited* approach can be curative – especially when the myopathy is drug‑triggered and the offending agent is withdrawn.  

## Quick Refresher: What Is IMNM?  

- **Pathology**: Necrotising myofibers with minimal lymphocytic infiltrate; macrophage‐rich “myophagocytosis” and complement deposition.  
- **Serology**: ≈ ⅔ are seronegative; the remainder often have anti‑HMGCR (statin‑related) or anti‑SRP antibodies.  
- **Clinical picture**: Rapidly progressive proximal weakness, often with dysphagia/dysphonia; CK typically 1 000–10 000 U/L.  
- **Prognosis**: Without treatment, functional recovery is poor; with immunosuppression, many achieve remission, but relapse rates are high when steroids are tapered too quickly or when the trigger persists.  

## The Core of the Treatment Algorithm  

| Step | Typical Practice | What This Case Shows |
|------|------------------|----------------------|
| **1️⃣ Initial high‑dose steroids** | IV methylprednisolone 1 g/day × 3‑5 days, then prednisone 1 mg/kg/day. | A 5‑day pulse of 1 g/day methylprednisolone was enough to blunt the inflammatory surge. |
| **2️⃣ Steroid taper** | Slow taper over ≥ 6 months, often with steroid‑sparing agents. | Rapid taper (≈ 2 months) worked because the trigger (semaglutide) was removed and there was no serologic autoantibody suggesting a relentless autoimmune drive. |
| **3️⃣ IVIG** | 2 g/kg divided over 2‑5 days, repeated every 4‑6 weeks for refractory disease. | One course (2 g/kg over 4 days) gave a decisive CK drop and functional gain; no repeat needed. |
| **4️⃣ Steroid‑sparing agents** (AZA, MMF, MTX, tacrolimus, rituximab) | Added when steroids cannot be tapered, or in seropositive disease. | Not required here; patient stayed off all additional agents. |
| **5️⃣ Monitoring** | CK, strength testing, MRI, repeat biopsy if relapse. | Serial CK and clinical exam sufficed; CK normalized early and stayed low. |

### Key Decision Points  

1. **Is the myopathy drug‑related?**  
   - Temporal relationship: onset within hours of semaglutide; CK rose progressively after drug exposure.  
   - Withdrawal: cessation of semaglutide coincided with therapeutic response.  

2. **Do you need long‑term immunosuppression?**  
   - Seronegative IMNM + clear trigger → short‑course may be adequate.  
   - Seropositive (HMGCR/SRP) or idiopathic disease → higher relapse risk → consider steroid‑sparing agents.  

3. **When to give IVIG?**  
   - Severe weakness (e.g., dysphagia, inability to sit) or CK > 5 000 U/L despite steroids.  
   - In this case, IVIG was given after steroids alone did not halt functional decline.  

4. **How fast can you taper?**  
   - Aim for a symptom‑free, CK‑normal state before rapid taper.  
   - Monitor CK weekly for the first month of taper, then bi‑weekly.  

## Practical “How‑to” for a Short‑Course Regimen  

1. **Day 0‑5:** Methylprednisolone 1 g IV daily.  
2. **Day 6‑?** Transition to oral prednisone 1 mg/kg daily.  
   - Assess CK and strength after the pulse; if CK is falling > 30 % and strength is improving, proceed.  
3. **IVIG:** 2 g/kg split over 4 days *once*, ideally within the first two weeks of steroids if weakness is profound.  
4. **Taper:**  
   - Reduce prednisone by 10 % every week until 20 mg/day, then by 5 % per week.  
   - Stop completely once < 10 mg/day and CK is stable for ≥ 2 weeks.  
5. **Follow‑up:**  
   - CK, AST/ALT, and clinical strength assessment at weeks 1, 2, 4, 8, then monthly for 6 months.  
   - If CK rises > 2× upper limit of normal or patient reports new weakness → consider repeat IVIG or add a steroid‑sparing agent.  

## When to Consider Re‑Treating with IVIG  

- CK rebounds > 2 × baseline after taper.  
- New dysphagia or respiratory muscle involvement.  
- Lack of improvement after 2 weeks of high‑dose steroids (rare).  

In drug‑induced IMNM, relapse after withdrawal of the culprit is uncommon, so repeat IVIG is often unnecessary.  

## Monitoring for Relapse  

- **CK trends** are the most sensitive early marker; a rising trajectory should prompt earlier clinic review.  
- **Functional assessment** (hand‑grip dynamometry, 6‑minute walk test) can uncover subtle weakness before CK spikes.  
- **Imaging**: MRI of proximal thigh muscles can document residual edema; useful if CK is equivocal.  

## Take‑Home Messages (Bullet‑Point Summary)  

- **Drug‑triggered IMNM can be controlled with a brief high‑dose steroid pulse plus a single IVIG course** once the offending agent is stopped.  
- **Seronegative disease and absence of persistent autoantibodies favor a short‑course strategy**; seropositive cases often need longer immunosuppression.  
- **Rapid steroid taper is safe when CK normalizes and clinical strength returns**, minimizing steroid‑related toxicity.  
- **IVIG is reserved for severe functional deficits or steroid‑refractory weakness**, and a single 2 g/kg course may be sufficient.  
- **Close CK and strength monitoring is essential** during taper; intervene early at the first sign of relapse.  
- **Maintain a high index of suspicion for newer metabolic agents (e.g., GLP‑1 agonists) as potential myopathy triggers**; reporting adverse events improves pharmacovigilance.  

---

**Bottom line:** Not every case of IMNM needs a lifelong cocktail of immunosuppressants. When a clear drug trigger is identified and removed, a focused, short‑duration regimen of pulse steroids plus one round of IVIG can restore muscle health and spare patients the long‑term complications of chronic immunosuppression. Keep your eyes on the CK, listen to the patient’s functional status, and you’ll often know when it’s safe to stop the meds. Happy treating!



# Cardiac Involvement in Immune‑Mediated Necrotising Myopathy: Myocarditis or Stress Cardiomyopathy?

## Why This Case Grabs Your Attention  

When a 60‑year‑old woman develops an immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) after a single dose of semaglutide, the story is already headline‑worthy. But what really makes this case a teaching gem is the “cardiac subplot”: a modest troponin rise, an echo with borderline right‑ventricular pressure, and a cardiac MRI that shows myocardial oedema without classic late gadolinium enhancement (LGE). Is this myocarditis riding the wave of systemic inflammation, or a stress‑induced cardiomyopathy triggered by the metabolic shock of acute illness? Understanding how to tease these entities apart is crucial because it dictates monitoring intensity, immunosuppression decisions, and long‑term follow‑up.

## Quick Recap of the Patient’s Course  

- **Baseline:** Obesity, NAFLD, hypertension, hypothyroidism; no statins or known myotoxic agents.  
- **Trigger:** First (and only) subcutaneous semaglutide dose (0.25 mg) → severe nausea/vomiting, AKI, metabolic acidosis. CK modestly ↑ (337 U/L) at that time.  
- **Four months later:** Progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia/dysphonia; CK peaks at **3992 U/L**. Muscle biopsy confirms IMNM (necrotic fibers, macrophage infiltrate, MAC deposition).  
- **Cardiac work‑up:**  
  * HS‑troponin = 62 pg/mL (upper limit 53) – a mild elevation.  
  * Echo: LVEF 65‑70 %; RV systolic pressure 46 mm Hg.  
  * Cardiac MRI: focal myocardial oedema in mid‑anteroseptal/anterior walls, no LGE → ambiguous “early myocarditis vs. stress cardiomyopathy”.  

She received high‑dose IV methylprednisolone, oral prednisone, and a 2 g/kg IVIG course. CK fell rapidly, functional status returned to baseline, and repeat cardiac imaging normalized.

## Myocarditis vs. Stress (Takotsubo‑type) Cardiomyopathy – The Differential

| Feature | Acute Immune‑Mediated Myocarditis | Stress‑Induced Cardiomyopathy (Takotsubo) |
|---------|-----------------------------------|-------------------------------------------|
| **Clinical trigger** | Autoimmune/infectious inflammation; often systemic (e.g., IMNM) | Sudden catecholamine surge from physical/psychologic stress |
| **Troponin** | Often markedly elevated (often >10× ULN) | Mild‑moderate rise (usually <5× ULN) |
| **ECG** | Diffuse ST changes, T‑wave inversion, sometimes PR depression | ST elevation in precordial leads or deep T‑wave inversions, usually transient |
| **Echocardiography** | Global or segmental LV dysfunction, sometimes pericardial effusion | Classic apical ballooning or reverse patterns; LV EF often low (30‑45 %) |
| **Cardiac MRI (CMR)** | **Oedema (T2‑weighted)** + **LGE (usually subepicardial or mid‑myocardial)** | Oedema may be present, but **LGE is absent or minimal**; wall‑motion pattern characteristic |
| **Course** | May improve with immunosuppression; risk of persistent LV dysfunction | Usually self‑limited, recovery within weeks; recurrence rare |
| **Management** | Immunosuppression (steroids, IVIG, colchicine, etc.) ± anti‑arrhythmic | Supportive care, beta‑blocker/ACE‑I as needed |

### Applying the Framework to Our Patient  

1. **Troponin** – Slightly above the ULN (62 pg/mL). Not the dramatic rise we’d expect for fulminant myocarditis.  
2. **Echocardiogram** – Preserved LVEF, modest RV pressure elevation, no obvious wall‑motion abnormality described.  
3. **CMR** – Myocardial oedema present, **no LGE** – this leans toward stress‑type injury. LGE is the hallmark of necrotic or fibrotic myocardium in true myocarditis.  
4. **Clinical context** – The patient endured a severe metabolic crisis (vomiting, AKI, ICU‑level respiratory support) that is a textbook catecholamine surge scenario.  

All signs point more toward a **stress‑induced myocardial injury** rather than classic autoimmune myocarditis. However, the overlap isn’t zero: IMNM itself is an immune process that can involve the heart, and a modest troponin rise can be the “tip of the iceberg”. The safe approach is to **treat the systemic inflammation aggressively (as was done) and monitor the heart**.

## Role of Cardiac MRI – The Gold Standard  

- **T2 mapping / STIR imaging** detects myocardial water content → sensitive for any acute injury (inflammation, edema, catecholamine‐mediated injury).  
- **Late Gadolinium Enhancement (LGE)** distinguishes irreversible injury (fibrosis/necrosis) from reversible edema.  
- In our case, the **absence of LGE** argues against permanent myocardial damage, supporting a reversible stress phenomenon.  
- Serial CMR can be valuable: a repeat scan 4‑6 weeks later can confirm resolution of edema and rule out delayed LGE that could signal evolving myocarditis.

## Practical Recommendations – Cardiac Monitoring in Acute IMNM  

1. **Baseline cardiac biomarkers** (troponin, BNP) at presentation, especially if the patient has systemic inflammation, severe electrolyte shifts, or a pro‑arrhythmic comorbidity (e.g., atrial fibrillation).  
2. **Early transthoracic echo** to assess LV function and right‑heart pressures. Even a normal EF doesn’t rule out myocarditis; look for subtle regional wall‑motion abnormalities.  
3. **Cardiac MRI** within the first 2 weeks if troponin is >2× ULN or the echo is equivocal. Prioritize T2‑weighted sequences and LGE.  
4. **Telemetry monitoring** during high‑dose steroids/IVIG phases; both the disease and its treatment can precipitate arrhythmias.  
5. **Follow‑up imaging** (echo or CMR) 4–8 weeks after discharge to document resolution, especially if the initial study showed edema.  
6. **Multidisciplinary discussion** (rheumatology, cardiology, neurology) for patients with overlapping systemic autoimmunity and cardiac involvement.

## Take‑Home Messages  

- **Cardiac involvement in IMNM is not rare**; differentiate myocarditis from stress cardiomyopathy by integrating troponin magnitude, ECG, echo, and especially CMR (presence vs. absence of LGE).  
- **In the setting of a dramatic systemic stressor** (e.g., severe vomiting, AKI, ICU admission), a modest troponin rise and CMR edema without LGE most likely represent **stress‑induced myocardial injury**.  
- **Cardiac MRI is the decisive tool**: it can confirm reversible edema and exclude irreversible injury, guiding the duration and intensity of immunosuppression.  
- **Standardized cardiac monitoring** (baseline biomarkers, early echo, CMR when indicated, and follow‑up imaging) should be part of the work‑up for any acute IMNM, particularly when the trigger is a drug with systemic effects such as GLP‑1 agonists.  

By keeping a high index of suspicion and using CMR wisely, we can avoid overtreatment of benign stress injury while still catching true myocarditis that warrants prolonged immunosuppression. As GLP‑1 receptor agonists become ubiquitous, clinicians should stay alert not only for muscle toxicity but also for the cardiac sequelae that can ride the same inflammatory wave.



# Pharmacogenomics and Risk Stratification for GLP‑1 Agonist‑Induced Myopathy  

## Why this case matters  
When semaglutide arrived on the scene it was heralded as a “miracle drug” for obesity and type‑2 diabetes. Its side‑effect profile was thought to be limited to nausea, vomiting and a modest loss of lean mass. Then a 60‑year‑old woman showed up with progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia and a CK that climbed from a modest 300 U/L to almost 4 000 U/L over four months—yet she had received **only a single dose** of semaglutide. Muscle biopsy proved immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). The patient responded to a short course of steroids and IVIG, and after avoiding GLP‑1 agonists she returned to baseline without chronic immunosuppression.

The story is striking for three reasons:  

1. **Temporal proximity** – symptoms began within hours of the first semaglutide injection.  
2. **Phenotype** – the disease course matched an autoimmune myopathy rather than a classical drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis that resolves with hydration and drug withdrawal.  
3. **Resolution with brief immunosuppression** – unlike idiopathic IMNM, which usually needs prolonged, aggressive therapy, this patient improved rapidly once the drug was stopped.

If GLP‑1 receptor agonists can trigger IMNM in a subset of patients, the next logical step is to ask: **who is at risk?** The answer likely lies in the emerging field of pharmacogenomics and metabolic phenotyping.

## What we know about drug‑induced IMNM  
* Statins are the classic example – HMGCR antibodies are present in many cases, but roughly a third of statin‑associated IMNM is seronegative.  
* The tirzepatide case series described a fulminant rhabdomyolysis with CK > 50 000 U/L that resolved in days after drug cessation, consistent with direct toxicity rather than auto‑immunity.  
* In the present semaglutide case, CK rose gradually, the biopsy showed necrosis with **macrophage‑rich infiltrates** and only sparse T‑cell involvement – the histologic hallmarks of immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy.

These observations suggest that GLP‑1 agonists may act as **immune triggers** in predisposed individuals rather than merely causing muscle breakdown by metabolic stress.

## Candidate risk factors – lessons from the case  

| Potential Factor | Rationale | How to test it |
|------------------|-----------|----------------|
| **Pre‑existing hepatic steatosis / NAFLD** | Liver disease is associated with systemic inflammation and altered cytokine profiles; could modulate immune tolerance to muscle antigens. | Cross‑sectional studies correlating baseline liver fat (MRI‑PDFF) with incidence of myopathy in GLP‑1 trials. |
| **Autoantibody landscape** | The patient was seronegative for classic myositis antibodies but had a low‑titer SS‑B. Subclinical autoimmunity may prime the muscle for an autoimmune hit. | Baseline myositis panels (including novel antigens) before initiating GLP‑1 therapy; longitudinal monitoring. |
| **Prior drug intolerance (e.g., severe GI reaction)** | The immediate nausea/vomiting suggests a heightened innate immune response to the peptide formulation; could reflect a broader hypersensitivity phenotype. | Pharmacovigilance databases: track the rate of severe GI adverse events and subsequent autoimmune sequelae. |
| **Genetic variants in immune regulation** | HLA‑DR alleles are linked to statin‑associated IMNM; similar HLA‑risk could exist for GLP‑1 agents. | Genome‑wide association studies (GWAS) on patients who develop myopathy vs matched controls on GLP‑1 agonists. |
| **Pharmacokinetic differences** | Reduced renal clearance or altered hepatic metabolism could increase systemic exposure to the peptide or its metabolites, enhancing immune presentation. | Pharmacogenomic markers of drug metabolism (e.g., CYP2C9, ABC transporters) and population PK modeling. |
| **Gut microbiome composition** | GLP‑1 analogues act on entero‑endocrine cells; microbiome‑mediated modulation of mucosal immunity could influence systemic autoimmunity. | 16S rRNA sequencing before and after initiation; look for dysbiosis patterns that predict myopathy. |

## A research agenda to turn suspicion into evidence  

1. **Create a GLP‑1 Myopathy Registry**  
   *Prospective collection* of baseline demographics, liver imaging, autoantibody panels, HLA typing, and genomic DNA from all patients starting semaglutide, liraglutide, dulaglutide, tirzepatide, etc. Follow CK, muscle strength, and adverse‑event reporting for at least 12 months.

2. **Leverage existing trial biobanks**  
   Large phase III diabetes and obesity trials already have stored plasma and DNA. Retrospective serologic testing for myositis antibodies and HLA typing could identify those who later manifested CK elevation or clinical myopathy.

3. **Pharmacogenomic GWAS**  
   Use the registry to conduct a case‑control GWAS: cases = patients with CK > 5 × ULN or biopsy‑proven IMNM; controls = drug‑exposed patients without muscle injury. Focus on immune‑related loci (HLA‑DRB1, PTPN22, CTLA4) and drug‑metabolism genes.

4. **Metabolomics & Lipidomics**  
   GLP‑1 agonists affect lipid handling. Profiling circulating metabolites before drug exposure may uncover signatures (elevated ceramides, altered acyl‑carnitines) that predict muscle susceptibility.

5. **Mechanistic studies in vitro**  
   *Human myotubes* cultured with patient‑derived serum before and after GLP‑1 exposure to see if complement deposition or MAC formation occurs. Co‑culture with autologous immune cells could reveal pathogenic pathways.

6. **Risk‑prediction algorithm**  
   Combine significant genetic, serologic, and metabolic markers into a scoring system. Validate prospectively—patients above a certain threshold could be steered toward non‑GLP‑1 weight‑loss strategies or monitored with scheduled CK checks.

## Practical take‑home points for the bedside clinician  

- **Maintain a low threshold for CK testing** in patients who develop new GI intolerance to GLP‑1 agonists, especially if they have baseline hepatic steatosis or a history of autoimmune disease.  
- **Think beyond rhabdomyolysis**: a slowly rising CK, proximal weakness, dysphagia, or dysphonia after GLP‑1 exposure should prompt neurology referral and possible muscle biopsy.  
- **If IMNM is suspected, stop the GLP‑1 agent immediately** and consider early immunosuppression (high‑dose steroids ± IVIG). In reported cases, brief therapy can suffice once the trigger is removed.  
- **Document the adverse event thoroughly** (including drug lot numbers, timing, and any prior intolerances) to enrich pharmacovigilance databases that may later inform risk models.  
- **Discuss alternative therapies** with patients who have risk factors (e.g., severe NAFLD, positive myositis antibodies, known HLA‑risk alleles) and consider agents without GLP‑1 activity.

## Looking ahead  
As GLP‑1 receptor agonists become first‑line for obesity and diabetes, the field must anticipate rare but serious immune complications. By integrating pharmacogenomics, autoantibody profiling, and metabolic phenotyping into both research and routine practice, we can move from reactive case reports to proactive risk stratification—ensuring that the “miracle drugs” remain safe for the millions who will benefit from them.
