Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# Cardiac Involvement in Immune‑Mediated Necrotising Myopathy: Myocarditis or Stress Cardiomyopathy?

## Why This Case Grabs Your Attention  

When a 60‑year‑old woman develops an immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) after a single dose of semaglutide, the story is already headline‑worthy. But what really makes this case a teaching gem is the “cardiac subplot”: a modest troponin rise, an echo with borderline right‑ventricular pressure, and a cardiac MRI that shows myocardial oedema without classic late gadolinium enhancement (LGE). Is this myocarditis riding the wave of systemic inflammation, or a stress‑induced cardiomyopathy triggered by the metabolic shock of acute illness? Understanding how to tease these entities apart is crucial because it dictates monitoring intensity, immunosuppression decisions, and long‑term follow‑up.

## Quick Recap of the Patient’s Course  

- **Baseline:** Obesity, NAFLD, hypertension, hypothyroidism; no statins or known myotoxic agents.  
- **Trigger:** First (and only) subcutaneous semaglutide dose (0.25 mg) → severe nausea/vomiting, AKI, metabolic acidosis. CK modestly ↑ (337 U/L) at that time.  
- **Four months later:** Progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia/dysphonia; CK peaks at **3992 U/L**. Muscle biopsy confirms IMNM (necrotic fibers, macrophage infiltrate, MAC deposition).  
- **Cardiac work‑up:**  
  * HS‑troponin = 62 pg/mL (upper limit 53) – a mild elevation.  
  * Echo: LVEF 65‑70 %; RV systolic pressure 46 mm Hg.  
  * Cardiac MRI: focal myocardial oedema in mid‑anteroseptal/anterior walls, no LGE → ambiguous “early myocarditis vs. stress cardiomyopathy”.  

She received high‑dose IV methylprednisolone, oral prednisone, and a 2 g/kg IVIG course. CK fell rapidly, functional status returned to baseline, and repeat cardiac imaging normalized.

## Myocarditis vs. Stress (Takotsubo‑type) Cardiomyopathy – The Differential

| Feature | Acute Immune‑Mediated Myocarditis | Stress‑Induced Cardiomyopathy (Takotsubo) |
|---------|-----------------------------------|-------------------------------------------|
| **Clinical trigger** | Autoimmune/infectious inflammation; often systemic (e.g., IMNM) | Sudden catecholamine surge from physical/psychologic stress |
| **Troponin** | Often markedly elevated (often >10× ULN) | Mild‑moderate rise (usually <5× ULN) |
| **ECG** | Diffuse ST changes, T‑wave inversion, sometimes PR depression | ST elevation in precordial leads or deep T‑wave inversions, usually transient |
| **Echocardiography** | Global or segmental LV dysfunction, sometimes pericardial effusion | Classic apical ballooning or reverse patterns; LV EF often low (30‑45 %) |
| **Cardiac MRI (CMR)** | **Oedema (T2‑weighted)** + **LGE (usually subepicardial or mid‑myocardial)** | Oedema may be present, but **LGE is absent or minimal**; wall‑motion pattern characteristic |
| **Course** | May improve with immunosuppression; risk of persistent LV dysfunction | Usually self‑limited, recovery within weeks; recurrence rare |
| **Management** | Immunosuppression (steroids, IVIG, colchicine, etc.) ± anti‑arrhythmic | Supportive care, beta‑blocker/ACE‑I as needed |

### Applying the Framework to Our Patient  

1. **Troponin** – Slightly above the ULN (62 pg/mL). Not the dramatic rise we’d expect for fulminant myocarditis.  
2. **Echocardiogram** – Preserved LVEF, modest RV pressure elevation, no obvious wall‑motion abnormality described.  
3. **CMR** – Myocardial oedema present, **no LGE** – this leans toward stress‑type injury. LGE is the hallmark of necrotic or fibrotic myocardium in true myocarditis.  
4. **Clinical context** – The patient endured a severe metabolic crisis (vomiting, AKI, ICU‑level respiratory support) that is a textbook catecholamine surge scenario.  

All signs point more toward a **stress‑induced myocardial injury** rather than classic autoimmune myocarditis. However, the overlap isn’t zero: IMNM itself is an immune process that can involve the heart, and a modest troponin rise can be the “tip of the iceberg”. The safe approach is to **treat the systemic inflammation aggressively (as was done) and monitor the heart**.

## Role of Cardiac MRI – The Gold Standard  

- **T2 mapping / STIR imaging** detects myocardial water content → sensitive for any acute injury (inflammation, edema, catecholamine‐mediated injury).  
- **Late Gadolinium Enhancement (LGE)** distinguishes irreversible injury (fibrosis/necrosis) from reversible edema.  
- In our case, the **absence of LGE** argues against permanent myocardial damage, supporting a reversible stress phenomenon.  
- Serial CMR can be valuable: a repeat scan 4‑6 weeks later can confirm resolution of edema and rule out delayed LGE that could signal evolving myocarditis.

## Practical Recommendations – Cardiac Monitoring in Acute IMNM  

1. **Baseline cardiac biomarkers** (troponin, BNP) at presentation, especially if the patient has systemic inflammation, severe electrolyte shifts, or a pro‑arrhythmic comorbidity (e.g., atrial fibrillation).  
2. **Early transthoracic echo** to assess LV function and right‑heart pressures. Even a normal EF doesn’t rule out myocarditis; look for subtle regional wall‑motion abnormalities.  
3. **Cardiac MRI** within the first 2 weeks if troponin is >2× ULN or the echo is equivocal. Prioritize T2‑weighted sequences and LGE.  
4. **Telemetry monitoring** during high‑dose steroids/IVIG phases; both the disease and its treatment can precipitate arrhythmias.  
5. **Follow‑up imaging** (echo or CMR) 4–8 weeks after discharge to document resolution, especially if the initial study showed edema.  
6. **Multidisciplinary discussion** (rheumatology, cardiology, neurology) for patients with overlapping systemic autoimmunity and cardiac involvement.

## Take‑Home Messages  

- **Cardiac involvement in IMNM is not rare**; differentiate myocarditis from stress cardiomyopathy by integrating troponin magnitude, ECG, echo, and especially CMR (presence vs. absence of LGE).  
- **In the setting of a dramatic systemic stressor** (e.g., severe vomiting, AKI, ICU admission), a modest troponin rise and CMR edema without LGE most likely represent **stress‑induced myocardial injury**.  
- **Cardiac MRI is the decisive tool**: it can confirm reversible edema and exclude irreversible injury, guiding the duration and intensity of immunosuppression.  
- **Standardized cardiac monitoring** (baseline biomarkers, early echo, CMR when indicated, and follow‑up imaging) should be part of the work‑up for any acute IMNM, particularly when the trigger is a drug with systemic effects such as GLP‑1 agonists.  

By keeping a high index of suspicion and using CMR wisely, we can avoid overtreatment of benign stress injury while still catching true myocarditis that warrants prolonged immunosuppression. As GLP‑1 receptor agonists become ubiquitous, clinicians should stay alert not only for muscle toxicity but also for the cardiac sequelae that can ride the same inflammatory wave.
