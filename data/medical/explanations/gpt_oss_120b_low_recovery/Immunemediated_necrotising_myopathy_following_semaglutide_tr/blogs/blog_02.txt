Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# Beyond Rhabdomyysis: Distinguishing Toxic Myopathy from Immune‑Mediated Necrotising Myopathy  

## Why this case grabbed my attention  

The surge of GLP‑1 receptor agonists (semaglutide, tirzepatide, etc.) has transformed diabetes and obesity management, but it has also introduced new, uncharted adverse‑effect territory.  A 60‑year‑old woman who received a **single 0.25 mg dose of semaglutide** developed acute GI distress, renal failure, and a modest CK rise (≈ 300 U/L).  Two months later she was struggling to rise from a chair, swallowing liquids, and her CK had exploded to almost 4 000 U/L.  A muscle biopsy confirmed **immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM)**—a diagnosis that would have been missed if the team had assumed a classic rhabdomyolysis picture.  

The contrast between a toxic, self‑limited rhabdomyolysis and a true autoimmune myopathy is subtle but critical.  Mislabeling an IMNM as “just” drug‑induced muscle injury delays steroids/IVIG and may lead to permanent disability.  Let’s walk through the pearls that separate these entities, using the semaglutide case as a concrete example.

## Toxic (classic) rhabdomyolysis – the textbook pattern  

| Feature | Typical Presentation |
|---|---|
| **Trigger** | Direct myocyte injury (statins, illicit drugs, crush injury, severe electrolyte shifts). |
| **CK kinetics** | Sky‑high CK (often > 10 000 U/L) that peaks within 24–72 h and falls rapidly once the offending agent is stopped and aggressive hydration is instituted. |
| **Symptoms** | Severe muscle pain/pressure, dark urine, possible compartment syndrome. Systemic signs stem from volume depletion or electrolyte derangements. |
| **Renal impact** | Acute tubular necrosis from myoglobin → oliguria, rising creatinine; often improves with fluids. |
| **Biopsy** | Diffuse myofibre necrosis with little or no inflammatory infiltrate; macrophages present for clean‑up but no organized immune response. |
| **Treatment** | Prompt volume resuscitation, urine alkalinisation (if indicated), avoidance of the inciting drug. No immunosuppression required. |
| **Outcome** | CK normalises within days‑weeks; muscle strength returns assuming no secondary complications. |

Key takeaway: **CK is massive and falls quickly** once you address the toxin and volume status.

## Immune‑Mediated Necrotising Myopathy (IMNM) – the “autoimmune” mimicker  

| Feature | Typical Presentation |
|---|---|
| **Trigger** | Often idiopathic; can be statin‑associated (HMGCR antibodies) or, as emerging reports suggest, linked to newer agents (GLP‑1 agonists, immune checkpoint inhibitors). |
| **CK kinetics** | Very high CK (often > 5 000 U/L) that **rises subacutely** over weeks‑months and **does not normalize** with hydration alone. |
| **Symptoms** | Progressive proximal weakness (shoulder/hip girdle), dysphagia, dysphonia; pain may be mild or absent; systemic features (rash, fever) often lacking. |
| **Renal impact** | Usually mild; kidney injury is more a consequence of severe CK rise rather than direct toxic effect. |
| **Biopsy** | **Necrotic and regenerating fibres** with prominent **macrophage infiltrates (CD68+),** complement deposition (MAC), and occasional scarce CD3+ T‑cells. Lymphocytic infiltrates are **paucicellular** compared with polymyositis. |
| **Serologies** | Often positive for **anti‑HMGCR** or **anti‑SRP**; ~ 30 % seronegative, requiring reliance on histology and clinical course. |
| **Treatment** | High‑dose steroids ± IVIG; early immunosuppression dramatically shortens disease course. |
| **Outcome** | Responds to steroids/IVIG; if untreated, progresses to severe disability. Relapse can occur if the trigger is not removed. |

Key takeaway: **CK stays high despite fluids, and muscle weakness progresses; biopsy shows an immune‑driven necrosis pattern.**

## The semaglutide case in a nutshell  

1. **Temporal clues** – She had **one dose** of semaglutide, developed nausea/vomiting within hours, and was hospitalized for AKI. CK was modest (337 U/L) at that time—**not the massive CK surge of classic rhabdo**.  
2. **Delayed sub‑acute progression** – Four months later, CK surged to **≈ 4 000 U/L** and she manifested **proximal weakness, dysphagia, dysphonia**—classic IMNM features, not just muscle soreness.  
3. **Biopsy evidence** – Muscle tissue showed necrotic fibres with **macrophage infiltration, MAC deposition**, and only rare T‑cells → **immune‑mediated necrosis**, not pure toxic injury.  
4. **Serology** – Negative for the usual myositis antibodies (HMGCR, SRP, Jo‑1), placing her in the **seronegative IMNM** subgroup.  
5. **Response to therapy** – Aggressive IV methylprednisolone followed by oral prednisone and a **single course of IVIG** produced rapid CK drop and functional recovery, **without prolonged immunosuppression**.  
6. **Drug withdrawal** – No further exposure to semaglutide; the disease did not recur, supporting a **drug‑associated trigger**.

## Practical pearls for the front‑line clinician  

- **Don’t let CK magnitude alone dictate the diagnosis.** A modest rise early after a drug exposure can hide a later autoimmune process. Track CK trends over weeks, not just the initial value.  
- **Look for progressive proximal weakness and bulbar symptoms.** These are red flags for IMNM, even if the initial presentation seemed like a typical drug reaction.  
- **Hydration is necessary but not sufficient.** If CK fails to decline after adequate volume resuscitation, consider early rheumatology input and muscle imaging/biopsy.  
- **Order a comprehensive myositis panel** (including HMGCR, SRP, Mi‑2, MDA‑5, TIF1‑γ, NXP‑2, etc.) **even when the drug culprit is suspected**; a negative panel does not exclude IMNM.  
- **Muscle MRI can guide biopsy** – edema patterns in proximal thigh muscles are highly suggestive of active myositis and help pinpoint the best site for histology.  
- **Early immunosuppression saves function.** A short pulse of steroids plus IVIG, as in this case, can reverse the disease process quickly; delayed therapy risks irreversible muscle loss.  
- **Document drug exposure meticulously** and report to pharmacovigilance databases. Emerging agents like GLP‑1 analogues are still being mapped for rare immune adverse events.  

## How to approach a patient with suspected drug‑related myopathy  

1. **Initial assessment** – Detailed medication history (including recent start, dose, and any previous tolerability). Evaluate for classic rhabdo triggers (trauma, extreme exertion, illicit drugs).  
2. **Laboratories** – CK, renal panel, electrolytes, urinalysis (myoglobin), inflammatory markers (ESR, CRP). If CK > 5 000 U/L or CK fails to fall after 48‑72 h of hydration, move to step 3.  
3. **Imaging/biopsy** – MRI of affected muscle groups; schedule a **muscle biopsy** if CK remains elevated or weakness progresses. Request immunohistochemistry for MAC, CD68, CD3.  
4. **Serology** – Myositis antibody panel; remember seronegatives constitute ~1/3 of IMNM.  
5. **Management decision tree**  
   - **CK falling + symptoms improving →** continue hydration, monitor.  
   - **CK plateauing / rising + progressive weakness →** start high‑dose steroids (e.g., methylpred 1 g IV × 3‑5 days) and consider IVIG (2 g/kg).  
   - **Identify and discontinue offending drug** – even if the drug is not a classic myotoxin, removal may help prevent ongoing immune stimulation.  

## Take‑home messages  

- **Toxic rhabdomyolysis and IMNM can share a drug trigger but diverge dramatically in CK kinetics, symptom evolution, and pathology.**  
- **A single modest CK rise after a new medication does not rule out a later autoimmune myopathy; close follow‑up is essential.**  
- **Muscle biopsy remains the gold standard for distinguishing immune‑mediated necrosis from pure toxin‑induced injury.**  
- **Early immunosuppression (steroids ± IVIG) is the cornerstone of IMNM treatment and can lead to rapid, durable recovery if instituted promptly.**  
- **Clinicians prescribing GLP‑1 receptor agonists should be aware of this rare but serious potential adverse effect and educate patients to seek care for unexplained weakness or dysphagia.**  

In the era of ever‑expanding pharmacologic toolkits, vigilance for atypical immune adverse events is as important as mastering the classic side‑effect profiles. Recognizing the subtle cues that separate rhabdomyolysis from IMNM will spare patients from permanent disability and keep us ahead of the curve in drug safety.
