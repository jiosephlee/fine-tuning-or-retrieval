Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# Immunosuppression Strategies for Acute Immune‑Mediated Necrotising Myopathy (IMNM): Steroids, IVIG, and the Art of Short‑Course Therapy  

## Why This Case Is Worth Your Attention  

When a 60‑year‑old woman received a *single* 0.25 mg dose of semaglutide and, months later, presented with a CK nearing 4 000 U/L, profound proximal weakness, dysphagia and dysphonia, most of us would instinctively think “statin‑induced necrotising myopathy” or “severe rhabdomyolysis.” Instead, the biopsy showed classic IMNM – necrotic fibers, scant lymphocytes, macrophage infiltrates, and membrane‑attack‑complex deposition.  

What makes it a teaching moment is that **her disease resolved after a brief pulse of methylprednisolone followed by a short oral prednisone taper and a single 2 g/kg course of IVIG**. She never needed long‑term immunosuppression, and she has stayed off steroids and IVIG for > 7 months with normal CK and functional recovery.  

In an era where IMNM is often managed with aggressive, multi‑drug regimens (high‑dose steroids, steroid‑sparing agents, repeated IVIG), this case reminds us that a *targeted, time‑limited* approach can be curative – especially when the myopathy is drug‑triggered and the offending agent is withdrawn.  

## Quick Refresher: What Is IMNM?  

- **Pathology**: Necrotising myofibers with minimal lymphocytic infiltrate; macrophage‐rich “myophagocytosis” and complement deposition.  
- **Serology**: ≈ ⅔ are seronegative; the remainder often have anti‑HMGCR (statin‑related) or anti‑SRP antibodies.  
- **Clinical picture**: Rapidly progressive proximal weakness, often with dysphagia/dysphonia; CK typically 1 000–10 000 U/L.  
- **Prognosis**: Without treatment, functional recovery is poor; with immunosuppression, many achieve remission, but relapse rates are high when steroids are tapered too quickly or when the trigger persists.  

## The Core of the Treatment Algorithm  

| Step | Typical Practice | What This Case Shows |
|------|------------------|----------------------|
| **1️⃣ Initial high‑dose steroids** | IV methylprednisolone 1 g/day × 3‑5 days, then prednisone 1 mg/kg/day. | A 5‑day pulse of 1 g/day methylprednisolone was enough to blunt the inflammatory surge. |
| **2️⃣ Steroid taper** | Slow taper over ≥ 6 months, often with steroid‑sparing agents. | Rapid taper (≈ 2 months) worked because the trigger (semaglutide) was removed and there was no serologic autoantibody suggesting a relentless autoimmune drive. |
| **3️⃣ IVIG** | 2 g/kg divided over 2‑5 days, repeated every 4‑6 weeks for refractory disease. | One course (2 g/kg over 4 days) gave a decisive CK drop and functional gain; no repeat needed. |
| **4️⃣ Steroid‑sparing agents** (AZA, MMF, MTX, tacrolimus, rituximab) | Added when steroids cannot be tapered, or in seropositive disease. | Not required here; patient stayed off all additional agents. |
| **5️⃣ Monitoring** | CK, strength testing, MRI, repeat biopsy if relapse. | Serial CK and clinical exam sufficed; CK normalized early and stayed low. |

### Key Decision Points  

1. **Is the myopathy drug‑related?**  
   - Temporal relationship: onset within hours of semaglutide; CK rose progressively after drug exposure.  
   - Withdrawal: cessation of semaglutide coincided with therapeutic response.  

2. **Do you need long‑term immunosuppression?**  
   - Seronegative IMNM + clear trigger → short‑course may be adequate.  
   - Seropositive (HMGCR/SRP) or idiopathic disease → higher relapse risk → consider steroid‑sparing agents.  

3. **When to give IVIG?**  
   - Severe weakness (e.g., dysphagia, inability to sit) or CK > 5 000 U/L despite steroids.  
   - In this case, IVIG was given after steroids alone did not halt functional decline.  

4. **How fast can you taper?**  
   - Aim for a symptom‑free, CK‑normal state before rapid taper.  
   - Monitor CK weekly for the first month of taper, then bi‑weekly.  

## Practical “How‑to” for a Short‑Course Regimen  

1. **Day 0‑5:** Methylprednisolone 1 g IV daily.  
2. **Day 6‑?** Transition to oral prednisone 1 mg/kg daily.  
   - Assess CK and strength after the pulse; if CK is falling > 30 % and strength is improving, proceed.  
3. **IVIG:** 2 g/kg split over 4 days *once*, ideally within the first two weeks of steroids if weakness is profound.  
4. **Taper:**  
   - Reduce prednisone by 10 % every week until 20 mg/day, then by 5 % per week.  
   - Stop completely once < 10 mg/day and CK is stable for ≥ 2 weeks.  
5. **Follow‑up:**  
   - CK, AST/ALT, and clinical strength assessment at weeks 1, 2, 4, 8, then monthly for 6 months.  
   - If CK rises > 2× upper limit of normal or patient reports new weakness → consider repeat IVIG or add a steroid‑sparing agent.  

## When to Consider Re‑Treating with IVIG  

- CK rebounds > 2 × baseline after taper.  
- New dysphagia or respiratory muscle involvement.  
- Lack of improvement after 2 weeks of high‑dose steroids (rare).  

In drug‑induced IMNM, relapse after withdrawal of the culprit is uncommon, so repeat IVIG is often unnecessary.  

## Monitoring for Relapse  

- **CK trends** are the most sensitive early marker; a rising trajectory should prompt earlier clinic review.  
- **Functional assessment** (hand‑grip dynamometry, 6‑minute walk test) can uncover subtle weakness before CK spikes.  
- **Imaging**: MRI of proximal thigh muscles can document residual edema; useful if CK is equivocal.  

## Take‑Home Messages (Bullet‑Point Summary)  

- **Drug‑triggered IMNM can be controlled with a brief high‑dose steroid pulse plus a single IVIG course** once the offending agent is stopped.  
- **Seronegative disease and absence of persistent autoantibodies favor a short‑course strategy**; seropositive cases often need longer immunosuppression.  
- **Rapid steroid taper is safe when CK normalizes and clinical strength returns**, minimizing steroid‑related toxicity.  
- **IVIG is reserved for severe functional deficits or steroid‑refractory weakness**, and a single 2 g/kg course may be sufficient.  
- **Close CK and strength monitoring is essential** during taper; intervene early at the first sign of relapse.  
- **Maintain a high index of suspicion for newer metabolic agents (e.g., GLP‑1 agonists) as potential myopathy triggers**; reporting adverse events improves pharmacovigilance.  

---

**Bottom line:** Not every case of IMNM needs a lifelong cocktail of immunosuppressants. When a clear drug trigger is identified and removed, a focused, short‑duration regimen of pulse steroids plus one round of IVIG can restore muscle health and spare patients the long‑term complications of chronic immunosuppression. Keep your eyes on the CK, listen to the patient’s functional status, and you’ll often know when it’s safe to stop the meds. Happy treating!
