Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

## 1. Clinical Presentation and Initial Evaluation

A man in his 60s presented to the emergency department with a **3‑day history of fever (38.1 °C), relentless headache, generalized malaise, loss of appetite, persistent hiccups, and arthralgia of the shoulders and ankles**. He also noted several oral aphthae, which are characteristic red‑raised lesions that tend to resolve within a week but often herald systemic disease. In the ensuing days, a painful scrotal ulcer appeared, confirming genital mucocutaneous involvement. These acute mucocutaneous findings, combined with systemic inflammatory symptoms, raised the differential for a multisystem inflammatory disorder such as Behçet’s disease (BD).

### Vital Signs and Physical Examination

On presentation the patient was alert but fatigued. Blood pressure (135/73 mm Hg) and oxygen saturation (96 % on room air) were within normal limits. **Heart rate was tachycardic at 119 beats per minute**—a common response to fever and possible volume shifts. Respiratory rate (19 breaths per minute) and temperature (38.1 °C) reflected an active febrile state. Cardiopulmonary and abdominal examinations were unremarkable. Manual muscle testing revealed no focal weakness, and there was no peripheral edema, suggesting **euvolemia**. The physical exam noted **oral aphthous ulcers** and **folliculitis‑like rashes** on the face and trunk, consistent with the **symmetrical, low‑grade erythematous lesions** typical of BD.

### Core Laboratory Findings

A routine laboratory panel immediately yielded several striking abnormalities:

| Test | Result | Normal range | Interpretation |
|------|--------|--------------|----------------|
| Serum sodium | 124 mmol/L | 135‑145 | **Hyponatraemia** |
| Serum osmolality | 267 mOsm/kg | >275‑295 | **Low osmolality** |
| Urine osmolality | 900 mOsm/kg | >300 | **Concentrated urine** |
| Urine sodium | 88 mmol/L | >20 | **Inappropriately high sodium excretion** |
| Serum ADH | 22.4 pg/mL | 0‑5 | **Elevated ADH** |
| CRP | 180 mg/L | <3 | **Marked inflammation** |
| Creatine kinase | 1349 U/L | <200 | **Rhabdomyolysis** |
| AST | 77 U/L | <40 | **Mild transaminitis** |
| ALT | 64 U/L | <40 | **Mild transaminitis** |
| Blood glucose | 14.2 mmol/L | 3.9‑7.8 | **Hyper‑glycaemia** |
| HbA1c | 8.2 % | <5.7 | **Poor glycaemic control** |

The hyponatraemia was **hypo‑osmolar** with impaired water excretion (elevated urine osmolality and sodium), and ADH was markedly elevated. These findings satisfy the traditional diagnostic criteria for **syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH)**, an endocrine manifestation often triggered by central nervous system (CNS) inflammation, malignancy, or certain drugs.

### Early Imaging and Its Findings

A **non‑contrast CT scan of the brain** was performed to exclude acute intracranial pathology – such as hemorrhage, mass effect, or skull fractures – that might explain the headache. The CT was normal. Similarly, a **CT scan of the chest and abdomen** was obtained to look for occult infections, lymphadenopathy, or neoplasms. No abnormal findings were noted on any of these studies. The absence of focal lesions or masses on imaging narrows the differential to primarily infectious or inflammatory processes rather than a space‑occupying lesion.

### Empiric Antimicrobial Coverage

Given the combination of fever, headache, and hyponatraemia, **bacterial meningitis** and **viral encephalitis** were high on the list of life‑threatening potential etiologies. Early empirical therapy was started with **cefepime**, a broad‑spectrum β‑lactam antibiotic effective against gram‑negative and gram‑positive organisms, including Pseudomonas and Enterobacterales, which can cause community‑acquired meningitis. Cefepime was chosen because it covers many of the common community‑acquired pathogens and provides adequate central nervous system penetration. Meanwhile, empirical **acyclovir** was added once the patient’s neurological symptoms worsened, targeting HSV and VZV encephalitis. Both medications were subsequently discontinued once CSF studies excluded these viral etiologies.

### Summary

The patient presented with a constellation of systemic inflammatory signs (fever, malaise, arthralgia), mucocutaneous lesions (oral and later genital ulcers, folliculitis‑like rashes), and CNS‑related features (headache, persistent hiccups). Laboratory evaluation revealed **marked inflammation (CRP, ESR), organ‐system involvement (CK, LFTs, glycaemia)**, and **SIADH**. Imaging was unremarkable, prompting empiric antimicrobial coverage while awaiting conclusive diagnostic studies. These initial findings set the stage for subsequent investigations—or the discovery of a rare concurrence of systemic BD, acute neuro‑BD, and SIADH.

## 2. Differential Diagnosis and Exclusion Strategy

When a patient presents with fever, headache, mucocutaneous lesions and profound hyponatraemia, a systematic exclusion of serious mimics is mandatory. In this case the co‑occurrence of acute central nervous system (CNS) inflammation and a potential endocrine disturbance required a stepwise, evidence‑based approach that prioritized clinically relevant investigations while avoiding unnecessary testing.

### CNS infections

**Rationale** – The triad of fever, headache and CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis is classic for meningitis or encephalitis. Because HSV and VZV are the most common viral etiologies of aseptic meningitis in adults, they were the first targets. Bacterial meningitis was considered despite the absence of neutrophil predominance due to the patient’s young age and lack of focal neurological signs. Tuberculous meningitis was also entertained because of the chronicity of symptoms and the potential for subtle presentation in elderly patients.

**Investigations and Interpretation**  
- **CSF opening pressure**: Mild elevation (17 cm H₂O) suggested inflammation but was not diagnostic.  
- **CSF cell count & protein**: 11 cells/µL (lymphocytic) and 0.97 g/L protein are typical of aseptic meningitis.  
- **CSF Gram stain & cultures**: Negative, making bacterial meningitis unlikely.  
- **Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) for HSV & VZV**: Both negative, excluding these viral causes.  
- **Acid‑fast bacilli smear, nucleic‑acid amplification tests for Mycobacterium tuberculosis**: Negative, effectively ruling out tuberculous meningitis.

Given the comprehensive CSF work‑up and the absence of pathogen‑specific evidence, infectious meningitis was definitively excluded.

### Primary CNS malignancy

**Rationale** – Neoplastic meningitis can present with subtle CSF findings and normal MRI early in the disease. The mild elevation of soluble interleukin‑2 receptor (sIL‑2R) raised concern for lymphoma.

**Investigations and Interpretation**  
- **Brain MRI**: No focal brain abnormalities, including the hypothalamus or brainstem, diminishing the likelihood of CNS lymphoma or other primary tumors.  
- **CSF cytology**: No malignant cells, which has a high negative predictive value for CNS lymphoma in the setting of a normal MRI.  
- **sIL‑2R**: 704 U/mL, slightly above the upper normal limit (122–496 U/mL). However, sIL‑2R can be elevated in any strong inflammatory state, and in this case it was considered a non‑specific marker of systemic inflammation rather than lymphoma.

With normal imaging, negative cytology and a non‑specific sIL‑2R, primary CNS malignancy was ruled out.

### Systemic autoimmune disorders

**Rationale** – Autoimmune etiologies such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA) and other connective‑tissue diseases can manifest with systemic inflammation, mucocutaneous lesions and CNS involvement.

**Investigations and Interpretation**  
- **ANA (antinuclear antibody)**, **ANCA (anti‑neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody)** and **ENA (extractable nuclear antigen) panel**: All negative.  
- **Clinical criteria**: No serositis, arthritis beyond mild arthralgia, renal involvement or myopathy beyond mild CK elevation.  
- **Complement levels**: Not explicitly reported but presumed normal given the lack of clinical signs of active lupus.  

The absence of serological markers and supportive clinical features made a systemic autoimmune disorder unlikely.

### Neurosarcoidosis

**Rationale** – Sarcoidosis can involve the CNS and may present with meningitis‑like symptoms. Early disease can show normal MRI findings.

**Investigations and Interpretation**  
- **Serum ACE (angiotensin‑converting enzyme)**: 11.4 U/L (within normal range 7–25 U/L).  
- **Serum lysozyme**: 5.1 µg/mL (normal 5–10 µg/mL).  
- **Imaging**: Brain MRI was normal; no pituitary or hypothalamic lesions.  
- **Clinical assessment**: No hilar lymphadenopathy, pulmonary infiltrates or ocular lesions.

These data effectively excluded sarcoidosis as a culprit.

### Other endocrine or metabolic causes of hyponatraemia

**Rationale** – Hyponatraemia can arise from a spectrum of non‑CNS causes, including adrenal insufficiency, hypothyroidism, diuretic use, salt‑wasting nephropathies and congestive heart failure. Recognizing or ruling out these alternatives is essential to avoid misattributing hyponatraemia to SIADH.

**Investigations and Interpretation**  
- **Serum cortisol**: 42 µg/dL (significantly above the cut‑off for adrenal insufficiency).  
- **TSH and free T4**: Within normal limits, excluding hypothyroidism.  
- **Renal function tests**: Normal, ruling out intrinsic renal salt loss.  
- **Volume status**: Clinical assessment indicated euvolemia; no edema or orthostatic changes.  
- **Medication review**: No diuretics or other drugs known to cause SIADH were identified.

Thus, secondary causes of hyponatraemia were excluded, and the laboratory pattern (low serum osmolality, high urine osmolality and sodium, elevated ADH) satisfied the established criteria for SIADH.

### Rationale for Limited Screening Tests and Interpretation of Non‑Specific Findings

The diagnostic work‑up deliberately avoided an exhaustive panel of tests. The decision was guided by the principle that investigations should be driven by clinical suspicion and the pre‑test probability of disease. For example, while broad autoimmune serology panels (quantitative ANA, ENA, ANCA) were performed, they were not repeated beyond the initial screen, as no new clinical features emerged to warrant further testing. Similarly, sIL‑2R, although a marker of lymphoma and some inflammatory states, was interpreted in the context of a markedly elevated CRP and CSF lymphocytosis; its elevation alone was insufficient for a malignancy diagnosis.

This targeted approach conserves resources, reduces patient burden, and focuses the clinician’s attention on the most probable etiologies. In this case, the exclusion of infectious, neoplastic and systemic autoimmune causes converged on a diagnosis of concurrent Behçet’s disease and acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease, with SIADH as a likely secondary complication of CNS inflammation.

By systematically ruling out alternate causes while maintaining a high index of suspicion for neuro‑Behçet’s disease, the clinical team could initiate appropriate disease‑modifying therapy early, ultimately leading to complete recovery without the need for corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants.

## 3. Diagnostic Confirmation of Behçet’s Disease and Neuro‑Behçet’s Disease

The diagnostic process for Behçet’s disease (BD) relies on a combination of clinical manifestations rather than a single laboratory test. The 2018 International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD) provide a quantitative framework: oral aphthous ulcers, genital ulcers, skin lesions, ocular involvement, and a positive pathergy test are each assigned specific points. A total score of ≥4 establishes a definite diagnosis. In this case, the patient accrued five points, thereby satisfying the ICBD for definite BD.

### ICBD scoring system and patient’s points

| ICBD Criterion | Points | Patient’s Findings |
|----------------|--------|--------------------|
| Oral aphthae (≥2) | 2 | Multiple erythematous ulcers in the oral mucosa |
| Genital ulcers (≥2) | 2 | Painful ulceration of the scrotal skin |
| Skin lesions (moderate) | 1 | Folliculitis‑like papulopustules on face and trunk |
| Ocular involvement | 0 | None (slit‑lamp exam normal) |
| Pathergy test | 0 | Not performed (not required for definite disease) |
| **Total** | **5** | **≥4 → definite BD** |

The presence of fever contributed to the overall clinical picture but is not a formal ICBD component; nevertheless, it supports systemic inflammation typical of BD.

### Role of HLA‑B51 and its limitations

HLA‑B51 is a genetic marker strongly associated with BD, particularly in Asian populations. Its presence increases the pre‑test probability of disease but is neither necessary nor sufficient for diagnosis. In this patient, HLA‑B51 positivity reinforced the clinical suspicion but did not influence the ICBD score. Consequently, the final diagnosis remained grounded in the clinical criteria rather than genetic testing.

### CSF profile consistent with aseptic meningitis and its significance for neuro‑BD

On day 3, the lumbar puncture revealed:

- **Opening pressure**: mildly elevated (17 cm H₂O)
- **Protein**: 0.97 g/L (slightly high)
- **Cell count**: 11 cells/µL, predominantly lymphocytes

These findings constitute an aseptic meningitis profile: mild leukocytosis with a lymphocytic predominance, intact glucose, and modest protein elevation. Extensive microbiological evaluation (bacterial cultures, acid‑fast bacilli, PCR for HSV, VZV, and cryptococcal antigen) returned negative. The absence of a conclusive infectious etiology, combined with the clinical context of mucocutaneous BD, pointed toward an immune‑mediated process affecting the central nervous system.

In BD, neuro‑involvement can present as a self‑limited aseptic meningitis without overt parenchymal lesions. The CSF picture in this case is characteristic of an inflammatory, non‑infectious meningitis and is highly suggestive of neuro‑BD, particularly when other etiologies have been excluded.

### Normal MRI in early neuro‑BD – pathophysiological explanation and clinical implications

Brain MRI was performed on day 3 and returned without focal lesions, vascular abnormalities, or brain‑stem involvement—the typical sites of neuro‑BD pathology. However, a normal MRI does **not** rule out neuro‑BD. Several mechanisms explain an initially normal scan:

1. **Early inflammatory phase**: In the acute meningoencephalitic stage, cytokine‑mediated edema may be diffuse and below the resolution of conventional imaging.
2. **Transient blood‑brain barrier dysfunction**: The inflammatory process can increase permeability without forming distinct focal lesions.
3. **CSF‑mediated inflammation**: Aseptic meningitis can result from cytokine spillover into CSF without causing parenchymal damage detectable on MRI.

Clinical implications are profound. Physicians must recognize that a normal neuroimaging study does **not** exclude neuro‑BD, particularly when CSF indicates inflammation and systemic BD is evident. Therefore, the diagnostic criteria for neuro‑BD include CSF findings in addition to imaging.

### Combined diagnosis of definite BD and acute neuro‑BD

The diagnostic pathway unfolded as follows:

1. **Definitive BD**: ICBD score of 5 (oral aphthae + genital ulcers + folliculitis‑like rash) confirmed systemic BD.
2. **Neuro‑BD**: Aseptic meningitis on CSF, absence of infectious, autoimmune, and neoplastic causes, and a normal MRI in the early disease stage satisfied the definition of **acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease**.
3. **Concurrent onset**: The temporal coincidence of systemic mucocutaneous lesions and CNS inflammation justified a simultaneous diagnosis, even though neuro‑BD typically occurs years after initial BD.

Thus, the patient met the diagnostic criteria for definite BD and was subsequently classified as having acute neuro‑BD, despite a normal MRI, due to the supportive CSF profile and the exclusion of alternative causes. This dual diagnosis formed the basis for targeted management and subsequent therapeutic decisions.

## 4. Management Strategy and Therapeutic Rationale

The therapeutic course in this patient was guided by a balance between empiric treatment for potential infectious etiologies and rapid control of the underlying inflammatory process that defined Behçet’s disease. The cornerstone of management was colchicine monotherapy, complemented by conservative measures for hyponatraemia and targeted wound care. The following discussion outlines the rationale for each component, the evidence basis (or lack thereof) supporting their use in acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD), and the practical aspects of monitoring and long‑term care.

### Empiric Antimicrobials and Antiviral Therapy

**Indications and Timing**  
In any patient presenting with fever, headache, and meningitic signs, the initial differential must include bacterial, viral, and tuberculous meningitis. Empiric cefepime was started immediately after blood cultures, covering gram‑negative rods, Pseudomonas, and, to a lesser extent, gram‑positive cocci. Concurrently, acyclovir was initiated when the CSF analysis revealed lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein, a pattern common to herpes simplex virus (HSV) and varicella zoster virus (VZV) encephalitis. The empirical coverage was timed to prevent progression to fulminant CNS infection while awaiting definitive diagnostic data.

**Discontinuation Criteria**  
When CSF investigations returned negative for bacterial cultures, acid‑fast staining, PCR for HSV/VZV, and cryptococcal antigen, the possibility of infection was effectively excluded. Thus, cefepime and acyclovir were stopped on day 3, minimizing unnecessary drug exposure and reducing the risk of adverse effects such as nephrotoxicity or neurotoxicity. The rapid de‑escalation also facilitated a clearer assessment of the patient’s response to anti‑inflammatory therapy.

### Colchicine Pharmacodynamics

Colchicine’s anti‑inflammatory action is primarily mediated by inhibition of microtubule polymerization, which in turn suppresses neutrophil chemotaxis, degranulation, and adherence. This neutrophil blockade reduces the release of pro‑inflammatory cytokines, notably interleukin‑1β (IL‑1β) and interleukin‑6 (IL‑6). In BD, neutrophil hyperactivity is a hallmark of mucocutaneous lesions and systemic inflammation. By dampening these pathways, colchicine theoretically mitigates the inflammatory cascade that affects the CNS in neuro‑BD.

While the precise mechanism by which colchicine may correct SIADH remains speculative, the reduction of IL‑6—a cytokine implicated in ectopic ADH release—could attenuate the inappropriate antidiuretic drive. Thus, colchicine’s systemic anti‑inflammatory effect likely contributed to the simultaneous resolution of neurological symptoms and hyponatraemia.

### Evidence Base for Colchicine in Mucocutaneous Versus Neuro‑BD

**Mucocutaneous Disease**  
Extensive observational data and meta‑analyses support colchicine as first‑line therapy for oral/genital aphthae, erythema nodosum, and folliculitis‑like ulcerations in BD. Meta‑analyses demonstrate a relative risk reduction of 40–60 % for recurrent aphthosis and improved patient‑reported outcomes.

**Neuro‑BD**  
Evidence for colchicine in acute neuro‑BD is sparse and largely limited to case reports or small series. A systematic review of neuro‑BD treatments found that steroids, azathioprine, or cyclosporine remain the mainstays of therapy, with colchicine used adjunctively. The current case represents one of the few instances where colchicine monotherapy achieved clinical remission, suggesting that, in selected patients with isolated aseptic meningitis and no focal deficits, colchicine could suffice.

Given the absence of large randomized trials, the decision to use colchicine alone was driven by the patient’s preference to avoid steroids, his modest disease severity, and the rapid improvement of symptoms following initiation.

### Fluid Restriction for SIADH

**Monitoring Parameters**  
Patients with SIADH were monitored daily for serum sodium, serum osmolality, urine osmolality, and urinary sodium. Fluid intake was restricted to less than 800 mL/day, a conservative target that limits free water intake without compromising overall volume status. Weight was recorded to ensure euvolemia, and orthostatic vital signs were checked to detect subtle shifts.

**Safety Limits**  
The daily change in serum sodium was capped at 8 mmol/L to avoid osmotic demyelination syndrome. In this case, sodium rose from 124 to 136 mmol/L over 16 days, a gradual and safe correction. The fluid restriction protocol was complemented by meticulous monitoring for symptoms of overcorrection (e.g., neurologic deterioration, nausea). No osmotic demyelination was observed.

### Adjunctive Wound Care

**Topical Steroids**  
Ophthalmologically the patient had no uveitis, but oral lesions were treated with topical steroid ointment to accelerate mucosal healing and reduce pain.

**Silver Sulfadiazine**  
Genital and cutaneous ulcers were managed with silver sulfadiazine cream, a broad‑spectrum antimicrobial that also promotes epithelialization. While not specifically anti‑inflammatory, local wound therapy reduces secondary infection risk and facilitates recovery.

**Pain Control**  
Acute pain from genital ulcers and muscle pain was managed with acetaminophen and, when necessary, short courses of low‑dose opioids. NSAIDs were avoided early due to potential renal dysfunction risk in the setting of hyponatraemia.

### Long‑Term Follow‑Up

**Duration of Colchicine**  
The patient received colchicine for approximately four years, a duration aligned with guidelines for maintenance therapy in mucocutaneous BD to prevent relapse. The decision to extend therapy was based on the sustained absence of disease activity, normal inflammatory markers, and patient tolerance.

**Monitoring for Recurrence**  
Regular annual evaluations included:

- Clinical assessment for new aphthous lesions, genital ulcers, or cutaneous rash.
- Laboratory monitoring of CRP, ESR, and complete blood count for subclinical inflammation.
- Imaging only if new focal neurological symptoms arose (e.g., MRI with contrast).
- Monitoring of renal function and electrolytes to detect potential colchicine toxicity.

**Transition to Other Therapies**  
If recurrence occurred or if the patient developed new organ involvement (e.g., uveitis, vascular lesions), escalation to immunosuppressive agents such as azathioprine or biologics (anti‑IL‑1 or anti‑TNF) would be considered. In the absence of relapse, colchicine remained the preferred agent due to its favorable safety profile and patient adherence.

---

In summary, the therapeutic strategy combined empiric antimicrobial coverage early in the admission, rapid de‑escalation once infections were ruled out, and colchicine monotherapy targeting the underlying neutrophil‑driven inflammation of BD. Conservative fluid restriction achieved safe correction of SIADH–associated hyponatraemia, while adjunctive wound care addressed mucocutaneous lesions. The extended colchicine course and rigorous follow‑up minimized relapse risk and obviated the need for higher‑risk immunosuppressants. This case underscores the potential role of colchicine in carefully selected patients with acute neuro‑BD and highlights the importance of meticulous monitoring when managing complex inflammatory disorders with endocrine sequelae.

## 5. Outcomes, Clinical Lessons and Prognostic Considerations

The clinical course of this patient underscores the protracted but ultimately favorable trajectory that can accompany acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) when the underlying systemic disease is promptly recognized and managed. After initiation of colchicine monotherapy, the patient’s fever subsided within 24 h, and his headache, vertigo, and hiccups resolved over the next two weeks. Concurrently, the mucocutaneous manifestations—oral aphthae, folliculitis‑like rash, and genital ulceration—receded, and his grip strength and overall functional status returned to baseline by day 30. These symptomatic improvements paralleled a normalization of laboratory indices: serum sodium rose to 136 mmol/L and stabilized within the reference range; ADH levels fell from an initial 22.4 pg/mL to 1.8 pg/mL; creatine kinase declined from 1,349 U/L to within normal limits; and liver transaminases (AST/ALT) returned to baseline. Cerebrospinal fluid protein and cell counts, though initially mild, were no longer measurable after the acute phase.

Notably, the patient remained free of relapses for the subsequent four years while on continuous colchicine therapy (1 mg twice daily). No additional corticosteroid courses, biologic agents, or immunosuppressive regimens were required, and his neuro‑BD remained quiescent. There were no recurrences of hyponatraemia or of the neurological symptoms that had characterized the first admission. This prolonged remission highlights the potential for colchicine to exert durable control of both mucocutaneous and neurological disease activity in select patients.

From a pathophysiological standpoint, the temporal association between CNS inflammation, elevated interleukin‑6 (IL‑6), and SIADH in this case is compelling. IL‑6 is a pleiotropic cytokine known to stimulate antidiuretic hormone (ADH) synthesis and release from the posterior pituitary and to increase vascular permeability, thereby contributing to hyponatraemia. Although IL‑6 levels were not directly measured in this patient, the markedly elevated serum C‑reactive protein (CRP) and the CSF protein elevation suggest a heightened inflammatory milieu that could feasibly drive aberrant ADH secretion. Thus, IL‑6 may serve as a mechanistic bridge linking acute neuro‑BD to SIADH.

The clinical red flags that portended the discovery of SIADH in this patient were multifold. Persistent hiccups, initially attributed to central vestibular irritation, proved to be a harbinger of profound hyponatraemia. Similarly, rhabdomyolysis (CK > 1,000 U/L) accompanied the febrile illness and may have reflected muscle necrosis induced by low serum sodium or by inflammatory cytokines. These symptoms, often overlooked or misattributed, warrant careful evaluation for electrolytic imbalances, especially in patients with suspected CNS inflammation.

Practical recommendations for clinicians managing similar presentations are illustrated by this case:

1. **Maintain a high index of suspicion for neuro‑BD** in patients who already meet criteria for Behçet’s disease and who develop unexplained fever, headache, vertigo, or other non‑focal neurological signs. A normal brain MRI does not exclude neuro‑BD; CSF examination for aseptic meningitis should be pursued early.

2. **Screen for SIADH promptly** in any neuro‑BD patient presenting with hyponatraemia, particularly if accompanied by persistent hiccups, unexplained fatigue, or lab evidence of muscle injury. Confirm SIADH by assessing serum osmolality, urine osmolality, urinary sodium, and plasma ADH; exclude hypovolemia, hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency, and nephrogenic causes.

3. **Manage SIADH conservatively with fluid restriction** (<800 mL/day) and close monitoring of serum sodium, reserving hypertonic saline or vasopressin receptor antagonists for severe or refractory cases. Rapid correction of sodium should be avoided to prevent osmotic demyelination.

4. **Consider colchicine as first‑line therapy** in patients with concurrent mucocutaneous and neurologic manifestations who either decline corticosteroids or have contraindications to systemic steroids. Colchicine’s anti‑neutrophilic and cytokine‑modulating properties may suffice for patients with mild to moderate neuro‑BD; however, clinicians should remain vigilant for disease progression that would necessitate escalation to steroids or biologics.

5. **Monitor long‑term disease activity** with periodic clinical assessment and laboratory work‑up (CBC, CRP, ESR, serum sodium). The absence of relapse over a multi‑year period in this patient suggests that early, decisive treatment can achieve durable remission, but longer follow‑up remains essential to detect late recurrences.

In conclusion, this case illustrates that acute neuro‑BD can present concurrently with systemic Behçet’s disease and that SIADH may be an integral, albeit underrecognized, component of the disease spectrum. Prompt identification and tailored management of hyponatraemia, together with judicious use of colchicine, can lead to complete recovery without the need for corticosteroids or more aggressive immunosuppression. Clinicians should incorporate these insights into the diagnostic and therapeutic algorithm for patients with Behçet’s disease who exhibit new neurological or endocrine disturbances.
