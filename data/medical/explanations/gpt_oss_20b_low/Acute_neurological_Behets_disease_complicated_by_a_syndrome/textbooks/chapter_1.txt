Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

Chapter 1: ## 1. Clinical Presentation and Initial Evaluation

A man in his 60s presented to the emergency department with a **3‑day history of fever (38.1 °C), relentless headache, generalized malaise, loss of appetite, persistent hiccups, and arthralgia of the shoulders and ankles**. He also noted several oral aphthae, which are characteristic red‑raised lesions that tend to resolve within a week but often herald systemic disease. In the ensuing days, a painful scrotal ulcer appeared, confirming genital mucocutaneous involvement. These acute mucocutaneous findings, combined with systemic inflammatory symptoms, raised the differential for a multisystem inflammatory disorder such as Behçet’s disease (BD).

### Vital Signs and Physical Examination

On presentation the patient was alert but fatigued. Blood pressure (135/73 mm Hg) and oxygen saturation (96 % on room air) were within normal limits. **Heart rate was tachycardic at 119 beats per minute**—a common response to fever and possible volume shifts. Respiratory rate (19 breaths per minute) and temperature (38.1 °C) reflected an active febrile state. Cardiopulmonary and abdominal examinations were unremarkable. Manual muscle testing revealed no focal weakness, and there was no peripheral edema, suggesting **euvolemia**. The physical exam noted **oral aphthous ulcers** and **folliculitis‑like rashes** on the face and trunk, consistent with the **symmetrical, low‑grade erythematous lesions** typical of BD.

### Core Laboratory Findings

A routine laboratory panel immediately yielded several striking abnormalities:

| Test | Result | Normal range | Interpretation |
|------|--------|--------------|----------------|
| Serum sodium | 124 mmol/L | 135‑145 | **Hyponatraemia** |
| Serum osmolality | 267 mOsm/kg | >275‑295 | **Low osmolality** |
| Urine osmolality | 900 mOsm/kg | >300 | **Concentrated urine** |
| Urine sodium | 88 mmol/L | >20 | **Inappropriately high sodium excretion** |
| Serum ADH | 22.4 pg/mL | 0‑5 | **Elevated ADH** |
| CRP | 180 mg/L | <3 | **Marked inflammation** |
| Creatine kinase | 1349 U/L | <200 | **Rhabdomyolysis** |
| AST | 77 U/L | <40 | **Mild transaminitis** |
| ALT | 64 U/L | <40 | **Mild transaminitis** |
| Blood glucose | 14.2 mmol/L | 3.9‑7.8 | **Hyper‑glycaemia** |
| HbA1c | 8.2 % | <5.7 | **Poor glycaemic control** |

The hyponatraemia was **hypo‑osmolar** with impaired water excretion (elevated urine osmolality and sodium), and ADH was markedly elevated. These findings satisfy the traditional diagnostic criteria for **syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH)**, an endocrine manifestation often triggered by central nervous system (CNS) inflammation, malignancy, or certain drugs.

### Early Imaging and Its Findings

A **non‑contrast CT scan of the brain** was performed to exclude acute intracranial pathology – such as hemorrhage, mass effect, or skull fractures – that might explain the headache. The CT was normal. Similarly, a **CT scan of the chest and abdomen** was obtained to look for occult infections, lymphadenopathy, or neoplasms. No abnormal findings were noted on any of these studies. The absence of focal lesions or masses on imaging narrows the differential to primarily infectious or inflammatory processes rather than a space‑occupying lesion.

### Empiric Antimicrobial Coverage

Given the combination of fever, headache, and hyponatraemia, **bacterial meningitis** and **viral encephalitis** were high on the list of life‑threatening potential etiologies. Early empirical therapy was started with **cefepime**, a broad‑spectrum β‑lactam antibiotic effective against gram‑negative and gram‑positive organisms, including Pseudomonas and Enterobacterales, which can cause community‑acquired meningitis. Cefepime was chosen because it covers many of the common community‑acquired pathogens and provides adequate central nervous system penetration. Meanwhile, empirical **acyclovir** was added once the patient’s neurological symptoms worsened, targeting HSV and VZV encephalitis. Both medications were subsequently discontinued once CSF studies excluded these viral etiologies.

### Summary

The patient presented with a constellation of systemic inflammatory signs (fever, malaise, arthralgia), mucocutaneous lesions (oral and later genital ulcers, folliculitis‑like rashes), and CNS‑related features (headache, persistent hiccups). Laboratory evaluation revealed **marked inflammation (CRP, ESR), organ‐system involvement (CK, LFTs, glycaemia)**, and **SIADH**. Imaging was unremarkable, prompting empiric antimicrobial coverage while awaiting conclusive diagnostic studies. These initial findings set the stage for subsequent investigations—or the discovery of a rare concurrence of systemic BD, acute neuro‑BD, and SIADH.
