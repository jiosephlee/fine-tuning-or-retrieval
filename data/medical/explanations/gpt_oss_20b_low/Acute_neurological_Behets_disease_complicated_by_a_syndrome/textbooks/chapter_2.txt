Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

Chapter 2: ## 2. Differential Diagnosis and Exclusion Strategy

When a patient presents with fever, headache, mucocutaneous lesions and profound hyponatraemia, a systematic exclusion of serious mimics is mandatory. In this case the co‑occurrence of acute central nervous system (CNS) inflammation and a potential endocrine disturbance required a stepwise, evidence‑based approach that prioritized clinically relevant investigations while avoiding unnecessary testing.

### CNS infections

**Rationale** – The triad of fever, headache and CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis is classic for meningitis or encephalitis. Because HSV and VZV are the most common viral etiologies of aseptic meningitis in adults, they were the first targets. Bacterial meningitis was considered despite the absence of neutrophil predominance due to the patient’s young age and lack of focal neurological signs. Tuberculous meningitis was also entertained because of the chronicity of symptoms and the potential for subtle presentation in elderly patients.

**Investigations and Interpretation**  
- **CSF opening pressure**: Mild elevation (17 cm H₂O) suggested inflammation but was not diagnostic.  
- **CSF cell count & protein**: 11 cells/µL (lymphocytic) and 0.97 g/L protein are typical of aseptic meningitis.  
- **CSF Gram stain & cultures**: Negative, making bacterial meningitis unlikely.  
- **Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) for HSV & VZV**: Both negative, excluding these viral causes.  
- **Acid‑fast bacilli smear, nucleic‑acid amplification tests for Mycobacterium tuberculosis**: Negative, effectively ruling out tuberculous meningitis.

Given the comprehensive CSF work‑up and the absence of pathogen‑specific evidence, infectious meningitis was definitively excluded.

### Primary CNS malignancy

**Rationale** – Neoplastic meningitis can present with subtle CSF findings and normal MRI early in the disease. The mild elevation of soluble interleukin‑2 receptor (sIL‑2R) raised concern for lymphoma.

**Investigations and Interpretation**  
- **Brain MRI**: No focal brain abnormalities, including the hypothalamus or brainstem, diminishing the likelihood of CNS lymphoma or other primary tumors.  
- **CSF cytology**: No malignant cells, which has a high negative predictive value for CNS lymphoma in the setting of a normal MRI.  
- **sIL‑2R**: 704 U/mL, slightly above the upper normal limit (122–496 U/mL). However, sIL‑2R can be elevated in any strong inflammatory state, and in this case it was considered a non‑specific marker of systemic inflammation rather than lymphoma.

With normal imaging, negative cytology and a non‑specific sIL‑2R, primary CNS malignancy was ruled out.

### Systemic autoimmune disorders

**Rationale** – Autoimmune etiologies such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA) and other connective‑tissue diseases can manifest with systemic inflammation, mucocutaneous lesions and CNS involvement.

**Investigations and Interpretation**  
- **ANA (antinuclear antibody)**, **ANCA (anti‑neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody)** and **ENA (extractable nuclear antigen) panel**: All negative.  
- **Clinical criteria**: No serositis, arthritis beyond mild arthralgia, renal involvement or myopathy beyond mild CK elevation.  
- **Complement levels**: Not explicitly reported but presumed normal given the lack of clinical signs of active lupus.  

The absence of serological markers and supportive clinical features made a systemic autoimmune disorder unlikely.

### Neurosarcoidosis

**Rationale** – Sarcoidosis can involve the CNS and may present with meningitis‑like symptoms. Early disease can show normal MRI findings.

**Investigations and Interpretation**  
- **Serum ACE (angiotensin‑converting enzyme)**: 11.4 U/L (within normal range 7–25 U/L).  
- **Serum lysozyme**: 5.1 µg/mL (normal 5–10 µg/mL).  
- **Imaging**: Brain MRI was normal; no pituitary or hypothalamic lesions.  
- **Clinical assessment**: No hilar lymphadenopathy, pulmonary infiltrates or ocular lesions.

These data effectively excluded sarcoidosis as a culprit.

### Other endocrine or metabolic causes of hyponatraemia

**Rationale** – Hyponatraemia can arise from a spectrum of non‑CNS causes, including adrenal insufficiency, hypothyroidism, diuretic use, salt‑wasting nephropathies and congestive heart failure. Recognizing or ruling out these alternatives is essential to avoid misattributing hyponatraemia to SIADH.

**Investigations and Interpretation**  
- **Serum cortisol**: 42 µg/dL (significantly above the cut‑off for adrenal insufficiency).  
- **TSH and free T4**: Within normal limits, excluding hypothyroidism.  
- **Renal function tests**: Normal, ruling out intrinsic renal salt loss.  
- **Volume status**: Clinical assessment indicated euvolemia; no edema or orthostatic changes.  
- **Medication review**: No diuretics or other drugs known to cause SIADH were identified.

Thus, secondary causes of hyponatraemia were excluded, and the laboratory pattern (low serum osmolality, high urine osmolality and sodium, elevated ADH) satisfied the established criteria for SIADH.

### Rationale for Limited Screening Tests and Interpretation of Non‑Specific Findings

The diagnostic work‑up deliberately avoided an exhaustive panel of tests. The decision was guided by the principle that investigations should be driven by clinical suspicion and the pre‑test probability of disease. For example, while broad autoimmune serology panels (quantitative ANA, ENA, ANCA) were performed, they were not repeated beyond the initial screen, as no new clinical features emerged to warrant further testing. Similarly, sIL‑2R, although a marker of lymphoma and some inflammatory states, was interpreted in the context of a markedly elevated CRP and CSF lymphocytosis; its elevation alone was insufficient for a malignancy diagnosis.

This targeted approach conserves resources, reduces patient burden, and focuses the clinician’s attention on the most probable etiologies. In this case, the exclusion of infectious, neoplastic and systemic autoimmune causes converged on a diagnosis of concurrent Behçet’s disease and acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease, with SIADH as a likely secondary complication of CNS inflammation.

By systematically ruling out alternate causes while maintaining a high index of suspicion for neuro‑Behçet’s disease, the clinical team could initiate appropriate disease‑modifying therapy early, ultimately leading to complete recovery without the need for corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants.
