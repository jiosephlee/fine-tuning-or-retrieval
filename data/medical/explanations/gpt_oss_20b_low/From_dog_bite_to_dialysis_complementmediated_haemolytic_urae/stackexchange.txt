Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Clinical Pearls] What clinical features helped distinguish Capnocytophaga canimorsus sepsis from other causes of sepsis in this patient, and why is rapid identification of this organism important for management?

**Clinical Pearls – Capnocytophaga canimorsus Sepsis**

**Key distinguishing features**

| Feature | Why it points to C. canimorsus |
|---------|--------------------------------|
| **Recent dog bite (upper lip)** | The organism is a normal canine oral commensal. A bite is the classic exposure route. |
| **Rapid onset of fever (>40 °C), hypoglycaemia, neutropenia and marked leukopaenia** | C. canimorsus bacteremia frequently produces fulminant sepsis with profound cytopenias, a pattern less common in other septic pathogens. |
| **Severe coagulopathy with DIC‑like picture (prolonged PT/INR, low fibrinogen, high D‑dimer)** | Although DIC can occur with any septicemia, the combination of pancytopenia and coagulopathy shortly after a dog bite strongly suggests this organism. |
| **Early‑onset renal dysfunction (creatinine rise, proteinuria, hematuria) with no prior kidney disease** | C. canimorsus may trigger endothelial injury and complement activation early, predisposing to AKI before other organ failures. |
| **Normal ADAMTS13 activity (~44 %)** | Rules out TTP; the timing of hemolysis improving after antibiotics and before complement blockade favors a post‑septic TMA rather than primary TTP. |
| **Negative Shiga‑toxin PCR and absence of prodromal diarrhoea** | Excludes STEC‑HUS, the most common TMA in adults; the dog‑bite context shifts suspicion to a zoonotic trigger. |

**Why rapid identification matters**

1. **Antibiotic stewardship** – C. canimorsus is intrinsically susceptible to β‑lactams but is slow‑growing; early confirmation allows de‑escalation to narrower therapy (e.g., penicillin‑G) and limits broad‑spectrum use.

2. **Infection‑triggered complement‑mediated TMA (CM‑HUS)** – The organism’s capsule can activate the alternative complement pathway. Prompt detection permits early initiation of plasma exchange and, critically, complement blockade with eculizumab. Delayed therapy is associated with irreversible renal injury.

3. **Infection‑related safety of complement inhibition** – Complement remains essential for defense against encapsulated bacteria. Knowing the etiologic agent and ensuring clearance before eculizumab minimizes the risk of life‑threatening infections (e.g., Neisseria spp. and further C. canimorsus replication).

4. **Prognostication and monitoring** – Identification of the pathogen guides prognosis (30 % mortality in untreated C. canimorsus sepsis) and informs close surveillance for evolving TMA, guiding timing of renal biopsy and complement testing.

**Bottom line:** A history of dog bite, coupled with rapid septic deterioration, pancytopenia, coagulopathy, and early renal impairment, should prompt suspicion of C. canimorsus. Confirming the organism early not only tailors antibiotics but also alerts clinicians to the possibility of infection‑triggered CM‑HUS, where early complement blockade can spare renal function and improve survival.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Which laboratory findings during the ICU course first suggested the development of a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) rather than ongoing DIC, and how did they change the diagnostic approach?

**Answer**

In this patient the key laboratory shift that tipped the balance from “ongoing sepsis‑related DIC” to a “superimposed thrombotic microangiopathy” occurred when the haemoglobin fell abruptly, haptoglobin dropped to zero, LDH rose, and 15–20 % schistocytes appeared on the peripheral smear, while the coagulation tests (PT, aPTT, fibrinogen) were already normalizing.  

**Why these findings suggest TMA rather than DIC**

| Parameter | DIC pattern | TMA pattern in this case |
|-----------|-------------|--------------------------|
| **Coagulation profile** | Persistent prolongation of PT/aPTT, low fibrinogen, high D‑dimer | Gradual normalization of PT/aPTT and fibrinogen; D‑dimer modestly elevated but not all‑encompassing |
| **Platelets** | Fluctuating thrombocytopenia, often > 50 × 10⁹/L in early DIC | Persistently low platelets (30–40 × 10⁹/L) with no clear trend toward recovery |
| **Red cell indices** | Mild anaemia; reticulocytosis may be present | Sudden drop in Hb (111 → 68 g/L) with uncontrolled hemolysis (haptoglobin < 0.03 g/L, LDH > 2× ULN) |
| **Peripheral smear** | Occasionally schistocytes, but usually few (< 5 %) | 15–20 % schistocytes indicating microangiopathic haemolysis |
| **Complement** | No specific abnormality | Normal C3/C4 but later a marked fall in alternative‑pathway activity (AH50 6 %) consistent with complement activation |

The emergence of schistocytes and a **haemolysis‑platelet discordant pattern** (i.e., platelets remaining low while haemoglobin collapses) is a classic red flag for TMA, because DIC usually resolves the first step (coagulation) before haemolysis wanes.  In our case the coagulation parameters had already begun to correct, yet haemolysis intensified, which is incompatible with a “pure” DIC course.

**Impact on the diagnostic workflow**

1. **Rapid confirmation of TMA** – The schistocyte count and LDH immediately prompted an “immediate‑home” TMA work‑up rather than continuing to monitor for DIC resolution.  
2. **ADAMTS13 assay** – A quick ADAMTS13 activity assay (reported 43.5 %) ruled out TTP, allowing us to focus on complement‑mediated HUS.  
3. **Complement studies** – Normal C3/C4 did not deter further testing; the clinical picture forced us to order functional complement assays (AH50), which later confirmed diminished alternative‑pathway activity.  
4. **Therapeutic shift** – The confirmation of TMA led to the initiation of plasma exchange (as a bridge) and, crucially, the later start of eculizumab, the definitive therapy for CM‑HUS.  
5. **Renal biopsy** – With the TMA diagnosis secured, a renal biopsy was obtained, showing fibrin thrombi and crescents—pathognomonic for TMA and reassuring that the haemolysis was not secondary to a coagulopathy or sepsis alone.

**Bottom line**

The abrupt haemolysis with persistent thrombocytopenia, schistocytosis, and the normalization of coagulation parameters pinpointed a thrombotic microangiopathy superimposed on sepsis‑related DIC. Recognizing this diagnostic pivot shifted the care from supportive DIC management to targeted complement‑inhibitor therapy, dramatically altering the patient’s outcome.

### [Pathophysiology] Explain the pathophysiological mechanism by which complement‑mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM‑HUS) can be triggered by an infection such as C. canimorsus, including the role of the alternative complement pathway.

**Pathophysiological link between a C. canimorsus infection and complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS)**  

1. **Initial infection and complement activation**  
   - *C. canimorsus* is an encapsulated, gram‑negative rod that is normally protected from serum killing by its polysaccharide capsule.  
   - In most hosts, the plasma alternative pathway is the first line of defense: spontaneous hydrolysis of C3 → C3(H₂O) → formation of C3 convertase (C3bBb) that deposits C3b on the bacterial surface. This opsonizes the organism and, if uncontrolled, leads to formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC).  
   - In the described patient, blood cultures grew *C. canimorsus* 4 days after bite; initial immune responses likely involved activation of the alternative pathway to clear the organism.

2. **Triggering of a secondary, unregulated complement cascade**  
   - CM‑HUS occurs when the alternative pathway is **inadequately regulated**, typically due to loss‑of‑function variants (e.g., factor H, factor I, membrane cofactor protein) or autoantibodies.  
   - Even in the absence of a detectable genetic defect—as in this patient—severe infections can overwhelm regulatory mechanisms. The massive bacterial burden may release pathogen‑associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) that bind complement and generate excess C3 convertase complexes.  
   - The continued presence of bacterial lipopolysaccharide and other surface molecules keeps the alternative pathway persistently active, producing large amounts of C5‑b and, eventually, MAC.  

3. **Endothelial injury and thrombotic microangiopathy**  
   - Endothelial cells are exquisitely sensitive to MAC deposition. Sublytic MAC (C5b‑9) assembly triggers endothelial release of von Willebrand factor, tissue factor, and adhesion molecules, promoting platelet adhesion and aggregation.  
   - Activated platelets form microthrombi in arterioles and capillaries; fibrin deposition ensues, leading to “shistocytes” (fragmented erythrocytes) and consumptive thrombocytopenia observed in the case.  
   - The kidney, with its dense microvasculature, is the most affected organ, explaining the abrupt rise in creatinine, anuria, and biopsy‑proven TMA with fibrin thrombi and crescents.

4. **Clinical course in this case**  
   - Early sepsis produced disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) and acute renal failure.  
   - As the bacterial load fell, the complement system remained hyperactive, unimpeded by adequate regulation, and serum complement components (notably functional alternative‑pathway activity, AH50) became markedly low (6 %), indicating consumption and/or block‑down.  
   - The delayed appearance of hemolysis and thrombocytopenia, after the inflammatory markers were subsiding, fits the “second‑hit” model: infection triggers a burst of complement activation that, in a susceptible host, leads to CM‑HUS once the initial inflammatory drive has abated.

5. **Therapeutic implications**  
   - Plasma exchange (PEX) transiently removed circulating complement fragments and inflammatory mediators but did not correct the underlying dysregulation.  
   - Complement blockade with eculizumab (anti‑C5) halted further MAC formation, allowing endothelial repair and resolution of hemolysis, as demonstrated by hematologic remission and partial renal recovery.

In summary, a *C. canimorsus* infection can initiate excessive alternative‑pathway activation; in a host with reduced regulatory capacity (genetic or functional), this leads to uncontrolled MAC deposition, endothelial injury, and the classic thrombotic microangiopathy of CM‑HUS. This pathophysiologic cascade explains the temporal relationship between infection resolution and onset of TMA seen in the reported patient.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Why was thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) considered unlikely in this patient, and what clinical score and laboratory value were most decisive?

**Answer**

TTP was considered unlikely because the patient’s *PLASMIC* score was only 4 / 7—below the threshold (>6) that predicts a high pre‑test probability of severe ADAMTS13 deficiency—and the ADAMTS13 activity measured was 43.5 %.  A value above 10 % virtually excludes the severe (<10 %) ADAMTS13 deficiency characteristic of TTP.  Thus, the combination of a low PLASMIC score and an ADAMTS13 activity well above the diagnostic cutoff was the decisive evidence against TTP in this case.

### [Pathophysiology] What are the key histopathologic findings on renal biopsy that confirm TMA in the setting of CM‑HUS, and why were immune complex deposits ruled out?

**Key histopathologic findings that confirm TMA in CM‑HUS**

1. **Endothelial injury with in‑situ fibrin thrombi**  
   Light microscopy shows red‑cell–rich, fibrin‑laden microthrombi occluding arterioles, capillaries, and small glomerular arteries. These thrombi are often confluent, creating “corkscrew” or “sieve‑like” patterns typical of TMA.

2. **Acute tubular necrosis (ATN) and ischemic glomeruli**  
   The biopsy in this patient demonstrated severe ATN and “ischemic” glomeruli—i.e., shrunken capillary loops with loss of normal staining and occasional glassy hyaline casts—reflecting the sequela of microvascular occlusion.

3. **Crescent formation**  
   Crescentic lesions were “scattered” and not typical of an immune‑complex glomerulonephritis. They represent a response to capillary wall rupture and platelet thrombi rather than antibody‑mediated injury.

4. **Absence of immune complex deposition**  
   Immunofluorescence was negative for IgG, IgM, IgA, C3 or C1q in the glomeruli, supporting a non‑immune‑complex mechanism. Electron microscopy showed no subendothelial or subepithelial “humps” or electron‑dense deposits.

5. **Schistocytosis on peripheral smear** – though not a biopsy finding, it corroborated the microscopic evidence of microangiopathic hemolysis.

**Why immune‑complex deposits were ruled out**

- **Immunofluorescence studies were negative** for all classic immune‑complex components (IgG, IgM, IgA, C3, C1q).  
- **Electron microscopy** did not reveal the subendothelial/elevated electron‑dense deposits characteristic of post‑streptococcal, IgA or lupus glomerulonephritis.  
- The clinical picture (normal ANA and ANCA, negative stool PCR for STEC, negative complement C3/C4 in the acute phase) and the temporal relationship to a Capnocytophaga bacteremia were more consistent with complement activation rather than an antibody‑mediated glomerulopathy.

Thus, the renal biopsy findings—fibrin thrombi, ischemic glomeruli, ATN, scattered crescents, and absence of immune deposition—are the pathognomonic histologic hallmarks of TMA secondary to complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS) in this case.

### [Management] Discuss the rationale for initiating plasma exchange (PEX) before starting eculizumab in this patient, including its potential benefits and limitations in CM‑HUS.

**Why start plasma exchange (PEX) before eculizumab in this CM‑HUS patient?**

1. **Rapid haemodynamic stabilization while awaiting definitive therapy**  
   • In the acute phase, the patient had persistent MAHA, thrombocytopenia and anuria.  PEX removes circulating complement split‑products, soluble cytokines, and any pathogenic auto‑antibodies that may be contributing to endothelial injury.  This can transiently halt ongoing micro‑thrombi formation and improve organ perfusion.  In the case report, diuresis returned after the third PEX session—evidence that the procedure can quickly relieve microvascular obstruction.  

2. **Diagnostic clarification in a complex septic setting**  
   • Severe infection (C. canimorsus sepsis) can mimic or coexist with TMA.  PEX allows removal of plasma‑derived mediators that might mask or mimic traditional diagnostic criteria (e.g., hemolysis could be secondary to DIC).  By exchanging plasma, clinicians could obtain clear post‑exchange labs (e.g., schistocyte count, haptoglobin, LDH) and better assess the TMA’s activity before committing to lifelong eculizumab.  

3. **Avoidance of premature complement blockade during active bacteremia**  
   • Eculizumab disables terminal complement and increases risk of severe infection by encapsulated organisms (Neisseria, Capnocytophaga).  Initiating PEX first provided a “bridge” that allowed the organism to be eradicated (blood cultures negative) and antibiotics to be de‑escalated before complement inhibition.  This strategy aligns with the authors’ recommendation of waiting for culture clearance and providing prophylactic antibiotics and meningococcal vaccination before initiating eculizumab.  

4. **Logistical and safety advantages**  
   • For patients with limited access to rapid eculizumab induction, multiple PEX sessions are often more practicable and cost‑effective in the ICU until the therapeutic regimen is firmly established.  

**Potential benefits**

- **Short‑term haemodynamic and renal improvement** (e.g., resolution of anuria).  
- **Improved laboratory assessment** (clearer TMA markers).  
- **Temporary reduction of inflammatory mediators** that may perpetuate endothelial injury.  
- **Protection during the infectious phase** (by delaying terminal complement blockade).  

**Limitations in CM‑HUS**

- **No disease‑modifying effect**: PEX does not correct the underlying dysregulation of the alternative complement pathway; any benefit is transient.  
- **Potential delay in definitive therapy**: In purely CM‑HUS, early eculizumab is the mortality‑reducing intervention; unnecessary or prolonged PEX can postpone it.  
- **Risk of plasma‑related complications**: volume shifts, allo‑antibody formation, and cost.  
- **Not universally available**: requires apheresis equipment and expertise, which may not be present in all centres.  

In this patient, the team judiciously used PEX as a bridge, allowing withdrawal of antibiotics, confirmation of a negative culture, and acquisition of extended complement testing before commencing eculizumab.  The subsequent sustained haematologic remission and partial renal recovery confirm that, while PEX alone is insufficient, it can provide critical stabilization and diagnostic clarity in CM‑HUS complicated by active sepsis.

### [Management] What infectious risks are associated with eculizumab therapy, and how were these risks mitigated in the management of this patient?

**Infectious risks of eculizumab and how they were managed**

Eculizumab blocks terminal complement (C5→C5a/C5b) and therefore abolishes the killing of encapsulated organisms that rely on complement‑mediated opsonophagocytosis and membrane attack complex (MAC) formation. The most frequently reported fatal infections in patients receiving eculizumab are meningococcal sepsis (Neisseria meningitidis serogroups A, C, Y, W, and B) and other encapsulated bacteria (Haemophilus influenzae, Streptococcus pneumoniae). Capnocytophaga canimorsus, the causative agent in this case, is also an encapsulated gram‑negative rod that is traditionally susceptible to complement‑mediated killing. Consequently, initiating eculizumab in the setting of active Capnocytophaga bacteremia would theoretically increase the risk of uncontrolled infection and rapid deterioration.

**Risk mitigation strategy used in this patient**

1. **Timing of initiation**  
   * Eculizumab was started only after the patient’s blood cultures converted to negative. The case report states: “Following the second and third PEX sessions, the patient’s spontaneous diuresis returned… Following the first doses of meningococcal vaccines… eculizumab induction was initiated (900 mg iv per week for 4 weeks).” The authors explicitly note that the therapy was initiated after clearance of the bacteremia, thereby reducing the risk of an overwhelming infection when complement is blocked.

2. **Prophylactic vaccination**  
   * The patient received Menveo (serogroups A, C, Y, W) and Bexsero (serogroup B) prior to the first eculizumab dose. These vaccines cover all meningococcal serogroups that are most commonly implicated in eculizumab‑associated meningitis. Vaccination is a cornerstone of infection prevention in this setting.

3. **Antimicrobial prophylaxis**  
   * Prophylactic amoxicillin‑clavulanate (Apocillin) was continued after catheter removal and during the first 4 weeks of eculizumab. The authors note that “prophylactic Apocillin was given, as the vaccines had not been administered 2 weeks before starting complement blockade.” This strategy protects against both Neisseria and Capnocytophaga, the latter being the original offending organism.

4. **Close clinical surveillance**  
   * The patient was monitored for clinical signs of infection (fever, leukocytosis, hemodynamic instability) and lab markers (CRP, procalcitonin, repeat cultures). The rapid improvement of inflammatory parameters after antibiotics and the absence of new febrile episodes during the eculizumab induction phase suggest successful infection control.

5. **Sequential therapy**  
   * Plasma exchange (PEX) was used initially to reduce circulating complement fragments and inflammatory mediators while awaiting a definitive diagnosis. PEX also served as a bridge while the bacteremia was being cleared, allowing a safe transition to eculizumab once cultures were negative.

**Take‑home point**

When giving eculizumab in a patient with a recent or ongoing infection by an encapsulated organism (such as C. canimorsus), ensure that the bacteremia has been eradicated, administer meningococcal vaccines promptly, give appropriate antibiotic prophylaxis, and monitor closely for breakthrough infection. The management in this case exemplifies these precautions and demonstrates that, with rigorous infection control, complement blockade can be safely initiated even when a recent severe infection has occurred.

### [Pathophysiology] Describe how the alternative pathway activity assay (AH50) result influenced the interpretation of complement dysregulation in this patient, and why the result might have been affected by earlier treatments.

**Answer**

The alternative‑pathway functional assay (AH 50) was the key laboratory data that helped us distinguish between active complement‑mediated damage and a transient depletion of complement caused by treatment.  
* **Interpretation of the AH 50 result** – The assay measures the amount of haemolysis that occurs in sensitised donor erythrocytes when the patient’s serum is added, reflecting the capacity of the alternative pathway to generate C3 convertase. In this patient the AH 50 was only 6 % (normal > 80 %). This marked reduction indicated that the alternative pathway was either exhausted or inhibited. Because factor H, I and B were normal and no anti‑factor H antibody was detected, the low activity was interpreted as a functional shutdown of the pathway rather than an inherited loss‑of‑function mutation. Thus, the result supported a diagnosis of complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS) rather than a primary thrombotic microangiopathy that would not involve the alternative pathway.

* **Effect of earlier treatments on the AH 50** – The patient had received three sessions of plasma exchange (PEX) with Octaplas and one dose of eculizumab before the AH 50 was measured. Both interventions can profoundly blunt the alternative‑pathway activity measured in vitro:

  1. **Plasma exchange** removes soluble complement regulators and active fragments from circulation, temporarily lowering the functional alternative‑pathway pool. Because the assay uses a patient serum sample, any replacement plasma that still contains complement components can actually increase activity, but the net effect after several exchanges is usually a transient suppression until the patient’s own complement pool re‑establishes.
  2. **Eculizumab** blocks the terminal C5 cleavage step, preventing formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC). While this blockade does not directly inhibit C3 convertase, chronic inhibition reduces downstream consumption of C3 and may feedback to decrease upstream alternative‑pathway activity, reflected as a low AH 50.

Therefore, the low AH 50 obtained eight days after the last PEX session and four days after the first eculizumab dose is most consistent with treatment‑induced suppression rather than permanent genetic deficiency. The clinical picture—rapid improvement after complement blockade and lack of pathogenic complement variants—confirms that the reduced AH 50 was a transient phenomenon secondary to therapeutic interventions, not an intrinsic complement dysregulation.

### [Management] If this patient had shown pathogenic variants in complement factor H or factor I, how might that have changed her long‑term management plan?

**Long‑term management if pathogenic factor H or factor I variants had been present**

Identification of a disease‑causing variant in *CFH* or *CFI* would have shifted the patient’s long‑term strategy from a “watch‑and‑wait” approach to a *predetermined lifelong* complement blockade policy.  
1. **Durability of therapy** – In patients with a confirmed complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS) predisposition, the risk of relapse after drug discontinuation is high (≈ 30 – 40 % in the first year). Therefore, guidelines recommend maintenance eculizumab (1200 mg IV q2 weeks) until the patient’s renal function is stable for at least 12 months and there is no evidence of residual TMA activity.  
2. **Early discontinuation not advised** – Even after renal recovery, the presence of a pathogenic *CFH* or *CFI* allele obliges continuation of therapy, because partial pediatric‑style “taper” or short courses have proven unsafe in this genetic population.  
3. **Peri‑transplant considerations** – If the patient progresses to end‑stage renal disease and requires dialysis or transplant, the complement gene defect mandates that any kidney graft be managed under the same lifelong eculizumab regimen. Pre‑transplant screening (including anti‑factor H antibody testing) and prophylactic vaccination remain mandatory, but the drug is not stopped merely for transplantation.  
4. **Monitoring strategy** – With a known pathogenic variant, the clinician would schedule quarterly laboratory reviews (CBC, LDH, sC5b‑9, complement functional assays) and biannual renal imaging to detect subclinical relapse.  
5. **Patient education** – Patients with inherited complement defects carry a higher burden of infection risk with eculizumab. The clinician would counsel on early signs of infection, maintain strict vaccination schedules (meningococcal and other encapsulated organisms), and consider long‑term antibiotic prophylaxis that may be shorter than the 30‑day course used in our infection‑triggered case.

**In our patient** no pathogenic *CFH* or *CFI* variants were found, so the plan is to continue maintenance eculizumab for at least 4 months and reassess renal function; discontinuation is being considered only if the kidney stabilizes for ≥12 months with no clinical or laboratory evidence of TMA. If a pathogenic variant had been identified, the maintenance phase would have begun immediately after the first dose and would likely become lifelong.

### [Clinical Pearls] What are the clinical and laboratory criteria for diagnosing meningococcal risk when starting a complement inhibitor, and how were these criteria applied before initiating eculizumab?

**Clinical Pearls – When to Screen for Meningococcal Risk Before Starting a Complement Inhibitor**

**Bottom line:**  
Before commencing eculizumab (or any terminal‑complement blocker) in a patient with complement‑mediated TMA, you must ensure the patient is **protected from encapsulated organisms**—most importantly *Neisseria meningitidis*. The guidelines require:

1. **MenACWY vaccination** (Menveo®) **≥2 weeks before** the first dose.  
2. **MenB vaccination** (Bexsero®) **≥2 weeks before** the first dose (unless the patient already has a documented MenB response).  
3. **Antibiotic prophylaxis** (e.g., penicillin V or amoxicillin) until the vaccination series is complete.  
4. **Baseline blood cultures** and a **clear antibiotic strategy** if the patient is bacteremic or at risk of infection.

These steps are grounded in the case report:  
- The patient received **Menveo** (serogroups A, C, Y, W) and **Bexsero** (serogroup B) **before** the first eculizumab dose.  
- **Penicillin prophylaxis** (Apocillin) was started because the vaccines had not been given >2 weeks prior.  
- Blood cultures were negative and antibiotics were de‑escalated to **ampicillin** (safe in a complement‑deficient host) before complement blockade began.

**Why the timing matters**

Eculizumab blocks C5, disabling formation of the membrane‑attack complex (MAC) that is essential for clearing encapsulated organisms. A 2‑week interval allows the host to mount a protective antibody response to the kexin‑produced conjugate or protein subunits in the vaccine before the complement system is shut down. Starting the drug too early, while vaccines are still immunogenic or before the antibody titres rise, could expose the patient to fulminant meningococcal sepsis.

**Practical checklist for the attending:**

| Step | What to Do | Timing Relative to First Eculizumab Dose |
|------|------------|------------------------------------------|
| 1 | Administer MenACWY (Menveo) | ≥ 14 days prior |
| 2 | Administer MenB (Bexsero) | ≥ 14 days prior |
| 3 | Verify serotype‑specific antibody titres if available | Not mandatory, but useful in high‑risk scenarios |
| 4 | Start antibiotic prophylaxis (e.g., penicillin V 500 mg PO q12 h) | Concurrent with first vaccine dose; continue until second vaccine dose is given |
| 5 | Obtain blood cultures | At first vaccine dose; repeat if clinically indicated |
| 6 | Document all dates and doses | Essential for compliance and future surveillance |

**How the criteria were applied in this case**

1. **Vaccination schedule**: The patient received both Menveo and Bexsero **before** any eculizumab infusion. The case report notes “following the second and third PEX sessions, the patient’s spontaneous diuresis returned. Following the first doses of meningococcal vaccines—Menveo and Bexsero—eculizumab induction was initiated.”  
2. **Prophylaxis**: “Apocillin was given, as the vaccines had not been administered 2 weeks before starting complement blockade.”  
3. **Infection control**: Blood cultures were negative and antibiotics were de‑escalated to a safe regimen (ampicillin) before eculizumab started, acknowledging the risk that eculizumab compromises clearance of *Capnocytophaga* and *Neisseria*.  

By strictly following the 2‑week vaccination window, verifiable prophylaxis, and ensuring a sterile or adequately treated infection state, the team minimized meningococcal risk while initiating life‑saving complement inhibition.
