Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# Kidney Biopsy in the Setting of Sepsis‑Associated TMA – What the Pathologist Says

## Introduction: Why a Biopsy Matters When Sepsis Meets TMA

Sepsis can wreak havoc on the microvasculature, and when the clinical picture drifts toward microangiopathic hemolysis, thrombocytopenia, and kidney failure, a renal biopsy can be the compass that directs us from “sepsis‑driven acute tubular necrosis” to “complement‑mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (CM‑HUS)”. In the case of the dog‑bite patient described in the clinical report, the biopsy was the linchpin that convinced ICU and nephrology teams to abandon plasma‑exchange alone and launch eculizumab.

## Histologic Hallmarks of the Trifecta

On light microscopy, the specimen was a story in thirds:

1. **Acute tubular necrosis (ATN)** – The classic “slit‐like” cytoplasmic vacuolization, loss of brush border, and granular casts that dominate the tubular lumen. ATN is almost the automatic response to shock, DIC, and sepsis‑induced hypoperfusion.  
2. **Microvascular TMA lesions** – Small arterioles and capillaries packed with fibrin thrombi, erythrocyte aggregates, and endothelial swelling. In this patient, the arterial and glomerular capillaries showed subendothelial widening with hyaline thrombi, a hallmark of TMA.  
3. **Crescentic changes** – A minority of glomeruli displayed parietal epithelial cell proliferation leading to what the histologists call crescents. In infectious TMA, crescents are usually **non‑immune complex** and reflect endothelial injury rather than deposition of IgG or IgA.

The coexistence of ATN and TMA is not unexpected in a septic patient; the key is that the TMA lesion is **not masked** but rather superimposed on the ATN.

## Immunofluorescence: The “clean bill of health” for immune complexes

The immunofluorescence study was negative for IgG, IgA, IgM, C3, and C1q deposition. In a setting where the differential includes:

- **Immune‑complex glomerulonephritis** (e.g., post–streptococcal GN, lupus nephritis) – would show linear or granular deposition.  
- **Complement‑mediated (typical or atypical) HUS** – usually **no immune deposits** because the pathogenesis is endothelial complement overactivation, not immune complex deposition.

Thus, the clean immunofluorescence strongly argued against an underlying immune‑complex disease and pulled the needle toward a complement‑driven process.

## Electron microscopy: Checking the “neonatal” pattern

While the clinical report did not present EM images, it noted that “no evidence of primary glomerulopathy” was found. In practice, EM would reveal subendothelial widening, “humps”, or electron‑dense deposits in immune complexes, none of which were seen. Moreover, the failure to show an electron‑dense “hump” pattern further supports the absence of immune‑complex injury.

## Clinical Take‑Better Points

- **When sepsis and TMA overlap, a biopsy can differentiate reversible ATN from irreversible endothelial injury requiring complement blockade.**  
- **Negative IgG/IgA/IgM/C3/C1q on immunofluorescence is a red flag for CM‑HUS versus immune‑complex GN.**  
- **Crescents in the context of negative immune deposits are best interpreted as endothelial proliferation rather than immune‑mediated crescents.**  
- **Early kidney biopsy (within 7–10 days of symptom onset) in sepsis‑associated TMA is feasible and can dramatically alter management.**  
- **Always coordinate with the treating team before biopsy: the decision to start eculizumab hinges on biopsy confirmation of TMA without immune deposits.**  

## Bottom Line: The Biopsy Was the Decision‑Making Tool

In this dog‑bite case, the biopsy’s ability to separate ATN, TMA, and crescentic disease, coupled with a clean immunofluorescence canvas, was the fulcrum that tipped the balance toward complement blockade. It underscores the pathologist’s central role in multimodal decision‑making when the bedside is filled with steroids, plasma‑exchange, and antibiotics.
