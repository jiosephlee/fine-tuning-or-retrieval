Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# Capnocytophaga canimorsus: The Silent Instigator of a Catastrophic TMA

## The Surprise in the Socket of a Dog Bite  
Someone gets a dog bite, reviews the clinic’s list of “bites that bite back,” and then—chain‑reaction. A 45‑year‑old woman reports a mild upper‑lip laceration, a tetanus booster, and a prescription of phenoxymethylpenicillin that never makes it to her tongue. Five days later she is wheezing, hypoglycaemic, and spending a night in the ICU with a septic picture that resembles early DIC. Three weeks in, she’s dialysis‑dependent, her hemoglobin has dropped faster than a heart attack can take a pulse, and schistocytes appear on the smear. The final puzzle piece? Capnocytophaga *canimorsus* blood culture.  

What makes this case worth a FOAM‑style post isn’t just the rare pathogen; it’s how a “fastidious” microbe can *activate* the complement cascade so liberally that an otherwise healthy adult spirals into complement‑mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM‑HUS). Below we dissect the microbiology, the immunologic fireworks, and the clinical decision points that turned a septic dog‑bite into a TMA crisis.

---

## Microbiology 101: “Fastidious” but not *Fast*  
- **Gram‑negative, capsule‑producing rod** found in dogs’ and cats’ oropharynx.  
- **Growth lag**: requires 48‑72 h for colonies and is exquisitely sensitive to sub‑optimal media or transport conditions.  
- **Why it drops in labs**: routine cultures often miss it; real‑time sequencing and MALDI‑TOF are now the workhorses for identification.  
- **Beta‑lactam susceptibility is broad**, but the capsule can grant selective resistance to complement lysis.

### A Hidden Capsule: The Double‑Edged Sword
The bacterial capsule protects *C. canimorsus* from phagocytosis and complement‑mediated lysis. Yet, in the face of an overwhelming infection, the capsule’s very presence drives the alternative complement pathway into overdrive, tipping the balance toward endothelial injury and TMA.

---

## Complement: The Perfect Match‑Made‑in‑Hell for CM‑HUS  
1. **Binding triggers**: bacterial lipopolysaccharide (LPS) can activate the lectin and alternative pathways via properdin, factor H binding, etc.  
2. **Unchecked activation**: In individuals with partial deficiencies in regulators (factor H, I, B) or in *susceptible* hosts, the cascade runs unchecked.  
3. **Endothelial damage**: C5a and MAC formation cause microvascular irritation → platelet aggregation, schistocyte formation, acute tubular necrosis (ATN).  

**Key lab pearls**  
- Typical complement panel (C3/C4) can be *normal* during CM‑HUS attacks.  
- Functional assays (AH50, AP50) or genetic panels are the real first‑line work‑up.  
- **ADAMTS13** >10 % essentially rules out TTP; ∼43 % in our patient confirmed this.

### In One Sentence:  
Think of *C. canimorsus* as the “volcano” that can erupt the complement pathway just fine when the host’s safety valves (regulators) are compromised—yours or silently inherited.

---

## the Clinical Dilemma: When to Pull the Plug on Infection vs. Complement

| Question | What the data says | What you do |
|----------|-------------------|-------------|
| **Is the infection still septic?** | Positive blood cultures 7 days after presentation. | **Wait until clearance** if possible. |
| **Is the patient septic?** | Stabilizing vitals, resolving lactate, no new organ failures. | **Hold eculizumab** until cultures are negative. |
| **Do we need plasma exchange?** | ADAMTS13 normal, TTP unlikely. | **Use PEX as bridge** to complement blocker; remove circulating complement fragments & autoantibodies temporarily. |
| **When to start eculizumab?** | Renal function improving after PEX but MAHA persists. | **Initiate immediately** after cultures clear, with meningococcal vaccines and antibiotic prophylaxis. |

---

## Take‑Home Pearls

- **Capnocytophaga canimorsus is a fastidious pathogen whose capsule can both protect the bacteria and fan the flames of complement activation, leading to CM‑HUS even in immunocompetent patients.**  
- **Early recognition of CM‑HUS requires a high index of suspicion for TMA in septic patients who develop persistent thrombocytopenia, hemolysis (↓haptoglobin, ↑LDH), and worsening AKI.**  
- **Normal C3/C4 does not rule out CM‑HUS; functional tests or genetic panels add diagnostic clarity.**  
- **Plasma exchange is useful as a temporary stabilizer but does not curb the underlying complement dysregulation.**  
- **Eculizumab improves both hematologic and renal outcomes, but must be delayed until the infection is cleared and life‑saving vaccinations and prophylaxis are in place.**  
- **Use the PLASMIC score to triage for TTP—scores <4 effectively rule it out and focus work‑up on CM‑HUS.**  
- **Infectious triggers of TMA aren’t limited to the usual suspects; consider zoonotic pathogens like *C. canimorsus* in any septic patient with unexplained TMA.**

### Closing Thought  
When a dog bite looks routine, remember that the bite may be a *gateway* for a pathogen that can flip the immune system into “overdrive.” In the senior emergency physician’s playbook, “watch for sepsis” must now also include “watch for TMA.” Prompt recognition and early complement blockade can turn a potential “death spiral” into a survivable outcome—just ask the dog‑bite survivor who now drinks tea with her renal‑function‑lowered kidney; her story is a testament that with the right timing, even the quietest pathogen can trigger the loudest catastrophe.
