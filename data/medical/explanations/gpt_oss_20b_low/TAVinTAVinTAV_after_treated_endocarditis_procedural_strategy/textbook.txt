Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

## 1. Clinical Presentation and Initial Management

### Patient demographics, comorbidities and risk scores  
The patient was a 78‑year‑old man with a complex cardiopulmonary and vascular history. His major comorbidities included arterial hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and a multi‑vessel coronary artery disease that had been addressed with percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) of the right coronary artery (2009), left anterior descending artery (2010), recurrent LAD intervention (2012), and circumflex artery (2014). He also had a third‑degree atrioventricular block for which a permanent pacemaker was implanted in 2014, and recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding since 2022.  

Baseline surgical risk assessment via the EuroSCORE II yielded a value of 27.36 %, placing him in the high‑risk category for conventional surgery. The STS‑PROM score was not reported, but the clinical profile (multiple comorbidities, prior sternotomy‑free PCI, and high frailty indices) indicated a prohibitive surgical risk. These data supported an initial transcatheter strategy.

### History of aortic valve disease and initial 2014 Lotus TAVI: indications and procedural details  
The patient’s aortic valve disease manifested as progressive severe calcific stenosis. In 2014, he was evaluated for severe aortic stenosis with a valve area of <0.8 cm², mean transvalvular gradient ≥40 mm Hg, and a reduced left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction (EF) of 54 %. His high surgical risk, coupled with the presence of multiple coronary stents and a pacemaker lead, made TAVI a preferable option. 

A 27 mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific) was deployed under sedation. The Lotus is a controller‑balloon valve with a self‑expanding nitinol frame and a fully retrievable balloon, designed to sit subannularly and preserve coronary access. Deployment was uncomplicated, and the patient recovered with no immediate complications. Post‑procedure transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated a mean gradient of 22 mm Hg, a valve area of 1.85 cm², and a preserved EF of 54 %.

### Early postoperative course and first valve performance  
For the first 2–3 years following implantation, the patient remained asymptomatic. Serial office visits revealed stable echocardiographic parameters: mean gradient around 20–25 mm Hg, valve area >1.7 cm², and EF maintained at 54 %. Serial computed tomography (CT) scans were not performed routinely during this period as the valve was functioning nominally and the patient was asymptomatic.

### Clinical decline in 2021: symptom progression, echocardiographic findings, and the decision to re‑intervene  
In December 2021, the patient reported worsening exertional dyspnoea and reduced exercise tolerance (walking 200–300 m). A repeat TTE showed a mean gradient of 35 mm Hg, valve area of 0.9 cm², and a decline in EF to 42 %. These findings were consistent with progressive bioprosthetic valve degeneration (structural valve deterioration). The EuroSCORE II remained high, but the clinical picture—relevant symptoms plus hemodynamic evidence of severe stenosis—warranted intervention. 

An evaluation of the coronary anatomy by coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) showed no critical lesions adjacent to the prosthesis, and the late systolic expansion of the Lotus frame appeared preserved. There were no signs of endocarditis or annular abscess. The heart team, comprising interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, and imaging specialists, agreed that a valve‑in‑valve (TAV‑in‑TAV) approach would be the least invasive, with acceptable procedural risk.

### Pre‑procedure assessment for TAV‑in‑TAV: imaging modalities, hemodynamic thresholds, and risk stratification  
The pre‑procedure work‑up incorporated multimodality imaging. Echocardiography confirmed severe prosthesis‑related stenosis and moderate regurgitation. Dual‑source CT was performed to evaluate the anatomy of the annulus, the distance from the coronary ostia to the valve annulus, and the lumen size of the existing Lotus valve. The annulus was measured at 26.5 mm by multiphase CT with a virtual transcatheter heart valve sizing algorithm, yielding a nominal 23 mm sizing for the Evolut R valve, which was selected for the valve‑in‑valve procedure. The minimum lumen diameter of the Lotus valve was 14 mm, providing sufficient space for the new device. Coronary ostial height exceeded 13 mm, leaving a safe margin for coronary flow.

Hemodynamic thresholds were based on the ESC/EACTS consensus: a mean gradient >40 mm Hg or valve area <1.0 cm² in a patient with symptoms or progressive LV dysfunction indicated a need for reintervention. The risk stratification incorporated the Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) score (not available), the pre‑existing prosthesis frame, and the presence of a pacemaker. Given the high surgical risk and favorable transcatheter anatomics, the decision to proceed with a TAV‑in‑TAV was made. The patient was counseled on the potential for coronary obstruction, patient‑prosthesis mismatch, and the need for careful frame placement to preserve future coronary access. Documentation of informed consent included an explanation of these specific risks and the strategy of using a self‑expanding valve (Medtronic Evolut) with a low profile.

## 2. TAV‑in‑TAV and the Development of Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis

The patient’s first transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) in 2014 placed a 27 mm Lotus valve in a native aortic root. Five years later, progressive structural valve deterioration (SVD) manifested as a rise in mean transvalvular pressure gradient to 83 mm Hg and a decline in left‑ventricular ejection fraction (EF) from 54 % to 42 %. The clinical picture prompted a second transcatheter procedure—a TAV‑in‑TAV in 2022—with implantation of a 23 mm Medtronic Evolut self‑expanding valve within the original Lotus frame. Although this redo procedure initially restored valve function, it was accompanied by significant procedural complications and ultimately set the stage for infective prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) a few months later.

### 2.1 2022 Evolution of the TAV‑in‑TAV Procedure

**Access and Sizing.**  
The Evolut valve was delivered through a transfemoral approach. Pre‑procedural computed tomography (CT) measured the sinotubular junction at 28 mm and the annulus perimeter at 22 mm (area 421 mm²), favoring a 23 mm Evolut model according to the manufacturer’s sizing charts. The Lotus valve’s radial frame had already occupied the annulus, leaving a residual annular area compliant with the Evolut’s lithographic depth. The surgeons favored a supra‑annular design to mitigate patient‑prosthesis mismatch (PPM) given the patient’s small derived effective orifice area (EOA) in the first valve.

**Procedural Course.**  
- *Anesthesia and Anticoagulation*: Local anesthesia with conscious sedation was chosen; unfractionated heparin was administered to achieve an activated clotting time >250 s.
- *Delivery*: A 14 Fr access sheath was inserted via the common femoral artery. A 6 Fr multipurpose catheter was advanced into the aortic root for coaxial alignment.
- *Predilution*: A 18 mm balloon was inflated under rapid ventricular pacing (180 bpm) to maximal tolerable pressure, facilitating valve seating by temporarily reducing left‑ventricular outflow resistance.
- *Deployment*: The Evolut valve was positioned immediately above the Lotus stent frame, ensuring that the new valve’s inner leaflets would sit within the existing frame’s luminal space. Fluoroscopic markers on the Evolut frame were used to align commissures, and the valve was partially recapturable – a feature exploited if the centricity was suboptimal.
- *Release*: Incremental torque was applied until the valve was fully deployed, with the waist of the Evolut contacting the outer knuckle of the Lotus.

**Complications.**  
A pulsatile femoral hematoma and a superficial femoral pseudoaneurysm developed during or after the procedure, necessitating open‑surgical repair. The vascular complication was likely related to the large sheath size relative to the caliber of the patient’s left femoral artery, underscored by the presence of pre‑existing peripheral vascular disease.

### 2.2 Post‑Operative Ventricular Remodeling and Echocardiographic Response

Immediately after the TAV‑in‑TAV, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated:
- A mean transvalvular gradient of 18 mm Hg, a marked improvement from the pre‑operative 83 mm Hg.
- EF increased from 42 % to 54 %, reflecting improved forward stroke volume and reduced afterload.
- The aortic valve area (AVA) calculated by continuity equation rose to 1.5 cm².

These changes corroborated the procedural success of restoring valvular competence. The LV wall thickness and end‑diastolic volume showed modest regression when repeated at follow‑up imaging three months later, indicative of reverse remodeling secondary to reduced pressure overload.

### 2.3 Presentation of Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis (PVE)

**Clinical Features.**  
Three months after the Evolut implantation, the patient developed a low‑grade fever (up to 38.2 °C), malaise, and worsening dyspnea on exertion. Laboratory work revealed leukocytosis (12 × 10⁹/L) and a mild elevation of pro‑calcitonin. He also reported a new “spicy” chest sensation during physical activity, suggestive of sub‑aortic obstruction and/or embolic phenomena.

**Microbiology.**  
Blood cultures, drawn at admission, grew Enterococcus faecalis in two separate sets after 48 hours. Enterococcus is a common pathogen in nosocomial and prosthetic valve infections, especially in older adults with comorbidities such as diabetes and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.

**Diagnostic Work‑up.**  
- *Transesophageal Echocardiography (TEE)*: A mobile echo density measuring 7 × 5 mm was visualized on the non‑coronary cusp of the Evolut valve. No perivalvular abscess or significant paravalvular leak was evident. The density was mobile, attached to the leaflet, and had imaging characteristics consistent with a vegetation.
- *Multislice Computed Tomography (MSCT)*: The CT scan confirmed the presence of an echo density that corresponded to the same region on TEE, and showed no new structural damage to the valve or root. Importantly, the anterior aortic root and sinuses of Valsalva remained intact without evidence of abscess formation.
- *Laboratory Confirmation*: Serial blood cultures remained positive for E. faecalis on days 3 and 5, supporting the diagnosis of definite PVE according to modified Duke criteria.

### 2.4 Antimicrobial Therapy Regimen

The patient received definitive therapy based on susceptibility profiles and standard guidelines for Enterococcus faecalis endocarditis:
- **Intravenous ampicillin** 2 g every 4 hours for 6 weeks, intermittently combined with a beta‑lactamase inhibitor (e.g., amoxicillin) to counter potential beta‑lactamase producers.
- **Oral ciprofloxacin** 500 mg twice daily for an additional 2 weeks as a step‑down strategy, chosen for its excellent bioavailability and CNS penetration, which is relevant given the possibility of embolic complications.
- **Adjunctive gentamicin** was avoided due to the patient’s advanced age and reduced renal function, which increased nephrotoxicity risk.

**Criteria for Infection Resolution** included:
1. Sustained negative blood cultures (three consecutive sets drawn over 48 hours).
2. Clinical afebrile state for >48 hours.
3. Normalization of inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein <5 mg/L, pro‑calcitonin <0.2 µg/L).
4. Absence of new valvular lesions on repeat TEE and MSCT.

Upon meeting these criteria, antimicrobial therapy was concluded, and the patient was discharged on the oral follow‑up protocol for a total of eight weeks.

### 2.5 Impact of PVE on Valve Biomechanics and Reintervention Decision‑Making

**Valvular Pathophysiology.**  
The vegetation’s attachment fragmented portions of the prosthetic leaflets, creating asymmetrical loading that accelerated leaflet scarring and calcification. Repeated cycles of infection, inflammation, and mechanical stress contributed to:
- **Leaflet thickening** and reduced mobility (as seen on TTE with a mean gradient of 35 mm Hg post‑infection).
- **Micro‑fractures** in the stent frame, compromising radial support and allowing increased leaflet fenestration.
- **Structural valve degeneration (SVD)** compounded by ongoing immunologic assault, resulting in leaflet tearing and regurgitation.

**Hemodynamic Consequences.**  
The PVE episode precipitated significant bioprosthetic valve restenosis, evidenced by a mean gradient of 35 mm Hg and an AVA of 0.8 cm². Unfortunately, the coexistence of moderate‑to‑severe paravalvular regurgitation worsened the patient’s symptoms and left‑ventricular function (EF fell to 42 % during the acute phase).

**Reintervention Strategy.**  
Given the fistful resolution of the infection and the emergent functional deterioration, the heart team considered the feasibility of a third transcatheter valve on the basis of:
- *Anatomical Re‑assessment*: MSCT confirmed a residual lumen within the Evolut stent of only 13.7 mm, insufficient for a new valve to provide optimal function without severe PPM.
- *Surgical Risk*: EuroSCORE II of 27.36 % and a history of complex coronary disease (three‑vessel disease with prior percutaneous interventions) rendered surgical replacement too high risk.
- *Long‑term Prognosis*: The anticipated burden of recurrent infection and the likelihood of further prosthetic failure warranted an aggressive but minimally invasive approach.

The team elected for a CT‑guided, strategy‑driven TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV, selecting an Acurate neo2 valve to exploit its supra‑annular leaflets, low frame height, and alignment capabilities. The pre‑procedural imaging identified adequate coronary ostial height (≥18 mm) and sufficient sinus dimension (≥30 mm) to allow high implantation and preserve coronary access. Post‑deployment, the patient’s symptoms resolved, and one‑year follow‑up confirmed durable valve function.

---

Through this case, we see how a seemingly straightforward TAV‑in‑TAV procedure can evolve into a complex cascade of complications, including a fatal infectious sequela. The pathogenesis of PVE reveals how infection can accelerate structural valve dysfunction, necessitating meticulous early evaluation and tailored reintervention strategies in a high‑risk cohort.

## 3. Pre‑procedural Imaging and Strategy Development for TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV

In complex serial transcatheter valve procedures, the balance between procedural feasibility and long‑term valve performance hinges on meticulous pre‑procedural imaging and a tailored implantation strategy. The third intervention in this case was guided by multidetector computed tomography (MDCT) in conjunction with three‑dimensional (3‑D) virtual reconstruction, enabling a systematic assessment of the remaining native annulus, the two existing transcatheter valve frames, and the coronary anatomy. The imaging data were then translated into concrete procedural decisions: valve selection, implantation depth, commissural alignment, and coronary protection measures.

### Multislice CT parameters

The pre‑procedural MDCT acquisition was performed in the electrocardiographic‑triggered, low‑contrast phase (phase 75 % of the cardiac cycle) to capture the annular dimensions in diastole. Key measurements included:

- **Annular sizing**: The true annular area and perimeter were calculated from the end‑diastolic cross‑sections, allowing selection of a prosthesis with a diameter within the recommended 0.5–1 mm oversizing range for self‑expanding devices. The 27‑mm Lotus and 23‑mm Evolut landmarks were used to define the remaining functional annulus, which measured 21.4 mm in diameter.
- **Valve‑to‑coronary distances**: The distance from the outer edge of the outermost (Evolut) frame to each coronary ostium was measured at the nadir of the sinus. The left coronary artery ostium lay 7.3 mm above the outer leaflet of the Evolut frame; the right coronary ostium was 5.6 mm distant.
- **Sinus dimensions**: The sinus of Valsalva diameters were 33–35 mm, adequate to accommodate a third valve without risking coronary obstruction when a high‑implant strategy was applied.
- **Coronary ostial heights**: Heights from the nadir of the annulus to the coronary ostia were 18.2 mm (LCA) and 17.3 mm (RCA). These values informed the safety margin for the anticipated neo‑skirt created by the Acurate neo2.

These metrics provided a quantitative framework for deciding the most appropriate prosthesis size, the required implantation depth, and the risk of coronary compromise.

### Assessment of overlapping valve frames and expected neo‑skirt geometry

Overlaying the CT images of the two existing transcatheter valves revealed that the Lotus prosthesis had a fully expanded nitinol frame, while the Evolut valve was partially buried within the native annulus. Virtual 3‑D reconstructions were used to estimate the cumulative frame thickness and the predicted skirt that the new valve would generate upon expansion. The neo‑skirt—formed by the new valve’s upper stent mesh and the underlying Evolut stent—was projected to conform to the shape of the existing Evolut rim, thereby limiting the risk of a roof‑like obstruction of the coronary ostia. The modelling also demonstrated that the neo‑skirt would lie below the coronary ostia, keeping at least a 5 mm clearance when the Acurate neo2 was deployed at a high position.

### Integration of 3‑D modeling and fluoroscopic landmarks for commissural alignment

Because the new device’s commissures must align with those of the pre‑existing valve to preserve coronary access, the team used CT‑derived fluoroscopic markers. The first step was to identify the relative positions of the Evolut commissures on the mid‑annular plane. Reference dots were placed on the CT surface corresponding to the projected positions of the Evolut commissures. During the procedure, fluoroscopic C‑arm positioning was calibrated so that the same dot patterns were visible, allowing the operators to align the Acurate neo2 commissures with the Evolut landmarks. A short‑axis view at the aortic annulus served as a key orientation guide; matching the three commissural marks ensured that the neo‑skirt would be centred over the coronary ostia and that future coronary engagement would be feasible.

### Choice of the Acurate neo2

The Acurate neo2 was selected for several complementary features:

- **Self‑expanding, supra‑annular leaflets**: The supra‑annular design yields a larger effective orifice area (EOA) in a constrained annulus, mitigating the risk of patient‑prosthesis mismatch (PPM), which was a concern given the already reduced lumen (minimum 13.7 mm) after the Evolut implantation.
- **Low frame height** (≈10 mm): A shorter stent profile reduces protrusion into the sinuses of Valsalva, thereby maintaining coronary clearance and minimizing the neo‑skirt height.
- **Facilitated commissural alignment**: The manufacturer’s commissural alignment slots correspond to the 3‑D imaging template, enabling precise positioning relative to the underlying Evolut commissures.
- **Modest radial force**: Compared to balloon‑expandable valves, the lower radial force decreases the likelihood of damaging the nested Evolut frame or creating significant radial mismatch that could exacerbate PPM.

The device’s size S (nominal 23 mm) matched the annular dimensions and projected EOA when deployed at a high position.

### Pre‑implantation planning: balloon pre‑dilation, rapid pacing strategy, and high‑implantation targets

A 22 × 40 mm balloon was chosen for pre‑dilation based on the minimal lumen diameter (13.7 mm) measured on CT. Rapid ventricular pacing at 180 beats/min was used to temporarily reduce cardiac output and create a low‑gradient state during balloon inflation, thereby minimizing the risk of aortic regurgitation and limiting balloon rupture.

High‑implantation targets were defined by aligning the lower edge of the Acurate basket with the outer ring of the Evolut leaflets, effectively “stacking” the new valve on top of the existing one without encroaching on the coronary ostia. This strategy also prevented leaflet folding and created a more favorable central flow pathway, as visualized in the pre‑procedural 3‑D model.

### Contingency planning for coronary obstruction and haemodynamic failure

The procedural plan included several contingencies:

1. **Coronary protection**: A jailed wire was placed in the left main coronary artery via radial access before balloon inflation, allowing immediate access if sudden obstruction occurred.
2. **Rapid deployment and post‑dilation**: The operators prepared larger‑diameter balloons (20 × 40 mm) for post‑dilatation should the mean gradient remain high or the valve show suboptimal expansion.
3. **Valve‑in‑valve fracture**: Though not pursued, the presence of a high‑pressure balloon and a compatible valve allowed the potential for bioprosthetic valve fracture (BAVFr) should the Evolut frame impede effective expansion.
4. **Defining bailout thresholds**: If the post‑deployment mean gradient exceeded 45 mm Hg or if the EOA fell below 0.9 cm², the team would have considered an additional valve implantation or surgical referral, despite the high surgical risk.

By integrating multimodal imaging with a detailed strategy, the team achieved a successful third transcatheter aortic valve implantation that preserved coronary flow, maximised effective orifice area, and addressed the patient’s severe prosthetic valve dysfunction without compromising procedural safety.

## 4. Technical Execution and Immediate Post‑procedure Assessment

The third transcatheter aortic valve implantation was meticulously planned and executed with the overarching goal of preserving coronary flow, achieving an adequate effective orifice area, and minimizing the risk of valve‑patient mismatch (PPM). The entire procedure adhered to Valve Academic Research Consortium‑3 (VARC‑3) recommendations for intervention, imaging, and outcome reporting.

### 4.1 Access strategy, anesthesia, and hemodynamic monitoring

Because the patient had a heavily pre‑stented aortic root and a history of large–femoral femoral arterial complications, a dual‑access approach was adopted. A left femoral arterial sheath was inserted for delivery of the valve system and for rapid ventricular pacing, while a left radial sheath provided an alternate route for coronary interrogation and protection if needed. Light sedation with conscious‑patient monitoring was chosen to facilitate intra‑procedural hemodynamic assessment and rapid communication with the imaging team. Continuous arterial pressure monitoring and invasive left ventricular pressure tracing guided pacing rates and ensured prompt recognition of any hemodynamic instability during rapid pacing.

### 4.2 Guidewire navigation, balloon pre‑dilation, and coronary assessment

A 0.035” Safari guidewire was coaxially advanced across the diseased valve and positioned in the left ventricle over a 0.014” support wire, ensuring stable coaxiality and minimizing the risk of balloon shearing. Balloon pre‑dilation was performed under rapid pacing (180 bpm) using a 22 × 40 mm balloon. While the balloon was inflated, simultaneous aortography via the radial access confirmed that the balloon did not impinge on the coronary ostia and that antegrade flow remained unobstructed. This step also provided a live confirmation that the valve lumen could accommodate a third device and helped delineate the interaction between the existing 27‑mm Lotus frame and 23‑mm Evolut frame.

### 4.3 Valve positioning, commissural alignment, and stair‑stepped implantation

The self‑expanding Acurate‑Neo2 (size S) was advanced over the guidewire and positioned in the aortic annulus. Pre‑procedure computed tomography (CT) had demonstrated that a high‑implantation strategy would allow direct apposition of the third valve’s supra‑annular leaflets to the Evolut leaflet hinge, thereby preserving an adequate effective orifice area and mitigating the risk of patient‑prosthesis mismatch. During deployment, fluoroscopic landmarks were matched to the pre‑procedural CT modeling, and the valve was intentionally seated at a “stair‑stepped” depth relative to the outer plane of the Evolut stent frame. This technique ensured that the neo‑skirt of the third valve lay above the coronary ostia and reduced the likelihood of coronary obstruction. The careful alignment of the commissures, guided by CT‑derived commissural marks, also maintained future feasibility for coronary artery access.

### 4.4 Post‑dilation and radial expansion mechanics

After deployment, a 20 × 40 mm balloon was inflated under rapid pacing to optimize radial expansion of the Acurate‑Neo2 frame. This step also had the ancillary effect of partially “fracturing” the radial stiffness of the underlying Evolut framework, thereby improving leaflet unfolding and reducing transvalvular gradients. The balloon size was chosen to match the nominal sizing of the final valve while keeping the peak pressure below 20 atm to limit the risk of annular rupture.

### 4.5 Immediate post‑procedural imaging: angiography and trans‑oesophageal echocardiography (TOE)

A completion aortogram confirmed correct valve position, preserved coronary flow, and the absence of paravalvular leak. In parallel, TOE was performed to assess leaflet mobility, transvalvular gradients, and residual regurgitation. The TOE confirmed that all leaflets were co‑moving, there was no central systolic jet, and that transthoracic measurement of mean gradient and aortic valve area (AVA) aligned with the angiographic findings. A Doppler velocity index (DVI) of 0.24 was noted immediately post‑procedure, indicating severe PPM; however, no significant perivalvular leak or obstruction was seen.

### 4.6 VARC‑3 endpoint assessment

Device success was defined according to VARC‑3 as: (1) no procedural mortality, (2) successful deployment of the device at the intended location, (3) absence of left‑ventricular dysfunction, (4) no permanent pacemaker implantation related to conduction disturbance, (5) absence of major vascular complications, and (6) no requirement for in‑hospital re‑intervention. In this case, all criteria were met. Hemodynamically, the immediate post‑procedural gradient was 67 mm Hg with an indexed AVA of 0.4 cm² / m². Although these values indicated severe PPM per VARC‑3, the patient remained hemodynamically stable with no data of acute left‑ventricular failure, and the valve function was deemed acceptable. Subsequent TTE at 1 month and 1 year showed progressive improvement in gradient (down to 27 mm Hg) and AVA (up to 1.1 cm²), satisfying the VARC‑3 criteria for longitudinal success.

In conclusion, the staged, image‑guided deployment of the Acurate‑Neo2 in a triple‑stented aortic root achieved procedural success, preserved coronary flow, and set the stage for favorable mid‑term hemodynamic improvement, all while adhering to the stringent VARC‑3 framework for immediate and early post‑procedure assessment.

## 5. Follow‑up, Outcomes, and Long‑Term Surveillance

Immediately after the third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV), transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) was performed to confirm device positioning and assess hemodynamic performance. The valve leaflets were fully mobile with a complete opening, and there was no residual aortic regurgitation. However, the transvalvular mean gradient remained markedly elevated at 67 mm Hg, with an aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.9 cm² and an indexed AVA of 0.4 cm²/m². The Doppler velocity index (DVI) was 0.24, findings that collectively satisfied the Valve Academic Research Consortium‑3 definition of severe patient‑prosthesis mismatch (PPM). Despite the high early gradient, the absence of perivalvular leakage and the restoration of leaflet motion suggested that the significant gradient was largely due to the residual PPM rather than structural valve dysfunction.

Within one month, repeat TTE demonstrated a dramatic improvement in valve hemodynamics. The mean gradient decreased to 27 mm Hg and the AVA increased to 1.1 cm², with an indexed AVA of 0.5 cm²/m². The DVI improved to 0.36. Left ventricular reverse remodeling was evident, with a reduction in end‑diastolic and end‑systolic volumes, and left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) rose to 65%. Clinically, the patient reported marked improvement, regaining a New York Heart Association (NYHA) class I status and walking > 500 m without dyspnea. Functional capacity at a 6‑minute walk test increased from 250 m to 380 m, aligning with the echocardiographic improvements.

At 3.5 months the trend continued: the patient remained asymptomatic, and TTE showed stable hemodynamics (mean gradient 26 mm Hg, AVA 1.1 cm²). No evidence of transvalvular or paravalvular regurgitation emerged. The patient’s exercise tolerance remained unchanged, and no new arrhythmias or conduction disturbances were recorded.

One year after the third valve implantation, the patient was clinically stable, with no complaints of dyspnea or fatigue. TTE confirmed durable valve function: mean gradient 26 mm Hg, AVA 1.1 cm², indexed AVA 0.5 cm²/m², and DVI 0.36. EF remained robust at 65%, and there was no progression of PPM. Serial imaging had not revealed structural valve deterioration, leaflet thickening, or calcific pannus formation, underscoring the procedural durability in this complex triple‑stented anatomy.

The persistent, yet low‑gradient, PPM observed early post‑procedure can be attributed to the cumulative frame height and close apposition of the three bioprostheses. Low‑gradient PPM frequently resolves after a period of reverse remodeling, as seen here, likely due to improvement in left ventricular contractility and reduction in afterload. Clinical evidence suggests that low‑gradient PPM is associated with a better prognosis than high‑gradient PPM, provided that significant gradients are transient and ameliorate over time. In patients where PPM persists, options include pharmacologic optimization of afterload, consideration of higher‑profile or supra‑annular valves in future reinterventions, or selective post‑dilation to encourage further radial expansion of the underlying stent frames. However, the latter carries a risk of valve fracture or coronary obstruction, especially in layered anatomies; hence, it was not pursued in this case.

Long‑term surveillance for this patient follows a structured protocol to detect late complications, monitor valve performance, and surveil for recurrent infection. Six‑monthly TTE is performed for the first two years post‑procedure to reassess gradients, valve motion, and EF, followed by annual imaging thereafter if stable. Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) is reserved for any clinical suspicion of endocarditis, new conduction abnormalities, or unexplained heart failure. Dual‑energy multislice computed tomography (MSCT) is scheduled at 1 year and every 3 years thereafter to evaluate prosthetic valve integrity, leaflet thickening, and to reassess the coronary ostial clearance to foresee potential coronary access challenges in the event of a future reintervention. Routine blood cultures are not required in the absence of symptoms; however, a full infectious disease evaluation is mandatory if fever, new murmurs, or elevated inflammatory markers arise. Given the patient’s history of prosthetic valve endocarditis, a low threshold is maintained for initiating empirical antibiotic therapy and employing a multidisciplinary heart team review in case of suspected relapse.

Analysis of procedural durability indicates that this case aligns with emerging series on serial TAVI, where long‑term follow‑up has demonstrated preserved valve function and low rates of structural valve deterioration up to five years. The absence of valve‑related adverse events and the durable reversal of PPM support the feasibility of TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV interventions in carefully selected high‑risk patients. Continued longitudinal data will refine risk stratification, inform valve‑selection algorithms, and guide surveillance strategies for this expanding patient cohort.

## 6. Clinical Lessons, Future Directions, and Ethical Considerations

**Key take‑aways**  
This case underscores that the feasibility of a *TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV* procedure hinges on meticulous, imaging‑driven planning. Multislice computed tomography (MSCT) was indispensable for (i) sizing the neo‑annulus, (ii) measuring the valve‑to‑coronary distance, and (iii) visualising the interaction between successive transfemoral valves. Commissural alignment – aligning the new valve leaflet hinges with those of the underlying Evolut prosthesis – was intentionally abandoned in favour of a supravalvular, high‑implantation strategy that preserved coronary perfusion without a protective balloon. The procedural success demonstrates the practicality of a tailored “strategy‑driven” approach when preprocedural imaging and thoughtful valve selection guide every deployment step.

**Implications for lifetime valve management**  
Patients treated with transcatheter aortic valves are living longer, and many will eventually require redo interventions. This case illustrates several principles that should influence early strategy discussions:

1. **Choice of valve design at the outset.** Self‑expanding valves with low frame heights (e.g., Acurate neo2) or balloon‑expandable valves with minimal subvalvular frames may be preferred in patients expected to survive long enough for a possible re‑intervention.  
2. **Preservation of future coronary access.** Even if the first intervention is successful, coronary access may become essential later (e.g., for stenting of a previously untreated coronary artery). Commissural alignment and low‑profile frames reduce the risk of future procedural difficulty.  
3. **Surgical versus transcatheter routes.** While the initial procedure was justified by prohibitive surgical risk, the cumulative impact of earlier valves on the aortic root eventually makes surgical redo increasingly complex. A global “lifetime management plan” that anticipates future iterations can mitigate anatomic collapse and enable easier surgery if it does become necessary.

**Emerging solutions for future failure**  
When structural degeneration or infection arises after a second valve, non‑surgical methods may preserve the native anatomy:  

- **Valve fracture (bioprosthetic valve fracture).** This involves using a high‑pressure balloon to break the stent struts of the implanted valve, allowing the new valve to expand fully. Although promising, its safety in a triple‑stent setting remains unproven.  
- **BASILICA (Bioprosthetic or Native Aortic Scallop Intentional Laceration to prevent iatrogenic Coronary Artery obstruction).** By lacerating the native or bioprosthetic leaflets, coronary obstruction can be avoided. The technique might be useful after the first or second valve but may be technically prohibitive once several frames have been layered.  
- **Leaflet laceration or intentional leaflet fenestration** are adjuncts to BASILICA that might be considered in the future, yet no data exist for their use after multiple transcatheter valves.

**Ethical and shared decision‑making**  
The procedural plan involved off‑label use of the Acurate neo2, a decision that required rigorous informed consent and a multidisciplinary ethics review. Core elements of the consent process included:

- **Transparent risk communication.** The patient was briefed on the high procedural risk, potential for PPM, possible need for future coronary interventions, and the lack of long‑term data for triple‑valve configurations.  
- **Shared decision‑making.** The heart team, patient, and family weighed the risks and benefits of repeated transcatheter versus surgical redo. The patient’s values and preferences – particularly the desire to avoid major surgery – guided the final choice.  
- **Documentation of off‑label use.** Institutional approval and registry entry were obtained to ensure accountability and to contribute data to collective learning.

**Guideline alignment**  
ESC 2025 guidelines recommend surgical valve replacement for prosthetic valve endocarditis, yet they also highlight that “management should be individualised, taking into account infection control, surgical risk and anatomical feasibility.” In this case, due to the patient’s advanced age, comorbidities, and the complexity of a redo root surgery, the guideline‑endorsed individualisation was applied: the valve as a bridging strategy was chosen after infection clearance. This approach aligns with the ESC recommendation that mechanical removal of the prosthesis is not mandatory if the infection has resolved.

**Future research needs**  
To translate isolated successes into standard care, systematic data collection and analytical tools are required:

1. **National and international registries** that record serial TAVI procedures, including patient‑specific imaging, procedural details, and outcomes.  
2. **Prospective registrational trials** that compare different valve types, implantation strategies (high vs. low), and the role of commissural alignment in patients with multiple transcatheter valves.  
3. **Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) and finite‑element modeling** that simulate valve overlap, flow patterns, and potential for fracture or obstruction. These models can help predict implant depth, neo‑annulus area, and PPM, thereby refining pre‑procedural planning.  
4. **Device innovation** with modular frames that allow controlled expansion or fracture, as well as improved imaging markers that aid in rapid identification of at‑risk anatomies.

In summary, this case illustrates that a carefully planned, imaging‑driven TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV strategy can safely restore valve function even after endocarditis, but it also highlights the need for early lifetime planning, judicious valve selection, and robust patient‑centered decision‑making. As the field evolves, multidisciplinary collaboration, rigorous data collection, and technological innovation will be essential to expand the safety net for patients who will inevitably require serial transcatheter valve interventions.
