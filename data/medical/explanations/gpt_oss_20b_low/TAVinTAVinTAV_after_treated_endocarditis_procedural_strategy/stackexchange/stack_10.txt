Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Morning Report] What future catheter‑based techniques could be considered if the third valve fails, given the complex layered anatomy in this case?

**Future catheter‑based options if the third valve (Acurate Neo‑2) degrades or fails**

When a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV prosthesis eventually abandons optimal function, the usual surgical approach becomes prohibitive because of the layered stent frame, adhesions to the native aorta and the patient’s high operative risk (EuroSCORE II ≈ 27%).  The subsequent interventional strategies must therefore balance feasibility against the complex scaffold that now occupies the aortic root.  The following catheter‑based options could be considered, each with a brief discussion of how the ultra‑conformal, multi‑stent anatomy of the presented case would influence feasibility, safety and likely outcome.

| Technique | Rationale | Feasibility in triple‑stent anatomy | Key risks / limitations |
|-----------|-----------|--------------------------------------|--------------------------|
| **Bioprosthetic valve fracture (BVF)** | Expands the stent frame of a previous bioprosthesis by high‑pressure ballooning, thereby enlarging the effective orifice area and reducing PPM. | The Acurate Neo‑2 is supra‑annular and relatively low‑profile; its stent frame can be dissected from the underlying Evolut frame.  In a triple‑stent construct, the outermost valve is the only one that needs to be fractured.  The Evolut and Lotus frames remain intact beneath and will not be affected by the high‑pressure inflation. | Requires a compliant, high‑pressure balloon (e.g., 24–26 mm, 10–12 atm).  Over‑inflation can dislodge a leaf or damage the aortic wall.  Prior evaluation with post‑deployment CT to identify the neo‑annulus and the presence of calcification is mandatory. |
| **BASILICA (Bioprosthetic or Native Aortic Scallop Intentional Laceration to Prevent iatrogenic Coronary Obstruction)** | Intentionally lacerates the commissural leaflets of a tricuspid bioprosthesis to prevent coronary occlusion during a subsequent valve‑in‑valve. | If a repeat TAV‑in‑TAV is pursued (e.g., if the next valve is a 20‑mm Sapien 3 that would sit below the Neo‑2 and risk coronary obstruction), BASILICA could pre‑emptively split the Neo‑2 leaflets, allowing the new valve to sit deeper without covering the coronaries.  The technique has been reported through a single, heavily calcified stent frame (Lotus/Evolut), and can be adapted to a third, supra‑annular frame. | Requires trans‑aortic access, fluoroscopic guidance, and a dedicated catheter.  In a triple‑stent environment, the risk of transecting the underlying Evolut or Lotus stent is low, but the risk of a plano‑bifurcation injury to the native aortic wall is higher.  Requires pre‑procedural imaging to confirm that the coronary ostia are ≥10 mm above the Neo‑2 leaflets. |
| **Surgical removal of the outer valve via percutaneous extraction (DAVID or NEAR‑S)** | Mechanical retrieval of the latest valve without full surgical aortic root replacement. | This is an experimental concept; the device tip would have to engage the Acurate Neo‑2 stent struts.  In a duplex‑layered prosthesis, the stent is firmly embedded and adhesion to the native aortic wall is significant.  Early experience suggests high failure rates. | Very high procedural risk, unlikely to be pursued once the third valve is implanted unless a salvage strategy is urgently needed. |
| **Valve‑in‑valve with a lower‑profile valve (e.g., 23 mm Sapien 3 Ultra)** | Shortens the overall implant height, thereby creating more space for the next layer and reducing statistical chance of coronary obstruction. | In a triple‑stented root, the inner lumen is already constrained.  Using a low‑profile balloon‑expandable valve would reduce the radial force and could preserve some coronary clearance.  Post‑deployment, a balloon‑expansion might open the underlying Evolut frame slightly, improving effective orifice area. | Potential for higher transvalvular gradients; the backup frame may limit complete expansion.  Coronary obstruction remains a concern if the Neo‑2 is high‑placed. |
| **Valve‑in‑valve with a supra‑annular, low‑profile valve (e.g., 27 mm Evolut‑T** | Continues the self‑expanding design, preserving commissural alignment and avoiding the need for large‑balloon dilatation. | The triple‑stent environment may still obstruct the valve’s expansion due to the underlying Lotus struts.  Re‑implanting a self‑expanding valve in the same place may not appreciably improve flow unless a controlled fracture of the outer Neo‑2 stent is performed beforehand. | Requires careful sizing; risk of aortic trauma if balloon post‑dilation exceeds the capacity of the outer composite frame. |
| **Surgical aortic root replacement with mechanical or biological valve** | Definitive solution but technically demanding in a patient with heavy calcification and previous endocarditis. | Historically the “gold standard” for valve failure after PVE, but in this case the authors and ESC guidelines already opted against surgery because of prohibitive risk. | Not a catheter option, but remains on the back‑board if all catheter options fail. |

**Practical pathway for the next interventional step**

1. **Re‑imaging** – A contrast–enhanced, high‑resolution CT scan with dedicated valve‑framing analysis should be acquired.  This will guide whether the Neo‑2 stent can be fractured (BVF) or whether BASILICA is required before a further valve‑in‑valve.  The neo‑annulus, valve lumen, and coronary ostia height must be quantified again because the existing frames can alter with time.

2. **Decide on primary strategy** –  
   *If BVF is feasible*, a high‑pressure balloon (e.g., 24 mm E-sheath, 12 atm) can be used to separately expand the Neo‑2’s stent.  Post‑fracture CT and echocardiography confirm increased orifice area and absence of leaflet tear or annular rupture.  
   *If coronary obstruction risk is high*, BASILICA of the Neo‑2 leaflets should be performed prior to a valve‑in‑valve, ideally with a Sapien 3 Ultra or a low‑profile Evolut.  

3. **Redo valve‑in‑valve** – After BVF/BASILICA, a new valve can be implanted with a controlled, low‑height deployment.  Using a self‑expanding design that allows commissural realignment (e.g., Evolut‑F) can preserve future coronary access.

4. **Follow‑up** – Serial TTE and CT at 1 month, 3 months and 1 year to monitor gradients, AVA and left‑ventricular remodeling.  Any rise in gradient beyond 30 mm Hg warrants early re‑intervention planning.

**Conclusion**

In a triple‑stent aortic root, the next catheter‑based step is most likely *bioprosthetic valve fracture* with subsequent valve‑in‑valve, or *BASILICA* if coronary obstruction is predicted.  These techniques have been largely reported in two‐stent environments and are technically adaptable to the layered anatomy described.  Ongoing imaging surveillance and multidisciplinary review remain essential, as future interventions will inevitably be constrained by the cumulative stent burden and may require increasingly novel strategies such as percutaneous valve extraction or the development of evolvable, lower‑profile valves.
