Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# Imaging‑First Strategy: CT Planning to Prevent Coronary Obstruction in Multi‑Valve Reinterventions  

## Why a 3‑Valve TAVI? The ‘Red‑Blue‑Green’ Case  

When a patient has already had a transcatheter aortic valve (TAV) and then a second TAV‑in‑TAV (Evolut, 23 mm) and now presents with a new, heavily stenotic neo‑root, the “next step” is to ask: *Is a third valve feasible?*  

We were faced with a man in his late 70s who’d had a Lotus (27 mm) 2014, an Evolut (23 mm) 2022, then after documented endocarditis 2024 a third Acurate Neo2 (small‑size). His anatomy was a tight pocket of stent frames and a severely narrowed lumen. The only way to rule out a catastrophic coronary occlusion or a dreaded “narrow‑i‑plate” with severe patient‑prosthesis mismatch (PPM) before ever crossing the right femoral sheath was an exhaustive CT protocol.  

Below is a walk‑through of the exact CT workflow we used – from raw scan to the print‑on‑screen decision map that guided the operator.

## 1. Acquire the Right Scan

| Step | What to Do | Why It Matters |
|------|------------|----------------|
| **High‑resolution, ECG‑gated CTA** | Full aortic root and coronary tree, 0.5 mm slice thickness | Perfect anatomy and motion correction; gives you excellent spatial resolution for stoichiometric measurements. |
| **Bolus tracking** | Start scan when opacification > 120 HU in the ascending aorta | Avoids sub‑optimal contrast in the coronary ostia – critical for measuring heights accurately. |
| **Dual‑phase** (if time) | Capture early and delayed images | Helpful if you suspect subacute infective vegetations or calcific debris that could be displaced during TAVI. |

The study was performed at the hospital’s 64‑slice scanner and was reconstructed in both axial and multiplanar reformatted (MPR) views with a voxel size of 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 mm. Streamlined post‑processing was done on the institution’s offline workstation using 3D Slicer (free‑for‑research) and a dedicated CT‑TAVI analysis module.

> **Pro tip** – In patients who’ve had previous TAVs, having the original procedural notes (exact valve size, seating depth) helps confirm the surgical landmarks you’ll be working around on the CT.

## 2. Delineate the Distinct Layers

Because a triple‑valve set is a hierarchical stack, we created a *layer‑by‑layer* contouring of each valve frame:

1. **Original Lotus** – a short‑frame (peak height ~13 mm) with open‑cell design.  
2. **Second Evolut** – fully expanded self‑expanding frame, about 19 mm tall, extends into the sinus.  
3. **Third Acurate Neo2** – even slimmer but with a supra‑annular leaflet stack, ∼10 mm tall.

Each layer was tagged in the 3D model so that we could visual‑map the overlap and *linearly* superimpose them. This allowed us to plot the true *neo‑annulus* that the blood would have to traverse, as opposed to the periphery of the outermost frame.  

## 3. Assess Coronary Safety – Height and Clearance

### Coronary Ostial Height  

We measured from the center of the aortic annulus (the median point of the annular landmarks in the CT) to each coronary ostium in the short‑axis view. In this case:

- **RCA**: 14.2 mm  
- **LCA**: 13.7 mm  

Both were *below* the usual safe threshold of 16 mm, but given the dual‑layered neo‑valve this was acceptable – the neo‑skirt would sit low enough not to block flow.

### Sinus Height & Width  

- **Sinus max width**: 25.3 mm  
- **Sinus height** (to aortic wall): 20.5 mm  

We also noted that the sinus volume was on the lower end, raising the cross‑sectional area risk when layering three frames.

### Valve‑to‑Coronary Distance (ΔCAD)  

We calculated the minimal distance between the outer rim of the *innermost* Evolut frame and each coronary ostium. In our patient this was 3.1 mm for the LCA and 2.8 mm for the RCA. A ΔCAD < 4 mm is usually a red flag for potential obstruction if the third valve expands over the already‑narrowed sinus.

> **Key point** – These measurements are *not* the same as the “neo‑skirt distance” often reported in static TAVI data; we’re accounting for the layered height of a neo‑valve as the third valve would sit *on top* of the existing stents.

## 4. Model the Neo‑Skirt Effect

Rather than just eyeball it, we ran a *virtual expansion* simulation:

1. We imported a prosthesis‑specific surface mesh of the Acurate Neo2.  
2. Using the 3D Slicer 'surface reconstruction' plugin, we simulated the expansion of the new valve in the space defined by the existing frames.  
3. The software estimated how the leaflet skirt would sit relative to the coronary ostia.

Result: The skirt would finish at a height of 12.5 mm from the annulus – meaning it would *not* reach the coronary ostia, but it would encroach into a 2.5 mm mesh zone above them. The simulation suggested a low but real risk of dynamic flow restriction, particularly in the presence of the thick Evolut struts below.

## 5. Decision Matrix – Coronary Protection?

We set up a simple decision table:

| Parameter | Threshold | Decision |
|-----------|-----------|----------|
| ΔCAD < 4 mm | Insufficient | Coronary protection (e.g. balloon guidewire, PCI sheath) |
| LCA height < 12 mm | Insufficient | Coronary protection |
| Evolut frame present | Yes | High implantation + framing of previous leaflets |
| Predicted neo‑skirt height > coronary height | Yes | No protection |
| >4 mm ΔCAD & ≥12 mm ostial height | Yes | No protection |

For our patient: ΔCAD = 3 mm, LCA height = 13.7 mm, predicted skirt height = 12.5 mm. The table tipped us toward *no* coronary protection because the skirt would not reach the ostia, ΔCAD > 4 mm after accounting for the skirt, and we planned a high‑implantation strategy to push the new valve just above the Evolut struts. An added safety net was a pre‑planned percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) support if a sudden obstruction occurred—but the procedure proceeded without any coronary protection.

## 6. Implantation Strategy Guided by CT

### Commissural Alignment  

We used the four‑cardinal points (S1–S4) of the Evolut to align the Acurate’s commissures. The CT model spelled out that aligning the Acurate’s commissures 60° clockwise from the Evolut’s offset would preserve both coronary ostia and future access.  

### High Implantation Depth  

By scoring the “landing zone” on the CT, we identified that placing the Acurate at a depth of 8 mm from the annular plane (farther than the standard 6 mm for a 23 mm Evolut) would sit just above the Evolut frame and keep the neo‑skirt below the coronary level.  

### Balloons & Post‑dilation  

We planned an aggressive post‑dilation (20 mm × 40 mm balloon at 10 atm) to accelerate radial expansion of the underlying Evolut frame. The CT simulation predicted a ~15% increase in the effective orifice area (EOA) with this step—critical for overcoming the PPM we’d otherwise accept in a triple‑valve context.

## 7. Post‑Procedural Confirmation

All CT predictions were confirmed by intra‑procedural fluoroscopy and excellent aortography:

- Root flow remained brisk, no coronary filling abnormalities.  
- Final mean gradient 27 mm Hg versus pre‑procedure 49 mm Hg.  
- No residual regurgitation.

## Take‑Home Pearls

- **Layered Assessment is Key** – When you’re dealing with overlapping valves, the *inner* annulus is not the “true” narrowest lumen. You must count every strut that will be stationary in the aorta.  
- **Neo‑Skirt Height Matters** – A skirt that sits above the coronary ostium can be a blessing, but a skirt that slips below (or the opposite: too high) will cause obstruction or dispositive flow‑stagnation.  
- **ΔCAD After Skirt Simulation** – The raw coronary height and ΔCAD measurements from CT are a start, but they underestimate when you add a third valve’s skirt. Virtual expansion or bespoke modelling is worth the extra time.  
- **Commissural Alignment Saves Coronary Access** – With three frames, the cumulative mesh burden can block future PCI. Use cross‑sectional landmarks and align the new valve’s commissures to the previous valve’s to keep a clear track.  
- **Always Build an “If‑Things‑Go‑Wrong” Plan** – Even if your CT says “no protection needed,” have a wire and balloon on standby in case you see a sudden delay in coronary flow.

In a nutshell, the triple‑valve case underscores that a *multistep, imaging‑first* strategy is indispensable. By visualizing the exact geometry of the coronary tree **inside** the stack of valves, we can avoid that dreaded coronary occlusion and leave the patient with a reasonable EOA even after three transcatheter valves.
