# Managing Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis in a Transcatheter‑Redirection Era

## Why This Case is a “Red‑Flag” in the TAVI Era

We’re all familiar with the joy of “bioprostheses that last forever.” In reality, those valves are more like carefully tuned instruments that will eventually wear out, especially in younger, healthier patients who live past their 70s and 80s. When a valve fails, the story can become a living‑organ maze of annular calcification, stent fragmentation, calcified cusp tissue, and—if infection is involved—a whole other layer of complexity.

The patient in this report is a textbook example: three transcatheter valves with a history of infective endocarditis (IE) and a recent structural valve deterioration (SVD) after resolution of infection. His journey highlights one of the most debated topics in structural heart disease today: **Can we safely perform a second or third TAVI after treating prosthetic valve endocarditis?**

## The “Standard” Answer: Surgery vs. Catheter

### Surgical Gold Standard

The 2025 ESC guidelines still picture open surgical replacement (O‑S ± root or ascending aorta replacement) as the recommended therapy for prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). The rationale? Removal of all infected tissue, definitive debridement, and the ability to replace the valve and surrounding structures in a single operation.

But surgery isn’t always “feasible.” In the patient’s case, the cumulative EuroSCORE II was a staggering 27%. He had COPD, diabetes, a pacemaker, prior GI bleeding, and the first two TAVIs had already anchored into the aortic wall—making an open root replace‑ment practically a “mega‑surgery” with a very high likelihood of morbidity, prolonged recovery and uncertain outcomes.

### Catheter‐Based Redirection

The hedging question is: **When is it safe to go the transcatheter route after a treated PVE?**

The main pillars of safety emerge from three well‑established criteria:

1. **Complete Resolution of Infection**  
   * No fevers or positive cultures.  
   * No vegetations on TEE, no paravalvular leaks.  
   * Absence of systemic septic emboli.

2. **Anatomical Feasibility**  
   * Adequate coronary ostial height and sinus dimensions.  
   * Sufficient landing zone to avoid coronary obstruction or paravalvular flow.  
   * A stable prior prosthesis that can act as an “anchor” for the new valve.

3. **Risk/Benefit Balance**  
   * Extremely high surgical operative risk, or high technical risk of valve explant.^1  
   * Long‑term durability and functional benefit that outweigh the potential “unknowns” of a third valve.

In this patient’s case, all three hoops were sat: cultures were negative, echo/CT showed no debris, imaging confirmed sufficient sinus dimensions and coronary clearance, and the surgical risk was prohibitive. So the heart team chose a third TAVI.

## The TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV Strategy: What Went Right

### Step 1 – Preprocedural Planning with CT

The article emphasized a three‑dimensional, real‑time in‑silico modeling approach. Key metrics:

- Minimum lumen diameter inside the previously implanted Evolut (13.7 mm) – the “sieve” size for the next valve.  
- Coronary ostial height versus anticipated neo‑skirt after leaflet deployment.  
- Potential for “stacked” stent frames to encroach on the coronary ostia.

The team simulated different valve sizes and offsets to identify a high‑implantation strategy that would nest the new valve just above the Evolut leaflets, thereby preserving a continuous open lumen and protecting the coronaries.

### Step 2 – Device Selection & Valve Depth

An Acurate neo2 (self‑expanding, supra‑annular, short frame) was the logical choice:  
- **Supra‑annular** design gives a better effective orifice area (EOA) in a narrowed annulus.  
- **Low profile** reduces “mesh burden” on coronary flow.  
- **Commissural alignment** helps keep future coronary access viable.

High implantation (just above the Evolut leaflets) ensured that the new valve’s skirt would not “cups” the coronary ostia or fold the old leaflets, mitigating patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM) risk.

### Step 3 – Intra‑procedural Protection

Interestingly, no explicit coronary protection was used. The team relied on pre‑dilation and a careful pacing strategy to keep the coronary ostia unobstructed. The CT modelling had predicted sufficient clearance (>4 mm) even after deployment, which proved accurate when aortography confirmed unobstructed flow.

### Step 4 – Post‑dilation & Commissural Alignment

After deployment, a 20/40 mm balloon post‑dilation optimized expansion (helping to gently push the Evolut stent into the new valve’s frame). The final aortogram showed:

- No paravalvular leak (PVL).  
- No residual aortic regurgitation.  
- Coronary arteries patent.

The patient’s immediate post‑op echo showed a high pressure gradient (67 mm Hg) initially, but a month later, at 27 mm Hg, with an indexed EOA of 1.1 cm²—well above the PPM threshold.

## Clinical Pearls From a Third TAVI After Treated PVE

| Take‑Home Point | Why It Matters |
|-----------------|----------------|
| **Never Rely Solely on “No Fever”** | Persistent subclinical infection can still jeopardise a new prosthesis. Use a broad panel of biomarkers (CRP, ESR, troponin) and serial imaging. |
| **CT Is the New Spirometry** | Quantify the actual lumen in the misaligned, layered stent frames—don’t just trust the annular “size.” |
| **Coronary Protection Is Not a Play‑Check** | Verify intra‑op with aortography *and* fluoroscopic markers. If clearance is borderline (<4 mm), plan for balloon protection or even a “BASILICA”‑style technique. |
| **High‑Implantation Can Reduce PPM** | In stacked valve scenarios, aiming just above the previous leaflet tips can preserve effective orifice area. |
| **Commissural Alignment Is Future‑Proof** | Future coronary access is still a concern despite the small added mesh layer. Aligning the new valve commissures to the old stent’s is a good practice. |
| **Post‑dilation Is Essential** | A gentle, cautious balloon step can coax the “stuck” stent into the new valve frame and smooth out flow, as seen in this case. |

## Why the “Controversial” Label?

The debate isn’t only about safety; it’s also about the future of valve‑disease management. 

1. **Growing Volume of Re‑interventions** – With the 10‑year mortality of TAVI around 30–40 % in high‑risk cohorts, more patients will have to live with a transcatheter valve for decades.  
2. **Complex Anatomy in Re‑interventions** – Each subsequent valve adds a “layer” of stent architecture, making both surgical explant and future catheter access more challenging.  
3. **Infection vs. Longevity** – The risk–benefit calculus shifts dramatically when surgery is impossible or carries exorbitant risk.  

So, guidelines may still favour surgery, but real‑world evidence such as this case demonstrates the feasibility and durability of a third TAVI after treated IE—provided the infection is truly eradicated and imaging rules out problematic anatomy.

## Bottom Line for the Frontline

When dealing with prosthetic valve endocarditis in the TAVI era:

1. **Confirm infection resolution** with cultures, TEE, CT, and evolve lab markers.  
2. **Model the anatomy** with high‑definition CT (and dedicated software if available).  
3. **Weigh surgical risk** – if EuroSCORE II+ is >20–25 %, consider a catheter‑based approach.  
4. **Plan for future coronary access** – commissure alignment and high implantation help but don’t eliminate the issue.  
5. **Stay flexible** – in the eventuality of re‑failure, options are limited; avoid “over‑implanting” a valve that may be difficult to remove later.

The patient’s 1‑year follow‑up—symptom‑free, stable gradients, preserved EF—shows that a careful, multidisciplinary, imaging‑driven approach can yield durable, viable results in the most complex scenarios.
