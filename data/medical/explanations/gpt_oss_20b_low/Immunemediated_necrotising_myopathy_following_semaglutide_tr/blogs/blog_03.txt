Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# From Nausea to Dysphagia: When a First‑Dose GI Reaction Signals a Beneath Myopathy  

## The “First‑Dose Pseudo‑Rejection” that Turns Out to be a Myopathy  
A 68‑year‑old woman came to the ED 24 hours after her **first** subcutaneous semaglutide injection. She was racing for the couch after a dose that should have helped her lose weight, but she ended up throwing up, unable to drink, and developed acute kidney injury. On the first shift, the lab people ran a CK and got 337 U/L – mildly elevated, nothing that screams rhabdomyolysis.  

That was the last “big” thing we knew about her for almost 4 months — until she was found unable to sit up or swallow liquids or solids. CK? 3992 U/L. The diagnosis: **immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM)**. The culprit? Semaglutide.  

What makes this case ruby‑red is the paradox: a single, apparently benign dose of a GLP‑1 agonist that set off a cascade of chronic, disabling muscle disease.

## What Clocked the Puzzle?  
| Timeline | Symptom | Lab/Impression |
|----------|---------|----------------|
| -3 yrs | Fatty liver biopsy | NAFLD |
| 0 yr | Initiated semaglutide 0.25 mg | GI upset, nausea, vomiting |
| ~0.5 yr | Hospitalised for renal failure + 337 CK | mild CK, no myalgia |
| 4 mo | Progressive weakness | 3992 CK, dysphagia, dysphonia |

The dramatic jump from 337 to > 3900 CK over 4 months is textbook for a **necrotising** process, not a clean‑cut drug toxicity that resolves within hours or days. The classic “one‑shot” toxicity picture (tirzepatide case) would show a CK over 20 000 that drops within days of stopping the drug. This patient’s CK stayed low for months, climbed, and then fell only after immunosuppression.  

## Key Features that Should Alert the Emergency Physician  

1. **Early GI upset + renal dysfunction** can be a red flag for drug‑induced muscle injury.  
2. **Serial CK**: The first measurement was 337 U/L. Four months later, it was 3992 U/L. Nothing else was explaining the rise — no statin, no supplements, no massive exercise.  
3. **Proximal weakness** with dysphagia/dysphonia. In an older woman, think myopathy before neuro‑plastic changes.  
4. **Negative ANA, negative myositis panel**. Seronegative IMNM is real – especially when you have a plausible drug trigger.  

## What to Do When a ‘Typical’ Adverse Effect Precedes Muscle Failure  

1. **Document baseline CK** when a patient has an acute reaction to a new drug, even if the CK is normal or mildly elevated.  
2. **Repeat CK** as part of the work‑up for any persistent weakness (e.g., every 48–72 hrs in the acute phase, then weekly if the patient is still deteriorating).  
3. **Hold the suspect drug** immediately if a patient shows any CK rise > 10× ULN or develops symptoms of weakness.  
4. **Early rheumatology/neurology consult** if CK keeps climbing or the patient develops dyspnea, dysphagia, or severe proximal weakness.  
5. **Order a muscle MRI** if the clinical picture is unclear – PROFUSE study says MRI can pick up the “diaphragm sign” and guide biopsy.  

## The Diagnostic Work‑up – What We Did, What We Could Learn  

- **Biopsy:** Necrotising fibres, macrophage infiltrate (CD68+), rare T‑cells, membrane attack complex – classic for IMNM.  
- **Autoantibodies:** HMGCR, SRP, and the full myositis panel were negative. Only a low‑titre SS‑B. Seronegative doesn't mean seronegative – could just be under‑powered panel.  
- **Cardiac Work‑up:** Mild myocarditis versus stress cardiomyopathy; resolved after immunosuppression.  
- **Liver:** Persistent steatosis, not a new hepatotoxicity driver.  

The story stresses that **histology remains king** when suspecting IMNM, especially if autoantibodies are negative.

## Treatment Layman's Story

- **High‑dose IV methylprednisolone (1 g ×5 d)** → drop in CK over a week.  
- **Oral prednisone 1 mg/kg** → tapered over 2 months.  
- **IVIG 2 g/kg** over 4 days because the weakness was life‑threatening (swallowing, breathing).  
- **No further IVIG** due to cost and good recovery.  
- **No statins or other immunosuppressants** needed thereafter.

By 10 months, the patient was back to baseline, off steroids, and never restarted on a GLP‑1.

## Pearls to Remember  

- *A single dose of a GLP‑1 agonist can trigger a delayed, progressive IMNM*.  
- **Serial CK is your compass**. Do it pre‑ and post‑drug initiation, especially when GI or renal side‑effects occur.  
- **Weakness with dysphagia in a patient with a recent drug reaction = think myopathy, not stroke**.  
- **Early rheumatology/neurology referral** shortens time to diagnosis and improves outcomes.  
- **IVIG can be lifesaving** in severe IMNM even when steroids alone seem inadequate.  
- **Stop the offending drug immediately**; recancelling it may be life‑saving even if you’re unsure of causality.  

## Take‑home Message  

A seemingly innocuous nausea episode doesn’t always mean the drug is fine. In patients who develop proximal weakness weeks to months later, consider drug‑induced myopathy. Serial CK, prompt imaging, and a low threshold for biopsy and immunotherapy can make the difference between a long, disabling course and a quick, full recovery.

**Bottom line:** If a patient who has just tolerated a first‑dose of semaglutide (or any new agent) later complains of progressive weakness, ask the kitchen question: *Did you feel worse earlier?* If yes — start CK trending a **today**. And if the CK climbs, dial the neuro‑ or rheum consult right away.
