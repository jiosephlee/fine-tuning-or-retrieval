Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# When a ‘Big’ Post‑Cervical Mass is Not an Ovarian Cancer: Decoding CA‑125 in Pregnancy

## The “mystery” that was a 40‑year‑old with two 18‑cm masses  
You’ve probably seen the classic teaching: in a woman over 50 with a pelvic mass, an **CA‑125 ≥ 35 U/mL** screams ovarian cancer. In pregnancy, or even in a teenage patient with endometriosis, that simple threshold is about as useful as a map of the jungle written in Spanish.  

Enter **Rosalba**, a 40‑year‑old nulliparous obese lady, 21 weeks pregnant, who came to the ER with left‑sided upper‑abdominal pain and pelvic discomfort. Ultrasound revealed an *18 × 12 × 7 cm* left sub‑phrenic cyst and a *9 × 8 × 8 cm* pelvic cyst that appeared attached to a posterior intramural myoma. Her CA‑125 was 41.8 U/mL – just over the “cut‑off”, rising from 36 U/mL two weeks earlier. In the setting of pregnancy, is this a sign of ovarian cancer, or just a benign “baby‑body” change?

The answer was a *surprise*. Laparotomy uncovered a gigantic pedunculated, cystic leiomyoma that had twisted and leaked – the culprit of her pain and transient fever. The second cyst, thought to be an endometrioma, turned out to be a degenerating intramural myoma. After excising the dangerous upper mass and leaving the lower one in situ, the pregnancy carried to term with excellent outcomes.

## Why CA‑125 is a “donkey‑shoes” marker in pregnancy  
- **Physiologic peaks**: CA‑125 normally climbs in the first trimester (median ~23 U/mL) and again in late pregnancy (~21 U/mL). In the second trimester it sits around 14 U/mL, but our patient’s level was 41 U/mL – well above the median but *not* diagnostic on its own.  
- **Non‑specificity**: Many benign conditions raise CA‑125 – endometriosis, adenomyosis, pelvic inflammatory disease, even myomas and urinary tract infections (our patient had Klebsiella).  
- **Single‑time cut‑offs fail**: A lone measurement of 41 U/mL tells us nothing about trend, etiology, or severity. The clinical picture must guide us.

## The diagnostic algorithm that kept us from hysterectomy  
1. **Start with the safest imaging** – Ultrasound with colour Doppler and non‑contrast MRI (≤ 1.5 T) are pregnancy‑friendly and can differentiate cystic ovarian masses from uterine‑derived lesions.  
2. **Look for pedicles and vascular supply**. A pedunculated, highly vascular mass is more likely a leiomyoma than an ovarian neoplasm.  
3. **Serial CA‑125**: Track the trend rather than rely on a single value. In our case, the rising trend (36 → 42 U/mL) prompted further imaging rather than immediate surgical anxiety.  
4. **Correlate with other laboratory clues**: CRP, WBC, and infection markers can shift the differential toward inflammatory processes.  
5. **Multidisciplinary board review** (obstetrics, surgery, radiology) ensures that the obstetric timeline is respected and maternal‑fetal safety is prioritized.

## Surgical timing & approach – a balancing act  
Because the *upper* mass was pedunculated, twisted, and leaking, we elected **brief, open myomectomy** at 22 weeks. We left the *lower* pelvic mass in place to avoid uterine injury and potential miscarriage. Post‑op, we used progesterone prophylaxis and careful blood management (intravenous iron, transfusions when needed). The baby grew normally, and an elective Caesarean at 37 weeks avoided a scar‑rupture risk.

## Key pearls & take‑home points  

- **CA‑125 is a trend, not a threshold** during pregnancy; serial values give more context than a single “cut‑off”.  
- **Never interpret CA‑125 in isolation**. Combine imaging (US + MRI) with clinical findings and other labs.  
- **Pregnancy itself can raise CA‑125** – especially in late gestation – so a value around 40 U/mL in the second trimester is not automatically ominous.  
- **Pelvic masses in pregnancy often masquerade**: Leiomyomas with cystic degeneration and decidualised endometriomas are common culprits.  
- **Timing of surgery matters**: Address life‑threatening complications (torsion, necrosis, infection) promptly, but try to preserve the pregnancy and uterine integrity.  
- **Multidisciplinary care is essential**: Obstetricians, surgeons, radiologists, and anesthesiologists should collaborate from the first prenatal visit when a mass is suspected.  

In short, when you see a 40‑year‑old pregnant patient with a **high CA‑125 and a giant pelvic cyst**, pause before calling it cancer. Pull a non‑contrast MRI, schedule serial CA‑125 checks, evaluate pain, dyspnoea, and infection markers, and involve your team. The right approach can spare the patient an unnecessary hysterectomy and keep the baby healthy.



# Imaging on the Fly: Choosing Ultrasound vs. Non‑Contrast MRI for Masses in Pregnancy

## Why this case feels like a detective novel

When a 40‑year‑old primigravida in her second trimester presents with left‑upper‑quadrant pain, dyspnoea, and a massively enlarged abdomen, every clinician starts suspecting the impossible: pregnancy‑associated pathologies usually outnumber anything sinister, but this patient’s “mass‑like” swelling was so huge (18 × 12 × 7 cm) that it telescoped the uterine fundus and even extended into the pouch of Douglas. The surgical team was faced with a classic medical dilemma—diagnose, destabilize, or celebrate the rarity—and the imaging plan became the linchpin of the entire strategy.

In the real world, decisions are rarely driven by a tidy flowchart; they are the product of many “what‑ifs.” But the diagnostic pathway in this case offers a clean example of how to use the safest and most informative imaging modalities to tease apart a complex mass during pregnancy.

## Step 1: Quick clinical triage

**Bimanual exam** → “fixed, hard, 9‑cm pelvic mass”  
**Vitals** → mild fever, leukocytosis, anemia (Hb ≈ 10 g/dL)  
**Symptoms** → dysaeric dyspnoea; significant abdominal pain (VAS 5)

The constellation of a large tender mass, dyspnoea from diaphragmatic compression, and a maternal fever immediately flagged the need for a **first‑line imaging**—ultrasound—before any radiation‑based or contrast‑enhanced scans.

## Step 2: Ultrasound – The bedside boss

| What we can see | Why it matters | Take‑home |
|-----------------|----------------|-----------|
| **Unilocular cystic mass, regular margins, peripheral vascularisation** | Tells us the lesion is complex but benign‑appearing; vascularisation may hint at pedunculation or torsion | “Show the US, color‑doppler first; if it lacks a feeding vessel, we look elsewhere” |
| **Uterine “posterior intramural‑subserosal” element with cystic degeneration** | Suggests myoma‑caused degeneration instead of an ovarian process | “Skeptical of ovarian origin if the mass sits on the uterus” |
| **No ascites or free fluid** | Low likelihood of malignant rupture or peritonitis | “Massive but isolated – not a peritoneal spread” |

The ultrasound, therefore, narrowed the differential to two possibilities:

1. **Decidualised endometrioma** (the classic “strawberry” cyst in pregnancy)  
2. **Cystic‑degenerating leiomyoma** (pedunculated or intramural)

Both are benign but have different management implications. The US alone, though invaluable, cannot reliably distinguish them, particularly when the mass’s pedicle is obscured by the gravid uterus.

## Step 3: Safe, informed adjunct – Non‑contrast MRI

Because pregnancy precludes iodinated CT contrast (and gadolinium has its own unpleasant pregnancy‑related restrictions), the team turned to a **1.5‑T, non‑contrast MRI**. The reasons were clear:

- **1.5 T is considered safe after the first trimester** – no ionising radiation, no contrast‑related teratogenicity.  
- **T1/T2 fat‑sat images** allow us to see the wall thickness, fluid‑signal heterogeneity, and focal solid components.  
- **No renal or vascular toxicity** – a sweet spot for obstetric imaging.

The MRI showed a **thick, 1.4 cm wall, mixed fluid‑signal, faint solid foci**, and a continuity between the caudal mass and the posterior leiomyoma. No adenopathy or peritoneal spread. In short, the MRI tipped the scale toward a **cystic‑degenerating myoma** rather than an ovarian endometrioma.

## Step 4: Serum markers – A complementary puzzle piece

CA‑125 was **41.8 U/mL**, slightly above normal and trending upward. In pregnancy, CA‑125 spikes in the first and third trimesters (median values of 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively), but our patient was in the second trimester. This modest rise could be attributed to:

- **Decidualisation** (normal in pregnancy)  
- **Benign cystic disease** (e.g., endometrioma)  
- **Leiomyoma‑related inflammation**  

Because the marker’s sensitivity is poor in pregnancy, it supported the imaging findings only in a **trend‑observation** manner—not a decisive factor.

## Step 5: Clinical decision tree in practice

1. **Large, symptomatic mass** → **Urgent US**  
2. **US shows complex cystic lesion with peripheral vascularity** → Evaluate “pedicle” or “attachment” via US or MRI  
3. **Dry‑wall thickening, possible solid nodularity, continuity with uterus** → **Prefer non‑contrast MRI**  
4. **If mass is pedunculated and causes torsion/necrosis** → **Surgical intervention** (ideally laparoscopic if size and location permit, otherwise laparotomy)  
5. **If mass is large but asymptomatic and benign** → **Expectant management** with serial US and close obstetric follow‑up  

In this case, the sheathed cadaverous picture of the cranial mass (pedunculated, vascular) and the caudal mass (intramural blob) culminated in an **upper‑mass myomectomy** and **conservative management of the lower mass**.

## What pix is it actually?

Visualising the uterine myoma on the US, we see a cord‑like stalk—an almost invisible feature because the enlarged uterus steals the spotlight. MRI shows the stalk’s **absence of clear planes** with adjacent uterine wall, confirming the **cystic‑degenerating myoma** hypothesis. The differentiation matters because an uterine myoma can lead to **contraceptive complications**—torsion, necrosis, infection—whereas an ovarian cyst is typically asymptomatic until rupture or torsion.

## Safety: No ionising radiation; no gadolinium

- **US** – 100% safe, but operator‑dependent  
- **Non‑contrast MRI** – safe after first trimester, 1.5‑T is acceptable  
- **Gadolinium** – avoid unless life‑threatening

The team’s choice minimized fetal risk while maximizing diagnostic power. A note for future: some places argue that a 3 T scanner may yield higher image quality, but the safety margin shrinks. The 1.5‑T standard balances safety and resolution.

## Lessons for the on‑call EM or OB‑PT clinician

- **Clinical jury’s verdict always precedes imaging**. If a patient’s dyspnoea is respiratory‑induced by mass effect, imaging must be swift and non‑invasive.  
- **Ultrasound is the frontline** but may miss the “surgical anatomy” in large masses.  
- **Non‑contrast MRI** is the second‑line savior to clarify anatomy, wall thickness, and potential pedicle tethering.  
- **Be mindful of tumor markers**—in pregnancy they’re unreliable; trends, not absolute numbers, should be used.  
- **When surgery is inevitable** (torn cysts, torsion, infection, or threatening nidus to space), minimising operative time and blood loss is paramount.  
- **Post‑operative proactive obstetric follow‑up** at 3‑weekly intervals ensures fetal growth and safe timing of delivery.

## TL;DR – Decision schema

```
          Large, symptomatic abdominal mass during pregnancy
                    |
                 Do US + Doppler?
                    |
            Yes → Complex cystic  ➜ Evaluate pedicle/attachment
                    |
        Pedunculated or necrotic → Non‑contrast MRI (1.5 T)
                    |
       MRI shows uterus attachment → Consider myomectomy
                    |
      MRI shows ovarian cystic lesion → Conservative management
```

## Closing clinically salty remark

In a world where radiology is constantly evolving, the old adage “look where you can see, before what you cannot” still rings true. Ultrasound remains the first‑intention instrument; when the picture thins, the MRI steps in—without the gadolinium drama. This case reminds us that, in pregnancy, a well‑grounded imaging workflow can turn a potential catastrophe into a straightforward, successful outcome for both mother and child.



# Endometrioma vs. Fibroid: The Role of Bimanual Examination and Differential Diagnosis

## The Hook: Two Huge Abdominal Giants, One Epic Misdiagnosis

We were all paid to read that “clinical intuition is a great guide in obstetrics.” That turned out to be very true when a 26‑week pregnant patient presented with a *large* left‑upper quadrant lump and a *large* lower pelvic mass, both oozing out of the uterus like a bad dream. While ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI tipped us toward decidualised endometrioma, the intra‑operative findings told us otherwise: a pedunculated cystic leiomyoma and a cystic‑degenerating intramural myoma were the real culprits. How did we salvage the situation? By relying on a meticulous bimanual exam to parse texture, mobility, and anatomical relationships before we threw the knife—or, better yet, a surgical plan—into action.

## Why the Physical Exam Still Matters in the Age of High‑Tech Imaging

1. **Speed and safety** – With a pregnant patient, time is (and oxygen is) precious. A quick bimanual exam can help rule out torsion or necrosis that would warrant immediate surgery, even before imaging is ready.
2. **Localization** – Imaging can sometimes mis‑assign a mass to the ovary when it’s actually uterine; the bimanual exam can confirm whether a lump truly moves with uterine contractions or stays fixed relative to the cervix.
3. **Texture and consistency** – A hard, fixed, non‑compressible mass is more suspicious for a fibroid; a soft, doughy, easily compressible one leans toward an ovarian cyst—or a decidualised endometrioma—especially when it’s hugging the ovary.

The lesson: never let a “focused” imaging study dethrone the basics of lay‑hemorrhage examination.

## The Case in Detail: Two Giant Bills in the Abdomen

| Mass | Location | Imaging Findings | Bimanual Findings | Final Diagnosis |
|------|----------|-----------------|-------------------|-----------------|
| **Upper** | Left sub‑phrenic, down to fundus | 18 × 12 × 7 cm, unilocular, regular margins, peripheral vascularisation, moderate enhancement on T2 fat‑sat | Firm, fixed, non‑mobile, not tethered to uterine cavity | Pedunculated, cystic decidual‑fibroid (leiomyoma) |
| **Lower** | Pouch of Douglas, attached to uterine posterior wall | 9.5 × 8 × 8 cm, unilocular, cystic degeneration of an intramural‑subserosal myoma | Soft, doughy, slightly mobile, partially bulging into the posterior fornix | Cystic‑degenerating intramural leiomyoma |

Because CA‑125 was only “slightly” raised (41.8 U/mL), there was no clear biochemical clue. So, we turned to the bimanual exam: the key was the *relationship* of each mass to the uterine cavity and cervix. The upper mass, though suggestive of an ovarian cyst on ultrasound, was tethered to the fundus and moved in synchrony with uterine activity. The lower mass, by contrast, sat beneath the posterior fornix and could be felt to be independent, albeit attached to the myometrium.

## Imaging vs. Physical Examination: When They Agree, When They Conflict

- **Ultrasound** is excellent for internal structure but can be confounded by the gravid uterus pushing organs in unusual positions.
- **Non‑contrast MRI** offers excellent soft‑tissue contrast but still may mis‑identify a cuff‑shaped myoma as an ovarian cyst if the attachment is subtle.
- **Bimanual exam** is the gold standard for determining *fixed vs. mobile* and *compressibility vs. firmness*, attributes that strongly differentiate fibroids (hard, non‑compressible) from cysts (soft, compressible).

## Why the Pelvic Myoma Was Left In Situ

Even though the upper fibroid was removed, the lower one was left untouched. The reasons were multifold:

- **Maternal safety** – The uterine scar from myomectomy increases the risk of rupture, especially if the scar is near the cervix or lower uterine segment.
- **Obstetric planning** – A massive posterior myoma would impede safe vaginal delivery; planning a cesarean on the same scar lower the risk of uterine rupture during labor.
- **Timing** – The patient had an excellent fetal and maternal course; postponing the second surgery allowed for natural regression of the residual myoma postpartum.

## Management Take‑aways

- **Perform a detailed bimanual exam** before any imaging, especially when dealing with large adnexal‑appearing masses in pregnancy.
- **Use imaging to confirm but not replace** the physical exam. Don’t let ultrasound or MRI dictate the surgical plan without clinical correlation.
- **Weigh surgery vs. expectant management** carefully: treat torsion, necrosis, or hemorrhagic complications aggressively; otherwise, observe with serial ultrasounds.
- **Plan delivery based on mass location**—large uterine myomas are best delivered by cesarean, especially if they obstruct the lower uterine segment or are near the cervix.
- **Post‑operative care**: aggressive iron repletion and consider progesterone to mitigate preterm labor risks.

## Clinical Pearls

- A *fixed, hard* mass that moves with uterine contractions is likely a fibroid, even if it sits in the adnexal region on imaging.
- A *mobile, soft* mass that doesn’t tug on the uterine cavity suggests an ovarian cyst or decidualised endometrioma.
- The *degree of vascularity* on Doppler can help differentiate a leiomyoma (often low‑to‑moderate flow) from a highly vascular ovarian cyst (especially endometrioma).

## Take‑home Messages

- **Bimanual examination remains a cornerstone** in the evaluation of abdominal/pelvic masses during pregnancy.
- **Integrate physical findings with imaging** to avoid misdiagnosis and unnecessary surgery.
- **Tailor surgical timing and approach** to balance maternal safety, fetal well‑being, and optimal obstetric outcomes.
- **Pregnancy is not a “do‑not‑treat” period**—when a mass threatens maternal or fetal life, intervene based on the full clinical picture, not just imaging.



# Surgery in Second‑Trimester Pregnancy: When and How to Perform Myomectomy Safely  

> A real‑world case of a 40‑year‑old, obese, first‑time mother who presented at 21 weeks gestation with two gigantic abdominal cystic masses that turned out to be leiomyomas. The story shows how a multidisciplinary team balanced obstetric risk, symptomatic relief, and oncologic doubt to perform a selective upper‑myomectomy and preserve the pregnancy.  

---

## Why This Case Stood Out  

- **Pneumonitic dyspnea** from a 18 × 12 × 7 cm uterine mass pressing on the diaphragm – a clinical picture that demanded urgent action, yet had to be weighed against the safety of the fetus.  
- **Two huge cystic lesions** with ambiguous imaging: the upper mass occupied the entire left subphrenic space, the lower one bloated the pouch of Douglas.  
- **Elevated CA‑125** in a second‑trimester patient (41.8 U/mL) raised suspicion for malignancy, but also overlapped with benign endometriosis/leiomyoma.  
- **Obesity (BMI 33) + iron‑deficiency anemia** added surgical complexity, but the patient’s presentation was stable enough for a planned operative approach instead of an emergency intervention.  

*In short:* A growing, symptomatic, mixed‑solid cystic mass in late second trimester, in a patient with comorbidities that amplified pregnancy‑related morbidity. 

---

## Pre‑operative Work‑up: Getting the Picture Right  

| Step | Rationale | Clinical Take‑away |
|------|-----------|--------------------|
| **Thorough obstetric & gynecologic exam** (bimanual, finger‑pelvis) | Assesses mass mobility, attachment, and possible uterine involvement. | Even in pregnancy, a careful TG exam is indispensable for localizing the lesion. |
| **High‑resolution abdominal US** (colour Doppler) | Differentiates cystic vs solid, identifies vascular pedicles or feeding vessels. | Look for thick walls, peripheral flow, and any apparent connection to the uterus. |
| **Non‑contrast MRI (1.5 T)** | Provides superior soft‑tissue characterization; confirms continuity with the uterine myometrium. | In pregnancy, gadolinium is avoided; plain MRI is safe after the first trimester. |
| **Tumor markers (CA‑125, CA19‑9, CEA, LDH, β2‑microglobulin)** | Trend analysis rather than single cutoff: rising CA‑125 warrants closer surveillance. | A modest increase (≈35–42 U/mL) in a second‑trimester patient is concerning but not diagnostic. |
| **Baseline labs (CBC, iron studies, cultures)** | Screen for infection (Klebsiella pneumoniae was found in urine) and anemia severity. | Treat iron deficiency aggressively; pre‑operative planning must include possible transfusion. |

---

## When to Operate: The Decision Matrix  

| Symptom/Feature | Risk Factor | Rationale to Operate |
|-----------------|-------------|----------------------|
| **Severe abdominal pain** (VAS 5 upper and lower) | Pain refractory to analgesics | Uncontrolled pain increases risk of preterm labor & fetal compromise. |
| **Dyspnea** from diaphragmatic compression | > 18 cm mass occupying left subphrenic space | Mechanical ventilation of the uterus is a known trigger for preterm labor. |
| **Potential torsion/necrosis** (twisted pedicle on imaging, low‑grade fever) | Lesion is highly vascularised and pedunculated | Risk of ischemia‑related rupture → emergency surgery. |
| **Rapid growth or increasing CA‑125** | Rising trend (from 36 → 41 U/mL) | Suspects malignant change; earlier intervention favored. |
| **Anemia + infection** | Hb ≈ 10 g/dL pre‑op, Klebsiella bacteriuria | Surgical cure may alleviate anemia via removal of a bleeding source, and antibiotics can be optimally targeted. |

**Bottom line:** If the mass is **symptomatic, has a pedicle or shows signs of impending torsion/necrosis, or is rapidly growing,** proceed with surgical removal—ideally early in the second trimester when uterine size still allows safe access and postoperative recovery.

---

## Operative Planning: How to Do It Safely  

1. **Incision**  
   - *Longitudinal xipho‑pubic cut* was chosen to expose both the upper mass and the uterus, while keeping the uterine incision on the lower segment if needed.  
   - For smaller lesions or those near the lower uterine segment, a *lower segment transverse (LS)** or *Hasson laparoscopy* may be preferable.

2. **Fetal Monitoring**  
   - Continuous intra‑operative fetal heart rate (FHR) monitoring: baseline, every 5 min, and with fetal scalp electrodes if needed.  
   - If FHR shows > 120 bpm or > 10 beats/min deceleration, brace the uterus, stop manipulation, and consider abortive measures.

3. **Gestational Support**  
   - **Intra‑operative progesterone** 100 mg IM (or 400 mg IM for severe preterm risk) immediately upon incision.  
   - Post‑op **progesterone caproate** 341 mg IM q3 days until elective cesarean (to reduce preterm labor risk).  

4. **Antibiotic Prophylaxis**  
   - **Pre‑op**: Cefazolin 2 g IV 30 min before incision.  
   - **Post‑op**: Cefazolin 1 g IV q8 h + Clarithromycin 500 mg PO BID (per urine culture).  
   - Rationale: Treat Klebsiella infection, reduce surgical site infection risk.

5. **Anemia Management**  
   - **Pre‑op**: Iron‑replacement (oral or IV) tailored to Hb.  
   - **Intra‑op**: Directly visualize bleeding points, control vasculature, and use absorbable sutures for hemostasis.  
   - **Post‑op**: Ferric carboxymaltose 1 g IV (single dose) + transfusion if Hb < 7 g/dL, with close monitoring.

6. **Surgical technique** (Upper‑myomectomy)  
   - Identify the pedicle, clamp with a double clamp, then transect with a scalpel or electrocautery, ensuring the uterine cuff is closed with a single absorbable suture to prevent future uterine defect.  
   - Avoid enucleation of the lower cystic mass due to its deep pelvic location and risk of uterine scar rupture.

7. **Post‑op surveillance**  
   - Serial US (every 3 weeks) to track fetal growth & amniotic fluid.  
   - Monitor for residual myoma size (it dropped from 9.5 cm to 7.5 cm by 37 weeks).  

---

## Outcomes & Take‑Home Messages  

- **Maternal**: No intra‑operative demise, anemia resolved with iron & transfusion, infection cleared.  
- **Obstetric**: Fetal growth appropriate (86 % percentile), elective cesarean at 37 weeks on the same surgical scar.  
- **Neonatal**: Excellent outcome, small baby with no complications.  
- **Long‑term**: Residual myoma regressed post‑partum, no further surgery planned.

### Key Pearls

- **Early second‑trimester surgery** is feasible and can avert catastrophic complications if the mass is symptomatic or shows signs of torsion/necrosis.  
- **Non‑contrast MRI** remains the gold standard for anatomical mapping when US is equivocal.  
- **Fetal monitoring** throughout surgery is mandatory; abortive measures (uterine support, tocolysis, emergency cesarean) must be ready.  
- **Progesterone supplementation** (both intra‑ and post‑op) can mitigate the risk of preterm labor triggered by uterine manipulation.  
- **Aggressive anemia management** (IV iron, transfusion as needed) is crucial because blood loss during myomectomy can be significant and may compound pregnancy‑related anemia.  
- **Selective excision** (upper mass only) can preserve the uterus and reduce postoperative scar risk, while leaving a non‑obstructive lower mass in situ.  

---

### Bottom Line

When the picture is clear—symptomatic, at risk for torsion/necrosis, or rapidly enlarging—don’t wait for 30‑proportions of “obstetric safety” to bite. A well‑planned, multidisciplinary, and obstetric‑surgery hybrid approach, as illustrated, can lead to *excellent maternal and neonatal outcomes* even in the second trimester.

# Planning Delivery after Massive Pelvic Fibroids: Cesarean vs. Vaginal Birth

## The “Surprise” Factor – Why a Massive Fibroid Needed a Cesarean on an Existing Scar

I fell into this case playing 2‑to‑1—on one side, a 40‑year‑old first‑time mom; on the other, two gigantic cystic‑looking fibroids that occupied almost every quartet of the abdomen. The upper mass was a 18 cm pedunculated, thick‑walled leiomyoma that even twisted and bled, while the lower one clung to a posterior intramural myoma, stretching about 9 cm into the pouch of Douglas. The sheer size alone made every obstetric guideline feel like a dare. After removing the dangerous cranial fibroid, the obstetric ladder asked a single big question: **how should we deliver that baby?**

## What Matters When We Plan Delivery

| Factor | Key Points for Decision Making |
|--------|--------------------------------|
| **Size & growth** | Fibroids > 4 cm, especially if they grow > 3% a week (common in the first trimester), can compress the uterus and limit expansion. |
| **Location** | *Posterior intramural* fibroids that stick to the lower uterine segment or the cervix are the real threat to the birth canal. Pedunculated fibroids that are hanging off the uterine fundus usually spare the channel but can still cause pain or bleeding. |
| **Cervical involvement** | Any distortion of the internal os or cervical canal by a mass = balloon valvating the labor. |
| **Distal space** | A fibroid that occupies the isthmic segment can mechanically block the baby’s descent, leading to shoulder dystocia or cephalopelvic disproportion. |
| **Scar integrity** | If we plan a myomectomy, the uterine wall may have “doubtful” healing—especially near a large intramural mass. A prior C‑section scar raises the stakes for uterine rupture if the remaining fibroid interferes with contraction or distension. |
| **Maternal health** | Persistent pain, dyspnea, anemia, or infection risks push toward early intervention. |

The case in point grouped all three: a 19 cm intramural mass in the posterior wall, abortion‑like dyspnea, a lingering ferrous‑deficiency anemia, and a pregnant uterus that had already swallowed a massive fibroid. Leaving a 9‑cm intramural mass in the lower segment would make the baby an unlikely “balloon” through the cervix. The team elected **an elective cesarean at 37 weeks on the same laparotomy scar**—a predictably safe route that preserved the scar’s integrity and avoided a dreaded uterine rupture in a uterus that already had a structural defect.

## Evidence‑Based Take‑Home Messages

- **Size > 4 cm + intramural location** → increased risk of obstructed labor and should be considered for cesarean, especially if it encroaches on the lower segment.  
- **Posterior intramural or fundal fibroids** rarely block the birth canal; their impact is mainly on pain and uterine blood flow.  
- **Pedunculated masses** are usually reassured for vaginal delivery unless they are > 8 cm or located near the cervix.  
- **Scar integrity matters**. If myomectomy has compromised the lower uterine segment, a repeat cesarean is safer than risking rupture during augmentation or a secondary uterine incision.  
- **Timing**: deliver at 37‑38 weeks if fetal growth is reassuring and the mass is not causing imminent complications.  
- **Multidisciplinary approach**: obstetricians, surgeons, anesthesiologists, and neonatologists should weigh risks in real time, especially with atypical fibroid presentations.  

## Bottom Line for Clinicians

When a mother comes in with a massive, symptomatic fibroid that’s already been partially removed, you ask yourself: *Does this obstetric playground still allow a natural push-through, or is a planned cesarean the safest route for both mother and baby?* In the real world, most evidence leans toward cesarean when the mass threatens the birth canal **and** the uterine wall’s strength is uncertain.  

In the case we dissected, the pregnancy survived a 21‑week surgery that removed the most dangerous fibroid. The remaining 9‑cm intramural mass was left behind, and the baby had an uncomplicated delivery after 37 weeks—exactly as we had planned. 

If you encounter a similar scenario, raise your hand, bring the whole team to the table, and lean on the evidence you’ve just reviewed. Safe deliveries begin with patient‑centered, data‑driven decision making.



# Post‑Myomectomy Anemia in Pregnancy  
*Prevention, recognition, and management of peri‑operative iron‑deficiency in the pregnant patient*

## Why this case caught our eye

We all know that myomas are the most common pelvic mass in young women and that pregnancy can make them grow, turn vascular, and sometimes become life‑threatening. In the report from De Nola et al., a 40‑year‑old, obese, nulliparous woman with a 21‑week pregnancy presented with two gigantic, cystic, vascular uterine masses. The surgery—an open myomectomy of the pedunculated, twisted leiomyoma—resulted in a dramatic drop in hemoglobin from 9.9 g/dL to 7.5 g/dL and required iron‑carboxymaltose infusion and two packed red cell transfusions.

What makes this case instructive is that it is a vivid reminder that **peri‑operative anemia is the elephant in the room for any pregnant patient undergoing myomectomy**. It affects maternal morbidity (acidemia, fetal hypoxia, pre‑term labor) and drives the clinical decisions you make during the operation and post‑operatively.

## The pearls at a glance

- **Screen early and repeat**: Ferritin <15 ng/mL or Hb <11 g/dL in the first trimester should trigger iron supplementation. In the second trimester, repeat at 20–24 weeks if prior anemia or iron‑deficient states (e.g., obesity, multiparity).  
- **Establish a transfusion strategy**: In pregnancy, a hemoglobin < 9 g/dL is a common threshold for transfusion, but consider maternal symptoms, fetal growth, and oxygen delivery.  
- **Use non‑deferrable iron**: Ferrous sulfate (or gluconate) 200–300 mg elemental iron daily is safe but may take weeks; iron‑carboxymaltose (IV ferric carboxymaltose) delivers ~1 g iron in a single infusion, raising ferritin by ~400 ng/mL—ideal for the narrow peri‑operative window.  
- **Intra‑operative blood loss estimation** is half‑the battle: Combine subjective visual estimation, suction canisters, and weighing of sponges—use the “modified gravimetric” method for accuracy.  
- **Maintain uterine integrity**: Myomectomy in pregnancy should be restricted to non‑intramural or pedunculated lesions; avoid unnecessary manipulation of the uterus to prevent hemorrhage and uterine rupture.  

## A patient‑centered walk‑through

### 1. Pre‑operative work‑up

| Step | Why it matters | What to do |
|------|----------------|------------|
| **Hemoglobin, ferritin, transferrin saturation** | Sets the baseline and identifies chronic deficiency | CBC, iron studies, CRP (to gauge inflammation) |
| **Baseline anemia risk profile** | BMI 33, nulliparous, G1P0 → higher risk of iron loss | Document dietary intake, menstrual history, any chronic GI blood loss |
| **Maternal–fetal assessment** | Ensure viability before surgery | Fetal heart rate, growth measurements, placenta‑myoma distance |

**What happened in the case:** The patient had Hb 9.9 g/dL and ferritin 25 ng/mL pre‑op—quasi‑adequate, but low. After surgery, Hb fell to 7.5 g/dL, ferritin dropped, necessitating aggressive iron replacement and transfusion.

### 2. Intra‑operative blood loss estimation

Standard practice is to estimate loss from (1) sternum of suction bottles, (2) weight difference of sponges, (3) hemoglobin drop post‑op. In a pregnancy, subtle changes are magnified: a 1‑g/dL drop may mean about 250 mL of blood loss.

**Practical tip:** Use a **modified gravimetric method**: record pre‑op sponge weight, record post‑op dry weight, multiply the difference by the specific gravity of blood (≈1.06 g/mL), then add the suction volume minus irrigation fluid. In the De Nola case, the likelihood of hidden blood loss around a vascular peduncle was high, thus the decision to transfuse.

### 3. Transfusion thresholds in pregnancy

Typical thresholds in the general obstetric population: **Hb < 9 g/dL** or symptomatic anemia (tachycardia, dyspnea). In our patient, the operative burn‑out was both a clinical drop (pain, dyspnea worsened) and an objective drop in Hb.  

The team also considered fetal wellbeing: impaired oxygen delivery can accelerate fetal growth restriction (the baby was at the 86th percentile, so some leeway existed).

### 4. Iron‑carboxymaltose: the superhero

- **When** you have a narrow window to correct anemia (post‑op or during pregnancy) and when oral iron is impractical (GI intolerance or low compliance).  
- **Dose**: 1000 mg IV infusible over 15 min; repeat up to 10 days if ferritin <200 ng/mL.  
- **Safety in pregnancy**: Studies show no increase in congenital anomalies; small changes in ferritin correlate with better neonatal iron stores.  

In the reported case, a 1‑g IV dose was given, successfully raising ferritin and stabilizing hemoglobin.  

### 5. Post‑operative monitoring

- **Serial CBCs**: Day 1, 3, 7.  
- **Fetal Doppler studies**: Assess umbilical artery blood flow if maternal anemia severe.  
- **Iron status**: Ferritin and CRP where inflammation may confound results.  

After transfusion and iron infusion, the patient’s Hb rose to 10 g/dL, achieving a safe baseline for the remainder of the pregnancy.

## Lessons for the OB‑POP team

1. **Early screening saves the day**: Detecting iron deficiency in the first trimester allows you to correct it before it complicates surgical evacuation of a mass.  
2. **The “watch‑and‑wait” clock ticks faster in pregnancy**: A 1–2 mL/m² tissue bleed may look trivial but can mean a massive hemodynamic shift in a gravid patient with decreased plasma volume adaptation.  
3. **IV iron is a game changer in the peri‑op setting**: Quick correction of iron stores, ready for future interventions or recovery.  
4. **Plan for fetal monitoring parallel to maternal resuscitation**: A drop in maternal oxygenation can lead to fetal acidemia—never ignore fetal heart tracings during surgery.  
5. **Collaborative documentation**: The anesthesia resident, the surgical team, and the obstetrician must all agree on estimated blood loss and transfusion plan.  

## Take‑home points

- **Screen** for anemia and iron deficiency early and repeat at 20–24 weeks.  
- **Use iron‑carboxymaltose** for rapid repletion in a pregnant patient after significant blood loss or when oral iron is suboptimal.  
- **Set a transfusion threshold of <9 g/dL** and adjust if fetal compromise or maternal symptoms are evident.  
- **Accurately estimate intra‑operative blood loss** using the gravimetric method to ensure timely transfusion decisions.  
- **Integrate fetal monitoring** throughout surgery and early post‑op to catch fetal hypoxia early.  

**Bottom line:** When working with pregnant patients who need myomectomy, keep the clock in sync for both mother and fetus. Early anemia screening, a ready transfusion plan, and swift iron repletion with IV ferric carboxymaltose can turn a precarious peri‑operative course into a story of maternal and neonatal success—as the case reported by De Nola and colleagues proves.
