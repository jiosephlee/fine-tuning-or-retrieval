Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# Imaging on the Fly: Choosing Ultrasound vs. Non‑Contrast MRI for Masses in Pregnancy

## Why this case feels like a detective novel

When a 40‑year‑old primigravida in her second trimester presents with left‑upper‑quadrant pain, dyspnoea, and a massively enlarged abdomen, every clinician starts suspecting the impossible: pregnancy‑associated pathologies usually outnumber anything sinister, but this patient’s “mass‑like” swelling was so huge (18 × 12 × 7 cm) that it telescoped the uterine fundus and even extended into the pouch of Douglas. The surgical team was faced with a classic medical dilemma—diagnose, destabilize, or celebrate the rarity—and the imaging plan became the linchpin of the entire strategy.

In the real world, decisions are rarely driven by a tidy flowchart; they are the product of many “what‑ifs.” But the diagnostic pathway in this case offers a clean example of how to use the safest and most informative imaging modalities to tease apart a complex mass during pregnancy.

## Step 1: Quick clinical triage

**Bimanual exam** → “fixed, hard, 9‑cm pelvic mass”  
**Vitals** → mild fever, leukocytosis, anemia (Hb ≈ 10 g/dL)  
**Symptoms** → dysaeric dyspnoea; significant abdominal pain (VAS 5)

The constellation of a large tender mass, dyspnoea from diaphragmatic compression, and a maternal fever immediately flagged the need for a **first‑line imaging**—ultrasound—before any radiation‑based or contrast‑enhanced scans.

## Step 2: Ultrasound – The bedside boss

| What we can see | Why it matters | Take‑home |
|-----------------|----------------|-----------|
| **Unilocular cystic mass, regular margins, peripheral vascularisation** | Tells us the lesion is complex but benign‑appearing; vascularisation may hint at pedunculation or torsion | “Show the US, color‑doppler first; if it lacks a feeding vessel, we look elsewhere” |
| **Uterine “posterior intramural‑subserosal” element with cystic degeneration** | Suggests myoma‑caused degeneration instead of an ovarian process | “Skeptical of ovarian origin if the mass sits on the uterus” |
| **No ascites or free fluid** | Low likelihood of malignant rupture or peritonitis | “Massive but isolated – not a peritoneal spread” |

The ultrasound, therefore, narrowed the differential to two possibilities:

1. **Decidualised endometrioma** (the classic “strawberry” cyst in pregnancy)  
2. **Cystic‑degenerating leiomyoma** (pedunculated or intramural)

Both are benign but have different management implications. The US alone, though invaluable, cannot reliably distinguish them, particularly when the mass’s pedicle is obscured by the gravid uterus.

## Step 3: Safe, informed adjunct – Non‑contrast MRI

Because pregnancy precludes iodinated CT contrast (and gadolinium has its own unpleasant pregnancy‑related restrictions), the team turned to a **1.5‑T, non‑contrast MRI**. The reasons were clear:

- **1.5 T is considered safe after the first trimester** – no ionising radiation, no contrast‑related teratogenicity.  
- **T1/T2 fat‑sat images** allow us to see the wall thickness, fluid‑signal heterogeneity, and focal solid components.  
- **No renal or vascular toxicity** – a sweet spot for obstetric imaging.

The MRI showed a **thick, 1.4 cm wall, mixed fluid‑signal, faint solid foci**, and a continuity between the caudal mass and the posterior leiomyoma. No adenopathy or peritoneal spread. In short, the MRI tipped the scale toward a **cystic‑degenerating myoma** rather than an ovarian endometrioma.

## Step 4: Serum markers – A complementary puzzle piece

CA‑125 was **41.8 U/mL**, slightly above normal and trending upward. In pregnancy, CA‑125 spikes in the first and third trimesters (median values of 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively), but our patient was in the second trimester. This modest rise could be attributed to:

- **Decidualisation** (normal in pregnancy)  
- **Benign cystic disease** (e.g., endometrioma)  
- **Leiomyoma‑related inflammation**  

Because the marker’s sensitivity is poor in pregnancy, it supported the imaging findings only in a **trend‑observation** manner—not a decisive factor.

## Step 5: Clinical decision tree in practice

1. **Large, symptomatic mass** → **Urgent US**  
2. **US shows complex cystic lesion with peripheral vascularity** → Evaluate “pedicle” or “attachment” via US or MRI  
3. **Dry‑wall thickening, possible solid nodularity, continuity with uterus** → **Prefer non‑contrast MRI**  
4. **If mass is pedunculated and causes torsion/necrosis** → **Surgical intervention** (ideally laparoscopic if size and location permit, otherwise laparotomy)  
5. **If mass is large but asymptomatic and benign** → **Expectant management** with serial US and close obstetric follow‑up  

In this case, the sheathed cadaverous picture of the cranial mass (pedunculated, vascular) and the caudal mass (intramural blob) culminated in an **upper‑mass myomectomy** and **conservative management of the lower mass**.

## What pix is it actually?

Visualising the uterine myoma on the US, we see a cord‑like stalk—an almost invisible feature because the enlarged uterus steals the spotlight. MRI shows the stalk’s **absence of clear planes** with adjacent uterine wall, confirming the **cystic‑degenerating myoma** hypothesis. The differentiation matters because an uterine myoma can lead to **contraceptive complications**—torsion, necrosis, infection—whereas an ovarian cyst is typically asymptomatic until rupture or torsion.

## Safety: No ionising radiation; no gadolinium

- **US** – 100% safe, but operator‑dependent  
- **Non‑contrast MRI** – safe after first trimester, 1.5‑T is acceptable  
- **Gadolinium** – avoid unless life‑threatening

The team’s choice minimized fetal risk while maximizing diagnostic power. A note for future: some places argue that a 3 T scanner may yield higher image quality, but the safety margin shrinks. The 1.5‑T standard balances safety and resolution.

## Lessons for the on‑call EM or OB‑PT clinician

- **Clinical jury’s verdict always precedes imaging**. If a patient’s dyspnoea is respiratory‑induced by mass effect, imaging must be swift and non‑invasive.  
- **Ultrasound is the frontline** but may miss the “surgical anatomy” in large masses.  
- **Non‑contrast MRI** is the second‑line savior to clarify anatomy, wall thickness, and potential pedicle tethering.  
- **Be mindful of tumor markers**—in pregnancy they’re unreliable; trends, not absolute numbers, should be used.  
- **When surgery is inevitable** (torn cysts, torsion, infection, or threatening nidus to space), minimising operative time and blood loss is paramount.  
- **Post‑operative proactive obstetric follow‑up** at 3‑weekly intervals ensures fetal growth and safe timing of delivery.

## TL;DR – Decision schema

```
          Large, symptomatic abdominal mass during pregnancy
                    |
                 Do US + Doppler?
                    |
            Yes → Complex cystic  ➜ Evaluate pedicle/attachment
                    |
        Pedunculated or necrotic → Non‑contrast MRI (1.5 T)
                    |
       MRI shows uterus attachment → Consider myomectomy
                    |
      MRI shows ovarian cystic lesion → Conservative management
```

## Closing clinically salty remark

In a world where radiology is constantly evolving, the old adage “look where you can see, before what you cannot” still rings true. Ultrasound remains the first‑intention instrument; when the picture thins, the MRI steps in—without the gadolinium drama. This case reminds us that, in pregnancy, a well‑grounded imaging workflow can turn a potential catastrophe into a straightforward, successful outcome for both mother and child.
