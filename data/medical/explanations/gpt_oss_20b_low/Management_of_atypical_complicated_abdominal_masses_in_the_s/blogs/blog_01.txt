Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# When a ‘Big’ Post‑Cervical Mass is Not an Ovarian Cancer: Decoding CA‑125 in Pregnancy

## The “mystery” that was a 40‑year‑old with two 18‑cm masses  
You’ve probably seen the classic teaching: in a woman over 50 with a pelvic mass, an **CA‑125 ≥ 35 U/mL** screams ovarian cancer. In pregnancy, or even in a teenage patient with endometriosis, that simple threshold is about as useful as a map of the jungle written in Spanish.  

Enter **Rosalba**, a 40‑year‑old nulliparous obese lady, 21 weeks pregnant, who came to the ER with left‑sided upper‑abdominal pain and pelvic discomfort. Ultrasound revealed an *18 × 12 × 7 cm* left sub‑phrenic cyst and a *9 × 8 × 8 cm* pelvic cyst that appeared attached to a posterior intramural myoma. Her CA‑125 was 41.8 U/mL – just over the “cut‑off”, rising from 36 U/mL two weeks earlier. In the setting of pregnancy, is this a sign of ovarian cancer, or just a benign “baby‑body” change?

The answer was a *surprise*. Laparotomy uncovered a gigantic pedunculated, cystic leiomyoma that had twisted and leaked – the culprit of her pain and transient fever. The second cyst, thought to be an endometrioma, turned out to be a degenerating intramural myoma. After excising the dangerous upper mass and leaving the lower one in situ, the pregnancy carried to term with excellent outcomes.

## Why CA‑125 is a “donkey‑shoes” marker in pregnancy  
- **Physiologic peaks**: CA‑125 normally climbs in the first trimester (median ~23 U/mL) and again in late pregnancy (~21 U/mL). In the second trimester it sits around 14 U/mL, but our patient’s level was 41 U/mL – well above the median but *not* diagnostic on its own.  
- **Non‑specificity**: Many benign conditions raise CA‑125 – endometriosis, adenomyosis, pelvic inflammatory disease, even myomas and urinary tract infections (our patient had Klebsiella).  
- **Single‑time cut‑offs fail**: A lone measurement of 41 U/mL tells us nothing about trend, etiology, or severity. The clinical picture must guide us.

## The diagnostic algorithm that kept us from hysterectomy  
1. **Start with the safest imaging** – Ultrasound with colour Doppler and non‑contrast MRI (≤ 1.5 T) are pregnancy‑friendly and can differentiate cystic ovarian masses from uterine‑derived lesions.  
2. **Look for pedicles and vascular supply**. A pedunculated, highly vascular mass is more likely a leiomyoma than an ovarian neoplasm.  
3. **Serial CA‑125**: Track the trend rather than rely on a single value. In our case, the rising trend (36 → 42 U/mL) prompted further imaging rather than immediate surgical anxiety.  
4. **Correlate with other laboratory clues**: CRP, WBC, and infection markers can shift the differential toward inflammatory processes.  
5. **Multidisciplinary board review** (obstetrics, surgery, radiology) ensures that the obstetric timeline is respected and maternal‑fetal safety is prioritized.

## Surgical timing & approach – a balancing act  
Because the *upper* mass was pedunculated, twisted, and leaking, we elected **brief, open myomectomy** at 22 weeks. We left the *lower* pelvic mass in place to avoid uterine injury and potential miscarriage. Post‑op, we used progesterone prophylaxis and careful blood management (intravenous iron, transfusions when needed). The baby grew normally, and an elective Caesarean at 37 weeks avoided a scar‑rupture risk.

## Key pearls & take‑home points  

- **CA‑125 is a trend, not a threshold** during pregnancy; serial values give more context than a single “cut‑off”.  
- **Never interpret CA‑125 in isolation**. Combine imaging (US + MRI) with clinical findings and other labs.  
- **Pregnancy itself can raise CA‑125** – especially in late gestation – so a value around 40 U/mL in the second trimester is not automatically ominous.  
- **Pelvic masses in pregnancy often masquerade**: Leiomyomas with cystic degeneration and decidualised endometriomas are common culprits.  
- **Timing of surgery matters**: Address life‑threatening complications (torsion, necrosis, infection) promptly, but try to preserve the pregnancy and uterine integrity.  
- **Multidisciplinary care is essential**: Obstetricians, surgeons, radiologists, and anesthesiologists should collaborate from the first prenatal visit when a mass is suspected.  

In short, when you see a 40‑year‑old pregnant patient with a **high CA‑125 and a giant pelvic cyst**, pause before calling it cancer. Pull a non‑contrast MRI, schedule serial CA‑125 checks, evaluate pain, dyspnoea, and infection markers, and involve your team. The right approach can spare the patient an unnecessary hysterectomy and keep the baby healthy.
