Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a pregnant patient with a large abdominal cystic mass, how would you interpret a mildly elevated CA‑125 level, and what other conditions could explain this finding?

**Clinical Teaching Answer**

In pregnancy, a CA‑125 level that is only modestly above the laboratory upper limit is often a physiologic reflection of the gestation rather than an ominous sign of malignancy. In the case reported by De Nola *et al.*, a CA‑125 of 41.8 U/mL (cut‑off 35 U/mL) was noted at 21 weeks’ gestation and had risen from 36 U/mL two weeks earlier. Two points should guide interpretation:

1. **Pregnancy‑associated elevations** – CA‑125 peaks in the first trimester (median ≈ 23 U/mL) and again in the third trimester (≈ 21 U/mL). Values above 35 U/mL are uncommon in the second trimester, so any increase should prompt a focused work‑up, but the absolute rise is modest and parallels the gradual rise seen in this patient’s serial measurements.

2. **Benign conditions that raise CA‑125** – endometriosis, adenomyosis, pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, and, notably, uterine leiomyomas can elevate CA‑125. The authors explicitly cite that myomas may produce CA‑125 levels below 19 U/mL on average, yet individual variability is large. In this patient, imaging showed two large cystic lesions with thick, vascularised walls and focal solid components – features compatible with cystic‑degenerating leiomyomas as ultimately confirmed by pathology.

**Other benign explanations for a mild CA‑125 rise in pregnancy**

| Condition | Typical CA‑125 range | Why it fits the case |
|-----------|----------------------|----------------------|
| **Decidualised endometrioma** | 19–80 U/mL | Ultrasound described a unilocular cystic mass; CA‑125 was mildly increased. |
| **Cystic degeneration of a leiomyoma** | 10–40 U/mL | MRI showed thick walls, mixed T1/T2 signal, and focal solid areas—classical for cystic‑degenerating myoma. |
| **Pelvic inflammatory disease** | 35–200 U/mL | The patient had a low‑grade fever and Klebsiella bacteriuria, but peritoneal fluid was culture‑negative and inflammation markers were only modestly elevated. |
| **Adhesive disease (e.g., tubo‑ovarian abscess)** | 35–200 U/mL | No imaging evidence of abscess; CA‑125 modest. |
| **Early pregnancy (first or third trimester)** | 15–30 U/mL | Not applicable – patient was in second trimester. |

**Clinical Reasoning in the Report**

The multidisciplinary team weighed the slight CA‑125 rise against imaging data: two large, well‑circumscribed cystic masses with vascularisation and solid foci. Serial laboratory trends (41.8 U/mL increasing from 36 U/mL) suggested a growing process but remained within the benign range. Because the masses were symptomatic (pain, dyspnea) and posed a risk of torsion/necrosis, the team opted for surgical exploration. The intra‑operative findings confirmed that the upper mass was a pedunculated, cystic leiomyoma, while the lower mass was an intramural‑subserosal myoma with cystic degeneration. The pathology was benign.

**Bottom Line**

- A mildly elevated CA‑125 in the second trimester should be interpreted with caution; serial measurements and imaging are essential.
- Benign entities—especially uterine leiomyomas (cystic degeneration), decidualised endometriomas, and pelvic inflammatory disease—can mimic a malignancy‑driven rise.
- In the presented case, the imaging and clinical context supported a benign cystic‑degenerating leiomyoma as the source of the CA‑125 elevation.
