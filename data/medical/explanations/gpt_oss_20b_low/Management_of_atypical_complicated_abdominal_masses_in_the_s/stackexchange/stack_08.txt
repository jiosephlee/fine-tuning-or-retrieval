Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Clinical Pearls] Why might a pregnant woman with iron deficiency anemia have a normal ferritin level, and how should iron deficiency be interpreted in the context of anemia of pregnancy?

**Why a pregnancy‑associated iron‑deficiency anaemia can coexist with a “normal” ferritin level, and how we should interpret ferritin in this setting**

1. **Ferritin is an acute‑phase reactant.**  
   In the presence of infection (our patient had a Klebsiella urinary tract infection and a low‑grade fever) or any inflammatory process, IL‑6‑stimulated hepcidin up‑regulates ferritin synthesis. The net effect is that serum ferritin rises even when tissue iron stores are low. In the case report, ferritin was 25 µg/L (reference 15–300 µg/L). Though this value is within the “normal” laboratory range, it lies in the lower third of the reference interval and is perfectly compatible with iron loss or chronic blood loss that has not yet depleted storage enough to drop ferritin below the normal lower limit.  

2. **Pregnancy itself alters iron kinetics.**  
   • **Dilutional anaemia (physiologic haemodilution) raises plasma volume by ~30 %** while red‑cell mass rises by only ~10 %. This lowers haemoglobin and can mask the severity of iron deficiency.  
   • **Increased fetal iron demand (≈30 mg/day)** pulls iron from maternal stores in the second and third trimesters.  
   • **Hepcidin rises early in pregnancy** and then falls in the third trimester; the dynamic changes can cause transiently elevated ferritin despite ongoing iron loss.  

3. **The clinical picture must guide interpretation.**  
   * Hemoglobin 9.9 g/dL in a 21‑week gestation is below the trimester‑specific reference (≈11.5–12.5 g/dL) and indicates anaemia.  
   * Serum ferritin of 25 µg/L is low‑normal and, when combined with the Hb drop, strongly suggests iron‑deficiency anaemia.  
   * Inflammation can mask the true ferritin deficit; thus a ferritin <30 µg/L in a pregnant patient with anaemia should be treated as iron deficiency regardless of whether it is technically “normal.”  

4. **Diagnostic strategy in pregnancy**  
   * **Ferritin <30 µg/L**: treat as iron deficiency.  
   * **Ferritin 30–100 µg/L with anaemia**: repeat ferritin in 1–2 weeks after anti‑inflammatory or antibiotic therapy; if it remains <80 µg/L, iron deficiency is likely.  
   * **Concurrent CRP or ESR**: if these are elevated, assume an inflammatory contribution and proceed with iron therapy while treating the infection.  

5. **Management in the case report**  
   The patient’s Hb fell to 7.5 g/dL after surgery, prompting intravenous ferric carboxymaltose (1 g) and two transfusions. The low‑normal ferritin did not obviate treatment because the haemoglobin level and ongoing blood loss (surgical and ongoing infection) unequivocally indicated iron replacement is needed. The decision to give IV iron rather than oral iron was driven by the severity of anaemia and the need for rapid correction in a pregnant woman with expected haemodilution and ongoing bleeding risk.

**Bottom line:**  
In pregnancy, ferritin must be interpreted in the context of gestational age, haemoglobin, inflammatory markers and clinical state. A “normal” ferritin in an anemic pregnant patient—especially when <30 µg/L—should be treated as indicative of iron deficiency. Persistent low or low‑normal ferritin coupled with haemoglobin below trimester‑specific norms warrants iron supplementation, regardless of the formal reference range.
