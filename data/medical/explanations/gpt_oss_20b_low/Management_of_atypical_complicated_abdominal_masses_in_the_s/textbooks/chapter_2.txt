Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

Chapter 2: ## Diagnostic Imaging and Laboratory Workup

### Ultrasound technique and key findings  
During the acute assessment at 21 weeks’ gestation the obstetric sonographer performed a transabdominal scan equipped with colour‑Doppler in order to delineate the mass‐to‑uterus relationship and to assess vascularity. Two large, superficially unilocular cystic structures were identified: an upper lesion measuring 18 × 12 × 7 cm occupying the left sub‑phrenic space and extending to the uterine fundus, and a lower lesion of 9.5 × 8 × 8 cm located in the pouch of Douglas and adherent to a posterior intramural‑subserosal myoma. Both masses displayed regular, well‑defined margins and peripheral vascular signals on colour Doppler. The thickened wall of the caudal lesion and the presence of focal hyper‑vascular solid septations suggested a complex cyst rather than a simple serous cyst. The sonographic appearance of the upper mass—large, cystic, with a thick wall and peripheral blood flow—was suggestive of a decidualised adnexal cyst (endometrioma) but could also reflect a cystic degeneration of a pedunculated myoma.

### Bimanual examination and pelvic mass description  
A vaginal palpation revealed a fixed, rounded, hard mass approximately 9 cm in diameter located in the lesser pelvis, bulging posteriorly into the posterior fornix. The firmness and lack of mobility indicated an adnexal mass that was unlikely to be a simple fluid collection and hinted at a fibrotic or fibrodysplastic origin, such as a leiomyoma or a thick‑walled endometrioma. The palpable mass coincided with the lower cystic lesion seen on ultrasound, reinforcing the idea of a close relationship between the cyst and the underlying myoma.

### Non‑contrast MRI: technique, safety in pregnancy, and signal characteristics  
Given the large size of the cystic lesions and the need to better delineate their relationship to the uterus, a 1.5‑T MRI without gadolinium was performed after the first trimester. The protocol included T1‑weighted, T2‑weighted, and T2 fat‑saturation sequences. The safety profile of non‑contrast MRI in pregnancy is well established; absence of ionising radiation eliminates fetal risk, and gadolinium is avoided unless absolutely necessary due to concerns about fetal gadolinium deposition.

On MRI, both lesions appeared round and unilocular. The upper mass exhibited a thick (≈1.4 cm) wall and inhomogeneous fluid content that was hyperintense on T1‑weighted images and hyperintense on T2‑fat‑sat sequences, with focal solid‑appearing components that were T1‑short‑T2‑long. This signal pattern is typical of a cystic myoma with degenerated blood products or of an endometrioma, as both can contain sub‑acute blood. The caudal mass showed a similar appearance but was contiguous with the posterior intramural‑subserosal leiomyoma noted on ultrasound; the lack of a discrete capsule separating the cyst from the uterine wall suggested myofibroblastic tissue rather than a true parietal ovarian cyst. Neither mass displayed the diffusion restriction or the solid nodularity characteristic of malignant tumours. Importantly, no adenopathy or extra‑uterine spread was seen.

### Tumour marker profile and its gestational interpretation  
Serum tumour markers were obtained: CA‑125 was 41.8 U/mL (normal <35 U/mL) with a rising trend from 36 U/mL two weeks earlier; CAC 19‑9, CA 15‑3, CEA, β‑2‑microglobulin, and LDH were all within normal limits. During pregnancy, CA‑125 normally peaks in the first and third trimesters (median 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively) and remains lower in the second trimester (median ≈14 U/mL). A level of 41.8 U/mL in the second trimester is therefore above the expected physiological range and may reflect a substantial epithelial surface area of a large cystic mass, as seen in endometriomas or large leiomyomas undergoing degeneration. Importantly, the absence of other oncologic markers and the favourable imaging features argued against a malignant process.

### Integration of imaging and serology into a narrowed differential  
The convergence of clinical, sonographic, magnetic‑resonance, and serologic data allowed the multidisciplinary team to focus the differential diagnosis on:

1. **Decidualised endometrioma** – large, thick‑walled cystic lesions with peripheral vascularisation and a CA‑125 rise fit this profile; however, the histological continuity with the myoma on MRI was inconsistent with a purely ovarian process.

2. **Cystic‑degenerating leiomyoma** – the upper mass’ vascularity, thick wall, and CA‑125 increase could be explained by hemorrhagic or myxoid degeneration of a pedunculated subserosal myoma; the lower mass’ direct attachment to a known intramural‑subserosal leiomyoma reinforced this possibility.

3. **Pseudo‑cystic ovarian tumour** – a rare ovarian carcinoma variant could present as a thick‑walled cyst, but the lack of solid nodularity, diffusion restriction, or elevated CA‑125/other markers made this less likely.

Given the significant size of the masses, the symptomatic burden, and the risk of torsion, rupture, or infection, the team elected to proceed with surgical excision of the upper mass while leaving the lower lesion in situ. This decision balanced the need for definitive diagnosis with the imperative to preserve the ongoing pregnancy.
