Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

Chapter 4: ## Surgical Management and Peri‑operative Care

### Indications for surgery in the second trimester  

The patient’s clinical deterioration—persistent, severe abdominal pain, dyspnoea from diaphragmatic compression, and a low‑grade fever suggestive of infection—constituted a clear operative indication. In pregnancy, surgery is reserved for circumstances that pose an imminent threat to the mother, fetus, or both. Complications such as torsion, rupture, or infection of a large adnexal or uterine mass, as well as progressive mass size that can compromise fetal growth or future labour, warrant intervention despite the inherent risks of operative anaesthesia during the second trimester. The decision in this case balanced the risk of a large, highly vascularised lesion against the potential for preterm delivery, maternal morbidity, and fetal compromise.

### Choice of laparotomy versus laparoscopy in large intramural masses  

Large intramural or pedunculated myomas occupying the abdominal cavity and crossing the uterine fundus are difficult to retrieve laparoscopically, especially when the uterus is displaced by the uterus itself and the mass exerts pressure on distant structures. In the present case, the upper mass measured 18 × 12 × 7 cm and extended to the uterine fundus, while the lower mass occupied the pouch of Douglas. Laparotomy provided direct access, rapid control of a potentially bleeding pedicle, and immediate visualization of fetal cardiac activity. It also allowed a quick decision to leave the large caudal mass in situ, thereby avoiding extensive dissection that could jeopardise the uterine scar or result in uterine rupture. In contrast, laparoscopy would have required a mini‑umbilical incision, insufflation that could worsen maternal dyspnoea, and longer operative times for large masses—factors that outweigh the minimally invasive benefits in this scenario.

### Intra‑operative assessment of the cranial mass pedicle and vascularity  

During entry, the caudal mass was noted to be fixed and solid, suggesting an intramural subserosal orientation. The cranial mass, however, appeared as a pedunculated cystic lesion attached to the anterior uterine wall. Careful exploration of the pedicle revealed a thick, twisted stalk that supplied the mass with abundant blood flow—a classic risk factor for torsion and haemorrhage. The presence of a free‑flipping pedicle, coupled with the detection of local inflammatory changes (irregular wall thickening and vascularization), made it imperative to proceed with myomectomy to prevent sudden haemorrhage or rupture, as the patient’s fever and inflammatory markers implied possible infection of the broken pedicle.

### Decision to leave the caudal mass in situ  

The caudal lesion’s attachment to the posterior intramural‑subserosal myoma and its smooth, cystic appearance suggested that it would not pose an acute threat during the operative window. Removing it would have required extensive manipulation of the posterior uterine wall, potentially damaging the adjacent myometrium and increasing the risk of postoperative bleeding, uterine scar weakness, and future recurrence of myoma. Furthermore, preserving the mass maintained the pelvis’s capacity to accommodate fetal descent in later gestation. Therefore, the multidisciplinary team opted for a conservative approach: leave the caudal mass intact, monitor it serially, and plan for elective caesarean delivery once the fetus reached term.

### Use of progesterone and antibiotic prophylaxis  

Because the surgery was performed in the second trimester and involved a uterine incision, postoperative luteal support with progesterone was instituted to reduce the risk of miscarriage and to honour early obstetric guidelines that recommend supplemental progesterone after uterine surgery in pregnancy. Intramuscular progesterone (100 mg daily) was given intraoperatively and continued until discharge, followed by a sustained‑release formulation (341 mg every three days) until delivery to ensure placental luteal support.

Antibiotic prophylaxis followed standard protocols for uterine surgery. A 2 g intramuscular dose of first‑generation cephalosporin (cefazolin) was given at induction, then continued intravenously postoperatively. This was complemented by clarithromycin (500 mg twice daily) based on urine culture sensitivity, targeting the Klebsiella pneumoniae infection that the patient had developed. The dual strategy addressed both peri‑operative surgical contamination risk and the pre‑existing urinary tract infection.

### Blood loss management and transfusion criteria  

Hemoglobin dropped from 9.9 g/dL pre‑operatively to 7.5 g/dL post‑operatively, reflecting operative blood loss and pre‑existing iron‑deficiency anemia. The threshold for transfusion in pregnancy varies but is often set at Hb < 7.0 g/dL for uncomplicated cases, with a more permissive threshold (Hb < 8.0 g/dL) for patients with cardiac comorbidities or ongoing bleeding. In this case, the patient received two units of packed red cells to maintain Hb ≥ 10.0 g/dL, aligning with obstetric recommendations for maternal safety and optimal fetal oxygenation. Simultaneous intravenous iron therapy (ferric carboxymaltose 1 g) addressed the underlying iron deficit, preventing postoperative anaemia and facilitating recovery.

Overall, the surgical plan combined a rapid, open approach to the risky cranial pedunculated mass, conservative management of the stable caudal lesion, and comprehensive peri‑operative care—including progesterone support, targeted antibiotics, and judicious transfusion—to safeguard maternal and fetal outcomes.
