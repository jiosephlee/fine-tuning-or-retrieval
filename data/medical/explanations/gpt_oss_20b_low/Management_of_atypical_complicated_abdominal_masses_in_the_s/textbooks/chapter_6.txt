Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

Chapter 6: ## Clinical Lessons, Guidelines and Future Directions

### Risk assessment framework for abdominal masses in pregnancy
Key to any management plan is a systematic risk stratification that incorporates **size, location, growth dynamics, symptom burden, and potential for malignant transformation**. The present case illustrates the practicality of a layer‑based approach: (1) **clinical presentation** (pain, dyspnoea, fever), (2) **imaging characteristics** (unilocular cystic vs. solid components, vascularity, continuity with myometrium), (3) **serum biomarkers** (CA‑125 trend, other tumour markers), and (4) **potential obstetric impact** (uterine enlargement, placental interface, fetal head‑uterus relationship). A structured assessment enables objective discussion in the multidisciplinary setting and aligns surgical timing with fetal maturity.

### Multidisciplinary decision‑making processes
The multidisciplinary team (MDT) in this case comprised obstetricians, gynaecologic surgeons, radiologists, anaesthetists, and neonatologists. Evidence suggests that **MDT deliberations within 24 h of mass identification improve maternal‑fetal outcomes** (e.g., reduced need for emergent intervention, optimized timing of delivery). Core decision points include:
- **Diagnostic certainty**: non‑contrast MRI clarifies origin when ultrasound is equivocal.
- **Maternal symptom severity**: significant pain or dyspnoea warranted early surgical exploration.
- **Fetal viability and development**: ongoing fetal monitoring during surgery is indispensable.
- **Surgical risk vs. obstetric benefit**: in this case, removal of the pedunculated upper mass mitigated imminent torsion/rupture risk without jeopardising the pregnancy.

Future MDT models should incorporate *structured communication tools* (e.g., SBAR) and *real‑time intra‑operative fetal monitoring* protocols to streamline deliberations and enhance safety.

### Safety of non‑contrast MRI and surgery timing
The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) and the European Society of Radiology endorse non‑contrast MRI for abdominal lesions in the second trimester, provided the magnetic field strength ≤ 1.5 T. In this report, the MRI provided critical diagnostic detail that altered surgical planning. The **surgery was performed at 21 + 6 weeks**, a time when **the placenta has typically implanted in the lower uterine segment**, reducing the risk of placental transection during uterine entry. The case confirms that, with careful planning, **early second‑trimester myomectomy is feasible and can be performed safely** when the mass poses imminent risk.

### Impact of myoma size and location on mode of delivery
Large intramural or cervical‑prominent myomas can compromise fetal head descent and create obstetric dystocia. In the present case, a 9‑cm posterior intramural‑subserosal mass was left in situ; the obstetric team elected a **planned caesarean at 37 weeks** to avoid obstructed labour and uterine scar rupture. Current guidelines (ACOG Committee Opinion No. 603) recommend caesarean for myomas > 5 cm that directly obstruct the lower uterine segment, while **smaller, non‑obstructive myomas may permit vaginal birth** given appropriate monitoring and the availability of vacuum or forceps. The case underscores the need for **size‑specific delivery algorithms** and the incorporation of **leukocyte‑differentiated myoma profiles** (e.g., vascularity, location) into delivery planning.

### Potential benefits of conservative vs surgical approaches
Conservative management remains the default for asymptomatic, non‑complicated masses. However, this case demonstrates that **early surgical intervention can avert life‑threatening complications** (torsion, rupture, infection) and potentially reduce preterm birth risk. Comparative data are limited but emerging evidence suggests that **selective myomectomy during pregnancy may shorten hospital stays and reduce neonatal morbidity** when performed by experienced teams. Conversely, surgery carries inherent risks of haemorrhage, uterine rupture, and preterm labour. Decision‑making should balance these factors using a **shared‑decision model** that incorporates patient values and preferences.

### Recommendations for clinical practice and research priorities
1. **Develop a standardized risk‑assessment tool** combining imaging, biomarkers, and symptomatology to guide MDT discussions in pregnant patients with abdominal masses.
2. **Adopt non‑contrast MRI protocols** (≤ 1.5 T, fat‑suppressed T1 & T2) for evaluation of complex adnexal or myometrial lesions during the second trimester, and audit outcomes to refine safety profiles.
3. **Institute real‑time intra‑operative fetal monitoring** during any abdominal surgery in pregnancy, as recommended by ACOG and International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO).
4. **Create evidence‑based delivery algorithms** stratified by myoma size, location, and compliance with the uterine cavity, incorporating options for vacuum, forceps, or caesarean.
5. **Fund multicentre prospective registries** capturing outcomes of conservative vs. surgical management of massive ovarian or myometrial lesions in pregnancy to inform future guidelines.
6. **Encourage translational research** on the pathophysiology of myoma growth dynamics during pregnancy, focusing on hormonal, vascular, and genetic drivers that may predict rapid expansion or degeneration.
7. **Policy development** should aim to streamline access to high‑quality imaging and surgical expertise in tertiary centres, ensuring equitable management for women with complex abdominal masses during gestation.

By integrating these lessons into routine obstetric‑gynecologic care, clinicians can better navigate the delicate balance between maternal safety and fetal wellbeing in the context of atypical abdominal masses in pregnancy.
