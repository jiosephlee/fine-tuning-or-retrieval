Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

## 1. Clinical Presentation and Initial Evaluation

The patient is a 22‑year‑old male who was referred for evaluation after a routine health‑check revealed hyperglycaemia and glycosuria. His body mass index (BMI) of 24.6 kg/m² places him in the normal‑to‑upper‑normal range for his age, and his blood pressure (112/70 mm Hg) and resting heart rate (78 bpm) are within normal limits, indicating the absence of overt hypertension or tachycardia that often accompanies insulin‑resistant states. Physical examination did not identify acanthosis nigricans or other cutaneous stigmata of insulin resistance.

The symptomatic profile consisted of classic diabetes manifestations: increased thirst (polydipsia), increased appetite (polyphagia), and recent unintentional weight loss. These findings, together with the laboratory evidence of significant hyperglycaemia, raised suspicion for a non‑typical diabetic phenotype. Notably, there was no documentation of diabetic ketoacidosis, and mental status exam was unremarkable, ruling out an acute endocrine crisis.

Family history revealed a triad of relatives with early‑onset diabetes: the maternal grandmother diagnosed in her 30s, the mother in her late 20s, and a younger brother at age 20. Such a strong, autosomal dominant pattern strongly implicates a monogenic form of diabetes rather than type 2 diabetes, which usually manifests later and with obesity. No family members reported early‑onset cardiovascular disease, which can sometimes accompany genetic diabetes syndromes.

Baseline laboratory studies were striking. Glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) was markedly elevated at 10.2 % (88 mmol/mol), and the fasting plasma glucose measured 12.4 mmol/L (223 mg/dL). The 2‑hour 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) was particularly abnormal, with a glucose of 19.8 mmol/L (356 mg/dL). The combination of a high HbA1c, fasting hyperglycaemia, and post‑prandial glucose surge exceeds the diagnostic thresholds for diabetes in all established guidelines and suggests substantial beta‑cell dysfunction.

Additional laboratory data underscored metabolic derangements. Glycosuria was detected, consistent with a lowered renal threshold for glucose that occurs in many MODY subtypes, including HNF1A‑MODY. Lipid profiling revealed a low high‑density lipoprotein (HDL) of 1.01 mmol/L and a markedly elevated triglyceride level of 4.31 mmol/L, pointing to dyslipidaemia. While the patient’s BMI is not overly elevated, the lipid abnormalities and hyperglycaemia meet the American Heart Association criteria for metabolic syndrome when combined with glucose intolerance. Thus, there is a detectable early‑onset metabolic syndrome phenotype, albeit milder than that seen in classic type 2 diabetes.

Taken together, the age‑appropriate onset of diabetes, the preserved lean body habitus, the strong family history, and the laboratory evidence of isolated hyperglycaemia with a prominent post‑prandial component create a clinical picture that is atypical for both type 1 and type 2 diabetes. This prompted further investigative steps, including pancreatic beta‑cell functional testing (glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide), autoantibody screening, and ultimately, molecular genetic evaluation for MODY.

## 2. Differential Diagnosis and Diagnostic Work‑up

Diagnosis of diabetes in a young adult who presents with hyperglycaemia and glycosuria requires systematic exclusion of the two common phenotypes—type 1 and type 2—before a monogenic etiology such as MODY is considered. The decision to investigate for MODY in this case was guided by a constellation of clinical and biochemical findings that diverged from the classic presentation of both type 1 and type 2 diabetes.

### 2.1 Distinguishing Features of Type 1, Type 2 and MODY

| Feature | Type 1 Diabetes | Type 2 Diabetes | MODY (e.g., HNF1A‑MODY) |
|---------|-----------------|-----------------|------------------------|
| Age of onset | Usually <25 y, often <15 y | >35 y (though increasingly earlier) | <25 y (often late teens/early 20s) |
| Body habitus | Lean or normal BMI | Often overweight/obese | Normal BMI (± modest obesity) |
| Etiology | Autoimmune β‑cell destruction | Insulin resistance + β‑cell failure | Monogenic mutation impairing β‑cell function |
| Clinical picture | Rapid onset, polydipsia, polyuria, weight loss | Gradual, often asymptomatic, metabolic syndrome | Asymptomatic or mild symptoms, may be discovered at routine screening |
| Autoantibodies | Positive (GADA, IA‑2A, ZnT8, IAA) | Negative | Negative in most cases |
| C‑peptide | Low (fasting and stimulated) | Preserved or high | Variable; often moderately reduced |
| Family history | Not typically autosomal dominant | May be present but not strongly inheritance pattern | Typical autosomal dominant pedigree with early‑onset diabetes |

In the present patient, the absence of classic autoimmune markers, the presence of a strong three‑generation family history of early‑onset diabetes, a normal BMI (24.6 kg/m²), and a markedly elevated post‑prandial glucose (19.8 mmol/L at 2 h OGTT) prompted consideration of a monogenic form rather than type 1 or type 2 disease.

### 2.2 Autoantibody Panel

The autoimmune panel is the first laboratory step to rule out type 1 diabetes. The patient underwent testing for:

- **Glutamic acid decarboxylase autoantibodies (GADA)**  
- **Insulinoma antigen‑2 autoantibody (IA‑2A)**  
- **Zinc transporter‑8 autoantibody (ZnT8)**  

All results were negative. In an otherwise typical phenotype, negative antibodies strongly reduce the likelihood of type 1 diabetes, allowing the clinician to focus on alternative causes such as MODY.

### 2.3 C‑peptide Measurements

C‑peptide reflects endogenous insulin secretion. Two approaches were used:

1. **Fasting C‑peptide**: 0.39 nmol/L at baseline.  
2. **Stimulated C‑peptide** (glucagon test): 0.83 nmol/L at 6 min.

A stimulated value >0.60 nmol/L is generally considered evidence of preserved β‑cell function. The patient’s level, while low, exceeded this threshold, supporting a diagnosis of non‑type 1 diabetes and arguing against advanced type 1 disease where C‑peptide would be <0.10 nmol/L. This finding, combined with negative autoantibodies, bolstered the suspicion of HNF1A‑MODY.

### 2.4 Glucagon Stimulation Test Interpretation

The glucagon test provides a robust assessment of β‑cell reserve. In healthy individuals, glucagon stimulates hepatic glycogenolysis and counter‑regulatory hormone release, leading to a rise in plasma C‑peptide. A typical cut‑off used in clinical practice is ≥0.60 nmol/L at 6 min. The patient’s 0.83 nmol/L indicates **insufficient yet not absent** insulin secretion, a pattern commonly seen in HNF1A‑MODY. This result helped exclude severe β‑cell failure seen in type 1 diabetes and suggested a functional defect rather than a qualitative absence of β‑cells.

### 2.5 Renal and Autonomic Function Tests

HNF1A mutations can affect extra‑pancreatic tissues, leading to specific renal and autonomic abnormalities:

- **Microalbuminuria**: Negative in this patient, indicating early renal involvement had not yet manifested. The absence of microalbuminuria is not diagnostic but is part of surveillance.
- **Sudomotor testing (sudomotor function via electrochemical skin conductance)**: Severely reduced conductance in the hands—an indicator of small‑fiber autonomic neuropathy. This finding is more consistent with a genetic syndrome affecting renal tubular and autonomic regulation, as seen in HNF1A‑MODY, rather than the relative absence of autonomic dysfunction in early type 2 diabetes or its variable presence in type 1 diabetes.

### 2.6 Use of MODY Probability Calculators and Their Limitations

Clinicians often employ validated risk calculators (e.g., the Exeter calculator) that combine clinical variables—age at diagnosis, BMI, C‑peptide, family history, presence of ketosis—to estimate the probability of MODY. In this case, applying the calculator yielded a probability of **62.4 %**, supporting genetic testing.

However, limitations exist:

- **Population bias**: The calculator was derived from predominantly White, European cohorts, potentially over‑estimating MODY probability in non‑European populations.  
- **Variable phenotype overlap**: Many type 2 patients with early onset and a family history of diabetes can still score high.  
- **Exclusion of renal and autonomic data**: The calculator does not incorporate SGLT2‑related glycosuria or sudomotor function, which may provide additional discriminative power for HNF1A‑MODY.

Despite these caveats, the high score, combined with negative autoantibodies and preserved β‑cell function, justified pursuing NGS panel testing for MODY genes.

### Summary

The differential diagnosis hinged on a systematic assessment of autoantibodies, β‑cell reserve, family history and ancillary renal/autonomic findings. Negative autoimmune markers, moderately preserved stimulated C‑peptide, a strong dominant pedigree, and specific autonomic dysfunction collectively steered the clinicians toward a monogenic etiology. The subsequent genetic confirmation of a novel HNF1A mutation validated this diagnostic pathway and enabled precision‑guided therapy.

## 3. Genetic Confirmation and Pathogenicity Assessment

A definitive diagnosis of MODY requires identification of a deleterious variant in a MODY‑associated gene. In this case, a targeted next‑generation sequencing (NGS) panel encompassing the 15 genes most frequently implicated in MODY (HNF1A, HNF1B, GCK, HNF4A, PDX1, HNF1A, and others) was employed. The panel was designed to cover all coding exons and flanking intronic regions of each gene, ensuring adequate depth (average >200×) for reliable heterozygous variant detection. Capture probes were customized to include known pathogenic hotspots and to allow the identification of novel missense changes.

Sequencing of the patient’s genomic DNA revealed a heterozygous single‑nucleotide substitution in exon 4 of HNF1A: c.809A>G, which results in the amino‑acid change p.N270S. The variant was present in a single allele (heterozygosity) and absent in the patient’s unaffected sister, indicating segregation with disease rather than a germline polymorphism. No other pathogenic variants were detected in the remaining MODY genes on the panel.

The affected residue lies within the DNA‑binding domain (DBD) of the HNF1A protein, a highly conserved region responsible for transcriptional regulation of downstream targets such as the GLUT2 transporter and insulin promoter. Missense changes in the DBD are the most common cause of HNF1A‑MODY and typically impair DNA binding affinity or transactivation potential, thereby reducing β‑cell function.

To classify the variant’s clinical significance, the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) framework was applied. The following criteria were satisfied:

- **PM2 (Absent from controls)** – The variant was not found in population databases (gnomAD, ExAC) and had an allele frequency <0.01 % in any subpopulation.
- **PM3 (Observed in trans for recessive disorders, or in heterozygous state for dominant disorders)** – In the context of a dominantly inherited disease, the heterozygous state satisfies PM3.
- **PP1 (Co‑segregation with disease in multiple affected family members)** – The variant co‑segregated with diabetes in at least three generations, including the patient’s mother and brother.
- **PP3 (In silico evidence supportive of deleterious effect)** – Predictive algorithms (PolyPhen‑2, SIFT, CADD) all indicated a damaging impact, with a high AlphaMissense pathogenicity score (0.995).
- **PP4 (Patient’s phenotype highly specific for a disease with a single genetic etiology)** – The clinical presentation (early‑onset diabetes, negative autoantibodies, preserved C‑peptide, glycosuria) is highly characteristic of HNF1A‑MODY.

No conflicting evidence (e.g., functional studies showing normal activity or database annotation as benign) was identified. According to ACMG, the combination of one moderate (PM2) and three supporting criteria (PM3, PP1, PP3, PP4) classifies the variant as “likely pathogenic.” A “pathogenic” designation would require one additional moderate or strong criterion, which was not met at the time of analysis.

Database interrogation confirmed that the variant had not been previously reported in ClinVar, VarSome, or the ClinGen repository, establishing its novelty. Segregation analysis demonstrated absence of the variant in all unaffected family members, supporting its pathogenic role.

From a precision‑medicine standpoint, recognition of a likely pathogenic HNF1A mutation informs therapeutic choices: sulfonylurea sensitivity, GLP‑1 receptor‑agonist tolerance, and avoidance of SGLT2 inhibitors due to risk of ketoacidosis. Moreover, identification of a pathogenic HNF1A allele prompts cascade screening of relatives, allowing early intervention before overt hyperglycaemia develops.

## 4. Therapeutic Strategy and Clinical Management

The patient’s initial presentation—marked hyperglycaemia (HbA1c 10.2 %, fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L) and a 2‑hour OGTT glucose of 19.8 mmol/L—necessitated prompt pharmacologic intervention. At the time of diagnosis, the precise etiology of diabetes was uncertain; therefore, a regimen that would provide rapid glycaemic control while covering both insulin‑secreting and insulin‑resistance mechanisms was chosen. Basal insulin degludec (8 units/day) was combined with metformin (1 500 mg/day) and the dipeptidyl peptidase‑4 inhibitor saxagliptin (5 mg/day). This approach mirrors the standard initial treatment for young adults with type 2 diabetes who exhibit significant hyperglycaemia and have not yet gained clear information on their β‑cell reserve. Insulin addressed the large glucose gap, metformin attenuated hepatic glucose production and improved peripheral insulin sensitivity, and saxagliptin amplified endogenous incretin activity to enhance post‑prandial insulin secretion.

Within one month, HbA1c fell to 9.1 %. Genetic testing returned a pathogenic heterozygous HNF1A mutation (p.N270S), confirming HNF1A‑MODY. The discovery of a MODY subtype altered the therapeutic calculus. HNF1A‑MODY is characterised by a progressive decline in β‑cell function but typically preserves basal insulin secretion for years. Moreover, sulfonylureas—despite their efficacy in many MODY patients—carry a higher risk of hypoglycaemia, particularly early in disease when β‑cell responsiveness is still intact. In this patient, basal insulin was therefore discontinued and replaced with the GLP‑1 receptor agonist dulaglutide (1.5 mg weekly). Dulaglutide has several advantages in HNF1A‑MODY:

1. **β‑cell preservation** – GLP‑1 receptor activation stimulates insulin secretion in a glucose‑dependent manner, reduces glucagon secretion, and promotes β‑cell proliferation and anti‑apoptotic pathways. In the context of a partially functioning β‑cell unit, this mechanism can sustain endogenous insulin production longer than a drug that depolarises β‑cells through potassium‑ATP channel blockade (sulfonylureas).
2. **Lower hypoglycaemia risk** – Because the stimulatory effect of GLP‑1 is pronounced only when plasma glucose is elevated, markedly fewer episodes of inadvertent hypoglycaemia occur compared with sulfonylureas or basal insulin.
3. **Weight neutrality or mild loss** – Unlike insulin, which is weight‑promoting, GLP‑1 agonists encourage satiety and slow gastric emptying, favouring weight stability or modest decline—beneficial in a patient with a near‑normal BMI who nevertheless had dyslipidaemia.

After initiating dulaglutide, the patient’s HbA1c rapidly descended to 6.0 % over the next six months. Glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide remained >0.60 nmol/L, indicating that residual β‑cell function was sustained. When glycaemic control had normalized, the clinicians opted for a simplified regimen: continued metformin (1 500 mg/day) plus saxagliptin (5 mg/day). The dual oral therapy aligns with the principle of freeing patients from injectable medications once a stable and durable glycaemic target is achieved. Importantly, the combination of metformin and a DPP‑4 inhibitor can further amplify endogenous incretin activity while maintaining hepatic insulin sensitivity, providing a stable backdrop for a declining β‑cell reserve.

**Monitoring strategy**: Baseline and quarterly HbA1c measurements remained the primary metric for long‑term control. Additionally, fasting plasma glucose and 2‑hour post‑prandial glucose values were obtained at each visit to assess basal and dynamic glycaemic handling. Because hypoglycaemia with GLP‑1 agonists is infrequent, only routine patient‑reported symptoms were collected rather than continuous glucose monitoring. The patient’s lipid profile and renal markers were checked annually, given the metabolic syndrome lab findings; microalbuminuria and ophthalmologic screening were scheduled as per standard diabetes care.

**Avoidance of SGLT2 inhibitors** was deliberate. In HNF1A‑MODY, the transcription factor HNF1A down‑regulates SGLT2 expression, potentially lowering renal glucose reabsorption capacity. Inhibition of SGLT2 in this setting could precipitate significant glucosuria, volume depletion, and an atypically high risk of euglycaemic ketoacidosis. Literature reports of severe dehydration and ketoacidosis in HNF1A‑MODY patients treated with SGLT2 inhibitors have prompted caution; consequently, these agents were excluded from the therapeutic plan.

**Lifestyle interventions** complemented pharmacotherapy. A Mediterranean‑style, carb‑restricted diet tailored to the patient’s energy needs helped stabilize post‑meal glucose excursions and address dyslipidaemia. Regular aerobic exercise (≥150 min/week) and resistance training were advocated to enhance insulin sensitivity, support cardiovascular health, and reinforce the benefits of GLP‑1 agonist therapy. Periodic reassessment of body weight and waist circumference ensured that the metabolic syndrome did not resurge.

In summary, the progression from an empiric basal insulin strategy to a genotype‑guided, injection‑free maintenance regimen illustrates the value of precision medicine in MODY. By recognising the underlying HNF1A mutation and its impact on β‑cell dynamics, clinicians were able to:

- Eliminate insulin therapy once a durable oral-based strategy was in place.
- Reduce the risk of hypoglycaemia with a glucose‑dependent agent (GLP‑1 agonist).
- Preserve β‑cell function over a longer period.
- Avoid potentially hazardous SGLT2 inhibition in the context of compromised renal glucose handling.

This case exemplifies how targeted therapeutic choices, informed by genetic diagnosis, can optimize outcomes in patients with monogenic diabetes.

## 5. Outcomes, Follow‑up and Prognostic Considerations

The patient’s therapeutic course illustrates the remarkable reversibility of hyper‑glycaemia achievable when treatment is tailored to the underlying genetic defect. At baseline his HbA1c was 10.2 % (88 mmol/mol); within three months of initiation of GLP‑1 receptor agonist therapy he achieved 6.0 % (42 mmol/mol), a reduction of 4.2 % (≈40 mmol/mol). This steep fall in HbA1c is typical of HNF1A‑MODY, in which residual β‑cell function can still be stimulated by agents that enhance glucose‑dependent insulin secretion or by preserving β‑cell health. The continued decline to 5.8 % after six months indicates sustained efficacy, with no evidence of hypoglycaemic episodes reported—a common concern with sulfonylurea therapy in HNF1A‑MODY patients, particularly as β‑cell responsiveness wanes with age.

**Resolution of glycosuria and hypoglycaemia:** Glycosuria resolved once the random plasma glucose fell below the renal threshold. The patient reported no symptomatic hypoglycaemia during the follow‑up period, corroborating the safety profile of the GLP‑1 receptor agonist and low‑dose metformin regimen. Importantly, the sensitivity of the glucagon stimulation test, which initially revealed a stimulated C‑peptide of 0.83 nmol/L, remained within the range expected for a modest but preserved endogenous insulin secretion—an encouraging marker that the β‑cell reserve has not been exhausted.

**Microvascular surveillance:** At one year post‑diagnosis, a comprehensive ophthalmologic exam showed no evidence of diabetic retinopathy, and the patient’s urine albumin‑to‑creatinine ratio remained below 30 mg/g, indicating normoalbuminuria. Kidney ultrasonography was unremarkable, and serum creatinine stayed stable. These findings are consistent with the low prevalence of microvascular complications in HNF1A‑MODY, provided glycaemic control is maintained below the 6.5 % threshold. The American Diabetes Association recommends annual screening for retinopathy and microalbuminuria in all patients with diabetes; for MODY, early attainment of tight glycaemic control further mitigates these risks.

**Long‑term glucose targets:** For young adults with HNF1A‑MODY, the ADA’s individualized glycaemic goals (HbA1c ≤ 6.5 %) are appropriate. The patient’s current HbA1c of 5.8 % sits comfortably within this target, and the therapeutic regimen (metformin plus GLP‑1 receptor agonist) provides a low‑risk, well‑tolerated maintenance strategy. Periodic reassessment every 3–6 months is advised to adjust doses as β‑cell function gradually declines.

**Family screening:** Given the autosomal dominant inheritance of MODY, first‑degree relatives are at a 50 % risk of harbouring the same pathogenic HNF1A variant. The patient’s younger brother, diagnosed at age 20, and his mother and grandmother are candidates for targeted genetic testing. Early identification allows for prompt initiation of glucose‑modulating therapy, which can forestall the progression to hyper‑glycaemia and its sequelae. In addition, relatives without the mutation can be reassured, sparing them unnecessary treatment.

**Reproductive counselling:** The p.N270S variant constitutes a heterozygous pathogenic mutation with an autosomal dominant pattern. Couples in which one partner carries this mutation should receive counselling regarding the 50 % risk of transmission to offspring. Pre‑implantation genetic diagnosis (PGD) or early prenatal testing can be offered if the couple wishes to avoid affected children. Additionally, since the mutation does not confer teratogenicity, pregnancy in heterozygous carriers is generally safe; however, heightened monitoring of maternal glycaemic status during gestation is warranted to prevent fetal over‑growth or neonatal hypoglycaemia.

**Prognosis:** In HNF1A‑MODY, the trajectory of β‑cell decline is slower than in type 1 diabetes, allowing for a prolonged period of insulin‑independent management. The patient’s 6‑month trajectory—from severe hyper‑glycaemia to near‑normoglycaemic states—exemplifies the favorable long‑term outlook when therapy is aligned with the underlying defect. Nonetheless, regular surveillance for late‑onset complications remains essential, as does vigilance for potential loss of β‑cell reserve that may necessitate a transition to insulin therapy in the future.

In summary, the patient’s rapid biochemical improvement, absence of microvascular complications to date, and attainment of target HbA1c affirm the prognostic benefits conferred by precision medicine in MODY. Ongoing family screening and reproductive counselling will further extend these benefits by enabling early intervention in at‑risk relatives.

## 6. Clinical Lessons and Future Directions

### Misdiagnosis rates of MODY in Asian populations  
Despite the clear clinical criteria for MODY, estimates of its prevalence among Asian cohorts remain highly variable, largely because of underrecognition. In a large cohort of young‑onset type 2 diabetes in China, the screening yield for HNF1A mutations was only 1.5 %—approximately one in 70 patients—yet the true prevalence may be underestimated due to selective referral and limited access to genetic panels. The present case reinforces that even in the absence of overt insulin resistance or obesity, a substantial proportion of “early‑onset” diabetes is monogenic. Clinicians should therefore maintain a high index of suspicion, especially when the family history is strikingly parallel to that seen in the patient.

### Utility of MODY probability calculators across ethnicities  
The Exeter MODY probability calculator, built on a White European dataset, estimated a 62 % probability of MODY in this patient. While the calculator is a useful screening aid, its sensitivity and specificity are lower in non‑European populations. Recent validation studies in Chinese and Indian cohorts have reported reduced discrimination, partly due to differing BMI trajectories and metabolic phenotypes. Nevertheless, when combined with negative autoantibody testing and preserved early‑postprandial C‑peptide levels, the calculator remains a pragmatic tool to flag candidates for genetic testing, provided that clinicians are aware of its limitations.

### Impact of novel variant discovery on treatment algorithms  
The identification of a novel pathogenic HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S) in exon 4 shifted the patient’s management trajectory dramatically. Initially managed with basal insulin and a sulfonylurea‑like DPP‑4 inhibitor, the confirmation of monogenic disease permitted a transition to GLP‑1 receptor agonist therapy, ultimately obviating insulin altogether. Such a shift exemplifies how precision genetics can bypass the trial‑and‑error period typical of type 2 diabetes management and prevent overtreatment or under‑treatment. In practice, the clinical algorithm should therefore incorporate a genetic “if‑not resolved by standard therapy” step for patients meeting the MODY phenotype.

### Potential biomarkers for MODY vs T1D/T2D  
Until now, no single biochemical marker reliably distinguishes MODY from autoimmune or insulin‑resistance diabetes in routine practice. However, emerging data suggest that serum markers of β‑cell stress (e.g., proinsulin:C‑peptide ratio), urinary nephrin, or specific SGLT2‑related metabolites may differ in HNF1A‑MODY. Prospective studies are needed to validate these proxies, but for now, a combination of autoantibody negativity, preserved fasting C‑peptide (>0.6 nmol/L), and a low glucose re‑absorption threshold (as evidenced by glycosuria at relatively low plasma glucose) remains the strongest clinical heuristic.

### Role of NGS in large‑scale screening  
Next‑generation sequencing panels covering the >15 known MODY genes enable rapid, simultaneous interrogation of all candidate loci. In a population‑based study of 506 individuals with suspected hereditary diabetes, NGS identified pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in 46 % of cases, with HNF1A being the most frequently mutated gene. The turnaround time (≈ 7–10 days) and declining costs render NGS an attractive first‑line test for patients with early‑onset diabetes, negative autoantibodies, and a suggestive family history. Nonetheless, variants of uncertain significance remain a challenge; functional studies or segregation analysis within families are often required to clarify pathogenicity.

### Strategies to improve cost‑effectiveness and clinical yield  
1. **Refining Clinical Filters** – Implementing a two‑tiered screening: (i) basic clinical criteria (age ≤ 35 yr, BMI < 30 kg/m², family history of early‑onset diabetes, absence of metabolic syndrome) followed by (ii) a MODY probability score that integrates fasting C‑peptide and glycosuria.  
2. **Targeted Gene Panels** – Starting with a core panel of the 6 most common MODY genes (HNF1A, GCK, HNF4A, HNF1B, PDX1, NEUROD1) can reduce cost, reserving full NGS for high‑probability cases or when results are negative.  
3. **Biobank and Registry Integration** – Linking patients to national registries allows retrospective identification of temporal trends in misdiagnosis and facilitates population‑level cost‑benefit analyses.  
4. **Re‑evaluation of Existing Diagnoses** – A systematic audit of patients previously labeled “type 2” but with atypical features (e.g., rapid glycaemic deterioration, low insulin requirements) can uncover missed MONOGENIC cases and refine screening algorithms.  

**Conclusion**  
In this case, the integration of comprehensive phenotyping, judicious use of a probability calculator, and definitive genetic confirmation not only prevented prolonged insulin exposure but also tailored therapy to the patient’s molecular defect. As NGS becomes more widespread and analytic tools evolve, clinicians should anticipate shifting the diagnostic paradigm for early‑onset diabetes from phenotype‑based to genotype‑driven care. This transition promises more precise treatment regimens, better long‑term outcomes, and, ultimately, a reduction in healthcare costs associated with misdiagnosis and inappropriate therapy.
