Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

Chapter 1: ## 1. Clinical Presentation and Initial Evaluation

The patient is a 22‑year‑old male who was referred for evaluation after a routine health‑check revealed hyperglycaemia and glycosuria. His body mass index (BMI) of 24.6 kg/m² places him in the normal‑to‑upper‑normal range for his age, and his blood pressure (112/70 mm Hg) and resting heart rate (78 bpm) are within normal limits, indicating the absence of overt hypertension or tachycardia that often accompanies insulin‑resistant states. Physical examination did not identify acanthosis nigricans or other cutaneous stigmata of insulin resistance.

The symptomatic profile consisted of classic diabetes manifestations: increased thirst (polydipsia), increased appetite (polyphagia), and recent unintentional weight loss. These findings, together with the laboratory evidence of significant hyperglycaemia, raised suspicion for a non‑typical diabetic phenotype. Notably, there was no documentation of diabetic ketoacidosis, and mental status exam was unremarkable, ruling out an acute endocrine crisis.

Family history revealed a triad of relatives with early‑onset diabetes: the maternal grandmother diagnosed in her 30s, the mother in her late 20s, and a younger brother at age 20. Such a strong, autosomal dominant pattern strongly implicates a monogenic form of diabetes rather than type 2 diabetes, which usually manifests later and with obesity. No family members reported early‑onset cardiovascular disease, which can sometimes accompany genetic diabetes syndromes.

Baseline laboratory studies were striking. Glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) was markedly elevated at 10.2 % (88 mmol/mol), and the fasting plasma glucose measured 12.4 mmol/L (223 mg/dL). The 2‑hour 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) was particularly abnormal, with a glucose of 19.8 mmol/L (356 mg/dL). The combination of a high HbA1c, fasting hyperglycaemia, and post‑prandial glucose surge exceeds the diagnostic thresholds for diabetes in all established guidelines and suggests substantial beta‑cell dysfunction.

Additional laboratory data underscored metabolic derangements. Glycosuria was detected, consistent with a lowered renal threshold for glucose that occurs in many MODY subtypes, including HNF1A‑MODY. Lipid profiling revealed a low high‑density lipoprotein (HDL) of 1.01 mmol/L and a markedly elevated triglyceride level of 4.31 mmol/L, pointing to dyslipidaemia. While the patient’s BMI is not overly elevated, the lipid abnormalities and hyperglycaemia meet the American Heart Association criteria for metabolic syndrome when combined with glucose intolerance. Thus, there is a detectable early‑onset metabolic syndrome phenotype, albeit milder than that seen in classic type 2 diabetes.

Taken together, the age‑appropriate onset of diabetes, the preserved lean body habitus, the strong family history, and the laboratory evidence of isolated hyperglycaemia with a prominent post‑prandial component create a clinical picture that is atypical for both type 1 and type 2 diabetes. This prompted further investigative steps, including pancreatic beta‑cell functional testing (glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide), autoantibody screening, and ultimately, molecular genetic evaluation for MODY.
