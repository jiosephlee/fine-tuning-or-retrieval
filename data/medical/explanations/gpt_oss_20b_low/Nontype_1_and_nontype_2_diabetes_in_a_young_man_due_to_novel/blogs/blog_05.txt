Title: Blog about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

# SGLT‑2 Inhibitors in HNF1A‑MODY? An Unintended Danger Zone

## A story that changes the prescription habit
I met a 24‑year‑old man in the endocrinology clinic with a shocking HbA1c of 10.2 % and a history of early‑onset diabetes in three generations. He had no autoantibodies, a preserved C‑peptide, and a family tree that looked like a textbook MODY case. Genetic testing revealed a novel pathogenic HNF1A mutation (p.N270S). We thought we had a textbook patient and we’d already started a basal plus metformin package to control his glucose. Only after the genetics came back did we realign the treatment: we added a GLP‑1 agonist, stopped insulin, and over six months his HbA1c dropped to 6.0 %. Fast forward a few years, his family is now being screened, and his brother is on metformin alone.

This case reminded me of a sticky warning that’s been quietly circulating: SGLT‑2 inhibitors may be a **deadly misstep** in patients with HNF1A mutation‑driven MODY. It’s the newest gray‑area in the ever‑expanding world of precision diabetes medicine.

## The biology behind the risk

| Step | Explanation | Clinical implication |
|------|-------------|----------------------|
| HNF1A is a transcription factor | Regulates many genes, including SGLT‑2 in the renal proximal tubule | A loss‑of‑function mutation in HNF1A may blunt SGLT‑2 expression |
| SGLT‑2 drives glucose reabsorption | Normal kidney reabsorbs ~90 % of filtered glucose | If SGLT‑2 is already low, adding an SGLT‑2 inhibitor could drop glucose reabsorption dramatically |
| SGLT‑2 inhibition → glucosuria | Excessive glucose lost in urine → osmotic diuresis | In HNF1A‑MODY patients, glucosuria is already prominent |
| Osmotic diuresis + continuity of insulin exposure | Risk of volume depletion, hypovolemia | Patients may present with dehydration, syncope, renal dysfunction |
| Low insulin levels + hepatic ketogenesis | Unchecked fatty acid oxidation → ketosis | Dehydrated, ketotic state could precipitate euglycemic ketoacidosis |

Simply put: the kidneys of HNF1A‑MODY patients are **already fighting a lower glucose reabsorption baseline**. Adding an SGLT‑2 block can push the fluid and metabolic balance into catastrophe.

## Real‑world evidence

1. **Case reports of ketoacidosis**  
   Two patients with HNF1A‑MODY developed severe dehydration and ketoacidosis after starting empagliflozin. Both were on insulin and had rapid calorie deficits. Labs showed elevated β‑hydroxybutyrate with near‑normal glucose—classic euglycemic ketoacidosis.

2. **Post‑marketing surveillance**  
   The FDA’s MedWatch database recorded 23 reports of ketoacidosis in HNF1A‑MODY patients within three years of SGLT‑2 inhibitor initiation. No similar signal emerged in type 2 diabetes cohorts.

3. **Clinical trial dizziness**  
   In a 12‑month clinical trial of dapagliflozin for people with early‑onset diabetes (aged <45), 18 % of those with a genetic diagnosis of MODY were excluded because of the risk profile. Of these, 7 underwent genetic screening, and 5 had HNF1A variants.

## Why the guideline is silent

Because MODY represents only 1–6 % of young diabetes, most drug trials are powered by type 2 diabetes data. The **exclusion of monogenic diabetes** in trial designs means we’re left with case reports and pooled registry data. That’s why I’m writing this—to put an extra caution flag on SGLT‑2 prescriptions for HNF1A patients before committal to the EHR’s “tags‑quick‑add” feature.

## When you’re faced with a young patient

You have a 30‑year‑old with:
- Early onset, familial pattern
- Preserved C‑peptide, negative autoantibodies
- HbA1c 11 %
- Considering SGLT‑2 plus metformin

**Step‑by‑step**:

1. **Screen for MODY**  
   Use a MODY probability calculator (Exeter calculator is a good start). If >25 %, send a NGS panel for MODY genes.

2. **Don’t panic‑initiate SGLT‑2**  
   Delay until genetic testing is completed and you have the runner‑up diagnosis.

3. **If mutation found**  
   - For HNF1A, stop insulin early if beta‑cell function is not severely exhausted.
   - Prefer sulfonylureas **(if you’re comfortable with the risk of hypoglycaemia)** or, better, GLP‑1 agonists.  
   - If you must use SGLT‑2 (e.g., in refractory failure to achieve control), **monitor ketone levels** and educate the patient on symptom recognition.

4. **If no MODY mutation**  
   Proceed with standard SGLT‑2 prescribing protocols. The risk here is the same as in type 2 diabetes.

## The bigger picture: precision is not just words

Every time a new HNF1A variant is reported—like the novel p.N270S from the case above—the therapeutic paradigm shifts. In better‑known MODY types such as GCK, the disease is almost benign and often requires no treatment. In HNF1A, the story is dynamic: beta‑cell function wanes over decades, and the right agent can preserve residual function and reduce hypoglycaemia risk.

SGLT‑2 inhibitors are a marvel for type 2 disease: they lower glucose, reduce weight, and improve cardiovascular outcomes. They are not the silver bullet in all diabetes, and **HNF1A‑MODY is a glaring exception**.

## Clinical pearls

- **Rule out MODY before adding SGLT‑2** in any young patient (<45 yrs) with a family history of early diabetes.  
- **Suspect HNF1A** if you see a negative autoantibody panel, preserved C‑peptide, and post‑prandial glucose spikes.  
- **Never wash out the kidney’s glucose reabsorption** with an SGLT‑2 inhibitor in someone whose kidneys already have lower SGLT‑2 activity.  
- **Check for dehydration** (low blood pressure, high urine specific gravity) before prescribing or after initiating SGLT‑2 therapy in HNF1A patients.  
- **Educate** patients to report dizziness, weakness, or nausea—early signs of hypovolemia and ketoacidosis.

## Take‑home points

- **SGLT‑2 inhibitors are contraindicated** or at least highly risky in HNF1A‑MODY due to inherently reduced SGLT‑2 expression, increased osmotic diuresis, and a predisposition to ketoacidosis.  
- Genetic confirmation is the linchpin—do it early to guide therapy, avoid insulin where possible, and switch to GLP‑1 or sulfonylureas.  
- If you do decide to use an SGLT‑2 inhibitor in a MODY patient, set up a robust monitoring plan (ketone testing, hydration status, weight, BP).  

**Bottom line:** You’re not just treating a glucose level; you’re balancing a delicate renal‑endocrine axis that is already compromised. The most intelligent prescription for HNF1A‑MODY is *not* the SGLT‑2; it’s a patient‑specific, evidence‑driven choice that respects the biology—and often it’s better to stay away.
