Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

## 1. Clinical Presentation and Early Postoperative Course

### Baseline demographics and cardiovascular comorbidities  
The patient was an elderly man in his 70s with a long history of exertional dyspnoea and chronic atrial fibrillation managed with oral apixaban (5 mg twice daily). His cardiovascular status was further complicated by a previously undiagnosed systolic murmur, prompting a diagnostic heart catheterisation. No other significant comorbidities were noted at presentation that would predispose him to marked postoperative bleeding or thrombosis.

### Pre‑operative anticoagulation and diagnostic catheterisation  
Unlike the routine practice of withholding direct oral anticoagulants before coronary angiography, apixaban was not held in this case. Consequently, the interventional cardiologist administered a reduced dose of unfractionated heparin (UFH) during the transradial coronary angiogram (2 000 U). This low‑dose exposure was the patient’s only pre‑operative contact with heparin and would later prove pivotal in the pathogenesis of his postoperative haemorrhagic‑thrombotic syndrome.

### Surgical procedure and peri‑operative heparin exposure (CPB and catheterisation)  
The patient underwent a complex aortic valve replacement (bioprosthesis) and ascending aortic grafting on cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) using 45 000 U of UFH for intra‑operative anticoagulation. The surgical course was uncomplicated, without significant bleeding, and the patient was weaned from CPB with stable haemodynamics. Thus, the operative regimen conferred two distinct heparin exposures: a low dose pre‑operative bolus during catheterisation and the higher anticoagulant dose required for CPB.

### Post‑operative anticoagulation strategy (LMWH, fondaparinux)  
Post‑operative prophylaxis was initiated with low‑molecular‑weight heparin (LMWH, dalteparin) on postoperative day (POD) 1, escalating in dose over the first four days. Dalteparin was withheld on POD 5; at that time, prophylactic‑dose fondaparinux (2.5 mg) was started due to concerns about potential bleeding risk with LMWH. This therapeutic shift represented a typical institutional approach to postoperative thromboprophylaxis following cardiac surgery.

### Platelet count trajectory from POD 1 to POD 6  
The patient’s platelet count fell progressively from a pre‑operative level of 210 × 10⁹ L⁻¹ to 155 × 10⁹ L⁻¹ on POD 1, a modest decline within the expected range after CPB. However, between POD 1 and POD 5 the count continued to drop, reaching 50 × 10⁹ L⁻¹ on POD 5 and further declining to 26 × 10⁹ L⁻¹ on POD 6. This marked thrombocytopenia developed over a very short interval (within six postoperative days) and was unprecedented when compared to typical post‑cardiac‑surgery thrombocytopenia, which usually manifests between POD 5 and POD 10. The rapid slope and severe nadir raised immediate concern for an immune‑mediated process such as heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (HIT).

### Development of delirium and subsequent imaging findings (parietal infarct)  
The patient’s clinical picture changed when worsening delirium was noted on POD 5‑6. Although no focal deficits were initially evident, the degree of encephalopathy prompted a head CT scan, which revealed an acute left parietal infarct measuring 3.2 × 1.8 cm (with cortical extension). The emergence of a thrombotic event within the brain in the setting of progressive thrombocytopenia was an alarm signal for a hypercoagulable state rather than a bleeding complication. This neuroimaging result further steered the diagnostic suspicion toward HIT.

### Differential emergence of thrombocytopenia in the early postoperative period  
The sudden fall in platelets before the classic HIT window (before POD 5) suggested an atypical mechanism. Several possibilities were considered: transfusion‐related platelet consumption (none transfused), surgical blood loss (stable haemoglobin), sepsis or infection (no cultures positive), drug‑induced immune thrombocytopenia (no new drugs recent), thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (normal haptoglobin and no schistocytes early on) or post‑cardiac‑surgery platelet consumption. The only factor temporally aligned with the early platelet drop was the pre‑operative UFH exposure during catheterisation and the intra‑operative dose of UFH, which together can precipitate HIT even before the usual 5‑day latency period. This led to the prompt ordering of HIT antibody testing, cementing the probable diagnosis and explaining the patient’s severe and early thrombocytopenia.

## 2. Laboratory Evaluation of Thrombocytopenia

The postoperative course of the patient prompted a systematic laboratory work‑up to determine whether the pronounced drop in platelet count was attributable to a common, non‑immune cause or whether an immune‑mediated process such as heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) was involved. Each laboratory evaluation, from simple peripheral smear to specialized functional assays, contributed incremental information that narrowed the differential diagnosis.

### Peripheral smear assessment

A routine peripheral blood film revealed a marked thrombocytopenia without evidence of platelet clumping or giant platelets. Importantly, there were no schistocytes or other fragmented red cells, which would have suggested microangiopathic hemolysis (e.g., thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura). The absence of typical circulating platelet aggregates also argued against platelet consumption in conditions such as DIC unrelated to a humoral trigger. Thus, the smear excluded mechanical destruction or platelet sequestration as the primary explanation.

### Coagulation profile and acute‑phase reactants

Early in the postoperative period, the patient’s coagulation studies were within normal limits: prothrombin time (PT) and international normalised ratio (INR) remained at the upper normal end of the reference range, and activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) was 34 s (25–37 s). Fibrinogen was markedly elevated (6.59 g/L, RR 2.0–3.9 g/L) and D‑dimer peaked around POD12. These findings are typical for the early postoperative state, where systemic inflammation and surgical trauma elicit a hypercoagulable profile with increased fibrinogen and fibrin degradation products. The normal PT/aPTT argued against a vitamin‑K antagonist effect or intrinsic coagulation factor deficiency, while the raised fibrinogen was an expected acute‑phase response rather than a consumptive coagulopathy in the absence of schistocytes and normal PT/aPTT.

### Serum biochemistry to exclude hemolysis and marrow suppression

The hemoglobin level fell modestly (97 g/L) compared with pre‑operative values, but remained above the threshold for significant bleeding. A reticulocyte count of 73 × 10⁹/L (RR 10–100 × 10⁹/L) and an elevated haptoglobin (3.19 g/L, RR 0.32–1.97 g/L) indicated no active intravascular hemolysis. Vitamin‑B12 was within normal limits, ruling out megaloblastic pancytopenia. Thus, marrow suppression or hemolytic processes were unlikely contributors to thrombocytopenia.

### PF4/polyanion ELISA for HIT antibody screening

The high‑optical‑density result (2.2 OD, RR < 0.4 OD) on the PF4/polyanion enzyme‑linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was strongly positive. This assay detects IgG, IgM, and IgA antibodies against the platelet factor 4 (PF4)/heparin complex and, while highly sensitive, is not specific for pathogenic antibodies. A strongly positive ELISA is one of the first steps in the HIT diagnostic algorithm and warrants functional confirmation.

### Serotonin‑release assay (SRA) – distinguishing heparin‑dependent vs independent activity

The serotonin‑release assay (SRA) is the gold‑standard functional test for HIT, measuring platelet activation mediated by patient antibodies. In this patient, the SRA showed robust serotonin release at therapeutic heparin concentrations (0.1 U/mL and 0.3 U/mL, 97–99 % release), but no release at a supratherapeutic concentration (100 U/mL) where PF4/heparin complexes cannot form. This biphasic pattern is characteristic of heparin‑dependent HIT antibodies. However, a distinctive additional finding was almost complete activation at 0 U/mL (96 % release), demonstrating that the antibodies could trigger platelet degranulation without exogenous heparin. This heparin‑independent activity is diagnostic of autoimmune HIT (aHIT). The absence of activation at high‐dose heparin is a key discriminating feature: in classic HIT, platelet activation diminishes sharply at high heparin concentrations because the PF4/heparin complex cannot form, whereas in aHIT the antibodies can bind PF4 alone or in low‑affinity complexes, allowing activation even without added heparin.

### Role of FcγRIIa blocking antibody (IV.3)

The functional effect of platelet FcγRIIa (CD32a) clustering is central to HIT pathogenesis: the Fc portion of IgG antibodies bridges adjacent platelets, triggering activation. To confirm this mechanism, the monoclonal antibody IV.3, which blocks FcγRIIa, is added during the SRA. In classic HIT, IV.3 abrogates almost all serotonin release (> 80 % inhibition), because activating antibodies rely on FcγRIIa engagement. In this case, only partial inhibition occurred (76 % release remained in the presence of IV.3), indicating that the antibodies possessed an atypical, high‑avidity or Fab‑mediated activation component. This partial resistance is an uncommon feature of aHIT that further underscores the severity and atypical serologic profile of the patient’s antibodies.

### Summary of laboratory interpretation

The combination of a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, heparin‑dependent SRA reactivity at therapeutically relevant heparin concentrations, exquisite activation in the absence of heparin, and incomplete FcγRIIa blockade confirms a diagnosis of autoimmune HIT. The ancillary laboratory data—normal coagulation parameters, absence of hemolysis, and a typical early‑postoperative hypercoagulable profile—excluded other common causes of thrombocytopenia such as DIC, thrombotic microangiopathy, or marrow suppression. Thus, the laboratory evaluation focused the diagnostic algorithm on HIT, guided therapeutic decisions, and provided a framework for monitoring disease activity and response to treatment.

## 3. Diagnostic Reasoning: From Classical HIT to Autoimmune HIT

The diagnostic journey in this case began with the observation that a patient’s platelet count had fallen persistently from postoperative day 1, reaching a nadir of 13 × 10⁹ /L by day 7. In the setting of cardiac surgery, a falling platelet count between days 5–10 is the textbook pattern for heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). However, the chronology here was atypical: the decline commenced on day 1, long before the usual five‑to‑ten‑day window, and preceded the administration of any postoperative heparin prophylaxis. This prompted a careful review of all perioperative heparin exposure.

### Temporal relationship between heparin exposure and platelet count fall
Immediately after CPB, the patient received 45 000 U of unfractionated heparin (UFH). Although this did not explain the early drop (the platelet count was 155 × 10⁹ /L on day 1), a closer look revealed that UFH was also administered during the pre‑operative coronary angiography (2 000 U). In patients who develop classic HIT, a second exposure to heparin within 4 hours of the first can trigger a very early onset of thrombocytopenia. The presence of this early exposure was therefore a critical piece of the puzzle, reconciling the unusually early platelet fall with a heparin‑dependent process.

### Exclusion of alternative etiologies
Before labeling the thrombocytopenia as HIT, other common causes had to be excluded:

| Possible etiology | Supporting evidence | Why it was ruled out |
|-------------------|---------------------|----------------------|
| **Sepsis / infection** | Cultures negative; no fever; normal WCC | No clinical or laboratory signs of infection |
| **Cardiogenic shock / critical illness** | Hemodynamics stable; normal lactate | Stable postoperative course |
| **Drug‑induced thrombocytopenia (DITP)** | Drugs started after day 1; onset too early | Onset timeline inconsistent; no known platelet‑reactive drugs |
| **Post‑transfusion purpura** | No transfusions given | N/A |
| **Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP)** | No schistocytes initially; normal haptoglobin | Later appearance of schistocytes, but highly unlikely to be classic TTP |

The exclusion of these alternatives sharpened focus on an immune‑mediated, heparin‑dependent process, consistent with HIT.

### Criteria for aHIT: early onset, heparin‑independent platelet activation
Autoimmune HIT (aHIT) is defined by the presence of HIT antibodies that can activate platelets even in **the absence of heparin**. The key diagnostic criteria for aHIT include:

1. **Early onset** (≤ 4 days after first heparin exposure)  
   – The patient’s platelet decline began on day 1, supporting early-onset aHIT rather than classic HIT.

2. **Strong platelet activation at low or zero heparin concentrations**  
   – The serotonin‑release assay (SRA) showed > 90 % serotonin release at 0 U/mL UFH, a hallmark of aHIT.

3. **Persistence of platelet activation after high‑dose heparin**  
   – No activation at 100 U/mL UFH, consistent with the classic biphasic response of HIT but retained low‑dose activation.

These findings collectively satisfy the diagnostic criteria for aHIT.

### Significance of strong serotonin release at 0 U/mL UFH
In classic HIT, patient serum does **not** activate donor platelets in the absence of heparin. Activation requires the formation of PF4‑heparin complexes that bridge IgG Fc regions and platelet FcγRIIa. A 96 % serotonin release at 0 U/mL UFH indicates that the patient’s antibodies can bind PF4 or other platelet antigens independently of heparin, forming immune complexes that directly engage FcγRIIa. This heparin‑independent reactivity is a defining feature of aHIT and explains why the patient’s thrombocytopenia and thrombotic stroke occurred despite the absence of postoperative heparin prophylaxis.

### Partial inhibition by IV.3 antibody – implications for antibody avidity and FcR engagement
The anti‑FcγRIIa monoclonal antibody IV.3 is routinely used in SRA to confirm that platelet activation is FcγRIIa–mediated. In classic HIT, addition of IV.3 reduces serotonin release to < 20 %. In this case, 76 % release persisted despite IV.3, indicating **partial resistance** to FcγRIIa blockade. Possible explanations include:

- **High avidity** of the antibodies for the FcγRIIa, making them less susceptible to inhibition.  
- **Co‑receptor engagement** or antibody subclass differences that reduce the efficacy of IV.3.  
- **A conformational change** in the FcγRIIa complex when bound by aHIT antibodies, diminishing IV.3 access.

Clinically, the partial inhibition suggests a more aggressive or “tight‑binding” antibody phenotype, correlating with the severe thrombocytopenia and poor response to intravenous immunoglobulin.

### Evolution of SRA profile over time (persistent heparin‑independent activity to later heparin‑dependent pattern)
Serial SRA testing revealed a dynamic antibody phenotype:

- **Early samples (day 5–7)**: Persistent > 90 % serotonin release at 0 U/mL UFH with complete inhibition at 100 U/mL UFH – classic aHIT profile.  
- **Later samples (after POD 14)**: Reduced release at 0 U/mL (15 %) but preserved maximal release (≥ 90 %) at 0.3 U/mL UFH – a shift toward a more conventional, heparin‑dependent HIT pattern.

This temporal evolution likely reflects a natural waning of high‑avidity aHIT antibodies and a relative rise of lower‑avidity, heparin‑dependent antibodies as the immune response resolves. The change coincided with platelet count recovery, providing a functional marker of disease regression.

### Clinical relevance of patient‑specific antibody characteristics
The unique antibody phenotype in this patient has several practical implications:

1. **Treatment selection**: The high‑avidity, heparin‑independent antibodies necessitated avoidance of low‑molecular‑weight heparin (LMWH) and prompted early use of a direct thrombin inhibitor (argatroban).  
2. **Monitoring**: Fibrinogen, d‑dimer, and schistocyte counts were useful surrogate markers of ongoing hypercoagulability and helped gauge response to anticoagulation.  
3. **Prognostication**: The persistence of hypercoagulability despite intravenous Ig underscored that a subset of aHIT patients may require prolonged or alternative anticoagulation (fondaparinux) once the high‑avidity antibodies wane.  
4. **Future research**: The partial IV.3 blockade highlights a previously unreported serologic signature that may define a more aggressive aHIT sub‑phenotype, warranting further study of antibody structure and FcγRIIa interactions.

In sum, the stepwise diagnostic reasoning moved from a classical “platelet drop after cardiothoracic surgery” to a nuanced understanding of an early‑onset, heparin‑independent, high‑avidity auto‑immune process. Recognizing the temporal clues, carefully ruling out alternative causes, and interpreting the distinctive SRA features were essential to identify autoimmune HIT and to guide effective, individualized therapy.

## 4. Management Strategy for Severe Autoimmune HIT

Severe autoimmune HIT (aHIT) presents a therapeutic dilemma: platelet activation persists independently of heparin, so conventional strategies that simply discontinue heparin are often insufficient. In the case described, a multi‑modality approach was required to halt thrombotic complications, reverse consumptive coagulopathy, and allow eventual transition back to long‑term oral anticoagulation.

### Rationale for discontinuing fondaparinux and starting argatroban

Fondaparinux, a synthetic pentasaccharide that indirectly inhibits factor Xa through antithrombin, is effective for heparin‑independent HIT when used at therapeutic doses. However, its pharmacokinetics (long half‑life of 17–21 h) do not permit rapid reversal should bleeding occur. In the immediate postoperative setting of this patient, the risk of hemorrhage was heightened by recent intracerebral infarction and profound thrombocytopenia. Argatroban, a small‑molecule direct thrombin inhibitor with short half‑life (35–80 min) and clear renal excretion, allows tight control via activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) and can be stopped or titrated rapidly if bleeding develops. Therefore, upon confirming evidence of aHIT, the prophylactic fondaparinux was discontinued and therapeutic argatroban infusion commenced (0.75 µg kg⁻¹ min⁻¹) with a target aPTT of 55–85 s. This immediate change aimed to suppress thrombin generation while providing a therapeutic window for rapid dose adjustment.

### Monitoring via aPTT, fibrinogen, D‑dimer, and peripheral smear for consumptive coagulopathy

Effective anticoagulation in aHIT requires continual assessment of both therapeutic adequacy and organ‑system involvement.

| Parameter | Rationale | Target/Observation |
|-----------|-----------|---------------------|
| aPTT | Guideline‑based adjustment of argatroban dose. | 55–85 s |
| Fibrinogen | Acute‑phase reactant but decreases reflect consumptive coagulopathy; a falling fibrinogen level suggests ongoing clot formation. | ↓ trend; nadir ≈ 2.5 g L⁻¹ |
| D‑dimer | Marker of fibrin turnover and systemic thrombosis. | ↑ trend during early treatment → ↓ upon adequate control |
| Peripheral smear | Presence of schistocytes (fragmented RBCs) signals microangiopathic hemolysis from in situ clotting. | ↑ schistocytes → indicative of uncontrolled thrombosis |

Monitoring lasted until the patient’s platelet count rose above 50 × 10⁹ L⁻¹ and fibrinogen had stabilized, indicating that a consumptive process had been addressed.

### High‑dose intravenous Ig protocol and expected platelet response

High‑dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) is used in aHIT to block FcγRIIa‑mediated platelet activation. The protocol employed (100 g over 2 h on two consecutive days followed by a third 100 g dose on day three) was chosen because it has been shown to expedite platelet recovery in aHIT. The expected mechanism involves competitive inhibition of IgG of HIT antibodies for Fc receptors on platelets, thereby reducing immune complex‑mediated activation. In most aHIT patients, a brisk rise in platelet count within 48–72 h is typical. In this case, however, the rise was modest (from 13 × 10⁹ L⁻¹ to 54 × 10⁹ L⁻¹ by POD14), reflecting the severity of the autoimmune response. The incremental platelet recovery nevertheless provided a clinical basis to consider changing anticoagulation.

### Assessment of anticoagulation efficacy (fibrinogen decline, D‑dimer trend, schistocyte appearance)

During argatroban therapy, the patient’s fibrinogen levels progressively fell (to a nadir of 2.49 g L⁻¹ on POD17) while D‑dimer peaked around POD12. These findings, coupled with the emergence of schistocytes (POD9–16), indicated that argatroban alone was insufficient to completely suppress hypercoagulability in this severe aHIT. The decision to pivot to fondaparinux was thus driven by biochemical evidence that the hypercoagulable state remained active despite therapeutic anticoagulation. Importantly, the patient's platelet count had already begun to recover by POD14, so the risk of exacerbating thrombocytopenia by switching anticoagulants was acceptable.

### Switch to fondaparinux – indications, dosing, antithrombin supplementation

Fondaparinux was re‑introduced at a therapeutic dose (10 mg once daily SC) on POD14. Clinically, fondaparinux offers once‑daily dosing, eliminating the need for continuous infusion and frequent aPTT monitoring—a vital advantage when the patient’s bleeding risk had already subsided. However, fondaparinux efficacy depends on adequate antithrombin (AT) levels; the patient’s AT was mildly reduced (0.70 U mL⁻¹, RR 0.80–1.40 U mL⁻¹). Therefore, 1 000 U of AT concentrate was administered immediately before fondaparinux initiation to ensure sufficient AT for optimal factor Xa inhibition. Subsequent AT levels increased to within the therapeutic range, confirming that fondaparinux could be safely maintained.

### Criteria for transition back to apixaban and timing of resumption

The decision to return to long‑term oral anticoagulation with apixaban was based on three criteria:

1. **Platelet Count Normalization** – Achieved at 155 × 10⁹ L⁻¹ (POD62), allowing safe continuation of warfarin‑like agents.
2. **Resolution of Hypercoagulability** – Fibrinogen had returned to normal ranges, D‑dimer had normalized, and peripheral smears were devoid of schistocytes.
3. **Stable Clinical Status** – No further thrombotic events, and neurological recovery complete.

Apixaban (5 mg twice daily) was resumed on POD65, when fondaparinux was stopped, and the patient had shown no recurrence of thrombosis. This timing aligns with guideline recommendations that oral anticoagulation can be re‑initiated once platelet counts have stabilized and the risk of HIT‑induced thrombosis has abated.

### Long‑term anticoagulation considerations in aHIT

Patients with aHIT have a heightened risk of recurrent thrombosis, especially in the first months after diagnosis. Therefore, extended anticoagulation beyond the acute phase is advised. In this case, apixaban was continued beyond hospital discharge, with periodic follow‑up of platelet counts and clinical evaluation. Serial serotonin‑release assays remained positive for several months but gradually shifted toward a classic heparin‑dependent pattern, reflecting waning aHIT antibody activity. Despite the persistence of detectable antibodies, the absence of clinical thrombosis and the fact that the patient tolerated apixaban support the safety of long‑term direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) in aHIT, provided the platelet count remains stable and no contraindications arise.

In summary, the management of severe aHIT required early recognition of the heparin‑independent nature of the platelet activation, prompt shift to a short‑acting direct thrombin inhibitor, vigilant monitoring of coagulation markers to detect ongoing consumption, adjunctive high‑dose IVIG to blunt the immune response, and a carefully timed transition to fondaparinux and eventually a DOAC. This comprehensive strategy allowed the patient to recover fully without further thrombotic or hemorrhagic complications.

## 5. Outcome, Follow‑up and Clinical Lessons

### Platelet count recovery and neuro‑functional outcomes

After the switch to therapeutic fondaparinux on postoperative day 14, the patient’s platelet count entered a steady upward trajectory. Recovery was slow, reflecting the profound degree of consumptive thrombopoiesis mediated by the initially aggressive aHIT antibodies. Platelets rose to 54 ×10⁹/L by day 14, 155 ×10⁹/L by day 62, and 202 ×10⁹/L by day 87, comfortably above the published threshold (>50 ×10⁹/L) considered safe for resuming oral anticoagulation. Concomitantly, the patient’s neurologic status improved from delirium and a left‑parietal infarct on imaging to complete resolution of all focal deficits. Neurological examination at 3 months post‑discharge was normal, and there were no further radiologic changes on follow‑up MRI.

### Serial serotonin‑release assay (SRA) profiles

The initial “indeterminate” SRA on day 5 demonstrated robust serotonin release at low heparin concentrations (0.1 and 0.3 U/mL) and, notably, at 0 U/mL, confirming a heparin‑independent component. The partially resistant IV.3 blockade raised the possibility of atypical Fc‐γIIa receptor engagement. Over the ensuing weeks, serial SRAs revealed a gradual shift toward a more classic heparin‑dependent pattern: by day 124 the assay showed minimal release (≤15 %) in the absence of heparin but a full release at 0.3 U/mL. This transition mirrors the clinical course: as the pathological antibodies waned, the remaining reactivity became attributable to low‑affinity, heparin‑dependent IgG typical of “classical” HIT.

Persistently high SRA reactivity even after overt clinical remission underscores the dissociation between laboratory markers and clinical status in aHIT. The patient remained SRA positive for months, reflecting residual low‑level antibody production. Although the clinical significance of this sustained reactivity is unclear, it cautions against premature cessation of non‑heparin anticoagulation solely on the basis of serologic negativity.

### Lessons for early postoperative thrombocytopenia

1. **Early onset is a red flag**: Seventeen days of pre‑operative catheterization delivered 2 000 U of UFH. The platelets fell within the first 5 days after cardiac surgery, a time period normally too early for classic postoperative HIT. A prior exposure to UFH can precipitate aHIT within 48–72 h, so clinicians should keep this possibility in mind when thrombocytopenia occurs unusually early.

2. **Serological nuance matters**: A strong PF4–ELISA accompanied by an atypical SRA – notably serotonin release at 0 U/mL and partial resistance to IV.3 – should trigger a diagnosis of aHIT. Recognizing this pattern avoids misclassifying the patient as “non‑HIT”, preventing the dangerous continuation of heparin or LMWH.

3. **Therapeutic implications**: Because aHIT antibodies may be heparin‑independent, low‑molecular‑weight heparin or direct thrombin inhibitors are not guaranteed to block platelet activation. In this case, the choice of argatroban was driven by the need for rapid, reversible anticoagulation in the setting of recent stroke and severe thrombocytopenia. The eventual switch to fondaparinux, after AT normalization, proved effective and easier to administer, highlighting the importance of individualized anticoagulant selection.

4. **Monitoring strategy**: Routine coagulation panels (PT, aPTT) are inadequate for tracking aHIT activity. Serial SRA or functional platelet activation assays (e.g., heparin‑induced platelet aggregation) should be employed where available, especially in patients with a strong ELISA but ambiguous clinical picture. However, the cost and availability of such assays limit their routine use; clinical judgment remains paramount.

### Recommendations for clinicians

- **Vigilance for pre‑operative heparin exposure**: A detailed medication and procedural history is essential. Even a single intra‑arterial catheterization dose can set the stage for aHIT.
- **Early laboratory work‑up**: For postoperative thrombocytopenia that occurs <5 days after surgery, obtain a PF4–ELISA and, if positive, request a confirmatory functional assay. Wait for day 5 or 6 to repeat the ELISA if the initial result is equivocal, but do not delay empiric therapy if clinical suspicion is high.
- **Avoid heparin derivatives**: Once aHIT is suspected or confirmed, discontinue all heparin products. Direct thrombin inhibitors or factor Xa inhibitors (fondaparinux, rivaroxaban) are preferable unless contraindicated.
- **High‑dose intravenous Ig**: Consider early high‑dose IV Ig (0.5–1 g/kg) to accelerate platelet recovery in severe aHIT, especially when antiplatelet therapy is needed urgently. Monitor platelet trend closely; a delayed response may indicate a more refractory antibody population.
- **Peri‑operative heparin stewardship**: Use the lowest effective UFH dose during cardiac surgery and limit the duration of exposure. Consider alternatives (bivalirudin) in patients with a history of heparin exposure or unusual laboratory findings.

### Implications for peri‑operative heparin management

The case demonstrates that a single dose of UFH during a coronary catheterisation can elicit a robust autoimmune response that manifests weeks later. In the catheterization–surgery continuum, we must:

- **Coordinate anticoagulation plans** across specialties, ensuring that the last heparin exposure is documented and considered in peri‑operative risk assessment.
- **Plan postoperative antithrombotic prophylaxis** with agents that do not depend on heparin epitope recognition if the patient has a history of early post‑operative thrombocytopenia or is otherwise high risk for HIT.
- **Educate teams** on the temporal patterns of HIT, emphasizing that “early” can be misnomer when prior exposure exists.

### Future directions

Current HIT diagnostic panels are tailored to classic heparin‑dependent antibodies. The emergence of aHIT, especially the subset with partial IV.3 inhibition, highlights a gap in detection. Future assays could:

- **Employ dual‑antigen panels** that simultaneously assess heparin‑dependent and -independent platelet activation.
- **Incorporate IC‑5 blocking or anti‑FcγRIIa functional inhibition** to differentiate between classic and autoimmune HIT.
- **Use high‑throughput flow cytometry** to measure platelet activation in response to patient sera across a gradient of heparin concentrations.
- **Develop point‑of‑care tests** allowing rapid assessment of infectious versus immune platelet activation, facilitating timely therapeutic decisions.

In sum, this case illustrates that severe aHIT can arise from even a single pre‑operative heparin dose, present with early thrombocytopenia and stroke, and display an atypical laboratory profile that evolves over time. Recognition of the early commencement of aHIT, the importance of comprehensive serology, and the potential for partial IV.3 inhibition are essential for ensuring prompt, appropriate treatment and safeguarding patient outcomes.
