Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

Chapter 4: ## 4. Management Strategy for Severe Autoimmune HIT

Severe autoimmune HIT (aHIT) presents a therapeutic dilemma: platelet activation persists independently of heparin, so conventional strategies that simply discontinue heparin are often insufficient. In the case described, a multi‑modality approach was required to halt thrombotic complications, reverse consumptive coagulopathy, and allow eventual transition back to long‑term oral anticoagulation.

### Rationale for discontinuing fondaparinux and starting argatroban

Fondaparinux, a synthetic pentasaccharide that indirectly inhibits factor Xa through antithrombin, is effective for heparin‑independent HIT when used at therapeutic doses. However, its pharmacokinetics (long half‑life of 17–21 h) do not permit rapid reversal should bleeding occur. In the immediate postoperative setting of this patient, the risk of hemorrhage was heightened by recent intracerebral infarction and profound thrombocytopenia. Argatroban, a small‑molecule direct thrombin inhibitor with short half‑life (35–80 min) and clear renal excretion, allows tight control via activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) and can be stopped or titrated rapidly if bleeding develops. Therefore, upon confirming evidence of aHIT, the prophylactic fondaparinux was discontinued and therapeutic argatroban infusion commenced (0.75 µg kg⁻¹ min⁻¹) with a target aPTT of 55–85 s. This immediate change aimed to suppress thrombin generation while providing a therapeutic window for rapid dose adjustment.

### Monitoring via aPTT, fibrinogen, D‑dimer, and peripheral smear for consumptive coagulopathy

Effective anticoagulation in aHIT requires continual assessment of both therapeutic adequacy and organ‑system involvement.

| Parameter | Rationale | Target/Observation |
|-----------|-----------|---------------------|
| aPTT | Guideline‑based adjustment of argatroban dose. | 55–85 s |
| Fibrinogen | Acute‑phase reactant but decreases reflect consumptive coagulopathy; a falling fibrinogen level suggests ongoing clot formation. | ↓ trend; nadir ≈ 2.5 g L⁻¹ |
| D‑dimer | Marker of fibrin turnover and systemic thrombosis. | ↑ trend during early treatment → ↓ upon adequate control |
| Peripheral smear | Presence of schistocytes (fragmented RBCs) signals microangiopathic hemolysis from in situ clotting. | ↑ schistocytes → indicative of uncontrolled thrombosis |

Monitoring lasted until the patient’s platelet count rose above 50 × 10⁹ L⁻¹ and fibrinogen had stabilized, indicating that a consumptive process had been addressed.

### High‑dose intravenous Ig protocol and expected platelet response

High‑dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) is used in aHIT to block FcγRIIa‑mediated platelet activation. The protocol employed (100 g over 2 h on two consecutive days followed by a third 100 g dose on day three) was chosen because it has been shown to expedite platelet recovery in aHIT. The expected mechanism involves competitive inhibition of IgG of HIT antibodies for Fc receptors on platelets, thereby reducing immune complex‑mediated activation. In most aHIT patients, a brisk rise in platelet count within 48–72 h is typical. In this case, however, the rise was modest (from 13 × 10⁹ L⁻¹ to 54 × 10⁹ L⁻¹ by POD14), reflecting the severity of the autoimmune response. The incremental platelet recovery nevertheless provided a clinical basis to consider changing anticoagulation.

### Assessment of anticoagulation efficacy (fibrinogen decline, D‑dimer trend, schistocyte appearance)

During argatroban therapy, the patient’s fibrinogen levels progressively fell (to a nadir of 2.49 g L⁻¹ on POD17) while D‑dimer peaked around POD12. These findings, coupled with the emergence of schistocytes (POD9–16), indicated that argatroban alone was insufficient to completely suppress hypercoagulability in this severe aHIT. The decision to pivot to fondaparinux was thus driven by biochemical evidence that the hypercoagulable state remained active despite therapeutic anticoagulation. Importantly, the patient's platelet count had already begun to recover by POD14, so the risk of exacerbating thrombocytopenia by switching anticoagulants was acceptable.

### Switch to fondaparinux – indications, dosing, antithrombin supplementation

Fondaparinux was re‑introduced at a therapeutic dose (10 mg once daily SC) on POD14. Clinically, fondaparinux offers once‑daily dosing, eliminating the need for continuous infusion and frequent aPTT monitoring—a vital advantage when the patient’s bleeding risk had already subsided. However, fondaparinux efficacy depends on adequate antithrombin (AT) levels; the patient’s AT was mildly reduced (0.70 U mL⁻¹, RR 0.80–1.40 U mL⁻¹). Therefore, 1 000 U of AT concentrate was administered immediately before fondaparinux initiation to ensure sufficient AT for optimal factor Xa inhibition. Subsequent AT levels increased to within the therapeutic range, confirming that fondaparinux could be safely maintained.

### Criteria for transition back to apixaban and timing of resumption

The decision to return to long‑term oral anticoagulation with apixaban was based on three criteria:

1. **Platelet Count Normalization** – Achieved at 155 × 10⁹ L⁻¹ (POD62), allowing safe continuation of warfarin‑like agents.
2. **Resolution of Hypercoagulability** – Fibrinogen had returned to normal ranges, D‑dimer had normalized, and peripheral smears were devoid of schistocytes.
3. **Stable Clinical Status** – No further thrombotic events, and neurological recovery complete.

Apixaban (5 mg twice daily) was resumed on POD65, when fondaparinux was stopped, and the patient had shown no recurrence of thrombosis. This timing aligns with guideline recommendations that oral anticoagulation can be re‑initiated once platelet counts have stabilized and the risk of HIT‑induced thrombosis has abated.

### Long‑term anticoagulation considerations in aHIT

Patients with aHIT have a heightened risk of recurrent thrombosis, especially in the first months after diagnosis. Therefore, extended anticoagulation beyond the acute phase is advised. In this case, apixaban was continued beyond hospital discharge, with periodic follow‑up of platelet counts and clinical evaluation. Serial serotonin‑release assays remained positive for several months but gradually shifted toward a classic heparin‑dependent pattern, reflecting waning aHIT antibody activity. Despite the persistence of detectable antibodies, the absence of clinical thrombosis and the fact that the patient tolerated apixaban support the safety of long‑term direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) in aHIT, provided the platelet count remains stable and no contraindications arise.

In summary, the management of severe aHIT required early recognition of the heparin‑independent nature of the platelet activation, prompt shift to a short‑acting direct thrombin inhibitor, vigilant monitoring of coagulation markers to detect ongoing consumption, adjunctive high‑dose IVIG to blunt the immune response, and a carefully timed transition to fondaparinux and eventually a DOAC. This comprehensive strategy allowed the patient to recover fully without further thrombotic or hemorrhagic complications.
