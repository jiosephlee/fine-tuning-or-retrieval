Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

Chapter 2: ## 2. Laboratory Evaluation of Thrombocytopenia

The postoperative course of the patient prompted a systematic laboratory work‑up to determine whether the pronounced drop in platelet count was attributable to a common, non‑immune cause or whether an immune‑mediated process such as heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) was involved. Each laboratory evaluation, from simple peripheral smear to specialized functional assays, contributed incremental information that narrowed the differential diagnosis.

### Peripheral smear assessment

A routine peripheral blood film revealed a marked thrombocytopenia without evidence of platelet clumping or giant platelets. Importantly, there were no schistocytes or other fragmented red cells, which would have suggested microangiopathic hemolysis (e.g., thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura). The absence of typical circulating platelet aggregates also argued against platelet consumption in conditions such as DIC unrelated to a humoral trigger. Thus, the smear excluded mechanical destruction or platelet sequestration as the primary explanation.

### Coagulation profile and acute‑phase reactants

Early in the postoperative period, the patient’s coagulation studies were within normal limits: prothrombin time (PT) and international normalised ratio (INR) remained at the upper normal end of the reference range, and activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) was 34 s (25–37 s). Fibrinogen was markedly elevated (6.59 g/L, RR 2.0–3.9 g/L) and D‑dimer peaked around POD12. These findings are typical for the early postoperative state, where systemic inflammation and surgical trauma elicit a hypercoagulable profile with increased fibrinogen and fibrin degradation products. The normal PT/aPTT argued against a vitamin‑K antagonist effect or intrinsic coagulation factor deficiency, while the raised fibrinogen was an expected acute‑phase response rather than a consumptive coagulopathy in the absence of schistocytes and normal PT/aPTT.

### Serum biochemistry to exclude hemolysis and marrow suppression

The hemoglobin level fell modestly (97 g/L) compared with pre‑operative values, but remained above the threshold for significant bleeding. A reticulocyte count of 73 × 10⁹/L (RR 10–100 × 10⁹/L) and an elevated haptoglobin (3.19 g/L, RR 0.32–1.97 g/L) indicated no active intravascular hemolysis. Vitamin‑B12 was within normal limits, ruling out megaloblastic pancytopenia. Thus, marrow suppression or hemolytic processes were unlikely contributors to thrombocytopenia.

### PF4/polyanion ELISA for HIT antibody screening

The high‑optical‑density result (2.2 OD, RR < 0.4 OD) on the PF4/polyanion enzyme‑linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was strongly positive. This assay detects IgG, IgM, and IgA antibodies against the platelet factor 4 (PF4)/heparin complex and, while highly sensitive, is not specific for pathogenic antibodies. A strongly positive ELISA is one of the first steps in the HIT diagnostic algorithm and warrants functional confirmation.

### Serotonin‑release assay (SRA) – distinguishing heparin‑dependent vs independent activity

The serotonin‑release assay (SRA) is the gold‑standard functional test for HIT, measuring platelet activation mediated by patient antibodies. In this patient, the SRA showed robust serotonin release at therapeutic heparin concentrations (0.1 U/mL and 0.3 U/mL, 97–99 % release), but no release at a supratherapeutic concentration (100 U/mL) where PF4/heparin complexes cannot form. This biphasic pattern is characteristic of heparin‑dependent HIT antibodies. However, a distinctive additional finding was almost complete activation at 0 U/mL (96 % release), demonstrating that the antibodies could trigger platelet degranulation without exogenous heparin. This heparin‑independent activity is diagnostic of autoimmune HIT (aHIT). The absence of activation at high‐dose heparin is a key discriminating feature: in classic HIT, platelet activation diminishes sharply at high heparin concentrations because the PF4/heparin complex cannot form, whereas in aHIT the antibodies can bind PF4 alone or in low‑affinity complexes, allowing activation even without added heparin.

### Role of FcγRIIa blocking antibody (IV.3)

The functional effect of platelet FcγRIIa (CD32a) clustering is central to HIT pathogenesis: the Fc portion of IgG antibodies bridges adjacent platelets, triggering activation. To confirm this mechanism, the monoclonal antibody IV.3, which blocks FcγRIIa, is added during the SRA. In classic HIT, IV.3 abrogates almost all serotonin release (> 80 % inhibition), because activating antibodies rely on FcγRIIa engagement. In this case, only partial inhibition occurred (76 % release remained in the presence of IV.3), indicating that the antibodies possessed an atypical, high‑avidity or Fab‑mediated activation component. This partial resistance is an uncommon feature of aHIT that further underscores the severity and atypical serologic profile of the patient’s antibodies.

### Summary of laboratory interpretation

The combination of a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, heparin‑dependent SRA reactivity at therapeutically relevant heparin concentrations, exquisite activation in the absence of heparin, and incomplete FcγRIIa blockade confirms a diagnosis of autoimmune HIT. The ancillary laboratory data—normal coagulation parameters, absence of hemolysis, and a typical early‑postoperative hypercoagulable profile—excluded other common causes of thrombocytopenia such as DIC, thrombotic microangiopathy, or marrow suppression. Thus, the laboratory evaluation focused the diagnostic algorithm on HIT, guided therapeutic decisions, and provided a framework for monitoring disease activity and response to treatment.
