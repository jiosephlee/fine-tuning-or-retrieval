Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# When a Young Woman’s Fatigue Is a Red Flag:  
### Recognizing Telomere Biology Disorders in Adults

---

## The Case That Made Us Stop and Think

You’re sitting in the clinic with a young woman in her late twenties. She’s complained of fatigue for five years, recently started feeling more breathless on exertion, and even noticed that her hair has started graying prematurely. On exam, she has mild pallor, splenomegaly, and a few skin changes—just a faint patch of hyperpigmented nail beds. A full blood count shows pancytopenia with a low reticulocyte count. Vitamin B12 is down, folate is low, and you’re tempted to diagnose “nutritional pancytopenia.” But the picture isn’t that simple. The “normal” work‑up is tasting like a puzzle missing a critical piece.  

We eventually found a monoallelic heterozygous variant in **PARN** (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) in her germline DNA. The variant was initially a *variant of uncertain significance*, but the clinical story—pre‑mature greying, bone marrow failure, hepatic fibrosis, and diffuse pulmonary fibrosis—was hard to explain otherwise. The diagnosis? A telomere biology disorder (TBD).  

---

## Why That Matters

TBDs are a group of inherited disorders caused by mutations in genes that maintain telomere length. Classic examples are Dyskeratosis Congenita (DC) and its severe neonatal forms (Hoyeraal‑Hreidarsson, Revesz, Coats‑plus). Traditionally, clinicians think of the mucocutaneous triad (hair greying, skin hyperpigmentation, nail dystrophy) when considering TBDs. But in adults, the presentation can be subtle: bone marrow failure, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, or even just unexplained fatigue.

Missing the diagnosis can lead to **missed opportunities for early surveillance** and **inappropriate therapeutic choices**—for instance, giving a myelosuppressive drug to a patient whose marrow is already frail.  

---

## The Red‑Flag Combination

When you’re evaluating a young adult with chronic fatigue and cytopenia, keep these clues in mind:

- **Low reticulocyte count** despite anemia → ineffective erythropoiesis rather than simple hemolysis.
- **Premature hair greying** without chronic disease (think “unexplained hair changes”).
- **Splenomegaly or hepatomegaly** on a bland exam.
- **Bone pain/osteopenia** unexplained by usual osteoporosis aetiologies.
- **Subclinical pulmonary involvement** (diffuse ground‑glass opacities, fibrosis on imaging).
- **Preserved skin/nail appearance**—the classic triad may be absent.

If you see any of these **in clusters**, suspect a TBD. The pre‑clinical clues can be so subtle that you might miss them unless you actively look for them.

---

## Diagnostic Workflow—A Pragmatic Approach

| Step | What to Do | Rationale |
|------|------------|-----------|
| 1. Full Blood Count & Retics | Obtain CBC with differential and retic count | Low retics + pancytopenia suggests marrow failure |
| 2. Peripheral Smear | Look for basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, hypersegmented neutrophils | Ineffective erythropoiesis features |
| 3. Iron, Selenium, Vitamin B12/Folate | Rule out nutritional deficiencies | Common causes need to be stripped away first |
| 4. Bone Marrow Aspirate/Biopsy | Evaluate for dysplasia, fibrosis, or infiltrative disease | Confirms marrow pathology independent of systemic causes |
| 5. Telomere Length Testing | Flow-FISH or qPCR | Short telomeres are the biochemical hallmark |
| 6. Genetic Panel (or Exome) | Targeted telomere genes (TERT, TERC, RTEL1, PARN, etc.) | Provides definitive diagnosis, informs prognosis and family counseling |
| 7. Imaging for Fibrosis | HRCT chest, transient elastography, ultrasound | Detects pulmonary and hepatic involvement early |
| 8. Multidisciplinary Discussion | Hematology, pulmonology, hepatology, genetics, genetics counseling | TBDs are multi‑organ; care must be coordinated |

A **step‑wise elimination** approach keeps your differential narrow and ensures you don't overlook a genetic cause when the presentation is partially overlapping with common acquired conditions.

---

## When the Genetic Test Returns a Variant of Uncertain Significance

In our patient, the PARN variant was *uncertain*. However, consider the entire clinical context alongside the ACMG/AMP criteria:

- **PM1** – Variant in a critical functional domain (R3H domain).
- **PM2** – Extremely rare in population databases.
- **PP3** – Multiple in‑silico tools predict damaging effect.
- **PP2** – Gene with low benign missense variation.

Even when the variant cannot be called pathogenic, a **“likely pathogenic”** label can be justified if the phenotype aligns strongly. Importantly, it changes management: ```Stay vigilant for marrow failure, routine telomere length monitoring, and discuss familial implications.```

---

## Take‑Home Pearls

- **Don’t rely on classic mucocutaneous triad**; adults can present with marrow, hepatic, or pulmonary fibrosis and minimal skin findings.
- **Low reticulocyte count** in the setting of pancytopenia is a flag for ineffective erythropoiesis and potential marrow frailty.
- **Premature greying or early hair shedding** should trigger a TBI work‑up even if skin lesions are absent.
- **Screen for organ fibrosis** (liver and lungs) early; imaging can reveal subclinical disease that may guide surveillance.
- **Call genetics early**. A judicious interdisciplinary assessment can prevent unnecessary treatments that could further compromise marrow or other organs.

---

## Bottom Line

A young woman with chronic fatigue, pancytopenia, and subtle emerging signs of premature aging can be a red‑flag snapshot of a telomere biology disorder. The key is to **connect the dots**—imaging, marrow pathology, and subtle genetic clues—rather than waiting for a classic mucocutaneous triad. Early, multidisciplinary evaluation and genetic counseling can change the trajectory of care, allowing for proactive monitoring and family planning in a disease that flies under the radar when framed within the typical DC symptomology.

*Remember:* In the era of precision medicine, the absence of the “classic” tells physicians to look a little deeper. Happy sleuthing!
