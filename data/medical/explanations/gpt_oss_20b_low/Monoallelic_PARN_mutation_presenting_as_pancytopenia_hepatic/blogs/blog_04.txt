Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# Hepatic and Pulmonary Fibrosis in the Young: An Overlooked Manifestation of Telomere Biology Disorders

## The “hidden” hook  
When a 28‑year‑old woman presents with five years of fatigue, progressive weakness, and an unexplained constellation of pancytopenia, liver enlargement, and diffuse lung scarring, most clinicians stop at “multisystem failure” and consider infectious or autoimmune etiologies. It was only after a meticulous work‑up, including a full exome panel, that the culprit emerged: a monoallelic PARN mutation. The take‑away? Fibrosis of the liver *and* the lungs can be the first clue that a patient has a telomere biology disorder (TBD) – even if they lack the classic dusty patches of dyskeratosis congenita (DC).  

In this post we walk through the imaging pearls, the pathophysiology that ties fibrosis to telomere shortening, and a practical screening “roadmap” for anyone who sees early fibrotic disease in a young patient.

---

## What made this case unusual?

- **Age**: late 20s with NO family history of fibrosis or premature ageing.
- **Two organs, same problem**: hepatic fibrosis (cirrhotic‑like elastography) + idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) on CT.
- **Multifaceted presentation**: pancytopenia, B12 deficiency, neuropathy, osteoporosis, greying hair, and a subtle but clear “premature ageing” picture.
- **Uncommon genetics**: a single pathogenic‑likeness variant in *PARN* – usually we think of *TERT*, *TERC*, *RTEL1*, *DKC1* in TBDs.

---

## The imaging story

### Liver  
- **Transient elastography (FibroScan)**: stiffness 18.1 kPa (cirrhosis‑grade 4), with a controlled attenuation parameter 295 dB/m (moderate steatosis).  
- **Ultrasound**: hepatomegaly + splenomegaly, no focal lesions.

> High liver stiffness in a young adult *almost always* prompts a search for non‑hepatitis viral infections, alcohol use, metabolic syndromes, or genetic cholestatic disorders. In the absence of those, a TBD should be considered.

### Lungs  
- **PET‑CT**: ground‑glass opacities + subpleural fibrosis bilaterally.  
- **HRCT**: classic IPF pattern – honeycombing, traction bronchiectasis, and diffuse subpleural scarring.

> The CT shows “H” (honeycomb) and “G” (ground‑glass), the typical IPF hallmarks. But in TB pathologies, lung disease can be the *only* manifestation early on.

---

## Why do telomere disorders cause fibrotic organ damage?

Telomeres protect chromosome ends. Each stem‑cell division shortens them a few base pairs. When telomeres become dangerously short → cellular senescence or apoptosis.  

- **Liver**: The hepatocyte progenitor cells (oval cells), Kupffer cells, and biliary epithelium must proliferate during injury. Short, dysfunctional telomeres limit this repair response → chronic injury → fibrogenesis.  
- **Lung**: Alveolar type II pneumocytes are the main self‑renewing cells of the lung. Telomere attrition leads to alveolar collapse, fibroblast activation, and the fixed scarring seen in IPF.

> PARN is crucial for the maturation of the telomerase RNA component (TERC). Even a single loss‑of‑function allele can disrupt TERC stability enough to impair telomerase, especially under stress like chronic inflammation or high turnover.

## Screening algorithm: when to order telomere length?

| Step | What to do | Why it matters |
|------|------------|----------------|
| 1 | **Clinical red flags** <br>- unexplained pancytopenia <br>- atypical liver injury/ fibrosis <br>- early interstitial lung disease <br>- unexplained premature hair greying, nail dystrophy, or mucosal leukoplakia <br>- aplastic anemia or IPF in a young adult | Identifies those at highest yield for telomere testing. |
| 2 | **Basic labs**: CBC (including retic), vitamin B12, folate, iron panel, liver function tests, lipid panel, auto‑immune panel (ANA, SS-A/B, RF, etc.) | Excludes common nutritional or autoimmune mimics. |
| 3 | **Imaging**: HRCT of chest if pulmonary symptoms; FibroScan for liver stiffness if hepatic abnormality. | Detects fibrotic changes that warrant genetic evaluation. |
| 4 | **Telomere length measurement**: Flow‑FISH or qPCR in peripheral blood lymphocytes. | Telomere length < 10th percentile for age strongly suggests TBD. |
| 5 | **Gene panel**: First‑line TBD panel (TERT, TERC, RTEL1, PARN, DKC1, etc.). | Rapid mutation identification; informs prognosis and family counselling. |
| 6 | **Multidisciplinary follow‑up**: Hematology, pulmonology, hepatology, genetics, and primary care. | Promotes surveillance and timely interventions. |

*Tip*: If telomere length is borderline and no mutation is found, consider exome sequencing, as many TBDs are monogenic but rare.

---

## Surveillance strategies once a TBD is confirmed

| Organ | Suggested Tests & Time Frame | Rationale |
|-------|----------------------------|-----------|
| **Bone Marrow** | CBC + reticulocyte count every 3–6 months. | Early detection of cytopenia or failure. |
| **Lungs** | HRCT every 12 months or sooner if symptomatic. pulmonary function tests every 6 months. | IPF progression is slow but irreversible; early intervention (e.g., antifibrotics) may help. |
| **Liver** | FibroScan or MRI elastography every 12 months. LFTs every 6 months. | Monitor fibrosis progression; detect early cirrhosis. |
| **Cardiovascular** | Echocardiogram at baseline, then every 2 years. | Pulmonary hypertension can develop secondary to IPF. |
| **Reproductive** | Discuss fertility preservation; consider pre‑implantation genetic screening if planning family. | Many TBD carriers have reproductive complications (e.g., miscarriages). |
| **Lifestyle & Medications** | Avoid estrogen‑containing oral contraceptives; limit alcohol; optimize iron status; counsel on smoking cessation. | Protective measures against telomerase inhibition or oxidative stress. |

---

## The clinical pearls

- **If a young adult has unexplained fibrosis (hepatic or pulmonary) *and* cytopenia, run a telomere length test.**  
- **PARN for single‑allele missense variants is an emerging TB disorder cause—don’t overlook it.**  
- **Telomere length is the gold standard but not mandatory if a pathogenic variant is found; the presence of telomeric shortening corroborates the diagnosis.**  
- **Early detection is crucial because bone‑marrow transplant (HCT) offers cure for hematologic disease, but lung and liver fibrosis are largely irreversible.**  
- **Screen family members for the variant and offer reproductive counselling – many adolescents with TBD are at risk of reproduction complications.**

---

## Take‑home messages

- Fibrosis of the liver and lungs can *precede* or *co‑exist with* classic mucocutaneous signs of DC.  
- PARN mutations convey a broad phenotype—pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, bone marrow failure, and premature ageing.  
- A systematic, multidisciplinary screening and surveillance plan dramatically improves outcomes by catching organ dysfunction early.  
- Always think of TBDs in young patients with multi‑organ, non‑infectious fibrosis; a single gene panel can unveil a treatable or manageable condition.  

Keep those liver‑stiffness numbers in your differential, and you might just save a patient from an irreversible cascade.
