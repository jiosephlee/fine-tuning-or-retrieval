Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

Chapter 2: ## 2. Differential Diagnosis and Red‑Flag Features

A systematic approach to the patient’s pancytopenia, organ fibrosis and premature‑ageing clues initially guided clinicians through more common, treatable conditions before considering a telomere‑related etiology. Table 1 (not shown here) outlines the evolution of the diagnostic work‑up.

### Vitamin B12 and folate deficiency with ineffective erythropoiesis  
The most straightforward explanation for a low reticulocyte count, dimorphic erythrocytes, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings and hypersegmented neutrophils is a severe folate or vitamin B12 deficiency. These nutrients are indispensable for DNA synthesis; their lack stalls erythroblast maturation, producing ineffective erythropoiesis that mimics marrow failure. In the present case, serum B12 was markedly reduced (1.5 pg/mL) and folate was deficient (7 ng/mL), explaining the anaemia, neuropathy and gastrointestinal symptoms. Correcting the deficiency with high‑dose cyanocobalamin and folic acid markedly improved the haematological indices, yet the persistence of cytopenia and organ damage elevated suspicion for a deeper problem.

### Immune‑mediated cytopenia  
The presence of a positive direct antiglobulin test (IgG 2+) might point toward autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. However, the laboratory profile was discordant: lactate dehydrogenase and indirect bilirubin were only mildly elevated, and the reticulocyte response remained blunted. Moreover, the indirect Coombs test was negative, and the patient’s autoantibody panel (ANA, anti‑ENA, anti‑hepatitis antibodies) was unremarkable. Therefore, the DAT positivity was deemed incidental or due to a low‑grade, clinically silent autoimmune reaction rather than the driver of the anaemia.

### Hepatic fibrosis in a young adult  
Transient elastography demonstrated cirrhosis (liver stiffness 18.1 kPa). In an individual in his late twenties, the differential includes autoimmune hepatitis, viral hepatitis (HBV, HCV, HEV), alcohol‑related injury, non‑alcoholic fatty liver disease and hereditary cholestatic or metabolic disorders. A comprehensive work‑up—negative hepatitis serologies, no history of alcohol excess, normal metabolic panel (cholesterol, triglycerides) and a normal liver ultrasound apart from steatosis—excluded these common etiologies. When viral, metabolic and autoimmune causes are ruled out, the remaining possibility is a primary, often genetically driven, fibrosing disorder such as a telomere biology disorder.

### Pulmonary fibrosis  
Chest CT revealed diffuse ground‑glass opacities with subpleural fibrosis—typical of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF). Age, environmental exposures (e.g., smoking, occupational dust) and connective‑tissue disease markers were absent, favoring a “idiopathic” classification. IPF in a young adult is uncommon unless an inherited predisposition exists. Chronic infections (e.g., tuberculosis) or drug toxicity were excluded by serial microbiology and medication review. Thus, ILD was considered part of a multi‑organ fibrosing syndrome.

### Systemic premature‑ageing signs  
The patient exhibited greying hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation, bald tongue, atrophic lingual papillae and skeletal changes (scoliosis, osteoporosis). These features are classic mucocutaneous manifestations of dyskeratosis congenita (DC). However, in this case, the triad was incomplete: there was no nail dystrophy or oral leukoplakia. Subclinical mucocutaneous findings, when coupled with bone marrow failure or fibrosis, suggest an underlying telomere‑related defect.

### Inclusion of telomere biology disorders (TBDs) in the differential  
When a patient presents with simultaneous bone marrow dysplasia, organ fibrosis, and premature‑ageing features—yet lacks a clear autoimmune, infectious or metabolic cause—TBDs should be high on the list. The spectrum of TBDs ranges from classic DC (with the mucocutaneous triad) to isolated pulmonary fibrosis or bone marrow failure. Importantly, monoallelic variants in genes such as TERT, TERC, RTEL1, and PARN may manifest as adult‑onset IPF, marrow failure, or combined phenotypes.

### Red‑flags prompting genetic testing  
Several clinical clues tipped the balance toward a genetic evaluation:

1. **Persistent cytopenia after B12/folate correction** – failure to achieve durable haemoglobin recovery suggests an intrinsic marrow defect.
2. **Early‑onset, multifocal organ fibrosis** – cirrhosis and IPF in a young adult have a high yield for inherited causes.
3. **Early‑onset osteoporosis** – secondary to hypogonadism or marrow failure, but also described in UITBDs.
4. **Neuropathy** – asymmetric sensorimotor axonal loss may result from telomere‑mediated neuronal degeneration.
5. **Absence of a family history** – while suggestive of de‑novo mutation, many TBDs have incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity, making family history unreliable.
6. **Subclinical mucocutaneous findings** – greying, nail changes, or oral mucosal atrophy, even if isolated, can be the first hint of a telomerase defect.

These red‑flags support the decision to pursue next‑generation sequencing, with a focus on telomere‑related genes. In this case, clinical exome sequencing identified a monoallelic PARN missense variant (c.613T>C), aligning the genetic diagnosis with the multi‑system presentation and cementing the diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder.
