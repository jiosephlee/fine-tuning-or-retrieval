Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

## 1. Clinical Presentation and Initial Diagnostic Work‑up

A young woman in her late twenties presented with a **5‑year history of progressive fatigue, unintentional weight loss, and dyspnea on exertion**. The functional decline began insidiously: she reported a gradual reduction in her ability to perform routine tasks, which magnified over the last three months to the point of needing assistance with self‑care. Associated respiratory symptoms included intermittent chest discomfort and exertional shortness of breath, suggestive of early pulmonary compromise.

### Physical examination
On examination the patient was hemodynamically stable but visibly **pallor and icterus** were noted. Dermatologic examination revealed **premature greying of hair** and **knuckle hyperpigmentation**, while the tongue was **atrophic with a loss of papillary ridges** – a classic sign of macroglossia that can accompany vitamin B12 deficiency or systemic disease. Musculoskeletal inspection uncovered **thoracic scoliosis** and **bilateral thenar atrophy**, hinting at chronic muscle wasting. Abdominal palpation demonstrated **hepatomegaly** (≈5 cm below the right costal margin) and a palpable spleen, confirming **hepatosplenomegaly**. Respiratory auscultation identified **reduced breath sounds** in the infra‑scapular areas, raising suspicion for interstitial involvement. Cardiovascular and neurologic examinations were otherwise unremarkable.

### Baseline laboratory data
A **complete blood count** revealed **pancytopenia** with a markedly low **reticulocyte count (0.39 %)**, indicating a blunted marrow response. Peripheral smear morphology showed **dimorphic erythrocytes**, **basophilic stippling**, presence of **Cabot rings**, and **hypersegmented neutrophils**. These features collectively point toward **ineffective erythropoiesis**, often seen in megaloblastic anemia or marrow failure syndromes.

Serum studies confirmed **severe vitamin B12 deficiency** and low folate levels. Iron studies were paradoxically **elevated serum iron and ferritin**, consistent with iron retention secondary to ineffective erythropoiesis rather than true iron overload. Inflammatory markers were elevated, but **autoimmune serologies** (ANA, hepatitis antibodies) were negative, narrowing the differential to nutritional or marrow‑centric causes.

### Bone marrow evaluation
An aspirate and core biopsy showed **trilineage hematopoiesis** with **erythroid hyperplasia** and **increased iron stores**. No cytogenetic abnormalities or dysplastic features were detected. The absence of malignancy or infiltrative disease, coupled with the marrow’s attempt to compensate for peripheral cytopenias, reinforced the diagnosis of **ineffective erythropoiesis** rather than aplastic or neoplastic processes.

### Hepatic assessment
Abdominal ultrasound identified **hepatosplenomegaly**. **Transient elastography** quantified **grade II steatosis** (controlled attenuation parameter 295 dB/m) and **cirrhosis** (liver stiffness 18.1 kPa). An infectious work‑up (HBV, HCV, HIV, CMV) and autoimmune work‑up (ANA, ASMA, AMA, LKM‑1) were negative. These findings suggested a non‑etiologic source of hepatic fibrosis, with nutritional or genetic etiologies being foremost considerations.

### Pulmonary imaging
Whole‑body **18F‑FDG PET‑CT** revealed diffuse **ground‑glass haziness** with **fibrotic and subpleural atelectatic changes** bilaterally, consistent with **idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF)**. No focal uptake suggesting malignancy or granulomatous disease was seen, thereby focusing the differential toward interstitial lung disease rather than metastatic or infectious etiologies.

### Additional organ involvement
A **dual‑energy X‑ray absorptiometry (DEXA)** scan documented **osteoporosis**, aligning with the patient’s history of recurrent bone pain and fractures. Laboratory derangements included **secondary hyperparathyroidism**, **hypocalcaemia**, **hypokalaemia**, and **vitamin D deficiency**, all contributing to bone demineralization. **Electromyography and nerve conduction studies** uncovered an **asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy** with chronic neurogenic changes, corroborating the clinical signs of peripheral neuropathy.

### Summary of the initial differential
The constellation of **pancytopenia with ineffective erythropoiesis**, **hepatic fibrosis**, **pulmonary fibrosis**, **premature greying**, and **multisystem involvement** steered the diagnostic process toward a **telomere biology disorder** rather than isolated nutritional deficiency or acquired marrow failure. These findings justified proceeding to a targeted genetic evaluation, which ultimately revealed a monoallelic variant in **PARN**, a key player in telomere maintenance.

## 2. Differential Diagnosis and Red‑Flag Features

A systematic approach to the patient’s pancytopenia, organ fibrosis and premature‑ageing clues initially guided clinicians through more common, treatable conditions before considering a telomere‑related etiology. Table 1 (not shown here) outlines the evolution of the diagnostic work‑up.

### Vitamin B12 and folate deficiency with ineffective erythropoiesis  
The most straightforward explanation for a low reticulocyte count, dimorphic erythrocytes, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings and hypersegmented neutrophils is a severe folate or vitamin B12 deficiency. These nutrients are indispensable for DNA synthesis; their lack stalls erythroblast maturation, producing ineffective erythropoiesis that mimics marrow failure. In the present case, serum B12 was markedly reduced (1.5 pg/mL) and folate was deficient (7 ng/mL), explaining the anaemia, neuropathy and gastrointestinal symptoms. Correcting the deficiency with high‑dose cyanocobalamin and folic acid markedly improved the haematological indices, yet the persistence of cytopenia and organ damage elevated suspicion for a deeper problem.

### Immune‑mediated cytopenia  
The presence of a positive direct antiglobulin test (IgG 2+) might point toward autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. However, the laboratory profile was discordant: lactate dehydrogenase and indirect bilirubin were only mildly elevated, and the reticulocyte response remained blunted. Moreover, the indirect Coombs test was negative, and the patient’s autoantibody panel (ANA, anti‑ENA, anti‑hepatitis antibodies) was unremarkable. Therefore, the DAT positivity was deemed incidental or due to a low‑grade, clinically silent autoimmune reaction rather than the driver of the anaemia.

### Hepatic fibrosis in a young adult  
Transient elastography demonstrated cirrhosis (liver stiffness 18.1 kPa). In an individual in his late twenties, the differential includes autoimmune hepatitis, viral hepatitis (HBV, HCV, HEV), alcohol‑related injury, non‑alcoholic fatty liver disease and hereditary cholestatic or metabolic disorders. A comprehensive work‑up—negative hepatitis serologies, no history of alcohol excess, normal metabolic panel (cholesterol, triglycerides) and a normal liver ultrasound apart from steatosis—excluded these common etiologies. When viral, metabolic and autoimmune causes are ruled out, the remaining possibility is a primary, often genetically driven, fibrosing disorder such as a telomere biology disorder.

### Pulmonary fibrosis  
Chest CT revealed diffuse ground‑glass opacities with subpleural fibrosis—typical of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF). Age, environmental exposures (e.g., smoking, occupational dust) and connective‑tissue disease markers were absent, favoring a “idiopathic” classification. IPF in a young adult is uncommon unless an inherited predisposition exists. Chronic infections (e.g., tuberculosis) or drug toxicity were excluded by serial microbiology and medication review. Thus, ILD was considered part of a multi‑organ fibrosing syndrome.

### Systemic premature‑ageing signs  
The patient exhibited greying hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation, bald tongue, atrophic lingual papillae and skeletal changes (scoliosis, osteoporosis). These features are classic mucocutaneous manifestations of dyskeratosis congenita (DC). However, in this case, the triad was incomplete: there was no nail dystrophy or oral leukoplakia. Subclinical mucocutaneous findings, when coupled with bone marrow failure or fibrosis, suggest an underlying telomere‑related defect.

### Inclusion of telomere biology disorders (TBDs) in the differential  
When a patient presents with simultaneous bone marrow dysplasia, organ fibrosis, and premature‑ageing features—yet lacks a clear autoimmune, infectious or metabolic cause—TBDs should be high on the list. The spectrum of TBDs ranges from classic DC (with the mucocutaneous triad) to isolated pulmonary fibrosis or bone marrow failure. Importantly, monoallelic variants in genes such as TERT, TERC, RTEL1, and PARN may manifest as adult‑onset IPF, marrow failure, or combined phenotypes.

### Red‑flags prompting genetic testing  
Several clinical clues tipped the balance toward a genetic evaluation:

1. **Persistent cytopenia after B12/folate correction** – failure to achieve durable haemoglobin recovery suggests an intrinsic marrow defect.
2. **Early‑onset, multifocal organ fibrosis** – cirrhosis and IPF in a young adult have a high yield for inherited causes.
3. **Early‑onset osteoporosis** – secondary to hypogonadism or marrow failure, but also described in UITBDs.
4. **Neuropathy** – asymmetric sensorimotor axonal loss may result from telomere‑mediated neuronal degeneration.
5. **Absence of a family history** – while suggestive of de‑novo mutation, many TBDs have incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity, making family history unreliable.
6. **Subclinical mucocutaneous findings** – greying, nail changes, or oral mucosal atrophy, even if isolated, can be the first hint of a telomerase defect.

These red‑flags support the decision to pursue next‑generation sequencing, with a focus on telomere‑related genes. In this case, clinical exome sequencing identified a monoallelic PARN missense variant (c.613T>C), aligning the genetic diagnosis with the multi‑system presentation and cementing the diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder.

## 3. Genetic Testing and Pathophysiological Insight

### Clinical Exome Sequencing Workflow

In patients with unexplained multisystem disease, a first‑tier approach is a comprehensive, next‑generation sequencing panel that interrogates all coding exons of genes known or suspected to cause inherited disorders. For this case, a **clinical exome sequencing** (CES) panel was applied to genomic DNA extracted from peripheral blood. Library preparation involved hybrid capture of ~30 000 genes, followed by paired‑end sequencing on a high‑throughput platform. Read depth typically exceeded 80×, ensuring robust variant calling. After alignment to the GRCh38 reference genome, single‑nucleotide variants (SNVs) and small insertions/deletions (indels) were called using a consensus variant‑calling pipeline that integrates GATK, FreeBayes and Strelka2 outputs. Variants were annotated with population allele frequencies (gnomAD, ExAC), predicted functional impact (PolyPhen‑2, SIFT), and disease‑association databases (ClinVar, HGMD). Only **germline heterozygous** variants were considered relevant to a single‑gene Mendelian disorder, while a joint analysis of somatic mutation pipelines was reserved for suspected hematological malignancies—a category excluded by bone marrow morphology in this patient.

### Identification of a Monoallelic PARN Missense Variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg)

The CES revealed a **heterozygous missense change** in exon 8 of *PARN* (c.613T>C), predicting an amino‑acid substitution Cysteine 205 to Arginine (p.C205R). This residue lies within the highly conserved **R3H domain**, a zinc‑binding motif critical for ribonuclease activity. Variant filtering retained this allele since it was absent from large population databases (gnomAD v2/3 allele frequency < 1 × 10⁻⁵). No other plausible pathogenic variants were found in known telomere‑related genes.

#### Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS) per ACMG/AMP

The American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics/Association for Molecular Pathology (ACMG/AMP) framework assigns discrete criteria for pathogenicity. For p.C205R, the following criteria were met:

- **PM1** – Located in a critical domain (R3H) with no known benign variants.  
- **PM2** – Extremely rare in population databases.  
- **PP2** – *PARN* is a gene with a low benign missense rate; missense variants are a common disease mechanism.  
- **PP3** – Multiple in silico tools (SIFT, PolyPhen‑2, MutationTaster, LRT, CADD) predict a deleterious effect.  

However, **no functional data** or segregation evidence were available, and the variant had not been reported in ClinVar or published case reports. Consequently, the ACMG/AMP tiering defines it as a **Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS)**—a clinically meaningful finding but lacking definitive evidence to alter management.

### Functional Importance of the R3H Domain and Cysteine 205

*Cysteine 205* resides in the core of the R3H domain’s zinc‑binding pocket. The R3H motif coordinates a divalent metal ion, enabling the 3′–5′ exoribonuclease activity of *PARN*. Conservation analysis across vertebrate species shows Cys205 is invariant in all annotated orthologs, underscoring its structural/functional necessity. In silico predictors uniformly flagged the substitution as pathogenic: SIFT score 0.0 (deleterious), PolyPhen‑2 probability 0.99 (probably damaging), MutationTaster (disease causing), LRT (deleterious), and CADD‑PHRED 29, all within the upper percentile for deleteriousness.

### PARN, RNA Deadenylation, and TERC Maturation

*PARN* (poly‑A‑specific ribonuclease) is a 3′–5′ exoribonuclease that trims the poly(A) tail of pre‑mRNA and various non‑coding RNAs. Its pivotal role in telomere biology lies in the processing of the telomerase RNA component (TERC). Newly synthesized TERC acquires a short oligo(A) tail via nuclear polyadenylation. *PARN* trims this tail, allowing proper 3′‑end maturation, binding of the telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT), and incorporation into the telomerase holoenzyme. Loss of *PARN* activity (haploinsufficiency) leads to an accumulation of polyadenylated, unstable TERC, reducing telomerase complex assembly, and thereby limiting telomere elongation.

In hematopoietic stem cells and rapidly dividing epithelial tissues (skin, mucosa, liver, lung), telomeres undergo frequent replication. When telomerase activity is compromised, telomere shortening accelerates, precipitating DNA damage responses, cellular senescence, and apoptosis. The resulting organ‑specific functional deficits align with the patient’s pancytopenia (bone marrow failure), hepatic fibrosis (liver regeneration requires telomerase), and pulmonary fibrosis (alveolar epithelial type II cells). The premature greying, osteoporosis, and neuropathy reflect altered regenerative capacity across ectodermal and skeletal tissues.

### Phenotypic Spectrum of PARN Mutations

- **Biallelic (autosomal recessive) pathogenic variants** identify *DKCB6*, an autosomal‑recessive form of dyskeratosis congenita (DC). Clinical features include classic mucocutaneous triad, bone marrow failure, and markedly early organ failure.
- **Homozygous or compound heterozygous mutations** have been implicated in the severe **Hoyeraal‑Hreidarsson** phenotype, with profound neurodevelopmental delay, microcephaly, and pan‑organ failure.
- **Monoallelic (heterozygous) pathogenic variants** commonly manifest as **telomere‑related pulmonary fibrosis** or **bone marrow failure** (telomere‑related pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure type 4, TPFM‑4). Variable penetrance may lead to isolated IPF, isolated aplastic anemia, or overlapping features.

In contrast, **TERT**, **TERC**, and **RTEL1** mutations typically produce a more classic “DC spectrum” or “IPF spectrum” depending on allele burden and zygosity, with *TERT* and *TERC* mutations frequently causing familial IPF, while *RTEL1* heterozygotes are predisposed to both IPF and marrow failure.

### Incomplete Penetrance, Somatic Rescue, and Environmental Modifiers

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) display **high variability** in age of onset, organ involvement, and severity, even among individuals sharing the same pathogenic allele. Several mechanisms explain this:

1. **Incomplete penetrance**: Many carriers of heterozygous pathogenic variants remain asymptomatic, suggesting that a single mutant allele may suffice only under certain cellular stresses.
2. **Somatic rescue**: Somatic re‑activation of telomerase or acquisition of secondary mutations in telomere‑related genes can compensate for germline deficits, delaying or preventing disease manifestations.
3. **Variable expressivity**: The same mutation may lead to distinct phenotypes—IPF in one patient, bone marrow failure in another—depending on tissue turnover rates and stem‑cell demands.
4. **Environmental and lifestyle modifiers**: Smoking, alcohol, chronic infections, and metabolic stress can accelerate telomere attrition. For example, chronic hypoxia or inflammatory cytokines in the lung may precipitate fibrosis in a genetically susceptible host. Similarly, chronic liver insult (viral hepatitis, fatty liver disease) can unmask underlying hepatocellular senescence in a PARN carrier.

In the present case, the patient’s **early fibrosis** in both liver and lung, despite a single heterozygous PARN variant, likely reflects a combination of a highly deleterious allele (in silico damaging, highly conserved), a background of subclinical B12 deficiency (stress on hematopoiesis), and possible environmental exposures (unknown but plausible, given the patient’s history of weight loss and anorexia). The presence of **hypersegmented neutrophils**, **ineffective erythropoiesis**, and **unusual nail and hair findings** further support a telomeric defect rather than a purely acquired process.

---

In summary, the integration of germline exome sequencing with careful variant interpretation revealed a monoallelic, damaging *PARN* variant. Functional understanding of *PARN*’s role in TERC maturation clarifies how a haploinsufficient enzyme can propagate telomere shortening across multiple organ systems. Recognizing the broad phenotypic spectrum of *PARN* mutations, alongside the concepts of incomplete penetrance and environmental modulation, enables a nuanced appreciation of why a single germline lesion can produce such a complex clinical picture.

## 4. Management Strategy and Prognostic Considerations

The management of a telomere biology disorder (TBD) with a monoallelic **PARN** variant requires a multidisciplinary approach that addresses the acute manifestations of cytopenia and organ fibrosis while anticipating the progressive nature of telomere dysfunction. The therapeutic plan is largely supportive, guided by the severity of each organ system’s involvement and the evolving literature on disease‐modifying interventions.

### Correction of B12 deficiency

The patient’s severe vitamin B12 deficiency accounted for a significant portion of the haematological derangements. A standardized parenteral cyanocobalamin protocol was instituted: 1 mg IV immediately, followed by daily 1 mg injections for five consecutive days. Maintenance involved weekly injections for four weeks, then monthly thereafter. Response was monitored by serial complete blood counts (CBCs) and calculation of the reticulocyte index (RI). An RI > 2.5 in the setting of a normal or elevated absolute reticulocyte count is considered a favorable response, indicating marrow recovery. In this patient, RI improved from 0.5 to 1.8 within two weeks, with complete resolution of absolute reticulocytopenia by the end of the first month. Vitamin B12 status was confirmed by serum holotranscobalamin and methylmalonic acid measurements, which normalized after therapy.

### Supportive hematology

The patient’s pancytopenia warranted a transfusion strategy that balances patient comfort with the risk of iron overload. Red blood cell transfusions were reserved for hemoglobin < 7 g/dL or symptomatic anemia (fatigue, dyspnea). Platelet transfusions were considered when counts fell below 10 × 10⁹/L with bleeding or procedural indications. White blood cell support was not routinely required given the lack of neutropenia and absence of significant infection.

Folic acid 1 mg daily was continued indefinitely to support erythropoiesis. Iron homeostasis was monitored by serum ferritin and transferrin saturation; ferritin values > 300 ng/mL prompted chelation therapy with deferasirox to prevent hemosiderosis, a well‑documented complication in patients receiving frequent transfusions. Bone marrow evaluation was repeated at six months to rule out evolving aplastic anemia, given the predisposition in TBDs. No clonal cytogenetic abnormalities were detected on repeat karyotyping.

### Bone health

The dual‑energy X‑ray absorptiometry (DEXA) scan demonstrated reduced T‑scores (–2.8 in the lumbar spine and –2.4 in the femoral neck), confirming osteoporosis. The patient received calcium 1 000 mg elemental daily and ergocalciferol 60 000 IU weekly, with serum 25‑hydroxyvitamin D titrated to > 30 ng/mL. Secondary hyperparathyroidism was managed by calcitriol 0.25 µg twice daily and maintaining serum calcium within the upper normal range. Bisphosphonate therapy (e.g., oral alendronate 70 mg monthly) was discussed; however, given the young age, potential adverse effects on bone remodeling, and lack of fractures at presentation, this was deferred pending longitudinal fracture surveillance. The patient was encouraged to engage in weight‑bearing, low‑impact exercise (walking, tai chi) and to avoid smoking and excessive alcohol, both of which accelerate bone loss.

### Cardiopulmonary monitoring

High‑resolution computed tomography (HRCT) of the chest revealed early subpleural traction bronchiectasis and ground‑glass opacities consistent with early idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF). Pulmonary function tests (PFTs) documented a mild restrictive defect (FEV1 80 % pred, TLC 70 % pred, DLCO 60 % pred). Baseline spirometry, diffusing capacity, and six‑minute walk test (6MWT) were recorded, with repeat studies scheduled every six months to detect trajectory. The patient was started on a low‑dose prednisone taper (initially 0.5 mg/kg for two weeks) to reduce inflammatory activity, with close monitoring of blood glucose and bone density. In light of progressive fibrosis over the subsequent year, antifibrotic therapy with pirfenidone (600 mg BID) was initiated; nintedanib was considered as a second‑line agent if pirfenidone caused intolerable gastrointestinal side effects. The response to antifibrotics is monitored by PFTs (decline in FVC < 10 % over 12 months) and HRCT changes. Ultimately, the decision to proceed with lung transplantation is reserved for decompensated disease (FVC < 50 % pred, rising supplemental oxygen needs) and will involve a transplant center’s multidisciplinary review.

### Hepatic surveillance

Transient elastography (FibroScan) quantified significant hepatic fibrosis (liver stiffness 18.1 kPa) and steatosis (CAP 295 dB/m). Serial elastography every six months, coupled with monthly liver function panels (ALT, AST, ALP, GGT, bilirubin, INR), allows early detection of progression to cirrhosis. The patient’s portal hypertension was clinically silent; however, liver ultrasound with Doppler and platelet counts were evaluated monthly to screen for varices. Liver biopsy was deferred given the safety concerns and adequate imaging data. If liver decompensation ensues (ascites, variceal bleeding, hepatic encephalopathy), evaluation for orthotopic liver transplantation is warranted, with careful consideration of the patient’s pulmonary disease to assess transplant candidacy.

### Future therapeutic avenues

Telomere elongation strategies such as androgen therapy (danazol or nandrolone) have shown promise in preclinical models and small clinical cohorts, improving blood counts and potentially prolonging telomere length. In this patient, the potential benefits must be weighed against androgenic adverse effects (hirsutism, virilization, erythrocytosis). Close monitoring of hemoglobin, estradiol, and androgen levels is required. Clinical trials of telomerase activators are ongoing and may offer future disease‑modifying options.

For patients who develop frank aplastic anemia or progressive marrow failure unresponsive to supportive care, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) remains the only curative approach. Conditioning regimens tailored to telomere disease (reduced‑intensity, alkylator‑free protocols) are recommended to diminish graft‑versus‑host disease and organ toxicity. Early referral to a transplant center specializing in inherited bone marrow failure is prudent, especially given the potential for early marrow exhaustion in TBD carriers.

### Genetic counseling

The discovery of a monoallelic **PARN** variant classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS) necessitates cautious communication. Counselors explain that while the variant is located in a functional domain and predicted pathogenic, definitive causality has not been established. Consequently, family members may or may not be at risk. Cascade testing is recommended for first‑degree relatives with a similar clinical phenotype (cytopenia, fibrosis, premature aging signs). Prenatal genetic testing or preimplantation genetic diagnosis (PGD) can be offered to at‑risk couples if the variant’s pathogenicity is later resolved. Ethically, the patient’s autonomy and informed decision‑making are paramount; the VUS status should not delay management of her current organ complications.

In summary, the management strategy for this patient integrates targeted correction of metabolic deficiencies, vigilant haematologic support, organ‑specific surveillance, and the judicious use of emerging disease‑modifying therapies. Prognostically, the key determinants are the extent of telomere attrition (measured by peripheral leukocyte telomere length), the degree of organ fibrosis, and the patient’s response to supportive interventions. Regular multidisciplinary follow‑up remains the cornerstone of care in this evolving clinical landscape.
