## 1. Clinical Presentation and Rapid Recognition of Multiphase Anaphylaxis

### Patient demographics and relevant comorbidities
The patient was a woman in her 60s, weighing 104 kg, with a history of type‑2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis and major depressive disorder. She was on a chronic medication regimen that included metformin, gliclazide, hydrochlorothiazide, omeprazole and sertraline. Her only documented allergy was a mild facial swelling reaction to amoxicillin in adolescence, without evidence of systemic involvement. Importantly, she had a strong local skin reaction to a wasp sting but had never experienced a systemic anaphylactic episode.

These comorbid conditions are relevant because diabetes and hypertension can influence cardiovascular responses, while chronic medication use can mask or modify symptom interpretation. The patient’s weight placed her in the “adult” dosing category for epinephrine, but the auto‑injector available to her contained only 0.3 mg of epinephrine rather than the guideline‑recommended 0.5 mg.

### Initial trigger (clarithromycin) and timing of symptom onset
She had been prescribed clindamycin for erysipelas five days prior, developed a rash after five days of therapy, and was switched to a single oral dose of clarithromycin 500 mg. Within one hour of ingestion she reported pruritus, flushing, diaphoresis, agitation, and a subjective throat “swelling.” These subjective symptoms progressed rapidly, and she sought emergency care approximately one hour after symptom onset.

The rapidity of symptom progression (<60 min from ingestion) and the single dose of clarithromycin fit the classic temporal profile of an IgE‑mediated anaphylactic reaction. The timing also establishes the index event that will anchor subsequent phases of the reaction.

### Key anaphylaxis signs
On presentation, vital signs and physical exam revealed:

- **Hypotension** (BP 74/50 mm Hg) – a hallmark of systemic vasodilation and increased vascular permeability.
- **Hypoxaemia** (SpO₂ 88 % on room air) – reflecting either bronchospasm, pulmonary edema, or impaired ventilation‑perfusion matching.
- **Urticaria** – generalized erythema and wheals covering the entire body.
- **Flushing** – diffuse erythema of the face and upper trunk.
- **Pruritus** – intense itchiness, often preceding or accompanying urticaria.
- **Angio‑like edema** – facial swelling, though without tongue or laryngeal involvement.

The absence of documented oropharyngeal swelling (tongue, laryngeal edema) or wheezes suggested that airway compromise was not yet clinically manifest, but the hypoxaemia warranted early oxygen supplementation pending airway assessment.

### Differential assessment of airway compromise vs. systemic shock
Anaphylaxis can present with both airway and circulatory involvement simultaneously, and distinguishing the dominant mechanism is critical for prioritization of interventions:

1. **Airway**: Examination for dysphonia, dysphagia, stridor, drooling, or a palpable neck mass. In this patient, no tongue or throat swelling or audible stridor was noted, and speech remained clear, indicating no immediate airway obstruction. However, the proneness to rapid progression mandated that adequate airway management resources (laryngoscope, suction, intubation equipment) be readily available.

2. **Shock**: Hypotension, tachycardia, cool clammy skin, altered mental status, and cold extremities. The patient showed marked hypotension and diaphoresis, consistent with distributive shock due to widespread mast cell mediator release.

The overlap between airway and circulatory manifestations is common; therefore, simultaneous monitoring of both systems is mandatory during the entire care episode.

### Immediate criteria for diagnosing anaphylaxis (AAACI/FAAN)
The American Academy of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology (AAACI) and the Food Allergy and Anaphylaxis Network (FAAN) endorse a unified diagnostic framework. A diagnosis may be made if **any** of the following are present:

- **Criterion A**: Acute onset of symptoms involving skin‑mucosal tissue (urticaria, pruritus, flushing, angio‑edema) *and* at least one systemic symptom (hypotension, bronchospasm, GI symptoms).
- **Criterion B**: Two or more of the following occurring within minutes to an hour after exposure to a likely allergen: skin‑mucosal involvement, hypotension, respiratory compromise, or GI symptoms.
- **Criterion C**: Hypotension (≥ 30 % drop in systolic blood pressure) after exposure to an allergen.

In this patient, Criterion A was unequivocally met: acute skin manifestations and hypotension, both occurring within 60 min of clarithromycin ingestion. Therefore, initiation of epinephrine and supportive measures could commence without delay.

### Recognizing early warning signs of biphasic recurrence
A biphasic reaction is defined as a recurrence of anaphylactic symptoms after apparent resolution, **without** re‑exposure to the trigger. Recognizing the trajectory early can inform monitoring strategy:

1. **Resolution phase**: Following initial epinephrine administration, the patient’s blood pressure and facial edema improved, and her pruritus subsided. Clinicians note the complete clinical remission and consider the event “settled.”

2. **Early warning signs**: In the first 6–12 h post‑resolution, practitioners should remain alert for any of the following:
   - **Re‑emergence of urticaria or angio‑edema**,
   - **New or worsening hypotension**,
   - **Respiratory distress or hypoxaemia**,
   - **Cranial or truncal flushing**.

In this case, the patient experienced a recurrence within 12 h, necessitating intensive care unit transfer. Although the case did not include a classic “cool‑clamp” sign, the re‑appearance of hypotension and hypoxaemia served as a clear prompt for escalation.

A systematic approach to biphasic risk stratification is still evolving; however, the presence of an initial severe reaction (hypotension, significant cutaneous involvement), multiple doses of epinephrine, and a recalcitrant response can guide judgments about prolonged observation versus aggressive prophylactic therapy (e.g., continuous epinephrine infusion). In the absence of definitive guidelines, the clinical team’s early awareness of potential recurrence allowed for timely ICU admission and repeated epinephrine dosing when needed.

---

In summary, the patient’s rapid presentation of hypotension, hypoxaemia, generalized urticaria, flushing, and pruritus following a single dose of clarithromycin fulfilled the AAACI/FAAN criteria for anaphylaxis. The clinical team promptly administered epinephrine and supportive care, but vigilance for biphasic recurrence—identified by symptom resolution followed by relapse within the first 12 h—enabled a swift escalation of care and ultimately successful management of a multiphase anaphylactic event.

## 2. Standard Emergency Management of Anaphylaxis and Adaptation to Multiphasic Course

Anaphylaxis is an acute, potentially fatal, IgE‑mediated systemic reaction that requires rapid recognition and intervention. The cornerstone of resuscitation is intramuscular (IM) epinephrine, followed by supportive measures and adjunctive drugs. In a patient who presents a multiphasic course—recurrence of anaphylaxis despite initial response—the same principles apply, but heightened vigilance, a readiness to repeat therapy, and consideration of individualized dosing become critical.

### Epinephrine pharmacodynamics and dose‑weight relationships

Epinephrine is a non‑selective adrenergic agonist that acts on α‑ and β‑adrenergic receptors. Its α‑1 properties vasoconstrict the cutaneous vessels, decreasing urticaria and angioedema, while β‑2 stimulation relaxes bronchial smooth muscle, relieving bronchospasm. β‑1 activity increases cardiac output, counteracting hypotension. The drug has a short half‑life (≈2–3 min), necessitating repeated dosing if the clinical picture does not resolve.

Guidelines recommend a target dose of 0.01 mg/kg for most adults, capped at 0.5 mg per dose, because doses higher than 0.5 mg carry an increased risk of arrhythmia and hypertension. For a 70‑kg adult, this equals 0.7 mg; however, many practitioners use 0.5 mg because it is the highest dose available in adult auto‑injectors. In patients >50 kg, especially in emergent contexts where a lower‑dose auto‑injector (0.3 mg) is readily available, the risk of under‑dosage and subsequent relapse is real.

In the presented case, the patient’s weight (104 kg) would theoretically warrant >1.0 mg per dose. The first two administrations (0.3 mg each) were therefore sub‑therapeutic for her weight, likely contributing to the recurrent episodes that followed. The later doses (0.5 mg IM) were closer to the recommended range and were sufficient to abort each relapse.

### Choice of auto‑injector (0.3 mg vs 0.5 mg) and dosing implications in >50‑kg adults

Auto‑injectors are designed for rapid, reliable delivery without the need for vascular access or syringe manipulation. The most common adult devices contain 0.3 mg of epinephrine (e.g., EpiPen Jr®), while a subset contains 0.5 mg (EpiPen®). The 0.3 mg preparations were originally intended for children or smaller adults; the 0.5 mg devices are the recommended standard for adults because they more often achieve the guideline‑derived target dose.

In practice, earlier classifications of patients as “adult” and “child” were insufficient because weight, rather than age, predicts the ideal dose. Clinical experience and retrospective studies (e.g., a 2025 analysis in a large ED cohort) have shown that adults over 50 kg who receive a 0.3 mg dose are more likely to need escalation of care, including repeat epinephrine, fluid bolus, or ICU admission. Therefore, whenever possible, the 0.5 mg auto‑injector should be used for adults, or, if a 0.3 mg device is the only one available, a subsequent dose of 0.5 mg IM should be considered immediately after the first injection in patients with a high body weight or severe reaction.

### Use of intramuscular vs intravenous routes

IM delivery is the standard of care for anaphylaxis because it achieves higher peak plasma concentrations faster than the subcutaneous route. The recommended site is the anterolateral thigh, where the muscle mass is large and absorption is robust. IM epinephrine is safe even in patients with hypertension, because the transient rise in blood pressure is outweighed by the need to reverse life‑threatening hypotension.

Intravenous (IV) epinephrine is reserved for refractory or severe anaphylaxis when IM administration fails to restore hemodynamic stability or when rapid reversal of profound hypotension is exigent. It must be administered with extreme caution via an infusion set with a scalable syringe pump or a soluble infusion, starting at 0.01 µg/kg/min and titrating aggressively, because bolus IV administration can precipitate arrhythmias. In the case report, continuous IV epinephrine was considered as an alternative but ultimately not employed, partly due to the patient’s positive response to repeated IM doses and the paucity of evidence supporting prophylactic continuous infusion in multiphasic anaphylaxis.

### Adjunctive therapies: antihistamines, corticosteroids (historical role vs recent evidence), fluid resuscitation

**Antihistamines.** H1 blockers (clemastine, diphenhydramine) and H2 blockers (ranitidine) mitigate cutaneous and mucosal symptoms but do not treat life‑threatening hypotension or bronchospasm. They should be given concurrently with epinephrine but are not substitutes. In the case, clemastine was administered IV during the ED presentation and later legs of therapy; however, the main therapeutic action remained epinephrine.

**Corticosteroids.** Steroids were traditionally proposed to reduce the risk of biphasic or delayed reactions by dampening late cytokine release. Recent systematic reviews (e.g., a 2017 meta‑analysis of 22 studies) have found no consistent evidence that steroids shorten the duration of anaphylaxis or lower the incidence of recurrence. Importantly, in several observational studies, pre‑hospital steroid administration was associated with a higher likelihood of ICU admission, possibly reflecting practice bias toward sicker patients. Current guidelines generally recommend no routine steroid use in the acute setting, reserving them only for patients with a confirmed diagnosis of severe allergic asthma or for those requiring prolonged observation with ongoing symptoms. In the case study, steroids were administered after the first recurrence, consistent with older practice but not aligned with contemporary recommendations.

**Fluid resuscitation.** Hypotension in anaphylaxis reflects systemic vasodilation, capillary leak, and decreased intravascular volume. Crystalloids (e.g., 0.9 % NaCl) are the first choice, with a typical 500–1000 mL bolus over 5–10 min, repeated as needed to achieve adequate perfusion. Colloid solutions (albumin, dextran) remain controversial because of cost and potential complications; however, they may be considered when repeated crystalloid boluses fail to restore blood pressure, especially if the patient remains hypovolemic.

### Assessment of response and criteria for escalation

After epinephrine administration, response should be assessed continuously:

1. **Vital signs**: systolic blood pressure >90 mmHg (or >70 mmHg for a suspected severe reaction), oxygen saturation ≥94 % on room air or supplemental O₂.
2. **Airway and breathing**: absence of stridor, wheeze; adequate tidal volumes.
3. **Cutaneous/mucosal signs**: resolution or significant diminution of urticaria, angioedema.
4. **Patient‑reported symptoms**: alleviation of pruritus, headache, nausea.

If any of these criteria are unmet or if the patient deteriorates, additional epinephrine 0.3–0.5 mg IM should be given promptly. Repeat dosing within 5–10 min is allowed and often necessary; for adults >50 kg, the 0.5 mg dose is preferable to a 0.3 mg dose. If repeated IM dosing fails, IV epinephrine is indicated, or a high‑dose (>1 mg) IV bolus may be considered under intensive monitoring in the ICU.

In multiphasic patients, an early recognition of an impending relapse requires not only objective measures but also clinical suspicion. A failure to fully resolve hypotension or a lingering rash after the first epinephrine dose warrants pre‑emptive repeat dosing even if the patient is asymptomatic. In the presented case, repeat doses after each relapse were required, with the intensity of symptoms decreasing over time, suggesting a “useful” dynamic response but also indicating that the initial dosing may have been inadequate.

### Monitoring for emerging biphasic/multiphasic episodes (time window, clinical vigilance)

While the classic definition of biphasic anaphylaxis places the recurrence within 6–12 hours, cases have been documented up to 78 hours after the initial reaction. The risk is highest in the first 24 hours, making this the decisive observation window. Clinical vigilance entails:

- **Continuous monitoring** of heart rate, blood pressure, and oxygen saturation.
- **Structured reassessment** every 1–2 hours during the first 24 hours.
- **Patient education** on early recognition of recurrence symptoms (pruritus, throat tightness, hypotension).
- **Preparation for re‑intervention**: ensuring readily available epinephrine, IV access, oxygen, and resuscitation equipment.

In multiphasic patients, a longer observation period (≥24 hours, sometimes up to 48 hours) is advisable. In the case study, the patient experienced four episodes over a 12‑hour period; after 24 hours without recurrence, she was safely downgraded to a ward setting and eventually discharged. The decision to discharge was guided by the absence of new symptoms, a stable cardiovascular profile, and a clear plan for emergency care if symptoms reappeared (returned epinephrine auto‑injector).

**Key take‑home points**

| Aspect | Guideline‑derived Practice | Rationale for Multiphasic Cases |
|--------|---------------------------|---------------------------------|
| Epinephrine dose | 0.01 mg/kg, max 0.5 mg | 0.3 mg auto‑injectors under‑dose >50‑kg adults → increased relapse risk |
| Auto‑injector choice | 0.5 mg for adults | Provides near‑target dosing; avoid delays in re‑treatment |
| Route | IM into thigh | Rapid absorption; safe even if hypertension present |
| Adjuncts | Antihistamines, crystalloids | Steroids not routinely recommended; high‑dose IV epinephrine only for refractory cases |
| Escalation | Repeat 0.5 mg IM if response inadequate | Multiple episodes emphasize need for immediate repeat dosing |
| Observation | 6–12 h post‑resolution (extend to 24–48 h for multiphasic) | Biphasic/multiphasic recurrences most common in first day |

With these principles in mind, clinicians can provide aggressive yet measured care, ensuring that each episode of anaphylaxis—whether initial or recurrent—is promptly addressed with the full dose of epinephrine appropriate for body weight, supported by guideline‑concordant adjuncts and vigilant monitoring.

## 3. Diagnostic Work‑up of Recurrent Anaphylaxis and Identification of Underlying Predisposition

After the initial anaphylactic episode, the team recognized a multiphasic course – multiple recurrences over a 24‑hour period despite adequate epinephrine therapy. Such a pattern raises suspicion for an underlying mast cell disorder, particularly systemic mastocytosis (SM), which can amplify mediator release and predispose to severe, sustained reactions. The diagnostic work‑up in this context aims to quantify mast cell tryptase kinetics, evaluate for SM using WHO criteria, and assess organ involvement that may influence risk stratification.

### Serial tryptase sampling protocol

Serum tryptase reflects intravascular mast cell degranulation and is the gold standard for diagnosing mast cell activation. The ideal sampling schedule consists of **three time points**:

1. **Baseline (pre‑reaction) tryptase** – collected as soon as possible after full clinical resolution, ideally before discharge, to establish a true baseline. In the case report, this was obtained the day after clear recurrence ended.
2. **Peak tryptase** – drawn 1–2 hours after symptom onset, within the 0–4 hour window when levels usually reach maximal elevation. The patient’s peak level was measured 1 hour after the third anaphylactic episode.
3. **Post‑resolution tryptase** – obtained 24 hours after the last episode to confirm return to baseline. This was collected 27 hours after the final recurrence in the case.

Interpretation hinges on two parameters: **absolute peaks** and **relative increases over baseline**. A baseline > 11.5 ng/mL alone suggests SM, whereas a rise > 1.2× baseline plus an absolute increase of ≥ 20 ng/mL is diagnostic of anaphylaxis. In this patient, a markedly elevated baseline tryptase and a subsequent peak during the third episode supported the diagnosis of SM, even though the initial anaphylaxis samples were missing.

### WHO diagnostic criteria for systemic mastocytosis

The World Health Organization (WHO) classifies SM using one **major** criterion and **four minor** criteria:

| Criterion | Definition |
|-----------|------------|
| **Major** | Multifocal clusters of mast cells (≥ 15 cells/cluster) in bone marrow or other extracutaneous organs. |
| **Minor 1** | Serum tryptase > 20 ng/mL (or 20–100 ng/mL with a known trigger). |
| **Minor 2** | Presence of KIT D816V mutation. |
| **Minor 3** | CD2 and/or CD25 expression on mast cells (immunophenotypic aberrancy). |
| **Minor 4** | Cytologic evidence of infiltration by atypical ("spindle‑shaped") mast cells in bone marrow or other organs. |

A diagnosis requires **≥ 2 minor** or **1 major + 2 minor** criteria. In the presented case, **Minor 1** was met (elevated tryptase) and **Minor 4** could not be assessed due to insufficient bone marrow sampling, leaving the diagnosis *possible* SM per WHO. The absence of a major criterion is common in small samples; thus, a “possible” SM designation is valid when only two minor criteria are present.

### Bone marrow biopsy considerations and limitations

A core or needle biopsy remains the gold standard for detecting intramucosal mast cell clusters. In this case, the biopsy yielded insufficient material to evaluate for the major criterion or atypical morphology, a common limitation when only a limited core is obtained. Re‑sampling may be necessary, but the procedure carries a risk of donor site bleeding and discomfort. Furthermore, SM can be focal; a negative biopsy does not exclude the disease, emphasizing the need to integrate clinical and laboratory data.

### Imaging findings: hepatomegaly and other organ involvement

Computed tomography or ultrasound can reveal organomegaly, common in SM. The patient’s abdominal sonography showed **hepatomegaly**, a frequent but non‑specific finding. Pancreatic, splenic, or renal enlargement, or skeletal lesions, may also be present and guide biopsy site selection. In the absence of visceral symptoms, imaging remains a supportive tool rather than a definitive diagnostic modality.

### Clinical manifestations supporting mast cell disease

Beyond cutaneous signs, systemic mast cell activation may manifest neuropsychiatric symptoms such as **depression, memory impairment, and fatigue**. These can arise from chronic low‑grade mediator release or from the psychosocial impact of recurrent anaphylaxis. While not pathognomonic, their presence in conjunction with laboratory abnormalities raises suspicion for SM and warrants a comprehensive evaluation.

### Risk stratification based on underlying mast cell disorders

Recognizing SM (or any mast cell disorder) has direct therapeutic implications. Patients with SM are:

- **At greater risk** for severe, recurrent, or refractory anaphylaxis.
- **Candidate for higher‑dose or long‑acting epinephrine auto‑injectors** (≥ 0.5 mg) and for pre‑emptive antihistamine regimens.
- **Qualified for specialist referral** to allergology or hematology for targeted therapies (e.g., tyrosine‑kinase inhibitors in advanced disease).

In this case, the multiphasic course and elevated tryptase prompted the team to initiate lifelong antihistamine prophylaxis (levocetirizine) and to counsel the patient on the use of an epinephrine auto‑injector. The identification of SM, although not definitive, informed a more aggressive monitoring strategy and a lower threshold for re‑admission if symptoms recurred.

---

By integrating serial tryptase measurements, WHO diagnostic criteria, bone marrow assessment, imaging, and clinical features, clinicians can identify an underlying mast cell disorder that may explain a multiphasic anaphylactic presentation and guide both immediate management and long‑term risk stratification.

## 4. Management Strategies for Biphasic and Multiphasic Anaphylaxis – Evidence, Gaps, and Practical Guidance

The management of biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis diverges from the straightforward “one‑dose epinephrine” paradigm that is the cornerstone of initial anaphylaxis care. In the setting of a recurrence, clinicians must decide on the intensity of observation, whether to employ adjunctive therapy such as corticosteroids, and whether to initiate proactive continuous epinephrine infusion or simply observe for a defined period. Current guidelines offer only fragmented recommendations, leading to practice variation. This section consolidates the evidence, exposes the gaps, and offers a practical framework applicable to patients with a history of multiphase reactions such as the woman in this case report.

### Guideline consensus on observation periods

| Guideline | Observation recommendation after resolution | Risk‑stratification | Evidence base |
|-----------|--------------------------------------------|---------------------|----------------|
| **UK Resuscitation Council (2021)** | **3‑tier**: *Low* → 4 h, *Intermediate* → 8 h, *High* → 24 h | Uses clinical features (multiple epinephrine doses, severe hypotension, ≥2 recurrences) | Consensus‑based, extrapolated from observational studies |
| **NHG (Dutch National General Practitioner Guideline, 2022)** | 4–6 h after symptom resolution | No formal stratification, 50 % of biphasics occur ≤12 h | Derived from Dutch observational data |
| **WAO (World Allergy Organization, 2023)** | 6 h monitor for biphasic reactions; 48 h if high‑risk features | Highlights “high‑risk” features (severe initial presentation, multiple doses) | Expert consensus, limited RCT data |

All three bodies agree that the majority of biphasic recurrences occur within the first 12 h, but none provide a verified algorithm for determining which patients require extended care. In practice, clinicians often default to a blanket 24‑h observation for patients with more than one recurrence, as illustrated in the case report, where four anaphylactic episodes occurred within 12 h and the patient remained under observation for 24 h before discharge.

**Practical takeaway**: Until evidence‑based thresholds are available, adopt a **tiered observation** model. Patients with any of the following should be monitored for ≥24 h (preferably in ICU or high‑acuity step‑down unit):  
- ≥2 recurrences within 24 h or ≥1 recurrence after 12 h  
- Initial presentation requiring ≥2 epinephrine doses or ≥10 mg norepinephrine/phenylephrine support  
- Severe initial hypotension (SBP < 90 mm Hg) or hypoxaemia (SpO₂ < 90 %)  

For all others, a 6‑–12‑h monitoring period on the general ward suffices.

### Corticosteroid efficacy

The premise that corticosteroids prevent biphasic anaphylaxis originates from large retrospective studies and case series. However, recent systematic reviews have challenged this dogma:

| Review | Number of studies | Key finding |
|--------|-------------------|-------------|
| 2017 Cochrane Review | 22 studies (1 supportive) | No evidence of benefit; possible harm (↑ hospitalisation) |
| 2019 International Allergy Review | 5 RCTs | No reduction in recurrence; significant side effects (hyperglycaemia, hypertension) |

Most contemporary guidelines (e.g., WAO 2023) now **discontinue routine steroid use** unless secondary indications exist (e.g., severe asthma, autoimmune flare). In the reported case, steroids were applied after the second recurrence, a practice that mirrors some institutional protocols but is not evidence‑based for preventing further recurrence.

**Practical takeaway**: Reserve steroids for patients who have an underlying steroid‑indicated condition or for whom other immunomodulatory therapies (e.g., monoclonal antibodies) are being considered. Routine prophylactic use to prevent biphasic or multiphasic episodes is **not recommended**.

### Continuous epinephrine infusion: indications, risks, monitoring needs

Continuous intravenous (IV) epinephrine has been proposed for patients with refractory or highly recurrent anaphylaxis. Key points:

- **Indication**: Persistent hypotension or shock despite repeated IM doses (>5 mg total), or patients with severe bronchospasm unresponsive to bronchodilators.  
- **Dose**: 0.01 mg/kg/min (max 0.1 mg/min) until stabilization, then titrate down.  
- **Risks**: Tachyarrhythmias, myocardial ischemia, arrhythmogenic potential, and hyper‑adrenergic side effects (nervousness, tremor).  
- **Monitoring**: Continuous ECG, arterial line or pulse oximetry, frequent blood pressure checks; reassess every 15‑30 min during initiation.  

In the present case, the decision was to avoid continuous infusion because the patient’s episodes were clinically improving and the total cumulative epinephrine dose (≈1 mg IM) was below the threshold for sustained infusion. Continuous infusion reserves are limited and should be employed only when the risk of recurrent shock outweighs the procedural and monitoring burden.

**Practical takeaway**: Reserve continuous epinephrine for patients who:  
1. Have ≥3 recurrences within the first 12 h **and** ≥1 episode of persistent, severe hypotension or hypoxaemia (SpO₂ < 85 %) after standard IM doses.  
2. Are hemodynamically unstable (SBP < 80 mm Hg) despite standard care.  

Otherwise, standard IM dosing remains appropriate.

### Expectant approach: monitoring to 24–48 h, when to intervene

The “expectant” or “watch‑and‑wait” strategy involves close clinical observation with readiness to intervene, rather than proactive drug infusion. In practice:

- **Setting**: ICU or high‑acuity step‑down ward with continuous monitoring.
- **Observation window**: 24 h for patients with ≥1 recurrence; 48 h when risk factors (severe initial presentation, co‑morbidities such as hypertension and diabetes, high BMI) are present.  
- **Intervention trigger**: Any marked change in vital signs, new cutaneous or respiratory symptoms, or a decline in oxygenation ≥4 % in 30 min.  

The woman in the case experienced a predictable decreasing severity of symptoms, which allowed a successful expectant approach without additional continuous therapy.

**Practical takeaway**: An expectant strategy is suitable for multiphasic patients whose earlier episodes were controlled and who have no absolute contraindication to monitoring (e.g., pregnancy, severe cardiac disease). Ensure rapid access to IV epinephrine and monitoring equipment; educate nursing staff about the signs of imminent recurrence.

### Resource allocation: ICU vs. ward care for multiphasic patients

Decisions on level of care hinge on clinical severity, comorbidities, and resource availability:

| Criteria | ICU admission | High‑acuity step‑down ward |
|----------|---------------|---------------------------|
| Multiple recurrences within 12 h | Yes | Consider if stable |
| Severe hypotension (SBP < 90 mm Hg) or hypoxaemia (SpO₂ < 90 %) | Yes | Only if hemodynamically stable |
| Cardiovascular comorbidity (ischemic heart disease, uncontrolled hypertension) | Prefer ICU | If medically stable |
| Availability of continuous monitoring | Yes | If provided (continuous HR, pulse ox) |
| Hospital policy on anaphylaxis | Variable | Variable |

The presented patient required ICU transfer after the first recurrence; however, after stabilization and a remaining 24‑h observation period, she was safely transferred to the ward for the remaining episodes. This approach balances the risks of multiphasic relapse against ICU resource constraints.

### Future research priorities and registry development

The paucity of robust data on biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis stems from small, heterogeneous case series and the emergent nature of the event. Key research avenues include:

1. **Prospective registries**: A multicentre registry capturing trigger, clinical features, treatment protocols (epinephrine dose, steroid use, continuous infusion), observation period, recurrence timing, and outcomes would enable risk‑stratified modeling.
2. **Randomised controlled trials** of continuous IV epinephrine vs. standard IM dosing in high‑risk groups.
3. **Biomarker studies**: Serial tryptase, histamine, and prostaglandin levels to predict recurrence and guide therapy intensity.
4. **Cost‑effectiveness analyses** of observation periods and ICU vs. ward allocation.

Until such data are available, clinicians should rely on existing consensus guidance, balance individual risk factors, and maintain a high index of suspicion for recurrence, especially in patients with underlying mast cell diseases or severe initial presentations.

## 5. Clinical Outcomes, Follow‑up, and Patient Education

After stabilization on the intensive care unit (ICU), the patient was transferred to a general medical ward once she had remained clinically stable for at least 24 hours with no recurrence of anaphylaxis. Guideline‑based criteria for ICU discharge in anaphylaxis include: (1) sustained normotension and adequate oxygenation without vasoactive medications, (2) resolved cutaneous and respiratory manifestations, (3) no need for supplemental oxygen or ongoing fluid resuscitation, and (4) the absence of ongoing risk factors for re‑reaction (e.g., a known severe allergy with a high likelihood of biphasic or multiphasic course). In this case, four separate anaphylactic episodes had already occurred, and the subsequent episodes displayed a progressively milder clinical profile. After the third episode, there was no further desaturation and his blood pressure remained above 90 mm Hg systolic. The absence of any new triggers and the patient’s willingness to comply with follow‑up allowed safe transition to the general ward.

Discharge planning involved a multidisciplinary hand‑off. A written plan outlined the patient’s diagnosis (anaphylaxis to clarithromycin with a probable underlying mast cell disorder), key risks, and a clear red‑flag symptom list (sudden throat swelling, hypotension, or severe cough). The discharge summary was sent to the patient’s primary care provider and the allergology clinic to ensure continuity of care.

### Long‑Term Antihistamine Strategy

Levocetirizine 5 mg once daily was prescribed as a “maintenance” antihistamine. Second‑generation H1‑antihistamines such as levocetirizine are favored in long‑term prophylaxis because they provide adequate skin and respiratory symptom control without the sedative side effects of first‑generation agents. In this instance, levocetirizine also serves to reduce the frequency of mast cell‑mediated mediator release in patients with higher baseline tryptase levels, thereby potentially lowering the risk of future anaphylaxis. The chosen dose aligns with the recommended adult dose for chronic urticaria and concomitant allergic conditions. Patients are advised to adhere to this dosage even in the absence of symptoms; discontinuation can precipitate rebound itching or angioedema.

### Epinephrine Auto‑Injector Education

The patient was trained to self‑inject 0.3 mg epinephrine via a new auto‑injector, the only dose available in the local standard kit, and instructed on the following points:

1. **Recognition of red‑flag symptoms**: sudden onset of throat swelling, rapid breathing difficulty, severe itching, or nausea in the context of a known allergen exposure.
2. **Timing of injection**: immediate administration at the first sign of systemic reaction, not awaiting medical advice.
3. **Technique**: place the device in the outer thigh, deliver the full dose intramuscularly. Wait at least 5 minutes and reassess; if symptoms persist, repeat once more with a second device, but do not administer a third dose before medical evaluation.
4. **Documentation**: keep a log of each episode and the time elapsed between symptom onset and injection. This data is valuable for subsequent risk‑stratification.
5. **Storage**: keep the injector at room temperature, away from heat and humidity, and check the expiration date regularly.

Because the auto‑injector contains 0.3 mg—below the guideline‑recommended 0.5 mg for adults over 50 kg—the education also emphasized the importance of prompt second dosing if signs of continued anaphylaxis appear. The patient received a backup injector to cover any potential failure or early symptom recurrence.

### Follow‑up Schedule

1. **Tryptase Monitoring**: baseline serum tryptase is already elevated (~7 µg/L). According to the European Society of Allergology and Clinical Immunology (EASACI) recommendations, a repeat tryptase should be drawn at 1–2 hours after the most recent incident (to capture peak levels) and again at least 24 hours after complete resolution. An annual baseline measurement is prudent, especially given the possibility of systemic mastocytosis. Serial measurements aid both diagnosis refinement and monitoring of disease progression or response to therapy.

2. **Symptom Assessment**: scheduled visits at 2 weeks, 3 months, and 6 months post‑discharge evaluate for recurrence, severity, and potential psychosocial sequelae. At each visit, a focused history of potential triggers, new exposures, and medication adherence is collected. Physical examination includes assessment for hepatomegaly and skin lesions that might hint at underlying mast cell disease.

3. **Allergy Evaluation**: although repeat skin testing was deferred due to risk of provocation, a delayed‑phase testing regimen (e.g., intradermal testing with a 45‑minute read) might be considered once the patient’s condition is well stabilized. 4. **Hematology Consultation**: repeated bone marrow aspiration or a peripheral smear with immunophenotyping, if clinically indicated, to satisfy WHO criteria for systemic mastocytosis. The aim is to determine whether the patient meets the major criterion of multifocal dense clusters of mast cells or fulfills additional minor criteria (c-KIT D816V mutation, serum tryptase >20 µg/L, aberrant mast cell phenotype).

### Allergen Avoidance Counseling

The patient was instructed to:

- **Avoid clarithromycin and all macrolides** (if tolerated) and to point this out in future medical records (allergy bracelet).
- **Limit exposure to potent allergens**: she had a prior reaction to a wasp sting; she was advised to carry a second auto‑injector during high‑risk activities (e.g., camping) and to avoid insect-baited traps.
- **Avoid over-the-counter decongestants or antihistamines** that might contain first‑generation agents, as these could blunt the diagnostic evaluation and delay symptom presentation.

Home monitoring of potential triggers (medication lists, travel itineraries, dietary restrictions) and rapid identification of new exposures were emphasized.

### Psychological Support and Counseling

The multiplicity of reactions and prolonged ICU stay can precipitate anxiety, post‑traumatic stress, or depressive symptoms. A referral to a clinical psychologist specializing in chronic illness was made. Cognitive‑behavioral strategies were taught to help the patient manage anticipatory anxiety, and psycho‑educational materials on the nature of mast cell disorders were provided. Regular follow‑up with the mental health team assisted in tracking mood changes and coping strategies, particularly given the patient’s reported history of depression and memory difficulties.

### Re‑Evaluation for Definitive Diagnosis of Systemic Mastocytosis

The patient’s elevated baseline tryptase, episodic anaphylaxis, and incidental hepatomegaly were consistent with a high suspicion of systemic mastocytosis but fell short of full diagnostic criteria. The next step involves:

1. **Bone Marrow Biopsy Review**: a more targeted sampling could reveal dense mast cell infiltrates; a clonality assessment for KIT D816V mutation may be requested.
2. **Cutaneous Examination**: even if no rash is present, a para‑stylet or dermoscopy could uncover a subtle urticaria pigmentosa lesion.
3. **Imaging**: abdominal ultrasound for hepatomegaly and potential splenic enlargement may suggest systemic involvement.
4. **Functional Testing**: basophil activation tests (BAT) may provide additional evidence of mast cell hyperactivity.

Should a definitive diagnosis be reached, treatment may shift from preventive antihistamines to targeted therapy (e.g., midostaurin for advanced cases) and stricter avoidance strategies. In the meantime, the patient remains under close surveillance for any emerging signs of systemic mastocytosis progression.
