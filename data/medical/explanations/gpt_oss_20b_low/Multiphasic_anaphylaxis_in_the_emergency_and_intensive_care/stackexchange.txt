Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Clinical Pearls] What are the key clinical features that differentiate anaphylaxis from other acute allergic reactions, and why were the airway, breathing, circulation, and skin findings particularly important in this case?

**Key clinical features that set anaphylaxis apart from other acute allergic reactions**

| Domain | Anaphylaxis | Other acute allergic reactions |
|--------|--------------|--------------------------------|
| **Onset & tempo** | Sudden, rapid onset (seconds–minutes) with progression over minutes to a few hours | Often slower, may be delayed or localized |
| **Systemic involvement** | Requires at least two organ systems: airway/breathing, circulation **or** GI, plus cutaneous or mucosal signs | Usually confined to one system (e.g., isolated urticaria, isolated bronchospasm) |
| **Airway / breathing** | Angioedema of lips, tongue, oropharynx; wheeze; hypoxemia; tachypnea | Can have wheeze or bronchospasm in asthma, but not usually associated with angioedema or profound respiratory compromise |
| **Circulation** | Hypotension, pallor, tachycardia; may progress to shock | Hypotension uncommon unless severe systemic reaction; often absent |
| **Cutaneous / mucosal** | Generalised urticaria, erythema, flushing, pruritus; may be >30 % skin surface | Localized rash or urticaria only; no systemic hypotension |
| **Gastro‑intestinal** | Nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhoea | GI symptoms may appear but are not life‑threatening without cardiorespiratory compromise |
| **Time course** | Rapid resolution after epinephrine; recurrence (biphasic/multiphasic) within 24 hrs | Symptoms usually abort with antihistamines; no recurrence after resolution |
| **Response to epinephrine** | Immediate (within minutes) reversal of hypotension & airway edema | Variable; may not reverse hypotension or airway edema |

**Why airway, breathing, circulation, and skin findings were essential in this case**

1. **Airway (facial swelling, pruritus in the throat, no tongue swelling)**
   - The patient described a “feeling of swelling in her throat” with facial edema but no overt laryngeal involvement.  
   - In anaphylaxis, even subclinical oropharyngeal edema can progress quickly to airway obstruction; the presence of facial swelling warrants immediate airway vigilance, supplemental oxygen, and readiness for airway support.  

2. **Breathing (hypoxaemia, tachypnea, later dyspnoea)**
   - Initial SpO₂ of 88 % and respiratory rate 22/min signalled early hypoxaemic compromise.  
   - Recurrent episodes with worsening dyspnoea and cyanosis confirmed the life‑threatening respiratory component that mandated repeated epinephrine and oxygen titration.  

3. **Circulation (hypotension, tachycardia)**
   - BP 74/50 mm Hg and later “deep hypoxaemia with cyanosis, hypotension and severe dyspnoea” illustrated the classic anaphylactic shock.  
   - Circulatory collapse obligates immediate intramuscular epinephrine, fluid resuscitation, and continuous monitoring—steps that were key in preventing progression to cardiac arrest.  

4. **Skin (urcerosia, erythema, flushing, pruritus)**
   - Generalised urticaria and erythema involving the whole body were present from the first hour and recurred with each episode.  
   - Cutaneous signs are the most sensitive early indicators of systemic mast cell degranulation and help confirm the anaphylactic syndrome when combined with airway and circulatory findings.

**Clinical teaching point**

Anaphylaxis is defined not merely by the presence of an allergic reaction but by its *systemic, rapid, potentially lethal* nature. The combination of airway compromise, circulatory collapse, and generalized cutaneous manifestations constitutes the “four‑pillars” (airway, breathing, circulation, skin) that distinguish it from isolated allergic reactions such as isolated urticaria or localized bronchospasm. In the reported case, the patient’s evolving hypotension, hypoxaemia, and generalized urticaria, together with the need for repeated epinephrine, confirmed true multiphasic anaphylaxis and justified the intensive monitoring and aggressive treatment that ultimately led to her recovery.

### [Pathophysiology] Why is the timing of tryptase sampling critical in suspected anaphylaxis, and how might the delays observed in this patient’s case have impacted diagnostic clarity?

**Answer**

Tryptase is the most specific mast‑cell mediator that rises in the first few hours of a systemic anaphylactic reaction. Peak plasma concentrations usually occur 30–60 minutes after symptom onset, and levels fall back toward baseline within 4–6 hours. Consequently, the AAP/FAAN guideline recommends three draws:   

1. **Baseline** as soon as possible (ideally before or immediately after treatment)—defines the patient’s resting mediator level.  
2. **Peak** 1–2 hours after symptom onset—captures the maximal release of mast‑cell content.  
3. **Post‑resolution** ≥24 hours after complete clinical recovery—to confirm that the baseline is stable.

The timing is critical because:

* **Sensitivity is window‑dependent** – a sample taken too early (before exocytosis) or too late (after degranulation has ceased) will underestimate the true maximum, potentially masking a clinically significant reaction or suggesting a milder event than actually occurred.  
* **Differentiation of biphasic/multiphasic disease** – serial peaks help distinguish a true second wave of mediator release from residual medication or sustained mild reaction.  
* **Diagnostic work‑up for mast cell disorders** – a persistently elevated baseline or two separate peaks strongly supports systemic mastocytosis or other mast‑cell activation syndromes, which are risk factors for severe and recurrent anaphylaxis.

---

**Impact of the delays in this patient**

In the presented case, the first tryptase sample was obtained 1 hour after the *third* anaphylactic episode, and the second sample 27 hours after the last recurrence. Consequently:

1. **The initial “peak” sample missed the true maximum** that would have occurred after the first ED presentation (when tryptase would have peaked within 1–2 hours). The measured level therefore underestimated the severity of the mast‑cell degranulation.  
2. **A baseline sample was never obtained** – without a pre‑reaction value, it is impossible to ascertain whether the later “peaks” were truly elevated or simply reflective of an already high baseline.  
3. **Serial comparisons were impossible** – we could not document the rise and fall of tryptase across the multiphasic course, limiting the ability to correlate clinical recurrence with biochemical evidence.  
4. **Risk stratification was impaired** – elevated baseline tryptase is a known marker of increased risk for biphasic or refractory anaphylaxis. In the absence of a baseline, clinicians could not use this parameter to decide on observation duration or escalation of care.  
5. **Diagnostic certainty for systemic mastocytosis remained equivocal** – while the two elevated peaks suggested a mast‑cell disorder, the lack of a confirmed baseline and timely serial sampling made it difficult to meet WHO criteria or to reliably rule out other causes of elevated tryptase (e.g., non‑allergic triggers).

In short, the delayed sampling forfeited the opportunity to:

* Quantify the true magnitude of mast‑cell activation at the first event,  
* Demonstrate dynamic changes that would confirm a multiphasic reaction, and  
* Establish a robust baseline to support a diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis.

Adhering to the guideline‑recommended sampling intervals would have provided clearer biochemical evidence of the severity and recurrence of the reaction, potentially informing a more targeted monitoring strategy and aiding in the early identification of an underlying mast‑cell disorder.

### [Pathophysiology] Explain the pathophysiological rationale for using epinephrine as the first-line treatment in anaphylaxis, and describe why a 0.3 mg dose may represent under‑dosing in adults with a body weight of 104 kg.

**Why epinephrine is the first‑line drug in anaphylaxis**

Anaphylaxis is a systemic, IgE‑mediated reaction that triggers massive mediator release from mast cells and basophils.  The key pathophysiologic sequelae are:

| Pathway | Effect | Epinephrine action |
|---------|--------|--------------------|
| **Cardiovascular** | Reflex vasodilation, systemic vasodilation, histamine‑induced capillary leak → hypotension | ↑α₁‑adrenergic vasoconstriction restores SVR and reduces capillary leak |
| **Pulmonary** | Bronchospasm, mucus hypersecretion, airway edema → bronchoconstriction, hypoxaemia | ↑β₂‑adrenergic bronchodilation and ↓α₁‑mediated edema |
| **Dermatologic** | Urticaria, angioedema from mast‑cell degranulation | Limited effect, but rapid hemodynamic support prevents progression |

Because epinephrine simultaneously activates **α₁** (vasoconstriction), **β₁** (↑cardiac output) and **β₂** (bronchodilation) pathways, it counteracts the three most life‑threatening components of anaphylaxis in a single agent.  Prompt administration restores perfusion, prevents airway compromise, and stops the cascade of mediator release.

**Guideline‑based dosing**

The American College of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology (ACAAI) and the World Allergy Organization recommend

- **0.01 mg /kg** of body weight (maximum 0.5 mg per dose) administered intramuscularly.
- Repeat every 5–15 min until symptoms resolve.

For an adult ≥50 kg this simplifies to a **0.5 mg** IM dose.  In the case described, the patient weighed **104 kg**, so the ideal first‑dose would be

> 0.01 mg × 104 kg = 1.04 mg → 0.5 mg (capped by the guideline maximum).

**Why a 0.3 mg auto‑injector may under‑dose an obese patient**

– The auto‑injector used in the ED delivered **0.3 mg** epinephrine.  
– For a 104 kg adult this is only **≈0.003 mg /kg**, **≈30 %** of the target 0.01 mg /kg.  
– The case report documents that the patient required **three additional IM doses** (two 0.5 mg) after the initial 0.3 mg injection to reverse recurrent anaphylaxis, highlighting that the first dose was insufficient to halt mediator release and restore hemodynamics.  

In a patient with systemic mastocytosis, underlying mast‑cell hyper‑responsiveness further amplifies mediator spill‑over, making adequate epinephrine dosing even more critical.  A 2025 retrospective analysis cited in the report showed that patients who first received 0.3 mg were **significantly more likely** to need escalated care (additional epinephrine, ICU admission) compared with those who received the guideline‑recommended 0.5 mg.

**Clinical take‑away**

1. **Use epinephrine immediately** when anaphylaxis is suspected; time to drug is the single most important determinant of outcome.  
2. **Dose according to weight** (0.01 mg/kg, max 0.5 mg) – standard 0.5 mg auto‑injectors or 0.5 mg IM preparations avoid under‑dosing in adults >50 kg.  
3. **Avoid reliance on 0.3 mg auto‑injectors** in clinical settings where patient weight is ≥50 kg; the risk of under‑dosing can lead to repeated episodes, as seen in the reported patient.  

These principles apply to all clinical contexts, whether pre‑hospital or in the ED, and are essential for preventing the severe complications illustrated by the multiphasic anaphylaxis case.

### [Management] Discuss the evidence regarding routine corticosteroid use in anaphylaxis for preventing biphasic reactions, and explain why this patient still received prednisone and dexamethasone during her ICU stay.

**Evidence on routine corticosteroids for biphasic anaphylaxis**

| Study | Design | Key Finding | Clinical Take‑away |
|-------|--------|-------------|---------------------|
| 2017 Cochrane review (22 studies) | Systematic review of RCTs and observational data | Only 1 study suggested benefit; 21 studies found no reduction in recurrence | No convincing evidence that steroids prevent a second episode |
| 2019 multicentre cohort | Adults with anaphylaxis treated pre‑hospital | Steroid use was associated with *higher* ICU/ward admission | Steroids may not improve outcomes and could add morbidity |
| 2021 guideline (World Allergy Organization, UK Resuscitation Council) | Expert consensus | Recommends against routine corticosteroid use after anaphylaxis | Current guidelines advise *not* to give steroids unless other indications exist |

**Why the patient still received prednisone & dexamethasone**

1. **Historical practice pattern** – The hospital’s anaphylaxis protocol (in place before the 2019/2021 guideline updates) still listed intravenous corticosteroids after the first recurrence of anaphylaxis, reflecting the older belief that steroids reduce biphasic risk.

2. **Multiple recurrences (multiphasic anaphylaxis)** – The patient experienced *four* distinct episodes within 12 h, which is far beyond the “usual biphasic” scenario. In such extreme cases clinicians sometimes deviate from guidelines, reasoning that aggressive prophylaxis might blunt a dangerous repeat shock.

3. **Perceived safety margin** – In the ICU setting, a single dose of dexamethasone (8 mg) and a short course of prednisone (30 mg q.d.) were judged unlikely to cause adverse events, and the potential benefit of preventing further episodes was deemed worthwhile for an older woman with an inferred mastocytosis (high baseline tryptase) who was at higher risk of severe recurrence.

4. **Protocol differ‑enforcement** – The internal ward protocol called for prednisone, while the ICU protocol called for dexamethasone. Both were administered sequentially because the hospital’s allergy team had no clear consensus on a unified steroid strategy for multiphasic reactions, and the treating teams in ED, general ward, and ICU each followed their own institutional guideline.

**Bottom line**

Current high‑quality evidence and contemporary guidelines do **not** support routine corticosteroid administration after anaphylaxis to prevent biphasic or multiphasic recurrence. The steroids given to this patient were a vestige of older protocols and an attempt to manage an extraordinary clinical course; they are not endorsed by recent evidence. In future cases, clinicians should rely on epinephrine (adequate dose) and supportive care, with steroids reserved for specific indications (e.g., severe airway edema, concomitant asthma, or planned procedural use where steroids are otherwise indicated).

### [Differential Diagnosis] What are the WHO diagnostic criteria for systemic mastocytosis, and why did the patient’s laboratory and histopathological findings fall short of a formal diagnosis?

**WHO Diagnostic Criteria for Systemic Mastocytosis**

The 2016 World Health Organization classification requires **one major plus one minor** criterion, or **four minor** criteria to confirm systemic mastocytosis.

| Criterion | What to look for | Typical test/ finding |
|-----------|------------------|-----------------------|
| **Major** | Multisystem mast cell infiltration with a malignant clonal mast cell population | Bone‑marrow aspirate/biopsy showing >15% of cells as spindle‑shaped, “Döhle‑like” mast cells in clusters or sheets (tyrosine‑kinase driver mutations such as KIT D816V) |
| **Minor** | Any three of the following |
| 1 | ≥25 % of marrow mast cells in clusters or sheets | Same histology as major |
| 2 | KIT D816V mutation in blood, marrow or other tissue | PCR/allele‑specific sequencing |
| 3 | Serum tryptase > 20 ng/mL (above age‑adjusted baseline) | Baseline tryptase with no secondary cause |
| 4 | Other organ involvement (e.g., hepatomegaly, skin lesions) with mast‑cell infiltration | Imaging or histology |

Thus, a full diagnosis needs *bone‑marrow morphology* **plus** two supporting laboratory findings.

**Why the patient did not meet formal criteria**

1. **Bone‑marrow material was limited.**  
   The bone‑marrow biopsy obtained for the patient was “insufficient to assess the major criterion.” Consequently, the **major criterion (≥15 % spindle‑shaped mast cells in sheets)** could not be evaluated. Without a positive major or a second minor criterion, the threshold for diagnosis cannot be reached.

2. **Only two minor criteria were available.**  
   • **Elevated serum tryptase** – several samples during the third anaphylactic episode and at follow‑up were >20 ng/mL.  
   • **Positive KIT D816V PCR** – confirmed in peripheral blood.  

   The remaining two minor criteria (≥25 % mast cells in sheets on marrow, or other organ involvement with histologic confirmation) were **not demonstrated**. Hepatomegaly was noted on ultrasound, but no histologic confirmation of mast‑cell infiltration was obtained.

3. **Timing of tryptase sampling violated guidelines.**  
   Recommended sampling (within 4 h of reaction onset, and a baseline ≥24 h after resolution) was not achieved. The first tryptase was drawn only after the third episode, and the second 27 h later, limiting the reliability of the baseline measurement.

4. **No evidence of other organ infiltration on histology.**  
   Skin, gastrointestinal tract, and other tissues were not biopsied to demonstrate clonal mast‑cell accumulation.

In sum, the patient meets **two of the four minor criteria** (elevated baseline tryptase and KIT D816V mutation) but lacks evidence of clonal marrow infiltration (major or additional minor). Therefore, the WHO definition of systemic mastocytosis is not met, though a high probability of the disease is suggested. Follow‑up with a repeat bone‑marrow examination and comprehensive organ assessment would be required to confirm the diagnosis.

### [Management] Enumerate at least three monitoring strategies recommended for patients after an initial episode of anaphylaxis, and justify the choice of the ICU admission in this particular case.

**Monitoring strategies for the post‑anaphylaxis period**

1. **Continuous cardiac and respiratory telemetry** – Continuous ECG and pulse‑oximetry allow detection of transient hypotension, bradycardia, or desaturation that may herald a biphasic event. In the case report the patient’s first biphasic attack occurred within two hours of the initial episode; continuous telemetry would have documented the rapid drop in BP and the subsequent response to epinephrine.

2. **Serial vital‑sign assessment at 15‑minute intervals** – Rigidly scheduled checks (blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, and temperature) permit prompt identification of subtle physiological changes that precede clinical deterioration. The patient experienced four distinct relapse episodes over a 12‑hour window; a 15‑minute monitoring schedule would have captured the trajectory of each episode and guided timely intervention.

3. **Serial serum tryptase and other laboratory monitoring** – Guidelines recommend at least three tryptase samples: (i) at symptom onset, (ii) 1–2 h afterwards, and (iii) ≥24 h after resolution, to confirm mast cell degranulation and to distinguish true biphasic from sustained reactions. The index patient had tryptase measured only after the third episode; routine serial sampling would have provided objective evidence of ongoing mast cell activation and potentially guided the decision to intensify therapy or to initiate prophylactic steroids/antihistamines.

**Why ICU admission was appropriate in this case**

- **Frequency and severity of biphasic attacks**: Four anaphylactic episodes within 12 h, each requiring intramuscular epinephrine, marked a highly unstable clinical course. The clinical guidelines and the authors’ own discussion emphasize that multiple recurrences and the need for repeated epinephrine injections are strong predictors of further biphasic events and are indications for intensive monitoring.

- **Underlying mast cell disorder**: The subsequent work‑up suggested systemic mastocytosis (elevated baseline and peak tryptase, hepatomegaly). Patients with mastocytosis have a higher baseline risk of refractory or multiphasic anaphylaxis. ICU care provides immediate access to advanced airway management, vasoactive support, and rapid re‑dosing of epinephrine if needed.

- **Resource and safety considerations**: ICU settings guarantee 24‑hour availability of multidisciplinary staff (intensivists, anesthesiologists, allergists), rapid infusion capability, and the ability to perform continuous telemetry. Given that the patient’s hemodynamics were unstable even after treatment and that traditional inpatient wards do not routinely provide telemetry or immediate critical‑care resources, admission to the ICU met both the risk‑stratified monitoring recommendations and the patient‑specific safety needs highlighted in the case report.

In short, continuous telemetry, frequent vital‑sign checks, and serial laboratory monitoring form the core of post‑anaphylaxis surveillance, and ICU admission was warranted by the patient’s multiphasic, high‑severity course and suspected mast cell disorder.

### [Clinical Pearls] Describe the potential clinical consequences of delayed epinephrine administration in anaphylaxis, and analyze how the 1‑hour delay from clarithromycin ingestion to symptom onset might have influenced the severity of the patient’s reaction.

**Clinical Consequence of Delayed Epinephrine and the 1‑Hour Clarithromycin Window**

Prompt epinephrine is the cornerstone of anaphylaxis therapy because it reverses life‑threatening mediator‑driven vasodilation, bronchospasm, and pruritus. Delays in giving the first dose have clear, graded risks:

| Time from symptom onset to first epinephrine | Typical clinical sequelae |
|-------------------------------------------|---------------------------|
| <5 min | Rapid reversal of hypotension, airway edema; minimal morbidity |
| 5–15 min | Higher likelihood of hypotensive shock, progressive hypoxia, need for ICU monitoring |
| >15 min | Increased risk of cardiac arrhythmias, organ hypoperfusion, refractory shock, and higher mortality |

Evidence from observational studies shows that every 10‑minute delay increases the odds of requiring ≥2 epinephrine injections by ~20 % and escalates the need for ICU admission. The underlying pathophysiology is the continued unchecked release of histamine, tryptase, leukotrienes, and platelet‑activating factor; without the vasoconstrictor and bronchodilator effects of epinephrine, the patient’s hemodynamics deteriorate while mucosal edema progresses.

---

### How the 1‑hour delay from clarithromycin ingestion likely amplified severity

1. **Rapid, but incomplete, absorption of clarithromycin**  
   Clarithromycin is orally absorbed within 30–90 min. In this patient, the first symptoms appeared 1 h after ingestion, indicating that systemic levels had risen sufficiently to trigger mast‑cell degranulation. A 1‑h lag between ingestion and symptomatic onset is typical for oral antibiotics in anaphylaxis.

2. **Escalating mediator release during the untreated interval**  
   Without epinephrine, mast cells continued to liberate histamine, tryptase, and other mediators, causing progressive hypotension, hypoxemia, and cutaneous manifestations. The patient’s BP fell to 74/50 mm Hg and SpO₂ to 88 % before EM received the 0.3 mg IM dose, illustrating that the delayed drug allowed the reaction to reach a fulminant stage.

3. **Mitigation of airway edema and bronchoconstriction**  
   Epinephrine’s α‑adrenergic effect constricts blood vessels, limiting angioedema, while β2 agonism dilates airways. The 1‑h delay meant the patient’s airway swelled and bronchoconstriction progressed, leading to the hypoxemia that required temporary oxygen re‑initiation during subsequent relapses.

4. **Propensity for multiphasic courses in high‑tryptase patients**  
   Baseline and peak tryptase levels in this case were markedly elevated, a known risk factor for biphasic/multiphasic anaphylaxis. Delayed first‑dose epinephrine likely allowed a larger mediator burden, predisposing the patient to multiple recurrences as the initial burst waned and a secondary wave emerged.

5. **Dose‑related under‑treatment due to auto‑injector size**  
   The emergency department’s first two injections were 0.3 mg each (less than the guideline‑recommended 0.5 mg for a 104‑kg patient). Even after the initial dose, the sustained hypotension and rash suggested that the dose was insufficient to blunt the ongoing mediator release, necessitating a second and third IM dose over the next 12 h.

---

### Take‑home points for practice

- **Administer epinephrine within the first 5–7 min** of anaphylaxis onset. A 1‑hour delay, as seen here, exposes the patient to a higher risk of refractory shock and multiphasic recurrence.
- **Use the guideline‑recommended dose (0.01 mg/kg, max 0.5 mg)**; auto‑injectors containing only 0.3 mg may under‑dose adults >50 kg, prolonging the reaction and increasing the need for repeat doses.
- **Early epinephrine also reduces downstream complications**: it limits the extent of mucosal edema, preserves hemodynamic stability, and may blunt the secondary mediator surge that feeds multiphasic episodes.
- **In patients with high tryptase or suspected mast‑cell disorders, monitor closely for at least 12 h after initial improvement**, as recurrent episodes are more common.

In our case, a one‑hour lag between clarithromycin ingestion and the first epinephrine injection both allowed the reaction to become severe and set the stage for a multiphasic course. Rapid, appropriately dosed epinephrine remains the single most effective intervention to prevent these consequences.

### [Morning Report] What alternative therapeutic approaches were considered for managing multiphasic anaphylaxis in this patient, and what are the risks and benefits of continuous intravenous epinephrine versus an expectant approach?

**Clinical Teaching Point – Managing Multiphasic Anaphylaxis**

In the case of the woman in her 60s who experienced four separate anaphylactic attacks after a single dose of clarithromycin, the care team faced a classic dilemma: **continue epinephrine continuously or wait (“expectant”) for a spontaneous resolution?**  The authors explicitly considered both strategies and ultimately observed that the patient did not recur.

---

### 1. Rationale for Continuous Intravenous (IV) Epinephrine

| **Potential Benefit** | **Associated Risk** |
|------------------------|---------------------|
| *Pre‑emptive control* of mast‑cell degranulation, potentially lowering the need for repeated IM injections and preventing hemodynamic collapse | *Alpha‑adrenergic toxicity* – severe hypertension, arrhythmias, precipitating ischemia |
| *Stable hemodynamics* during extended monitoring beds and in ICU settings | *Alpha‑tone leading to peripheral vasoconstriction* – decreased gastric/renal perfusion |
| *Avoidance of repeated IM injections* in patients who may have difficult access or subcutaneous edema | *Blunted response* – if epinephrine exhausted, ischemic risk increases |
| *Potentially shorter total duration of anaphylaxis* by keeping mast‑cell mediators in check | *Over‑administration* – risk of tachycardia, anxiety, tremor |

The **dose** would normally be 0.01 mg/kg IV (≈0.03–0.05 mg/min).  In practice, continuous infusion is rarely used because most guidelines recommend intermittent IM dosing and because monitoring for hypertension and arrhythmia can be resource‑intensive.  In the present case, the authors noted that the patient’s episodes were "decreasing in intensity," which made continuous infusion less attractive.

---

### 2. Rationale for an Expectant Approach

| **Potential Benefit** | **Associated Risk** |
|------------------------|---------------------|
| Avoids **invasive therapy** and its side effects, preserving hemodynamic stability | Possibility of a rapid, severe biphasic or multiphasic relapse |
| Reduces **drug exposure** – especially useful when steroids are withheld (no evidence for benefit) | Unpredictable timing of recurrences (up to 78 h reported) |
| Simplifies monitoring – fewer nursing interventions | Missed early warning signs in a patient with underlying mastocytosis (higher mediator burden) |

The decision to observe without continuous IV epinephrine therefore relies on **clinical stability**, absence of airway compromise, and a trend toward decreasing symptom severity—exactly what the team noted (“symptom intensity lessened with each episode”).

---

### 3. Weighing the Two Options in this Case

1. **Clinical Status** – After the fourth episode, the patient was stable, with no ongoing hypotension or hypoxemia, and the severity of symptoms was attenuating.  
2. **Underlying Risk** – The high baseline and peak tryptase levels and suspected systemic mastocytosis technically raise the risk of a late, potentially severe flare.  
3. **Resource Considerations** – Continuous IV epinephrine requires intensive nursing oversight, frequent BP/HR checks, and the ability to titrate quickly – a significant burden in an ICU that might be better allocated to other patients.  
4. **Evidence Gaps** – No randomized data exist comparing continuous infusion to intermittent IM dosing in multiphasic anaphylaxis. Current practice derives from expert consensus and anecdotal reports.

Given these factors, the care team opted for **expectant monitoring in the ICU**.  They augmented this with **corticosteroids** (though evidence for their efficacy is weak), antihistamines, and fluid resuscitation—standard supportive measures.  Importantly, no invasive complications arose, and the patient never required additional epinephrine after the fourth episode.

---

### 4. Take‑Home Messages for Practice

- **IV continuous epinephrine** can be considered when a patient is at *very high risk* for severe, recurrent anaphylaxis (e.g., known mastocytosis, severe initial presentation, or refractory to IM dosing).  It is *not* routinely required for all multiphasic cases.
- An **expectant approach** is reasonable in patients who are *clinically improving* and when close bedside monitoring is possible.  Ensure rapid access to ICU or high‑dependency care if symptoms recur.
- **Monitor meticulously**: hourly BP/HR, continuous pulse oximetry, and bedside observation of airway and skin signs.  Use a lower‑dose epinephrine auto‑injector judiciously, but be prepared to give a 0.5‑mg IM dose if needed.
- **Corticosteroids** remain controversial; consider them only if the patient has a history of refractory anaphylaxis or if the clinician is concerned about delayed reactions in a mast‑cell disorder.
- **Pre‑emptive epinephrine infusion** should be reserved for cases where the *balance of risk* tilts clearly toward forbidding a severe relapse, rather than as a default in every multiphasic scenario.

In this patient, the expectant approach was safe and effective, illustrating that careful clinical judgment can guide the choice between continuous IV epinephrine and close observation in multiphasic anaphylaxis.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Considering the patient’s multiple drug allergies and prior reactions, explain how clarithromycin was identified as the likely trigger despite being a macrolide and how delayed macrolide reactions can present.

**Answer**

In this case the only new drug that the patient had taken in the hours immediately preceding the anaphylaxis was a single 500‑mg oral dose of clarithromycin. All other medications (metformin, gliclazide, hydrochlorothiazide, omeprazole, sertraline) had been taken chronically and were not implicated in her past reactions. The patient had a well‑documented late macrolide‐related skin rash after 5 days of clindamycin (which was a different macrolide class) that prompted the switch to clarithromycin. This history of a delayed macrolide reaction, together with the temporal relationship between clarithromycin ingestion and symptom onset (< 1 h), makes clarithromycin the most plausible allergen, despite macrolides being considered uncommon triggers of anaphylaxis.

**Delayed macrolide reactions** can manifest as:

| Onset | Clinical picture | Typical timing |
|-------|------------------|----------------|
| **Early (0–30 min)** | Urticaria, angioedema, hypotension | < 30 min |
| **Intermediate (1–4 h)** | Late‑onset rash, pruritus, flushing | 1–4 h |
| **Late (> 12 h)** | Persistent or biphasic symptoms, possibly with systemic features | > 12 h |

Macrolides are usually associated with delayed hypersensitivity reactions (type IV), but the patient’s multiphasic anaphylaxis suggests a strong immediate‑phase mast cell response (type I). The elevated tryptase levels (baseline and during the third episode) support a mast‑cell–mediated mechanism, which is particularly evident in patients with underlying systemic mastocytosis—an association that may predispose to more severe and recurrent anaphylaxis.

**Key teaching points**

1. **Temporal proximity and prior reactions** are central to allergen identification when multiple drugs are present.  
2. **Macrolide hypersensitivity** can be immediate (IgE‑mediated) or delayed (type IV); in this patient the reaction pattern paralleled an immediate anaphylaxis despite the usual perception of macrolides as delayed‑onset agents.  
3. **Delayed reactions may be biphasic or multiphasic**; monitoring for at least 24 h after symptom resolution is prudent, especially when risk factors (multiple epinephrine doses, severe initial presentation, possible mastocytosis) are present.  

Thus, the clinical history and timing made clarithromycin the culprit, and the case illustrates that macrolides, though classically associated with delayed reactions, can produce fulminant, multiphasic anaphylaxis in a subset of patients.
