Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Clinical Pearls] What are the key clinical features that differentiate anaphylaxis from other acute allergic reactions, and why were the airway, breathing, circulation, and skin findings particularly important in this case?

**Key clinical features that set anaphylaxis apart from other acute allergic reactions**

| Domain | Anaphylaxis | Other acute allergic reactions |
|--------|--------------|--------------------------------|
| **Onset & tempo** | Sudden, rapid onset (seconds–minutes) with progression over minutes to a few hours | Often slower, may be delayed or localized |
| **Systemic involvement** | Requires at least two organ systems: airway/breathing, circulation **or** GI, plus cutaneous or mucosal signs | Usually confined to one system (e.g., isolated urticaria, isolated bronchospasm) |
| **Airway / breathing** | Angioedema of lips, tongue, oropharynx; wheeze; hypoxemia; tachypnea | Can have wheeze or bronchospasm in asthma, but not usually associated with angioedema or profound respiratory compromise |
| **Circulation** | Hypotension, pallor, tachycardia; may progress to shock | Hypotension uncommon unless severe systemic reaction; often absent |
| **Cutaneous / mucosal** | Generalised urticaria, erythema, flushing, pruritus; may be >30 % skin surface | Localized rash or urticaria only; no systemic hypotension |
| **Gastro‑intestinal** | Nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhoea | GI symptoms may appear but are not life‑threatening without cardiorespiratory compromise |
| **Time course** | Rapid resolution after epinephrine; recurrence (biphasic/multiphasic) within 24 hrs | Symptoms usually abort with antihistamines; no recurrence after resolution |
| **Response to epinephrine** | Immediate (within minutes) reversal of hypotension & airway edema | Variable; may not reverse hypotension or airway edema |

**Why airway, breathing, circulation, and skin findings were essential in this case**

1. **Airway (facial swelling, pruritus in the throat, no tongue swelling)**
   - The patient described a “feeling of swelling in her throat” with facial edema but no overt laryngeal involvement.  
   - In anaphylaxis, even subclinical oropharyngeal edema can progress quickly to airway obstruction; the presence of facial swelling warrants immediate airway vigilance, supplemental oxygen, and readiness for airway support.  

2. **Breathing (hypoxaemia, tachypnea, later dyspnoea)**
   - Initial SpO₂ of 88 % and respiratory rate 22/min signalled early hypoxaemic compromise.  
   - Recurrent episodes with worsening dyspnoea and cyanosis confirmed the life‑threatening respiratory component that mandated repeated epinephrine and oxygen titration.  

3. **Circulation (hypotension, tachycardia)**
   - BP 74/50 mm Hg and later “deep hypoxaemia with cyanosis, hypotension and severe dyspnoea” illustrated the classic anaphylactic shock.  
   - Circulatory collapse obligates immediate intramuscular epinephrine, fluid resuscitation, and continuous monitoring—steps that were key in preventing progression to cardiac arrest.  

4. **Skin (urcerosia, erythema, flushing, pruritus)**
   - Generalised urticaria and erythema involving the whole body were present from the first hour and recurred with each episode.  
   - Cutaneous signs are the most sensitive early indicators of systemic mast cell degranulation and help confirm the anaphylactic syndrome when combined with airway and circulatory findings.

**Clinical teaching point**

Anaphylaxis is defined not merely by the presence of an allergic reaction but by its *systemic, rapid, potentially lethal* nature. The combination of airway compromise, circulatory collapse, and generalized cutaneous manifestations constitutes the “four‑pillars” (airway, breathing, circulation, skin) that distinguish it from isolated allergic reactions such as isolated urticaria or localized bronchospasm. In the reported case, the patient’s evolving hypotension, hypoxaemia, and generalized urticaria, together with the need for repeated epinephrine, confirmed true multiphasic anaphylaxis and justified the intensive monitoring and aggressive treatment that ultimately led to her recovery.
