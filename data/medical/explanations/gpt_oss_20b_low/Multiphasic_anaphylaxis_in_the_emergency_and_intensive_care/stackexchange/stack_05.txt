Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Management] Discuss the evidence regarding routine corticosteroid use in anaphylaxis for preventing biphasic reactions, and explain why this patient still received prednisone and dexamethasone during her ICU stay.

**Evidence on routine corticosteroids for biphasic anaphylaxis**

| Study | Design | Key Finding | Clinical Take‑away |
|-------|--------|-------------|---------------------|
| 2017 Cochrane review (22 studies) | Systematic review of RCTs and observational data | Only 1 study suggested benefit; 21 studies found no reduction in recurrence | No convincing evidence that steroids prevent a second episode |
| 2019 multicentre cohort | Adults with anaphylaxis treated pre‑hospital | Steroid use was associated with *higher* ICU/ward admission | Steroids may not improve outcomes and could add morbidity |
| 2021 guideline (World Allergy Organization, UK Resuscitation Council) | Expert consensus | Recommends against routine corticosteroid use after anaphylaxis | Current guidelines advise *not* to give steroids unless other indications exist |

**Why the patient still received prednisone & dexamethasone**

1. **Historical practice pattern** – The hospital’s anaphylaxis protocol (in place before the 2019/2021 guideline updates) still listed intravenous corticosteroids after the first recurrence of anaphylaxis, reflecting the older belief that steroids reduce biphasic risk.

2. **Multiple recurrences (multiphasic anaphylaxis)** – The patient experienced *four* distinct episodes within 12 h, which is far beyond the “usual biphasic” scenario. In such extreme cases clinicians sometimes deviate from guidelines, reasoning that aggressive prophylaxis might blunt a dangerous repeat shock.

3. **Perceived safety margin** – In the ICU setting, a single dose of dexamethasone (8 mg) and a short course of prednisone (30 mg q.d.) were judged unlikely to cause adverse events, and the potential benefit of preventing further episodes was deemed worthwhile for an older woman with an inferred mastocytosis (high baseline tryptase) who was at higher risk of severe recurrence.

4. **Protocol differ‑enforcement** – The internal ward protocol called for prednisone, while the ICU protocol called for dexamethasone. Both were administered sequentially because the hospital’s allergy team had no clear consensus on a unified steroid strategy for multiphasic reactions, and the treating teams in ED, general ward, and ICU each followed their own institutional guideline.

**Bottom line**

Current high‑quality evidence and contemporary guidelines do **not** support routine corticosteroid administration after anaphylaxis to prevent biphasic or multiphasic recurrence. The steroids given to this patient were a vestige of older protocols and an attempt to manage an extraordinary clinical course; they are not endorsed by recent evidence. In future cases, clinicians should rely on epinephrine (adequate dose) and supportive care, with steroids reserved for specific indications (e.g., severe airway edema, concomitant asthma, or planned procedural use where steroids are otherwise indicated).
