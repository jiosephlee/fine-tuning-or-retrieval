Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

Chapter 2: ## 2. Diagnostic Reasoning in Dermatomyositis

Diagnosing dermatomyositis, particularly the anti‑SAE1 antibody subset, requires a systematic approach that integrates the clinical picture with biochemical, serological, imaging and histopathologic data. The case of the 40‑year‑old male with neck swelling demonstrates how a seemingly unrelated presentation can be unraveled by keeping the red flag for an inflammatory myopathy in mind.

### Key clinical red flags for inflammatory myopathy
In the emergency setting, progressive proximal muscle weakness that is out of proportion to exertion, dysphagia, or a combination of muscle pain and systemic symptoms should prompt consideration of an inflammatory myopathy. Neck swelling, especially when it is bilateral, non‑tender, and associated with inflammatory markers, is an atypical but notable red flag, given the reported 6–8 % incidence of pseudo‑angioedema in dermatomyositis. Absence of fever, normal white cell count, and a lack of focal infectious signs further point toward a non‑infectious inflammatory process.

### Role of creatine kinase and inflammatory markers
Elevated serum creatine kinase (CK) is a hallmark of active muscle injury. In this patient, CK rose to 16,184 U/L, far above the normal upper limit, confirming significant myofiber damage. While the acute‑phase reactants, such as C‑reactive protein (CRP), were markedly elevated (55.6 mg/L), they lack specificity for myositis but support an inflammatory milieu. Normal renal function and absent blood myoglobin ruled out rhabdomyolysis with systemic consequences.

### Autoantibody testing: ANA, myositis panel, SAE1 specificity
A high‑titer anti‑nuclear antibody (ANA) with a speckled pattern is common in dermatomyositis but is nonspecific. The Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay, interrogating 16 myositis‑associated antigens, revealed a strongly positive anti‑SAE1 antibody (signal intensity 173 vs <35 normal). Anti‑SAE1 antibodies are pathogenic against the small ubiquitin‑like modifier activating enzyme, a protein involved in post‑translational modification by sumoylation. No other myositis‑specific antibodies (anti‑Mi‑2α, anti‑TIF1‑γ, anti‑Jo‑1, anti‑OJ) were detected, further narrowing the diagnosis to anti‑SAE1 dermatomyositis. Concurrently, the patient’s complement profile (C3, C4, C1 esterase activity) and C1q were within normal ranges, effectively excluding complement‑driven hereditary or acquired angioedema.

### Complement and angioedema work‑up (C1 esterase, C3/C4)
Idiopathic angioedema typically presents with episodic histamine‑ or bradykinin‑mediated swelling. Laboratory evaluation often shows reduced C1 esterase inhibitor activity or low C4 in hereditary angioedema. In this case, the normal C1 esterase activity and normal C4 supported the exclusion of a classic angioedema pathway, directing the diagnostic focus toward a different mechanism of edema—namely, inflammatory myositis‑related muscle swelling.

### Muscle imaging: MRI, FDG‑PET patterns
Muscle MRI is the imaging modality of choice for detecting active inflammation. T2‑weighted short tau inversion recovery (STIR) sequences revealed high signal intensity in the bilateral obturator internus and externus, with concomitant involvement of adductors, gracilis, and iliopsoas—findings typical of dermatomyositis and indicative of active myositis. FDG‑PET imaging corroborated these findings by showing diffuse, metabolically active uptake in multiple muscle groups, most prominently in the head and neck, deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdomen, and pelvis. This global hyper‑FDG pattern is characteristic of inflammatory myopathy and helps rule out focal infectious or neoplastic pathologies.

### Muscle biopsy findings: perifascicular necrosis, MHC‑Ia, C5b‑9 deposition
Histopathology remains the gold standard for confirming dermatomyositis. The patient's thigh biopsy exhibited classic features: necrotic, vacuolated, and regenerating myofibres arranged in a perifascicular pattern, with scattered perimysial infiltration by histiocytes. Immunohistochemistry for the membrane attack complex (C5b‑9) demonstrated deposition in perifascicular capillaries—an attribute almost pathognomonic for dermatomyositis, reflecting complement‑mediated microvascular injury. The presence of upregulated MHC‑I on muscle fibers (though not explicitly described in the report, it is commonly seen) further supports an immune‑mediated process.

### Correlating skin manifestations (Holster sign, periungual erythema) with systemic disease
Dermatomyositis is a multisystem disease, and cutaneous signs often herald systemic involvement. In this patient, subtle skin findings, such as the Holster sign (a V‑shaped erythema over the hips) and periungual erythema with capillary changes seen on nailfold capillaroscopy, were pivotal clues. These cutaneous manifestations correlate with underlying muscle inflammation and are essential in tying together the clinical, serologic, and histologic data. Notably, classic features like Gottron’s papules or heliotrope rash were absent, underscoring the variability of skin findings and the need for a high index of suspicion.

---

**Diagnostic pathway summary:**  
1. **Clinical suspicion** triggered by progressive proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, unexplained neck swelling, and elevated inflammatory markers.  
2. **Laboratory assessment** revealing extremely high CK and a positive ANA.  
3. **Autoantibody panel** identifying strong anti‑SAE1 positivity, with normal complement work‑up to exclude angioedema.  
4. **Imaging** (MRI and FDG‑PET) confirming widespread muscle inflammation.  
5. **Confirmatory muscle biopsy** showing perifascicular necrosis and C5b‑9 deposition.  
6. **Skin examination** supporting systemic disease (Holster sign, periungual changes).  

This stepwise integration of multidisciplinary data led to a definitive diagnosis of anti‑SAE1 antibody–positive dermatomyositis, guiding the subsequent aggressive immunosuppressive therapy that resulted in clinical remission.
