Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

Chapter 5: ## 5. Clinical Pearls and Lessons Learned  

**Endoscopic appearance of neck edema:**  
Flexible naso‑endoscopy in this case revealed **no intraluminal obstruction or perforation**; the only abnormality noted was increased secretions and mis‑directed coughing.  In true angioedema, the submucosal tissues are markedly edematous, but the airway mucosa itself is typically smooth and the secretions are not excessive.  A careful inspection for **pseudo‑tubular swelling or a “wrinkled” epithelium** can help differentiate a **vascular swelling** from inflammatory myositis of the strap muscles, which may appear “blurry” on imaging but is not visualised on the endoscope.  

**Value of early CK measurement in unexplained soft‑tissue swelling:**  
A serum creatine kinase (CK) level was requested **anticipating a myopathic cause** because the neck swelling was persistent, non‑painful, and **inconsistent with typical angioedema** (no urtication, no known triggers).  The CK was >16 000 U/L—an unmistakable indicator of muscle injury that shifted the diagnostic trajectory away from ENT infection toward an inflammatory myopathy.  Routine CK testing should therefore be part of the work‑up for any patient with **diffuse, facial/neck edema plus proximal weakness**, even when skin signs are subtle or absent.  

**Recognizing subtle cutaneous signs when rash is absent:**  
In this patient, **Gottron’s papules and heliotrope rash were negative**, yet the combination of **periungual telangiectasia, nailfold micro‑haemorrhages, and a faint Holster (shawl) sign** were telling.  Dermatologic examination should extend beyond the typical rashes; palpation of fingers for “frosted glass” periungual changes and inspection of the nasolabial folds for faint erythema can reveal early dermatomyositis manifestations that might otherwise escape notice.  

**Importance of autoantibody profiling in guiding therapy intensity:**  
The presence of **anti‑SAE1 antibodies** provided a diagnostic anchor and a prognostic cue: these antibodies are linked to a **higher likelihood of dysphagia, interstitial lung disease (ILD), and a more aggressive disease course**.  In practice, once SAE1 positivity is confirmed, clinicians should anticipate **intensive immunosuppression** (high‑dose steroids plus early steroid‑sparing agents) and schedule **serial pulmonary function tests** to monitor ILD progression.  Autoantibody panels should therefore be ordered promptly in any suspected dermatomyositis case, rather than reserved for refractory disease.  

**Balancing immunosuppression with organ‑specific considerations (lung disease):**  
Methotrexate was deliberately avoided because of its known pulmonary toxicity and the patient’s pre‑existing restrictive pattern on spirometry.  Instead, **mycophenolate mofetil** (oral) and **rituximab** (IV) were chosen, recognising that these agents have a more favourable respiratory profile.  The decision to use **IVIG** was influenced by the severe dysphagia and need for rapid improvement in swallowing function.  Clinicians should routinely assess baseline organ function before initiating immunosuppression, tailoring drug choice to minimise iatrogenic injury while still achieving disease control.  

**Documentation of multidisciplinary care pathways in complex autoimmune cases:**  
This case underscores the necessity of a **structured, multidisciplinary approach**: ENT for initial assessment, immunology for serology and initial therapy, rheumatology for ongoing management, pulmonary medicine for ILD surveillance, and gastroenterology for malignancy screening.  A formal care pathway that includes **standardised imaging (CT neck, MRI thigh, FDG‑PET), laboratory checkpoints (CK, complement, autoantibody panel)**, and **scheduled follow‑up visits** facilitated early diagnosis and prevented treatment delays.  For residency training, outlining such pathways in the electronic medical record helps ensure consistency and completeness of care.  

---

**Take‑Home Points**

1. **Neck swelling with proximal weakness warrants early CK testing**; a markedly elevated CK should trigger a myopathy work‑up.  
2. **Endoscopic clues are subtle**; lack of mucosal disease favors myositis over classic angioedema.  
3. **Dermatologic examination must look beyond visible rashes**—periungual changes and subtle erythema can be diagnostic.  
4. **Autoantibody profiling informs both diagnosis and treatment intensity**; SAE1 positivity predicts a more aggressive course.  
5. **Immunosuppression must be balanced against organ vulnerability**—avoid toxic agents when baseline organ disease is present.  
6. **Multidisciplinary documentation and care pathways improve outcomes** by preventing diagnostic delays and ensuring systematic monitoring.
