Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# From Dysphagia to Recovery: Long‑Term Follow‑Up and Outcomes in Dermatomyositis  

**Case in a nutshell**  
A previously healthy male in his 40s presented to the ED with a 3‑week history of progressive neck swelling. CT revealed diffuse soft‑tissue oedema, prompting a differential of Ludwig’s angina vs idiopathic angioedema. A nailfold capillaroscopy, subtle peri‑ungual erythema and an elevated CK (16 184 U/L) turned the diagnosis to anti‑SAE1 dermatomyositis. The patient underwent aggressive immunosuppression (IV methylprednisolone, IVIg, mycophenolate, rituximab) and required a nasogastric tube for 2 months due to severe dysphagia. By 12 months all major clinical indices (CK, pulmonary function, imaging) had returned to baseline and the patient was symptom‑free. The story is instructive for clinicians who manage airway compromise and dysphagia in inflammatory myopathy.

---

## Why This Case Stands Out  

* **Neck swelling as a red flag** – Dermatomyositis rarely presents with pseudo‑angioedema confined to the neck. That an otherwise well male presented with isolated cervical oedema could have led to unnecessary antibiotics or surgical drainage if not for the high CK and capillaroscopy findings.  
* **Rapid response to steroids + immunotherapy** – A 3‑day pulse of methylprednisolone was followed by a sustained taper with IVIg and mycophenolate, illustrating that aggressive early therapy can reverse even severe airway muscle involvement.  
* **Longitudinal monitoring** – Serial CK, PFTs and imaging were used to gauge remission and to epochally adjust therapy, demonstrating a pragmatic approach to disease activity.  

---

## The Natural History of Dysphagia in Dermatomyositis  

| Timepoint | Key Clinical Features | Management Milestone |
|-----------|-----------------------|----------------------|
| **Presentation** | Severe odynophagia, retropharyngeal oedema, neck swelling, proximal muscle weakness | Secure airway, rule out infection, obtain CK & autoantibody panel |
| **Early Hospitalization** | Progressive dysphagia to liquids; aspiration evident on swallow study | Initiate high‑dose IV steroids + consider IVIg; start enteral feeding (NG tube) |
| **Week 3–6** | CK trending down, neck oedema improves | Trial of oral liquids; repeat swallow; consider early swallow therapy |
| **Month 3** | CK normal, PFTs improving, swallow normal | Discharge with oral meds; wean steroids, taper immunosuppression |
| **Month 6–12** | Full muscular strength, no dysphagia, normal CK | Long‑term maintenance (mycophenolate ± IVIg, occasional steroid burst) |

**Take‑home points**

- **Early recognition** of dysphagia in myositis is crucial; a high CK or major antibody (e.g., anti‑SAE1) should prompt a swallow study even if oral intake seems fine.  
- **Enteral feeding** is not a last resort; in severe cases, an NG tube for 4–8 weeks can prevent aspiration pneumonia and give the immune system time to recover.  
- **Weaning decisions** should be guided by a combination of clinical exam, serial CK, and objective swallow testing.  
- **Pulmonary function testing** (PFTs) at baseline and every 3–6 months is valuable for detecting early ILD and for prognostication.  

---

## Serial CK: The “Neurospecific” Biomarker  

1. **Baseline CK** – Dramatically elevated in active myositis; in our case >16 000 U/L.  
2. **Weekly in acute phase** – Rapid drop (often >50 % by day 7) is a good sign of treatment response.  
3. **Post‑taper CK** – Levels should remain <4× upper limit of normal; a sudden rise may herald relapse.  
4. **Long‑term** – CK can be normal while ILD progresses; therefore CK alone is insufficient for monitoring.  

**Practical advice:**  
- For type I muscle fiber disease (like dermatomyositis), CK correlates closely with clinical activity.  
- If CK declines but patient continues to have dysphagia or weakness, consider imaging or repeat biopsy.  
- In patients on mycophenolate with prednisone taper, keep CBC and CMP in mind as mycophenolate can mask CK trends.

---

## Pulmonary Function Tests: Early Detection of ILD  

- **Baseline**: Exertional hypoxia and reduced DLCO are ominous. In this case, a restrictive pattern without significant bronchodilator response was noted.  
- **Follow‑up**: Repeat FVC, DLCO every 3–6 months for the first year, then annually if stable.  
- **Abnormal Trends**: FVC ↓ ≥ 10 % or DLCO ↓ ≥ 15 % should prompt high‑dose steroids or escalation to rituximab/IVIg.  
- **Radiological correlation**: PET or high‑resolution CT can pick up subclinical fibrosis before PFTs change.

**Take‑home points**

- Dermatomyositis patients with dysphagia are at higher risk for aspiration‑related lung injury; early PFT surveillance can differentiate ILD from aspiration.  
- A stable or improving PFT trajectory correlates with a better likelihood of long‑term remission.

## From “Dysphagia‑Risk” to “Dysphagia‑Free”  

| Milestone | Intervention | Clinical Reasoning |
|-----------|--------------|--------------------|
| **Day 1–7** | High‑dose IV methylprednisolone (1 g × 3 days) | Rapid suppression of immune-mediated muscle inflammation |
| **Day 5–10** | IVIg 2 g/kg over 5 days | Modulates immune complexes, complements membrane attack complex deposition |
| **Week 2** | Initiate mycophenolate 1 g BID | Steroid‑sparing; effective for chronic disease control |
| **Week 4** | First rituximab 1000 mg IV | Target CD20+ B‑cells; persistent high ANA titers appease relapse risk |
| **Month 2** | Repeat swallow study | Objective assessment for safe oral intake |
| **Month 3** | Taper prednisolone to 5 mg daily | Maintain disease remission; reduce steroid‑related morbidity |
| **Month 6–12** | Transition to monthly IVIg (if needed) | Maintain remission in high‑risk anti‑SAE1 patients |
| **Month 12** | Evaluate for reversal of ILD (HRCT, DLCO) | Confirm durability of pulmonary improvement |

---

## Counseling Patients: What to Expect

1. **Initial Severity** – “You may need a feeding tube for a few months; this is common and not a sign of permanent disability.”  
2. **Time to Full Recovery** – “In most anti‑SAE1 cases, CT, CK and PFTs normalize within 6–12 months.”  
3. **Long‑Term Monitoring** – “We’ll do annual CT chest scans for 3 years and repeat GI endoscopies if symptoms arise.”  
4. **Risks of Recurrence** – “A sudden rise in CK or new weakness warrants immediate evaluation.”  
5. **Lifestyle Modifications** – “Maintain a balanced diet, avoid hot or spicy foods that may trigger muscle pain, and adhere to the medication schedule.”

---

## Key Clinical Pearls  

- **Always order CK in patients with unexplained neck swelling or dysphagia; a high CK changes the entire differential.**  
- **Neck pseudo‑angioedema in dermatomyositis is usually muscle‑based, not histamine or bradykinin‑mediated; treat accordingly.**  
- **Combine immunosuppression with IVIg early when dysphagia is severe; delays correlate with longer NG tube dependence.**  
- **Serial PFTs and high‑resolution CT are vital for catching ILD early, especially in anti‑SAE1 disease which has a ~43 % ILD rate.**  
- **A robust multidisciplinary approach (ENT, rheumatology, pulmonology, nutrition) drives optimal outcomes.**  

---

## Bottom Line  

From a terrifying triad of neck swelling, dysphagia and extreme CK elevation to a full resolution at 12 months, this patient’s journey underscores the importance of high‑index suspicion, aggressive early therapy, vigilant monitoring (CK, PFTs, imaging), and patient‑centred counseling. In dermatomyositis, the airway can be compromised, but with timely diagnosis and a structured treatment algorithm, the prognosis is favorable—even in the most severe phenotypes.
