Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Morning Report] Discuss the potential reasons for the patient’s initial misdiagnosis as Ludwig’s angina and how early recognition of dermatomyositis could alter emergency department management.

**Key Learning Points**

1. **Why Ludwig’s angina was initially favored**
   - *Classic red flag*: **severe, progressive neck swelling** with a CT showing diffuse strap‑muscle oedema and retropharyngeal involvement.
   - *Demographics*: a previously well adult male with no known infection risk factors.
   - *Overlap of signs*: neck oedema, mild fever‑like inflammatory markers (CRP 55 mg/L, WCC 9 × 10⁹/L), and no obvious skin findings early on.
   - *Absence of obvious muscle weakness*: dysphagia and proximal weakness were subtle at presentation, leading clinicians to dismiss a myopathic process.

2. **How dermatomyositis can masquerade as angioedema**
   - **Non‑histaminergic/ non‑bradykinin oedema** from cytokine‑mediated vascular leakage secondary to inflammatory myositis.
   - **Epidemiology**: only ~6–8 % of dermatomyositis patients develop pseudoweedting; neck swellings are even rarer (<1 %).
   - **Clinical clues missed**: subtle peri‑ungual erythema/ microhaemorrhages, mild facial erythema, and early skin changes (e.g., a positive Holster sign) that were not the classic heliotrope or Gottron papules at presentation.

3. **Early recognition would have altered ED management**
   - **Prompt CK and muscle enzyme panel**: CK 16 184 U/L would have shifted suspicion toward a myopathy rather than an infection.
   - **Avoidance of unnecessary antibiotics/ imaging**: Once myopathy is suspected, CT neck may be de‑emphasised, and a muscle biopsy or MRI/MIBI‑PET could be targeted sooner.
   - **Early high‑dose steroids**: Initiating methylprednisolone within 24 h could prevent progression of muscle inflammation, airway compromise, and dysphagia.
   - **Avoidance of angioedema therapies**: Avoid unnecessary epinephrine, antihistamines, or C1‑esterase inhibitors, which are ineffective and could delay appropriate treatment.

4. **Take‑home teaching for the ED**
   - **Screen for myositis in any acute neck swelling** that is:
     - **Non‑responding to antibiotics**,
     - **Lacks classic infectious signs** (fever, purulent discharge),
     - **Has associated proximal muscle weakness or dysphagia**.
   - **Order CK, AST/ALT, LDH, and ANA** early, even if cutaneous signs are subtle.
   - **Do a thorough skin and nail exam**: peri‑ungual telangiectasias, microhaemorrhages, and the “Holster” or “shawl” signs can be first clues.
   - **Consider a rapid muscle biopsy** if imaging suggests myositis (FDG‑PET or MRI showing diffuse uptake) and CK is markedly elevated.

5. **Bottom line** – Dermatomyositis can present with pseudo‑angioedema of the neck; early recognition by a systematic skin/muscle work‑up changes the therapeutic trajectory from broad antibiotics/Biopsy to aggressive immunosuppression, ultimately improving airway safety, decreasing ICU admissions, and accelerating recovery.
