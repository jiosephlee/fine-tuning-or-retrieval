Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Sepsis Workup

The case begins with a previously healthy woman in her late 40s who presented to the emergency department in rapidly declining clinical condition. Four days before admission, she had been bitten by her dog on the upper lip. She received tetanus prophylaxis and was prescribed phenoxymethylpenicillin on the day of the incident, but she never collected the prescription and thus never started antibiotic treatment. This detail is clinically important because bite wounds—particularly those involving the highly vascularized tissues of the face—carry a significant risk of invasive bacterial infection, and timely prophylactic antibiotics are recommended for high-risk bite wounds. The four-day interval between the bite and presentation is consistent with the incubation period of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, which typically causes clinical illness two to five days after inoculation but can take longer.

Her presenting symptoms reflected rapidly progressive systemic illness. She developed a high fever exceeding 40°C, malaise, and reduced oral intake, and she sought medical attention only when numbness and discoloration of her feet appeared. At a primary-care emergency facility, she was found to be hypoglycemic—refractory to both oral glucose and glucagon, with a nadir of 0.6 mmol/L (normal range generally 3.5–5.5 mmol/L)—which prompted urgent transfer to the tertiary emergency department. Hypoglycemia of this severity in the setting of sepsis reflects depleted glycogen stores, impaired gluconeogenesis, and the hypermetabolic catabolism characteristic of overwhelming infection, and it is a marker of physiologic decompensation. On arrival, she was alert and responsive but appeared overtly septic, with mottled and cool lower extremities indicating poor peripheral perfusion. She had no exposure to herbal remedies or unusual substances, and no contacts were ill, arguing against toxin-mediated or infectious contact-related etiologies.

The initial laboratory workup revealed multisystem derangement. Blood gas analysis demonstrated metabolic acidosis with a pH of 7.27 and hyperlactatemia at 5.7 mmol/L, findings consistent with tissue hypoperfusion and anaerobic metabolism in septic shock. She had pancytopenia affecting all three cell lines, acute kidney injury, liver enzyme elevation, and coagulopathy. Urinalysis showed proteinuria (2+) and hematuria (3+), raising early concern for renal parenchymal involvement. The constellation of fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy, acute kidney injury, and lactic acidosis was interpreted as disseminated intravascular coagulation secondary to bacteremia and early sepsis. Her qSOFA score was 1 out of 3, though it should be noted that qSOFA is a bedside screening tool with limited sensitivity and should not be used in isolation to rule out sepsis, particularly when the clinical picture is this dramatic. A surgical cause of the mottled extremities, including necrotizing fasciitis, was specifically excluded through clinical and radiological evaluation—an important step because Capnocytophaga infection has been reported to cause necrotizing soft tissue infections that require surgical intervention.

Based on the bite history and the severity of presentation, *C. canimorsus* was suspected from the outset. This gram-negative, encapsulated rod is a slow-growing, fastidious organism found in the normal oropharyngeal flora of dogs and cats, and human infections, though rare, can range from localized wound infections to fulminant sepsis with mortality approaching 30%. Because of its fastidious nature, blood cultures can take up to a week or more to become positive, which creates a diagnostic delay that can be clinically dangerous. Accordingly, empiric broad-spectrum intravenous piperacillin-tazobactam was started immediately to cover dog-associated pathogens including *Capnocytophaga* species. This empiric choice is notable: while the originally prescribed phenoxymethylpenicillin has activity against *Capnocytophaga*, the severity of this patient's presentation and the need for broader gram-negative and anaerobic coverage justified escalation to a beta-lactam/beta-lactamase inhibitor. The suspicion was confirmed seven days later when both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures grew *C. canimorsus*.

Given the severity of pancytopenia and coagulopathy, hematology was consulted early, and a broad differential diagnosis was considered, including acute leukemia and hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH). Hemolytic uremic syndrome was also discussed but was initially felt to be unlikely because of the leukopenia, the marked inflammatory response, a normal haptoglobin level, and non-compensatory reticulocyte counts. Flow cytometry showed no increase in CD34+ blasts or abnormal phenotypes, and the planned bone marrow biopsy was cancelled. While these results were pending, the patient received supportive transfusions of platelets and fibrinogen.

After transfer to the intensive care unit, she became completely anuric with rapidly worsening renal function. Aggressive fluid resuscitation and loop diuretics were attempted but failed to restore urine output. She then developed septic cardiomyopathy with fluid overload, necessitating continuous renal replacement therapy. Autoimmune testing—including antinuclear antibodies and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies—was negative, helping to exclude lupus- or vasculitis-associated kidney injury. On day 3 in the ICU, she developed diffuse skin bleeding with ecchymoses and distal cyanotic and ischemic changes affecting several toes and fingers, though pulses and sensation were preserved. These findings were consistent with microthrombotic phenomena in the context of DIC. Notably, she never required vasopressor support beyond stress-dose corticosteroids administered during the initial septic phase, which is somewhat remarkable given the severity of her presentation and suggests that her hemodynamic compromise, while significant, was managed effectively with supportive measures.

This initial phase of the illness established the clinical framework within which the later diagnostic pivot would occur. The sepsis picture was dominated by *C. canimorsus* bacteremia, DIC, and multiorgan failure, and the patient appeared to be improving with antibiotics and supportive care. The subsequent development of isolated hemolysis and persistent thrombocytopenia—after the sepsis and DIC had begun to resolve—would prove to be the critical clue that a secondary, distinct process was underway, ultimately leading to the recognition of complement-mediated HUS.

## Capnocytophaga canimorsus: Pathogen Biology and Infection Spectrum

*Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is a slow-growing, encapsulated, gram-negative rod that resides as part of the normal oropharyngeal flora in dogs and cats. It is the *Capnocytophaga* species most frequently associated with invasive zoonotic disease in humans. Transmission to humans occurs through bites, scratches, or even close animal contact, meaning that a thorough exposure history is essential when evaluating patients presenting with unexplained sepsis or pancytopenia. In this case, the patient was bitten on the upper lip by her own dog four days before presenting with fulminant illness, and this history immediately raised clinical suspicion for *C. canimorsus* infection, guiding the choice of empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics.

The clinical spectrum of *C. canimorsus* infection in humans is remarkably wide, ranging from isolated meningitis to fulminant sepsis with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), multiorgan failure, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). This variability means that the pathogen may not be immediately suspected in patients who lack the classic constellation of bite-related cellulitis. In the present case, the patient presented with fever exceeding 40°C, pancytopenia, coagulopathy, lactic acidosis, and rapidly progressive acute kidney injury—features that were initially interpreted as DIC secondary to bacteraemia. The subsequent development of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia and persistent thrombocytopenia after the sepsis phase began to resolve ultimately pointed toward a superimposed TMA, illustrating how *C. canimorsus* can produce both direct septic complications and downstream complement-mediated endothelial injury.

Diagnosis of *C. canimorsus* infection is frequently delayed because the organism is fastidious and grows slowly in culture. In this case, blood cultures confirmed the organism seven days after admission, even though clinical suspicion was present from the outset. This delay is a well-recognized challenge and has prompted increasing use of molecular methods such as real-time nanopore sequencing, which can identify the pathogen more rapidly in centers where such technology is available. The slow growth and specialized culture requirements underscore why empiric antibiotic coverage for dog-associated pathogens must not await microbiological confirmation in severely ill patients.

Despite its broad susceptibility to antibiotics—including penicillins, cephalosporins, and carbapenems—mortality from invasive *C. canimorsus* infection can approach 30%. This paradox of broad susceptibility yet high mortality reflects the organism's ability to cause rapid, overwhelming disease before antibiotics can take effect, particularly in patients who present late or with advanced multiorgan dysfunction. In the present case, the patient had been prescribed phenoxymethylpenicillin after the dog bite but never initiated treatment because she could not collect her prescription, and she presented four days later in septic shock with profound metabolic derangement. This sequence highlights the critical importance of early antibiotic prophylaxis after animal bites, particularly when the bite involves the face or other high-risk areas, and the potentially devastating consequences of even short delays in treatment.

From a clinical reasoning perspective, the dog bite history serves as an essential diagnostic clue that should actively shape empiric management. In patients presenting with sepsis of unclear etiology, a recent animal bite or close canine exposure should prompt consideration of *C. canimorsus* and other Pasteurellaceae, guiding the selection of broad-spectrum antibiotics such as piperacillin-tazobactam that provide reliable coverage while culture results are pending. In this case, the treating team suspected *C. canimorsus* from admission, initiated piperacillin-tazobactam, and later de-escalated to ampicillin and then oral phenoxymethylpenicillin once sensitivities were confirmed. This structured approach to empiric coverage, culture confirmation, and de-escalation demonstrates how awareness of the pathogen's biology and transmission patterns directly informs clinical decision-making in time-critical illness.

## Clinical Evolution: From Resolving Sepsis to Thrombotic Microangiopathy

### ICU Course: Anuria, Septic Cardiomyopathy, and Renal Replacement Therapy

After admission to the intensive care unit, the patient's renal function deteriorated rapidly. Despite initially maintaining adequate diuresis on arrival, she became completely anuric—a concerning sign indicating that acute kidney injury had progressed to a severity requiring renal replacement therapy. Aggressive fluid resuscitation and loop diuretics were attempted but failed to restore urine output, underscoring that the kidney injury was not simply prerenal physiology but represented intrinsic, likely structural, damage from the septic process and microcirculatory insult.

The clinical picture was further complicated by the development of septic cardiomyopathy, a well-recognized phenomenon of sepsis-induced myocardial dysfunction characterized by biventricular dilatation, reduced ejection fraction, and impaired contractility. This reversible form of cardiomyopathy is mediated by inflammatory cytokines, nitric oxide, and mitochondrial dysfunction rather than by coronary ischemia. In this patient, septic cardiomyopathy contributed to fluid overload, making the already anuric state more clinically dangerous and hastening the need for continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT). CRRT, as opposed to intermittent hemodialysis, is generally preferred in hemodynamically unstable ICU patients because it provides gradual solute and fluid removal, minimizing shifts in intravascular volume and osmolarity.

It is worth noting that despite multiorgan involvement, the patient never required vasopressor support beyond stress-dose corticosteroids. This is somewhat atypical for septic shock but may reflect early and aggressive supportive care, as well as the particular pathophysiology of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* infection, which can cause severe endothelial activation and microvascular compromise without the classic distributive shock physiology seen in other gram-negative bacteremias.

### Microthrombotic Phenomena: A Clue to Endothelial Injury

On day 3 in the ICU, the patient developed diffuse skin bleeding with ecchymoses, along with distal cyanotic and ischemic changes affecting several toes and fingers. Pulses and sensation were preserved, which is an important clinical distinction: the ischemia was not due to large-vessel occlusion but rather to microvascular thrombosis at the level of the capillaries and arterioles supplying the digital circulation.

These findings were initially interpreted as microthrombotic phenomena in the context of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), and this interpretation was reasonable at that stage. DIC, driven by tissue factor release and cytokine-mediated activation of coagulation, produces widespread microthrombi alongside consumption of platelets and clotting factors. However, in retrospect, these microthrombotic changes are also characteristic of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), a condition defined by endothelial injury and microvascular platelet-rich thrombi. The clinical and histologic overlap between DIC and TMA is substantial, and distinguishing the two on clinical grounds alone can be extremely difficult, particularly early in the course when both may coexist.

For the student or resident, the key teaching point is that digital ischemia in a septic patient with thrombocytopenia and coagulopathy should raise concern for both DIC and TMA. While DIC typically improves as the underlying infection is controlled, persistent or worsening microthrombotic phenomena—particularly when accompanied by ongoing hemolysis—should prompt reconsideration of TMA as a separate or superimposed process.

### Transfer to the Nephrology Ward: De-escalation and Initial Recovery

After 8 days of hospitalization, the patient was transferred to the nephrology ward for ongoing dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury and continued intravenous antibiotic therapy. At this point, she was clinically stable but anuric, with generalized edema and multiple hematomas, yet alert and oriented. The transfer marked a transition from the acute resuscitative phase to a more protracted phase of supportive care and diagnostic evaluation.

Antibiotic therapy was de-escalated based on blood culture results: from broad-spectrum piperacillin/tazobactam to intravenous ampicillin and subsequently to oral phenoxymethylpenicillin. This de-escalation reflects sound antimicrobial stewardship—narrowing coverage once the causative organism and its susceptibility profile are known. *C. canimorsus* is generally susceptible to penicillins, and the transition to oral therapy indicated that the bacteremia was being adequately treated.

On the nephrology ward, laboratory trends were initially encouraging. The inflammatory profile improved, leukocytes showed a reactive rebound from the earlier leukopenia, platelets were recovering, hemoglobin was stable, and coagulation parameters normalized. These improvements indicated that the septic phase and associated DIC were resolving. Kidney function tests, however, showed persistent azotemia, reflecting the severe acute tubular injury that had occurred and the ongoing need for dialysis. The normalization of coagulation parameters was particularly important, as it would later help distinguish residual or recurrent pathology from a new process.

### The Diagnostic Turning Point: Sudden Isolated Hemoglobin Drop

The critical diagnostic turning point occurred after this initial improvement. The patient developed a sudden, isolated drop in hemoglobin from 111 to 68 g/L over 6 days, without any evidence of bleeding or hemodynamic instability. This pattern—anemia developing in the absence of overt blood loss—immediately raised the question of hemolysis as the underlying mechanism.

For the clinician, a falling hemoglobin without a clear bleeding source should always prompt evaluation for hemolytic anemia. The approach begins with asking whether the anemia is due to destruction (hemolysis) or underproduction, and if hemolysis is suspected, whether it is immune-mediated or non-immune. The speed of the drop—43 g/L in 6 days—is consistent with an acute hemolytic process rather than marrow suppression or iron deficiency. In the context of a patient recovering from sepsis with known microthrombotic complications, this finding was a sentinel event that demanded a structured diagnostic workup.

### Laboratory Evidence of Hemolysis: Assembling the Picture

The laboratory workup confirmed ongoing hemolysis. Haptoglobin became undetectable, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels rose. Haptoglobin is a plasma protein that binds free hemoglobin released during intravascular hemolysis; because the haptoglobin-hemoglobin complex is rapidly cleared by the liver, haptoglobin levels fall precipitously and serve as a sensitive marker of intravascular hemolysis. LDH, particularly the LDH-2 isoenzyme, is released from lysed erythrocytes and further supports the hemolytic process. The combination of low haptoglobin and elevated LDH is the classic biochemical signature of intravascular hemolysis.

Critically, platelets remained persistently low (30–40 × 10⁹/L) despite the earlier recovery trend. This persistence of thrombocytopenia alongside hemolysis is a crucial observation because it recapitulates the classic triad of TMA: microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and organ injury (in this case, acute kidney injury). The concurrent presence of these three elements, particularly after the coagulopathy of DIC had resolved, strongly suggested that a TMA process was now driving the hematologic derangement rather than residual sepsis-related consumption.

### The False-Positive Direct Antiglobulin Test: A Diagnostic Detour

The initial workup took a brief detour when the direct antiglobulin test (DAT, or Coombs test) returned positive for IgG, raising suspicion for autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA). AIHA is caused by antibodies directed against the patient's own red blood cells, leading to extravascular hemolysis via splenic macrophage destruction of antibody-coated erythrocytes. Based on this result, high-dose prednisolone was started, and several blood transfusions were administered.

However, the hemolytic process failed to respond to corticosteroid therapy, and platelets remained low—a pattern atypical for AIHA, which generally does not cause significant thrombocytopenia unless Evans syndrome (AIHA coexisting with immune thrombocytopenia) is present. This lack of response prompted re-evaluation.

The initially positive DAT was likely a false positive. False-positive DAT results can occur in several settings: recent blood transfusions can lead to passive antibody transfer or alloantibody formation, and certain drugs—including penicillin—can bind to red blood cell surfaces and act as haptens, inducing antibody binding that is detected on the DAT without causing true autoimmune hemolysis. In this patient, both prior transfusions and penicillin exposure were present, providing plausible explanations for the false-positive result. Repeat DAT testing was negative, confirming that the initial result did not represent true immune-mediated hemolysis.

This detour illustrates an important clinical lesson: a single positive test, particularly one with known false-positive triggers, should be interpreted in the full clinical context. When the response to therapy does not match the suspected diagnosis, the diagnosis should be questioned rather than the treatment intensified.

### Peripheral Blood Smear: Schistocytes and the Recognition of TMA

The pivotal diagnostic finding came from the peripheral blood smear, which revealed 15%–20% schistocytes. Schistocytes are fragmented red blood cells produced when erythrocytes are mechanically sheared as they traverse microvessels obstructed by platelet-rich thrombi. This process—mechanical fragmentation within the microcirculation—is the defining mechanism of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA) and is the hematologic hallmark of TMA.

The magnitude of schistocytosis matters. Small numbers of schistocytes (less than 1%) can be seen in various conditions, but a level of 15%–20% is substantial and strongly supports the diagnosis of MAHA. In the context of this patient—persistent thrombocytopenia, acute kidney injury, and now confirmed MAHA—the constellation of findings was diagnostic of TMA. It is important to recognize that schistocytes are not specific to a particular etiology of TMA; they are seen in thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), Shiga toxin–associated HUS (STEC-HUS), complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS), and secondary TMAs associated with malignancy, pregnancy, or autoimmune disease. The presence of schistocytes thus established the syndrome of TMA but did not, by itself, identify the underlying cause.

For the learner, the key sequence in this case is instructive: a patient recovering from sepsis and DIC developed new, unexplained hemolysis and persistent thrombocytopenia after coagulopathy had resolved. The initial suspicion of immune-mediated hemolysis was disproven by the steroid-unresponsive course, the negative repeat DAT, and—most importantly—the finding of schistocytes on peripheral smear. This sequence shifted the diagnostic framework from autoimmune hemolysis to microangiopathic hemolysis and established TMA as the dominant process, setting the stage for the subsequent workup that would distinguish among TTP, STEC-HUS, and CM-HUS.

## Differential Diagnosis of TMA: A Systematic Diagnostic Approach

Thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) is not a single disease but an umbrella syndrome defined by a shared clinicopathological triad: microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and organ damage resulting from microvascular thrombus formation. The MAHA component reflects mechanical fragmentation of erythrocytes as they traverse fibrin-rich microthrombi, producing schistocytes on peripheral blood smear, elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), undetectable haptoglobin, and a negative direct antiglobulin test. Thrombocytopenia reflects platelet consumption at sites of endothelial injury. Organ involvement—most commonly the kidney, but also the brain, gut, and heart—depends on the territory of microvascular occlusion. Because several distinct entities produce this identical triad through different mechanisms, the diagnostic task is not simply to recognise TMA but to determine which TMA syndrome is operating, as the treatments diverge fundamentally: plasma exchange is lifesaving in thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), whereas complement inhibition with eculizumab is the standard of care for complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS).

In this patient, the initial presentation was dominated by sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), and pancytopenia, which obscured the TMA. It was only after the septic picture began to improve that a sudden isolated haemoglobin drop (from 111 to 68 g/L over 6 days), persistent thrombocytopenia (30–40 × 10⁹/L), undetectable haptoglobin, and rising LDH signalled ongoing haemolysis. A peripheral smear showing 15%–20% schistocytes confirmed MAHA and shifted the diagnostic focus toward a TMA syndrome superimposed on the resolving septic episode.

The first diagnostic priority in any adult presenting with MAHA and thrombocytopenia is to exclude TTP, because untreated TTP carries a mortality exceeding 90% but responds dramatically to plasma exchange. TTP arises from a severe deficiency of ADAMTS13, the metalloprotease that cleaves ultra-large von Willebrand factor multimers. When ADAMTS13 activity falls below 10%—typically due to an acquired autoantibody or, less commonly, an inherited deficiency—ultra-large multimers accumulate on endothelial surfaces, causing uncontrolled platelet adhesion and microvascular thrombosis. In this patient, the PLASMIC score—a validated clinical prediction tool incorporating platelet count, haemolysis markers, absence of active cancer or transplant, mean corpuscular volume, INR, and creatinine—was 4/7, indicating a low pretest probability of TTP. The definitive result came with ADAMTS13 activity at 43.5%, well above the 10% threshold required for a TTP diagnosis. This effectively excluded TTP and obviated the need to continue plasma exchange as a disease-specific therapy, although it was continued briefly as a bridge to eculizumab.

Shiga toxin-producing Escherichia coli-associated HUS (STEC-HUS) was the next entity to exclude. STEC-HUS is the most common form of HUS in children and is classically preceded by a prodromal episode of bloody diarrhoea caused by Shiga toxin–producing organisms such as E. coli O157:H7. The toxin binds to and damages glomerular endothelial cells, triggering a thrombotic cascade. In adults, the absence of a preceding enteric illness provides an important clinical clue, but microbiological confirmation is essential. In this patient, there was no history of diarrhoeal illness, and stool PCR analysis for Shiga toxin was negative, excluding STEC-HUS.

With TTP and STEC-HUS excluded, attention turned to complement-mediated HUS. CM-HUS—historically termed atypical HUS—arises from dysregulated activation of the alternative complement pathway on endothelial surfaces, leading to uncontrolled C3 convertase activity, terminal complement complex (C5b-9) deposition, endothelial injury, and microvascular thrombosis. A critical diagnostic principle is that CM-HUS is a clinicopathological diagnosis of exclusion: there is no single confirmatory laboratory test. Standard complement assays (C3 and C4) are frequently normal during the disease course, and normal levels do not exclude the diagnosis. In this patient, locally available C3 and C4 were within reference ranges, and functional assays of the classical, alternative, and lectin pathways were also normal. These results, while not supporting CM-HUS, did not exclude it. The diagnosis ultimately rested on the constellation of persistent haemolysis, schistocytosis, dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury, and renal biopsy findings consistent with TMA—after TTP and STEC-HUS had been excluded.

Renal biopsy provided the histopathological confirmation. Light microscopy demonstrated severe acute tubular necrosis, ischaemic glomeruli containing fibrin thrombi, and scattered crescent formation. These findings are characteristic of TMA: fibrin thrombi occlude glomerular capillaries and arterioles, producing ischaemic injury to both glomeruli and tubulointerstitium. Immunofluorescence showed no immune complex deposition, and electron microscopy revealed no evidence of a primary glomerulopathy, excluding alternative renal diagnoses. Importantly, the biopsy was performed only after the platelet count had risen to 77 × 10⁹/L, because performing an invasive procedure in the setting of severe thrombocytopenia would carry an unacceptable bleeding risk. The biopsy findings, combined with the exclusion of TTP and STEC-HUS and the rapid clinical improvement following eculizumab, solidified the diagnosis of CM-HUS.

Extended complement testing was sent to a reference laboratory to investigate for hereditary complement-mediated TMA. This testing included a quantitative alternative pathway haemolytic assay (AH50), which was markedly reduced at 6%. However, the interpretation of this result is complicated by the timing of the sample. The extended complement panel was obtained after three sessions of plasma exchange with Octaplas had been completed and after one induction dose of eculizumab had been administered. Eculizumab blocks terminal complement activation (C5), and plasma exchange alters circulating complement component concentrations. Therefore, the reduced AH50 most likely reflects a treatment effect rather than intrinsic complement depletion or dysregulation. Complement factors H, I, and B were normal, anti-factor H antibodies were negative, and extended genetic testing did not identify any pathogenic variants. This timing issue is clinically important: to obtain meaningful baseline complement data, samples ideally should be drawn before the initiation of plasma exchange or complement-blocking therapy. In practice, however, the urgency of treating a critically ill patient with suspected CM-HUS often precludes waiting for results, and clinicians must interpret complement assays in the context of concurrent interventions.

This case illustrates that the differential diagnosis of TMA requires a structured, stepwise approach: recognise the triad of MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and organ damage; exclude TTP using the PLASMIC score and ADAMTS13 activity; exclude STEC-HUS through stool PCR and clinical history; and consider CM-HUS as a diagnosis of exclusion, supported by biopsy evidence of TMA and uninhibited by normal C3/C4 levels. The case also demonstrates that in the real-world setting of acutely ill patients, diagnostic and therapeutic decisions frequently proceed in parallel, and the clinician must interpret laboratory data with an understanding of how ongoing treatments—particularly plasma exchange and complement inhibition—may alter the very assays being used to establish the diagnosis.

## Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: Complement Dysregulation and Infectious Triggers

Complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS), historically termed "atypical HUS," arises from uncontrolled activation of the alternative complement pathway. To understand how this pathway becomes dysregulated, it helps to first recall its normal physiology. The alternative pathway is constitutively active at a low level through spontaneous hydrolysis of C3, generating a fluid-phase C3 convertase (C3bBb) that amplifies complement activation on any surface lacking adequate regulatory protection. Under normal conditions, this tickover is tightly controlled by a group of regulatory proteins that protect host cells from self-destruction. Factor H is the major soluble regulator: it competes with factor B for binding to C3b, accelerates decay of the C3 convertase, and serves as a cofactor for factor I, a serine protease that cleaves and inactivates C3b. Factor B, in contrast, is a positive regulator—it stabilizes the C3 and C5 convertases and promotes amplification. Membrane cofactor protein (CD46), expressed on the surface of most nucleated cells, acts as a cofactor for factor I–mediated cleavage of C3b and C4b. Together, these proteins ensure that complement activation remains focused on foreign surfaces such as microbial membranes while host endothelium is spared. When any of these regulatory components are defective—whether through loss of function in an inhibitor such as factor H or gain of function in an activator such as factor B—the alternative pathway escapes control, generating sustained C5 convertase activity, terminal complement complex formation (C5b-9, the membrane attack complex), and downstream endothelial injury that manifests as thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

In approximately 50% to 70% of CM-HUS patients, this regulatory failure can be traced to identifiable genetic variants or autoantibodies affecting one or more of these complement proteins. Loss-of-function mutations in the genes encoding factor H (*CFH*), factor I (*CFI*), or CD46 (*MCP*), and gain-of-function mutations in factor B (*CFB*) or C3, are among the most commonly recognized. Autoantibodies against factor H, often occurring in children, can also disable regulation. In the patient described in this case, extended complement genetic testing was sent to a reference laboratory and did not identify any pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants, an important negative result that distinguishes her from the majority of CM-HUS patients with a demonstrable molecular predisposition.

A critical concept for understanding why CM-HUS does not develop in all carriers of complement mutations is incomplete disease penetrance. Even among family members sharing the same pathogenic variant, clinical expression is variable, and penetrance is estimated at approximately 50% or less for many mutations. This observation led to the "two-hit" model of disease: a genetic or autoantibody-mediated predisposition constitutes the first hit, rendering the complement system vulnerable, but a second hit—an environmental stressor that acutely activates complement—is typically required to precipitate a clinically apparent episode. The most commonly recognized second hits are infections, but pregnancy, autoimmune disease, and certain medications can serve the same role. In this framework, the baseline state is one of compensated complement dysregulation, and the trigger tips the balance toward uncontrolled activation and endothelial damage.

Infections are particularly effective triggers because they directly engage the complement system. Bacterial surfaces activate the alternative and lectin pathways, generating C3b deposition and amplification loop activity. In a host with normal complement regulation, this response is self-limited and contributes to pathogen clearance. In a host with marginal regulatory capacity—whether due to a subtle genetic variant, an autoantibody, or even transient endothelial vulnerability—the surge in complement activation can overwhelm the remaining regulatory reserve, resulting in sustained C5 activation, membrane attack complex deposition on endothelial cells, and the characteristic TMA lesion. The temporal pattern in this case fits this model: the patient's haemolysis and worsening thrombocytopenia emerged only after the initial septic phase had begun to improve, suggesting that the infection triggered a self-sustaining complement-mediated process that persisted even as the bacteraemia was cleared.

*C. canimorsus* occupies a particularly interesting niche in this pathophysiological framework. This gram-negative rod displays variable susceptibility to complement-mediated killing in serum. Many isolates are complement-sensitive and are readily cleared, but strains expressing a polysaccharide capsule show partial resistance, meaning that terminal complement components contribute meaningfully to host defence against this organism. This creates a clinical paradox that is central to understanding this case. On one hand, complement activation is necessary for clearing *C. canimorsus*; on the other hand, the same activation may, in a susceptible host, trigger uncontrolled endothelial damage and TMA. The clinical implication is that the infection simultaneously requires and potentially weaponizes the complement system. A previously reported case by Bjørkto and colleagues illustrates this paradigm: a patient with *C. canimorsus*-induced CM-HUS was found to harbour a complement factor B variant, providing a clear genetic first hit and fitting neatly into the two-hit model. That patient was successfully treated with eculizumab, supporting the role of terminal complement activation in the disease mechanism.

The present case is notable because it contrasts with that report in an instructive way. Despite extended genetic testing, no pathogenic or likely pathogenic complement gene variants were identified in this patient, and anti–factor H antibodies were negative. Her medical history was unremarkable, with no prior episodes suggesting an underlying complement disorder. These findings weaken the hypothesis of a pre-existing inherited complement defect requiring a second hit and instead support a model in which the *C. canimorsus* infection itself was sufficient to trigger CM-HUS, even without a demonstrable genetic predisposition. This does not exclude the possibility of a subtle or as-yet undetected regulatory vulnerability—current genetic panels do not capture all relevant variants, and epigenetic or nongenetic factors may also modulate complement regulation—but it does broaden the conceptual framework. CM-HUS may, in some cases, represent an infection-driven phenomenon in which the intensity and nature of the complement stimulus, rather than an intrinsic host defect, is the primary driver. This distinction has practical implications: it underscores that a negative genetic workup does not exclude CM-HUS, and it highlights the importance of recognizing unusual infectious triggers in patients who present with new-onset TMA without an obvious family history or prior complement-related disease.

## Treatment Strategy, Clinical Dilemmas, and Outcome

The management of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) in the setting of recent or active bacteraemia presents a clinical dilemma that requires balancing the urgent need for complement blockade against the infectious consequences of disabling a key arm of innate immunity. In this case, the treatment strategy evolved in three phases: plasma exchange (PEX) as immediate bridging therapy, initiation of eculizumab once blood cultures had cleared, and long-term maintenance with ongoing infectious risk mitigation. Each phase illustrates important principles in the management of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) and complement-directed therapy.

### Plasma Exchange as Bridging Therapy

Once the diagnosis of TMA was supported by peripheral blood smear findings—15%–20% schistocytes—and ADAMTS13 activity excluded thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), plasma exchange was initiated as bridging therapy while definitive complement testing and kidney biopsy results were pending. This decision reflects an important practical principle: when TMA is confirmed but the subtype remains uncertain, PEX serves as a reasonable interim measure because it is lifesaving in TTP and may provide some benefit in CM-HUS, even though it does not address the underlying complement dysregulation.

The rationale for PEX in TTP is mechanistically sound: TTP results from severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (activity <10%), typically due to autoantibodies, and PEX both removes the inhibitory antibodies and replaces functional ADAMTS13 through donor plasma. In CM-HUS, however, the pathology lies in dysregulated alternative complement pathway activation, and PEX can only transiently reduce the burden of circulating complement fragments, inflammatory mediators, and potential autoantibodies. It does not correct the fundamental complement regulatory defect. This distinction was borne out in this patient's course: spontaneous diuresis returned after the second and third PEX sessions, suggesting a degree of temporary stabilisation, but sustained haematological and renal recovery occurred only after eculizumab was introduced. This temporal pattern underscores that PEX in CM-HUS is best viewed as a bridge to definitive complement blockade rather than a standalone therapy.

### Eculizumab: Mechanism and Dosing

Eculizumab is a humanised monoclonal antibody that binds the terminal complement component C5, preventing its cleavage into C5a and C5b. By blocking this step, eculizumab inhibits both the generation of the potent anaphylatoxin C5a and the assembly of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9), which mediates terminal complement-mediated endothelial injury. Because the alternative complement pathway amplification loop operates upstream of C5, eculizumab does not interfere with proximal complement activation or opsonisation but specifically prevents the downstream end-organ damage that drives TMA.

The standard induction regimen consists of 900 mg intravenously weekly for four weeks, followed by maintenance dosing of 1200 mg every two weeks. This patient received the full induction course, with maintenance therapy continuing at 1200 mg every two weeks for four months post-induction. The clinical response—achievement of haematological remission with normalisation of platelet counts, cessation of haemolysis, and stabilisation of haemoglobin—was consistent with the expected efficacy of terminal complement blockade in CM-HUS.

A critical therapeutic principle demonstrated by this case is that early initiation of eculizumab is one of the most important predictors of favourable outcome. Delayed initiation is associated with incomplete renal recovery and a higher risk of progression to end-stage kidney disease, likely because ongoing complement-mediated endothelial injury produces irreversible microvascular damage. In this patient, eculizumab was initiated promptly once the diagnosis was established and blood cultures were negative, contributing to her partial renal recovery.

### The Therapeutic Dilemma: Complement Blockade and Infection Risk

The central therapeutic dilemma in this case was the timing of eculizumab initiation relative to active C. canimorsus bacteraemia. Terminal complement components, particularly the membrane attack complex, are essential for defence against encapsulated organisms such as *Neisseria* species. *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* itself shows variable complement sensitivity, with capsule-expressing strains demonstrating partial resistance, but complement-mediated killing remains an important component of host defence against this organism. Initiating C5 blockade during active bacteraemia could therefore theoretically risk uncontrolled infection by impairing the patient's ability to clear the pathogen.

This concern was managed by ensuring that blood cultures were negative before eculizumab was administered. By the time the drug was initiated, the patient had received appropriate antibiotic therapy—initially broad-spectrum piperacillin/tazobactam, later de-escalated to ampicillin and then oral phenoxymethylpenicillin based on culture sensitivities—and the infection appeared to be microbiologically controlled. This sequencing illustrates a general principle: when complement blockade is necessary in the setting of recent or treated infection, confirming culture negativity and ensuring adequate antimicrobial coverage provides a reasonable safety framework.

### Vaccination and Antimicrobial Prophylaxis

Because eculizumab significantly increases the risk of meningococcal disease—with reported incidence rates approximately 1,000 to 2,000 times higher than the background population—vaccination against *Neisseria meningitidis* is mandatory before or concurrent with treatment initiation. In this case, the patient received the first doses of two meningococcal vaccines: Menveo, a quadrivalent conjugate vaccine covering serogroups A, C, Y, and W, and Bexsero, a protein-based vaccine covering serogroup B. Ideally, vaccination should be completed at least two weeks before the first dose of eculizumab to allow adequate immunogenic response, but this is not always clinically feasible when TMA is progressing and complement blockade cannot be delayed.

Because the vaccines were not administered two weeks before starting eculizumab, prophylactic phenoxymethylpenicillin was given to cover the interval during which vaccine-mediated protection had not yet developed. This is a common and recommended strategy when the urgency of eculizumab initiation precludes the ideal vaccination window. The second vaccine doses were planned on an outpatient basis eight weeks later, reflecting the standard schedule for these vaccines. Ongoing penicillin prophylaxis during complement blockade is recommended by many experts, particularly in the early period after vaccination, and this patient received appropriate coverage.

### Clinical Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient's clinical trajectory following complement blockade illustrates the staged recovery typical of CM-HUS. After the second and third PEX sessions, spontaneous diuresis returned, suggesting that some reduction in circulating complement fragments and inflammatory mediators had provided transient benefit. However, it was only after eculizumab induction that sustained haematological remission was achieved, with resolution of haemolysis and recovery of platelet counts. This sequence reinforces the concept that PEX provides temporary stabilisation, while complement blockade addresses the disease mechanism.

Renal recovery was partial. At follow-up, the patient was in good general condition with adequate urine output, but her serum creatinine remained elevated at 175 µmol/L, and she continued to be followed in the nephrology outpatient clinic. This outcome is consistent with current evidence showing that the degree of renal recovery in CM-HUS depends heavily on two factors: the baseline severity of renal injury at the time of eculizumab initiation and the time elapsed from disease onset to complement blockade. This patient had already progressed to complete anuria and dialysis dependence before treatment, indicating severe acute tubular injury, as confirmed by kidney biopsy showing acute tubular necrosis, ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi, and scattered crescents. Partial rather than complete recovery under these circumstances is expected.

The kidney biopsy findings deserve emphasis as they illustrate the histopathological substrate of CM-HUS. Light microscopy revealed fibrin thrombi within glomerular capillaries—the hallmark of TMA—together with ischaemic glomerular changes and crescent formation, reflecting severe endothelial injury. Immunofluorescence showed no immune complex deposition, and electron microscopy demonstrated no evidence of a primary glomerulopathy, excluding other causes of glomerular disease and supporting the diagnosis of complement-mediated TMA.

### Genetic Testing and Duration of Therapy

Extended complement genetic testing was performed to evaluate for hereditary complement-mediated TMA, with samples sent to a reference laboratory. Notably, sampling occurred after three PEX sessions and one dose of eculizumab, which complicates the interpretation of functional complement assays. The markedly reduced alternative pathway activity (AH50 of 6%) was most consistent with treatment effect rather than intrinsic complement deficiency, as eculizumab blocks terminal complement and PEX alters the composition of circulating complement components. This timing illustrates a practical challenge: the urgency of treatment often precludes obtaining a complete pretreatment complement workup, and results must be interpreted in the context of ongoing interventions.

No pathogenic or likely pathogenic complement gene variants were identified. This result has implications for the duration of complement blockade. In patients with identified pathogenic complement gene abnormalities, long-term or even lifelong eculizumab therapy is typically recommended with close monitoring for relapse, because the underlying genetic predisposition persists. In patients without identifiable genetic variants, as in this case, the decision to continue or discontinue therapy is more nuanced. The severity of the initial presentation and the risk of relapse must be weighed against the infectious risks and costs of ongoing complement blockade. In this patient, eculizumab was continued for four months post-induction, with planned reassessment for discontinuation if renal function stabilised. This approach reflects a reasonable strategy when no genetic predisposition is identified: treat through the acute illness, monitor for stability, and reassess the need for ongoing therapy.

### Broader Clinical Implications

This case reinforces several broader lessons for clinicians encountering TMA in the setting of infection. First, CM-HUS can be triggered by uncommon infectious agents, including *C. canimorsus*, and should be considered even in immunocompetent adults without a prior history of complement-related disease. The absence of a known complement genetic variant does not exclude infection-triggered CM-HUS, as an external trigger may activate complement dysregulation in a host without a demonstrable inherited predisposition. Second, the temporal relationship between infection and TMA onset may be delayed—this patient developed overt haemolysis only after the sepsis had begun to improve—meaning that CM-HUS can emerge or become recognisable only after the initial infectious insult is controlled. Third, normal complement levels (C3 and C4) do not exclude CM-HUS, and the diagnosis remains a clinicopathological one based on the constellation of MAHA, thrombocytopenia, AKI, supportive biopsy findings, and exclusion of alternative causes such as TTP and STEC-HUS. Finally, the favourable outcome in this case—haematological remission with partial renal recovery—highlights that early recognition, systematic differential diagnosis, and prompt initiation of complement blockade are the most important modifiable determinants of prognosis in CM-HUS.
