Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Clinical Pearls] Capnocytophaga canimorsus is a slow-growing, encapsulated gram-negative rod found in the normal oropharyngeal flora of dogs and cats. After a dog bite, what clinical presentations should raise suspicion for invasive C. canimorsus infection, and why is microbiological diagnosis often delayed?

After a dog bite, *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* infection should be suspected when a patient presents with rapidly progressive sepsis, often within 2 to 7 days of the exposure. In this case, a previously healthy woman developed high fever, malaise, refractory hypoglycemia, and mottled, cool lower extremities four days after being bitten on the upper lip. The clinical picture quickly evolved to include septic shock, pancytopenia, disseminated intravascular coagulation, lactic acidosis, liver enzyme elevation, and acute kidney injury progressing to complete anuria. Broadly, invasive *C. canimorsus* infection can range from isolated meningitis to fulminant sepsis with multiorgan failure, and mortality may approach 30% despite broad antibiotic susceptibility. As illustrated here, a history of dog bite—or even close canine contact—combined with sepsis, coagulopathy, and cytopenias should prompt early empiric coverage for *Capnocytophaga* species rather than waiting for microbiological confirmation.

Microbiological diagnosis is frequently delayed because *C. canimorsus* is fastidious and slow-growing in culture. In this patient, blood cultures required seven days before confirming bacteraemia, despite clinical suspicion from the outset. The organism's slow growth in both aerobic and anaerobic conditions, along with its morphologic overlap with other gram-negative rods, can further obscure early identification. These limitations have prompted increasing interest in molecular methods such as real-time nanopore sequencing for more rapid diagnosis. The key clinical takeaway is that when the epidemiologic history and presentation align—particularly sepsis with DIC and cytopenias following a dog bite—empiric broad-spectrum antibiotic coverage should be initiated immediately, and alternative diagnostic strategies should be considered if cultures remain unrevealing.

### [Differential Diagnosis] This patient initially presented with fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy, and lactic acidosis consistent with DIC, but later developed isolated hemolysis, persistent thrombocytopenia, and worsening AKI with schistocytes on peripheral smear. How do you clinically distinguish thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) from ongoing DIC, and what features in this case suggested a superimposed TMA rather than persistent DIC alone?

# Distinguishing Thrombotic Microangiopathy from Ongoing DIC

**DIC and TMA can coexist and share overlapping laboratory features — including schistocytes, thrombocytopenia, and elevated D-dimers — but they are pathophysiologically and clinically distinct. The key to distinguishing them lies in the temporal trajectory, the pattern of hematologic derangement, and the response to treatment of the underlying trigger.**

## Core Pathophysiologic Distinction

DIC is driven by overwhelming tissue factor activation and systemic thrombin generation, consuming **all** coagulation factors and platelets. It produces a global coagulopathy: prolonged PT and aPTT, hypofibrinogenemia, markedly elevated D-dimers, and broad multiorgan dysfunction. TMA, by contrast, is a localized endothelial injury process — most often complement-mediated — that causes platelet adhesion and microvascular thrombosis with **relative sparing of clotting factors**. In pure TMA, PT, aPTT, and fibrinogen are typically normal or near-normal.

## What This Case Showed

This patient's **initial presentation** was a textbook DIC picture in the setting of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* sepsis: pancytopenia, coagulopathy, lactic acidosis, purpuric bleeding, and microthrombotic digital ischemia — all consistent with sepsis-driven consumption coagulopathy. Importantly, haptoglobin was initially normal and reticulocytes were non-compensatory, arguing against active hemolysis at that stage.

The critical inflection point came **after sepsis began resolving**: inflammatory markers improved, leukopenia resolved into reactive leukocytosis, and **coagulation parameters normalized** — yet she developed a sudden isolated hemoglobin drop (111 → 68 g/L), undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH, and persistent thrombocytopenia (30–40 × 10⁹/L). Fifteen to twenty percent schistocytes on peripheral smear confirmed MAHA. The **dissociation between normalized coagulation studies and ongoing hemolysis-thrombocytopenia-AKI** was the pivotal finding: this constellation is not explained by persistent DIC, in which coagulopathy and factor consumption should parallel — not diverge from — the hemolytic process.

## The Diagnostic Framework Applied

The team systematically excluded other TMA etiologies:
- **TTP ruled out**: ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5% (well above the <10% threshold), and the PLASMIC score was low (4/7).
- **STEC-HUS ruled out**: No prodromal diarrhea and negative stool Shiga toxin PCR.
- **Autoimmune hemolysis ruled out**: Initial DAT positivity was a false positive (likely from penicillin or transfusions); repeat testing was negative, and hemolysis was non-responsive to corticosteroids.

The **kidney biopsy** provided definitive evidence: ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi and scattered crescents, without immune complex deposition — morphologically consistent with TMA rather than residual DIC.

## The Critical Teaching Point

The most important clinical clue was the **temporal dissociation**: hemolysis and worsening AKI emerged as coagulopathy resolved and sepsis improved. In ongoing DIC, improving sepsis should bring parallel improvement in all hematologic parameters. When consumption coagulopathy resolves but MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and renal failure persist or worsen, **a second, superimposed process must be suspected** — and complement-mediated TMA is a leading consideration. Normal C3 and C4 levels do not exclude CM-HUS, as the case report emphasizes; functional complement assays and, ultimately, response to eculizumab confirmed the diagnosis.

### [Pathophysiology] Complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS) arises from dysregulated activation of the alternative complement pathway. In this case, standard complement levels (C3, C4) and classical/lectin pathway functional assays were normal, yet the alternative pathway activity (AH50) was markedly reduced at 6%. Why can C3 and C4 be normal in CM-HUS, and what does the isolated reduction in alternative pathway activity signify?

## Clinical Teaching Answer

**Why C3 and C4 can be normal in CM-HUS:**

Complement-mediated HUS is fundamentally a disorder of *regulation* of the alternative pathway at the cell surface, not a disorder of systemic complement depletion. The alternative pathway is constitutively active through a process of "tick-over," generating low levels of C3b continuously. Under normal conditions, this is held in check by regulatory proteins—factor H, factor I, CD46 (MCP), and thrombomodulin—which ensure that C3b is inactivated and the C3 convertase (C3bBb) is degraded on host cell surfaces. In CM-HUS, loss-of-function variants in these regulators (or gain-of-function variants in C3 or factor B) allow unchecked amplification of the alternative pathway C3 convertase specifically on endothelial and glomerular surfaces. This surface-restricted dysregulation may not produce a measurable drop in circulating C3 because consumption is localized rather than systemic, and because C3 is an acute-phase reactant that may be upregulated during the inflammatory response. C4 is entirely a component of the classical and lectin pathways and is not consumed in pure alternative pathway dysregulation. As the case report emphasizes: *"normal complement levels (C3/C4) do not exclude CM-HUS, as levels may remain within reference ranges during the disease course."*

**What the isolated reduction in AH50 signifies in this case:**

The AH50 assay measures the functional integrity of the entire alternative pathway through to terminal complement activation and membrane attack complex formation. An isolated reduction in AH50 with normal classical and lectin pathway function would typically suggest a specific defect within the alternative pathway—such as factor D, factor B, properdin deficiency, or dysregulation. However, the clinical context here is critical: the extended complement testing, including AH50, was performed *after* three sessions of plasma exchange and *four days after the first dose of eculizumab*. Eculizumab blocks C5 cleavage, which is downstream of C3 convertase amplification but is essential for the terminal lytic activity that AH50 measures. Therefore, the markedly reduced AH50 of 6% does not reflect intrinsic alternative pathway dysregulation but rather the expected pharmacodynamic effect of C5 blockade. The authors explicitly acknowledge this: *"The observed reduction in the alternative pathway is most consistent with treatment effects rather than intrinsic complement depletion or activation."*

This case carries an essential practical lesson: **complement functional assays must be interpreted in the context of when they are drawn relative to plasma exchange and complement-inhibiting therapy.** Ideally, complement studies should be obtained before eculizumab initiation, though clinical necessity may preclude this. The normal factor H, factor I, factor B, negative anti-factor H antibodies, and absence of identified pathogenic genetic variants in this patient support an infection-triggered mechanism of CM-HUS without a demonstrable inherited complement defect. The rapid clinical response to eculizumab—sustained hematological remission and partial renal recovery—remains the strongest functional evidence that terminal complement activation was central to her TMA, even when standard complement biomarkers were unrevealing.

### [Differential Diagnosis] The patient's initial DAT (direct antiglobulin test) was positive for IgG, raising suspicion for autoimmune hemolytic anemia, but the test was later deemed a false positive and repeat testing was negative. What are common causes of a false-positive DAT, and how should a peripheral blood smear change the diagnostic approach when steroid-refractory hemolysis with thrombocytopenia is observed?

**Causes of a False-Positive DAT and the Role of the Peripheral Smear in Redirecting the Diagnostic Workup**

A positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) does not invariably signify autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA). False-positive DATs are common, and in this case, two mechanisms were specifically relevant: **drug-dependent antibodies** and **recent blood transfusion**. Penicillin-class antibiotics are classic offenders—penicillin can bind covalently to the red cell surface and act as a hapten, eliciting IgG-coating of erythrocytes that yields a positive DAT without true autoantibody-mediated destruction. Similarly, recent transfusions can produce a positive DAT through passive transfer of donor antibodies or alloantibody coating of transfused cells. Other common causes of false-positive DATs include IVIG administration (passive antibody transfer), nonspecific protein adsorption in states of intense inflammation or infection, and technical or sample-handling artifacts. When the clinical course is inconsistent with AIHA—as it was here, given steroid-refractory hemolysis and concurrent thrombocytopenia—repeat DAT testing and careful review of the peripheral smear are essential.

The peripheral blood smear is the pivotal diagnostic maneuver in this scenario. In this patient, the finding of **15%–20% schistocytes** established the hemolysis as **microangiopathic** rather than immune-mediated. Schistocytes are fragmented red cells generated by mechanical shearing within diseased microvasculature, and their presence at this level redirects the entire diagnostic framework away from AIHA and toward a **thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA)** syndrome. This is a critical fork in the road: while AIHA would warrant continued immunosuppression, TMA demands urgent consideration of TTP, STEC-HUS, and complement-mediated HUS, each with fundamentally different and time-sensitive therapies.

The smear finding, combined with persistent thrombocytopenia and dialysis-dependent AKI, appropriately prompted measurement of ADAMTS13 activity (43.5%, excluding TTP), Shiga toxin PCR (negative, excluding STEC-HUS), and complement studies, culminating in a kidney biopsy confirming TMA and a diagnosis of **complement-mediated HUS** triggered by *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* infection. The key teaching point is this: when hemolysis is steroid-refractory and accompanied by thrombocytopenia, a smear showing schistocytosis should immediately shift the differential from immune-mediated destruction to microangiopathic destruction, and the clinician must pursue the TMA diagnostic algorithm without delay—because the specific therapy (plasma exchange for TTP, eculizumab for CM-HUS) depends on rapid etiologic clarification.

### [Morning Report] In adults presenting with microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury, the first diagnostic priority is to exclude thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) by measuring ADAMTS13 activity. What ADAMTS13 activity threshold defines severe deficiency consistent with TTP, and in this patient with a PLASMIC score of 4/7 and ADAMTS13 activity of 43.5%, what is the next step in the differential diagnostic workup?

**Severe ADAMTS13 deficiency is defined as activity below 10% (often reported as <10 IU/dL).** This threshold distinguishes TTP, in which a critical deficiency of the von Willebrand factor-cleaving protease ADAMTS13 leads to unchecked platelet adhesion and microthrombus formation, from other thrombotic microangiopathies. Clinical decision tools such as the PLASMIC score help stratify pretest probability while awaiting the assay, but definitive exclusion is laboratory-based.

In this patient, the PLASMIC score of 4/7 indicated a low pretest probability of TTP, and the ADAMTS13 activity of 43.5% effectively ruled it out. With TTP excluded, the next diagnostic priorities are to evaluate for the other major TMA syndromes. Shiga toxin–producing *E. coli*–associated HUS (STEC-HUS) should be ruled out by stool PCR for Shiga toxin; in this case, the absence of prodromal enteric illness and a negative PCR argued against it. Concurrently, complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS) should be considered—a clinicopathological diagnosis supported by persistent MAHA, thrombocytopenia, dialysis-dependent AKI, and renal biopsy findings of thrombotic microangiopathy. Notably, normal C3 and C4 levels do not exclude CM-HUS, as complement levels may remain within reference ranges during the disease course. Extended complement functional and genetic testing can further characterize the underlying dysregulation, though as illustrated here, the absence of identified pathogenic variants does not preclude the diagnosis, particularly when an infectious trigger such as *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is implicated.

### [Management] Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting terminal complement component C5, is the standard of care for CM-HUS. What infectious risks are associated with complement blockade, and what specific preventive measures—vaccinations and antimicrobial prophylaxis—must be implemented before and during eculizumab therapy? Why is penicillin prophylaxis particularly important even after meningococcal vaccination?

Eculizumab blocks cleavage of C5, preventing generation of C5a and the membrane attack complex (C5b-9). While this effectively halts the terminal complement-mediated endothelial injury driving CM-HUS, it simultaneously abolishes one of the body's most critical defense mechanisms against *Neisseria meningitidis*—a gram-negative organism for which bactericidal killing depends almost entirely on complement-mediated lysis. The reported risk of meningococcal infection in eculizumab-treated patients is roughly 1–2%, which is hundreds of times higher than the baseline population risk.

**Vaccination requirements:** Before initiating eculizumab, patients should be immunized against *N. meningitidis* using both the quadrivalent conjugate vaccine (covering serogroups A, C, Y, W135—in this case, Menveo) and the serogroup B vaccine (in this case, Bexsero). Ideally, vaccination should be completed at least two weeks before the first dose of eculizumab to allow adequate antibody responses. As illustrated in this case, this interval was not achievable due to the urgency of the patient's active TMA; the first doses of both vaccines were administered immediately before induction, and antimicrobial prophylaxis was therefore initiated concurrently.

**Antimicrobial prophylaxis:** Current guidelines recommend antibiotic prophylaxis for the duration of eculizumab therapy (and for a period after discontinuation), particularly when vaccination has not been completed at least two weeks before treatment initiation. Oral penicillin V (as used here—phenoxymethylpenicillin/Apocillin) is the most commonly prescribed agent, though alternatives such as moxifloxacin or rifampin may be used in penicillin-allergic patients. The case also highlights the importance of ensuring blood cultures are negative and active infection is controlled before complement blockade is started, as terminal complement is also relevant for defense against other encapsulated organisms—including, notably, *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*, the very pathogen that triggered this patient's disease.

**Why penicillin is essential even after vaccination:** Meningococcal vaccines induce serogroup-specific antibody responses, but the actual *killing* of *Neisseria* still requires the terminal complement pathway to form the membrane attack complex. In a patient on C5 blockade, vaccine-induced antibodies can bind the organism and promote opsonophagocytosis, but the primary bactericidal mechanism—complement lysis—is functionally absent. Antibody titers also wane over time and may not confer sterilizing immunity, particularly against serogroup B. Furthermore, non-vaccine serogroups (such as X, Z, and 29E) remain potential threats. For all of these reasons, penicillin prophylaxis provides a necessary second layer of protection that is independent of complement and antibody-mediated immunity, directly inhibiting meningococcal growth in the nasopharynx and bloodstream. Vaccination reduces risk, but it does not eliminate it—and the consequences of breakthrough meningococcal disease on complement blockade can be rapidly fatal.

### [Management] This patient was treated with plasma exchange (PEX) as bridging therapy before eculizumab initiation. While PEX is lifesaving in TTP, its role in CM-HUS is different. What is the rationale for using PEX as a bridge in CM-HUS, why is it insufficient as standalone therapy, and what clinical observation in this case demonstrated the transient nature of PEX benefit compared to sustained recovery after complement blockade?

In CM-HUS, the core pathophysiology is uncontrolled activation of the alternative complement pathway leading to endothelial injury and thrombotic microangiopathy. Plasma exchange (PEX) can provide a degree of transient benefit by removing circulating complement fragments, inflammatory mediators, and any potential autoantibodies from the plasma, and by supplying functional complement regulatory proteins such as factor H and factor I through replacement fluid. This rationale makes PEX a reasonable bridging strategy when the diagnosis of TMA has not yet been definitively established and TTP remains a possibility—particularly while awaiting ADAMTS13 activity results, as was the case here.

However, PEX is insufficient as standalone therapy in CM-HUS because it does not address the underlying complement dysregulation driving the disease. Unlike in TTP, where PEX removes pathogenic autoantibodies against ADAMTS13 and replaces the deficient enzyme—directly correcting the causal defect—in CM-HUS the problem is sustained, dysregulated alternative pathway activation that PEX cannot durably suppress. Once PEX is stopped, the complement cascade resumes its unchecked activity, and the TMA process reasserts itself. This is why eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody that blocks cleavage of C5 and thereby halts terminal complement activation, is the standard of care and the definitive therapy.

The clinical observation in this case that most clearly demonstrated the transient nature of PEX benefit was the return of spontaneous diuresis following the second and third PEX sessions but before eculizumab had taken effect. This partial renal recovery reflected temporary stabilization from removal of circulating complement fragments and inflammatory mediators. However, sustained haematological and renal recovery—maintenance of remission and progressive improvement in kidney function—occurred only after complement blockade was established with eculizumab. The contrast between these two phases of the clinical course illustrates the critical teaching point: PEX can buy time in CM-HUS, but only terminal complement blockade can convert a temporary response into durable disease control.

### [Pathophysiology] C. canimorsus infection is a rarely reported trigger of CM-HUS. The case discussion notes a clinical paradox: complement activation is important for clearing the pathogen, yet it may also trigger uncontrolled endothelial damage and TMA in susceptible individuals. How does this paradox inform the therapeutic dilemma of initiating complement blockade during or after active bacteremia, and what timing strategy was used in this case?

## Clinical Teaching Answer

The paradox here is fundamental to understanding both the pathogenesis and the treatment strategy in this case.

**The Pathophysiologic Paradox:** *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is an encapsulated gram-negative rod, and complement activation—particularly via the alternative pathway—is an important arm of innate host defense against it. Many isolates are complement-sensitive, meaning that terminal complement components (C5b-9, the membrane attack complex) contribute directly to bacterial killing. However, in a susceptible host, the same complement activation that helps clear the organism can spiraling into uncontrolled endothelial activation, microvascular thrombosis, and TMA—the hallmark of CM-HUS. The infection serves as the "second hit" that tips a dysregulated or vulnerable complement system into a self-amplifying injurious cycle.

**The Therapeutic Dilemma:** Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody against C5, is the standard of care for CM-HUS and is most effective when initiated early to halt ongoing endothelial injury and preserve renal function. However, blocking terminal complement during active bacteremia is potentially dangerous: by disabling the membrane attack complex, eculizumab could impair clearance of the very encapsulated organism driving the disease—worsening sepsis rather than treating its complication. This is analogous to the well-established risk of eculizumab-associated *Neisseria* infection, and *Capnocytophaga* shares relevant biological features as an encapsulated commensal.

**The Timing Strategy in This Case:** The clinical team resolved this dilemma sequentially. First, they focused on source control—broad-spectrum antibiotics (piperacillin-tazobactam, later de-escalated to ampicillin then oral penicillin) and supportive care—while awaiting blood culture clearance. Plasma exchange was initiated as a **bridging therapy** to remove circulating complement fragments and inflammatory mediators, providing temporary stabilization (notably, diuresis returned after PEX sessions but before eculizumab, reflecting only transient benefit). Only **after blood cultures were documented as negative** was eculizumab induction initiated, thereby minimizing the risk of uncontrolled infection while complement blockade was active. Meningococcal vaccination (both Menveo and Bexsero) was administered before the first eculizumab dose, and prophylactic penicillin was given because the vaccines had not been completed the recommended 2 weeks prior to complement blockade.

**The Clinical Lesson:** This sequential approach—eradicate the pathogen first, bridge with plasma exchange, then initiate complement blockade—is a reasoned strategy when the infectious trigger itself is complement-sensitive. It balances two competing imperatives: the need for early complement blockade to limit renal injury in CM-HUS against the risk of disarming innate immunity during active infection with an encapsulated organism. The partial renal recovery observed in this patient, despite the delay inherent in this approach, is consistent with current evidence that time-to-eculizumab is a key determinant of renal outcome, while also acknowledging that some delay is unavoidable when the trigger is an active, complement-susceptible bacteremia.

### [Management] Approximately 50–70% of patients with CM-HUS harbor genetic variants or autoantibodies affecting complement regulatory proteins such as factors H, I, B, or membrane cofactor protein (CD46), yet disease penetrance is incomplete and an external trigger is typically required. In this patient, no pathogenic complement gene variants were identified. How does the absence of identified complement variants influence decisions about the duration of eculizumab therapy and relapse monitoring?

The absence of identified pathogenic complement variants in this patient has important but nuanced implications for treatment duration and relapse surveillance.

First, it is critical to recognize that a negative genetic result does **not** exclude CM-HUS. Roughly 30–50% of patients with clinically confirmed CM-HUS have no identifiable complement variant or autoantibody on current testing panels. Our understanding of complement regulatory genetics remains incomplete, and rare variants in non-coding regions, epigenetic modifiers, or proteins not yet routinely sequenced may escape detection. Therefore, the diagnosis stands on clinicopathologic grounds—MAHA, thrombocytopenia, biopsy-confirmed TMA, exclusion of TTP and STEC-HUS, and a clear response to complement blockade.

That said, the absence of a demonstrable inherited complement abnormality, combined with this patient's previously unremarkable medical history and a well-defined infectious trigger (*Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteremia), supports a model of **infection-triggered CM-HUS without a clear constitutive complement defect**. In such patients, the assumption is that the inciting event—rather than an intrinsic regulatory failure—drove transient complement dysregulation. Once the trigger is eliminated and the endothelial injury has resolved, the rationale for indefinite complement blockade weakens.

This contrasts with patients harboring pathogenic variants in genes such as *CFH*, *CFI*, *CFB*, or *CD46*, where the underlying regulatory defect persists and the risk of relapse—particularly with future infections, pregnancy, or other inflammatory stressors—remains substantial. In those patients, long-term or even lifelong eculizumab is generally favored, with vigilant monitoring for relapse after any discontinuation attempt.

In this case, eculizumab was continued through induction and into maintenance (1200 mg every two weeks) because of the severity of the initial presentation—complete anuria, dialysis dependence, and biopsy-proven TMA with crescent formation. The plan, as described, is to **reassess for discontinuation once renal function stabilizes**, rather than committing to indefinite therapy. This is a reasonable strategy: the negative genetic result lowers—but does not eliminate—the estimated relapse risk, and the severity of the index episode justifies a cautious, staged approach rather than premature withdrawal.

In practical terms, if eculizumab is discontinued, relapse monitoring should include regular surveillance for hematologic markers of TMA—hemoglobin, platelet count, lactate dehydrogenase, haptoglobin, and creatinine—particularly during intercurrent illnesses. Any unexplained thrombocytopenia, hemolytic anemia, or worsening renal function should prompt urgent reevaluation and consideration of rapid reinstatement of complement blockade.

In summary: the absence of identified complement variants does not refute the diagnosis, but it does shift the risk-benefit calculus toward a **time-limited treatment course with structured reassessment** rather than indefinite therapy, while maintaining vigilance for relapse driven by future infectious or inflammatory triggers.
