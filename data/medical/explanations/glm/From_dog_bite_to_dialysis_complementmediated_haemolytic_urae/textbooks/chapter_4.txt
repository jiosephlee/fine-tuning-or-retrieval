Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

Chapter 4: ## Differential Diagnosis of TMA: A Systematic Diagnostic Approach

Thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) is not a single disease but an umbrella syndrome defined by a shared clinicopathological triad: microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and organ damage resulting from microvascular thrombus formation. The MAHA component reflects mechanical fragmentation of erythrocytes as they traverse fibrin-rich microthrombi, producing schistocytes on peripheral blood smear, elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), undetectable haptoglobin, and a negative direct antiglobulin test. Thrombocytopenia reflects platelet consumption at sites of endothelial injury. Organ involvement—most commonly the kidney, but also the brain, gut, and heart—depends on the territory of microvascular occlusion. Because several distinct entities produce this identical triad through different mechanisms, the diagnostic task is not simply to recognise TMA but to determine which TMA syndrome is operating, as the treatments diverge fundamentally: plasma exchange is lifesaving in thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), whereas complement inhibition with eculizumab is the standard of care for complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS).

In this patient, the initial presentation was dominated by sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), and pancytopenia, which obscured the TMA. It was only after the septic picture began to improve that a sudden isolated haemoglobin drop (from 111 to 68 g/L over 6 days), persistent thrombocytopenia (30–40 × 10⁹/L), undetectable haptoglobin, and rising LDH signalled ongoing haemolysis. A peripheral smear showing 15%–20% schistocytes confirmed MAHA and shifted the diagnostic focus toward a TMA syndrome superimposed on the resolving septic episode.

The first diagnostic priority in any adult presenting with MAHA and thrombocytopenia is to exclude TTP, because untreated TTP carries a mortality exceeding 90% but responds dramatically to plasma exchange. TTP arises from a severe deficiency of ADAMTS13, the metalloprotease that cleaves ultra-large von Willebrand factor multimers. When ADAMTS13 activity falls below 10%—typically due to an acquired autoantibody or, less commonly, an inherited deficiency—ultra-large multimers accumulate on endothelial surfaces, causing uncontrolled platelet adhesion and microvascular thrombosis. In this patient, the PLASMIC score—a validated clinical prediction tool incorporating platelet count, haemolysis markers, absence of active cancer or transplant, mean corpuscular volume, INR, and creatinine—was 4/7, indicating a low pretest probability of TTP. The definitive result came with ADAMTS13 activity at 43.5%, well above the 10% threshold required for a TTP diagnosis. This effectively excluded TTP and obviated the need to continue plasma exchange as a disease-specific therapy, although it was continued briefly as a bridge to eculizumab.

Shiga toxin-producing Escherichia coli-associated HUS (STEC-HUS) was the next entity to exclude. STEC-HUS is the most common form of HUS in children and is classically preceded by a prodromal episode of bloody diarrhoea caused by Shiga toxin–producing organisms such as E. coli O157:H7. The toxin binds to and damages glomerular endothelial cells, triggering a thrombotic cascade. In adults, the absence of a preceding enteric illness provides an important clinical clue, but microbiological confirmation is essential. In this patient, there was no history of diarrhoeal illness, and stool PCR analysis for Shiga toxin was negative, excluding STEC-HUS.

With TTP and STEC-HUS excluded, attention turned to complement-mediated HUS. CM-HUS—historically termed atypical HUS—arises from dysregulated activation of the alternative complement pathway on endothelial surfaces, leading to uncontrolled C3 convertase activity, terminal complement complex (C5b-9) deposition, endothelial injury, and microvascular thrombosis. A critical diagnostic principle is that CM-HUS is a clinicopathological diagnosis of exclusion: there is no single confirmatory laboratory test. Standard complement assays (C3 and C4) are frequently normal during the disease course, and normal levels do not exclude the diagnosis. In this patient, locally available C3 and C4 were within reference ranges, and functional assays of the classical, alternative, and lectin pathways were also normal. These results, while not supporting CM-HUS, did not exclude it. The diagnosis ultimately rested on the constellation of persistent haemolysis, schistocytosis, dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury, and renal biopsy findings consistent with TMA—after TTP and STEC-HUS had been excluded.

Renal biopsy provided the histopathological confirmation. Light microscopy demonstrated severe acute tubular necrosis, ischaemic glomeruli containing fibrin thrombi, and scattered crescent formation. These findings are characteristic of TMA: fibrin thrombi occlude glomerular capillaries and arterioles, producing ischaemic injury to both glomeruli and tubulointerstitium. Immunofluorescence showed no immune complex deposition, and electron microscopy revealed no evidence of a primary glomerulopathy, excluding alternative renal diagnoses. Importantly, the biopsy was performed only after the platelet count had risen to 77 × 10⁹/L, because performing an invasive procedure in the setting of severe thrombocytopenia would carry an unacceptable bleeding risk. The biopsy findings, combined with the exclusion of TTP and STEC-HUS and the rapid clinical improvement following eculizumab, solidified the diagnosis of CM-HUS.

Extended complement testing was sent to a reference laboratory to investigate for hereditary complement-mediated TMA. This testing included a quantitative alternative pathway haemolytic assay (AH50), which was markedly reduced at 6%. However, the interpretation of this result is complicated by the timing of the sample. The extended complement panel was obtained after three sessions of plasma exchange with Octaplas had been completed and after one induction dose of eculizumab had been administered. Eculizumab blocks terminal complement activation (C5), and plasma exchange alters circulating complement component concentrations. Therefore, the reduced AH50 most likely reflects a treatment effect rather than intrinsic complement depletion or dysregulation. Complement factors H, I, and B were normal, anti-factor H antibodies were negative, and extended genetic testing did not identify any pathogenic variants. This timing issue is clinically important: to obtain meaningful baseline complement data, samples ideally should be drawn before the initiation of plasma exchange or complement-blocking therapy. In practice, however, the urgency of treating a critically ill patient with suspected CM-HUS often precludes waiting for results, and clinicians must interpret complement assays in the context of concurrent interventions.

This case illustrates that the differential diagnosis of TMA requires a structured, stepwise approach: recognise the triad of MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and organ damage; exclude TTP using the PLASMIC score and ADAMTS13 activity; exclude STEC-HUS through stool PCR and clinical history; and consider CM-HUS as a diagnosis of exclusion, supported by biopsy evidence of TMA and uninhibited by normal C3/C4 levels. The case also demonstrates that in the real-world setting of acutely ill patients, diagnostic and therapeutic decisions frequently proceed in parallel, and the clinician must interpret laboratory data with an understanding of how ongoing treatments—particularly plasma exchange and complement inhibition—may alter the very assays being used to establish the diagnosis.
