Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

Chapter 2: ## Capnocytophaga canimorsus: Pathogen Biology and Infection Spectrum

*Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is a slow-growing, encapsulated, gram-negative rod that resides as part of the normal oropharyngeal flora in dogs and cats. It is the *Capnocytophaga* species most frequently associated with invasive zoonotic disease in humans. Transmission to humans occurs through bites, scratches, or even close animal contact, meaning that a thorough exposure history is essential when evaluating patients presenting with unexplained sepsis or pancytopenia. In this case, the patient was bitten on the upper lip by her own dog four days before presenting with fulminant illness, and this history immediately raised clinical suspicion for *C. canimorsus* infection, guiding the choice of empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics.

The clinical spectrum of *C. canimorsus* infection in humans is remarkably wide, ranging from isolated meningitis to fulminant sepsis with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), multiorgan failure, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). This variability means that the pathogen may not be immediately suspected in patients who lack the classic constellation of bite-related cellulitis. In the present case, the patient presented with fever exceeding 40°C, pancytopenia, coagulopathy, lactic acidosis, and rapidly progressive acute kidney injury—features that were initially interpreted as DIC secondary to bacteraemia. The subsequent development of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia and persistent thrombocytopenia after the sepsis phase began to resolve ultimately pointed toward a superimposed TMA, illustrating how *C. canimorsus* can produce both direct septic complications and downstream complement-mediated endothelial injury.

Diagnosis of *C. canimorsus* infection is frequently delayed because the organism is fastidious and grows slowly in culture. In this case, blood cultures confirmed the organism seven days after admission, even though clinical suspicion was present from the outset. This delay is a well-recognized challenge and has prompted increasing use of molecular methods such as real-time nanopore sequencing, which can identify the pathogen more rapidly in centers where such technology is available. The slow growth and specialized culture requirements underscore why empiric antibiotic coverage for dog-associated pathogens must not await microbiological confirmation in severely ill patients.

Despite its broad susceptibility to antibiotics—including penicillins, cephalosporins, and carbapenems—mortality from invasive *C. canimorsus* infection can approach 30%. This paradox of broad susceptibility yet high mortality reflects the organism's ability to cause rapid, overwhelming disease before antibiotics can take effect, particularly in patients who present late or with advanced multiorgan dysfunction. In the present case, the patient had been prescribed phenoxymethylpenicillin after the dog bite but never initiated treatment because she could not collect her prescription, and she presented four days later in septic shock with profound metabolic derangement. This sequence highlights the critical importance of early antibiotic prophylaxis after animal bites, particularly when the bite involves the face or other high-risk areas, and the potentially devastating consequences of even short delays in treatment.

From a clinical reasoning perspective, the dog bite history serves as an essential diagnostic clue that should actively shape empiric management. In patients presenting with sepsis of unclear etiology, a recent animal bite or close canine exposure should prompt consideration of *C. canimorsus* and other Pasteurellaceae, guiding the selection of broad-spectrum antibiotics such as piperacillin-tazobactam that provide reliable coverage while culture results are pending. In this case, the treating team suspected *C. canimorsus* from admission, initiated piperacillin-tazobactam, and later de-escalated to ampicillin and then oral phenoxymethylpenicillin once sensitivities were confirmed. This structured approach to empiric coverage, culture confirmation, and de-escalation demonstrates how awareness of the pathogen's biology and transmission patterns directly informs clinical decision-making in time-critical illness.
