Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

Chapter 3: ## Clinical Evolution: From Resolving Sepsis to Thrombotic Microangiopathy

### ICU Course: Anuria, Septic Cardiomyopathy, and Renal Replacement Therapy

After admission to the intensive care unit, the patient's renal function deteriorated rapidly. Despite initially maintaining adequate diuresis on arrival, she became completely anuric—a concerning sign indicating that acute kidney injury had progressed to a severity requiring renal replacement therapy. Aggressive fluid resuscitation and loop diuretics were attempted but failed to restore urine output, underscoring that the kidney injury was not simply prerenal physiology but represented intrinsic, likely structural, damage from the septic process and microcirculatory insult.

The clinical picture was further complicated by the development of septic cardiomyopathy, a well-recognized phenomenon of sepsis-induced myocardial dysfunction characterized by biventricular dilatation, reduced ejection fraction, and impaired contractility. This reversible form of cardiomyopathy is mediated by inflammatory cytokines, nitric oxide, and mitochondrial dysfunction rather than by coronary ischemia. In this patient, septic cardiomyopathy contributed to fluid overload, making the already anuric state more clinically dangerous and hastening the need for continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT). CRRT, as opposed to intermittent hemodialysis, is generally preferred in hemodynamically unstable ICU patients because it provides gradual solute and fluid removal, minimizing shifts in intravascular volume and osmolarity.

It is worth noting that despite multiorgan involvement, the patient never required vasopressor support beyond stress-dose corticosteroids. This is somewhat atypical for septic shock but may reflect early and aggressive supportive care, as well as the particular pathophysiology of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* infection, which can cause severe endothelial activation and microvascular compromise without the classic distributive shock physiology seen in other gram-negative bacteremias.

### Microthrombotic Phenomena: A Clue to Endothelial Injury

On day 3 in the ICU, the patient developed diffuse skin bleeding with ecchymoses, along with distal cyanotic and ischemic changes affecting several toes and fingers. Pulses and sensation were preserved, which is an important clinical distinction: the ischemia was not due to large-vessel occlusion but rather to microvascular thrombosis at the level of the capillaries and arterioles supplying the digital circulation.

These findings were initially interpreted as microthrombotic phenomena in the context of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), and this interpretation was reasonable at that stage. DIC, driven by tissue factor release and cytokine-mediated activation of coagulation, produces widespread microthrombi alongside consumption of platelets and clotting factors. However, in retrospect, these microthrombotic changes are also characteristic of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), a condition defined by endothelial injury and microvascular platelet-rich thrombi. The clinical and histologic overlap between DIC and TMA is substantial, and distinguishing the two on clinical grounds alone can be extremely difficult, particularly early in the course when both may coexist.

For the student or resident, the key teaching point is that digital ischemia in a septic patient with thrombocytopenia and coagulopathy should raise concern for both DIC and TMA. While DIC typically improves as the underlying infection is controlled, persistent or worsening microthrombotic phenomena—particularly when accompanied by ongoing hemolysis—should prompt reconsideration of TMA as a separate or superimposed process.

### Transfer to the Nephrology Ward: De-escalation and Initial Recovery

After 8 days of hospitalization, the patient was transferred to the nephrology ward for ongoing dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury and continued intravenous antibiotic therapy. At this point, she was clinically stable but anuric, with generalized edema and multiple hematomas, yet alert and oriented. The transfer marked a transition from the acute resuscitative phase to a more protracted phase of supportive care and diagnostic evaluation.

Antibiotic therapy was de-escalated based on blood culture results: from broad-spectrum piperacillin/tazobactam to intravenous ampicillin and subsequently to oral phenoxymethylpenicillin. This de-escalation reflects sound antimicrobial stewardship—narrowing coverage once the causative organism and its susceptibility profile are known. *C. canimorsus* is generally susceptible to penicillins, and the transition to oral therapy indicated that the bacteremia was being adequately treated.

On the nephrology ward, laboratory trends were initially encouraging. The inflammatory profile improved, leukocytes showed a reactive rebound from the earlier leukopenia, platelets were recovering, hemoglobin was stable, and coagulation parameters normalized. These improvements indicated that the septic phase and associated DIC were resolving. Kidney function tests, however, showed persistent azotemia, reflecting the severe acute tubular injury that had occurred and the ongoing need for dialysis. The normalization of coagulation parameters was particularly important, as it would later help distinguish residual or recurrent pathology from a new process.

### The Diagnostic Turning Point: Sudden Isolated Hemoglobin Drop

The critical diagnostic turning point occurred after this initial improvement. The patient developed a sudden, isolated drop in hemoglobin from 111 to 68 g/L over 6 days, without any evidence of bleeding or hemodynamic instability. This pattern—anemia developing in the absence of overt blood loss—immediately raised the question of hemolysis as the underlying mechanism.

For the clinician, a falling hemoglobin without a clear bleeding source should always prompt evaluation for hemolytic anemia. The approach begins with asking whether the anemia is due to destruction (hemolysis) or underproduction, and if hemolysis is suspected, whether it is immune-mediated or non-immune. The speed of the drop—43 g/L in 6 days—is consistent with an acute hemolytic process rather than marrow suppression or iron deficiency. In the context of a patient recovering from sepsis with known microthrombotic complications, this finding was a sentinel event that demanded a structured diagnostic workup.

### Laboratory Evidence of Hemolysis: Assembling the Picture

The laboratory workup confirmed ongoing hemolysis. Haptoglobin became undetectable, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels rose. Haptoglobin is a plasma protein that binds free hemoglobin released during intravascular hemolysis; because the haptoglobin-hemoglobin complex is rapidly cleared by the liver, haptoglobin levels fall precipitously and serve as a sensitive marker of intravascular hemolysis. LDH, particularly the LDH-2 isoenzyme, is released from lysed erythrocytes and further supports the hemolytic process. The combination of low haptoglobin and elevated LDH is the classic biochemical signature of intravascular hemolysis.

Critically, platelets remained persistently low (30–40 × 10⁹/L) despite the earlier recovery trend. This persistence of thrombocytopenia alongside hemolysis is a crucial observation because it recapitulates the classic triad of TMA: microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and organ injury (in this case, acute kidney injury). The concurrent presence of these three elements, particularly after the coagulopathy of DIC had resolved, strongly suggested that a TMA process was now driving the hematologic derangement rather than residual sepsis-related consumption.

### The False-Positive Direct Antiglobulin Test: A Diagnostic Detour

The initial workup took a brief detour when the direct antiglobulin test (DAT, or Coombs test) returned positive for IgG, raising suspicion for autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA). AIHA is caused by antibodies directed against the patient's own red blood cells, leading to extravascular hemolysis via splenic macrophage destruction of antibody-coated erythrocytes. Based on this result, high-dose prednisolone was started, and several blood transfusions were administered.

However, the hemolytic process failed to respond to corticosteroid therapy, and platelets remained low—a pattern atypical for AIHA, which generally does not cause significant thrombocytopenia unless Evans syndrome (AIHA coexisting with immune thrombocytopenia) is present. This lack of response prompted re-evaluation.

The initially positive DAT was likely a false positive. False-positive DAT results can occur in several settings: recent blood transfusions can lead to passive antibody transfer or alloantibody formation, and certain drugs—including penicillin—can bind to red blood cell surfaces and act as haptens, inducing antibody binding that is detected on the DAT without causing true autoimmune hemolysis. In this patient, both prior transfusions and penicillin exposure were present, providing plausible explanations for the false-positive result. Repeat DAT testing was negative, confirming that the initial result did not represent true immune-mediated hemolysis.

This detour illustrates an important clinical lesson: a single positive test, particularly one with known false-positive triggers, should be interpreted in the full clinical context. When the response to therapy does not match the suspected diagnosis, the diagnosis should be questioned rather than the treatment intensified.

### Peripheral Blood Smear: Schistocytes and the Recognition of TMA

The pivotal diagnostic finding came from the peripheral blood smear, which revealed 15%–20% schistocytes. Schistocytes are fragmented red blood cells produced when erythrocytes are mechanically sheared as they traverse microvessels obstructed by platelet-rich thrombi. This process—mechanical fragmentation within the microcirculation—is the defining mechanism of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA) and is the hematologic hallmark of TMA.

The magnitude of schistocytosis matters. Small numbers of schistocytes (less than 1%) can be seen in various conditions, but a level of 15%–20% is substantial and strongly supports the diagnosis of MAHA. In the context of this patient—persistent thrombocytopenia, acute kidney injury, and now confirmed MAHA—the constellation of findings was diagnostic of TMA. It is important to recognize that schistocytes are not specific to a particular etiology of TMA; they are seen in thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), Shiga toxin–associated HUS (STEC-HUS), complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS), and secondary TMAs associated with malignancy, pregnancy, or autoimmune disease. The presence of schistocytes thus established the syndrome of TMA but did not, by itself, identify the underlying cause.

For the learner, the key sequence in this case is instructive: a patient recovering from sepsis and DIC developed new, unexplained hemolysis and persistent thrombocytopenia after coagulopathy had resolved. The initial suspicion of immune-mediated hemolysis was disproven by the steroid-unresponsive course, the negative repeat DAT, and—most importantly—the finding of schistocytes on peripheral smear. This sequence shifted the diagnostic framework from autoimmune hemolysis to microangiopathic hemolysis and established TMA as the dominant process, setting the stage for the subsequent workup that would distinguish among TTP, STEC-HUS, and CM-HUS.
