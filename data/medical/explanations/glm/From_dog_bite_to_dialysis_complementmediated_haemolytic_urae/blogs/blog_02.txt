Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# When Sepsis Improves But Haemolysis Worsens: Recognizing Superimposed Thrombotic Microangiopathy

## The Pattern That Should Make You Stop and Rethink

Your septic patient is turning the corner. Inflammatory markers are settling, white cells are recovering, coagulopathy has resolved, and you're starting to feel like the hard part is over. Then the haemoglobin tanks — 111 down to 68 g/L in six days — with no bleeding source. Platelets stay stuck in the basement. Haptoglobin disappears. What do you do?

This is exactly the scenario that unfolded in a previously healthy woman in her late 40s, four days after a dog bite to the upper lip. Her story is a masterclass in how the initial clinical narrative — fulminant sepsis with DIC — can obscure a second, entirely different pathological process that declares itself only when the first is resolving. The teaching point isn't just about Capnocytophaga canimorsus or even complement-mediated HUS. It's about temporal pattern recognition: when a new problem emerges that doesn't fit the trajectory of the original diagnosis, you need to broaden your differential, send the right tests, and resist anchoring.

## The Case: From Dog Bite to Dialysis

Four days after being bitten by her dog on the upper lip, this previously healthy woman presented in septic shock with fever over 40°C, mottled extremities, refractory hypoglycaemia (nadir 0.6 mmol/L), pancytopenia, AKI, liver enzyme elevation, and coagulopathy consistent with DIC. She had been prescribed phenoxymethylpenicillin at the time of the bite but never collected it. Broad-spectrum piperacillin-tazobactam was started empirically in the ED, covering dog-associated pathogens including Capnocytophaga. Blood cultures confirmed *C. canimorsus* bacteraemia seven days later.

She became completely anuric, required continuous renal replacement therapy, and developed diffuse ecchymoses with distal cyanotic changes to toes and fingers — microthrombotic phenomena in the context of DIC. She never required vasopressors beyond stress-dose steroids. After eight days, she was transferred to nephrology, dialysis-dependent but clinically stable.

Then the picture shifted. Her inflammatory profile improved. Coagulation normalised. Platelets had started to recover. But then: sudden isolated haemoglobin drop, persistent thrombocytopaenia (30–40 × 10⁹/L), undetectable haptoglobin, and rising LDH. This was not a continuation of the sepsis story. This was something new.

## The DAT Trap: A False-Positive Coombs Test Leading Down the Wrong Path

Here's where the case takes a turn that should make every clinician pause. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT / Coombs) came back positive for IgG. The team reasonably suspected autoimmune haemolytic anaemia and started high-dose prednisolone. It didn't work. Haemolysis continued. Platelets stayed low.

The repeat DAT was negative. The initial positive result was almost certainly a false positive — likely driven by penicillin exposure (penicillin can coat red cells and cause a positive DAT without true autoimmune haemolysis) or by recent transfusions. This is a critical pitfall. A single positive DAT in a patient who has been on beta-lactams and received blood products should be interpreted with extreme caution, especially when the clinical picture doesn't fully fit autoimmune haemolytic anaemia — and in this case, the persistent thrombocytopaenia should have been the clue that something else was going on.

The team then did the test that should have been done earlier: a peripheral blood smear. It showed 15–20% schistocytes. This was microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA). The diagnosis was thrombotic microangiopathy, not autoimmune haemolysis. The steroids were a detour.

## The TMA Diagnostic Algorithm: Exclude, Exclude, Then Commit

Once schistocytes are identified, the question becomes: what type of TMA? The algorithm is straightforward but requires discipline, especially when you're managing a critically ill patient and waiting on send-out labs.

**Exclude TTP first.** Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura is defined by severe ADAMTS13 deficiency, typically below 10%. This patient's ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%, and her PLASMIC score was only 4/7 — both arguing strongly against TTP. While waiting for ADAMTS13 results, plasma exchange was appropriately initiated as bridging therapy, because the downside of delaying PEX in possible TTP is catastrophic.

**Exclude STEC-HUS.** Shiga toxin-producing E. coli HUS was ruled out with a negative stool PCR, and there was no prodromal enteric illness.

**Then consider complement-mediated HUS.** The constellation of persistent MAHA, dialysis-dependent AKI, schistocytes on smear, and kidney biopsy showing acute tubular necrosis with ischaemic glomeruli and fibrin thrombi cemented the diagnosis. Crucially, basic complement levels (C3 and C4) were normal — and this is a point that cannot be overstated. Normal C3 and C4 do not exclude CM-HUS. Complement-mediated TMA is a clinicopathological diagnosis of exclusion, not a diagnosis that requires abnormal standard complement panels.

## The Treatment Dilemma: Complement Blockade After Sepsis

Eculizumab — a monoclonal antibody targeting terminal complement component C5 — is the standard of care for CM-HUS, and early initiation is the single most important predictor of renal recovery. Delayed initiation is associated with incomplete renal recovery and higher risk of progression to end-stage kidney disease.

But here's the tension: terminal complement components are critical for defence against encapsulated bacteria, including *Neisseria* and, notably, *Capnocytophaga*. Starting eculizumab during active bacteraemia could risk uncontrolled infection. The team waited until blood cultures were negative before initiating complement blockade. Meningococcal vaccination (Menveo and Bexsero) was administered before the first dose, with prophylactic penicillin given because the vaccines hadn't been completed two weeks prior to starting eculizumab — the standard requirement for adequate antibody response.

Plasma exchange was used as bridging therapy — three sessions with Octaplas. Diuresis returned after the final PEX sessions but before eculizumab, reflecting only transient stabilisation. Sustained haematological and renal recovery occurred only after complement blockade was initiated. This is consistent with the evidence: PEX removes circulating complement fragments and inflammatory mediators but doesn't correct the underlying complement dysregulation. It buys time; eculizumab fixes the problem.

## What Makes This Case Unique: Infection-Triggered CM-HUS Without a Genetic Predisposition

Most cases of CM-HUS occur in patients with identifiable genetic variants or autoantibodies affecting complement regulatory proteins — factors H, I, B, or membrane cofactor protein. Disease penetrance is incomplete, and a trigger is usually needed: infection, pregnancy, autoimmune disease, or certain medications.

This patient had no pathogenic complement gene variants identified on extended genetic testing. She had no significant medical history. Her CM-HUS appears to have been purely infection-triggered — *C. canimorsus* activating the alternative complement pathway in a host without a demonstrable inherited predisposition. This is rare but not unprecedented; a prior report by Bjørkto et al. described *C. canimorsus*-induced CM-HUS in a patient harbouring a complement factor B variant. This case adds to that literature but suggests the trigger alone can be sufficient, even without a genetic "first hit."

The clinical paradox is worth reflecting on: complement activation helps clear *C. canimorsus* (many isolates are complement-sensitive, though capsule-expressing strains show partial resistance), but in susceptible hosts — or perhaps even in some apparently non-susceptible hosts — that same activation may trigger uncontrolled endothelial damage and TMA.

## Clinical Pearls

- **Temporal pattern recognition is a diagnostic tool.** When haemolysis emerges as sepsis resolves, it's not the same disease getting worse — it's a new process. Stop and reassess.
- **Send a peripheral smear early when haemolysis doesn't fit the clinical trajectory.** Schistocytes change the entire diagnostic framework. This patient's smear was the turning point.
- **A positive DAT in a patient on beta-lactams or with recent transfusions may be a false positive.** If steroids aren't working and platelets are also low, rethink — the combination of MAHA and thrombocytopaenia points toward TMA, not autoimmune haemolysis.
- **Normal C3 and C4 do not exclude CM-HUS.** This is a clinicopathological diagnosis of exclusion. Don't be reassured by a normal complement panel.
- **Exclude TTP first (ADAMTS13 < 10%), then STEC-HUS (stool PCR), then commit to CM-HUS.** Use PLASMIC scoring to triage while awaiting results, but confirm with labs.
- **Plasma exchange is bridging therapy in CM-HUS, not definitive treatment.** Sustained recovery requires complement blockade with eculizumab.
- **Initiate eculizumab early.** Time-to-initiation is the main determinant of renal recovery. Vaccinate, give prophylactic antibiotics, and start complement blockade once bacteraemia has cleared.
- **Consider unusual infectious triggers for TMA.** *C. canimorsus* is rare, but dog bites are not. When a patient presents with sepsis after a dog bite and later develops TMA, this pathogen should be on your radar.

## The Bottom Line

This case illustrates one of the hardest things in medicine: recognising when a patient who is "getting better" is actually getting worse from a different process. The sepsis-to-CM-HUS transition is subtle, the initial DIC picture is a convincing narrative, and a false-positive DAT can send you down the wrong path for days. The peripheral smear is the key that unlocks the correct diagnosis. When haemolysis appears after sepsis improves, think TMA, send the smear, run the exclusion algorithm, and don't let a normal C3 or C4 talk you out of complement-mediated HUS.
