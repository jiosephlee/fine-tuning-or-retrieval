Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# When Neuro-Behçet's Presents at the Same Time as Systemic Disease: A Diagnostic Red Flag

## The Punchline Up Front

Here's a case that should make you rethink your approach to any patient presenting with fever, headache, and hyponatremia of unclear etiology. A man in his 60s walked into the emergency department with three days of fever, headache, malaise, persistent hiccups, and oral aphthae. His sodium was 124 mmol/L. By day five, he developed a scrotal ulcer — and the full picture of Behçet's disease finally snapped into focus. But here's what makes this case truly remarkable: not only did his neurological involvement present *concurrently* with his systemic BD diagnosis (which happens in fewer than 5% of patients), but his SIADH resolved completely with colchicine monotherapy alone. No steroids. No immunosuppressants. Just colchicine.

That should grab your attention.

## Why Concurrent Neuro-BD Is a Diagnostic Trap

Behçet's disease is a chronic, multisystem inflammatory disorder characterized by recurrent oral and genital ulcers, uveitis, and skin lesions. Neurological involvement — neuro-Behçet's disease — occurs in roughly 10–20% of patients, but here's the critical timing issue: major organ involvement like CNS lesions typically develops around **four years** after the initial BD diagnosis. When neuro-BD does occur, it most commonly presents as a self-limiting meningoencephalitis, with or without focal neurological deficits.

So when a patient presents with CNS symptoms *before* the full mucocutaneous picture has declared itself, your differential diagnosis naturally broadens — and that's exactly what happened here.

This patient initially had fever, headache, and oral aphthae. Those findings alone don't get you to BD. Genital ulcers didn't appear until day five. Without the complete clinical picture, the team reasonably pursued viral meningoencephalitis, bacterial meningitis, tuberculous meningitis, primary CNS malignancy, neurosarcoidosis, and autoimmune conditions. That's a broad workup driven by the absence of a unifying diagnosis — and it's a pattern many of us have fallen into.

## The ICBD Scoring System: What Got This Patient to "Definite BD"

Once the genital ulcer appeared on day five, the diagnostic puzzle pieces fell into place. The International Criteria for Behçet's Disease (ICBD) use a point-based scoring system:

- Oral aphthae: 2 points
- Genital aphthosis: 2 points
- Folliculitis-like rash: 1 point
- Ocular lesions: 2 points
- Neurological manifestations: 2 points
- Vascular lesions: 1 point
- Positive pathergy test: 1 point

A score of ≥4 indicates definite BD. This patient scored 5 points from mucocutaneous findings alone (oral aphthae + genital aphthosis + folliculitis-like rash), meeting criteria for definite BD before even factoring in his neurological involvement.

**Clinical pearl:** Genital aphthosis is considered a specific finding of BD, and its appearance should significantly raise your suspicion — but its absence early in the presentation does not rule the disease out.

## HLA-B51: Supportive, Not Diagnostic

HLA-B51 was positive in this patient, but here's the key point: **international BD criteria do not require HLA-B51 and rely mainly on clinical features.** HLA-B51 is associated with BD, particularly in populations along the Silk Road, but it is neither necessary nor sufficient for diagnosis. A positive result may support your clinical suspicion, but a negative result should not dissuade you if the clinical picture fits.

In this case, HLA-B51 testing did not contribute meaningfully to establishing the diagnosis. The diagnosis was clinical, built on mucocutaneous findings and CNS involvement after exclusion of other causes.

## Aseptic Meningitis with a Normal Brain MRI

Here's another trap. Brain MRI in this patient showed no abnormalities — not in the brainstem, not in the hypothalamus, nowhere. Yet CSF analysis revealed mildly elevated opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), increased protein (0.97 g/L), and mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). All infectious workups — bacterial, mycobacterial, HSV, VZV, cryptococcal — were negative.

Acute neuro-BD may present as aseptic meningitis without parenchymal brain lesions, and a normal brain MRI, particularly in the early phase of the disease, **does not exclude the diagnosis.** The typical BD lesion sites include the brainstem, but radiological findings may lag behind clinical presentation or may never appear in purely meningeal involvement.

**Clinical pearl:** In a patient with mucocutaneous findings suggestive of BD and new neurological symptoms, aseptic meningitis with a negative infectious workup and a normal MRI should still prompt serious consideration of neuro-BD.

## SIADH: The Endocrine Complication You're Not Looking For

This patient's initial sodium was 124 mmol/L. He was clinically euvolemic. Serum osmolality was low (267 mOsm/kg), urine osmolality was elevated (900 mOsm/kg), urinary sodium was high (88 mmol/L), and ADH was elevated at 22.4 pg/mL. Thyroid function and cortisol were normal. This met diagnostic criteria for SIADH.

SIADH is a well-known cause of hyponatremia, often resulting from CNS inflammation, malignancy, or medications. What's less appreciated is that acute neuro-BD, through CNS inflammation, may trigger dysregulated ADH secretion. The proposed mechanism involves interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine elevated in the CSF of patients with acute neuro-BD, which has been associated with increased ADH release. While IL-6 levels weren't directly measured in this case, the markedly elevated CRP (180 mg/L) may reflect an underlying IL-6-mediated inflammatory response.

Drug-induced SIADH has been reported in BD patients receiving cyclophosphamide, but SIADH occurring solely from CNS inflammation related to acute neuro-BD — without pharmacological triggers — remains poorly characterized. This case adds to the evidence that such an association exists.

## Two Overlooked Clues: Hiccups and Rhabdomyolysis

Two clinical features in this case deserve special attention because they're easy to dismiss:

**Persistent hiccups.** This patient had hiccups for three days before presentation. Previous reports have identified persistent hiccups as an important clinical clue to severe hyponatremia caused by SIADH secondary to CNS inflammation, including meningitis. If your patient has intractable hiccups, check their sodium.

**Rhabdomyolysis.** The initial CK was 1349 U/L with mild transamidase elevations, consistent with rhabdomyolysis — a recognized but often overlooked complication of severe hyponatremia. This isn't something we routinely investigate in hyponatremic patients, but it's a real complication that can occur and may contribute to the clinical picture.

**Clinical pearl:** In the context of neuroinflammatory conditions, apparently unrelated symptoms like hiccups or rhabdomyolysis may reflect underlying electrolyte disturbances. Don't dismiss them.

## Colchicine Monotherapy: Enough to Fix Everything?

Here's where this case gets provocative. The standard treatment for acute neuro-BD is corticosteroids, and most patients respond favorably. Systematic reviews have shown that colchicine is **ineffective** for acute neuro-BD and that immunosuppressive agents are generally required for adequate disease control. Colchicine has typically been used as an adjunct to corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive therapies, not as monotherapy.

Yet in this case, colchicine monotherapy led to resolution of mucocutaneous symptoms, neurological manifestations, and hyponatremia. His fever resolved the day after starting colchicine. Over two weeks, vertigo, malaise, hiccups, and all mucocutaneous lesions gradually improved. By day 16, sodium normalized to 136 mmol/L and ADH decreased to 1.8 pg/mL. CK returned to normal. He was discharged after one month with no recurrence over four years of follow-up.

The authors acknowledge that spontaneous recovery cannot be excluded — acute neuro-BD presenting solely with meningoencephalitis-like symptoms has been reported to resolve spontaneously. But the temporal relationship between colchicine initiation and symptom resolution is striking.

Colchicine exerts anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting neutrophil migration and activation and by suppressing cytokine production, including IL-1β and IL-6. If IL-6-mediated inflammation drove both the neuro-BD and the SIADH in this case, colchicine's suppression of IL-6 could explain the concurrent resolution of neurological symptoms and hyponatremia.

This is a single case report, not a trial. But it raises the question: could colchicine monotherapy be a viable option for acute neuro-BD with SIADH, particularly when corticosteroids are undesirable or contraindicated?

## The Diagnostic Lesson: Pattern Recognition Over Indiscriminate Testing

The authors themselves acknowledge that the absence of genital ulcers at presentation led to a broad differential diagnosis and a wide-ranging workup that included autoimmune serologies, serum ACE, and soluble IL-2 receptor — tests that ultimately did not meaningfully contribute to the diagnosis. The mildly elevated sIL-2R was a non-specific finding that initially raised concern for CNS lymphoma but proved to be an inflammatory epiphenomenon.

This is a common scenario in clinical medicine: when the full clinical pattern hasn't yet declared itself, we cast a wide net. But the lesson here is about the importance of **serial reassessment.** When this patient's genital ulcer appeared on day five, the entire diagnostic landscape shifted. The key was not abandoning the initial presentation but re-examining the patient and integrating new findings as they emerged.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Neuro-BD can present concurrently with systemic BD.** While neurological involvement typically emerges years after BD diagnosis, concurrent presentation occurs in fewer than 5% of patients. Maintain suspicion even when the timeline seems "too early."
- **A normal brain MRI does not exclude neuro-BD.** Acute neuro-BD may present as aseptic meningitis without parenchymal lesions, especially early in the disease course.
- **Use the ICBD scoring system.** A score of ≥4 confirms definite BD. This patient met criteria from mucocutaneous findings alone — oral aphthae (2) + genital aphthosis (2) + folliculitis-like rash (1) = 5 points.
- **HLA-B51 is supportive, not diagnostic.** International criteria rely on clinical features. Don't let a negative HLA-B51 dissuade you if the clinical picture fits.
- **SIADH may complicate acute neuro-BD through IL-6-mediated inflammation.** In patients with BD presenting with hyponatremia and non-specific CNS symptoms, consider SIADH as a potential manifestation of neuro-BD.
- **Persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis may signal severe hyponatremia.** Don't overlook these as unrelated complaints in neuroinflammatory conditions.
- **Colchicine monotherapy resolved neuro-BD and SIADH in this case.** While spontaneous recovery cannot be excluded and steroids remain standard of care, this case suggests colchicine may be a viable option when corticosteroids are contraindicated.
- **Serial reassessment matters.** The genital ulcer that appeared on day five transformed the diagnostic approach. When new findings emerge, re-examine your differential and streamline your workup.



# A Normal Brain MRI Does Not Exclude Acute Neuro-Behçet's Disease

## The Case That Stops You in Your Tracks

A man in his 60s walks into your emergency department with three days of fever, headache, malaise, persistent hiccups, and arthralgia. He's got oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rashes on his face and trunk. His sodium is 124 mmol/L. You're thinking infection, maybe something autoimmune, maybe malignancy — the differential is appropriately broad and appropriately uncomfortable. Then his headache and vertigo worsen on day 3, so you chase the CNS. Brain MRI? Completely normal. No brainstem lesions, no thalamic enhancement, no parenchymal signal abnormality — nothing. And yet, this patient has acute neuro-Behçet's disease, with CNS inflammation confirmed by CSF analysis, and he goes on to recover fully with colchicine monotherapy alone.

This case, published by Kakehi and colleagues, is a masterclass in why you cannot let a clean MRI talk you out of a diagnosis that the clinical picture is screaming at you to make. It also layers in two fascinating twists — concurrent SIADH and rhabdomyolysis as complications of profound hyponatraemia — that make it required reading for anyone managing complex inflammatory presentations in the emergency or inpatient setting.

## When the MRI Lies to You

Here's the trap: acute neuro-Behçet's disease most commonly presents as meningoencephalitis with parenchymal brain lesions, and the classic teaching is that those lesions love the brainstem and thalamus. So when your MRI comes back pristine, the natural cognitive reflex is to cross neuro-BD off your list. That reflex can be dangerous.

In this case, brain MRI showed no abnormalities at any of the typical BD lesion sites, including the hypothalamic region. The diagnosis of acute neuro-BD was instead established by CSF analysis revealing a lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL), elevated protein (0.97 g/L), and an aseptic pattern — after systematic exclusion of bacterial, viral (including HSV and VZV), mycobacterial, and cryptococcal etiologies. The opening pressure was mildly elevated at 17 cmH₂O. CSF cytology showed no malignant cells.

This is the critical teaching point: acute neuro-BD can present as aseptic meningitis without any parenchymal brain lesions visible on MRI, particularly in the early phase of disease. A normal brain MRI does not exclude the diagnosis. CSF analysis is the linchpin of the workup when imaging is unrevealing — and the CSF profile in neuro-BD typically shows a lymphocytic-predominant pleocytosis with elevated protein and negative infectious studies. The absence of neutrophilic predominance and negative cultures help distinguish it from bacterial meningitis, while the lymphocytic pattern and aseptic profile rule against the viral encephalitides that often sit higher on our differential when fever and headache coexist.

## Building the Diagnosis From the Ground Up

What makes this case particularly instructive is that BD and neuro-BD were diagnosed concurrently — an uncommon scenario. Neurological involvement in BD typically develops several years after the initial diagnosis, and neurological manifestations at presentation occur in fewer than 5% of patients. So the deck was stacked against recognizing neuro-BD from the outset.

The systemic BD diagnosis itself was built on mucocutaneous findings alone, using the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease: oral aphthae (2 points), genital aphthosis (2 points), and folliculitis-like rash (1 point) yielded a total score of 5, meeting the threshold of ≥4 for definite disease. Notably, the genital ulcer did not appear until day 5 of admission — meaning the BD diagnosis was not immediately apparent at presentation, and the broad initial differential was entirely appropriate. HLA-B51 was positive, but this did not contribute to establishing the diagnosis, as international criteria rely on clinical features rather than HLA typing.

The diagnostic approach here deserves emphasis as a model of systematic exclusion. The team prioritized infection initially, empirically covering with cefepime and then acyclovir while CSF studies were pending. When infectious workup came back negative, they evaluated for malignancy (brain MRI, CSF cytology, sIL-2R), sarcoidosis (serum ACE, lysozyme), and other autoimmune diseases (autoantibody screening) — all of which were either unrevealing or non-diagnostic. Only after this exclusion-based process, combined with the mucocutaneous constellation, did the diagnosis of acute neuro-BD crystallize. The authors themselves caution against indiscriminate testing, noting that mildly elevated sIL-2R and other screening markers should only be ordered when clinically supported and interpreted with caution.

## SIADH, Hiccups, and Rhabdomyolysis — A Trio Worth Knowing

The hyponatraemia in this case was not incidental. With a serum sodium of 124 mmol/L, serum osmolality of 267 mOsm/kg, urine osmolality of 900 mOsm/kg, urinary sodium of 88 mmol/L, euvolemic exam, and elevated ADH of 22.4 pg/mL — with normal thyroid and adrenal function — this was textbook SIADH. No hypertonic saline or vasopressin antagonists were needed; fluid restriction to less than 800 mL/day and careful sodium monitoring sufficed.

What elevates this from textbook to memorable are two underappreciated clinical features that should be on every clinician's radar. First, persistent hiccups — which this patient had from the outset — have been reported as a clinical clue to severe hyponatraemia from SIADH secondary to CNS inflammation, including meningitis. Hiccups are easy to dismiss as a nuisance symptom. In the right context, they are a red flag. Second, the markedly elevated CK of 1349 U/L revealed rhabdomyolysis — a recognized but routinely overlooked complication of severe hyponatraemia that is not routinely investigated. The authors measured CK as part of their initial evaluation of fever, arthralgia, and fatigue, which may reflect inflammatory or metabolic muscle damage. The lesson: in profound hyponatraemia, check a CK, and don't be surprised when it's elevated.

The proposed mechanistic link here is IL-6. Proinflammatory IL-6 is associated with increased ADH release, aberrant monocyte activation in BD leads to spontaneous IL-6 production, and elevated IL-6 has been detected in the CSF of patients with acute neuro-BD. While IL-6 was not directly measured in this case, the markedly elevated CRP of 180 mg/L may reflect an underlying IL-6-mediated inflammatory response. The authors are appropriately cautious, noting that the temporal association may reflect a non-specific response to CNS inflammation rather than a disease-specific mechanism. Still, the conceptual framework is useful: CNS inflammation drives cytokine release, cytokines dysregulate ADH, and SIADH ensues.

## Colchicine Monotherapy — Surprising but Notable

Perhaps the most surprising therapeutic element of this case is what was not used. Corticosteroids are the mainstay for acute neuro-BD, and most patients respond favorably. Systematic reviews suggest colchicine is ineffective for acute neuro-BD and that immunosuppressive agents are generally required. In previously reported cases, colchicine has been used as an adjunct, not as monotherapy.

Yet in this patient, colchicine alone — initiated on day 8 after the BD diagnosis was established — led to resolution of fever, improvement in appetite within 24 hours, gradual resolution of vertigo, malaise, hiccups, and all mucocutaneous lesions over 2 weeks, normalization of serum sodium to 136 mmol/L, and a decline in ADH to 1.8 pg/mL. No systemic corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants were used. The patient was discharged after one month and remained recurrence-free for 4 years on continued colchicine.

Spontaneous recovery cannot be fully excluded — acute neuro-BD presenting solely with meningoencephalitis-like symptoms has been reported to resolve without intervention. But the temporal relationship between colchicine initiation and multi-system improvement is compelling, and colchicine's known anti-inflammatory mechanism — inhibiting neutrophil migration and suppressing IL-1β and IL-6 production — provides a biologically plausible explanation for its effect on both the neuroinflammation and the SIADH. The authors' suggestion that colchicine may be a practical option when corticosteroids are undesirable or contraindicated is worth filing away, even as it contradicts conventional wisdom.

## When to Reimage

The case raises a practical question that the blog post description specifically asks about: when should you repeat the MRI? The authors note that a normal brain MRI, particularly in the early phase, does not exclude neuro-BD. They do not specify exactly when repeat imaging should occur, but the clinical implication is clear. If clinical suspicion for neuro-BD remains high and the initial MRI is normal — especially if CSF shows an aseptic lymphocytic pattern and infectious workup is negative — repeat MRI should be considered if symptoms persist, worsen, or new focal deficits emerge. The classic brainstem and thalamic lesions may declare themselves with time, and their appearance would solidify the diagnosis even as clinical improvement is already underway.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **A normal brain MRI does not exclude acute neuro-BD.** Acute neuro-BD can present as aseptic meningitis without parenchymal lesions, especially early in the disease course. CSF analysis — not imaging — is the key diagnostic tool when MRI is unrevealing.
- **The expected CSF profile in neuro-BD is a lymphocytic-predominant pleocytosis with elevated protein and negative infectious studies.** Diagnosis requires systematic exclusion of bacterial, viral, mycobacterial, cryptococcal, neoplastic, and other inflammatory etiologies.
- **Concurrent diagnosis of BD and neuro-BD is rare but real.** Neurological manifestations at BD presentation occur in fewer than 5% of patients. When mucocutaneous features of BD accompany CNS symptoms, consider neuro-BD even without a prior BD history.
- **Persistent hiccups can signal profound hyponatraemia from SIADH in the setting of CNS inflammation.** Do not dismiss them as a nuisance symptom.
- **Check a CK in severe hyponatraemia.** Rhabdomyolysis is a recognized but overlooked complication and was present in this case at 1349 U/L.
- **Colchicine monotherapy improved all manifestations in this case, including neuro-BD and SIADH.** While spontaneous recovery cannot be excluded and corticosteroids remain standard, colchicine may be a viable option when steroids are undesirable — a hypothesis-generating finding that warrants further study.
- **SIADH may complicate acute neuro-BD through IL-6-mediated dysregulation of ADH.** This association, while not definitively causal, provides a useful mechanistic framework and underscores the need to evaluate endocrine complications alongside neurological ones in newly diagnosed BD.



# SIADH as a Complication of CNS Inflammation: The IL-6 Connection and What It Means for Your Hyponatremic Patient

## The Hook: When Hyponatremia Is the loudest clue

A man in his 60s walks into your ED with fever, headache, oral aphthae, and a sodium of 124 mmol/L. You start running the usual infectious and endocrine workup. But here's the twist: his hyponatremia isn't a incidental lab finding — it's a window into the severity of his CNS inflammation, and it resolves not with hypertonic saline or vasopressin antagonists, but with colchicine monotherapy targeting the underlying inflammatory disease. This case, published by Kakehi and colleagues, forced the treating team to confront a question we don't ask often enough: when a neurologically ill patient develops SIADH, is the hyponatremia just a complication, or is it a barometer of how active the CNS inflammation really is?

## The Case in Brief

An Asian man in his 60s presented with three days of fever, headache, malaise, anorexia, persistent hiccups, and arthralgia. He had a history of untreated type 2 diabetes. On exam he was euvolemic, with oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rashes on his face and trunk. Initial labs showed sodium 124 mmol/L, CRP 180 mg/L, CK 1349 U/L, and normal renal function. Endocrine evaluation confirmed SIADH: low serum osmolality (267 mOsm/kg), elevated urine osmolality (900 mOsm/kg), elevated urinary sodium (88 mmol/L), elevated ADH (22.4 pg/mL), and normal thyroid and adrenal function.

On day 3 his headache and vertigo worsened. Brain MRI was normal. CSF showed mildly elevated opening pressure, elevated protein (0.97 g/L), and mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL) with negative bacterial, viral, and fungal studies — aseptic meningitis. On day 5 a painful scrotal ulcer appeared, and the diagnostic picture snapped into focus: oral aphthae, genital aphthosis, folliculitis-like rash — Behçet's disease (BD), with a total score of 5 on the International Criteria. HLA-B51 was positive. The diagnosis was concurrent systemic BD and acute neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD), complicated by SIADH.

The team started colchicine monotherapy on day 8. Fever resolved the next day. Over two weeks, all symptoms — vertigo, hiccups, mucocutaneous lesions — gradually improved. Sodium normalized to 136 mmol/L by day 16, and ADH fell to 1.8 pg/mL. No corticosteroids, no immunosuppressants, no hypertonic saline. Four years of colchicine follow-up with zero recurrence.

## The IL-6 Hypothesis: Connecting CNS Inflammation to ADH Dysregulation

This is where the pathophysiology gets genuinely interesting. We know that CNS inflammation can cause SIADH — it's a well-established association with meningitis, encephalitis, and other neuroinflammatory processes. But the *mechanism* has often been hand-waved as "inflammation irritates the hypothalamus." This case, and the literature around it, points to a more specific mediator: interleukin-6.

Here's the reasoning. IL-6 is a proinflammatory cytokine that has been shown to directly stimulate ADH release from the posterior pituitary. In BD specifically, there is aberrant monocyte activation leading to spontaneous IL-6 production, and elevated IL-6 levels have been detected in the CSF of patients with acute neuro-BD. So the proposed chain is: BD → CNS inflammation (neuro-BD) → elevated IL-6 in the CNS → stimulated ADH secretion → SIADH → hyponatremia.

Now, the authors didn't measure IL-6 directly in this patient's serum or CSF. But they had a surrogate marker staring them in the face: a CRP of 180 mg/L. CRP is primarily driven by IL-6-mediated hepatic synthesis, so a CRP that dramatically elevated is consistent with significant IL-6 activity. This doesn't prove the mechanism, but the temporal association is compelling — as the inflammation resolved with colchicine (which suppresses IL-6 among other cytokines), the SIADH resolved in parallel.

The broader principle here extends well beyond Behçet's. Any CNS inflammatory process that generates significant IL-6 — bacterial meningitis, viral encephalitis, autoimmune encephalitis, neurosarcoidosis, CNS vasculitis — has the potential to drive ADH secretion through this pathway. When you see SIADH in a neurologically ill patient, you should be asking not just "what's causing the hyponatremia?" but "how active is the underlying CNS inflammation, and am I treating it adequately?"

## Why This Case Was Hard to Diagnose

Several factors conspired to make this diagnosis difficult. First, neuro-BD typically develops years after the initial BD diagnosis — major organ involvement usually appears around 4 years out, and neurological manifestations at initial presentation occur in fewer than 5% of patients. So seeing BD and neuro-BD diagnosed concurrently is rare, and it's not going to be the first thing on your differential.

Second, the brain MRI was normal. Acute neuro-BD most commonly presents as meningoencephalitis with parenchymal lesions, particularly in the brainstem. But it can also present as aseptic meningitis without any parenchymal findings, and a normal MRI in the early phase does not exclude the diagnosis. This is a critical point: in a patient with clinical signs of CNS inflammation and a normal MRI, you still need to pursue CSF analysis.

Third, the genital ulcer — arguably the most specific finding for BD — didn't appear until day 5. Before that, the team was appropriately working through a broad differential: viral meningoencephalitis, bacterial meningitis, tuberculous meningitis, primary CNS malignancy, neurosarcoidosis, and autoimmune conditions. They ran a panel of autoimmune serologies, ACE, and soluble IL-2 receptor as screening tests, most of which were unhelpful. The authors themselves acknowledge that this broad testing was driven by diagnostic uncertainty early on, and they caution against indiscriminate testing when clinical features don't support specific diagnoses.

## SIADH Diagnostic Criteria: A Quick Refresher

Since this case hinges on a SIADH diagnosis, let's review the criteria the team used. The diagnosis requires:

- Hypo-osmolality (serum osmolality <275 mOsm/kg) — this patient had 267
- Inappropriately concentrated urine (urine osmolality >100 mOsm/kg, typically much higher) — this patient had 900
- Clinical euvolemia — no edema, no dehydration, no signs of heart failure or cirrhosis
- Elevated urinary sodium (>40 mmol/L on normal salt and water intake) — this patient had 88
- Normal adrenal function — cortisol was 42 µg/dL, well above the threshold
- Normal thyroid function — TSH and free T4 were normal
- Absence of other causes: no diuretics, no recent fluid manipulation, no renal failure

This patient met every criterion cleanly. ADH was directly measured and elevated at 22.4 pg/mL despite hypo-osmolar hyponatremia, which is the pathophysiological hallmark — ADH should be suppressed in the setting of low serum osmolality, but in SIADH it is inappropriately elevated.

## Two Overlooked Clues: Hiccups and Rhabdomyolysis

Two clinical features in this case deserve special attention because they're the kind of things that get dismissed as incidental but are actually diagnostic clues.

Persistent hiccups. This patient had hiccups for days before admission. Prior reports have documented that persistent hiccups can be an important clinical clue to severe hyponatremia secondary to CNS inflammation, including meningitis. The mechanism relates to irritation of the medullary hiccup center, which can be driven by both the hyponatremia itself and the underlying CNS inflammation. When a patient presents with intractable hiccups and neurological symptoms, check the sodium.

Rhabdomyolysis. The CK was 1349 U/L on presentation. Rhabdomyolysis is a recognized but frequently overlooked complication of severe hyponatremia. It's not something we routinely investigate in hyponatremic patients, but it can occur, and it can complicate fluid management if you're not aware of it. In this case, the CK normalized as the sodium corrected, suggesting the hyponatremia was the driver rather than independent muscle inflammation from the BD.

## Treatment: Why Colchicine Monotherapy Is Remarkable Here

This is the part of the case that should make you sit up. Corticosteroids are the mainstay of treatment for acute neuro-BD. Systematic reviews have found colchicine ineffective for acute neuro-BD as monotherapy, and most reported cases use colchicine as an adjunct to corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants. So the fact that this patient resolved completely on colchicine alone — mucocutaneous symptoms, neurological symptoms, and SIADH all resolving without steroids — is remarkable.

Is this just spontaneous recovery? The authors acknowledge that possibility. Spontaneous resolution has been reported in neuro-BD cases presenting solely with meningoencephalitis-like symptoms. But the temporal relationship is hard to ignore: fever resolved the day after colchicine was started, and the parallel improvement of all three domains — mucocutaneous, neurological, and endocrine — over the subsequent two weeks suggests a therapeutic effect rather than coincidence.

The mechanistic rationale is plausible. Colchicine inhibits neutrophil migration and activation, and it suppresses production of IL-1β and IL-6. If IL-6 is driving both the CNS inflammation and the ADH dysregulation, then suppressing IL-6 with colchicine could address both the neuro-BD and the SIADH simultaneously. This is speculative based on a single case, but it's mechanistically coherent and clinically important — particularly when corticosteroids are contraindicated or undesirable, as they were in this patient with poorly controlled diabetes.

## The Big Picture: Hyponatremia as a Barometer of CNS Inflammation

Here's the take-home that extends beyond Behçet's disease. When you encounter a patient with CNS inflammation and hyponatremia, the SIADH is not just a metabolic complication to be managed with fluid restriction or hypertonic saline. It is a signal. The presence of SIADH tells you that the inflammatory process is active enough to dysregulate hypothalamic-pituitary function, and the severity of the hyponatremia may correlate with the intensity of the inflammatory drive. A sodium of 124 with an ADH of 22.4 and a CRP of 180 is telling you something about the vigor of the inflammation.

This means two things for your practice. First, when you see SIADH in a neurologically ill patient, make sure you've identified and are treating the underlying CNS inflammatory process — not just managing the sodium. Second, monitor the sodium as a marker of treatment response. In this case, the sodium tracked with clinical improvement: as the neuro-BD was treated, the ADH normalized and the sodium corrected. That parallel resolution is not just reassuring — it's diagnostic. If you're treating the CNS inflammation and the SIADH isn't improving, you may need to reassess whether your treatment is adequate.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **CNS inflammation can cause SIADH through IL-6-mediated ADH stimulation.** This isn't limited to Behçet's — any neuroinflammatory process (meningitis, encephalitis, autoimmune neuroinflammation) can trigger this pathway. When you see SIADH in a neurologically ill patient, think about the mechanism, not just the sodium number.

- **A normal brain MRI does not exclude acute neuro-BD.** Acute neuro-BD can present as aseptic meningitis without parenchymal lesions, especially early in the disease course. If your clinical suspicion is high, CSF analysis is essential regardless of imaging.

- **Neuro-BD at initial presentation is rare but real.** BD and neuro-BD diagnosed concurrently is uncommon, but when mucocutaneous features accompany neurological symptoms, don't wait for the typical 4-year lag to consider the diagnosis.

- **Persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis can be clues to severe hyponatremia.** Don't dismiss hiccups in a neurologically ill patient, and consider checking CK in patients with profound hyponatremia.

- **CRP may serve as a surrogate for IL-6 activity.** When IL-6 isn't measured directly, a markedly elevated CRP can support the hypothesis of IL-6-driven inflammation contributing to SIADH.

- **Colchicine monotherapy resolved neuro-BD and SIADH in this case.** While this is a single case and spontaneous recovery can't be excluded, the mechanistic rationale (IL-6 suppression) and temporal response make colchicine a consideration when steroids are contraindicated. This is hypothesis-generating, not practice-changing.

- **Track the sodium as a marker of treatment response.** If the SIADH resolves as the CNS inflammation is treated, you've confirmed the link. If it doesn't, reconsider your diagnosis or treatment adequacy.



# Hiccups and Rhabdomyolysis: The Underappreciated Clinical Clues to Profound Hyponatremia

## The Case That Stops You in Your Tracks

A man in his 60s walks into the ED with fever, headache, malaise, arthralgia, oral aphthae, and — here's where you lean forward — **persistent hiccups**. Three days of symptoms. Vital signs show tachycardia at 119 bpm and a temperature of 38.1°C. Labs come back with a sodium of **124 mmol/L**, a CK of **1349 U/L**, and a CRP through the roof at 180 mg/L. His brain MRI? Completely normal. His CSF? Aseptic meningitis — mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, mildly elevated protein, everything else negative.

Oh, and he also has genital ulcers that appear on day 5, folliculitis-like rashes, and oral aphthae. That's Behçet's disease, diagnosed concurrently with neuro-Behçet's and complicated by SIADH. All three diagnosed at the same time — something that happens in fewer than 5% of BD patients.

But what makes this case genuinely instructive isn't the rarity of concurrent BD and neuro-BD. It's the **hiccups and the rhabdomyolysis** — two manifestations of profound hyponatremia that almost certainly would have been dismissed or overlooked in many EDs and inpatient settings. These are the kind of clues that separate pattern recognizers from the rest of us.

## Intractable Hiccups: Not a Nuisance, a Neurogenic Red Flag

Let's be honest — when a patient complains of hiccups, most of us file it under "annoying but not dangerous." We might think GERD, maybe some irritation from an NG tube, or just... idiosyncratic. But persistent, intractable hiccups in the setting of hyponatremia should set off a very different alarm.

The pathophysiology here is straightforward but underappreciated. Hyponatremia causes cerebral edema as water shifts into brain cells along the osmotic gradient. The structures involved in the hiccup reflex arc — primarily the medulla and brainstem — are exquisitely sensitive to these osmotic shifts. When brainstem neurons become edematous and dysfunctional, the hiccup reflex, which involves a complex pathway from the medulla through the phrenic and vagus nerves, can become pathologically activated. This is neurogenic dysfunction from osmotic injury, not gastrointestinal irritation.

Previous case reports have specifically identified persistent hiccups as a clinical clue to severe hyponatremia secondary to CNS inflammation, including meningitis. The mechanism is the same in both contexts — the medullary structures governing the hiccup reflex are disrupted by the combination of cerebral edema from hyponatremia and the local inflammatory milieu.

The problem in clinical practice is twofold. First, hiccups are rarely volunteered by patients as a chief complaint — they get buried in the review of systems or mentioned almost as an afterthought, as happened in this case where persistent hiccups appeared alongside fever and headache. Second, even when mentioned, clinicians tend to attribute them to whatever is most convenient — the fever, the infection, the medication, anything other than the sodium. This patient had his hiccups documented on presentation, and there's no evidence from the case report that they were initially recognized as a marker of the severity of his electrolyte disturbance.

## Rhabdomyolysis: The Osmotic Injury Nobody Checks For

Now here's where it gets even more interesting. This patient's CK was 1349 U/L on initial labs. That's not a mild elevation — that's rhabdomyolysis. And it was found because someone measured CK as part of a fever-and-arthralgia workup, not because anyone was specifically looking for hyponatremia-induced muscle injury.

The pathophysiology of hyponatremia-induced rhabdomyolysis involves osmotic shifts damaging muscle cell membranes. When serum sodium falls rapidly or profoundly, the osmotic gradient doesn't just affect the brain — it affects every cell in the body. Skeletal muscle cells undergo osmotic swelling, membrane instability, and eventual rupture, releasing intracellular contents including CK and myoglobin. The lower and faster the sodium drops, the greater the membrane damage.

But why is this missed so often? Because **CK is not a routine part of the hyponatremia workup**. When we see a sodium of 124, we check serum osmolality, urine osmolality, urine sodium, TSH, cortisol — the standard SIADH panel. We do not routinely check CK. And yet, rhabdomyolysis is a recognized complication of profound hyponatremia. It's hiding in plain sight, right there in the pathophysiology, but we don't look for it because our mental model of hyponatremia complications centers almost exclusively on the brain — cerebral edema, seizures, coma — while the muscle gets ignored.

In this case, the CK elevation could have been attributed to multiple other causes — the patient had arthralgia, general fatigue, fever, and an inflammatory condition that can cause myositis. Behçet's disease itself can cause myositis. The differential for an elevated CK in this patient was broad. But the temporal relationship with severe hyponatremia (sodium 124, CK 1349) and the resolution of both with correction of the sodium and treatment of the underlying condition strongly suggests that the rhabdomyolysis was, at least in significant part, a direct complication of the electrolyte disturbance.

## The Overlap Problem: When Multiple Etiologies Compete

This case illustrates a diagnostic trap that goes beyond hiccups and rhabdomyolysis individually. The patient had an inflammatory disease (BD) capable of causing myositis, a neurological condition (neuro-BD) causing CNS inflammation, and an endocrine complication (SIADH) causing profound hyponatremia — all simultaneously. Each of these could independently explain individual symptoms. The hiccups could be neurological. The CK elevation could be inflammatory myositis. The hyponatremia could be... well, the hyponatremia *was* SIADH, but that wasn't immediately obvious either.

The point is that in complex patients, we tend to attribute each finding to the most obvious system involved. Neurological symptom → neurological disease. Muscle enzyme elevation → muscle disease. Electrolyte abnormality → endocrine or renal issue. But what if the unifying explanation is simpler and more dangerous than the individual pieces suggest? What if the hiccups, the rhabdomyolysis, and the neurological symptoms are all downstream consequences of a sodium of 124 driven by CNS inflammation?

This is where the discussion of IL-6 becomes relevant. The authors note that IL-6, a proinflammatory cytokine frequently elevated in neuroinflammatory disorders, is associated with increased ADH release. In BD, aberrant monocyte activation leads to spontaneous IL-6 production, and elevated IL-6 levels have been detected in the CSF of patients with acute neuro-BD. The markedly elevated CRP in this patient (180 mg/L) may reflect this IL-6-mediated inflammatory response, which in turn drives ADH secretion, which causes the hyponatremia, which causes the hiccups and rhabdomyolysis. One inflammatory cascade, multiple downstream manifestations. The authors couldn't measure IL-6 directly, so this mechanism remains plausible but unproven — and they appropriately acknowledge this limitation.

## A Practical Framework for Evaluating the Hyponatremic Patient

So how should this change our practice? When evaluating a patient with hyponatremia, particularly moderate to severe (≤125 mmol/L), consider the following:

- **Ask specifically about hiccups.** Don't wait for the patient to mention them. Persistent hiccups in a hyponatremic patient suggest brainstem involvement from cerebral edema and should heighten your concern about the severity of the disturbance. They may also signal CNS inflammation as the underlying cause of SIADH, as in this case.
- **Check CK in patients with profound hyponatremia**, especially if there's any complaint of myalgias, arthralgias, weakness, or dark urine. The osmotic injury to muscle membranes is real, and missing rhabdomyolysis in a patient who already has a deranged electrolyte milieu adds renal injury to an already complicated picture.
- **Don't dismiss apparently unrelated symptoms.** Hiccups, muscle pain, and fatigue may all be downstream manifestations of the same electrolyte disturbance. When multiple "weird" symptoms cluster together, look for the unifying mechanism rather than assigning each to a different organ system.
- **Treat the sodium as a clinical emergency, not just a laboratory value.** A sodium of 124 with hiccups and rhabdomyolysis is not a number to correct leisurely over several days while you work up the underlying cause. The complications are already happening. This patient was managed with fluid restriction alone — no hypertonic saline, no vasopressin antagonists — and the sodium normalized to 136 mmol/L by day 16. That's appropriate given the clinical course, but the decision to avoid rapid correction was deliberate and monitored, not accidental.
- **Recognize that SIADH can be a manifestation of CNS inflammation, not just a standalone endocrine diagnosis.** When SIADH appears in the context of fever, headache, and aseptic meningitis, the CNS inflammation *is* the cause — the ADH secretion is the mechanism, not the primary disease.

## The Treatment Lesson: Colchicine Monotherapy and the Question of Steroid-Sparing

One more point worth highlighting from this case. The patient was treated with colchicine monotherapy — no systemic corticosteroids, no other immunosuppressants — and had complete resolution of mucocutaneous symptoms, neurological manifestations, and hyponatremia. The sodium normalized, the ADH dropped from 22.4 pg/mL to 1.8 pg/mL, the CK normalized, and there was no recurrence over 4 years of follow-up on colchicine alone.

This is striking because corticosteroids are the mainstay of treatment for acute neuro-BD. Colchicine is widely used for mucocutaneous BD, but systematic reviews have shown it's ineffective for neuro-BD, and immunosuppression is generally considered necessary for adequate disease control. The authors appropriately note that spontaneous recovery cannot be excluded, and one case does not change standard practice. But the mechanism is at least biologically plausible — colchicine inhibits neutrophil migration and suppresses IL-1β and IL-6 production, and if IL-6 is driving both the ADH release (and thus the SIADH) and the neuroinflammation, then colchicine's anti-IL-6 effects could address both the underlying inflammation and the endocrine complication simultaneously.

The takeaway isn't that colchicine should replace steroids for neuro-BD. It's that in patients where corticosteroids are contraindicated or undesirable — and in the ED, we see plenty of those — colchicine monotherapy may be a viable bridge or alternative for the acute phase, and it deserves further study.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Persistent hiccups in a hyponatremic patient** reflect brainstem osmotic dysfunction, not gastrointestinal irritation. Ask about them directly and take them seriously as a marker of severity.
- **Rhabdomyolysis is a recognized but routinely missed complication of profound hyponatremia.** Check CK in patients with sodium ≤125 mmol/L, especially with any musculoskeletal complaints.
- **In complex patients with multiple concurrent diagnoses** (as in BD with neuro-BD and SIADH), look for the unifying mechanism — IL-6-mediated inflammation driving ADH release is one such mechanism — rather than assigning each symptom to a different organ system.
- **SIADH in the setting of CNS inflammation** is not an incidental endocrine finding. The inflammation *is* the cause. The CNS inflammatory process drives the ADH dysregulation.
- **A sodium of 124 is not just a number.** When it presents with hiccups, rhabdomyolysis, and neurological symptoms, the complications are already manifest. Treat the severity as a clinical emergency even as you investigate the underlying etiology.
- **Colchicine monotherapy resolved all manifestations in this case**, including neuro-BD and SIADH, without steroids — a finding that challenges standard treatment paradigms and warrants further investigation, particularly for patients where corticosteroids are contraindicated.

## Final Thought

The next time you see a patient with hyponatremia and hiccups, don't reach for the chlorpromazine. Reach for the CK level and think about what the sodium is actually doing to the brainstem and the muscle cells. The electrolyte disturbance is never just an electrolyte disturbance — it's a whole-body osmotic event, and the symptoms that seem unrelated are often the most direct evidence of its severity.



# Colchicine Monotherapy for Acute Neuro-Behçet's: Challenging the Steroid-First Paradigm

## The Case That Shouldn't Have Worked

A man in his 60s walks in with fever, headache, oral aphthae, folliculitis-like rashes, and a sodium of 124. Over the next several days, he develops worsening headache and vertigo, aseptic meningitis on CSF analysis, a painful scrotal ulcer, and confirmed SIADH. He gets diagnosed with concurrent systemic Behçet's disease and acute neuro-Behçet's disease. Standard teaching says you reach for corticosteroids — often high-dose — and possibly additional immunosuppressants. Instead, this team started colchicine alone. Everything resolved. Fever broke the next day. Sodium normalized by day 16. Neurological symptoms gone. Mucocutaneous lesions healed. No steroids, no cyclophosphamide, no biologics. Four years of follow-up with colchicine, no recurrence.

If you're like me, you read that and think: that shouldn't have worked. Systematic reviews say colchicine is ineffective for neuro-BD. Corticosteroids are the mainstay. Immunosuppressive agents are generally required. So what happened here — and should it change how we think about mild acute neuro-BD?

## What Makes This Case Unusual on Multiple Levels

Before we get to the treatment paradox, let's appreciate just how many unusual features are packed into this single presentation.

First, neuro-BD was diagnosed concurrently with systemic BD. That's rare. Major organ involvement like CNS disease typically surfaces around four years after the initial BD diagnosis, and neurological manifestations at presentation occur in fewer than 5% of patients. Most clinicians seeing oral aphthae and a folliculitis-like rash won't be thinking "Behçet's with CNS involvement" on day one — especially when the genital ulcer hadn't even appeared yet.

Second, the brain MRI was completely normal. Acute neuro-BD classically involves the brainstem, and we're taught to look for parenchymal lesions. But this patient had aseptic meningitis without any radiological abnormality. The authors remind us that a normal brain MRI, particularly early in the disease course, does not exclude neuro-BD. This is a critical point: if you're waiting for MRI findings to confirm neuro-BD, you'll miss it.

Third, SIADH complicated the picture. The patient presented with a sodium of 124, low serum osmolality (267), elevated urine osmolality (900), elevated urinary sodium (88), and an ADH level of 22.4 pg/mL despite hypo-osmolality. No medications known to trigger SIADH. No malignancy. No pulmonary disease. The SIADH arose purely from CNS inflammation related to acute neuro-BD — a phenomenon that has been described with other forms of CNS inflammation but remains poorly characterized in neuro-BD specifically. The authors propose an IL-6-mediated mechanism: aberrant monocyte activation in BD drives spontaneous IL-6 production, and IL-6 is known to stimulate ADH release. They didn't measure IL-6 directly, but the markedly elevated CRP (180 mg/L) is consistent with an IL-6-driven inflammatory state.

And here's a clinical pearl worth filing away: this patient had persistent hiccups as part of his presentation. Persistent hiccups have been reported as a clinical clue to severe hyponatraemia from SIADH secondary to CNS inflammation, including meningitis. It's the kind of symptom that's easy to dismiss as a nuisance — but in the right context, it's a red flag. The same goes for the elevated CK (1349 U/L), which turned out to be rhabdomyolysis — a recognized but routinely under-investigated complication of profound hyponatraemia.

## The Treatment Paradox

Now the central question. Corticosteroids are the accepted mainstay for acute neuro-BD. Colchicine is established for mucocutaneous BD but systematic reviews have found it ineffective for neuro-BD specifically. In reported cases where colchicine appears in neuro-BD treatment, it has been used as an adjunct to corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants — never as monotherapy.

Yet in this case, colchicine alone resolved everything: mucocutaneous lesions, neurological symptoms, and SIADH. The improvement was temporally compelling — fever resolved the day after colchicine was started, and all symptoms gradually resolved over two weeks with sodium normalizing by day 16.

So we have to ask honestly: was this genuine therapeutic benefit, or spontaneous resolution of a self-limiting illness?

## Spontaneous Resolution: A Real Phenomenon

The authors acknowledge this possibility explicitly. Spontaneous resolution has been reported in cases of neuro-BD presenting solely with meningoencephalitis-like symptoms — that is, without parenchymal brain lesions or focal neurological deficits. This patient's presentation fits that profile: aseptic meningitis with a normal brain MRI, no cranial nerve involvement, no focal deficits, no parenchymal lesions. He had headache and vertigo, but these are non-specific.

If spontaneous recovery was the trajectory, colchicine may have simply been along for the ride. The natural history of this particular phenotype of neuro-BD — isolated meningoencephalitis without parenchymal involvement — may include self-limited episodes. We don't have great data on this, because most of what we know about neuro-BD outcomes comes from patients treated with corticosteroids, which muddies the waters.

## But Don't Dismiss Colchicine Too Quickly

Here's the counterargument, and it's mechanistically plausible. Colchicine inhibits neutrophil migration and activation and suppresses production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β and IL-6. If IL-6 is a driver of both the CNS inflammation and the ADH dysregulation in this patient's SIADH, then colchicine's IL-6 suppression could theoretically address both problems simultaneously. The temporal relationship — fever resolving the day after colchicine initiation — is at least consistent with a therapeutic effect, though clearly not proof of causation.

The SIADH resolution is particularly interesting. SIADH in this context is presumably driven by CNS inflammation. If colchicine suppresses that inflammation, ADH secretion normalizes, and sodium corrects. The authors managed SIADH conservatively with fluid restriction alone — no hypertonic saline, no vaptans — and sodium rose steadily from 124 to 136 over 16 days. This is a reasonable correction rate for chronic hyponatraemia, and the fact that it paralleled the resolution of neurological symptoms supports the idea that the underlying inflammatory driver was being addressed.

## The Evidence Gap

Let's be honest about what we don't know. There are no randomized trials of colchicine monotherapy for neuro-BD. The systematic reviews showing ineffectiveness are based on limited data, and colchicine in those contexts may have been evaluated in patients with more severe or parenchymal disease. The evidence base for colchicine in BD overall is strongest for mucocutaneous manifestations and for prevention of flares. For neuro-BD specifically, the data are sparse, and the patients studied may not represent the mild end of the spectrum that this case occupies.

What this case does is raise a question that the existing evidence base can't fully answer: is there a subset of acute neuro-BD patients — specifically those with isolated aseptic meningitis, no parenchymal lesions, and no focal deficits — who might respond to colchicine alone? The answer from this case is "possibly," which is not the same as "yes," but it's enough to warrant consideration.

## When to Escalate: A Framework

This is where clinical judgment matters most. The authors don't suggest colchicine should replace corticosteroids as first-line for all neuro-BD. What they suggest — and what I think is reasonable — is that colchicine may be a practical option in select patients, particularly when corticosteroids are undesirable or contraindicated.

So what should guide the decision? Several factors seem relevant based on this case and the broader literature.

Parenchymal brain involvement is the biggest red flag. This patient's MRI was normal. Brainstem lesions, focal neurological deficits, or evidence of tissue injury on imaging should push you toward corticosteroids without hesitation. The risk of permanent neurological damage from inadequately treated parenchymal neuro-BD is real, and colchicine is not the tool for that job.

Severity of CNS inflammation matters. This patient had a mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL) and modestly elevated CSF protein (0.97 g/L). More aggressive CSF profiles — higher cell counts, neutrophilic predominance, markedly elevated protein — should lower your threshold for escalation.

Clinical trajectory is critical. This patient improved within 24 hours of colchicine initiation. If you start colchicine and the patient is not clearly improving within a few days — or is worsening — you need to escalate. Colchicine is not a drug you persist with in the face of clinical deterioration from CNS inflammation.

Comorbidities that make steroids dangerous were present here: uncontrolled diabetes (HbA1c 8.2%, glucose 14.2 mmol/L) and limited health literacy. In a patient like this, a steroid-sparing approach has genuine appeal. The risk-benefit calculus shifts when steroids could precipitate hyperglycaemic emergencies or when adherence to complex immunosuppressive regimens is unlikely.

The presence of complications like SIADH is a double-edged consideration. On one hand, severe hyponatraemia with SIADH adds urgency. On the other, if the SIADH is inflammatory and the inflammation responds to colchicine, conservative management with fluid restriction may suffice — as it did here. But a sodium that's dropping despite appropriate conservative management, or neurological symptoms from hyponatraemia itself, should prompt escalation.

## What I Take From This Case

This case doesn't overturn the steroid-first paradigm, and it shouldn't. But it does something valuable: it identifies a potential niche where colchicine monotherapy might be reasonable, and it forces us to think more precisely about which neuro-BD patients actually need high-dose immunosuppression versus which ones might be managed more conservatively.

The idea that all acute neuro-BD requires immediate corticosteroids may be overly broad. Isolated aseptic meningitis without parenchymal involvement, particularly in patients where steroids are problematic, may represent a phenotype where colchicine — with very close monitoring and a low threshold to escalate — is a defensible choice. This case can't prove that, but it provides a proof of concept that deserves further study.

The SIADH association is the other piece I'll carry forward. If you see a patient with known or suspected BD who presents with headache, fever, and hyponatraemia, neuro-BD with SIADH should be on your differential. A normal MRI doesn't exclude it. Persistent hiccups and elevated CK are not incidental — they may be clues to the severity of the electrolyte disturbance. And if the SIADH is inflammatory, treating the underlying inflammation — whether with colchicine or steroids — may be the most effective way to correct the sodium.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Neuro-BD can present concurrently with systemic BD and with a normal brain MRI.** Don't let a clean scan exclude the diagnosis in a patient with mucocutaneous findings and CNS symptoms. CSF analysis showing aseptic meningitis is key.
- **SIADH may complicate acute neuro-BD through IL-6-mediated ADH stimulation, even without pharmacological triggers.** Consider this mechanism in BD patients with hyponatraemia and CNS symptoms.
- **Persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis can be clinical clues to profound hyponatraemia from SIADH.** Don't dismiss them. Check the sodium and the CK.
- **Colchicine monotherapy resolved mucocutaneous symptoms, neurological manifestations, and SIADH in this case — but spontaneous resolution of self-limiting meningoencephalitis cannot be excluded.** The evidence base for colchicine in neuro-BD is insufficient to recommend it as standard first-line therapy.
- **Colchicine may be a reasonable steroid-sparing option in select neuro-BD patients:** those with isolated aseptic meningitis, no parenchymal lesions on MRI, no focal deficits, mild CSF findings, and clinical improvement within days of initiation — particularly when corticosteroids are contraindicated.
- **Have a low threshold to escalate to corticosteroids or immunosuppression** if there is parenchymal involvement, severe CNS inflammation, clinical deterioration, or failure to improve promptly on colchicine.
- **Conservative management of SIADH with fluid restriction can be effective when the underlying inflammatory driver is treated.** Monitor sodium correction carefully — avoid rapid correction.



# Diagnostic Parsimony in Complex Presentations: Lessons from a Case That Triggered an Indiscriminate Workup

## The Setup

A man in his 60s walks into the ED with three days of fever, headache, malaise, arthralgia, persistent hiccups, and oral aphthae. His sodium is 124. He's euvolemic. His CRP is through the roof at 180 mg/L. Your brain immediately runs in a dozen directions — meningitis? Encephalitis? Sarcoidosis? Lymphoma? Autoimmune? — and you start ordering everything to cover your bases. Sound familiar?

This is exactly what happened in a case reported by Kakehi and colleagues, and it's a near-perfect illustration of a trap we all fall into: when the presentation is non-specific and the diagnosis is uncertain, we reach for broad, exclusion-based testing rather than letting the clinical picture guide us. The eventual diagnosis — neuro-Behçet's disease complicated by SIADH — was not made by any of the expensive panels sent early in the workup. It was made by *watching the patient* and recognizing a mucocutaneous pattern as it unfolded over five days.

## What Went Down

The patient presented with fever, headache, oral aphthae, folliculitis-like rashes, and profound hyponatremia (Na 124 mmol/L). He was euvolemic. SIADH was confirmed: low serum osmolality (267 mOsm/kg), elevated urine osmolality (900 mOsm/kg), elevated urinary sodium (88 mmol/L), and inappropriately elevated ADH (22.4 pg/mL). So far, this is a clean SIADH workup — well done.

But then the diagnostic reasoning starts to broaden. On day 3, his headache and vertigo worsened. CSF was obtained: mildly elevated opening pressure, protein 0.97 g/L, mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL), and *everything else negative* — bacterial cultures, acid-fast stain, TB DNA, HSV, VZV, cryptococcal antigen, cytology. Brain MRI was normal. Serum ACE was sent. Soluble IL-2 receptor was sent. Autoimmune serologies were sent. Hepatitis B and C panels, HTLV-1 antibody — all sent.

Most of these tests came back normal or non-contributory. The sIL-2R was mildly elevated at 704 U/mL (reference 122–496), which the authors themselves acknowledged was non-specific and likely reflected inflammatory activity rather than lymphoma. ACE was normal. Autoimmune markers were negative. The viral and bacterial CSF panels were negative. None of this narrowed the diagnosis.

What *did* make the diagnosis? On day 5, a painful scrotal ulcer appeared. Combined with the oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rash that had been present since day 1, the patient now met the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease with a score of 5 (≥4 = definite BD): oral aphthae (2 points), genital aphthosis (2 points), folliculitis-like rash (1 point). HLA-B51 was positive but, importantly, is not part of the diagnostic criteria.

## The Core Problem: Exclusion-Based Reasoning vs. Pattern Recognition

Here's the uncomfortable truth: the diagnostic answer was partially visible on day 1. The oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rash were there from the start. But because the patient didn't yet have genital ulcers, and because the neurologic symptoms and hyponatremia dominated the clinical picture, the team defaulted to an exclusion-based approach — ruling out infections, malignancies, and autoimmune conditions one by one rather than building a positive case for BD from the mucocutaneous findings already in front of them.

This is a deeply relatable cognitive pattern. When faced with a sick patient with nonspecific symptoms and a dangerous-sounding problem like hyponatremia plus headache, we anchor on the worst-case scenarios — HSV encephalitis, bacterial meningitis, CNS lymphoma — and order broadly to exclude them. Empiric cefepime was started on day 1. Acyclovir was added on day 3. These are defensible moves in a febrile patient with neurologic symptoms and a CSF profile that could be early meningitis. But the *panel of screening tests* — ACE, sIL-2R, autoimmune serologies — was not driven by specific clinical features. It was driven by diagnostic uncertainty.

## The Limitations of the Tests That Were Sent

Let's be precise about why so much of this workup was low-yield:

**Serum ACE** has well-documented poor sensitivity for sarcoidosis — it can be normal in active disease and elevated in many non-sarcoid conditions. In this patient, it was normal. Even if it had been elevated, it would not have been diagnostic. Sending it without radiographic or clinical features of sarcoidosis (bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy, uveitis, pulmonary infiltrates, characteristic skin lesions) is a classic example of a test that cannot move your pretest probability meaningfully in either direction.

**Soluble IL-2 receptor** is a more interesting case. It can be a useful marker for certain T-cell malignancies and for hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, but mild elevations are non-specific and occur in any inflammatory state. A value of 704 U/mL in a patient with a CRP of 180 mg/L is exactly what you'd expect from systemic inflammation — it tells you the patient is inflamed, which you already knew. Interpreting it as a potential marker for CNS lymphoma in a patient with a normal brain MRI and negative CSF cytology was a reach.

**Broad CSF infectious panels** (HSV, VZV, cryptococcal antigen, acid-fast stain, TB DNA) are more justifiable in a patient with aseptic meningitis and fever, and the authors rightly note that these were sent as part of a reasonable exclusion process. But the key teaching point is that once these came back negative, the team was left with the same clinical question they started with — and the answer was still sitting on the patient's skin and mucosa.

## Could the Diagnosis Have Been Made Earlier?

Arguably yes. The ICBD criteria are purely clinical and require a score of ≥4. On day 1, the patient had oral aphthae (2 points) and folliculitis-like rash (1 point) — a score of 3, which is below the threshold but should have placed BD squarely on the differential, especially given the neurologic symptoms and SIADH. Neuro-BD is classically associated with aseptic meningitis, and SIADH secondary to CNS inflammation is a known (if underrecognized) phenomenon. A patient presenting with oral aphthae, folliculitis, headache, fever, and aseptic meningitis should prompt active surveillance for the remaining BD criteria — particularly genital ulcers and ocular involvement — rather than a broad workup for unrelated conditions.

The genital ulcer appeared on day 5. If the team had been watching for it, the diagnosis could have been locked in the moment it appeared, which is essentially what happened. But the intervening days were consumed by testing that, in retrospect, did not contribute.

## Why We Over-Test

The cognitive forces at play here are worth naming explicitly:

- **Diagnostic uncertainty aversion**: We are uncomfortable not knowing, and ordering tests feels like productive action even when the tests have low pretest probability.
- **Anchoring on catastrophic but unlikely diagnoses**: HSV encephalitis and CNS malignancy are high-stakes, low-probability conditions that drive broad testing because the consequences of missing them are severe.
- **Premature closure in the wrong direction**: Once the team committed to an exclusion-based framework, each negative test narrowed the differential not toward BD but toward "still unknown," perpetuating further testing.
- **Failure to weight positive clinical evidence**: The mucocutaneous findings were present from the start and were more diagnostically informative than most of the tests that were sent, but they were initially treated as incidental rather than central.

## The SIADH Connection: A Useful Clinical Pearl

While the diagnostic parsimony angle is the main lesson, there's a secondary teaching point worth highlighting from this case that is easy to miss: **persistent hiccups can be a clue to profound hyponatremia from SIADH secondary to CNS inflammation.** The authors note this association, and it's one of those exam findings that sounds like a curiosity until you see it in practice. Similarly, the elevated CK (1349 U/L) in this patient likely represented rhabdomyolysis secondary to severe hyponatremia — a recognized but underappreciated complication. If you see hiccups and rhabdomyolysis in a patient with CNS inflammation, check the sodium.

## The Treatment Story: Colchicine Monotherapy

Perhaps the most surprising clinical detail is that this patient was treated with **colchicine monotherapy** — no systemic corticosteroids, no immunosuppressants — and had complete resolution of mucocutaneous symptoms, neurologic manifestations, and hyponatremia. SIADH was managed conservatively with fluid restriction (<800 mL/day). Sodium normalized to 136 mmol/L by day 16, and ADH levels dropped to 1.8 pg/mL. The patient had no recurrence over 4 years of follow-up.

This is remarkable because corticosteroids are the standard of care for acute neuro-BD, and colchicine is generally considered insufficient for neurologic involvement. The authors acknowledge that spontaneous recovery cannot be excluded. But the temporal relationship — improvement beginning the day after colchicine initiation — and the proposed mechanism (colchicine inhibits neutrophil migration and suppresses IL-1β and IL-6, which may in turn reduce ADH secretion) make a compelling case that colchicine may have real efficacy in this specific phenotype of neuro-BD: aseptic meningitis without parenchymal lesions, complicated by SIADH, in a patient with predominantly mucocutaneous disease.

This is not a recommendation to treat all neuro-BD with colchicine alone. But it is a reminder that disease phenotype matters — a patient with isolated aseptic meningitis and mucocutaneous disease may have a different trajectory than one with brainstem parenchymal lesions.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Mucocutaneous findings are not incidental.** In a patient with fever, headache, and hyponatremia, oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rash are not background noise — they are diagnostic data. Apply the ICBD criteria early and actively surveil for genital ulcers and ocular involvement if the score is approaching threshold.
- **Normal brain MRI does not exclude neuro-BD.** Acute neuro-BD can present as aseptic meningitis without parenchymal lesions, particularly early in the disease course. A negative MRI in a patient with CSF evidence of inflammation and compatible mucocutaneous findings should not push you away from the diagnosis.
- **ACE has poor sensitivity for sarcoidosis and should not be used as a screening test without clinical or radiographic evidence.** A normal ACE doesn't rule it out, and an elevated ACE doesn't rule it in.
- **Mildly elevated sIL-2R in a patient with a CRP of 180 mg/L is non-specific** and should not, by itself, prompt a workup for CNS lymphoma — particularly with a normal MRI and negative CSF cytology.
- **Persistent hiccups and elevated CK may be manifestations of severe hyponatremia** from SIADH in the setting of CNS inflammation. Don't overlook the sodium when evaluating these findings.
- **Colchicine monotherapy may have a role in select neuro-BD phenotypes** — specifically, aseptic meningitis without parenchymal lesions in patients with predominantly mucocutaneous disease — though corticosteroids remain the standard for parenchymal neuro-BD.
- **When the presentation is non-specific, resist the urge to order everything.** Build a positive differential from the findings you *do* have rather than excluding every catastrophic diagnosis you can think of. Diagnostic parsimony is not laziness — it's intellectual discipline.
