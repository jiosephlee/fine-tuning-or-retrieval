Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

Chapter 2: ## Diagnostic Reasoning and Differential Diagnosis

When a patient presents simultaneously with fever, headache, and severe hyponatraemia, the differential diagnosis is necessarily broad. In this case, the initial clinical picture raised concern for a central nervous system (CNS) infection, a systemic inflammatory disorder, or a malignancy. The diagnostic strategy employed by the treating team was one of systematic exclusion: infectious causes were addressed first given the urgency of treatable CNS infections, followed by neoplastic and autoimmune or granulomatous diseases. Only after these categories were excluded did the diagnosis of Behçet's disease (BD) with neurological involvement become clear, supported by the accumulation of mucocutaneous findings over the first week of hospitalisation.

### The Broad Initial Differential: Fever, Headache, and Hyponatraemia

The combination of fever, headache, and a serum sodium of 124 mmol/L in a euvolaemic patient with low serum osmolality and inappropriately concentrated urine pointed toward syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) as the cause of the electrolyte abnormality. However, SIADH is not a diagnosis in itself; it is a syndrome with multiple potential triggers, including CNS infection, malignancy, medications, and inflammatory conditions. The clinical question therefore became: what is driving the CNS inflammation that is presumably causing SIADH? The presence of oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rashes at presentation hinted at a systemic inflammatory disorder, but these findings alone were insufficient to establish a diagnosis, particularly since the characteristic genital ulcers of BD had not yet appeared. This uncertainty necessitated a broad initial evaluation.

### Exclusion of Infectious Causes

CNS infection was the first priority. The patient's fever, headache, and signs of meningeal irritation on cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis demanded exclusion of viral, bacterial, and tuberculous meningitis. Empirical therapy with cefepime and later acyclovir was appropriately initiated while diagnostic data were pending, reflecting the principle that treatment of suspected life-threatening CNS infections should not be delayed pending definitive results.

Viral meningoencephalitis, particularly due to herpes simplex virus (HSV) or varicella zoster virus (VZV), was a leading initial consideration. However, CSF testing for viral antigens and antibodies was negative, and acyclovir was discontinued once these results returned. Bacterial meningitis was excluded based on several converging findings: the CSF displayed a lymphocytic rather than neutrophilic pleocytosis, cultures were negative, and the overall clinical course was not consistent with a pyogenic process. Tuberculous meningitis was considered given the chronic inflammatory profile but was ruled out by negative acid-fast staining and tuberculosis DNA testing.

The CSF analysis itself was instrumental in narrowing the differential. The findings of mildly elevated opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), increased protein (0.97 g/L), and mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL) with negative microbiological studies established a pattern of aseptic meningitis. This pattern—characterised by lymphocytic predominance and absence of a identifiable pathogen—is the hallmark of non-purulent CNS inflammation and is the most common CSF profile seen in acute neuro-BD. In this case, the aseptic meningitis pattern served as a critical clue: it directed attention away from acute pyogenic infections and toward inflammatory or autoimmune CNS processes.

### Exclusion of Neoplastic Causes

Primary CNS malignancy was considered given the neurological symptoms, but brain MRI showed no mass lesion or abnormal enhancement, and CSF cytology revealed no malignant cells. Carcinomatous or lymphomatous meningitis, which can produce headache, cranial nerve palsies, and aseptic meningitis-like CSF profiles, was therefore effectively excluded.

A mildly elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) level of 704 U/mL (reference, 122–496 U/mL) raised the question of haematological malignancy, particularly CNS lymphoma. However, sIL-2R is a non-specific marker of T-cell activation and can be elevated in any inflammatory state. In isolation, without supportive imaging or cytological findings, this mild elevation was insufficient to support a diagnosis of lymphoma. The negative human T-cell leukaemia virus type 1 antibody further reduced concern for a retroviral-driven haematological process. This aspect of the case illustrates an important diagnostic principle: screening tests with limited specificity, such as sIL-2R, must be interpreted in context and should not be used in isolation to infer a diagnosis for which they lack adequate positive predictive value.

### Exclusion of Autoimmune and Granulomatous Diseases

Connective tissue diseases were considered as potential causes of CNS inflammation and aseptic meningitis. Screening autoantibody panels were negative, and there were no characteristic systemic features such as rash, arthritis pattern, or renal disease to suggest a specific autoimmune disorder.

Neurosarcoidosis was included in the differential because it can cause aseptic meningitis, cranial neuropathies, and hypothalamic-pituitary dysfunction, and because brain MRI may be normal in early disease. However, serum angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) and lysozyme levels were within normal ranges. While these tests have limited sensitivity for sarcoidosis, their normal values, combined with the absence of pulmonary or ocular involvement and the evolution of the clinical picture toward BD, made neurosarcoidosis unlikely.

The diagnostic team's approach here merits attention. The measurement of autoimmune serologies, ACE, and sIL-2R was performed as part of a broad, exclusion-based evaluation rather than because of strong clinical suspicion. As the authors note in their discussion, this strategy carries the risk of indiscriminate testing and potential for misleading results. The mild sIL-2R elevation exemplifies how non-specific abnormalities can complicate, rather than clarify, the diagnostic process. A more targeted approach—guided by evolving clinical features—might have narrowed the evaluation sooner.

### Role and Limitations of Brain MRI in Acute Neuro-BD

Brain MRI is a cornerstone of neurological evaluation, but its role in acute neuro-BD requires nuanced interpretation. In this case, MRI showed no abnormalities, including in the brainstem and hypothalamic region—the sites most commonly affected in parenchymal neuro-BD. This normal result did not exclude the diagnosis. Acute neuro-BD can present as aseptic meningitis without parenchymal lesions, and early in the disease course, MRI may be normal even when clinical symptoms are prominent. The diagnostic weight therefore shifted to the CSF findings and the clinical context rather than imaging.

This point is pedagogically important. In many neurological conditions, a normal MRI is reassuring and points away from serious pathology. In acute neuro-BD presenting with meningoencephalitis, a normal MRI is consistent with the diagnosis and should not be used to exclude it. The absence of radiological abnormalities in this case actually complicated the diagnostic process, as it removed an objective structural finding that might have accelerated the diagnosis.

### Application of the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease

The diagnosis of BD was established using the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease (ICBD), which assigns points based on specific clinical findings: oral aphthae (2 points), genital aphthosis (2 points), ocular lesions (2 points), skin lesions (1 point, including folliculitis-like rash), vascular manifestations (1 point), and a positive pathergy test (1 point). A total score of 4 or more indicates definite BD.

In this patient, the accumulated findings were oral aphthae (2 points), genital aphthosis (2 points, once the scrotal ulcer appeared on day 5), and folliculitis-like rash (1 point), yielding a total score of 5 and establishing a diagnosis of definite BD. Notably, the patient did not exhibit ocular involvement (no uveitis on examination) or a positive pathergy test, and vascular manifestations were not identified. The diagnosis therefore relied entirely on mucocutaneous features, underscoring the central role of these findings in the ICBD framework.

The timing of diagnosis is worth emphasising. At presentation on day 1, oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rash were present, but genital ulcers had not yet appeared. With only 3 points, the patient did not yet meet the ICBD threshold. It was not until day 5, when the scrotal ulcer emerged, that the score reached 5 and the diagnosis of definite BD was confirmed. This delay illustrates a recurring challenge in BD diagnosis: the symptoms accumulate over time, and early in the presentation, the pattern may be incomplete.

### Diagnostic Significance of HLA-B51

HLA-B51 is the allele most strongly associated with BD, particularly in populations along the historical Silk Road, including East Asian, Mediterranean, and Middle Eastern patients. In this case, HLA-B51 was positive. However, its diagnostic utility is limited. HLA-B51 is neither necessary nor sufficient for a diagnosis of BD. Many patients with BD are HLA-B51–negative, and the allele is present in a significant proportion of healthy individuals in endemic populations. The ICBD does not incorporate HLA-B51 testing into its scoring system, and the diagnosis remains fundamentally clinical. In this case, the positive HLA-B51 result was supportive but not diagnostic, and the authors correctly note that it did not meaningfully contribute to establishing the diagnosis. Its primary value was retrospective, adding supportive evidence to an already clinically established diagnosis.

### Challenges of Early Diagnosis and the Importance of Pattern Recognition

The diagnostic process in this case was prolonged by the absence of genital ulcers at the initial presentation. Genital aphthosis is one of the most specific findings of BD and carries significant weight in the ICBD scoring system. Without it, the early clinical picture of fever, headache, oral aphthae, folliculitis-like rash, and aseptic meningitis could fit multiple diagnoses, and the team appropriately pursued a broad differential.

This case demonstrates the importance of clinical pattern recognition and serial observation. The diagnosis of BD required the emergence of the genital ulcer on day 5, together with the concurrent neurological findings, to coalesce into a coherent clinical picture. The lesson for clinicians is that BD is a diagnosis of accumulation: individual findings may be non-specific, but their combination and temporal evolution can be highly suggestive. When a patient presents with mucocutaneous findings and unexplained CNS symptoms, BD should be considered early, even if the full clinical criteria are not yet met. Waiting for all features to appear before considering the diagnosis can delay appropriate management.

The concurrent diagnosis of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD in this case is itself rare. Neurological involvement typically develops years after the initial BD diagnosis, and neurological symptoms at presentation occur in fewer than 5% of patients. The simultaneous emergence of systemic and neurological manifestations made this case particularly challenging, as there was no pre-existing BD diagnosis to contextualise the neurological findings. The team's success in establishing the diagnosis relied on maintaining BD in the differential as the clinical picture evolved, rather than anchoring on the initially negative findings.
