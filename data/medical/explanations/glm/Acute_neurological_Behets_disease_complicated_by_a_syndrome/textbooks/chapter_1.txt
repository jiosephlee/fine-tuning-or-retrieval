Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and Initial Assessment

Behçet's disease (BD) is a chronic, multisystem inflammatory disorder of unknown etiology characterized by recurrent oral and genital ulcers, uveitis, and skin lesions. The disease has a notable geographic distribution, being most prevalent along the ancient Silk Road, including Turkey, the Middle East, and East Asia, and it typically manifests in young adults between the third and fourth decades of life. BD is classified as a vasculitis affecting both arteries and veins of variable caliber, and its clinical course is marked by relapsing-remitting episodes of mucocutaneous, ocular, articular, vascular, gastrointestinal, and neurological inflammation. The International Criteria for Behçet's Disease, established in 2014, use a scoring system based on clinical findings—oral aphthosis, genital aphthosis, ocular lesions, skin lesions, and a positive pathergy test—with a total score of four or more indicating definite disease. HLA-B51 is associated with BD but is neither necessary nor sufficient for diagnosis, and international criteria do not require it.

Neurological involvement in BD, designated neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD), occurs in approximately 10%–20% of patients and is one of the most serious complications of the disease. A defining feature of neuro-BD is its timing: it typically develops several years after the initial diagnosis of BD, often around four years later, with neurological manifestations at initial presentation being uncommon, occurring in fewer than 5% of patients. This delayed onset reflects the cumulative inflammatory burden of the disease on the central nervous system. Acute neuro-BD most commonly presents as meningoencephalitis, with or without focal neurological deficits, frequently involving the brainstem. When neuro-BD is diagnosed concurrently with systemic BD—as in this case—it represents a rare clinical scenario that poses significant diagnostic challenges, as clinicians may not initially consider BD in a patient presenting de novo with neurological symptoms and hyponatremia.

The patient in this case was an Asian man in his 60s who presented to the emergency department with a three-day history of fever, headache, general malaise, loss of appetite, persistent hiccups, and arthralgia affecting the shoulders and ankles. He also reported oral aphthae. His medical history was notable only for type 2 diabetes mellitus, for which he was not receiving treatment due to limited awareness of his condition. He had no recent travel, animal exposure, sick contacts, or sexual activity. This presentation—combining fever, headache, systemic symptoms, and mucocutaneous findings—immediately suggested a systemic inflammatory or infectious process, but the specific combination of features did not initially point to a single diagnosis. The persistent hiccups, a seemingly minor symptom, would later prove clinically significant as a recognized indicator of severe hyponatremia secondary to central nervous system inflammation.

On physical examination, the patient was alert but appeared fatigued. He was febrile at 38.1°C, tachycardic at 119 beats per minute, and normotensive. Importantly, his volume status was clinically assessed as euvolemic—there was no peripheral edema, and cardiopulmonary and abdominal examinations were unremarkable. The finding of euvolemia in a patient with hyponatremia is a critical diagnostic clue, as it narrows the differential diagnosis toward causes of hypotonic hyponatremia without hypervolemia or hypovolemia, including the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). Mucocutaneous examination revealed oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rashes on the face and trunk. No muscle weakness was detected on manual muscle testing. The absence of neck stiffness or cranial nerve involvement on the initial examination did not exclude central nervous system inflammation, as these signs may be absent in early or mild meningoencephalitis. A painful scrotal ulcer appeared later, on day 5 of admission, which, in combination with the oral aphthae and skin lesions, completed the mucocutaneous triad that led to the diagnosis of BD.

Initial laboratory tests revealed several abnormalities that demanded urgent attention. The most striking finding was severe hyponatremia, with a serum sodium concentration of 124 mmol/L. Inflammatory markers were markedly elevated, with C-reactive protein at 180 mg/L and an erythrocyte sedimentation rate of 70 mm/hour, signaling an active systemic inflammatory process. Creatine kinase was elevated at 1349 U/L, accompanied by mild elevations in aspartate aminotransferase and alanine aminotransferase, raising concern for rhabdomyolysis—a recognized but frequently overlooked complication of profound hyponatremia. The patient also presented with hyperglycemia, with a plasma glucose of 14.2 mmol/L and an HbA1c of 8.2%, reflecting his untreated type 2 diabetes. Renal function was normal, and hepatitis B and C markers were negative. The combination of severe hyponatremia, marked systemic inflammation, and elevated muscle enzymes in a euvolemic patient established a clinical picture that prompted evaluation for SIADH, central nervous system inflammation, and an underlying systemic inflammatory disorder.

The concurrent presentation of mucocutaneous lesions and neurological symptoms at the initial encounter was central to the diagnostic process. In most cases of BD, mucocutaneous manifestations precede neurological involvement by years, allowing clinicians to establish a diagnosis of BD before neurological complications arise. When both presentations occur simultaneously, the diagnostic challenge is compounded: the neurological symptoms and hyponatremia may dominate the clinical picture and overshadow the mucocutaneous findings, or the latter may not yet be fully apparent. In this case, the oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rashes were present at admission, but the genital ulcer did not appear until day 5, meaning the complete mucocutaneous syndrome required days to unfold. This case illustrates the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis that includes multisystem inflammatory diseases when evaluating a patient with concurrent neurological and dermatological findings, and it underscores the necessity of serial physical examinations, as key diagnostic features may emerge over the course of hospitalization.
