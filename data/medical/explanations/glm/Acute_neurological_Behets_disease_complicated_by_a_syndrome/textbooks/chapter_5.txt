Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

Chapter 5: ## Clinical Outcome, Follow-up, and Key Lessons

The patient's clinical response to colchicine was rapid and encompassed multiple organ systems. Within one day of initiating colchicine on day 8 of admission, his fever resolved and his appetite improved. Over the subsequent two weeks, vertigo, general malaise, persistent hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions — including oral aphthae, folliculitis-like rashes, and the scrotal ulcer — gradually resolved. This temporal sequence is noteworthy because colchicine is established as a treatment for the mucocutaneous manifestations of Behçet's disease (BD) but is not generally considered effective for acute neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD), where corticosteroids and other immunosuppressants are regarded as mainstay therapy. The concurrent improvement of neurological symptoms and mucocutaneous lesions with a single agent suggests that the underlying inflammatory process driving both manifestations was responsive to colchicine's anti-inflammatory mechanisms, which include inhibition of neutrophil migration and suppression of interleukin-1β and interleukin-6 production.

The biochemical markers of disease activity paralleled the clinical improvement. By day 16, serum sodium had normalized to 136 mmol/L, and antidiuretic hormone (ADH) levels had decreased from 22.4 pg/mL to 1.8 pg/mL, confirming resolution of the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). The normalization of sodium and ADH in tandem with the resolution of neurological symptoms supports the proposition that central nervous system (CNS) inflammation from acute neuro-BD was the driving force behind inappropriate ADH secretion. Additionally, the elevated creatine kinase, which had been 1349 U/L on presentation — reflecting rhabdomyolysis secondary to profound hyponatraemia — returned to normal, as did the inflammatory markers C-reactive protein and erythrocyte sedimentation rate. The resolution of rhabdomyolysis underscores the importance of recognizing severe hyponatraemia as a cause of muscle injury and monitoring creatine kinase in patients with SIADH, as this complication is often underappreciated.

The patient's hospitalisation lasted one month, during which the acute phase of the illness was managed, hyponatraemia was carefully monitored, and rehabilitation was provided for the decline in physical function associated with his prolonged inflammatory illness. The management of SIADH was conservative throughout, consisting of fluid restriction to less than 800 mL per day with daily serum sodium monitoring and avoidance of rapid sodium correction. Hypertonic saline and vasopressin receptor antagonists were not required, which is consistent with the principle that SIADH secondary to a treatable underlying condition often resolves with management of the primary disease process, provided the hyponatraemia is not immediately life-threatening.

Long-term follow-up demonstrated sustained remission. Colchicine therapy was continued for approximately four years, during which the patient experienced no recurrence of BD symptoms or neurological manifestations. Notably, no systemic corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants were required at any point. After his BD stabilized, biguanide therapy was initiated for his previously untreated type 2 diabetes mellitus, and good glycaemic control was subsequently maintained. The successful delayed introduction of diabetes medication illustrates how an acute systemic illness can complicate the management of comorbid chronic conditions and how prioritizing the treatment of the acute inflammatory process can create the clinical stability needed to address longstanding metabolic disease.

Several broader clinical lessons emerge from this case. First, the concurrent diagnosis of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD at initial presentation is rare — neurological involvement typically develops several years after the initial BD diagnosis, and neurological manifestations at presentation occur in fewer than 5% of patients. Clinicians should therefore maintain a high index of suspicion for neuro-BD when patients with mucocutaneous features of BD present with non-specific CNS symptoms such as headache and fever, even when brain MRI is normal. Acute neuro-BD can present as aseptic meningitis without parenchymal lesions, and a normal MRI, particularly early in the disease course, does not exclude the diagnosis. The diagnosis of neuro-BD in this case was established through a combination of clinical mucocutaneous findings, CSF evidence of aseptic meningitis, and exclusion of infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune causes.

Second, this case highlights the importance of considering SIADH in patients with neuro-BD who present with hyponatraemia. The mechanism is thought to involve CNS inflammation-driven dysregulation of ADH secretion, potentially mediated by interleukin-6, which is elevated in the CSF of patients with acute neuro-BD. Although SIADH has been reported in BD patients receiving cyclophosphamide, its occurrence in the absence of pharmacological triggers — attributable solely to CNS inflammation — has been less well characterized. Clinicians evaluating patients with BD and neurological symptoms should routinely assess serum sodium, serum and urine osmolality, and urinary sodium concentration to identify SIADH, as persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis may serve as clinical clues to severe hyponatraemia.

Third, the diagnostic process in this case illustrates the value of pattern recognition over indiscriminate testing. The initial absence of genital ulcers made the diagnosis of BD uncertain, prompting a broad evaluation that included autoimmune serologies, serum angiotensin-converting enzyme, and soluble interleukin-2 receptor. These tests were performed as part of an exclusion-based approach rather than based on strong clinical suspicion, and several yielded mildly abnormal results that were ultimately nonspecific. The case underscores that clinicians should perform such investigations only when supported by appropriate clinical features and should interpret minor abnormalities with caution, as they can introduce diagnostic confusion rather than clarity.

Fourth, the successful treatment with colchicine monotherapy raises the possibility that colchicine may be a viable alternative to corticosteroids in selected cases of acute neuro-BD, particularly when corticosteroid therapy is undesirable or contraindicated. However, this observation must be interpreted carefully. Spontaneous resolution of acute neuro-BD presenting solely with meningoencephalitis-like symptoms has been reported, and the present case cannot distinguish between a therapeutic effect of colchicine and natural disease resolution. Systematic reviews have found colchicine ineffective for acute neuro-BD, and in previously reported cases, colchicine has been used as an adjunct to corticosteroids rather than as monotherapy. The findings from this single case are therefore hypothesis-generating rather than practice-changing.

Finally, the case has important limitations. A definitive causal link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH cannot be established from a single case report. The temporal association between the resolution of CNS inflammation and the normalization of ADH and sodium levels is consistent with a causal relationship but does not prove one. The absence of serum or CSF interleukin-6 measurements further limits mechanistic conclusions. Nonetheless, the case provides a compelling clinical observation that should alert clinicians to the potential for SIADH as a complication of acute neuro-BD and encourage vigilance for electrolyte disturbances in patients presenting with neuroinflammatory symptoms in the context of BD.
