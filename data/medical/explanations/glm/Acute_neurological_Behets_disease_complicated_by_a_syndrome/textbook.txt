Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Assessment

Behçet's disease (BD) is a chronic, multisystem inflammatory disorder of unknown etiology characterized by recurrent oral and genital ulcers, uveitis, and skin lesions. The disease has a notable geographic distribution, being most prevalent along the ancient Silk Road, including Turkey, the Middle East, and East Asia, and it typically manifests in young adults between the third and fourth decades of life. BD is classified as a vasculitis affecting both arteries and veins of variable caliber, and its clinical course is marked by relapsing-remitting episodes of mucocutaneous, ocular, articular, vascular, gastrointestinal, and neurological inflammation. The International Criteria for Behçet's Disease, established in 2014, use a scoring system based on clinical findings—oral aphthosis, genital aphthosis, ocular lesions, skin lesions, and a positive pathergy test—with a total score of four or more indicating definite disease. HLA-B51 is associated with BD but is neither necessary nor sufficient for diagnosis, and international criteria do not require it.

Neurological involvement in BD, designated neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD), occurs in approximately 10%–20% of patients and is one of the most serious complications of the disease. A defining feature of neuro-BD is its timing: it typically develops several years after the initial diagnosis of BD, often around four years later, with neurological manifestations at initial presentation being uncommon, occurring in fewer than 5% of patients. This delayed onset reflects the cumulative inflammatory burden of the disease on the central nervous system. Acute neuro-BD most commonly presents as meningoencephalitis, with or without focal neurological deficits, frequently involving the brainstem. When neuro-BD is diagnosed concurrently with systemic BD—as in this case—it represents a rare clinical scenario that poses significant diagnostic challenges, as clinicians may not initially consider BD in a patient presenting de novo with neurological symptoms and hyponatremia.

The patient in this case was an Asian man in his 60s who presented to the emergency department with a three-day history of fever, headache, general malaise, loss of appetite, persistent hiccups, and arthralgia affecting the shoulders and ankles. He also reported oral aphthae. His medical history was notable only for type 2 diabetes mellitus, for which he was not receiving treatment due to limited awareness of his condition. He had no recent travel, animal exposure, sick contacts, or sexual activity. This presentation—combining fever, headache, systemic symptoms, and mucocutaneous findings—immediately suggested a systemic inflammatory or infectious process, but the specific combination of features did not initially point to a single diagnosis. The persistent hiccups, a seemingly minor symptom, would later prove clinically significant as a recognized indicator of severe hyponatremia secondary to central nervous system inflammation.

On physical examination, the patient was alert but appeared fatigued. He was febrile at 38.1°C, tachycardic at 119 beats per minute, and normotensive. Importantly, his volume status was clinically assessed as euvolemic—there was no peripheral edema, and cardiopulmonary and abdominal examinations were unremarkable. The finding of euvolemia in a patient with hyponatremia is a critical diagnostic clue, as it narrows the differential diagnosis toward causes of hypotonic hyponatremia without hypervolemia or hypovolemia, including the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). Mucocutaneous examination revealed oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rashes on the face and trunk. No muscle weakness was detected on manual muscle testing. The absence of neck stiffness or cranial nerve involvement on the initial examination did not exclude central nervous system inflammation, as these signs may be absent in early or mild meningoencephalitis. A painful scrotal ulcer appeared later, on day 5 of admission, which, in combination with the oral aphthae and skin lesions, completed the mucocutaneous triad that led to the diagnosis of BD.

Initial laboratory tests revealed several abnormalities that demanded urgent attention. The most striking finding was severe hyponatremia, with a serum sodium concentration of 124 mmol/L. Inflammatory markers were markedly elevated, with C-reactive protein at 180 mg/L and an erythrocyte sedimentation rate of 70 mm/hour, signaling an active systemic inflammatory process. Creatine kinase was elevated at 1349 U/L, accompanied by mild elevations in aspartate aminotransferase and alanine aminotransferase, raising concern for rhabdomyolysis—a recognized but frequently overlooked complication of profound hyponatremia. The patient also presented with hyperglycemia, with a plasma glucose of 14.2 mmol/L and an HbA1c of 8.2%, reflecting his untreated type 2 diabetes. Renal function was normal, and hepatitis B and C markers were negative. The combination of severe hyponatremia, marked systemic inflammation, and elevated muscle enzymes in a euvolemic patient established a clinical picture that prompted evaluation for SIADH, central nervous system inflammation, and an underlying systemic inflammatory disorder.

The concurrent presentation of mucocutaneous lesions and neurological symptoms at the initial encounter was central to the diagnostic process. In most cases of BD, mucocutaneous manifestations precede neurological involvement by years, allowing clinicians to establish a diagnosis of BD before neurological complications arise. When both presentations occur simultaneously, the diagnostic challenge is compounded: the neurological symptoms and hyponatremia may dominate the clinical picture and overshadow the mucocutaneous findings, or the latter may not yet be fully apparent. In this case, the oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rashes were present at admission, but the genital ulcer did not appear until day 5, meaning the complete mucocutaneous syndrome required days to unfold. This case illustrates the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis that includes multisystem inflammatory diseases when evaluating a patient with concurrent neurological and dermatological findings, and it underscores the necessity of serial physical examinations, as key diagnostic features may emerge over the course of hospitalization.

## Diagnostic Reasoning and Differential Diagnosis

When a patient presents simultaneously with fever, headache, and severe hyponatraemia, the differential diagnosis is necessarily broad. In this case, the initial clinical picture raised concern for a central nervous system (CNS) infection, a systemic inflammatory disorder, or a malignancy. The diagnostic strategy employed by the treating team was one of systematic exclusion: infectious causes were addressed first given the urgency of treatable CNS infections, followed by neoplastic and autoimmune or granulomatous diseases. Only after these categories were excluded did the diagnosis of Behçet's disease (BD) with neurological involvement become clear, supported by the accumulation of mucocutaneous findings over the first week of hospitalisation.

### The Broad Initial Differential: Fever, Headache, and Hyponatraemia

The combination of fever, headache, and a serum sodium of 124 mmol/L in a euvolaemic patient with low serum osmolality and inappropriately concentrated urine pointed toward syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) as the cause of the electrolyte abnormality. However, SIADH is not a diagnosis in itself; it is a syndrome with multiple potential triggers, including CNS infection, malignancy, medications, and inflammatory conditions. The clinical question therefore became: what is driving the CNS inflammation that is presumably causing SIADH? The presence of oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rashes at presentation hinted at a systemic inflammatory disorder, but these findings alone were insufficient to establish a diagnosis, particularly since the characteristic genital ulcers of BD had not yet appeared. This uncertainty necessitated a broad initial evaluation.

### Exclusion of Infectious Causes

CNS infection was the first priority. The patient's fever, headache, and signs of meningeal irritation on cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis demanded exclusion of viral, bacterial, and tuberculous meningitis. Empirical therapy with cefepime and later acyclovir was appropriately initiated while diagnostic data were pending, reflecting the principle that treatment of suspected life-threatening CNS infections should not be delayed pending definitive results.

Viral meningoencephalitis, particularly due to herpes simplex virus (HSV) or varicella zoster virus (VZV), was a leading initial consideration. However, CSF testing for viral antigens and antibodies was negative, and acyclovir was discontinued once these results returned. Bacterial meningitis was excluded based on several converging findings: the CSF displayed a lymphocytic rather than neutrophilic pleocytosis, cultures were negative, and the overall clinical course was not consistent with a pyogenic process. Tuberculous meningitis was considered given the chronic inflammatory profile but was ruled out by negative acid-fast staining and tuberculosis DNA testing.

The CSF analysis itself was instrumental in narrowing the differential. The findings of mildly elevated opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), increased protein (0.97 g/L), and mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL) with negative microbiological studies established a pattern of aseptic meningitis. This pattern—characterised by lymphocytic predominance and absence of a identifiable pathogen—is the hallmark of non-purulent CNS inflammation and is the most common CSF profile seen in acute neuro-BD. In this case, the aseptic meningitis pattern served as a critical clue: it directed attention away from acute pyogenic infections and toward inflammatory or autoimmune CNS processes.

### Exclusion of Neoplastic Causes

Primary CNS malignancy was considered given the neurological symptoms, but brain MRI showed no mass lesion or abnormal enhancement, and CSF cytology revealed no malignant cells. Carcinomatous or lymphomatous meningitis, which can produce headache, cranial nerve palsies, and aseptic meningitis-like CSF profiles, was therefore effectively excluded.

A mildly elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) level of 704 U/mL (reference, 122–496 U/mL) raised the question of haematological malignancy, particularly CNS lymphoma. However, sIL-2R is a non-specific marker of T-cell activation and can be elevated in any inflammatory state. In isolation, without supportive imaging or cytological findings, this mild elevation was insufficient to support a diagnosis of lymphoma. The negative human T-cell leukaemia virus type 1 antibody further reduced concern for a retroviral-driven haematological process. This aspect of the case illustrates an important diagnostic principle: screening tests with limited specificity, such as sIL-2R, must be interpreted in context and should not be used in isolation to infer a diagnosis for which they lack adequate positive predictive value.

### Exclusion of Autoimmune and Granulomatous Diseases

Connective tissue diseases were considered as potential causes of CNS inflammation and aseptic meningitis. Screening autoantibody panels were negative, and there were no characteristic systemic features such as rash, arthritis pattern, or renal disease to suggest a specific autoimmune disorder.

Neurosarcoidosis was included in the differential because it can cause aseptic meningitis, cranial neuropathies, and hypothalamic-pituitary dysfunction, and because brain MRI may be normal in early disease. However, serum angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) and lysozyme levels were within normal ranges. While these tests have limited sensitivity for sarcoidosis, their normal values, combined with the absence of pulmonary or ocular involvement and the evolution of the clinical picture toward BD, made neurosarcoidosis unlikely.

The diagnostic team's approach here merits attention. The measurement of autoimmune serologies, ACE, and sIL-2R was performed as part of a broad, exclusion-based evaluation rather than because of strong clinical suspicion. As the authors note in their discussion, this strategy carries the risk of indiscriminate testing and potential for misleading results. The mild sIL-2R elevation exemplifies how non-specific abnormalities can complicate, rather than clarify, the diagnostic process. A more targeted approach—guided by evolving clinical features—might have narrowed the evaluation sooner.

### Role and Limitations of Brain MRI in Acute Neuro-BD

Brain MRI is a cornerstone of neurological evaluation, but its role in acute neuro-BD requires nuanced interpretation. In this case, MRI showed no abnormalities, including in the brainstem and hypothalamic region—the sites most commonly affected in parenchymal neuro-BD. This normal result did not exclude the diagnosis. Acute neuro-BD can present as aseptic meningitis without parenchymal lesions, and early in the disease course, MRI may be normal even when clinical symptoms are prominent. The diagnostic weight therefore shifted to the CSF findings and the clinical context rather than imaging.

This point is pedagogically important. In many neurological conditions, a normal MRI is reassuring and points away from serious pathology. In acute neuro-BD presenting with meningoencephalitis, a normal MRI is consistent with the diagnosis and should not be used to exclude it. The absence of radiological abnormalities in this case actually complicated the diagnostic process, as it removed an objective structural finding that might have accelerated the diagnosis.

### Application of the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease

The diagnosis of BD was established using the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease (ICBD), which assigns points based on specific clinical findings: oral aphthae (2 points), genital aphthosis (2 points), ocular lesions (2 points), skin lesions (1 point, including folliculitis-like rash), vascular manifestations (1 point), and a positive pathergy test (1 point). A total score of 4 or more indicates definite BD.

In this patient, the accumulated findings were oral aphthae (2 points), genital aphthosis (2 points, once the scrotal ulcer appeared on day 5), and folliculitis-like rash (1 point), yielding a total score of 5 and establishing a diagnosis of definite BD. Notably, the patient did not exhibit ocular involvement (no uveitis on examination) or a positive pathergy test, and vascular manifestations were not identified. The diagnosis therefore relied entirely on mucocutaneous features, underscoring the central role of these findings in the ICBD framework.

The timing of diagnosis is worth emphasising. At presentation on day 1, oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rash were present, but genital ulcers had not yet appeared. With only 3 points, the patient did not yet meet the ICBD threshold. It was not until day 5, when the scrotal ulcer emerged, that the score reached 5 and the diagnosis of definite BD was confirmed. This delay illustrates a recurring challenge in BD diagnosis: the symptoms accumulate over time, and early in the presentation, the pattern may be incomplete.

### Diagnostic Significance of HLA-B51

HLA-B51 is the allele most strongly associated with BD, particularly in populations along the historical Silk Road, including East Asian, Mediterranean, and Middle Eastern patients. In this case, HLA-B51 was positive. However, its diagnostic utility is limited. HLA-B51 is neither necessary nor sufficient for a diagnosis of BD. Many patients with BD are HLA-B51–negative, and the allele is present in a significant proportion of healthy individuals in endemic populations. The ICBD does not incorporate HLA-B51 testing into its scoring system, and the diagnosis remains fundamentally clinical. In this case, the positive HLA-B51 result was supportive but not diagnostic, and the authors correctly note that it did not meaningfully contribute to establishing the diagnosis. Its primary value was retrospective, adding supportive evidence to an already clinically established diagnosis.

### Challenges of Early Diagnosis and the Importance of Pattern Recognition

The diagnostic process in this case was prolonged by the absence of genital ulcers at the initial presentation. Genital aphthosis is one of the most specific findings of BD and carries significant weight in the ICBD scoring system. Without it, the early clinical picture of fever, headache, oral aphthae, folliculitis-like rash, and aseptic meningitis could fit multiple diagnoses, and the team appropriately pursued a broad differential.

This case demonstrates the importance of clinical pattern recognition and serial observation. The diagnosis of BD required the emergence of the genital ulcer on day 5, together with the concurrent neurological findings, to coalesce into a coherent clinical picture. The lesson for clinicians is that BD is a diagnosis of accumulation: individual findings may be non-specific, but their combination and temporal evolution can be highly suggestive. When a patient presents with mucocutaneous findings and unexplained CNS symptoms, BD should be considered early, even if the full clinical criteria are not yet met. Waiting for all features to appear before considering the diagnosis can delay appropriate management.

The concurrent diagnosis of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD in this case is itself rare. Neurological involvement typically develops years after the initial BD diagnosis, and neurological symptoms at presentation occur in fewer than 5% of patients. The simultaneous emergence of systemic and neurological manifestations made this case particularly challenging, as there was no pre-existing BD diagnosis to contextualise the neurological findings. The team's success in establishing the diagnosis relied on maintaining BD in the differential as the clinical picture evolved, rather than anchoring on the initially negative findings.

## SIADH: Pathophysiology, Diagnosis, and Association with Neuro-BD

The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) is a central diagnostic and mechanistic feature of this case, linking the patient's profound hyponatremia to the central nervous system (CNS) inflammation of acute neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD). Understanding how SIADH was confirmed, what alternative diagnoses were excluded, and why it likely developed in this patient requires a systematic approach to hyponatremia evaluation and an appreciation of how CNS inflammation can disrupt the regulation of arginine vasopressin (ADH).

### Establishing the Diagnosis of SIADH

SIADH is defined by the non-physiologic, autonomous release of ADH, which impairs free water excretion and produces hypo-osmolar hyponatremia in a patient who is clinically euvolemic. The diagnosis is never made by a single laboratory value; rather, it requires a constellation of findings that together demonstrate impaired water excretion in the absence of an appropriate physiologic stimulus for ADH release. The essential criteria include hypo-osmolar hyponatremia (serum sodium below 135 mmol/L with serum osmolality below 275 mOsm/kg), clinical euvolemia (no signs of hypovolemia or hypervolemia), urine that is not maximally dilute (urine osmolality above 100 mOsm/kg), and elevated urinary sodium concentration (typically above 40 mmol/L) in the setting of normal dietary sodium intake and normal renal function. Additionally, the absence of other causes of hyponatremia—particularly adrenal insufficiency, hypothyroidism, and renal failure—is required, as these conditions can mimic SIADH biochemically.

This patient met each of these criteria. His serum sodium was 124 mmol/L, and his serum osmolality was low at 267 mOsm/kg, establishing hypo-osmolar hyponatremia. On physical examination, he had no peripheral edema, no signs of dehydration, and his volume status was assessed as euvolemic. His urine osmolality was markedly elevated at 900 mOsm/kg, far exceeding the threshold that would be expected if ADH were appropriately suppressed in the setting of low serum osmolality. His urinary sodium concentration was elevated at 88 mmol/L, consistent with ongoing renal sodium excretion despite hyponatremia—a hallmark of SIADH. Notably, his ADH level was elevated at 22.4 pg/mL, a finding that is pathologic in the context of hypo-osmolality, because low serum osmolality should normally suppress ADH secretion to near-undetectable levels. This inappropriately elevated ADH level provides direct evidence of non-physiologic vasopressin release and was a key confirmatory finding in this case.

### Excluding Alternative Causes of Hyponatremia

Before SIADH can be diagnosed, clinicians must systematically exclude other causes of hyponatremia that can produce a similar biochemical picture. The most important exclusions are adrenal insufficiency, hypothyroidism, and significant renal dysfunction. Adrenal insufficiency causes hyponatremia through a combination of reduced cortisol production—which increases ADH release—and mineralocorticoid deficiency leading to volume depletion; both mechanisms can produce euvolemic or hypovolemic hyponatremia with elevated urinary sodium, closely mimicking SIADH. Hypothyroidism can cause hyponatremia through reduced cardiac output and increased ADH secretion, and renal failure impairs the kidney's ability to dilute or concentrate urine, which can confound the interpretation of urinary indices.

In this patient, renal function was normal, ruling out a renal contribution to the electrolyte disturbance. The endocrine evaluation was particularly important: serum cortisol was 42 µg/dL, well above the reference threshold of greater than 6 µg/dL, effectively excluding adrenal insufficiency. Thyroid-stimulating hormone was 1.11 mIU/L (reference range 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), and free T4 was 1.25 ng/dL (reference range 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), both within normal limits, excluding hypothyroidism. These exclusionary tests were essential because treating SIADH in a patient who actually had adrenal insufficiency or hypothyroidism would not address the underlying cause and could delay critical therapy, such as glucocorticoid replacement. With these conditions excluded and no evidence of volume depletion, cardiac failure, or liver disease, the diagnosis of SIADH was firmly established.

### Pathophysiology: Linking CNS Inflammation to Dysregulated ADH Secretion

SIADH can be triggered by a diverse array of conditions, including malignancies (particularly small cell lung cancer through ectopic ADH production), pulmonary diseases, and numerous medications. However, CNS inflammation is a well-recognized cause, and the pathophysiology in this case is best understood through the lens of neuroinflammation. Under normal physiology, ADH secretion is tightly regulated by osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus and by baroreceptors in the carotid sinus and aortic arch. When serum osmolality falls, ADH release should be suppressed, allowing the kidney to excrete dilute urine and restore normal serum sodium. In SIADH secondary to CNS inflammation, this regulatory mechanism is disrupted, and ADH is released independent of osmotic signals.

The proposed molecular link in this case centers on interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine that has been shown to stimulate ADH release from the posterior pituitary. Several lines of evidence support this mechanism. First, IL-6 is frequently elevated in neuroinflammatory disorders, and experimental studies have demonstrated that IL-6 can directly promote vasopressin secretion from hypothalamic neurons. Second, in Behçet's disease (BD), aberrant monocyte activation leads to spontaneous IL-6 production, and elevated IL-6 levels have been detected in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) of patients with acute neuro-BD. Third, this patient had a markedly elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) of 180 mg/L, which is an indirect marker of IL-6–mediated hepatic acute-phase response, suggesting a prominent IL-6–driven inflammatory milieu. Although IL-6 levels were not directly measured in the serum or CSF in this case, the clinical and laboratory context is consistent with IL-6–mediated stimulation of ADH as the mechanism linking CNS inflammation to SIADH.

It is important to note, as the authors of this case report acknowledge, that a definitive causal link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH cannot be established from a single case. The temporal association—the simultaneous presence of aseptic meningitis, elevated inflammatory markers, and SIADH, all of which resolved together—suggests a shared inflammatory mechanism but does not prove disease specificity. SIADH may represent a nonspecific response to CNS inflammation rather than a mechanism unique to neuro-BD. Nevertheless, the case highlights that clinicians evaluating patients with neuro-BD and hyponatremia should consider SIADH as a potential complication.

### Underrecognized Clinical Consequences of Profound Hyponatremia

Two clinical features in this case deserve special attention because they represent underrecognized manifestations of severe hyponatremia: persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis. The patient presented with persistent hiccups, which might appear to be an incidental nuisance symptom but has been reported as an important clinical clue to severe hyponatremia secondary to CNS inflammation. The mechanism likely involves irritation of CNS structures mediating the hiccup reflex, and in the context of meningitis and SIADH, persistent hiccups should prompt evaluation of serum sodium. This symptom was present from the outset alongside fever and headache, and its resolution paralleled the correction of hyponatremia, supporting its relationship to the electrolyte disturbance.

The patient also had an elevated creatine kinase (CK) of 1349 U/L, indicating rhabdomyolysis. Rhabdomyolysis is a recognized but frequently overlooked complication of profound hyponatremia. The mechanism involves osmotic shifts that disrupt skeletal muscle cell integrity, particularly when serum sodium falls rapidly or to very low levels. In this case, CK was measured as part of the initial evaluation of fever, arthralgia, and general fatigue, and the finding of rhabdomyolysis was not anticipated on clinical grounds alone. This underscores the importance of checking CK in patients with severe hyponatremia, even when myalgia or weakness is not a prominent complaint, because unrecognized rhabdomyolysis can lead to acute kidney injury and complicate fluid management. Both persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis resolved as the serum sodium normalized, further supporting their relationship to the hyponatremic state. For clinicians managing patients with neuro-BD, awareness of these apparently unrelated symptoms can facilitate earlier recognition of SIADH and its complications.

## Treatment Strategy and Management Decisions

The therapeutic approach in this case evolved in three phases: empirical coverage for presumed CNS infection while the diagnosis remained uncertain, targeted anti-inflammatory therapy with colchicine once systemic BD and acute neuro-BD were confirmed, and conservative management of SIADH-related hyponatraemia. Each phase illustrates a principle critical to the management of acutely ill patients with overlapping neurological and systemic inflammatory features—namely, that broad empirical therapy must be initiated promptly when life-threatening infections cannot be excluded, but it should be curtailed just as promptly once the evidence redirects the diagnostic and therapeutic strategy.

On the day of admission, the patient presented with fever, headache, and marked hyponatraemia in the absence of a clear aetiology. Given this combination, bacterial meningitis remained a plausible and dangerous possibility that could not be safely dismissed before lumbar puncture results were available. Empirical cefepime, a fourth-generation cephalosporin with broad gram-positive and gram-negative coverage including *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* and excellent CSF penetration, was therefore initiated after blood cultures were drawn. This decision reflects standard practice for patients with suspected CNS infection in whom the causative organism has not yet been identified, particularly when presentation includes fever and neurological symptoms. The absence of a localised infectious focus did not diminish the justification for coverage, because meningitis and disseminated infection may present without a clear primary source.

When the patient's headache and vertigo worsened on day 3, the clinical team broadened the empirical regimen to include acyclovir, targeting herpes simplex virus and varicella zoster virus encephalitis. Viral encephalitis carries high morbidity and mortality when treatment is delayed, and the consequences of withholding acyclovir in a patient who truly has HSV encephalitis far exceed the risks of a short course of empirical therapy. This addition reflects a low-threshold approach to antiviral coverage in any patient with suspected CNS infection and deteriorating neurological status. Importantly, acyclovir was discontinued once CSF testing for HSV and VZV antigens and antibodies returned negative. Cefepime, too, was stopped once bacterial cultures proved negative and the overall clinical picture shifted toward a non-infectious systemic inflammatory disorder. The willingness to de-escalate empirical therapy as data accumulated was essential, because unnecessary continuation of broad-spectrum antimicrobials would have confounded the diagnostic picture, exposed the patient to medication-related adverse effects, and delayed recognition of BD.

Once systemic BD was confirmed by the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease and acute neuro-BD was established through CSF evidence of aseptic meningitis after exclusion of infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune causes, colchicine was started on day 8. Colchicine is an anti-inflammatory agent with a well-established mechanism of action that is distinct from corticosteroids and conventional immunosuppressants. It binds to tubulin and inhibits microtubule polymerisation, which in turn disrupts neutrophil chemotaxis, migration, and activation at sites of inflammation. By interfering with the cytoskeletal machinery required for neutrophil motility and degranulation, colchicine effectively limits the inflammatory cascade driven by these cells. In addition, colchicine suppresses the production of proinflammatory cytokines, most notably interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), both of which are central to the inflammatory physiology of BD. IL-6 is of particular relevance in this case because it has been associated with increased ADH release from the posterior pituitary, providing a plausible mechanistic link between the neuroinflammation of acute neuro-BD and the development of SIADH.

In BD specifically, colchicine has a firmly established role in the management of mucocutaneous manifestations, including oral aphthae, genital ulcers, and folliculitis-like rashes. Its efficacy in reducing the frequency and severity of these lesions is well documented, and it is widely regarded as a first-line agent for this purpose. By contrast, the role of colchicine in neuro-BD is far more limited and controversial. Systematic reviews have concluded that colchicine is ineffective as monotherapy for acute neuro-BD and that corticosteroids and other immunosuppressive agents, such as azathioprine or cyclophosphamide, are generally required for adequate disease control when parenchymal CNS involvement is present. In previously reported cases, colchicine has been used primarily as an adjunct to corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive therapies rather than as an independent treatment for neurological involvement. In this case, however, colchicine was used as monotherapy, and the patient's mucocutaneous symptoms, neurological manifestations, and hyponatraemia all resolved without the need for systemic corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants. Several factors underpinned this decision. The patient's neurological presentation was confined to aseptic meningitis without parenchymal brain lesions on MRI, a form of neuro-BD that is often self-limiting. Furthermore, the patient had poorly controlled type 2 diabetes mellitus, a condition in which systemic corticosteroids would risk significant worsening of glycaemic control—a consideration that strengthened the rationale for avoiding them if an alternative could be justified. Colchicine, which does not induce hyperglycaemia to the same degree as corticosteroids, was therefore an attractive option. The case report acknowledges that spontaneous recovery cannot be excluded, but the close temporal relationship between colchicine initiation and symptom resolution, along with sustained remission over four years of follow-up, suggests that colchicine may have contributed meaningfully to the outcome. This observation does not overturn the broader evidence favouring corticosteroids for acute neuro-BD, but it raises the possibility that colchicine monotherapy may be a reasonable option in selected patients, particularly those with isolated aseptic meningitis and contraindications to corticosteroid use.

Mucocutaneous lesions were managed concurrently with topical agents. Oral aphthous lesions were treated with topical corticosteroid ointment, which delivers anti-inflammatory activity directly to the ulcerated mucosa with minimal systemic absorption. Genital ulcers and cutaneous lesions were managed with silver sulfadiazine cream, a topical antimicrobial agent that helps prevent secondary bacterial infection in disrupted skin barriers and supports wound healing. These measures were appropriate adjuncts to systemic colchicine therapy and address the practical point that mucocutaneous lesions in BD often require localised treatment in addition to systemic anti-inflammatory control.

SIADH was managed conservatively, in keeping with established guidelines for the treatment of euvolemic hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia. The cornerstone of management was fluid restriction, limited to less than 800 mL per day, which reduces the intake of free water that the patient was unable to excrete due to persistent, inappropriate ADH secretion. Serum sodium was monitored daily to track the trajectory of correction and to ensure that the rate of correction remained within safe limits. Rapid correction of chronic hyponatraemia, defined as an increase exceeding 8–10 mmol/L in any 24-hour period, carries a significant risk of osmotic demyelination syndrome, a devastating neurological complication characterised by irreversible brain injury. Therefore, even though this patient's sodium was dangerously low at 124 mmol/L on presentation, the clinical team intentionally avoided aggressive correction with hypertonic saline. The patient's neurological status was stable, he did not exhibit seizures or severe obtundation that would necessitate emergent sodium correction, and his hyponatraemia was anticipated to improve as the underlying CNS inflammation was treated. For these reasons, hypertonic saline—a 3% sodium chloride solution reserved for severe symptomatic hyponatraemia with life-threatening neurological manifestations—was not required. Similarly, vasopressin receptor antagonists, known as vaptans, were not used. These agents promote aquaresis by blocking ADH activity at the renal collecting duct and can be effective for SIADH, but they carry their own risks of overly rapid sodium correction and are generally reserved for cases that do not respond to fluid restriction or in which restriction is poorly tolerated. In this patient, fluid restriction alone proved sufficient, and sodium normalised to 136 mmol/L by day 16, coinciding with the resolution of neurological symptoms and a decline in ADH to 1.8 pg/mL. This parallel resolution of SIADH with the treatment of neuro-BD further supports the proposition that the hyponatraemia was driven by CNS inflammation rather than by an independent or pharmacological cause.

The overall management strategy in this case illustrates several teachable principles. First, empirical therapy for life-threatening infections should be initiated when the diagnosis is uncertain but de-escalated decisively once evidence excludes infection. Second, the choice of anti-inflammatory agent in BD should be tailored to the specific disease manifestations, the severity of neurological involvement, and patient-specific factors such as comorbid diabetes that constrain the use of corticosteroids. Third, SIADH management must be guided by the severity of hyponatraemia, the presence or absence of severe neurological symptoms, and the anticipated reversibility of the underlying cause; conservative measures are appropriate when the trigger is expected to resolve with treatment of the primary disease. Finally, close monitoring of sodium correction rates is essential to prevent the iatrogenic harm of overly rapid correction, even when the initial value appears alarming.

## Clinical Outcome, Follow-up, and Key Lessons

The patient's clinical response to colchicine was rapid and encompassed multiple organ systems. Within one day of initiating colchicine on day 8 of admission, his fever resolved and his appetite improved. Over the subsequent two weeks, vertigo, general malaise, persistent hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions — including oral aphthae, folliculitis-like rashes, and the scrotal ulcer — gradually resolved. This temporal sequence is noteworthy because colchicine is established as a treatment for the mucocutaneous manifestations of Behçet's disease (BD) but is not generally considered effective for acute neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD), where corticosteroids and other immunosuppressants are regarded as mainstay therapy. The concurrent improvement of neurological symptoms and mucocutaneous lesions with a single agent suggests that the underlying inflammatory process driving both manifestations was responsive to colchicine's anti-inflammatory mechanisms, which include inhibition of neutrophil migration and suppression of interleukin-1β and interleukin-6 production.

The biochemical markers of disease activity paralleled the clinical improvement. By day 16, serum sodium had normalized to 136 mmol/L, and antidiuretic hormone (ADH) levels had decreased from 22.4 pg/mL to 1.8 pg/mL, confirming resolution of the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). The normalization of sodium and ADH in tandem with the resolution of neurological symptoms supports the proposition that central nervous system (CNS) inflammation from acute neuro-BD was the driving force behind inappropriate ADH secretion. Additionally, the elevated creatine kinase, which had been 1349 U/L on presentation — reflecting rhabdomyolysis secondary to profound hyponatraemia — returned to normal, as did the inflammatory markers C-reactive protein and erythrocyte sedimentation rate. The resolution of rhabdomyolysis underscores the importance of recognizing severe hyponatraemia as a cause of muscle injury and monitoring creatine kinase in patients with SIADH, as this complication is often underappreciated.

The patient's hospitalisation lasted one month, during which the acute phase of the illness was managed, hyponatraemia was carefully monitored, and rehabilitation was provided for the decline in physical function associated with his prolonged inflammatory illness. The management of SIADH was conservative throughout, consisting of fluid restriction to less than 800 mL per day with daily serum sodium monitoring and avoidance of rapid sodium correction. Hypertonic saline and vasopressin receptor antagonists were not required, which is consistent with the principle that SIADH secondary to a treatable underlying condition often resolves with management of the primary disease process, provided the hyponatraemia is not immediately life-threatening.

Long-term follow-up demonstrated sustained remission. Colchicine therapy was continued for approximately four years, during which the patient experienced no recurrence of BD symptoms or neurological manifestations. Notably, no systemic corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants were required at any point. After his BD stabilized, biguanide therapy was initiated for his previously untreated type 2 diabetes mellitus, and good glycaemic control was subsequently maintained. The successful delayed introduction of diabetes medication illustrates how an acute systemic illness can complicate the management of comorbid chronic conditions and how prioritizing the treatment of the acute inflammatory process can create the clinical stability needed to address longstanding metabolic disease.

Several broader clinical lessons emerge from this case. First, the concurrent diagnosis of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD at initial presentation is rare — neurological involvement typically develops several years after the initial BD diagnosis, and neurological manifestations at presentation occur in fewer than 5% of patients. Clinicians should therefore maintain a high index of suspicion for neuro-BD when patients with mucocutaneous features of BD present with non-specific CNS symptoms such as headache and fever, even when brain MRI is normal. Acute neuro-BD can present as aseptic meningitis without parenchymal lesions, and a normal MRI, particularly early in the disease course, does not exclude the diagnosis. The diagnosis of neuro-BD in this case was established through a combination of clinical mucocutaneous findings, CSF evidence of aseptic meningitis, and exclusion of infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune causes.

Second, this case highlights the importance of considering SIADH in patients with neuro-BD who present with hyponatraemia. The mechanism is thought to involve CNS inflammation-driven dysregulation of ADH secretion, potentially mediated by interleukin-6, which is elevated in the CSF of patients with acute neuro-BD. Although SIADH has been reported in BD patients receiving cyclophosphamide, its occurrence in the absence of pharmacological triggers — attributable solely to CNS inflammation — has been less well characterized. Clinicians evaluating patients with BD and neurological symptoms should routinely assess serum sodium, serum and urine osmolality, and urinary sodium concentration to identify SIADH, as persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis may serve as clinical clues to severe hyponatraemia.

Third, the diagnostic process in this case illustrates the value of pattern recognition over indiscriminate testing. The initial absence of genital ulcers made the diagnosis of BD uncertain, prompting a broad evaluation that included autoimmune serologies, serum angiotensin-converting enzyme, and soluble interleukin-2 receptor. These tests were performed as part of an exclusion-based approach rather than based on strong clinical suspicion, and several yielded mildly abnormal results that were ultimately nonspecific. The case underscores that clinicians should perform such investigations only when supported by appropriate clinical features and should interpret minor abnormalities with caution, as they can introduce diagnostic confusion rather than clarity.

Fourth, the successful treatment with colchicine monotherapy raises the possibility that colchicine may be a viable alternative to corticosteroids in selected cases of acute neuro-BD, particularly when corticosteroid therapy is undesirable or contraindicated. However, this observation must be interpreted carefully. Spontaneous resolution of acute neuro-BD presenting solely with meningoencephalitis-like symptoms has been reported, and the present case cannot distinguish between a therapeutic effect of colchicine and natural disease resolution. Systematic reviews have found colchicine ineffective for acute neuro-BD, and in previously reported cases, colchicine has been used as an adjunct to corticosteroids rather than as monotherapy. The findings from this single case are therefore hypothesis-generating rather than practice-changing.

Finally, the case has important limitations. A definitive causal link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH cannot be established from a single case report. The temporal association between the resolution of CNS inflammation and the normalization of ADH and sodium levels is consistent with a causal relationship but does not prove one. The absence of serum or CSF interleukin-6 measurements further limits mechanistic conclusions. Nonetheless, the case provides a compelling clinical observation that should alert clinicians to the potential for SIADH as a complication of acute neuro-BD and encourage vigilance for electrolyte disturbances in patients presenting with neuroinflammatory symptoms in the context of BD.
