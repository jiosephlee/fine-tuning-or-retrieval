Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Differential Diagnosis] After correction of severe vitamin B12 deficiency with parenteral cyanocobalamin, this patient's cytopenia and multisystem dysfunction persisted disproportionately to the nutritional deficiency alone. What specific features of the presentation—including hepatic fibrosis without identifiable cause, pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying, osteoporosis, and neuropathy—argued against nutritional deficiency, autoimmune disease, or infiltrative malignancy as the sole unifying diagnosis, and how did the clinical reasoning evolve from a acquired to a genetic etiology?

After initial correction of severe vitamin B12 deficiency with parenteral cyanocobalamin, this patient's ongoing pancytopenia and multisystem dysfunction demanded a broader diagnostic framework. Several features argued decisively against a purely nutritional, autoimmune, or malignant explanation and ultimately redirected the evaluation toward a telomere biology disorder (TBD).

**Why nutritional deficiency alone was insufficient.** While severe B12 deficiency accounted for the megaloblastic marrow findings and hypersegmented neutrophils, it did not explain the concurrent hepatic fibrosis (demonstrated on transient elastography with a liver stiffness of 18.1 kPa), the pulmonary fibrotic changes seen on PET-CT, the premature greying, the osteoporosis with recurrent fractures, or the chronic sensorimotor axonal neuropathy. These are not expected sequelae of B12 deficiency, and their persistence after adequate repletion signaled a separate, unifying process.

**Why autoimmune disease was excluded.** A positive direct antiglobulin test and elevated inflammatory markers initially raised concern for immune-mediated cytopenia or connective tissue disease. However, the reticulocyte response remained inappropriately low, there was no evidence of true hemolysis, autoimmune hepatitis and antinuclear antibodies were negative, and no systemic features of a rheumatologic disorder were present.

**Why infiltrative malignancy was excluded.** Bone marrow examination showed trilineage hematopoiesis without dysplasia or infiltration, cytogenetics were normal, and PET-CT demonstrated no overt malignancy or granulomatous disease.

**The pivot to a genetic etiology.** The critical observation was the convergence of fibrotic organ disease—hepatic and pulmonary—in a young adult with cytopenia and stigmata of accelerated aging. Telomere biology disorders arise from defective telomere maintenance, causing premature senescence in highly proliferative tissues, including bone marrow, hepatocytes, and pulmonary epithelium. PARN, an exoribonuclease essential for TERC 3′-end maturation, is among the genes least tolerant to loss-of-function variation. Monoallelic PARN variants are an established cause of telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure. In this patient, the monoallelic p.Cys205Arg variant within the conserved R3H domain, combined with the clinical phenotype, provided a coherent explanation for marrow dysfunction, hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying, and osteoporosis.

This case illustrates that TBDs may present in adulthood without the classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita. When unexplained multiorgan fibrosis coexists with cytopenia and premature aging features, a telomere biology disorder should be actively considered, even when an acquired cause—such as B12 deficiency—is initially identified.
