Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Morning Report] This patient lacked the classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita (oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation), yet was ultimately diagnosed with a telomere biology disorder. In a young adult presenting with unexplained multisystem disease, what specific constellation of clinical findings should prompt consideration of a TBD even in the absence of classical mucocutaneous features, and why are adult-onset TBDs frequently misdiagnosed?

In a young adult presenting with unexplained multisystem disease, the specific constellation that should prompt consideration of a telomere biology disorder (TBD)—even in the absence of the classical mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita—is the combination of **bone marrow failure or cytopenia, organ-specific fibrosis (particularly pulmonary and/or hepatic), and features of accelerated aging** such as premature greying, osteoporosis, or neuropathy occurring at an unusually young age. In this patient, the coexistence of pancytopenia, idiopathic hepatic fibrosis with cirrhosis, pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying, osteoporosis with recurrent fractures, and chronic sensorimotor polyneuropathy in a woman in her late 20s constituted a phenotype incompatible with any single acquired condition. Notably, she lacked oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation—the triad that classically defines dyskeratosis congenita—yet the multiorgan fibrotic and hematologic phenotype was strongly consistent with a TBD, as ultimately supported by identification of a monoallelic *PARN* variant.

The reason adult-onset TBDs are frequently misdiagnosed is multifactorial. First, the classical mucocutaneous triad is often absent in adults, especially in patients carrying monoallelic variants in genes such as *PARN*, *TERT*, *TERC*, or *RTEL1*, which tend to present with isolated organ disease—most commonly idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis or bone marrow failure—rather than the full dyskeratosis congenita phenotype. Second, the individual organ manifestations are clinically indistinguishable from their far more common acquired counterparts: cytopenias are attributed to nutritional deficiency or aplastic anemia, hepatic fibrosis to chronic viral hepatitis or autoimmune disease, and pulmonary fibrosis to environmental or occupational exposures. In this case, severe vitamin B12 deficiency initially provided a plausible explanation for the cytopenias and neurologic findings, and it was only after correction of nutritional deficiencies that the persistence and disproportionate severity of the multisystem involvement became apparent. Third, TBDs exhibit marked genetic heterogeneity, variable penetrance, and variable expressivity influenced by environmental and lifestyle factors, meaning that even within a single family, carriers may manifest different organ systems at different ages. The absence of a family history—as in this patient—does not exclude a TBD, as de novo mutations or reduced penetrance can obscure inheritance patterns.

The key clinical takeaway is this: **unexplained fibrosis affecting two or more organs, or fibrosis combined with cytopenia, in a young adult should trigger evaluation for a TBD regardless of mucocutaneous findings.** The diagnostic standard includes next-generation sequencing of telomere-related genes and flow cytometry–based FISH measurement of telomere length. Early recognition is essential not only because it unifies seemingly disparate findings under a single pathophysiology—accelerated telomere attrition leading to premature senescence of rapidly dividing tissues—but also because it has direct management implications, including genetic counseling, surveillance for progressive marrow failure and fibrotic complications, and consideration of hematopoietic stem cell transplantation in cases with significant hematologic disease.
