Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Pathophysiology] The PARN gene encodes a poly(A)-specific ribonuclease primarily known for its role in mRNA deadenylation and regulation of gene expression. How does loss of PARN function specifically lead to telomere shortening, and what is the mechanistic link between PARN's ribonuclease activity and maturation of the telomerase RNA component (TERC)?

## Mechanistic Link Between PARN Loss of Function and Telomere Shortening

PARN (poly[A]-specific ribonuclease) is best known for its role in cytoplasmic mRNA deadenylation, but its connection to telomere biology arises from a distinct and critical nuclear function: the 3′-end maturation of the telomerase RNA component (TERC). Understanding this link requires appreciating that telomerase is a ribonucleoprotein enzyme composed of a catalytic reverse transcriptase (TERT) and a noncoding RNA subunit (TERC), which serves as the template for telomeric repeat addition. Without adequate levels of properly processed, mature TERC, telomerase cannot maintain telomere length, and replicative tissues—bone marrow, pulmonary epithelium, and hepatic parenchyma among them—undergo accelerated telomere attrition with each cell division.

The mechanistic bridge between PARN and TERC lies in post-transcriptional RNA quality control. After transcription, TERC acquires short oligo(A) tails at its 3′ end through the action of noncanonical poly(A) polymerases. These oligo(A) tails function as degradation signals—they mark aberrant or incompletely processed nuclear RNAs for exonucleolytic destruction by the nuclear exosome. PARN counteracts this process by removing the oligo(A) tails from TERC, thereby protecting it from degradation and permitting proper 3′-end trimming and maturation. When PARN function is lost or impaired, TERC retains its oligo(A) tail, is recognized as a substrate for degradation, and fails to accumulate in its mature, functional form. The downstream consequence is a reduction in steady-state levels of bioavailable TERC, impaired assembly of the telomerase holoenzyme, insufficient telomere elongation, and progressive telomere shortening.

This mechanism is directly relevant to the case under discussion. The patient carried a monoallelic missense variant in PARN (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) located within the R3H domain, a single-stranded nucleic acid–binding region that is critical for substrate recognition and catalytic processing. The substitution of a highly conserved cysteine residue at this position is predicted to be deleterious by multiple in silico tools and affects a domain with no known benign variation. Because PARN is among the 20% of human genes least tolerant to rare disruptive variants, haploinsufficiency—even from a single mutant allele—is sufficient to compromise its enzymatic activity. The resulting partial deficit in TERC maturation would reduce telomerase capacity below the threshold needed to sustain telomere length in highly proliferative tissues, producing the clinical phenotype observed: pancytopenia from compromised hematopoietic stem cell reserve, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis.

It is worth emphasizing that this mechanism distinguishes PARN-associated telomere biology disorders from those caused by direct mutations in TERT or TERC themselves. In PARN-related disease, the telomerase protein and the TERC gene are intact; the defect lies one step upstream—in the post-transcriptional maturation and stabilization of the TERC RNA. This explains why PARN mutations can produce a phenotype that overlaps with TERT and TERC mutations (bone marrow failure, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis) despite acting through a ribonuclease whose primary annotated function is mRNA deadenylation. It also illustrates a broader principle in telomere biology disorders: the genetic defect need not reside within the telomerase complex itself; any gene required for the proper biogenesis, stability, or localization of its components can produce the same end result—critically short telomeres and premature failure of proliferative tissues.

In this case, the absence of the classical mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita and the initial attribution of cytopenias to severe vitamin B12 deficiency illustrate how PARN-mediated telomere dysfunction can be clinically cryptic. The key clinical lesson is that when unexplained multisystem fibrosis, cytopenias, and premature aging features coexist—particularly in a young adult—a telomere biology disorder should be considered regardless of whether the classical phenotype is present, and genetic testing should include genes involved in telomerase RNA processing, not just those encoding telomerase components directly.
