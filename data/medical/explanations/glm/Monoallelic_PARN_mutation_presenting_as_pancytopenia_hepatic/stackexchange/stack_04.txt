Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Differential Diagnosis] This patient had a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT, IgG 2+) with elevated LDH and indirect hyperbilirubinemia—findings that might initially suggest autoimmune hemolytic anemia. However, the low reticulocyte count (0.39%) and bone marrow findings of erythroid hyperplasia with increased iron stores pointed instead toward ineffective erythropoiesis. How can you distinguish ineffective erythropoiesis from peripheral hemolytic anemia when both can present with elevated LDH and indirect hyperbilirubinemia, and in what clinical scenarios might a positive DAT be misleading?

## Distinguishing Ineffective Erythropoiesis from Peripheral Hemolysis

Elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and indirect hyperbilirubinemia indicate increased red cell turnover, but they do not specify *where* that turnover occurs. The critical discriminator is the **reticulocyte count**.

In **peripheral hemolytic anemia**, the bone marrow is intact and responds appropriately to accelerated destruction with robust reticulocytosis—typically >2–3% or an absolute reticulocyte count >100 × 10⁹/L. The marrow releases young red cells into the circulation, and LDH elevation is predominantly LDH-2 (from mature erythrocytes). Haptoglobin is typically depleted.

In **ineffective erythropoiesis**, erythroid precursors proliferate in the marrow but undergo **intramedullary apoptosis** before completing maturation. The marrow is hypercellular with erythroid hyperplasia, but few reticulocytes escape into the periphery. This was exactly the pattern seen in this patient: a reticulocyte count of only 0.39% despite marrow erythroid hyperplasia. The elevated LDH reflects destruction of nucleated erythroid precursors (predominantly LDH-1 and LDH-3), and indirect hyperbilirubinemia arises from catabolism of heme released by dying marrow precursors—both mimicking peripheral hemolysis. The **increased iron stores** on marrow examination provided an additional clue: in ineffective erythropoiesis, iron is delivered to the marrow but cannot be incorporated into hemoglobin effectively because erythroid maturation is arrested, leading to iron accumulation. In true peripheral hemolysis with adequate marrow response, iron stores are typically normal or reduced as iron is consumed for accelerated erythropoiesis.

The peripheral smear further supported the diagnosis. Basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils are hallmarks of megaloblastic/ineffective erythropoiesis—not features of immune-mediated destruction, where spherocytes, schistocytes, or polychromasia would predominate.

## When a Positive DAT Can Be Misleading

A positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) demonstrates IgG or complement bound to the red cell surface, but it does not by itself establish a diagnosis of autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA). Several scenarios produce a positive DAT without clinically significant immune hemolysis:

- **Passive adsorption** of donor antibodies following transfusion or IVIG administration
- **Autoantibodies** in patients with lymphoproliferative disorders, infections, or autoimmune disease that bind red cells without triggering meaningful destruction
- **Drug-dependent antibodies** or nonspecific protein coating in inflammatory states

The key is to interpret the DAT in the full clinical context. In AIHA, you expect a **corrected reticulocyte response**, evidence of active hemolysis (falling hemoglobin, elevated LDH, low haptoglobin, spherocytes on smear), and usually a positive **indirect antiglobulin test** (IAT), which detects free autoantibody in the serum. In this patient, the IAT was negative, the reticulocyte count was suppressed, and the marrow demonstrated ineffective erythropoiesis with increased iron stores—none of which fit immune-mediated peripheral destruction. The DAT positivity was a red herring, potentially reflecting nonspecific IgG binding in the setting of systemic inflammation or an underlying immune dysregulation associated with her telomere biology disorder, but it was not driving her anemia.

## Clinical Takeaway

When evaluating a patient with elevated LDH and indirect hyperbilirubinemia, the single most useful initial test is the **reticulocyte count**. A low or inappropriately normal reticulocyte count in the setting of anemia should redirect the diagnostic reasoning away from peripheral destruction and toward ineffective erythropoiesis or marrow failure—even when the DAT is positive. Bone marrow examination with iron staining and careful peripheral smear review will confirm the mechanism, as it did in this case, where megaloblastic features and increased iron stores established intramedullary destruction rather than immune-mediated peripheral hemolysis.
