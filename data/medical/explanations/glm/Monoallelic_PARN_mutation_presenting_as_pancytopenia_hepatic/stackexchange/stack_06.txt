Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Morning Report] The gold standard for diagnosing a suspected telomere biology disorder combines genetic testing via next-generation sequencing with flow cytometry-based FISH (Flow-FISH) to measure telomere length. In this case, a monoallelic PARN missense variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) was classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS). Using ACMG/AMP criteria, what specific evidence supported this variant's potential pathogenicity, and how should a VUS in a telomere-related gene be interpreted when the clinical phenotype is strongly compatible with a TBD?

## Answer

Under the ACMG/AMP framework, the PARN c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg) variant was classified as a VUS because it met several *supportive* but no *strong* or *very strong* evidence criteria. The specific evidence cited in this case was:

- **PM1 (moderate):** The substitution alters a highly conserved cysteine residue within the R3H domain (residues 178–245), a critical and well-established functional domain of PARN with no known benign variation.
- **PM2 (moderate):** The variant is absent or extremely rare in population databases, arguing against a common polymorphism.
- **PP3 (supporting):** Four of five in silico prediction tools (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT) predict a damaging effect on protein function.
- **PP2 (supporting):** PARN has a low rate of benign missense variation—missense variants are a recognized disease mechanism, and PARN ranks among the 20% of genes least tolerant to rare disruptive variants, supporting haploinsufficiency.

Additional circumstantial evidence included parallels with other PARN missense variants (p.Lys59Arg and p.Ala383Val) classified as likely pathogenic in ClinVar. However, absent functional assay data, segregation data, or de novo confirmation, these criteria collectively remain insufficient to upgrade the variant beyond VUS.

The critical teaching point is how to interpret a VUS in a telomere-related gene when the phenotype is strongly concordant. **Variant classification under ACMG/AMP guidelines is intended to be genotype-driven and independent of the patient's phenotype to avoid circular reasoning.** Therefore, a VUS must not be reclassified as pathogenic solely because the clinical picture fits. However, clinical decision-making need not be paralyzed by the VUS label. When a patient presents with a multisystem phenotype highly suggestive of a TBD—bone marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, premature greying, and osteoporosis—and genetic testing identifies a rare, computationally damaging variant in a gene with a well-established role in telomere biology, the clinical–genetic correlation can reasonably support a *presumptive* TBD diagnosis for the purposes of counseling, surveillance, and family evaluation. This is analogous to the broader principle in medical genetics: a VUS guides uncertainty in the variant report, but the clinician integrates the entire clinical context for patient management.

In this case, the authors appropriately used the phenotypic concordance to justify treating the patient as having a TBD—arranging genetic counseling, planning longitudinal monitoring of marrow and organ function, and considering future transplant evaluation—while acknowledging that the variant itself requires further evidence (such as functional telomere length assays by Flow-FISH, family segregation studies, or RNA studies demonstrating impaired PARN deadenylation activity) before definitive pathogenicity can be assigned. This approach—strict genotype classification coupled with pragmatic phenotype-integrated clinical reasoning—is the correct framework for managing VUS findings in suspected Mendelian disorders.
