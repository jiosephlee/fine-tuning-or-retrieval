Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# Beyond the Triad: Recognizing Telomere Biology Disorders When Dyskeratosis Congenita's Classic Features Are Absent

## The Case That Stops You in Your Tracks

Picture this: a woman in her late 20s walks into your tertiary care centre with five years of progressive fatigue that has now deteriorated to the point where she needs assistance with self-care. She has pancytopenia, cirrhosis, pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying, osteoporosis with recurrent fractures, and a chronic axonal polyneuropathy. You correct her severe B12 deficiency, but the multisystem wreckage persists. No mucocutaneous triad. No family history. No obvious unifying diagnosis — until clinical exome sequencing lands on a monoallelic PARN missense variant and everything snaps into focus.

This is a telomere biology disorder (TBD) hiding in plain sight, and it illustrates a critical diagnostic trap: we are conditioned to think of dyskeratosis congenita (DC) as a paediatric disease defined by oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation. But TBDs can present in adulthood, with isolated organ involvement, and without a single feature of the classical triad. If you are not actively considering TBDs in young adults with unexplained bone marrow failure, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), or hepatic fibrosis, you are going to miss them.

## The Diagnostic Journey: Why Nutritional Deficiency Was a Red Herring

This patient's initial workup was dominated by severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency, which explained a good chunk of her haematological picture — dimorphic anaemia, hypersegmented neutrophils, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and a bone marrow showing erythroid hyperplasia with increased iron stores consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis. She received appropriate replacement therapy. But several features refused to resolve into a nutritional diagnosis: cirrhosis on transient elastography (liver stiffness 18.1 kPa) with no identifiable viral, autoimmune, or metabolic cause; diffuse ground-glass haziness and fibrotic changes in both lung fields on PET-CT; osteoporosis with secondary hyperparathyroidism in a twenty-something; and a positive DAT that did not fit with the low reticulocyte response or marrow findings.

The team systematically excluded haematologic malignancy, infiltrative disease, autoimmune cytopenia, connective tissue disease, and infectious causes of hepatic fibrosis. When the dust settled, the remaining constellation — cytopenia, premature greying, hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, osteoporosis, and neuropathy in a young adult — pointed squarely toward a genetic or syndromic aetiology. Clinical exome sequencing identified a monoallelic PARN variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) in the R3H domain, classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance but predicted damaging by four of five in silico tools and absent from population databases. The clinical-genetic correlation was consistent with a TBD.

## PARN: A Gene at the Crossroads of Pulmonary and Hepatic Fibrosis

PARN encodes a poly(A)-specific ribonuclease that regulates mRNA deadenylation and is essential for 3'-end maturation of TERC, the telomerase RNA component. It is among the 20% of genes least tolerant to rare disruptive variants, supporting haploinsufficiency as a disease mechanism even in monoallelic carriers. Biallelic PARN variants cause autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6) and have been reported in Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome, while monoallelic variants are more typically associated with telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

What makes this case particularly instructive is that this patient carried only a single PARN allele yet developed both pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis — a combination more classically linked to TERT or TERC mutations. This expands the recognised phenotypic spectrum of monoallelic PARN variants and aligns with emerging data from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry, where 88.8% of adults with TERC or TERT mutations had at least moderate hepatic fibrosis and 33.3% had cirrhosis. The take-home: hepatic fibrosis in TBDs is likely under-recognised, and PARN mutations may produce a broader fibrotic phenotype than previously appreciated.

## The Broader TBD Spectrum and Variable Penetrance

TBDs encompass a wide clinical range. DC is the prototype, classically defined by the mucocutaneous triad or by two of those features plus bone marrow failure. Severe childhood subtypes include Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome (cerebellar hypoplasia, immunodeficiency, aplastic anaemia), Revesz syndrome (exudative retinopathy, intracranial calcifications), and Coats plus syndrome (retinal telangiectasia, intracranial calcifications, bone marrow failure). But adult presentations without the triad are increasingly recognised and are frequently misdiagnosed, especially in non-specialised centres. These patients may be labelled as having aplastic anaemia, myelodysplastic syndrome, cryptogenic cirrhosis, or sporadic IPF — all of which may actually be TBDs in disguise.

Variable penetrance, multiple causative genes (DKC1, TERT, TERC, RTEL1, PARN, and others), inheritance patterns, environmental factors, and somatic genetic rescue all influence which organ systems manifest and when. This patient's severe B12 deficiency may have acted as a haematopoietic stressor that unmasked an underlying TBD-predisposed marrow, a reminder that telomere shortening compromises replicative capacity of haematopoietic stem cells and may render patients more vulnerable to stressors that a normal marrow could weather.

## When to Suspect a TBD: A Practical Framework

This case offers a pragmatic clinical approach. Consider a TBD in any young adult presenting with unexplained or disproportionate combinations of the following:

Persistent cytopenia or marrow failure without an alternative explanation, especially when disproportionate to nutritional deficiency or when it fails to correct after appropriate treatment. Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis in a young patient or anyone without typical environmental or occupational risk factors. Hepatic fibrosis or cirrhosis of unknown aetiology with negative autoimmune, infectious, and metabolic workups. Features of accelerated ageing — premature greying, osteoporosis with recurrent fractures in a young adult, or neuropathy. A family history of early-onset haematologic, pulmonary, or hepatic disease, though de novo mutations occur and absence of family history does not exclude the diagnosis.

The gold standard for diagnosis involves next-generation sequencing of telomere-related genes and flow cytometry-based FISH to measure telomere length. Treatment is largely supportive and organ-specific, with haematopoietic stem cell transplantation as a potentially curative option for progressive marrow failure. Androgens like danazol and nandrolone have shown telomere elongation in preclinical and small clinical studies, but long-term efficacy and safety remain uncertain.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Do not anchor on the mucocutaneous triad.** TBDs can present in adulthood with isolated organ involvement — bone marrow failure, IPF, or hepatic fibrosis — and no classic features of dyskeratosis congenita.
- **Premature greying, osteoporosis, and cytopenia in a 20-something should trigger genetic evaluation.** This combination is not normal ageing and demands investigation beyond nutritional and autoimmune workups.
- **Hepatic fibrosis in TBDs is under-recognised.** Data from the Aachen registry suggest it is far more prevalent than previously appreciated, even with PARN variants traditionally linked to pulmonary fibrosis.
- **Nutritional deficiency can be a red herring.** Correct it, but if multisystem dysfunction persists or seems disproportionate, think broader.
- **Monoallelic PARN mutations can produce a wider phenotype than IPF alone.** This case demonstrates hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis from a single PARN allele, expanding the recognised clinical spectrum.
- **Variable penetrance means absence of family history does not rule out a TBD.** De novo mutations, incomplete penetrance, and somatic genetic rescue all influence presentation.
- **Early recognition matters.** It opens the door to genetic counselling, surveillance for progressive organ involvement, and timely consideration of haematopoietic stem cell transplantation in progressive marrow failure.
- **Telomere length testing and clinical exome sequencing are the diagnostic tools.** If you suspect a TBD, do not rely on clinical features alone — confirm with genetic and telomere length analysis.
