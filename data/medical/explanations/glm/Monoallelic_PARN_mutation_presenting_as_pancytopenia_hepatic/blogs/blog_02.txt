Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# PARN Mutations: Monoallelic vs. Biallelic and the Expanding Spectrum from Pulmonary Fibrosis to Hepatic Cirrhosis

## Why This Case Stops You in Your Tracks

A woman in her late 20s walks in with five years of progressive fatigue, and you find pancytopenia, cirrhosis, pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying, osteoporosis, and a polyneuropathy. You correct her severe B12 deficiency, and she still has a multiorgan disease that doesn't fit neatly into any single acquired diagnosis. No mucocutaneous triad. No family history. Just a constellation of premature ageing and fibrosis that screams "something genetic" but lacks the textbook features you'd expect. Then clinical exome sequencing returns a monoallelic PARN variant — a gene classically associated with pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure in its single-allele form, not hepatic cirrhosis. This case forces us to reconsider what we think we know about genotype-phenotype correlations in telomere biology disorders.

## Telomere Biology Disorders: A Quick Refresher

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) arise from Mendelian defects in telomere maintenance genes. Every time a cell divides, telomeres shorten. When they reach a critical length, the cell undergoes senescence or apoptosis. Tissues with high turnover — bone marrow, skin, mucosa, hair follicles, hepatocytes — bear the brunt. The prototypical TBD is dyskeratosis congenita (DC), classically defined by the mucocutaneous triad of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation, often accompanied by bone marrow failure. Severe childhood subtypes include Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Here's the critical point for the bedside clinician: TBDs don't always show up with the classical triad. They can present in adulthood with isolated pulmonary fibrosis, cryptogenic cirrhosis, or apparently idiopathic bone marrow failure. Without the skin and nail findings, and without a family history, these patients get misdiagnosed — sometimes for years.

## PARN Genetics: Monoallelic vs. Biallelic — It Matters

PARN encodes a poly(A)-specific ribonuclease that regulates mRNA deadenylation and, critically, is required for proper 3'-end maturation of TERC (the telomerase RNA component). So when PARN is dysfunctional, telomerase function suffers, and telomeres shorten prematurely.

The genotype-phenotype split has been reasonably tidy up to now. Biallelic PARN variants cause autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), with severe childhood presentations including Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome. Monoallelic PARN variants, by contrast, have been associated with telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4) — a phenotype that tends to present in adulthood, often as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) discovered on imaging or at lung biopsy. The initial link between PARN and IPF came from whole-exome sequencing studies identifying monoallelic PARN rare variants in sporadic IPF cases, and over 65 cases have now been documented in the literature.

This patient's variant — c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg) — is monoallelic, classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. It affects a highly conserved residue within the R3H domain, a critical functional region with no known benign variation. Four of five in silico prediction tools call it deleterious. While formal pathogenicity remains uncertain, the clinical-genetic correlation in this patient is compelling. PARN is among the 20% of genes least tolerant to rare disruptive variants, supporting the concept of haploinsufficiency even in monoallelic carriers. The parallel with other PARN missense variants classified as likely pathogenic in ClinVar — such as p.Lys59Arg and p.Ala383Val — further strengthens the case.

## The Case: What Happened on the Clinical Journey

This woman presented with five years of progressive weakness that had escalated to requiring assistance with self-care. She had exertional dyspnoea, weight loss, and anorexia. On exam, she looked older than her years: premature greying, pallor, icterus, knuckle hyperpigmentation, and a bald atrophic tongue. She had thoracic scoliosis, bilateral thenar atrophy, hepatomegaly, and reduced breath sounds in the infra-scapular regions.

Her labs revealed pancytopenia with a reticulocyte count of 0.39%, dimorphic anaemia, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils — classic megaloblastic morphology. Severe B12 and folate deficiency were confirmed. Iron studies showed elevated serum iron and ferritin, consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis rather than iron deficiency. Bone marrow showed trilineage haematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia and increased iron stores, no malignancy or dysplasia. The DAT was positive for IgG, but the low reticulocyte response and marrow picture argued against immune-mediated destruction.

But here's where it gets interesting. Her liver stiffness on transient elastography was 18.1 kPa — cirrhotic. Autoimmune serologies were negative. Infectious workup was unrevealing. PET-CT showed diffuse ground-glass haziness with fibrotic and subpleural changes in both lungs, hepatosplenomegaly, and ascites — but no malignancy or granulomatous disease. She also had osteoporosis on DEXA, secondary hyperparathyroidism, and an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy on EMG. Even after B12 repletion, the systemic dysfunction was disproportionate and persistent. That's what triggered the genetic workup.

## The Surprising Part: Hepatic Fibrosis with Monoallelic PARN

This is where the case challenges conventional genotype-phenotype thinking. Hepatic fibrosis in TBDs has been under-recognised, but the existing literature links it primarily to TERT and TERC mutations. A retrospective analysis from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry found that 88.8% of adult patients with pathogenic TERT or TERC mutations had at least moderate hepatic fibrosis on transient elastography, and 33.3% had cirrhosis. One previously reported case described a patient with pancytopenia and hepatic fibrosis who carried monoallelic variants in both PARN and TERT — raising the question of whether the TERT variant was driving the liver phenotype.

This patient, however, had only a monoallelic PARN variant — no TERT or TERC variant identified. Yet she developed both pulmonary fibrosis and cirrhosis. The PARN variant's downstream impact on telomerase function via impaired TERC maturation provides a plausible biological mechanism for hepatic fibrosis, expanding the recognised clinical spectrum of PARN-associated TBDs beyond what we've classically expected.

## Why the Phenotype Varies: Haploinsufficiency, Penetrance, and the Environment

If this variant is pathogenic, why doesn't every monoallelic PARN carrier develop cirrhosis and pulmonary fibrosis? The answer lies in the complex interplay of genetic background, incomplete penetrance, somatic genetic rescue, and environmental exposures that differentially affect organ systems. Multiple telomere-related genes can contribute. Environmental and lifestyle factors influence the replicative stress placed on different tissues. In this patient, severe B12 deficiency may have served as an additional haematopoietic stressor on an already compromised marrow — telomere shortening reduces the replicative reserve of haematopoietic stem cells, making them more vulnerable to nutritional insults and accelerating the march toward bone marrow failure. The cytopenia was likely multifactorial, but the PARN variant underscores the need for ongoing haematological surveillance.

This is a critical teaching point: the absence of a family history doesn't exclude a hereditary syndrome. De novo mutations happen. Variable penetrance means affected relatives may be undiagnosed. And TBDs can masquerade as acquired disease for years before the pattern becomes recognisable.

## Diagnostic Approach and What to Do When You Suspect a TBD

The gold standard for diagnosis combines next-generation sequencing of telomere-related genes with flow cytometry-based FISH assays to measure telomere length in leucocyte subsets. In a young patient with unexplained multisystem disease — especially combinations of cytopenia, pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, or features of premature ageing — even without the classical mucocutaneous triad, a TBD should be on the differential.

Treatment is currently largely supportive and organ-specific. Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation can be potentially curative for haematologic manifestations. Androgens like danazol and nandrolone have shown some telomere-elongating effects in preclinical and small clinical studies, but long-term outcomes remain unknown. Genetic counselling for the family is essential once a TBD is confirmed. And for the patient herself, ongoing surveillance of pulmonary, hepatic, and haematological function is the name of the game.

## The Bottom Line

This case is a reminder that genotype-phenotype correlations in TBDs are still evolving. A monoallelic PARN variant, classically linked to pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure, can produce hepatic cirrhosis — a phenotype previously attributed to TERT and TERC mutations. When you see a young adult with unexplained multiorgan fibrosis, cytopenia, and features of premature ageing, think telomere biology. Don't wait for the mucocutaneous triad. Don't be reassured by a negative family history. And don't let a corrected B12 deficiency close the diagnostic loop prematurely.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Monoallelic PARN variants** classically present as telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4); biallelic variants cause autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6) and Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome
- **Hepatic fibrosis can occur with monoallelic PARN mutations** — this case expands the recognised phenotype beyond TERT/TECR-associated cirrhosis
- **PARN is among the 20% of genes least tolerant to disruptive variants**, supporting haploinsufficiency as a plausible disease mechanism even in monoallelic carriers
- **TBDs can present without the classical mucocutaneous triad** — suspect them in young adults with unexplained combinations of cytopenia, pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, or premature ageing features, even without a family history
- **Nutritional deficiencies can unmask or exacerbate an underlying TBD** — severe B12 deficiency may represent a haematopoietic stressor that reveals reduced marrow reserve from telomere shortening
- **Diagnosis requires genetic testing plus telomere length measurement** by flow cytometry-based FISH; treatment is largely supportive, with HSCT as a potential curative option for haematologic manifestations and genetic counselling for the family
- **Correcting the deficiency doesn't correct the disease** — if the systemic phenotype persists after nutritional repletion, keep the diagnostic loop open
