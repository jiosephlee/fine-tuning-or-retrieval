Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# What Do I Do With a Variant of Uncertain Significance? A Clinician's Guide to ACMG Framework and Clinical-Genetic Correlation

## The Case That Stops You in Your Tracks

Picture this: a woman in her late 20s walks in with five years of progressive fatigue, and by the time you see her, she needs help with self-care. She's got pancytopenia, dimorphic anaemia, severe B12 deficiency, cirrhosis, pulmonary fibrosis, osteoporosis, premature greying, neuropathy, and hepatosplenomegaly. You correct her B12 deficiency — and the systemic dysfunction persists, disproportionate and unrelenting. The infectious workup is essentially negative. Autoimmune serologies are negative. No malignancy on PET-CT. No family history. So you send clinical exome sequencing, and you get back a single monoallelic missense variant in *PARN*: c.613T>C, p.Cys205Arg. And the lab report says: **Variant of Uncertain Significance**.

Now what?

This is the scenario described in a recent case report by Soni and colleagues, and it captures a dilemma that clinicians face increasingly often as next-generation sequencing becomes more accessible. You have a patient with a phenotype that screams "something genetic," a gene that fits the story, and a variant that the lab won't commit to calling pathogenic. Do you wait? Do you act? Do you dismiss it?

Let's walk through how this team reasoned through it, because the framework they used is one every clinician ordering genomic testing should understand.

## Why a VUS Is Not the Same as "Noise"

A Variant of Uncertain Significance does not mean "probably benign." It means the available evidence is insufficient to classify the variant as pathogenic, likely pathogenic, benign, or likely benign under the ACMG/AMP framework. The classification system — established by the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and the Association for Molecular Pathology in 2015 — uses a combination of evidence types, each assigned a weight, to arrive at a five-tier classification. The tiers combine population data, computational predictions, functional evidence, segregation data, and the gene's established disease mechanism.

The critical point for clinicians is this: **a VUS can accumulate evidence over time and migrate to a higher classification**. Your job is not to override the lab but to assess whether the clinical phenotype is consistent enough with the gene's known disease spectrum to justify treating the result as clinically actionable — while acknowledging the uncertainty honestly with the patient.

## Applying the ACMG Framework to p.Cys205Arg

The clinical team in this case did not simply accept the VUS label and move on. They examined the specific ACMG criteria the variant met and weighed them against the clinical picture. Here is what they found:

**PM1 — Located in a critical functional domain.** The p.Cys205Arg substitution falls within the R3H domain of PARN (residues 178–245). This domain is highly conserved, and no benign variation has been described in this region. PM1 applies when a variant sits in a well-established functional domain where missense changes are known to cause disease. The R3H domain qualifies — it is involved in RNA binding and substrate recognition, central to PARN's deadenylation function.

**PM2 — Absent from population databases.** The variant is either absent or present at an extremely low frequency in gnomAD and other population databases. A variant that is absent from large control populations is more likely to be pathogenic, particularly in a gene like PARN that is not highly tolerant to loss-of-function variation. PARN is among the 20% of genes least tolerant to rare, disruptive variants.

**PP3 — Computational tools predict a damaging effect.** Four of five in silico prediction tools — LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT — predict that p.Cys205Arg is deleterious. While in silico predictions are supportive evidence only and never sufficient on their own, their concordance strengthens the case.

**PP2 — PARN has a low rate of benign missense variation.** PARN is a gene where missense variants are a known mechanism of disease, and the rate of benign missense variation is low. This means that when you encounter a missense variant in PARN, the prior probability of pathogenicity is higher than it would be in a gene where missense variation is common and harmless.

Now, if you tally these up using the ACMG point system, PM1 and PM2 are moderate evidence, and PP2 and PP3 are supporting evidence. That combination does not reach the threshold for "likely pathogenic" — which requires at least moderate and supporting evidence in specific combinations — hence the VUS classification. The lab got it right by the book.

But here is where clinical correlation enters the picture.

## Clinical-Genetic Correlation: The Piece the Lab Cannot Assess

The lab evaluates the variant in isolation. It cannot weigh the variant against your patient's phenotype — only you can do that. And in this case, the phenotype was remarkably congruent with the known PARN-associated disease spectrum.

Monoallelic PARN variants are established causes of telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4). The literature now documents over 65 cases of monoallelic PARN variants associated with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. Biallelic PARN variants cause a more severe phenotype — dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), and Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome. This patient had a monoallelic variant, which aligns with the milder, later-onset end of the spectrum.

Her clinical features — pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, cytopenia, and features of accelerated ageing such as premature greying and osteoporosis in a young adult — fit squarely within the telomere biology disorder (TBD) spectrum. The hepatic fibrosis is particularly noteworthy. While monoallelic PARN is more commonly associated with pulmonary fibrosis, emerging data from telomere biology disease registries show that hepatic fibrosis is under-recognised across TBDs. A registry analysis found that 88.8% of adults with TERT or TERC mutations had at least moderate hepatic fibrosis, and 33.3% had cirrhosis. The PARN variant's downstream impact on telomerase function — PARN is required for 3'-end maturation of TERC — plausibly explains hepatic fibrosis through the same mechanism seen with TERT and TERC mutations.

The clinical team also looked to ClinVar for precedent. Other PARN missense variants — p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1) — are classified as "likely pathogenic." These variants were evaluated under the same ACMG framework and crossed the threshold that p.Cys205Arg narrowly misses. The parallel is informative: it demonstrates that PARN missense variants in conserved residues can and do reach pathogenic classification, and that the gap between VUS and likely pathogenic for this variant may close with additional functional or segregation data.

## The Tension: Wait or Act?

Here is the uncomfortable reality of clinical genetics in progressive disease. You cannot always wait for the variant to be reclassified before making clinical decisions.

In this case, the team used the PARN VUS not to make a definitive molecular diagnosis but to **support a clinical diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder**. The distinction matters. They did not tell the patient she definitively had a pathogenic mutation. They told her that her clinical phenotype was consistent with a TBD, that the genetic finding was supportive but not conclusive, and that the combination warranted a TBD management approach.

What does that management approach look like? It means ongoing surveillance of haematologic, hepatic, and pulmonary function. It means genetic counselling for the family, because even a VUS in a gene with autosomal dominant or recessive implications has relevance for relatives who may carry the same variant with different expressivity. It means considering telomere length testing — flow cytometry-based FISH assays — as an independent line of evidence. And in progressive cases with marrow failure, it means early referral for haematopoietic stem cell transplantation evaluation, because the window for successful transplant narrows as organ fibrosis advances.

Supportive measures also matter. Androgen therapy with danazol has shown telomere elongation in preclinical and small clinical studies, though long-term outcomes remain unknown. For this patient, the team corrected nutritional deficiencies, provided transfusion support, addressed osteoporosis, and arranged physiotherapy — pragmatic management regardless of the genetic certainty.

## What This Means for Your Practice

The take-home message is not that you should override VUS classifications. It is that you should understand them — what evidence supports them, what evidence is missing, and how your patient's phenotype fits the gene's known disease spectrum. A VUS is a starting point for clinical correlation, not an endpoint.

- **Understand the ACMG criteria behind the classification.** Ask which specific evidence codes the variant meets and which are missing. The gap between VUS and likely pathogenic is often one or two pieces of evidence — functional data, segregation data, or additional unrelated cases.
- **Assess clinical-phenotypic congruence independently.** The lab cannot do this. If your patient's phenotype matches the gene's established disease spectrum, document that reasoning explicitly in the chart.
- **Use telomere length testing as complementary evidence.** Flow-FISH telomere length measurement is the gold standard for TBD diagnosis and can provide independent support for a clinical diagnosis even when the variant classification is uncertain.
- **Do not wait for reclassification in progressive disease.** Initiate TBD-appropriate surveillance and counselling based on clinical suspicion. Re-evaluation of the variant as new data accumulate is appropriate — request periodic updates from the lab or check ClinVar.
- **Be transparent with patients about uncertainty.** Explain that the variant is supportive but not definitive, that the clinical diagnosis is based on the totality of evidence, and that management decisions are driven by the clinical picture — not solely by the genetic result.

The patient in this case was managed supportively, received genetic counselling, and is under ongoing surveillance. Her blood counts stabilised at two-week follow-up. The PARN variant remains a VUS. But the clinical diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder gave her and her clinicians a framework for understanding her multisystem disease, for anticipating complications, and for making informed decisions about the future. Sometimes, that is what a VUS can do — not close the case, but open a clinical pathway.
