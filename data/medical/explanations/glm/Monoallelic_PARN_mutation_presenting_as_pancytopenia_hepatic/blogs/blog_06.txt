Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# Managing Telomere Biology Disorders: Surveillance, Androgens, Transplantation, and the Counseling Conversation

## When the Zebras Hide in Plain Sight

Picture this: a woman in her late 20s walks in with five years of progressive fatigue, pancytopenia, premature greying, cirrhosis, pulmonary fibrosis, osteoporosis, and neuropathy. You correct her severe B12 deficiency — and she improves, but only partially. The cytopenias don't fully resolve. The fibrosis doesn't regress. The premature ageing features certainly don't reverse. That's the moment you realise you're not looking at nutritional deficiency or an occult autoimmune process. You're looking at a telomere biology disorder (TBD), and the diagnosis changes everything about how you counsel her, surveil her, and discuss what "curative" actually means.

What makes this case — reported by Soni and colleagues — so instructive is not just the rarity of a monoallelic PARN mutation presenting without the classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita. It's that the patient was eventually diagnosed, counselled, and managed with a thoughtful, realistic plan that acknowledges the limits of what we can offer while maximising what we can control. That conversation — the one where you tell a young adult their genotype carries a progressive, multisystem disease with limited curative options — is one every clinician should be prepared to have.

## The Diagnostic Gold Standard: Genetics Plus Telomere Length

The case team arrived at the diagnosis through clinical exome sequencing, which identified a monoallelic PARN missense variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. They then correlated the genetic finding with the clinical phenotype — cytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, and premature ageing features — to support a TBD diagnosis. This clinical-genetic correlation approach is essential because variant classification alone often leaves you in limbo, particularly for rare genes like PARN where the full phenotypic spectrum is still being defined.

The gold standard for diagnosing suspected TBDs combines two modalities. Next-generation sequencing (or clinical exome sequencing) identifies the causative variant, while flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridisation (flow-FISH) directly measures telomere length in leukocyte subsets. Flow-FISH is critical because it provides functional evidence — you can see whether the telomeres are abnormally short for age, which supports pathogenicity even when a variant is labelled "uncertain." In this case, the team leaned on the gene's established role in TBD biology, in silico predictions, ACMG/AMP supportive criteria, and parallels with other likely pathogenic PARN missense variants to build their case.

The practical pearl here: if you suspect a TBD clinically, don't rely on the genetic report alone. The variant classification may say "uncertain," but the telomere length assay and the clinical phenotype may tell a different story. You need all three pieces — genotype, telomere length, and phenotype — to make the call.

## Supportive Care: Organ by Organ, Symptom by Symptom

Once the diagnosis is established, management is largely supportive and tailored to the specific organ systems involved. In this patient, the initial intervention was correcting severe nutritional deficiencies — intravenous cyanocobalamin, oral folic acid, vitamin D, and calcium — which produced symptomatic improvement and stabilised blood counts at her two-week follow-up. She also received whole blood transfusions for symptomatic anaemia, electrolyte correction for hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia, and physiotherapy for her neuromuscular complications.

But supportive care in TBD is not just about treating what's broken today. It's about surveillance for what will break tomorrow. The bone marrow, lungs, and liver are the three organs most commonly and progressively involved. This patient continues outpatient monitoring of hepatic and pulmonary function alongside haematological surveillance, watching for evolution toward frank marrow failure. The osteoporosis requires ongoing bone health management. The neuropathy and musculoskeletal complications require rehabilitation input.

The framework to keep in mind: TBD management is longitudinal. Each clinic visit is an opportunity to ask three questions. Is the marrow failing further? Are the lungs or liver fibrosing? Are new organ systems becoming involved? The answers determine whether you escalate from supportive care to transplantation or experimental therapies.

## Hematopoietic Stem Cell Transplantation: When and for Whom

Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) is the only potentially curative option for the haematological manifestations of TBDs, and it is indicated in patients with progressive bone marrow failure. The decision to transplant is not taken lightly — these patients carry inherent risks of organ fibrosis that complicate transplantation, and telomere shortening itself impairs engraftment and increases transplant-related morbidity.

In this case, the patient's cytopenia was most likely attributable to severe B12 deficiency rather than intrinsic marrow failure, though the presence of the PARN variant raises the possibility that her genetically compromised marrow was more susceptible to haematopoietic stress. Her counts stabilised after nutritional repletion, so HSCT was not indicated at the time of the report. However, the treating team explicitly flagged ongoing haematological monitoring to assess for any evolving marrow failure phenotype, recognising that a predisposed individual can shift from compensated cytopenia to frank marrow failure over time.

The clinical pearl: if you're following a patient with a known or suspected TBD and cytopenia, don't assume that correcting a reversible trigger closes the chapter. Telomere-compromised stem cells have reduced replicative reserve. What looks like a nutritional deficiency unmasking an underlying vulnerability today may declare itself as irreversible marrow failure tomorrow. Transplant referral pathways should be identified early, even if not immediately activated.

## Androgens: Promising but Unproven

The discussion around androgens — primarily danazol and nandrolone — in TBD management is one of the most active areas of investigation, and it comes up in nearly every counselling conversation because patients and families ask about it. Preclinical models and small clinical studies suggest that androgens can elongate telomeres, likely through upregulation of telomerase activity. Danazol has shown telomere lengthening in early-phase studies involving patients with TBDs and cytopenia, generating significant excitement in the community.

However, the long-term effects remain unknown. The studies are small, the follow-up is limited, and questions persist about durability of response, impact on non-haematological organ involvement (does lung or liver fibrosis slow?), and adverse effects of chronic androgen therapy in young patients — particularly virilisation in females, hepatic toxicity in an already fibrotic liver, and cardiovascular risk. In this case, the treating team did not initiate androgens, opting instead for supportive care and surveillance, which reflects the current reality that androgens remain experimental rather than standard of care.

The honest framing for patients: androgens may become a meaningful therapy for TBDs, and early data are encouraging, but we don't yet have the evidence to recommend them routinely. If they are used, it should ideally be in the context of a clinical trial or with expert consultation.

## The Counselling Conversation: What We Can and Cannot Offer

Perhaps the most clinically important aspect of this case is what happened after the diagnosis: the family received genetic counselling, and the patient herself understood the nature of her condition and accepted that curative options were not feasible at that time. She continued surveillance. She maintained stable counts. She had no new clinical deterioration at follow-up. That outcome — acceptance, stability, and a plan — is a clinical success even in the absence of a cure.

The genetic counselling conversation in TBDs is complex. PARN-related TBDs can follow autosomal recessive inheritance (biallelic variants causing DKCB6) or manifest with monoallelic variants (associated with pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure). This patient's monoallelic variant raises questions about penetrance, variable expressivity, and the possibility that relatives carrying the same variant could develop pulmonary fibrosis, marrow failure, or hepatic fibrosis — or may never manifest disease at all. First-degree relatives should be offered genetic testing and baseline telomere length assessment, and they should be counselled about surveillance even if asymptomatic.

The prognostic conversation with the patient requires honesty without fatalism. TBDs are chronic, progressive conditions. The trajectory is variable. Some patients maintain stable organ function for years; others progress rapidly to marrow failure or respiratory failure. The role of the clinician is to set realistic expectations — this is a lifelong condition requiring ongoing monitoring — while avoiding a purely nihilistic framing. Surveillance catches progression early. Transplantation can be curative for marrow disease. Lung and liver transplantation are options in select cases. And the therapeutic landscape is evolving.

## Key Take-Home Points

- **Consider TBDs in young adults with unexplained multisystem disease** — cytopenia, organ fibrosis (lung, liver), and premature ageing features — even when the classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita is absent.
- **Diagnosis requires three pillars**: genetic sequencing (NGS or clinical exome), flow-FISH telomere length measurement, and clinical phenotype correlation. A variant labelled "uncertain significance" does not exclude a TBD if the clinical picture fits.
- **PARN is an expanding phenotype**: monoallelic variants classically associate with pulmonary fibrosis and marrow failure, but hepatic fibrosis is increasingly recognised. Don't anchor on IPF alone.
- **Correcting reversible triggers matters but doesn't close the case**: this patient's B12 deficiency caused her cytopenia, but her PARN variant means ongoing haematological surveillance is mandatory — telomere-compromised marrow has limited reserve.
- **HSCT is the only potentially curative option for haematological manifestations** and should be considered early in progressive marrow failure. Identify transplant referral pathways before you need them.
- **Androgens (danazol, nandrolone) are experimental**: early data on telomere elongation are promising, but long-term safety and efficacy are unknown. Use in trial contexts or with expert consultation.
- **Genetic counselling is non-negotiable**: relatives may carry the same variant with variable penetrance. Offer testing, baseline telomere assessment, and surveillance to first-degree family members.
- **Frame prognosis honestly but not nihilistically**: TBDs are progressive and chronic, but the trajectory is variable. Surveillance, transplantation, and evolving therapies offer meaningful intervention points. Acceptance and a structured plan — as modelled by this case patient — are legitimate and important clinical outcomes.
