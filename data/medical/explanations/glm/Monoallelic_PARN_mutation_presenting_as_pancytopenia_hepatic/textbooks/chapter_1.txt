Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

Chapter 1: ## Case Presentation and Initial Diagnostic Workup

The patient was a woman in her late 20s who presented to a tertiary care centre after five years of progressively worsening generalised weakness and early fatigability. Her functional capacity had declined over the years to the point where she could no longer keep up with her peers in performing daily activities. In the three months preceding presentation, her weakness had worsened sufficiently to impair self-care and require assistance. She also reported exertional dyspnoea, intermittent chest discomfort, weight loss, and anorexia. The insidious onset and relentlessly progressive nature of her symptoms over five years, culminating in functional decline by the late 20s, immediately signalled a chronic, systemic process rather than an acute illness. For a clinician, a symptom timeline spanning years in a young adult should prompt consideration of slowly progressive constitutional diseases—including chronic nutritional deficiencies, occult malignancy, autoimmune disorders, and inherited syndromes—rather than acute infections or acute haematologic emergencies.

On examination, the patient was haemodynamically stable, but several physical findings pointed toward multisystem pathology. Pallor and icterus suggested anaemia with possible haemolysis or hepatic dysfunction. Premature greying of hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation, and a bald tongue with atrophy of lingual papillae were particularly noteworthy. Knuckle hyperpigmentation and a smooth, atrophic tongue are classic stigmata of vitamin B12 deficiency, resulting from impaired mucosal cell turnover and megaloblastic changes in rapidly dividing epithelial cells. Premature greying, however, is less easily explained by nutritional deficiency alone and raised the possibility of a premature ageing syndrome. Musculoskeletal examination revealed thoracic scoliosis and bilateral thenar atrophy, the latter suggesting median nerve compression or a more generalised neuropathic process. Abdominal examination identified hepatomegaly (5 cm below the right costal margin, with a liver span of 18 cm), and respiratory examination revealed reduced breath sounds in the bilateral infra-scapular regions, suggesting possible pleural effusion, atelectasis, or parenchymal consolidation. Taken together, the physical examination already implicated the haematologic, hepatic, pulmonary, neurologic, and musculoskeletal systems—a pattern that would prove critical in redirecting the diagnostic approach away from single-organ disease.

Initial laboratory evaluation confirmed pancytopenia—reductions in all three haematopoietic cell lines—with a notably low reticulocyte count of 0.39%. A low reticulocyte response in the setting of cytopenia indicates that the bone marrow is not appropriately compensating, which points toward ineffective haematopoiesis or marrow failure rather than peripheral destruction or sequestration. The peripheral smear demonstrated a dimorphic erythrocyte population (two distinct red cell populations differing in size), basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils. Hypersegmented neutrophils—defined as neutrophils with six or more nuclear lobes—are a hallmark of megaloblastic anaemia, in which impaired DNA synthesis due to vitamin B12 or folate deficiency leads to asynchronous maturation of the nucleus and cytoplasm. Basophilic stippling (aggregated ribosomes visible as punctate blue inclusions) and Cabot rings (ring-shaped nuclear remnants) further support dysregulated erythropoiesis. A dimorphic smear can reflect a combined nutritional deficiency, early response to supplementation, or a mixed marrow disorder. Serum studies confirmed severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency, while iron studies revealed elevated serum iron and ferritin—a pattern consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis, in which iron is absorbed and recycled but not efficiently incorporated into red blood cells because erythroid precursors fail to mature and instead undergo intramedullary apoptosis.

Bone marrow examination demonstrated trilineage haematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia and increased iron stores, but without evidence of malignancy, dysplasia, or infiltrative disease. Cytogenetic analysis was normal. Erythroid hyperplasia with increased iron stores in the context of peripheral pancytopenia is the marrow correlate of ineffective erythropoiesis: the marrow is hypercellular in the erythroid lineage, but the cells fail to mature into functional circulating blood cells. The absence of dysplastic changes, clonal cytogenetic abnormalities, or malignant infiltration effectively excluded myelodysplastic syndrome, acute leukaemia, and infiltrative marrow diseases such as lymphoma or metastatic carcinoma—diagnoses that would have been high on the differential for a young adult with pancytopenia. Markers of haemolysis were also evaluated and revealed elevated lactate dehydrogenase and indirect hyperbilirubinaemia, and a direct antiglobulin test (DAT) was positive for IgG (2+). A positive DAT suggests antibody-mediated coating of red blood cells and typically raises concern for autoimmune haemolysis. However, the low reticulocyte response, the marrow findings of ineffective erythropoiesis rather than compensatory hyperproduction, and the overall clinical picture argued against clinically significant peripheral immune-mediated destruction. This distinction is clinically important because a positive DAT can sometimes be an incidental finding, and treating haemolysis with immunosuppression in a patient whose primary problem is marrow dysfunction would be inappropriate and potentially harmful.

To investigate possible gastrointestinal malabsorption as a cause for the severe B12 deficiency, upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was performed and appeared macroscopically normal. Duodenal biopsy showed mild chronic gastritis with reduced parietal cells, which could contribute to impaired intrinsic factor production and consequent B12 malabsorption. However, the clinical team recognised that the severity and breadth of the patient's systemic findings were disproportionate to what nutritional deficiency alone could explain. This recognition—that a single unifying diagnosis had not yet been identified despite finding a plausible partial explanation—is a critical step in diagnostic reasoning and ultimately drove the workup toward a syndromic or genetic aetiology.

Hepatic dysfunction prompted further evaluation. Abdominal ultrasound revealed hepatosplenomegaly. Transient elastography, a noninvasive method of estimating liver stiffness by measuring shear wave velocity through hepatic tissue, showed grade II hepatic steatosis (controlled attenuation parameter of 295 dB/m) and cirrhosis (liver stiffness of 18.1 kPa). In transient elastography, a liver stiffness measurement exceeding approximately 12–14 kPa is generally consistent with cirrhosis, and this patient's value of 18.1 kPa firmly placed her in the cirrhotic range. Inflammatory markers were elevated, but autoimmune serologies—including antinuclear antibodies and autoimmune hepatitis antibodies—were negative. Infectious workup for viral hepatitis (B and C), HIV, cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus was largely unrevealing except for positive Epstein-Barr virus IgG, which reflects past exposure and does not establish active infection. The exclusion of autoimmune, infectious, and common metabolic causes of cirrhosis in a young woman left the hepatic fibrosis unexplained by conventional mechanisms, a finding that would later take on great significance.

To evaluate for occult malignancy or granulomatous disease that could unify the multisystem findings, a positron emission tomography–CT (PET-CT) was performed. It demonstrated inhomogeneous ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) uptake in the axial and appendicular bone marrow, a pattern that can reflect reactive marrow stimulation but does not by itself indicate malignancy. The pulmonary window revealed diffuse ground-glass haziness with multiple fibrotic and subpleural atelectatic changes in both lung fields. Ground-glass opacity on CT represents increased lung density that does not obscure underlying bronchial and vascular margins, and it can reflect inflammation, oedema, or early fibrosis. The fibrotic and subpleural changes are significant because idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) characteristically involves the subpleural regions and lower lobes. The PET-CT also confirmed hepatosplenomegaly and ascites but showed no evidence of overt malignancy or granulomatous disease, effectively excluding lymphoma, metastatic carcinoma, and sarcoidosis as explanations for the patient's multisystem presentation.

Additional systemic evaluation further expanded the clinical picture. Secondary hyperparathyroidism, hypocalcaemia, hypokalaemia, and vitamin D deficiency were identified. Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) confirmed osteoporosis, explaining her recurrent bone pain and fractures. In a young adult, osteoporosis without a clear secondary cause (such as chronic steroid use, hypogonadism, or malabsorption) is itself unusual and contributes to the picture of premature ageing. Electromyography and nerve conduction studies revealed an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy with chronic neurogenic changes in both proximal and distal muscle groups, providing a physiologic explanation for her bilateral thenar atrophy and functional weakness. An axonal polyneuropathy can result from nutritional deficiency, but the asymmetry and chronic neurogenic changes raised the possibility of an additional underlying process.

By the conclusion of this initial workup, the diagnostic landscape had shifted dramatically. The patient had documented pancytopenia with ineffective erythropoiesis, severe B12 and folate deficiency, cirrhosis without a conventional cause, pulmonary fibrosis, osteoporosis, premature greying, and a sensorimotor neuropathy. While the nutritional deficiencies explained part of the haematologic and neurologic picture, the persistence of hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, and premature ageing features in a young woman could not be attributed to B12 or folate deficiency. This realisation—that the patient's findings spanned multiple organ systems in a pattern suggesting accelerated cellular ageing and fibrosis—shifted the diagnostic reasoning toward a syndromic or inherited disorder and set the stage for clinical exome sequencing.
