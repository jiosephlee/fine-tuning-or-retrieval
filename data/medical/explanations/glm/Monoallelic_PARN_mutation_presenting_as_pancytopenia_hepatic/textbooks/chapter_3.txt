Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

Chapter 3: ## Telomere Biology Disorders and the PARN Gene: Pathophysiology and Genetics

Telomeres are repetitive DNA sequences that cap the ends of chromosomes, protecting them from degradation, end-to-end fusion, and inappropriate recombination. With each somatic cell division, the conventional DNA replication machinery cannot fully copy the lagging strand, causing telomeres to shorten progressively. When telomeres reach a critical length, cells enter replicative senescence or undergo apoptosis. In germline and stem cells, the ribonucleoprotein enzyme telomerase counteracts this attrition by adding telomeric repeats de novo. Telomerase comprises two core components: telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT), the catalytic subunit, and telomerase RNA component (TERC), the RNA template that TERT uses to synthesize telomeric DNA. Pathogenic variants in genes encoding components of the telomerase complex—or in genes involved in telomere maintenance more broadly—impair this restorative function, leading to premature telomere shortening and the cluster of inherited conditions known as telomere biology disorders (TBDs).

The pathophysiology of TBDs centers on accelerated telomere attrition. Because telomere length is a critical determinant of a cell's replicative lifespan, tissues with high turnover and active cell division are disproportionately affected. Bone marrow, skin, mucosa, hair follicles, and hepatocytes are among the most vulnerable cell populations. As telomeres in these compartments shorten prematurely, stem cell exhaustion and cellular senescence ensue, manifesting clinically as bone marrow failure, premature greying, mucocutaneous abnormalities, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis. The patient in this case exemplifies this principle: her clinical presentation featured pancytopenia, premature greying of hair, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis—all reflecting dysfunction of actively dividing tissues.

Dyskeratosis congenita (DC) is the prototypical TBD. It is classically defined by a mucocutaneous triad of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation, typically accompanied by bone marrow failure. The genetic architecture of DC is heterogeneous, with X-linked recessive variants in DKC1 having first established the link between DC and short telomeres. Beyond DC, the TBD spectrum includes severe childhood-onset subtypes such as Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome, each caused by variants in genes implicated in telomere maintenance. However, TBDs do not always present with the classical DC triad. Adult-onset presentations may feature isolated organ involvement—such as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis, or bone marrow failure—without mucocutaneous features, leading to frequent misdiagnosis. This patient's presentation is illustrative: she lacked oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular pigmentation, yet her constellation of cytopenia, organ fibrosis, and premature ageing features ultimately pointed toward a TBD. This case underscores that TBDs must be considered in young adults with unexplained multisystem disease even when classical mucocutaneous features are absent.

The PARN gene (poly(A)-specific ribonuclease) belongs to a highly conserved family of exoribonucleases. PARN functions primarily by shortening the poly(A) tails of messenger RNAs through deadenylation, thereby regulating gene expression at the post-transcriptional level. Critically for telomere biology, PARN is required for the removal of oligo(A) tails that are post-transcriptionally added to the 3-prime end of TERC RNA. These oligo(A) tails target nuclear RNAs for degradation, and their removal by PARN is necessary for proper 3-prime-end maturation of TERC. Without functional PARN, TERC processing is impaired, reducing the availability of mature telomerase RNA and compromising telomerase activity. In this way, PARN mutations indirectly lead to defective telomere elongation, linking a gene involved in general RNA metabolism to the telomere maintenance pathway.

PARN is among the approximately 20% of human genes least tolerant to rare, disruptive variants, reflecting its essential role in cellular function and supporting the concept of haploinsufficiency in carriers. This low tolerance means that even a single mutated allele may be insufficient to sustain normal function, predisposing heterozygous carriers to disease. The inheritance pattern of PARN-related TBD is dose-dependent. Biallelic PARN variants—where both alleles carry pathogenic changes—are associated with dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6) and have been reported in severe phenotypes such as Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome. Monoallelic PARN variants, by contrast, more commonly underlie telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4), a phenotype that tends to present in adulthood with milder but still significant organ involvement. The patient in this case harbored a monoallelic missense variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) in the R3H domain of PARN, classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Despite this classification, its potential pathogenicity is supported by several factors: the affected residue is highly conserved, the variant is absent from population databases, multiple in silico prediction tools predict a deleterious effect, and PARN has a low rate of benign missense variation. When ACMG/AMP criteria are applied, the variant meets several supportive thresholds (PM1, PM2, PP3, PP2), and parallels can be drawn with other PARN missense variants classified as likely pathogenic in ClinVar.

Although monoallelic PARN mutations are more commonly associated with IPF rather than primary haematological manifestations, the clinical spectrum continues to expand. The initial link between PARN and pulmonary fibrosis emerged from the identification of novel monoallelic variants in patients with sporadic IPF, and subsequent whole-exome sequencing studies confirmed rare PARN variants in a subset of IPF cases. Over 65 cases have now been documented in the literature. In this patient, the presence of both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis in association with a monoallelic PARN variant is particularly notable. While hepatic fibrosis has been well documented in carriers of TERC and TERT mutations—studies from telomere biology disease registries have found moderate hepatic fibrosis in the vast majority of adult patients with these variants—its association with monoallelic PARN mutations is still emerging. This patient's hepatic fibrosis may reflect downstream impairment of telomerase function via defective TERC maturation, producing a phenotype more typical of TERT or TERC mutations. Comparable cases in the literature have described patients with pancytopenia and hepatic fibrosis carrying monoallelic variants in both PARN and TERT, but this patient developed both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis with only a monoallelic PARN variant. These findings support an expanding clinical spectrum for PARN-associated TBDs and highlight hepatic fibrosis as an important component of the phenotype.

The variable expressivity of TBDs, even among individuals carrying the same variant, is influenced by multiple factors. Variable penetrance means that not all carriers of a pathogenic variant will manifest clinical disease, and the severity and organ involvement can differ widely. Somatic genetic rescue—a phenomenon in which a somatic mutation in a hematopoietic stem cell reverts a pathogenic germline variant—can partially restore function in the bone marrow compartment while leaving other organs unaffected. Environmental and lifestyle factors, such as infections, nutritional deficiencies, and toxin exposures, can further influence the replicative stress placed on different organ systems. In this patient, severe vitamin B12 deficiency may have acted as a hematopoietic stressor that unmasked an underlying bone marrow vulnerability conferred by the PARN variant. While her cytopenia was most likely attributable to the nutritional deficiency itself, the presence of a PARN variant raises the possibility that telomere shortening rendered her hematopoietic stem cells less capable of compensating for the added stress. This underscores the importance of ongoing hematological monitoring in genetically predisposed individuals, even when the initial cytopenia appears to have an acquired explanation. The interplay of these factors—genetic dose, penetrance, somatic rescue, and environmental stressors—provides a framework for understanding how a single monoallelic PARN variant can produce a multisystem phenotype encompassing bone marrow dysfunction, pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, and features of accelerated ageing.
