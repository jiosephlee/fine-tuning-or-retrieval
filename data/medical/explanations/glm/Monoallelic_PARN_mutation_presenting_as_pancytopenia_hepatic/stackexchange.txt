Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Pathophysiology] The PARN gene encodes a poly(A)-specific ribonuclease primarily known for its role in mRNA deadenylation and regulation of gene expression. How does loss of PARN function specifically lead to telomere shortening, and what is the mechanistic link between PARN's ribonuclease activity and maturation of the telomerase RNA component (TERC)?

## Mechanistic Link Between PARN Loss of Function and Telomere Shortening

PARN (poly[A]-specific ribonuclease) is best known for its role in cytoplasmic mRNA deadenylation, but its connection to telomere biology arises from a distinct and critical nuclear function: the 3′-end maturation of the telomerase RNA component (TERC). Understanding this link requires appreciating that telomerase is a ribonucleoprotein enzyme composed of a catalytic reverse transcriptase (TERT) and a noncoding RNA subunit (TERC), which serves as the template for telomeric repeat addition. Without adequate levels of properly processed, mature TERC, telomerase cannot maintain telomere length, and replicative tissues—bone marrow, pulmonary epithelium, and hepatic parenchyma among them—undergo accelerated telomere attrition with each cell division.

The mechanistic bridge between PARN and TERC lies in post-transcriptional RNA quality control. After transcription, TERC acquires short oligo(A) tails at its 3′ end through the action of noncanonical poly(A) polymerases. These oligo(A) tails function as degradation signals—they mark aberrant or incompletely processed nuclear RNAs for exonucleolytic destruction by the nuclear exosome. PARN counteracts this process by removing the oligo(A) tails from TERC, thereby protecting it from degradation and permitting proper 3′-end trimming and maturation. When PARN function is lost or impaired, TERC retains its oligo(A) tail, is recognized as a substrate for degradation, and fails to accumulate in its mature, functional form. The downstream consequence is a reduction in steady-state levels of bioavailable TERC, impaired assembly of the telomerase holoenzyme, insufficient telomere elongation, and progressive telomere shortening.

This mechanism is directly relevant to the case under discussion. The patient carried a monoallelic missense variant in PARN (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) located within the R3H domain, a single-stranded nucleic acid–binding region that is critical for substrate recognition and catalytic processing. The substitution of a highly conserved cysteine residue at this position is predicted to be deleterious by multiple in silico tools and affects a domain with no known benign variation. Because PARN is among the 20% of human genes least tolerant to rare disruptive variants, haploinsufficiency—even from a single mutant allele—is sufficient to compromise its enzymatic activity. The resulting partial deficit in TERC maturation would reduce telomerase capacity below the threshold needed to sustain telomere length in highly proliferative tissues, producing the clinical phenotype observed: pancytopenia from compromised hematopoietic stem cell reserve, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis.

It is worth emphasizing that this mechanism distinguishes PARN-associated telomere biology disorders from those caused by direct mutations in TERT or TERC themselves. In PARN-related disease, the telomerase protein and the TERC gene are intact; the defect lies one step upstream—in the post-transcriptional maturation and stabilization of the TERC RNA. This explains why PARN mutations can produce a phenotype that overlaps with TERT and TERC mutations (bone marrow failure, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis) despite acting through a ribonuclease whose primary annotated function is mRNA deadenylation. It also illustrates a broader principle in telomere biology disorders: the genetic defect need not reside within the telomerase complex itself; any gene required for the proper biogenesis, stability, or localization of its components can produce the same end result—critically short telomeres and premature failure of proliferative tissues.

In this case, the absence of the classical mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita and the initial attribution of cytopenias to severe vitamin B12 deficiency illustrate how PARN-mediated telomere dysfunction can be clinically cryptic. The key clinical lesson is that when unexplained multisystem fibrosis, cytopenias, and premature aging features coexist—particularly in a young adult—a telomere biology disorder should be considered regardless of whether the classical phenotype is present, and genetic testing should include genes involved in telomerase RNA processing, not just those encoding telomerase components directly.

### [Clinical Pearls] In PARN-associated telomere biology disorders, biallelic variants classically cause autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6) and severe phenotypes such as Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome, while monoallelic variants are more commonly associated with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4). How does this case expand the known clinical spectrum of monoallelic PARN mutations, and what does it suggest about the concept of haploinsufficiency in PARN?

# Clinical Teaching Answer

## Expanding the Phenotypic Spectrum of Monoallelic PARN Mutations

Classically, the genotype-phenotype correlation in *PARN*-associated telomere biology disorders (TBDs) follows a recognizable pattern: biallelic loss-of-function variants produce severe, early-onset phenotypes such as autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6) and Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome, while monoallelic variants are typically linked to a narrower adult-onset spectrum—principally idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4). Hepatic fibrosis, when described in TBDs, has been predominantly associated with mutations in *TERT* or *TERC*, not *PARN*.

This case meaningfully expands that spectrum. The patient—a woman in her late 20s—harbored a single monoallelic *PARN* missense variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) yet developed a striking multisystem phenotype encompassing pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis with cirrhosis, and pulmonary fibrosis, alongside premature greying, osteoporosis, and neuropathy. Critically, the prior literature documenting hepatic fibrosis in a *PARN* carrier involved a patient who also carried a monoallelic *TERT* variant, leaving open the possibility that the hepatic phenotype was driven by *TERT* rather than *PARN*. This patient had no additional telomere-related gene variant identified, establishing that monoallelic *PARN* dysfunction alone can extend beyond the lung and marrow to include hepatic fibrosis—a manifestation previously considered atypical for this genotype.

## Implications for Haploinsufficiency in PARN

*PARN* ranks among the approximately 20% of human genes least tolerant to rare, disruptive variants, and it functions as a poly(A)-specific ribonuclease essential for the 3′-end maturation of TERC, the RNA component of telomerase. Even a single nonfunctional allele can reduce effective telomerase activity below the threshold needed to maintain telomere length in highly proliferative tissues. This case reinforces the concept that *PARN* haploinsufficiency is not a binary or organ-restricted phenomenon. Rather, a single variant can produce a continuum of telomere-driven organ injury spanning the bone marrow, lung, and liver—the same triad more typically associated with *TERT* or *TERC* mutations.

The case also highlights important modifiers of penetrance. The patient's pancytopenia was initially attributed to severe vitamin B12 deficiency, but the persistence of multisystem dysfunction after nutritional correction suggests that telomere-mediated stem cell exhaustion may have rendered her hematopoietic compartment—and potentially her hepatic parenchyma—unusually vulnerable to superimposed stress. This is consistent with the broader TBD principle: environmental insults, replicative demand, and somatic genetic rescue can differentially influence which organ systems manifest and when.

In sum, this case argues that monoallelic *PARN* mutations should be considered within a broader differential than IPF and bone marrow failure alone. Clinicians evaluating young adults with unexplained hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, or cytopenia—even in the absence of the classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita—should consider TBDs and pursue genetic testing and telomere length measurement, recognizing that *PARN* haploinsufficiency may carry a wider phenotypic reach than previously appreciated.

### [Morning Report] This patient lacked the classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita (oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation), yet was ultimately diagnosed with a telomere biology disorder. In a young adult presenting with unexplained multisystem disease, what specific constellation of clinical findings should prompt consideration of a TBD even in the absence of classical mucocutaneous features, and why are adult-onset TBDs frequently misdiagnosed?

In a young adult presenting with unexplained multisystem disease, the specific constellation that should prompt consideration of a telomere biology disorder (TBD)—even in the absence of the classical mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita—is the combination of **bone marrow failure or cytopenia, organ-specific fibrosis (particularly pulmonary and/or hepatic), and features of accelerated aging** such as premature greying, osteoporosis, or neuropathy occurring at an unusually young age. In this patient, the coexistence of pancytopenia, idiopathic hepatic fibrosis with cirrhosis, pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying, osteoporosis with recurrent fractures, and chronic sensorimotor polyneuropathy in a woman in her late 20s constituted a phenotype incompatible with any single acquired condition. Notably, she lacked oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation—the triad that classically defines dyskeratosis congenita—yet the multiorgan fibrotic and hematologic phenotype was strongly consistent with a TBD, as ultimately supported by identification of a monoallelic *PARN* variant.

The reason adult-onset TBDs are frequently misdiagnosed is multifactorial. First, the classical mucocutaneous triad is often absent in adults, especially in patients carrying monoallelic variants in genes such as *PARN*, *TERT*, *TERC*, or *RTEL1*, which tend to present with isolated organ disease—most commonly idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis or bone marrow failure—rather than the full dyskeratosis congenita phenotype. Second, the individual organ manifestations are clinically indistinguishable from their far more common acquired counterparts: cytopenias are attributed to nutritional deficiency or aplastic anemia, hepatic fibrosis to chronic viral hepatitis or autoimmune disease, and pulmonary fibrosis to environmental or occupational exposures. In this case, severe vitamin B12 deficiency initially provided a plausible explanation for the cytopenias and neurologic findings, and it was only after correction of nutritional deficiencies that the persistence and disproportionate severity of the multisystem involvement became apparent. Third, TBDs exhibit marked genetic heterogeneity, variable penetrance, and variable expressivity influenced by environmental and lifestyle factors, meaning that even within a single family, carriers may manifest different organ systems at different ages. The absence of a family history—as in this patient—does not exclude a TBD, as de novo mutations or reduced penetrance can obscure inheritance patterns.

The key clinical takeaway is this: **unexplained fibrosis affecting two or more organs, or fibrosis combined with cytopenia, in a young adult should trigger evaluation for a TBD regardless of mucocutaneous findings.** The diagnostic standard includes next-generation sequencing of telomere-related genes and flow cytometry–based FISH measurement of telomere length. Early recognition is essential not only because it unifies seemingly disparate findings under a single pathophysiology—accelerated telomere attrition leading to premature senescence of rapidly dividing tissues—but also because it has direct management implications, including genetic counseling, surveillance for progressive marrow failure and fibrotic complications, and consideration of hematopoietic stem cell transplantation in cases with significant hematologic disease.

### [Differential Diagnosis] This patient had a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT, IgG 2+) with elevated LDH and indirect hyperbilirubinemia—findings that might initially suggest autoimmune hemolytic anemia. However, the low reticulocyte count (0.39%) and bone marrow findings of erythroid hyperplasia with increased iron stores pointed instead toward ineffective erythropoiesis. How can you distinguish ineffective erythropoiesis from peripheral hemolytic anemia when both can present with elevated LDH and indirect hyperbilirubinemia, and in what clinical scenarios might a positive DAT be misleading?

## Distinguishing Ineffective Erythropoiesis from Peripheral Hemolysis

Elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and indirect hyperbilirubinemia indicate increased red cell turnover, but they do not specify *where* that turnover occurs. The critical discriminator is the **reticulocyte count**.

In **peripheral hemolytic anemia**, the bone marrow is intact and responds appropriately to accelerated destruction with robust reticulocytosis—typically >2–3% or an absolute reticulocyte count >100 × 10⁹/L. The marrow releases young red cells into the circulation, and LDH elevation is predominantly LDH-2 (from mature erythrocytes). Haptoglobin is typically depleted.

In **ineffective erythropoiesis**, erythroid precursors proliferate in the marrow but undergo **intramedullary apoptosis** before completing maturation. The marrow is hypercellular with erythroid hyperplasia, but few reticulocytes escape into the periphery. This was exactly the pattern seen in this patient: a reticulocyte count of only 0.39% despite marrow erythroid hyperplasia. The elevated LDH reflects destruction of nucleated erythroid precursors (predominantly LDH-1 and LDH-3), and indirect hyperbilirubinemia arises from catabolism of heme released by dying marrow precursors—both mimicking peripheral hemolysis. The **increased iron stores** on marrow examination provided an additional clue: in ineffective erythropoiesis, iron is delivered to the marrow but cannot be incorporated into hemoglobin effectively because erythroid maturation is arrested, leading to iron accumulation. In true peripheral hemolysis with adequate marrow response, iron stores are typically normal or reduced as iron is consumed for accelerated erythropoiesis.

The peripheral smear further supported the diagnosis. Basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils are hallmarks of megaloblastic/ineffective erythropoiesis—not features of immune-mediated destruction, where spherocytes, schistocytes, or polychromasia would predominate.

## When a Positive DAT Can Be Misleading

A positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) demonstrates IgG or complement bound to the red cell surface, but it does not by itself establish a diagnosis of autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA). Several scenarios produce a positive DAT without clinically significant immune hemolysis:

- **Passive adsorption** of donor antibodies following transfusion or IVIG administration
- **Autoantibodies** in patients with lymphoproliferative disorders, infections, or autoimmune disease that bind red cells without triggering meaningful destruction
- **Drug-dependent antibodies** or nonspecific protein coating in inflammatory states

The key is to interpret the DAT in the full clinical context. In AIHA, you expect a **corrected reticulocyte response**, evidence of active hemolysis (falling hemoglobin, elevated LDH, low haptoglobin, spherocytes on smear), and usually a positive **indirect antiglobulin test** (IAT), which detects free autoantibody in the serum. In this patient, the IAT was negative, the reticulocyte count was suppressed, and the marrow demonstrated ineffective erythropoiesis with increased iron stores—none of which fit immune-mediated peripheral destruction. The DAT positivity was a red herring, potentially reflecting nonspecific IgG binding in the setting of systemic inflammation or an underlying immune dysregulation associated with her telomere biology disorder, but it was not driving her anemia.

## Clinical Takeaway

When evaluating a patient with elevated LDH and indirect hyperbilirubinemia, the single most useful initial test is the **reticulocyte count**. A low or inappropriately normal reticulocyte count in the setting of anemia should redirect the diagnostic reasoning away from peripheral destruction and toward ineffective erythropoiesis or marrow failure—even when the DAT is positive. Bone marrow examination with iron staining and careful peripheral smear review will confirm the mechanism, as it did in this case, where megaloblastic features and increased iron stores established intramedullary destruction rather than immune-mediated peripheral hemolysis.

### [Clinical Pearls] Hepatic fibrosis and cirrhosis are increasingly recognized as manifestations of telomere biology disorders, yet they remain under-diagnosed. What evidence from registries and prior case reports supports hepatic fibrosis as part of the TBD phenotype, and what proportion of patients with pathogenic TERT or TERC mutations show significant hepatic fibrosis? Why might hepatic involvement be overlooked in patients with TBDs?

## Clinical Teaching Answer

Hepatic fibrosis and cirrhosis are now recognized as integral manifestations of telomere biology disorders (TBDs), though they remain underdiagnosed in clinical practice.

**Evidence from registries and case reports.** The strongest systematic evidence comes from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry, which used transient elastography to screen adult patients with pathogenic *TERT* or *TERC* mutations. In that cohort, **88.8% of patients had at least moderate hepatic fibrosis, and 33.3% had established cirrhosis**. This is a strikingly high prevalence that firmly establishes hepatic fibrosis as part of the TBD phenotype rather than an incidental finding. Additional case reports reinforce this association: one described a patient with pancytopenia and hepatic fibrosis who carried monoallelic variants in both *PARN* (c.1613C>G) and *TERT* (c.2476G>A). The present case extends this spectrum further by demonstrating hepatic fibrosis—confirmed by transient elastography showing a liver stiffness of 18.1 kPa, consistent with cirrhosis—in a patient with a monoallelic *PARN* variant alone, even in the absence of a concurrent *TERT* or *TERC* mutation. Collectively, these observations support the concept that impaired telomere maintenance, whether through telomerase core complex genes (*TERT*, *TERC*) or telomerase regulatory genes such as *PARN*, can produce clinically significant hepatic fibrosis.

**Why hepatic involvement is overlooked.** Several factors contribute. First, patients with TBDs frequently lack the classical mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita—oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation—especially when they present in adulthood. As in the present case, the patient had no mucocutaneous features, and suspicion for a genetic disorder arose only after multiple organ systems were found to be involved simultaneously. Second, hepatic fibrosis in TBDs is clinically silent until advanced; it may be discovered incidentally or attributed to more common etiologies. In this case, the initial hepatic evaluation focused on autoimmune hepatitis and viral hepatitis, both of which were excluded, before a syndromic cause was considered. Third, because TBDs are rare and exhibit variable penetrance, incomplete expression, and organ-specific predominance (e.g., pulmonary fibrosis or bone marrow failure presenting first), the hepatic component may be missed unless specifically screened for—particularly with noninvasive tools such as transient elastography. The high prevalence of hepatic fibrosis found in the Aachen registry only when actively screened underscores this point: without systematic assessment, hepatic involvement goes unrecognized.

In summary, hepatic fibrosis is a common but underappreciated feature of TBDs. Registry data show that nearly nine in ten adults with pathogenic *TERT* or *TERC* mutations have at least moderate hepatic fibrosis, yet this manifestation is frequently overlooked because TBDs lack pathognomonic features in many patients, hepatic fibrosis is subclinical until late, and alternative diagnoses are pursued first. Active screening with transient elastography in patients with known or suspected TBDs is therefore warranted.

### [Morning Report] The gold standard for diagnosing a suspected telomere biology disorder combines genetic testing via next-generation sequencing with flow cytometry-based FISH (Flow-FISH) to measure telomere length. In this case, a monoallelic PARN missense variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) was classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS). Using ACMG/AMP criteria, what specific evidence supported this variant's potential pathogenicity, and how should a VUS in a telomere-related gene be interpreted when the clinical phenotype is strongly compatible with a TBD?

## Answer

Under the ACMG/AMP framework, the PARN c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg) variant was classified as a VUS because it met several *supportive* but no *strong* or *very strong* evidence criteria. The specific evidence cited in this case was:

- **PM1 (moderate):** The substitution alters a highly conserved cysteine residue within the R3H domain (residues 178–245), a critical and well-established functional domain of PARN with no known benign variation.
- **PM2 (moderate):** The variant is absent or extremely rare in population databases, arguing against a common polymorphism.
- **PP3 (supporting):** Four of five in silico prediction tools (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT) predict a damaging effect on protein function.
- **PP2 (supporting):** PARN has a low rate of benign missense variation—missense variants are a recognized disease mechanism, and PARN ranks among the 20% of genes least tolerant to rare disruptive variants, supporting haploinsufficiency.

Additional circumstantial evidence included parallels with other PARN missense variants (p.Lys59Arg and p.Ala383Val) classified as likely pathogenic in ClinVar. However, absent functional assay data, segregation data, or de novo confirmation, these criteria collectively remain insufficient to upgrade the variant beyond VUS.

The critical teaching point is how to interpret a VUS in a telomere-related gene when the phenotype is strongly concordant. **Variant classification under ACMG/AMP guidelines is intended to be genotype-driven and independent of the patient's phenotype to avoid circular reasoning.** Therefore, a VUS must not be reclassified as pathogenic solely because the clinical picture fits. However, clinical decision-making need not be paralyzed by the VUS label. When a patient presents with a multisystem phenotype highly suggestive of a TBD—bone marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, premature greying, and osteoporosis—and genetic testing identifies a rare, computationally damaging variant in a gene with a well-established role in telomere biology, the clinical–genetic correlation can reasonably support a *presumptive* TBD diagnosis for the purposes of counseling, surveillance, and family evaluation. This is analogous to the broader principle in medical genetics: a VUS guides uncertainty in the variant report, but the clinician integrates the entire clinical context for patient management.

In this case, the authors appropriately used the phenotypic concordance to justify treating the patient as having a TBD—arranging genetic counseling, planning longitudinal monitoring of marrow and organ function, and considering future transplant evaluation—while acknowledging that the variant itself requires further evidence (such as functional telomere length assays by Flow-FISH, family segregation studies, or RNA studies demonstrating impaired PARN deadenylation activity) before definitive pathogenicity can be assigned. This approach—strict genotype classification coupled with pragmatic phenotype-integrated clinical reasoning—is the correct framework for managing VUS findings in suspected Mendelian disorders.

### [Management] A patient with a confirmed telomere biology disorder develops progressive bone marrow failure. What are the current therapeutic options, and what unique considerations must be addressed when planning hematopoietic stem cell transplantation in patients with TBDs compared to patients with acquired marrow failure syndromes? Additionally, what is the evidence and current uncertainty surrounding the use of androgens such as danazol for telomere elongation in these patients?

## Clinical Teaching Answer

### Therapeutic Options for Progressive Bone Marney Failure in TBD

As illustrated by our patient with a monoallelic *PARN* variant, the management of bone marrow failure in telomere biology disorders (TBDs) proceeds along several tiers, and decisions must be individualized based on the pace of marrow failure, the extent of extrahematologic organ involvement, and genetic factors.

**Supportive care** remains the first pillar. In our patient, the initial cytopenias were confounded by severe vitamin B12 deficiency, which was corrected with parenteral cyanocobalamin, alongside transfusions for symptomatic anemia, folate supplementation, and bone health management. However, as the authors emphasize, even after nutritional correction, the underlying TBD predisposes to ongoing marrow stress and progressive failure, mandating longitudinal hematologic surveillance.

**Androgens**—including danazol, oxymetholone, and nandrolone—represent a medical strategy aimed at slowing telomere attrition. The rationale is that androgens may upregulate telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) expression, thereby enhancing telomerase activity and potentially lengthening telomeres or decelerating their shortening. A small phase I/II study by Townsley and colleagues (NEJM, 2016) demonstrated that oral danazol (800 mg daily) stabilized or lengthened telomere length in patients with TBDs and improved hematologic parameters in a subset. However, as the case report explicitly notes, "the long-term effects remain unknown." Critical uncertainties persist regarding durability of response, optimal dosing, whether telomere elongation translates into meaningful clinical stabilization across all affected organs, and how to balance benefit against adverse effects—including hepatotoxicity, which is of particular concern in TBD patients who, like ours, may already harbor hepatic fibrosis. Indeed, the significant prevalence of hepatic fibrosis in TBD patients—up to 88.8% in one registry of adult patients with *TERC* or *TERT* mutations—demands caution. Androgens are therefore best considered a temporizing or adjunctive measure, not a definitive therapy.

**Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT)** is the only potentially curative option for the hematologic manifestations of TBD. However, as the authors note, HSCT is "potentially curative in patients with haematologic manifestations"—a deliberately narrow statement reflecting that transplantation addresses marrow failure but does not halt or reverse pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, or other organ-level disease that drove the patient's overall clinical picture.

### Unique Considerations for HSCT in TBDs vs. Acquired Marrow Failure

Several features distinguish HSCT planning in TBD patients from those with acquired aplastic anemia or other non-genetic marrow failure syndromes:

1. **Conditioning regimen toxicity.** Patients with TBDs have intrinsically short telomeres, impairing the DNA damage response and tissue regenerative capacity across multiple organs. Standard myeloablative conditioning regimens—particularly those incorporating high-dose radiation or busulfan—cause unacceptable rates of transplant-related mortality and late organ complications in TBD patients. Reduced-intensity conditioning (RIC) regimens, typically fludarabine-based with lower-dose alkylating agents and avoiding total body irradiation, are now the standard approach. This was learned through hard experience: early HSCT outcomes in dyskeratosis congenita using standard regimens yielded mortality exceeding 50%, driven by pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic failure, and other organ toxicities rather than graft failure or rejection.

2. **Donor selection and genetic screening.** Because many TBD variants are autosomal dominant (including monoallelic *PARN*, *TERT*, *TERC*, and *RTEL1* variants, as in our patient's case), first-degree relatives who might otherwise serve as related donors may themselves carry the pathogenic variant. Any potential related donor must undergo genetic testing for the known familial variant and telomere length measurement. An apparently healthy sibling may carry the same mutation with incomplete penetrance and could have subclinical telomere shortening, which would result in poor stem cell reserve and potential donor-derived complications. Unrelated or unrelated umbilical cord donors may be preferable when a genetically confirmed unaffected related donor is unavailable.

3. **Multidisciplinary pre-transplant organ assessment.** Unlike acquired marrow failure, TBD patients require rigorous pre-transplant evaluation of pulmonary function (pulmonary function tests and high-resolution CT), hepatic function (transient elastography and imaging), and renal function. Our patient's established pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis with cirrhosis (liver stiffness 18.1 kPa), osteoporosis, and neuropathy would each complicate transplant eligibility and post-transplant management. Pulmonary fibrosis, in particular, may be exacerbated by conditioning and is a leading cause of post-transplant mortality in TBD patients.

4. **Post-transplant non-hematologic surveillance.** HSCT corrects marrow failure but does not correct the underlying telomere defect in non-hematopoietic tissues. Patients remain at ongoing risk for pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, gastrointestinal manifestations, and both hematologic and solid malignancies. Lifelong surveillance of these organ systems is required—a point directly relevant to our patient, whose pulmonary and hepatic involvement would persist regardless of any future HSCT.

5. **Timing of transplant.** The decision of when to proceed to HSCT is nuanced. In acquired aplastic anemia, transplant is often pursued early, particularly in younger patients with matched donors. In TBD, the competing risks of progressive non-hematologic organ disease must be weighed against the risk of delaying transplant until marrow failure is more advanced and the patient less fit.

### Relevance to the Presented Case

In our patient, the coexistence of established cirrhosis, pulmonary fibrosis, and neuropathy in addition to cytopenias would significantly complicate any HSCT decision. The authors report that the patient "understood the nature of her condition and accepted that curative options were not feasible," reflecting the clinical reality that, in TBD patients with advanced multisystem fibrosis, the risks of HSCT may outweigh the benefits, and management appropriately shifts toward supportive care, surveillance, and genetic counseling for the family. This case underscores that the threshold for transplant in TBDs is not determined solely by marrow failure severity but by a comprehensive assessment of organ-level disease burden that is unique to this population.

### [Differential Diagnosis] After correction of severe vitamin B12 deficiency with parenteral cyanocobalamin, this patient's cytopenia and multisystem dysfunction persisted disproportionately to the nutritional deficiency alone. What specific features of the presentation—including hepatic fibrosis without identifiable cause, pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying, osteoporosis, and neuropathy—argued against nutritional deficiency, autoimmune disease, or infiltrative malignancy as the sole unifying diagnosis, and how did the clinical reasoning evolve from a acquired to a genetic etiology?

After initial correction of severe vitamin B12 deficiency with parenteral cyanocobalamin, this patient's ongoing pancytopenia and multisystem dysfunction demanded a broader diagnostic framework. Several features argued decisively against a purely nutritional, autoimmune, or malignant explanation and ultimately redirected the evaluation toward a telomere biology disorder (TBD).

**Why nutritional deficiency alone was insufficient.** While severe B12 deficiency accounted for the megaloblastic marrow findings and hypersegmented neutrophils, it did not explain the concurrent hepatic fibrosis (demonstrated on transient elastography with a liver stiffness of 18.1 kPa), the pulmonary fibrotic changes seen on PET-CT, the premature greying, the osteoporosis with recurrent fractures, or the chronic sensorimotor axonal neuropathy. These are not expected sequelae of B12 deficiency, and their persistence after adequate repletion signaled a separate, unifying process.

**Why autoimmune disease was excluded.** A positive direct antiglobulin test and elevated inflammatory markers initially raised concern for immune-mediated cytopenia or connective tissue disease. However, the reticulocyte response remained inappropriately low, there was no evidence of true hemolysis, autoimmune hepatitis and antinuclear antibodies were negative, and no systemic features of a rheumatologic disorder were present.

**Why infiltrative malignancy was excluded.** Bone marrow examination showed trilineage hematopoiesis without dysplasia or infiltration, cytogenetics were normal, and PET-CT demonstrated no overt malignancy or granulomatous disease.

**The pivot to a genetic etiology.** The critical observation was the convergence of fibrotic organ disease—hepatic and pulmonary—in a young adult with cytopenia and stigmata of accelerated aging. Telomere biology disorders arise from defective telomere maintenance, causing premature senescence in highly proliferative tissues, including bone marrow, hepatocytes, and pulmonary epithelium. PARN, an exoribonuclease essential for TERC 3′-end maturation, is among the genes least tolerant to loss-of-function variation. Monoallelic PARN variants are an established cause of telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure. In this patient, the monoallelic p.Cys205Arg variant within the conserved R3H domain, combined with the clinical phenotype, provided a coherent explanation for marrow dysfunction, hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying, and osteoporosis.

This case illustrates that TBDs may present in adulthood without the classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita. When unexplained multiorgan fibrosis coexists with cytopenia and premature aging features, a telomere biology disorder should be actively considered, even when an acquired cause—such as B12 deficiency—is initially identified.
