Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# Beyond the Triad: Recognizing Telomere Biology Disorders When Dyskeratosis Congenita's Classic Features Are Absent

## The Case That Stops You in Your Tracks

Picture this: a woman in her late 20s walks into your tertiary care centre with five years of progressive fatigue that has now deteriorated to the point where she needs assistance with self-care. She has pancytopenia, cirrhosis, pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying, osteoporosis with recurrent fractures, and a chronic axonal polyneuropathy. You correct her severe B12 deficiency, but the multisystem wreckage persists. No mucocutaneous triad. No family history. No obvious unifying diagnosis — until clinical exome sequencing lands on a monoallelic PARN missense variant and everything snaps into focus.

This is a telomere biology disorder (TBD) hiding in plain sight, and it illustrates a critical diagnostic trap: we are conditioned to think of dyskeratosis congenita (DC) as a paediatric disease defined by oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation. But TBDs can present in adulthood, with isolated organ involvement, and without a single feature of the classical triad. If you are not actively considering TBDs in young adults with unexplained bone marrow failure, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), or hepatic fibrosis, you are going to miss them.

## The Diagnostic Journey: Why Nutritional Deficiency Was a Red Herring

This patient's initial workup was dominated by severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency, which explained a good chunk of her haematological picture — dimorphic anaemia, hypersegmented neutrophils, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and a bone marrow showing erythroid hyperplasia with increased iron stores consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis. She received appropriate replacement therapy. But several features refused to resolve into a nutritional diagnosis: cirrhosis on transient elastography (liver stiffness 18.1 kPa) with no identifiable viral, autoimmune, or metabolic cause; diffuse ground-glass haziness and fibrotic changes in both lung fields on PET-CT; osteoporosis with secondary hyperparathyroidism in a twenty-something; and a positive DAT that did not fit with the low reticulocyte response or marrow findings.

The team systematically excluded haematologic malignancy, infiltrative disease, autoimmune cytopenia, connective tissue disease, and infectious causes of hepatic fibrosis. When the dust settled, the remaining constellation — cytopenia, premature greying, hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, osteoporosis, and neuropathy in a young adult — pointed squarely toward a genetic or syndromic aetiology. Clinical exome sequencing identified a monoallelic PARN variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) in the R3H domain, classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance but predicted damaging by four of five in silico tools and absent from population databases. The clinical-genetic correlation was consistent with a TBD.

## PARN: A Gene at the Crossroads of Pulmonary and Hepatic Fibrosis

PARN encodes a poly(A)-specific ribonuclease that regulates mRNA deadenylation and is essential for 3'-end maturation of TERC, the telomerase RNA component. It is among the 20% of genes least tolerant to rare disruptive variants, supporting haploinsufficiency as a disease mechanism even in monoallelic carriers. Biallelic PARN variants cause autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6) and have been reported in Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome, while monoallelic variants are more typically associated with telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

What makes this case particularly instructive is that this patient carried only a single PARN allele yet developed both pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis — a combination more classically linked to TERT or TERC mutations. This expands the recognised phenotypic spectrum of monoallelic PARN variants and aligns with emerging data from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry, where 88.8% of adults with TERC or TERT mutations had at least moderate hepatic fibrosis and 33.3% had cirrhosis. The take-home: hepatic fibrosis in TBDs is likely under-recognised, and PARN mutations may produce a broader fibrotic phenotype than previously appreciated.

## The Broader TBD Spectrum and Variable Penetrance

TBDs encompass a wide clinical range. DC is the prototype, classically defined by the mucocutaneous triad or by two of those features plus bone marrow failure. Severe childhood subtypes include Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome (cerebellar hypoplasia, immunodeficiency, aplastic anaemia), Revesz syndrome (exudative retinopathy, intracranial calcifications), and Coats plus syndrome (retinal telangiectasia, intracranial calcifications, bone marrow failure). But adult presentations without the triad are increasingly recognised and are frequently misdiagnosed, especially in non-specialised centres. These patients may be labelled as having aplastic anaemia, myelodysplastic syndrome, cryptogenic cirrhosis, or sporadic IPF — all of which may actually be TBDs in disguise.

Variable penetrance, multiple causative genes (DKC1, TERT, TERC, RTEL1, PARN, and others), inheritance patterns, environmental factors, and somatic genetic rescue all influence which organ systems manifest and when. This patient's severe B12 deficiency may have acted as a haematopoietic stressor that unmasked an underlying TBD-predisposed marrow, a reminder that telomere shortening compromises replicative capacity of haematopoietic stem cells and may render patients more vulnerable to stressors that a normal marrow could weather.

## When to Suspect a TBD: A Practical Framework

This case offers a pragmatic clinical approach. Consider a TBD in any young adult presenting with unexplained or disproportionate combinations of the following:

Persistent cytopenia or marrow failure without an alternative explanation, especially when disproportionate to nutritional deficiency or when it fails to correct after appropriate treatment. Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis in a young patient or anyone without typical environmental or occupational risk factors. Hepatic fibrosis or cirrhosis of unknown aetiology with negative autoimmune, infectious, and metabolic workups. Features of accelerated ageing — premature greying, osteoporosis with recurrent fractures in a young adult, or neuropathy. A family history of early-onset haematologic, pulmonary, or hepatic disease, though de novo mutations occur and absence of family history does not exclude the diagnosis.

The gold standard for diagnosis involves next-generation sequencing of telomere-related genes and flow cytometry-based FISH to measure telomere length. Treatment is largely supportive and organ-specific, with haematopoietic stem cell transplantation as a potentially curative option for progressive marrow failure. Androgens like danazol and nandrolone have shown telomere elongation in preclinical and small clinical studies, but long-term efficacy and safety remain uncertain.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Do not anchor on the mucocutaneous triad.** TBDs can present in adulthood with isolated organ involvement — bone marrow failure, IPF, or hepatic fibrosis — and no classic features of dyskeratosis congenita.
- **Premature greying, osteoporosis, and cytopenia in a 20-something should trigger genetic evaluation.** This combination is not normal ageing and demands investigation beyond nutritional and autoimmune workups.
- **Hepatic fibrosis in TBDs is under-recognised.** Data from the Aachen registry suggest it is far more prevalent than previously appreciated, even with PARN variants traditionally linked to pulmonary fibrosis.
- **Nutritional deficiency can be a red herring.** Correct it, but if multisystem dysfunction persists or seems disproportionate, think broader.
- **Monoallelic PARN mutations can produce a wider phenotype than IPF alone.** This case demonstrates hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis from a single PARN allele, expanding the recognised clinical spectrum.
- **Variable penetrance means absence of family history does not rule out a TBD.** De novo mutations, incomplete penetrance, and somatic genetic rescue all influence presentation.
- **Early recognition matters.** It opens the door to genetic counselling, surveillance for progressive organ involvement, and timely consideration of haematopoietic stem cell transplantation in progressive marrow failure.
- **Telomere length testing and clinical exome sequencing are the diagnostic tools.** If you suspect a TBD, do not rely on clinical features alone — confirm with genetic and telomere length analysis.



# PARN Mutations: Monoallelic vs. Biallelic and the Expanding Spectrum from Pulmonary Fibrosis to Hepatic Cirrhosis

## Why This Case Stops You in Your Tracks

A woman in her late 20s walks in with five years of progressive fatigue, and you find pancytopenia, cirrhosis, pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying, osteoporosis, and a polyneuropathy. You correct her severe B12 deficiency, and she still has a multiorgan disease that doesn't fit neatly into any single acquired diagnosis. No mucocutaneous triad. No family history. Just a constellation of premature ageing and fibrosis that screams "something genetic" but lacks the textbook features you'd expect. Then clinical exome sequencing returns a monoallelic PARN variant — a gene classically associated with pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure in its single-allele form, not hepatic cirrhosis. This case forces us to reconsider what we think we know about genotype-phenotype correlations in telomere biology disorders.

## Telomere Biology Disorders: A Quick Refresher

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) arise from Mendelian defects in telomere maintenance genes. Every time a cell divides, telomeres shorten. When they reach a critical length, the cell undergoes senescence or apoptosis. Tissues with high turnover — bone marrow, skin, mucosa, hair follicles, hepatocytes — bear the brunt. The prototypical TBD is dyskeratosis congenita (DC), classically defined by the mucocutaneous triad of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation, often accompanied by bone marrow failure. Severe childhood subtypes include Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Here's the critical point for the bedside clinician: TBDs don't always show up with the classical triad. They can present in adulthood with isolated pulmonary fibrosis, cryptogenic cirrhosis, or apparently idiopathic bone marrow failure. Without the skin and nail findings, and without a family history, these patients get misdiagnosed — sometimes for years.

## PARN Genetics: Monoallelic vs. Biallelic — It Matters

PARN encodes a poly(A)-specific ribonuclease that regulates mRNA deadenylation and, critically, is required for proper 3'-end maturation of TERC (the telomerase RNA component). So when PARN is dysfunctional, telomerase function suffers, and telomeres shorten prematurely.

The genotype-phenotype split has been reasonably tidy up to now. Biallelic PARN variants cause autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), with severe childhood presentations including Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome. Monoallelic PARN variants, by contrast, have been associated with telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4) — a phenotype that tends to present in adulthood, often as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) discovered on imaging or at lung biopsy. The initial link between PARN and IPF came from whole-exome sequencing studies identifying monoallelic PARN rare variants in sporadic IPF cases, and over 65 cases have now been documented in the literature.

This patient's variant — c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg) — is monoallelic, classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. It affects a highly conserved residue within the R3H domain, a critical functional region with no known benign variation. Four of five in silico prediction tools call it deleterious. While formal pathogenicity remains uncertain, the clinical-genetic correlation in this patient is compelling. PARN is among the 20% of genes least tolerant to rare disruptive variants, supporting the concept of haploinsufficiency even in monoallelic carriers. The parallel with other PARN missense variants classified as likely pathogenic in ClinVar — such as p.Lys59Arg and p.Ala383Val — further strengthens the case.

## The Case: What Happened on the Clinical Journey

This woman presented with five years of progressive weakness that had escalated to requiring assistance with self-care. She had exertional dyspnoea, weight loss, and anorexia. On exam, she looked older than her years: premature greying, pallor, icterus, knuckle hyperpigmentation, and a bald atrophic tongue. She had thoracic scoliosis, bilateral thenar atrophy, hepatomegaly, and reduced breath sounds in the infra-scapular regions.

Her labs revealed pancytopenia with a reticulocyte count of 0.39%, dimorphic anaemia, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils — classic megaloblastic morphology. Severe B12 and folate deficiency were confirmed. Iron studies showed elevated serum iron and ferritin, consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis rather than iron deficiency. Bone marrow showed trilineage haematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia and increased iron stores, no malignancy or dysplasia. The DAT was positive for IgG, but the low reticulocyte response and marrow picture argued against immune-mediated destruction.

But here's where it gets interesting. Her liver stiffness on transient elastography was 18.1 kPa — cirrhotic. Autoimmune serologies were negative. Infectious workup was unrevealing. PET-CT showed diffuse ground-glass haziness with fibrotic and subpleural changes in both lungs, hepatosplenomegaly, and ascites — but no malignancy or granulomatous disease. She also had osteoporosis on DEXA, secondary hyperparathyroidism, and an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy on EMG. Even after B12 repletion, the systemic dysfunction was disproportionate and persistent. That's what triggered the genetic workup.

## The Surprising Part: Hepatic Fibrosis with Monoallelic PARN

This is where the case challenges conventional genotype-phenotype thinking. Hepatic fibrosis in TBDs has been under-recognised, but the existing literature links it primarily to TERT and TERC mutations. A retrospective analysis from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry found that 88.8% of adult patients with pathogenic TERT or TERC mutations had at least moderate hepatic fibrosis on transient elastography, and 33.3% had cirrhosis. One previously reported case described a patient with pancytopenia and hepatic fibrosis who carried monoallelic variants in both PARN and TERT — raising the question of whether the TERT variant was driving the liver phenotype.

This patient, however, had only a monoallelic PARN variant — no TERT or TERC variant identified. Yet she developed both pulmonary fibrosis and cirrhosis. The PARN variant's downstream impact on telomerase function via impaired TERC maturation provides a plausible biological mechanism for hepatic fibrosis, expanding the recognised clinical spectrum of PARN-associated TBDs beyond what we've classically expected.

## Why the Phenotype Varies: Haploinsufficiency, Penetrance, and the Environment

If this variant is pathogenic, why doesn't every monoallelic PARN carrier develop cirrhosis and pulmonary fibrosis? The answer lies in the complex interplay of genetic background, incomplete penetrance, somatic genetic rescue, and environmental exposures that differentially affect organ systems. Multiple telomere-related genes can contribute. Environmental and lifestyle factors influence the replicative stress placed on different tissues. In this patient, severe B12 deficiency may have served as an additional haematopoietic stressor on an already compromised marrow — telomere shortening reduces the replicative reserve of haematopoietic stem cells, making them more vulnerable to nutritional insults and accelerating the march toward bone marrow failure. The cytopenia was likely multifactorial, but the PARN variant underscores the need for ongoing haematological surveillance.

This is a critical teaching point: the absence of a family history doesn't exclude a hereditary syndrome. De novo mutations happen. Variable penetrance means affected relatives may be undiagnosed. And TBDs can masquerade as acquired disease for years before the pattern becomes recognisable.

## Diagnostic Approach and What to Do When You Suspect a TBD

The gold standard for diagnosis combines next-generation sequencing of telomere-related genes with flow cytometry-based FISH assays to measure telomere length in leucocyte subsets. In a young patient with unexplained multisystem disease — especially combinations of cytopenia, pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, or features of premature ageing — even without the classical mucocutaneous triad, a TBD should be on the differential.

Treatment is currently largely supportive and organ-specific. Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation can be potentially curative for haematologic manifestations. Androgens like danazol and nandrolone have shown some telomere-elongating effects in preclinical and small clinical studies, but long-term outcomes remain unknown. Genetic counselling for the family is essential once a TBD is confirmed. And for the patient herself, ongoing surveillance of pulmonary, hepatic, and haematological function is the name of the game.

## The Bottom Line

This case is a reminder that genotype-phenotype correlations in TBDs are still evolving. A monoallelic PARN variant, classically linked to pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure, can produce hepatic cirrhosis — a phenotype previously attributed to TERT and TERC mutations. When you see a young adult with unexplained multiorgan fibrosis, cytopenia, and features of premature ageing, think telomere biology. Don't wait for the mucocutaneous triad. Don't be reassured by a negative family history. And don't let a corrected B12 deficiency close the diagnostic loop prematurely.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Monoallelic PARN variants** classically present as telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4); biallelic variants cause autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6) and Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome
- **Hepatic fibrosis can occur with monoallelic PARN mutations** — this case expands the recognised phenotype beyond TERT/TECR-associated cirrhosis
- **PARN is among the 20% of genes least tolerant to disruptive variants**, supporting haploinsufficiency as a plausible disease mechanism even in monoallelic carriers
- **TBDs can present without the classical mucocutaneous triad** — suspect them in young adults with unexplained combinations of cytopenia, pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, or premature ageing features, even without a family history
- **Nutritional deficiencies can unmask or exacerbate an underlying TBD** — severe B12 deficiency may represent a haematopoietic stressor that reveals reduced marrow reserve from telomere shortening
- **Diagnosis requires genetic testing plus telomere length measurement** by flow cytometry-based FISH; treatment is largely supportive, with HSCT as a potential curative option for haematologic manifestations and genetic counselling for the family
- **Correcting the deficiency doesn't correct the disease** — if the systemic phenotype persists after nutritional repletion, keep the diagnostic loop open



# What Do I Do With a Variant of Uncertain Significance? A Clinician's Guide to ACMG Framework and Clinical-Genetic Correlation

## The Case That Stops You in Your Tracks

Picture this: a woman in her late 20s walks in with five years of progressive fatigue, and by the time you see her, she needs help with self-care. She's got pancytopenia, dimorphic anaemia, severe B12 deficiency, cirrhosis, pulmonary fibrosis, osteoporosis, premature greying, neuropathy, and hepatosplenomegaly. You correct her B12 deficiency — and the systemic dysfunction persists, disproportionate and unrelenting. The infectious workup is essentially negative. Autoimmune serologies are negative. No malignancy on PET-CT. No family history. So you send clinical exome sequencing, and you get back a single monoallelic missense variant in *PARN*: c.613T>C, p.Cys205Arg. And the lab report says: **Variant of Uncertain Significance**.

Now what?

This is the scenario described in a recent case report by Soni and colleagues, and it captures a dilemma that clinicians face increasingly often as next-generation sequencing becomes more accessible. You have a patient with a phenotype that screams "something genetic," a gene that fits the story, and a variant that the lab won't commit to calling pathogenic. Do you wait? Do you act? Do you dismiss it?

Let's walk through how this team reasoned through it, because the framework they used is one every clinician ordering genomic testing should understand.

## Why a VUS Is Not the Same as "Noise"

A Variant of Uncertain Significance does not mean "probably benign." It means the available evidence is insufficient to classify the variant as pathogenic, likely pathogenic, benign, or likely benign under the ACMG/AMP framework. The classification system — established by the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and the Association for Molecular Pathology in 2015 — uses a combination of evidence types, each assigned a weight, to arrive at a five-tier classification. The tiers combine population data, computational predictions, functional evidence, segregation data, and the gene's established disease mechanism.

The critical point for clinicians is this: **a VUS can accumulate evidence over time and migrate to a higher classification**. Your job is not to override the lab but to assess whether the clinical phenotype is consistent enough with the gene's known disease spectrum to justify treating the result as clinically actionable — while acknowledging the uncertainty honestly with the patient.

## Applying the ACMG Framework to p.Cys205Arg

The clinical team in this case did not simply accept the VUS label and move on. They examined the specific ACMG criteria the variant met and weighed them against the clinical picture. Here is what they found:

**PM1 — Located in a critical functional domain.** The p.Cys205Arg substitution falls within the R3H domain of PARN (residues 178–245). This domain is highly conserved, and no benign variation has been described in this region. PM1 applies when a variant sits in a well-established functional domain where missense changes are known to cause disease. The R3H domain qualifies — it is involved in RNA binding and substrate recognition, central to PARN's deadenylation function.

**PM2 — Absent from population databases.** The variant is either absent or present at an extremely low frequency in gnomAD and other population databases. A variant that is absent from large control populations is more likely to be pathogenic, particularly in a gene like PARN that is not highly tolerant to loss-of-function variation. PARN is among the 20% of genes least tolerant to rare, disruptive variants.

**PP3 — Computational tools predict a damaging effect.** Four of five in silico prediction tools — LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT — predict that p.Cys205Arg is deleterious. While in silico predictions are supportive evidence only and never sufficient on their own, their concordance strengthens the case.

**PP2 — PARN has a low rate of benign missense variation.** PARN is a gene where missense variants are a known mechanism of disease, and the rate of benign missense variation is low. This means that when you encounter a missense variant in PARN, the prior probability of pathogenicity is higher than it would be in a gene where missense variation is common and harmless.

Now, if you tally these up using the ACMG point system, PM1 and PM2 are moderate evidence, and PP2 and PP3 are supporting evidence. That combination does not reach the threshold for "likely pathogenic" — which requires at least moderate and supporting evidence in specific combinations — hence the VUS classification. The lab got it right by the book.

But here is where clinical correlation enters the picture.

## Clinical-Genetic Correlation: The Piece the Lab Cannot Assess

The lab evaluates the variant in isolation. It cannot weigh the variant against your patient's phenotype — only you can do that. And in this case, the phenotype was remarkably congruent with the known PARN-associated disease spectrum.

Monoallelic PARN variants are established causes of telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4). The literature now documents over 65 cases of monoallelic PARN variants associated with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. Biallelic PARN variants cause a more severe phenotype — dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), and Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome. This patient had a monoallelic variant, which aligns with the milder, later-onset end of the spectrum.

Her clinical features — pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, cytopenia, and features of accelerated ageing such as premature greying and osteoporosis in a young adult — fit squarely within the telomere biology disorder (TBD) spectrum. The hepatic fibrosis is particularly noteworthy. While monoallelic PARN is more commonly associated with pulmonary fibrosis, emerging data from telomere biology disease registries show that hepatic fibrosis is under-recognised across TBDs. A registry analysis found that 88.8% of adults with TERT or TERC mutations had at least moderate hepatic fibrosis, and 33.3% had cirrhosis. The PARN variant's downstream impact on telomerase function — PARN is required for 3'-end maturation of TERC — plausibly explains hepatic fibrosis through the same mechanism seen with TERT and TERC mutations.

The clinical team also looked to ClinVar for precedent. Other PARN missense variants — p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1) — are classified as "likely pathogenic." These variants were evaluated under the same ACMG framework and crossed the threshold that p.Cys205Arg narrowly misses. The parallel is informative: it demonstrates that PARN missense variants in conserved residues can and do reach pathogenic classification, and that the gap between VUS and likely pathogenic for this variant may close with additional functional or segregation data.

## The Tension: Wait or Act?

Here is the uncomfortable reality of clinical genetics in progressive disease. You cannot always wait for the variant to be reclassified before making clinical decisions.

In this case, the team used the PARN VUS not to make a definitive molecular diagnosis but to **support a clinical diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder**. The distinction matters. They did not tell the patient she definitively had a pathogenic mutation. They told her that her clinical phenotype was consistent with a TBD, that the genetic finding was supportive but not conclusive, and that the combination warranted a TBD management approach.

What does that management approach look like? It means ongoing surveillance of haematologic, hepatic, and pulmonary function. It means genetic counselling for the family, because even a VUS in a gene with autosomal dominant or recessive implications has relevance for relatives who may carry the same variant with different expressivity. It means considering telomere length testing — flow cytometry-based FISH assays — as an independent line of evidence. And in progressive cases with marrow failure, it means early referral for haematopoietic stem cell transplantation evaluation, because the window for successful transplant narrows as organ fibrosis advances.

Supportive measures also matter. Androgen therapy with danazol has shown telomere elongation in preclinical and small clinical studies, though long-term outcomes remain unknown. For this patient, the team corrected nutritional deficiencies, provided transfusion support, addressed osteoporosis, and arranged physiotherapy — pragmatic management regardless of the genetic certainty.

## What This Means for Your Practice

The take-home message is not that you should override VUS classifications. It is that you should understand them — what evidence supports them, what evidence is missing, and how your patient's phenotype fits the gene's known disease spectrum. A VUS is a starting point for clinical correlation, not an endpoint.

- **Understand the ACMG criteria behind the classification.** Ask which specific evidence codes the variant meets and which are missing. The gap between VUS and likely pathogenic is often one or two pieces of evidence — functional data, segregation data, or additional unrelated cases.
- **Assess clinical-phenotypic congruence independently.** The lab cannot do this. If your patient's phenotype matches the gene's established disease spectrum, document that reasoning explicitly in the chart.
- **Use telomere length testing as complementary evidence.** Flow-FISH telomere length measurement is the gold standard for TBD diagnosis and can provide independent support for a clinical diagnosis even when the variant classification is uncertain.
- **Do not wait for reclassification in progressive disease.** Initiate TBD-appropriate surveillance and counselling based on clinical suspicion. Re-evaluation of the variant as new data accumulate is appropriate — request periodic updates from the lab or check ClinVar.
- **Be transparent with patients about uncertainty.** Explain that the variant is supportive but not definitive, that the clinical diagnosis is based on the totality of evidence, and that management decisions are driven by the clinical picture — not solely by the genetic result.

The patient in this case was managed supportively, received genetic counselling, and is under ongoing surveillance. Her blood counts stabilised at two-week follow-up. The PARN variant remains a VUS. But the clinical diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder gave her and her clinicians a framework for understanding her multisystem disease, for anticipating complications, and for making informed decisions about the future. Sometimes, that is what a VUS can do — not close the case, but open a clinical pathway.



# The Disproportionate Cytopenia: When B12 Deficiency Is the Tip of the Iceberg

## The Hook

A woman in her late 20s walks in with five years of progressive fatigue, exertional dyspnoea, and weight loss. She's got severe B12 deficiency — dimorphic anaemia, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, hypersegmented neutrophils, the works. Her DAT is positive. Open-and-shut case of megaloblastic anaemia with maybe some autoimmune haemolysis thrown in, right?

Not so fast. She also has cirrhosis, pulmonary fibrosis, osteoporosis, premature greying, and a reticulocyte count of 0.39% that refuses to budge appropriately. This is the kind of case that should make you pause at the bedside and ask: *Does this diagnosis actually explain all of this?*

## The Case in Detail

This patient presented to a tertiary care centre with profoundly impaired functional capacity — unable to perform self-care, requiring assistance for daily activities. On exam, she had pallor, icterus, premature greying, knuckle hyperpigmentation, and a bald tongue with lingual papillary atrophy. Musculoskeletal exam revealed thoracic scoliosis and bilateral thenar atrophy. She had hepatomegaly (5 cm below the costal margin, span 18 cm) and reduced breath sounds bilaterally in the infra-scapular regions.

Her initial labs showed pancytopenia with a reticulocyte count of 0.39% — strikingly low. The peripheral smear showed dimorphic erythrocytes, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils. Severe B12 and folate deficiency were confirmed. Iron studies showed elevated serum iron and ferritin, consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis. Bone marrow showed trilineage haematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia and increased iron stores — no malignancy, no dysplasia, normal cytogenetics.

So far, this all fits megaloblastic anaemia. But here's where it gets interesting.

## The Distractors

Her DAT came back positive for IgG (2+), with a negative indirect test. LDH was elevated, and she had indirect hyperbilirubinaemia. The team briefly considered immune-mediated haemolysis. But the reticulocyte response was inappropriately low for haemolysis, and the marrow findings pointed to ineffective erythropoiesis, not peripheral destruction. The positive DAT here was a red herring — a distractor that could have easily derailed the diagnostic reasoning if taken at face value.

This is a critical teaching point. A positive DAT in the context of a low reticulocyte count and marrow evidence of ineffective erythropoiesis should not lead you down the autoimmune haemolysis pathway. The marrow is telling you the problem is at the production level, not the destruction level. Listen to the marrow.

## The Disproportionate Presentation

The B12 and folate were replaced aggressively — intravenous cyanocobalamin 1000 µg daily for 5 days, then weekly for 4 weeks, then monthly. Folate was supplemented. The nutritional deficiency was corrected.

But the systemic dysfunction didn't resolve. She still had cirrhosis (liver stiffness 18.1 kPa on transient elastography). She still had pulmonary fibrosis (diffuse ground-glass haziness with fibrotic and subpleural atelectatic changes on PET-CT). She still had osteoporosis with recurrent fractures. She still had an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy. She still had premature greying.

This is what we mean by "disproportionate presentation." The severity and breadth of systemic involvement was out of proportion to what B12 deficiency alone could explain. B12 deficiency can cause cytopenia, neuropathy, and glossitis — but it does not cause hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, osteoporosis with fractures in a young adult, and premature greying. When the primary diagnosis doesn't account for the full clinical picture, you need to broaden the differential.

## The Unifying Diagnosis

The team suspected a syndromic or genetic aetiology and sent for clinical exome sequencing. The result: a monoallelic missense variant in **PARN** (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, exon 8), affecting a highly conserved residue in the R3H domain. Classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance by ACMG criteria, but predicted damaging by four of five in silico tools, absent from population databases, and occurring in a gene with a low rate of benign missense variation.

PARN encodes a poly(A)-specific ribonuclease involved in TERC maturation — a critical step in telomerase function. Monoallelic PARN variants are associated with telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4). Biallelic variants cause dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6).

The clinical-genetic correlation supported a diagnosis of a **telomere biology disorder (TBD)**.

## Why This Matters: Telomeres and the Susceptible Marrow

Here's the conceptual framework that makes this case so instructive. Telomeres cap chromosome ends and preserve genomic integrity. With each cell division, they shorten. When they reach a critical length, cells undergo senescence or apoptosis. Telomerase — comprising TERT and TERC — maintains telomere length, but pathogenic variants in telomere-related genes impair this maintenance, leading to premature telomere attrition.

Actively dividing tissues bear the brunt: bone marrow, skin, mucosa, hair, hepatocytes, pneumocytes. This is why TBDs manifest as bone marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, premature greying, and mucocutaneous abnormalities.

Now here's the key insight from this case: **telomere shortening may render bone marrow more susceptible to haematopoietic stress.** Severe B12 deficiency is a profound stressor on the marrow — ineffective erythropoiesis, increased cellular turnover, accelerated senescence. In a patient with already compromised telomere maintenance, this stress may unmask or exacerbate marrow dysfunction in a way that is disproportionate to the nutritional deficiency alone.

In other words, the B12 deficiency was real, but it was the tip of the iceberg. The underlying TBD made the marrow's response to that deficiency pathologic and persistent.

## The Absence of the Classical Triad

Dyskeratosis congenita — the prototype TBD — is classically defined by the mucocutaneous triad of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation. But TBDs can present in adulthood without any of these features, manifesting instead as "idiopathic" pulmonary fibrosis, cryptogenic cirrhosis, or aplastic anaemia. This patient had none of the classical triad. If the team had waited for the textbook features before considering a TBD, the diagnosis would have been missed entirely.

The emerging literature supports this. Data from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry showed that 88.8% of adult patients with TERC or TERT mutations had at least moderate hepatic fibrosis, and 33.3% had cirrhosis. Hepatic fibrosis in TBDs is under-recognised but likely more prevalent than appreciated. And while monoallelic PARN variants are more commonly associated with pulmonary fibrosis than haematological manifestations, this case demonstrates that the spectrum is expanding.

## The Positive DAT: A Lesson in Diagnostic Anchoring

Let's return to the positive DAT for a moment. It would have been easy to anchor on "autoimmune haemolysis" and stop there. But the reticulocyte count was the clue that something else was going on. In true haemolysis, you expect a brisk reticulocyte response — typically >2%, often much higher. A reticulocyte count of 0.39% in the setting of severe anaemia is a production problem, not a destruction problem.

The lesson: **a low reticulocyte response disproportionate to the degree of anaemia should always prompt further investigation.** Don't let a positive serologic test override the physiology in front of you.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Disproportionate presentation is a diagnostic trigger.** When correcting a nutritional deficiency doesn't resolve the systemic dysfunction — or when the breadth of organ involvement exceeds what the primary diagnosis can explain — broaden the differential and think about genetic or syndromic causes.

- **A positive DAT can be a distractor.** In the setting of a low reticulocyte count and marrow evidence of ineffective erythropoiesis, a positive DAT does not equal autoimmune haemolysis. Trust the marrow and the reticulocyte response over the serology.

- **Telomere biology disorders can present without the classical triad.** No oral leukoplakia, no nail dystrophy, no reticular pigmentation? You can still have a TBD. Consider it in young adults with unexplained multisystem disease — especially when bone marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis, or hepatic fibrosis coexist.

- **Correcting a deficiency doesn't exclude a concomitant genetic disorder.** The B12 deficiency was real and needed treatment. But it was sitting on top of a telomere biology disorder that was the real driver of the multisystem phenotype. Both can coexist, and the genetic disorder may make the marrow more vulnerable to haematopoietic stress.

- **A low reticulocyte count disproportionate to anaemia is never normal.** It points to ineffective erythropoiesis or marrow failure. If it persists after correcting reversible causes, investigate further — genetic testing may be warranted.

- **Hepatic fibrosis is an under-recognised feature of TBDs.** Don't attribute young-onset cryptogenic cirrhosis to "probably autoimmune" or "probably NAFLD" without considering telomere-related causes, especially when accompanied by cytopenia or pulmonary fibrosis.

- **PARN-associated TBDs have an expanding phenotype.** Monoallelic PARN variants are classically linked to pulmonary fibrosis, but this case demonstrates hepatic fibrosis and bone marrow susceptibility as part of the spectrum. The full clinical range remains poorly understood.

## Final Thought

This case is a masterclass in diagnostic humility. The team could have stopped at B12 deficiency. They could have stopped at a positive DAT. They could have attributed the cirrhosis to "probably something else" and the pulmonary fibrosis to "idiopathic." Instead, they noticed that the presentation was disproportionate — that the whole picture didn't fit — and they kept looking. That instinct to ask "does this actually explain everything?" is what separates pattern matching from genuine diagnostic reasoning.

In the age of genomic medicine, the iceberg is smaller than it used to be. But only if you look beneath the surface.



## The Forgotten Organ: Hepatic Fibrosis in Telomere Biology Disorders and Why You Should Screen for It

Picture this: a woman in her late 20s walks into your clinic with fatigue, pancytopenia, and cirrhosis. You work up the liver — viral hepatitis negative, autoimmune serologies negative, metabolic workup unrevealing. You work up the marrow — no malignancy, no dysplasia, just ineffective erythropoiesis from severe B12 deficiency. You fix the B12. She still has cirrhosis. She also has pulmonary fibrosis, osteoporosis, and premature grey hair. What do you do?

This is exactly the scenario described in a recent case report from Soni and colleagues, and it makes a critical point that every clinician managing telomere biology disorders (TBDs) needs to internalize: **hepatic fibrosis is not a footnote in TBDs — it may be one of the most prevalent and underrecognized manifestations.**

### The Case That Reframes Our Thinking

This patient presented with a 5-year history of progressive fatigue, ultimately found to have pancytopenia, dimorphic anemia with severe B12 deficiency, and — critically — hepatic fibrosis on transient elastography with a liver stiffness of 18.1 kPa, consistent with cirrhosis. She had no infectious, autoimmune, or metabolic explanation for her liver disease. She also had ground-glass haziness and fibrotic changes in both lungs on PET-CT, hepatosplenomegaly, osteoporosis with recurrent fractures, and premature greying. Clinical exome sequencing identified a monoallelic PARN missense variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance but consistent with the expanding spectrum of PARN-associated TBDs.

Here's what makes this case so instructive: monoallelic PARN variants are classically associated with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure — not hepatic fibrosis. Hepatic fibrosis has traditionally been linked to TERT and TERC mutations. Yet this patient, carrying only a monoallelic PARN variant, developed both pulmonary *and* hepatic fibrosis. The lungs we might have predicted. The liver? Most clinicians would have missed the connection entirely.

### The Data We've Been Ignoring

The Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry has been quietly accumulating evidence that hepatic fibrosis in TBDs is far more common than we've appreciated. In a retrospective analysis using transient elastography, **88.8% of adult patients with pathogenic TERC or TERT mutations had at least moderate hepatic fibrosis, and 33.3% had cirrhosis.** Let that sink in. Nearly nine in ten adult TBD patients in that cohort had meaningful liver fibrosis. One in three had cirrhosis. These aren't patients presenting with liver complaints — these are patients identified through systematic screening.

If hepatology isn't part of your TBD surveillance plan, you're likely missing disease in a substantial proportion of your patients.

### A Expanding Phenotypic Spectrum

The case report also references a comparable published case: a patient with pancytopenia and hepatic fibrosis who carried monoallelic variants in *both* PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). In that case, one might attribute the hepatic fibrosis to the TERT variant and the pulmonary phenotype to PARN. But the patient in this report had only a monoallelic PARN variant — no TERT, no TERC — and still developed cirrhosis. This suggests that the downstream impact of PARN on telomerase function (through its role in TERC 3'-end maturation) may produce hepatic phenotypes more commonly associated with TERT or TERC mutations directly.

The mechanism makes biological sense. PARN is required for removing post-transcriptionally acquired oligo(A) tails that target nuclear RNAs for degradation — a process essential for proper 3'-end maturation of TERC. Impaired PARN function → impaired TERC processing → impaired telomerase activity → accelerated telomere attrition. Actively dividing cells — bone marrow, skin, mucosa, hair, hepatocytes, and pneumocytes — bear the brunt. The liver has enormous regenerative capacity, but that capacity depends on hepatocyte replication, which in turn depends on telomere integrity. Short telomeres → senescence → fibrosis. It's a straight line from gene to organ.

### Why This Matters in the Emergency and Acute Care Setting

You might be thinking: TBDs are rare, I'll never see this. Maybe. But consider the patient who presents with unexplained cytopenias and is incidentally found to have elevated liver enzymes or elastography scores. Or the young adult with "idiopathic" pulmonary fibrosis who also has mild thrombocytopenia and an enlarged liver on exam. The temptation is to compartmentalize — refer to hematology for the counts, to pulmonology for the lungs, to hepatology for the liver — and miss the unifying diagnosis entirely.

The absence of the classical mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita (oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, reticular skin pigmentation) does not exclude a TBD. As this case demonstrates, TBDs can present in adulthood with isolated organ involvement and no family history, with a de novo mutation being a possibility. The premature greying, osteoporosis, and neuropathy in this patient were easy to dismiss individually — but together with pancytopenia and fibrotic organ disease, they pointed toward a telomere biology disorder.

### Practical Approach

For patients with known or suspected TBDs, regardless of the specific genetic variant identified:

- **Transient elastography should be part of routine surveillance.** This is non-invasive, widely available, and based on the Aachen data, likely to detect clinically significant fibrosis in the majority of adult TBD patients.
- **Don't assume the genotype predicts the organ phenotype.** PARN variants can cause hepatic fibrosis. TERT variants can cause marrow failure. The genotype-phenotype correlations in TBDs are imperfect, likely influenced by variable penetrance, environmental factors, and possibly somatic genetic rescue.
- **Consider TBD in young adults with unexplained multisystem disease**, especially when fibrosis (pulmonary, hepatic, or both) coexists with cytopenias or features of accelerated aging — even without the classical DC triad.
- **Monitor for evolving marrow failure** even when cytopenias have an alternative explanation. This patient's pancytopenia was largely attributed to B12 deficiency, but the presence of a PARN variant means her bone marrow may be uniquely vulnerable to hematopoietic stress, and ongoing surveillance is essential.

### The Bottom Line

Hepatic fibrosis in telomere biology disorders has been hiding in plain sight. The Aachen registry data tells us it's not a rare complication — it's the rule, not the exception, in adult TBD patients with TERT or TERC mutations. This case suggests we should extend that expectation to PARN variant carriers as well. If you're managing a patient with a known or suspected TBD and you haven't checked their liver with transient elastography, you're practicing incomplete medicine. The liver is not a footnote in TBDs. It's a target organ, and it demands our attention.

**Key Take-Home Messages:**

- **Hepatic fibrosis is highly prevalent in TBDs** — up to 88.8% of adult patients with TERT/TERC mutations in the Aachen registry had at least moderate fibrosis; 33.3% had cirrhosis.
- **PARN variants, classically linked to pulmonary fibrosis and marrow failure, can also cause hepatic fibrosis** — as demonstrated by this case of a patient with a monoallelic PARN variant and cirrhosis (liver stiffness 18.1 kPa) without any alternative hepatic explanation.
- **Transient elastography should be routine surveillance** for all patients with known or suspected TBDs, regardless of the primary genetic variant.
- **TBDs can present without the classical mucocutaneous triad** — consider the diagnosis in young adults with unexplained multisystem fibrosis, cytopenias, and premature aging features.
- **Genotype does not reliably predict organ phenotype in TBDs** — variable penetrance, environmental modifiers, and somatic genetic rescue all contribute to clinical heterogeneity.



# Managing Telomere Biology Disorders: Surveillance, Androgens, Transplantation, and the Counseling Conversation

## When the Zebras Hide in Plain Sight

Picture this: a woman in her late 20s walks in with five years of progressive fatigue, pancytopenia, premature greying, cirrhosis, pulmonary fibrosis, osteoporosis, and neuropathy. You correct her severe B12 deficiency — and she improves, but only partially. The cytopenias don't fully resolve. The fibrosis doesn't regress. The premature ageing features certainly don't reverse. That's the moment you realise you're not looking at nutritional deficiency or an occult autoimmune process. You're looking at a telomere biology disorder (TBD), and the diagnosis changes everything about how you counsel her, surveil her, and discuss what "curative" actually means.

What makes this case — reported by Soni and colleagues — so instructive is not just the rarity of a monoallelic PARN mutation presenting without the classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita. It's that the patient was eventually diagnosed, counselled, and managed with a thoughtful, realistic plan that acknowledges the limits of what we can offer while maximising what we can control. That conversation — the one where you tell a young adult their genotype carries a progressive, multisystem disease with limited curative options — is one every clinician should be prepared to have.

## The Diagnostic Gold Standard: Genetics Plus Telomere Length

The case team arrived at the diagnosis through clinical exome sequencing, which identified a monoallelic PARN missense variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. They then correlated the genetic finding with the clinical phenotype — cytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, and premature ageing features — to support a TBD diagnosis. This clinical-genetic correlation approach is essential because variant classification alone often leaves you in limbo, particularly for rare genes like PARN where the full phenotypic spectrum is still being defined.

The gold standard for diagnosing suspected TBDs combines two modalities. Next-generation sequencing (or clinical exome sequencing) identifies the causative variant, while flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridisation (flow-FISH) directly measures telomere length in leukocyte subsets. Flow-FISH is critical because it provides functional evidence — you can see whether the telomeres are abnormally short for age, which supports pathogenicity even when a variant is labelled "uncertain." In this case, the team leaned on the gene's established role in TBD biology, in silico predictions, ACMG/AMP supportive criteria, and parallels with other likely pathogenic PARN missense variants to build their case.

The practical pearl here: if you suspect a TBD clinically, don't rely on the genetic report alone. The variant classification may say "uncertain," but the telomere length assay and the clinical phenotype may tell a different story. You need all three pieces — genotype, telomere length, and phenotype — to make the call.

## Supportive Care: Organ by Organ, Symptom by Symptom

Once the diagnosis is established, management is largely supportive and tailored to the specific organ systems involved. In this patient, the initial intervention was correcting severe nutritional deficiencies — intravenous cyanocobalamin, oral folic acid, vitamin D, and calcium — which produced symptomatic improvement and stabilised blood counts at her two-week follow-up. She also received whole blood transfusions for symptomatic anaemia, electrolyte correction for hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia, and physiotherapy for her neuromuscular complications.

But supportive care in TBD is not just about treating what's broken today. It's about surveillance for what will break tomorrow. The bone marrow, lungs, and liver are the three organs most commonly and progressively involved. This patient continues outpatient monitoring of hepatic and pulmonary function alongside haematological surveillance, watching for evolution toward frank marrow failure. The osteoporosis requires ongoing bone health management. The neuropathy and musculoskeletal complications require rehabilitation input.

The framework to keep in mind: TBD management is longitudinal. Each clinic visit is an opportunity to ask three questions. Is the marrow failing further? Are the lungs or liver fibrosing? Are new organ systems becoming involved? The answers determine whether you escalate from supportive care to transplantation or experimental therapies.

## Hematopoietic Stem Cell Transplantation: When and for Whom

Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) is the only potentially curative option for the haematological manifestations of TBDs, and it is indicated in patients with progressive bone marrow failure. The decision to transplant is not taken lightly — these patients carry inherent risks of organ fibrosis that complicate transplantation, and telomere shortening itself impairs engraftment and increases transplant-related morbidity.

In this case, the patient's cytopenia was most likely attributable to severe B12 deficiency rather than intrinsic marrow failure, though the presence of the PARN variant raises the possibility that her genetically compromised marrow was more susceptible to haematopoietic stress. Her counts stabilised after nutritional repletion, so HSCT was not indicated at the time of the report. However, the treating team explicitly flagged ongoing haematological monitoring to assess for any evolving marrow failure phenotype, recognising that a predisposed individual can shift from compensated cytopenia to frank marrow failure over time.

The clinical pearl: if you're following a patient with a known or suspected TBD and cytopenia, don't assume that correcting a reversible trigger closes the chapter. Telomere-compromised stem cells have reduced replicative reserve. What looks like a nutritional deficiency unmasking an underlying vulnerability today may declare itself as irreversible marrow failure tomorrow. Transplant referral pathways should be identified early, even if not immediately activated.

## Androgens: Promising but Unproven

The discussion around androgens — primarily danazol and nandrolone — in TBD management is one of the most active areas of investigation, and it comes up in nearly every counselling conversation because patients and families ask about it. Preclinical models and small clinical studies suggest that androgens can elongate telomeres, likely through upregulation of telomerase activity. Danazol has shown telomere lengthening in early-phase studies involving patients with TBDs and cytopenia, generating significant excitement in the community.

However, the long-term effects remain unknown. The studies are small, the follow-up is limited, and questions persist about durability of response, impact on non-haematological organ involvement (does lung or liver fibrosis slow?), and adverse effects of chronic androgen therapy in young patients — particularly virilisation in females, hepatic toxicity in an already fibrotic liver, and cardiovascular risk. In this case, the treating team did not initiate androgens, opting instead for supportive care and surveillance, which reflects the current reality that androgens remain experimental rather than standard of care.

The honest framing for patients: androgens may become a meaningful therapy for TBDs, and early data are encouraging, but we don't yet have the evidence to recommend them routinely. If they are used, it should ideally be in the context of a clinical trial or with expert consultation.

## The Counselling Conversation: What We Can and Cannot Offer

Perhaps the most clinically important aspect of this case is what happened after the diagnosis: the family received genetic counselling, and the patient herself understood the nature of her condition and accepted that curative options were not feasible at that time. She continued surveillance. She maintained stable counts. She had no new clinical deterioration at follow-up. That outcome — acceptance, stability, and a plan — is a clinical success even in the absence of a cure.

The genetic counselling conversation in TBDs is complex. PARN-related TBDs can follow autosomal recessive inheritance (biallelic variants causing DKCB6) or manifest with monoallelic variants (associated with pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure). This patient's monoallelic variant raises questions about penetrance, variable expressivity, and the possibility that relatives carrying the same variant could develop pulmonary fibrosis, marrow failure, or hepatic fibrosis — or may never manifest disease at all. First-degree relatives should be offered genetic testing and baseline telomere length assessment, and they should be counselled about surveillance even if asymptomatic.

The prognostic conversation with the patient requires honesty without fatalism. TBDs are chronic, progressive conditions. The trajectory is variable. Some patients maintain stable organ function for years; others progress rapidly to marrow failure or respiratory failure. The role of the clinician is to set realistic expectations — this is a lifelong condition requiring ongoing monitoring — while avoiding a purely nihilistic framing. Surveillance catches progression early. Transplantation can be curative for marrow disease. Lung and liver transplantation are options in select cases. And the therapeutic landscape is evolving.

## Key Take-Home Points

- **Consider TBDs in young adults with unexplained multisystem disease** — cytopenia, organ fibrosis (lung, liver), and premature ageing features — even when the classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita is absent.
- **Diagnosis requires three pillars**: genetic sequencing (NGS or clinical exome), flow-FISH telomere length measurement, and clinical phenotype correlation. A variant labelled "uncertain significance" does not exclude a TBD if the clinical picture fits.
- **PARN is an expanding phenotype**: monoallelic variants classically associate with pulmonary fibrosis and marrow failure, but hepatic fibrosis is increasingly recognised. Don't anchor on IPF alone.
- **Correcting reversible triggers matters but doesn't close the case**: this patient's B12 deficiency caused her cytopenia, but her PARN variant means ongoing haematological surveillance is mandatory — telomere-compromised marrow has limited reserve.
- **HSCT is the only potentially curative option for haematological manifestations** and should be considered early in progressive marrow failure. Identify transplant referral pathways before you need them.
- **Androgens (danazol, nandrolone) are experimental**: early data on telomere elongation are promising, but long-term safety and efficacy are unknown. Use in trial contexts or with expert consultation.
- **Genetic counselling is non-negotiable**: relatives may carry the same variant with variable penetrance. Offer testing, baseline telomere assessment, and surveillance to first-degree family members.
- **Frame prognosis honestly but not nihilistically**: TBDs are progressive and chronic, but the trajectory is variable. Surveillance, transplantation, and evolving therapies offer meaningful intervention points. Acceptance and a structured plan — as modelled by this case patient — are legitimate and important clinical outcomes.
