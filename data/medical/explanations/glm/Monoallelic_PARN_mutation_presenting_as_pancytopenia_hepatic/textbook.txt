Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

## Case Presentation and Initial Diagnostic Workup

The patient was a woman in her late 20s who presented to a tertiary care centre after five years of progressively worsening generalised weakness and early fatigability. Her functional capacity had declined over the years to the point where she could no longer keep up with her peers in performing daily activities. In the three months preceding presentation, her weakness had worsened sufficiently to impair self-care and require assistance. She also reported exertional dyspnoea, intermittent chest discomfort, weight loss, and anorexia. The insidious onset and relentlessly progressive nature of her symptoms over five years, culminating in functional decline by the late 20s, immediately signalled a chronic, systemic process rather than an acute illness. For a clinician, a symptom timeline spanning years in a young adult should prompt consideration of slowly progressive constitutional diseases—including chronic nutritional deficiencies, occult malignancy, autoimmune disorders, and inherited syndromes—rather than acute infections or acute haematologic emergencies.

On examination, the patient was haemodynamically stable, but several physical findings pointed toward multisystem pathology. Pallor and icterus suggested anaemia with possible haemolysis or hepatic dysfunction. Premature greying of hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation, and a bald tongue with atrophy of lingual papillae were particularly noteworthy. Knuckle hyperpigmentation and a smooth, atrophic tongue are classic stigmata of vitamin B12 deficiency, resulting from impaired mucosal cell turnover and megaloblastic changes in rapidly dividing epithelial cells. Premature greying, however, is less easily explained by nutritional deficiency alone and raised the possibility of a premature ageing syndrome. Musculoskeletal examination revealed thoracic scoliosis and bilateral thenar atrophy, the latter suggesting median nerve compression or a more generalised neuropathic process. Abdominal examination identified hepatomegaly (5 cm below the right costal margin, with a liver span of 18 cm), and respiratory examination revealed reduced breath sounds in the bilateral infra-scapular regions, suggesting possible pleural effusion, atelectasis, or parenchymal consolidation. Taken together, the physical examination already implicated the haematologic, hepatic, pulmonary, neurologic, and musculoskeletal systems—a pattern that would prove critical in redirecting the diagnostic approach away from single-organ disease.

Initial laboratory evaluation confirmed pancytopenia—reductions in all three haematopoietic cell lines—with a notably low reticulocyte count of 0.39%. A low reticulocyte response in the setting of cytopenia indicates that the bone marrow is not appropriately compensating, which points toward ineffective haematopoiesis or marrow failure rather than peripheral destruction or sequestration. The peripheral smear demonstrated a dimorphic erythrocyte population (two distinct red cell populations differing in size), basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils. Hypersegmented neutrophils—defined as neutrophils with six or more nuclear lobes—are a hallmark of megaloblastic anaemia, in which impaired DNA synthesis due to vitamin B12 or folate deficiency leads to asynchronous maturation of the nucleus and cytoplasm. Basophilic stippling (aggregated ribosomes visible as punctate blue inclusions) and Cabot rings (ring-shaped nuclear remnants) further support dysregulated erythropoiesis. A dimorphic smear can reflect a combined nutritional deficiency, early response to supplementation, or a mixed marrow disorder. Serum studies confirmed severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency, while iron studies revealed elevated serum iron and ferritin—a pattern consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis, in which iron is absorbed and recycled but not efficiently incorporated into red blood cells because erythroid precursors fail to mature and instead undergo intramedullary apoptosis.

Bone marrow examination demonstrated trilineage haematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia and increased iron stores, but without evidence of malignancy, dysplasia, or infiltrative disease. Cytogenetic analysis was normal. Erythroid hyperplasia with increased iron stores in the context of peripheral pancytopenia is the marrow correlate of ineffective erythropoiesis: the marrow is hypercellular in the erythroid lineage, but the cells fail to mature into functional circulating blood cells. The absence of dysplastic changes, clonal cytogenetic abnormalities, or malignant infiltration effectively excluded myelodysplastic syndrome, acute leukaemia, and infiltrative marrow diseases such as lymphoma or metastatic carcinoma—diagnoses that would have been high on the differential for a young adult with pancytopenia. Markers of haemolysis were also evaluated and revealed elevated lactate dehydrogenase and indirect hyperbilirubinaemia, and a direct antiglobulin test (DAT) was positive for IgG (2+). A positive DAT suggests antibody-mediated coating of red blood cells and typically raises concern for autoimmune haemolysis. However, the low reticulocyte response, the marrow findings of ineffective erythropoiesis rather than compensatory hyperproduction, and the overall clinical picture argued against clinically significant peripheral immune-mediated destruction. This distinction is clinically important because a positive DAT can sometimes be an incidental finding, and treating haemolysis with immunosuppression in a patient whose primary problem is marrow dysfunction would be inappropriate and potentially harmful.

To investigate possible gastrointestinal malabsorption as a cause for the severe B12 deficiency, upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was performed and appeared macroscopically normal. Duodenal biopsy showed mild chronic gastritis with reduced parietal cells, which could contribute to impaired intrinsic factor production and consequent B12 malabsorption. However, the clinical team recognised that the severity and breadth of the patient's systemic findings were disproportionate to what nutritional deficiency alone could explain. This recognition—that a single unifying diagnosis had not yet been identified despite finding a plausible partial explanation—is a critical step in diagnostic reasoning and ultimately drove the workup toward a syndromic or genetic aetiology.

Hepatic dysfunction prompted further evaluation. Abdominal ultrasound revealed hepatosplenomegaly. Transient elastography, a noninvasive method of estimating liver stiffness by measuring shear wave velocity through hepatic tissue, showed grade II hepatic steatosis (controlled attenuation parameter of 295 dB/m) and cirrhosis (liver stiffness of 18.1 kPa). In transient elastography, a liver stiffness measurement exceeding approximately 12–14 kPa is generally consistent with cirrhosis, and this patient's value of 18.1 kPa firmly placed her in the cirrhotic range. Inflammatory markers were elevated, but autoimmune serologies—including antinuclear antibodies and autoimmune hepatitis antibodies—were negative. Infectious workup for viral hepatitis (B and C), HIV, cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus was largely unrevealing except for positive Epstein-Barr virus IgG, which reflects past exposure and does not establish active infection. The exclusion of autoimmune, infectious, and common metabolic causes of cirrhosis in a young woman left the hepatic fibrosis unexplained by conventional mechanisms, a finding that would later take on great significance.

To evaluate for occult malignancy or granulomatous disease that could unify the multisystem findings, a positron emission tomography–CT (PET-CT) was performed. It demonstrated inhomogeneous ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) uptake in the axial and appendicular bone marrow, a pattern that can reflect reactive marrow stimulation but does not by itself indicate malignancy. The pulmonary window revealed diffuse ground-glass haziness with multiple fibrotic and subpleural atelectatic changes in both lung fields. Ground-glass opacity on CT represents increased lung density that does not obscure underlying bronchial and vascular margins, and it can reflect inflammation, oedema, or early fibrosis. The fibrotic and subpleural changes are significant because idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) characteristically involves the subpleural regions and lower lobes. The PET-CT also confirmed hepatosplenomegaly and ascites but showed no evidence of overt malignancy or granulomatous disease, effectively excluding lymphoma, metastatic carcinoma, and sarcoidosis as explanations for the patient's multisystem presentation.

Additional systemic evaluation further expanded the clinical picture. Secondary hyperparathyroidism, hypocalcaemia, hypokalaemia, and vitamin D deficiency were identified. Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) confirmed osteoporosis, explaining her recurrent bone pain and fractures. In a young adult, osteoporosis without a clear secondary cause (such as chronic steroid use, hypogonadism, or malabsorption) is itself unusual and contributes to the picture of premature ageing. Electromyography and nerve conduction studies revealed an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy with chronic neurogenic changes in both proximal and distal muscle groups, providing a physiologic explanation for her bilateral thenar atrophy and functional weakness. An axonal polyneuropathy can result from nutritional deficiency, but the asymmetry and chronic neurogenic changes raised the possibility of an additional underlying process.

By the conclusion of this initial workup, the diagnostic landscape had shifted dramatically. The patient had documented pancytopenia with ineffective erythropoiesis, severe B12 and folate deficiency, cirrhosis without a conventional cause, pulmonary fibrosis, osteoporosis, premature greying, and a sensorimotor neuropathy. While the nutritional deficiencies explained part of the haematologic and neurologic picture, the persistence of hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, and premature ageing features in a young woman could not be attributed to B12 or folate deficiency. This realisation—that the patient's findings spanned multiple organ systems in a pattern suggesting accelerated cellular ageing and fibrosis—shifted the diagnostic reasoning toward a syndromic or inherited disorder and set the stage for clinical exome sequencing.

## Differential Diagnosis and Diagnostic Reasoning

The diagnostic journey in this case illustrates a fundamental principle of clinical reasoning: when a unifying explanation fails to account for the full breadth of a patient's presentation, the clinician must broaden the differential and reconsider whether seemingly unrelated findings share a common underlying cause. For this young woman, the initial clinical picture suggested several plausible explanations—nutritional deficiency, autoimmune disease, and chronic infection—but each proved insufficient upon systematic evaluation, ultimately redirecting attention toward a rare inherited disorder.

**Nutritional Deficiency as an Initial Explanation**

The patient's severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency provided a tempting initial diagnosis. Severe B12 deficiency can produce pancytopenia through ineffective erythropoiesis, in which hematopoietic precursors fail to mature properly due to impaired DNA synthesis. The peripheral smear findings—dimorphic erythrocytes, hypersegmented neutrophils, basophilic stippling, and Cabot rings—were classic megaloblastic features, and the bone marrow showed erythroid hyperplasia with increased iron stores, all consistent with this diagnosis. Her neurological findings, including polyneuropathy and bilateral thenar atrophy, were also compatible with B12 deficiency, which can cause subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord and peripheral neuropathy.

However, several features remained disproportionate to nutritional deficiency alone. Hepatic fibrosis and cirrhosis, pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying of hair, osteoporosis with recurrent fractures, and the persistent decline in functional capacity over five years are not explained by B12 or folate deficiency. Even after correction of these deficiencies with parenteral cyanocobalamin and oral folate, the patient's multisystem dysfunction persisted. This discrepancy between the identified deficiency and the clinical severity was an early signal that a more fundamental process was at work.

**Ineffective Erythropoiesis Versus Haemolysis**

A positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) for IgG initially raised concern for autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. This finding, combined with elevated lactate dehydrogenase and indirect hyperbilirubinaemia—both markers typically associated with haemolysis—could have led clinicians down an autoimmune pathway. However, the clinical reasoning correctly prioritized the reticulocyte count and bone marrow findings. In true immune-mediated haemolysis, the marrow responds with compensatory erythroid hyperplasia and a robust reticulocytosis as the body attempts to replace destroyed red cells. This patient's reticulocyte count was markedly low at 0.39%, indicating a hypoproliferative state rather than peripheral destruction. The bone marrow showed no evidence of dysplasia, malignancy, or infiltrative disease but did demonstrate ineffective erythropoiesis. The elevated iron and ferritin levels further supported this interpretation, as ineffective erythropoiesis traps iron within the marrow due to failed red cell maturation rather than releasing it through haemolysis. Thus, despite the positive DAT—a finding that can sometimes represent a nonspecific or incidental antibody—the weight of evidence pointed toward marrow dysfunction, not immune-mediated destruction.

**Evaluation of Hepatic Fibrosis Aetiology**

The discovery of cirrhosis on transient elastography in a woman in her late twenties prompted a thorough search for common hepatic causes. Autoimmune hepatitis was excluded by negative antinuclear antibodies and autoimmune hepatitis-specific serologies. Infectious causes were systematically ruled out: viral hepatitis panels, HIV, cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus testing were unrevealing. The only positive infectious marker was Epstein-Barr virus IgG, indicating past exposure rather than active infection. The inflammatory markers were elevated, but without a specific autoimmune or infectious target, this finding remained nonspecific. The absence of a clear hepatic aetiology in a young patient with cirrhosis was itself a diagnostic clue, as early-onset fibrosis without an identifiable cause should prompt consideration of metabolic, genetic, or syndromic disorders.

**Gastrointestinal Evaluation**

To evaluate possible malabsorption contributing to the B12 deficiency, upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was performed. The macroscopic appearance was normal, and duodenal biopsy showed only mild chronic gastritis with reduced parietal cells. While reduced parietal cell mass could impair intrinsic factor production and contribute to B12 malabsorption, this finding was too modest to explain the severity of the patient's deficiency or her multisystem involvement. No evidence of celiac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, or other significant gastrointestinal pathology was identified. The gastrointestinal workup thus reinforced the impression that nutritional deficiency was a contributing factor but not the primary driver of her illness.

**The Diagnostic Turning Point**

The critical shift in diagnostic reasoning occurred when the clinicians stepped back from individual organ findings and recognized the pattern connecting them. The constellation of premature greying of hair, osteoporosis with recurrent fractures, persistent cytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, and neuropathy in a young adult represented a syndrome of accelerated ageing and tissue failure. Individually, each finding had been evaluated and partially explained, but collectively they pointed toward a unifying diagnosis. Premature greying and osteoporosis in a patient under 30, combined with fibrosis of two organs (liver and lung) and bone marrow dysfunction, is not a pattern typical of nutritional deficiency, autoimmune disease, or infection. This recognition that the whole was greater—and more informative—than the sum of its parts redirected the diagnostic approach toward genetic testing.

**Absence of Family History and De Novo Mutation**

The lack of any family history of similar disease initially argued against a hereditary syndrome. However, this reasoning was appropriately challenged by the possibility of a de novo mutation, which can occur without any familial precedent. Additionally, variable penetrance in telomere biology disorders means that relatives carrying the same variant may be asymptomatic or present with milder, unrecognized manifestations. The absence of a family history, while relevant, does not exclude a genetic diagnosis and should not terminate that line of investigation when clinical suspicion is high.

**Hypothesis of a Telomere Biology Disorder**

The hypothesis of a telomere biology disorder (TBD) emerged from the recognition that TBDs can mimic acquired conditions and present with variable penetrance, often without the classical mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita—oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation. This patient lacked all three features, which is consistent with the increasingly recognized presentation of TBDs in adulthood with isolated or atypical manifestations. Telomere biology disorders may underlie what initially appear to be idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, aplastic anaemia, bone marrow failure syndromes, or cryptogenic cirrhosis. The key diagnostic insight was that these seemingly separate entities—cytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis—can share a single genetic basis in telomere dysfunction. This hypothesis was subsequently confirmed by clinical exome sequencing, which identified a monoallelic PARN missense variant, providing a unifying genetic explanation for the patient's multisystem disease.

The diagnostic process in this case underscores the importance of pattern recognition across organ systems, the willingness to reconsider an initial diagnosis when the clinical picture remains disproportionate, and the value of genetic testing when standard evaluations fail to provide an adequate explanation for early-onset multi-organ disease.

## Telomere Biology Disorders and the PARN Gene: Pathophysiology and Genetics

Telomeres are repetitive DNA sequences that cap the ends of chromosomes, protecting them from degradation, end-to-end fusion, and inappropriate recombination. With each somatic cell division, the conventional DNA replication machinery cannot fully copy the lagging strand, causing telomeres to shorten progressively. When telomeres reach a critical length, cells enter replicative senescence or undergo apoptosis. In germline and stem cells, the ribonucleoprotein enzyme telomerase counteracts this attrition by adding telomeric repeats de novo. Telomerase comprises two core components: telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT), the catalytic subunit, and telomerase RNA component (TERC), the RNA template that TERT uses to synthesize telomeric DNA. Pathogenic variants in genes encoding components of the telomerase complex—or in genes involved in telomere maintenance more broadly—impair this restorative function, leading to premature telomere shortening and the cluster of inherited conditions known as telomere biology disorders (TBDs).

The pathophysiology of TBDs centers on accelerated telomere attrition. Because telomere length is a critical determinant of a cell's replicative lifespan, tissues with high turnover and active cell division are disproportionately affected. Bone marrow, skin, mucosa, hair follicles, and hepatocytes are among the most vulnerable cell populations. As telomeres in these compartments shorten prematurely, stem cell exhaustion and cellular senescence ensue, manifesting clinically as bone marrow failure, premature greying, mucocutaneous abnormalities, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis. The patient in this case exemplifies this principle: her clinical presentation featured pancytopenia, premature greying of hair, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis—all reflecting dysfunction of actively dividing tissues.

Dyskeratosis congenita (DC) is the prototypical TBD. It is classically defined by a mucocutaneous triad of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation, typically accompanied by bone marrow failure. The genetic architecture of DC is heterogeneous, with X-linked recessive variants in DKC1 having first established the link between DC and short telomeres. Beyond DC, the TBD spectrum includes severe childhood-onset subtypes such as Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome, each caused by variants in genes implicated in telomere maintenance. However, TBDs do not always present with the classical DC triad. Adult-onset presentations may feature isolated organ involvement—such as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis, or bone marrow failure—without mucocutaneous features, leading to frequent misdiagnosis. This patient's presentation is illustrative: she lacked oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular pigmentation, yet her constellation of cytopenia, organ fibrosis, and premature ageing features ultimately pointed toward a TBD. This case underscores that TBDs must be considered in young adults with unexplained multisystem disease even when classical mucocutaneous features are absent.

The PARN gene (poly(A)-specific ribonuclease) belongs to a highly conserved family of exoribonucleases. PARN functions primarily by shortening the poly(A) tails of messenger RNAs through deadenylation, thereby regulating gene expression at the post-transcriptional level. Critically for telomere biology, PARN is required for the removal of oligo(A) tails that are post-transcriptionally added to the 3-prime end of TERC RNA. These oligo(A) tails target nuclear RNAs for degradation, and their removal by PARN is necessary for proper 3-prime-end maturation of TERC. Without functional PARN, TERC processing is impaired, reducing the availability of mature telomerase RNA and compromising telomerase activity. In this way, PARN mutations indirectly lead to defective telomere elongation, linking a gene involved in general RNA metabolism to the telomere maintenance pathway.

PARN is among the approximately 20% of human genes least tolerant to rare, disruptive variants, reflecting its essential role in cellular function and supporting the concept of haploinsufficiency in carriers. This low tolerance means that even a single mutated allele may be insufficient to sustain normal function, predisposing heterozygous carriers to disease. The inheritance pattern of PARN-related TBD is dose-dependent. Biallelic PARN variants—where both alleles carry pathogenic changes—are associated with dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6) and have been reported in severe phenotypes such as Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome. Monoallelic PARN variants, by contrast, more commonly underlie telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4), a phenotype that tends to present in adulthood with milder but still significant organ involvement. The patient in this case harbored a monoallelic missense variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) in the R3H domain of PARN, classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Despite this classification, its potential pathogenicity is supported by several factors: the affected residue is highly conserved, the variant is absent from population databases, multiple in silico prediction tools predict a deleterious effect, and PARN has a low rate of benign missense variation. When ACMG/AMP criteria are applied, the variant meets several supportive thresholds (PM1, PM2, PP3, PP2), and parallels can be drawn with other PARN missense variants classified as likely pathogenic in ClinVar.

Although monoallelic PARN mutations are more commonly associated with IPF rather than primary haematological manifestations, the clinical spectrum continues to expand. The initial link between PARN and pulmonary fibrosis emerged from the identification of novel monoallelic variants in patients with sporadic IPF, and subsequent whole-exome sequencing studies confirmed rare PARN variants in a subset of IPF cases. Over 65 cases have now been documented in the literature. In this patient, the presence of both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis in association with a monoallelic PARN variant is particularly notable. While hepatic fibrosis has been well documented in carriers of TERC and TERT mutations—studies from telomere biology disease registries have found moderate hepatic fibrosis in the vast majority of adult patients with these variants—its association with monoallelic PARN mutations is still emerging. This patient's hepatic fibrosis may reflect downstream impairment of telomerase function via defective TERC maturation, producing a phenotype more typical of TERT or TERC mutations. Comparable cases in the literature have described patients with pancytopenia and hepatic fibrosis carrying monoallelic variants in both PARN and TERT, but this patient developed both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis with only a monoallelic PARN variant. These findings support an expanding clinical spectrum for PARN-associated TBDs and highlight hepatic fibrosis as an important component of the phenotype.

The variable expressivity of TBDs, even among individuals carrying the same variant, is influenced by multiple factors. Variable penetrance means that not all carriers of a pathogenic variant will manifest clinical disease, and the severity and organ involvement can differ widely. Somatic genetic rescue—a phenomenon in which a somatic mutation in a hematopoietic stem cell reverts a pathogenic germline variant—can partially restore function in the bone marrow compartment while leaving other organs unaffected. Environmental and lifestyle factors, such as infections, nutritional deficiencies, and toxin exposures, can further influence the replicative stress placed on different organ systems. In this patient, severe vitamin B12 deficiency may have acted as a hematopoietic stressor that unmasked an underlying bone marrow vulnerability conferred by the PARN variant. While her cytopenia was most likely attributable to the nutritional deficiency itself, the presence of a PARN variant raises the possibility that telomere shortening rendered her hematopoietic stem cells less capable of compensating for the added stress. This underscores the importance of ongoing hematological monitoring in genetically predisposed individuals, even when the initial cytopenia appears to have an acquired explanation. The interplay of these factors—genetic dose, penetrance, somatic rescue, and environmental stressors—provides a framework for understanding how a single monoallelic PARN variant can produce a multisystem phenotype encompassing bone marrow dysfunction, pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, and features of accelerated ageing.

## Genetic Diagnosis and Variant Interpretation

After the extensive clinical workup revealed a multisystem disorder affecting the bone marrow, liver, and lungs—without a unifying acquired explanation—the diagnostic focus shifted toward a genetic etiology. Clinical exome sequencing was pursued and identified a monoallelic missense variant in the PARN gene: c.613T>C, resulting in an amino acid substitution of cysteine to arginine at position 205 (p.Cys205Arg) in exon 8. This nucleotide change alters a residue within the R3H domain (spanning amino acids 178–245), a region of the PARN protein that is highly conserved across species. Conservation at a given residue suggests that evolutionary pressure has maintained it because it is critical to protein structure or function; a substitution at such a site is therefore more likely to be deleterious. In this case, the cysteine at position 205 was replaced by arginine—a change that disrupts the chemical character of the residue, as cysteine's unique thiol side chain can participate in disulfide bonding and metal coordination, functions arginine cannot fulfill. The location of this variant within a well-characterized functional domain, combined with its evolutionary conservation, provided early biological signals that it could be clinically meaningful.

To estimate the likely functional impact of the variant, four of five in silico missense prediction tools—LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT—predicted a damaging effect. These computational algorithms evaluate factors such as amino acid conservation, physicochemical properties of the substitution, and protein domain context to generate probability scores for pathogenicity. While none of these tools is diagnostic in isolation, their convergence on a deleterious prediction strengthens the case that a variant disrupts protein function. In clinical practice, in silico predictions serve as supportive evidence rather than primary proof, and they must always be interpreted in the context of the patient's phenotype and population-level data.

The formal framework used to classify this variant was the ACMG/AMP (American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics/Association for Molecular Pathology) standards, which provide a standardized, evidence-based system for categorizing sequence variants along a spectrum from benign to pathogenic. The PARN c.613T>C variant met several supportive criteria. **PM1** was applied because the substitution occurs within the R3H domain, a critical and well-established functional region of PARN with no known benign variation—meaning no healthy individuals in databases carry alterations at this site. **PM2** was satisfied because the variant is absent or extremely rare in large population databases such as gnomAD, indicating it is not a common polymorphism. **PP3** was met because multiple computational tools predicted a damaging effect on the gene product. Finally, **PP2** was applied because PARN has a low rate of benign missense variation overall, and missense variants are a recognized mechanism of disease in this gene—consistent with the observation that PARN is among the 20% of human genes least tolerant to rare, disruptive variants, supporting the concept of haploinsufficiency in carriers. Despite meeting these criteria, the evidence was insufficient to classify the variant as likely pathogenic or pathogenic under the ACMG/AMP framework, and it was therefore designated a **Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS)**.

A VUS represents a category of genetic variants for which the available evidence is inadequate to determine whether the variant is benign or pathogenic. This designation is a deliberate acknowledgment of uncertainty: it prevents clinicians from overcalling pathogenicity based on incomplete data, but it simultaneously creates a challenge for clinical decision-making. A VUS cannot be used to confirm a molecular diagnosis in isolation, nor should it be used to guide cascade family testing or reproductive counseling as a pathogenic variant would. In practice, the significance of a VUS must be assessed through clinical correlation—asking whether the patient's phenotype is consistent with the gene's known disease spectrum—and through ongoing reevaluation as new cases, functional studies, and population data accumulate over time.

To contextualize the potential pathogenicity of this variant, the authors drew parallels with other PARN missense variants reported in the ClinVar database. Two specific variants—p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1)—are classified as likely pathogenic. These variants, like p.Cys205Arg, are single missense changes in PARN identified in patients with clinical features suggestive of a telomere biology disorder. The existence of other likely pathogenic missense variants in the same gene supports the principle that missense alterations in PARN can be disease-causing, and it provides a comparative framework for evaluating the plausibility of the current variant's pathogenicity. However, it should be noted that each variant must be assessed on its own merits, as different missense changes within the same gene can have vastly different functional consequences depending on their location and the nature of the amino acid substitution.

The critical step in reaching a diagnostic conclusion in this case was **clinical-genetic correlation**. The patient presented with a constellation of findings—pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying, osteoporosis, and neuropathy—that collectively aligned with the known spectrum of telomere biology disorders (TBDs). PARN has an established role in this disease family: biallelic PARN variants cause autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), while monoallelic variants are increasingly recognized in telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4). Although the specific variant identified in this patient remained classified as a VUS by formal ACMG/AMP criteria, the strong phenotypic overlap with PARN-associated TBDs provided the clinical context necessary to support a diagnosis. This case illustrates a fundamental principle in clinical genetics: variant classification and clinical diagnosis are related but distinct processes. A VUS does not exclude a genetic disease, and when the clinical picture strongly suggests a particular disorder, the combination of a plausible candidate gene variant and a matching phenotype can justify a clinical diagnosis—even when the variant itself cannot yet be definitively classified as pathogenic.

The diagnostic gold standards for suspected TBDs include genetic testing through next-generation sequencing—of which clinical exome sequencing is one modality—and flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridization (flow-FISH) to directly measure telomere length in leukocyte subsets. Flow-FISH quantifies telomere fluorescence in individual cells and compares values to age-matched reference ranges, allowing clinicians to identify the abnormally short telomeres that are the molecular hallmark of TBDs. In this case, whole-exome sequencing was the primary tool that identified the PARN variant, and clinical correlation with the multisystem phenotype provided the diagnostic framework. The case underscores the complementary roles of genetic and functional testing: sequencing identifies the responsible variant, while telomere length measurement can provide independent confirmation of the downstream biological consequence. Together, these modalities form the foundation of modern TBD diagnostics, and their integration with clinical assessment is essential for accurate diagnosis, genetic counseling, and appropriate surveillance planning.

## Management, Outcome, and Clinical Lessons

The management of this patient required addressing both the acute, reversible contributors to her clinical decline and the underlying telomere biology disorder (TBD) that unified her multisystem presentation. Because no curative therapy was immediately available or appropriate at the time of diagnosis, treatment was primarily supportive and targeted at the most clinically significant derangements.

Severe vitamin B12 deficiency was among the most immediately treatable abnormalities and likely exacerbated the patient's pancytopenia. She received intravenous cyanocobalamin at a dose of 1000 µg daily for five days, followed by weekly administration for four weeks and then monthly maintenance dosing. This parenteral repletion regimen is standard for severe, symptomatic B12 deficiency, particularly when intrinsic factor production is impaired, as was suggested by the reduced parietal cell density on duodenal biopsy. Oral folic acid (1 mg daily) was provided concurrently, because folate deficiency was also documented and because folate repletion alone in the setting of untreated B12 deficiency can precipitate or worsen neurological complications. The importance of correcting these deficiencies was twofold: it addressed a genuine nutritional deficit that contributed to ineffective erythropoiesis, and it served as a diagnostic test of sorts—persistent cytopenia and multisystem dysfunction despite adequate repletion strengthened the case for an underlying genetic disorder.

Supportive care measures extended beyond nutritional correction. The patient received whole blood transfusions for symptomatic anaemia, providing symptomatic relief while nutritional repletion took effect. Vitamin D deficiency and osteoporosis, confirmed on dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry and contributing to her recurrent bone pain and fractures, were managed with high-dose vitamin D (60,000 IU weekly) and elemental calcium (1000 mg daily). Secondary hyperparathyroidism and electrolyte disturbances, including hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia, were corrected with standard supplementation. Physiotherapy was incorporated to address the functional consequences of her neuropathy, thenar atrophy, and generalised deconditioning, all of which had progressed to the point of impairing self-care. These supportive interventions collectively improved her functional status even though they did not alter the underlying telomere pathology.

Following the genetic diagnosis, the patient and her family received genetic counselling. This is a critical component of management for TBDs, because the implications extend beyond the individual patient. Although no family history of similar illness was identified, variable penetrance, variable expressivity, and the possibility of a de novo mutation mean that relatives may carry the same variant without manifesting disease. Counselling addressed the nature of the monoallelic PARN variant, its classification as a Variant of Uncertain Significance, and the clinical–genetic correlation that nonetheless supported a TBD diagnosis. The patient demonstrated understanding of her condition and acceptance that curative options were not currently feasible, an important element of shared decision-making in rare genetic disease management.

At her two-week follow-up, the patient showed symptomatic improvement. Her blood counts had stabilised, fatigue had lessened, and no new clinical concerns were identified. While this short-term outcome is encouraging, it must be interpreted cautiously in the context of a progressive genetic disorder. The improvement likely reflects correction of the superimposed nutritional deficiencies and supportive measures rather than stabilisation of the underlying telomere biology defect.

Long-term surveillance is therefore essential and forms a central pillar of ongoing management. The patient continues to receive regular outpatient monitoring for hepatic function, pulmonary function, and haematological parameters. This surveillance strategy is designed to detect progression of bone marrow failure, worsening hepatic fibrosis or cirrhosis, and advancement of pulmonary fibrosis—each of which represents a potentially life-threatening complication of TBD. The monitoring schedule must be proactive rather than reactive, because early detection of organ-specific progression may create windows for intervention that would otherwise be missed.

Several future therapeutic considerations merit discussion, even if they were not immediately pursued in this case. Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) is the only potentially curative therapy for the haematological manifestations of TBD and is generally considered when bone marrow failure becomes progressive or severe. In this patient, cytopenia was largely attributable to B12 deficiency and had stabilised with repletion; however, the presence of a PARN variant places her at ongoing risk for evolving marrow failure, and the treating team must maintain vigilance for this possibility. It is also important to note that HSCT in TBD patients carries unique risks, because telomere shortening affects multiple organ systems and can complicate engraftment, increase transplant-related toxicity, and accelerate pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis post-transplant. Additionally, preclinical and small clinical studies have suggested that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone may promote telomere elongation and improve cytopenias in some TBD patients, but the long-term efficacy and safety of these agents remain uncertain, and their use is generally reserved for selected cases under specialist guidance.

Several broader clinical lessons emerge from this case that are directly relevant to the practising clinician. First, TBDs should be considered in young adults presenting with unexplained multisystem disease, particularly when the constellation includes cytopenia, organ fibrosis, and features of accelerated ageing such as premature greying and osteoporosis. The absence of a family history should not dissuade clinicians from suspecting a genetic disorder, because de novo mutations, variable penetrance, and somatic genetic rescue can obscure inheritance patterns. In this case, the initial presentation with fatigue and pancytopenia could easily have been attributed solely to severe B12 deficiency, and the positive DAT and elevated inflammatory markers could have led down an autoimmune diagnostic path. It was the persistence of multisystem findings—particularly hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis—after correction of nutritional deficiencies that ultimately shifted the diagnostic trajectory.

Second, TBDs can present without the classical mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita, which consists of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation. Adult-onset TBDs frequently manifest with isolated organ involvement, such as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, bone marrow failure, or hepatic fibrosis, and may never develop the cutaneous features that define classical DC. This patient had premature greying and knuckle hyperpigmentation but lacked the full triad, illustrating how the absence of textbook mucocutaneous findings can delay recognition. Clinicians should understand that the phenotypic spectrum of TBDs is broad and that relying on classical features as a prerequisite for genetic evaluation will result in missed diagnoses.

Third, monoallelic PARN mutations may present with a broader phenotype than idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis alone. While biallelic PARN variants are associated with autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita and the more severe Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome, monoallelic variants are more commonly linked to telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure. This case demonstrates that a single PARN allele may also be associated with hepatic fibrosis, a feature more typically attributed to TERT or TERC mutations. The expanding clinical spectrum of PARN-associated TBDs suggests that the downstream effects of impaired PARN-mediated TERC maturation can produce phenotypes resembling those caused by direct telomerase component defects. Clinicians and geneticists should therefore avoid anchoring on the canonical genotype–phenotype associations when evaluating patients with suspected TBDs.

Fourth, this case illustrates that TBDs may render the bone marrow more susceptible to haematopoietic stress, such as severe nutritional deficiency. Telomere shortening compromises the replicative reserve of haematopoietic stem cells, reducing their capacity to respond to increased demand. In a patient with intact telomere biology, severe B12 deficiency would cause megaloblastic anaemia but would typically resolve fully with repletion. In this patient, the persistence of cytopenia-related concerns and the severity of her marrow dysfunction may reflect the interaction between an exogenous stressor and an intrinsically compromised stem cell compartment. This concept has practical implications: patients with known or suspected TBDs who develop superimposed nutritional, infectious, or inflammatory insults should be monitored closely, because their haematological recovery may be incomplete or delayed, and these episodes may unmask an underlying marrow failure phenotype.

In summary, this case underscores that the recognition of TBDs requires a high index of suspicion, a willingness to pursue genetic testing when multisystem disease lacks a unifying conventional explanation, and an appreciation that the phenotypic boundaries of individual telomere-related genes are still being defined. Early diagnosis does not always lead to immediate disease-modifying therapy, but it enables surveillance planning, genetic counselling, and the timely consideration of transplant or investigational therapies as the clinical picture evolves.
