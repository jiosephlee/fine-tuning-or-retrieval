Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Pathophysiology] How do uterine leiomyomas typically change in size across the trimesters of pregnancy and into the puerperium, and what does this natural history imply for the timing of conservative versus surgical management?

Uterine leiomyomas exhibit a well-characterized but non-linear growth pattern during pregnancy that has direct implications for management decisions. Data from large multicentric studies, as cited in this case, demonstrate that leiomyomas increase in size by approximately 6.90% per week during gestation, but this growth is front-loaded: the rate is 6.12% between the first and second trimesters (p<0.001) and slows to 3.10% ± 0.39% between the second and third trimesters (p<0.05). Critically, this trend reverses after the third trimester, with a significant mean size reduction of 44.25% ± 36.05% by six weeks postpartum (p<0.05).

This natural history underpins the standard recommendation for conservative management of leiomyomas diagnosed during pregnancy. Because the majority of myomas will substantially regress after delivery, surgical intervention during pregnancy carries risks—severe bleeding, pregnancy loss, preterm labor, and uterine rupture—without a clear long-term benefit in most cases. Myomectomy during pregnancy should therefore be reserved for specific complications: torsion of a pedunculated myoma, persistent severe pain refractory to conservative measures, suspicion of malignancy, or, as in this case, mass-related complications such as restrictive dyspnea from diaphragmatic compression and concern for impending rupture or infection of a twisted, leaking lesion.

The present case illustrates this principle precisely. The cranial pedunculated leiomyoma, found intraoperatively to be twisted with areas of ischaemic necrosis, justified surgical excision at 21 weeks + 6 days because of acute complications and the risk of further torsion or rupture as uterine enlargement continued. In contrast, the caudal intramural-subserosal myoma originating from the uterine torus was left in situ to minimize obstetric risk. This decision was vindicated by the postpartum course: the residual pelvic myoma partially regressed from approximately 9.5 cm to 7.5 cm, the patient remained asymptomatic, and she declined further surgery. The natural history of postpartum involution thus supports a strategy of expectant management whenever feasible, with surgical intervention timed to the postpartum period unless acute complications mandate earlier action.
