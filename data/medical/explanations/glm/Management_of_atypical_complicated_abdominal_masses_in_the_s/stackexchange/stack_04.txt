Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a pregnant patient with large cystic abdominal masses and a slightly elevated CA-125, ultrasound suggests two decidualized endometriomas. However, MRI suggests the caudal mass is in continuity with a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma. What features favor a uterine leiomyoma with cystic degeneration over an endometrioma, and why is this distinction clinically important during pregnancy?

## Clinical Teaching Answer

Several features favored a uterine leiomyoma with cystic degeneration over an endometrioma in this patient. The most critical clue was the **anatomical continuity** between the caudal mass and the posterior uterine wall, demonstrated on both ultrasound and MRI. On bimanual examination, this mass was described as fixed, rounded, and hard — characteristics more consistent with a myomatous lesion than a cystic endometrioma. MRI further showed thick walls (1.4 cm), inhomogeneous fluid content on T1 and T2 fat-sat sequences, and focal hyperintense solid components — imaging features that align with cystic degeneration within a leiomyoma rather than the homogeneous low-signal "ground-glass" appearance typical of an endometrioma on T1-weighted sequences. Peripheral vascularization and hypertrophied feeding vessels, as seen here, also support a vascularized uterine neoplasm over an endometriotic cyst.

The slight CA-125 elevation (41.8 U/mL) initially biased the differential toward endometrioma, but as the authors emphasize, CA-125 physiologically fluctuates during pregnancy and can be elevated in benign conditions including leiomyomas, endometriosis, and even normal first- and third-trimester pregnancy. A single value is therefore not diagnostic.

This distinction carries major clinical importance during pregnancy. Leiomyomas carry unique pregnancy-related risks — including **torsion of a pedunculated mass (as occurred with the cranial lesion in this case), red or cystic degeneration, necrosis, and infection** — that may necessitate urgent surgical intervention. Their location can also mechanically obstruct labor, as the caudal intramural-subserosal myoma situated at the uterine torus would have prevented fetal head descent, directly influencing the decision for elective cesarean delivery. Furthermore, leiomyomas may enlarge during pregnancy and regress postpartum, which affects both the urgency and timing of any intervention. In contrast, endometriomas are typically managed conservatively during pregnancy unless rupture or torsion of the ipsilateral ovary occurs. Accurate preoperative differentiation thus informs surgical timing, delivery mode, and counseling — and helps avoid both undertreatment of a complicated myoma and overtreatment of a benign adnexal cyst.
