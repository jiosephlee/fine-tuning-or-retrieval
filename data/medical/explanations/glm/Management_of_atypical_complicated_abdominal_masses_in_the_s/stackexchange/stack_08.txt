Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Clinical Pearls] After undergoing laparotomy and myomectomy at 21 weeks gestation, a pregnant patient develops worsening anemia (Hb 7.5 g/L) with iron deficiency. What is the appropriate postoperative hematologic management, and what tocolytic or progesterone prophylaxis was used in this case to reduce the risk of preterm labor?

## Postoperative Hematologic Management and Progesterone Prophylaxis After Myomectomy in Pregnancy

After laparotomy and myomectomy at 21 weeks' gestation, this patient developed significant postoperative anemia with a hemoglobin decline to 7.5 g/dL and hematocrit of 24.3%, accompanied by persistent iron deficiency (ferritin 25 mcg/L preoperatively). The appropriate management in this case combined **two units of packed red blood cell transfusion** with **intravenous iron repletion using ferric carboxymaltose 1 gram**, successfully raising the hemoglobin to 10.0 g/dL and hematocrit to 30.4%. This dual approach is clinically sound: blood transfusion provides immediate restoration of oxygen-carrying capacity in a symptomatic, severely anemic pregnant patient, while parenteral iron addresses the underlying iron deficiency and replenishes iron stores more reliably and rapidly than oral iron, which is often poorly tolerated in pregnancy and limited by gastrointestinal absorption. Intravenous ferric carboxymaltose is considered safe in the second and third trimesters and avoids the delays inherent in oral supplementation.

Regarding tocolytic and progesterone prophylaxis, this patient did not receive a conventional tocolytic agent (such as indomethacin or a calcium channel blocker). Instead, **progesterone prophylaxis** was used to reduce the risk of preterm labor. The regimen was initiated intraoperatively with **progesterone 100 mg intramuscularly**, continued daily at the same dose until discharge, and then transitioned to **17-hydroxyprogesterone caproate 341 mg intramuscularly every 3 days** until the time of elective cesarean delivery at 37 weeks. This approach reflects the rationale that progesterone plays a critical role in maintaining uterine quiescence during pregnancy and that surgical manipulation of the gravid uterus—particularly myomectomy—may increase the risk of uterine irritability and preterm contractions. The use of 17-hydroxyprogesterone caproate for ongoing prophylaxis after discharge is consistent with its historical role in reducing recurrent spontaneous preterm birth, although it is worth noting that its use has become more controversial following the 2019 PROLONG trial, which did not demonstrate a significant reduction in recurrent preterm birth. Nonetheless, in the context of postoperative uterine manipulation at 21 weeks, the multidisciplinary team elected to use this agent as prophylaxis.

The overall management strategy underscores several key principles: first, that significant intraoperative and postoperative blood loss in a pregnant patient warrants prompt transfusion combined with parenteral iron to address both acute and chronic deficiencies; and second, that progesterone-based prophylaxis—rather than traditional tocolysis—was selected in this case to maintain uterine quiescence following surgical intervention on the gravid uterus. The excellent maternal and neonatal outcome, with delivery at 37 weeks, supports the appropriateness of this approach in this clinical scenario.
