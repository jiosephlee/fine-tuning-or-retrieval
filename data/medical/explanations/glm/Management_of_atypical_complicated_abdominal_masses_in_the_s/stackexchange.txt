Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Clinical Pearls] A pregnant patient in her second trimester is found to have an adnexal mass on ultrasound. What imaging modalities are considered safe and appropriate for further characterization of the mass during pregnancy, and what specific MRI technique is generally avoided and why?

**Imaging of Adnexal Masses in Pregnancy: Safe Modalities and the Avoidance of Gadolinium**

When an adnexal mass is discovered during pregnancy, further characterization relies on two imaging modalities that are considered safe in this setting: **ultrasound with color Doppler** and **non-contrast MRI**.

Ultrasound remains the first-line imaging tool. It is noninvasive, widely available, and free of ionizing radiation, making it ideal for initial evaluation and serial monitoring. As illustrated in the present case, ultrasound with color Doppler effectively identified two large cystic masses with regular margins and peripheral vascularisation, and was particularly valuable in demonstrating the relationship between the caudal mass and an underlying posterior intramural-subserosal leiomyoma with cystic degeneration.

When ultrasound is inconclusive—or when the size, number, or anatomical location of the masses requires more precise delineation—**non-contrast MRI** offers superior diagnostic accuracy. MRI is especially useful for characterizing multiple, large, intramural, or posterior leiomyomas, as it can precisely determine the distance between a myoma and the uterine cavity. In this case, non-contrast MRI clarified the thick walls, inhomogeneous fluid content, and focal solid components of both masses and confirmed the absence of adenopathy. The authors note that MRI scanners operating at 1.5 T or less are considered reasonably safe after the first trimester.

The MRI technique that is **generally avoided during pregnancy is gadolinium-enhanced MRI**. Gadolinium-based contrast agents cross the placenta and enter the fetal circulation, where they are excreted into the amniotic fluid and subsequently re-swallowed by the fetus, prolonging fetal exposure. Epidemiologic studies have associated gadolinium administration during pregnancy with an increased risk of rheumatological, inflammatory, or infiltrative skin conditions in the child, as well as an increased risk of stillbirth and neonatal death. For these reasons, gadolinium-enhanced MRI should not be performed during pregnancy unless strictly necessary—meaning that the potential diagnostic benefit clearly outweighs these risks and no alternative modality can provide the needed information.

In summary, the approach to characterizing an adnexal mass in pregnancy follows a stepwise, conservative imaging strategy: **ultrasound with color Doppler first, followed by non-contrast MRI at 1.5 T or less if further anatomical or tissue-level detail is required**, with **gadolinium-based contrast avoided** due to fetal safety concerns. This framework, combined with careful multidisciplinary assessment and serial clinical follow-up, allows for accurate diagnosis while minimizing risk to both mother and fetus.

### [Morning Report] A 40-year-old woman at 21 weeks gestation presents with acute left upper quadrant abdominal pain, dyspnea, and a palpable 18 cm abdominal mass. She also has a 9 cm fixed pelvic mass on bimanual examination. What should be included in the initial diagnostic approach, and what findings on ultrasound and MRI would help distinguish between decidualized endometriomas and cystic degeneration of uterine leiomyomas?

In this pregnant patient presenting with acute abdominal pain, dyspnea, and two large masses — one subphrenic and one pelvic — the initial diagnostic approach should combine **bimanual pelvic examination**, **ultrasound with color Doppler**, **non-contrast MRI**, and **serial serum tumor markers**, interpreted within the physiologic context of pregnancy.

Ultrasound demonstrated two large, unilocular cystic masses with regular margins and peripheral vascularization. The caudal mass appeared adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma showing cystic degeneration, suggesting continuity with uterine tissue — a feature favoring a myomatous origin. However, for the upper mass, no connecting stalk or feeding vessel could be identified due to the gravid uterus obscuring anatomical relationships, and this is precisely where imaging limitations become clinically relevant.

Non-contrast MRI added critical detail: the masses displayed thick walls (1.4 cm), inhomogeneous fluid content on T1 and T2 fat-saturated sequences, and focal solid components that were hyperintense on both T1 and T2. The caudal mass appeared in continuity with the posterior uterine leiomyoma, supporting cystic degeneration of a myoma rather than an adnexal lesion. Had decidualized endometriomas been present, one would expect T1-hyperintense (hemorrhagic) content with shading on T2 and no continuity with myometrial tissue.

The key distinguishing principle is anatomical: **continuity with uterine tissue on MRI strongly favors leiomyoma with cystic degeneration**, while a discrete adnexal location with hemorrhagic content supports an endometrioma. The slightly elevated CA-125 (41.8 U/mL) initially biased the differential toward endometriosis, but as this case illustrates, CA-125 is unreliable in isolation during pregnancy, as it may rise physiologically and in benign conditions including myomas. Serial trends, rather than single values, are more meaningful.

This case powerfully demonstrates that despite thorough imaging, large pelvic masses in pregnancy can be mischaracterized preoperatively. The upper mass — initially presumed to be an endometrioma — proved at laparotomy to be a twisted, pedunculated cystic leiomyoma. When clinical deterioration (worsening pain, dyspnea, fever) signals complication risk — torsion, rupture, or infection — surgical exploration may become necessary even without definitive preoperative diagnosis, always with the goal of preserving the pregnancy.

### [Pathophysiology] How does serum CA-125 typically fluctuate throughout the trimesters of an uncomplicated pregnancy, and what benign gynecologic conditions besides ovarian cancer can cause an elevated CA-125? In the presented case, the CA-125 was 41.8 U/mL during the second trimester — how should this be interpreted?

**Serum CA-125 in Pregnancy and Interpretation of the Case Value**

CA-125 is a glycoprotein antigen expressed by tissues of coelomic and Müllerian origin, and its serum levels are influenced by a variety of physiologic and pathologic processes beyond ovarian malignancy. In an uncomplicated pregnancy, CA-125 characteristically exhibits two physiologic peaks: one during the first trimester (median approximately 23 U/mL) and another during the third trimester (median approximately 21 U/mL). The second trimester is associated with the lowest values (median approximately 14 U/mL), making elevations during this period more noteworthy — though still not diagnostic of malignancy.

Numerous benign gynecologic and non-gynecologic conditions can elevate CA-125. These include endometriosis and adenomyosis (often with levels above 19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, early pregnancy, near-term pregnancy, risk of miscarriage, and uterine leiomyomas. Non-gynecologic causes such as peritoneal inflammation, hepatic disease with ascites, pleural effusion, and even benign peritoneal irritation can also raise levels. Thus, CA-125 lacks specificity, particularly in reproductive-age women and during pregnancy, where the physiologic milieu itself can alter marker expression.

In the presented case, the CA-125 of 41.8 U/mL — measured at 21 weeks of gestation — exceeded both the standard non-pregnant cutoff of 35 U/mL and the expected second-trimester median of approximately 14 U/mL. Furthermore, serial measurement showed a rising trend from 36 U/mL two weeks prior. This mild but progressive elevation initially oriented the differential diagnosis toward a decidualized endometrioma, a known benign cause of CA-125 elevation. However, the ultimate surgical pathology revealed benign uterine leiomyomas with cystic degeneration and ischemic necrosis near a twisted pedicle — another benign process capable of producing CA-125 release, likely via peritoneal irritation from the degenerating, leaking myoma.

The critical teaching point is that an isolated or mildly elevated CA-125 in pregnancy — even when it exceeds 35 U/mL — must never be interpreted in isolation. As the authors emphasize, serial measurements providing trend data are more informative than single values, and clinical decision-making must integrate imaging characteristics, symptomatology, and risk of mechanical or ischemic complications. In this case, the slightly elevated and rising CA-125 contributed to the multidisciplinary decision to proceed with surgical exploration, but the driving indications were the large, symptomatic, and potentially complicated masses — not the marker value alone. The benign final pathology underscores the limited specificity of CA-125 in pregnancy and reinforces the principle that elevated tumor markers in gravid patients warrant cautious, contextual interpretation rather than automatic assumption of malignancy.

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a pregnant patient with large cystic abdominal masses and a slightly elevated CA-125, ultrasound suggests two decidualized endometriomas. However, MRI suggests the caudal mass is in continuity with a posterior intramural-subserosal uterine myoma. What features favor a uterine leiomyoma with cystic degeneration over an endometrioma, and why is this distinction clinically important during pregnancy?

## Clinical Teaching Answer

Several features favored a uterine leiomyoma with cystic degeneration over an endometrioma in this patient. The most critical clue was the **anatomical continuity** between the caudal mass and the posterior uterine wall, demonstrated on both ultrasound and MRI. On bimanual examination, this mass was described as fixed, rounded, and hard — characteristics more consistent with a myomatous lesion than a cystic endometrioma. MRI further showed thick walls (1.4 cm), inhomogeneous fluid content on T1 and T2 fat-sat sequences, and focal hyperintense solid components — imaging features that align with cystic degeneration within a leiomyoma rather than the homogeneous low-signal "ground-glass" appearance typical of an endometrioma on T1-weighted sequences. Peripheral vascularization and hypertrophied feeding vessels, as seen here, also support a vascularized uterine neoplasm over an endometriotic cyst.

The slight CA-125 elevation (41.8 U/mL) initially biased the differential toward endometrioma, but as the authors emphasize, CA-125 physiologically fluctuates during pregnancy and can be elevated in benign conditions including leiomyomas, endometriosis, and even normal first- and third-trimester pregnancy. A single value is therefore not diagnostic.

This distinction carries major clinical importance during pregnancy. Leiomyomas carry unique pregnancy-related risks — including **torsion of a pedunculated mass (as occurred with the cranial lesion in this case), red or cystic degeneration, necrosis, and infection** — that may necessitate urgent surgical intervention. Their location can also mechanically obstruct labor, as the caudal intramural-subserosal myoma situated at the uterine torus would have prevented fetal head descent, directly influencing the decision for elective cesarean delivery. Furthermore, leiomyomas may enlarge during pregnancy and regress postpartum, which affects both the urgency and timing of any intervention. In contrast, endometriomas are typically managed conservatively during pregnancy unless rupture or torsion of the ipsilateral ovary occurs. Accurate preoperative differentiation thus informs surgical timing, delivery mode, and counseling — and helps avoid both undertreatment of a complicated myoma and overtreatment of a benign adnexal cyst.

### [Management] A pregnant patient at 21 weeks gestation has a large, symptomatic pedunculated uterine leiomyoma causing worsening pain, dyspnea from mass effect, and intermittent low-grade fever. What are the indications for surgical intervention (myomectomy) for uterine leiomyomas during pregnancy, and what intraoperative measures should be taken to preserve the pregnancy?

# Indications and Intraoperative Considerations for Myomectomy During Pregnancy

## Indications for Surgical Intervention

Uterine leiomyomas are common in pregnancy, with an incidence of 0.3–10.1%, and the overwhelming majority are managed expectantly. Myomectomy during pregnancy is reserved for well-defined clinical scenarios, as surgical intervention carries inherent risks of severe hemorrhage, pregnancy loss, preterm labor, and uterine rupture. The threshold for operative management should be driven by specific clinical indications, not by the mere presence of a leiomyoma.

As illustrated in this case of a nulliparous woman at 21 weeks' gestation, the key indications for surgical intervention are:

1. **Refractory or progressive symptomatic mass effect.** Despite conservative management with analgesia and positional maneuvers, the patient's abdominal pain worsened, and she developed restrictive dyspnea from mechanical compression by an 18 cm cranial mass. Failure of conservative measures — not the size of the myoma alone — established the need for intervention.

2. **Impending or actual complications of a pedunculated myoma.** At laparotomy, the cranial mass was found to be a pedunculated leiomyoma that had undergone torsion with leakage, explaining the patient's intermittent low-grade fever. Torsion of a pedunculated myoma, with its attendant risk of ischemic necrosis, infarction, and peritonitis, is among the clearest indications for surgical removal during pregnancy. The pathological examination ultimately confirmed areas of ischaemic necrosis near the twisted pedicle, validating the decision to operate.

3. **Clinical complications: rupture, torsion, or infection.** The authors explicitly identify these as indications for surgical consideration. In this case, the risk of torsion had already materialized, and the leaking mass posed an ongoing infectious risk.

4. **Diagnostic uncertainty with oncologic concern.** The slight CA-125 elevation (41.8 U/mL, exceeding the normal <35 U/mL threshold and higher than the expected physiologic second-trimester median of ~14 U/mL), combined with imaging features of large mixed solid-cystic masses, necessitated a conclusive histologic diagnosis. The authors note that surgical management should be considered in the setting of oncological suspicion or clinical complications, always aiming to preserve pregnancy and avoid overtreatment.

5. **Anticipated compromise of uterine enlargement.** The prospect of continued uterine and myoma growth — leiomyomas increase by approximately 6.12% between the first and second trimesters and 3.10% between the second and third — threatened further mass effect and potential degeneration, necrosis, or rupture, supporting the decision to intervene at 21 weeks + 6 days rather than delay.

Notably, the caudal 9.5 cm mass, which was a posterior intramural-subserosal leiomyoma with cystic degeneration, was deliberately left in situ. This illustrates an essential principle: only the mass causing complications or posing an immediate threat should be removed. The intramural location of the caudal myoma would have carried a substantially higher risk of hemorrhage and uterine rupture, and its removal was not clinically justified at that time.

## Intraoperative Measures to Preserve the Pregnancy

Once the decision to operate is made, several intraoperative strategies are critical to minimize obstetric risk:

**Fetal monitoring.** Fetal cardiac activity should be verified at the start of the procedure and documented before, during, and after surgery, as emphasized by Zhao et al. In this case, the surgical team verified fetal cardiac activity immediately upon entering the abdomen and employed serial and intermittent transvaginal ultrasound monitoring throughout the operation.

**Tocolytic prophylaxis.** Progesterone prophylaxis was initiated intraoperatively (100 mg intramuscularly) and continued daily through discharge, followed by hydroxyprogesterone caproate (341 mg IM every 3 days) until delivery. This reflects the principle that uterine quiescence should be pharmacologically supported from the intraoperative period onward.

**Minimizing operative time and uterine manipulation.** The authors stress that surgery must be as short as possible to preserve the pregnancy. In this case, the procedure lasted 2 hours 25 minutes — relatively brief given the complexity. The surgeon used a double clamp technique to gently excise the pedunculated mass from its uterine implant, secured the stalk with a single absorbable suture at the exposed root, and avoided any manipulation of the gravid uterus itself. No incision into the uterine wall was required because the myoma was pedunculated, which significantly reduced the risk of hemorrhage, uterine rupture, and pregnancy loss.

**Limiting the extent of resection.** The decision to remove only the torsed, symptomatic pedunculated myoma while leaving the caudal intramural-subserosal myoma in situ was deliberate. This conservative surgical philosophy minimizes obstetric risk and is consistent with the principle that only the complication-driving lesion warrants intervention. The residual myoma was managed expectantly and ultimately required only cesarean delivery planning due to its potential to obstruct labor.

**Antibiotic prophylaxis and infection control.** Intraoperative cefazolin was administered, followed by postoperative antibiotics tailored to the patient's Klebsiella pneumoniae urinary tract infection, which had been identified preoperatively. This underscores the importance of screening for and treating concurrent infections that could threaten pregnancy viability in the perioperative period.

**Blood loss anticipation and management.** Although the pedunculated approach minimized bleeding, the patient still developed significant postoperative anemia (hemoglobin 7.5 g/L), requiring two transfusions and intravenous iron supplementation. This highlights that even seemingly straightforward myomectomies during pregnancy carry substantial hemorrhagic risk, and blood products should be available and anticipated.

## Key Teaching Point

Myomectomy during pregnancy is not a routine procedure — it is a carefully justified intervention reserved for pedunculated myomas with torsion, refractory symptomatic mass effect, or diagnostic uncertainty with oncologic concern. The decision to operate should always follow a failed trial of conservative management and multidisciplinary consensus. When surgery is indicated, the operative strategy should prioritize the shortest possible duration, removal of only the complicating lesion, continuous fetal monitoring, progesterone prophylaxis, and meticulous hemostasis — precisely the approach that yielded an excellent maternal and neonatal outcome in this case, with elective cesarean delivery at 37 weeks.

### [Pathophysiology] How do uterine leiomyomas typically change in size across the trimesters of pregnancy and into the puerperium, and what does this natural history imply for the timing of conservative versus surgical management?

Uterine leiomyomas exhibit a well-characterized but non-linear growth pattern during pregnancy that has direct implications for management decisions. Data from large multicentric studies, as cited in this case, demonstrate that leiomyomas increase in size by approximately 6.90% per week during gestation, but this growth is front-loaded: the rate is 6.12% between the first and second trimesters (p<0.001) and slows to 3.10% ± 0.39% between the second and third trimesters (p<0.05). Critically, this trend reverses after the third trimester, with a significant mean size reduction of 44.25% ± 36.05% by six weeks postpartum (p<0.05).

This natural history underpins the standard recommendation for conservative management of leiomyomas diagnosed during pregnancy. Because the majority of myomas will substantially regress after delivery, surgical intervention during pregnancy carries risks—severe bleeding, pregnancy loss, preterm labor, and uterine rupture—without a clear long-term benefit in most cases. Myomectomy during pregnancy should therefore be reserved for specific complications: torsion of a pedunculated myoma, persistent severe pain refractory to conservative measures, suspicion of malignancy, or, as in this case, mass-related complications such as restrictive dyspnea from diaphragmatic compression and concern for impending rupture or infection of a twisted, leaking lesion.

The present case illustrates this principle precisely. The cranial pedunculated leiomyoma, found intraoperatively to be twisted with areas of ischaemic necrosis, justified surgical excision at 21 weeks + 6 days because of acute complications and the risk of further torsion or rupture as uterine enlargement continued. In contrast, the caudal intramural-subserosal myoma originating from the uterine torus was left in situ to minimize obstetric risk. This decision was vindicated by the postpartum course: the residual pelvic myoma partially regressed from approximately 9.5 cm to 7.5 cm, the patient remained asymptomatic, and she declined further surgery. The natural history of postpartum involution thus supports a strategy of expectant management whenever feasible, with surgical intervention timed to the postpartum period unless acute complications mandate earlier action.

### [Management] A pregnant patient has a 9.5 cm posterior intramural-subserosal uterine leiomyoma in the pouch of Douglas that was managed conservatively and left in situ during a second-trimester myomectomy of a separate pedunculated fibroid. What factors should influence the decision regarding the mode and timing of delivery in a patient with a residual pelvic uterine myoma?

## Clinical Teaching Answer

In a pregnant patient with a residual pelvic uterine myoma, the decision regarding mode and timing of delivery should be guided by three principal factors: **the size, number, and anatomical location of the myoma** — specifically, whether it will mechanically obstruct labor — balanced against the risk of uterine rupture at the prior myomectomy scar.

**Mode of delivery** is determined chiefly by the relationship of the residual myoma to the birth canal. In this case, the 9.5 cm posterior intramural-subserosal leiomyoma occupying the pouch of Douglas was adherent to the posterior uterine wall at the isthmic site. As the authors note, this lesion "would have represented a huge obstacle for the fetal head progression during the expulsive phase of labour," functioning as a fixed pelvic mass that could impede descent and rotation. For this reason, an elective cesarean delivery was selected rather than a trial of labor. More broadly, anterior or fundal myomas that do not encroach on the lower uterine segment or cervix may not preclude vaginal delivery, whereas posterior, lower-segment, or cervical myomas — particularly those of large diameter — are more likely to cause mechanical dystocia and warrant cesarean delivery.

**Timing of delivery** must account for both fetal maturity and the integrity of the uterine wall following antepartum myomectomy. In this patient, the pedunculated fundal leiomyoma had been excised at 21 weeks + 6 days via longitudinal laparotomy, leaving a uterine scar. To minimize the risk of scar rupture while ensuring fetal maturity, the team performed the elective cesarean at 37 weeks' gestation — the earliest acceptable full-term threshold — using the same surgical incision. This approach reflects the general principle that delivery timing should balance the increasing risk of uterine rupture or dehiscence with advancing gestation against the benefits of achieving term gestational age. The case report also references the inverse correlation between myoma size and delivery week, suggesting that larger or more numerous myomas may necessitate earlier delivery planning.

Additional considerations include the potential for myoma growth during pregnancy — leiomyomas increase in size by approximately 6–7% per week, with continued growth into the third trimester — and the partial postpartum regression that may occur, which the authors observed as the residual myoma decreased from 9.5 cm to 7.5 cm. These dynamics underscore the value of serial ultrasound surveillance throughout pregnancy to reassess myoma size and location and to refine the delivery plan accordingly. In summary, the mode and timing of delivery in such patients should be individualized through multidisciplinary assessment, integrating serial imaging of myoma characteristics with evaluation of prior uterine scars and the mechanical relationship of the mass to the pelvic birth canal.

### [Clinical Pearls] After undergoing laparotomy and myomectomy at 21 weeks gestation, a pregnant patient develops worsening anemia (Hb 7.5 g/L) with iron deficiency. What is the appropriate postoperative hematologic management, and what tocolytic or progesterone prophylaxis was used in this case to reduce the risk of preterm labor?

## Postoperative Hematologic Management and Progesterone Prophylaxis After Myomectomy in Pregnancy

After laparotomy and myomectomy at 21 weeks' gestation, this patient developed significant postoperative anemia with a hemoglobin decline to 7.5 g/dL and hematocrit of 24.3%, accompanied by persistent iron deficiency (ferritin 25 mcg/L preoperatively). The appropriate management in this case combined **two units of packed red blood cell transfusion** with **intravenous iron repletion using ferric carboxymaltose 1 gram**, successfully raising the hemoglobin to 10.0 g/dL and hematocrit to 30.4%. This dual approach is clinically sound: blood transfusion provides immediate restoration of oxygen-carrying capacity in a symptomatic, severely anemic pregnant patient, while parenteral iron addresses the underlying iron deficiency and replenishes iron stores more reliably and rapidly than oral iron, which is often poorly tolerated in pregnancy and limited by gastrointestinal absorption. Intravenous ferric carboxymaltose is considered safe in the second and third trimesters and avoids the delays inherent in oral supplementation.

Regarding tocolytic and progesterone prophylaxis, this patient did not receive a conventional tocolytic agent (such as indomethacin or a calcium channel blocker). Instead, **progesterone prophylaxis** was used to reduce the risk of preterm labor. The regimen was initiated intraoperatively with **progesterone 100 mg intramuscularly**, continued daily at the same dose until discharge, and then transitioned to **17-hydroxyprogesterone caproate 341 mg intramuscularly every 3 days** until the time of elective cesarean delivery at 37 weeks. This approach reflects the rationale that progesterone plays a critical role in maintaining uterine quiescence during pregnancy and that surgical manipulation of the gravid uterus—particularly myomectomy—may increase the risk of uterine irritability and preterm contractions. The use of 17-hydroxyprogesterone caproate for ongoing prophylaxis after discharge is consistent with its historical role in reducing recurrent spontaneous preterm birth, although it is worth noting that its use has become more controversial following the 2019 PROLONG trial, which did not demonstrate a significant reduction in recurrent preterm birth. Nonetheless, in the context of postoperative uterine manipulation at 21 weeks, the multidisciplinary team elected to use this agent as prophylaxis.

The overall management strategy underscores several key principles: first, that significant intraoperative and postoperative blood loss in a pregnant patient warrants prompt transfusion combined with parenteral iron to address both acute and chronic deficiencies; and second, that progesterone-based prophylaxis—rather than traditional tocolysis—was selected in this case to maintain uterine quiescence following surgical intervention on the gravid uterus. The excellent maternal and neonatal outcome, with delivery at 37 weeks, supports the appropriateness of this approach in this clinical scenario.
