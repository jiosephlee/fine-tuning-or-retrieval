Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# The Great Mimicker: When Uterine Fibroids Disguise as Adnexal Masses in Pregnancy

## The Setup: A Pregnant Abdomen, Two Giant Masses, and a Diagnosis That Was Completely Wrong

Picture this: a woman in her 40s, first pregnancy, 21 weeks along, BMI of 33, walks into the emergency department with severe upper left quadrant and pelvic pain rated 5/10 on the VAS, plus mild dyspnea. Her abdomen is markedly more enlarged than expected for gestational age. On bimanual exam, there's a fixed, hard, 9 cm mass in the pouch of Douglas. Ultrasound reveals two giant unilocular cystic masses — one 18×12×7 cm extending from the left subphrenic space to the uterine fundus, another 9.5×8×8 cm in the pelvis. CA-125 is mildly elevated at 41.8 U/mL. The multidisciplinary team convenes, reviews the imaging, and concludes these are most likely decidualised endometriomas — with the caudal one possibly adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal fibroid with cystic degeneration.

They were half right. And the half they got wrong nearly changed everything.

## What Imaging Couldn't See — and Why It Mattered

The critical diagnostic failure here wasn't negligence — it was physics. The gravid uterus at 21 weeks creates what the authors describe as "steric bulk," a space-occupying wall of tissue that physically obscured the connecting stalk and feeding vessels of the upper mass. Without visualization of a pedicle or tumour-feeding vessel, the imaging — both ultrasound and non-contrast MRI — naturally oriented toward an ovarian origin. The upper mass sat in the left subphrenic region. It was cystic. CA-125 was trending upward. Endometrioma seemed a reasonable working diagnosis.

But here's the trap: pedunculated uterine leiomyomas can look exactly like adnexal cystic masses on imaging, particularly when they undergo cystic degeneration. This patient had two fibroids, both degenerating, both large enough to distort regional anatomy. The upper one had outgrown its blood supply, twisted on its pedicle, and was leaking — a fact that no preoperative imaging could establish because the stalk itself was invisible behind the pregnant uterus.

The MRI, performed without gadolinium (appropriately, given pregnancy), showed thick walls measuring 1.4 cm, inhomogeneous fluid content on T1 and T2 fat-sat sequences, and focal hyperintense solid components. These are features compatible with both decidualised endometriomas and degenerating leiomyomas. The caudal mass appeared to be in continuity with a posterior uterine myoma, which correctly suggested myomatous origin for that lesion. But the cranial mass? No stalk, no feeding vessel, no clear connection to the uterus. The diagnosis went astray.

## CA-125 in Pregnancy: A Number Without Context

The slightly elevated CA-125 (41.8 U/mL, with a rising trend from 36 U/mL two weeks prior) deserves scrutiny. CA-125 physiologically peaks during the first trimester (median ~23 U/mL) and again in the third trimester (median ~21 U/mL), with second-trimester values typically lower (median ~14 U/mL). This patient's value was significantly above the expected second-trimester median, which supported the endometrioma hypothesis.

But here's the thing: CA-125 elevation in pregnancy is nonspecific. It rises with endometriosis, adenomyosis, pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, risk of miscarriage, and — yes — even fibroids. A single value, or even a trend, cannot reliably distinguish between these entities. The authors make the important point that isolated measurements are not diagnostic. Serial trends can be informative, but in this case, the trend simply added confidence to a wrong answer.

The lesson: in pregnancy, CA-125 should never be the deciding factor for surgical intervention. The decision to operate must rest on clinical severity, risk of complications, and oncologic suspicion — not on a number that is physiologically volatile and diagnostically ambiguous in the gravid patient.

## The Intraoperative Plot Twist

At 21 weeks + 6 days, the team performed a longitudinal xiphos-pubic laparotomy. After confirming fetal cardiac activity, they entered the abdomen and found something neither ultrasound nor MRI had predicted: the cranial mass was a pedunculated uterine leiomyoma, twisted on its stalk and leaking. This was the source of the patient's low-grade fever during the hospital stay — not the Klebsiella urinary tract infection, which may have been a coincidental finding. The twisted, ischemic pedicle with areas of necrosis on pathology confirmed the diagnosis.

The surgical plan adapted immediately. They excised the upper pedunculated fibroid using a double clamp technique, dividing the stalk and closing the uterine root with a single absorbable suture. The caudal mass — an intramural-subserosal leiomyoma originating from the uterine torus — was left in situ to minimize obstetric risk. This was the right call. Removing an intramural myoma from the posterior uterine wall at 21 weeks carries a substantial risk of severe bleeding, pregnancy loss, and preterm labour. The pedunculated one, however, had to go — it had already torsed, and leaving a necrotic, leaking mass in the abdomen would have been far more dangerous.

## The Postoperative Course: What Followed

The surgery lasted 2 hours and 25 minutes. The patient received intraoperative progesterone prophylaxis (100 mg IM), continued as daily injections until discharge, followed by hydroxyprogesterone caproate 341 mg IM every 3 days until delivery. Postoperatively, her hemoglobin dropped to 7.5 g/dL, requiring two blood transfusions and intravenous iron supplementation. She was discharged on postoperative day 10.

The peritoneal fluid cytology and cultures were negative. Pathology confirmed benign uterine leiomyomas with ischaemic necrosis near the twisted pedicle. The residual pelvic myoma measured 7.5 cm and was positioned at the uterine torus — which would have obstructed fetal head descent during vaginal labour. An elective caesarean was performed at 37 weeks through the same surgical scar. Excellent neonatal outcome. The patient is breastfeeding, asymptomatic, and has declined further surgical management given partial postpartum regression of the residual fibroid.

## Why This Case Matters

This case illustrates several principles that should change how we think about abdominal masses in pregnancy:

- **Fibroids are the great mimicker.** A pedunculated leiomyoma with cystic degeneration can be indistinguishable from an adnexal mass on ultrasound and non-contrast MRI, especially when the gravid uterus obscures the connecting stalk. Always keep fibroids on the differential for any cystic-appearing mass in pregnancy, even when it's located far from the uterus.

- **Imaging has hard limits in pregnancy.** The "steric bulk" of the enlarging uterus can hide critical anatomy — feeding vessels, stalks, connections to uterine tissue. Non-contrast MRI is the best available modality, but it is not omniscient. When imaging cannot identify a stalk or feeding vessel, the clinician must maintain diagnostic humility and keep both ovarian and uterine etiologies on the table.

- **CA-125 is noise in pregnancy.** It physiologically fluctuates, rises with numerous benign conditions, and cannot reliably differentiate endometriomas from degenerating fibroids. Do not anchor on it.

- **Surgical decision-making should be driven by complications, not diagnosis alone.** The indications for surgery here were sound: worsening pain, dyspnea from mass effect, risk of rupture or torsion, and the prospect of continued uterine growth compressing the masses further. The fact that the preoperative diagnosis was wrong did not change the fact that this patient needed an operation.

- **When you operate, do the minimum that addresses the danger.** The team removed the torsed, leaking pedunculated fibroid and left the stable intramural one alone. This preserved the pregnancy and avoided the higher-risk dissection. The patient subsequently delivered at term with an excellent outcome.

- **Delivery mode should be individualized based on residual mass location.** The caudal myoma at the uterine torus would have obstructed labour, making elective caesarean the correct and evidence-based choice.

## The Bottom Line

When you encounter large cystic abdominal masses in pregnancy, resist the urge to anchor on a single diagnosis. Pedunculated fibroids can and do mimic ovarian cysts, endometriomas, and other adnexal pathology — and the enlarging uterus can hide the very features that would distinguish them. Use ultrasound and non-contrast MRI to characterize what you can, but recognize their limitations. Let clinical severity and complication risk — not tumor markers or imaging alone — drive the decision to operate. And when you do operate, confirm fetal viability throughout, use progesterone prophylaxis, and remove only what threatens the patient or the pregnancy. This patient's excellent outcome wasn't the result of getting the diagnosis right preoperatively — it was the result of making sound clinical decisions in the face of diagnostic uncertainty.



# CA-125 in Pregnancy: A Tale of False Positives and Physiological Peaks

## The Setup

A 40-year-old nulliparous woman, BMI 33, presents at 21 weeks gestation with severe abdominal pain and mild dyspnoea. Ultrasound reveals two large cystic abdominal masses — one 18 cm, the other 9.5 cm. Tumour markers? All normal except CA-125, which sits at 41.8 U/mL, up from 36 U/mL two weeks prior. The team anchors on that mildly elevated CA-125 and the imaging appearance, leaning toward a diagnosis of decidualised endometriomas. At laparotomy, the surprise: both masses are benign cystic leiomyomas — one pedunculated, twisted, and leaking, the other an intramural-subserosal myoma with cystic degeneration. No endometrioma. No malignancy. The CA-125 elevation? A physiological red herring amplified by the presence of myomas themselves.

## CA-125 in Pregnancy: What's Normal?

Here's where it gets interesting. CA-125 doesn't hold steady during pregnancy — it has a bimodal distribution. The median value peaks in the first trimester at approximately 23 U/mL, dips to a median of 14 U/mL in the second trimester, and rises again in the third trimester to a median of 21 U/mL. This patient's value of 41.8 U/mL at 21 weeks was clearly above the second-trimester median, which understandably raised concern. But "above median" and "above the oncologic cut-off of 35 U/mL" are two very different clinical thresholds, and in a pregnant patient, the standard cut-off has limited discriminatory value.

## The Benign Differential for an Elevated CA-125 Is Vast

The problem with CA-125 is that it is not an ovarian cancer marker — it is a peritoneal mesothelial reactivity marker. Anything that irritates the peritoneum can elevate it. The list of benign conditions associated with elevated CA-125 is long: endometriosis and adenomyosis (>19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, early pregnancy, near-term pregnancy, and yes — even uterine leiomyomas. In this case, the patient had not one but two large degenerating myomas, one of which was torsed and leaking. That alone is more than enough to explain a CA-125 of 41.8 U/mL. The team's initial diagnostic lean toward endometrioma was understandable given the imaging appearance and the marker, but it illustrates a classic cognitive trap: anchoring on a single non-specific data point in a patient with low pretest probability of malignancy.

## Serial Measurements: Trend Over Threshold

The authors make an important point that deserves emphasis: isolated CA-125 measurements during pregnancy are essentially uninterpretable. Serial measurements, on the other hand, can provide useful trend information for differential diagnosis and follow-up. In this case, the trend from 36 to 41.8 U/mL over two weeks was interpreted as concerning, but this is a narrow window and the magnitude of change is small. A steeply rising serial CA-125 with escalating clinical symptoms might warrant more aggressive investigation, but a mildly elevated value with a slow upward drift in a pregnant patient with known large pelvic masses is well within the range of expected physiological and benign-pathology variation.

## Imaging Should Lead, Not Tumour Markers

The diagnostic backbone in this case was ultrasound followed by non-contrast MRI. Ultrasound identified the two cystic masses, their vascularisation, and the caudal mass's continuity with a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma. MRI without gadolinium — appropriately avoided in pregnancy — provided superior soft tissue characterisation, showing thick walls, inhomogeneous fluid content, and the relationship of the caudal mass to the uterine torus. Notably, MRI could not definitively identify a connecting stalk or feeding vessel for the cranial mass due to the sheer bulk of the gravid uterus, which is why the diagnosis of pedunculated leiomyoma was only confirmed intraoperatively. The CA-125 value added noise, not signal, to the diagnostic process.

## When to Operate During Pregnancy

The decision to proceed with laparotomy at 21+6 weeks was driven by clinical deterioration — worsening pain, dyspnoea from mass effect, and the risk of complications including torsion, rupture, and infection — not by the CA-125 value. The surgical team appropriately limited the intervention to the cranial mass, which had a twisted pedicle and was actively leaking, while leaving the caudal myoma in situ to minimise obstetric risk. Intraoperative fetal heart rate monitoring and prophylactic progesterone were used. The caudal mass, fixed in the pelvis, ultimately dictated the mode of delivery — elective caesarean at 37 weeks through the same surgical scar. The lesson: in pregnancy, the indication for surgery should be complications or oncologic suspicion, not an isolated mildly elevated marker.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **CA-125 has physiological peaks in the first and third trimesters** (medians ~23 and ~21 U/mL respectively) and a nadir in the second trimester (~14 U/mL). A value of 41.8 U/mL at 21 weeks is elevated relative to the second-trimester median but remains in benign-disease territory.
- **The standard oncologic cut-off of ≥35 U/mL has poor discriminatory value in pregnancy.** Benign conditions including myomas, endometriosis, adenomyosis, and pregnancy itself can elevate CA-125 above this threshold.
- **Serial measurements over time are more informative than isolated values**, but trends must be interpreted in clinical context — a slow drift upward in a patient with large degenerating myomas is expected, not alarming.
- **Anchoring on a single tumour marker in a pregnant patient with low pretest probability of malignancy can bias diagnostic reasoning.** In this case, it steered the team toward endometrioma when the reality was cystic degenerating leiomyomas.
- **Imaging — ultrasound first, non-contrast MRI when needed — should drive the diagnostic process**, not tumour markers. MRI without gadolinium is safe after the first trimester and provides critical anatomical detail.
- **Surgery during pregnancy should be reserved for complications or genuine oncologic concern**, not for diagnostic certainty driven by marker anxiety. When operating, limit the intervention to what is necessary and preserve the pregnancy.

## The Bottom Line

This case is a textbook reminder that CA-125 is a mesothelial reactivity marker, not an ovarian cancer marker — and in pregnancy, the peritoneum is reactive by default. Two large degenerating myomas can easily produce a CA-125 of 41.8 U/mL, and the decision to operate should rest on clinical deterioration and imaging findings, not on a number that was never designed to carry the diagnostic weight we sometimes assign it.



# Scalpel vs. Patience: Surgical Decision-Making for Symptomatic Pelvic Masses in Pregnancy

## The Setup That Fooled Everyone

Picture this: a nulliparous woman in her 40s, 21 weeks pregnant, walks into the ER with severe upper left quadrant and pelvic pain, mild dyspnea, and an abdomen that's visibly larger than expected for gestational age. Ultrasound shows two huge cystic masses — one 18 cm stretching from the left subphrenic space to the uterine fundus, another 9.5 cm filling the pouch of Douglas. CA-125 is mildly elevated at 41.8 U/mL. The imaging looks like decidualised endometriomas. Everyone is thinking ovarian pathology.

At laparotomy, the cranial mass turns out to be a **twisted, leaking pedunculated uterine leiomyoma** — not an ovarian cyst at all. The torsion and leakage were likely responsible for the low-grade fever she developed during admission. This is the kind of case that reminds us how reliably imaging can mislead when the pregnant uterus creates steric bulk and anatomical distortion.

## Why Not Just Wait?

The default approach to uterine myomas in pregnancy is conservative — and for good reason. Myomectomy during pregnancy carries real risks of severe bleeding, pregnancy loss, preterm labour, and uterine rupture. Most leiomyomas follow a non-linear growth pattern: they grow fastest in the first trimester (about 6.12% per week), slow to 3.10% per week in the second trimester, and then often **shrink significantly** in the third trimester and postpartum (mean reduction of about 44%). For many women, patience wins.

But this case was different. Several factors converged to push the multidisciplinary team toward surgery at 21+6 weeks:

- **Worsening clinical status** — pain wasn't controlled and dyspnea from mass effect on the diaphragm was restricting her breathing
- **Real complication risk** — large, vascularised, mixed solid-cystic masses carry genuine risk of rupture, torsion, and infection
- **Growth trajectory concern** — even with slower second-trimester growth, continued uterine enlargement against these massive lesions risked ischaemia, necrosis, and further degeneration
- **Diagnostic uncertainty** — the slight CA-125 elevation and mixed imaging features couldn't exclude malignancy, and leaving a potentially tumorous mass in situ through the third trimester was not acceptable
- **Mechanical impingement** — the sheer size of these lesions was already compromising respiratory function and would increasingly limit space for the growing fetus

The team's reasoning is worth studying: they didn't operate because the masses were large. They operated because the masses were large *and* symptomatic, *and* imaging couldn't exclude malignancy, *and* the clinical trajectory was worsening. Each condition alone might not have justified surgery. Together, they did.

## The Intraoperative Surprise and the Principle of Targeted Surgery

Here's where this case gets instructive. The cranial mass — the one everyone thought was an endometrioma — was actually a **pedunculated leiomyoma that had undergone torsion and was actively leaking**. That changed everything. A twisted, leaking pedunculated myoma is a surgical emergency in any patient. The decision to excise it from its uterine implant using a double clamp, close the stalk with a single absorbable suture, and get out was textbook.

But the caudal mass — a 9.5 cm cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma originating from the uterine torus — was left in situ. This is the critical decision point. Removing an intramural myoma from the posterior uterine wall at 21 weeks carries a substantially higher risk of bleeding, uterine rupture, and pregnancy loss than removing a pedunculated one. The team recognised that this mass was not actively torsed or leaking, and that its intramural location made resection far more dangerous. They chose to preserve pregnancy over complete excision.

This is the principle of **targeted, pregnancy-preserving surgery**: address the lesion that is causing complications or poses an immediate threat, and leave the rest for postpartum management. The caudal myoma was later delivered via elective caesarean at 37 weeks — performed through the same surgical scar to minimise rupture risk — and had partially regressed postpartum, which is exactly what the natural history data predicted.

## Laparotomy or Laparoscopy?

The laparoscopic approach is generally preferred for myomectomy when feasible — it's associated with better maternal outcomes and higher rates of vaginal birth. But there's a clear selection bias in the literature. Laparoscopy works well for smaller, fewer, non-intramural myomas in early gestation, ideally using an open Hasson technique. This patient had two large masses, one intramural, at 21+6 weeks with a gravid uterus occupying significant abdominal space. A laparotomy was the pragmatic choice.

The key message: don't read the laparoscopy-versus-laparotomy literature without appreciating that the patients selected for each approach are fundamentally different. Comparing outcomes across groups without accounting for myoma size, number, location, and gestational age is comparing apples to oranges.

## Tumour Markers in Pregnancy: Useful Trend, Useless Snapshot

The CA-125 story in this case deserves attention. CA-125 has two physiological peaks during pregnancy — one in the first trimester (median 23 U/mL) and one in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL) — with the second trimester being the nadir (median 14 U/mL). This patient's value of 41.8 U/mL was above the standard cut-off of 35 U/mL and represented an increasing trend from 36 U/mL two weeks prior. That trend, not the absolute number, is what mattered.

CA-125 is elevated in endometriosis, adenomyosis, pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, early pregnancy, near-term pregnancy, miscarriage risk, and even myomas. A single value tells you almost nothing. Serial values showing a rising trend in the context of complex masses during pregnancy — that's the signal worth acting on.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Default to conservative management** for asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic uterine myomas in pregnancy; most will shrink postpartum (mean reduction ~44%)
- **Surgery is justified when**: there is clinical deterioration (pain, dyspnea, mass effect), real complication risk (torsion, rupture, infection), diagnostic uncertainty with suspicious imaging features, or concern that continued growth will outpace uterine accommodation
- **Remove only what you must**: excise the lesion causing the complication (especially a torsed pedunculated myoma) and leave intramural or posterior wall lesions in situ unless malignancy is confirmed — the goal is pregnancy preservation, not complete resection
- **Tumour markers are trends, not thresholds**: serial CA-125 measurements are more useful than single values; remember the second trimester is normally the nadir, so any elevation during this period warrants attention
- **Non-contrast MRI** (≤1.5 T, after first trimester) is the imaging modality of choice when ultrasound is inconclusive; gadolinium is avoided unless strictly necessary due to associations with stillbirth and neonatal death
- **Laparoscopy is preferred when feasible** but recognise the selection bias — larger, multiple, intramural, or later-gestation masses are legitimately managed with laparotomy
- **Monitor fetal heart rate** before, during, and after surgery, and consider intraoperative progesterone prophylaxis
- **Plan delivery around the residual mass**: the caudal myoma in this case would have obstructed vaginal delivery, making elective caesarean at 37 weeks through the same scar the right call

## The Bottom Line

This case is a masterclass in the principle that surgery in pregnancy should be **targeted, not exhaustive**. The team removed a torsed pedunculated myoma because it was actively complicating, and they left an intramural posterior wall myoma alone because removing it would have endangered the pregnancy without clear benefit. The patient went home, delivered at term, breastfed, and her residual myoma partially regressed. That's what a good decision looks like when the easy answer — "operate on everything" — would have been the wrong one.



# Blind Spots: Navigating Imaging Limitations in the Gravid Abdomen

A nulliparous woman in her 40s, 21 weeks pregnant, walks into your emergency department with severe abdominal pain and dyspnea. Ultrasound reveals two massive cystic masses — one 18 cm, one 9.5 cm — compressing everything in sight. MRI without contrast shows thick-walled, inhomogeneous cysts with solid components. CA-125 is mildly elevated and trending upward. You need to figure out what these are, whether they're dangerous, and whether to operate on a gravid uterus — all while your imaging options are deliberately handcuffed by pregnancy. This case, published by De Nola and colleagues, is a masterclass in just how blind we can be when the gravid abdomen defies even our best non-invasive tools.

## The Setup: Two Masses, Zero Certainty

This patient had two large unilocular cystic masses with peripheral vascularisation on ultrasound. The upper lesion extended from the left subphrenic space to the uterine fundus; the lower one sat in the pouch of Douglas, apparently adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma with cystic degeneration. Both ultrasound and non-contrast MRI suggested these were decidualised endometriomas — the slight CA-125 elevation (41.8 U/mL, up from 36 two weeks prior) supported that interpretation for the cranial mass, while the caudal lesion's continuity with uterine tissue pointed toward a myomatous origin.

Here is where it gets humbling: at laparotomy, the upper "endometrioma" turned out to be a huge pedunculated cystic leiomyoma — twisted, leaking, and likely the source of the patient's low-grade fever. The imaging couldn't identify the connecting stalk or feeding vessel because the pregnant uterus created so much steric bulk that vascular pedicles were simply invisible. The lower mass was also a cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma, left in situ to protect the pregnancy.

The preoperative diagnosis was wrong for the cranial mass. And that is the central lesson: in pregnancy, even a careful multimodal workup can mislead you.

## Why Imaging Falls Short in Pregnancy — and What We Can Still Do

The imaging toolkit in pregnancy is intentionally limited. Ultrasound with colour Doppler is your frontline modality — it is safe, repeatable, and excellent for characterising anterior wall, submucosal, or pedunculated leiomyomas on the gravid uterus. But when masses are posterior, multiple, intramural, or buried behind a 21-week uterus in a patient with a BMI of 33, ultrasound hits a wall. In this case, no feeding vessel or stalk was identified for the upper mass, which would have immediately clarified its uterine origin.

Non-contrast MRI is the recommended second-line modality and offers superior diagnostic accuracy for large, multiple, or posterior myomas — particularly because it can precisely delineate the distance between a myoma and the uterine cavity. MRI in this case added real value: it characterised the thick walls (1.4 cm), the inhomogeneous fluid content, the focal solid components, and confirmed the caudal mass's continuity with the posterior uterine wall. But it could not resolve the cranial mass's origin.

Gadolinium-enhanced MRI would have been the logical next step to characterise vascular supply and solid components — but it is avoided in pregnancy due to associations with rheumatological, inflammatory, and infiltrative skin conditions in the neonate, as well as risk of stillbirth and neonatal death. The authors used a 1.5-T non-enhanced MRI, which is considered reasonably safe after the first trimester, and is the current standard when MRI is indicated during pregnancy. Avoiding gadolinium is not a trivial sacrifice — it directly reduces your ability to characterise complex cystic masses with solid components, which is exactly the scenario where you need the most clarity.

So you are left with a deliberate trade-off: you protect the fetus by accepting reduced diagnostic certainty. The question is what to do with that uncertainty.

## When the Clinical Picture Forces Your Hand

The multidisciplinary team here did not operate because of the imaging findings — they operated despite them. The decision-making hinged on several converging factors. First, the masses were huge and vascularised with mixed solid and cystic components, carrying real risk of rupture, torsion, or infection. Second, the clinical picture was worsening — pain and restrictive dyspnea from mass effect, plus a fever that, in retrospect, was likely from the twisted, leaking pedunculated myoma. Third, as the uterus continued to grow, the masses would grow too — uterine leiomyomas increase by roughly 6.9% per week during pregnancy, with the fastest growth in the first trimester — increasing the risk of ischaemia, necrosis, and degeneration. Fourth, there was a slight but rising CA-125, which, while not diagnostic in pregnancy, added to the oncological uncertainty.

The surgical team performed a longitudinal xiphos-pubic laparotomy at 21 weeks + 6 days. They collected peritoneal fluid for culture and cytology, confirmed fetal cardiac activity, and discovered that the cranial mass was a twisted pedunculated leiomyoma — not an ovarian cyst. They removed only the upper mass and left the lower one in situ to minimise obstetric risk. The surgery lasted 2 hours and 25 minutes, and the patient received intraoperative and postoperative progesterone prophylaxis to reduce uterine irritability.

The pathology confirmed benign uterine leiomyomas with ischaemic necrosis near the twisted pedicle. Peritoneal fluid was negative. The patient was discharged 10 days later after requiring two blood transfusions for postoperative anaemia. She delivered via elective repeat cesarean at 37 weeks on the same surgical scar, with an excellent neonatal outcome.

## Integrating What You Have When You Can't See Everything

The practical takeaway is that when imaging cannot give you a definitive answer in pregnancy, you integrate what you have and make a risk-benefit calculation. Here is how the pieces fit together in this case and how they should inform your approach.

Bimanual examination remains valuable and underused. The vaginal exam here revealed a fixed, hard, rounded 9 cm mass in the pelvis — a finding that contributed to the decision to leave it in situ, because it would clearly obstruct vaginal delivery regardless of its exact histology. Physical examination also flagged the 18 cm upper quadrant mass and the absence of ascites, which helped frame the differential away from advanced malignancy.

Tumour markers in pregnancy require serial interpretation, not isolated cutoffs. CA-125 physiologically peaks in the first and third trimesters (medians of 23 and 21 U/mL respectively) and is lowest in the second trimester (median 14 U/mL). This patient's value of 41.8 U/mL in the second trimester was above the expected median and showed an upward trend — not diagnostic of malignancy, but a signal worth incorporating. Other markers (CA 19-9, CA 15-3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, LDH) were normal, which provided some reassurance. The key point: never hang your hat on a single CA-125 in pregnancy. Trend it.

The decision to operate during pregnancy should be driven by complications and risk trajectory, not by the imaging diagnosis itself. Conservative management is the default for uterine myomas in pregnancy, given risks of bleeding, pregnancy loss, preterm labour, and uterine rupture. But when you have worsening pain, mass effect, a twisted or degenerating pedunculated myoma, or oncological suspicion you cannot exclude, the calculus shifts. The goal is the shortest, safest intervention possible — remove what is dangerous, leave what is stable, and preserve the pregnancy.

## Key Take-Home Points

- **Ultrasound with colour Doppler is first-line, but recognise its limits.** Posterior, intramural, or large masses in a gravid abdomen may have invisible feeding vessels and pedicles — exactly the features that distinguish a pedunculated myoma from an ovarian cyst.

- **Non-contrast 1.5-T MRI after the first trimester is your best second-line tool.** It adds spatial resolution and can characterise myoma location relative to the uterine cavity, but it cannot replace gadolinium-enhanced sequences for complex cystic masses with solid components. Accept that gap.

- **Gadolinium is avoided in pregnancy for good reason.** Do not order it unless absolutely necessary — the fetal risks are documented. Work with what you have.

- **Trend your tumour markers.** A single CA-125 is meaningless in pregnancy. Serial values that rise against the expected second-trimester nadir warrant attention but are not diagnostic alone.

- **Do not let imaging uncertainty delay action when the clinical picture is deteriorating.** This patient had worsening pain, dyspnea, fever, and masses large enough to threaten complication — the surgical team operated because the risk of doing nothing exceeded the risk of uncertainty.

- **Operate selectively and minimally.** Remove the mass that is dangerous; leave the stable one. Use progesterone prophylaxis, monitor fetal heart rate before, during, and after surgery, and keep the operation as short as possible.

- **Plan delivery around what remains.** The caudal myoma stayed in the pelvis and would have obstructed labour — an elective cesarean at 37 weeks on the existing scar was the right call. The mode of delivery should depend on the size, number, and anatomical location of any remaining uterine masses.

- **Myomas regress postpartum.** Leiomyomas decrease by a mean of 44% in the third trimester and 6 weeks postpartum, which is why conservative management of the residual mass — rather than immediate re-operation — is often appropriate.

## The Bottom Line

Pregnancy forces you to accept diagnostic compromise. Your job is to integrate ultrasound, non-contrast MRI, bimanual exam, serial tumour markers, and the clinical trajectory into a coherent risk assessment — and to know when that risk justifies operating on a gravid uterus. This team got it right: they removed the dangerous mass, preserved the pregnancy, and planned delivery around what remained. The lesson is not that imaging failed — it is that imaging in pregnancy will always have blind spots, and your clinical judgment has to fill them.



# Protecting the Pregnancy: Tocolysis, Progesterone Prophylaxis, and Post-Myomectomy Care

## When the Mass Isn't What You Think It Is

A nulliparous woman in her 40s, 21 weeks pregnant, walks in with severe abdominal pain and dyspnea. Ultrasound shows two large cystic masses — one 18 cm sitting under the left diaphragm, another 9.5 cm in the pouch of Douglas. CA-125 is mildly elevated at 41.8 U/mL. The multidisciplinary team reasonably worries about decidualized endometriomas, possibly something worse. They take her to surgery at 21+6 weeks.

Here's the punchline: the cranial "ovarian cyst" was actually a **twisted, leaking, pedunculated uterine leiomyoma**. Not an adnexal mass at all. The caudal mass was another myoma with cystic degeneration, left in situ. The fever during admission? Probably the torsion and leak. The mildly elevated CA-125? A red herring amplified by pregnancy physiology.

This case is a striking reminder that imaging, even with non-contrast MRI, can mislead you when the gravid uterus distorts anatomy and sterically obscures connecting stalks and feeding vessels. But what makes this case especially instructive isn't just the diagnostic surprise — it's the perioperative obstetric management. How do you preserve a 21-week pregnancy when you're operating on the uterus itself?

## The Perioperative Bundle: What They Did

Let's lay out the full obstetric perioperative strategy used in this case:

- **Intraoperative progesterone 100 mg IM**, continued daily until discharge
- **Hydroxyprogesterone caproate 341 mg IM every 3 days** continued until elective delivery at 37 weeks
- **Serial transvaginal ultrasound** documenting fetal heart rate before, during, and after surgery
- **Intraoperative cefazolin 2 g IV**, followed by cefazolin 1 g IV TID plus clarithromycin 500 mg BID, guided by a urine culture growing *Klebsiella pneumoniae*
- **Postoperative anemia management**: Hb dropped to 7.5 g/dL, treated with two units of packed red cells and IV ferric carboxymaltose 1 g
- **10-day hospitalization** followed by obstetric follow-up every 3 weeks
- **Elective cesarean at 37 weeks** via the same surgical scar

The outcome was excellent — healthy baby, good maternal recovery, residual pelvic myoma slightly regressed postpartum to 7.5 cm. But the question we should ask is: which of these interventions were evidence-based, and which were reflexive?

## Progesterone Prophylaxis: Physiologically Appealing, Evidence Thin

The use of progesterone after myomectomy in pregnancy is not standardised. The rationale is sound — progesterone maintains myometrial quiescence, and surgery on the gravid uterus risks irritability and preterm contractions. But the evidence supporting progesterone specifically for post-myomectomy tocolysis in the second trimester is largely extrapolative.

What we do know: progesterone supplementation reduces recurrent preterm birth in high-risk women, and 17-OH progesterone caproate was historically used for this indication (though the FDA withdrew approval for the branded product in 2023 due to lack of efficacy in the confirmatory trial). For tocolysis after uterine surgery, the data are scarce and largely derived from small case series and expert opinion rather than randomised trials.

In this case, the team used immediate postoperative progesterone 100 mg IM daily, then transitioned to hydroxyprogesterone caproate every 3 days until delivery. The hydroxyprogesterone caproate was essentially used as maintenance tocolysis for the remainder of pregnancy — 16 weeks of injections. Whether this contributed to the excellent outcome or whether the pregnancy would have done just as well without it is unknowable from a single case. It's a reasonable intervention in a high-risk scenario, but we should be honest that it reflects clinical reasoning rather than robust evidence.

## Fetal Monitoring During Surgery: Document Everything

The case report references serial and intermittent transvaginal ultrasounds monitoring fetal viability throughout the operation, consistent with the recommendation from Zhao et al. to check and document fetal heart rate before, during, and after surgery. This is a critical point. If you're operating on a pregnant patient — whether for myomectomy, appendectomy, or adnexal surgery — continuous or intermittent fetal heart rate monitoring should be a standard part of your intraoperative checklist. The gestational age determines the modality: before 23–24 weeks, intermittent ultrasound confirmation of cardiac activity is appropriate. After viable gestational age, continuous electronic fetal monitoring is warranted.

This team did it right. Before you close that abdomen, confirm the fetal heart rate one more time.

## Antibiotic Selection: Let the Culture Guide You

The patient had *Klebsiella pneumoniae* bacteriuria detected during admission. The surgical team used cefazolin for standard surgical prophylaxis but then extended to a therapeutic course of cefazolin plus clarithromycin, guided by the antibiogram. This is a practical example of integrating concurrent infection management with perioperative prophylaxis.

Standard surgical prophylaxis for laparotomy in pregnancy is a single dose of cefazolin. Extending to a therapeutic course is justified when there's a documented concurrent infection — and a urinary tract infection in pregnancy is not something to dismiss, given the risk of pyelonephritis and associated preterm labour. The clarithromycin addition reflects the specific antibiogram sensitivity pattern. The take-home: don't reflexively escalate antibiotics without a culture, but when you have one, let it drive your postoperative management.

## Postoperative Anemia: Expect It, Treat It Aggressively

The hemoglobin dropped from 9.9 g/dL preoperatively to 7.5 g/dL postoperatively — a meaningful drop in a pregnant patient who was already iron deficient. The team transfused two units and gave IV ferric carboxymaltose 1 g. This is appropriate and aggressive management.

Myomectomy, even of a pedunculated fibroid, carries bleeding risk. The pregnant uterus is hyperemic. The patient had pre-existing iron deficiency anemia. The combination of surgical blood loss and baseline deficiency predictably produced a significant postoperative drop. The lesson: optimise iron stores preoperatively when you have the chance, and have a low threshold for both transfusion and IV iron repletion postoperatively in pregnant patients. The target hemoglobin matters — maternal anemia in pregnancy is associated with adverse outcomes, and postoperative anemia in this context deserves correction, not watchful waiting.

## The 10-Day Stay and 3-Weekly Follow-Up: Defensive or Reasonable?

A 10-day hospitalization after myomectomy at 21+6 weeks is longer than typical for many gynecologic procedures, but in this context it reflects several factors: the complexity of the surgery, the postoperative anemia requiring transfusion, the concurrent UTI requiring IV antibiotics, and the need for ongoing pregnancy monitoring. Given the risks of preterm labour, rupture, or bleeding in the immediate postoperative period, a prolonged inpatient observation is defensible — if not necessarily evidence-based by randomised trial standards.

The 3-weekly follow-up schedule thereafter is more intensive than standard obstetric care but appropriate given the residual pelvic myoma, the uterine scar, and the surgical complexity. The team was watching for preterm labour, scar integrity, and myoma-related complications.

## What's Evidence-Based vs. Reflexive?

Let's be direct about what we can and cannot justify:

- **Serial fetal heart rate monitoring before, during, and after surgery**: Evidence-based. This should be standard.
- **Standard surgical antibiotic prophylaxis (cefazolin)**: Evidence-based.
- **Culture-guided therapeutic antibiotics for concurrent UTI**: Evidence-based.
- **Aggressive management of postoperative anemia with transfusion and IV iron**: Evidence-based and clinically sound.
- **Progesterone prophylaxis and maintenance tocolysis**: Physiologically rational, but evidence is extrapolative. Reasonable in a high-risk scenario, but acknowledge the uncertainty.
- **Hydroxyprogesterone caproate until delivery**: This is where the evidence is weakest. The 2023 FDA withdrawal of Makena approval should give us pause. In a post-myomectomy context, it may be reasonable, but it is not evidence-based.
- **10-day hospitalization**: Clinically reasonable given the complexity, but not evidence-based by any trial.
- **3-weekly follow-up**: Reasonable surveillance given the residual myoma and surgical scar.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Fibroids can masquerade as adnexal masses in pregnancy.** When the gravid uterus distorts anatomy, even MRI can't always identify the connecting stalk. Maintain a broad differential.
- **CA-125 in pregnancy is unreliable.** It physiologically rises in the first and third trimesters. A mildly elevated value in the second trimester is not diagnostic of malignancy or endometriosis.
- **Operate only when necessary — but when necessary, operate decisively.** The indications here were pain, dyspnea from mass effect, risk of torsion/rupture/infection, and diagnostic uncertainty. The twisted, leaking pedunculated myoma justified the intervention.
- **Leave stable masses in situ when possible.** The caudal myoma was left alone, and the pregnancy proceeded to 37 weeks without issue. Avoid overtreatment.
- **Document fetal heart rate throughout surgery.** Before, during, and after. This is non-negotiable.
- **Expect postoperative anemia.** Transfuse and replete iron aggressively in pregnant patients.
- **Progesterone prophylaxis is reasonable but unproven.** Use it as part of a comprehensive strategy, but be transparent with patients about the evidence base.
- **Plan delivery based on residual anatomy.** The pelvic myoma would have obstructed labour — elective cesarean at 37 weeks via the same scar was the right call.
- **Conservative management is the default.** Myomectomy during pregnancy is the exception, not the rule. Reserve it for complications: torsion, severe pain unresponsive to conservative measures, or diagnostic uncertainty with oncologic concern.

This case shows what excellent multidisciplinary obstetric-surgical care looks like in a rare, high-stakes scenario. Not every intervention was backed by a randomised trial — but the team balanced diagnostic uncertainty, surgical risk, and pregnancy preservation with a thoughtful, individualised approach. That's what we should aspire to: evidence-informed, patient-centred, and honest about where the evidence ends and clinical judgment begins.



## The Residual Mass: Planning Delivery and Postpartum Management of Persistent Fibroids

When you're operating on a pregnant patient, the intraoperative discovery that upends your preoperative diagnosis is nerve-wracking enough. But the harder question often comes after you've dealt with the acute problem: what do you do with what you *didn't* remove? This case — a 40-something nulliparous woman at 21 weeks with two massive abdominal masses masquerading as bilateral endometriomas — is a masterclass in that exact dilemma, and the obstetric decision-making that follows the decision to leave a 9 cm fibroid behind is worth dissecting in detail.

## The Setup: Two Masses, One Surprise

This patient presented with severe left upper quadrant and pelvic pain (VAS 5 at both sites) and mild dyspnea at 21 weeks gestation. She was obese with a BMI of 33, and her abdomen was visibly more enlarged than expected for gestational age, with a tender 18 cm mass occupying the entire left upper quadrant and a fixed 9 cm mass palpable in the posterior fornix on bimanual exam. Ultrasound showed two large unilocular cystic masses with peripheral vascularization — the cranial one extending from the left subphrenic region to the uterine fundus, the caudal one adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma with cystic degeneration. CA-125 was mildly elevated at 41.8 U/mL with an upward trend, which, combined with the imaging, pointed the team toward decidualized endometriomas. Non-contrast MRI couldn't find a connecting stalk or feeding vessel for the upper mass, reinforcing the ovarian origin hypothesis.

At laparotomy, the cranial mass turned out to be a huge pedunculated cystic leiomyoma — twisted, leaking, and likely responsible for the patient's low-grade fever during admission. The caudal mass, originating from the uterine torus, was a large cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma. The team excised the twisted cranial mass and deliberately left the caudal one in situ. That decision — surgically sound in the moment — set up every subsequent obstetric decision for the remainder of the pregnancy.

## Why Leave It? The Calculus of Conservative Management

The rationale for leaving the caudal myoma was straightforward: it was fixed, intramural-subserosal, and originating from the uterine torus. Removing it would have meant a deep myometrial resection at a critical uterine location in a 21-week pregnancy — a setup for catastrophic bleeding, pregnancy loss, or preterm labor. The cranial mass had to go because it was pedunculated, twisted, and actively leaking. The caudal mass had none of those acute complications. The team correctly recognized that the risk-benefit ratio favored conservation, even with a 9 cm tumor sitting in the pelvis.

This is the first critical pearl: *partial myomectomy in pregnancy is not failure — it is risk stratification in real time.* The goal is not to remove everything; it is to remove what threatens the pregnancy now and manage what can wait.

## The Residual Mass Drives Delivery Planning

This is where the case gets really instructive. The team followed the patient with serial ultrasounds every 3 weeks, documenting appropriate fetal growth (86th percentile per WHO charts) and normal amniotic fluid. But the elephant in the room — or rather, the pelvis — was that 9 cm myoma sitting at the uterine torus, posteriorly, in the exact path the fetal head would need to take during vaginal delivery.

The team's assessment was direct: the caudal myoma would have behaved as a mechanical obstacle to fetal head descent during the expulsive phase. This is not a theoretical concern. A posterior intramural-subserosal leiomyoma at the torus occupies the same anatomical space that the fetal head must navigate through the birth canal. With a mass that size, trial of labor risked arrest of descent, prolonged second stage, and potential emergency operative delivery under far less controlled circumstances than a planned cesarean.

The decision: elective cesarean at 37 weeks, performed through the same longitudinal xiphos-pubic incision used for the myomectomy. The rationale for using the same scar was to avoid creating a second uterine incision and to minimize the risk of uterine scar rupture — a critical consideration in a patient who had already undergone myomectomy at 21 weeks on the same uterus. Timing at 37 weeks balanced the risk of continuing the pregnancy with the residual mass against fetal maturity.

## The Natural History Problem: What Happens to Fibroids in Pregnancy?

Here is where counseling becomes everything. Uterine leiomyomas do not grow linearly during pregnancy — they follow a non-linear pattern that every clinician managing these patients needs to understand. The growth data from the literature is striking: leiomyomas increase by approximately 6.9% per week overall, with 6.12% growth between the first and second trimesters (p<0.001) but only 3.10% between the second and third trimesters (p<0.05). In other words, the steepest growth happens early, and it slows substantially by the time most patients present for management — which is exactly what happened here, with the patient presenting at 21 weeks.

More importantly for postpartum counseling: leiomyomas significantly *regress* in the third trimester and by 6 weeks postpartum, with a mean reduction of 44.25% (±36.05%, p<0.05). This is not a marginal effect — nearly half the volume, on average, disappears. This regression is driven by the postpartum drop in estrogen and progesterone, the same hormonal milieu that fueled the growth during pregnancy.

In this case, that is exactly what happened. The residual pelvic myoma shrank from 9 cm to 7.5 cm postpartum, and the patient remained asymptomatic. She declined further surgery — a decision the authors framed as reasonable given the partial regression, absence of symptoms, and her fulfillment of motherhood.

There is also a delivery timing signal worth noting: a study by Sakinci et al. showed a statistically significant *inverse* correlation between myoma size and the delivery week (p=0.035) — larger fibroids are associated with earlier delivery. The same study demonstrated that cesarean myomectomy (CS plus myomectomy) did not differ from cesarean alone in terms of hemoglobin change or transfusion rates, though hospitalization and operative time were significantly longer. The mean myoma size in that cohort was 8.3 ± 4.1 cm, with a positive correlation between myoma size and length of stay (p=0.01).

## The Postpartum Counseling Framework

For patients like this one — pregnant with a residual fibroid after partial myomectomy, or simply with large fibroids managed conservatively — postpartum counseling should address several concrete questions. Will the fibroid keep shrinking? In most cases, yes, with the most significant regression occurring within 6 weeks of delivery, driven by the hormonal withdrawal of the puerperium, particularly in breastfeeding patients whose estrogen levels remain suppressed. Should the patient undergo deferred myomectomy? That depends on symptoms, residual size, and future reproductive plans. This patient declined, and given the regression and absence of symptoms, that was a defensible choice. What about future pregnancies? A prior myomectomy scar — especially on the posterior uterine wall — carries implications for future trial of labor and rupture risk, and patients should be counseled that subsequent pregnancies may require repeat cesarean depending on the depth of the original myometrial incision.

The broader message is this: in the absence of definitive guidelines for pelvic masses in pregnancy, multidisciplinary decision-making must weigh the natural history of the disease against the surgical risk. This team's approach — remove what is complicated, leave what is stable, plan delivery around what remains, and let postpartum physiology do the rest — resulted in an excellent maternal and neonatal outcome.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- When performing myomectomy in pregnancy, partial resection is appropriate when the residual mass carries lower surgical risk than removal — the goal is preserving the pregnancy, not achieving complete excision.
- A posterior intramural-subserosal myoma at the uterine torus can mechanically obstruct fetal head descent and should factor directly into the decision regarding mode of delivery.
- Elective cesarean at 37 weeks via the previous surgical incision is a reasonable strategy when a large residual pelvic myoma would obstruct labor, minimizing both mechanical obstruction and additional uterine scar risk.
- Leiomyoma growth in pregnancy is non-linear: steepest in the first trimester (~6% per week), slowing significantly by the second and third trimesters.
- Expect significant postpartum regression — mean 44% reduction by 6 weeks postpartum — which should inform counseling regarding deferred surgery.
- Serial postpartum surveillance, not immediate surgery, is appropriate for asymptomatic patients with regressing residual fibroids, particularly those who are breastfeeding and have completed their family planning.
- CA-125 trends, not single values, matter in pregnancy — physiological elevations occur in the first and third trimesters, and benign conditions like endometriosis and myoma can elevate levels above the standard 35 U/mL cutoff.
