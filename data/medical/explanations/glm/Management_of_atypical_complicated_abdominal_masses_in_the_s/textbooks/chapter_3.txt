Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

Chapter 3: ## Differential Diagnosis and Multidisciplinary Decision-Making

Constructing an accurate differential diagnosis for abdominal masses in pregnancy is complicated by the physiological enlargement of the gravid uterus, which distorts normal anatomy and limits the sensitivity of physical examination. In this case, the patient presented at 21 weeks' gestation with two large cystic masses—one in the left upper quadrant measuring 18 cm and one in the pouch of Douglas measuring approximately 9 cm—along with a known posterior intramural-subserosal leiomyoma with cystic degeneration. The diagnostic challenge was to determine whether these masses represented ovarian pathology, uterine pathology, or a combination of both, a distinction with critical implications for surgical planning and obstetric management.

### Epidemiologic Framework

The differential diagnosis begins with an appreciation of the relative prevalence of uterine and adnexal masses during pregnancy. Uterine leiomyomas are detected in 0.3–10.1% of pregnancies, while adnexal masses are identified in approximately 1–6%. Although leiomyomas are more common, their identification is not always straightforward, particularly when they undergo cystic degeneration and acquire imaging characteristics that overlap with adnexal cysts. This epidemiologic context framed the initial diagnostic considerations: both categories of pathology were plausible, and the team had to use imaging characteristics, tumor markers, and anatomical relationships to distinguish between them.

### Imaging-Based Differential: Endometriomas versus Degenerating Leiomyomas

Ultrasound demonstrated two large, unilocular cystic masses with regular margins and peripheral vascularization. The upper mass extended from the left subphrenic region to the uterine fundus, while the lower mass appeared adherent to the posterior intramural-subserosal myoma. Non-contrast MRI was subsequently obtained to improve diagnostic accuracy, given that gadolinium-enhanced MRI is contraindicated in pregnancy except in strictly necessary circumstances. The MRI revealed thick walls (1.4 cm), inhomogeneous fluid content on T1 and T2 fat-sat sequences, and focal hyperintense solid components, but it could not definitively identify a connecting stalk or feeding vessel for the upper mass.

The imaging features initially suggested two large decidualized endometriomas. Endometriomas in pregnancy can undergo decidualization, a process in which ectopic endometrial stroma responds to high progesterone levels and transforms into decidual tissue, altering the cyst's sonographic and MRI appearance. Decidualized endometriomas may display thickened walls, heterogeneous internal echoes, and hypervascular peripheral walls, features that overlap substantially with the findings in this case. The slight elevation of CA-125 (41.8 U/mL, normal <35 U/mL) further supported this interpretation, as CA-125 is frequently elevated in endometriosis. However, the caudal mass demonstrated apparent continuity with the posterior uterine wall on both ultrasound and MRI, a critical finding that favored a myomatous origin with cystic degeneration over an ovarian origin.

### Diagnostic Ambiguity of the Upper Mass

The upper mass proved more difficult to characterize. Despite its large size and extension from the left subphrenic space to the uterine fundus, no connecting stalk or tumor-feeding vessel could be identified at either ultrasound or MRI. The gravid uterus occupied significant abdominal space and may have compressed or obscured potential vascular connections. Without evidence of continuity with the uterine wall, the diagnostic reasoning defaulted toward an ovarian origin, with endometrioma representing the leading hypothesis given the cystic appearance and slight CA-125 elevation. This case illustrates a key diagnostic limitation: when imaging cannot establish the organ of origin due to mass effect from the gravid uterus, the differential diagnosis must remain broad, and definitive characterization may require direct surgical inspection.

### Diagnostic Reasoning for the Caudal Mass

In contrast to the upper mass, the caudal mass demonstrated a clear anatomical relationship to the uterus. Both ultrasound and MRI showed the mass in continuity with the previously documented posterior intramural-subserosal leiomyoma. This continuity was the decisive imaging feature that oriented the diagnosis toward a degenerating uterine myoma rather than an adnexal cyst. Leiomyomas can undergo cystic degeneration, particularly during pregnancy when rapid growth outstrips the vascular supply, leading to ischemia, central necrosis, and cystic transformation. The appearance of a thick-walled cystic structure adherent to uterine tissue is therefore consistent with this pathophysiologic process. The inability to clearly define a cleavage plane between the caudal mass and the uterus on MRI further reinforced the myomatous interpretation.

### Attribution of Dyspnea

The patient presented with mild dyspnea, but her oxygen saturation was normal (97%), heart rate and blood pressure were within normal limits, and the ECG was unremarkable. With no evidence of pulmonary or cardiac dysfunction, the dyspnea was attributed to mechanical compression from the massive abdominal masses. The upper lesion, occupying the entire left subphrenic space and extending to the uterine fundus, likely restricted diaphragmatic excursion. This mechanical explanation was further supported by the observation that a semi-orthostatic position partially relieved her symptoms, reducing pain from 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale. Recognizing dyspnea as a mass effect rather than a primary cardiopulmonary problem was essential, because it identified the masses themselves as the therapeutic target.

### Multidisciplinary Team and Decision-Making

The patient's evaluation and management involved a multidisciplinary team including surgeons, gynecologists, obstetricians, and radiologists. This collaboration was critical given the complexity of the case, the absence of definitive management guidelines for atypical pelvic masses in pregnancy, and the need to balance maternal and fetal considerations. The team's decision-making incorporated the imaging findings, tumor marker trends, clinical symptomatology, and anticipated trajectory of pregnancy. Serial CA-125 measurements showed an increasing trend (from 36 to 41.8 U/mL over two weeks), which, although not diagnostic, contributed to the collective risk assessment. The team recognized that isolated tumor marker values during pregnancy are unreliable, as CA-125 physiologically rises during the first trimester and can be elevated in numerous benign conditions, but the upward trend provided additional data supporting further investigation and intervention.

### Risk Factors Favoring Surgical Intervention

Several factors collectively justified proceeding to laparotomy despite the absence of definitive guidelines. First, the patient's clinical condition was worsening, with persistent abdominal pain and dyspnea from restrictive lung compression. Second, the masses carried real risks of acute complications—rupture, torsion, and infection—any of which could precipitate an obstetric emergency. The patient did in fact develop a low-grade fever during her hospital stay, later found at surgery to correlate with a twisted, leaking pedunculated leiomyoma. Third, the masses were large and vascularized with mixed solid and cystic components, features associated with potential growth, hypoxia, necrosis, and further degeneration as pregnancy advanced. Fourth, the slight elevation of CA-125 introduced uncertainty regarding possible neoplastic pathology that could not be excluded noninvasively. Fifth, the sheer size of the masses posed a spatial problem: further uterine enlargement with fetal growth would increase compression and the risk of complications, and the caudal mass would likely obstruct labor. Finally, the inability to definitively characterize the upper mass noninvasively meant that surgical exploration offered both diagnostic and therapeutic value.

### Individualized Risk Assessment in the Absence of Guidelines

There are no definitive management guidelines for atypical or complicated pelvic masses in pregnancy, making individualized, multidisciplinary risk assessment essential. The general principle is to favor conservative, expectant management when possible, reserving surgery for cases with oncologic suspicion or clinical complications, always with the goal of preserving pregnancy and avoiding overtreatment. In this case, the convergence of worsening symptoms, mass-related complication risk, diagnostic uncertainty, and space limitations shifted the balance toward intervention. The team opted for a longitudinal laparotomic approach, which provided the exposure necessary for a mass of this size and allowed direct inspection of both lesions. The decision to remove only the upper mass while leaving the caudal mass in situ exemplified the principle of minimizing obstetric risk: the upper pedunculated myoma was twisted and leaking, posing an immediate threat, while the caudal intramural-subserosal myoma was fixed within the uterine wall, and its removal would have carried unacceptable risk of hemorrhage, preterm labor, or uterine rupture. This targeted surgical strategy reflected the core lesson of the case: in pregnancy, surgical management of abdominal masses should be guided by the specific anatomy, clinical risk, and feasibility of safe resection rather than by a uniform protocol.
