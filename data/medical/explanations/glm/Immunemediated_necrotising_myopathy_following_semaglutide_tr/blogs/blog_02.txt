Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# GLP-1 Receptor Agonists and Skekeletal Muscle: Beyond the GI Side Effects We Talk About

## One Dose. Four Months of Decline. A New Adverse Effect Hiding in Plain Sight?

You've prescribed semaglutide. Your patients are on it. Your colleagues are on it. GLP-1 receptor agonists have become one of the most widely prescribed drug classes in modern medicine, and for good reason — they work. We talk endlessly about the GI side effects: nausea, vomiting, delayed gastric emptying. But what about skeletal muscle? We know these medications cause measurable loss of lean muscle mass. There's a published case of tirzepatide-associated rhabdomyolysis. And now, we have what appears to be the **first reported case of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) probably triggered by a single dose of semaglutide.**

Let that sink in for a moment. Not rhabdomyolysis from a direct toxic effect. An autoimmune myopathy — necrotising, progressive, requiring immunosuppression — after *one dose*.

This case should give us pause not because GLP-1 agonists are bad drugs, but because we may be at the very beginning of a recognition curve that we've seen before with another wildly popular drug class: statins.

## The Case: A Single Dose, Then a Slow Unraveling

A woman in her 60s with obesity, NAFLD, hypertension, and hypothyroidism was started on semaglutide for persistent fatty liver disease and obesity. Within hours of her first and only 0.25 mg subcutaneous dose, she developed nausea and vomiting. Two days later, she was hospitalized with severe GI symptoms, acute renal failure, and metabolic acidosis requiring brief non-invasive ventilation in the ICU. Her CK on admission was 337 U/L — elevated but nowhere near rhabdomyolysis territory. She was hydrated, stabilized, and discharged after five days with resolved GI symptoms but noticeable deconditioning.

Then things got worse. Much worse. Over the next four months, she developed progressive proximal muscle weakness to the point where she could no longer stand or sit without assistance. She developed dysphagia to both liquids and solids and dysphonia. Her CK climbed to 3,992 U/L — a 12-fold increase from her admission CK — despite having been off semaglutide for the entire four-month period. She had no prior statin use, no red yeast rice or mushroom supplements, no family history of myopathy, no excessive exercise, no illicit drug use.

Her workup at a tertiary center revealed a negative full myositis panel including anti-SRP, anti-HMGCR, Jo-1, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-γ, NXP-2, and all others tested. ANA was negative. She had a low-titer SS-B antibody (2.4, normal <1.0) with negative SS-A, which was unexpected and of unclear significance. MRI of bilateral femurs showed symmetric muscular edema in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps. Cardiac MRI showed mild mid-anteroseptal and anterior wall hypokinesis with myocardial edema — potentially early myocarditis or stress cardiomyopathy. A muscle biopsy of the right vastus lateralis was the key: scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibres, CD68+ macrophage infiltration with myophagocytosis, membrane attack complex deposition on both necrotising and non-necrotising myofibres, and only rare CD3+ T-cells. This was textbook IMNM.

## Why This Isn't Rhabdomyolysis — And Why That Distinction Matters

The temporal course here is the critical clue. This patient's CK *rose* over four months after a single dose of the drug. In drug-induced rhabdomyolysis from a direct toxic effect, you expect a rapid CK spike while the drug is on board and a prompt fall within days of discontinuation and supportive care. That's exactly what was seen in the previously reported tirzepatide case — CK of 57,014 at presentation with improvement within days of withdrawal and hydration, no immunosuppression needed.

This patient's course was the opposite. Her CK was modestly elevated (337 U/L) at presentation shortly after the dose, then climbed steadily to nearly 4,000 U/L over four months *while off the drug entirely*. Her muscle biopsy showed an immune-mediated necrotising process with complement activation, not a toxic myopathy. And critically, her condition improved with immunosuppression — pulse methylprednisolone followed by oral prednisone and a single course of IVIG — plus continued avoidance of semaglutide. Her CK normalized within two weeks of treatment. She was weaned off prednisone over just two months, received no additional IVIG, and ten months later had regained full baseline function off all immunosuppression for seven to eight months.

This clinical pattern — persistent progression after drug cessation, response to immunosuppression, and sustained remission after a short course — aligns with drug-triggered IMNM rather than drug-induced rhabdomyolysis. The WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre criteria classify this as *probable* causality, acknowledging that a rechallenge would be unethical given the severity of the reaction.

## The Statin Parallel: Are We Seeing the Same Movie Again?

Here's where this case gets genuinely thought-provoking. Consider the history of statin-associated myopathy. Before the discovery of anti-HMGCR autoantibodies, patients with statin-associated necrotising myopathy were often classified as having toxic myopathy or polymyositis. Many had persistent myopathy and CK elevation despite statin cessation — a pattern inconsistent with a pure toxic effect. It was only after recognizing that these patients responded to immunosuppression and that autoantibodies were present that statin-associated IMNM was identified as a distinct autoimmune entity.

Importantly, about one-third of IMNM patients are seronegative for both anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP. This patient was seronegative on a comprehensive myositis panel. The absence of a known autoantibody doesn't exclude IMNM — it suggests there are associated autoantibodies and triggers we haven't yet discovered. GLP-1 receptor agonists could be one such trigger, and the relevant autoantibody may not yet exist in our testing arsenal.

The authors make a compelling point: given the exponential adoption of GLP-1 agonists, if even a tiny fraction of patients develop immune-mediated muscle disease, we will see more cases. The question is whether we'll recognize them or attribute progressive weakness to "deconditioning," "aging," or "weight loss–related sarcopenia" — all of which are expected with these medications.

## What About the Muscle Mass Loss We Already Know About?

The skeletal muscle story with GLP-1 agonists isn't new. These medications cause significant loss of lean body mass as part of their weight loss effect — in some trials, up to 25-40% of total weight lost is lean mass. This is generally framed as a "known, expected" effect rather than an adverse event. But here's the concern: if patients are already losing muscle mass from the drug's metabolic effects, and a subset develop an immune-mediated necrotising process on top of that, the clinical picture could be muddy. A patient on semaglutide who reports weakness could be experiencing sarcopenia from weight loss, or they could be developing IMNM. Without checking a CK, you won't distinguish them. And without a muscle biopsy, you won't know if it's autoimmune.

## What Should Clinicians Actually Watch For?

This is a single case report, and correlation is not causation. But the temporal relationship, the biopsy-proven IMNM pathology, the progressive course despite drug cessation, and the complete response to immunosuppression with drug avoidance make this a credible signal worth taking seriously. ## Key Take-Home Points

- **GLP-1 agonists may be associated with immune-mediated necrotising myopathy, not just direct toxic rhabdomyolysis or expected lean mass loss.** This is the first reported probable case, but given the massive prescribing volume, it likely won't be the last.

- **The temporal pattern distinguishes IMNM from rhabdomyolysis.** Rhabdomyolysis = acute CK spike while on drug, rapid improvement with cessation. IMNM = progressive CK rise and weakness *after* drug cessation, requiring immunosuppression. If you see progressive weakness and rising CK in a patient who stopped a GLP-1 agonist weeks or months ago, think IMNM, not delayed rhabdo.

- **Check a CK in any patient on a GLP-1 agonist with unexplained weakness.** Don't assume it's deconditioning or sarcopenia from weight loss. The CK is cheap, fast, and may be the only clue that something autoimmune is brewing.

- **Seronegative IMNM is common.** About one-third of IMNM patients are negative for anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP. A negative myositis panel does not exclude IMNM. Muscle biopsy is the gold standard.

- **Biopsy findings in IMNM are distinct.** Look for necrotic and regenerating fibres, macrophage-predominant infiltrate (CD68+), membrane attack complex positivity, and a paucity of T-cells. This is not polymyositis and not a toxic myopathy.

- **The statin-IMNM story is a cautionary parallel.** It took years to recognize that persistent myopathy after statin cessation was autoimmune, not toxic. We should be vigilant that a similar recognition curve may be starting with GLP-1 agonists.

- **Drug-triggered IMNM may require only short-course immunosuppression if the trigger is withdrawn.** This patient responded to pulse steroids, a single IVIG course, and brief prednisone taper — and remained in remission off all immunosuppression. Idiopathic IMNM typically requires prolonged, intensive immunosuppression. This difference supports a drug-triggered mechanism.

- **Pharmacovigilance matters more than ever.** Rare autoimmune adverse effects may not appear in pre-marketing trials but can emerge with population-level prescribing at this scale. Report suspected cases. The signal only becomes real when enough dots are connected.

## Bottom Line

We're prescribing GLP-1 receptor agonists at an unprecedented scale. We know about the GI effects, the pancreatitis risk, the muscle mass loss. We're learning about rhabdomyolysis. And now we have a credible first case of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy probably triggered by a single dose of semaglutide. Is this a one-in-a-million idiosyncratic reaction, or the tip of an iceberg we can't yet see? We don't know. But the history of statin-associated IMNM tells us that the answer only comes when clinicians are alert enough to check the CK, biopsy the muscle, and report the case. Consider this your alert.
