Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# Myositis and the Heart: Making Sense of Cardiac Findings in Inflammatory Myopathy

## When the Heart Confuses the Picture

A woman in her 60s presents with four months of progressive proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia. Her CK is nearly 4,000. Her muscle biopsy confirms immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). But here's where it gets interesting — she also has a mildly elevated high-sensitivity troponin (62 pg/mL, normal ≤53), a proBNP through the roof at 4,358 pg/mL, an echo showing a borderline RVSP of 46 mmHg, and a cardiac MRI with myocardial edema and mid-anteroseptal hypokinesis but no late gadolinium enhancement (LGE). So does she have myocarditis? Stress cardiomyopathy? Or is the troponin just noise from severe skeletal muscle injury?

This case — the first reported IMNM probably associated with semaglutide — forces us to confront one of the most vexing scenarios in the acute care of myopathy patients: what do we do with ambiguous cardiac findings when the skeletal muscle is on fire?

## The Troponin Problem in Severe Myopathy

Let's start with the troponin. Her hs-troponin was 62 pg/mL — only mildly above the reference range. In a patient with a CK of 3,992 U/L and active necrotising myopathy, how worried should we be?

This is where clinical context matters enormously. The traditional teaching is that cardiac troponin assays are highly cardiac-specific, and for the most part, that holds — modern hs-troponin assays detect cardiac-specific isoforms that are not typically expressed in significant quantities by skeletal muscle. However, there is accumulating evidence that skeletal muscle can express cardiac troponin T in certain disease states, particularly in regenerating skeletal muscle, and patients with severe myopathy or rhabdomyolysis can have troponin elevations without true myocardial injury. The key distinction here is magnitude: a troponin of 62 pg/mL in the setting of a CK near 4,000 is proportionally quite modest. If this were true active myocarditis with enough necrosis to produce MRI-visible edema and wall motion abnormalities, you would often expect a more pronounced troponin elevation. The discordance — massive skeletal muscle injury with only a whisper of troponin elevation — should make you pause before calling this primary myocarditis.

That said, a mildly elevated troponin in this setting is not something to dismiss entirely. You still need the imaging to sort it out.

## The Cardiac MRI: Edema Without LGE — What Does It Mean?

Her cardiac MRI showed mild hypokinesis of the mid-anteroseptal and anterior wall with associated myocardial edema but no definitive LGE. This is a genuinely ambiguous finding, and the authors of the case report appropriately acknowledge the uncertainty — listing both "myocardial inflammation/early myocarditis" and "focal stress-induced cardiomyopathy" as possibilities.

Here's how to think about it. In classic acute myocarditis, the Lake Louise Criteria call for both tissue edema (T2-based) and non-ischemic LGE to establish the diagnosis with higher confidence. The typical LGE pattern in myocarditis is mid-wall or subepicardial, often in the lateral or inferolateral walls, reflecting the epicardial-to-endocardial spread of inflammation. The mid-anteroseptal location in this case is somewhat atypical for classic myocarditis, and the complete absence of LGE makes a definitive diagnosis of myocarditis harder to sustain — though early or mild myocarditis can present without LGE, particularly if the necrosis has not progressed enough to produce a detectable fibrotic or necrotic signal on imaging.

Stress-induced cardiomyopathy (Takotsubo) is the other serious consideration. This patient had been critically ill — acute renal failure, metabolic acidosis, ICU-level care, new atrial fibrillation — all triggered within days of her semaglutide dose. Stress cardiomyopathy can produce regional wall motion abnormalities, myocardial edema on MRI, and troponin and natriuretic peptide elevations, often with minimal or no LGE initially. The mid-ventricular or anteroseptal distribution, while not the classic apical ballooning pattern, is a recognized variant. The markedly elevated proBNP is also consistent with stress cardiomyopathy, which characteristically produces disproportionate natriuretic peptide elevation relative to troponin.

## What About the proBNP and the RVSP?

The proBNP of 4,358 pg/mL is striking. While this could reflect LV dysfunction from myocarditis or stress cardiomyopathy, the echo showed a preserved LVEF of 65–70%. So where is the proBNP coming from? Several contributors are plausible in this patient: new-onset paroxysmal atrial fibrillation during her initial hospitalization, RV strain from the elevated RVSP of 46 mmHg, volume overload related to her acute kidney injury, and the general systemic stress of critical illness. The RVSP itself is borderline-mild and could reflect left heart disease, pulmonary disease, deconditioning, or even her prior history of DVT/PE, though no acute PE was documented in this presentation.

The point is that in a critically ill patient with multi-system involvement, proBNP and a borderline RVSP are not specific for myocarditis. They are part of the systemic stress picture and need to be interpreted in that light.

## Cardiac Involvement in IMNM Specifically — What Do We Know?

This is an important nuance. IMNM is a distinct entity from polymyositis and dermatomyositis, characterized by muscle fiber necrosis with minimal lymphocytic infiltration and macrophage-predominant pathology — exactly what was seen on this patient's biopsy (CD68+ macrophages with myophagocytosis, membrane attack complex deposition, rare CD3+ T cells). Cardiac involvement has been described across the inflammatory myopathy spectrum, but the data specific to IMNM are more limited. Anti-SRP positive IMNM, in particular, has been associated with higher rates of cardiac involvement, including myocarditis and arrhythmia. This patient was seronegative for both anti-SRP and anti-HMGCR, placing her in the approximately one-third of IMNM patients who are autoantibody-negative — a group about whom we know even less regarding cardiac risk.

The practical takeaway: IMNM patients can have cardiac involvement, but it is not the norm, and when cardiac abnormalities are present, they need to be interpreted alongside the entire clinical picture — the severity of muscle injury, the temporal relationship to drug exposure, the presence of critical illness, and the imaging pattern.

## Follow-Up Imaging: The Great Clarifier

Perhaps the most instructive part of this case is what happened on follow-up. Her repeat echocardiogram and cardiac MRI after treatment and recovery showed complete normalization of all prior cardiac abnormalities. She also had no recurrence of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

This resolution pattern is diagnostically powerful. Active autoimmune myocarditis — particularly in the context of an ongoing systemic autoimmune process like IMNM — would be expected to require targeted cardiac immunosuppression and would carry a risk of residual fibrosis or persistent dysfunction, often detectable as persistent LGE even after functional recovery. The rapid and complete normalization without cardiac-specific immunosuppression, in a patient who was weaned off prednisone over just two months and received only a single course of IVIG, is more consistent with a transient, stress-related process or a self-limited inflammatory insult than with a committed autoimmune myocarditis. The absence of LGE on the initial MRI and the absence of residual findings on follow-up both support this interpretation.

## Putting It Together: A Framework for the Bedside

When you encounter a patient with severe inflammatory myopathy and ambiguous cardiac findings, here is a practical approach:

First, contextualize the troponin. A mild elevation in the setting of massive CK elevation is less alarming than the same troponin in a patient with minimal muscle injury. Proportionality matters. But do not ignore it — get the imaging.

Second, interpret the cardiac MRI pattern carefully. Edema without LGE in an atypical location for myocarditis, in a patient who has been critically ill, should raise stress cardiomyopathy as a serious diagnostic contender. The Lake Louise Criteria require both edema and non-ischemic LGE for a confident MRI diagnosis of myocarditis; absence of LGE does not exclude myocarditis but makes it less certain and should prompt you to consider alternatives.

Third, do not over-attribute proBNP and RVSP to myocarditis. In the critically ill myopathy patient, these values are frequently driven by arrhythmia, RV strain, volume status, and systemic stress.

Fourth, let time and follow-up imaging be your adjudicator. If the cardiac findings resolve completely after treatment of the underlying myopathy and withdrawal of the offending agent — as in this case — the transient, stress-related explanation gains substantial credibility.

## Key Take-Home Points

- **Mild troponin elevation in severe myopathy is common and may reflect skeletal muscle injury or critical illness rather than primary myocarditis, but it still warrants cardiac imaging to adjudicate.**
- **Cardiac MRI with edema but no LGE is diagnostically ambiguous — it can represent early myocarditis, stress cardiomyopathy, or a transient inflammatory insult. Location and clinical context help differentiate.**
- **Stress-induced cardiomyopathy is a serious consideration in IMNM patients who have been critically ill, and can produce wall motion abnormalities, edema, and disproportionate proBNP elevation.**
- **IMNM can involve the heart, but cardiac involvement is not universal; anti-SRP positive disease carries higher cardiac risk, and seronegative IMNM (as in this case) remains poorly characterized.**
- **Follow-up imaging is the key adjudicator — complete resolution without cardiac-specific immunosuppression favors a transient process over committed autoimmune myocarditis.**
- **If the initial MRI lacks LGE and the follow-up MRI is clean, think twice before labeling it myocarditis — the pattern is more consistent with stress cardiomyopathy or a self-limited inflammatory insult.**

## Final Thought

This case is a reminder that in the era of ubiquitous GLP-1 receptor agonist use, we need to keep IMNM on our differential when patients develop progressive proximal weakness and soaring CKs — and when the cardiac workup muddies the waters, a systematic, context-driven approach will keep you from over-diagnosing myocarditis while not missing it when it is truly there. The follow-up image, not the first one, often tells the real story.
