Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# The Seronegative Myopathy Dilemma: When the Autoantibody Panel Comes Back Empty

## The Case That Refuses to Fit the Box

Picture this: a woman in her 60s presents with four months of progressive proximal weakness so severe she can no longer stand or sit without assistance. She's also developed dysphagia and dysphonia. Her CK is 3,992 U/L. You order a myositis panel — the full buffet: anti-SRP, anti-HMGCR, anti-Jo-1, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-γ, NXP-2, and on down the list. Every single one comes back negative. ANA? Negative. So what do you do with a patient who has all the clinical hallmarks of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) and none of the serologic signatures?

This is exactly the scenario described in a recent case report by Lam and colleagues, and it's a scenario that should make every clinician uncomfortable — because approximately one-third of IMNM patients are seronegative. That's not a rounding error. That's a third of patients who will be missed if you anchor on antibody testing.

## Why Seronegative IMNM Is a Diagnostic Trap

The two antibodies most associated with IMNM — anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP — have become the cornerstones of diagnosis, and understandably so. Anti-HMGCR is classically linked to statin exposure, and its discovery helped reclassify patients who had previously been lumped into "toxic myopathy" or "polymyositis" categories for years. But here's the critical lesson from the statin story: before anti-HMGCR was identified, those patients had persistent myopathy and CK elevation despite drug cessation, and they improved with immunosuppression. The clinical behavior pointed to an autoimmune process before the antibody confirmed it. The same logic must be applied to seronegative patients today. The absence of a known antibody does not exclude IMNM — it means we haven't yet discovered the relevant antibody (or trigger) for that subset of patients.

In this case, the patient had never taken statins, denied red yeast rice or mushroom consumption, and had no family history of myopathy. Her myositis panel was completely negative. Yet her clinical course — progressive proximal weakness, CK climbing 12-fold over four months, histologic confirmation on biopsy, and response to immunosuppression — was textbook IMNM.

## The Muscle Biopsy: Where the Diagnosis Actually Lives

When the antibody panel is empty, muscle biopsy shifts from confirmatory to essential. The histologic hallmark of IMNM is necrotising myofibres with a paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates — and that distinction from polymyositis matters enormously. Polymyositis classically shows endomysial inflammation with CD8+ T cells surrounding and invading non-necrotic fibres. IMNM, by contrast, is dominated by necrosis and regeneration with macrophage-predominant infiltrates. The inflammatory cells you see are mostly there to clean up dead muscle, not to attack living fibres.

In this patient's biopsy of the right vastus lateralis, the findings were archetypal: dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibres, CD68-positive macrophages with myophagocytosis, and membrane attack complex deposition on both necrotising and non-necrotising myofibres. Rare CD3-positive T cells were present but sparse. This is the histologic fingerprint of IMNM — and it is what separated this diagnosis from a toxic myopathy, where you would expect to see regenerative changes without the complement-mediated attack pattern.

## Distinguishing IMNM from Drug-Induced Rhabdomyolysis

The temporal relationship to semaglutide in this case is striking — the patient received a single dose and within hours developed nausea, vomiting, and metabolic derangement. But the muscle story didn't unfold like rhabdomyolysis. A prior case report of tirzepatide-associated rhabdomyolysis showed the classic pattern: CK in the tens of thousands at presentation, with rapid improvement within days of drug withdrawal and hydration alone, no immunosuppression needed. That is the signature of direct toxic injury.

This patient's CK was only modestly elevated (337 U/L) at initial presentation and then climbed to 3,992 U/L over four months — while she was off the drug entirely. That trajectory — worsening after withdrawal, requiring immunosuppression to resolve — is incompatible with a purely toxic mechanism. It is, however, entirely consistent with an immune-mediated process that was triggered (or unmasked) by the drug but then became self-sustaining. This is the same pattern that was observed with statin-associated IMNM before anti-HMGCR was discovered, and it is the clinical reasoning that allowed the authors to classify this as probable drug-induced IMNM using WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre causality criteria.

## The SS-B Red Herring

Here's a detail that could have derailed the diagnosis: the patient had a low-titer positive SS-B antibody (2.4, normal <1.0) in the setting of a negative ANA and negative SS-A. There are no published reports linking isolated SS-B positivity — without ANA or SS-A — to IMNM. The patient had no sicca symptoms, no synovitis, no other features of Sjögren's syndrome or connective tissue disease. In context, this appears to be an incidental finding, the kind of low-titer autoantibody that can appear in healthy individuals and muddle an already complex picture. The lesson is straightforward: do not let a weakly positive, contextually isolated autoantibody pull you away from a clinical and histologic diagnosis that fits.

## How to Approach the Seronegative Patient

The reasoning chain in this case provides a template for approaching any patient with progressive proximal weakness, significant CK elevation, and a negative antibody panel. First, confirm the temporal course — is the CK rising despite withdrawal of any potential trigger? That distinguishes immune-mediated necrotising myopathy from direct toxic injury. Second, obtain a muscle biopsy and look for the specific pattern: necrotising and regenerating fibres, macrophage-predominant infiltrates, membrane attack complex deposition, and sparse T-cell infiltration. Third, assess the response to immunosuppression — IMNM should respond to corticosteroids and IVIG, and drug-induced IMNM may resolve with a shorter course than idiopathic disease. Finally, consider the possibility that an as-yet unidentified autoantibody or trigger is at play, and maintain vigilance for pharmacovigilance as new medications like GLP-1 receptor agonists are used at exponentially increasing rates.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **One-third of IMNM patients are seronegative.** A negative myositis panel does not exclude the diagnosis. The absence of a known antibody means the relevant antibody or trigger hasn't been discovered yet.
- **Muscle biopsy is essential when serology is negative.** Look for necrotising myofibres, macrophage-predominant infiltrates (CD68+), and membrane attack complex deposition — with a paucity of T-cell inflammation. This distinguishes IMNM from polymyositis and toxic myopathy.
- **The clinical course distinguishes IMNM from rhabdomyolysis.** Toxic injury produces a rapid CK spike that normalizes within days of drug withdrawal. IMNM produces a CK that climbs over weeks to months despite drug cessation and requires immunosuppression to resolve.
- **A drug trigger doesn't mean a toxic mechanism.** Drug-induced IMNM is an immune process that becomes self-sustaining after the trigger — the same pattern seen with statin-associated anti-HMGCR myopathy before the antibody was discovered.
- **Don't anchor on isolated weak autoantibodies.** A low-titer SS-B with negative ANA and SS-A, in the absence of any clinical features of connective tissue disease, should not redirect your diagnostic reasoning.
- **GLP-1 receptor agonists may be a new trigger for IMNM.** With exponentially increasing use of semaglutide and related agents, clinicians should be aware of this potential association and maintain pharmacovigilance. Rechallenge would be unethical given the severity of the reaction.
