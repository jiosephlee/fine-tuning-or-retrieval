Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Clinical Pearls] Which two myositis-specific autoantibodies are most strongly associated with IMNM? What medication class is a well-known trigger for one of these antibody-associated forms, and approximately what proportion of IMNM patients are seronegative for both?

The two myositis-specific autoantibodies most strongly associated with immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) are **anti–hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (anti-HMGCR)** and **anti–signal recognition particle (anti-SRP)** antibodies. These antibodies define the major serologic subgroups of IMNM and are important not only for diagnosis but also for understanding the pathogenesis and potential triggers of the disease.

**Statin medications** are the well-known trigger for the anti-HMGCR–associated form. As described in the case report, the recognition of this association emerged from clinical observation: patients treated with statins for metabolic disorders developed necrotising myopathy with persistent CK elevation and progressive weakness even after statin discontinuation — features inconsistent with simple toxic myopathy. Their clinical improvement with immunosuppression ultimately led to the identification of anti-HMGCR autoantibody as a hallmark of this disease entity. This pattern — an immune-mediated process triggered by a medication but persisting after drug withdrawal — is a key diagnostic principle that distinguishes IMNM from direct drug-induced toxic myopathy or rhabdomyolysis.

Importantly, approximately **one-third of IMNM patients are seronegative** for both anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP antibodies. This seronegative subgroup poses significant diagnostic challenges and suggests that additional, yet-to-be-identified autoantibodies and environmental or pharmacologic triggers remain to be discovered. The presented case exemplifies this gap: the patient had a complete myositis panel negative for anti-HMGCR, anti-SRP, and all other known myositis-specific autoantibodies, yet her clinical course — progressive proximal weakness, marked CK elevation, necrotising myopathy on biopsy with macrophage-predominant infiltrate and membrane attack complex deposition, and sustained response to immunosuppression — was fully consistent with IMNM. Her temporal association with semaglutide raises the possibility that GLP-1 receptor agonists may represent a novel trigger in a seronegative IMNM subset, though causality remains unproven.

This case reinforces several practical clinical principles: (1) IMNM should be suspected in patients with severe proximal weakness, marked CK elevation, and necrotising myopathy on biopsy even when the autoantibody panel is negative; (2) a paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates on histology — with macrophage-predominant myophagocytosis and complement activation — distinguishes IMNM from polymyositis; (3) persistent or progressive myopathy after withdrawal of a potential trigger, rather than rapid improvement, favors an immune-mediated process requiring immunosuppression; and (4) the expanding therapeutic landscape for metabolic disease may introduce new pharmacologic triggers for IMNM that clinicians should remain vigilant about.
