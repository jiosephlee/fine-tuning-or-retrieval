Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

Chapter 2: ## Diagnostic Workup and Investigations

The diagnostic evaluation of a patient with suspected myopathy must systematically answer three questions: Is there muscle damage? What is the pattern and severity of involvement? And what is the underlying etiology? In this case, a coordinated workup of laboratory studies, imaging, and tissue biopsy allowed the clinical team to confirm immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) while excluding alternative diagnoses.

### Laboratory Findings

Creatine kinase (CK) is the single most informative serum marker for muscle injury. CK is an intracellular enzyme abundant in skeletal muscle, and its extracellular presence reflects disruption of the myocyte membrane. In this patient, CK was markedly elevated at 3992 U/L (normal range 12–191 U/L) at the outside hospital, and remained elevated at 1237 U/L at the time of transfer to the tertiary center. This degree of CK elevation is well beyond what would be expected from mild toxic injury or deconditioning, and is consistent with an active necrotising process. The continued rise in CK over 4 months—despite the patient having received only a single dose of semaglutide and having no ongoing exposure to known myotoxic drugs—was a critical observation that distinguished a self-limited toxic rhabdomyolysis from a sustained immune-mediated process.

Serum aminotransferases were also elevated (AST 191 U/L, ALT 165 U/L). Both AST and ALT are found in skeletal muscle as well as liver, and their elevation in the setting of a markedly abnormal CK raises the important question of whether transaminitis reflects muscle breakdown, hepatic dysfunction, or both. This patient's prior biopsy-proven non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and persistently borderline transaminase elevation years before her myopathy developed indicated a hepatic contribution. The ALT-greater-than-AST pattern noted 3 years earlier, together with a normal platelet count, albumin, bilirubin, and alkaline phosphatase at that time, was consistent with steatohepatitis rather than muscle injury. The key teaching point is that in any patient with a myopathy and elevated transaminases, the clinician must determine whether the liver enzymes are rising from muscle or liver. A muscle source typically produces an AST-to-ALT ratio greater than 3 and a CK that is disproportionately elevated relative to the transaminases, whereas a hepatic source produces a pattern dominated by ALT and is independent of CK. In this case, both processes likely contributed, and the liver biopsy obtained during admission helped confirm this.

The autoantibody workup is central to subclassifying inflammatory myopathies and identifying patients with IMNM. Anti-signal recognition particle (anti-SRP) and anti-3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (anti-HMGCR) are the two myositis-specific autoantibodies most strongly associated with IMNM. Anti-HMGCR antibodies are particularly linked to statin exposure, and their discovery helped reclassify many patients previously labeled as having toxic myopathy or polymyositis. In this patient, a comprehensive myositis panel was negative for anti-SRP and anti-HMGCR, as well as for the antisynthetase antibodies (Jo-1, PL-7, PL-12, EJ, OJ), anti-Mi-2, anti-MDA-5, anti-TIF1-γ, anti-NXP-2, and anti-cN-1A. A negative ANA and negative anti-SSA were also documented. She did have a low-titer anti-SSB antibody (2.4, normal <1.0), but in the absence of ANA and anti-SSA positivity and without clinical features of Sjögren syndrome, this isolated finding was considered incidental and not contributory. This case illustrates an important diagnostic principle: approximately one-third of patients with IMNM are seronegative for known autoantibodies. A negative myositis panel does not exclude IMNM when the clinical and histological features are supportive, and the existence of seronegative cases suggests that additional autoantibodies and triggers remain to be discovered.

Inflammatory markers were mildly elevated, with an ESR of 22 mm/hour and a C-reactal protein of 22.8 mg/L. These modest elevations are consistent with IMNM, which characteristically produces less systemic inflammation than other inflammatory myopathies such as dermatomyositis or antisynthetase syndrome. Complement levels, creatinine, and complete blood counts were normal, helping to exclude concurrent renal failure, cytopenias, or a systemic autoimmune disorder. TSH was normal, ruling out hypothyroid myopathy, a common and reversible cause of CK elevation.

Cardiac biomarkers warrant separate discussion. A high-sensitivity troponin of 62 pg/mL (normal ≤53 pg/mL) and a proBNP of 4358 pg/mL (normal 0–125 pg/mL) were elevated, raising concern for myocardial involvement. Troponin can be elevated from skeletal muscle injury alone, as cardiac and skeletal muscle troponin isoforms share epitopes detected by some assays. However, the magnitude of proBNP elevation suggested possible cardiac strain or dysfunction, and prompted dedicated cardiac imaging, discussed below.

### Musculoskeletal Imaging

MRI of the bilateral femurs revealed mild, symmetric muscular edema most evident in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps muscles, with associated subcutaneous edema along the lateral thighs. Muscle MRI is a valuable noninvasive tool in the evaluation of myopathy because it can detect edema, fatty replacement, and atrophy before these changes are clinically apparent. Edema on T2-weighted sequences with fat suppression or short tau inversion recovery (STIR) indicates active inflammation or necrosis. The proximal and symmetric distribution in this patient's MRI paralleled her clinical proximal weakness, supporting a diffuse myopathic process rather than a focal injury. The degree of edema was described as mild, which is notable because IMNM can sometimes produce striking MRI changes due to extensive necrosis; however, the severity of MRI edema does not always correlate with the degree of histological necrosis, and biopsy remains the definitive diagnostic step.

### Cardiac Evaluation

Given the elevated troponin and proBNP, cardiac evaluation was essential to determine whether the myopathic process extended to the myocardium. An echocardiogram showed a preserved left ventricular ejection fraction of 65%–70% but an elevated right ventricular systolic pressure of 46 mm Hg, suggesting mild pulmonary hypertension. A cardiac MRI demonstrated mild hypokinesis of the mid-anteroseptal and anterior walls with associated myocardial edema but no definite late gadolinium enhancement (LGE). The absence of LGE argued against established myocardial scarring or infarction. Myocardial edema without LGE can represent early myocarditis, but in this clinical context—severe systemic illness, deconditioning, and a recent intensive care unit admission—focal stress-induced cardiomyopathy was also a plausible explanation. The cardiac findings were therefore inconclusive for active myocarditis. The teaching point is that inflammatory myopathies, particularly IMNM and dermatomyositis, can involve the heart, and cardiac MRI is the preferred modality for evaluating myocardial inflammation, edema, and fibrosis. In this patient, the cardiac abnormalities later normalized on repeat echocardiogram and cardiac MRI, which favored a transient process rather than ongoing myocarditis.

### Histopathological Confirmation

The definitive diagnostic procedure in this case was a muscle biopsy of the right vastus lateralis. Histopathology is the cornerstone for diagnosing IMNM and distinguishing it from other inflammatory myopathies and toxic muscle injury.

The biopsy demonstrated dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers, the hallmark of a necrotising myopathy. Unlike polymyositis or dermatomyositis, IMNM is characterized by muscle fiber necrosis as the predominant finding, with only a sparse lymphocytic infiltrate. This paucity of lymphocytes is a key histological distinction; the immune attack in IMNM is thought to be mediated primarily by complement activation and macrophage recruitment rather than direct T-cell cytotoxicity.

Immunohistochemistry provided further critical detail. CD68 staining, a macrophage marker, was positive with evidence of myophagocytosis—macrophages actively engulfing necrotic muscle fiber debris. Acid phosphatase staining, a lysosomal enzyme enriched in macrophages, was also positive. These findings confirm active macrophage-mediated cleanup of necrotic fibers, a process common to many myopathies but expected in IMNM. The membrane attack complex (MAC, C5b-9) deposition was positive on both necrotic and non-necrotic myofibers. MAC deposition on non-necrotic fibers is particularly significant because it indicates complement-mediated injury is actively damaging intact muscle cells, supporting an immune-mediated process rather than purely primary toxic necrosis. Rare CD3-positive T cells were present, and there were perivascular inflammatory cells. The sparse T-cell infiltrate, the presence of MAC deposition, and the predominance of macrophages over lymphocytes collectively established the histological pattern of IMNM.

This biopsy was essential because it established the disease category that directed treatment. A toxic myopathy would be expected to show necrosis with minimal or no immune activation. Polymyositis would show endomysial CD8-positive T cells invading non-necrotic fibers. Dermatomyositis would show perifascicular atrophy and perivascular inflammation with MAC deposition on capillaries. The pattern here—necrosis with macrophage predominance, complement deposition, and minimal lymphocytic infiltration—fit IMNM.

### Liver Biopsy and Transaminitis

A repeat liver biopsy was performed during the admission to clarify the source of the patient's transaminitis and to assess progression of her known NAFLD. The biopsy showed macrovesicular steatosis involving approximately 70% of the submitted parenchyma, with no significant portal inflammation or interface hepatitis. These findings were consistent with progression of her underlying steatohepatitis (NAFLD Activity Score grade 5/8, stage 1) and showed no evidence of autoimmune hepatitis or drug-induced liver injury.

This biopsy was diagnostically valuable for two reasons. First, it confirmed that the patient's transaminase elevation had a substantial hepatic component independent of her muscle disease. This is important because transaminase elevation in a myopathy patient can be misattributed entirely to muscle breakdown, potentially leading to unnecessary hepatologic evaluation or delayed recognition of a myopathy if CK is not simultaneously checked. Second, the absence of interface hepatitis, autoimmune serology, and other features of autoimmune liver disease narrowed the evaluation and supported the conclusion that her hepatic and muscular processes were distinct. The teaching point is that when evaluating a patient with both transaminitis and myopathy, the clinician should always consider dual pathology and use CK-to-transaminase ratios, imaging, and biopsy when necessary to disentangle the contributions of each organ.

### Integrating the Workup

Taken together, the diagnostic workup established IMNM through the convergence of characteristic clinical features (progressive proximal weakness, 12-fold CK rise), compatible imaging (symmetric proximal muscle edema), and confirmatory histology (necrotic fibers, macrophage predominance, complement deposition). The negative myositis panel placed this patient in the seronegative IMNM subset, which does not preclude the diagnosis. The cardiac and hepatic evaluations were essential not to diagnose IMNM itself, but to define the extent of involvement and exclude alternative or overlapping processes. The comprehensive workup allowed the team to proceed with confidence to immunosuppressive therapy, which is discussed in the treatment section.
