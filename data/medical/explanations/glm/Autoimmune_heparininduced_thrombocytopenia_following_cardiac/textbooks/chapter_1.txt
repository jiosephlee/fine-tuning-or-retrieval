Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and Preoperative Context

The patient was a man in his 70s who presented with a two-year history of exertional dyspnea and longstanding atrial fibrillation, for which he was anticoagulated with oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily. A previously undetected systolic murmur prompted further cardiac evaluation. Diagnostic heart catheterization revealed 60% stenosis in the obtuse marginal coronary artery, moderate aortic stenosis, and a dilated ascending aorta—a constellation of findings that indicated the need for combined cardiac surgery. This patient's comorbidities and the planned complexity of his surgery are relevant context, as cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) is the clinical setting in which heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) most commonly occurs, with an estimated frequency of 0.5%–1.0% depending on the type and duration of postoperative heparin thromboprophylaxis.

A critical detail in the preoperative course emerged from the heart catheterization. The patient had not held his apixaban before the procedure, which influenced the cardiologist's approach in two important ways. First, the procedure was performed via a transradial artery approach rather than the more common femoral approach, likely to reduce bleeding risk in the setting of continued anticoagulation. Second, a lower-than-usual intraprocedural dose of unfractionated heparin (UFH)—only 2000 units—was administered. While this dose was small compared with the heparin doses used during cardiac surgery, it was sufficient to serve as an immunizing exposure. This preoperative UFH administration would prove to be the key to understanding the unusually early onset of thrombocytopenia that followed, as it primed the patient's immune system before the much larger intraoperative heparin exposure one week later.

The cardiac surgery was performed one week after catheterization using CPB, with anticoagulation achieved using 45,000 units of UFH—a standard high-dose regimen for cardiac surgery. The procedure included aortic valve replacement with a bioprosthesis, replacement of the ascending aorta with a Dacron graft, a single coronary artery bypass using a saphenous vein graft to the obtuse marginal coronary artery, and left atrial appendage excision. The operation proceeded without major blood loss or transfusion requirement, and the patient was easily separated from cardiopulmonary bypass with excellent hemodynamics. The uncomplicated intraoperative course makes the subsequent dramatic platelet decline all the more notable, as it argues against perioperative events such as major hemorrhage or protamine-related platelet activation as explanations for the thrombocytopenia.

The postoperative platelet count trajectory is central to understanding this case. The immediate preoperative platelet count was 210×10⁹/L, within the normal reference range of 150–400×10⁹/L. On postoperative day 1 (POD1), the platelet count was 155×10⁹/L, representing a relatively modest early decline consistent with the expected hemodilution and consumption associated with cardiac surgery and CPB. Routine postoperative thromboprophylaxis with low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) was commenced on POD1, with 5000 units of dalteparin administered subcutaneously, followed by higher doses (7500 units) on POD2, POD3, and POD4. However, from POD1 onward, the platelet count progressively and unexpectedly declined, reaching 50×10⁹/L on POD5—a level far below what would be expected from routine post-cardiac surgery platelet count recovery alone. This pattern of a precipitous fall to a profoundly low platelet count by POD5, accompanied by thrombotic stroke, is characteristic of autoimmune HIT rather than classic HIT, as discussed in subsequent sections.

The timing of the platelet count decline deserves special attention. Classic HIT typically manifests between POD5 and POD10 in patients who have undergone cardiac surgery, reflecting the approximately 5-day delay required for a primary immune response against PF4/heparin complexes. Onset before POD5 is unusual, occurring in only about 10% of cardiac surgery patients who develop HIT. When early-onset HIT does occur, it is characteristically linked to recent preoperative heparin exposure, which pre-sensitizes the patient's immune system. In this patient, the 2000-unit UFH dose during heart catheterization one week before surgery served as the sensitizing event, enabling a rapid anamnestic immune response upon re-exposure to the large intraoperative heparin dose. This temporal relationship between preoperative heparin exposure and early postoperative platelet decline is a critical diagnostic clue that the clinician must actively seek when evaluating suspected early-onset HIT.

On POD5, the absence of new focal neurological findings initially provided reassurance, but worsening postoperative delirium combined with a further platelet count decline to 26×10⁹/L on POD6 (confirmed at 24×10⁹/L on repeat testing) prompted computed tomography (CT) imaging of the brain. This imaging revealed an acute cerebral infarct in the left parietal lobe, with an area of hypodensity measuring approximately 3.2 cm × 1.8 cm and extending to the cerebral cortex. The discovery of thrombotic stroke in the setting of severe, otherwise unexplained thrombocytopenia is a hallmark clinical presentation of HIT. HIT is paradoxical among drug-induced thrombocytopenias because it is strongly associated with thrombosis rather than bleeding, with a relative risk for thrombosis of approximately 15. The combination of a falling platelet count and new thrombosis—whether arterial, venous, or microvascular—should always raise clinical suspicion for HIT, even when the temporal pattern is atypical, as it was in this case with onset before POD5. The parietal stroke observed here represents the thrombotic complication that, together with the platelet count decline, solidified the clinical suspicion for HIT and prompted laboratory testing for HIT antibodies.
