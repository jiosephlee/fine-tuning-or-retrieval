Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

Chapter 2: ## Differential Diagnosis and Initial Laboratory Evaluation

When a patient develops severe thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery, the clinician faces a broad differential diagnosis that spans benign expected postoperative changes, common serious complications, and rare but dangerous immune-mediated disorders. In this case, the platelet count fell from 155×10⁹/L on postoperative day 1 (POD1) to 50×10⁹/L on POD5 — a decline too steep and too rapid to dismiss as routine. The systematic evaluation that followed illustrates a logical, stepwise approach to post-cardiac-surgery thrombocytopenia, one that integrates the clinical picture, routine laboratory data, and careful temporal reasoning to arrive at the correct diagnosis.

### Ruling Out Common Causes

The most frequent explanations for severe thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery are those related to the patient's overall clinical condition. Cardiogenic shock and critical illness can produce thrombocytopenia through mechanisms such as disseminated intravascular coagulation, platelet consumption at sites of endothelial injury, or bone marrow suppression from systemic inflammation. Infection — whether bloodstream, urinary, or pulmonary — can similarly drive thrombocytopenia through sepsis-related consumptive processes. Both of these explanations were quickly evaluated and discarded in this patient. His hemodynamics were excellent: he had come off cardiopulmonary bypass without difficulty, and he was clinically stable apart from postoperative delirium. Blood and urine cultures were drawn and ultimately returned negative, and the chest X-ray showed no major abnormalities. With normal hemodynamics, absence of fever or other infectious signs, and negative cultures, both critical-illness thrombocytopenia and infection-related thrombocytopenia were effectively excluded.

### Ruling Out Rare Causes

Several rare entities were also considered and systematically ruled out. Post-transfusion purpura (PTP) is a dramatic immune-mediated thrombocytopenia that typically occurs 5–10 days after transfusion, caused by alloantibodies against platelet antigens such as HPA-1a. However, this patient received no blood transfusions during or after surgery, making PTP impossible. Post-surgical thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) is another rare postoperative complication, characterized by severe ADAMTS13 deficiency leading to microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and organ damage. In this patient, however, the initial laboratory markers did not support hemolysis: haptoglobin was elevated (3.19 g/L; reference range 0.32–1.97 g/L), the reticulocyte count was unremarkable, and the early peripheral blood film showed no schistocytes. The elevated haptoglobin is consistent with the early postoperative state, where haptoglobin rises as part of the acute-phase response and actually argues against active intravascular hemolysis. Drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia was also considered, but the temporal pattern excluded this possibility: drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia typically requires 5–10 days of exposure to a newly introduced drug before antibodies form and destroy platelets. The platelet count fall began on POD1, far too early to be attributable to any postoperative medication. The drugs given only on the day of surgery (vancomycin, cefazolin, tranexamic acid) and those given postoperatively (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, midodrine) were all newly started within a timeframe that would not allow an immune-mediated drug reaction to manifest by POD1–POD5.

### Initial Laboratory Workup

The complete blood count on POD5 provided essential diagnostic information beyond the platelet count itself. The hemoglobin was 97 g/L, similar to values obtained over the preceding three days, consistent with expected early surgical blood losses without ongoing hemorrhage. The white blood cell count was normal at 7.8×10⁹/L with a normal differential, which helped exclude systemic infection or a leukemoid reaction. A normal white cell count and differential in the setting of isolated severe thrombocytopenia narrows the differential considerably — it makes marrow failure syndromes and systemic infection less likely and points toward a process selectively consuming or destroying platelets.

### Coagulation Studies

Coagulation studies were performed to assess whether the thrombocytopenia might be part of a broader consumptive coagulopathy. The prothrombin time was normal, corresponding to an international normalized ratio (INR) of 1.1, and the partial thromboplastin time (PTT) was normal at 34 seconds. These normal results indicated that the clotting factor pathways were intact and that there was no significant disseminated intravascular coagulation at this early stage. The fibrinogen level, however, was elevated at 6.59 g/L (reference range 2.00–3.93 g/L). This is an expected finding in the early postoperative period because fibrinogen is an acute-phase reactant, and levels typically rise substantially after major surgery. The elevated fibrinogen is therefore not pathological in isolation but serves as an important baseline value against which subsequent changes can be measured — as will be seen later, the progressive fall in fibrinogen during the following days provided critical evidence of worsening consumptive coagulopathy.

### Blood Film Review

On POD7, peripheral blood film review confirmed the presence of true thrombocytopenia by demonstrating the absence of platelet clumps, thereby excluding pseudothrombocytopenia — a spurious low platelet count caused by EDTA-dependent platelet agglutination in the collection tube. Importantly, the film at this early stage showed no schistocytes or "toxic" white blood cell changes. The absence of schistocytes initially argued against microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (as seen in TTP or disseminated intravascular coagulation) at this point in the illness, though schistocytes did appear later (POD9–POD16) as evidence of severe HIT-associated consumptive coagulopathy developed.

### Temporal Reasoning and the Key Insight: Preoperative Heparin Exposure

With the common and rare causes of postoperative thrombocytopenia systematically excluded, attention turned to HIT. However, a critical diagnostic challenge remained: the temporal profile of the platelet count decline did not fit the expected pattern for classic HIT after cardiac surgery. Classic HIT typically begins between POD5 and POD10, reflecting the 5–10 days required for the immune response to generate clinically significant levels of anti-PF4/heparin IgG antibodies after a new heparin exposure. This patient's platelet count was already falling on POD1 — too early to be explained by the large dose of unfractionated heparin (45,000 U) given intraoperatively during cardiopulmonary bypass on the day of surgery.

The resolution of this puzzle came from careful review of the patient's preoperative history. One week before cardiac surgery, the patient had undergone heart catheterization, during which he received 2000 U of UFH via the transradial artery approach. This preoperative heparin exposure provided the necessary antecedent immune priming event. The concept of "early-onset HIT" in patients with recent preoperative heparin exposure is well established: when a patient has been exposed to heparin within the preceding 5–100 days, HIT antibodies may already be present or rapidly boosting at the time of subsequent heparin re-exposure, so the platelet count fall can begin immediately — even before POD5. Only about 10% of post-cardiac-surgery HIT cases exhibit such early onset, and it is invariably linked to recent preoperative heparin exposure, as was documented here. The combination of early-onset thrombocytopenia, the strong clinical suspicion of thrombotic stroke on imaging, and this identifiable preoperative heparin exposure made HIT the most probable diagnosis.

### Decision to Send HIT Antibody Testing

Based on this integrated reasoning, a blood sample drawn on POD5 was sent to a reference laboratory for HIT antibody testing. This decision followed from the convergence of three factors: (1) exclusion of alternative explanations for postoperative thrombocytopenia; (2) identification of the temporal pattern as consistent with early-onset HIT given the preoperative heparin exposure; and (3) the concurrent finding of an acute cerebral infarct on brain imaging, which raised the clinical stakes and strengthened the need to confirm or exclude a thrombophilic disorder. The workup now shifted from the broad differential diagnosis of postoperative thrombocytopenia to specific laboratory confirmation of HIT — a step that, as the subsequent investigations revealed, would itself yield surprising and diagnostically challenging results.
