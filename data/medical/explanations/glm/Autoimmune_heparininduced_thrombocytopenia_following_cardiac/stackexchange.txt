Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

### [Clinical Pearls] What is the typical temporal window for the onset of HIT-related thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery, and what preoperative event in this patient explained the unusually early platelet count decline that began before postoperative day 5?

The typical temporal window for the onset of HIT-related thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery is between postoperative days 5 and 10 (inclusive). This timing reflects the ~5-day minimum required for generation of pathogenic anti-PF4/heparin IgG antibodies following a *first* heparin exposure. When thrombocytopenia begins before POD5 in a post-cardiac surgery patient, a preoperative heparin exposure within the prior ~1–2 weeks should be suspected, as this prior exposure "primes" the immune system such that re-exposure to heparin during surgery triggers an anamnestic antibody response and accelerated platelet activation.

In this patient, the unusually early platelet count decline—beginning on POD1 and reaching 50 × 10⁹/L by POD5—was explained by intraprocedural unfractionated heparin (2000 U) administered during transradial cardiac catheterization performed one week before surgery. This preoperative UFH exposure initiated anti-PF4/heparin antibody formation, so that the large intraoperative heparin dose (45,000 U for cardiopulmonary bypass) served as a rapid secondary immunizing stimulus, producing a "rapid-onset HIT" phenotype rather than the more typical 5- to 10-day postoperative onset. Only about 10% of cardiac surgery patients who develop HIT exhibit a platelet count fall before POD5, and prior preoperative heparin exposure is the key explanatory factor, as was documented here.

### [Pathophysiology] In classic HIT, IgG antibodies form immune complexes with heparin–PF4 that activate platelets through FcγIIa receptors in a strictly heparin-dependent fashion. What is the key pathophysiologic feature that distinguishes autoimmune HIT from classic HIT, and how does it explain the serotonin release assay finding of 96% serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin?

## Pathophysiologic Distinction: Autoimmune HIT vs. Classic HIT

The key distinguishing pathophysiologic feature of autoimmune HIT (aHIT) is that the patient's IgG anti-PF4 antibodies can form platelet-activating immune complexes **independently of heparin**. In classic HIT, heparin serves as an obligate cofactor: it binds PF4 and induces a conformational change that creates the neoepitope recognized by anti-PF4 antibodies. Immune complex formation, FcγIIa receptor clustering on platelets, and subsequent platelet activation all require heparin to be present. This is why the serotonin release assay (SRA) in classic HIT shows minimal release at 0 U/mL heparin—the immune complexes simply cannot assemble without the polyanionic cofactor.

In aHIT, however, the antibodies recognize PF4 in a conformation that permits immune complex formation and robust FcγIIa receptor engagement **even in the absence of heparin**. The antibodies effectively stabilize PF4 tetramers in a configuration that mimics the heparin-bound state, or bind with sufficient affinity that PF4 alone is enough to form the activating complex. This heparin-independent platelet activation is the hallmark that defines aHIT as a distinct pathophysiologic entity.

This directly explains the SRA finding in this case: 96% serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin indicates that the patient's serum activated donor platelets almost maximally without any heparin present. In classic HIT, this condition would yield less than 20% release. The near-complete release at 0 U/mL heparin tells us the antibodies are activating platelets through their own interaction with PF4, unaided by exogenous heparin.

An additional novel feature of this case further reinforces the severity of the autoimmune phenomenon: the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3 produced only partial inhibition of serotonin release (76% release persisted at 0.1 U/mL heparin despite IV.3), rather than the near-complete inhibition expected in classic HIT. This indicates that the patient's antibodies were sufficiently potent in clustering FcγIIa receptors that even receptor blockade could not fully abrogate activation—a serological profile not previously reported in aHIT and consistent with the unusually severe clinical course observed, including profound thrombocytopenia (nadir 13 × 10⁹/L), consumptive coagulopathy with falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimer, and schistocyte formation.

In summary: the 96% serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin is the laboratory signature of heparin-independent platelet activation—the defining pathophysiologic feature that separates aHIT from the strictly heparin-dependent immune complex formation of classic HIT.

### [Morning Report] The serotonin release assay in this patient showed strong release at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin, but the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3 only partially inhibited platelet activation, with 76% release persisting at 0.1 U/mL heparin rather than the expected <20%. What does this atypical IV.3 resistance pattern suggest about the patient's HIT antibodies, and why might it lead to an initially 'indeterminate' SRA result?

## Clinical Teaching Answer

In the standard serotonin release assay (SRA), the Fc receptor–blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3 serves as a critical specificity control. In classic heparin-dependent HIT, IgG–PF4–heparin immune complexes cross-link platelet FcγIIa receptors, and IV.3 should abolish this activation, reducing serotonin release to less than 20%. When the reporting laboratory observed 76% release persisting despite IV.3 at 0.1 U/mL heparin, this violated a key criterion used to confirm that platelet activation was truly HIT-antibody mediated. The result was therefore designated "indeterminate," as it did not fit the laboratory's validated interpretive algorithm for a definitively positive SRA.

The explanation lies in the unusually severe autoimmune phenotype of this patient's HIT antibodies. The strong serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin (96%) already signaled heparin-independent platelet activation — the hallmark of autoimmune HIT (aHIT) — indicating that these antibodies recognize and activate platelets through PF4 in the absence of heparin. The partial IV.3 resistance extends this concept further: the antibodies were so potent, or present at such high functional titers, that they could continue to activate platelets even when Fc receptor cross-linking was pharmacologically blocked. In other words, the patient's antibodies appear to engage FcγIIa receptors with such high affinity or avidity that IV.3 only partially disrupts the interaction, rather than fully preventing it as it would with typical HIT antibodies.

This pattern has two important implications. First, it underscores a spectrum of antibody pathogenicity within aHIT, where some antibodies exhibit not only heparin-independent activation but also partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade — suggesting an exceptionally prothrombotic antibody profile. Second, it illustrates a diagnostic pitfall: an "indeterminate" SRA driven by IV.3 resistance should not exclude HIT, particularly when other features — strong-positive ELISA, heparin-independent platelet activation, thrombosis, and severe thrombocytopenia with consumptive coagulopathy — point toward aHIT. Clinicians and laboratory teams should recognize that IV.3 resistance may itself be a marker of an unusually severe aHIT subtype rather than evidence against the diagnosis.

### [Management] This patient was initially anticoagulated with argatroban and then switched to therapeutic-dose fondaparinux on postoperative day 14. What clinical and laboratory findings prompted this transition, and why was antithrombin concentrate administered before initiating fondaparinux?

## Clinical and Laboratory Findings Prompting the Transition to Fondaparinux

Several converging indicators of **ongoing, poorly controlled HIT-associated consumptive coagulopathy** during argatroban therapy prompted the switch to therapeutic-dose fondaparinux on postoperative day 14 (POD14). Specifically, serial laboratory monitoring revealed **progressively falling fibrinogen levels** (nadir 2.49 g/L by POD17, down from markedly elevated early postoperative values), **rising d-dimer levels** peaking around POD12, and the **new appearance of schistocytes** on peripheral blood film from POD9 to POD16. Together, these findings indicated that argatroban was not adequately controlling the profound prothrombotic state characteristic of autoimmune HIT (aHIT). The clinical picture was further supported by an **incomplete platelet count recovery** after high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin—the count rose only from a nadir of 13 × 10⁹/L (POD7) to an interim peak of 54 × 10⁹/L (POD14)—far less brisk than typically observed when IVIG is administered for aHIT. Given these signs of persistent hypercoagulability, combined with the practical advantage of once-daily subcutaneous administration, fondaparinux was selected for more potent, sustained factor Xa inhibition.

## Rationale for Antithrombin Concentrate Administration

Fondaparinux is an **indirect, antithrombin (AT)-dependent inhibitor of factor Xa**; it requires adequate circulating AT to exert its anticoagulant effect. In this patient, the **pre-infusion AT level was mildly reduced at 0.70 U/mL** (reference range, 0.80–1.40 U/mL), likely a consequence of the severe ongoing consumptive coagulopathy. Because therapeutic efficacy of fondaparinux would be compromised in the setting of AT deficiency, **1000 U of AT concentrate was administered prior to the first fondaparinux dose** to ensure sufficient AT activity for effective anticoagulation. This preemptive supplementation reflects an important pharmacologic principle: indirect factor Xa inhibitors, unlike direct thrombin inhibitors such as argatroban, are dependent on a functional AT cofactor, and in the setting of consumptive coagulopathy with depleted AT, supplementation may be necessary to achieve therapeutic anticoagulation.

### [Differential Diagnosis] What are the major diagnostic considerations for severe thrombocytopenia occurring after cardiac surgery, and what specific clinical and laboratory features of this case made alternative diagnoses such as cardiogenic shock, infection, post-transfusion purpura, post-surgical TTP, and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia less likely?

## Major Diagnostic Considerations for Severe Thrombocytopenia After Cardiac Surgery

Severe thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery demands a systematic differential diagnosis. The major considerations include cardiogenic shock or critical illness–associated thrombocytopenia, infection/sepsis, post-transfusion purpura (PTP), post-surgical thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia (DIT), and heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) — including its autoimmune subtype (aHIT). In this case, several clinical and laboratory features systematically narrowed the differential and pointed toward HIT, specifically aHIT triggered by preoperative heparin exposure.

**Cardiogenic shock and critical illness** were effectively excluded by the patient's stable postoperative course. He came off cardiopulmonary bypass with excellent hemodynamics, and there was no clinical evidence of hypoperfusion or multiorgan dysfunction to explain a platelet count falling to 13 × 10⁹/L.

**Infection** was unlikely given negative blood and urine cultures, a normal white blood cell count (7.8 × 10⁹/L) with normal differential, and an unremarkable chest X-ray. No fever or other clinical signs of sepsis were present.

**Post-transfusion purpura** was excluded by the explicit history that no blood transfusions were administered during or after surgery, eliminating the requisite antigen exposure for this antibody-mediated syndrome.

**Post-surgical TTP** was considered but made unlikely by an elevated haptoglobin (3.19 g/L) and the initial absence of schistocytes on the peripheral blood film. While schistocytes later appeared from POD9 to POD16, these were attributed to severe HIT-associated consumptive coagulopathy — supported by concurrently falling fibrinogen and rising D-dimer levels — rather than to a primary thrombotic microangiopathy.

**Drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia** was unlikely on temporal grounds. The platelet count decline was already underway from POD1, far too early for immune-mediated destruction from newly introduced postoperative medications (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, midodrine) or even intraoperative agents (vancomycin, cefazolin, tranexamic acid), which typically require days to a week of exposure before triggering thrombocytopenia.

The remaining diagnosis — HIT — was strongly supported by three converging features: the strong-positive PF4/polyanion ELISA (2.2 OD units), the occurrence of thrombotic stroke (consistent with HIT's prothrombotic nature), and the characteristic SRA pattern showing maximal platelet activation at therapeutic heparin concentrations with inhibition at supratherapeutic levels. Although the onset before POD5 was earlier than expected for typical post-cardiac-surgery HIT, subsequent review identified preoperative UFH exposure during transradial cardiac catheterization one week prior, providing the sensitizing event. The atypical SRA features — strong serotonin release even in the absence of heparin and only partial inhibition by Fc receptor blockade with monoclonal antibody IV.3 — ultimately established the diagnosis of autoimmune HIT, a severe subtype capable of producing the profound thrombocytopenia and consumptive coagulopathy observed in this patient.

### [Management] What is the rationale for administering high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin in autoimmune HIT, and what does this patient's suboptimal platelet count response—an interim peak of only 54×10⁹/L on POD14 despite three 100-g doses—suggest about the severity of his autoimmune HIT?

## Rationale for High-Dose IVIG and Interpretation of the Suboptimal Response

In autoimmune HIT (aHIT), pathogenic IgG antibodies directed against PF4 can activate platelets through FcγIIa receptors even in the absence of heparin, as demonstrated by this patient's strong serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin (96%). Simply discontinuing heparin therefore does not eliminate the platelet-activating stimulus. High-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) is administered to saturate Fcγ receptors on platelets and reticuloendothelial cells, thereby competitively blocking binding of HIT antibody immune complexes and interrupting the Fc receptor–mediated platelet activation loop. In typical aHIT, this maneuver produces a brisk platelet count rise, often within days.

This patient's platelet count rose only from a nadir of 13×10⁹/L (POD7) to an interim peak of 54×10⁹/L (POD14), despite receiving three 100-g IVIG doses—a response far blunter than expected. This suboptimal recovery signals an unusually severe phenotype of aHIT, consistent with the unique laboratory finding in this case: only partial inhibition of serotonin release by the Fc receptor–blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3 (76% release at 0.1 U/mL heparin in the presence of IV.3, rather than suppression below 20%). In other words, the in vitro resistance to Fc receptor blockade directly parallels the in vivo resistance to IVIG, indicating that this patient's antibodies activated platelets through mechanisms or with potency that Fc receptor saturation alone could not fully suppress. The concurrent decline in fibrinogen, rising d-dimer, and appearance of schistocytes during this period further confirmed ongoing, robust HIT-associated consumptive coagulopathy despite anticoagulation with argatroban. Taken together, the attenuated platelet count response to high-dose IVIG identifies this as an exceptionally severe form of aHIT, one in which the antibody's heparin-independent platelet-activating capacity exceeded what standard immunomodulatory blockade could contain.

### [Pathophysiology] During this patient's course, he exhibited progressively falling fibrinogen levels (nadir 2.49 g/L), rising d-dimer levels peaking around POD12, and the transient appearance of schistocytes on peripheral blood film from POD9 to POD16. How do these findings relate to the pathophysiology of severe autoimmune HIT, and what do they indicate about the degree of HIT-associated consumptive coagulopathy despite ongoing anticoagulation?

## Falling Fibrinogen, Rising D-Dimer, and Schistocytes in Severe Autoimmune HIT

In autoimmune HIT (aHIT), HIT antibodies exhibit heparin-independent platelet-activating properties, meaning they trigger platelet activation through FcγRIIA receptor clustering even in the absence of heparin. This sustained platelet activation generates procoagulant microparticles, leading to explosive thrombin generation and a systemic prothrombotic state. In this patient, the antibody phenotype was particularly severe—serotonin-release assay showed strong platelet activation at 0 U/mL heparin and only partial inhibition by the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3—indicating unusually potent antibody-platelet interactions that overwhelmed standard protective mechanisms.

The triad of falling fibrinogen, rising d-dimer, and schistocytes reflects the downstream consumptive coagulopathy driven by this uncontrolled platelet activation. Fibrinogen, an acute-phase reactant, is expected to rise markedly in the early postoperative period; this patient's initial fibrinogen was 6.59 g/L. The progressive decline to a nadir of 2.49 g/L by POD17 is therefore pathologic, indicating ongoing fibrinogen consumption through continuous thrombin generation and fibrin clot formation that outstripped hepatic acute-phase synthesis. The simultaneously rising d-dimer, peaking around POD12, confirms active intravascular coagulation with secondary fibrinolysis. The transient appearance of schistocytes from POD9 to POD16 indicates microangiopathic hemolysis—mechanical fragmentation of red cells as they traverse microvessels partially occluded by platelet-fibrin thrombi.

Critically, these abnormalities progressed despite therapeutic-dose argatroban initiated on POD7. The direct thrombin inhibitor neutralizes fluid-phase thrombin but does not address the upstream platelet activation driving the consumptive process. In severe aHIT, the sheer intensity of platelet activation—amplified by the heparin-independent and IV.3-resistant antibody phenotype—can sustain a level of procoagulant activity that surpasses what a direct thrombin inhibitor alone can counter. The persistence of these coagulation markers was thus interpreted as evidence of insufficient control of the HIT-associated hypercoagulability, directly motivating the switch to therapeutic-dose fondaparinux on POD14, supplemented with antithrombin concentrate given the mildly reduced antithrombin level of 0.70 U/mL.

Taken together, these three laboratory findings constituted indirect evidence of the depth of the consumptive coagulopathy: fibrinogen consumption, active thrombin generation with fibrinolysis, and microvascular thrombosis with red cell shearing. They signaled that the aHIT antibody-platelet interaction was driving a DIC-like state that remained incompletely suppressed despite ongoing anticoagulation, underscoring both the severity of this autoimmune phenotype and the rationale for intensifying the anticoagulant strategy.
