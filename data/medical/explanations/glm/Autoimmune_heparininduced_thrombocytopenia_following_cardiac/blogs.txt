Title: Blog about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

# Before Day 5: When HIT Comes Early After Cardiac Surgery

## The Trap We All Know Too Well

Every medical student learns the same HIT timeline: platelet count falls 5–10 days after heparin exposure, nadir typically above 30 × 10⁹/L, and thrombosis is a feared complication. When a post-cardiac surgery patient's platelets are dropping by postoperative day 3 or 4, most of us mentally file that under "post-CPB thrombocytopenia" and move on. But what if that early drop *is* HIT—and not just ordinary HIT, but autoimmune HIT with heparin-independent platelet activation and a platelet count crashing toward 13 × 10⁹/L?

That is exactly what happened in a recently published case from Warkentin and colleagues, and it offers a masterclass in how the classic "day 5 to 10" teaching can lull us into a false sense of security if we don't ask one critical question: *Did this patient receive heparin before surgery?*

## The Case: A Straightforward Operation, a Complicated Course

A man in his 70s with atrial fibrillation on apixaban presented for elective cardiac surgery—aortic valve replacement, ascending aorta graft replacement, single-vessel CABG, and left atrial appendage excision. The operation went beautifully: uncomplicated cardiopulmonary bypass, no major blood loss, excellent hemodynamics coming off pump. The preoperative platelet count was 210 × 10⁹/L, dropping to a fairly typical 155 × 10⁹/L on POD1. Nothing alarming yet.

Then the platelet count kept falling: 50 × 10⁹/L by POD5, 26 × 10⁹/L by POD6, and a stunning nadir of 13 × 10⁹/L by POD7. Dalteparin thromboprophylaxis had been started on POD1 and stopped when the fall became apparent, switched to prophylactic fondaparinux on POD5. A CT brain obtained for worsening delirium revealed an acute left parietal infarct. The PF4/polyanion ELISA came back strongly positive at 2.2 OD units. HIT was now the leading diagnosis—but the timing seemed wrong, and the serotonin-release assay (SRA) initially returned as "indeterminate," a result that could have derailed the diagnosis entirely.

## The Missing Piece: Preoperative Heparin at Cath

The key to understanding the early onset lay in a detail that was only uncovered on subsequent inquiry. One week before surgery, the patient underwent diagnostic heart catheterization. Because he had not held his apixaban, the cardiologist used a transradial approach and administered a reduced intraprocedural heparin dose of 2000 U UFH. That exposure—small, forgotten, seemingly insignificant—was enough to prime the immune system. When the patient received 45,000 U of UFH during CPB a week later, the anamnestic immune response was already loaded and ready. The platelet count began falling on POD1, well before the classic HIT window.

This is the critical teaching point: only about 10% of post-cardiac surgery HIT cases present before POD5, and virtually all of these are linked to recent preoperative heparin exposure. The "day 5 to 10" rule assumes the *current* heparin course is the *first* immunizing exposure. When there has been a preceding exposure within the prior few weeks, the immune system is already sensitized, and the platelet fall can begin almost immediately upon re-exposure—sometimes within 24 hours, indistinguishable in timing from benign post-CPB thrombocytopenia.

## When "Indeterminate" Actually Means Autoimmune HIT

The SRA in this case showed several striking abnormalities that collectively pointed to autoimmune HIT (aHIT), a subtype in which antibodies activate platelets even in the absence of heparin. The patient's serum caused 96% serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin—diagnostic of heparin-independent platelet activation. Release was appropriately suppressed at supratherapeutic heparin concentrations (100 U/mL), consistent with HIT antibodies rather than a nonspecific platelet activator. But then came the truly novel finding: the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3, which normally abolishes HIT-mediated platelet activation, only *partially* inhibited the reaction. Serotonin release persisted at 76% despite IV.3, far above the usual threshold of <20%. To the authors' knowledge, this partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade has never been previously reported in aHIT. An initial laboratory readout of "indeterminate" could easily have led a clinician to dismiss HIT and pursue alternative diagnoses—a potentially catastrophic error in a patient with active cerebral infarction and a platelet count of 13 × 10⁹/L.

## A Severe, Prolonged Course — and a Treatment Escalation

The severity of this case extended well beyond the platelet nadir. During argatroban anticoagulation, fibrinogen levels progressively fell (nadir 2.49 g/L by POD17), D-dimer levels peaked around POD12, and schistocytes appeared on peripheral blood film from POD9 to POD16. These findings signaled ongoing, severe HIT-associated consumptive coagulopathy despite therapeutic anticoagulation. High-dose IVIG was administered—100 g on two consecutive days, then a third 100 g dose when the platelet count failed to rise briskly after the initial doses. The platelet response was modest and delayed, peaking at only 54 × 10⁹/L by POD14, far less than the robust recovery typically seen with IVIG in aHIT. This blunted response further underscored the unusually severe antibody phenotype.

Given persistent hypercoagulability on argatroban, the team switched to therapeutic-dose fondaparinux (10 mg subcutaneously once daily) on POD14, preceded by antithrombin concentrate supplementation due to a mildly reduced AT level of 0.70 U/mL. This transition addressed both practical concerns—once-daily subcutaneous dosing versus PTT-titrated infusion—and biochemical evidence that argatroban was insufficient to control the consumptive process. Over the following weeks, the platelet count gradually recovered to 155 × 10⁹/L by POD62 and 202 × 10⁹/L by POD87. The patient made a complete neurological recovery. Fondaparinux was continued until POD65, at which point apixaban was resumed for long-term anticoagulation.

## Why This Case Matters for Your Next Postop Patient

The central lesson is not simply that HIT can occur before day 5—most seasoned clinicians have heard this at some point. The lesson is that *the specific preoperative history changes everything about your pre-test probability*. A post-cardiac surgery patient with platelets falling on POD2 or POD3 is common and usually benign. But if that same patient had a cardiac catheterization with heparin a week before surgery, the early platelet fall is suddenly much harder to dismiss. The standard temporal rules assume a single immunizing exposure; when there are two, the clock starts earlier than you think.

Beyond timing, this case also reminds us that aHIT exists, that it can produce platelet counts far lower than classic HIT, that it can cause stroke, and that the SRA profile may look bizarre enough to be called "indeterminate" by a laboratory that does not regularly encounter the pattern. When the clinical picture screams HIT but the lab says "indeterminate," pick up the phone, ask for the raw SRA data, and specifically inquire about serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin and the degree of IV.3 inhibition. The diagnosis may depend on it.

## Key Take-Home Points

- **Always ask about preoperative heparin exposure.** Recent UFH exposure (even low-dose, even during a radial cardiac cath) can pre-prime the immune system, causing HIT onset before the classic POD5–10 window. This applies to roughly 10% of post-cardiac surgery HIT cases and should be specifically screened for in every early postop thrombocytopenia evaluation.
- **Early platelet fall after CPB is usually benign—but not always.** When the platelet count continues to fall progressively beyond POD1–2, especially into a severe range, HIT moves higher on the differential regardless of timing.
- **Autoimmune HIT can activate platelets without heparin.** Look for strong serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin on the SRA. An "indeterminate" SRA result in a patient with a strongly positive ELISA and a compatible clinical picture should prompt direct discussion with the reference laboratory.
- **Profound thrombocytopenia (nadir <20 × 10⁹/L) in HIT suggests aHIT.** Classic HIT typically bottoms out above 30 × 10⁹/L. When you see 13 × 10⁹/L with a strong PF4 ELISA, think autoimmune phenotype and consider IVIG early.
- **Monitor fibrinogen, D-dimer, and peripheral smear during treatment.** Falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimer, and new schistocytes signal ongoing consumptive coagulopathy and may warrant escalation from argatroban to fondaparinux, particularly if IVIG response is blunted.
- **Partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade on SRA is a newly described aHIT pattern.** Awareness of this phenotype can prevent a missed diagnosis when the laboratory reports an atypical or indeterminate activation profile.



# Autoimmune HIT: When Antibodies Don't Need Heparin to Cause Havoc

## When "Typical HIT" Behaves Atypically

Picture this: a man in his 70s undergoes uneventful cardiac surgery — aortic valve replacement, ascending aorta graft, single CABG, left atrial appendage excision. He comes off pump with excellent hemodynamics. No major blood loss, no transfusions. Platelets drop from 210 to 155 × 10⁹/L on POD1 — par for the course after cardiac surgery. But then the platelet count keeps falling: 50 × 10⁹/L by POD5, 26 × 10⁹/L by POD6, and eventually a nadir of 13 × 10⁹/L. CT brain reveals an acute parietal stroke. The PF4 ELISA comes back strongly positive at 2.2 OD units. So far, this looks like HIT — but the serotonin-release assay (SRA) tells a stranger story. Strong serotonin release at 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL heparin? That's classic HIT. But 96% serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin — meaning platelet activation with no heparin present at all? That's autoimmune HIT (aHIT). And when they add the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3 to the assay, it only partially inhibited activation — 76% release persisted instead of the expected suppression below 20%. This serological profile has never been previously reported, and it signals a particularly severe subtype of an already dangerous condition.

## Classic HIT vs. Autoimmune HIT: What's Different?

In classic HIT, IgG antibodies form immune complexes with PF4 bound to heparin. These complexes cross-link platelet FcγIIa receptors, triggering platelet activation, thrombocytopenia, and a roughly 15-fold increase in thrombosis risk. The key pathophysiological point: heparin is required to form the antigen complex. Remove heparin, and the antibody has nothing to bind.

Autoimmune HIT upends this model. The antibodies can activate platelets *without heparin* — they recognize PF4 in configurations that don't require polyanionic binding. This means platelet activation continues even after you've stopped all heparin exposure. The antibody is, in effect, auto-reactive. This has profound implications for treatment, because simply stopping heparin and starting an alternative anticoagulant may not be enough to halt the consumptive coagulopathy.

## Clinical Red Flags That Should Make You Suspect aHIT

This case illustrates several features that should push you toward considering autoimmune HIT rather than classic HIT:

- **Profoundly low platelet nadir.** Classic HIT typically bottoms out in the 40–80 × 10⁹/L range. This patient hit 13 × 10⁹/L. That degree of thrombocytopenia should make you question whether you're dealing with standard HIT.
- **Consumptive coagulopathy despite anticoagulation.** While on therapeutic argatroban, this patient's fibrinogen fell from elevated postoperative levels to a nadir of 2.49 g/L, D-dimer peaked around POD12, and schistocytes appeared on peripheral smear from POD9–POD16. This is the biochemical signature of ongoing platelet-driven consumption that anticoagulation alone isn't controlling.
- **Thrombosis despite therapeutic anticoagulation.** This patient stroked while on prophylactic anticoagulation, and the consumptive process continued even after escalation to therapeutic argatroban.
- **Incomplete or delayed response to IVIG.** High-dose IVIG typically produces a brisk platelet rise in aHIT. This patient got 100 g daily for two days, then a third dose, and only rose from 13 to 54 × 10⁹/L — a much blunted response.
- **Early onset before POD5.** HIT classically presents between POD5 and POD10. This patient's platelet fall began before POD5 — explained by preoperative heparin exposure during cardiac catheterization one week prior (2000 U UFH via transradial approach). Only about 10% of post-cardiac surgery HIT occurs this early, and it's almost always linked to recent preoperative heparin.

## The SRA Pattern: Reading the Tea Leaves

The SRA is the gold standard confirmatory test for HIT. In classic HIT, you see strong serotonin release at low heparin concentrations (0.1–0.3 U/mL), no release at very high heparin (100 U/mL, because immune complexes can't form at saturating concentrations), and — critically — no release at 0 U/mL heparin, because the antibody needs heparin to form the antigenic target.

In aHIT, you see strong release at low heparin, no release at 100 U/mL, and strong release at 0 U/mL — because the antibody doesn't need heparin. This patient had all of that, plus a novel finding: IV.3 only partially inhibited platelet activation. The initial lab read was "indeterminate," which could have led to a missed diagnosis. The lesson: when the SRA shows release at 0 U/mL heparin, think aHIT even if other features seem atypical, and don't let partial Fc receptor blockade fool you into dismissing the diagnosis.

## Treatment Implications: Why Recognizing aHIT Matters

Recognizing aHIT changes management in two important ways:

First, **add high-dose IVIG to anticoagulation**. In classic HIT, anticoagulation alone is standard. In aHIT, the antibody is driving platelet activation independently of the coagulation cascade, so blocking the Fc receptor pathway with IVIG addresses the root problem. This patient received 100 g IVIG on three occasions. Even though the response was blunted compared to typical aHIT cases, IVIG remains the right adjunct.

Second, **choose your anticoagulant with the consumptive coagulopathy in mind**. This team started with argatroban — reasonable given the short half-life, recent stroke, and severe thrombocytopenia. But when fibrinogen kept falling and D-dimer kept rising on argatroban, they switched to therapeutic-dose fondaparinux (10 mg SC daily), with antithrombin concentrate given first because the patient's AT level was low at 0.70 U/mL. The rationale: fondaparinux provides more sustained factor Xa inhibition than PTT-titrated argatroban, and once acute bleeding risk was deemed manageable, priority shifted to robust control of consumption. Platelets gradually recovered to 155 × 10⁹/L by POD62, and fondaparinux was continued until POD65 before transitioning back to apixaban.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Autoimmune HIT = HIT antibodies that activate platelets without heparin.** The SRA shows strong serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin — this is the diagnostic hallmark.
- **Suspect aHIT when you see:** profoundly low platelet nadir, consumptive coagulopathy despite therapeutic anticoagulation (falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimer, schistocytes), thrombosis despite anticoagulation, or blunted response to IVIG.
- **Early-onset HIT (before POD5) after cardiac surgery** is rare and almost always indicates preoperative heparin exposure primed the immune response.
- **Treatment is therapeutic-dose non-heparin anticoagulation PLUS high-dose IVIG.** Don't rely on anticoagulation alone in aHIT.
- **If anticoagulation isn't controlling consumption**, consider switching agents and giving antithrombin concentrate if AT levels are low.
- **aHIT is a spectrum.** This case demonstrates a novel serological phenotype with partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade — if the lab calls an SRA "indeterminate," dig deeper before accepting it's not HIT.
- **Even severe aHIT can have a good outcome.** This patient made a complete neurological recovery with platelet normalization by POD62.



# The Indeterminate SRA: A Novel Serological Profile in Autoimmune HIT

## When the Lab Says "Indeterminate" But the Patient Says "Autoimmune"

You order a serotonin-release assay (SRA) for suspected HIT, and the reference laboratory calls it "indeterminate." What do you do? If you're like most clinicians, you might feel stuck — unsure whether to treat for HIT, escalate therapy, or look for an alternative diagnosis. But as this case powerfully demonstrates, an "indeterminate" SRA might not mean ambiguity at all. It might mean you're dealing with autoimmune HIT (aHIT) — a more severe, heparin-independent variant — and the laboratory findings that don't fit the textbook pattern are actually telling you exactly what's wrong, if you know how to read them.

This case report from St Bernard, Nazy, and Warkentin describes an elderly man who underwent cardiac surgery with standard UFH anticoagulation for cardiopulmonary bypass. His platelet count fell precipitously to 50 × 10⁹/L by postoperative day 5 (POD5) and continued dropping to a nadir of 13 × 10⁹/L by POD7 — all accompanied by an acute parietal stroke. But it was the laboratory profile that truly set this case apart: a SRA pattern never previously reported in the literature, one that initially read as "indeterminate" but ultimately revealed a uniquely severe serological phenotype of aHIT.

## HIT Diagnostics 101: A Quick Refresher

Before unpacking what made this case so unusual, let's briefly review how we typically diagnose HIT in the laboratory. The standard approach involves two layers of testing. The first is a screening immunoassay — the PF4/polyanion ELISA — which detects antibodies against PF4 bound to polyanionic surfaces. It's sensitive but not specific, meaning a positive result doesn't necessarily mean clinical HIT. The optical density (OD) value provides a sense of antibody strength; values above 1.0 OD units are strongly associated with platelet-activating antibodies and clinical HIT.

The second layer is the functional confirmatory assay — the serotonin-release assay (SRA). This is the gold standard. Patient serum is incubated with washed platelets from healthy donors, and serotonin release is measured in the presence of varying heparin concentrations. In classic HIT, the SRA shows strong platelet activation at therapeutic heparin concentrations (0.1–0.3 U/mL) and no activation at very high heparin concentrations (100 U/mL), because excess heparin disrupts the PF4-heparin immune complexes. Crucially, in classic HIT, there should be no significant serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin — the antibodies are heparin-dependent, after all. Additionally, when the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3 is added, it should completely inhibit serotonin release, because classic HIT antibodies activate platelets through FcγIIa receptor clustering. That's the pattern you expect. Now let's look at what actually happened in this patient.

## The SRA That Broke the Rules

This patient's PF4/polyanion ELISA was strongly positive at 2.2 OD units — no surprises there, and certainly consistent with clinical HIT. But the SRA told a much more complex story. At therapeutic heparin concentrations (0.1 and 0.3 U/mL), the patient's serum triggered 97% and 99% serotonin release respectively — strongly positive. At the supratherapeutic heparin concentration of 100 U/mL, there was 0% release — also classic. So far, so good for a diagnosis of HIT.

But then came the curveballs. First, the patient's serum caused 96% serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin — in the complete absence of heparin. This is the hallmark of autoimmune HIT: the antibodies can activate platelets without heparin, because they recognize PF4 in a conformation that doesn't require the polyanion. This finding alone should prompt consideration of aHIT, not classic HIT.

Second, and even more remarkably, when the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3 was added, it failed to fully inhibit platelet activation. At 0.1 U/mL heparin with IV.3, serotonin release was still 76% — far above the expected threshold of less than 20%. In classic HIT, IV.3 should nearly abolish serotonin release by blocking the FcγIIa receptor. In previously described cases of aHIT, IV.3 also typically inhibits platelet activation effectively. But in this patient, the antibodies exhibited only partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade — a profile that, to the authors' knowledge, has never been previously reported.

This combination — heparin-independent activation plus partial resistance to Fc blockade — initially led the reporting laboratory to designate the SRA result as "indeterminate." And you can understand why. The test didn't fit the pattern of classic HIT, but it also didn't cleanly fit the previously described pattern of aHIT. The result existed in a diagnostic no-man's-land, which could easily have led to a missed diagnosis or inappropriate management.

## From "Indeterminate" to Autoimmune HIT: Connecting the Dots

The breakthrough came through close collaboration with the reference laboratory — the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory — and careful clinical reasoning. When the treating team and the laboratory experts reviewed the SRA results together, the full picture emerged. The strong serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin was diagnostic of aHIT. The partial resistance to IV.3 didn't exclude aHIT; it indicated a uniquely severe serological phenotype in which the patient's antibodies were so potent that even near-complete Fc receptor blockade couldn't fully shut down platelet activation. This wasn't a contradiction — it was a novel point on the spectrum of aHIT severity.

Several additional clinical and laboratory features supported the diagnosis of severe aHIT. The platelet count fell to an extraordinarily low nadir of 13 × 10⁹/L — far deeper than is typical for classic HIT. The patient developed falling fibrinogen levels (nadir 2.49 g/L), rising d-dimers (peak around POD12), and transient schistocytes on peripheral blood film from POD9 to POD16 — all markers of severe HIT-associated consumptive coagulopathy that we typically associate with the aHIT variant rather than classic HIT. Furthermore, the patient's response to high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin was notably blunted: the platelet count only rose from 13 × 10⁹/L (POD7) to an interim peak of 54 × 10⁹/L (POD14), which is a far more modest recovery than is typically seen when IVIG is administered for aHIT. Even the treatment response was atypical, reinforcing that this was an unusually severe form of the disease.

The early onset also merits attention. The platelet count began falling before POD5, which is earlier than the classic HIT temporal window of POD5–10 for post-cardiac surgery patients. Only about 10% of post-cardiac surgery HIT cases fall before POD5, and this is typically linked to recent preoperative heparin exposure. In this case, the patient had received 2000 U of UFH during transradial heart catheterization one week before surgery — enough to prime the immune system so that the massive intraoperative heparin exposure during CPB triggered a rapid secondary immune response. This preoperative exposure was initially overlooked, and its identification was essential for explaining both the diagnosis and the unusually early onset.

## The Evolving Serology: What Serial SRA Testing Revealed

One of the most instructive aspects of this case is what happened to the SRA profile over time. The authors performed serial SRA testing over several months following the initial diagnosis, and the results beautifully illustrate the dynamic nature of aHIT antibodies.

On the initial POD5 sample, the SRA showed the atypical profile described above: strong heparin-independent activation (96% release at 0 U/mL) with partial resistance to IV.3. As the patient's platelet count gradually recovered — reaching 155 × 10⁹/L by POD62 and 202 × 10⁹/L by POD87 — subsequent SRA profiles began to show a more characteristic aHIT pattern, with continued heparin-independent activation but more complete inhibition by IV.3. Then, on the last sample tested at POD124, the SRA profile shifted to one consistent with predominantly heparin-dependent antibodies: only 15% serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin but 100% release at 0.3 U/mL. In other words, the autoimmune component waned, and what remained was a pattern more typical of classic, heparin-dependent HIT antibodies — which themselves eventually cleared entirely.

This serial evolution is profoundly important for understanding aHIT. It tells us that the autoantibody response is not static — it has a natural history of emergence, peak severity, and gradual waning. It also has practical implications: a single "indeterminate" SRA result does not define the diagnosis. Repeat testing over time can clarify the picture, reveal the autoimmune nature of the antibodies, and ultimately demonstrate their resolution. If your patient has an atypical SRA result and you're unsure, don't just stop investigating. Engage the reference laboratory, send serial samples, and track the evolving serology alongside the clinical course.

## Clinical Management: Beyond the Lab

The management of this patient also highlights key principles for treating severe aHIT. Anticoagulation was initially transitioned from prophylactic-dose fondaparinux to therapeutic-dose argatroban (a direct thrombin inhibitor), with the choice driven by the shorter half-life — a sensible precaution given the recent stroke and profound thrombocytopenia. High-dose IVIG was administered (100 g daily for two days, then a third 100 g dose after the initial lack of platelet count response), targeting the Fc receptor-mediated platelet activation from a different angle than anticoagulation alone.

Despite argatroban and IVIG, markers of consumptive coagulopathy worsened, prompting a switch on POD14 to therapeutic-dose fondaparinux (10 mg once daily subcutaneously), supplemented with antithrombin concentrate given the mildly reduced AT level of 0.70 U/mL. The rationale was multifactorial: easier subcutaneous administration, concern that argatroban was insufficiently controlling the hypercoagulability, and the established off-label efficacy of fondaparinux for HIT. This switch was followed by gradual platelet count recovery and eventual transition back to apixaban for long-term anticoagulation.

The key message here is that aHIT can be more resistant to standard HIT therapies than classic HIT. When the platelet count isn't recovering or markers of consumption are worsening despite appropriate anticoagulation, consider intensifying treatment — whether that means adding IVIG, switching anticoagulants, or supplementing with antithrombin concentrate.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **An "indeterminate" SRA is not the end of the diagnostic road.** It may signal aHIT with an atypical serological profile. Strong serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin is the key clue — it indicates heparin-independent platelet activation, the defining feature of aHIT.

- **Partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade by IV.3 is a novel finding.** This patient's HIT antibodies were so potent that even near-complete FcγIIa receptor blockade couldn't fully inhibit platelet activation. This has never been previously described and represents a uniquely severe serological phenotype of aHIT. Don't let it throw you off the diagnosis.

- **Early postoperative HIT (before POD5) should prompt inquiry about preoperative heparin exposure.** This patient received UFH during heart catheterization one week before surgery, priming the immune response. The intraoperative CPB heparin then triggered a rapid secondary response.

- **Severe thrombocytopenia, falling fibrinogen, rising d-dimers, and schistocytes suggest aHIT, not classic HIT.** These markers of consumptive coagulopathy should escalate your concern and prompt consideration of intensifying therapy beyond standard anticoagulation.

- **Serial SRA testing over months reveals the evolving nature of aHIT antibodies.** The autoantibody component wanes over time, eventually leaving only heparin-dependent antibodies, which also ultimately clear. Repeat testing can confirm the diagnosis retrospectively and guide decisions about reintroducing heparin in the future if needed.

- **Close collaboration with the reference laboratory is essential.** An "indeterminate" result demands a conversation, not a shrug. The experts at the reference lab can provide critical interpretive context that may not be apparent from the raw numbers on the report.

- **aHIT can be more treatment-resistant than classic HIT.** If the platelet count isn't recovering or markers of consumption are worsening despite appropriate anticoagulation, consider adding or intensifying high-dose IVIG, switching anticoagulants, and checking antithrombin levels.



# Severe Thrombocytopenia After Cardiac Surgery: Building a Rational Differential

## The Setup: When "Post-Bypass" Doesn't Cover It

An elderly man undergoes an uncomplicated cardiac surgery — aortic valve replacement, ascending aorta graft, single CABG, left atrial appendage excision. He comes off pump with excellent hemodynamics, no major blood loss, no transfusions. His platelet count drops from 210 to 155 on POD1 — unremarkable. But then it keeps falling. By POD5 he's at 50. By POD6 he's at 24. By POD7 he's at 13. And a CT brain reveals an acute left parietal infarct.

This is not your garden-variety post-bypass thrombocytopenia. And the laboratory workup that follows reveals something even the reference lab initially called "indeterminate." What follows is a masterclass in how to systematically tear apart a differential diagnosis when the platelets are crashing and the clock is ticking.

## The Clinical Problem

Severe thrombocytopenia in the early post-cardiac surgery period — especially when the nadir dips below 30 — forces you to think beyond hemodilution and CPB-related consumption. The differential is broad, the stakes are high, and the treatments for each entity are radically different. Give IVIG for the wrong diagnosis and you've wasted time and money. Give antibiotics for sepsis that doesn't exist and you've missed a thrombophilia. Anticoagulate the wrong patient and you may precipitate catastrophic bleeding.

Let's walk through how the treating team systematically ruled entities in or out — and how a seemingly "indeterminate" lab result turned out to be the key to unlocking a diagnosis of autoimmune HIT.

## Building the Differential: A Structured Approach

When faced with severe post-cardiac surgery thrombocytopenia, the major entities to consider are:

- **Cardiogenic shock / critical illness–associated thrombocytopenia**
- **Infection and sepsis**
- **Post-transfusion purpura (PTP)**
- **Post-surgical thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP)**
- **Drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia (DITP)**
- **Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT)**

Each can be interrogated along several axes: timing of onset relative to surgery and drug exposure, presence or absence of hemolysis, transfusion history, medication timeline, hemodynamic status, and key laboratory markers. Here's how this case disambiguated.

## Ruling Out the "Sick Patient" Causes

**Cardiogenic shock and critical illness** were effectively excluded by the clinical picture. The patient had excellent hemodynamics coming off bypass and remained hemodynamically stable throughout. There was no pressor requirement, no multi-organ failure, no乳酸osis — nothing to suggest the consumptive thrombocytopenia of critical illness. The normal white cell count with a normal differential, the negative blood and urine cultures, and the absence of a chest X-ray abnormality effectively excluded **infection and sepsis** as drivers.

This is an important early fork in the road: when your post-op cardiac patient is hemodynamically rock-solid and their platelets are in freefall, "sick patient thrombocytopenia" should be a diagnosis of exclusion, not a default.

## Ruling Out the Transfusion and Hemolysis Causes

**Post-transfusion purpura** was definitively excluded by one simple fact: the patient received no blood products. PTP is classically seen 5–10 days after transfusion, but it requires a transfusion history. None here. Done.

**Post-surgical TTP** was less straightforward but ultimately excluded. The initial reticulocyte count was unremarkable at 73 × 10⁹/L. Haptoglobin was actually elevated at 3.19 g/L — the opposite of what you'd expect with hemolysis. The peripheral blood film initially showed no schistocytes. Coagulation studies showed a normal PT/INR and elevated fibrinogen at 6.59 g/L, consistent with a postoperative acute phase response — not the DIC pattern that can accompany severe TTP. Of note, schistocytes did appear transiently from POD9 to POD16, but this was interpreted in the context of severe HIT-associated consumptive coagulopathy rather than primary TTP. The key teaching point: the initial absence of hemolysis and the elevated haptoglobin steered the team away from TTP early, and the subsequent schistocyte appearance was reinterpreted through the lens of the working diagnosis rather than prompting a diagnostic pivot.

## Ruling Out Drug-Induced Immune Thrombocytopenia

**DITP** was considered but excluded on timing grounds. The drugs given on the day of surgery were vancomycin, cefazolin, and tranexamic acid. The drugs started postoperatively were furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, and midodrine. DITP classically requires 5–7 days of exposure for a primary immune response — meaning a drug newly administered on the day of surgery or in the early postoperative period should not trigger immune-mediated platelet destruction by POD5. The platelet count decline began too early for any newly introduced postoperative drug to be the culprit. This is a subtle but critical inference: when the timeline doesn't fit DITP, you need to look for a preoperative drug exposure that could have already sensitized the patient.

## Ruling In HIT — But Which HIT?

This is where the case gets fascinating. Classic HIT following cardiac surgery typically shows a platelet count fall beginning between POD5 and POD10, driven by heparin-dependent IgG antibodies against platelet factor 4 (PF4) complexed with heparin. The platelet count rarely falls below 20, and the SRA — the gold-standard functional assay — shows heparin-dependent serotonin release that is fully inhibited by the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3.

This patient broke nearly every rule.

**Timing.** The platelet count fall began before POD5 — a pattern seen in only about 10% of post-cardiac surgery HIT, and classically linked to recent preoperative heparin exposure. The team's subsequent inquiry revealed that the patient had received 2000 units of UFH during a transradial heart catheterization just one week before surgery. That preoperative exposure primed the immune response, so the intraoperative UFH (45,000 units during CPB) functioned as a re-exposure, collapsing the typical 5-day onset window.

**Severity.** The platelet count nadir of 13 × 10⁹/L is extraordinarily low for HIT. This degree of thrombocytopenia, accompanied by falling fibrinogen, rising d-dimer, and transient schistocytosis, signaled a severe consumptive coagulopathy — a hallmark of autoimmune HIT rather than classic HIT.

**Serology.** The PF4/polyanion ELISA was strongly positive at 2.2 OD units. But the SRA told a deeper story. There was strong serotonin release at 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL heparin (97% and 99%, respectively) with no release at supratherapeutic 100 U/mL — consistent with HIT. But there was also 96% release at 0 U/mL heparin, meaning the antibodies were activating platelets without any heparin at all. This is the signature of autoimmune HIT. And, unprecedented in the literature, the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3 only partially inhibited the reaction — 76% release persisted even with IV.3 on board, rather than the expected suppression below 20%. The reference lab called it "indeterminate." The clinicians recognized it as aHIT with a uniquely severe serological phenotype.

## The Treatment Arc

The anticoagulation strategy evolved as the clinical picture revealed itself to be more severe and more refractory than expected. Prophylactic-dose fondaparinux was started on POD5 when HIT was first suspected, then discontinued in favor of therapeutic-dose argatroban given the recent stroke and extreme thrombocytopenia — the shorter half-life offering a safety margin if bleeding occurred. High-dose IVIG was given (100 g daily for 2 days, then a third dose), but the platelet response was surprisingly blunted, rising only from 13 to 54 — far less than the brisk recovery typically seen with IVIG in aHIT. Meanwhile, fibrinogen was falling and d-dimer was rising, suggesting argatroban was not adequately controlling the consumptive process. On POD14, the team switched to therapeutic-dose fondaparinux (10 mg SC daily), preceded by antithrombin concentrate to optimize its efficacy. The platelet count gradually recovered to 155 by POD62 and 202 by POD87, and the patient made a complete neurological recovery.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Don't dismiss early severe thrombocytopenia as "post-bypass."** When platelets keep falling past POD1 and especially when the nadir is below 30, you need a structured differential — not a shoulder shrug.

- **Timing is the single most powerful discriminating feature.** DITP needs days of exposure. PTP needs a transfusion. Classic HIT needs ~5 days from immunizing exposure. Early-onset HIT implies preoperative sensitization — always ask about recent heparin exposure, including catheterization, line flushes, and preoperative prophylaxis.

- **A "normal" hemodynamic profile and negative cultures are not incidental — they are active exclusions.** In a stable, non-infected post-op patient with crashing platelets, critical illness and sepsis are effectively ruled out by the clinical picture, not by a single lab value.

- **The SRA at 0 U/mL heparin is your window into autoimmune HIT.** Strong serotonin release in the absence of heparin means the antibodies don't need heparin to activate platelets. This is aHIT, and it behaves differently — more thrombosis, lower platelet nadirs, more consumptive coagulopathy, and often a more refractory treatment course.

- **Partial inhibition by IV.3 doesn't exclude aHIT — it may signal a uniquely severe subtype.** This case documents the first reported instance of aHIT antibodies showing only partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade. An "indeterminate" SRA in the right clinical context may still be autoimmune HIT.

- **IVIG response can be blunted in severe aHIT.** A poor platelet recovery after IVIG doesn't refute the diagnosis — it may indicate a more aggressive antibody phenotype requiring additional therapeutic escalation.

- **Fondaparinux can rescue refractory aHIT.** When argatroban fails to control consumption, therapeutic-dose fondaparinux — supplemented with antithrombin concentrate if needed — can be an effective off-label strategy.

## The Bottom Line

Severe thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery demands a systematic approach, not reflex. Build your differential. Interrogate the timeline. Don't let a strong ELISA and an "indeterminate" SRA talk you out of a diagnosis that the clinical picture is screaming at you. And when the platelets are at 13 and the fibrinogen is falling, recognize that you may be dealing with autoimmune HIT — an entity that breaks the classic rules but still demands aggressive, evolving anticoagulation. This patient walked out neurologically intact because the team kept thinking when the lab results were confusing and the platelets were devastating.



# Managing Refractory aHIT: Anticoagulant Sequencing and the Role of IVIG

## When HIT Becomes Autoimmune: A Case That Breaks the Rules

Most of us learned HIT as a relatively straightforward diagnosis: platelet drop 5–10 days post-heparin, positive PF4 antibody, switch anticoagulants, watch platelets recover. But what happens when the platelets keep falling, the stroke has already happened, the serotonin-release assay looks bizarre, and IVIG barely makes a dent? That is exactly the scenario Warkentin and colleagues describe in a remarkable case of autoimmune HIT (aHIT) following cardiac surgery — and the management decisions they made under pressure offer a masterclass in navigating refractory disease.

The patient is a man in his 70s who underwent AVR, ascending aorta replacement, and CABG. His platelet count fell from 210 to 50 × 10⁹/L by POD5, and CT brain revealed an acute left parietal infarct. The PF4 ELISA was strongly positive at 2.2 OD units. But here is where things get strange: his serotonin-release assay showed near-maximal serotonin release even in the *absence* of heparin (96% at 0 U/mL), and the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3 only partially inhibited platelet activation — a profile never previously reported. This is aHIT in its most severe serological form, and it would go on to challenge every standard assumption about treatment.

## The Anticoagulant Dilemma: Argatroban First, But Not Because It Is Best

When HIT is diagnosed, the reflex is to stop all heparin and start a non-heparin anticoagulant. In this case, the team had already switched from dalteparin to prophylactic fondaparinux on POD5 when HIT was suspected, but once the diagnosis solidified, they pivoted to therapeutic-dose argatroban at 0.75 mcg/kg/min with a PTT target of 55–85 seconds.

The reasoning here is worth pausing on. Fondaparinux at therapeutic doses is a recognized off-label option for HIT and has genuine advantages — once-daily subcutaneous dosing, no PTT monitoring, and a well-established safety profile. So why did the treating haematologist choose argatroban instead? Two words: **reversibility and bleeding risk**. The patient had a recent ischemic stroke and a platelet count that would ultimately bottom out at 13 × 10⁹/L. In that setting, the short half-life of argatroban (~45 minutes) was an intentional safety valve. If the patient bled, you could turn off the infusion and have anticoagulation wash out within an hour. That is a luxury you do not have with fondaparinux, whose half-life of 17–21 hours and renal clearance make it far less forgiving in the face of hemorrhage.

This is a critical management pearl: **in profound thrombocytopenia with recent bleeding-prone events, the shortest-acting anticoagulant is not a weakness — it is a feature**.

## When Argatroban Is Not Enough: The Switch to Fondaparinux

By POD14, it became clear that argatroban was not adequately controlling the HIT-associated consumptive coagulopathy. Three converging signals told the story:

- **Fibrinogen levels progressively fell** from POD6 to POD17, reaching a nadir of 2.49 g/L — striking for the early postoperative period, when fibrinogen as an acute phase reactant should be climbing, not falling.
- **D-dimer levels peaked around POD12**, indicating ongoing thrombin generation and fibrin turnover despite therapeutic argatroban.
- **Schistocytes appeared on peripheral blood film from POD9 to POD16**, signaling microangiopathic consumption.

Together, these markers indicated that the hypercoagulability of aHIT was outrunning the anticoagulant. Whether this reflects argatroban's limitations in severe aHIT specifically or simply the intensity of this patient's disease process is impossible to disentangle from a single case, but the practical decision was clear: the team switched to therapeutic-dose fondaparinux, 10 mg once daily subcutaneously, on POD14.

This switch also carried a practical rationale. Argatroban requires continuous IV infusion and serial PTT monitoring — a burden for any ward team, especially over a prolonged treatment course. Fondaparinux is once-daily subcutaneous, requires no monitoring, and allowed the patient to transition toward outpatient readiness. In a patient whose platelet recovery would ultimately take two months, that practical reality matters enormously.

## IVIG in aHIT: Why Three Doses?

High-dose IVIG has emerged as a key adjunct in aHIT, based on the rationale that aHIT antibodies can activate platelets *independently of heparin* — meaning anticoagulation alone may not shut off the platelet activation driving the consumptive process. IVIG is thought to work by saturating Fc receptor pathways and interfering with the autoantibody-mediated platelet activation, though the precise mechanism remains debated.

In this case, the patient received 100 g IVIG on two consecutive days — and the platelet response was underwhelming. The count rose from a nadir of 13 × 10⁹/L on POD7 to only 54 × 10⁹/L by POD14. That is a far more blunted response than is typically seen when IVIG is given for aHIT, where brisk platelet count corrections are the expected pattern. Because of this inadequate response, a third 100 g dose was administered.

The take-home point here is not that IVIG failed — it is that **the magnitude and speed of platelet recovery after IVIG is itself a diagnostic clue**. A brisk rise suggests classic aHIT responsive to Fc receptor blockade. A blunted or absent rise, as in this case, should raise concern for an unusually severe antibody profile — which is exactly what the partial IV.3 resistance on SRA confirmed. When IVIG does not work as expected in suspected aHIT, do not abandon the diagnosis. Reconsider the severity of the antibody phenotype and maintain aggressive anticoagulation.

## Antithrombin Supplementation Before Fondaparinux: A Nuanced Move

Before initiating therapeutic fondaparinux, the team checked an antithrombin level — it was mildly reduced at 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40). They supplemented with 1000 U of antithrombin concentrate prior to the first fondaparinux dose.

This decision reflects a pharmacological reality that is easy to overlook in the heat of clinical management: **fondaparinux is an indirect factor Xa inhibitor**. It requires antithrombin to function. In the setting of severe consumptive coagulopathy — where fibrinogen is falling, D-dimer is rising, and schistocytes are appearing — antithrombin levels can be depleted by ongoing thrombin generation and consumption. Starting a fondaparinux-based regimen with suboptimal antithrombin levels risks therapeutic failure not because the drug is wrong, but because its cofactor is insufficient.

This is a sophisticated but practical point: **if you are switching to an antithrombin-dependent anticoagulant in the setting of active consumption, check the antithrombin level and replete it if low**. It is the biochemical equivalent of making sure your car actually has oil before you drive it.

## Serial Fibrinogen and D-Dimer: The Coagulopathy Dashboard

One of the most underappreciated aspects of managing severe HIT — particularly aHIT — is that you cannot rely on platelet count alone to tell you whether the disease is controlled. The platelet count is a lagging indicator. The leading indicators of ongoing HIT-driven consumption are fibrinogen and D-dimer.

In this case, the team tracked both serially, and the trends told a story that platelet counts alone would have missed:

- Fibrinogen fell progressively from POD6 to POD17 despite argatroban, indicating the anticoagulant was not fully suppressing thrombin generation.
- D-dimer peaked around POD12, consistent with intense fibrin formation and turnover.
- Schistocytes emerged transiently, confirming microvascular consumption.

After the switch to fondaparinux, these markers eventually normalized in parallel with gradual platelet recovery — confirming that the new anticoagulant strategy was gaining control of the consumptive process.

**Fibrinogen trending downward in HIT is a red flag for severe consumptive coagulopathy.** Fibrinogen is an acute phase reactant that should be elevated in the early postoperative period. If it is falling instead — especially while the patient is on a non-heparin anticoagulant — the HIT is not controlled. D-dimer directionality provides complementary information: rising D-dimer confirms ongoing thrombin generation. Together, these markers form a pragmatic dashboard for assessing anticoagulant efficacy in HIT when you cannot simply follow the platelet count and assume all is well.

## Putting It Together: A Framework for Refractory aHIT

This case forces us to think beyond the algorithm. When aHIT is severe and refractory, the standard playbook — stop heparin, start a direct thrombin inhibitor, give IVIG, watch platelets rise — may not be enough. The lessons from this case coalesce into a practical framework:

- **Choose your first anticoagulant with bleeding risk in mind.** In profound thrombocytopenia with recent stroke or bleeding-prone physiology, argatroban's short half-life is a feature, not a compromise.
- **Use serial fibrinogen and D-dimer as your coagulopathy dashboard.** Falling fibrinogen and rising D-dimer on anticoagulation mean the HIT is not controlled — change something.
- **Do not assume IVIG will fix everything quickly.** A blunted platelet response to IVIG in suspected aHIT signals an unusually severe antibody phenotype. Reassess and maintain aggressive anticoagulation.
- **Check antithrombin before starting fondaparinux in active consumption.** If it is low, replete it — fondaparinux without adequate antithrombin is like a brake pedal without hydraulic fluid.
- **Transition to fondaparinux for practicality and efficacy when the acute phase is stabilizing.** Once bleeding risk decreases and the need for prolonged anticoagulation is clear, once-daily subcutaneous dosing and no monitoring are major advantages over a PTT-titrated IV infusion.
- **Expect a long recovery.** This patient's platelets did not normalize until POD62. Patience and serial monitoring are essential, and premature cessation of anticoagulation could be catastrophic.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **aHIT can present before POD5 after cardiac surgery** — preoperative heparin exposure (even low-dose, as in this patient's 2000 U during heart catheterization) resets the immunological clock.
- **A blunted platelet response to IVIG suggests unusually severe aHIT** — do not abandon the diagnosis; instead, escalate anticoagulation and reassess.
- **Serial fibrinogen and D-dimer are essential surrogate markers** of HIT-associated consumptive coagulopathy and anticoagulant efficacy — use them as your dashboard.
- **Argatroban's short half-life is advantageous in profound thrombocytopenia with bleeding risk** — but if consumption continues despite adequate dosing, switch to fondaparinux.
- **Check and replete antithrombin before initiating fondaparinux** in the setting of active consumptive coagulopathy.
- **Partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade on SRA is a novel marker of severe aHIT** — if you see an "indeterminate" SRA with persistent heparin-independent platelet activation, think aHIT with an aggressive antibody phenotype.
- **Platelet recovery in severe aHIT can take months** — plan for prolonged anticoagulation and outpatient follow-up, with eventual transition to an oral anticoagulant once platelets normalize and HIT antibodies wane.



# HIT-Associated Consumptive Coagulopathy: Beyond Thrombosis and Thrombocytopenia

## When HIT Looks Like DIC

We teach HIT as a prothrombotic, *non-consumptive* disorder. Thrombocytopenia? Yes — but from platelet activation and clearance, not consumption. Thrombosis? Absolutely — that's the signature. But falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimer, and schistocytes on the blood film? That's DIC, not HIT. Except when it isn't.

A man in his 70s underwent cardiac surgery — aortic valve replacement, ascending aorta graft, single CABG, left atrial appendage excision — with standard UFH anticoagulation for cardiopulmonary bypass. Surgery was uncomplicated. He came off pump with excellent hemodynamics. Platelet count on POD1 was 155 × 10⁹/L, a routine post-bypass dip. Dalteparin thromboprophylaxis was started. And then the platelet count fell — steadily, relentlessly — reaching 50 × 10⁹/L by POD5 and a nadir of 13 × 10⁹/L by POD7. A CT brain performed for worsening delirium revealed an acute left parietal infarct. The PF4/polyanion ELISA came back strongly positive at 2.2 OD units. So far, this looks like aggressive post-cardiac-surgery HIT. But the laboratory trajectory told a deeper and more unusual story.

## The Consumptive Signature

Here is what caught our attention. During the first week of argatroban therapy, this patient developed a biochemical profile that looked for all the world like a consumptive coagulopathy. Fibrinogen fell progressively from its postoperative peak of 6.59 g/L — itself an expected acute-phase elevation — down to a nadir of 2.49 g/L by POD17. D-dimer rose, peaking around POD12. And schistocytes appeared on the peripheral blood film from POD9 through POD16, a finding initially absent on the POD7 film. These are the laboratory hallmarks of DIC. But this wasn't DIC in the classic sense — it was HIT-associated consumptive coagulopathy, a phenomenon that is underrecognized and particularly characteristic of autoimmune HIT.

The mechanism makes sense when you think it through. In HIT, IgG antibodies form complexes with PF4 and heparin on the platelet surface, clustering FcγIIa receptors and triggering massive platelet activation. This activation releases procoagulant microparticles and exposes phosphatidylserine, fueling thrombin generation far beyond the platelet-rich microthrombi we classically associate with the syndrome. When this thrombin generation is severe enough — as it can be in aHIT, where antibodies activate platelets even in the absence of heparin — the coagulation cascade is driven hard enough to consume fibrinogen and generate cross-linked fibrin degradation products. The result is a secondary consumptive coagulopathy that sits biochemically between isolated HIT and full-blown DIC.

## Why This Points Toward aHIT

Classic HIT can produce thrombocytopenia and thrombosis, but it typically does *not* produce falling fibrinogen, schistocytes, or a platelet count nadir of 13 × 10⁹/L. When you see these features in a patient with suspected HIT, autoimmune HIT should jump to the top of your differential.

In this case, the serotonin-release assay revealed the telltale signature of aHIT: strong platelet activation even in the *absence* of heparin — 96% serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin. This is the hallmark of heparin-independent platelet activation. Classic HIT antibodies need heparin to form the PF4-containing immune complexes that cluster Fc receptors; aHIT antibodies are configured such that they can activate platelets without it. The clinical implication is profound: these patients generate thrombin continuously, not just when heparin is onboard, which explains why the consumptive features can be so much more severe and why the thrombocytopenia can be so profound.

This case also revealed something genuinely novel. The Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3, which normally abolishes serotonin release in classic HIT by preventing FcγIIa receptor clustering, only *partially* inhibited platelet activation in this patient's serum — 76% serotonin release persisted even in the presence of IV.3. This has not been previously reported and suggests an unusually reactive antibody profile capable of activating platelets through mechanisms not fully dependent on Fc receptor cross-linking. For the laboratory, this initially produced an "indeterminate" SRA result — a potential diagnostic trap. When you encounter an SRA that doesn't behave as expected, with partial resistance to Fc blockade, think severe aHIT.

## Real-Time Monitoring: Fibrinogen and D-Dimer as Disease Activity Markers

One of the most practically useful lessons from this case is how fibrinogen and D-dimer served as real-time indicators of disease severity and anticoagulant adequacy. The treating team used serial fibrinogen and D-dimer levels to gauge whether argatroban was controlling the HIT-associated hypercoagulability. The answer, in this case, was arguably no — fibrinogen continued to fall and D-dimer continued to rise on therapeutic-dose argatroban, with schistocytes appearing on film. This biochemical evidence of ongoing consumption prompted the switch to therapeutic-dose fondaparinux on POD14, after which the trajectory improved.

This is a clinical pearl worth internalizing. In severe HIT, particularly aHIT, the platelet count alone may lag behind the underlying thrombin generation. A platelet count that is slow to recover on appropriate anticoagulation is concerning, but falling fibrinogen and rising D-dimer tell you the hypercoagulability is not yet controlled. These markers give you a window into disease activity that the platelet count alone cannot provide. If fibrinogen is falling and D-dimer is rising despite therapeutic anticoagulation, your patient may need a change in strategy.

## The TTP Trap

The appearance of schistocytes in a thrombocytopenic postoperative patient is a red flag that immediately raises the specter of thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura. This is a critical distinction, because the management of TTP — plasma exchange, caplacizumab — is fundamentally different from the management of HIT, where plasma exchange is not indicated and where the key intervention is stopping all heparin and initiating a non-heparin anticoagulant.

In this case, several features helped steer away from TTP and toward aHIT. The schistocytes appeared relatively late — not present on the initial POD7 film but emerging from POD9 onward — and they coincided with a known strongly positive PF4 ELISA and an SRA profile consistent with aHIT. The haptoglobin was elevated, not depressed, arguing against overt hemolysis. The initial absence of schistocytes, together with the timing of their appearance in the context of documented HIT antibodies and falling fibrinogen, supported a diagnosis of HIT-associated consumptive coagulopathy rather than primary TTP. That said, the distinction can be genuinely difficult, and ADAMTS13 testing should be sent if there is real diagnostic uncertainty. The key teaching point: schistocytes in HIT do not automatically mean TTP, but they do mean you are dealing with a severe, consumptive phenotype that demands aggressive anticoagulation and close monitoring.

## Treatment Considerations: When Standard Therapy Isn't Enough

This case highlights that aHIT can be more therapeutically challenging than classic HIT. High-dose IVIG, which often produces a brisk platelet count recovery in aHIT by blocking Fc receptors and dampening platelet activation, produced only a modest rise in this patient — from 13 × 10⁹/L to an interim peak of 54 × 10⁹/L after three 100-gram doses. This muted response, consistent with the partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade seen in the SRA, underscores that aHIT exists on a spectrum of severity, and some patients have antibody profiles that are unusually resistant to standard immunomodulatory approaches.

The eventual transition to therapeutic-dose fondaparinux — with supplemental antithrombin concentrate given the mildly reduced AT level of 0.70 U/mL — was associated with gradual but complete recovery. Platelet counts normalized to 155 × 10⁹/L by POD62 and 202 × 10⁹/L by POD87, with full neurological recovery. The patient was ultimately transitioned back to apixaban for long-term anticoagulation.

## Key Take-Home Points

- **HIT can be a consumptive disorder.** Falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimer, and schistocytes — the classic DIC profile — can occur in severe HIT, particularly autoimmune HIT. When you see this pattern in suspected HIT, escalate your concern for aHIT.
- **Heparin-independent platelet activation defines aHIT.** Strong serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin on the SRA is the laboratory signature. In this case, partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade by IV.3 was a novel finding suggesting an unusually severe antibody phenotype.
- **Fibrinogen and D-dimer are real-time disease activity markers.** Use serial levels to assess whether your anticoagulant is adequately controlling HIT-associated hypercoagulability. A falling fibrinogen or rising D-dimer on therapeutic anticoagulation means you are not yet winning.
- **Schistocytes in HIT do not equal TTP.** The appearance of schistocytes in a patient with documented HIT antibodies and falling fibrinogen supports HIT-associated consumptive coagulopathy. Send an ADAMTS13 if uncertain, but do not let the schistocytes distract you from appropriate HIT management.
- **Early postoperative HIT requires preoperative heparin exposure.** Platelet count falls before POD5 in a post-cardiac-surgery patient should prompt inquiry about recent preoperative heparin exposure, such as during heart catheterization. This patient received 2000 U of UFH one week before surgery — enough to prime the immune response.
- **aHIT can be resistant to standard therapy.** A muted platelet count response to high-dose IVIG, or biochemical evidence of ongoing consumption despite argatroban, should prompt escalation — consider therapeutic-dose fondaparinux with attention to antithrombin levels.
