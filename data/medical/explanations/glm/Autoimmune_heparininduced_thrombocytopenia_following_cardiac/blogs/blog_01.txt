Title: Blog about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

# Before Day 5: When HIT Comes Early After Cardiac Surgery

## The Trap We All Know Too Well

Every medical student learns the same HIT timeline: platelet count falls 5–10 days after heparin exposure, nadir typically above 30 × 10⁹/L, and thrombosis is a feared complication. When a post-cardiac surgery patient's platelets are dropping by postoperative day 3 or 4, most of us mentally file that under "post-CPB thrombocytopenia" and move on. But what if that early drop *is* HIT—and not just ordinary HIT, but autoimmune HIT with heparin-independent platelet activation and a platelet count crashing toward 13 × 10⁹/L?

That is exactly what happened in a recently published case from Warkentin and colleagues, and it offers a masterclass in how the classic "day 5 to 10" teaching can lull us into a false sense of security if we don't ask one critical question: *Did this patient receive heparin before surgery?*

## The Case: A Straightforward Operation, a Complicated Course

A man in his 70s with atrial fibrillation on apixaban presented for elective cardiac surgery—aortic valve replacement, ascending aorta graft replacement, single-vessel CABG, and left atrial appendage excision. The operation went beautifully: uncomplicated cardiopulmonary bypass, no major blood loss, excellent hemodynamics coming off pump. The preoperative platelet count was 210 × 10⁹/L, dropping to a fairly typical 155 × 10⁹/L on POD1. Nothing alarming yet.

Then the platelet count kept falling: 50 × 10⁹/L by POD5, 26 × 10⁹/L by POD6, and a stunning nadir of 13 × 10⁹/L by POD7. Dalteparin thromboprophylaxis had been started on POD1 and stopped when the fall became apparent, switched to prophylactic fondaparinux on POD5. A CT brain obtained for worsening delirium revealed an acute left parietal infarct. The PF4/polyanion ELISA came back strongly positive at 2.2 OD units. HIT was now the leading diagnosis—but the timing seemed wrong, and the serotonin-release assay (SRA) initially returned as "indeterminate," a result that could have derailed the diagnosis entirely.

## The Missing Piece: Preoperative Heparin at Cath

The key to understanding the early onset lay in a detail that was only uncovered on subsequent inquiry. One week before surgery, the patient underwent diagnostic heart catheterization. Because he had not held his apixaban, the cardiologist used a transradial approach and administered a reduced intraprocedural heparin dose of 2000 U UFH. That exposure—small, forgotten, seemingly insignificant—was enough to prime the immune system. When the patient received 45,000 U of UFH during CPB a week later, the anamnestic immune response was already loaded and ready. The platelet count began falling on POD1, well before the classic HIT window.

This is the critical teaching point: only about 10% of post-cardiac surgery HIT cases present before POD5, and virtually all of these are linked to recent preoperative heparin exposure. The "day 5 to 10" rule assumes the *current* heparin course is the *first* immunizing exposure. When there has been a preceding exposure within the prior few weeks, the immune system is already sensitized, and the platelet fall can begin almost immediately upon re-exposure—sometimes within 24 hours, indistinguishable in timing from benign post-CPB thrombocytopenia.

## When "Indeterminate" Actually Means Autoimmune HIT

The SRA in this case showed several striking abnormalities that collectively pointed to autoimmune HIT (aHIT), a subtype in which antibodies activate platelets even in the absence of heparin. The patient's serum caused 96% serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin—diagnostic of heparin-independent platelet activation. Release was appropriately suppressed at supratherapeutic heparin concentrations (100 U/mL), consistent with HIT antibodies rather than a nonspecific platelet activator. But then came the truly novel finding: the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3, which normally abolishes HIT-mediated platelet activation, only *partially* inhibited the reaction. Serotonin release persisted at 76% despite IV.3, far above the usual threshold of <20%. To the authors' knowledge, this partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade has never been previously reported in aHIT. An initial laboratory readout of "indeterminate" could easily have led a clinician to dismiss HIT and pursue alternative diagnoses—a potentially catastrophic error in a patient with active cerebral infarction and a platelet count of 13 × 10⁹/L.

## A Severe, Prolonged Course — and a Treatment Escalation

The severity of this case extended well beyond the platelet nadir. During argatroban anticoagulation, fibrinogen levels progressively fell (nadir 2.49 g/L by POD17), D-dimer levels peaked around POD12, and schistocytes appeared on peripheral blood film from POD9 to POD16. These findings signaled ongoing, severe HIT-associated consumptive coagulopathy despite therapeutic anticoagulation. High-dose IVIG was administered—100 g on two consecutive days, then a third 100 g dose when the platelet count failed to rise briskly after the initial doses. The platelet response was modest and delayed, peaking at only 54 × 10⁹/L by POD14, far less than the robust recovery typically seen with IVIG in aHIT. This blunted response further underscored the unusually severe antibody phenotype.

Given persistent hypercoagulability on argatroban, the team switched to therapeutic-dose fondaparinux (10 mg subcutaneously once daily) on POD14, preceded by antithrombin concentrate supplementation due to a mildly reduced AT level of 0.70 U/mL. This transition addressed both practical concerns—once-daily subcutaneous dosing versus PTT-titrated infusion—and biochemical evidence that argatroban was insufficient to control the consumptive process. Over the following weeks, the platelet count gradually recovered to 155 × 10⁹/L by POD62 and 202 × 10⁹/L by POD87. The patient made a complete neurological recovery. Fondaparinux was continued until POD65, at which point apixaban was resumed for long-term anticoagulation.

## Why This Case Matters for Your Next Postop Patient

The central lesson is not simply that HIT can occur before day 5—most seasoned clinicians have heard this at some point. The lesson is that *the specific preoperative history changes everything about your pre-test probability*. A post-cardiac surgery patient with platelets falling on POD2 or POD3 is common and usually benign. But if that same patient had a cardiac catheterization with heparin a week before surgery, the early platelet fall is suddenly much harder to dismiss. The standard temporal rules assume a single immunizing exposure; when there are two, the clock starts earlier than you think.

Beyond timing, this case also reminds us that aHIT exists, that it can produce platelet counts far lower than classic HIT, that it can cause stroke, and that the SRA profile may look bizarre enough to be called "indeterminate" by a laboratory that does not regularly encounter the pattern. When the clinical picture screams HIT but the lab says "indeterminate," pick up the phone, ask for the raw SRA data, and specifically inquire about serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin and the degree of IV.3 inhibition. The diagnosis may depend on it.

## Key Take-Home Points

- **Always ask about preoperative heparin exposure.** Recent UFH exposure (even low-dose, even during a radial cardiac cath) can pre-prime the immune system, causing HIT onset before the classic POD5–10 window. This applies to roughly 10% of post-cardiac surgery HIT cases and should be specifically screened for in every early postop thrombocytopenia evaluation.
- **Early platelet fall after CPB is usually benign—but not always.** When the platelet count continues to fall progressively beyond POD1–2, especially into a severe range, HIT moves higher on the differential regardless of timing.
- **Autoimmune HIT can activate platelets without heparin.** Look for strong serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin on the SRA. An "indeterminate" SRA result in a patient with a strongly positive ELISA and a compatible clinical picture should prompt direct discussion with the reference laboratory.
- **Profound thrombocytopenia (nadir <20 × 10⁹/L) in HIT suggests aHIT.** Classic HIT typically bottoms out above 30 × 10⁹/L. When you see 13 × 10⁹/L with a strong PF4 ELISA, think autoimmune phenotype and consider IVIG early.
- **Monitor fibrinogen, D-dimer, and peripheral smear during treatment.** Falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimer, and new schistocytes signal ongoing consumptive coagulopathy and may warrant escalation from argatroban to fondaparinux, particularly if IVIG response is blunted.
- **Partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade on SRA is a newly described aHIT pattern.** Awareness of this phenotype can prevent a missed diagnosis when the laboratory reports an atypical or indeterminate activation profile.
