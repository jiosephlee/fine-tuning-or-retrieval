Title: Blog about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

# Anchoring Bias in Action: From Empiric TB Therapy to Pancreatopleural Fistula

Imagine a child presenting three times to the emergency department with recurrent, massive, dark red pleural effusions. You tap a liter of fluid each time, he gets better temporarily, and then he bounces right back. You have a mildly elevated pleural fluid ADA and a family history of tuberculosis. Do you start empiric antitubercular therapy? That is exactly what happened in this recently published case of a pediatric pancreatopleural fistula (PPF). It is a masterclass in how anchoring bias and premature closure can lead us down the wrong diagnostic path, causing us to treat the symptom while completely missing the pathology lurking below the diaphragm.

## The Case: A Pulmonary Wolf in Pancreatic Sheep's Clothing

A boy in late childhood, obese with a BMI of 35, presented to the outpatient department with a week of progressive dyspnea and right-sided chest pain. He was hypoxic, tachycardic, and clinical exam confirmed a massive right-sided pleural effusion. A chest X-ray and bedside lung ultrasound agreed: there was a liter of fluid in there. The team performed a thoracentesis, draining a liter of dark red pleural fluid. The child improved dramatically, was weaned off oxygen, and was discharged. 

Six days later, he was back. Same symptoms, same massive right pleural effusion. Another liter of dark red fluid was aspirated. This time, the team leaned on a slightly elevated pleural fluid ADA and a history of his grandfather having pulmonary tuberculosis. Empiric antitubercular therapy was started. But five days after that, the child returned for his third admission. It was only then, after re-interrogating the history, that the boy revealed he had suffered blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle one month prior. 

A CT scan of the chest finally looked beyond the pleural space, revealing a multiloculated hypodense collection tracking from the right paraesophageal region all the way to the tail of the pancreas. The diagnosis was clinched with an MRCP and pleural fluid enzymes: the fluid had an amylase of 6118 U/L and a lipase of 7211 U/L. The antitubercular drugs were stopped, and treatment for a pancreatopleural fistula was initiated.

## The Cognitive Autopsy: Where Did We Drift?

This case is a brilliant, albeit cringe-inducing, example of cognitive bias in action. Let's break down the diagnostic errors across the three admissions. 

On the first admission, the team fell victim to **search satisfaction** and **premature closure**. The patient had a clear problem—respiratory distress from a pleural effusion. The intervention—thoracentesis—provided immediate symptomatic relief. By draining the fluid and seeing the child improve, the team closed the loop on the encounter without asking the most critical question: *Why does a child have a liter of dark red pleural fluid in the first place?* They treated the symptom and discharged the patient. 

On the second admission, **anchoring bias** took over. When the effusion recurred, the team didn't broaden their differential; they looked for evidence to support a common, familiar diagnosis. A mildly elevated ADA and a family history of TB provided just enough cognitive cover to anchor on tuberculosis. They ignored a massive red flag: the rapid reaccumulation of a hemorrhagic-looking effusion. Tuberculous effusions are lymphocytic exudates and rarely present as acutely recurrent, massive dark red fluid collections requiring repeated large-volume taps within days. By prematurely starting empiric ATT, they introduced unnecessary medication risks and delayed the actual diagnosis.

It wasn't until the third admission that the anchor broke. The team stepped back, took a repeated, thorough history, and finally uncovered the blunt abdominal trauma. Once the CT showed the tracking collection to the pancreas, the "pulmonary mask" fell off. 

## Looking Below the Diaphragm: The Anatomy of a PPF

Pancreatopleural fistulas are rare in adults and exceedingly rare in children, with only 47 pediatric cases reported in the literature. The pathology is simple but easily missed. When the pancreas is injured—by trauma, or chronic/alcoholic pancreatitis in adults—the pancreatic duct can rupture. If the rupture is posterior, the pancreatic fluid leaks into the retroperitoneum. From there, it follows the path of least resistance, tracking superiorly through the aortic or esophageal hiatus, breaking through the pleura, and filling the chest. The child presents with respiratory distress, but the disease is in the abdomen.

The fluid is often dark red or hemorrhagic, which can mimic a hemothorax or malignancy, further muddying the waters. But the diagnostic key is in the fluid analysis. A pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L is pathognomonic for a PPF. If you don't send it, you won't find it. 

Once diagnosed, the management shifted entirely. The child was made nil per oral, started on total parenteral nutrition, and given an octreotide infusion to suppress pancreatic exocrine function. Over 10 days, the chest tube output decreased and stopped, and the child was discharged without needing endoscopic or surgical intervention.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Beware the "Pulmonary Mask":** Recurrent, massive pleural effusions can have an abdominal origin. If a chest tube keeps filling, look below the diaphragm. 
*   **Don't stop at symptom relief:** Performing a successful thoracentesis is not a diagnosis. Always ask why the fluid is there, especially when it is hemorrhagic and reaccumulates rapidly.
*   **Send pleural fluid amylase and lipase:** In any recurrent or unexplained pleural effusion, especially with a history of abdominal trauma or pancreatitis, amylase and lipase levels are critical. A pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L is pathognomonic for a PPF.
*   **Anchor on data, not narratives:** A mildly elevated ADA and a TB contact history are weak threads for a diagnosis of massive recurrent hemorrhagic pleural effusion. Don't let availability bias push you toward empiric therapy when the clinical picture doesn't fit.
*   **Repeat the history:** When a patient bounces back, your initial history is flawed. Re-interrogate the patient. The missing piece of the puzzle (the bicycle handle trauma) was there all along.
*   **Consider PPF in children:** While rare, blunt abdominal trauma is a leading cause of pancreatic injury in kids. If they present with recurrent effusions weeks later, think of a fistulous track.
