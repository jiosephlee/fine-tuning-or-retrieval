Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

### [Differential Diagnosis] Besides pancreatopleural fistula, what other conditions can produce an amylase-rich pleural effusion, and what specific clinical, laboratory, or imaging findings help exclude each of these alternative diagnoses in a pediatric patient?

Amylrigh-rich pleural effusion, defined by a pleural fluid amylase level exceeding the serum reference range (and classically ≥1000 IU/L in pancreatopleural fistula), is not specific to pancreatic disease. In the pediatric patient described, several alternative diagnoses were systematically considered and excluded through a combination of clinical, laboratory, and imaging findings.

**Esophageal perforation (Boerhaave's syndrome)** can produce an amylase-rich effusion because salivary amylase leaks into the pleural space through the esophageal tear. In this case, the diagnosis was excluded because the child had no history of vomiting — the classic precipitant of Boerhaave's — and was passing stools normally without evidence of esophageal disruption on contrast-enhanced CT imaging.

**Malignancy**, including lung carcinoma, leukemia, lymphoma, and metastatic disease, can also generate amylase-rich pleural fluid, typically of the salivary isoenzyme type. In this child, the absence of fever and weight loss, a normal complete blood count and peripheral smear, and — most importantly — pleural fluid cytology showing no malignant cells effectively excluded this category.

**Tuberculous pleuritis** was a serious consideration given the child's documented Koch's contact and a mildly elevated pleural fluid ADA. However, the absence of chronic cough, weight loss, and fever, combined with a negative GeneXpert for *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*, argued against tuberculosis. Empirically started antitubercular therapy was appropriately discontinued once MRCP confirmed the pancreatic origin.

**Empyema** was excluded by the absence of fever and the lack of a neutrophilic, purulent exudate on pleural fluid analysis.

**Hepatic hydrothorax** was ruled out because liver function tests and hepatic imaging were normal and there was no history of chronic liver disease.

**Acquired diaphragmatic hernia** was excluded because no abdominal viscera were visualized within the thoracic cavity on CT.

**Renal pathology**, including hydronephrosis with urinothorax, was excluded by normal renal function tests and normal renal imaging.

A critical diagnostic principle, underscored by this case, is that **amylase isoenzyme typing** can further refine the differential: pancreatopleural fistula produces pancreatic-type amylase in the pleural fluid, whereas esophageal perforation and malignancy typically produce salivary-type amylase. In this child, the combination of a markedly elevated pleural fluid amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L), a history of blunt abdominal trauma, and MRCP demonstration of a fistulous tract from the pancreatic tail through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity confirmed PPF and rendered the alternative diagnoses untenable.
