Title: Blog about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

# The Pulmonary Mask: When the Pancreas Hides Behind the Pleura

## The Setup

A child presents with recurrent right-sided pleural effusions. Each time you drain it, you get a liter of dark red fluid. He improves temporarily, then bounces back days later with the same complaint. You've got a positive TB contact history and a mildly elevated pleural fluid ADA — so you start antitubercular therapy. But the effusion keeps coming back. After the third admission, someone finally sits down with the kid and asks the right question. Turns out, he took a bicycle handle to the abdomen a month ago. The pancreas has been quietly leaking into his chest the entire time.

This is pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) — and it is one of the great imposters in pediatric medicine.

## What Makes This Case Dangerous

The temptation in a case like this is to stay locked into a thoracic differential. The patient has chest pain, respiratory distress, and a unilateral pleural effusion — everything points you toward the lungs. But the pathology is in the abdomen, and unless you ask the right question and send the right test, you can spiral into unnecessary procedures, unnecessary medications, and unnecessary delay.

In this case, the child was started on empiric antitubercular therapy based on a contact history and a mildly elevated ADA. It wasn't until the third admission that a careful re-history revealed blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle one month prior. A CT chest showed a multiloculated collection tracking from the right paraesophageal region to the tail of the pancreas. MRCP confirmed a fistulous tract from the pancreatic duct, through the retroperitoneum, through the esophageal hiatus, and into the right pleural cavity. Pleural fluid amylase was 6118 U/L and lipase was 7211 U/L. The antitubercular drugs were stopped. The actual disease was found.

## The Anatomic Mechanism: How Pancreas Gets Into the Chest

This is where the anatomy matters enormously. When the pancreatic duct or a pseudocyst ruptures anteriorly, pancreatic fluid leaks into the peritoneal cavity and you get ascites. But when the rupture occurs posteriorly — as it did in this child — pancreatic fluid enters the retroperitoneum and follows the path of least resistance superiorly. It tracks upward through natural diaphragmatic openings, most commonly the esophageal or aortic hiatus, enters the mediastinum, and then ruptures through the pleura to create a pleural effusion.

Think of it as a leaky pipe. The pancreas is the source, the retroperitoneum is the conduit, and the pleural cavity is the sink. The fluid travels a remarkable distance to get there, which is precisely why the clinical presentation is so misleading. The CT in this case captured this beautifully — an elongated, lobulated, multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right pleural space, through the right paraesophageal region, to the upper retroperitoneum and the pancreatic tail.

## The Pulmonary Mask: Why Everyone Gets Fooled

The data is striking. In a literature review of PPF cases, 68% presented with predominant respiratory symptoms — massive recurrent unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, dyspnea, and cough. Fewer than 20% had abdominal symptoms like epigastric pain, vomiting, or weight loss. About 24% had constitutional symptoms like fever. In adults, the pattern is the same — Uchiyama et al.'s landmark review of 113 Japanese cases found chest symptoms predominated in 68% of patients.

This makes sense anatomically. The fistula drains cephalad, away from the abdomen. The pancreas may be injured, but the symptoms manifest where the fluid accumulates — in the chest. The child in this case had no abdominal pain, no vomiting, no epigastric tenderness. He had tachypnea, hypoxia to 88–89% on room air, decreased breath sounds, and stony dullness on the right. Everything about the presentation said "pleural disease." Nothing about it said "pancreas."

## When to Suspect PPF

The key clinical trigger is recurrence. A pleural effusion that reforms rapidly after drainage — especially a unilateral hemorrhagic one — should make you pause and think beyond the chest. In this case, the child was aspirated three times, each time yielding approximately one liter of dark red fluid, with symptom recurrence within 5–6 days.

The specific diagnostic anchor is pleural fluid amylase. A pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L is considered pathognomonic for PPF. In this case, it was 6118 U/L with a lipase of 7211 U/L. But here is the nuance — other conditions can also produce amylase-rich pleural fluid, including esophageal perforation, malignancy (lung, pancreatic, or metastatic), and even tuberculosis. What distinguishes PPF is that the amylase is pancreatic-type, whereas salivary-type amylase elevation is seen in malignancy and esophageal rupture. If your lab can perform amylase isoenzyme typing, it can help you narrow the differential before committing to advanced imaging.

Serum amylase and lipase may also be elevated, as they were in this case, but they are not always reliable — the fistula can drain directly into the pleural space without significant systemic elevation.

## Imaging: What to Order and What It Shows

MRCP is the diagnostic modality of choice for suspected PPF, offering 70–80% sensitivity for visualizing the fistulous tract and pancreatic duct anatomy. In this case, MRCP demonstrated the tubular cystic collection in the distal pancreatic body and tail, tracking posteriorly through the retroperitoneum and superiorly through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity, with a narrow communication to the left pleural cavity as well. It also showed communication with the distal pancreatic duct and proximal ductal narrowing.

CT is useful as an initial investigation and may show the collection tracking from the pleura toward the pancreas, but it only depicts the actual fistula in 33–47% of cases. ERCP remains the gold standard for therapeutic planning, with a fistula visualization rate of 38–78% in children — but it is invasive and typically reserved for cases where intervention is planned, not just diagnosis.

## Management: A Stepwise Approach

PPF lacks standardized treatment guidelines in pediatrics — only 47 cases have been reported in the pediatric literature. Management is stratified based on whether there is ductal obstruction.

Conservative therapy is first-line when there is no pancreatic duct anomaly. This includes bowel rest (nil per oral), total parenteral nutrition, somatostatin analogues (octreotide at 10–40 µg/kg/day, this child received 30 µg/kg/day), pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy, and thoracic drainage via intercostal tube. The rationale is straightforward: octreotide suppresses exocrine pancreatic secretion, bowel rest reduces pancreatic stimulation, and the fistula is given time to close. However, the success rate of conservative management in pediatric PPF is only about 12%.

If conservative therapy fails after 2–3 weeks, endoscopic intervention via ERCP is the next step. Transpapillary stenting reduces intraductal pressure and allows the fistula to close, typically within 2–3 weeks. ERCP has shown a 100% success rate in reported pediatric cases, with long-term success exceeding 90% in mixed-age cohorts.

Surgery is reserved for cases where stent placement is not possible due to severe duct obstruction or distally located fistulas, or when there is no response to medical or endoscopic management over 3–4 weeks. Options include distal pancreatectomy, pancreatojejunostomy (Puestow procedure), or cystojejunostomy. Surgery carries a 93% success rate in the 15 reported pediatric cases, with operative mortality under 5% and recurrence around 11%.

In this child, conservative management worked. Over 10 days of NPO, TPN, octreotide, and PERT, drainage from the intercostal tube gradually decreased and stopped. The tube was removed and the child was discharged with no recurrent effusions on follow-up.

## Clinical Pearls

- **Always think pancreas in recurrent unilateral hemorrhagic pleural effusion.** The fluid is dark red because it is hemorrhagic and enzyme-rich — not because of infection or malignancy until proven otherwise.
- **Send pleural fluid amylase and lipase early.** A pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L is pathognomonic for PPF. If you do not send it, you cannot make the diagnosis.
- **Take a trauma history repeatedly.** The child in this case concealed his bicycle injury initially. Pediatric histories are unreliable, especially when bullying or embarrassment is involved. Ask again. Ask differently.
- **Do not be anchored by TB contact history and mildly elevated ADA.** This child was started on antitubercular therapy before the diagnosis was reconsidered. A mildly elevated ADA in a hemorrhagic effusion is not diagnostic of tuberculosis.
- **MRCP over CT for fistula visualization.** CT may show the collection tracking toward the pancreas, but MRCP is the modality that defines the fistulous anatomy and ductal anatomy you need for management decisions.
- **Posterior rupture goes up, anterior rupture goes down.** If the pancreatic duct ruptures posteriorly, fluid tracks retroperitoneally through the hiatus and presents as a pleural effusion. If it ruptures anteriorly, you get ascites. This anatomic distinction explains the entire clinical presentation.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- Pancreatopleural fistula is rare in children (only 47 reported cases) but should be suspected in any child with recurrent unilateral pleural effusion of unclear etiology, especially with a history of abdominal trauma or pancreatitis.
- Over 68% of PPF cases present with predominant respiratory symptoms and fewer than 20% with abdominal symptoms — the pancreas hides behind the pleura.
- Pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L is pathognomonic; always send it in recurrent or hemorrhagic effusions.
- MRCP (70–80% sensitivity) is the diagnostic modality of choice; CT depicts the fistula in only 33–47% of cases.
- Conservative management (NPO, TPN, octreotide, PERT, chest drainage) is first-line but succeeds in only ~12% of pediatric cases. ERCP with stenting has a 100% success rate in reported pediatric cases and should be considered early if conservative therapy fails.
- Ask about abdominal trauma repeatedly in children — the history may not surface on the first encounter.

## The Bottom Line

PPF is a disease that wears a pulmonary mask. The child presents to you with respiratory distress, chest pain, and a pleural effusion — and you will be tempted to look only at the chest. But if the effusion keeps coming back, if the fluid is dark red, and if the ADA is only mildly elevated in the setting of a TB contact, stop and ask: could this be the pancreas? Send the amylase. Get the MRCP. And ask about trauma — again.



# Pleural Fluid Amylase: The Most Underutilized Test in Unexplained Effusions

## The Case That Stopped Us in Our Tracks

A child presents with recurrent right-sided pleural effusions. Dark red fluid, liter after liter, drained on three separate admissions. There's a history of TB contact. The ADA is mildly elevated. So naturally, you start antitubercular therapy. Except the effusion keeps coming back. And back. And then—only on the third admission, only after repeated history-taking—does the child mention he took a bicycle handle to the abdomen a month ago.

That one detail flipped the entire diagnostic trajectory. A CT chest revealed a multiloculated collection tracking from the right paraesophageal region all the way to the tail of the pancreas. Pleural fluid amylase came back at 6118 U/L. Lipase at 7211 U/L. MRCP confirmed bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas (PPF). Antitubercular drugs were stopped. The child was managed with bowel rest, TPN, octreotide, and pancreatic enzyme replacement—and recovered fully within 10 days.

This case, published by Vaish and colleagues, is a masterclass in diagnostic anchoring. The team did what any reasonable clinician would do: worked up the effusion thoracically, chased TB, and treated empirically. But the pathology was never in the chest. It was in the pancreas, leaking enzyme-rich fluid through the esophageal hiatus into the pleural space, wearing what the authors so aptly call a "pulmonary mask."

## Why Pleural Fluid Amylase Isn't Just for Pancreatologists

Here is the key question: when was the last time you sent pleural fluid amylase on a routine effusion workup? If you're like most of us, the answer is rarely or never. Yet this single, inexpensive, widely available test would have short-circuited this entire diagnostic odyssey on the very first thoracocentesis.

The literature is clear on this point: a pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L is essentially pathognomonic for a pancreatopleural fistula. In this case, the amylase was over 6000 IU/L—six times the diagnostic threshold. That result was sitting in a tube of dark red fluid on day one, and nobody thought to check for it because the clinical gaze was locked squarely on the chest.

The anatomy explains the misdirection. When the pancreatic duct or a pseudocyst ruptures posteriorly, fluid follows the path of least resistance through the retroperitoneum, ascends through the aortic or esophageal hiatus, and enters the mediastinum and pleural cavity. The patient has no abdominal symptoms—less than 20% of PPF cases do—and presents with dyspnea, cough, and chest pain. Over 68% of cases present with predominantly respiratory complaints. The pancreas is silent. The chest is screaming.

## The Differential: Not Every High Amylase Is Pancreatic

This is where it gets nuanced, and where amylase isoenzyme typing becomes critical. Several conditions produce amylase-rich pleural effusions, and the source of the amylase matters enormously:

- **Pancreatopleural fistula**: Pancreatic-type amylase, massively elevated (≥1000 IU/L), often with concomitantly elevated lipase
- **Boerhaave syndrome (esophageal perforation)**: Salivary-type amylase from leaked saliva, typically with history of vomiting and chest pain—though as this case reminds us, absence of vomiting doesn't exclude everything
- **Malignancy (lung, pancreatic, leukemia, lymphoma)**: Can produce amylase-rich effusions, but amylase is typically salivary-type; cytology, CBC, peripheral smear, and clinical context help differentiate
- **Tuberculosis**: Can mildly elevate pleural fluid amylase and ADA, as it did in this case—highlighting how easily TB becomes a false trail, especially with a positive contact history

The distinction between pancreatic-type and salivary-type amylase is the diagnostic linchpin. PPF produces pancreatic-type amylase. Boerhaave and most malignancies produce salivary-type. If your lab can perform isoenzyme typing, request it. If it can't, a markedly elevated amylase (≥1000 IU/L) with an elevated lipase in the right clinical context strongly favors a pancreatic source.

## Imaging: What to Order and When

Once you suspect PPF, the imaging pathway is well defined, though each modality has limitations worth knowing:

MRCP offers 70–80% sensitivity for visualizing the fistula and ductal anatomy and should be your go-to confirmatory study. CT reliably demonstrates pancreatic calcifications, pseudocysts, and ductal dilation but actually depicts the fistula in only 33–47% of cases—meaning a normal CT does not exclude PPF. ERCP remains the gold standard for therapeutic planning, with fistula visualization rates of 38–78% in children, and serves as both a diagnostic and therapeutic tool if stenting is required.

In this case, it was the CT that first suggested the diagnosis by revealing the tracking collection from paraesophageal region to pancreatic tail. MRCP then confirmed it, showing the collection communicating with the distal pancreatic duct with proximal ductal narrowing—the anatomic explanation for why secretions were being diverted posteriorly and superiorly rather than into the duodenum.

## A Practical Approach to Unexplained Effusions

This case begs the question of when to add pleural fluid amylase to your standard effusion workup. The answer is broader than you might think. Any recurrent or massive pleural effusion without a clear thoracic explanation warrants it. Any unilateral effusion with a history of pancreatitis, pancreatic trauma, or chronic abdominal symptoms warrants it. Any hemorrhagic or blood-stained effusion that keeps recurring after drainage warrants it. And frankly, given how inexpensive and available the test is, the threshold should be low.

The standard pleural fluid workup—cell count, differential, protein, LDH, glucose, pH, Gram stain, culture, cytology, ADA—covers infection, malignancy, and TB. But it completely misses pancreatic pathology. Adding amylase (and lipase, if available) costs almost nothing and would have prevented three hospital admissions, two thoracocenteses, an intercostal drain, and several days of unnecessary antitubercular therapy in this child.

## Treatment: A Stepwise Framework

PPF management follows a clear escalation pathway, though it's worth noting that conservative success rates in children are only about 12%—far lower than the anecdotal impression most clinicians carry from adult data.

First-line therapy is medical: bowel rest (NPO), total parenteral nutrition, somatostatin analogues (octreotide at 10–40 µg/kg/day), pancreatic enzyme replacement, and thoracic drainage via chest tube. Give it 2–3 weeks. In this case, it worked—the child's drainage tapered to zero over 10 days. But don't count on it.

If medical management fails, move to ERCP with transpapillary stenting, which reduces intraductal pressure and allows the fistula to close. Endoscopic intervention closes fistulas within 2–3 weeks in about 48% of patients, with a 100% technical success rate and over 90% long-term success in mixed-age cohorts.

If stenting isn't feasible—due to severe duct obstruction or a distally located fistula—or if there's no response after 3–4 weeks of medical therapy, surgery is the next step. Options include distal pancreatectomy, pancreatojejunostomy (Puestow procedure), or cystojejunostomy, with a 93% success rate and under 5% operative mortality in pediatric series.

## Key Take-Home Points

- **Send pleural fluid amylase on any recurrent, massive, or unexplained pleural effusion.** It's cheap, fast, and would have diagnosed this case on day one. A pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L is pathognomonic for pancreatopleural fistula.
- **Amylase isoenzyme typing separates pancreatic-type from salivary-type amylase** and distinguishes PPF from Boerhaave syndrome, malignancy, and other mimics. If your lab can do it, order it. If not, a massively elevated amylase with elevated lipase in the right clinical context is highly suggestive.
- **Pancreatopleural fistula wears a pulmonary mask.** Over 68% of cases present with respiratory symptoms and fewer than 20% have abdominal complaints. The pathology is in the pancreas; the symptoms are in the chest.
- **MRCP is the preferred confirmatory imaging modality** (70–80% sensitivity for fistula visualization). CT may show the tracking collection but misses the fistula in over half of cases. ERCP is the gold standard for therapeutic planning.
- **Blunt abdominal trauma is a major pediatric trigger** for PPF, comprising up to 32% of cases in one cohort. Take a detailed trauma history in any child with an unexplained recurrent pleural effusion—even if the injury seemed minor at the time.
- **Conservative management has only a 12% success rate in children.** Start with bowel rest, TPN, octreotide, PERT, and chest tube drainage, but have a low threshold to escalate to ERCP with stenting if drainage persists beyond 2–3 weeks.
- **Don't anchor on TB just because the ADA is mildly elevated and there's a contact history.** This child received unnecessary antitubercular therapy because the team anchored on a plausible but incomplete diagnosis. Elevated pleural fluid amylase would have redirected the workup immediately.



# Anchoring Bias in Action: From Empiric TB Therapy to Pancreatopleural Fistula

Imagine a child presenting three times to the emergency department with recurrent, massive, dark red pleural effusions. You tap a liter of fluid each time, he gets better temporarily, and then he bounces right back. You have a mildly elevated pleural fluid ADA and a family history of tuberculosis. Do you start empiric antitubercular therapy? That is exactly what happened in this recently published case of a pediatric pancreatopleural fistula (PPF). It is a masterclass in how anchoring bias and premature closure can lead us down the wrong diagnostic path, causing us to treat the symptom while completely missing the pathology lurking below the diaphragm.

## The Case: A Pulmonary Wolf in Pancreatic Sheep's Clothing

A boy in late childhood, obese with a BMI of 35, presented to the outpatient department with a week of progressive dyspnea and right-sided chest pain. He was hypoxic, tachycardic, and clinical exam confirmed a massive right-sided pleural effusion. A chest X-ray and bedside lung ultrasound agreed: there was a liter of fluid in there. The team performed a thoracentesis, draining a liter of dark red pleural fluid. The child improved dramatically, was weaned off oxygen, and was discharged. 

Six days later, he was back. Same symptoms, same massive right pleural effusion. Another liter of dark red fluid was aspirated. This time, the team leaned on a slightly elevated pleural fluid ADA and a history of his grandfather having pulmonary tuberculosis. Empiric antitubercular therapy was started. But five days after that, the child returned for his third admission. It was only then, after re-interrogating the history, that the boy revealed he had suffered blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle one month prior. 

A CT scan of the chest finally looked beyond the pleural space, revealing a multiloculated hypodense collection tracking from the right paraesophageal region all the way to the tail of the pancreas. The diagnosis was clinched with an MRCP and pleural fluid enzymes: the fluid had an amylase of 6118 U/L and a lipase of 7211 U/L. The antitubercular drugs were stopped, and treatment for a pancreatopleural fistula was initiated.

## The Cognitive Autopsy: Where Did We Drift?

This case is a brilliant, albeit cringe-inducing, example of cognitive bias in action. Let's break down the diagnostic errors across the three admissions. 

On the first admission, the team fell victim to **search satisfaction** and **premature closure**. The patient had a clear problem—respiratory distress from a pleural effusion. The intervention—thoracentesis—provided immediate symptomatic relief. By draining the fluid and seeing the child improve, the team closed the loop on the encounter without asking the most critical question: *Why does a child have a liter of dark red pleural fluid in the first place?* They treated the symptom and discharged the patient. 

On the second admission, **anchoring bias** took over. When the effusion recurred, the team didn't broaden their differential; they looked for evidence to support a common, familiar diagnosis. A mildly elevated ADA and a family history of TB provided just enough cognitive cover to anchor on tuberculosis. They ignored a massive red flag: the rapid reaccumulation of a hemorrhagic-looking effusion. Tuberculous effusions are lymphocytic exudates and rarely present as acutely recurrent, massive dark red fluid collections requiring repeated large-volume taps within days. By prematurely starting empiric ATT, they introduced unnecessary medication risks and delayed the actual diagnosis.

It wasn't until the third admission that the anchor broke. The team stepped back, took a repeated, thorough history, and finally uncovered the blunt abdominal trauma. Once the CT showed the tracking collection to the pancreas, the "pulmonary mask" fell off. 

## Looking Below the Diaphragm: The Anatomy of a PPF

Pancreatopleural fistulas are rare in adults and exceedingly rare in children, with only 47 pediatric cases reported in the literature. The pathology is simple but easily missed. When the pancreas is injured—by trauma, or chronic/alcoholic pancreatitis in adults—the pancreatic duct can rupture. If the rupture is posterior, the pancreatic fluid leaks into the retroperitoneum. From there, it follows the path of least resistance, tracking superiorly through the aortic or esophageal hiatus, breaking through the pleura, and filling the chest. The child presents with respiratory distress, but the disease is in the abdomen.

The fluid is often dark red or hemorrhagic, which can mimic a hemothorax or malignancy, further muddying the waters. But the diagnostic key is in the fluid analysis. A pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L is pathognomonic for a PPF. If you don't send it, you won't find it. 

Once diagnosed, the management shifted entirely. The child was made nil per oral, started on total parenteral nutrition, and given an octreotide infusion to suppress pancreatic exocrine function. Over 10 days, the chest tube output decreased and stopped, and the child was discharged without needing endoscopic or surgical intervention.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Beware the "Pulmonary Mask":** Recurrent, massive pleural effusions can have an abdominal origin. If a chest tube keeps filling, look below the diaphragm. 
*   **Don't stop at symptom relief:** Performing a successful thoracentesis is not a diagnosis. Always ask why the fluid is there, especially when it is hemorrhagic and reaccumulates rapidly.
*   **Send pleural fluid amylase and lipase:** In any recurrent or unexplained pleural effusion, especially with a history of abdominal trauma or pancreatitis, amylase and lipase levels are critical. A pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L is pathognomonic for a PPF.
*   **Anchor on data, not narratives:** A mildly elevated ADA and a TB contact history are weak threads for a diagnosis of massive recurrent hemorrhagic pleural effusion. Don't let availability bias push you toward empiric therapy when the clinical picture doesn't fit.
*   **Repeat the history:** When a patient bounces back, your initial history is flawed. Re-interrogate the patient. The missing piece of the puzzle (the bicycle handle trauma) was there all along.
*   **Consider PPF in children:** While rare, blunt abdominal trauma is a leading cause of pancreatic injury in kids. If they present with recurrent effusions weeks later, think of a fistulous track.



# From CT to MRCP to ERCP: Imaging the Invisible Leak

## The Case That Refused to Stay in the Chest

A boy in late childhood — obese, BMI 35 — presents with recurrent right-sided pleural effusions, chest pain, and respiratory distress. Three admissions in rapid succession. Each time, thoracocentesis drains a liter of dark red fluid. He gets temporarily better, then comes back. Empiric antitubercular therapy is started based on a positive family contact and mildly elevated pleural fluid ADA. Then, on the third admission, someone finally asks the right question: *has anything happened to your abdomen?* The boy recalls blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handlebar one month prior. The pleural fluid amylase comes back at 6118 U/L. The lipase? 7211 U/L. The pathology was never in the chest at all — it was in the pancreas, leaking through the esophageal hiatus into the pleural space via a pancreatopleural fistula (PPF).

This case is a textbook illustration of why PPF is called a disease with a "pulmonary mask." Over two-thirds of pediatric PPF cases present with predominant respiratory symptoms — massive recurrent pleural effusions, dyspnea, cough — and fewer than 20% report abdominal symptoms at all. The treating team chases thoracic etiologies: empyema, tuberculosis, malignancy, Boerhaave syndrome. Meanwhile, the pancreas is quietly emptying itself into the chest through a fistulous tract you cannot see on a chest X-ray. Only 47 pediatric cases of PPF have ever been reported. The diagnosis is almost always delayed, and the imaging strategy matters enormously in shortening that delay.

## Why CT Gets You Partway — But Not Far Enough

In this case, contrast-enhanced CT of the chest was the first cross-sectional study obtained, and it was genuinely informative. It showed patchy right-sided consolidation, a hydropneumothorax, and — critically — a multiloculated hypodense collection tracking from the right paraesophageal region down to the tail of the pancreas. That tracking collection was the breadcrumb trail. Without it, the team might have remained stuck in the thoracic differential for another admission cycle.

But here is the limitation that the literature makes clear: CT reliably demonstrates pancreatic calcifications, pseudocysts, and ductal dilation, but it visualizes the actual fistulous tract in only 33–47% of cases. CT tells you that *something* connects the pancreas to the pleural space — it shows the collection, the mass effect, the secondary pulmonary changes — but it frequently fails to delineate the fistula itself. In a disease where the fistula is the diagnosis, that is a meaningful gap. You can see the smoke on CT, but you often cannot see the fire.

In this case, CT was enough to shift the diagnostic momentum. The combination of a tracking hypodense collection, dark red pleural fluid, elevated pleural amylase, and the history of abdominal trauma pointed the team toward pancreatic pathology. But confirming the fistula — seeing its course, its communication with the pancreatic duct, the ductal anatomy proximal and distal to the leak — required the next step.

## MRCP: The Diagnostic Bridge

Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography was performed the same day the CT raised suspicion, and it delivered the definitive anatomical answer. MRCP revealed pancreatitis with a tubular cystic collection in the distal body and tail of the pancreas, extending posteriorly into the retroperitoneum, then tracking superiorly through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity. A narrow communication with the left pleural cavity was also identified. Crucially, MRCP demonstrated that the collection communicated with the distal pancreatic duct, with narrowing of the pancreatic duct proximal to that communication — a finding with direct therapeutic implications.

This is where MRCP earns its role as the diagnostic modality of choice for PPF. The literature reports 70–80% sensitivity for visualizing both the fistulous tract and the ductal anatomy. That dual capability is what makes MRCP uniquely valuable: it does not just show you the leak, it shows you the ductal anatomy around the leak, which determines whether conservative management has a chance or whether you need to escalate to endoscopic or surgical intervention.

In this child's case, the ductal narrowing proximal to the fistula communication was an important finding. A downstream stenosis like this maintains high intraductal pressure and prevents the fistula from closing spontaneously. The team proceeded with conservative management — NPO, total parenteral nutrition, octreotide at 30 µg/kg/day, and pancreatic enzyme replacement — and drainage tapered over 10 days. But the documented success rate of conservative management in pediatric PPF is only about 12%. The ductal anatomy on MRCP is what tells you whether you are in that 12% or whether you need to move to ERCP.

## ERCP: When Imaging Becomes Therapy

ERCP remains the gold standard for therapeutic planning in PPF, and its role is distinct from CT and MRCP because it is not purely diagnostic — it is interventional. Fistula visualization rates in children via ERCP range from 38–78%, and in pediatric case series, ERCP has demonstrated a 100% technical success rate with long-term success exceeding 90% in mixed-age cohorts.

The therapeutic logic is straightforward: if MRCP shows a ductal stenosis or a downstream obstruction maintaining pressure on the fistula, ERCP allows transpapillary stent placement to decompress the duct and bridge the leak. Stent insertion reduces intraductal pressure and diverts pancreatic secretions away from the fistulous tract, allowing it to heal. Endoscopic intervention typically closes the fistula within 2–3 weeks in approximately 48% of patients, with stents remaining in place from 4 weeks to 6 months.

When stent placement is not feasible — due to severe ductal obstruction, a distally located fistula inaccessible to endoscopic approach, or failure of 3–4 weeks of medical management — surgery becomes the next step. Options include distal pancreatectomy, pancreatojejunostomy (Puestow procedure), or cystojejunostomy, with a reported success rate of 93% in pediatric series, operative mortality under 5%, and recurrence around 11%.

This child was managed successfully without ERCP or surgery. The drainage stopped on conservative therapy, the chest tube was removed at 10 days, and there have been no recurrences on follow-up. But with only a 12% conservative success rate in children, this outcome should be viewed as the favorable exception, not the expected rule.

## A Stepwise Imaging Algorithm for Suspected PPF

The imaging pathway in this case mirrors what the literature supports, and it is worth articulating as an algorithm for both pediatric and adult presentations:

**Step 1 — Chest X-ray and lung ultrasound.** These identify the pleural effusion and quantify it. They are the entry point, but they cannot reveal etiology. In this case, ultrasound estimated one liter of fluid, and X-ray showed right-sided effusion with costophrenic blunting. Repeated presentations with the same finding should trigger a broader differential.

**Step 2 — Pleural fluid analysis with amylase and lipase.** This is not imaging, but it is the linchpin that determines whether imaging should pursue a pancreatic source at all. Pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L is pathognomonic for PPF. In this case, amylase was 6118 U/L and lipase was 7211 U/L. Amylase typing can further distinguish PPF (pancreatic-type amylase) from malignancy or esophageal perforation (salivary-type amylase). Without pleural fluid amylase, the imaging pathway may never be directed toward the pancreas.

**Step 3 — Contrast-enhanced CT of chest and abdomen.** CT identifies pancreatic calcifications, pseudocysts, ductal dilation, and any tracking collection bridging the retroperitoneum and pleural space. It visualizes the fistula itself in only 33–47% of cases, but it establishes whether a pancreatic process exists and whether there is anatomical continuity between pancreas and chest. In this case, the multiloculated hypodense collection tracking from the paraesophageal region to the pancreatic tail was the CT finding that redirected the entire workup.

**Step 4 — MRCP.** This is the diagnostic confirmation step. With 70–80% sensitivity for fistula visualization and the ability to delineate ductal anatomy — including stenosis, dilation, and the relationship of the fistula to the main pancreatic duct — MRCP determines whether conservative management is reasonable or whether ductal obstruction mandates endoscopic intervention. In this case, MRCP confirmed the fistula, identified bilateral pleural communication, and showed proximal ductal narrowing.

**Step 5 — ERCP.** Reserved for cases where ductal anatomy on MRCP suggests obstruction amenable to stenting, or where conservative management has failed after 2–3 weeks. ERCP is both diagnostic and therapeutic, with fistula visualization rates of 38–78% in children and therapeutic success approaching 100% in pediatric series. In adults, ERCP is often reached earlier given the predominance of chronic alcoholic pancreatitis and the higher likelihood of ductal pathology.

**Step 6 — Surgery.** Considered when ERCP is not feasible or has failed, when there is severe ductal obstruction with a distal fistula, or after 3–4 weeks of failed medical and endoscopic management.

## Clinical Pearls

- **PPF wears a pulmonary mask.** Over 68% of patients present with respiratory symptoms; fewer than 20% report abdominal symptoms. The pancreas is the last thing anyone looks at when the complaint is pleural effusion. Ask about abdominal trauma — in pediatric series, blunt abdominal injury accounts for roughly one-third of PPF cases.
- **Pleural fluid amylase is the single highest-yield test** in a recurrent pleural effusion of unclear etiology. A level ≥1000 IU/L is pathognomonic for PPF. Send it early, especially if the fluid is dark red or hemorrhagic in appearance and recurs after drainage.
- **CT shows the shadow; MRCP shows the fistula.** CT identifies the tracking collection and pancreatic pathology but misses the fistulous tract in over half of cases. MRCP visualizes both the tract and the ductal anatomy that determines management.
- **The ductal anatomy on MRCP drives the therapeutic decision.** A proximal stenosis means the fistula will not close with conservative management alone. In this case, the child responded to conservative therapy despite proximal ductal narrowing — an outcome that is the exception given the 12% pediatric conservative success rate.
- **ERCP is not just confirmatory — it is therapeutic.** Transpapillary stenting decompresses the duct and diverts secretions, closing the fistula in approximately 48% of cases within 2–3 weeks. In pediatric series, technical success approaches 100%.
- **In children, think genetics and trauma.** Unlike adults, where alcohol-related chronic pancreatitis dominates, pediatric PPF is driven by hereditary pancreatitis (PRSS1, SPINK1, CFTR mutations) and blunt abdominal trauma. A detailed family history of pancreatitis and a careful trauma history are essential.
- **Bilateral pleural effusions do not exclude PPF.** This child's MRCP demonstrated communication with both pleural cavities, right greater than left. The fistula can track through the esophageal or aortic hiatus and reach either hemithorax.

## The Take-Home

Recurrent pleural effusion in a child is not always thoracic. When the effusion is dark, recurrent, and unresponsive to standard thoracic management, send pleural fluid amylase and ask about abdominal trauma. If amylase is elevated, get a CT to look for a tracking collection, then confirm with MRCP — and let the ductal anatomy on MRCP guide whether you try conservative management or move straight to ERCP. In PPF, the chest is the bystander. The pancreas is the culprit. The imaging strategy is what connects the two.



# Conservative vs. Invasive: Navigating the Management of Pancreatopleural Fistula

## The Diagnostic Trap That Almost Cost This Kid Months of Unnecessary TB Therapy

A pre-teen boy shows up to your ED — three visits in two weeks — with recurrent right-sided pleural effusions, each time draining a liter of dark red fluid. You've ruled out the usual suspects. You start antitubercular therapy empirically because of a distant family contact and a mildly elevated ADA. Then, on the third admission, the kid finally mentions he took a bicycle handle to the abdomen a month ago. The pleural fluid amylase comes back at 6118 U/L. MRCP shows a fistulous track from the pancreatic tail, through the esophageal hiatus, into the right pleural cavity. Boom — pancreatopleural fistula. The TB drugs get stopped, the octreotide starts, and ten days later he's discharged. No recurrence since.

This case is a textbook reminder that PPF wears a pulmonary mask. The pancreas is the problem, but the chest is where the symptoms live. And once you've made the diagnosis, you face an even harder question: how do you treat something so rare that no guidelines exist?

## The Treatment Ladder: What the Literature Actually Shows

PPF management in pediatrics is built on case reports and small case series — not randomized trials. The treatment ladder has three rungs, and the jump from one to the next is driven by failure, not protocol.

**Conservative management** is always step one, assuming no ductal anomaly that demands immediate intervention. The principles are straightforward: stop feeding the pancreas, drain the chest, and suppress pancreatic exocrine function. Specifically, you put the child NPO, start total parenteral nutrition, initiate octreotide (typically 10–40 μg/kg/day, with 30 μg/kg/day used in this case), add pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy, and place an intercostal drainage tube. The logic is elegant — bowel rest reduces pancreatic stimulation, octreotide suppresses exocrine secretion, and the chest tube buys you time while the fistulous tract closes on its own.

But here is the sobering number: the success rate of conservative management in pediatric PPF is approximately 12%. That's not a typo. Twelve percent. In adults, conservative therapy does better, but in kids, the odds are stacked against you from the start.

**Endoscopic intervention** via ERCP is the next rung and represents the real workhorse of definitive therapy. Transpapillary stenting reduces intraductal pressure and allows the fistula to close. The acute success rate of ERCP in pediatric cases approaches 100%, with long-term success exceeding 90% in mixed-age cohorts. Stents typically stay in place from 4 weeks to 6 months, and fistula closure generally occurs within 2–3 weeks of stent placement, with success in about 48% of patients. When ductal stenosis is present, balloon dilation can be performed alongside stenting. ERCP also serves as the gold standard for therapeutic planning — it visualizes the fistula in 38–78% of pediatric cases and gives you the anatomy you need to decide whether surgery is necessary.

**Surgery** is the final rung, reserved for cases where stent placement is impossible due to severe duct obstruction or a distally located fistula, or when medical management fails after 3–4 weeks. Options include distal pancreatectomy, pancreatojejunostomy (the Puestow procedure), or cystojejunostomy. The surgical success rate in pediatric series is approximately 93%, with operative mortality under 5% and recurrence around 11%. These are strong numbers, but surgery carries real morbidity — especially in a child — so it remains a last resort.

## How Long Do You Wait Before Escalating?

This is the million-dollar question, and the honest answer is: nobody knows for sure. The literature suggests a conservative trial of 2–3 weeks before escalating to ERCP, and 3–4 weeks of failed medical management before considering surgery. In this case, the child responded to conservative therapy within 10 days — drainage tapered, the chest tube came out, and he went home. That puts him in the lucky 12%.

But consider the counterfactual. If this child had not responded, each day of ongoing pancreatic leakage into the pleural space means continued inflammation, risk of infection, and prolonged hospitalization. Given the low success rate of conservative therapy in kids, some clinicians argue for earlier ERCP — perhaps after 1–2 weeks of failed conservative management rather than the full 3 weeks — especially since ERCP is both diagnostic and therapeutic with near-universal acute success.

The lack of standardized guidelines means you are making this decision case by case, weighing the risks of endoscopy in a child against the low probability of spontaneous closure.

## What This Case Teaches Us

This child's journey illustrates several critical points. First, the diagnosis was delayed because nobody thought to ask about abdominal trauma until the third admission — and even then, it took repeated history-taking sessions for the child to mention the bicycle handle injury. Second, the team initially pursued a tuberculosis workup based on a mildly elevated ADA and a family contact history, which is understandable in an endemic setting but ultimately a red herring. Third, the pleural fluid appearance — dark red, recurrent, massive — was the clue that something unusual was happening, and the amylase level confirmed it.

The imaging ladder matters too. CT reliably shows pancreatic calcifications, pseudocysts, and ductal dilation but only visualizes the actual fistula in 33–47% of cases. MRCP offers better fistula detection at 70–80% sensitivity and was diagnostic in this case. ERCP remains the gold standard for both visualization and therapeutic planning but is invasive and requires specialized pediatric expertise.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L is pathognomonic for PPF.** In this case, it was 6118 U/L. If you see a recurrent hemorrhagic pleural effusion in a child, send amylase and lipase on the fluid. Pancreatic-type amylase distinguishes PPF from malignancy or esophageal perforation, which produce salivary-type amylase.
- **Conservative management works only ~12% of the time in kids.** Start it, but have a low threshold to escalate. NPO, TPN, octreotide (10–40 μg/kg/day), PERT, and chest tube drainage are the building blocks.
- **ERCP is the therapeutic workhorse.** Near-100% acute success rate, >90% long-term success, and fistula closure within 2–3 weeks of stenting. Consider it after 1–2 weeks of failed conservative therapy rather than waiting the full 3 weeks, especially in children where the conservative success rate is so low.
- **Surgery is the last resort with strong numbers.** Distal pancreatectomy, Puestow procedure, or cystojejunostomy carry a 93% success rate, <5% mortality, and ~11% recurrence. Reserve for cases where stenting is anatomically impossible or medical management fails after 3–4 weeks.
- **No standardized guidelines exist.** Every treatment decision is individualized. Document your reasoning, set clear timelines for reassessment, and involve pediatric gastroenterology and interventional radiology early.
- **Take a trauma history. Repeatedly.** This child concealed his bicycle handle injury, and the team only uncovered it on the third admission. Kids don't always volunteer trauma histories — especially when bullying is involved. Ask directly, ask multiple times, and ask the caregiver separately.
- **PPF wears a pulmonary mask.** The pathology is in the abdomen, but the symptoms are in the chest. Over two-thirds of cases present with respiratory complaints and less than 20% have abdominal symptoms. If your pleural effusion keeps coming back and the pulmonary workup is negative, look south — at the pancreas.



# Pediatric PPF: Why Children Are a Different Animal Than Adults

## The Case That Almost Went Down the Tuberculosis Rabbit Hole

A boy in late childhood — BMI of 35, frequent consumer of processed foods — shows up to the outpatient department with a week of progressive dyspnea, right-sided chest pain, and oxygen saturations of 88–89% on room air. Exam reveals classic right-sided pleural effusion findings: stony dullness, reduced fremitus, diminished breath sounds. Bedside ultrasound shows roughly a liter of fluid. You tap it. Dark red pleural fluid. One liter. He improves, goes home, and comes back six days later with the exact same problem. You tap it again. Another liter of dark red fluid. This time the ADA is mildly elevated and there's a known TB contact in the family — so empiric antitubercular therapy gets started. Five days later, he's back a third time.

It wasn't until the third admission that someone went back and took a really careful history. The boy recalled blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handlebar one month earlier. A CT chest showed a multiloculated hypodense collection tracking from the right paraesophageal region all the way to the tail of the pancreas. Pleural fluid amylase came back at 6118 U/L and lipase at 7211 U/L. MRCP confirmed bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas — right greater than left — with a pancreatic duct communication. This was post-traumatic pancreatopleural fistula (PPF), and the antitubercular drugs were stopped immediately.

What makes this case so instructive is not just the diagnosis itself — it's that it illustrates perfectly why pediatric PPF is a completely different clinical animal than what most of us learned about in training, where the entity is almost exclusively discussed in the context of adults with chronic alcoholic pancreatitis.

## The Adult Paradigm Doesn't Translate

If you read the adult literature on PPF, the story is remarkably consistent. The landmark single-country review by Uchiyama et al documented 113 cases from the Japanese literature alone, and a striking 99% were related to chronic alcoholic pancreatitis. The adult patient who walks in with a massive recurrent pleural effusion and a history of chronic pancreatitis is a pattern recognition success — the diagnosis may be delayed, but the pancreas is at least on the differential.

In children, the etiologic landscape is almost entirely different. Genetic mutations — PRSS1, SPINK1, CFTR, and CTRC — are the dominant drivers of chronic pancreatitis leading to PPF. SPINK1 and PRSS1 mutations alone account for up to 30% of pediatric chronic pancreatitis cases. The representative mutations are R122H and N291 in exon 3 and exon 2 of the PRSS1 gene, respectively. Blunt abdominal trauma is the other major non-hereditary trigger, comprising 32% of the 25-patient cohort analyzed by Yang et al. Idiopathic cases continue to appear, likely reflecting under-recognized genetic or ductal variants that haven't been characterized yet.

This etiologic divergence matters enormously at the bedside. You're not going to get the diagnosis of pediatric PPF by asking about alcohol use. You need to be asking about family history of pancreatitis, recurrent abdominal pain, and — as this case powerfully demonstrates — seemingly minor blunt abdominal trauma that the child may not even volunteer unless you ask directly and repeatedly.

## Why Pediatric PPF Hides Behind a Pulmonary Mask

The numbers tell the story of why this diagnosis is so easy to miss. Only 47 pediatric cases of PPF have been reported in the entire literature, compared with over 300 in adults. In those pediatric reports, 68% of cases presented with predominant respiratory symptoms — massive recurrent unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, dyspnea, cough. Less than 20% had abdominal symptoms. About 24% had constitutional symptoms like fever.

Think about what that means for the clinician. A child comes in with respiratory distress and a pleural effusion. You're going to work up TB, empyema, malignancy, para-pneumonic effusion. You're going to send pleural fluid for cell count, culture, cytology, ADA. You are probably not going to send amylase and lipase on that fluid unless something tips you off. And in children, there's often nothing abdominal in the history to tip you off — the abdominal symptoms that would prompt a pancreatic workup are absent in over 80% of cases.

The pathophysiology explains the clinical presentation. When the pancreatic duct or a pseudocyst ruptures posteriorly, pancreatic fluid follows the path of least resistance into the retroperitoneum, tracks superiorly through the aortic or esophageal hiatus, and breaks through the pleura. The pancreas is essentially a leaky pipe diverting its secretions into the chest. If the rupture is anterior, you get ascites. If it's posterior, you get a pleural effusion — and the pleural effusion is what the patient presents with.

## When to Chase the Pancreas in a Child With a Pleural Effusion

The diagnostic threshold that should change your practice is straightforward: pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L is considered pathognomonic for PPF. In this case, the pleural fluid amylase was 6118 U/L. But the key is that you have to send it. If you have a child with a recurrent pleural effusion — especially a hemorrhagic or dark-colored effusion — and the standard infectious and malignant workup is not yielding an answer, pleural fluid amylase and lipase need to be on your panel.

There's a nuance here that matters. Other conditions can give you elevated pleural fluid amylase — esophageal perforation (Boerhaave syndrome), malignancy (lung, leukemia, lymphoma, metastatic disease). But PPF produces pancreatic-type amylase, while these other conditions produce salivary-type amylase. Pleural fluid amylase isoenzyme typing can distinguish PPF from its mimics if the diagnosis is unclear.

Imaging follows a specific hierarchy. MRCP offers 70–80% sensitivity for visualizing the fistula and duct anatomy. CT is excellent for showing pancreatic calcifications, pseudocysts, and ductal dilation, but only depicts the actual fistulous tract in 33–47% of cases. ERCP remains the gold standard for therapeutic planning, with fistula visualization rates in children ranging from 38–78%.

## Genetic Testing: Who, When, and Why

The case report doesn't mention whether this child underwent genetic testing — and given that his PPF was clearly post-traumatic, that may be entirely appropriate. But the broader pediatric pancreatitis literature makes a strong case for genetic workup in specific scenarios. If a child presents with recurrent pancreatitis, features of chronic pancreatitis, or a family history of pancreatitis, the yield of genetic testing is substantial. PRSS1 mutations cause hereditary pancreatitis with autosomal dominant inheritance, so a thorough family history across multiple generations is essential. SPINK1 mutations, CFTR mutations, and CTRC mutations each contribute to the genetic architecture of pediatric pancreatitis in different ways. In any child with unexplained or recurrent pancreatitis — and certainly in any child who develops PPF without a clear traumatic trigger — genetic testing and referral to pediatric gastroenterology or medical genetics is appropriate.

## Treatment: Conservative First, But Know the Numbers

PPF lacks standardized treatment guidelines in children due to its rarity, so management is built from case reports and small series. The conservative approach — bowel rest, total parenteral nutrition, somatostatin analogues (octreotide at 10–40 µg/kg/day), pancreatic enzyme replacement, and thoracic drainage — is the recommended initial strategy when there's no major pancreatic duct anomaly. The mechanism is logical: octreotide suppresses exocrine pancreatic function, bowel rest reduces pancreatic stimulation, and the fistulous tract is given the opportunity to close.

But here's the number that should temper your enthusiasm for prolonged conservative management: the success rate of conservative methods in pediatric PPF is only about 12%. In this case, it worked — drainage decreased over 10 days, the chest tube came out, and the child remained effusion-free at follow-up. But you should not be surprised when conservative management fails, and you should escalate promptly.

Endoscopic intervention via ERCP — transpapillary stenting, balloon dilatation for ductal stenosis — is the next step. Stent insertion reduces intraductal pressure and leakage, and endoscopic intervention generally closes the fistula within 2–3 weeks. ERCP had a 100% success rate in the reported pediatric cases, with a long-term success rate exceeding 90% in mixed-age cohorts. If stent placement isn't feasible due to severe duct obstruction or a distally located fistula, or if there's no response to medical management after 3–4 weeks, surgical options include distal pancreatectomy, pancreatojejunostomy (Puestow procedure), or cystojejunostomy, with a reported 93% success rate and operative mortality under 5%.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Pediatric PPF is rare but real.** Only 47 cases reported in children. The adult paradigm of alcoholic chronic pancreatitis does not apply — think genetic mutations (PRSS1, SPINK1, CFTR, CTRC) and blunt abdominal trauma instead.
- **PPF wears a pulmonary mask.** Over two-thirds of pediatric cases present with respiratory symptoms and fewer than 20% have abdominal symptoms. The pancreas is the pathology, but the chest is the presentation.
- **Send pleural fluid amylase and lipase on any recurrent or unexplained pleural effusion in a child.** Pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L is pathognomonic. If the standard infectious and malignant workup is negative, this is the test that unlocks the diagnosis.
- **Ask about abdominal trauma — and ask again.** In this case, the history of bicycle handlebar trauma only emerged on the third admission after repeated questioning. Children may not volunteer this information, particularly if they were concealing it for social reasons.
- **Pursue genetic testing in children with recurrent or chronic pancreatitis, especially with a positive family history.** PRSS1, SPINK1, CFTR, and CTRC mutations account for a significant proportion of pediatric chronic pancreatitis.
- **Start conservative management but know that it fails in the majority of pediatric cases.** Octreotide, bowel rest, TPN, and thoracic drainage have only a ~12% success rate in children. Move to ERCP with stenting early if conservative management isn't working — pediatric ERCP success rates approach 100%.
