Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

### [Clinical Pearls] What pleural fluid amylase level is considered pathognomonic for a pancreatopleural fistula, and how can amylase isoenzyme typing help distinguish PPF from other causes of amylase-rich pleural effusions such as esophageal perforation or malignancy?

# Pleural Fluid Amylase in Pancreatopleural Fistula

A pleural fluid amylase level **≥1000 IU/L is considered pathognomonic for pancreatopleural fistula (PPF)**. In this case, the child's pleural fluid amylase was markedly elevated at 6118 U/L, with a correspondingly high lipase of 7211 U/L, well above this diagnostic threshold and strongly supporting the diagnosis later confirmed by MRCP.

However, an elevated pleural fluid amylase alone is not entirely specific. Several other conditions can produce amylase-rich pleural effusions, notably **esophageal perforation (Boerhaave's syndrome)** and **malignancy**—particularly lung or pancreatic tumors. This is where **amylase isoenzyme typing** becomes clinically valuable.

Pleural fluid amylase exists in two principal isoforms: **pancreatic-type (P-type)** and **salivary-type (S-type)** amylase. In PPF, the pleural fluid is rich in **pancreatic-type amylase**, reflecting the direct leakage of pancreatic secretions from a disrupted pancreatic duct into the pleural cavity. In contrast, esophageal perforation and malignancy typically produce **salivary-type amylase** predominance—salivary amylase from saliva entering the pleural space in Boerhaave's, and paraneoplastic production of salivary-type isoamylase by certain tumors. This biochemical distinction allows clinicians to narrow the differential diagnosis even before definitive imaging is obtained.

In this case, the diagnostic reasoning illustrates the point well. Boerhaave's syndrome was clinically excluded—the child had no history of vomiting or retching and was passing stools normally—while malignancy was ruled out by the absence of constitutional symptoms, a normal blood picture, and negative cytology. Ultimately, it was the combination of the markedly elevated pleural fluid amylase exceeding the pathognomonic threshold, the history of blunt abdominal trauma, and the MRCP demonstration of a fistulous tract from the pancreatic tail through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity that established the diagnosis.

**Key teaching point:** When evaluating a recurrent pleural effusion with elevated amylase, the magnitude of elevation (≥1000 IU/L) and the isoenzyme type (pancreatic vs. salivary) together substantially refine the differential. PPF should be suspected when pancreatic-type amylase predominates, particularly when accompanied by elevated pleural fluid lipase—a pattern that, as in this case, points away from esophageal or malignant etiologies and toward a pancreatic source.

### [Pathophysiology] In a pancreatopleural fistula, how does the anatomical location of the pancreatic duct or pseudocyst rupture determine whether a patient develops ascites versus a pleural effusion, and through which natural diaphragmatic openings does pancreatic fluid typically track to reach the pleural cavity?

In a pancreatopleural fistula, the anterior-to-posterior location of the pancreatic duct or pseudocyst rupture is the critical determinant of whether pancreatic enzymes enter the peritoneal cavity (producing ascites) or the pleural space (producing a pleural effusion). When the rupture occurs on the **anterior surface** of the pancreas, pancreatic secretions leak freely into the peritoneal cavity, resulting in pancreatic ascites. When the rupture occurs **posteriorly**, as in this case, the fluid enters the retroperitoneum rather than the peritoneum. From there, it follows the path of least resistance superiorly, tracking cephalad through natural diaphragmatic apertures—most commonly the **aortic hiatus** or the **esophageal hiatus**—into the mediastinum, where it then ruptures through the mediastinal pleura to establish a fistulous communication with the pleural cavity. Less commonly, fluid may reach the pleura via direct transdiaphragmatic erosion.

This anatomic principle is illustrated precisely in our patient. MRCP demonstrated a tubular cystic collection originating in the distal body and tail of the pancreas, extending **posteriorly** into the retroperitoneum and then **superiorly through the esophageal hiatus** into the right pleural cavity, with a narrower communication to the left. The CT similarly showed a multiloculated hypodense collection tracking from the right paraesophageal region to the pancreatic tail. The predominance of the right-sided effusion reflects the more voluminous right-sided fistulous tract, though bilateral involvement was documented.

The clinical implication is essential: a patient with a posterior pancreatic leak may present with **no abdominal symptoms whatsoever**, with respiratory complaints—recurrent massive pleural effusion, dyspnea, chest pain—serving as the sole manifestation. As the discussion notes, less than 20% of reported cases had abdominal symptoms, while 68% presented with predominant respiratory findings. This "pulmonary mask" explains why diagnosis is frequently delayed, as occurred in our patient, who underwent repeated thoracenteses and empirical antitubercular therapy before the pancreatic etiology was recognized after the history of blunt abdominal trauma was elicited.

**In short:** anterior rupture yields ascites; posterior rupture yields pleural effusion via retroperitoneal tracking through the esophageal or aortic hiatus. Recognizing this posterior-leak pathway is what allows one to look below the diaphragm for a thoracic problem's origin.

### [Morning Report] A child presents with a recurrent hemorrhagic unilateral pleural effusion. After two admissions focused on a pulmonary workup, a third careful history reveals blunt abdominal trauma one month prior. What features of the clinical presentation—pleural fluid appearance, pleural fluid biochemistry, and imaging findings—should shift the diagnostic focus from a thoracic to a pancreatic etiology, and why is the diagnosis of pancreatopleural fistula so frequently delayed?

# Pancreatopleural Fistula: When the Pancreas Hides Behind a Pulmonary Mask

## Key Clinical Features Shifting Diagnostic Focus

Three features should prompt consideration of a pancreatic etiology in a child with recurrent hemorrhagic pleural effusion:

**Pleural fluid appearance.** The pleural fluid in pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is characteristically dark red or hemorrhagic, recurrent, and massive — in this case, approximately one liter was drained on each of three separate admissions over a short interval. While hemorrhagic effusions raise concern for malignancy, tuberculosis, or trauma, the *recurrence* after complete drainage and the *absence* of a thoracic lesion to explain the bleeding should prompt a broader search. The hemorrhagic character reflects the caustic effect of activated pancreatic enzymes on the pleura, producing a inflammatory, exudative, and often bloody effusion.

**Pleural fluid biochemistry.** The single most critical laboratory finding is a markedly elevated pleural fluid amylase — in this case, 6118 U/L — with a pleural fluid lipase of 7211 U/L. A pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L is considered pathognomonic for PPF. This is not a subtle elevation; it is orders of magnitude above what would be expected in tuberculosis, malignancy, or parapneumonic effusion. Notably, the mildly elevated ADA and the history of Koch's contact in this patient initially led to empiric antitubercular therapy — a false trail that the amylase and lipase levels, once sent, decisively corrected. Isoenzyme typing can further distinguish pancreatic-type from salivary-type amylase, the latter being characteristic of esophageal perforation or certain malignancies.

**Imaging findings.** Contrast-enhanced CT of the chest was pivotal. It demonstrated not only right-sided hydropneumothorax and patchy consolidation, but critically, a multiloculated hypodense collection *tracking* from the right paraesophageal region through the retroperitoneum to the tail of the pancreas. This contiguous collection bridging the thorax and abdomen is the radiographic signature of PPF. MRCP then definitively confirmed the fistulous communication between the pancreatic duct and the pleural cavity, showing the collection extending posteriorly from the pancreatic body and tail through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural space. CT alone visualizes the fistula in only 33%–47% of cases, whereas MRCP offers 70%–80% sensitivity; ERCP remains the gold standard for both visualization and potential therapeutic intervention.

## Why Is the Diagnosis So Frequently Delayed?

The delay is rooted in a fundamental mismatch between the *site of pathology* and the *site of symptoms*. PPF is an internal pancreatic fistula: when the pancreatic duct or pseudocyst ruptures posteriorly, pancreatic secretions follow the path of least resistance through the retroperitoneum, ascend via the aortic or esophageal hiatus into the mediastinum, and break through the pleura. The patient therefore presents with dyspnea, chest pain, and cough — not abdominal pain. In the largest review of adult cases, 68% of patients had predominant respiratory symptoms, and fewer than 20% reported abdominal symptoms such as epigastric pain or vomiting. Children may additionally conceal or fail to volunteer the precipitating trauma, as occurred here — the history of blunt abdominal injury from a bicycle handlebar emerged only on the third admission, after repeated, careful questioning.

Clinicians naturally direct their workup toward the organ system that is symptomatic. In this case, two admissions were devoted to a pulmonary evaluation, including empiric antitubercular therapy based on a positive contact history and mildly elevated ADA. The rarity of PPF in children — only 47 documented pediatric cases — further reduces clinical suspicion. The result is a diagnostic delay that exposes patients to unnecessary investigations, empiric therapies, and the risk of recurrent pleural interventions and sepsis.

## Clinical Pearl

In any patient with a recurrent, large, hemorrhagic unilateral pleural effusion that reaccumulates rapidly after drainage and lacks a clear thoracic explanation, **send pleural fluid amylase and lipase** — and take a careful history for abdominal trauma or pancreatitis. A pleural fluid amylase exceeding 1000 IU/L should redirect the entire diagnostic evaluation from the chest to the pancreas, with cross-sectional imaging to trace a fistulous tract and MRCP to confirm ductal anatomy. As this case illustrates, PPF wears a pulmonary mask, and only a disciplined clinical history combined with targeted biochemistry will unmask it.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Besides pancreatopleural fistula, what other conditions can produce an amylase-rich pleural effusion, and what specific clinical, laboratory, or imaging findings help exclude each of these alternative diagnoses in a pediatric patient?

Amylrigh-rich pleural effusion, defined by a pleural fluid amylase level exceeding the serum reference range (and classically ≥1000 IU/L in pancreatopleural fistula), is not specific to pancreatic disease. In the pediatric patient described, several alternative diagnoses were systematically considered and excluded through a combination of clinical, laboratory, and imaging findings.

**Esophageal perforation (Boerhaave's syndrome)** can produce an amylase-rich effusion because salivary amylase leaks into the pleural space through the esophageal tear. In this case, the diagnosis was excluded because the child had no history of vomiting — the classic precipitant of Boerhaave's — and was passing stools normally without evidence of esophageal disruption on contrast-enhanced CT imaging.

**Malignancy**, including lung carcinoma, leukemia, lymphoma, and metastatic disease, can also generate amylase-rich pleural fluid, typically of the salivary isoenzyme type. In this child, the absence of fever and weight loss, a normal complete blood count and peripheral smear, and — most importantly — pleural fluid cytology showing no malignant cells effectively excluded this category.

**Tuberculous pleuritis** was a serious consideration given the child's documented Koch's contact and a mildly elevated pleural fluid ADA. However, the absence of chronic cough, weight loss, and fever, combined with a negative GeneXpert for *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*, argued against tuberculosis. Empirically started antitubercular therapy was appropriately discontinued once MRCP confirmed the pancreatic origin.

**Empyema** was excluded by the absence of fever and the lack of a neutrophilic, purulent exudate on pleural fluid analysis.

**Hepatic hydrothorax** was ruled out because liver function tests and hepatic imaging were normal and there was no history of chronic liver disease.

**Acquired diaphragmatic hernia** was excluded because no abdominal viscera were visualized within the thoracic cavity on CT.

**Renal pathology**, including hydronephrosis with urinothorax, was excluded by normal renal function tests and normal renal imaging.

A critical diagnostic principle, underscored by this case, is that **amylase isoenzyme typing** can further refine the differential: pancreatopleural fistula produces pancreatic-type amylase in the pleural fluid, whereas esophageal perforation and malignancy typically produce salivary-type amylase. In this child, the combination of a markedly elevated pleural fluid amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L), a history of blunt abdominal trauma, and MRCP demonstration of a fistulous tract from the pancreatic tail through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity confirmed PPF and rendered the alternative diagnoses untenable.

### [Management] What is the recommended stepwise approach to treating a pancreatopleural fistula in a pediatric patient, and how do the success rates of conservative medical management, endoscopic intervention via ERCP, and surgical options compare?

# Stepwise Management of Pancreatopleural Fistula in Pediatric Patients

The recommended approach to treating a pediatric pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) follows a three-tiered, stepwise strategy: **conservative medical management first, followed by endoscopic intervention via ERCP, and finally surgery** — escalated sequentially based on response. Given the extreme rarity of PPF in children (only 47 reported cases), this framework is derived from accumulated case reports rather than standardized guidelines.

## Tier 1: Conservative Medical Management

Initial treatment should always be conservative, particularly when there is no pancreatic duct anomaly. The components include bowel rest (nil per oral), total parenteral nutrition, somatostatin analogues such as octreotide (typically dosed at 10–40 µg/kg/day), pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT), and thoracic drainage via intercostal chest tube. Octreotide suppresses pancreatic exocrine function, while bowel rest allows the pancreas to rest and the fistulous tract to close. Conservative measures should be pursued for 2–3 weeks.

**However, the success rate of conservative therapy in pediatric PPF is disappointingly low — approximately 12%.**

In our case, despite this modest expected success rate, conservative management was effective. The child was kept nil per oral, received TPN and octreotide at 30 µg/kg/day, and had an intercostal drainage tube placed. Drainage gradually decreased over 10 days and ultimately ceased, allowing chest tube removal and discharge. He has remained symptom-free on follow-up with no recurrent effusions.

## Tier 2: Endoscopic Intervention (ERCP)

If conservative management fails, the next step is endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) with transpapillary stent placement. When ductal stenosis is present, ERCP may even be used as initial therapy given its minimal morbidity. Stent insertion works by reducing intraductal pressure and diverting pancreatic secretions away from the fistulous tract, typically closing the fistula within 2–3 weeks. Stents generally remain in place from 4 weeks to 6 months.

ERCP demonstrates markedly better success than conservative therapy — **100% success rate in reported pediatric cases**, with a long-term success rate exceeding 90% in mixed-age cohorts. ERCP also serves as the gold standard for therapeutic planning, with fistula visualization rates in children ranging from 38% to 78%.

## Tier 3: Surgical Intervention

Surgery is reserved for situations where stent placement is not feasible — such as severe ductal obstruction or a distally located fistula — or when there is no response to medical management after 3–4 weeks. Surgical options include distal pancreatectomy, pancreatojejunostomy (Puestow procedure), or cystojejunostomy, depending on fistula location and ductal anatomy.

Surgery carries a **93% success rate** based on 15 reported pediatric surgeries, with operative mortality below 5% and a recurrence rate of approximately 11%.

## Summary

| Modality | Success Rate (Pediatric) | Typical Duration Before Escalation |
|---|---|---|
| Conservative medical management | ~12% | 2–3 weeks |
| ERCP with stenting | ~100% (short-term); >90% (long-term) | 2–3 weeks to fistula closure |
| Surgery | ~93% | Reserved for refractory or anatomically unfavorable cases |

The key clinical takeaway is that while conservative management should always be attempted first, its low success rate in children means clinicians must have a low threshold to escalate to ERCP-guided stenting, which offers the highest success with the least morbidity. Surgery remains a durable backup for refractory or anatomically complex cases. As illustrated by our patient, however, a trial of conservative therapy is worthwhile — some patients, particularly those with post-traumatic PPF and favorable ductal anatomy, will respond completely without need for endoscopic or operative intervention.

### [Pathophysiology] How do the predominant etiologies of chronic pancreatitis leading to pancreatopleural fistula differ between adults and children, and which specific genetic mutations should be considered in a pediatric patient with recurrent or chronic pancreatitis?

## Etiology of Chronic Pancreatitis Leading to Pancreatopleural Fistula: Adults vs. Children

In adults, chronic alcohol-related pancreatitis is by far the predominant etiology of chronic pancreatitis leading to pancreatopleural fistula (PPF). The most comprehensive single-country review, conducted by Uchiyama et al in Japan, documented 113 cases and found that approximately 99% were attributable to chronic alcoholic pancreatitis. This stands in sharp contrast to the pediatric population, in which genetic causes predominate. Among children, SPINK1 and PRSS1 mutations together account for up to 30% of chronic pancreatitis cases. Additional important genetic contributors include CFTR (cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator) mutations and CTRC (chymotrypsin C) mutations. Notably, the most representative PRSS1 mutations are R122H (exon 3) and N29I (exon 2), both of which produce gain-of-function changes in trypsinogen that impair its normal degradation and promote premature intrapancreatic activation. In children, non-hereditary triggers—particularly blunt abdominal trauma—are also significant; in the 25-patient cohort analyzed by Yang et al, trauma accounted for 32% of cases, as illustrated by the present case, in which a bicycle handle injury precipitated the fistula.

The key clinical takeaway is this: when a pediatric patient presents with recurrent or chronic pancreatitis—especially with a PPF or another complication suggesting ductal disruption—a detailed family history should be obtained and hereditary pancreatitis must be actively considered. Genetic testing should include, at minimum, PRSS1, SPINK1, CFTR, and CTRC. This genetic workup is essential not only for diagnosis but also for counseling regarding recurrence risk and long-term pancreatic surveillance. As this case demonstrates, the etiologic framework in children is fundamentally different from that in adults, and failing to consider genetic causes risks both misdiagnosis and missed opportunities for familial screening.

### [Clinical Pearls] How do the diagnostic sensitivities of contrast-enhanced CT, MRCP, and ERCP compare for visualizing a pancreatopleural fistula and defining pancreatic duct anatomy, and which modality is considered the gold standard for therapeutic planning?

In pancreatopleural fistula (PPF), the choice of imaging modality is critical because the fistulous tract itself is often small and tortuous, and defining the pancreatic duct anatomy is essential for planning therapy. As illustrated in this case, contrast-enhanced CT provided important indirect evidence — a multiloculated hypodense collection tracking from the right paraesophageal region to the pancreatic tail — but it did not definitively delineate the fistula or the ductal communication. This is consistent with the known literature: CT reliably demonstrates pancreatic calcifications, pseudocysts, and ductal dilation, but depicts the actual fistulous tract in only approximately 33%–47% of cases. Its strength lies in identifying the broader anatomical context and excluding alternative thoracic or abdominal pathologies.

MRCP, as used in this patient, confirmed the diagnosis by demonstrating a tubular cystic collection in the distal pancreas extending posteriorly through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity, with clear visualization of communication with the distal pancreatic duct and proximal ductal narrowing. MRCP offers a sensitivity of approximately 70%–80% for visualization of the fistula and ductal anatomy, making it the preferred noninvasive diagnostic modality. It provides excellent ductal detail without radiation exposure — a particular advantage in the pediatric population — and does not require contrast administration into the ductal system.

ERCP, while invasive, remains the gold standard for therapeutic planning. In addition to diagnostic visualization, ERCP allows simultaneous therapeutic intervention — transpapillary stenting, balloon dilatation of ductal strictures, or sphincterotomy — all of which reduce intraductal pressure and facilitate fistula closure. Fistula visualization rates with ERCP in children range from approximately 38%–78%, but its therapeutic capability is what distinguishes it: reported success rates approach 100% in pediatric series, with long-term success exceeding 90% in mixed-age cohorts. In this case, however, conservative management with bowel rest, total parenteral nutrition, and octreotide succeeded without the need for ERCP intervention, and the child remained symptom-free on follow-up.

The practical teaching point is this: **CT identifies the anatomical pathway and supportive features, MRCP confirms the fistula and defines ductal anatomy noninvasively, and ERCP — while offering comparable or superior ductal delineation — is reserved as the gold standard when therapeutic intervention is required** because medical management has failed or when significant ductal obstruction is demonstrated on prior imaging.
