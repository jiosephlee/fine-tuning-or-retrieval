Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Evaluation of Recurrent Pleural Effusion

### Patient Demographics, Presenting Symptoms, and Vital Signs

The case involves a boy in late childhood who presented to the outpatient department accompanied by his mother with a one-week history of progressively worsening difficulty in breathing and right-sided chest pain. The child's body mass index was 35, calculated from a weight of 44 kg and height of 112 cm, placing him in the obese category for his age. This metabolic background is relevant because obesity can predispose to pancreatic pathology and may also accentuate respiratory symptoms by reducing chest wall compliance. On initial evaluation, his respiratory rate was 20 breaths per minute with mild tachypnoea, his heart rate was 110 beats per minute, and his oxygen saturation was 88%–89% on room air. These vital signs indicated significant respiratory compromise and hypoxia, necessitating admission to the pediatric intensive care unit and supplemental oxygen at 5 L/min via mask. The combination of hypoxia, tachycardia, and tachypnoea in a child with a unilateral chest finding immediately raised concern for a large pleural effusion compromising respiratory function.

### Physical Examination Findings

The respiratory system examination revealed a constellation of classic physical signs consistent with a large right-sided pleural effusion. Chest wall movements were reduced on the right side. Percussion over the right subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary regions produced a stony dull note, a hallmark of fluid accumulation in the pleural space. Vocal fremitus was significantly weaker on the right compared with the left, and breath sounds were decreased in the same regions. These findings together — reduced movement, stony dullness, diminished fremitus, and decreased breath sounds — form the traditional physical diagnostic triad for pleural effusion. The pathophysiology is straightforward: fluid in the pleural space dampens the transmission of sound and vibration from the lung to the chest wall, while simultaneously restricting lung expansion on the affected side.

Importantly, the abdominal examination was unremarkable. The abdomen was soft with no hepatosplenomegaly, and there was no tenderness or guarding. There was no pallor, cyanosis, icterus, clubbing, or lymphadenopathy. The absence of abdominal findings at this stage was clinically misleading, as it provided no clue to the pancreatic origin of the problem. The cardiovascular examination revealed only tachycardia without murmurs. The child was conscious and oriented. The absence of jaundice or abdominal tenderness was particularly significant in retrospect, because it meant there was no clinical pointer toward a hepatobiliary or pancreatic process at the bedside, allowing the diagnostic focus to remain entirely on the chest.

### Initial Investigations: Chest X-ray and Bedside Lung Ultrasound

A chest X-ray was ordered immediately and revealed a right-sided pleural effusion with blunting of the right costophrenic angle. Blunting of the costophrenic angle is the earliest radiographic sign of pleural effusion and typically appears when approximately 200–500 mL of fluid has accumulated in an adult; in children, smaller volumes may produce this sign. The X-ray confirmed what the physical examination had already suggested — a significant unilateral pleural collection.

Bedside lung ultrasound was then performed and demonstrated a pleural effusion of approximately one litre. Ultrasound is now recognized as a superior bedside tool for estimating pleural fluid volume and characterizing its nature, as it can distinguish between anechoic simple fluid and complex or septated collections. The ability to perform this rapidly at the bedside in a critically ill child makes it invaluable for guiding thoracocentesis. The large volume of approximately one litre in a child was remarkable and indicated that the underlying process was producing fluid at an unusually high rate, a feature that should have prompted early consideration of an uncommon cause. In addition, empirical intravenous antibiotics (piperacillin-tazobactam) were started after sending blood cultures, reflecting the clinical team's initial assumption that an infectious process — possibly bacterial pneumonia with parapneumonic effusion or empyema — was responsible.

### First Admission: Thoracocentesis, Pleural Fluid Appearance, and Temporary Improvement

Right-sided pleural aspiration was performed under aseptic precautions, and approximately one litre of dark red pleural fluid was drained. The appearance of the fluid was a critical observation. A dark red pleural effusion — sometimes called a hemorrhagic pleural effusion — raises a broad differential diagnosis that includes malignancy, tuberculosis, pulmonary embolism with infarction, trauma, and pancreaticopleural fistula. The pathophysiology of the dark red appearance in this case was related to the enzymatic action of pancreatic juices on pleural tissues: pancreatic enzymes leaking into the pleural space cause irritation, inflammation, and breakdown of small blood vessels, producing a serosanguineous or hemorrhagic appearance. This mechanism is distinct from true hemothorax, in which frank blood enters the pleural space from a vascular source.

Following thoracocentesis, the child's tachypnoea resolved, and his oxygen saturation improved to 95%–98% on room air. His chest pain improved, and he was weaned off oxygen support within 24 hours. He was shifted to the ward and discharged. This dramatic but temporary improvement after drainage is a characteristic pattern in recurrent pleural effusions: drainage relieves the mechanical compromise of lung expansion and improves gas exchange, but if the underlying cause of fluid production persists, the effusion will reaccumulate. The failure to identify the underlying cause at this first admission set the stage for recurrence.

### Second Admission: Recurrence, Elevated ADA, and Empirical Antitubercular Therapy

The child returned after six days with the same complaints of right-sided chest pain, tachypnoea, and difficulty breathing. Chest X-ray again confirmed a right-sided pleural effusion, and thoracocentesis was repeated, again draining approximately one litre of dark red fluid. At this point, the pleural fluid analysis revealed a mildly elevated adenosine deaminase (ADA) level. ADA is an enzyme involved in purine metabolism, and its elevation in pleural fluid is most strongly associated with tuberculous pleuritis. In regions where tuberculosis is endemic, an elevated ADA in the setting of a lymphocytic exudative pleural effusion has a high positive predictive value for tuberculosis.

The child also had a history of household contact with tuberculosis — his grandfather had been treated for pulmonary tuberculosis. The combination of a mildly elevated ADA and a known Koch's contact led the treating team to start empirical antitubercular therapy. This decision illustrates a common clinical pitfall: anchoring on a plausible but ultimately incorrect diagnosis when faced with incomplete data. In retrospect, the mildly elevated ADA was a nonspecific finding. ADA can be elevated in any inflammatory or infectious pleural process involving lymphocyte activation, including empyema, malignancy, and autoimmune pleuritis. A GeneXpert test for Mycobacterium tuberculosis was ultimately negative, and the child had no history of fever, chronic cough, or weight loss — features that would have been expected in genuine tuberculous pleural effusion. The dark red color of the fluid was also atypical for tuberculosis, which more commonly produces a straw-colored or serosanguineous exudate rather than dark red fluid.

### Third Admission: Recurrent Effusion, Intercostal Drainage, and the Critical History of Abdominal Trauma

Five days after the second discharge, the child was admitted for the third time with identical symptoms. The pattern of rapid reaccumulation after each drainage — approximately one litre each time within days — was itself an important diagnostic clue. Simple infectious effusions do not typically reaccumulate at this rate once drained, nor do tuberculous effusions re-form so quickly. This pattern pointed toward a continuous active source of fluid production.

Before inserting an intercostal drainage (ICD) tube this time, a contrast-enhanced CT of the chest was ordered. This decision marked the critical turning point in the case. The CT revealed patchy areas of airspace consolidation in the right lung, a right-sided hydropneumothorax, and — most importantly — an elongated, lobulated, multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right pleural space through the right paraesophageal region to the upper left retroperitoneum, terminating at the tail of the pancreas. This tracking collection was the radiographic fingerprint of a fistulous connection between the pancreas and the pleural space.

In parallel, the clinical team revisited the history with the child during this third admission, and the boy revealed that he had suffered blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle one month earlier. This was the missing piece. Blunt abdominal trauma — particularly from handlebar injuries — is a well-recognized cause of pancreatic injury in children. The handlebar compresses the pancreas against the vertebral column, typically injuring the pancreatic body or tail. The injury may initially be occult, with no immediate abdominal symptoms, but can lead to post-traumatic pancreatitis, pseudocyst formation, and ultimately ductal disruption. In this case, the timeline of approximately one month between the trauma and the onset of respiratory symptoms is consistent with the gradual development of a pancreatic pseudocyst or ductal leak that eventually eroded superiorly through the retroperitoneum and diaphragmatic hiatus into the pleural space.

An intercostal drain was inserted and again drained approximately one litre of dark red fluid. With the CT findings, the history of abdominal trauma, and the characteristic dark red pleural aspirate now converging, the diagnosis of a pancreatopleural fistula was strongly suspected. The clinical team then proceeded to confirm this with magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) and by sending serum and pleural fluid amylase and lipase levels, which revealed markedly elevated pleural fluid amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L). Antitubercular drugs were stopped.

### The Concept of the "Pulmonary Mask" and Diagnostic Delay

This case powerfully illustrates what the authors describe as a "pulmonary mask" — a situation in which the presenting symptoms point so convincingly toward a thoracic process that the clinician's entire diagnostic effort is directed at the chest, while the actual pathology resides in the abdomen. The child had chest pain, respiratory distress, hypoxia, physical signs of pleural effusion, and abnormal chest imaging. There were no abdominal symptoms, no abdominal findings on examination, and no initial historical clue to pancreatic disease. The natural and understandable response to this presentation is to pursue pulmonary diagnoses — parapneumonic effusion, empyema, tuberculosis, malignancy — which is exactly what happened.

The concept of a pulmonary mask is not unique to pancreatopleural fistula. It reflects a broader clinical principle: when a pleural effusion is recurrent, large, and atypical in appearance or behavior, the clinician must broaden the differential beyond primary thoracic causes and systematically consider distant sources of fluid. In this case, several features should have triggered earlier suspicion: the unusually large volume of fluid for a child, the rapidity of reaccumulation after drainage, the consistently dark red appearance, and the lack of systemic symptoms such as fever or weight loss that would be expected with infection or malignancy. The mildly elevated ADA, rather than being a definitive pointer to tuberculosis, should have been interpreted in the context of these atypical features.

The diagnostic delay in this case — spanning three admissions over approximately two weeks — is characteristic of pancreatopleural fistula. In the published literature, PPF is consistently described as a diagnosis of delayed recognition. The majority of reported cases underwent extensive pulmonary workup before the pancreatic origin was suspected, with some patients subjected to multiple thoracocenteses, prolonged empirical antibiotic or antitubercular therapy, and even invasive thoracic procedures before the true cause was identified. The lesson from this case is that a recurrent pleural effusion that does not behave as expected — whether because of rapid reaccumulation, atypical fluid appearance, failure to respond to targeted therapy, or absence of expected systemic features — demands a deliberate widening of the diagnostic lens to include abdominal pathology, particularly the pancreas.

## Pathophysiology and Background of Pancreatopleural Fistula

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is an uncommon complication of pancreatic disease in which pancreatic secretions gain access to the pleural cavity through an abnormal communication between the pancreatic ductal system and the thorax. It is an exceedingly rare entity, with only 47 documented cases in the pediatric age group compared with over 300 reported in adults. PPF arises as a complication of acute pancreatitis, chronic pancreatitis, or pancreatic trauma. Its rarity and its tendency to present with respiratory rather than abdominal symptoms make it a diagnostic challenge, as illustrated by this case, in which the child underwent repeated thoracentesis and empirical antitubercular therapy before the underlying pancreatic pathology was recognized.

Understanding how a pancreatic injury can produce a recurrent pleural effusion requires familiarity with the posterior abdominal wall and the diaphragmatic apertures that connect the retroperitoneum with the mediastinum. The diaphragm contains several hiatuses through which structures pass between the abdomen and the thorax, most notably the esophageal hiatus (transmitting the esophagus and vagal trunks) and the aortic hiatus (transmitting the aorta, azygos vein, and thoracic duct). These openings also serve as potential conduits for fluid. The retroperitoneal space is a continuous compartment that extends from the pancreas, lying retroperitoneally, upward behind the diaphragm and into the posterior mediastinum. When pancreatic fluid enters the retroperitoneum, it follows the path of least resistance cephalad, traversing one of these diaphragmatic hiatuses—most commonly the esophageal or aortic hiatus—and then breaks through the mediastinal pleura to enter the pleural cavity. In this case, the MRCP clearly demonstrated this anatomical pathway: a tubular cystic collection originating in the distal body and tail of the pancreas extended posteriorly into the retroperitoneum and then tracked superiorly through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity, with a narrow communication to the left pleural cavity as well.

The initial event in PPF formation is disruption of the pancreatic duct or rupture of a pancreatic pseudocyst, which releases enzyme-rich pancreatic fluid. The location of this rupture determines the clinical presentation. When the rupture occurs on the anterior surface of the pancreas, fluid leaks into the peritoneal cavity, producing pancreatic ascites. When the rupture occurs posteriorly, as was the case here, fluid is directed into the retroperitoneal space rather than the peritoneum. From there, digestive enzymes—particularly amylase and lipase—digest and inflame surrounding tissues, facilitating tracking upward through the diaphragmatic hiatuses and into the mediastinum and pleural space. The resulting pleural effusion is typically massive, recurrent, and often hemorrhagic or dark red in appearance, as seen in this patient, because the enzymatic fluid irritates and damages the pleural surfaces, producing a bloody exudate. The markedly elevated pleural fluid amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L) in this child reflect direct leakage of pancreatic secretions into the pleural space.

The etiology of PPF differs substantially between children and adults. In adults, chronic alcoholic pancreatitis is the dominant cause, with large case series documenting that nearly all adult PPF cases are related to alcohol-induced chronic pancreatitis. In children, the etiologic landscape is different. Genetic predispositions to recurrent or chronic pancreatitis play a prominent role, including mutations in the PRSS1 gene (encoding cationic trypsinogen), the SPINK1 gene (encoding a trypsin inhibitor), the cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) gene, and the chymotrypsin C gene. These mutations collectively account for a substantial proportion of pediatric chronic pancreatitis cases. The PRSS1 gene mutations R122H and N29I in exons 3 and 2, respectively, are the most representative hereditary pancreatitis variants. Blunt abdominal trauma is another important nonhereditary trigger in children, accounting for approximately 32% of cases in one 25-patient pediatric cohort analyzed by Yang et al. Idiopathic cases also continue to appear, possibly reflecting underrecognized genetic or ductal variants. In this case, the child's history of blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handlebar injury one month prior to presentation, combined with the absence of a family history of pancreatitis or known genetic mutations, points to post-traumatic pancreatitis as the most likely cause of the fistula.

A key clinical feature of PPF is its predominant respiratory presentation. In the literature, approximately 68% of cases present with massive, recurrent unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, dyspnea, cough, or chest pain, while fewer than 20% have abdominal symptoms such as epigastric pain, vomiting, or weight loss. This pattern occurs because the fistula diverts pancreatic secretions away from the abdominal cavity and into the thorax, so the mechanical and inflammatory consequences manifest primarily in the chest. The pancreas itself may be relatively quiescent in terms of symptoms, or the abdominal trauma may have been remote and overlooked—as in this case, where the child initially did not report the bicycle handlebar injury, and the history only emerged during the third admission after repeated questioning. This "pulmonary mask" is the central diagnostic pitfall of PPF: clinicians direct their workup toward thoracic pathology (tuberculosis, malignancy, empyema) while the actual disease process originates in the pancreas. Understanding this pathophysiological principle—that posterior pancreatic leaks track cephalad through diaphragmatic hiatuses to produce pleural effusions—is essential for recognizing PPF early and avoiding unnecessary or harmful interventions, as occurred in this patient's initial empirical antitubercular treatment.

## Differential Diagnosis and Diagnostic Workup

The diagnostic challenge in this case centered on a child presenting with recurrent, large, dark red right-sided pleural effusions—an unusual finding in pediatric practice that demands a systematic differential diagnosis. The combination of hemorrhagic appearance, recurrence despite thoracocentesis, and eventual recognition of elevated pleural fluid amylase and lipase progressively narrowed the differential and ultimately pointed to a pancreatic origin. Understanding how each potential cause was considered and excluded is essential for appreciating how the diagnosis of pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) was finally established.

### Building the Differential Diagnosis of Recurrent Hemorrhagic Pleural Effusion

Recurrent pleural effusion in a child prompts consideration of several broad categories: infection (including tuberculosis and bacterial empyema), malignancy, transudative causes (hepatic, renal, or cardiac), anatomic abnormalities (such as diaphragmatic hernia), and less commonly, fistulous communications from abdominal organs. The hemorrhagic or blood-stained nature of the fluid further raises concern for malignancy, tuberculosis, trauma, and pancreatic sources. In this case, the child's history of Koch's contact (his grandfather had pulmonary tuberculosis) and a mildly elevated pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) initially led the treating team toward a tuberculous etiology, and empirical antitubercular therapy was started. However, the failure of the effusion to resolve, combined with the subsequent recognition of elevated pleural fluid amylase, forced a reconsideration of the entire diagnostic framework.

The key pivot came when pleural fluid amylase and lipase were measured and found to be markedly elevated (amylase 6118 U/L, lipase 7211 U/L). A pleural fluid amylase level ≥1000 IU/L is considered pathognomonic for PPF. This finding shifted the differential from primarily thoracic pathology to a search for conditions that can introduce amylase-rich fluid into the pleural space. The differential diagnosis of elevated pleural fluid amylase specifically includes PPF, esophageal perforation (Boerhaave's syndrome), and malignancy (including lung cancer, leukemia, lymphoma, and metastatic tumors). Notably, pancreatic-type amylase in pleural fluid distinguishes PPF from these other conditions; malignancies and esophageal rupture typically produce salivary-type amylase. Although amylase isoenzyme typing was not explicitly performed in this case, the constellation of findings—abdominal trauma history, imaging evidence of a tract from the pancreas, and dramatically elevated lipase alongside amylase—made PPF the overwhelmingly likely diagnosis.

### Pleural Fluid Analysis: What Each Finding Signified

The pleural fluid in this case was consistently dark red, with approximately one liter aspirated on each of three occasions. The hemorrhagic character alone is nonspecific and can be seen in tuberculosis, malignancy, trauma, and pancreatic pleural effusion. The mildly elevated ADA, coupled with the known Koch's contact, was initially misleading. ADA elevation in pleural fluid is classically associated with tuberculous pleurisy, but mild elevations can occur in other inflammatory and malignant conditions, reducing its specificity. The critical negative finding was a negative GeneXpert (a rapid molecular test for *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*), which argued strongly against tuberculosis despite the epidemiologic context. The absence of fever, chronic cough, and weight loss further diminished the likelihood of tuberculosis.

The markedly elevated amylase and lipase in the pleural fluid became the cornerstone of diagnosis. Lipase is more specific to the pancreas than amylase, which is also produced by salivary glands. The concurrent elevation of both enzymes in pleural fluid, at levels far exceeding typical serum values, indicated that pancreatic secretions were directly entering the pleural cavity. Serum amylase and lipase were also elevated, supporting ongoing pancreatic pathology and leakage of pancreatic enzymes into the circulation, but the pleural fluid levels were disproportionately high relative to serum—a pattern consistent with direct fistulous communication rather than simple transudation across inflamed pleura.

### Systematically Excluding Alternative Diagnoses

Several conditions were specifically and deliberately ruled out. **Pulmonary tuberculosis** was considered given the Koch's contact and mild ADA elevation, but the negative GeneXpert, absence of constitutional symptoms, and ultimately the amylase profile excluded it; antitubercular drugs were stopped once PPF was confirmed. **Empyema** was excluded by the absence of fever and the lack of neutrophilic exudate or organisms in the pleural fluid. **Malignancy** was considered because amylase-rich pleural effusions can occur with lung cancer, leukemia, lymphoma, and metastatic tumors; however, the child had no fever, no weight loss, a normal blood picture, a normal peripheral smear, and pleural fluid cytology showed no malignant cells. **Boerhaave's syndrome** (esophageal perforation) was considered because it can produce amylase-rich pleural fluid from salivary leakage, but the child had no history of severe vomiting or retching, was passing stools normally, and had no clinical features of esophageal rupture. **Acquired diaphragmatic hernia** was excluded because no abdominal organs were visible within the chest on imaging. **Hepatic hydrothorax** was ruled out by normal liver function tests and liver imaging, with no history of chronic liver disease. **Renal pathology**, including nephrotic syndrome or hydronephrosis causing pleural effusion, was excluded by normal renal function and renal imaging.

### Imaging: From Chest X-ray to CT to MRCP

The initial chest X-ray revealed right-sided pleural effusion with costophrenic angle blunting, and bedside lung ultrasound confirmed approximately one liter of fluid—findings that explained the respiratory distress but not its cause. The critical imaging advance came with contrast-enhanced CT of the chest, performed before placing the intercostal drain on the third admission. CT demonstrated patchy airspace consolidation in the right lung, right-sided hydropneumothorax, and—an elongated, lobulated, multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right pleural space, tracking from the right paraesophageal level through the retroperitoneum to the tail of the pancreas. This finding of a continuous collection bridging the thorax and abdomen through the esophageal hiatus was highly suggestive of a fistulous tract and, combined with the history of abdominal trauma and the dark red aspirate, directed the team toward PPF.

Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) provided definitive anatomic confirmation. MRCP revealed pancreatitis with a tubular cystic collection in the distal body and tail of the pancreas, extending posteriorly into the retroperitoneum and then tracking superiorly through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity. A narrow communication with the left pleural cavity was also demonstrated, explaining the bilateral (right-predominant) involvement. Critically, MRCP showed the collection communicating with the distal pancreatic duct, with narrowing of the pancreatic duct proximal to the communication—establishing that the fistula originated from a pancreatic duct disruption, not merely a pseudocyst leak.

### Comparing Imaging Modalities for PPF

The choice and sequence of imaging in this case illustrate the relative strengths of CT, MRCP, and ERCP in evaluating PPF. CT reliably demonstrates pancreatic calcifications, pseudocysts, and ductal dilation, and it excels at showing the extent of pleural collections and associated pulmonary changes. However, CT visualizes the actual fistulous tract in only 33%–47% of cases. In this case, CT was valuable for identifying the tracking collection and prompting the suspicion of PPF, but it did not definitively show the fistula's connection to the pancreatic duct. MRCP offers superior soft tissue and ductal visualization without ionizing radiation, with a reported sensitivity of 70%–80% for demonstrating the fistula and duct anatomy. MRCP was the modality that definitively showed the fistulous communication between the pancreatic duct and the pleural cavity, confirming the diagnosis noninvasively—an important consideration in a child. Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) remains the gold standard for both diagnosis and therapeutic planning, with fistula visualization rates of 38%–78% in children. ERCP was not performed in this case because conservative management succeeded; however, had medical therapy failed, ERCP with possible transpapillary stenting would have been the next step.

### Integrating the Diagnostic Reasoning

The diagnostic journey in this case exemplifies a recurring theme in PPF: the disease wears a "pulmonary mask," and extensive thoracic workup often precedes recognition of the pancreatic origin. The child underwent two hospital admissions focused on managing the pleural effusion as a primary thoracic problem, including empirical antitubercular therapy, before the critical history of blunt abdominal trauma was elicited on the third admission. The convergence of three elements—the history of bicycle handle trauma one month prior, the CT finding of a collection tracking from the paraesophageal region to the pancreatic tail, and the markedly elevated pleural fluid amylase and lipase—collectively established the diagnosis. MRCP then provided the anatomic confirmation of a fistulous tract from the pancreatic duct through the esophageal hiatus into the pleural cavity. This sequence underscores the importance of repeated, detailed history-taking and the judicious use of cross-sectional imaging with attention to the pancreas when faced with recurrent pleural effusion of unclear etiology, particularly when pleural fluid amylase is elevated.

## Management of Pancreatopleural Fistula

The management of pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) follows a stepwise algorithm that escalates from conservative medical therapy to endoscopic intervention and, ultimately, to surgery if less invasive measures fail. Because PPF is exceedingly rare in children—only 47 pediatric cases have been reported in the literature—no standardized treatment guidelines exist. Instead, management principles are derived from adult data, small case series, and individual case reports, all adapted to the pediatric context. In this case, the child was successfully managed conservatively, but understanding the full treatment ladder is essential for any clinician who may encounter this condition.

### Principles of Conservative Management

The central goal of conservative therapy is to reduce pancreatic exocrine secretion to a minimum, thereby decreasing the volume of fluid traversing the fistulous tract and allowing the tract to close spontaneously. This is achieved through a combination of bowel rest, pharmacologic suppression of pancreatic secretion, pancreatic enzyme replacement, and drainage of the pleural collection. The rationale is mechanistic: a pancreatopleural fistula functions like a leaky pipe, constantly delivering enzyme-rich pancreatic fluid into the pleural cavity. If the output of that pipe can be suppressed, the fistula may seal on its own without the need for invasive procedures. Conservative measures are generally recommended as the initial approach when there is no significant anomaly of the pancreatic duct, and they should be pursued for 2–3 weeks before declaring failure and escalating to endoscopic or surgical therapy.

### Bowel Rest and Total Parenteral Nutrition

Keeping the patient nil per oral (NPO) is a foundational component of conservative management. Any oral intake—particularly fats and proteins—stimulates the exocrine pancreas to secrete digestive enzymes, which would increase flow through the fistula and impair closure. By eliminating enteral stimulation, bowel rest allows the pancreas to enter a quiescent state. Because the child cannot eat, total parenteral nutrition (TPN) is required to meet caloric and nutritional needs during what may be a prolonged treatment course. In this case, the child was placed NPO and started on TPN immediately after the diagnosis was confirmed by MRCP. TPN ensures that the growing child receives adequate macronutrients and micronutrients while the pancreas is rested, preventing the malnutrition that would otherwise complicate weeks of fasting.

### Octreotide: Mechanism, Dosing, and Monitoring

Octreotide is a synthetic somatostatin analogue that plays a key pharmacologic role in conservative management. Somatostatin is a naturally occurring inhibitory peptide that suppresses the release of multiple gastrointestinal hormones and directly inhibits exocrine pancreatic secretion. Octreotide, with its longer half-life compared to native somatostatin, reduces the volume of pancreatic juice and the concentration of digestive enzymes within it, effectively diminishing the driving force behind the fistulous flow. In this case, octreotide was administered as a continuous infusion at a dose of 30 µg/kg/day, which falls within the recommended pediatric range of 10–40 µg/kg/day. Monitoring during octreotide therapy includes tracking the daily output from the intercostal drain, watching for adverse effects such as hyperglycemia, abdominal cramping, and steatorrhea, and ensuring that nutritional status is maintained through TPN. The gradual decrease in drain output over the course of treatment serves as the primary clinical marker of response.

### Pancreatic Enzyme Replacement Therapy

Pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT) was also initiated in this case. At first glance, administering pancreatic enzymes to a patient whose problem is pancreatic enzyme leakage may seem counterintuitive. However, the rationale is twofold. First, when the pancreas is not delivering enzymes to the intestinal lumen because the secretions are being diverted through the fistula, the patient may develop pancreatic exocrine insufficiency with malabsorption and steatorrhea. PERT supplies these enzymes enterally to maintain digestive function. Second, enteral pancreatic enzymes can provide negative feedback inhibition of pancreatic exocrine secretion through the cholecystokinin-mediated pathway, further reducing the stimulus for pancreatic output. PERT is therefore a complementary component of the conservative regimen rather than a contradictory one.

### Thoracic Drainage

Although the primary pathology is in the pancreas, the immediate threat to the patient is respiratory compromise from the massive pleural effusion. In this case, the child presented three times with right-sided pleural effusions causing tachypnea, chest pain, and hypoxemia, each time requiring drainage of approximately one liter of dark red fluid. After two episodes of therapeutic thoracocentesis, an intercostal drainage (ICD) tube was inserted on the third admission. The ICD serves two purposes: it relieves the respiratory distress by evacuating the pleural collection, and it provides a measurable output that allows the clinicians to track the fistula's activity over time. As the conservative measures reduce pancreatic secretion, the daily ICD output should progressively diminish, which is exactly what happened in this case—drainage decreased over 10 days and eventually stopped, at which point the ICD was removed.

### Timeline and Expected Response

Conservative management of PPF requires patience. The expected timeline for response is 2–3 weeks of sustained therapy before the fistula closes. During this period, the patient remains NPO on TPN with continuous octreotide infusion and ICD in place. The clinical team monitors daily drain output, serial imaging as needed, and nutritional status. In this case, the response was relatively prompt—drainage ceased within 10 days, allowing ICD removal and discharge. This favorable outcome is not universal, however, and the clinician must be prepared to escalate if output persists beyond the expected window.

### Success Rate of Conservative Management in Pediatric PPF

It is important to set realistic expectations. The reported success rate of conservative management alone in pediatric PPF is only approximately 12%. This low rate means that the majority of children with PPF will ultimately require either endoscopic or surgical intervention to achieve fistula closure. Nevertheless, because conservative therapy is noninvasive and carries minimal morbidity, it remains the appropriate first step unless there is a clear ductal anomaly that would predict failure. In this case, conservative management succeeded despite the low overall success rate, likely because the fistula was related to acute traumatic pancreatitis rather than chronic ductal obstruction or hereditary pancreatitis with structural ductal disease.

### Endoscopic Intervention: ERCP and Transpapillary Stenting

When conservative management fails after 2–3 weeks, endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) with transpapillary stenting is the next step. ERCP serves both diagnostic and therapeutic functions: it allows direct visualization of the pancreatic duct anatomy, identification of the fistula site and any ductal strictures, and placement of a stent across the region of duct disruption or stenosis. The stent works by reducing intraductal pressure and rerouting pancreatic secretions through the papilla rather than through the fistulous tract, allowing the fistula to heal. Endoscopic intervention can also include balloon dilatation of ductal strictures if present. In pediatric cases, ERCP has a fistula visualization rate of 38–78% and a reported success rate of 100% in published series, with long-term success exceeding 90% in mixed-age cohorts. Stents typically remain in place for 4 weeks to 6 months. Because of its minimal morbidity and mortality, ERCP is preferred over surgery when stent placement is feasible—particularly when ductal stenosis is identified, as in the MRCP findings of this case, which showed narrowing of the pancreatic duct proximal to the fistula communication.

### Surgical Options

Surgery is reserved for cases where endoscopic stent placement is not possible—such as severe ductal obstruction, a distally located fistula, or failure of both conservative and endoscopic management after 3–4 weeks. The surgical options include distal pancreatectomy, which removes the segment of pancreas containing the fistula; the Puestow procedure (pancreatojejunostomy), which decompresses the pancreatic duct by draining it into a Roux-en-Y loop of jejunum; and cystojejunostomy, which internalizes a pseudocyst into the bowel lumen. In the pediatric literature, surgery has demonstrated a 93% success rate across 15 reported cases, with operative mortality below 5% and a recurrence rate of approximately 11%. While these outcomes are favorable, surgery carries greater morbidity than endoscopic approaches and is appropriately held in reserve as a last resort.

### Stepwise Treatment Algorithm

The treatment algorithm for pediatric PPF can be summarized as follows. First, establish the diagnosis with MRCP and pleural fluid amylase/lipase levels. Begin conservative management immediately: NPO, TPN, octreotide infusion (10–40 µg/kg/day), PERT, and ICD placement for thoracic drainage. Monitor daily drain output for 2–3 weeks. If the fistula closes and output ceases, remove the ICD and discharge with follow-up. If output persists, proceed to ERCP for ductal evaluation and transpapillary stenting, particularly if ductal stenosis or disruption is identified. If stent placement is not feasible or if the fistula persists despite stenting for 3–4 weeks, proceed to surgery—distal pancreatectomy, Puestow procedure, or cystojejunostomy depending on the ductal anatomy and fistula location.

### Why Conservative Management Succeeded in This Case

Several factors likely contributed to the success of conservative management in this child. The underlying cause was blunt abdominal trauma rather than chronic pancreatitis or hereditary ductal disease, meaning the ductal disruption was acute and potentially self-limited once pancreatic secretion was suppressed. The child had no evidence of chronic ductal obstruction, calcification, or pseudocyst that would sustain fistula flow. Prompt initiation of the full conservative regimen—NPO, TPN, octreotide, and PERT—immediately after MRCP confirmation likely minimized the duration of active fistula flow. The MRCP did reveal narrowing of the pancreatic duct proximal to the fistula communication, which in other cases might have prompted early ERCP, but the rapid decline in drain output within 10 days indicated that the fistula was closing spontaneously, obviating the need for endoscopic intervention. This case illustrates that while conservative management has a low overall success rate in pediatric PPF, it can be effective in carefully selected patients—particularly those with acute, post-traumatic fistulas—and its initial trial is justified before escalating to more invasive interventions.

## Clinical Reasoning, Outcomes, and Lessons Learned

### Clinical Outcome

The patient's outcome illustrates the potential efficacy of conservative management for pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) when initiated promptly after definitive diagnosis. Following confirmation of PPF on magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) and elevated pleural fluid amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L), the child was placed on nil per oral (NPO) status, total parenteral nutrition (TPN), octreotide infusion at 30 µg/kg/day, and pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT). An intercostal drainage (ICD) tube was already in place for the right pleural effusion. Over 10 days, drainage progressively decreased and ultimately ceased, allowing removal of the chest tube and discharge. On monthly follow-up, the child remained symptom-free with no recurrent pleural effusions, returned to full activity including strenuous exercise, and resumed regular school attendance. The child also adopted lifestyle modifications including a balanced diet and regular exercise, as advised, which may help reduce the risk of pancreatitis recurrence given the contributory role of his pre-existing obesity (BMI 35).

This outcome is notable because conservative management of PPF has a reported success rate of only approximately 12% in pediatric cases. The favorable response observed here may reflect the post-traumatic rather than chronic or genetic etiology, the absence of significant pancreatic ductal obstruction or stricture on MRCP (though some narrowing proximal to the fistulous communication was noted), and the relatively short duration of fistula formation. Nonetheless, the case reinforces that a trial of conservative therapy is reasonable as a first-line approach in children, reserving endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) with stent placement or surgical intervention for cases that fail to respond within 2–3 weeks.

### The Critical Role of Repeated History-Taking

One of the most instructive aspects of this case is the timeline of diagnostic discovery. The child was admitted three times over approximately two weeks before the critical historical detail of blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handlebar emerged. On the first two admissions, evaluation focused on pulmonary causes. The history of Koch's (tuberculosis) contact in the family and a mildly elevated pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) led to empiric antitubercular therapy. Only on the third admission, after the child was directly and repeatedly questioned, did he reveal that he had suffered blunt abdominal trauma one month prior to the onset of symptoms.

This sequence of events highlights a fundamental clinical principle: history-taking is an iterative process, particularly when the initial diagnosis does not fit the clinical trajectory. Several factors likely contributed to the delayed disclosure. The child was in late childhood, an age where patients may not volunteer sensitive information without direct questioning or may not recognize the relevance of an apparently minor injury. Additionally, as noted in a similar case described in the discussion, children may deliberately conceal trauma due to embarrassment or fear. The delayed emergence of the trauma history underscores the need for clinicians to return to the history when a patient's course deviates from expectations, and to ask about trauma specifically and repeatedly when the diagnosis remains elusive.

### Anchoring Bias and Premature Closure

The concept of the "pulmonary mask" is central to understanding the diagnostic challenges posed by PPF. The patient presented with respiratory distress, chest pain, and a large right-sided pleural effusion—all findings that naturally direct attention toward a thoracic pathology. The initial chest X-ray, lung ultrasound, and clinical examination all confirmed a pleural effusion, and management appropriately began with thoracocentesis and respiratory support. However, the persistence and rapid recurrence of the effusion after drainage, the consistently dark red (hemorrhagic) appearance of the pleural fluid, and the lack of response to empiric antitubercular therapy should have prompted earlier reconsideration of the underlying diagnosis.

The cognitive errors demonstrated in this case are anchoring bias—fixating on the initial impression of pulmonary disease—and premature closure, accepting a diagnosis before it has been fully verified. The presence of a tuberculosis contact and mildly elevated ADA provided apparent support for a tuberculous etiology, leading to empiric antitubercular therapy. However, the GeneXpert for *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* was negative, and the child lacked other typical features of tuberculosis such as fever, chronic cough, and weight loss. The discordance between the presumed diagnosis and the clinical picture should have triggered a broader differential earlier in the course.

### The Importance of Considering Abdominal Etiologies for Recurrent Pleural Effusion

This case demonstrates a broader clinical principle: recurrent or persistent pleural effusions that do not respond to standard management should prompt evaluation of non-pulmonary causes. The pathophysiology of PPF is illustrative. When the posterior pancreas is injured or inflamed, pancreatic secretions leak into the retroperitoneum and follow the path of least resistance superiorly through the esophageal or aortic hiatus into the mediastinum, eventually breaching the pleura to form a pleural effusion. The effusion is thus a secondary manifestation of an abdominal process, but the patient presents with exclusively thoracic symptoms.

Several features of the pleural fluid in this case should have raised suspicion for a non-pulmonary cause earlier. The fluid was consistently dark red and hemorrhagic, which is atypical for uncomplicated tuberculosis or parapneumonic effusions. The volume was large (approximately one liter each time) and reaccumulated rapidly within days. These characteristics warranted consideration of pancreatic, hepatic, diaphragmatic, and even esophageal etiologies. The differential diagnosis discussion in the case appropriately enumerates these alternatives, including Boerhaave's syndrome (which can also produce amylase-rich pleural fluid from salivary amylase), malignancy, hepatic hydrothorax, and acquired diaphragmatic hernia. The key distinguishing test—pleural fluid amylase—was ultimately the decisive piece of biochemical evidence.

### Risks of Empirical Treatment Without Definitive Diagnosis

The empiric initiation of antitubercular therapy in this child carries important clinical lessons. Antitubercular drugs (isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, ethambutol) are associated with significant adverse effects including hepatotoxicity, gastrointestinal intolerance, peripheral neuropathy, and optic neuritis. In a child with a body mass index of 35, the risk of drug-induced liver injury may be further compounded by underlying hepatic steatosis, though this was not specifically addressed in the report. Beyond the direct pharmacologic risks, unnecessary antitubercular therapy exposes the patient to prolonged treatment, potential drug resistance selection in the community, psychological burden of a tuberculosis diagnosis, and the opportunity cost of delayed appropriate therapy.

The case also illustrates that a mildly elevated pleural fluid ADA, while supportive of tuberculosis in the right clinical context, is not specific. ADA can be elevated in various inflammatory and malignant conditions involving the pleura. The combination of a positive contact history and mildly elevated ADA was insufficient to justify treatment in the absence of microbiologic confirmation (negative GeneXpert) and compatible clinical features. The antitubercular drugs were appropriately stopped once the diagnosis of PPF was established, but the interval during which the child received unnecessary therapy represents a preventable harm.

### Clinical Pearls: When to Suspect PPF and Key Diagnostic Tests

Several practical lessons emerge from this case for the clinician encountering a child with recurrent pleural effusion:

First, PPF should be considered in any child with a recurrent, large, unilateral (or bilateral) pleural effusion of unclear etiology, particularly when the fluid is hemorrhagic or serosanguineous and reaccumulates rapidly after drainage. A history of abdominal trauma, pancreatitis, or chronic abdominal symptoms should be actively sought.

Second, pleural fluid amylase is a critical and underutilized diagnostic test in this setting. A pleural fluid amylase level ≥1000 IU/L is considered pathognomonic for PPF. In this case, the pleural fluid amylase was 6118 U/L and lipase was 7211 U/L, both markedly elevated. Pleural fluid amylase should be sent early in the workup of any recurrent or atypical pleural effusion, particularly when hemorrhagic. Amylase isoenzyme typing can further distinguish pancreatic-type amylase (seen in PPF) from salivary-type amylase (seen in esophageal perforation and some malignancies), though this was not performed in the present case.

Third, cross-sectional imaging is essential for confirming the diagnosis and defining anatomy. Contrast-enhanced CT can demonstrate the fistulous tract and associated pancreatic findings in a subset of cases, but MRCP offers superior sensitivity (70–80%) for visualizing the fistula and pancreatic duct anatomy. MRCP is noninvasive and does not require contrast administration, making it particularly suitable for pediatric patients. ERCP remains the gold standard for both diagnosis and therapeutic planning but is typically reserved for cases requiring intervention.

Fourth, concurrent serum amylase and lipase should be measured alongside pleural fluid levels. In this case, both serum and pleural fluid enzymes were elevated, supporting pancreatic origin.

### Broader Significance and Rarity

PPF is an exceptionally rare entity in the pediatric population, with only 47 documented cases at the time of this report compared with over 300 cases in adults. This rarity contributes significantly to the diagnostic delay observed in this and other cases, as clinicians are unlikely to consider a condition they have never encountered. In adults, chronic alcoholic pancreatitis is the dominant etiology, whereas in children, genetic causes (PRSS1, SPINK1, CFTR, and CTRC mutations) and blunt abdominal trauma are the primary triggers. The post-traumatic mechanism in this case is consistent with the observation that blunt abdominal injury accounts for approximately 32% of pediatric PPF cases in published cohorts.

The rarity of PPF also means that treatment guidelines are not standardized. Management is extrapolated from adult data and individual case reports. The stepped approach—conservative measures first, followed by ERCP with stent placement if conservative therapy fails, and surgery as a last resort—is supported by available evidence but requires individualization. The success of conservative management in this case adds to the limited pediatric literature and suggests that a trial of conservative therapy is warranted before proceeding to more invasive interventions, particularly when there is no significant ductal obstruction.

### The Value of Early Diagnosis

The ultimate lesson of this case is that early recognition of PPF can prevent multiple sources of morbidity. In this child, three hospital admissions, two thoracocentesis procedures, one ICD insertion, unnecessary antitubercular therapy, and prolonged diagnostic uncertainty were all consequences of the delayed diagnosis. The child was exposed to repeated hospitalizations, invasive procedures, and medications with potential adverse effects before the correct diagnosis was established.

Early diagnosis of PPF requires a high index of suspicion, a willingness to revisit and re-expand the differential diagnosis when the clinical course is atypical, and the strategic use of pleural fluid biochemistry (particularly amylase and lipase) and cross-sectional imaging. The case powerfully illustrates that when a pleural effusion behaves atypically—recurring rapidly, failing to respond to standard therapy, or presenting with hemorrhagic fluid—the clinician must look beyond the chest. The pancreas, though anatomically distant from the pleural space, can produce recurrent massive pleural effusions through a fistulous connection that traverses the mediastinum. Recognizing this possibility early can spare patients unnecessary interventions, empiric treatments, and diagnostic delay.
