Title: Blog about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

# The Normal CT That Wasn't: Re-reviewing Old Imaging When Symptoms Persist

Imagine a patient who repeatedly desaturates into the 70s and 80s, feels absolutely no respiratory distress, and is repeatedly diagnosed with anxiety. Now imagine the key to unlocking her 14-year diagnostic odyssey was sitting in her electronic medical record the entire time, hiding on a CT scan that was officially reported as "normal." This is a masterclass in how anchoring bias and satisfaction of search can delay a critical neurological diagnosis for over a decade.

## The Case: "Forgetting to Breathe"
A woman in her early 40s presented with a frustrating 7-year history of recurrent choking episodes affecting both solids and liquids, alongside a 14-year history of chronic occipital and vertex headaches. Her swallowing workup was exhaustive—and completely normal. Three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper GI endoscopies showed nothing. 

The plot thickened during the COVID-19 pandemic when she started using a home pulse oximeter. Over several months, she documented recurrent, asymptomatic oxygen desaturations down to 70%–80%. She was completely unaware of these episodes until she checked her device. During an airport incident, she was found to have an O2 sat of 78%, experienced acute dizziness, and was denied boarding. Yet, she had no sensation of dyspnea. Multiple emergency evaluations attributed this to anxiety.

The lack of dyspnea despite profound hypoxia is the crucial clinical clue. Her brainstem was failing to transmit the afferent signals necessary to generate the conscious sensation of air hunger. In moments of distraction, she essentially "forgot to breathe"—a terrifying phenotype of acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse).

## The Hidden Clue in a 14-Year-Old CT
Given the combination of chronic headaches, progressive bulbar symptoms, and unexplained hypoxia, the treating team went hunting for a unifying neurological diagnosis. They pulled a non-contrast head CT scan from 14 years prior, performed immediately after a minor motor vehicle accident. At the time, it was reported as normal.

But a detailed re-review revealed the earliest clue to her condition: a crowded foramen magnum with complete loss of the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) space at the craniocervical junction. This prompted an MRI, which confirmed an 11 mm cerebellar tonsillar descent—consistent with Chiari malformation type I (CMI). A subsequent sleep study demonstrated mild sleep apnea (AHI 6.0) but with a striking predominance of central events (72%), solidifying the impact of her brainstem compression.

## Why Was This Missed? The Radiology Trap
How does a structural brainstem abnormality get missed on a head CT for 14 years? The answer lies in the cognitive biases that plague radiology interpretation. 

First, satisfaction of search likely played a major role. In the setting of trauma, the primary goal of the non-contrast head CT is to rule out emergent intracranial bleeding or fractures. Once the scan confirms there is no epidural hematoma or skull fracture, the interpreter's brain breathes a sigh of relief and stops actively searching for subtle, non-emergent anatomical variants. 

Second, anchoring bias took hold of her clinical trajectory. Because the initial CT was reported as normal, her subsequent headaches were anchored to a diagnosis of "nummular headache," leading to years of ineffective local scalp injections. Her choking was anchored to a gastrointestinal or ENT pathology, leading to years of negative endoscopies. Once the anchor was set, no one stepped back to ask if the entire clinical picture pointed somewhere else. 

## Recognizing a Crowded Foramen Magnum on CT
While MRI is the gold standard for diagnosing CMI, a non-contrast CT can show highly suggestive signs if you know where to look. 

Normally, the craniocervical junction should show a clear, dark rim of CSF space surrounding the cerebellar tonsils and medulla at the level of the foramen magnum. In CMI, the posterior fossa is developmentally small, forcing the cerebellar tonsils downward. On CT, this appears as a "crowded foramen magnum"—soft tissue density (the ectopic cerebellar tissue) obliterating the normal CSF cisterns at the cranio-cervical junction. If you are evaluating a patient with unexplained bulbar or respiratory symptoms, taking a second look at the craniocervical junction on old trauma imaging can be a game-changer.

## When to Pull the Old Charts
This case challenges us to aggressively re-evaluate our default diagnoses when the clinical picture doesn't fit. Dysphagia that affects solids and liquids equally, and recurs despite normal endoscopies, is neurogenic until proven otherwise. Central sleep apnea and unexplained hypoxia should prompt an investigation into structural brainstem pathology. 

If your patient has persistent, unexplained symptoms and a prior "normal" scan, don't just read the radiology report. Pull the actual images, look again with a new clinical question, or ask your radiologist to specifically re-review the region of interest. A 14-year diagnostic delay is a stark reminder that "normal" is sometimes just a missed finding waiting for the right clinical context.

## Key Take-Home Points
* **Dysphagia affecting solids and liquids equally is a red flag for neurogenic etiology.** If endoscopies are normal, look at the brainstem, not just the esophagus.
* **Profound hypoxia without dyspnea indicates brainstem pathology.** The absence of air hunger suggests a failure of afferent signal transmission, a hallmark of central hypoventilation.
* **Beware satisfaction of search on trauma CTs.** A "normal" head CT in trauma means no bleed, not no pathology. Always assess the craniocervical junction for CSF space crowding.
* **Re-review old imaging.** When symptoms persist and evolve over years, pulling old studies and re-evaluating them in light of new clinical data can break a diagnostic stalemate.
* **Chiari malformation type I can present purely with respiratory dysfunction.** Brainstem compression can cause central sleep apnea and acquired Ondine’s curse, and posterior fossa decompression can potentially reverse these symptoms.



# Seven Years, Five Scopes, Zero Answers: When Normal Endoscopy Means Think Neurologically

## The Case That Should Have Been Solved Sooner

A woman in her early 40s spent seven years choking — at meals, in her sleep, in public. She underwent three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper GI endoscopies. All normal. She was told it was anxiety. She started avoiding restaurants. She cut her hair short because the weight of it caused scalp pain. And during a pandemic, she casually documented her own oxygen saturations dropping into the 70s on a home pulse oximeter — without feeling short of breath.

The answer was sitting in her chart the entire time. A head CT performed 14 years earlier after a minor motor vehicle accident showed a crowded foramen magnum. Nobody had flagged it. When it was finally re-reviewed in the context of her progressive bulbar and respiratory symptoms, it pointed straight to Chiari malformation type I — confirmed on MRI with 11 mm of cerebellar tonsillar descent.

This case is a masterclass in diagnostic anchoring and the cost of failing to pivot when the data doesn't fit your frame.

## The Clue Hiding in Plain Sight: Solids AND Liquids

The most important diagnostic feature in this case was visible from day one: the patient had equal difficulty with solids and liquids. This is the single most useful bedside discriminator between mechanical and neurogenic dysphagia, and it was apparently overlooked across multiple specialist evaluations.

Mechanical dysphagia — strictures, rings, tumors, esophageal pathology — typically impacts solids first and progresses to liquids only as the obstruction worsens. You eat less, chew more, modify your diet, and eventually struggle with liquids. The progression is predictable and largely consistency-dependent.

Neurogenic dysphagia doesn't care what consistency the bolus is. The problem isn't in the pipe — it's in the neuromuscular coordination of the swallow itself. When lower cranial nerve nuclei are compressed, as they can be in Chiari malformation with brainstem impaction, the pharyngeal phase of swallowing degrades across all consistencies simultaneously. The modified barium swallow in this patient confirmed it: aspiration with all consistencies, consistent with a neurogenic pattern.

The lesson is straightforward but bears repeating because it wasn't followed here: **if your dysphagia patient chokes on water as easily as steak, stop scoping and start thinking about the brainstem.**

## Why Chiari Causes What It Causes

Chiari malformation type I is defined by caudal descent of the cerebellar tonsils at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum on sagittal MRI. The pathophysiology is a volumetric mismatch — a developmentally small posterior fossa cramming neural structures into a space that can't accommodate them. The consequences fall into a few recognizable patterns:

**Lower cranial nerve dysfunction** occurs in 15–26% of symptomatic CMI patients. This manifests as palatal weakness, tongue atrophy, cricopharyngeal achalasia, and — as in this case — oropharyngeal dysphagia with choking. The mechanism is direct compression of the medulla and lower cranial nerve nuclei at the craniocervical junction.

**Respiratory dysfunction** is an increasingly recognized complication. A prospective study of 90 adults with CMI found that 50% had sleep-related breathing disorders, with a central apnea index ≥5 in 6.7%. Case series have documented acute respiratory failure and life-threatening hypoxemia. The mechanism involves compression of brainstem respiratory control centers, disruption of CSF flow dynamics, and impaired afferent signaling — producing an acquired central hypoventilation syndrome analogous to congenital "Ondine's curse."

**Headache** is the cardinal symptom, present in over 80% of cases — typically paroxysmal occipital headache precipitated by Valsalva maneuvers. Interestingly, this patient's headaches were not Valsalva-triggered, which may have further delayed recognition, but they were occipital and had been present for 14 years.

The respiratory physiology in this case is particularly instructive. The patient preserved voluntary breathing — she could breathe on command, and did so normally during clinic encounters and pulmonary function testing. What she lost was automatic respiratory drive, the brainstem-mediated control that keeps you breathing when you're asleep or distracted. This is the same voluntary/automatic dissociation seen in congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, just produced by a different mechanism. The airport episode captures it perfectly: a moment of distraction, sats drop to 78%, no sensation of dyspnea, acute dizziness and nausea — the patient literally forgot to breathe, and her brainstem couldn't remind her.

The absence of dyspnea during profound desaturation is itself a critical clinical feature. The authors hypothesize that brainstem pathology interrupted the afferent neural pathways that normally ascend to the cortex to generate the conscious sensation of air hunger. Without those signals reaching the insular cortex, the patient had no subjective awareness of her physiological distress. This is dangerous — a patient who desaturates to the 70s without feeling short of breath is a patient who can die in her sleep without anyone recognizing the urgency.

## The Evidence You Need to Know

The numbers here should change practice:

- CMI is found in approximately 1% of adults on imaging, but symptomatic presentations occur in only about 0.1% of the general population.
- In a retrospective study of children with persistent unexplained dysphagia, CMI prevalence was 3.3% — considerably higher than the 0.1–0.77% reported in the general pediatric population.
- A prospective evaluation using validated swallowing assessments found dysfunction in 36% of CMI patients, with neurogenic patterns predominating.
- The condition has a modest female predominance (1.3:1) and typically presents in the third decade.

That 3.3% figure is the one that should stick. If you're working up persistent dysphagia and your endoscopies are normal, the pre-test probability of CMI in that population is more than triple the baseline population rate. That's not a rare zebraphone — that's a meaningful yield for a readily available investigation.

## A Practical Framework: When to Stop Scoping

This case begs for a decision rule, even an informal one. Here's what the clinical reasoning should look like:

After one normal laryngoscopy and one normal upper endoscopy in a patient with persistent choking, you should be asking: is this pattern mechanical or neurogenic? If the patient reports equal difficulty with solids and liquids, the answer is likely neurogenic, and you need to image the brainstem. A non-contrast CT of the head is a reasonable starting point — it's what was available in this case 14 years earlier, and it showed the crowded foramen magnum that was missed. But MRI of the brain and cervical spine is the definitive study, and it should be obtained if there's any suggestion of cranial nerve involvement, sleep-disordered breathing, or headaches consistent with CMI.

The patient in this case had a normal non-contrast head CT that was misread — or rather, read without attention to the craniocervical junction. This is a real and underappreciated failure mode. The foramen magnum is at the edge of the standard brain CT slice stack, and crowding can be subtle. If you're reviewing a CT for headache, trauma, or unexplained neurological symptoms, specifically examine the craniocervical junction. Look for effacement of the CSF space around the tonsils. If you see crowding, escalate to MRI.

Red flags that should trigger neurological imaging rather than a third or fourth endoscopy:

- Dysphagia affecting solids and liquids equally from the outset
- Nocturnal choking or gasping episodes that wake the patient
- Occipital headaches, especially if chronic or Valsalva-triggered
- Unexplained oxygen desaturation, particularly without dyspnea
- Sleep-disordered breathing with predominantly central events on polysomnography
- Cutaneous allodynia of the scalp or vertex (an unusual but documented association)
- Any combination of bulbar symptoms (swallowing difficulty, voice changes, tongue weakness) with headache or respiratory complaints

## The Sleep Study Caveat

One important methodological point: the initial sleep study in this case used a type III device, which lacks EEG monitoring. Without EEG, the apnea-hypopnea index is calculated using total recording time rather than total sleep time, which includes wakeful periods and underestimates event frequency. The reported AHI of 6.0 with 72% central events almost certainly undersells the true severity of this patient's sleep-disordered breathing. A type I polysomnogram with full EEG, end-tidal capnography, and video was arranged at an external center but had not been completed at the time of publication.

The practical takeaway: if you suspect central hypoventilation in a CMI patient and your limited sleep study shows even mild central-predominant events, take it seriously. The methodology may be masking more pathology than it reveals. Escalate to full polysomnography.

## What Happened to the Patient

After multidisciplinary consultation, the patient was offered posterior fossa decompression, which can restore normal CSF flow dynamics and potentially reverse both the respiratory and bulbar symptoms. She opted for conservative management while contemplating surgery — a reasonable choice given the chronicity and the fact that she was clinically stable. A structured monitoring plan was implemented: monthly clinical assessments, quarterly overnight oximetry, and annual MRI surveillance. At 8 months of follow-up, she remained under close observation.

The surgical evidence, while limited to case series and reports, is encouraging enough that decompression should be discussed early with any CMI patient demonstrating central hypoventilation — particularly one with documented desaturations into the 70s and progressive bulbar dysfunction.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Equal difficulty with solids and liquids is neurogenic dysphagia until proven otherwise.** If your endoscopy is normal, stop scoping and image the brainstem.
- **Chiari malformation is over-represented in patients with persistent unexplained dysphagia** — 3.3% vs. 0.1–0.77% baseline. That's a meaningful yield for a readily available investigation.
- **CMI can produce acquired central hypoventilation** — preserved voluntary breathing with loss of automatic respiratory drive during sleep or distraction. Patients may desaturate profoundly without dyspnea due to disrupted afferent signaling through the brainstem.
- **Review the craniocervical junction on every head CT** for headache, trauma, or unexplained neurological symptoms. A crowded foramen magnum can be missed at the edge of the slice stack.
- **Limited sleep studies underestimate severity in central hypoventilation.** If the pattern is central-predominant, escalate to full polysomnography with EEG.
- **Posterior fossa decompression can reverse respiratory and bulbar symptoms in CMI.** Don't delay surgical referral in patients with central hypoventilation or progressive cranial nerve dysfunction.
- **Seven years and five normal scopes is not a reassuring workup — it's a diagnostic failure.** The pattern recognition should have happened at the first encounter.

## The Bottom Line

This patient's 14-year diagnostic odyssey was preventable. The clinical pattern — equal dysphagia for solids and liquids, nocturnal choking, occipital headaches, and unexplained desaturation without dyspnea — pointed to the brainstem from the beginning. The normal endoscopies were not reassuring; they were diagnostic noise that confirmed the wrong frame. The CT that could have unlocked the diagnosis was performed in the first year and sat in the chart, its critical finding unrecognized.

When your scope is normal and the symptoms persist, the problem isn't in the larynx or the esophagus. It's in the question you're asking.



# Forgetting to Breathe: The Dissociation of Voluntary and Automatic Ventilation in Brainstem Compression

## The Case That Stops You in Your Tracks

Picture this: a woman in her early 40s is sitting at an airport, waiting for her flight, when she suddenly feels dizzy and nauseous. The medical team arrives, checks her oxygen saturation, and finds it at 78%. She is not gasping. She is not distressed. She is not clutching her chest. She just feels a bit off. Supplemental oxygen resolves her symptoms within minutes, and she is denied boarding.

Now consider that this same woman has been self-monitoring her oxygen saturations at home for months, repeatedly documenting drops into the 70–80% range — and has no awareness these episodes are even happening until she checks the device. Her emergency department visits have been attributed to anxiety.

This is not a patient with pulmonary disease. Her chest imaging, pulmonary function tests, and arterial blood gas are all normal. This is a patient whose brainstem has been slowly compressed by an 11 mm descent of her cerebellar tonsils through the foramen magnum — a Chiari malformation type I (CMI) that went undiagnosed for 14 years.

## A 14-Year Diagnostic Journey

This patient's presentation is a masterclass in how structural brainstem compression can masquerade as multiple unrelated complaints, leading clinicians down one blind alley after another. Her symptoms began after a minor motor vehicle accident with brief loss of consciousness, after which she developed persistent occipital and vertex headaches. Over the ensuing 14 years, she accumulated a constellation of findings that were individually investigated and individually dismissed:

- **Choking episodes** affecting solids and liquids equally, occurring both during meals and sleep (3–4 times per week nocturnally), prompting three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper GI endoscopies — all normal.
- **Chronic occipital and vertex headaches** with marked allodynia at the vertex, where even the weight of her hair triggered discomfort. She was diagnosed with nummular headache and received multiple local scalp injections with only transient relief. She ultimately cut her hair short, reducing baseline pain from 6/10 to 3/10 — a decision that conflicted with cultural expectations and added emotional distress.
- **Asymptomatic oxygen desaturations** into the 70–80% range, documented on home pulse oximetry during the COVID pandemic, attributed to anxiety on multiple emergency evaluations.

The unifying diagnosis was hiding in plain sight. A CT scan performed 14 years earlier after her accident — originally reported as normal — was re-reviewed and revealed a crowded foramen magnum with loss of CSF space at the craniocervical junction. MRI confirmed CMI with 11 mm cerebellar tonsillar descent.

## The Pathophysiology: Why She Forgets to Breathe

This is where the case becomes genuinely remarkable from a physiological standpoint. The respiratory manifestations of CMI revolve around a fundamental and clinically critical distinction: **voluntary versus automatic breathing control.**

Voluntary breathing is mediated by cortical pathways — you can choose to take a breath, hold your breath, or modulate your respiratory pattern consciously. This system was intact in our patient. When she was awake, alert, and focused, she breathed normally. Her room air saturation in clinic was 96%. Her ABG was normal.

Automatic breathing, however, is controlled by brainstem respiratory centres in the medulla — the dorsal respiratory group, the ventral respiratory group, and the pre-Bötzinger complex. This system operates below conscious awareness and is essential during sleep or when conscious attention is directed elsewhere. In CMI, the ectopic cerebellar tonsils compress these medullary structures, compromising the automatic respiratory drive.

This dissociation explains the airport episode perfectly. In a moment of distraction — waiting for a flight, attention elsewhere — her automatic ventilatory drive failed. Her oxygen saturation plummeted to 78%. She did not feel dyspnea because she literally was not generating the signal to breathe, and the warning system that should have told her she was hypoxic was also disrupted.

## Why No Dyspnea? The Afferent Pathway Problem

The absence of dyspnea during profound desaturation is perhaps the most striking feature of this case and deserves careful physiological consideration. Dyspnea is a form of interoception — the conscious perception of physiological state. It depends on afferent signals from chemoreceptors and mechanoreceptors ascending through the brainstem to reach cortical processing areas, particularly the anterior insular cortex, where the sensation of air hunger is generated.

The authors propose a compelling mechanism: brainstem compression in CMI interrupts these afferent pathways during their transit through the medulla. The cortex never receives the input needed to generate the conscious experience of breathlessness. The patient is hypoxic, but her brain does not know it.

This is not simply a failure of motor output — it is a simultaneous failure of sensory processing. The respiratory control system is compromised in both directions: the efferent drive to breathe is diminished, and the afferent warning signals about hypoxia and hypercapnia are not reaching consciousness. The patient is doubly unprotected.

## Ondine's Curse: Acquired vs. Congenital

The phenotype displayed here — preserved voluntary breathing with loss of automatic respiratory control — mirrors what has classically been termed Ondine's curse, now more formally known as central hypoventilation syndrome. The congenital form is associated with PHOX2B gene mutations affecting the autonomic nervous system development. Our patient's presentation represents an acquired form, secondary to structural brainstem compression rather than a genetic defect.

This distinction matters clinically. Congenital central hypoventilation syndrome presents in infancy, typically with hypoxia and hypercapnia during sleep, and is diagnosed through genetic testing. Acquired central hypoventilation from CMI can present at any age, may be progressive, and — critically — may be reversible with posterior fossa decompression surgery.

The respiratory manifestations of CMI are more common than many clinicians appreciate. A prospective study of 90 adults with CMI found that 50% had sleep-related breathing disorders, with a central apnea index of ≥5 in 6.7%. Case series have documented acute respiratory failure and life-threatening hypoxemia in CMI patients. Recent guidelines now recommend routine polysomnography for CMI patients with suspected sleep-disordered breathing.

## The Sleep Study: A Cautionary Tale About Methodology

The patient's sleep study deserves attention as a lesson in diagnostic limitations. The type III study (which lacks EEG monitoring) demonstrated an AHI of 6.0 with 72% central events — seemingly mild. However, this almost certainly underestimates the true severity. Without EEG data, AHI is calculated using total recording time, which includes wakeful periods, rather than true sleep time. A patient who is awake for significant portions of the study will have their respiratory events diluted across a longer denominator.

The authors appropriately acknowledge this limitation and have arranged a type I polysomnography with full EEG, end-tidal capnography, and video recording at an external sleep centre. The key clinical point: when central hypoventilation is suspected, a limited sleep study may significantly underestimate the problem. If the clinical picture and the polysomnographic data do not match, trust the clinical picture and get a better study.

## Dysphagia as a Red Flag

The patient's choking episodes — her most distressing symptom — were investigated repeatedly from a gastroenterological and otolaryngological perspective, with normal findings, over several years. This delay carries an important lesson: dysphagia or choking without a clear structural cause on endoscopy should prompt consideration of a neurological origin.

The equal difficulty with solids and liquids is a key discriminator — this pattern suggests neurogenic dysphagia rather than mechanical obstruction. In CMI, lower cranial nerve involvement is reported in 15–26% of patients, presenting with palatal weakness, tongue atrophy, or cricopharyngeal achalasia. A retrospective study found CMI in 3.3% of children with persistent dysphagia — considerably higher than the 0.1–0.77% prevalence in the general paediatric population, suggesting that persistent unexplained dysphagia may be a sentinel indicator of underlying CMI.

## The Trauma Question

Whether this patient's CMI was congenital or trauma-induced remains unresolved. The CT performed immediately after her motor vehicle accident already demonstrated a crowded foramen magnum, but no pre-trauma imaging exists for comparison. The immediate onset of occipital headaches post-accident, followed by progressive bulbar and respiratory symptoms over 14 years, is compatible with either trauma-induced tonsillar herniation or trauma that exacerbated or unmasked a previously asymptomatic congenital variant.

This uncertainty does not change the clinical management — the compression is present, symptomatic, and potentially amenable to surgical decompression — but it highlights the importance of carefully reviewing prior imaging when evaluating patients with new neurological symptoms after head trauma.

## The Patient's Choice and the Role of Surgery

After multidisciplinary consultation, the patient opted for conservative management while contemplating surgical options. Posterior fossa decompression was recommended given the documented brainstem compression, progressive symptoms, and potentially reversible respiratory dysfunction. She has been monitored for 8 months with monthly clinical assessments, quarterly overnight oximetry, and annual MRI surveillance, with the understanding that clinical deterioration may necessitate surgical intervention.

This is a reasonable approach in a stable patient, but the underlying message is clear: posterior fossa decompression can restore normal CSF flow dynamics and potentially reverse respiratory symptoms in CMI. The respiratory complications of CMI are not merely a nuisance — they can be life-threatening, and surgical intervention can be curative.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **CMI can present with central hypoventilation that closely mimics acquired Ondine's curse.** Consider structural brainstem abnormalities in patients with unexplained respiratory dysfunction, sleep-disordered breathing, or oxygen desaturations — especially when pulmonary evaluation is normal.

- **The absence of dyspnea during hypoxia is itself a red flag for brainstem pathology.** When a patient desaturates without feeling breathless, the afferent warning pathways may be disrupted. This is not anxiety — this is a neurological problem until proven otherwise.

- **Dysphagia affecting solids and liquids equally suggests neurogenic rather than mechanical origin.** Normal endoscopy does not exclude neurological causes. Persistent unexplained dysphagia warrants investigation for CMI and other structural brainstem pathology.

- **A limited (type III) sleep study can significantly underestimate central hypoventilation.** If the clinical suspicion is high and the polysomnographic data seem discordantly mild, pursue a full type I study with EEG and capnography.

- **Review prior imaging.** This patient's diagnosis was delayed 14 years in part because a CT scan showing a crowded foramen magnum was originally reported as normal. When the clinical picture suggests structural pathology, personally review prior studies — or get a second radiological opinion.

- **Posterior fossa decompression can reverse respiratory symptoms in CMI.** Central hypoventilation from tonsillar compression is potentially treatable. Do not relegate these patients to conservative management without a thorough surgical evaluation.

## Final Thought

This case is a reminder that the most dangerous patient is not the one who is screaming for help — it is the one who is silently hypoxic and does not know it. When a patient's oxygen saturation is 78% and they feel only dizzy, something is very wrong with the wiring between their body and their brain. Find the compression.



# AHI of 6.0 but Sats of 70%: How Your Sleep Study Can Underestimate Central Hypoventilation

## The Case That Shouldn't Add Up

A woman in her early 40s has been self-monitoring her oxygen saturation at home during the COVID-19 pandemic and repeatedly documenting saturations in the 70–80% range — without any sensation of dyspnea. At an international airport, she acutely desaturates to 78%, experiences dizziness and nausea, and is denied boarding. Supplemental oxygen resolves her symptoms within minutes. Her pulmonary workup, including chest imaging and pulmonary function tests, is entirely normal. Her arterial blood gas on room air? Unremarkable: pH 7.41, PaCO₂ 42, PaO₂ 88.

You order a sleep study. The AHI comes back at 6.0 — mildly elevated, barely above the threshold for mild sleep apnea. You might be tempted to close the book on this one.

Don't. Because this patient has documented oxygen desaturations to the 70% range, a 14-year history of progressive neurological symptoms, and an 11 mm cerebellar tonsillar descent consistent with Chiari malformation type I. The sleep study didn't just undercall it — it was structurally incapable of telling the full story.

## The Methodological Trap: Type III Sleep Studies and AHI Calculation

Here's the critical detail that's easy to miss if you don't think about what your sleep study actually measures.

The AHI — apnea-hypopnea index — is fundamentally a ratio: number of respiratory events divided by time. But which time? That depends on the type of study you're running.

A type I polysomnography includes full EEG monitoring, allowing the sleep technician to distinguish sleep from wakefulness. The denominator becomes true total sleep time. A type III sleep study — the kind used in this case due to practical constraints — relies on respiratory effort, airflow, oxygen saturation, and heart rate, but lacks EEG. Without EEG, there is no reliable way to determine when the patient is actually asleep. So the AHI is calculated using total recording time, which includes periods of wakefulness.

This matters enormously. If a patient lies in bed for 8 hours but only sleeps for 4, and all their respiratory events occur during sleep, the type III study dilutes those events across double the relevant time. The result is a systematically lower AHI. In a patient with central hypoventilation — where events cluster during sleep when automatic respiratory drive fails — this underestimation can be profound.

In this case, the AHI of 6.0 with 72% central events likely represents a significant underestimation of the true sleep-disordered breathing burden. The clinical corroboration is overwhelming: repeated asymptomatic desaturations to 70–80%, an acute symptomatic desaturation to 78% at an airport, and clear anatomical evidence of brainstem compression. The mild AHI doesn't contradict the clinical picture — it simply reflects the wrong denominator.

## When Clinical Discrepancy Demands Escalation

This case teaches a broader lesson about reconciling discrepant data. When your clinical suspicion is high and your confirmatory test seems reassuringly normal, you have two options: accept the test and move on, or interrogate the test's limitations. The right answer depends entirely on the pre-test probability and the severity of the clinical findings.

In this patient, several features should have triggered escalation to type I polysomnography regardless of the type III result. First, the predominance of central events at 72% — even within a low overall AHI — is a red flag for central hypoventilation, not obstructive sleep apnea. Second, the documented desaturations to the 70% range without patient awareness represent a level of physiological derangement that a mildly elevated AHI cannot explain. Third, the anatomical evidence of 11 mm tonsillar descent with brainstem compression provides a plausible mechanism for central respiratory failure.

The lesson isn't that type III studies are useless. They have an important role in screening and in settings where full polysomnography isn't feasible. But when the clinical picture strongly suggests central hypoventilation — particularly in the setting of known or suspected brainstem pathology — a type III study that appears reassuring should be interpreted with extreme caution. The absence of EEG, end-tidal capnography, and video monitoring means you're missing the tools needed to characterize central respiratory events accurately.

## The Bigger Picture: Don't Attribute to Anxiety What Structural Brainstem Compression Can Explain

This patient's 14-year diagnostic journey is a masterclass in anchoring bias and premature closure. Multiple emergency evaluations attributed her hypoxia to anxiety. Three laryngoscopies and two upper endoscopies were normal. Her choking — affecting solids and liquids equally, a pattern that should point toward neurogenic rather than mechanical dysphagia — was repeatedly investigated from a gastroenterological and otolaryngological perspective.

Meanwhile, a CT scan from 14 years earlier showed a crowded foramen magnum that had been overlooked. When it was finally re-reviewed in the context of her full clinical picture, it pointed directly toward the diagnosis.

The respiratory presentation of Chiari malformation type I is increasingly recognized. In a prospective study of 90 adults with CMI, 50% had sleep-related breathing disorders. The mechanism is elegant and terrifying: tonsillar descent compresses medullary respiratory centers, disrupting automatic breathing while leaving voluntary, cortical breathing pathways intact. The patient breathes normally when awake and attentive. During sleep — or distraction, as the airport episode demonstrated — automatic respiratory drive fails, and the patient quite literally forgets to breathe.

Perhaps the most dangerous feature is what this patient didn't feel: dyspnea. The authors hypothesize that brainstem pathology interrupts afferent signals that would normally ascend to the cortex — particularly the anterior insular cortex — to generate the conscious sensation of air hunger. The patient desaturates to 78% and feels dizzy, not breathless. This absence of the cardinal warning sign of respiratory failure is itself a warning sign of central origin.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **A type III sleep study calculates AHI using total recording time, not total sleep time.** Without EEG, wakeful periods dilute the denominator and systematically underestimate event frequency. A "mild" AHI on a type III study does not exclude significant central hypoventilation.
- **When clinical findings conflict with polysomnographic data, interrogate the study's methodology before accepting its conclusions.** Desaturations to 70–80% are never explained by an AHI of 6.0 — the test is wrong, not the patient.
- **Escalate to type I polysomnography with full EEG, end-tidal capnography, and video monitoring** whenever central hypoventilation is clinically suspected, particularly in the setting of known or suspected brainstem pathology.
- **Dysphagia affecting solids and liquids equally is neurogenic until proven otherwise.** Normal endoscopy in this context should prompt neurological evaluation, not reassurance.
- **Central hypoventilation can present without dyspnea.** If the brainstem can't relay the afferent signals that generate the sensation of air hunger, your patient may be profoundly hypoxemic without feeling breathless. Absence of dyspnea in the setting of desaturation is a red flag, not reassurance.
- **Consider structural brainstem pathology — including Chiari malformation — in patients with unexplained central respiratory dysfunction.** Posterior fossa decompression can potentially reverse these symptoms, but only if you make the diagnosis.



# The Weight of Her Hair: Scalp Allodynia, Vertex Pain, and Chiari Malformation

## A Headache That Changed a Haircut — and a Life

Picture a woman in her early 40s who cut her hair short — not for fashion, not for convenience, but because the sheer weight of her own hair against her scalp was excruciating. The pain at her vertex was so intense, so consistently 6 out of 10, that she finally took shears to it against her cultural norms. Her baseline pain dropped to 3 out of 10. She got her life back, partially. But for 14 years before that, no one connected her scalp allodynia, her occipital headaches, her choking episodes, and her unexplained oxygen desaturations to 70% on a home pulse oximeter. The unifying diagnosis? Chiari malformation type I (CMI) with 11 mm cerebellar tonsillar descent — hiding in plain sight on a CT scan performed 14 years earlier after a minor motor vehicle accident, originally reported as normal.

What makes this case from Mousa and colleagues so instructive is not just the delayed diagnosis. It is the headache phenotype itself — a presentation that does not fit neatly into the classic CMI symptom profile we were taught to look for, and one that exposes just how poorly equipped we are to categorize headache patterns in this patient population.

## The Cardinal Symptom — and What Happens When It Doesn't Look Cardinal

We know the textbook teaching: CMI presents with paroxysmal occipital headache precipitated by Valsalva manoeuvres, present in over 80% of cases. But this patient's headaches were constant, pressure-like, localized to a coin-sized area at the vertex, and notably *not* exacerbated by coughing, straining, or other Valsalva manoeuvres. Several physicians diagnosed her with nummular headache. She received multiple local scalp injections that offered only transient relief. No one stepped back to ask why a "nummular headache" was co-existing with progressive choking episodes and asymptomatic oxygen desaturations.

The vertex allodynia was the standout feature. Even the weight of her hair triggered discomfort — a detail so specific and so functionally devastating that it drove a decision with real social consequences. As a divorced mother in a cultural context where long hair carried expectations, cutting her hair was not a trivial choice. It contributed to emotional distress even as it relieved physical pain. This is the kind of quality-of-life trade-off that does not show up in an MRI report but dominates a patient's lived experience.

## Occipital Neuralgia, Burning Vertex Syndrome, or Something We Haven't Named Yet?

The authors of this case thoughtfully unpack three possible explanations for the vertex symptoms, and honestly, the literature does not let us choose among them with confidence.

**Occipital neuralgia associated with CMI** is the most mechanistically appealing option. Diagnostic criteria for occipital neuralgia require dysesthesia or allodynia apparent during innocuous stimulation of the scalp or hair — which this patient has in textbook fashion. The proposed mechanism involves compression or irritation of the greater occipital nerve complex at the craniocervical junction, precisely where CMI exerts its anatomical effect. Yet the association between occipital neuralgia and CMI is rarely reported in the literature, with no conclusive data establishing a causative link.

**Burning vertex syndrome** is a recently described entity characterized by scalp pain, cutaneous allodynia, and features that do not fit neatly into established primary headache classifications — not migraine, not cluster, not tension-type, not nummular. The hypothesized mechanism involves small-fibre pathways originating in the scalp, and patients respond variably to greater occipital nerve blocks. This patient's transient response to local scalp injections is at least consistent with this framework.

**A separate primary headache disorder occurring coincidentally** cannot be excluded. The temporal relationship between her motor vehicle accident and headache onset is suggestive but not definitive — the CT performed immediately after the accident already showed a crowded foramen magnum, meaning the malformation may have been pre-existing and congenital, with trauma unmasking or exacerbating it rather than causing it de novo.

The honest truth, as the authors acknowledge, is that systematic documentation of cutaneous allodynia patterns in CMI populations simply does not exist. There was no published instrument to assess specific symptoms in CMI patients prior to the development of the Chiari Symptom Profile. We are left observing an interesting clinical phenomenon without the tools to classify it rigorously.

## The Bigger Picture: Dysphagia, Desaturation, and the Cost of Siloed Thinking

While the blog post brief focuses on the headache story, it is impossible to separate the scalp allodynia from the broader diagnostic failure this case represents. This woman underwent three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper GI endoscopies — all normal — for choking episodes that affected solids and liquids equally. The equal difficulty with all consistencies is a classic neurogenic pattern, distinct from mechanical dysphagia. Normal endoscopies without a clear structural cause should have prompted neurological evaluation far earlier. The prevalence of CMI in a retrospective study of children with persistent dysphagia was 3.3%, considerably higher than the 0.1%–0.77% reported in the general paediatric population. Persistent dysphagia can be a sentinel indicator.

And then there were the oxygen saturations. Home pulse oximetry during the COVID-19 pandemic revealed asymptomatic desaturations to 70%–80%. At an international airport, she was found to have an oxygen saturation of 78% and was denied boarding. Multiple emergency evaluations attributed her symptoms to anxiety. The absence of dyspnoea during profound desaturation is itself a critical clue — the authors hypothesize that brainstem pathology interrupts afferent neural pathways ascending to the cortex, preventing the generation of conscious air hunger. She was literally forgetting to breathe, and no one recognized it.

## Clinical Pearls

The headache phenotype in CMI can be atypical and may not follow the classic Valsalva-precipitated occipital pattern. Vertex pain with marked scalp allodynia — even hair-triggered pain — deserves consideration of craniocervical junction pathology.

Dysphagia affecting solids and liquids equally, with normal endoscopic evaluation, should prompt a neurological workup rather than repeated GI procedures. Neurogenic dysphagia is a recognized but underappreciated CMI manifestation, reported in 15%–26% of patients.

The absence of dyspnoea during significant oxygen desaturation is a red flag for central hypoventilation, not anxiety. Brainstem compression can disrupt the afferent pathways necessary to generate the conscious sensation of breathlessness.

A "normal" CT report from years past is not a closed file. This patient's crowded foramen magnum was visible on a 14-year-old scan and was overlooked at initial interpretation. When clinical suspicion is high, re-review of prior imaging with fresh eyes — or with a neuroradiologist specifically asked about the craniocervical junction — can be diagnostic.

Symptomatic management decisions carry cultural and psychosocial weight. Cutting her hair relieved this patient's pain but conflicted with cultural expectations and contributed to emotional distress. Do not underestimate the cost of these trade-offs when counselling patients.

## Take-Home Messages

- **CMI headache phenotypes are broader than the textbook suggests.** Vertex pain, scalp allodynia, and non-Valsalva-triggered patterns are underrecognized and poorly classified in the existing literature.
- **Burning vertex syndrome and occipital neuralgia represent useful diagnostic frameworks** for scalp allodynia in CMI, but the evidence base for either association remains thin. Treat these as clinical hypotheses, not settled science.
- **Equal dysphagia to solids and liquids with normal endoscopy is neurogenic until proven otherwise.** Stop repeating GI procedures and start thinking brainstem.
- **Asymptomatic desaturation without dyspnoea is central hypoventilation, not anxiety.** The patient who "forgets to breathe" may have brainstem compression.
- **Prior imaging deserves re-review when the clinical picture changes.** A 14-year-old CT held the answer all along.
- **We need better symptom assessment tools for CMI.** The Chiari Symptom Profile is a start, but validated, standardized documentation of headache and allodynia patterns is still lacking — and cases like this show exactly why that gap matters.



# Watchful Waiting or Posterior Fossa Decompression: Managing Symptomatic Chiari with Central Apnea

## The Case That Makes You Rethink "Mild" Sleep Apnea

A woman in her early 40s presents with 7 years of choking episodes—solids and liquids, day and night—plus 14 years of occipital headaches. Three laryngoscopies, two endoscopies, all normal. During the pandemic, she starts checking her own oxygen saturation at home and repeatedly documents saturations in the 70–80% range. She doesn't feel short of breath during these episodes. Multiple emergency evaluations blame anxiety. Then, at an international airport, she acutely desaturates to 78%, is denied boarding, and recovers fully with supplemental oxygen within minutes. Pulmonary workup is normal. The unifying diagnosis? Chiari malformation type I with 11 mm cerebellar tonsillar descent, predominantly central sleep apnea (72% of events), and brainstem compression producing an acquired central hypoventilation phenotype.

What makes this case genuinely unsettling is the gap between the severity of the pathophysiology and how quietly it presents. This patient was dropping her oxygen to levels that should trigger gasping, panic, and air hunger—and she felt nothing. She was literally forgetting to breathe, and her brain was failing to tell her that anything was wrong. And yet the sleep study showed only an AHI of 6.0, which by standard criteria is "mild." When you see that number in a patient with documented saturations to 70%, you have to ask: what are we actually measuring, and what are we missing?

## When the Sleep Study Underestimates the Problem

The discrepancy between this patient's polysomnographic findings and her clinical severity is a critical lesson. The team used a type III sleep study—which records respiratory effort, airflow, oxygen saturation, and heart rate, but lacks EEG monitoring. Without EEG, you cannot accurately determine total sleep time. Instead, the AHI is calculated using total recording time, which includes wakeful periods. That means the denominator is inflated, and the AHI is artificially low. The true apnea-hypopnea index during actual sleep is almost certainly higher.

This is not a minor methodological footnote. It means that in resource-limited settings or when patients cannot tolerate full polysomnography, you may be systematically underestimating the burden of central respiratory events. If a patient with Chiari malformation comes back with a "mild" AHI but you are also hearing about choking in sleep, witnessed apneas, or unexplained daytime desaturations, trust the clinical picture and pursue type I polysomnography with full EEG, end-tidal capnography, and video recording before concluding the problem is trivial.

## The Respiratory Pathophysiology: Why Chiari Patients "Forget to Breathe"

The mechanism here is elegant and terrifying. Voluntary breathing—breathing on command, breathing when you consciously decide to—is mediated by cortical pathways that bypass the brainstem. This remains intact in Chiari patients. What fails is automatic breathing, the brainstem-controlled rhythm that sustains ventilation during sleep and when conscious attention is directed elsewhere. The cerebellar tonsils, descended through the foramen magnum, compress medullary respiratory control centers. The result is a phenotype that mimics congenital central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine's curse) but through a structural, potentially reversible mechanism.

The absence of dyspnea during profound desaturation is equally important and likely reflects disruption of ascending afferent pathways through the brainstem. The interoceptive signals that normally travel to the anterior insular cortex to generate the conscious sensation of air hunger are interrupted. The cortex never receives the message. This is why this patient could sit in an airport with a saturation of 78% and feel dizzy and nauseated but not breathless. It is also why repeated emergency evaluations attributed her symptoms to anxiety—she didn't look like someone suffocating, because her brain didn't know she was.

## The Decision: Decompression or Watchful Waiting?

This is where the case becomes clinically rich. Here is a patient with 11 mm tonsillar descent, predominantly central sleep apnea, documented life-threatening desaturations, a 14-year history of progressive neurogenic dysphagia, and chronic headaches. By most criteria, this is symptomatic Chiari with brainstem compromise. Posterior fossa decompression was recommended. And the patient chose to wait.

The question is whether that decision is reasonable, and what a responsible monitoring framework looks like when it is made. The authors of this case report frame the decision as patient-centered after multidisciplinary consultation, and they implement a structured surveillance plan. But let's be honest about the risk profile: this patient has already demonstrated desaturations into the 70% range without respiratory distress. She has a condition where the natural history may include further brainstem compression, worsening central hypoventilation, and the potential for catastrophic respiratory failure during sleep. The fact that she has chosen to wait does not eliminate that risk; it transfers part of the risk management burden to the surveillance strategy.

The evidence supporting surgery is not definitive but is mechanistically compelling. Posterior fossa decompression aims to restore normal CSF flow dynamics at the craniocervical junction and relieve brainstem compression. Case series of CMI patients with respiratory insufficiency have documented reversal of central apnea and normalization of ventilatory drive following decompression. The caveat is that most of this evidence comes from case reports and small series, not randomized trials. Surgical decision-making therefore relies on individualized assessment of symptom burden, anatomical severity, and progression.

## Structuring a Monitoring Plan for the Patient Who Defers Surgery

The monitoring plan implemented in this case is a reasonable template, and worth examining closely:

- **Monthly clinical assessments** focusing on neurological examination, new or worsening symptoms, and any reports of choking, witnessed apneas, or altered consciousness during sleep.
- **Quarterly overnight oximetry** to trend the frequency and depth of desaturation episodes. This is a pragmatic surrogate for disease progression, though it cannot distinguish central from obstructive events.
- **Annual MRI surveillance** to monitor for progressive tonsillar descent, development of syringomyelia, or new brainstem compression.
- **Type I polysomnography at an external sleep center** has been arranged but is pending. Once completed, it will provide the definitive characterization of sleep architecture and respiratory events that the type III study could not.

What is missing from this plan, or at least not explicitly described, is a clear trigger for re-engaging surgical consultation. If the overnight oximetry shows worsening desaturation depth or frequency, does that prompt reconsideration? If annual MRI shows new syringomyelia, is that a hard threshold for surgery? If the patient develops any episode of unconsciousness or near-arrest during sleep, does that become non-negotiable? These escalation criteria should be explicitly defined and documented, not left to ad-hoc reassessment. A patient who is defers surgery with this risk profile needs a plan that is not just monitoring, but monitoring with defined action thresholds.

## Clinical Pearls

- **Dysphagia without a structural cause is a neurology problem, not a GI problem.** This patient had seven years of normal laryngoscopies and endoscopies before anyone looked at her brainstem. Equal difficulty with solids and liquids is a hallmark of neurogenic dysphagia—distinguish this from mechanical obstruction, which typically affects solids first.
- **A "mild" AHI in a Chiari patient may be falsely reassuring.** Type III sleep studies without EEG systematically underestimate AHI by using total recording time rather than total sleep time. If the clinical picture doesn't match the numbers, upgrade the study.
- **Absence of dyspnea during hypoxia is a red flag for brainstem pathology.** When a patient desaturates to the 70s without feeling breathless, the problem may not be in the lungs—it may be in the ascending interoceptive pathways that should be carrying the alarm signal to consciousness.
- **Central events predominating over obstructive events should trigger a search for structural brainstem pathology.** Most sleep apnea is obstructive. When central events dominate, especially in a patient with headaches and bulbar symptoms, Chiari malformation belongs on the differential.
- **Old imaging deserves a second look.** The key to this case was a 14-year-old CT scan that showed a crowded foramen magnum, originally reported as normal. When symptoms are chronic and progressive, revisit prior imaging with fresh eyes and a new clinical question.

## The Bottom Line

- Chiari malformation type I can present primarily with respiratory dysfunction, mimicking acquired central hypoventilation syndrome, and the diagnosis can be delayed for over a decade when symptoms are attributed to reflux, anxiety, or idiopathic dysphagia.
- Central sleep apnea predominance, neurogenic dysphagia, occipital headaches, and asymptomatic desaturation form a recognizable constellation that should prompt MRI of the craniocervical junction.
- Posterior fossa decompression can restore CSF flow dynamics and potentially reverse respiratory symptoms, but evidence is limited to case series—surgical decisions require individualized, multidisciplinary assessment.
- When patients defer surgery, a structured monitoring plan with explicitly defined escalation thresholds is essential—not just surveillance, but a plan that specifies when "watchful waiting" becomes "time to operate."
- Type III sleep studies without EEG underestimate AHI in Chiari patients; pursue type I polysomnography when clinical severity exceeds the reported numbers.
