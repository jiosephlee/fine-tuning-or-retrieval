Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

### [Clinical Pearls] What imaging finding defines Chiari malformation type I on sagittal MRI, and how common is this condition in the adult population?

Chiari malformation type I is defined radiographically by caudal displacement of the cerebellar tonsils **at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum** on sagittal MRI. This measurement is the diagnostic standard, though it should be emphasized that tonsillar descent exists on a spectrum—from an asymptomatic anatomical variant to a clinically significant malformation—and management decisions must be individualized based on symptom correlation rather than imaging alone.

In terms of prevalence, contemporary imaging studies identify CMI in approximately **1% of the adult population**. However, the vast majority of these cases are asymptomatic; clinically symptomatic presentations occur in only about **0.1%** of the general population. The condition has a modest female predominance (1.3:1) and typically presents in the third decade of life.

In the case under discussion, the patient's MRI confirmed CMI with **11 mm of cerebellar tonsillar descent**—well beyond the 5 mm threshold—and provided the anatomical basis for her brainstem compression, explaining her central sleep apnea, oropharyngeal dysfunction, and acquired central hypoventilation phenotype. Notably, a retrospective review of a CT scan performed 14 years earlier had already revealed a crowded foramen magnum, a finding that was overlooked at the time. This underscores the important clinical lesson: while MRI is the diagnostic standard for CMI, a crowded foramen magnum with loss of CSF space at the craniocervical junction on CT should prompt further evaluation with MRI, particularly in patients with unexplained neurological, respiratory, or bulbar symptoms.
