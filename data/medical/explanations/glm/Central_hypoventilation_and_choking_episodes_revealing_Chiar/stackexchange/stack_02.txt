Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

### [Morning Report] A patient presents with recurrent choking episodes affecting both solids and liquids equally. Multiple laryngoscopies and upper endoscopies are normal. What does the equal difficulty with all consistencies suggest about the underlying mechanism, and what diagnostic category should be considered next?

When dysphagia affects solids and liquids equally from the outset, the key clinical inference is that the problem is **neurogenic rather than mechanical**. Mechanical or structural obstruction — such as a stricture, ring, or malignancy — typically impairs solids first, with liquids affected only later as the lumen narrows further. In contrast, equal impairment across all consistencies indicates a disorder of the neuromuscular coordination of swallowing — that is, a problem with the neural control of pharyngeal and laryngeal function rather than a luminal barrier.

In this patient's case, multiple laryngoscopies and upper endoscopies were normal, effectively excluding a mucosal or structural cause. Yet the evaluation remained anchored in gastroenterological and otolaryngological frameworks for years, delaying diagnosis. The critical pivot came when the neurogenic pattern of dysphagia was recognized alongside other red flags — chronic occipital headaches, unexplained oxygen desaturation, and absent dyspnea during hypoxic episodes — prompting neurological imaging. This ultimately revealed Chiari malformation type I with 11 mm tonsillar descent compressing brainstem and lower cranial nerve structures.

The teaching point is straightforward: **when dysphagia or choking lacks a structural explanation on endoscopy, and particularly when solids and liquids are equally affected, a neurological origin should be actively considered**. In this case, the underlying mechanism was compression of lower cranial nerve nuclei and brainstem respiratory centers at the craniocervical junction. The diagnostic category that should be considered next is **structural neurologic pathology — specifically, craniocervical junction and brainstem disorders, including Chiari malformation**.
