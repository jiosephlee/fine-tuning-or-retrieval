Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

### [Clinical Pearls] What is the significance of a predominantly central apnea pattern (72% of events) on polysomnography in a patient with Chiari malformation, and how might the use of a Type III sleep study (without EEG) lead to underestimation of the true apnea-hypopnea index?

A predominantly central apnea pattern on polysomnography in a patient with Chiari malformation type I is a critical finding because it directly points to brainstem compression of medullary respiratory control centers. In CMI, cerebellar tonsillar descent impairs the automatic (metabolic) control of breathing governed by the medulla, while voluntary cortical breathing pathways remain intact. This dissociation produces a phenotype analogous to acquired central hypoventilation syndrome—so-called Ondine's curse—in which the patient maintains normal ventilation while awake and attentive but loses respiratory drive during sleep or distraction. In this case, the 72% central event predominance, combined with documented oxygen desaturations to 70%–80% occurring without patient awareness or dyspnea, strongly supports the hypothesis that tonsillar compression of the brainstem is selectively disrupting automatic respiratory control rather than producing a peripheral or obstructive process.

Regarding the methodology: the Type III sleep study used here records respiratory effort, airflow, oxygen saturation, and heart rate, but lacks EEG monitoring. Without EEG, true sleep time cannot be distinguished from wakefulness within the recording period. Consequently, the apnea-hypopnea index is calculated using total recording time as the denominator rather than total sleep time. Because wakeful periods—during which respiratory events are infrequent—are included in this larger denominator, the AHI is artificially diluted downward. This explains the apparent paradox in this case: a reported AHI of only 6.0 (classified as "mild") alongside profound desaturations to 70%–80% and a predominantly central event pattern. The true AHI, once corrected for actual sleep time with full Type I polysomnography including EEG, would likely be substantially higher. This case underscores that when central hypoventilation from structural brainstem pathology is suspected, Type I polysomnography with EEG, end-tidal capnography, and video monitoring is necessary to accurately quantify respiratory events and characterize sleep architecture.
