Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

### [Differential Diagnosis] In the evaluation of bulbar symptoms and unexplained hypoxia, what conditions were considered in the differential diagnosis, and what specific findings excluded myasthenia gravis and gastroesophageal reflux disease in this case?

## Differential Diagnosis of Bulbar Symptoms and Unexplained Hypoxia

In this case, the combination of dysphagia affecting solids and liquids equally, recurrent choking episodes, and asymptomatic severe oxygen desaturation prompted a broad differential diagnosis encompassing gastroenterological, neuromuscular, pulmonary, and neurostructural etiologies.

**Gastroesophageal reflux disease with laryngopharyngeal reflux** was an early consideration, given the nocturnal choking episodes and coughing. However, this diagnosis was excluded by three key findings: normal esophageal and laryngeal endoscopies (performed on multiple occasions), normal pH studies, and—critically—a complete lack of response to maximal acid suppression therapy. The persistence of symptoms despite appropriate antisecretory treatment argued strongly against reflux as the underlying mechanism.

**Myasthenia gravis** was pursued because the bulbar symptoms—choking, dysphagia, and oropharyngeal dysfunction—can represent the presenting phenotype of a neuromuscular junction disorder. This diagnosis was excluded on three grounds: acetylcholine receptor antibodies and anti-MuSK antibodies were both negative, the patient demonstrated no fatigability on examination, and there was no diurnal variation in symptom severity. The absence of these hallmark features made a disorder of neuromuscular transmission untenable.

**Intrinsic pulmonary disease** was investigated after the documented desaturation episodes and was excluded by normal chest imaging and normal pulmonary function tests, including preserved maximal inspiratory and expiratory pressures.

The remaining and ultimately unifying diagnosis was **brainstem pathology secondary to Chiari malformation type I**. This explanation accounted for all features simultaneously: the equal impairment of solids and liquids signaled neurogenic rather than mechanical dysphagia; the central apneas (72% of events on polysomnography) reflected compression of medullary respiratory control centers; and the striking absence of dyspnea during profound desaturation pointed to disruption of brainstem afferent pathways mediating the conscious sensation of air hunger.

The central teaching point is this: when bulbar symptoms and unexplained hypoxia persist despite normal endoscopic and pulmonary evaluation, and when neuromuscular junction disorders are excluded by serology and clinical phenotype, a structural brainstem lesion—particularly at the craniocervical junction—must be considered. The equal difficulty with all consistencies is an especially important clue, as it distinguishes neurogenic dysphagia from mechanical obstruction and should redirect the diagnostic search toward neuroimaging rather than repeated endoscopic procedures.
