Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

## Case Presentation and Clinical History

Understanding a complex case like this one requires careful attention to the chronology of symptoms, because the sequence in which findings appear often reveals more about the underlying pathology than any single symptom in isolation. This patient's story unfolded over 14 years, and the delays in diagnosis stemmed partly from the fact that her symptoms were evaluated in isolation by different specialists rather than as components of a single neurological process.

### Initial Presentation: Motor Vehicle Accident and Headache Onset

The patient was a woman in her early 40s when she presented to a family medicine clinic, but her symptoms had begun 14 years earlier, shortly after a minor motor vehicle accident that involved a brief loss of consciousness. The immediate aftermath of the trauma was marked by the onset of persistent occipital and vertex headaches. These headaches were described as constant and pressure-like, often localized to a coin-sized area at the vertex of the skull. Notably, the headaches were not exacerbated by coughing, straining, or other Valsalva maneuvers. This absence of Valsalva exacerbation is clinically important because it represents a departure from the classic headache pattern of Chiari malformation type I (CMI), in which paroxysmal occipital headaches precipitated by Valsalva maneuvers occur in over 80% of symptomatic patients. The absence of this hallmark feature likely contributed to the diagnostic delay, as clinicians repeatedly evaluated her for primary headache syndromes rather than structural posterior fossa pathology. Several physicians diagnosed her with nummular headache, a distinct primary headache disorder characterized by localized, coin-shaped head pain. She underwent multiple local scalp injections that provided only transient relief, which is consistent with the variable and often short-lived response of nummular headache to local anesthetic blocks. The temporal relationship between the accident and symptom onset raises the important question of whether her CMI was congenital and merely unmasked by trauma, or whether the trauma itself induced tonsillar herniation. Both mechanisms are recognized in contemporary etiological classifications of Chiari malformations, which distinguish primary (congenital) forms from secondary or acquired forms resulting from head trauma or increased intracranial pressure.

### Evolution of Choking Episodes

Seven years before her eventual neurological evaluation, the patient developed a new and distressing symptom: recurrent choking episodes that occurred unpredictably during both meals and sleep. The episodes affected solids and liquids equally, a feature that carries significant diagnostic weight. Dysphagia that affects all consistencies uniformly suggests a neurogenic or muscular origin rather than a mechanical obstruction, which typically affects solids first and progresses to liquids only as the lumen narrows further. The episodes were described as sudden and distressing, often interrupting meals or awakening her from sleep with violent coughing and gasping, though they never resulted in complete airway obstruction. Nocturnal episodes occurred three to four times per week, occasionally twice in a single night. This frequency and pattern are consistent with lower cranial nerve dysfunction, which is reported in 15% to 26% of CMI patients, typically presenting with oropharyngeal dysfunction such as palatal weakness, tongue atrophy, or cricopharyngeal achalasia. The brainstem and lower cranial nerve nuclei (nucleus ambiguus, glossopharyngeal and vagal nuclei) reside at the cranio-cervical junction, precisely where tonsillar descent exerts its compressive effect. Over the course of 7 years, the patient underwent extensive evaluation for these choking episodes, including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies, all of which were reported as normal. The persistence of symptoms despite normal endoscopic findings is itself an important diagnostic clue: when structural laryngeal and esophageal evaluations are repeatedly normal in the setting of consistent dysphagia, the evaluation should shift toward neurological causes.

### Allodynia at the Vertex

One of the most distinctive features of this patient's history was marked cutaneous allodynia localized to the vertex of the scalp, where even the weight of her hair triggered discomfort. Allodynia—the perception of pain from a normally non-painful stimulus—is a phenomenon involving sensitization of nociceptive pathways and can occur with peripheral nerve injury, central sensitization, or both. The patient ultimately discovered that cutting her hair short reduced her baseline pain from 6/10 to 3/10, a pragmatic but telling self-intervention. This finding is difficult to categorize within established headache classifications. Occipital neuralgia, which occurs in association with CMI, can cause scalp hypersensitivity, and diagnostic criteria include dysesthesia or allodynia apparent during innocuous stimulation of the scalp or hair. However, the pain was localized to the vertex rather than the occipital region, which is atypical for occipital neuralgia. An alternative possibility is the recently described "burning vertex syndrome," which presents with scalp pain and cutaneous allodynia that does not fit neatly into established primary headache classifications. The hypothesized mechanism involves small fiber pathways originating in the scalp. In this patient, the vertex symptoms could represent occipital neuralgia related to CMI, burning vertex syndrome occurring coincidentally, or an entirely separate primary headache disorder. The decision to cut her hair short, while therapeutically effective, conflicted with cultural expectations and contributed to emotional distress—a reminder that clinical symptoms can carry consequences extending well beyond their physiological impact.

### Incidental Discovery of Oxygen Desaturation

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient began self-monitoring her oxygen saturation using a home pulse oximeter. Over several months, she repeatedly documented asymptomatic episodes of oxygen saturation dropping to the 70% to 80% range. These episodes were often unrecognized until she checked her device, meaning she experienced no respiratory distress, no dyspnea, and no subjective sensation of air hunger during the desaturation events. This finding is remarkable and clinically alarming. Oxygen saturations in the 70% to 80% range correspond to arterial partial pressures of oxygen well below 50 mm Hg, a level that under normal physiological conditions should trigger potent ventilatory drive and an intense sensation of breathlessness mediated through peripheral chemoreceptors (carotid bodies) and central chemoreceptors medullary respiratory centers, with afferent signals ascending through brainstem pathways to the cortex. The absence of dyspnea during such profound hypoxemia indicates a failure of this respiratory control circuit, and specifically points toward brainstem pathology interrupting the afferent neural pathways that must ascend through the brainstem to reach the cortex. In CMI, cerebellar tonsillar descent compresses medullary structures, which can disrupt both the chemoreceptor response and the ascending interoceptive signals that produce the conscious sensation of air hunger. Multiple emergency evaluations at this stage attributed her symptoms to anxiety, which represents a common diagnostic pitfall when patients present with subjective symptoms that are not immediately supported by routine objective testing.

### The Pivotal Airport Episode

A turning point in the patient's diagnostic journey occurred at an international airport. While waiting for her flight, she experienced acute dizziness and nausea. On-site medical personnel documented an oxygen saturation of 78%, and she was denied boarding. Administration of supplemental oxygen led to complete symptom resolution within minutes. This episode is instructive for several reasons. First, it provided an objective, externally documented measurement of profound hypoxemia during an acute symptomatic event, eliminating the possibility that the home pulse oximetry readings were artifactual. Second, the rapid resolution with supplemental oxygen confirms that the symptoms were directly related to hypoxemia rather than to anxiety or another cause. Third, the fact that the episode occurred during what was likely a period of reduced vigilance—waiting passively in an airport—illustrates a key feature of CMI-related respiratory dysfunction: the selective impairment of automatic breathing while voluntary (cortical) breathing remains intact. During moments of distraction, reduced arousal, or drowsiness, the brainstem-driven automatic respiratory control system should maintain ventilation, but in this patient, that system was compromised. The subsequent pulmonary evaluation, including chest imaging and pulmonary function testing, was entirely normal, effectively excluding intrinsic pulmonary disease and redirecting the investigation toward central causes.

### Prior Evaluations and Misattribution to Anxiety

The patient's diagnostic history illustrates the fragmented nature of care that patients with rare or atypical presentations often experience. Over the course of 14 years, she underwent three flexible laryngoscopies, two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies, multiple local scalp injections for headache, multiple emergency department visits for hypoxia, and a full pulmonary evaluation. All of these were either normal or non-diagnostic. The repeated attribution of her symptoms to anxiety is a recurrent theme worth examining. Anxiety can produce hyperventilation, choking sensations, and subjective dyspnea, but it does not produce asymptomatic oxygen saturations in the 70% to 80% range, nor does it produce dysphagia affecting all consistencies with normal endoscopic findings. The misattribution likely occurred because each specialist evaluated the symptom within their own domain: the otolaryngologist saw normal laryngoscopy and concluded there was no structural cause; the gastroenterologist saw normal endoscopy and excluded reflux; the emergency physician saw a normal-appearing patient with a pulse oximeter reading and considered anxiety. What was missing throughout was a unifying neurological diagnosis that could account for all symptoms simultaneously.

### Social and Emotional Impact

The social consequences of the patient's symptoms were substantial. She avoided restaurants and social gatherings due to fear of public choking episodes, leading to progressive social withdrawal and emotional distress. This is a clinically significant feature because the psychosocial burden of chronic undiagnosed illness can amplify symptom severity and complicate the clinical picture, potentially reinforcing the misattribution to psychiatric causes. The decision to cut her hair short, while providing meaningful pain relief, further contributed to distress because it conflicted with cultural expectations. She was a divorced mother of one teenage son and worked as a government administrator, details that contextualize the functional impact of her illness on her daily life and underscore the importance of arriving at a correct diagnosis not only for disease management but also for restoration of quality of life.

### Physical Examination Findings

At the time of neurological consultation, the patient's vital signs were notable for a blood pressure of 118/76 mm Hg, heart rate of 72 beats per minute, respiratory rate of 14 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation of 96% on room air. The normal oxygen saturation at rest is consistent with the intermittent nature of her desaturation episodes, which occurred during sleep or distraction rather than during active wakeful examination. The neurological examination findings, as documented in the case report, are presented in the investigations section with the corresponding imaging. The pertinent point for the clinical history is that the examination did not reveal overt cranial nerve palsies, motor deficits, or sensory findings that would have immediately localized the lesion, which is consistent with the often subtle neurological presentation of CMI in adults, where lower cranial nerve involvement may be functional rather than detectable on routine bedside examination.

### Past Medical and Family History, and the Significance of the Old CT Scan

The patient's past medical history was notable only for the motor vehicle accident that had prompted a non-contrast CT scan of the head 14 years earlier. This scan had been reported at the time as normal. Family history was negative for neurological conditions, which makes a purely genetic congenital explanation less straightforward, although CMI is typically sporadic and does not follow a simple Mendelian pattern. The retrieval and re-review of the 14-year-old CT scan proved pivotal. Although CT is less sensitive than MRI for evaluating the posterior fossa due to bone artifact, the scan clearly showed a crowded foramen magnum with cerebellar tissue extending to the level of the opisthion and loss of cerebrospinal fluid space at the cranio-cervical junction. These findings were suggestive of CMI but had been overlooked at the time of initial interpretation. This oversight carries an important lesson: the posterior fossa and craniocervical junction are anatomical regions that require deliberate attention on every head imaging study, particularly in trauma patients who may have subtle structural abnormalities. The missed finding on the CT scan resulted in a 14-year delay in diagnosis, during which the patient's symptoms progressed from headaches to bulbar dysfunction to respiratory compromise. The subsequent MRI of the brain and cervical spine, which confirmed 11 mm cerebellar tonsillar descent, was prompted not by the original CT findings but by the eventual clinical recognition that a unifying neurological diagnosis was needed.

## Background: Chiari Malformation Type I and Central Respiratory Control

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is defined radiologically by caudal displacement of the cerebellar tonsils at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum, measured on sagittal MRI. This threshold, while essential for diagnosis, must be interpreted in clinical context. Contemporary population-based imaging studies reveal tonsillar descent meeting radiographic criteria in approximately 1% of adults, yet only about 0.1% of the general population presents with symptoms attributable to the malformation. The condition shows a modest female predominance of 1.3:1 and typically becomes symptomatic during the third decade of life—the age range of the patient in this case report, a woman in her early 40s whose symptoms had evolved over 14 years.

The fundamental pathogenesis of CMI centers on a developmentally small posterior fossa, which creates a volumetric mismatch between the contained neural structures and the available osseous space. The crowded posterior fossa forces the cerebellar tonsils downward through the foramen magnum, potentially compressing the cervicomedullary junction and obstructing normal cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow at the craniocervical junction. Importantly, contemporary evidence has challenged the traditional view that all tonsillar descent is pathological. Volumetric studies demonstrate substantial demographic variability in posterior fossa measurements without consistent symptom correlation, supporting a conceptual model in which CMI represents a spectrum ranging from a normal anatomical variant to a clinically significant malformation. This spectrum concept has direct relevance to the case at hand: the patient's CT scan from 14 years prior showed a crowded foramen magnum that was initially overlooked, presumably because the finding was not recognized as potentially meaningful in an asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic patient. Distinguishing an asymptomatic anatomical variant from a pathological entity requiring intervention demands individualized, symptom-based clinical reasoning rather than relying on the imaging measurement alone.

When CMI does produce symptoms—the clinical entity termed Chiari syndrome—the presentation follows recognizable but varied patterns. The cardinal feature is a paroxysmal occipital headache precipitated by Valsalva manoeuvres such as coughing, straining, or sneezing, present in over 80% of symptomatic cases. Adults generally manifest more severe symptomatology than children, particularly when syringomyelia—a fluid-filled cavitation of the spinal cord—develops as a secondary consequence of impaired CSF flow. Lower cranial nerve involvement occurs in 15%–26% of patients and typically presents with oropharyngeal dysfunction, including palatal weakness, tongue atrophy, or cricopharyngeal achalasia. These bulbar manifestations are central to understanding this case report, where the patient's most distressing and earliest evaluated symptoms were recurrent choking episodes affecting both solids and liquids—a pattern consistent with neurogenic dysphagia from lower cranial nerve compression at the brainstem.

Respiratory manifestations of CMI represent an increasingly recognized but potentially underdiagnosed complication that is especially relevant here. In a prospective study of 90 adults with CMI, 50% were found to have sleep-related breathing disorders. A central apnoea index of 5 or greater was observed in 6.7% of patients, although pure central sleep apnoea occurred in only one case, reflecting the relative rarity of severe central respiratory dysfunction. The respiratory pathology in CMI, however, extends beyond sleep disturbance. Published case series have documented acute respiratory failure and life-threatening hypoxaemia in CMI patients, underscoring that brainstem compression can produce respiratory compromise that is neither trivial nor confined to sleep. In the present case, the patient's home pulse oximetry revealed desaturations to 70%–80% without accompanying respiratory distress, and an airport episode documented an oxygen saturation of 78%—findings that align with this recognized but dangerous manifestation of CMI.

The mechanisms by which CMI produces respiratory dysfunction involve three interrelated processes: direct compression of medullary respiratory control centres, obstruction of CSF outflow at the foramen magnum, and disruption of the neural pathways that subserve automatic ventilatory control. The medulla and lower pons house the dorsal respiratory group, the ventral respiratory group, and the pontine respiratory group—collections of neurons that collectively generate the rhythmic pattern of breathing without conscious effort. When cerebellar tonsils descend through the foramen magnum and compress these structures, the automatic respiratory drive can be selectively impaired. This pathophysiology distinguishes CMI-related respiratory dysfunction from peripheral pulmonary or neuromuscular causes of hypoventilation, which typically affect respiratory mechanics rather than the central generation of the breathing rhythm.

Understanding why the patient in this case report could desaturate profoundly without feeling short of breath requires a clear distinction between voluntary and automatic breathing. Voluntary breathing is mediated by cortical pathways originating in the motor cortex and descending to the spinal cord via the corticospinal tract, bypassing the brainstem respiratory pattern generators. This system allows a person to deliberately control breathing during activities such as speaking, singing, or breath-holding. Automatic breathing, in contrast, is controlled by brainstem centres that operate continuously and unconsciously, and it is this system that maintains ventilation during sleep and during periods when conscious attention is directed elsewhere. In CMI, tonsillar compression of the medulla can selectively impair the automatic breathing pathway while leaving voluntary breathing intact. The patient literally maintains the ability to breathe on command but loses the unconscious drive that sustains ventilation when she is distracted or asleep. The airport episode in this case—where a momentary distraction precipitated desaturation to 78%—vividly illustrates this selective failure of automatic control.

Furthermore, the patient's striking absence of dyspnoea during these profound desaturations requires additional explanation. The sensation of breathlessness is a form of interoception that depends on afferent signals from chemoreceptors, pulmonary stretch receptors, and other sensory inputs ascending through the brainstem to reach the anterior insular cortex, where the conscious perception of air hunger is generated. When brainstem pathology disrupts these afferent pathways in transit, the cortex never receives the necessary input to produce the feeling of breathlessness. This mechanism explains the dangerous lack of awareness that characterized the patient's episodes: she could desaturate to 70% without any subjective sensation of respiratory distress, a feature that delayed diagnosis and placed her at significant risk.

This phenotype—preserved voluntary breathing with impaired automatic control—closely resembles central hypoventilation syndrome, historically termed Ondine's curse after the mythological figure whose curse deprived her unfaithful lover of automatic bodily functions including breathing. The congenital form of central hypoventilation syndrome is a rare genetic disorder associated with mutations in the PHOX2B gene, which encodes a transcription factor essential for development of the autonomic nervous system and brainstem respiratory neurons. The acquired form, as illustrated by this case, arises from structural brainstem pathology such as CMI, rather than from a developmental genetic defect. Distinguishing acquired from congenital central hypoventilation is clinically important: the congenital form presents in infancy and follows a predictable genetic pattern, whereas the acquired form may emerge at any age following the development or progression of an underlying structural lesion. The patient in this case presented with acquired central hypoventilation in adulthood, with symptom onset following a motor vehicle accident—a temporal relationship that raises important questions about the role of trauma in CMI.

Oropharyngeal dysfunction in CMI merits specific attention because it was the symptom that dominated this patient's presentation and drove her initial diagnostic evaluations toward gastroenterology and otolaryngology rather than neurology. A prospective evaluation using validated swallowing assessments found dysfunction in 36% of CMI patients, with neurogenic patterns predominating. The hallmark of neurogenic dysphagia, as demonstrated in this case, is difficulty with all consistencies—solids and liquids equally—distinguishing it from mechanical dysphagia, where solids are typically affected more than liquids. This equal impairment reflects the fact that the problem lies in the coordinated neuromuscular control of swallowing rather than in a fixed structural obstruction. The mechanism involves compression of lower cranial nerve nuclei (glossopharyngeal, vagus, and hypoglossal nerves) at the cervicomedullary junction, which impairs palatal elevation, pharyngeal constriction, and laryngeal closure during deglutition. The clinical lesson is direct: when dysphagia or choking is evaluated by endoscopy and laryngoscopy and found to be normal—as this patient's three laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies were—a neurological cause should be considered, and structural brainstem abnormalities including CMI should be specifically sought.

The role of trauma in CMI adds a further layer of complexity relevant to this case. CMI is traditionally conceptualized as a congenital condition reflecting a developmentally small posterior fossa. Contemporary aetiological classifications, however, recognize both primary (congenital) and secondary or acquired forms, the latter resulting from trauma, increased intracranial pressure, or other processes that can drive tonsillar descent. In this case, the patient's CT scan performed immediately after her motor vehicle accident 14 years earlier already demonstrated a crowded foramen magnum. Without pre-trauma imaging, it is impossible to determine whether the trauma caused tonsillar herniation de novo, or whether it exacerbated or unmasked a previously asymptomatic congenital variant. What is clinically apparent is the temporal sequence: occipital headaches began immediately after the accident, and bulbar and respiratory symptoms progressed over the subsequent 14 years. This chronology illustrates that CMI can have a long latency between an inciting event and the full expression of symptoms, and that head trauma may serve as a trigger that initiates or accelerates the pathological process. Recognition of this potential link is important for clinicians evaluating patients with new neurological or respiratory symptoms following head injury, particularly when those symptoms are progressive and lack an alternative explanation.

Together, these concepts—radiographic definition, the spectrum from anatomical variant to symptomatic malformation, brainstem compression of respiratory and bulbar centres, the dissociation of voluntary and automatic breathing, acquired central hypoventilation, neurogenic dysphagia, and trauma as a potential trigger—provide the framework necessary to understand how a patient could present with 7 years of choking, 14 years of headaches, and profound unexplained hypoxia before CMI was finally identified. The following sections trace how the case unfolded diagnostically and how these pathophysiological principles were confirmed through investigation.

## Diagnostic Reasoning and Investigations

The diagnostic journey in this case illustrates a fundamental principle of clinical reasoning: when multiple organ systems produce symptoms that defy isolated explanation, a unifying diagnosis must be sought. This patient presented with three seemingly disparate problems—chronic occipital and vertex headaches, progressive dysphagia with choking, and unexplained oxygen desaturation—that had been investigated independently over seven years without resolution. The breakthrough came when her symptoms were reconsidered together through a neurological lens, and a forgotten imaging study was re-examined with new clinical context.

### A Systematic Approach to Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis was constructed around the three cardinal features: dysphagia affecting solids and liquids equally, episodic hypoxia without respiratory distress, and chronic occipital-vertex headaches. Each category was systematically evaluated and excluded through targeted investigations.

Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease with laryngopharyngeal reflux was the first consideration given the prominence of choking episodes. This is a reasonable starting point, as reflux-related microaspiration is a common cause of laryngeal irritation and choking sensations. However, the equal difficulty with solids and liquids is atypical for reflux, which more commonly causes liquid-related symptoms or laryngeal globus sensation. The patient had undergone three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies over the course of her illness, all reported as normal. Normal pH studies and the absence of symptomatic improvement with maximal acid suppression therapy effectively excluded this diagnosis. The repeated normal endoscopic evaluations also argued against structural oesophageal pathology such as strictures, rings, or motility disorders detectable on endoscopy, although manometry was not explicitly reported.

Myasthenia gravis was considered because of the bulbar distribution of symptoms. Myasthenia gravis can present with dysphagia, dysphonia, and fatigability of oropharyngeal muscles, and choking is a well-recognized manifestation. The pathophysiology involves autoantibodies against the postsynaptic acetylcholine receptor or muscle-specific kinase (MuSK), leading to variable weakness that worsens with sustained activity and improves with rest. In this patient, however, the clinical pattern was inconsistent with myasthenia: there was no fatigability, no diurnal variation in symptoms, and no fluctuating weakness on examination. Importantly, both acetylcholine receptor antibodies and anti-MuSK antibodies were negative. While seronegative myasthenia exists, the absence of the characteristic clinical phenotype alongside negative serology made this diagnosis untenable.

Primary pulmonary pathology was extensively investigated after the documentation of oxygen desaturations to the 70%–80% range. The airport incident, where oxygen saturation of 78% was recorded and supplemental oxygen produced rapid symptom resolution, prompted a thorough pulmonary workup. Chest imaging was normal, excluding parenchymal disease, pleural effusion, and pulmonary vascular abnormalities. Pulmonary function tests demonstrated values within or near the predicted range: forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) at 98% predicted and forced vital capacity (FVC) at 96% predicted, effectively excluding obstructive disease. Maximum inspiratory pressure (MIP) at 80% predicted and maximum expiratory pressure (MEP) at 75% predicted were mildly reduced but not low enough to suggest significant respiratory muscle weakness. An arterial blood gas on room air was reassuring: pH 7.41, PaCO₂ 42 mm Hg, PaO₂ 88 mm Hg, and SaO₂ 95%, all within normal ranges. This last finding is particularly instructive—it demonstrates that the patient maintained normal gas exchange during wakeful, voluntary breathing in the clinic setting, a observation that would become central to understanding her intermittent nocturnal and distraction-related desaturation episodes.

### The Critical Role of Historical Imaging Review

The turning point in this case was the re-examination of a 14-year-old non-contrast CT scan of the head. This scan had been performed following the patient's motor vehicle accident and had been reported as normal at the time. The decision to retrieve and re-review this study was prompted by the recognition that her symptoms began shortly after the accident and by the clinical need to evaluate for structural brainstem or craniocervical pathology.

On re-review, the CT scan revealed a crowded foramen magnum with cerebellar tissue extending to the level of the opisthion and loss of the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) space at the cranio-cervical junction. In the axial and sagittal planes of a CT scan, a crowded foramen magnum appears as obliteration of the normal CSF cisternal spaces surrounding the cerebellar tonsils and cervicomedullary junction. While CT lacks the soft tissue resolution of MRI, the loss of visible CSF space at the foramen magnum is a recognizable radiographic sign that should prompt further evaluation with MRI. The initial misinterpretation underscores a recurring lesson in radiology: findings may be present but unrecognized when the clinical question being asked does not direct attention to the relevant anatomical region. The original scan was performed to evaluate for traumatic intracranial injury, and the crowded foramen magnum was not clinically relevant to that question. This case demonstrates that historical imaging should be re-examined when the clinical question evolves.

### MRI Confirmation of Chiari Malformation Type I

MRI of the brain and cervical spine was the definitive diagnostic study, confirming Chiari malformation type I (CMI) with 11 mm cerebellar tonsillar descent below the foramen magnum. The diagnostic threshold for CMI is tonsillar herniation of at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum on sagittal MRI, making this patient's 11 mm descent clearly diagnostic. MRI is the gold standard for CMI because it provides high-resolution visualization of the posterior fossa, craniocervical junction, and cervical cord in multiple planes. The study also demonstrated compression at the craniocervical junction, the anatomical basis for the patient's brainstem dysfunction.

The degree of tonsillar descent correlates imperfectly with symptom severity, and current understanding of CMI recognizes a spectrum from asymptomatic anatomical variants to clinically significant malformations requiring intervention. The clinical significance of any given tonsillar descent must be interpreted in the context of symptoms, neurological findings, and evidence of functional impairment—precisely the integrative approach that this case required. The MRI findings alone would not have been sufficient to establish a causal relationship with the patient's respiratory and swallowing symptoms; it was the synthesis of imaging with the polysomnographic and clinical data that solidified the diagnosis.

### Videofluoroscopic Swallowing Study and Neurogenic Dysphagia

A videofluoroscopic swallowing study, also known as a modified barium swallow, was performed to characterize the dysphagia. This study uses fluoroscopic imaging to observe the oral, pharyngeal, and oesophageal phases of swallowing in real time, allowing assessment of bolus transit, timing, aspiration, and structural or functional abnormalities. The findings in this patient were consistent with a neurogenic pattern of dysphagia.

The key clinical observation that pointed toward a neurogenic mechanism was the equal difficulty with solids and liquids. Mechanical dysphagia, caused by structural obstruction such as strictures or rings, typically produces greater difficulty with solids than liquids. In contrast, neurogenic dysphagia—resulting from impaired coordination of the complex neuromuscular sequence of swallowing—affects all consistencies equally because the problem lies in the control of the swallowing apparatus rather than in the calibre of the passage. In CMI, compression of lower cranial nerve nuclei (glossopharyngeal, vagus, and hypoglossal nerves) at the brainstem disrupts the coordinated pharyngeal and laryngeal motor programmes essential for safe swallowing, producing exactly this pattern. The observation that this patient's most distressing symptom was investigated repeatedly from a gastroenterological and otolaryngological perspective over years, with normal findings each time, highlights the clinical aphorism that dysphagia without a demonstrable structural cause should prompt evaluation for neurological disease.

### Sleep Study: Central Event Predominance and Its Interpretation

A type III sleep study was performed and demonstrated an apnoea–hypopnoea index (AHI) of 6.0, which technically categorizes as mild sleep-disordered breathing (an AHI of 5–15 events per hour is classified as mild). However, the composition of these events was striking: 72% were central in origin, meaning that they involved a cessation of respiratory effort rather than an obstructive mechanical cause. This predominance of central events is the key finding and the strongest sleep-related evidence for brainstem-mediated respiratory dysfunction.

Central sleep apnoea arises from a failure of the brainstem respiratory control centres to initiate or maintain respiratory effort during sleep. In this patient, the pathophysiological mechanism is compression of medullary respiratory neurons by the herniated cerebellar tonsils. The brainstem houses the dorsal respiratory group in the nucleus tractus solitarius and the ventral respiratory group in the nucleus ambiguus and retroambiguus, which together generate the rhythmic pattern of breathing. Mechanical compression of these structures can abolish the automatic respiratory rhythm during sleep, when cortical voluntary drive to breathe is absent. This explains why the patient's desaturation episodes occurred predominantly during sleep and during moments of distraction—the voluntary, cortically driven breathing that she could summon while alert was sufficient to maintain gas exchange, but when cortical attention was withdrawn, the brainstem's automatic ventilatory drive was inadequate.

The marked discrepancy between the patient's dramatic clinical desaturation episodes (to 70%–80%) and the relatively mild AHI of 6.0 requires explanation and illustrates important methodological considerations in sleep medicine.

### Limitations of Type III Polysomnography

The sleep study performed was a type III study, which relies on respiratory effort belts, airflow sensors, pulse oximetry, and heart rate monitoring. It does not include electroencephalography (EEG), which is required to determine sleep stages and accurately identify periods of sleep versus wakefulness during the recording. This distinction is critical because the AHI is defined as the number of apnoea and hypopnoea events divided by total sleep time. Without EEG, the true sleep time cannot be determined, and the denominator becomes the total recording time instead. Because total recording time includes periods of wakefulness during which respiratory events are less frequent, the calculated AHI using total recording time will systematically underestimate the true AHI based on actual sleep time. In a patient who preserved voluntary breathing while awake but lost automatic ventilatory drive during sleep, this underestimation would be particularly pronounced: her wakeful periods in the sleep laboratory, during which respiratory drive was maintained, would dilute the event count.

Type I polysomnography, the gold standard for sleep-disordered breathing evaluation, includes full EEG, electro-oculography, chin electromyography for sleep staging, along with respiratory effort, airflow, oximetry, end-tidal capnography, and video recording. This comprehensive approach would allow accurate calculation of AHI based on true sleep time, identification of sleep architecture abnormalities, and correlation of respiratory events with specific sleep stages. The case authors arranged for a type I study at an external sleep centre, which was pending at the time of publication. Until that study is completed, the diagnosis of central hypoventilation remains a well-supported clinical hypothesis rather than a definitively confirmed polysomnographic finding. The authors appropriately acknowledged this limitation.

Despite the methodological constraints, several converging features supported the clinical diagnosis: the predominance of central events (72%) despite the low overall AHI, the documented desaturations to 70%–80% during sleep and distraction without patient awareness, and the anatomical evidence of significant brainstem compression (11 mm tonsillar descent). The clinical picture was internally consistent even if the polysomnographic quantification was imprecise.

### Laboratory Investigations as Exclusions

A comprehensive laboratory panel was performed to exclude systemic and autoimmune causes that could contribute to the symptom complex. The metabolic panel, thyroid function tests, vitamin B12 levels, and inflammatory markers were all normal. As noted above, acetylcholine receptor antibodies and anti-MuSK antibodies were negative, excluding the principal serological forms of myasthenia gravis. While these investigations did not positively contribute to the diagnosis, they were essential in the systematic exclusion of alternative explanations and in establishing that the patient's symptoms were not attributable to a systemic metabolic, endocrine, or autoimmune process.

### Synthesis: Assembling the Unifying Diagnosis

The diagnosis in this case was not established by any single investigation but by the convergence of multiple lines of evidence. The historical CT scan provided the first anatomical clue. The MRI confirmed the structural abnormality. The videofluoroscopic swallowing study demonstrated a neurogenic pattern of dysphagia consistent with lower cranial nerve dysfunction. The sleep study demonstrated central event predominance consistent with brainstem respiratory control impairment. The pulmonary evaluation excluded intrinsic lung disease and demonstrated normal gas exchange during voluntary wakeful breathing. The clinical history provided the temporal narrative linking the motor vehicle accident to the onset of symptoms and documented the distinctive phenomenon of asymptomatic desaturation—oxygen levels falling to dangerous levels without the patient experiencing dyspnoea, a finding that pointed specifically to dysfunction of the brainstem afferent pathways mediating the conscious sensation of air hunger.

This synthesis is the essence of diagnostic reasoning in complex presentations: no single test was definitive, and several tests were either normal (laryngoscopy, endoscopy, pH studies, chest imaging, pulmonary function, arterial blood gas) or limited in their sensitivity (type III sleep study). The diagnosis emerged from integrating what was present (central sleep events, tonsillar descent, neurogenic dysphagia, asymptomatic desaturation) with what was absent (reflux, myasthenia, intrinsic lung disease, systemic illness), and from recognizing that the normal results themselves were diagnostically informative—they redirected attention away from the organ systems being investigated toward the central nervous system.

## Management, Outcome, and Follow-up

The management of Chiari malformation type I (CMI) with respiratory manifestations requires careful individualised decision-making, weighing the risks of surgical intervention against the dangers of ongoing brainstem compression. In this case, the treatment strategy emerged from a multidisciplinary consultation involving neurology, neurosurgery, pulmonology, and sleep medicine. This collaborative approach is essential because CMI with central hypoventilation sits at the intersection of multiple specialties, and no single discipline can fully assess the breadth of neurological, respiratory, and surgical considerations. The neurology team provided the unifying diagnosis and ongoing neurological assessment; neurosurgery evaluated the anatomical severity and surgical candidacy; pulmonology characterised the degree of respiratory dysfunction; and sleep medicine interpreted the polysomnographic data and outlined ongoing respiratory surveillance.

Two management options were presented to the patient. The first was conservative management, a reasonable approach in patients whose symptoms are bothersome but not immediately life-threatening, particularly when the patient is not yet prepared to undergo posterior fossa surgery. Conservative management does not mean neglect; it involves active symptom-based strategies and close surveillance for deterioration. In this patient, coping measures included regular hair cutting to reduce vertex allodynia, dietary modifications to minimise choking episodes, and avoidance of situations that might precipitate desaturation events. The second option was surgical intervention via posterior fossa decompression, the definitive treatment for symptomatic CMI.

Posterior fossa decompression is the standard surgical procedure for CMI and involves suboccipital craniectomy with or without duraplasty to enlarge the posterior fossa and relieve compression of the cerebellar tonsils and brainstem. The physiological goal is to restore normal cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow dynamics at the craniocervical junction. In CMI, the developmentally small posterior fossa creates a volumetric mismatch, obstructing CSF outflow at the foramen magnum and compressing medullary respiratory centres. By enlarging this space, decompression can relieve pressure on the brainstem, re-establish CSF circulation, and potentially reverse respiratory symptoms. The literature documents cases in which posterior fossa decompression restored normal automatic breathing in CMI patients with central hypoventilation, underscoring the potential reversibility of respiratory dysfunction when the underlying structural compression is addressed.

The indications for surgical intervention in CMI with respiratory manifestations are particularly compelling in this case. Several factors favoured surgery: documented severe oxygen desaturation to 70%–80% on multiple occasions, anatomical evidence of significant brainstem compression with 11 mm tonsillar descent, predominantly central apnoea events on polysomnography, progressive bulbar dysfunction manifested as oropharyngeal dysphagia, and a 14-year trajectory of worsening symptoms. The risk of progression to life-threatening respiratory failure or permanent neurological injury from ongoing brainstem compression is a critical consideration. Central hypoventilation by its nature carries the danger of sudden, severe hypoxaemia occurring without subjective respiratory distress, as dramatically illustrated by the airport episode in which the patient desaturated to 78% without dyspnoea. This absent awareness of physiological compromise represents an inherent and unpredictable danger that conservative management cannot fully mitigate.

Despite these considerations, the patient opted to defer surgery while contemplating her options. This decision reflects the principle of shared decision-making, in which the clinician presents the risks and benefits of each approach and the patient exercises autonomy in choosing a path aligned with her values and circumstances. Several factors likely influenced her choice. Surgery on the posterior fossa carries genuine risks, including CSF leak, meningitis, cranial nerve injury, and posterior fossa haemorrhage, and recovery can be prolonged. The patient may also have weighed the chronicity of her symptoms against the sudden prospect of a major intracranial procedure. The role of patient autonomy is paramount here: the clinician's responsibility is to ensure the patient fully understands the risks of deferral, including the possibility of sudden deterioration, while respecting her right to choose the timing of an elective intervention.

Because the patient chose to delay surgery, a structured monitoring plan was implemented to detect any clinical deterioration that would prompt escalation. This plan includes monthly clinical assessments, quarterly overnight oximetry, and annual MRI surveillance. Monthly clinical assessments focus on detecting new or worsening neurological symptoms, particularly changes in bulbar function, headache pattern, motor or sensory deficits, or any episodes of loss of consciousness. Quarterly overnight oximetry provides objective data on nocturnal oxygenation trends, allowing comparison with prior recordings to identify worsening desaturation patterns before they become clinically apparent. Annual MRI surveillance monitors for progressive tonsillar descent, development or enlargement of syringomyelia, and any changes in the degree of brainstem compression. This tiered surveillance strategy balances the patient's wish to defer surgery with the clinical obligation to detect deterioration early.

At 8-month follow-up, the patient has remained clinically stable without evidence of deterioration. Her symptoms have not worsened, and no new neurological deficits have emerged. This stability, while reassuring, does not eliminate the underlying risk, and the patient remains under close observation. The conservative approach is understood by both the patient and the treating team to be a temporising strategy rather than a definitive solution, with the understanding that any clinical deterioration may necessitate surgical intervention.

An important planned investigation is type I polysomnography with full EEG monitoring, end-tidal capnography, and video recording, arranged at an external sleep centre. The initial type III sleep study, while informative, lacked EEG data and could not accurately distinguish sleep from wakefulness, leading to potential underestimation of the apnoea–hypopnoea index. Type I polysomnography will provide definitive characterisation of sleep architecture, accurately quantify central versus obstructive events relative to true sleep time, measure end-tidal carbon dioxide trends to detect nocturnal hypoventilation, and allow video correlation of any motor or behavioural events. Completion of this study is pending coordination of patient availability and scheduling at the remote facility, but it represents a critical step in fully characterising the respiratory physiology before any surgical decision is finalised.

The criteria that would trigger escalation to surgical intervention include any of the following: worsening frequency or severity of oxygen desaturation episodes on overnight oximetry, development of new lower cranial nerve deficits or progression of dysphagia, radiological evidence of progressive tonsillar descent or new syringomyelia on MRI, any episode of respiratory failure requiring acute intervention, neurological deterioration on clinical examination, or the patient's own decision to proceed with surgery. These thresholds ensure that conservative management does not become a passive stance, but rather an active, surveilled period during which the patient and treating team remain vigilant for the signs that would shift the balance decisively toward surgical decompression.

## Clinical Lessons and Broader Implications

This case offers several layered clinical lessons that extend beyond the individual patient's diagnostic journey. The 14-year delay in diagnosis reflects a recurrent and avoidable pattern in CMI presentations, in which symptoms that span multiple organ systems are evaluated individually rather than as components of a unifying neurological disorder. The patient's choking episodes were investigated repeatedly through laryngoscopy and upper endoscopy—procedures that legitimately exclude structural lesions of the larynx and oesophagus but cannot assess brainstem function. When these tests returned normal, the symptoms were effectively orphaned: no gastroenterological or otolaryngological explanation was available, yet no neurological evaluation was initiated. Her headaches were similarly compartmentalised, diagnosed as nummular headache and treated with local scalp injections without consideration of a posterior fossa process. Her hypoxic episodes were attributed to anxiety. This pattern of organ-specific tunnel vision is a well-documented cause of diagnostic delay in CMI and in other structural brainstem disorders. The critical lesson is that when symptoms across multiple domains—respiratory, swallowing, headache, and cranial nerve function—coexist without a single-organ explanation, the clinician should actively seek a unifying neurological diagnosis rather than treating each symptom in isolation.

The dissociation between voluntary and automatic breathing is central to understanding this patient's respiratory presentation and illustrates a fundamental principle of respiratory neurophysiology. Voluntary breathing is mediated by corticospinal pathways originating in the motor cortex, allowing the patient to breathe on command, hold her breath, or modulate respiration during speech and eating. Automatic breathing, by contrast, is generated and sustained by brainstem centres—principally the pre-Bötzinger complex in the medulla, with modulation from the pontine respiratory group and peripheral chemoreceptors—that function without conscious input, particularly during sleep. In CMI, tonsillar descent compresses the medulla and disrupts these brainstem respiratory centres, impairing automatic ventilation while sparing voluntary control. The airport episode provides a vivid clinical illustration: while distracted and waiting for her flight, the patient's automatic respiratory drive failed, her oxygen saturation fell to 78%, and she experienced dizziness and nausea rather than breathlessness. When supplemental oxygen was administered, her symptoms resolved within minutes. This selective failure of automatic control—where the patient literally 'forgets to breathe'—closely mirrors the phenotype of congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, although the underlying mechanism in this case is structural brainstem compression rather than a PHOX2B mutation. Clinicians should recognise that preserved voluntary breathing during wakefulness does not exclude impaired automatic control during sleep or distraction, and that unexplained desaturation without dyspnoea warrants evaluation for central respiratory control disorders.

Perhaps the most dangerous feature of this case was the patient's absence of breathlessness during profound hypoxia. Oxygen saturations in the 70%–80% range would normally trigger intense air hunger, driven by chemoreceptor afferent signals that ascend through the brainstem to the cortex, where they are processed by the anterior insular cortex and other interoceptive regions to generate the conscious sensation of breathlessness. The authors hypothesise that brainstem pathology in this patient interrupted these ascending afferent pathways at the level of transit through compressed medullary structures, preventing the cortical interoceptive network from receiving the signals necessary to produce the feeling of air hunger. This hypothesis is consistent with emerging understanding of dyspnoea as a form of interoception dependent on intact afferent signalling. The practical implication is sobering: patients with brainstem pathology may experience life-threatening hypoxia without the warning signal of breathlessness that normally prompts self-protective behaviours such as seeking medical attention or repositioning the airway. Absence of dyspnoea during documented hypoxia should therefore be treated as a red flag for neurological dysfunction, not as reassurance that the hypoxia is benign or anxiety-related.

Dysphagia was the patient's most distressing symptom and the primary reason for repeated specialist evaluation. The equal difficulty with solids and liquids is a critical clinical clue: mechanical obstruction typically produces greater difficulty with solids than liquids, with progressive worsening as the luminal narrowing advances. Neurogenic dysphagia, by contrast, affects all consistencies from the outset because the swallowing apparatus—mediated by lower cranial nerve nuclei in the brainstem—fails to coordinate the pharyngeal phase regardless of bolus texture. In CMI, compression of these nuclei produces exactly this pattern. A retrospective study cited by the authors found CMI in 3.3% of children with persistent dysphagia, a rate considerably higher than the 0.1%–0.77% prevalence in the general paediatric population. Although this statistic derives from a paediatric population and may not generalise directly to adults, it underscores a broader principle: persistent dysphagia or choking without a structural cause on endoscopy should prompt neurological evaluation, and CMI should be included in the differential diagnosis. The years of normal laryngoscopies and endoscopies in this patient represent missed opportunities to redirect the diagnostic search toward the nervous system.

The marked discrepancy between the patient's severe clinical desaturation episodes and the relatively mild apnoea–hypopnoea index (AHI) of 6.0 requires careful methodological explanation. The sleep study performed was a type III study, which records respiratory effort, airflow, oxygen saturation, and heart rate but lacks EEG monitoring. Without EEG, total sleep time cannot be accurately determined, and the AHI must instead be calculated using total recording time, which includes periods of wakefulness. Because the denominator is inflated by awake time during which respiratory events do not occur, the resulting AHI is systematically lower than the true sleep-based value. This methodological limitation likely explains the apparent paradox of a low AHI coexisting with documented oxygen saturations of 70%–80%. Three features support genuine central hypoventilation despite the low AHI: the predominance of central events (72% of all recorded events), the documented severe desaturations without associated dyspnoea, and the anatomical evidence of 11 mm tonsillar descent with brainstem compression. A full type I polysomnogram with EEG, end-tidal capnography, and video recording has been arranged at an external facility to provide definitive characterisation but has not yet been completed. The authors appropriately acknowledge that central hypoventilation remains a well-supported clinical hypothesis rather than a definitively confirmed diagnosis. The lesson for clinicians is that type III sleep studies may significantly underestimate the severity of sleep-disordered breathing, and that discordance between polysomnographic indices and clinical findings should prompt escalation to full in-laboratory polysomnography rather than dismissal of the clinical concern.

The patient's vertex pain and allodynia represent an unusual and understudied phenomenon. Occipital neuralgia is associated with CMI and can produce scalp hypersensitivity; the diagnostic criteria include dysesthesia or allodynia triggered by innocuous stimulation of the scalp or hair, a description that matches this patient's experience of pain triggered by the weight of her hair. The recently described burning vertex syndrome, characterised by scalp pain and cutaneous allodynia that does not fit established primary headache categories, offers an alternative framework. Without systematic documentation of cutaneous allodynia patterns in CMI populations—no validated instrument existed before the Chiari Symptom Profile—the relationship between vertex symptoms and CMI remains speculative. The patient's relief from cutting her hair short, reducing her baseline pain from 6/10 to 3/10, is a striking clinical observation, but the authors appropriately present it as an interesting finding rather than a diagnostic feature. Clinicians should be aware that CMI may produce cutaneous sensory phenomena beyond the classic occipital headache, though the evidence base for these associations remains limited.

The temporal relationship between the patient's motor vehicle accident and symptom onset raises important questions about congenital versus acquired CMI. Contemporary aetiological classifications recognise primary (congenital) CMI and secondary or acquired forms resulting from trauma or increased intracranial pressure. In this case, the CT scan performed immediately after the accident already demonstrated a crowded foramen magnum, but without pre-trauma imaging it is impossible to determine whether the trauma caused tonsillar herniation or merely unmasked a pre-existing asymptomatic variant. The immediate onset of occipital headaches after the accident, followed by progressive bulbar and respiratory symptoms over 14 years, is compatible with either mechanism. For clinical reasoning, the distinction may be less important than the recognition that trauma can precipitate or exacerbate CMI symptoms, and that new neurological symptoms following head injury warrant careful imaging review with attention to the craniocervical junction even when initial radiology reports are normal.

The authors reviewed five published case reports of CMI presenting with acute or prolonged respiratory insufficiency to contextualise their findings. These cases share the theme of significant diagnostic delay and the need for a high index of suspicion when respiratory symptoms lack a cardiopulmonary explanation. Presentations vary: some patients present with acute respiratory failure, others with chronic progressive hypoventilation, and the degree of response to posterior fossa decompression also differs. This variability reflects the spectrum of brainstem compression severity, the presence or absence of syringomyelia, and individual differences in posterior fossa anatomy. The common thread is that CMI is underrecognised as a cause of respiratory dysfunction, and that the diagnosis is typically reached only after extensive negative cardiopulmonary and gastrointestinal evaluations.

Several practical recommendations emerge from this case. First, CMI should be considered in any patient with unexplained central sleep apnoea, nocturnal desaturation, or central hypoventilation, particularly when dyspnoea is absent or disproportionate to the degree of hypoxia. Second, persistent dysphagia affecting solids and liquids equally, with normal endoscopic evaluation, warrants neurological assessment and imaging of the craniocervical junction. Third, occipital headaches—especially those precipitated by Valsalva manoeuvres, though atypical presentations also occur—should prompt consideration of CMI, and imaging should specifically evaluate tonsillar position relative to the foramen magnum. Fourth, polysomnography should be obtained in CMI patients with suspected sleep-disordered breathing, as recommended by current guidelines, and full type I studies with EEG should be pursued when type III studies yield discordant results. Finally, when multiple symptoms spanning respiratory, swallowing, and headache domains coexist, the clinician should resist the temptation to treat each in isolation within separate subspecialty silos and instead seek a unifying neurological explanation through careful history, physical examination, and targeted neuroimaging.
