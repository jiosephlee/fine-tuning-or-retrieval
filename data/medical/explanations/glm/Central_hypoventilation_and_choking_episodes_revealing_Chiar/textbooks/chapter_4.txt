Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

Chapter 4: ## Management, Outcome, and Follow-up

The management of Chiari malformation type I (CMI) with respiratory manifestations requires careful individualised decision-making, weighing the risks of surgical intervention against the dangers of ongoing brainstem compression. In this case, the treatment strategy emerged from a multidisciplinary consultation involving neurology, neurosurgery, pulmonology, and sleep medicine. This collaborative approach is essential because CMI with central hypoventilation sits at the intersection of multiple specialties, and no single discipline can fully assess the breadth of neurological, respiratory, and surgical considerations. The neurology team provided the unifying diagnosis and ongoing neurological assessment; neurosurgery evaluated the anatomical severity and surgical candidacy; pulmonology characterised the degree of respiratory dysfunction; and sleep medicine interpreted the polysomnographic data and outlined ongoing respiratory surveillance.

Two management options were presented to the patient. The first was conservative management, a reasonable approach in patients whose symptoms are bothersome but not immediately life-threatening, particularly when the patient is not yet prepared to undergo posterior fossa surgery. Conservative management does not mean neglect; it involves active symptom-based strategies and close surveillance for deterioration. In this patient, coping measures included regular hair cutting to reduce vertex allodynia, dietary modifications to minimise choking episodes, and avoidance of situations that might precipitate desaturation events. The second option was surgical intervention via posterior fossa decompression, the definitive treatment for symptomatic CMI.

Posterior fossa decompression is the standard surgical procedure for CMI and involves suboccipital craniectomy with or without duraplasty to enlarge the posterior fossa and relieve compression of the cerebellar tonsils and brainstem. The physiological goal is to restore normal cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow dynamics at the craniocervical junction. In CMI, the developmentally small posterior fossa creates a volumetric mismatch, obstructing CSF outflow at the foramen magnum and compressing medullary respiratory centres. By enlarging this space, decompression can relieve pressure on the brainstem, re-establish CSF circulation, and potentially reverse respiratory symptoms. The literature documents cases in which posterior fossa decompression restored normal automatic breathing in CMI patients with central hypoventilation, underscoring the potential reversibility of respiratory dysfunction when the underlying structural compression is addressed.

The indications for surgical intervention in CMI with respiratory manifestations are particularly compelling in this case. Several factors favoured surgery: documented severe oxygen desaturation to 70%–80% on multiple occasions, anatomical evidence of significant brainstem compression with 11 mm tonsillar descent, predominantly central apnoea events on polysomnography, progressive bulbar dysfunction manifested as oropharyngeal dysphagia, and a 14-year trajectory of worsening symptoms. The risk of progression to life-threatening respiratory failure or permanent neurological injury from ongoing brainstem compression is a critical consideration. Central hypoventilation by its nature carries the danger of sudden, severe hypoxaemia occurring without subjective respiratory distress, as dramatically illustrated by the airport episode in which the patient desaturated to 78% without dyspnoea. This absent awareness of physiological compromise represents an inherent and unpredictable danger that conservative management cannot fully mitigate.

Despite these considerations, the patient opted to defer surgery while contemplating her options. This decision reflects the principle of shared decision-making, in which the clinician presents the risks and benefits of each approach and the patient exercises autonomy in choosing a path aligned with her values and circumstances. Several factors likely influenced her choice. Surgery on the posterior fossa carries genuine risks, including CSF leak, meningitis, cranial nerve injury, and posterior fossa haemorrhage, and recovery can be prolonged. The patient may also have weighed the chronicity of her symptoms against the sudden prospect of a major intracranial procedure. The role of patient autonomy is paramount here: the clinician's responsibility is to ensure the patient fully understands the risks of deferral, including the possibility of sudden deterioration, while respecting her right to choose the timing of an elective intervention.

Because the patient chose to delay surgery, a structured monitoring plan was implemented to detect any clinical deterioration that would prompt escalation. This plan includes monthly clinical assessments, quarterly overnight oximetry, and annual MRI surveillance. Monthly clinical assessments focus on detecting new or worsening neurological symptoms, particularly changes in bulbar function, headache pattern, motor or sensory deficits, or any episodes of loss of consciousness. Quarterly overnight oximetry provides objective data on nocturnal oxygenation trends, allowing comparison with prior recordings to identify worsening desaturation patterns before they become clinically apparent. Annual MRI surveillance monitors for progressive tonsillar descent, development or enlargement of syringomyelia, and any changes in the degree of brainstem compression. This tiered surveillance strategy balances the patient's wish to defer surgery with the clinical obligation to detect deterioration early.

At 8-month follow-up, the patient has remained clinically stable without evidence of deterioration. Her symptoms have not worsened, and no new neurological deficits have emerged. This stability, while reassuring, does not eliminate the underlying risk, and the patient remains under close observation. The conservative approach is understood by both the patient and the treating team to be a temporising strategy rather than a definitive solution, with the understanding that any clinical deterioration may necessitate surgical intervention.

An important planned investigation is type I polysomnography with full EEG monitoring, end-tidal capnography, and video recording, arranged at an external sleep centre. The initial type III sleep study, while informative, lacked EEG data and could not accurately distinguish sleep from wakefulness, leading to potential underestimation of the apnoea–hypopnoea index. Type I polysomnography will provide definitive characterisation of sleep architecture, accurately quantify central versus obstructive events relative to true sleep time, measure end-tidal carbon dioxide trends to detect nocturnal hypoventilation, and allow video correlation of any motor or behavioural events. Completion of this study is pending coordination of patient availability and scheduling at the remote facility, but it represents a critical step in fully characterising the respiratory physiology before any surgical decision is finalised.

The criteria that would trigger escalation to surgical intervention include any of the following: worsening frequency or severity of oxygen desaturation episodes on overnight oximetry, development of new lower cranial nerve deficits or progression of dysphagia, radiological evidence of progressive tonsillar descent or new syringomyelia on MRI, any episode of respiratory failure requiring acute intervention, neurological deterioration on clinical examination, or the patient's own decision to proceed with surgery. These thresholds ensure that conservative management does not become a passive stance, but rather an active, surveilled period during which the patient and treating team remain vigilant for the signs that would shift the balance decisively toward surgical decompression.
