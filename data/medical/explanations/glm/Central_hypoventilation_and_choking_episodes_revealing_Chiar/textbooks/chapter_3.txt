Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Reasoning and Investigations

The diagnostic journey in this case illustrates a fundamental principle of clinical reasoning: when multiple organ systems produce symptoms that defy isolated explanation, a unifying diagnosis must be sought. This patient presented with three seemingly disparate problems—chronic occipital and vertex headaches, progressive dysphagia with choking, and unexplained oxygen desaturation—that had been investigated independently over seven years without resolution. The breakthrough came when her symptoms were reconsidered together through a neurological lens, and a forgotten imaging study was re-examined with new clinical context.

### A Systematic Approach to Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis was constructed around the three cardinal features: dysphagia affecting solids and liquids equally, episodic hypoxia without respiratory distress, and chronic occipital-vertex headaches. Each category was systematically evaluated and excluded through targeted investigations.

Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease with laryngopharyngeal reflux was the first consideration given the prominence of choking episodes. This is a reasonable starting point, as reflux-related microaspiration is a common cause of laryngeal irritation and choking sensations. However, the equal difficulty with solids and liquids is atypical for reflux, which more commonly causes liquid-related symptoms or laryngeal globus sensation. The patient had undergone three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies over the course of her illness, all reported as normal. Normal pH studies and the absence of symptomatic improvement with maximal acid suppression therapy effectively excluded this diagnosis. The repeated normal endoscopic evaluations also argued against structural oesophageal pathology such as strictures, rings, or motility disorders detectable on endoscopy, although manometry was not explicitly reported.

Myasthenia gravis was considered because of the bulbar distribution of symptoms. Myasthenia gravis can present with dysphagia, dysphonia, and fatigability of oropharyngeal muscles, and choking is a well-recognized manifestation. The pathophysiology involves autoantibodies against the postsynaptic acetylcholine receptor or muscle-specific kinase (MuSK), leading to variable weakness that worsens with sustained activity and improves with rest. In this patient, however, the clinical pattern was inconsistent with myasthenia: there was no fatigability, no diurnal variation in symptoms, and no fluctuating weakness on examination. Importantly, both acetylcholine receptor antibodies and anti-MuSK antibodies were negative. While seronegative myasthenia exists, the absence of the characteristic clinical phenotype alongside negative serology made this diagnosis untenable.

Primary pulmonary pathology was extensively investigated after the documentation of oxygen desaturations to the 70%–80% range. The airport incident, where oxygen saturation of 78% was recorded and supplemental oxygen produced rapid symptom resolution, prompted a thorough pulmonary workup. Chest imaging was normal, excluding parenchymal disease, pleural effusion, and pulmonary vascular abnormalities. Pulmonary function tests demonstrated values within or near the predicted range: forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) at 98% predicted and forced vital capacity (FVC) at 96% predicted, effectively excluding obstructive disease. Maximum inspiratory pressure (MIP) at 80% predicted and maximum expiratory pressure (MEP) at 75% predicted were mildly reduced but not low enough to suggest significant respiratory muscle weakness. An arterial blood gas on room air was reassuring: pH 7.41, PaCO₂ 42 mm Hg, PaO₂ 88 mm Hg, and SaO₂ 95%, all within normal ranges. This last finding is particularly instructive—it demonstrates that the patient maintained normal gas exchange during wakeful, voluntary breathing in the clinic setting, a observation that would become central to understanding her intermittent nocturnal and distraction-related desaturation episodes.

### The Critical Role of Historical Imaging Review

The turning point in this case was the re-examination of a 14-year-old non-contrast CT scan of the head. This scan had been performed following the patient's motor vehicle accident and had been reported as normal at the time. The decision to retrieve and re-review this study was prompted by the recognition that her symptoms began shortly after the accident and by the clinical need to evaluate for structural brainstem or craniocervical pathology.

On re-review, the CT scan revealed a crowded foramen magnum with cerebellar tissue extending to the level of the opisthion and loss of the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) space at the cranio-cervical junction. In the axial and sagittal planes of a CT scan, a crowded foramen magnum appears as obliteration of the normal CSF cisternal spaces surrounding the cerebellar tonsils and cervicomedullary junction. While CT lacks the soft tissue resolution of MRI, the loss of visible CSF space at the foramen magnum is a recognizable radiographic sign that should prompt further evaluation with MRI. The initial misinterpretation underscores a recurring lesson in radiology: findings may be present but unrecognized when the clinical question being asked does not direct attention to the relevant anatomical region. The original scan was performed to evaluate for traumatic intracranial injury, and the crowded foramen magnum was not clinically relevant to that question. This case demonstrates that historical imaging should be re-examined when the clinical question evolves.

### MRI Confirmation of Chiari Malformation Type I

MRI of the brain and cervical spine was the definitive diagnostic study, confirming Chiari malformation type I (CMI) with 11 mm cerebellar tonsillar descent below the foramen magnum. The diagnostic threshold for CMI is tonsillar herniation of at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum on sagittal MRI, making this patient's 11 mm descent clearly diagnostic. MRI is the gold standard for CMI because it provides high-resolution visualization of the posterior fossa, craniocervical junction, and cervical cord in multiple planes. The study also demonstrated compression at the craniocervical junction, the anatomical basis for the patient's brainstem dysfunction.

The degree of tonsillar descent correlates imperfectly with symptom severity, and current understanding of CMI recognizes a spectrum from asymptomatic anatomical variants to clinically significant malformations requiring intervention. The clinical significance of any given tonsillar descent must be interpreted in the context of symptoms, neurological findings, and evidence of functional impairment—precisely the integrative approach that this case required. The MRI findings alone would not have been sufficient to establish a causal relationship with the patient's respiratory and swallowing symptoms; it was the synthesis of imaging with the polysomnographic and clinical data that solidified the diagnosis.

### Videofluoroscopic Swallowing Study and Neurogenic Dysphagia

A videofluoroscopic swallowing study, also known as a modified barium swallow, was performed to characterize the dysphagia. This study uses fluoroscopic imaging to observe the oral, pharyngeal, and oesophageal phases of swallowing in real time, allowing assessment of bolus transit, timing, aspiration, and structural or functional abnormalities. The findings in this patient were consistent with a neurogenic pattern of dysphagia.

The key clinical observation that pointed toward a neurogenic mechanism was the equal difficulty with solids and liquids. Mechanical dysphagia, caused by structural obstruction such as strictures or rings, typically produces greater difficulty with solids than liquids. In contrast, neurogenic dysphagia—resulting from impaired coordination of the complex neuromuscular sequence of swallowing—affects all consistencies equally because the problem lies in the control of the swallowing apparatus rather than in the calibre of the passage. In CMI, compression of lower cranial nerve nuclei (glossopharyngeal, vagus, and hypoglossal nerves) at the brainstem disrupts the coordinated pharyngeal and laryngeal motor programmes essential for safe swallowing, producing exactly this pattern. The observation that this patient's most distressing symptom was investigated repeatedly from a gastroenterological and otolaryngological perspective over years, with normal findings each time, highlights the clinical aphorism that dysphagia without a demonstrable structural cause should prompt evaluation for neurological disease.

### Sleep Study: Central Event Predominance and Its Interpretation

A type III sleep study was performed and demonstrated an apnoea–hypopnoea index (AHI) of 6.0, which technically categorizes as mild sleep-disordered breathing (an AHI of 5–15 events per hour is classified as mild). However, the composition of these events was striking: 72% were central in origin, meaning that they involved a cessation of respiratory effort rather than an obstructive mechanical cause. This predominance of central events is the key finding and the strongest sleep-related evidence for brainstem-mediated respiratory dysfunction.

Central sleep apnoea arises from a failure of the brainstem respiratory control centres to initiate or maintain respiratory effort during sleep. In this patient, the pathophysiological mechanism is compression of medullary respiratory neurons by the herniated cerebellar tonsils. The brainstem houses the dorsal respiratory group in the nucleus tractus solitarius and the ventral respiratory group in the nucleus ambiguus and retroambiguus, which together generate the rhythmic pattern of breathing. Mechanical compression of these structures can abolish the automatic respiratory rhythm during sleep, when cortical voluntary drive to breathe is absent. This explains why the patient's desaturation episodes occurred predominantly during sleep and during moments of distraction—the voluntary, cortically driven breathing that she could summon while alert was sufficient to maintain gas exchange, but when cortical attention was withdrawn, the brainstem's automatic ventilatory drive was inadequate.

The marked discrepancy between the patient's dramatic clinical desaturation episodes (to 70%–80%) and the relatively mild AHI of 6.0 requires explanation and illustrates important methodological considerations in sleep medicine.

### Limitations of Type III Polysomnography

The sleep study performed was a type III study, which relies on respiratory effort belts, airflow sensors, pulse oximetry, and heart rate monitoring. It does not include electroencephalography (EEG), which is required to determine sleep stages and accurately identify periods of sleep versus wakefulness during the recording. This distinction is critical because the AHI is defined as the number of apnoea and hypopnoea events divided by total sleep time. Without EEG, the true sleep time cannot be determined, and the denominator becomes the total recording time instead. Because total recording time includes periods of wakefulness during which respiratory events are less frequent, the calculated AHI using total recording time will systematically underestimate the true AHI based on actual sleep time. In a patient who preserved voluntary breathing while awake but lost automatic ventilatory drive during sleep, this underestimation would be particularly pronounced: her wakeful periods in the sleep laboratory, during which respiratory drive was maintained, would dilute the event count.

Type I polysomnography, the gold standard for sleep-disordered breathing evaluation, includes full EEG, electro-oculography, chin electromyography for sleep staging, along with respiratory effort, airflow, oximetry, end-tidal capnography, and video recording. This comprehensive approach would allow accurate calculation of AHI based on true sleep time, identification of sleep architecture abnormalities, and correlation of respiratory events with specific sleep stages. The case authors arranged for a type I study at an external sleep centre, which was pending at the time of publication. Until that study is completed, the diagnosis of central hypoventilation remains a well-supported clinical hypothesis rather than a definitively confirmed polysomnographic finding. The authors appropriately acknowledged this limitation.

Despite the methodological constraints, several converging features supported the clinical diagnosis: the predominance of central events (72%) despite the low overall AHI, the documented desaturations to 70%–80% during sleep and distraction without patient awareness, and the anatomical evidence of significant brainstem compression (11 mm tonsillar descent). The clinical picture was internally consistent even if the polysomnographic quantification was imprecise.

### Laboratory Investigations as Exclusions

A comprehensive laboratory panel was performed to exclude systemic and autoimmune causes that could contribute to the symptom complex. The metabolic panel, thyroid function tests, vitamin B12 levels, and inflammatory markers were all normal. As noted above, acetylcholine receptor antibodies and anti-MuSK antibodies were negative, excluding the principal serological forms of myasthenia gravis. While these investigations did not positively contribute to the diagnosis, they were essential in the systematic exclusion of alternative explanations and in establishing that the patient's symptoms were not attributable to a systemic metabolic, endocrine, or autoimmune process.

### Synthesis: Assembling the Unifying Diagnosis

The diagnosis in this case was not established by any single investigation but by the convergence of multiple lines of evidence. The historical CT scan provided the first anatomical clue. The MRI confirmed the structural abnormality. The videofluoroscopic swallowing study demonstrated a neurogenic pattern of dysphagia consistent with lower cranial nerve dysfunction. The sleep study demonstrated central event predominance consistent with brainstem respiratory control impairment. The pulmonary evaluation excluded intrinsic lung disease and demonstrated normal gas exchange during voluntary wakeful breathing. The clinical history provided the temporal narrative linking the motor vehicle accident to the onset of symptoms and documented the distinctive phenomenon of asymptomatic desaturation—oxygen levels falling to dangerous levels without the patient experiencing dyspnoea, a finding that pointed specifically to dysfunction of the brainstem afferent pathways mediating the conscious sensation of air hunger.

This synthesis is the essence of diagnostic reasoning in complex presentations: no single test was definitive, and several tests were either normal (laryngoscopy, endoscopy, pH studies, chest imaging, pulmonary function, arterial blood gas) or limited in their sensitivity (type III sleep study). The diagnosis emerged from integrating what was present (central sleep events, tonsillar descent, neurogenic dysphagia, asymptomatic desaturation) with what was absent (reflux, myasthenia, intrinsic lung disease, systemic illness), and from recognizing that the normal results themselves were diagnostically informative—they redirected attention away from the organ systems being investigated toward the central nervous system.
