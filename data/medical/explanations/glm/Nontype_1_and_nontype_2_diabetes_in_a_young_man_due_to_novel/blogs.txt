Title: Blog about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

# Neither Type 1 Nor Type 2: When to Suspect MODY in Young-Onset Diabetes

## The Setup

A young adult walks into his annual health exam. He's got glycosuria, an HbA1c of 10.2%, fasting glucose of 12.4 mmol/L, and he's been losing weight with increased thirst and appetite. Your reflexive thought? Probably type 1 diabetes — he's young, symptomatic, and presenting with marked hyperglycemia. Or maybe early-onset type 2, given the BMI of 24.6 and the triglycerides of 4.31 mmol/L. Either way, you'd be wrong. This patient had a novel heterozygous mutation in HNF1A — a never-before-reported variant causing HNF1A-MODY, the most common form of monogenic diabetes. And the diagnosis didn't just satisfy academic curiosity — it fundamentally changed the treatment plan, allowing insulin to be discontinued entirely.

## The Diagnostic Dilemma That Won't Go Away

Here's the uncomfortable truth: MODY accounts for an estimated 1–6% of young-onset diabetes, and it is almost certainly underdiagnosed. The clinical features overlap so heavily with type 1 and type 2 that most patients get lumped into one of those two buckets and never get genetic testing. This case is a textbook example of why that happens — and why it matters.

The patient presented with symptoms we typically associate with type 1 diabetes: weight loss, polydipsia, polyphagia, and a markedly elevated HbA1c. But he also had a BMI in the overweight range, a lipid profile suggestive of possible metabolic syndrome, and no acanthosis nigricans. His autoantibodies (GADA and IA-2A) were negative. His stimulated C-peptide was low but preserved at 0.83 nmol/L. None of these findings individually clinches the diagnosis, but together they form a pattern that should make you pause before reaching for the insulin or metformin prescription pad.

## The Family History Is the loudest clue

The single most striking feature of this case is the family history: maternal grandmother diagnosed with diabetes in her 30s, mother in her late 20s, and a younger brother at age 20. That's three successive generations with early-onset diabetes — a classic autosomal dominant inheritance pattern. This is the kind of family history that should immediately trigger the question: "Is this really type 1 or type 2, or is this monogenic?"

Unfortunately, family history can be harder to interpret than it sounds. As the authors note, under-reporting, de novo mutations, and the high background prevalence of type 2 diabetes in the population can all muddy the waters. Still, when you see diabetes tracking through multiple generations with early onset and without the typical metabolic syndrome phenotype, MODY should be on your differential.

## The Clinical Approach: What Should Make You Suspect MODY?

The diagnostic algorithm, as illustrated by this case, comes down to a few key features that, taken together, build a compelling case for genetic testing.

First, early onset — MODY typically manifests before age 25, though as this case shows, it can be picked up later if screening is opportunistic. Second, negative islet autoantibodies — GADA, IA-2A, and ZnT8 should all be checked, and if they're negative, your suspicion for MODY should rise significantly. Third, preserved C-peptide — a stimulated C-peptide above 0.60 nmol/L suggests residual beta-cell function that is more consistent with MODY or type 2 than with classic type 1 diabetes, though early type 1 can still show preserved C-peptide initially, so serial monitoring matters. Fourth, the absence of insulin resistance markers — no acanthosis nigricans, no significant obesity — though as the authors rightly point out, rising global obesity rates are making BMI and metabolic syndrome less reliable discriminators than they used to be. And fifth, that multi-generational family history of early-onset diabetes.

The Exeter group's MODY probability calculator can help quantify suspicion, but as the authors note, it was derived from White European populations and may overestimate MODY probability in non-White groups. In this case, it returned a 62.4% probability — suggestive, but not definitive. That's why genetic testing via next-generation sequencing panels remains the gold standard for confirmation.

## Why the Diagnosis Changes Everything

Here's where this case really drives home the point. The patient was initially treated with basal insulin degludec, metformin, and saxagliptin — a reasonable empiric approach for a young patient with severe hyperglycemia where the diagnosis isn't yet clear. But once genetic testing confirmed HNF1A-MODY, insulin was discontinued and replaced with the GLP-1 receptor agonist dulaglutide. The result? HbA1c dropped from 10.2% to 6.0%, and the regimen was ultimately simplified to metformin and a DPP-4 inhibitor for maintenance.

This isn't just about avoiding injections. The treatment implications are specific to the molecular subtype. HNF1A-MODY classically responds to low-dose sulfonylureas as first-line therapy, but GLP-1 receptor agonists are emerging as a safer alternative with less hypoglycemia risk — a small randomized crossover trial showed equivalent glycemic control with liraglutide versus glimepiride in HNF1A-MODY patients, with fewer hypoglycemic events. And critically, SGLT-2 inhibitors should be used with extreme caution or avoided entirely in HNF1A-MODY, because HNF1A mutations already reduce SGLT2 expression in the renal tubules — adding an SGLT-2 inhibitor on top of that can precipitate severe dehydration and even diabetic ketoacidosis. That's a drug-class adverse effect you would never anticipate without knowing the molecular diagnosis.

The novel variant in this case — p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G) in exon 4 — was absent from ClinVar and VarSome, highlighting how much of the MODY genetic landscape remains uncharted. It was classified as likely pathogenic based on ACMG criteria, including a high AlphaMissense score, its location in the DNA-binding domain adjacent to known pathogenic residues, and segregation with diabetes across the family. As NGS becomes more accessible, we will undoubtedly uncover more variants like this, expanding the known spectrum of MODY mutations and refining our understanding of genotype-phenotype correlations.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Suspect MODY in any patient with early-onset diabetes, negative islet autoantibodies, preserved C-peptide, and a multi-generational family history of early-onset diabetes.** No single feature is diagnostic — it's the pattern that matters.
- **BMI is becoming a less reliable discriminator.** As obesity rates rise globally, MODY patients can overlap with type 2 diabetes phenotypically. Don't let a normal or elevated BMI talk you out of genetic testing when the rest of the picture fits.
- **Genetic testing is not academic — it changes treatment.** HNF1A-MODY patients can often transition off insulin onto oral agents or GLP-1 receptor agonists. SGLT-2 inhibitors may be dangerous in HNF1A-MODY due to intrinsic reduced SGLT2 expression.
- **GLP-1 receptor agonists are a viable alternative to sulfonylureas in HNF1A-MODY**, with comparable glycemic control and less hypoglycemia risk.
- **Next-generation sequencing panels are the diagnostic standard.** Novel variants like the one in this case won't appear in existing databases — pathogenicity assessment requires integration of computational predictions, segregation data, and clinical context using ACMG guidelines.
- **MODY is underdiagnosed.** If you're not thinking about it, you won't test for it — and your patient will be stuck on the wrong treatment plan indefinitely.

## The Bottom Line

This case isn't remarkable because the patient had an unusual presentation — it's remarkable precisely because the presentation was so ordinary. Young, symptomatic, elevated HbA1c, family history of diabetes — every clinic sees this. The difference here was that someone thought to ask whether this was really type 1 or type 2, ordered the genetic test, and then had the courage to stop insulin once the answer came back. In the era of precision medicine, the question isn't whether we can diagnose MODY — it's whether we remember to look for it.



# When the Database Comes Up Empty: Making Sense of Novel Genetic Variants in MODY

## The Case That Shouldn't Have Been MODY — Except It Was

A young adult walks into clinic with an HbA1c of 10.2%, fasting glucose of 12.4 mmol/L, glycosuria, and a family history that reads like a pedigree diagram: maternal grandmother diagnosed in her 30s, mother in her late 20s, younger brother at age 20. He's not obese (BMI 24.6), has no acanthosis nigricans, and his diabetic autoantibodies (GADA, IA-2A) come back negative. His glucagon stimulation test shows a stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L — preserved, but insufficient. You're thinking MODY, and you'd be right. But here's where it gets interesting: when the genetic panel comes back, it identifies a heterozygous HNF1A variant — p.N270S (c.809A>G) — that has never been reported anywhere. Not in ClinVar. Not in VarSome. Not in the published literature. So now you're sitting on a variant of unknown significance in a gene you're pretty sure is the culprit, and you have to make a clinical decision. Do you treat it as pathogenic? Do you wait? Do you dismiss it?

This case, reported by Liu and colleagues, is a masterclass in how to navigate that exact scenario — and it's a scenario that's going to become increasingly common as next-generation sequencing (NGS) panels proliferate and uncover variants faster than databases can curate them.

## The ACMG Framework: Not Just a Checkbox Exercise

The American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) guidelines for variant interpretation give us a structured way to classify variants as pathogenic, likely pathogenic, uncertain significance, likely benign, or benign. The key insight from this case is that novelty ≠ uncertainty. A variant's absence from databases says something about how much we've looked, not about whether it causes disease.

In this case, three independent lines of evidence were used to classify p.N270S as likely pathogenic. First, the variant affects an amino acid residue adjacent to known pathogenic residues in the HNF1A DNA-binding domain — a region where the majority of pathogenic MODY mutations cluster. Second, the AlphaMissense score was 0.995, indicating an extremely high computational probability that this substitution is deleterious to protein function. Third, the variant segregated with diabetes across three generations in the patient's family, consistent with the autosomal dominant inheritance pattern characteristic of HNF1A-MODY. Taken together, these met ACMG criteria for a likely pathogenic classification — even though no database had ever seen this variant before.

This is the critical conceptual leap: the absence of a variant from ClinVar or VarSome is not evidence of benignity. It may simply reflect that the populations represented in those databases — overwhelmingly White European — have been studied far more extensively than others. As the authors note, the MODY probability calculator from the Exeter group was derived from White European data and may overestimate MODY probability in non-White groups, but the deeper problem is that the variant databases themselves are skewed. Clinicians and geneticists working in underrepresented populations must be prepared to rely on functional prediction tools, structural biology reasoning, and segregation data when the databases fall short.

## From Genetic Diagnosis to Precision Treatment

What makes this case especially compelling is that the genetic diagnosis didn't just satisfy academic curiosity — it changed management. The patient was initially treated as type 2 diabetes, with basal insulin degludec, metformin, and saxagliptin. After one month, his HbA1c dropped from 10.2% to 9.1%. But once the HNF1A-MODY diagnosis was confirmed, insulin was discontinued and replaced with the GLP-1 receptor agonist dulaglutide. Within six months, his HbA1c plummeted to 6.0%, and the regimen was ultimately simplified to metformin plus a DPP-4 inhibitor for maintenance, with HbA1c consistently held below 6.5%.

This transition matters. HNF1A-MODY patients classically respond to low-dose sulfonylureas, but as β-cell function declines, GLP-1 receptor agonists offer a safer alternative with lower hypoglycaemia risk — a point supported by a small randomised crossover trial comparing liraglutide to glimepiride in HNF1A-MODY. There's also an important caution embedded in the molecular pathophysiology: HNF1A mutations reduce SGLT2 expression in renal tubules, which explains the glycosuria seen at presentation. Using SGLT2 inhibitors in these patients could push residual SGLT2 activity even lower, risking severe dehydration, vascular collapse, and diabetic ketoacidosis. The risks likely outweigh any glycaemic benefit. That's a contraindication you'd never know about without the genetic diagnosis.

## The NGS Revolution and the Expanding Mutational Landscape

Over 1,000 distinct HNF1A variants have been catalogued in ClinVar to date, spanning pathogenic, likely pathogenic, benign, and uncertain significance categories. Most pathogenic mutations cluster in the DNA-binding domain — which is exactly where this novel variant sits. The case report notes that some pathogenic HNF1A mutations have minimal functional impact on the protein in vitro, and that some variants insufficient to cause MODY alone may still modify T2D risk. This biological nuance is why computational tools like AlphaMissense, combined with segregation analysis, are indispensable when databases offer no precedent.

NGS panels make it feasible to screen large numbers of genes — over 15 MODY-associated genes are now recognised — in a single reaction. This is how novel variants are discovered and how the mutational spectrum expands. The ACMG framework gives us the tools to classify them. But the real-world bottleneck is clinical suspicion. MODY accounts for 1–6% of young-onset diabetes and is frequently misdiagnosed as type 1 or type 2. A study of young Chinese adults with early-onset T2D found a MODY prevalence of 1.5%, with 0.74% carrying pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants. A Russian study screening patients with hereditary diabetes features identified pathogenic variants in 46.4%, with HNF1A among the most commonly affected genes. These numbers suggest we are systematically missing these patients.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Novelty is not benignity.** A variant absent from ClinVar, VarSome, and the literature can still be classified as likely pathogenic using the ACMG framework. Multiple independent lines of evidence — functional prediction scores, structural context, and family segregation — can substitute for database precedent.

- **Database bias is real.** Variant databases like ClinVar are heavily skewed toward well-studied populations, primarily White European. Clinicians in underrepresented populations must be prepared to lean on computational tools (e.g., AlphaMissense) and segregation data when databases offer no precedent.

- **Genetic diagnosis changes treatment.** In this case, confirming HNF1A-MODY allowed discontinuation of insulin in favour of a GLP-1 receptor agonist, with excellent glycaemic outcomes. It also identified SGLT2 inhibitors as a potentially dangerous drug class for this patient.

- **Keep MODY on your differential.** Early-onset diabetes, negative autoantibodies, preserved C-peptide, and a strong family history of multi-generational diabetes should trigger genetic testing — even when the phenotype overlaps with type 1 or type 2 diabetes.

- **NGS panels are the future of MODY diagnosis.** With over 15 MODY-associated genes and continuously expanding variant databases, NGS allows comprehensive screening in a single reaction, making genetic diagnosis increasingly feasible and clinically actionable.

## Bottom Line

This case is a reminder that precision medicine isn't just about having the right test — it's about knowing how to interpret what the test gives you, especially when it gives you something no one has seen before. The ACMG framework exists precisely for these moments. When the database comes up empty, your job isn't done. It's just getting started.



# From Insulin to GLP-1: Precision Therapeutics in HNF1A-MODY

## The Case That Stops You in Your Tracks

A young adult walks into his annual physical. Routine labs show hyperglycemia and glycosuria. His HbA1c comes back at 10.2%. His fasting glucose is 12.4 mmol/L. He's lost weight, is thirsty all the time, and has polyphagia. You're thinking type 1 diabetes, maybe type 2 — except his BMI is 24.6, there's no acanthosis nigricans, and oh yeah, his mother was diagnosed with diabetes in her late 20s, his maternal grandmother in her 30s, and his younger brother at age 20. Three generations of early-onset diabetes. No autoantibodies. Moderately preserved C-peptide. This isn't type 1. It probably isn't classic type 2 either. This is MODY — specifically, HNF1A-MODY, caused by a novel mutation never before reported in any database. And here's where it gets interesting from a therapeutics standpoint: the genetic diagnosis didn't just satisfy academic curiosity — it fundamentally changed the treatment plan, allowed discontinuation of insulin, and got this patient to an HbA1c of 6.0% within months.

## The Diagnostic Dilemma

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young accounts for roughly 1–6% of young-onset diabetes cases, and HNF1A-MODY is the most common subtype, representing 30–65% of all MODY. Yet it remains vastly underdiagnosed. The clinical features overlap with both type 1 and type 2 diabetes, and in an era of rising obesity, you can no longer rely on a thin phenotype to clue you in. This patient had a BMI of 24.6, a triglyceride level of 4.31 mmol/L, and an HDL of 1.01 mmol/L — features suggesting possible metabolic syndrome. If you had used BMI alone to rule out MODY, you would have been wrong.

The key diagnostic clues here were the multi-generational family history of early-onset diabetes, negative islet autoantibodies (GADA and IA-2A), and a glucagon stimulation test showing a stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L — above the 0.60 nmol/L threshold suggesting preserved endogenous insulin secretion. The Exeter MODY probability calculator estimated a 62.4% probability of MODY for this patient, though it's worth noting this tool was derived from White European populations and may overestimate probability in other ethnic groups. Next-generation sequencing confirmed a heterozygous pathogenic variant in HNF1A: p.N270S (c.809A>G), located in the DNA-binding domain. This variant was absent from ClinVar, VarSome, and all published literature — a genuinely novel mutation, supported by a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), familial segregation with diabetes, and proximity to known pathogenic residues.

## The Treatment Pivot: Where Precision Medicine Meets the Bedside

Here's where the case becomes a practical lesson in therapeutics. At diagnosis, before genetic results were available, the team started empirically: basal insulin degludec (8 units/day), metformin (1500 mg/day), and saxagliptin (5 mg/day). Reasonable enough — you're staring at an HbA1c of 10.2% and you need control fast. One month in, the HbA1c dropped to 9.1%. But then the genetic confirmation came back, and the clinical reasoning shifted.

The team discontinued insulin entirely and initiated dulaglutide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist, at 1.5 mg weekly, continuing metformin. Within 6 months, the HbA1c dropped to 6.0%. The regimen was eventually simplified to metformin plus the DPP-4 inhibitor saxagliptin for long-term maintenance, with HbA1c consistently held below 6.5%. No microalbuminuria, no retinopathy, no signs of progressive complications on follow-up.

This transition deserves scrutiny. Why move away from insulin? Why a GLP-1 receptor agonist? And what's the evidence base?

## Evidence-Based Therapeutics in HNF1A-MODY

Traditional first-line therapy for HNF1A-MODY is low-dose sulfonylureas, which enhance insulin secretion by acting on ATP-sensitive potassium channels in beta cells. This approach has been validated over years of clinical experience and reflects the underlying pathophysiology — HNF1A mutations cause progressive beta-cell dysfunction with impaired insulin secretion, but the beta cells remain responsive to sulfonylurea stimulation. Many patients with HNF1A-MODY can be successfully managed on sulfonylureas for years before requiring insulin.

However, sulfonylureas carry a real hypoglycemia risk, and as beta-cell function declines over time, their effectiveness wanes while hypoglycemia risk persists or increases. This is where GLP-1 receptor agonists enter the conversation. A small randomized, double-blind, crossover trial demonstrated that liraglutide was as effective as glimepiride in reducing fasting plasma glucose and postprandial glucose fluctuations in HNF1A-MODY patients, with a notably lower risk of mild hypoglycemia. The mechanism makes sense — GLP-1 receptor agonists enhance glucose-dependent insulin secretion, meaning their effect is self-limiting at lower glucose levels, providing an intrinsic safeguard against hypoglycemia that sulfonylureas lack.

In this case, the patient's stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L indicated moderately preserved but insufficient beta-cell function — the sweet spot where a GLP-1 receptor agonist can augment endogenous insulin secretion without the need for exogenous insulin. The clinical decision-making process weighed three factors: residual beta-cell function (assessed via stimulated C-peptide), treatment burden (daily insulin injections versus weekly GLP-1 RA), and hypoglycemia risk. The result was a regimen that was not only more convenient but achieved superior glycemic control.

Metformin was maintained as adjunctive therapy throughout, which is appropriate given the patient's lipid profile suggesting some metabolic overlap, and because metformin's insulin-sensitizing effects complement the insulinotropic action of GLP-1 receptor agonists without additive hypoglycemia risk.

One critical pharmacologic caveat from the discussion: SGLT-2 inhibitors should be used with extreme caution, if at all, in HNF1A-MODY. HNF1A transcriptionally controls SGLT2 expression in the renal tubules, and patients with HNF1A-MODY already have reduced SGLT2 activity and glycosuria at lower glucose thresholds. Adding an SGLT-2 inhibitor on top of this can precipitate severe dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis. The risks — vascular collapse, genital infections, euglycemic DKA — likely outweigh any glycemic benefit in this specific population. This is a drug class that is broadly safe in type 2 diabetes but potentially dangerous in a monogenic form that looks like type 2 diabetes. If there's a stronger argument for genetic diagnosis before pharmacologic commitment, I haven't seen it.

## The Precision Medicine Lesson

This case is a concrete example of precision medicine in action — not as an abstract concept, but as a clinical decision that directly altered a patient's treatment trajectory. Molecular diagnosis informed the pharmacologic choice, reduced unnecessary treatment burden (daily insulin injections eliminated), potentially preserved endogenous beta-cell function by avoiding unnecessary exogenous insulin, and achieved a clinical outcome that was superior to the empiric regimen. The patient went from an HbA1c of 10.2% on insulin + metformin + DPP-4 inhibitor to 6.0% on a GLP-1 receptor agonist + metformin, and then maintained below 6.5% on metformin + DPP-4 inhibitor alone.

The broader lesson is that MODY is not rare — it is underdiagnosed. Approximately 1.5% of young Chinese adults with early-onset type 2 diabetes in one study carried pathogenic or likely pathogenic MODY variants. In a Danish study, 10% of patients with type 1 diabetes who lacked high-risk HLA haplotypes harbored HNF1A mutations. A Russian population-based study found pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in 46.4% of individuals suspected of having hereditary diabetes after autoimmune features were excluded. These numbers suggest that we are missing MODY cases routinely by not screening, and every missed case is a patient potentially on the wrong medication — sometimes for decades.

## Key Take-Home Points

- **Think MODY when:** early-onset diabetes with multi-generational family history, negative islet autoantibodies, and preserved C-peptide (stimulated >0.60 nmol/L). Don't let a normal or slightly elevated BMI talk you out of it — obesity rates in MODY are rising and BMI is no longer a reliable discriminator.

- **Genetic confirmation changes management.** In this case, it allowed discontinuation of insulin and transition to a GLP-1 receptor agonist, with superior glycemic outcomes. Without the genetic diagnosis, this patient might have remained on insulin indefinitely.

- **GLP-1 receptor agonists are a viable alternative to sulfonylureas in HNF1A-MODY.** Evidence supports equivalent glycemic efficacy with lower hypoglycemia risk, particularly as beta-cell function declines. The decision should be individualized based on residual C-peptide response, hypoglycemia risk, and treatment burden.

- **Avoid SGLT-2 inhibitors in HNF1A-MODY.** These patients already have reduced SGLT2 activity from the underlying transcription factor defect. Pharmacologic inhibition can precipitate severe dehydration and DKA. This is a precision medicine contraindication — safe in T2DM, dangerous in HNF1A-MODY.

- **Low-dose sulfonylureas remain the traditional first-line for HNF1A-MODY**, but GLP-1 receptor agonists are emerging as a safer alternative with growing evidence base. Stimulated C-peptide can help guide the choice — preserved beta-cell function supports GLP-1 RA use; severely depleted function may necessitate insulin.

- **MODY is underdiagnosed across populations.** Screening rates remain low, and clinical features overlap substantially with type 1 and type 2 diabetes. NGS-based MODY panels are increasingly accessible and can identify novel variants, as in this case, that standard databases do not yet contain. The absence of a variant in ClinVar does not mean it isn't pathogenic — ACMG guidelines integrate multiple lines of evidence including computational predictions, segregation data, and functional domain context.

- **Precision medicine isn't theoretical.** In this case, a single genetic result changed the prescription, reduced treatment burden, improved glycemic control, and spared a young patient years of unnecessary insulin therapy. That is the practical value of getting the diagnosis right.



# The SGLT-2 Trap: Why a Popular Drug Class May Be Dangerous in HNF1A-MODY

## The Setup: When the Best-Selling Drug Class Meets a Rare Diagnosis

SGLT-2 inhibitors are everywhere. Empagliflozin, dapagliflozin, canagliflozin — these drugs have become darlings of modern diabetology, with cardiovascular and renal benefits that extend well beyond glycemic control. Emergency physicians and hospitalists see them prescribed routinely, and we're increasingly familiar with their signature complication: euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis. But what if there were a subset of diabetes patients in whom SGLT-2 inhibitors weren't just occasionally dangerous, but *structurally* contraindicated — because the very gene causing their diabetes already cripples the renal transporter these drugs target?

A recent case report out of Taiwan describes a young man with HNF1A-MODY who presented with hyperglycemia and glycosuria on routine screening, a family history spanning three generations of early-onset diabetes, and an HbA1c of 10.2%. His care team ultimately identified a *novel* HNF1A mutation — but the pharmacologic decision-making in this case carries a lesson that every clinician prescribing diabetes medications should internalize: **SGLT-2 inhibitors may be uniquely dangerous in HNF1A-MODY, and we should think hard before reaching for them in any young-onset diabetes patient without a confirmed genetic diagnosis.**

## The Molecular Trap: HNF1A Already Does What SGLT-2 Inhibitors Do

Here's where the biology gets elegant — and dangerous. HNF1A (hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha) is a transcription factor expressed in the liver, kidneys, and pancreas. In the kidney, HNF1A directly controls transcription of the *SGLT2* gene, which encodes the sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 responsible for reabsorbing the vast majority of filtered glucose in the proximal tubule.

When HNF1A is mutated, SGLT2 expression falls. The renal threshold for glucose reabsorption drops. Patients develop glycosuria *at lower plasma glucose levels than normal* — and indeed, glycosuria is one of the classic clinical features of HNF1A-MODY, often detectable even when blood glucose isn't dramatically elevated. The patient in this case presented with glycosuria on routine screening.

Now add an SGLT-2 inhibitor on top of that. You're pharmacologically inhibiting a transporter that is *already* functionally impaired by the genetic defect. The result is excessive glucosuria — far beyond what you'd see in a typical T2DM patient on the same drug — with correspondingly greater risks of volume depletion, severe dehydration, and ketogenesis.

The authors of this case report reference a published report of HNF1A-MODY patients who developed **severe dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis** due to high inhibition of residual SGLT2 activity. The risks — dehydration, vascular collapse, genital infections, and DKA — may outright outweigh any glycemic benefit in this population.

## A Precision Pharmacology Paradigm

This drug-gene interaction is a textbook example of precision medicine in action. SGLT-2 inhibitors are broadly beneficial across type 2 diabetes, heart failure, and chronic kidney disease. But in HNF1A-MODY — which accounts for 30%–65% of all MODY cases — the pathophysiology of the disease itself makes this drug class a potential hazard. The same mechanism that makes glycosuria a diagnostic clue in HNF1A-MODY makes pharmacologic intensification of that glycosuria dangerous.

It's worth noting that the clinical team in this case avoided SGLT-2 inhibitors entirely. Their patient was initially treated with basal insulin, metformin, and a DPP-4 inhibitor while awaiting genetic results. Once HNF1A-MODY was confirmed, insulin was discontinued and replaced with the GLP-1 receptor agonist dulaglutide, which brought HbA1c from 9.1% down to 6.0% within six months. The patient was then maintained on metformin plus saxagliptin. GLP-1 receptor agonists represent a safer alternative for HNF1A-MODY patients, with trial data showing comparable efficacy to sulfonylureas (the traditional first-line agent) but with lower hypoglycemia risk.

## The Diagnostic Problem: MODY Hiding in Plain Sight

The reason this pharmacologic trap matters in the emergency and inpatient setting is that HNF1A-MODY is frequently misdiagnosed as type 1 or type 2 diabetes. Estimates suggest MODY accounts for 1%–6% of young-onset diabetes, but this is likely underestimated because genetic screening is typically reserved for patients who meet narrow clinical criteria — and those criteria were largely derived from White European populations.

Consider this patient's presentation: a young adult with hyperglycemia, weight loss, polydipsia, and polyphagia. He had triglycerides of 4.31 mmol/L and a BMI of 24.6. His maternal grandmother was diagnosed with diabetes in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at age 20. Negative autoantibodies (GADA and IA-2A) and a stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L on glucagon stimulation testing suggested non-type 1 diabetes. The Exeter MODY probability calculator estimated a 62.4% chance of MODY — but even this tool may overestimate probability in non-White populations.

In the emergency department or on admission, if this patient had been labeled as "type 2 diabetes, poorly controlled" and started on an SGLT-2 inhibitor — which is entirely consistent with current guidelines for T2DM — the consequences could have been severe.

## Practical Guidance: What to Do in the ED and on the Wards

Here's what this means for those of us managing hyperglycemic patients in the acute setting. When you encounter a young patient with new-onset hyperglycemia, particularly with a multigenerational family history of early-onset diabetes, think about MODY before reaching for the standard T2DM toolkit.

Check diabetes-related autoantibodies if available — GADA, IA-2A, and ZnT8. Assess C-peptide (a stimulated level >0.60 nmol/L suggests preserved beta-cell function, inconsistent with classic type 1). Look for the clinical signatures of HNF1A-MODY: glycosuria out of proportion to plasma glucose, strong autosomal dominant family history, onset before age 25, and absence of significant insulin resistance features like acanthosis nigricans. This patient had *none* of the latter — and his glycosuria was a presenting feature.

Until MODY has been genetically excluded in a young-onset diabetes patient with suggestive features, **avoid SGLT-2 inhibitors**. This doesn't mean every 25-year-old with newly diagnosed T2DM needs a genetic panel before starting medications, but in patients with a strong family history of early-onset diabetes across multiple generations, negative autoantibodies, and preserved C-peptide, the pretest probability of MODY is high enough that the risk-benefit calculus for SGLT-2 inhibitors shifts.

- **HNF1A mutations reduce renal SGLT2 transcription**, lowering the threshold for glucose reabsorption and causing glycosuria — this is a *diagnostic feature* of HNF1A-MODY, not just a lab curiosity.

# C-Peptide and the Glucagon Stimulation Test: Reclaiming a Forgotten Diagnostic Tool in Diabetes Subtyping

## The Case That Makes You Rethink "Straightforward" Diabetes

Imagine a young adult presenting with polydipsia, polyphagia, recent weight loss, and a staggering HbA1c of 10.2%. Your reflex diagnosis? Likely Type 1 diabetes, perhaps a kick of ketones, and a one-way ticket to an insulin pump clinic. But what happens when the clinical picture starts fraying at the edges? No DKA. A relatively lean BMI of 24.6 kg/m². No acanthosis nigricans. And a family history that reads like a generational curse: maternal grandmother diagnosed in her 30s, mother in her late 20s, and a younger brother at age 20. 

This is the exact scenario presented in a fascinating case report detailing a patient with a novel HNF1A mutation causing Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY). But before the next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel ever revealed the novel p.N270S variant, the clinical team utilized a classic, often-forgotten diagnostic tool to steer the ship away from a lifetime of exogenous insulin: the glucagon stimulation test (GST) and C-peptide measurement. 

## The Physiology of C-Peptide: More Than Just a Byproduct

To appreciate the diagnostic power of this case, we have to rewind to pancreatic beta-cell physiology. When proinsulin is cleaved to form active insulin, a byproduct is produced: C-peptide. Unlike insulin, which is rapidly cleared by the liver (making serum insulin levels a poor proxy for actual beta-cell secretion), C-peptide is cleared by the kidneys and has a longer, more stable half-life. This makes C-peptide the gold standard marker for endogenous insulin secretion. 

In the evaluation of ambiguous young-onset diabetes, a preserved fasting C-peptide level >0.6 ng/mL strongly argues against classic Type 1 diabetes. However, the authors of this case report highlight a critical pearl: normal C-peptide values can be observed in the early stages of Type 1 diabetes. A single snapshot is dangerous. Longitudinal monitoring is essential because the progressive autoimmune destruction of beta cells in Type 1 diabetes takes time, and residual function can persist longer than we traditionally teach.

## The Glucagon Stimulation Test (GST)

If fasting C-peptide can be misleadingly normal in early Type 1 diabetes, how do we stress-test the beta cell to see what it's actually capable of? Enter the glucagon stimulation test. 

The GST evaluates residual beta-cell function by dynamically challenging the pancreas. Glucagon is administered, and C-peptide is measured at baseline and at 6 minutes. Glucagon acts directly on the beta cell to trigger maximal insulin release. In clinical practice, a stimulated C-peptide level >0.60 nmol/L generally indicates preserved endogenous insulin secretion, whereas lower levels suggest significant beta-cell dysfunction. 

In this case, the patient’s baseline C-peptide was 0.39 nmol/L, but at 6 minutes post-glucagon, it rose to 0.83 nmol/L. He had crossed the threshold. He had moderately preserved, yet clearly insufficient, beta-cell function. 

## A Supportive Tool, Not a Definitive One

The authors are careful to emphasize that the GST should be regarded as supportive rather than definitive. Why? Because clinical overlap is a harsh reality in medicine. While a stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L argues against acute, complete beta-cell failure, it doesn't magically rule out early Type 1 diabetes. It also doesn't distinguish between MODY and slowly progressive Type 1 diabetes (LADA), or even insulin-deficient Type 2 diabetes.

The true power of the GST is realized when it is paired with other clinical data. In this patient, the moderately preserved stimulated C-peptide was interpreted alongside two other monumental clues: negative islet autoantibodies (GADA and IA-2A) and a dominant-pattern family history of early-onset diabetes. 

When you combine a positive GST, negative autoantibodies, and a multigenerational family history, the mathematical probability of MODY skyrockets. The Exeter group's MODY probability calculator—though derived from White European populations and noted to potentially overestimate risk in other demographics—calculated a 62.4% probability of MODY for this patient. That was more than enough justification to send the NGS panel.

## From Diagnosis to Precision Treatment

Here is where the integration of C-peptide data shifts from a diagnostic thought exercise to a life-changing treatment decision. 

Initially, facing an HbA1c of 10.2%, the team did the pragmatic thing: they started basal insulin (degludec 8 units/day) alongside metformin and saxagliptin to achieve rapid glycemic control. But one month later, as the HbA1c dropped to 9.1%, the genetic confirmation of HNF1A-MODY returned. 

The team now had a critical decision to make. Could they safely pull the patient off insulin? The glucagon stimulation test provided the safety net. Because the patient had demonstrable, moderately preserved beta-cell function (stimulated C-peptide 0.83 nmol/L), the team knew his pancreas wasn't dead. It just needed the right pharmacologic nudge.

Insulin was discontinued. The patient was transitioned to dulaglutide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist. This choice is deeply rooted in MODY literature: GLP-1 receptor agonists can serve as a safer and more effective alternative to sulfonylureas as beta-cell function declines, offering the same glycemic control but with a significantly lower risk of hypoglycemia. 

The result? Within 6 months, the patient's HbA1c plummeted from 9.1% to 6.0%. The regimen was eventually simplified to just metformin and a DPP-4 inhibitor, with HbA1c consistently maintained below 6.5% on long-term follow-up. 

## The Take-Home

C-peptide assessment is an underutilized, high-yield tool in the trenches of ambiguous young-onset diabetes. It is relatively cheap, fast, and provides dynamic data that a standard fasting glucose panel simply cannot match. 

- **Interpret C-peptide in context:** A preserved fasting C-peptide >0.6 ng/mL argues against Type 1 diabetes, but early Type 1 can show normal values initially. Don't anchor on a single lab value; monitor longitudinally.
- **The GST is supportive, not definitive:** A stimulated C-peptide >0.60 nmol/L proves beta cells are still in the fight, but you still need autoantibody testing and family history to build the full diagnostic picture.
- **C-peptide can guide de-escalation:** Demonstrating preserved beta-cell function via GST gives you the confidence to safely transition a patient off exogenous insulin once genetic testing confirms MODY.
- **GLP-1 RAs over sulfonylureas for HNF1A-MODY:** As beta-cell function declines, GLP-1 receptor agonists offer effective glycemic control with a lower risk of hypoglycemia compared to sulfonylureas. (And remember: avoid SGLT-2 inhibitors in HNF1A-MODY due to the underlying reduced renal threshold for glucose and increased risk of euglycemic DKA).
- **When in doubt, calculate:** Use the Exeter MODY probability calculator. A high score, combined with supportive C-peptide data, is your green light for genetic testing. Precision medicine only works if we use the tools we have to figure out who needs it.



# The Exeter MODY Calculator: A Useful Tool With a Population Problem

## A Young Man, a Strong Family History, and a Calculator That Said "Probably MODY"

A young adult walks into your clinic with an HbA1c of 10.2%, fasting glucose of 12.4 mmol/L, glycosuria, and a family history that reads like a generational curse: maternal grandmother diagnosed with diabetes in her 30s, mother in her late 20s, younger brother at age 20. No acanthosis nigricans, BMI of 24.6, negative GADA and IA-2A antibodies, and a stimulated C-peptide that is low but preserved at 0.83 nmol/L. You plug the clinical data into the Exeter MODY probability calculator, and it spits back a 62.4% probability — roughly a 1 in 1.6 chance. That is a compelling number. It certainly nudged the treating team toward genetic testing, which ultimately confirmed a novel heterozygous HNF1A variant (p.N270S, c.809A>G) and locked in a diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY.

But here is the wrinkle that should give every clinician pause: the very tool that helped flag this case was built almost entirely on White European data, and its performance in other populations is, to put it generously, unvalidated. This is not a knock on the Exeter group — their calculator is one of the most practically useful clinical decision aids in the monogenic diabetes space. It is a recognition that even excellent tools carry the biases of the populations they were trained on, and when we export them to genetically and phenotypically diverse settings, we need to interpret their outputs with appropriate humility.

## What the Exeter Calculator Actually Does

The MODY probability calculator, developed by the Exeter group, takes readily available clinical parameters — age at diagnosis, family history of diabetes, HbA1c at diagnosis, presence of islet autoantibodies, C-peptide levels, and features like BMI and insulin use — and generates an estimated probability that a given patient has MODY rather than type 1 or type 2 diabetes. It is accessible online, free, and designed to help clinicians decide who actually warrants expensive molecular genetic testing, which is the only way to definitively confirm a MODY diagnosis.

The logic is sound: MODY accounts for only an estimated 1%–6% of young-onset diabetes, and genetic testing is not cheap or universally available, so a pre-test probability tool serves as a critical gatekeeper. The problem is that the underlying model was derived and validated primarily in White European cohorts. When the authors of this case report entered their patient's clinical information, the calculator returned that 62.4% probability. On its face, that seems reassuringly high — and in this instance, the patient did indeed have MODY. But the authors themselves flag the concern: the tool may overestimate MODY probability in non-White groups because it was not calibrated using population-specific prevalence data or phenotypic distributions from those populations.

## Why Population Calibration Matters

Clinical prediction tools are not population-agnostic. The posterior probability that a patient has a condition depends critically on the pre-test probability, which is itself a function of disease prevalence in that population. If the baseline prevalence of MODY in a given ethnic group differs from the prevalence in the derivation cohort, the calculator's output will be miscalibrated — potentially substantially so. The Exeter calculator encodes not just the clinical features associated with MODY but also the relative prevalence of MODY versus type 1 and type 2 diabetes in the population from which its training data were drawn. Apply that same mathematical machinery to a population with a different prevalence of young-onset type 2 diabetes, different distributions of autoantibody positivity, or different familial clustering patterns of diabetes, and the resulting probability estimate may drift meaningfully from reality.

This is not a theoretical concern. The case report references several studies that underscore how much MODY prevalence and detection rates vary across populations. A study by Chen et al found that among 2,429 young Chinese adults with early-onset type 2 diabetes, the prevalence of MODY was 1.5%, with only 0.74% carrying pathogenic or likely pathogenic MODY variants. Møller et al demonstrated that in a Danish Caucasian population of patients with type 1 diabetes, 10% of 39 patients without high-risk HLA haplotypes harbored HNF1A mutations. A Russian population-based study screened 506 individuals suspected of having hereditary diabetes based on negative autoantibodies, preserved C-peptide, low insulin requirements, and absence of severe obesity — and identified pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in 46.4%, with HNF1A among the most commonly affected genes. These are dramatically different yields, reflecting differences in both true prevalence and selection criteria. A one-size-fits-all calculator cannot capture that variability.

## The Broader Problem: Algorithmic Export Without Validation

What we are really talking about here is a specific instance of a pervasive problem in clinical medicine: prediction models developed in one population are routinely applied to others without revalidation. We see this with cardiovascular risk scores, with sepsis prediction tools, with fracture risk calculators. The mathematical relationships between clinical features and outcomes may be broadly similar across populations, but the calibration — the mapping of a predicted probability to an actual observed frequency — frequently breaks down when you cross ethnic, geographic, or even temporal boundaries.

In the MODY context, the stakes are real. An overestimated MODY probability in a non-White population could lead to unnecessary genetic testing, misallocation of limited healthcare resources, and potential anxiety for patients and families told they "probably" have a monogenic disorder when the true pre-test probability in their population is considerably lower. Conversely, an underestimated probability — if, say, a population has a higher MODY prevalence than the derivation cohort — could mean missed diagnoses, with patients inappropriately placed on insulin when they might respond to sulfonylureas or GLP-1 receptor agonists, or vice versa.

## Back to the Case: Why the Diagnosis Still Mattered Enormously

Despite the calculator's limitations, the clinical suspicion in this case was well-founded. The autosomal dominant family history across three generations, the negative autoantibodies, the preserved-but-insufficient C-peptide, and the absence of classical type 2 features all pointed toward MODY independent of any algorithmic output. The genetic confirmation — a novel HNF1A variant in the DNA-binding domain, absent from ClinVar and VarSome but meeting ACMG criteria for likely pathogenic based on adjacency to known pathogenic residues, a high AlphaMissense score of 0.995, and family segregation — enabled a precision medicine approach that fundamentally changed management.

The patient was initially treated with basal insulin, metformin, and saxagliptin, reflecting a presumptive type 2 diabetes approach before genetic results were available. Once HNF1A-MODY was confirmed, insulin was discontinued and replaced with the GLP-1 receptor agonist dulaglutide, consistent with emerging evidence that GLP-1 receptor agonists can serve as an effective and safer alternative to sulfonylureas in HNF1A-MODY as beta-cell function declines, with lower hypoglycaemia risk. HbA1c plummeted from 10.2% to 6.0% within six months, and the regimen was subsequently simplified to metformin and saxagliptin for maintenance. That is a treatment trajectory directly enabled by a genetic diagnosis — not by a calculator estimate, but by the molecular confirmation that the calculator helped justify pursuing.

Notably, the case also reinforces a critical pharmacogenomic point: SGLT-2 inhibitors should be approached with caution in HNF1A-MODY. HNF1A transcriptionally controls SGLT2 expression in the renal tubules, and patients with HNF1A mutations already have reduced SGLT2 activity and lower renal glucose thresholds — explaining the glycosuria that can be an early clue to the diagnosis. Pharmacologically inhibiting SGLT2 in this population carries heightened risks of severe dehydration, euglycaemic diabetic ketoacidosis, and vascular collapse, as reported in the literature. The risks likely outweigh any glycaemic benefit.

## Where Do We Go From Here?

The Exeter MODY calculator remains a valuable starting point. It is accessible, transparent, and grounded in real data. It helps clinicians structure their thinking and provides a quantitative scaffold for what is otherwise an intuitive judgment call. The authors of this case report did exactly what you would want a thoughtful clinical team to do: they used the calculator as one input among several, weighed it against the broader clinical picture, and proceeded to genetic testing — which confirmed the diagnosis and transformed the treatment plan.

But the case also serves as a reminder that clinical prediction tools are not portable across populations without scrutiny. The calculator's 62.4% estimate may have been directionally correct in this instance, but we cannot assume it is calibrated for a Taiwanese patient, a Chinese cohort, a Russian cohort, or any other population that differs from its derivation sample. What we need are population-specific prevalence studies, locally validated prediction models, and clinician awareness that the number on the screen is a probability estimate conditioned on assumptions that may not hold for the patient sitting in front of you. The future of MODY diagnosis — and of precision medicine more broadly — depends not just on better sequencing technology but on prediction tools that reflect the full genetic and phenotypic diversity of the populations we actually serve.

## Key Take-Home Points

- The Exeter MODY probability calculator is a useful clinical decision aid but was derived and validated primarily in White European populations; its calibration in other ethnic groups is unknown and may overestimate or underestimate true MODY probability.
- Population-specific MODY prevalence data are essential for accurate pre-test probability estimation. Studies in Chinese (1.5% prevalence among young-onset T2D), Russian (46.4% yield in selected suspected hereditary diabetes cases), and Danish populations demonstrate markedly different detection rates.
- Despite the calculator's limitations, clinical features alone — autosomal dominant family history, negative autoantibodies, preserved C-peptide, absence of insulin resistance features — can and should drive the decision to pursue genetic testing.
- Genetic confirmation of HNF1A-MODY can fundamentally change management: this patient was transitioned from insulin to a GLP-1 receptor agonist with excellent glycaemic outcomes (HbA1c 10.2% → 6.0% in six months).
- Avoid SGLT-2 inhibitors in HNF1A-MODY: these patients already have reduced SGLT2 activity due to impaired HNF1A transcriptional control, and pharmacological inhibition increases the risk of severe dehydration, diabetic ketoacidosis, and vascular collapse.
- Novel HNF1A variants absent from standard databases can still be clinically pathogenic; ACMG framework analysis incorporating adjacency to known pathogenic residues, computational pathogenicity scores, and family segregation data is essential for interpretation.
- The future of MODY diagnosis requires population-calibrated prediction models, broader NGS screening strategies, and clinician awareness of the limitations of tools developed in narrow populations.
