Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

Chapter 3: ## HNF1A-MODY Pathophysiology and the Novel Mutation

Understanding how a single gene mutation produces diabetes requires examining the molecular role of the affected protein. The *HNF1A* gene is located on the q24 region of chromosome 12 and spans 23,790 base pairs across 10 exons. It encodes a 631-amino-acid protein expressed in the liver, kidneys, and pancreas. In the pancreas, HNF1A functions as a transcription factor that regulates genes essential for beta-cell development and function. When HNF1A is mutated, these downstream genes are not properly transcribed, producing a progressive decline in beta-cell function. Specifically, the mutant HNF1A protein impairs expression of the insulin gene and the GLUT2 glucose transporter gene, and it reduces pyruvate kinase activity. Together, these molecular defects diminish the beta cell's ability to sense glucose and secrete insulin in response, leading to decreased insulin secretion and progressive glucose intolerance. This mechanism distinguishes HNF1A-MODY from type 1 diabetes, where autoimmune destruction eliminates beta cells, and from type 2 diabetes, where insulin resistance is the primary defect. In this patient, the glucagon stimulation test demonstrated a stimulated C-peptide level of 0.83 nmol/L—above the 0.60 nmol/L threshold suggesting preserved endogenous insulin secretion, yet insufficient for normal glycaemic control. This finding of partially preserved but inadequate beta-cell function is consistent with the progressive secretory failure that characterises HNF1A-MODY.

A distinctive clinical feature of HNF1A-MODY illustrated by this patient is glycosuria, detected on his routine health examination. In HNF1A-MODY, glycosuria is not simply a consequence of hyperglycaemia exceeding the renal threshold. Rather, HNF1A directly controls transcription of the SGLT2 gene, which encodes the sodium-glucose cotransporter responsible for the majority of glucose reabsorption in the renal proximal tubule. When HNF1A function is impaired, SGLT2 expression is reduced, lowering the renal threshold for glucose reabsorption and causing urinary glucose excretion at lower blood glucose levels than would otherwise occur. This mechanism also has therapeutic implications: SGLT2 inhibitors are potentially hazardous in HNF1A-MODY patients, as further inhibition of already compromised SGLT2 activity may precipitate severe dehydration and even diabetic ketoacidosis, as noted in prior case reports.

The patient's genetic testing revealed a heterozygous variant designated p.N270S (*HNF1A*:c.809A>G) in exon 4. This substitution replaces asparagine with serine at position 270 of the protein. The variant was not reported in the literature, the VarSome database, or the ClinVar database at the time of analysis, establishing it as a novel variant. Evaluating a previously unreported variant requires a systematic framework, and the clinicians applied the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines for variant interpretation. Under these guidelines, multiple lines of evidence supported classifying the variant as likely pathogenic. First, the variant affects an amino acid residue adjacent to previously established pathogenic residues, suggesting that position 270 resides in a region of the protein critical for function. Second, the variant carries a high AlphaMissense score of 0.995, indicating a high computational probability that the amino acid substitution is deleterious. Third, the variant segregates with diabetes in the family: the maternal grandmother, mother, and younger brother all had early-onset diabetes, consistent with autosomal dominant transmission. Together, these criteria satisfied ACMG standards for a likely pathogenic classification despite the variant's absence from major databases.

The location of this novel variant within the protein's functional architecture further informs its pathogenicity. The HNF1A protein contains two key functional regions: a DNA-binding domain and a transactivation domain. Studies have shown that the majority of pathogenic HNF1A mutations cluster in the DNA-binding domain, while the transactivation domain has a lower mutation rate. The p.N270S variant identified in this patient is situated in the DNA-binding domain, which is consistent with the overall distribution pattern of pathogenic mutations. This location is mechanistically meaningful because defects in the DNA-binding domain directly impair the transcription factor's ability to bind target gene promoters, disrupting transcription of insulin, GLUT2, pyruvate kinase, and SGLT2.

The identification of this novel variant also highlights an important distinction in HNF1A genetics. Not all HNF1A variants are equally pathogenic. Over 1,000 distinct HNF1A variants have been catalogued in databases such as ClinVar, with classifications ranging from pathogenic and likely pathogenic to benign, likely benign, and variants of uncertain significance. Some studies have shown that certain HNF1A mutations, while classified as pathogenic, have minimal effects on protein function, and some variants that are insufficient to cause MODY independently may instead be associated with an increased risk of type 2 diabetes. Distinguishing a truly pathogenic MODY-causing variant from a risk allele for type 2 diabetes requires integration of genetic evidence, functional domain analysis, computational prediction, and family segregation data—precisely the approach taken in this case. The discovery of this novel variant expands the known spectrum of HNF1A mutations relevant to precision medicine, as each new pathogenic variant improves the sensitivity of genetic screening panels and the accuracy of future variant classification.
