Title: Blog about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

# When the Database Comes Up Empty: Making Sense of Novel Genetic Variants in MODY

## The Case That Shouldn't Have Been MODY — Except It Was

A young adult walks into clinic with an HbA1c of 10.2%, fasting glucose of 12.4 mmol/L, glycosuria, and a family history that reads like a pedigree diagram: maternal grandmother diagnosed in her 30s, mother in her late 20s, younger brother at age 20. He's not obese (BMI 24.6), has no acanthosis nigricans, and his diabetic autoantibodies (GADA, IA-2A) come back negative. His glucagon stimulation test shows a stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L — preserved, but insufficient. You're thinking MODY, and you'd be right. But here's where it gets interesting: when the genetic panel comes back, it identifies a heterozygous HNF1A variant — p.N270S (c.809A>G) — that has never been reported anywhere. Not in ClinVar. Not in VarSome. Not in the published literature. So now you're sitting on a variant of unknown significance in a gene you're pretty sure is the culprit, and you have to make a clinical decision. Do you treat it as pathogenic? Do you wait? Do you dismiss it?

This case, reported by Liu and colleagues, is a masterclass in how to navigate that exact scenario — and it's a scenario that's going to become increasingly common as next-generation sequencing (NGS) panels proliferate and uncover variants faster than databases can curate them.

## The ACMG Framework: Not Just a Checkbox Exercise

The American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) guidelines for variant interpretation give us a structured way to classify variants as pathogenic, likely pathogenic, uncertain significance, likely benign, or benign. The key insight from this case is that novelty ≠ uncertainty. A variant's absence from databases says something about how much we've looked, not about whether it causes disease.

In this case, three independent lines of evidence were used to classify p.N270S as likely pathogenic. First, the variant affects an amino acid residue adjacent to known pathogenic residues in the HNF1A DNA-binding domain — a region where the majority of pathogenic MODY mutations cluster. Second, the AlphaMissense score was 0.995, indicating an extremely high computational probability that this substitution is deleterious to protein function. Third, the variant segregated with diabetes across three generations in the patient's family, consistent with the autosomal dominant inheritance pattern characteristic of HNF1A-MODY. Taken together, these met ACMG criteria for a likely pathogenic classification — even though no database had ever seen this variant before.

This is the critical conceptual leap: the absence of a variant from ClinVar or VarSome is not evidence of benignity. It may simply reflect that the populations represented in those databases — overwhelmingly White European — have been studied far more extensively than others. As the authors note, the MODY probability calculator from the Exeter group was derived from White European data and may overestimate MODY probability in non-White groups, but the deeper problem is that the variant databases themselves are skewed. Clinicians and geneticists working in underrepresented populations must be prepared to rely on functional prediction tools, structural biology reasoning, and segregation data when the databases fall short.

## From Genetic Diagnosis to Precision Treatment

What makes this case especially compelling is that the genetic diagnosis didn't just satisfy academic curiosity — it changed management. The patient was initially treated as type 2 diabetes, with basal insulin degludec, metformin, and saxagliptin. After one month, his HbA1c dropped from 10.2% to 9.1%. But once the HNF1A-MODY diagnosis was confirmed, insulin was discontinued and replaced with the GLP-1 receptor agonist dulaglutide. Within six months, his HbA1c plummeted to 6.0%, and the regimen was ultimately simplified to metformin plus a DPP-4 inhibitor for maintenance, with HbA1c consistently held below 6.5%.

This transition matters. HNF1A-MODY patients classically respond to low-dose sulfonylureas, but as β-cell function declines, GLP-1 receptor agonists offer a safer alternative with lower hypoglycaemia risk — a point supported by a small randomised crossover trial comparing liraglutide to glimepiride in HNF1A-MODY. There's also an important caution embedded in the molecular pathophysiology: HNF1A mutations reduce SGLT2 expression in renal tubules, which explains the glycosuria seen at presentation. Using SGLT2 inhibitors in these patients could push residual SGLT2 activity even lower, risking severe dehydration, vascular collapse, and diabetic ketoacidosis. The risks likely outweigh any glycaemic benefit. That's a contraindication you'd never know about without the genetic diagnosis.

## The NGS Revolution and the Expanding Mutational Landscape

Over 1,000 distinct HNF1A variants have been catalogued in ClinVar to date, spanning pathogenic, likely pathogenic, benign, and uncertain significance categories. Most pathogenic mutations cluster in the DNA-binding domain — which is exactly where this novel variant sits. The case report notes that some pathogenic HNF1A mutations have minimal functional impact on the protein in vitro, and that some variants insufficient to cause MODY alone may still modify T2D risk. This biological nuance is why computational tools like AlphaMissense, combined with segregation analysis, are indispensable when databases offer no precedent.

NGS panels make it feasible to screen large numbers of genes — over 15 MODY-associated genes are now recognised — in a single reaction. This is how novel variants are discovered and how the mutational spectrum expands. The ACMG framework gives us the tools to classify them. But the real-world bottleneck is clinical suspicion. MODY accounts for 1–6% of young-onset diabetes and is frequently misdiagnosed as type 1 or type 2. A study of young Chinese adults with early-onset T2D found a MODY prevalence of 1.5%, with 0.74% carrying pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants. A Russian study screening patients with hereditary diabetes features identified pathogenic variants in 46.4%, with HNF1A among the most commonly affected genes. These numbers suggest we are systematically missing these patients.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Novelty is not benignity.** A variant absent from ClinVar, VarSome, and the literature can still be classified as likely pathogenic using the ACMG framework. Multiple independent lines of evidence — functional prediction scores, structural context, and family segregation — can substitute for database precedent.

- **Database bias is real.** Variant databases like ClinVar are heavily skewed toward well-studied populations, primarily White European. Clinicians in underrepresented populations must be prepared to lean on computational tools (e.g., AlphaMissense) and segregation data when databases offer no precedent.

- **Genetic diagnosis changes treatment.** In this case, confirming HNF1A-MODY allowed discontinuation of insulin in favour of a GLP-1 receptor agonist, with excellent glycaemic outcomes. It also identified SGLT2 inhibitors as a potentially dangerous drug class for this patient.

- **Keep MODY on your differential.** Early-onset diabetes, negative autoantibodies, preserved C-peptide, and a strong family history of multi-generational diabetes should trigger genetic testing — even when the phenotype overlaps with type 1 or type 2 diabetes.

- **NGS panels are the future of MODY diagnosis.** With over 15 MODY-associated genes and continuously expanding variant databases, NGS allows comprehensive screening in a single reaction, making genetic diagnosis increasingly feasible and clinically actionable.

## Bottom Line

This case is a reminder that precision medicine isn't just about having the right test — it's about knowing how to interpret what the test gives you, especially when it gives you something no one has seen before. The ACMG framework exists precisely for these moments. When the database comes up empty, your job isn't done. It's just getting started.
