Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Pathophysiology] SGLT2 inhibitors are generally avoided in patients with HNF1A-MODY. What is the pathophysiologic rationale for this caution, and what specific clinical complications have been described in this population?

## Pathophysiologic Rationale for Avoiding SGLT2 Inhibitors in HNF1A-MODY

HNF1A encodes a transcription factor expressed in the liver, kidneys, and pancreas that directly regulates the expression of the sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) in the renal proximal tubule. In HNF1A-MODY, loss-of-function mutations in HNF1A lead to **reduced SGLT2 expression** at the basolateral membrane of renal tubular cells. This is precisely why patients with HNF1A-MODY characteristically exhibit **glycosuria at lower plasma glucose concentrations** — they already have a reduced renal threshold for glucose reabsorption, a distinguishing clinical feature illustrated in the present case, where glycosuria was detected on routine screening without ketoacidosis.

The concern with prescribing an SGLT2 inhibitor in this population is that pharmacologic inhibition would further suppress the **already diminished residual SGLT2 activity**, producing near-complete blockade of renal glucose reabsorption. As noted in the case report's discussion, this can result in **severe dehydration, diabetic ketoacidosis, potential vascular collapse, and genital infections** — complications described in HNF1A-MODY patients given SGLT2 inhibitors. The volume depletion and ketoacidosis risk reflects the compounding effect of pharmacologic inhibition on a genetically impaired tubular transport system, where the physiologic reserve for glucose reabsorption is already compromised.

This stands in contrast to the treatment approach successfully employed in the present case. After genetic confirmation of the novel HNF1A variant (p.N270S), insulin was discontinued and the patient was transitioned to a GLP-1 receptor agonist (dulaglutide) with metformin, achieving an HbA1c reduction from 10.2% to 6.0%. This strategy leverages the preserved — though insufficient — β-cell function characteristic of HNF1A-MODY without imposing additional stress on the renal glucose transport pathway. The case underscores that **precision medicine in MODY extends beyond establishing a genetic diagnosis to selecting therapies compatible with the underlying molecular pathophysiology**, and that SGLT2 inhibitors represent a class whose mechanism directly conflicts with the renal tubular defect inherent to HNF1A-MODY.
