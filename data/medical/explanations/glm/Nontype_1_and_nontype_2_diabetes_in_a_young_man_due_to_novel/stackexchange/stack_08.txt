Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Management] After genetic confirmation of HNF1A-MODY, insulin was discontinued and replaced with dulaglutide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist. What clinical trial evidence supports the use of GLP-1 receptor agonists in HNF1A-MODY, and what advantages do they offer over sulfonylureas?

## Clinical Teaching Answer

The key trial evidence cited in this case report is a **small, randomised, double-blind, crossover trial** comparing the GLP-1 receptor agonist **liraglutide** with the sulfonylurea **glimepiride** in patients with genetically confirmed HNF1A-MODY. That trial demonstrated that both agents effectively reduced fasting plasma glucose and postprandial glucose fluctuations, with **no significant difference in glycaemic efficacy** between the two treatments. However, GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy was associated with a **lower risk of mild hypoglycaemia** compared with glimepiride.

This distinction matters because sulfonylureas have long been the first-line therapy for HNF1A-MODY, acting on ATP-sensitive potassium channels to enhance residual β-cell insulin secretion. While effective, sulfonylureas carry a dose-dependent hypoglycaemia risk that becomes increasingly problematic as β-cell function progressively declines—a hallmark of the natural history of HNF1A-MODY, which results from impaired insulin and GLUT2 gene expression and reduced pyruvate kinase activity. GLP-1 receptor agonists offer a mechanistically distinct approach: they potentiate glucose-dependent insulin secretion, meaning their glycaemic effect wanes as circulating glucose falls, thereby providing a built-in safeguard against hypoglycaemia. The trial data suggest that this safety advantage can be obtained without sacrificing glycaemic control.

In the present case, these principles were applied directly. After genetic confirmation of the novel HNF1A variant and recognition that the patient had moderately preserved but insufficient β-cell function (stimulated C-peptide 0.83 nmol/L), insulin was discontinued and dulaglutide was initiated. HbA1c fell from 9.1% to 6.0% within 6 months, and the regimen was subsequently simplified to metformin and a DPP-4 inhibitor for long-term maintenance. This outcome illustrates the practical value of the trial evidence: GLP-1 receptor agonists can serve as a safer and equally effective alternative to sulfonylureas, particularly in patients prone to hypoglycaemia or in those whose β-cell reserve is already compromised. It also underscores how genetic diagnosis enables precision medicine—transitioning this young patient away from lifelong insulin therapy toward an oral and injectable non-insulin regimen better suited to his underlying pathophysiology.
