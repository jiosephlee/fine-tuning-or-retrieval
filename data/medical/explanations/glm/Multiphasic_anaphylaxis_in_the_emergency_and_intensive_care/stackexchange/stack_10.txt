Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Pathophysiology] By what mechanisms do mast cell mediators such as histamine, tryptase, and prostaglandins produce the multisystem manifestations of anaphylaxis, and how does persistent mast cell activation in systemic mastocytosis contribute to both acute severity and risk of recurrence?

## Mechanisms of Mast Cell Mediators in Anaphylaxis and the Role of Systemic Mastocytosis

Mast cell degranulation in anaphylaxis releases a constellation of preformed and newly synthesized mediators that act on multiple organ systems simultaneously, producing the characteristic multisystem phenotype. Understanding these mechanisms is essential for appreciating why anaphylaxis can be both acutely severe and prone to recurrence, particularly in the setting of an underlying mast cell disorder.

**Histamine**, the most abundant preformed mediator stored in mast cell granules, acts primarily through H1 and H2 receptors distributed across vascular endothelium, bronchial smooth muscle, and cardiac tissue. H1 receptor activation produces vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, bronchoconstriction, coronary artery spasm, and pruritus — accounting for the flushing, urticaria, angioedema, hypotension, and dyspnea observed in our patient. H2 receptor stimulation contributes additionally to vasodilation, myocardial irritability, and increased gastric acid secretion. The hypotension (74/50 mm Hg) and hypoxemia (88% on room air) at initial presentation, along with generalized erythema and urticaria, are hallmark manifestations of histamine's rapid endothelial and smooth muscle effects.

**Tryptase**, a serine protease stored exclusively in mast cell granules, serves as both a mediator and a diagnostic biomarker. While its precise pathophysiologic contributions — including complement activation and metalloprotease stimulation — are less clinically prominent than those of histamine, its release kinetics make it invaluable for confirming mast cell degranulation. Critically, baseline tryptase elevation suggests an underlying mast cell burden disorder. In this case, both event-related and baseline tryptase levels were elevated, supporting a high probability of systemic mastocytosis despite the patient not meeting formal WHO diagnostic criteria (only two minor criteria fulfilled, with the major criterion not assessable due to limited bone marrow biopsy material).

**Prostaglandins**, particularly PGD2, are newly synthesized mediators generated through arachidonic acid metabolism via the cyclooxygenase pathway following IgE-mediated mast cell activation. PGD2 is a potent bronchoconstrictor and vasodilator that contributes to airway narrowing, hypotension, and flushing. These lipid mediators are synthesized de novo after cell activation, meaning their effects may persist beyond the initial histamine burst, contributing to sustained or recurrent symptom phases.

**Systemic mastocytosis and the risk of multiphasic recurrence.** In systemic mastocytosis, there is clonal expansion of mast cells with excessive, often constitutive, mediator release and multiorgan inflammation. The elevated baseline mast cell burden creates a substantially larger reservoir of preformed mediators available for release upon triggering. When an allergen such as clarithromycin cross-links IgE on this expanded mast cell population, the magnitude of mediator release is proportionally greater, explaining why systemic mastocytosis predisposes patients to severe anaphylaxis. This patient's four discrete anaphylactic episodes — each requiring intramuscular epinephrine — despite discontinuation of the trigger after a single 500 mg dose, illustrate how an expanded and persistently activatable mast cell population can generate recurrent waves of mediator release without re-exposure. The gradually diminishing intensity of each successive episode may reflect progressive depletion of preformed mediator stores, though ongoing de novo synthesis of lipid mediators and continued mast cell reactivation could sustain further relapses if the stimulus were not fully cleared or the mast cell population were sufficiently large.

This pathophysiologic framework also explains why standard adjunctive therapies such as antihistamines and corticosteroids may be insufficient in this context: antihistamines block only histamine receptor–mediated effects while leaving prostaglandin, leukotriene, and tryptase pathways unaddressed, and corticosteroids — whose benefit in preventing biphasic reactions is increasingly questioned — act too slowly to interrupt acute mast cell degranulation. Epinephrine remains the cornerstone because it directly reverses vasodilation and bronchoconstriction via alpha- and beta-adrenergic receptor agonism while also stabilizing mast cells through beta-2 receptor–mediated inhibition of further degranulation. The possibility of underlying systemic mastocytosis should be actively investigated in any patient presenting with multiphasic anaphylaxis, as it carries direct implications for long-term management, including baseline tryptase monitoring, avoidance counseling, consideration of mast cell stabilizing agents, and specialist referral.
