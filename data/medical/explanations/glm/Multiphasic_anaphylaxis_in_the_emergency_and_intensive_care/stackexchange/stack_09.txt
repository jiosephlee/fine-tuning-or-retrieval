Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Management] After an initial anaphylactic reaction resolves, what observation period is traditionally recommended, and how does the UK 2021 guideline's three-tier risk-stratified approach attempt to individualize the observation duration?

**Observation After Anaphylaxis: Traditional Approach and the UK 2021 Risk-Stratified Framework**

Traditionally, an observation period of **4 to 6 hours** after full symptom resolution has been recommended following an anaphylactic episode. This practice stems from the recognition that approximately half of biphasic reactions occur within 6–12 hours of the initial event, and the 4–6 hour window is intended to capture the majority of early recurrences while balancing healthcare resource utilization. Notably, several guidelines—including those from the Dutch NHG and the World Allergy Organization—acknowledge this timing of biphasic recurrence but stop short of providing specific monitoring recommendations.

The **Resuscitation Council UK 2021 guideline** moves beyond this one-size-fits-all approach by proposing a **three-tier, risk-stratified observation strategy**. Rather than applying a uniform observation period to all patients, this framework seeks to individualize monitoring duration based on the patient's estimated risk of a biphasic reaction. Patients deemed **low risk**—for example, those with rapid and complete response to a single dose of epinephrine and no concerning features—may be suitable for shorter observation or even discharge with safety-netting advice. Those at **moderate risk** warrant a more prolonged observation period, typically in the range of 6–12 hours. Patients at **high risk**—characterized by features such as severe initial presentation requiring multiple epinephrine doses, delayed epinephrine administration, history of biphasic anaphylaxis, or limited access to emergency care—justify extended observation, potentially up to 24 hours or beyond.

This case powerfully illustrates the clinical relevance of such stratification. The patient experienced **four separate anaphylactic episodes within 12 hours**, each requiring intramuscular epinephrine, despite initial full clinical resolution between episodes. She was appropriately escalated to the ICU after the first recurrence, and only after approximately **24 hours without renewed symptoms** was she transferred back to a standard ward. Her course exemplifies the high-risk category: severe initial presentation with hypotension and hypoxemia, requirement for multiple epinephrine doses, and an underlying predisposing condition (high probability of systemic mastocytosis with persistently elevated tryptase). A fixed 4–6 hour observation would have been insufficient, as her relapses occurred well beyond that window.

The UK 2021 approach represents a necessary evolution from blanket observation protocols toward individualized risk assessment, acknowledging that the vast majority of anaphylaxis cases are single-episode events while ensuring that the small but clinically critical subset of patients at risk for biphasic or multiphasic courses receive appropriately extended monitoring. As this case demonstrates, the absence of reliable predictors for recurrence—beyond broad risk factors—remains a challenge, and further research, potentially through case registries, will be essential to refine risk stratification and optimize resource allocation.
