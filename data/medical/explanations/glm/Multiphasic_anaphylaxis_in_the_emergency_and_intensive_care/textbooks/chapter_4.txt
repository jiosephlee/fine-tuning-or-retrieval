Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

Chapter 4: ## Patient Outcome, Discharge, and Long-term Management

After experiencing four distinct anaphylactic episodes, each requiring intramuscular epinephrine, the patient's condition stabilized without further symptom recurrence. The treating team, in consultation with the allergology department, had considered two management strategies: initiating a continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion to pre-empt subsequent episodes or adopting an expectant approach, closely observing for spontaneous resolution. Ultimately, neither strategy was necessary, as the anaphylactic episodes resolved spontaneously. The intensity of each successive episode had been progressively decreasing, from severe hypoxaemia with cyanosis and hypotension during the initial presentation to isolated dyspnoea, rash, and pruritus during the final recurrence. Following approximately 24 hours without renewed symptoms, the patient was step-down transferred from the intensive care unit (ICU) back to a standard care ward. This clinical course highlights the variable natural history of multiphasic anaphylaxis and the challenge clinicians face in deciding when to transition from active treatment to expectant observation.

The patient was ultimately discharged from the hospital after a 3-day admission. A central pillar of her discharge planning was the strict avoidance of the offending agent, clarithromycin. No confirmatory allergy testing, such as skin prick testing or oral challenge, was performed. Given the severity of the initial anaphylactic reaction and the relative ease with which clarithromycin can be avoided in the future, the risk of provoking another severe reaction through testing was deemed disproportionate to the benefit. While confirming the allergy through testing is often useful for medical record documentation and cross-reactivity assessment, clinical practice often favors strict avoidance when the causative agent is clearly identified and the reaction is severe. The patient was also advised to avoid beta-lactam antibiotics, given her known prior allergy.

Discharge pharmacotherapy focused on both symptomatic control and emergency preparedness. She was started on levocetirizine, a second-generation H1-antihistamine, as a new daily medication. While antihistamines are not a substitute for epinephrine in acute anaphylaxis, daily antihistamine therapy can help suppress cutaneous symptoms and reduce baseline mast cell mediator activity, particularly in patients with suspected mast cell disorders. More importantly, she was prescribed an epinephrine auto-injector containing 0.3 mg of epinephrine. This reflects the hospital’s preference for this dosage formulation, as auto-injectors are essential for prompt pre-hospital treatment of future episodes. It is important to note that at 104 kg, this dose may represent an underdose for her weight; current guidelines recommend 0.01 mg/kg (maximum 0.5 mg for adults). Therefore, while the 0.3 mg auto-injector is practical and readily available, clinicians must be aware of the potential for underdosing in larger patients and consider prescribing a higher dose or providing clear instructions to administer two doses if necessary.

The transition to outpatient care required comprehensive patient education. The patient was taught how to recognize the early signs and symptoms of anaphylaxis—such as pruritus, flushing, throat tightness, and dyspnoea—and instructed on the proper use of the epinephrine auto-injector. For patients who have experienced a life-threatening allergic reaction, understanding that epinephrine is the first-line treatment and that antihistamines should only be used as adjunctive therapy for cutaneous symptoms is critical. Patient education also emphasized the need to seek immediate medical attention after auto-injector use, given the risk of biphasic or multiphasic recurrence, as demonstrated by this case.

Long-term management involved outpatient follow-up with the allergology department. Due to the atypical and prolonged clinical course of this patient’s anaphylaxis, further investigation into underlying causes was initiated. This analysis revealed a high probability of systemic mastocytosis, although the findings did not meet formal WHO diagnostic criteria. Specifically, only two minor criteria were met, and the major criterion could not be assessed due to limited material obtained in the bone marrow biopsy. Systemic mastocytosis is characterized by an abnormal increase in mast cells, excessive mediator release, and multiorgan inflammation, which predisposes patients to severe anaphylaxis. Further supporting this suspicion were findings of hepatomegaly on abdominal ultrasound and possible neuropsychiatric symptoms, including depression and memory problems. A key diagnostic marker followed during her long-term care was the serum tryptase level. Tryptase levels during the third anaphylactic episode, as well as baseline levels measured after resolution, were elevated and remained increased during outpatient follow-up. Unfortunately, tryptase levels were not measured during the initial episodes, which deviates from recommended guidelines that advise obtaining samples at specific intervals (initial, 1–2 hours, and ≥24 hours post-resolution) to compare peak levels with baseline. This highlights the importance of adherence to diagnostic guidelines during acute anaphylaxis workup, even in busy clinical settings. After four distinct anaphylactic episodes requiring intramuscular epinephrine, the patient’s clinical course ultimately stabilized. Notably, the severity of each recurrence diminished progressively, ranging from deep hypoxaemia with cyanosis and hypotension during the initial presentation to dyspnoea, rash, and pruritus during the final episode. This gradual lessening of symptom intensity informed the clinical decision-making process: the treating team, in consultation with the allergology department, had weighed two potential strategies—initiating a continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion to pre-empt further relapses versus adopting an expectant, watchful approach. Ultimately, neither aggressive intervention was required, as the patient did not experience any further symptom recurrence. Following approximately 24 hours without renewed anaphylaxis, she was transferred from the intensive care unit back to a standard care ward. This outcome underscores an important clinical reality in multiphasic anaphylaxis: the lack of evidence-based guidelines means that management decisions must frequently rely on close clinical observation and expert judgment rather than standardized protocols.

The patient was discharged from the hospital after a total admission of three days. A central tenet of her discharge planning was strict avoidance of clarithromycin, the identified trigger. Notably, no formal allergy confirmation testing—such as skin prick testing or oral challenge—was performed. The treating clinicians determined that provoking renewed anaphylaxis through diagnostic re-exposure was not proportional to the benefit, particularly given the relative ease with which clarithromycin can be avoided in the future. This pragmatic decision reflects a common principle in anaphylaxis management: when the causative agent has been clearly identified through a compelling clinical history and the reaction was severe, the risks of confirmatory testing may outweigh the benefits. The patient was also advised to maintain avoidance of beta-lactam antibiotics, consistent with her pre-existing allergy history.

At discharge, the patient's pharmacotherapy was modified to include long-term preventive measures. She was started on levocetirizine, a second-generation H1-antihistamine, as a new standing medication. While antihistamines do not treat anaphylaxis itself—epinephrine remains the only first-line therapy—the addition of a daily antihistamine aims to suppress cutaneous symptoms and reduce mast cell mediator effects, which may be particularly relevant in a patient with suspected systemic mastocytosis. She was also prescribed and instructed in the use of an epinephrine auto-injector (0.3 mg). This is a critical component of discharge planning for any patient who has experienced anaphylaxis, as it provides a means of immediate intervention in the event of recurrence. It is worth noting that the 0.3 mg auto-injector dose is lower than the guideline-recommended 0.5 mg for adults over 50 kg—a discrepancy that has been associated with an increased need for escalating care, as noted in recent literature.

Patient education at discharge encompassed both recognition of anaphylaxis and proper use of rescue medication. The patient was taught to identify early warning signs such as pruritus, flushing, throat swelling, and dyspnoea, and to administer the epinephrine auto-injector promptly should these symptoms arise. Education also included instruction to seek emergency medical care immediately after auto-injector use, given the documented risk of biphasic or multiphasic recurrence—this patient's own clinical course being a vivid example of that risk.

Long-term follow-up was arranged with the allergology department, driven by the atypical and prolonged nature of the patient's presentation. This evaluation revealed findings consistent with a high probability of systemic mastocytosis, including elevated serum tryptase levels—both during the third anaphylactic episode and at baseline—which remained increased during follow-up. Additional supporting findings included hepatomegaly on abdominal sonography and neuropsychiatric symptoms such as depression and memory problems. However, the patient did not meet formal WHO diagnostic criteria for systemic mastocytosis, as only two minor criteria were fulfilled and the major criterion could not be assessed due to limited material obtained in the bone marrow biopsy. Systemic mastocytosis is characterized by an abnormal proliferation of mast cells and excessive mediator release, predisposing affected individuals to severe and atypical anaphylactic reactions. Although the connection between systemic mastocytosis and biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis has not been specifically addressed in the literature, the condition is recognized as a risk factor for severe anaphylaxis more broadly.

An important limitation in this patient's diagnostic workup was the timing of tryptase sampling. Current guidelines recommend a minimum of two, ideally three, tryptase measurements during an anaphylaxis episode: an initial sample as soon as feasible, a second sample 1–2 hours after symptom onset (when tryptase typically peaks), and a third sample at least 24 hours after full clinical resolution to establish baseline. In this case, the first tryptase sample was drawn 1 hour after the third anaphylactic episode, and the second was taken 27 hours after the last recurrence. This deviation from recommended timing underscores a practical challenge in acute anaphylaxis management: the urgency of stabilizing a critically ill patient can compromise adherence to diagnostic sampling protocols. Nevertheless, the elevated baseline tryptase provided valuable diagnostic information supporting the suspicion of an underlying mast cell disorder and reinforcing the importance of ongoing outpatient tryptase monitoring.
