Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# Corticosteroids for Anaphylaxis: A Ritual Whose Time Has Passed?

## The Case That Makes You Question Everything

A woman in her 60s presents to the ED with anaphylaxis after a single dose of oral clarithromycin. She gets IM epinephrine, antihistamines, fluids, and oxygen. She responds well. Then she relapses. And relapses again. And again. Four episodes of anaphylactic shock in total, each requiring intramuscular epinephrine, over the course of her first 12 hours of admission.

But here is the part that should make every clinician pause: after her first relapse, she received corticosteroids — not one type, but two. Prednisone 30 mg IV daily for 5 days *and* dexamethasone 8 mg IV once. Why both? Because the general ward anaphylaxis protocol recommended one, and the ICU anaphylaxis protocol recommended the other. She got both. And she still went on to have further anaphylactic episodes. This case, published by Schneider and colleagues, is a perfect microcosm of everything wrong with our enduring love affair with corticosteroids in anaphylaxis.

## The Ritual We Can't Quit

For decades, corticosteroids have been a cornerstone of anaphylaxis management. The logic always seemed reasonable on its face: mast cell activation triggers a cascade of inflammatory mediators, corticosteroids suppress inflammation, therefore corticosteroids should prevent recurrent or biphasic reactions. It is the kind of mechanistic reasoning that feels deeply satisfying. It also does not hold up to scrutiny.

A 2017 systematic review examined 22 studies on corticosteroid use for biphasic anaphylaxis prevention. Only one study supported their use. Twenty-one showed no evidence that corticosteroids reduce the risk of biphasic reactions. Then came the 2019 data showing that prehospital corticosteroid administration was associated with an *increased* likelihood of ICU or hospital ward admission. Not a neutral effect. Not a marginal benefit. Potential harm.

And yet, as this case beautifully illustrates, the reflex persists. This patient received dual corticosteroid regimens — overlapping, redundant, and driven by conflicting institutional protocols — despite having already demonstrated that her anaphylaxis was not going to be prevented by anything short of ongoing epinephrine. The steroids were administered not because the evidence demanded it, but because the protocol demanded it, and because the cognitive pull of "doing something" in the face of a frightening, recurrent, multiphasic reaction is nearly irresistible.

## Why We Still Reach for the Solumedrol

The persistence of corticosteroid use in anaphylaxis despite mounting evidence is a textbook example of what we might call therapeutic inertia — the tendency of clinical practice to lag years or decades behind evolving evidence. But it is also something more subtle. When a patient is lying in front of you with recurrent anaphylactic shock, the urge to escalate therapy is powerful. Epinephrine feels like a rescue medication, not a preventative one. Steroids feel like prevention. The fact that the evidence does not support this distinction matters less than it should in the heat of the moment.

There is also institutional momentum. This patient received two different steroid regimens because two different hospital protocols — one for the ward, one for the ICU — recommended different agents. When protocols conflict and both are followed, you get a snapshot of a system that has not reconciled its own guidance with the contemporary evidence. If your anaphylaxis protocol still lists corticosteroids as standard of care, it is worth asking when it was last reviewed.

## What This Case Teaches Us Beyond Steroids

This case is not only about corticosteroids. Several additional pearls deserve attention.

First, epinephrine dosing matters. The first two doses this patient received were 0.3 mg via auto-injector. Current guidelines recommend 0.01 mg/kg with a maximum of 0.5 mg for adults. For a 104 kg patient, the correct dose was 0.5 mg, not 0.3 mg. A 2025 retrospective study confirmed that patients who initially received 0.3 mg epinephrine required escalation of care significantly more often than those who received 0.5 mg. Auto-injectors are convenient and reduce treatment hesitancy, but the most commonly available adult auto-injectors in many systems deliver a sub-therapeutic dose for the majority of adults. Know your dosing. Draw it up if you have to, but do not underdose anaphylaxis.

Second, this patient's clinical course — multiphasic anaphylaxis with four distinct episodes — prompted investigation that revealed a high probability of systemic mastocytosis, with elevated tryptase levels, hepatomegaly, and neuropsychiatric symptoms. She did not meet full WHO diagnostic criteria, but the signal was strong enough to be clinically significant. The takeaway: when anaphylaxis follows an atypical, recurrent, or unusually severe course, think beyond the immediate trigger. Mast cell disorders predispose patients to severe and potentially multiphasic anaphylaxis, and while the connection to biphasic reactions specifically has not been well studied, the association with severe anaphylaxis is established.

Third, tryptase sampling in this case was suboptimal. The recommended approach is to obtain a baseline sample as soon as feasible, a second sample 1 to 2 hours after symptom onset when tryptase peaks, and a third at least 24 hours after full resolution to establish a new baseline. In this case, the first tryptase was drawn an hour after the *third* anaphylactic episode, and the second was drawn 27 hours after the last recurrence. The information was still useful — both were elevated — but the timing missed the window for optimal interpretation. If you are managing anaphylaxis, especially an atypical case, get your tryptases early, at the right intervals, and in triplicate.

Finally, the allergology team in this case considered two strategies for the recurrent episodes: continuous IV epinephrine infusion versus expectant management, reasoning that symptom intensity was decreasing with each episode. Both approaches carried risks, and neither was clearly supported by evidence. The patient ultimately required neither, as the episodes ceased. But this clinical decision point highlights the genuine void in guidance for multiphasic anaphylaxis. We need a registry of these cases. We need expert-based consensus. We need data.

## Building a Modern Anaphylaxis Protocol

So what should a contemporary, evidence-informed anaphylaxis protocol look like? Based on the current evidence and this case:

- **Epinephrine first, epinephrine early, epinephrine enough.** IM epinephrine at 0.5 mg for adults (0.3 mg for under 50 kg). Do not delay for anything else. Do not underdose.
- **Antihistamines for symptom control, not prevention.** H1 blockers for cutaneous symptoms, H2 blockers as adjunct. They treat itching and urticaria; they do not prevent recurrence.
- **Fluids for shock.** Standard supportive care.
- **Corticosteroids: skip them.** The evidence does not support routine use for biphasic prevention. If you feel you must, have a clear rationale and a single agent, not two overlapping regimens from conflicting protocols.
- **Observe based on risk stratification.** The 2021 UK guideline offers a three-tier approach. Patients with severe features, multiple epinephrine doses, delayed presentation, or known biphasic history warrant longer observation. A straightforward single-episode anaphylaxis with rapid response may not need prolonged monitoring.
- **Investigate atypical courses.** Multiphasic anaphylaxis, severe refractory reactions, or unusual triggers should prompt consideration of systemic mastocytosis, with properly timed tryptase sampling and referral to allergology.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- Corticosteroids do not prevent biphasic anaphylaxis. The 2017 systematic review of 22 studies found no benefit, and a 2019 study suggested potential harm with increased admission rates.
- Despite this, clinical practice and institutional protocols lag behind the evidence, as demonstrated by this patient receiving two overlapping steroid regimens from conflicting ward and ICU protocols.
- IM epinephrine dosing for adults should be 0.5 mg, not the 0.3 mg delivered by many commonly available auto-injectors. Underdosing is associated with increased need for escalation of care.
- Multiphasic anaphylaxis warrants investigation for underlying mast cell disorders. Properly timed tryptase sampling — early, at 1–2 hours, and at baseline — is essential.
- When anaphylaxis follows an atypical or recurrent course, the absence of evidence-based guidance is real. Continuous epinephrine infusion and expectant management are both defensible; neither is clearly superior. Document and contribute to registries when you encounter these cases.
- Audit your local anaphylaxis protocol. If it still routinely recommends corticosteroids for biphasic prevention, it is time for an update.

Anaphylaxis management has evolved significantly in recent years, but our clinical habits have not always kept pace. This case — with its four anaphylactic episodes, its dual corticosteroid regimens, its underdosed epinephrine, and its eventual unmasking of probable systemic mastocytosis — is a reminder that every aspect of our anaphylaxis practice deserves ongoing critical appraisal. The rituals we inherit are not the rituals we are obligated to keep.
