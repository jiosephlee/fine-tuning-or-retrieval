Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# The 0.3 mg Epinephrine Auto-Injector in Adults: Convenient, Fast, and Underdosing Your Patient

## The Setup: Four Episodes, Two Doses, One Critical Question

A woman in her 60s — 104 kg, diabetes, hypertension, beta-lactam allergy — presents to the ED with anaphylaxis one hour after her first-ever dose of clarithromycin. She's hypotensive, hypoxemic, flushed, and urticarial. She receives 0.3 mg intramuscular epinephrine via auto-injector and improves. Then she relapses. She gets another 0.3 mg auto-injector. She improves. Then she relapses again — and again. Four anaphylactic episodes in twelve hours, with the first two treated using 0.3 mg auto-injector doses and the latter two treated with 0.5 mg individually prepared intramuscular doses. Same patient, same reaction, different dosing. This case forces us to confront an uncomfortable reality: many of us are systematically underdosing epinephrine in adult anaphylaxis, and the very tools designed to reduce treatment delay may be contributing to the problem.

## The Dosing Gap You Already Know About (But Maybe Haven't Fixed)

Guidelines are clear. The recommended intramuscular epinephrine dose for anaphylaxis is 0.01 mg/kg, up to a maximum of 0.5 mg. For the vast majority of adults — anyone over 50 kg — this simplifies to 0.5 mg IM. Yet walk through many EDs, wards, and even ICUs, and the most readily available epinephrine delivery device is a 0.3 mg auto-injector. These devices are labeled for adults, stocked on crash carts, and handed to patients at discharge. For a 104 kg patient, 0.3 mg delivers roughly 0.003 mg/kg — about 70% of the recommended dose. That is not a trivial shortfall in a condition where timely and adequate epinephrine is the single most important intervention.

In this case, the rationale for using the auto-injector was explicit and well-intentioned: in time-sensitive settings, or in clinical environments where anaphylaxis treatment is infrequent and staff may hesitate when asked to draw up a vasoactive agent from an ampule, the auto-injector minimizes delay and reduces dosing errors. These are real benefits. A nurse or junior physician who rarely handles epinephrine may pause, second-guess, and lose precious minutes when a patient is crumping. The auto-injector removes that friction. The problem is that it may simultaneously remove 40% of the dose.

## What the Evidence Shows

A 2025 retrospective study directly addressed this question and the findings are striking: patients who initially received 0.3 mg epinephrine required escalation of care — additional epinephrine injections, further interventions — significantly more often than those who initially received 0.5 mg. More doses, more relapses, more ICU involvement. In the case presented here, the patient required four separate epinephrine administrations across her four anaphylactic episodes. While her multiphasic course was likely driven by underlying pathology rather than dosing alone, it is hard to ignore that her first two episodes were treated with a sub-therapeutic dose for her weight, and that both episodes recurred.

The diminishing intensity of her episodes — from deep hypoxemia with cyanosis and hypotension initially, down to rash and pruritus by the fourth episode — may reflect natural waning of mast cell mediator release over time. But it also raises the question of whether more aggressive initial dosing might have shortened the course. We cannot know from a single case, but the signal from the 2025 data is concerning enough to warrant a hard look at practice patterns.

## The Deeper Context: Why This Patient Was Special

This was not a straightforward anaphylaxis case. The multiphasic course itself is rare — biphasic reactions occur in roughly 4–6% of anaphylaxis cases, and multiphasic reactions with multiple relapses are rarer still. Subsequent workup revealed persistently elevated tryptase levels, hepatomegaly on abdominal sonography, and neuropsychiatric symptoms, all pointing toward a high probability of systemic mastocytosis, though formal WHO diagnostic criteria were not met. Systemic mastocytosis is a known risk factor for severe and atypical anaphylaxis, and there is essentially no literature specifically addressing its role in biphasic or multiphasic courses. This patient's mast cell burden may have driven the recurrent episodes regardless of dosing adequacy. But for the broader population of adults presenting with anaphylaxis who do *not* have an underlying mast cell disorder, the dosing gap remains a modifiable problem.

## The Practical Tension: Speed vs. Dose

The team in this case made a deliberate, documented choice to prioritize speed and reduce hesitancy by using the auto-injector. That is a legitimate trade-off to consider. Epinephrine delay is associated with worse outcomes, and a dose given promptly is almost certainly better than a correct dose given five minutes later. But the framing of this as a binary choice — fast but underdosed, versus correct but delayed — is a false dichotomy that we should refuse to accept. We can and should design systems that deliver both speed and correct dosing.

## Reconciling Practice with Guidelines

Several strategies can help bridge this gap without sacrificing the benefits that auto-injectors provide. First, institutions should ensure that 0.5 mg epinephrine auto-injectors are available alongside 0.3 mg devices, particularly in EDs and areas managing acute reactions. If auto-injectors are used to reduce hesitancy and delay, they should at least deliver an adequate dose. Second, pre-drawn epinephrine syringes or standardized anaphylaxis kits with 0.5 mg doses prepared and labeled can reduce the cognitive load and hesitation that comes with drawing up medication from an ampule under pressure. Third, simulation and routine competency training for nursing and medical staff on anaphylaxis management — including epinephrine ampule preparation — can reduce the hesitancy that makes auto-injectors feel like the only safe option. Fourth, and perhaps most importantly, protocols should explicitly state the weight-based dose and default to 0.5 mg for adults, so that when an auto-injector is used as a bridge, a second dose to reach the recommended total is immediately recognized as necessary rather than viewed as an escalation.

## A Note on the Discharge Prescription

This patient was discharged with a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector. At 104 kg, this is the same underdose she received in the ED. If we accept that 0.3 mg is inadequate for adults over 50 kg in the hospital setting — and the evidence suggests we should — then the same logic applies in the community. Patients deserve to leave with a device that gives them the best chance of surviving their next reaction. Prescribing a 0.5 mg auto-injector for adults over 50 kg is a simple, high-impact change that aligns discharge practice with treatment guidelines.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **0.3 mg is an underdose for most adults.** Guidelines recommend 0.01 mg/kg IM (max 0.5 mg), which simplifies to 0.5 mg for anyone over 50 kg. The 0.3 mg auto-injector falls short for the majority of adult patients.
- **Underdosing has consequences.** A 2025 retrospective study showed that adults receiving 0.3 mg epinephrine required significantly more escalation of care compared to those receiving 0.5 mg.
- **Auto-injectors solve a real problem — but create another.** They reduce treatment delay and staff hesitancy in time-sensitive or low-frequency settings, but the convenience of a 0.3 mg device should not come at the cost of inadequate dosing.
- **The speed-vs-dose trade-off is a false dichotomy.** Systems should be designed to deliver both rapid and correctly dosed epinephrine — through 0.5 mg auto-injectors, pre-drawn syringes, standardized kits, and staff training.
- **Discharge prescribing matters too.** Adults over 50 kg sent home with a 0.3 mg auto-injector carry the same underdosing risk into the community. Prescribe 0.5 mg devices for adults over 50 kg.
- **When anaphylaxis is atypical, think deeper.** Multiphasic courses, elevated baseline tryptase, hepatomegaly, and neuropsychiatric symptoms should prompt consideration of systemic mastocytosis — a major risk factor for severe and recurrent anaphylaxis.
