Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# The 0.3 mg Epinephrine Auto-Injector in Adults: Convenient, Fast, and Underdosing Your Patient

## The Setup: Four Episodes, Two Doses, One Critical Question

A woman in her 60s — 104 kg, diabetes, hypertension, beta-lactam allergy — presents to the ED with anaphylaxis one hour after her first-ever dose of clarithromycin. She's hypotensive, hypoxemic, flushed, and urticarial. She receives 0.3 mg intramuscular epinephrine via auto-injector and improves. Then she relapses. She gets another 0.3 mg auto-injector. She improves. Then she relapses again — and again. Four anaphylactic episodes in twelve hours, with the first two treated using 0.3 mg auto-injector doses and the latter two treated with 0.5 mg individually prepared intramuscular doses. Same patient, same reaction, different dosing. This case forces us to confront an uncomfortable reality: many of us are systematically underdosing epinephrine in adult anaphylaxis, and the very tools designed to reduce treatment delay may be contributing to the problem.

## The Dosing Gap You Already Know About (But Maybe Haven't Fixed)

Guidelines are clear. The recommended intramuscular epinephrine dose for anaphylaxis is 0.01 mg/kg, up to a maximum of 0.5 mg. For the vast majority of adults — anyone over 50 kg — this simplifies to 0.5 mg IM. Yet walk through many EDs, wards, and even ICUs, and the most readily available epinephrine delivery device is a 0.3 mg auto-injector. These devices are labeled for adults, stocked on crash carts, and handed to patients at discharge. For a 104 kg patient, 0.3 mg delivers roughly 0.003 mg/kg — about 70% of the recommended dose. That is not a trivial shortfall in a condition where timely and adequate epinephrine is the single most important intervention.

In this case, the rationale for using the auto-injector was explicit and well-intentioned: in time-sensitive settings, or in clinical environments where anaphylaxis treatment is infrequent and staff may hesitate when asked to draw up a vasoactive agent from an ampule, the auto-injector minimizes delay and reduces dosing errors. These are real benefits. A nurse or junior physician who rarely handles epinephrine may pause, second-guess, and lose precious minutes when a patient is crumping. The auto-injector removes that friction. The problem is that it may simultaneously remove 40% of the dose.

## What the Evidence Shows

A 2025 retrospective study directly addressed this question and the findings are striking: patients who initially received 0.3 mg epinephrine required escalation of care — additional epinephrine injections, further interventions — significantly more often than those who initially received 0.5 mg. More doses, more relapses, more ICU involvement. In the case presented here, the patient required four separate epinephrine administrations across her four anaphylactic episodes. While her multiphasic course was likely driven by underlying pathology rather than dosing alone, it is hard to ignore that her first two episodes were treated with a sub-therapeutic dose for her weight, and that both episodes recurred.

The diminishing intensity of her episodes — from deep hypoxemia with cyanosis and hypotension initially, down to rash and pruritus by the fourth episode — may reflect natural waning of mast cell mediator release over time. But it also raises the question of whether more aggressive initial dosing might have shortened the course. We cannot know from a single case, but the signal from the 2025 data is concerning enough to warrant a hard look at practice patterns.

## The Deeper Context: Why This Patient Was Special

This was not a straightforward anaphylaxis case. The multiphasic course itself is rare — biphasic reactions occur in roughly 4–6% of anaphylaxis cases, and multiphasic reactions with multiple relapses are rarer still. Subsequent workup revealed persistently elevated tryptase levels, hepatomegaly on abdominal sonography, and neuropsychiatric symptoms, all pointing toward a high probability of systemic mastocytosis, though formal WHO diagnostic criteria were not met. Systemic mastocytosis is a known risk factor for severe and atypical anaphylaxis, and there is essentially no literature specifically addressing its role in biphasic or multiphasic courses. This patient's mast cell burden may have driven the recurrent episodes regardless of dosing adequacy. But for the broader population of adults presenting with anaphylaxis who do *not* have an underlying mast cell disorder, the dosing gap remains a modifiable problem.

## The Practical Tension: Speed vs. Dose

The team in this case made a deliberate, documented choice to prioritize speed and reduce hesitancy by using the auto-injector. That is a legitimate trade-off to consider. Epinephrine delay is associated with worse outcomes, and a dose given promptly is almost certainly better than a correct dose given five minutes later. But the framing of this as a binary choice — fast but underdosed, versus correct but delayed — is a false dichotomy that we should refuse to accept. We can and should design systems that deliver both speed and correct dosing.

## Reconciling Practice with Guidelines

Several strategies can help bridge this gap without sacrificing the benefits that auto-injectors provide. First, institutions should ensure that 0.5 mg epinephrine auto-injectors are available alongside 0.3 mg devices, particularly in EDs and areas managing acute reactions. If auto-injectors are used to reduce hesitancy and delay, they should at least deliver an adequate dose. Second, pre-drawn epinephrine syringes or standardized anaphylaxis kits with 0.5 mg doses prepared and labeled can reduce the cognitive load and hesitation that comes with drawing up medication from an ampule under pressure. Third, simulation and routine competency training for nursing and medical staff on anaphylaxis management — including epinephrine ampule preparation — can reduce the hesitancy that makes auto-injectors feel like the only safe option. Fourth, and perhaps most importantly, protocols should explicitly state the weight-based dose and default to 0.5 mg for adults, so that when an auto-injector is used as a bridge, a second dose to reach the recommended total is immediately recognized as necessary rather than viewed as an escalation.

## A Note on the Discharge Prescription

This patient was discharged with a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector. At 104 kg, this is the same underdose she received in the ED. If we accept that 0.3 mg is inadequate for adults over 50 kg in the hospital setting — and the evidence suggests we should — then the same logic applies in the community. Patients deserve to leave with a device that gives them the best chance of surviving their next reaction. Prescribing a 0.5 mg auto-injector for adults over 50 kg is a simple, high-impact change that aligns discharge practice with treatment guidelines.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **0.3 mg is an underdose for most adults.** Guidelines recommend 0.01 mg/kg IM (max 0.5 mg), which simplifies to 0.5 mg for anyone over 50 kg. The 0.3 mg auto-injector falls short for the majority of adult patients.
- **Underdosing has consequences.** A 2025 retrospective study showed that adults receiving 0.3 mg epinephrine required significantly more escalation of care compared to those receiving 0.5 mg.
- **Auto-injectors solve a real problem — but create another.** They reduce treatment delay and staff hesitancy in time-sensitive or low-frequency settings, but the convenience of a 0.3 mg device should not come at the cost of inadequate dosing.
- **The speed-vs-dose trade-off is a false dichotomy.** Systems should be designed to deliver both rapid and correctly dosed epinephrine — through 0.5 mg auto-injectors, pre-drawn syringes, standardized kits, and staff training.
- **Discharge prescribing matters too.** Adults over 50 kg sent home with a 0.3 mg auto-injector carry the same underdosing risk into the community. Prescribe 0.5 mg devices for adults over 50 kg.
- **When anaphylaxis is atypical, think deeper.** Multiphasic courses, elevated baseline tryptase, hepatomegaly, and neuropsychiatric symptoms should prompt consideration of systemic mastocytosis — a major risk factor for severe and recurrent anaphylaxis.



# Corticosteroids for Anaphylaxis: A Ritual Whose Time Has Passed?

## The Case That Makes You Question Everything

A woman in her 60s presents to the ED with anaphylaxis after a single dose of oral clarithromycin. She gets IM epinephrine, antihistamines, fluids, and oxygen. She responds well. Then she relapses. And relapses again. And again. Four episodes of anaphylactic shock in total, each requiring intramuscular epinephrine, over the course of her first 12 hours of admission.

But here is the part that should make every clinician pause: after her first relapse, she received corticosteroids — not one type, but two. Prednisone 30 mg IV daily for 5 days *and* dexamethasone 8 mg IV once. Why both? Because the general ward anaphylaxis protocol recommended one, and the ICU anaphylaxis protocol recommended the other. She got both. And she still went on to have further anaphylactic episodes. This case, published by Schneider and colleagues, is a perfect microcosm of everything wrong with our enduring love affair with corticosteroids in anaphylaxis.

## The Ritual We Can't Quit

For decades, corticosteroids have been a cornerstone of anaphylaxis management. The logic always seemed reasonable on its face: mast cell activation triggers a cascade of inflammatory mediators, corticosteroids suppress inflammation, therefore corticosteroids should prevent recurrent or biphasic reactions. It is the kind of mechanistic reasoning that feels deeply satisfying. It also does not hold up to scrutiny.

A 2017 systematic review examined 22 studies on corticosteroid use for biphasic anaphylaxis prevention. Only one study supported their use. Twenty-one showed no evidence that corticosteroids reduce the risk of biphasic reactions. Then came the 2019 data showing that prehospital corticosteroid administration was associated with an *increased* likelihood of ICU or hospital ward admission. Not a neutral effect. Not a marginal benefit. Potential harm.

And yet, as this case beautifully illustrates, the reflex persists. This patient received dual corticosteroid regimens — overlapping, redundant, and driven by conflicting institutional protocols — despite having already demonstrated that her anaphylaxis was not going to be prevented by anything short of ongoing epinephrine. The steroids were administered not because the evidence demanded it, but because the protocol demanded it, and because the cognitive pull of "doing something" in the face of a frightening, recurrent, multiphasic reaction is nearly irresistible.

## Why We Still Reach for the Solumedrol

The persistence of corticosteroid use in anaphylaxis despite mounting evidence is a textbook example of what we might call therapeutic inertia — the tendency of clinical practice to lag years or decades behind evolving evidence. But it is also something more subtle. When a patient is lying in front of you with recurrent anaphylactic shock, the urge to escalate therapy is powerful. Epinephrine feels like a rescue medication, not a preventative one. Steroids feel like prevention. The fact that the evidence does not support this distinction matters less than it should in the heat of the moment.

There is also institutional momentum. This patient received two different steroid regimens because two different hospital protocols — one for the ward, one for the ICU — recommended different agents. When protocols conflict and both are followed, you get a snapshot of a system that has not reconciled its own guidance with the contemporary evidence. If your anaphylaxis protocol still lists corticosteroids as standard of care, it is worth asking when it was last reviewed.

## What This Case Teaches Us Beyond Steroids

This case is not only about corticosteroids. Several additional pearls deserve attention.

First, epinephrine dosing matters. The first two doses this patient received were 0.3 mg via auto-injector. Current guidelines recommend 0.01 mg/kg with a maximum of 0.5 mg for adults. For a 104 kg patient, the correct dose was 0.5 mg, not 0.3 mg. A 2025 retrospective study confirmed that patients who initially received 0.3 mg epinephrine required escalation of care significantly more often than those who received 0.5 mg. Auto-injectors are convenient and reduce treatment hesitancy, but the most commonly available adult auto-injectors in many systems deliver a sub-therapeutic dose for the majority of adults. Know your dosing. Draw it up if you have to, but do not underdose anaphylaxis.

Second, this patient's clinical course — multiphasic anaphylaxis with four distinct episodes — prompted investigation that revealed a high probability of systemic mastocytosis, with elevated tryptase levels, hepatomegaly, and neuropsychiatric symptoms. She did not meet full WHO diagnostic criteria, but the signal was strong enough to be clinically significant. The takeaway: when anaphylaxis follows an atypical, recurrent, or unusually severe course, think beyond the immediate trigger. Mast cell disorders predispose patients to severe and potentially multiphasic anaphylaxis, and while the connection to biphasic reactions specifically has not been well studied, the association with severe anaphylaxis is established.

Third, tryptase sampling in this case was suboptimal. The recommended approach is to obtain a baseline sample as soon as feasible, a second sample 1 to 2 hours after symptom onset when tryptase peaks, and a third at least 24 hours after full resolution to establish a new baseline. In this case, the first tryptase was drawn an hour after the *third* anaphylactic episode, and the second was drawn 27 hours after the last recurrence. The information was still useful — both were elevated — but the timing missed the window for optimal interpretation. If you are managing anaphylaxis, especially an atypical case, get your tryptases early, at the right intervals, and in triplicate.

Finally, the allergology team in this case considered two strategies for the recurrent episodes: continuous IV epinephrine infusion versus expectant management, reasoning that symptom intensity was decreasing with each episode. Both approaches carried risks, and neither was clearly supported by evidence. The patient ultimately required neither, as the episodes ceased. But this clinical decision point highlights the genuine void in guidance for multiphasic anaphylaxis. We need a registry of these cases. We need expert-based consensus. We need data.

## Building a Modern Anaphylaxis Protocol

So what should a contemporary, evidence-informed anaphylaxis protocol look like? Based on the current evidence and this case:

- **Epinephrine first, epinephrine early, epinephrine enough.** IM epinephrine at 0.5 mg for adults (0.3 mg for under 50 kg). Do not delay for anything else. Do not underdose.
- **Antihistamines for symptom control, not prevention.** H1 blockers for cutaneous symptoms, H2 blockers as adjunct. They treat itching and urticaria; they do not prevent recurrence.
- **Fluids for shock.** Standard supportive care.
- **Corticosteroids: skip them.** The evidence does not support routine use for biphasic prevention. If you feel you must, have a clear rationale and a single agent, not two overlapping regimens from conflicting protocols.
- **Observe based on risk stratification.** The 2021 UK guideline offers a three-tier approach. Patients with severe features, multiple epinephrine doses, delayed presentation, or known biphasic history warrant longer observation. A straightforward single-episode anaphylaxis with rapid response may not need prolonged monitoring.
- **Investigate atypical courses.** Multiphasic anaphylaxis, severe refractory reactions, or unusual triggers should prompt consideration of systemic mastocytosis, with properly timed tryptase sampling and referral to allergology.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- Corticosteroids do not prevent biphasic anaphylaxis. The 2017 systematic review of 22 studies found no benefit, and a 2019 study suggested potential harm with increased admission rates.
- Despite this, clinical practice and institutional protocols lag behind the evidence, as demonstrated by this patient receiving two overlapping steroid regimens from conflicting ward and ICU protocols.
- IM epinephrine dosing for adults should be 0.5 mg, not the 0.3 mg delivered by many commonly available auto-injectors. Underdosing is associated with increased need for escalation of care.
- Multiphasic anaphylaxis warrants investigation for underlying mast cell disorders. Properly timed tryptase sampling — early, at 1–2 hours, and at baseline — is essential.
- When anaphylaxis follows an atypical or recurrent course, the absence of evidence-based guidance is real. Continuous epinephrine infusion and expectant management are both defensible; neither is clearly superior. Document and contribute to registries when you encounter these cases.
- Audit your local anaphylaxis protocol. If it still routinely recommends corticosteroids for biphasic prevention, it is time for an update.

Anaphylaxis management has evolved significantly in recent years, but our clinical habits have not always kept pace. This case — with its four anaphylactic episodes, its dual corticosteroid regimens, its underdosed epinephrine, and its eventual unmasking of probable systemic mastocytosis — is a reminder that every aspect of our anaphylaxis practice deserves ongoing critical appraisal. The rituals we inherit are not the rituals we are obligated to keep.



# Biphasic, Multiphasic, Refractory: Making Sense of Complex Anaphylaxis Terminology and Management

You're managing a straightforward anaphylaxis case. IM epinephrine given, symptoms resolve, patient looks great — until four hours later when they crash again. Then again. Then *again*. Four discrete episodes of anaphylactic shock in twelve hours, each requiring intramuscular epinephrine, despite no re-exposure to the trigger. Now what? Do you start a continuous epinephrine infusion preemptively? Do you watch and wait? Do you call allergology and hope they have an answer? As it turns out, nobody really knows — and the literature offers essentially zero guidance for multiphasic anaphylaxis. Let's unpack why.

## The Case: Four Episodes, One Trigger, Zero Roadmap

A woman in her 60s presented to the ED with anaphylaxis one hour after taking her first-ever dose of oral clarithromycin 500 mg. She'd been switched to clarithromycin five days into a clindamycin course after developing a delayed rash — and her only prior allergic history was a mild facial swelling reaction to amoxicillin in adolescence and a strong local reaction to a wasp sting. On presentation, she was hypotensive (74/50), hypoxemic (88% on room air), with generalized erythema, urticaria, facial swelling, and throat tightness. She received 0.3 mg IM epinephrine via auto-injector, IV clemastine, oxygen, and 500 mL normal saline — and responded well.

Then it came back. Over the next 12 hours, she had three additional discrete anaphylactic episodes, each requiring IM epinephrine. The first two doses were 0.3 mg via auto-injector; the next two were 0.5 mg drawn up individually. After the first recurrence, she was transferred to the ICU, where allergology was consulted. Corticosteroids were added — both prednisone 30 mg IV daily for five days *and* a single dose of dexamethasone 8 mg, thanks to conflicting protocols between the ward and ICU. Her symptoms gradually diminished in intensity with each episode, from deep hypoxaemia with cyanosis and hypotension initially to rash and pruritus with dyspnea by the final episode. After 24 hours without recurrence, she stepped down to a standard ward and was discharged after three days.

Further workup revealed elevated tryptase — both during the third episode and at baseline — along with hepatomegaly on abdominal ultrasound and a history of depression and memory problems. This pointed toward systemic mastocytosis, though she met only two WHO minor criteria and fell short of a formal diagnosis. Notably, tryptase sampling did not follow recommended timing protocols, limiting diagnostic certainty.

## Getting the Terminology Right

One of the biggest problems in this space is that the terms are used inconsistently, and that inconsistency has real clinical consequences. Let's clarify.

**Biphasic anaphylaxis** is a recurrence of symptoms after apparent and complete resolution, occurring in the absence of any new allergen exposure. The asymptomatic interval is what distinguishes it from sustained or refractory disease. Most biphasic reactions occur within 6–12 hours, but reports extend to 78 hours. The incidence is reported anywhere from 0.4% to 23.3%, though most credible estimates land around 4–6%.

**Multiphasic anaphylaxis** — as illustrated by this case — involves *multiple* recurrences: three, four, or more discrete episodes separated by asymptomatic intervals. This is distinct from biphasic and far less studied. To our knowledge, no research has specifically examined the role of systemic mastocytosis in biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis.

**Refractory anaphylaxis** is something different entirely: persistent anaphylaxis that does not respond — or responds inadequately — to correctly administered epinephrine. There is no asymptomatic interval. The patient is sick continuously.

The clinical challenge is that differentiating a true multiphasic course from sustained anaphylaxis with only transient response to epinephrine — or from ongoing allergen absorption, as with slow-release medications or retained food allergens — can be extraordinarily difficult. In this case, the clarithromycin was a single oral dose, and each episode was separated by a clear period of clinical stability, making a true multiphasic course the most plausible interpretation.

## The Management Void

Here's where things get uncomfortable. When this patient kept relapsing, the treating team and the allergology consultant weighed two strategies: start a **continuous IV epinephrine infusion** to preemptively treat before recurrence, or take an **expectant approach** and observe whether symptoms would resolve on their own given the decreasing intensity with each episode. Both carry genuine risks. Continuous epinephrine in a patient who may not need it introduces its own hazards — tachyarrhythmias, hypertension, myocardial ischemia — particularly in a patient with diabetes and hypertension. Expectant observation risks a severe recurrence occurring between nursing checks.

In the end, neither was needed — the patient simply stopped relapsing. But you cannot plan around that. The deeper problem is that there is essentially no evidence to guide this decision. Guidelines for biphasic anaphylaxis primarily address observation duration, not active management of ongoing recurrences. For multiphasic disease specifically, the literature is essentially silent. No trials, no consensus statements, no expert-based protocols. The team in this case was flying without instruments.

## Epinephrine Dosing: The 0.3 mg Problem

This case also highlights a dosing issue that deserves more attention than it gets. The first two epinephrine doses were delivered via auto-injector at 0.3 mg. Guidelines recommend 0.01 mg/kg up to a maximum of 0.5 mg for adults — and most adults weigh more than 50 kg. At 104 kg, this patient's ideal dose was 0.5 mg, yet she received 40% less on her first two episodes. A 2025 retrospective study showed that patients who initially received 0.3 mg epinephrine needed escalation of care — additional epinephrine injections — significantly more often than those who received 0.5 mg initially.

The rationale for using auto-injectors in-hospital is understandable: they minimize treatment delay and reduce hesitancy among staff who rarely administer vasoactive medications. But the tradeoff is a potentially critical underdose. If you're going to use an auto-injector in an adult over 50 kg, recognize that you may need to supplement with an additional dose drawn from a vial. Better yet, train your team to draw up 0.5 mg from a vial as the default — and save auto-injectors for the prehospital setting where they belong.

## The Steroid Question

This patient received both prednisone *and* dexamethasone due to conflicting ward and ICU protocols — a perfect illustration of how entrenched steroid use remains despite the evidence shifting. A 2017 systematic review of 22 studies found only one supporting corticosteroid use for preventing biphasic anaphylaxis, with the other 21 showing no benefit. A 2019 study demonstrated an *increased* likelihood of ICU or ward admission after prehospital corticosteroid treatment. Most contemporary guidelines now advise against routine corticosteroid administration in anaphylaxis. Yet protocols lag behind, and clinicians continue to reach for them reflexively — sometimes, as in this case, in duplicate.

## Mastocytosis: The Hidden Predisposition

The elevated baseline tryptase, hepatomegaly, and neuropsychiatric symptoms in this patient strongly suggest systemic mastocytosis — a condition involving abnormal mast cell proliferation and excessive mediator release that predisposes patients to severe and potentially recurrent anaphylaxis. She did not meet formal WHO diagnostic criteria, but the probability was high. This is clinically important because patients with mast cell disorders may behave differently in terms of recurrence risk and treatment response, and identifying them changes your long-term management and counseling.

One critical pearl from this case: **tryptase timing matters, and it was done wrong here.** Guidelines recommend a minimum of two, ideally three samples: the first as soon as feasible without delaying treatment, a second 1–2 hours after symptom onset (when tryptase peaks), and a third at least 24 hours after full resolution for baseline. In this case, the first sample was taken an hour after the *third* anaphylactic episode — missing the recommended window entirely for the first two episodes — and the second was taken 27 hours after the last recurrence. If you suspect mastocytosis or need to confirm anaphylaxis biochemically, get the timing right. Your allergology colleagues will thank you, and your patient's diagnostic workup will actually be interpretable.

## A Framework for Recurrent Anaphylaxis

Until we have evidence, here's a practical approach drawn from this case and the available literature:

- **Confirm you're dealing with true multiphasic disease.** Rule out ongoing allergen exposure — check that no slow-release formulation or retained allergen is continuing to drive the reaction. Verify that each episode is separated by genuine asymptomatic intervals, not just partial response to epinephrine.
- **Dose epinephrine correctly from the start.** 0.5 mg IM for adults over 50 kg. If you use an auto-injector, be prepared to supplement.
- **Escalate monitoring.** These patients belong in a high-acuity setting with continuous monitoring. Repeated IM epinephrine without telemetry is asking for trouble.
- **Engage allergology early.** Not just for discharge planning — for real-time management input on whether to start continuous infusion versus observe.
- **Get tryptase levels at the right times.** This is your best shot at identifying underlying mast cell disease and confirming the diagnosis.
- **Think twice about steroids.** The evidence does not support them for preventing biphasic reactions. If your protocol still includes them, it's probably time to update it.
- **Have an escalation plan.** Decide in advance: if recurrence happens again, what is the threshold for starting an epinephrine infusion? Document it so the next clinician isn't starting from scratch.

## The Bottom Line

- Multiphasic anaphylaxis — multiple discrete recurrences separated by asymptomatic intervals — is distinct from biphasic and refractory disease, and the literature provides almost no guidance for its management.
- Differentiating true multiphasic reactions from sustained anaphylaxis with transient epinephrine response or ongoing allergen absorption requires careful clinical assessment; a clear asymptomatic interval and excluded ongoing exposure support a multiphasic diagnosis.
- Adult epinephrine dosing should be 0.5 mg IM; 0.3 mg auto-injectors underdose most adults and are associated with more frequent need for escalation of care.
- Corticosteroids do not prevent biphasic anaphylaxis based on current evidence; their routine use should be abandoned, and conflicting institutional protocols should be harmonized.
- Systemic mastocytosis is a likely predisposing condition for complex anaphylaxis and should be considered when tryptase is elevated at baseline — but tryptase must be sampled at the recommended timepoints to be diagnostically useful.
- There is no evidence-based or expert consensus guidance for choosing between continuous IV epinephrine infusion and expectant observation in multiphasic anaphylaxis; clinical judgment, monitoring intensity, and early allergology involvement are your best tools.
- A national or international registry of complex anaphylaxis cases — capturing patient characteristics, management strategies, and outcomes — is urgently needed to generate the data required for meaningful expert-based recommendations.



# When Anaphylaxis Won't Stop: Systemic Mastocytosis as a Hidden Driver of Severe, Recurrent Reactions

## The Case That Makes You Rethink "Standard" Anaphylaxis

You treated the anaphylaxis. The patient responded. You admitted for observation. Then it came back. And again. And again.

A woman in her 60s presented to the ED with anaphylaxis one hour after her first-ever dose of oral clarithromycin 500 mg — prescribed for a rash that developed during a course of clindamycin for erysipelas. She had a known beta-lactam allergy (facial swelling to amoxicillin in adolescence) and a strong local reaction to a wasp sting, but had never experienced anaphylaxis before.

At presentation: hypotensive (74/50), hypoxaemic (88% on room air), flushed, urticarial, diaphoretic, agitated, with facial swelling and throat tightness. She received IM epinephrine 0.3 mg via auto-injector, IV clemastine, oxygen, and 500 mL normal saline — and responded well. Blood pressure normalised. Swelling decreased. She was admitted to the acute internal ward.

Then the real challenge began.

Over the next 12 hours, she had three additional anaphylactic episodes — each requiring IM epinephrine — for a total of four discrete anaphylactic events from a single dose of clarithromycin. The intensity decreased with each episode, from profound hypoxaemia with cyanosis and hypotension initially, to dyspnoea, rash, and pruritus by the final episode. She was transferred to the ICU after the first relapse. Corticosteroids were added (prednisone 30 mg IV daily for 5 days plus a single dose of dexamethasone 8 mg IV — a discrepancy reflecting differing protocols between the general ward and ICU). No further relapses occurred after the fourth episode, and she was discharged after three days.

But the question that should keep you awake at night is: *why did this happen?*

## When "Severe Allergy" Isn't the Whole Story

The answer in this case — and one you should be actively looking for in your own refractory or recurrent anaphylaxis patients — is systemic mastocytosis.

Due to the atypical and prolonged clinical course, the treating team pursued further investigation. What they found was striking:

- **Elevated tryptase** during the third anaphylactic episode, with **persistently elevated baseline tryptase** on follow-up.
- **Hepatomegaly** on abdominal sonography.
- **Neuropsychiatric symptoms** including depression and memory problems.
- **Two WHO minor criteria** for systemic mastocytosis — but not enough for a formal diagnosis, as the bone marrow biopsy yielded insufficient material to assess the major criterion (multi-focal dense infiltrates of mast cells with tryptase staining).

This is a patient who almost certainly has systemic mastocytosis, but whose diagnosis was missed by the narrow margins of formal criteria. And that gap between "high probability" and "confirmed diagnosis" is exactly where many of these patients live — sometimes for years.

## What Is Systemic Mastocytosis and Why Does It Matter?

Systemic mastocytosis is a clonal mast cell disorder characterised by abnormal proliferation of mast cells, excessive mediator release, and multiorgan inflammation. The clinical consequence that brings these patients to the ED is straightforward and terrifying: they are predisposed to severe, recurrent, and sometimes refractory anaphylaxis.

Mast cells are the primary effector cells of anaphylaxis. When you have more of them — and when those cells are dysfunctional and primed for degranulation — the threshold for anaphylaxis drops, the severity of reactions escalates, and the response to treatment can become unpredictable. This is not a patient with a "strong allergy." This is a patient whose mast cell burden itself is the disease.

In the presented case, the patient had multiple features beyond the anaphylaxis itself that pointed toward a mast cell disorder: hepatomegaly (organ infiltration), neuropsychiatric symptoms (mast cell mediator effects on the central nervous system), and elevated baseline tryptase. The combination of high baseline tryptase with recurrent unprovoked-like anaphylactic episodes is the hallmark pattern clinicians need to recognise.

## Diagnostic Criteria — and Why So Many Patients Fall Through the Cracks

The WHO diagnostic criteria for systemic mastocytosis require either one major criterion plus one minor criterion, or three minor criteria. The major criterion is multi-focal dense infiltrates of mast cells (≥15 mast cells in aggregates) in bone marrow or other extracutaneous organs, confirmed by tryptase staining. The minor criteria include atypical mast cell morphology, aberrant CD25/CD2/CD30 expression, KIT D816V mutation, and elevated serum tryptase >20 ng/mL.

Here is the clinical reality: confirming the major criterion requires an adequate bone marrow biopsy. In this case, the biopsy did not yield enough material. The patient met two minor criteria but could not be formally diagnosed. This is not an unusual scenario. Bone marrow biopsies are invasive, not always definitive, and in many clinical settings are not pursued aggressively — especially when anaphylaxis has already resolved and the patient is being managed as an outpatient.

The practical implication is that **you will encounter patients who have systemic mastocytosis that you cannot formally prove**. Your clinical suspicion, supported by tryptase levels and organ involvement clues, may need to guide management in the absence of a tissue diagnosis.

## Tryptase: The Test You Are Probably Not Ordering Correctly

One of the most instructive failures in this case — and one that is almost certainly happening in your ED too — was the tryptase sampling.

Guidelines recommend a minimum of two, ideally three tryptase samples at specific intervals: an initial sample as soon as feasible without delaying treatment, a second sample 1–2 hours (but within 4 hours) of symptom onset when tryptase peaks, and a third sample at least 24 hours after full clinical resolution to establish baseline.

In this case, the first tryptase was drawn one hour after the *third* anaphylactic episode. The second was drawn 27 hours after the last recurrence. The recommended intervals were not adhered to — meaning the opportunity to document the dynamic rise and fall of tryptase was lost.

This matters enormously. A rise in acute tryptase of at least 20% plus 2 ng/mL above baseline supports the diagnosis of anaphylaxis. A persistently elevated baseline tryptase — as seen in this patient — points toward a mast cell disorder. But if you do not draw the samples at the right times, you lose both the acute anaphylaxis confirmation and the mastocytosis signal.

**If you are treating anaphylaxis and not drawing serial tryptase levels, you are flying blind.** And if you are drawing them but not at the right intervals, you are flying blind with the illusion of data.

## Clinical Clues That Should Trigger Suspicion

This case provides a practical checklist of features that should prompt you to think beyond "severe allergy" and toward a mast cell disorder:

- **Recurrent anaphylaxis** without re-exposure to the trigger — biphasic or multiphasic course.
- **Severe anaphylactic reaction to a trigger** that is not typically a high-risk allergen (clarithromycin is not a classic cause of life-threatening anaphylaxis).
- **Elevated baseline tryptase** — not just elevated during the acute event, but elevated on follow-up.
- **Organomegaly** — particularly hepatomegaly or splenomegaly, suggesting mast cell infiltration.
- **Neuropsychiatric symptoms** — depression, cognitive complaints, which are increasingly recognised as mast cell mediator effects.
- **History of severe local reactions** to insect stings — this patient had a strong local reaction to a wasp sting, which is a recognised red flag for mastocytosis in the allergy literature.
- **Treatment-resistant or recurrent course** despite standard management with appropriate IM epinephrine doses.

None of these in isolation is diagnostic. But the convergence of several should immediately shift your differential and your follow-up plan.

## How Identifying Mastocytosis Changes Everything

The long-term management of a patient with systemic mastocytosis is fundamentally different from the management of a patient with a "one-time" allergic reaction.

For the patient in this case, discharge planning included a new standing prescription for levocetirizine and an epinephrine auto-injector — which is appropriate for any anaphylaxis patient. But for a patient with mastocytosis, the stakes are higher. These patients need **perpetual trigger avoidance** with a heightened awareness that triggers may be unpredictable and that future reactions may be severe. They need **permanent epinephrine auto-injector carriage** — not just a single prescription, but ongoing education and reinforcement. And they need **specialist follow-up** with allergology and potentially haematology, because systemic mastocytosis is a disease with haematologic, dermatologic, gastrointestinal, and skeletal manifestations that extend far beyond the ED visit.

Interestingly, the team in this case elected not to perform formal allergy confirmation testing (e.g., skin testing for clarithromycin), reasoning that the risk of provoking another severe reaction was not proportional to the benefit, given that clarithromycin can be easily avoided. This is a reasonable and defensible approach — but it also underscores that in suspected mastocytosis, the specific trigger matters less than the underlying mast cell biology. Avoiding clarithromycin is necessary but not sufficient; the next trigger may be something entirely different.

## The Epinephrine Dosing Problem Hiding in Plain Sight

One more detail from this case deserves attention because it is relevant to every single anaphylaxis patient you treat: **epinephrine dosing.**

The first two epinephrine doses in this case were 0.3 mg via auto-injector. The second two were 0.5 mg drawn up individually. The recommended IM epinephrine dose for adults is 0.01 mg/kg, up to a maximum of 0.5 mg. At 104 kg, this patient's weight-based dose would have been approximately 0.5 mg — meaning the first two doses were significantly underdosed.

The case authors note that 0.3 mg auto-injectors are commonly used in adults despite the guideline recommendation, and a 2025 retrospective study showed that patients who initially received 0.3 mg required escalation of care significantly more often than those who received 0.5 mg. The convenience and reduced hesitancy of auto-injectors is real and valuable — but the trade-off is that many adults are receiving a subtherapeutic dose at the most critical moment.

If your department stocks only 0.3 mg auto-injectors for adult anaphylaxis, this should prompt a protocol review. The goal is not to eliminate auto-injector use but to ensure that when time permits — or when the first dose fails — you are giving the dose the guidelines actually recommend.

## The Corticosteroid Question

This patient received both prednisone and dexamethasone due to conflicting protocols between the general ward and ICU. The rationale was the traditional belief that corticosteroids prevent biphasic anaphylaxis. The evidence no longer supports this.

A 2017 systematic review of 22 studies found only one supporting corticosteroid use, with 21 showing no evidence of reduced biphasic risk. A 2019 study showed increased likelihood of ICU or hospital admission after prehospital corticosteroid treatment. Most contemporary guidelines now advise against routine corticosteroid administration for anaphylaxis — though clinical practice lags behind, as illustrated by the dual-protocol confusion in this very case.

The bottom line: this patient had multiphasic anaphylaxis despite receiving corticosteroids. Whether they helped, did nothing, or caused harm is unknowable from a single case — but the literature increasingly suggests the latter two are more likely than the first.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Multiphasic anaphylaxis is rare but real.** When symptoms recur without re-exposure, think beyond treatment failure — consider ongoing allergen absorption, refractory anaphylaxis, and underlying mast cell disorders.
- **Systemic mastocytosis should be on your differential for any patient with severe, recurrent, or atypically triggered anaphylaxis.** Clinical suspicion may need to guide management even when formal WHO diagnostic criteria are not met.
- **Draw serial tryptase levels at the right times.** Initial sample as soon as feasible, second at 1–2 hours (within 4) of symptom onset, third at ≥24 hours after resolution. If you miss the windows, you lose the data.
- **Elevated baseline tryptase is a red flag.** It is not just a marker of the acute event — it points toward a clonal mast cell disorder and warrants specialist follow-up.
- **Look for the extras.** Hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, neuropsychiatric symptoms, severe local reactions to insect stings, and cutaneous lesions (urticaria pigmentosa) are all clues that the anaphylaxis you are treating may be the tip of a much larger iceberg.
- **Give the right epinephrine dose.** Adults over 50 kg should receive 0.5 mg IM, not 0.3 mg. If your auto-injectors only deliver 0.3 mg, know when to switch to drawn-up dosing.
- **Rethink routine corticosteroids.** The evidence for preventing biphasic anaphylaxis is weak to absent, and the signal for harm is growing.
- **Identifying mastocytosis changes long-term management.** These patients need lifelong trigger vigilance, permanent epinephrine carriage, and multidisciplinary follow-up — not just discharge with an antihistamine and an auto-injector prescription.

## Final Thought

Anaphylaxis is common. Multiphasic anaphylaxis is not. But when you are standing at the bedside of a patient having their third anaphylactic episode in 12 hours, "rare" is not the word that comes to mind. The question is not just "how do I treat this episode?" but "why is this happening?" In a meaningful proportion of those cases, the answer may be systemic mastocytosis — a diagnosis you can suspect at the bedside, confirm with the right labs drawn at the right times, and act on with management that extends far beyond the ED doors.

The next time you see anaphylaxis that keeps coming back, ask yourself: have I checked the tryptase? And have I thought about the mast cells?



# Tryptase Timing in Anaphylaxis: The Missed Diagnostic Opportunity We Keep Repeating

## The Case That Should Make Us All Uncomfortable

A woman in her 60s presents to the ED with anaphylaxis after a single dose of oral clarithromycin. She's hypotensive, hypoxaemic, flushed, urticarial, and agitated. She gets epinephrine, antihistamines, oxygen, and fluids — and responds. Then she relapses. And relapses again. And again. Four anaphylactic episodes in total, each requiring intramuscular epinephrine, each slightly less severe than the last, over a 12-hour ICU admission.

Eventually the team steps back and asks the right question: *why is this happening?* They consult allergology. They check tryptase. And they find it's elevated — not just during the reaction, but at baseline too. That, combined with hepatomegaly on abdominal ultrasound and a history of neuropsychiatric symptoms, points toward systemic mastocytosis as the likely driver of this dramatic multiphasic course.

Here's the problem: the first tryptase sample wasn't drawn until the *third* anaphylactic episode. The second sample came 27 hours after the last relapse. The recommended serial sampling protocol — the one that would have given us the cleanest diagnostic picture — was never followed.

This isn't a criticism of the treating team. It's a recognition that *we keep doing this wrong*.

## Why Tryptase Matters More Than We Think

Serum tryptase is the closest thing we have to a biomarker for anaphylaxis severity and mast cell burden, yet it remains chronically underutilised and improperly timed in the acute setting. The enzyme is stored in mast cell secretory granules and released during degranulation, making it a relatively specific marker of mast cell activation — more so than histamine, which is also released from basophils and is far more transient.

What tryptase kinetics can tell us depends entirely on *when* you draw it. A peak level reflects the magnitude of mast cell activation during the reaction. A baseline level, drawn after full resolution, reflects the patient's underlying mast cell burden. And the *delta* — the difference between peak and baseline — tells you how much tryptase was released as a direct result of the anaphylactic event, separating reactive mast cell activation from constitutive elevation seen in systemic mastocytosis or other mast cell disorders.

In this case, both the acute and baseline tryptase were elevated, which is the pattern that should immediately make you think about systemic mastocytosis. A normal baseline with a high peak suggests a conventional allergic reaction against a trigger in a patient with normal mast cell mass. An elevated baseline with a further rise during reaction suggests a patient who already carries excess mast cells and is therefore primed for more severe, more prolonged, and more recurrent anaphylaxis.

## The Sampling Protocol We Should All Know

The guideline-recommended approach, endorsed by the World Allergy Organisation and others, calls for a minimum of two, ideally three tryptase samples drawn at specific intervals.

The first sample should be drawn as soon as feasible after onset of symptoms — ideally immediately, without delaying treatment. This is your baseline-in-reaction. The second should be drawn 1 to 2 hours after symptom onset (but within 4 hours), when tryptase typically reaches its peak. The third should be drawn at least 24 hours after full clinical resolution, to establish the patient's true baseline.

This three-point approach gives you the curve. You can see the rise, you can see the peak, you can see the return to baseline — or, critically, the failure to return to normal. Without all three points, you're interpreting fragments of a picture.

In this case, the clinical team only obtained two samples, both drawn well outside the recommended windows. The first was taken one hour after the *third* anaphylactic episode — which means it was many hours after the initial reaction onset. The second was taken 27 hours after the last relapse. We have no idea what the peak tryptase was during the first or second episodes. We have no sample drawn during the golden 1-to-2-hour window. The data we do have is suggestive of systemic mastocytosis, but it's incomplete — and the patient didn't even meet formal WHO diagnostic criteria, in part because of limited bone marrow biopsy material.

## Why We Keep Missing This

The barriers are real and they're multifactorial. In a busy ED, the immediate priority is airway, breathing, circulation, and epinephrine — as it should be. Tryptase sampling feels like a downstream, non-urgent task. There's often no standing order set that automatically triggers serial tryptase draws when anaphylaxis is identified. Nursing staff may be unfamiliar with the specific timing requirements. The patient may be moved between ED, acute ward, and ICU, with each handoff creating an opportunity for the sampling plan to fall through. And in many centres, tryptase results aren't available in real time anyway, which reduces the perceived urgency of obtaining them.

But the consequence is that we lose diagnostic information that cannot be recovered retroactively. You cannot go back and draw a peak tryptase 24 hours later. The window closes. And in patients with underlying mast cell disorders — who are precisely the ones most likely to have severe, biphasic, or multiphasic reactions — you lose the chance to identify a condition that fundamentally changes their long-term management.

## What Systemic Mastocytosis Changes

Systemic mastocytosis is characterised by an abnormal proliferation of mast cells in one or more extracutaneous organs, most commonly the bone marrow, but also potentially involving the liver, spleen, and gastrointestinal tract. These patients carry a constitutively elevated mast cell burden, which means more mast cells available to degranulate, more mediator release per reaction, and a higher threshold for severe anaphylaxis at lower levels of allergen exposure. They are also more likely to experience recurrent or biphasic courses — exactly the clinical picture seen in this case.

The diagnostic workup for systemic mastocytosis includes serum tryptase as a screening tool, with a persistently elevated baseline tryptase (typically >20 µg/L) serving as a WHO minor criterion. Bone marrow biopsy confirms the diagnosis, with the major criterion being multifocal dense infiltrates of mast cells and minor criteria including KIT mutation detection, atypical mast cell morphology, and elevated serum tryptase.

Identifying systemic mastocytosis after an anaphylactic event changes the long-term management plan. These patients need referral to a specialist centre, consideration of KIT-targeted therapy in selected cases, aggressive trigger avoidance, carry of multiple epinephrine auto-injectors, and in some cases, consideration of mast cell stabilisers or omalizumab. Without the diagnosis, they're discharged with an auto-injector and generic advice — which, given their elevated risk of recurrent severe anaphylaxis, is inadequate.

In this case, the patient's tryptase was elevated at baseline and during follow-up. She had hepatomegaly on ultrasound. She had a history of neuropsychiatric symptoms. She met two WHO minor criteria but not the full diagnostic threshold. With better tryptase sampling, the diagnostic picture might have been clearer, and the pathway to formal diagnosis and specialist management might have been faster and more definitive.

## Practical Solutions

The fix doesn't require new technology or new pharmacology. It requires system design.

Anaphylaxis order sets should automatically include serial tryptase sampling with pre-specified draw times: immediately, at 1–2 hours, and at 24 hours post-resolution. These should be built into the electronic order entry system so that the first draw happens as part of the initial anaphylaxis bundle, alongside epinephrine, oxygen, IV access, and fluids — not as an afterthought.

Nursing protocols should specify that tryptase is a time-critical lab draw, with the draw time documented alongside the collection. The 24-hour baseline sample should be a standing order that follows the patient through ward transfers, with the receiving team briefed on its importance at handoff.

Education matters here too. Emergency teams need to understand that tryptase isn't just a confirmatory test — it's a screening tool for an underlying disease that predicts future anaphylaxis severity and recurrence. When you draw tryptase properly, you're not just documenting the current reaction. You're investigating whether this patient is *built differently*.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Serial tryptase sampling is guideline-recommended but chronically underperformed.** The optimal protocol is three samples: immediately, 1–2 hours after onset, and ≥24 hours after full resolution.
- **The delta tryptase may matter more than any single value.** The difference between peak and baseline separates reactive mast cell activation from constitutive elevation seen in systemic mastocytosis.
- **Elevated baseline tryptase after anaphylaxis should trigger investigation for systemic mastocytosis**, particularly when accompanied by organomegaly, skin lesions, or neuropsychiatric symptoms.
- **Systemic mastocytosis predisposes to severe, recurrent, and multiphasic anaphylaxis** — and may have been the driver in this case, though formal diagnostic criteria were not met.
- **Build tryptase sampling into anaphylaxis order sets** so it happens automatically, not as an afterthought. The diagnostic window closes fast and cannot be reopened.
- **Corticosteroids did not prevent biphasic recurrence in this case** despite both prednisone and dexamethasone — consistent with mounting evidence that steroids do not reduce biphasic anaphylaxis risk.
- **Multiphasic anaphylaxis is rare enough that we lack evidence-based management strategies.** Registries of these cases, with standardised tryptase data, could provide the foundation for expert-based guidelines.

## The Bottom Line

Anaphylaxis management in the acute phase is getting better — earlier epinephrine, less reliance on steroids, clearer protocols. But the *diagnostic* side of anaphylaxis management is still lagging. Every time we miss the tryptase window, we lose information that can't be recovered and that might change a patient's life. The next patient who walks through the door with anaphylaxis might have systemic mastocytosis, a mast cell activation syndrome, or another underlying driver of severe recurrent reactions. We won't know unless we draw the right labs at the right time.

Draw the tryptase. Draw it early. Draw it serially. And make sure your system does it automatically, because if we leave it to the chaos of the acute setting, it won't happen.



# Observation After Anaphylaxis: How Long Is Long Enough When the Evidence Won't Commit?

Picture this: you've just successfully resuscitated a patient in anaphylactic shock. Epinephrine in, symptoms out, vitals normalized, skin cleared. You pat yourself on the back, admit for observation, and figure you're done. Then it happens again. And again. And again.

That's exactly what happened to a woman in her 60s who presented to the ED with anaphylaxis after her first-ever dose of clarithromycin—prescribed as a substitute for clindamycin, which had caused a rash five days into treatment for erysipelas. She had a known beta-lactam allergy from adolescence and a strong local reaction to a wasp sting, but had never experienced anaphylaxis before. Within an hour of taking 500 mg of clarithromycin orally, she developed pruritus, flushing, agitation, throat tightness, hypotension (74/50 mm Hg), and hypoxemia (88% on room air). Standard treatment with IM epinephrine, antihistamines, oxygen, and fluids produced a solid clinical response. She was admitted to the acute internal ward for monitoring. And then things got interesting.

## The Relapse Problem

Despite a full initial response, this patient went on to experience three additional anaphylactic episodes within the first 12 hours of admission, each requiring IM epinephrine. Four total episodes from a single dose of antibiotic. The intensity of each recurrence decreased progressively—from deep hypoxemia with cyanosis and severe hypotension in the first episode, down to dyspnea, rash, and pruritus in the fourth—but each one met criteria for anaphylactic shock. She was escalated to the ICU after the first relapse, and ultimately had no further episodes after the fourth. She was discharged after three days with an epinephrine auto-injector and a new prescription for levocetirizine, with no recurrences at follow-up.

This is what the authors call multiphasic anaphylaxis, and it sits at the extreme end of a clinical spectrum that we frankly do not understand well enough. Biphasic anaphylaxis—defined by the Resuscitation Council UK as a recurrence of symptoms after apparent resolution in the absence of re-exposure—is the more commonly recognized variant, with reported incidence ranging wildly from 0.4% to 23.3%, though most data cluster around 4–6%. Multiphasic reactions, with multiple relapses, are rarer still, and the literature is essentially silent on how to manage them prospectively.

## What makes this case particularly instructive is the timing

The patient's final anaphylactic episode occurred roughly 12 hours after initial presentation. If you had observed her for the traditionally recommended 4–6 hours after symptom resolution and discharged her, she could have been home when the second, third, or fourth episode struck. And while reports of biphasic reactions occurring up to 78 hours after initial allergen exposure exist, the bulk of recurrences happen in that 6–12 hour window—the very window where most guidelines go vague.

## What the Guidelines Say (and Don't Say)

Here's where it gets frustrating. The World Allergy Organization and the Dutch NHG guideline both acknowledge that approximately half of biphasic reactions occur within 6–12 hours after the initial event, but neither commits to a specific monitoring recommendation. The UK Resuscitation Council 2021 guideline is the most helpful, proposing a three-tier risk-stratified approach to observation, but even this is expert consensus, not evidence-based, and the evidence base it draws from is thin.

This matters because the tension here is real. On one side is patient safety: biphasic reactions can be severe, and we cannot reliably predict who will have one. On the other side is resource stewardship: with anaphylaxis being a common ED presentation and biphasic reactions occurring in only 4–6% of cases, prolonged observation for every patient is an enormous resource burden. We need risk stratification, and we need it to be better than what we have.

## Risk Predictors: The List That Doesn't Quite Deliver

Several predictors of biphasic anaphylaxis have been proposed: delayed epinephrine administration, severe initial presentation, need for multiple epinephrine doses, and prior history of biphasic reactions. These make pathophysiologic sense, and our patient hit several of them—severe initial presentation with hypotension and hypoxemia, multiple epinephrine doses required. But as the authors note, reliable predictors that allow targeted monitoring are not yet available. The sensitivity and specificity of these factors simply aren't good enough to safely triage patients into short versus long observation arms.

## The Steroid Question: Time to Let Go?

One particularly interesting detail in this case is the double corticosteroid regimen. After the first recurrence, the patient received both prednisone 30 mg IV daily for five days and dexamethasone 8 mg IV once, because the general ward anaphylaxis protocol and the ICU anaphylaxis protocol recommended different steroids. She still went on to have two more anaphylactic episodes.

This is consistent with the evolving evidence. A 2017 systematic review of 22 studies found only one supporting corticosteroid use, with the other 21 showing no evidence that steroids reduce the risk of biphasic anaphylaxis. A 2019 study even showed an increased likelihood of ICU or ward admission after prehospital corticosteroid treatment. Most contemporary guidelines now advise against routine corticosteroid administration in anaphylaxis, though entrenched practice habits die hard. This case is a sobering reminder that steroids did not prevent recurrence here, and the patient did fine once the mast cell degranulation cascade finally burned itself out.

## The Epinephrine Dosing Trap

A subtle but important point in this case involves epinephrine dosing. The first two episodes were treated with 0.3 mg IM epinephrine via auto-injector, while the subsequent two used 0.5 mg individually prepared doses. The guidelines recommend 0.01 mg/kg with a maximum of 0.5 mg for adults, simplified to 0.5 mg for most adults since the majority weigh over 50 kg. This patient weighed 104 kg, meaning she was significantly underdosed with the auto-injector for her first two episodes.

The authors make a compelling case for auto-injectors in time-sensitive or low-frequency settings, noting that they minimize treatment delay and reduce hesitancy among staff who rarely handle vasoactive medications. But the trade-off is clear: a 2025 retrospective study showed that patients who initially received 0.3 mg epinephrine needed escalation of care significantly more often than those who received 0.5 mg. The widespread availability of 0.3 mg auto-injectors—many marketed for adult use—creates a structural underdosing problem that we should be actively working to fix.

## The Elephant in the Room: Systemic Mastocytosis

The atypical and prolonged course of this patient's reaction prompted further investigation, which revealed a high probability of systemic mastocytosis. Her tryptase levels were elevated both during the third anaphylactic episode and at baseline, and remained elevated on follow-up. She had hepatomegaly on abdominal ultrasound and a history of depression and memory problems, both consistent with mast cell mediator effects. However, she did not meet formal WHO diagnostic criteria, with only two minor criteria fulfilled and the major criterion not assessable due to limited bone marrow biopsy material.

Systemic mastocytosis involves abnormal mast cell proliferation, excessive mediator release, and multiorgan inflammation, and it predisposes patients to severe and potentially atypical anaphylaxis. The connection to biphasic or multiphasic courses is logical—more mast cells, more mediators, longer cascades—but the authors note that no research has specifically addressed this relationship. This case suggests it should be on your differential when anaphylaxis follows an unusual trajectory, particularly when accompanied by elevated tryptase, hepatomegaly, or neuropsychiatric symptoms.

One important miss in this case: tryptase was not drawn at the recommended intervals. Guidelines call for an initial sample as soon as feasible, a second at 1–2 hours (within 4 hours) of symptom onset when tryptase peaks, and a third at least 24 hours after full resolution for baseline. In this case, the first sample was taken an hour after the third anaphylactic episode—far too late to capture the peak from the initial event. When you're managing a crashing anaphylaxis patient, the last thing on your mind may be serial tryptase draws, but the information is invaluable for retrospective diagnosis and for identifying underlying mast cell disorders.

## A Pragmatic Approach to Observation

So what do we do with all this uncertainty? The authors propose a registry for complex anaphylaxis cases to build the data infrastructure for evidence-based recommendations, and this is clearly needed. But in the meantime, here's a pragmatic synthesis:

For low-risk presentations—single epinephrine dose, rapid and complete response, mild initial presentation, no features suggesting mast cell disorder—observation for 4–6 hours after symptom resolution is probably adequate, though you should counsel patients about the small risk of late recurrence and ensure they have an epinephrine auto-injector and understand how to use it.

For higher-risk presentations—multiple epinephrine doses, severe initial reaction with hypotension or hypoxemia, delayed epinephrine, prior biphasic history, or any clinical features suggestive of systemic mastocytosis—extended observation for at least 12–24 hours is warranted. The UK three-tier approach is the best available framework, but it should be applied with the understanding that our risk prediction is imperfect.

For multiphasic reactions specifically, there is no evidence to guide you. The authors of this case considered two strategies: continuous IV epinephrine infusion to pre-emptively treat, versus expectant management given decreasing symptom intensity. Neither was needed in the end, as the patient stopped relapsing after her fourth episode. But the discussion itself highlights the clinical tightrope: too aggressive and you risk unnecessary epinephrine exposure and ICU resources; too conservative and you risk a life-threatening relapse on the ward or at home. Until we have better data, this decision will remain one of clinical judgment, serial reassessment, and honest conversation with the patient.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- Biphasic anaphylaxis occurs in roughly 4–6% of cases, with about half of recurrences happening in the 6–12 hour window. Traditional 4–6 hour observation periods may be inadequate for higher-risk patients.
- Proposed risk factors for biphasic reactions—delayed epinephrine, severe initial presentation, multiple epinephrine doses, prior biphasic history—make physiologic sense but lack the reliability needed for confident triage. The UK 2021 three-tier risk-stratified approach is the best available framework.
- Corticosteroids do not prevent biphasic anaphylaxis. The evidence is clear and accumulating, and contemporary guidelines are moving away from routine use. This case provides a vivid illustration: double steroids, still three more relapses.
- Watch your epinephrine dosing. 0.3 mg auto-injectors underdose most adults. The guideline-recommended dose is 0.5 mg IM for adults over 50 kg, and underdosing is associated with more frequent escalation of care.
- Consider systemic mastocytosis in any patient with an unusually severe, prolonged, or multiphasic anaphylactic course, especially with elevated tryptase, hepatomegaly, or neuropsychiatric symptoms. You won't always meet full WHO criteria, but the clinical implications for future management are significant.
- Draw serial tryptase levels. The schedule matters: initial, 1–2 hours, and 24+ hours post-resolution. Yes, it is hard to remember in the heat of the moment. Yes, it is worth it.
- When managing a multiphasic reaction, you are in uncharted territory. There is no evidence to guide continuous epinephrine infusion versus expectant management. Serial reassessment, early ICU involvement, and transparency with the patient about the uncertainty are your best tools.
- The bottom line: the evidence won't commit, so you have to. Use the risk factors we have, apply the most structured approach available, and when something about the case feels atypical—like anaphylaxis that keeps coming back—observe longer and investigate deeper.
