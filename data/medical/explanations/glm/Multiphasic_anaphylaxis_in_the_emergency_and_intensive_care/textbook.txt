Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

## Acute Presentation and Initial Stabilization

### Patient History and the Sequence of Antibiotic Exposures

The patient was a woman in her 60s with a body weight of 104 kg and a medical history notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. Her outpatient medications included metformin, gliclazide, hydrochlorothiazide, omeprazole, and sertraline. Crucially, she carried a known allergy to beta-lactam antibiotics, stemming from a mild reaction to amoxicillin in adolescence that was limited to facial swelling without oropharyngeal involvement, rash, hypotension, or dyspnea. She had also experienced a strong local skin reaction to a wasp sting in the past, but never any systemic allergic symptoms. This admission represented her first episode of anaphylaxis.

Five days before her emergency department (ED) presentation, her general practitioner had prescribed clindamycin for erysipelas. After five days of clindamycin therapy, she developed a rash on her abdomen, which was interpreted as a late allergic reaction to clindamycin. Her antibiotic regimen was therefore switched to clarithromycin, a macrolide antibiotic to which she had no prior exposure. Within one hour of taking the first 500 mg oral dose of clarithromycin, she developed pruritus, flushing, increased sweating, agitation, and a sensation of throat swelling. Symptoms progressed over the following hour, prompting her to seek emergency care.

This sequence illustrates an important clinical principle: when a patient with a known antibiotic allergy develops a new rash on one agent, the substitute chosen must be selected with cross-reactivity and prior exposure in mind. Clarithromycin, a macrolide, does not share significant cross-reactivity with beta-lactams or with clindamycin (a lincosamide), making it a reasonable choice from an allergenic standpoint. However, any drug to which a patient has never been exposed carries an inherent risk of a first-time hypersensitivity reaction, and this patient's history of multiple drug and venom sensitivities suggested an underlying predisposition to allergic reactions—a feature that would later prompt investigation for systemic mastocytosis.

### Clinical Criteria and Physical Examination Findings for Diagnosing Acute Anaphylaxis

Anaphylaxis is a clinical diagnosis defined by the sudden onset and rapid progression of symptoms involving the airway, breathing, circulation, and/or gastrointestinal tract, typically accompanied by cutaneous or mucosal manifestations such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. The widely used diagnostic criteria, established by the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases and the Food Allergy and Anaphylaxis Network, identify anaphylaxis when any one of three criteria is met: (1) acute onset of skin or mucosal involvement plus either respiratory compromise or hypotension; (2) involvement of two or more organ systems (skin-mucosal, respiratory, cardiovascular, gastrointestinal) after likely exposure to a known allergen; or (3) hypotension after exposure to a known allergen for that patient.

At ED presentation, this patient met multiple criteria simultaneously. Her vital signs demonstrated significant cardiovascular and respiratory compromise, with a non-invasive blood pressure of 74/50 mm Hg and a peripheral oxygen saturation of 88% on room air, at a respiratory rate of 22 breaths per minute. Physical examination revealed facial swelling, new-onset generalized erythema, and urticaria covering her entire body, accompanied by increased sweating and agitation. She reported a subjective sensation of throat swelling, though no objective tongue or oropharyngeal swelling was observed. Cardiac and lung auscultation were normal. Her blood glucose was elevated at 12.7 mmol/L, consistent with her known diabetes and the acute physiologic stress of anaphylaxis, and her ECG showed a sinus rhythm.

The constellation of acute cutaneous involvement (erythema, urticaria, flushing, pruritus), respiratory compromise (hypoxemia, subjective throat swelling), and circulatory compromise (hypotension) occurring within one hour of ingestion of a newly introduced medication made the diagnosis of anaphylaxis straightforward. Importantly, the absence of stridor, wheeze, or objective oropharyngeal swelling did not diminish the certainty of diagnosis, as anaphylaxis frequently presents with hypotension and hypoxemia without dramatic airway findings, and the rapid progression of symptoms is itself a hallmark feature.

### Initial Emergency Department Stabilization

The immediate management of anaphylaxis rests on three pillars: elimination of the trigger, prompt administration of intramuscular epinephrine, and supportive care. In this case, the trigger—clarithromycin—was immediately discontinued, with no additional doses taken beyond the single 500 mg oral tablet. Supportive measures were initiated concurrently with epinephrine administration. The patient received supplemental oxygen at 15 liters per minute via a non-rebreather mask to address her hypoxemia, and a 500 mL bolus of normal saline (0.9% sodium chloride) was given intravenously to support her blood pressure. She also received 2 mg of clemastine, a first-generation H1-antihistamine, by slow intravenous injection for treatment of her cutaneous symptoms, in accordance with local guidelines.

The rationale for intravenous fluid resuscitation in anaphylaxis relates to the underlying pathophysiology. Anaphylaxis is mediated by massive degranulation of mast cells and basophils, releasing histamine, leukotrienes, prostaglandins, and other vasoactive mediators. These mediators produce widespread vasodilation and increased capillary permeability, leading to a distributive form of shock characterized by relative intravascular volume depletion. While epinephrine addresses the root cause by acting as both a vasoconstrictor (via alpha-1 adrenergic receptors) and a bronchodilator (via beta-2 receptors), and stabilizes mast cells (via beta-2 receptors), intravenous fluids help restore the effective circulating volume. Antihistamines such as clemastine provide symptomatic relief of urticaria and pruritus by blocking H1 receptors but do not address the life-threatening cardiovascular or respiratory components of anaphylaxis and must never be used as a substitute for epinephrine.

### Administration of Intramuscular Epinephrine and the Initial Transient Clinical Response

Epinephrine is the first-line and only life-saving medication for anaphylaxis. Guidelines recommend an intramuscular dose of 0.01 mg per kilogram of body weight, up to a maximum of 0.5 mg, administered into the anterolateral thigh. This patient promptly received 0.3 mg of epinephrine intramuscularly via an auto-injector, the device available in the clinical setting at the time of her initial stabilization.

The use of the auto-injector in this case merits discussion. While the recommended dose for an adult of this patient's weight (104 kg) would be 0.5 mg, the auto-injector available contained only 0.3 mg. This underdosing is a recognized and important clinical issue: many adult auto-injectors contain 0.3 mg of epinephrine, a dose appropriate for patients weighing 30 to 50 kg, but insufficient for the majority of adults who weigh more than 50 kg. The clinical team's rationale for using the auto-injector despite the suboptimal dose was pragmatic—auto-injectors minimize treatment delay and reduce treatment hesitancy among personnel who rarely administer vasoactive medications. In time-sensitive, high-acuity situations such as initial ED stabilization, the benefit of immediate drug delivery may outweigh the drawback of a slightly lower dose. Nonetheless, recent evidence suggests that patients who initially receive 0.3 mg of epinephrine are significantly more likely to require escalation of care, such as additional epinephrine injections, compared with those who receive the full 0.5 mg dose.

The clinical response to the initial epinephrine dose was prompt and apparently complete. Blood pressure returned to normal levels, facial swelling and erythema decreased, and supplemental oxygen could be discontinued. On the strength of this response, the patient was admitted to the acute internal ward—a setting offering increased monitoring by specialized nursing staff but without continuous telemetry. This initial improvement, however, proved to be a transient remission rather than true resolution. Within the first 12 hours of admission, the patient experienced recurrent episodes of anaphylactic shock, each requiring additional intramuscular epinephrine, ultimately leading to her transfer to the intensive care unit after the first relapse. The initial good response thus illustrates a critical lesson in anaphylaxis management: clinical improvement after epinephrine does not guarantee that the reaction has fully run its course, and apparent remission must not be confused with definitive resolution, particularly when the patient has features that may predispose to a prolonged or multiphasic course.

## Multiphasic Course and Intensive Care Management

Anaphylaxis is typically a single, self-limited event that resolves with prompt epinephrine administration and supportive care. However, a subset of patients experience recurrent symptoms after an apparent complete resolution, a phenomenon termed biphasic anaphylaxis. The Resuscitation Council UK defines biphasic anaphylaxis as a recurrence of symptoms several hours after apparent symptom resolution in the absence of further allergen exposure. When multiple recurrences occur, the term multiphasic anaphylaxis is applied, though this entity is far less well characterized. It is important to distinguish biphasic and multiphasic reactions from refractory anaphylaxis, which refers to persistent anaphylaxis that does not respond, or responds insufficiently, to correctly administered epinephrine. This distinction matters clinically: a patient whose symptoms fully resolve before recurring is experiencing a biphasic or multiphasic process, while one whose symptoms never truly abate may have ongoing allergen absorption—such as from a slow-release medication—or an inadequate epinephrine response requiring continuous infusion. In the case under discussion, the patient demonstrated clear intervals of symptomatic resolution between episodes, confirming a true multiphasic course rather than refractory anaphylaxis.

After the patient's initial presentation and stabilization in the emergency department, she appeared to respond well to intramuscular epinephrine and ancillary therapy. Blood pressure normalized, facial swelling and erythema diminished, and supplemental oxygen was discontinued. She was admitted to the acute internal ward for monitoring. Despite this initial favorable response, she developed recurrent episodes of anaphylactic shock within the first 12 hours of admission, ultimately totaling four anaphylactic episodes including the original ED presentation. After the first relapse, she was transferred to the intensive care unit for closer monitoring. Each recurrence required intramuscular epinephrine for resolution. Notably, the intensity of symptoms progressively diminished with each episode: the initial presentation featured profound hypoxaemia with cyanosis, hypotension, and severe dyspnea, whereas the final episode was characterized primarily by dyspnea, recurrent rash, and pruritus. This gradual attenuation of severity, while reassuring, did not eliminate the need for active treatment of each episode.

The epinephrine dosing used in this case merits close attention. The first two episodes were treated with 0.3 mg of epinephrine administered via auto-injector, while the subsequent two episodes were treated with 0.5 mg doses individually prepared for intramuscular injection. The transition to the higher dose reflects the guideline-recommended dosing of 0.01 mg per kilogram of body weight, with a maximum adult dose of 0.5 mg. At a body weight of 104 kg, this patient's calculated dose would be approximately 1 mg, meaning even the 0.5 mg dose fell short of the weight-based target. The initial use of a 0.3 mg auto-injector represents a common but important clinical problem: many adult auto-injectors contain only 0.3 mg of epinephrine, which is subtherapeutic for adults weighing more than 50 kg. The treating team's rationale for using auto-injectors initially was pragmatic—they minimize treatment delay and reduce hesitancy among personnel who rarely administer vasoactive medications. However, this case illustrates the potential consequence of underdosing, as a retrospective study cited by the authors showed that patients initially receiving 0.3 mg epinephrine required escalation of care significantly more often than those receiving 0.5 mg. The switch to individually prepared 0.5 mg doses after ICU admission likely reflected both the availability of more resources for medication preparation and the recognition that the initial dose may have been insufficient.

In addition to epinephrine, corticosteroids were administered after the first recurrence of anaphylaxis, reflecting their traditional role in preventing biphasic reactions. The patient received both prednisone 30 mg intravenously daily for five days and a single dose of dexamethasone 8 mg intravenously, a discrepancy that arose from differences between the hospital's general ward and intensive care anaphylaxis protocols. This redundancy underscores a broader issue in anaphylaxis management: while corticosteroids were long recommended for biphasic prophylaxis, accumulating evidence has substantially undermined this practice. A 2017 systematic review of 22 studies found that only one study supported corticosteroid use, while the remaining 21 showed no evidence that corticosteroids reduce the risk of biphasic anaphylaxis. More recent data have suggested potential harm, with one 2019 study demonstrating an increased likelihood of ICU or hospital ward admission following prehospital corticosteroid administration. Contemporary guidelines have consequently shifted away from routine corticosteroid use in anaphylaxis, though their persistence in clinical protocols—as illustrated by the dual prescriptions in this case—remains a practical reality.

The early involvement of the allergology department reflects the complexity of managing multiphasic anaphylaxis, for which no evidence-based guidelines exist. Two strategic approaches were considered. The first was preemptive continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion, which would theoretically maintain therapeutic epinephrine levels and prevent further recurrences rather than waiting for symptoms to reappear. The second was an expectant approach, capitalizing on the observed trend of diminishing symptom severity, to allow potential spontaneous resolution while maintaining close monitoring and readiness to treat. Each strategy carried distinct risks: continuous epinephrine infusion requires careful titration and intensive monitoring, carries risks of tachyarrhythmia, hypertension, and myocardial ischemia, and had no established evidence base for multiphasic anaphylaxis. Conversely, the expectant approach risked undertreating a potentially life-threatening recurrence. Ultimately, neither strategy was required, as the patient experienced no further symptom relapse after the fourth episode. She was transferred back to a standard care ward after approximately 24 hours without renewed anaphylaxis and was discharged after three days of total admission. This outcome highlights a key clinical lesson: in multiphasic anaphylaxis, the natural history is poorly predictable, and management decisions must balance the risks of aggressive intervention against the risks of inadequate vigilance, often in the absence of reliable evidence to guide the choice.

## Diagnostic Workup and Investigation of Underlying Causes

The multiphasic course of this patient's anaphylaxis—four distinct episodes requiring epinephrine over 12 hours despite complete cessation of the triggering agent—prompted the treating team to investigate whether an underlying condition predisposed her to such an unusually severe and recurrent reaction. This reasoning is clinically sound: while most anaphylactic episodes are isolated events triggered by a recognizable allergen, multiphasic or unusually severe reactions should raise suspicion for an underlying mast cell disorder or other condition that amplifies mediator release and prolongs the allergic response. In this case, the allergology consultation initiated a workup centered on serum tryptase measurement and evaluation for systemic mastocytosis, a disorder characterized by abnormal proliferation of mast cells and excessive release of inflammatory mediators.

Serum tryptase is the key biomarker in the diagnostic evaluation of anaphylaxis and mast cell disorders. Tryptase is a protease stored predominantly in mast cell secretory granules, and its serum level rises following mast cell degranulation. In an acute anaphylactic reaction, tryptase typically begins to rise within 30 minutes of symptom onset, peaks at approximately 1 to 2 hours, and returns to baseline by 6 to 8 hours, though this timeline can vary. An elevated acute tryptase level that exceeds the patient's baseline tryptase by a meaningful margin provides objective evidence of mast cell activation and supports the clinical diagnosis of anaphylaxis. Additionally, a persistently elevated baseline tryptase—measured after complete resolution of all symptoms—raises suspicion for systemic mastocytosis or other clonal mast cell disorders, as chronically expanded mast cell mass leads to constitutively elevated tryptase secretion.

Current guidelines recommend obtaining a minimum of two, and ideally three, tryptase samples at specific intervals to maximize diagnostic yield. The first sample should be drawn as soon as feasible after presentation, without delaying treatment. A second sample should be obtained 1 to 2 hours (but no later than 4 hours) after symptom onset, corresponding to the expected peak. A third sample should be collected at least 24 hours after full clinical resolution to establish the patient's baseline tryptase. When all three samples are available, clinicians can compare the acute rise against the baseline, confirming mast cell activation and assessing whether baseline tryptase is independently elevated. This serial approach is particularly valuable when evaluating for underlying mastocytosis, as the pattern of tryptase elevation distinguishes an isolated acute reaction from a chronic mast cell proliferative state.

In this case, the recommended tryptase sampling protocol was not followed. The first sample was drawn 1 hour after the third anaphylactic episode—meaning two prior anaphylactic episodes had occurred without any tryptase measurement—and the second sample was obtained 27 hours after the last recurrence. The initial ED workup did not include tryptase, so the expected peak during the first and most severe episode was missed entirely. This deviation limited the diagnostic information available. Specifically, without a properly timed peak sample from the initial reaction, the team could not calculate the magnitude of acute tryptase rise relative to baseline, a comparison that helps confirm anaphylaxis objectively and assess the degree of mast cell burden. Despite these limitations, the available data were informative: both the sample drawn during the third episode and the baseline sample were elevated, and tryptase remained above normal during outpatient follow-up with the allergologist. The persistently elevated baseline tryptase was a critical finding, as it signaled a chronically increased mast cell load rather than a transient post-anaphylactic elevation.

The combination of persistently elevated tryptase, the unusual multiphasic clinical course, and additional findings on clinical evaluation led the consulting allergologists to investigate systemic mastocytosis. Systemic mastocytosis is a clonal myeloproliferative neoplasm in which abnormal mast cells infiltrate one or more extracutaneous organs, most commonly the bone marrow, but also the liver, spleen, gastrointestinal tract, and lymph nodes. The pathological expansion of mast cells leads to excessive and unpredictable release of mediators such as histamine, tryptase, and prostaglandins, which predisposes patients to severe, recurrent, or atypical anaphylactic reactions, sometimes with minimal or unusual triggers. The diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis is established using WHO criteria, which include one major criterion and several minor criteria. The major criterion is the demonstration of multifocal, dense infiltrates of mast cells (15 or more in an aggregate) in bone marrow or other extracutaneous tissues on biopsy. Minor criteria include atypical mast cell morphology (spindled shapes or aberrant immunophenotype), detection of a KIT D816V mutation, elevated baseline serum tryptase exceeding 20 ng/mL, and evidence of mast cell clonality through aberrant CD25 or CD2 expression.

In this patient, the workup for systemic mastocytosis yielded partial results. The bone marrow biopsy was performed but yielded limited material, rendering the major criterion technically unassessable—clinicians could not confirm or exclude the dense mast cell infiltrates required for the major criterion. However, two minor criteria were met, though the case report does not specify which two. Given that baseline tryptase was elevated and remained so on follow-up, an elevated tryptase exceeding 20 ng/mL likely constituted one minor criterion. The second minor criterion may have been aberrant mast cell morphology or immunophenotype on the limited bone marrow sample. Because the major criterion was unassessable and only two minor criteria were documented, the patient did not meet the formal WHO diagnostic threshold for systemic mastocytosis, which requires either the major criterion plus one minor criterion or three minor criteria in the absence of the major criterion. Nevertheless, the treating team concluded that systemic mastocytosis was highly probable, and the incomplete fulfillment of criteria was attributable to technical limitations of the biopsy rather than absence of disease.

Additional findings further supported the clinical suspicion. Abdominal sonography revealed hepatomegaly, consistent with possible mast cell infiltration of the liver, a recognized site of extracutaneous involvement in systemic mastocytosis. The patient also had a history of depression and memory problems, which may represent neuropsychiatric manifestations associated with chronic mast cell mediator release, though these symptoms are nonspecific and could also reflect her pre-existing medical conditions. Together, the elevated baseline tryptase, hepatomegaly, and neuropsychiatric symptoms formed a clinical picture consistent with systemic mastocytosis, even though formal diagnostic criteria were not fully met.

A notable decision in this patient's workup was the choice not to perform definitive allergy confirmation testing for clarithromycin. Standard allergological workup after anaphylaxis may include skin prick testing, intradermal testing, or oral challenge to confirm the causative allergen. However, given the severity of the initial reaction, the multiphasic course, and the likely underlying mast cell disorder, the consulting allergologists determined that provocation testing carried an unacceptable risk of precipitating another life-threatening anaphylactic episode. This risk was not deemed proportional to the benefit, particularly because clarithromycin is a medication that can be straightforwardly avoided in the future and the clinical history provided a strong temporal association between the single 500 mg dose and the onset of symptoms within one hour. This decision illustrates a key principle in post-anaphylaxis care: when the clinical history clearly identifies the trigger and the risk of re-exposure testing is high, especially in patients with possible mast cell pathology, empiric avoidance without formal confirmation may be the safer and more pragmatic approach. The patient was discharged with a new epinephrine auto-injector, prescribed levocetirizine as ongoing antihistamine therapy, and counseled on allergen avoidance, with outpatient follow-up arranged through the allergology department to monitor tryptase trends and further evaluate for systemic mastocytosis if clinically indicated.

## Patient Outcome, Discharge, and Long-term Management

After experiencing four distinct anaphylactic episodes, each requiring intramuscular epinephrine, the patient's condition stabilized without further symptom recurrence. The treating team, in consultation with the allergology department, had considered two management strategies: initiating a continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion to pre-empt subsequent episodes or adopting an expectant approach, closely observing for spontaneous resolution. Ultimately, neither strategy was necessary, as the anaphylactic episodes resolved spontaneously. The intensity of each successive episode had been progressively decreasing, from severe hypoxaemia with cyanosis and hypotension during the initial presentation to isolated dyspnoea, rash, and pruritus during the final recurrence. Following approximately 24 hours without renewed symptoms, the patient was step-down transferred from the intensive care unit (ICU) back to a standard care ward. This clinical course highlights the variable natural history of multiphasic anaphylaxis and the challenge clinicians face in deciding when to transition from active treatment to expectant observation.

The patient was ultimately discharged from the hospital after a 3-day admission. A central pillar of her discharge planning was the strict avoidance of the offending agent, clarithromycin. No confirmatory allergy testing, such as skin prick testing or oral challenge, was performed. Given the severity of the initial anaphylactic reaction and the relative ease with which clarithromycin can be avoided in the future, the risk of provoking another severe reaction through testing was deemed disproportionate to the benefit. While confirming the allergy through testing is often useful for medical record documentation and cross-reactivity assessment, clinical practice often favors strict avoidance when the causative agent is clearly identified and the reaction is severe. The patient was also advised to avoid beta-lactam antibiotics, given her known prior allergy.

Discharge pharmacotherapy focused on both symptomatic control and emergency preparedness. She was started on levocetirizine, a second-generation H1-antihistamine, as a new daily medication. While antihistamines are not a substitute for epinephrine in acute anaphylaxis, daily antihistamine therapy can help suppress cutaneous symptoms and reduce baseline mast cell mediator activity, particularly in patients with suspected mast cell disorders. More importantly, she was prescribed an epinephrine auto-injector containing 0.3 mg of epinephrine. This reflects the hospital’s preference for this dosage formulation, as auto-injectors are essential for prompt pre-hospital treatment of future episodes. It is important to note that at 104 kg, this dose may represent an underdose for her weight; current guidelines recommend 0.01 mg/kg (maximum 0.5 mg for adults). Therefore, while the 0.3 mg auto-injector is practical and readily available, clinicians must be aware of the potential for underdosing in larger patients and consider prescribing a higher dose or providing clear instructions to administer two doses if necessary.

The transition to outpatient care required comprehensive patient education. The patient was taught how to recognize the early signs and symptoms of anaphylaxis—such as pruritus, flushing, throat tightness, and dyspnoea—and instructed on the proper use of the epinephrine auto-injector. For patients who have experienced a life-threatening allergic reaction, understanding that epinephrine is the first-line treatment and that antihistamines should only be used as adjunctive therapy for cutaneous symptoms is critical. Patient education also emphasized the need to seek immediate medical attention after auto-injector use, given the risk of biphasic or multiphasic recurrence, as demonstrated by this case.

Long-term management involved outpatient follow-up with the allergology department. Due to the atypical and prolonged clinical course of this patient’s anaphylaxis, further investigation into underlying causes was initiated. This analysis revealed a high probability of systemic mastocytosis, although the findings did not meet formal WHO diagnostic criteria. Specifically, only two minor criteria were met, and the major criterion could not be assessed due to limited material obtained in the bone marrow biopsy. Systemic mastocytosis is characterized by an abnormal increase in mast cells, excessive mediator release, and multiorgan inflammation, which predisposes patients to severe anaphylaxis. Further supporting this suspicion were findings of hepatomegaly on abdominal ultrasound and possible neuropsychiatric symptoms, including depression and memory problems. A key diagnostic marker followed during her long-term care was the serum tryptase level. Tryptase levels during the third anaphylactic episode, as well as baseline levels measured after resolution, were elevated and remained increased during outpatient follow-up. Unfortunately, tryptase levels were not measured during the initial episodes, which deviates from recommended guidelines that advise obtaining samples at specific intervals (initial, 1–2 hours, and ≥24 hours post-resolution) to compare peak levels with baseline. This highlights the importance of adherence to diagnostic guidelines during acute anaphylaxis workup, even in busy clinical settings. ## Clinical Significance and Current Controversies in Anaphylaxis Guidelines

The case of this patient with multiphasic anaphylaxis to clarithromycin illustrates several critical gaps and controversies in current anaphylaxis management. While the initial emergency treatment of anaphylaxis is well established, the optimal approach to patients who experience recurrent episodes remains poorly defined, exposing important weaknesses in existing guidelines.

### Biphasic and Multiphasic Anaphylaxis: Incidence and Prediction

Biphasic anaphylaxis is defined as a recurrence of symptoms after apparent resolution, occurring in the absence of re-exposure to the triggering allergen. Reported incidence varies widely, from as low as 0.4% to as high as 23.3%, though most data converge on a range of 4–6%. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, involving multiple recurrences as seen in this patient's four separate anaphylactic episodes, is even rarer and less well characterized. This patient's presentation—repeated relapses into anaphylactic shock over 12 hours despite adequate epinephrine response each time—exemplifies the clinical challenge: each episode responded to intramuscular epinephrine, yet symptoms recurred without any additional allergen exposure, confirming this as a true multiphasic rather than sustained or refractory course.

Several predictors of biphasic reactions have been proposed, including delayed epinephrine administration, severe initial presentation, the need for multiple epinephrine doses, and a prior history of biphasic reactions. Notably, this patient exhibited several of these features: her initial presentation was severe, with hypotension and hypoxaemia, and she ultimately required four doses of epinephrine. However, these predictors lack sufficient reliability to guide individualized monitoring decisions. The absence of validated risk stratification tools means that clinicians cannot confidently identify which patients will relapse, forcing a choice between potentially unnecessary prolonged observation for many and inadequate monitoring for the few who truly need it.

### Observation Periods and Risk-Stratified Monitoring

Guidelines universally recommend a period of observation after anaphylaxis, even when symptoms have fully resolved, but the optimal duration remains debated. Traditional observation periods range from 4 to 6 hours after symptom resolution. However, data indicate that approximately half of biphasic reactions occur within 6–12 hours of the initial event, and reports document recurrences up to 78 hours later, as noted in this case's background. This patient's relapses occurred within the first 12 hours, consistent with the reported timeframe, and prompted ICU transfer after the first recurrence.

The Dutch NHG and World Allergy Organisation guidelines acknowledge the 6–12 hour window for many biphasic reactions but stop short of recommending a specific monitoring duration. The UK 2021 guideline takes a more structured approach, proposing a three-tier risk-stratified observation protocol. This case highlights the practical consequence of guideline ambiguity: the patient was initially admitted to an acute internal ward without telemetry, and only the occurrence of the first relapse prompted escalation to ICU-level monitoring. A clearer, evidence-based consensus on observation protocols would help clinicians balance healthcare resource utilization against patient safety, particularly given that the vast majority of anaphylaxis cases are single-episode events.

### Corticosteroids: Evolving Evidence and Changing Practice

The administration of corticosteroids has traditionally been recommended as part of anaphylaxis management, with the rationale that they might reduce the risk of biphasic reactions. In this case, corticosteroids were added after the first recurrence—30 mg of intravenous prednisone daily for 5 days and a single 8 mg dose of intravenous dexamethasone—reflecting the hospital's existing protocols. Notably, the discrepancy between the general ward and ICU anaphylaxis protocols led to the patient receiving two different corticosteroid preparations, illustrating how entrenched practice patterns can produce inconsistent care.

However, the evidence supporting corticosteroids for preventing biphasic anaphylaxis has weakened substantially. A 2017 systematic review of 22 studies found that only one study supported their use, while the remaining 21 showed no evidence that corticosteroids reduce the risk of recurrent reactions. More recent data further suggest potential harm, with a 2019 study demonstrating an increased likelihood of ICU or hospital ward admission following prehospital corticosteroid treatment. Consequently, most contemporary guidelines now advise against routine corticosteroid administration in anaphylaxis, though clinical practice has been slow to change, as this case demonstrates. Importantly, this patient continued to experience two more anaphylactic episodes after receiving corticosteroids, consistent with the evidence that these agents do not reliably prevent recurrence.

### Epinephrine Dosing: Auto-Injector Under-Dosing in Adults

Epinephrine remains the cornerstone of anaphylaxis treatment, with guidelines recommending a dose of 0.01 mg/kg intramuscularly, up to a maximum of 0.5 mg. For most adults weighing over 50 kg, this is simplified to a standard 0.5 mg dose. However, the first two episodes in this patient were treated with 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injectors, which are commonly marketed and stocked for adult use despite delivering a subtherapeutic dose for patients exceeding 50 kg. This patient weighed 104 kg, meaning the guideline-recommended dose would have been approximately 1.0 mg (capped at 0.5 mg), making the 0.3 mg dose a potentially significant under-dose.

The treating team's rationale for using auto-injectors—minimizing treatment delay and reducing hesitancy among staff who rarely administer vasoactive medications—is understandable and reflects real-world constraints. Nevertheless, recent evidence supports the concern about under-dosing: a 2025 retrospective study found that patients who initially received 0.3 mg epinephrine required escalation of care, such as additional injections, significantly more often than those who received 0.5 mg. The subsequent two episodes in this patient were treated with 0.5 mg doses prepared individually, and it is notable that the severity of symptoms decreased with each successive episode, though confounding by disease course makes it impossible to attribute this to the dose change alone. This case underscores the importance of adhering to guideline-recommended weight-based dosing and the need to address systemic barriers, including the widespread availability of 0.3 mg auto-injectors and practitioner familiarity with that dose.

### Mast Cell Disorders and Severe Anaphylaxis

The atypical and prolonged course of this patient's anaphylaxis prompted investigation into underlying predisposing conditions. Systemic mastocytosis, characterized by an abnormal proliferation of mast cells with excessive mediator release and multiorgan inflammation, is a recognized risk factor for severe and atypical anaphylaxis. The workup in this patient revealed elevated serum tryptase levels both during an anaphylactic episode and at baseline, along with hepatomegaly on abdominal sonography and possible neuropsychiatric symptoms—findings consistent with systemic mastocytosis. However, the bone marrow biopsy yielded insufficient material to assess the WHO major criterion, and only two minor criteria were met, falling short of formal diagnostic requirements.

This case illustrates several practical points about the evaluation of mast cell disorders in the context of anaphylaxis. First, tryptase sampling should follow guideline-recommended intervals: an initial sample as soon as feasible without delaying treatment, a second sample 1–2 hours (within 4 hours) after symptom onset when levels typically peak, and a third sample at least 24 hours after full clinical resolution to establish baseline. In this patient, tryptase was not measured during the first two episodes, and the samples that were obtained did not adhere to these intervals, limiting the diagnostic utility of the results. Second, even when a diagnosis cannot be confirmed, the presence of elevated baseline tryptase and compatible clinical features should alert clinicians to a heightened risk of severe or recurrent anaphylaxis and inform long-term management, including avoidance strategies and the provision of epinephrine auto-injectors for self-administration. The relationship between mast cell disorders and biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis specifically has not been studied, representing a gap that future research should address.

### The Need for Registries and Expert-Based Consensus

The rarity of multiphasic anaphylaxis poses a fundamental obstacle to developing evidence-based management strategies. Randomized controlled trials are impractical in this setting given the infrequency of the condition and the emergency context in which these patients present. In this case, the consulting allergology team considered two management options—continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion for pre-emptive treatment versus an expectant approach given the decreasing intensity of episodes—but neither could be supported by robust literature, and both carried significant risks. The patient ultimately did not require either intervention, as no further relapses occurred, but this outcome does not resolve the clinical dilemma for future cases.

Given these constraints, the development of clinical registries collecting standardized data on biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis cases—including patient characteristics, treatment strategies employed, and outcomes—could provide the foundation for data-driven, expert-based consensus guidelines. Such registries would help identify patterns in triggers, risk factors, and effective interventions, and could inform the design of observational studies. In the absence of higher-quality evidence, expert-based consensus recommendations represent a valuable interim solution, but their development also depends on the availability of case data that only systematic collection can provide. This case report itself contributes to that effort by documenting the clinical course, treatment decisions, and outcomes of a multiphasic anaphylaxis episode in a patient with probable systemic mastocytosis, offering a reference point for clinicians who encounter similar presentations.
