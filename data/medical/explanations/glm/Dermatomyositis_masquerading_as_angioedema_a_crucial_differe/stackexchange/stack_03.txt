Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Pathophysiology] Dermatomyositis has a characteristic immunopathology that distinguishes it from other idiopathic inflammatory myopathies. What is the mechanism of vascular injury in dermatomyositis, and how does it explain the perifascicular pattern of muscle fiber injury and the deposition of C5b-9 (membrane attack complex) in endomysial capillaries seen on biopsy?

Dermatomyositis is fundamentally a complement-mediated microangiopathy, and this mechanism is central to understanding its histopathology. Unlike polymyositis — which is primarily a CD8+ T-cell–mediated cytotoxic process directed against muscle fibers — dermatomyositis is a humorally driven vasculopathy in which the primary target is the endothelium of endomysial capillaries.

The process is initiated by activation of the classical complement pathway, likely triggered by autoantibodies directed against as-yet incompletely characterized endothelial or microvascular antigens. Deposition of C3b leads to formation of the C5b-9 membrane attack complex on endomysial capillary endothelial cells. This MAC deposition injures and ultimately destroys capillaries, producing a characteristic loss of capillary density. The remaining capillaries often become dilated, and the microvasculature is progressively depleted.

This capillary dropout is key to explaining the perifascicular pattern of muscle fiber injury. Perifascicular fibers — those at the outer rim of the fascicle — are the most distal in the capillary supply network and thus represent a watershed zone. As capillaries are destroyed by complement-mediated injury, perfusion to these peripheral fibers fails first, producing ischemic injury manifesting as necrosis, vacuolation, myophagocytosis, and eventual perifascicular atrophy and regeneration. This is the pathologic signature of dermatomyositis on muscle biopsy.

In the case presented, this pathophysiology is directly reflected. The biopsy demonstrated "moderate numbers of necrotic, vacuolated degenerate, myophagocytosed and regenerative myofibres in a perifascicular distribution," along with "scattered perimysial inflammation." Critically, immunohistochemistry revealed "C5b-9 deposition in perifascicular endomysial capillaries, a feature typical of dermatomyositis." This MAC deposition in capillaries rather than on the sarcolemma of individual muscle fibers is what distinguishes dermatomyositis from immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy, where MAC deposits on individual myofiber sarcolemma without a primary vasculopathic process.

It is also worth noting that the clinical features in this patient — severe neck swelling, strap muscle inflammation, dysphagia, and cutaneous signs — are downstream consequences of this same microangiopathic process. The diffuse edema and muscle inflammation reflect the ischemic and inflammatory cascade initiated by capillary injury. His anti-SAE1 autoantibody status further situates him within a specific serologic subgroup of dermatomyositis, but the immunopathology remains that of complement-mediated vascular injury.

In summary: **dermatomyositis is a complement-mediated microangiopathy. MAC (C5b-9) deposition on endomysial capillary endothelium leads to capillary destruction, and the perifascicular pattern of muscle injury reflects ischemic vulnerability of the watershed perifascicular fibers.** This mechanism is the pathophysiologic basis for the biopsy findings in this patient and is the distinguishing feature of dermatomyositis among the idiopathic inflammatory myopathies.
