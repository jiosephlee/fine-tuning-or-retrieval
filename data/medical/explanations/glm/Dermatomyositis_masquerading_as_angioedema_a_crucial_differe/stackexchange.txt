Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Clinical Pearls] What specific clinical features made this patient's neck swelling atypical for hereditary or acquired angioedema, and what single laboratory test was most critical in redirecting the diagnostic evaluation toward an inflammatory myopathy?

## Clinical Teaching Answer

Several features of this patient's presentation were inconsistent with both hereditary and acquired angioedema. First, he had no personal or family history of angioedema and was not taking any medications associated with bradykinin-mediated angioedema—specifically no ACE inhibitors, DPP-4 inhibitors, or NSAIDs. Second, the character of the swelling itself was atypical: the neck was erythematous, and CT imaging demonstrated edema infiltrating the strap muscles and extending into the retropharyngeal space, reflecting a muscular inflammatory process rather than discrete subcutaneous or mucosal swelling typical of angioedema. Third, and most importantly, the patient had concurrent proximal muscle weakness (hip flexor power 4/5 with mild myalgia), dysphagia (later confirmed as severe pharyngeal dysphagia on barium swallow), and subtle but recognizable cutaneous findings—including facial erythema over the nasolabial folds, periungual erythema with nailfold microhemorrhages and avascular areas on capillaroscopy, and a rash on the lateral thigh consistent with a Holster sign. None of these features—myalgia, proximal weakness, dysphagia, or characteristic dermatologic stigmata—are expected in hereditary or acquired angioedema, and their co-occurrence with neck swelling should prompt consideration of an inflammatory myopathy.

The single most critical laboratory test in redirecting the evaluation was the **creatine kinase (CK) level**, which returned markedly elevated at 16,184 U/L. This single result immediately shifted the diagnostic framework from a primary immunologic or allergic disorder (angioedema) to an inflammatory myopathy with muscle injury. While subsequent testing—including ANA, a myositis antibody panel positive for anti-SAE1, MRI of the thighs, and muscle biopsy—was essential for confirming dermatomyositis, it was the CK that served as the pivotal first clue. This case underscores the importance of performing a careful general physical examination in patients with apparently localized complaints and obtaining a CK when muscle weakness, dysphagia, or cutaneous signs coexist with otherwise unexplained soft tissue swelling.

### [Morning Report] A previously well man in his 40s presents with 3 weeks of progressive neck swelling, proximal muscle weakness, and an elevated CRP. What are the key differential diagnoses to consider for subacute neck swelling in this context, and what initial investigations would help distinguish among them?

## Clinical Teaching Answer

Subacute neck swelling with proximal muscle weakness and systemic inflammation demands a structured differential that spans infectious, vascular, allergic/immunologic, and inflammatory myopathic etiologies.

**Infectious causes** are the most immediately life-threatening and were the initial concern in this case. Deep neck space infections — including Ludwig's angina, retropharyngeal abscess, and cellulitis/erysipelas of the neck — typically present with fever, pain, dysphagia, and potential airway compromise. Bacterial sialadenitis may produce focal parotid or submandibular swelling. A CT neck with contrast, as performed here, is the critical first investigation: it delineates abscess formation, fascial plane involvement, and airway patency. In this patient, CT showed diffuse edema involving the strap muscles and retropharyngeal space but no drainable collection, and the patient was afebrile — features that argued against a primary infectious source and prompted further evaluation.

**Vascular and mechanical causes**, particularly superior vena cava syndrome, should be considered when neck swelling is accompanied by facial fullness or venous distension. CT imaging of the neck and chest helps exclude compressive mediastinal pathology.

**Angioedema** — whether histamine-mediated (allergic or drug-related) or bradykinin-mediated (hereditary or acquired, including ACE inhibitor–associated) — is a key consideration when neck swelling is diffuse and non-pitting. The clinical profile here was atypical: there was no exposure to ACE inhibitors, DPP-4 inhibitors, or NSAIDs, no personal or family history of angioedema, and no urticaria or pruritus. Complement studies (C3, C4, and C1 esterase inhibitor level and function) were normal, excluding hereditary and acquired angioedema. The presence of proximal muscle weakness and elevated inflammatory markers further pointed away from angioedema.

**Inflammatory myopathies** — particularly dermatomyositis, immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy, and inclusion body myositis — become the leading consideration when neck swelling coexists with proximal weakness and skin or nailfold changes. In this case, the crucial diagnostic clue was recognition of subtle cutaneous findings (periungual erythema, a Holster sign, and nailfold microhemorrhages with avascular areas on capillaroscopy), which, combined with the atypical nature of the neck edema, prompted measurement of **creatine kinase**. The markedly elevated CK (16,184 U/L), along with elevated transaminases and LDH, confirmed muscle injury. MRI of the thighs demonstrated active myositis, and a muscle biopsy showing perifascicular necrosis and C5b-9 deposition on endomysial capillaries confirmed dermatomyositis. A positive anti-SAE1 autoantibody provided serologic specificity.

The key teaching point is this: when neck swelling is subacute, accompanied by weakness or systemic inflammation, and does not conform to typical angioedema, **a creatine kinase level should be checked early**, and a thorough skin and nailfold examination should be performed. A normal CK would redirect attention toward infection, angioedema, or non-myopathic causes; a markedly elevated CK, as in this case, immediately narrows the differential toward an inflammatory myopathy and initiates the appropriate diagnostic and therapeutic pathway. Complement studies and drug history help exclude angioedema, while imaging and nasendoscopy exclude deep neck space infection. In dermatomyositis presenting with edema, the swelling reflects inflammation of subcutaneous tissue and muscle rather than bradykinin- or histamine-mediated vascular permeability — a distinction that is essential because the treatment is immunosuppression, not antihistamines or C1 esterase inhibitors, and because delayed recognition risks progressive muscle injury, aspiration, and respiratory compromise.

### [Pathophysiology] Dermatomyositis has a characteristic immunopathology that distinguishes it from other idiopathic inflammatory myopathies. What is the mechanism of vascular injury in dermatomyositis, and how does it explain the perifascicular pattern of muscle fiber injury and the deposition of C5b-9 (membrane attack complex) in endomysial capillaries seen on biopsy?

Dermatomyositis is fundamentally a complement-mediated microangiopathy, and this mechanism is central to understanding its histopathology. Unlike polymyositis — which is primarily a CD8+ T-cell–mediated cytotoxic process directed against muscle fibers — dermatomyositis is a humorally driven vasculopathy in which the primary target is the endothelium of endomysial capillaries.

The process is initiated by activation of the classical complement pathway, likely triggered by autoantibodies directed against as-yet incompletely characterized endothelial or microvascular antigens. Deposition of C3b leads to formation of the C5b-9 membrane attack complex on endomysial capillary endothelial cells. This MAC deposition injures and ultimately destroys capillaries, producing a characteristic loss of capillary density. The remaining capillaries often become dilated, and the microvasculature is progressively depleted.

This capillary dropout is key to explaining the perifascicular pattern of muscle fiber injury. Perifascicular fibers — those at the outer rim of the fascicle — are the most distal in the capillary supply network and thus represent a watershed zone. As capillaries are destroyed by complement-mediated injury, perfusion to these peripheral fibers fails first, producing ischemic injury manifesting as necrosis, vacuolation, myophagocytosis, and eventual perifascicular atrophy and regeneration. This is the pathologic signature of dermatomyositis on muscle biopsy.

In the case presented, this pathophysiology is directly reflected. The biopsy demonstrated "moderate numbers of necrotic, vacuolated degenerate, myophagocytosed and regenerative myofibres in a perifascicular distribution," along with "scattered perimysial inflammation." Critically, immunohistochemistry revealed "C5b-9 deposition in perifascicular endomysial capillaries, a feature typical of dermatomyositis." This MAC deposition in capillaries rather than on the sarcolemma of individual muscle fibers is what distinguishes dermatomyositis from immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy, where MAC deposits on individual myofiber sarcolemma without a primary vasculopathic process.

It is also worth noting that the clinical features in this patient — severe neck swelling, strap muscle inflammation, dysphagia, and cutaneous signs — are downstream consequences of this same microangiopathic process. The diffuse edema and muscle inflammation reflect the ischemic and inflammatory cascade initiated by capillary injury. His anti-SAE1 autoantibody status further situates him within a specific serologic subgroup of dermatomyositis, but the immunopathology remains that of complement-mediated vascular injury.

In summary: **dermatomyositis is a complement-mediated microangiopathy. MAC (C5b-9) deposition on endomysial capillary endothelium leads to capillary destruction, and the perifascicular pattern of muscle injury reflects ischemic vulnerability of the watershed perifascicular fibers.** This mechanism is the pathophysiologic basis for the biopsy findings in this patient and is the distinguishing feature of dermatomyositis among the idiopathic inflammatory myopathies.

### [Differential Diagnosis] How do clinical history, medication review, complement studies (C3, C4, C1 esterase inhibitor function and quantity), and the presence or absence of urticaria help distinguish histaminergic angioedema, hereditary angioedema due to C1 inhibitor deficiency, acquired C1 inhibitor deficiency, and non-mediator-mediated swelling such as the pseudoangioedema of dermatomyositis?

**Distinguishing Histaminergic Angioedema, Hereditary Angioedema, Acquired C1 Inhibitor Deficiency, and Pseudoangioedema**

The evaluation of angioedema begins with three key clinical anchors: the presence or absence of urticaria, the medication history, and the tempo and associated symptoms of the swelling. Together with complement studies, these features narrow the differential into histamine-mediated, bradykinin-mediated, and non-mediator-mediated categories.

**Histaminergic angioedema** is typically accompanied by urticaria and pruritus, reflects mast cell degranulation, and may be triggered by allergens, medications, or physical stimuli. It responds to antihistamines, epinephrine, and corticosteroids. Complement studies — C3, C4, and C1 esterase inhibitor (C1-INH) function and quantity — are characteristically normal. The swelling is episodic, self-limited (usually resolving within 24–48 hours), and lacks a family history of angioedema.

**Hereditary angioedema due to C1-INH deficiency** is bradykinin-mediated and distinguished by the *absence* of urticaria and the *failure* to respond to antihistamines or epinephrine. It is autosomal dominant, so a careful family history is critical. Attacks are typically episodic and may be provoked by trauma, dental procedures, stress, estrogen-containing medications, or ACE inhibitors. Complement studies show a low C4 (often even between attacks), normal or low C3, and reduced C1-INH function and/or quantity. C1q is typically normal.

**Acquired C1-INH deficiency** shares the bradykinin-mediated, non-urticarial phenotype but differs in lacking a family history and presenting later in life, typically in association with B-cell lymphoproliferative disorders or autoantibodies against C1-INH. The distinguishing laboratory feature is a low C1q (in contrast to hereditary angioedema, where C1q is normal), alongside low C4, low C1-INH function and quantity, and usually normal C3.

**Pseudoangioedema of dermatomyositis**, as illustrated by this case, is neither histamine- nor bradykinin-mediated. The swelling arises from inflammation of subcutaneous tissue and muscle rather than from vasoactive mediator release. Several features in this patient pointed away from mediator-mediated angioedema and toward an inflammatory myopathy:

- **Clinical history:** The swelling was subacute and progressive over three weeks, not episodic. He had no personal or family history of angioedema, which argued against hereditary angioedema. The absence of urticaria ruled against histaminergic angioedema.
- **Medication review:** He was not taking ACE inhibitors, DPP-4 inhibitors, NSAIDs, or any other agents known to precipitate bradykinin-mediated or histaminergic angioedema.
- **Complement studies:** C3, C4, and C1-INH function and quantity were all normal, excluding both hereditary and acquired C1-INH deficiency.
- **Associated features:** The key clues were proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, periungual erythema, nailfold microhaemorrhages on capillaroscopy, facial erythema, and an elevated CK of 16,184 U/L — none of which are features of histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema. The neck swelling was caused by myositis and soft-tissue inflammation, as confirmed by MRI, FDG-PET, and muscle biopsy showing perifascicular necrosis and C5b-9 deposition in endomysial capillaries, consistent with dermatomyositis.

In summary, urticaria points toward histaminergic angioedema; its absence shifts the focus toward bradykinin-mediated or non-mediator-mediated causes. A family history and low C4 with reduced C1-INH function point to hereditary angioedema, while low C1q and adult onset without family history suggest acquired C1-INH deficiency. When all complement studies are normal, urticaria is absent, the swelling is progressive rather than episodic, and there are features of systemic inflammation — particularly muscle weakness, dysphagia, and cutaneous signs such as periungual changes — the diagnosis of pseudoangioedema from an inflammatory myopathy such as dermatomyositis should be actively considered. A CK level is a rapid, inexpensive, and decisive test in this setting.

### [Clinical Pearls] Anti-SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis has a distinct clinical profile compared with other myositis-specific antibody subtypes. What are the characteristic cutaneous features, key extramuscular complications such as dysphagia and interstitial lung disease, and the associated malignancy risk that clinicians should be aware of?

Anti-SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis carries a distinct clinical signature that clinicians should recognize promptly. Cutaneously, it classically manifests with heliotrope rash, Gottron's papules, V-neck sign, and shawl sign — the hallmark dermatomyositis eruptions. As illustrated in this case, however, the presentation can be more subtle: the patient lacked both Gottron's papules and a heliotrope eruption, instead displaying periungual erythema, a positive Holster sign, violaceous facial erythema, and nailfold microhaemorrhages with avascular areas on capillaroscopy. These findings, paired with the dramatic but atypical feature of diffuse neck swelling from subcutaneous oedema and muscle inflammation, underscore that anti-SAE1 disease may initially evade recognition when classic signs are absent.

Extramuscular involvement is notably prominent. In a literature review of 215 patients with anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis, dysphagia occurred in 46% and interstitial lung disease in 43%. This patient exemplifies both: he developed severe pharyngeal dysphagia confirmed on video fluoroscopic swallow study, necessitating nasogastric feeding for approximately two months, and demonstrated abnormal pulmonary function tests with a restrictive pattern along with CT chest abnormalities. The high prevalence of dysphagia is particularly important because it contributes to aspiration risk — as seen with this patient's peribronchial consolidation attributed to micro-aspiration — and because it independently elevates malignancy risk stratification.

Regarding malignancy, anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis carries a substantial associated risk, reported at approximately 18% in the literature. Under the IMACS International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy–Associated Cancer Screening, this patient was classified as high risk by virtue of two high-risk factors (dermatomyositis diagnosis and dysphagia) and two intermediate-risk factors (SAE1 antibody positivity and male sex). This classification warranted comprehensive baseline screening with CT imaging, PET scan, tumor markers, and planned upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy, with ongoing interval surveillance.

In summary, anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis is characterized by classic — but sometimes subtle — cutaneous manifestations, a high frequency of dysphagia and interstitial lung disease, and a clinically significant malignancy risk warranting structured, risk-stratified cancer screening and longitudinal monitoring.

### [Management] In this case, mycophenolate mofetil was selected over methotrexate as the steroid-sparing agent of choice. What was the specific rationale for this decision, and what disease-specific and patient-specific factors should guide the choice between methotrexate, mycophenolate mofetil, azathioprine, tacrolimus, and rituximab in dermatomyositis?

In this case, mycophenolate mofetil was chosen over methotrexate for two explicit reasons documented by the treating team. First, methotrexate was avoided because of its known association with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis — a concern directly relevant here, as the patient had abnormal pulmonary function tests showing a restrictive pattern, along with peribronchial consolidation on CT chest. Second, the decision was made in discussion with the patient, who expressed a preference for a daily oral regimen and had concerns regarding methotrexate's side-effect profile. Azathioprine and tacrolimus were not pursued because mycophenolate was well tolerated, making a switch unnecessary.

This reasoning illustrates several important principles that guide selection of steroid-sparing agents in dermatomyositis.

**Methotrexate** is often a first-line steroid-sparing agent due to established efficacy in cutaneous and muscle disease and convenient weekly oral or subcutaneous dosing. However, it should be avoided or used with great caution in patients with existing interstitial lung disease (ILD) or abnormal pulmonary function, as it may precipitate or worsen pneumonitis and fibrosis. It is also relatively contraindicated in significant hepatic or renal impairment, and its mucosal, hepatic, and hematologic toxicities require monitoring. In this patient, the restrictive pattern on spirometry and imaging findings made methotrexate a poor choice.

**Mycophenolate mofetil** is particularly attractive when ILD is present or anticipated, as it has a favorable pulmonary safety profile and is commonly used in connective tissue disease–associated lung disease. It is well tolerated, dosed orally twice daily, and has established use in dermatomyositis for both muscle and skin involvement. Its main limitations are gastrointestinal side effects, myelosuppression, and teratogenicity requiring contraception.

**Azathioprine** remains a reasonable alternative but requires thiopurine methyltransferase (TPMT) testing to risk-stratify for severe myelosuppression. It carries risks of hepatotoxicity, pancreatitis, and significant drug interactions with allopurinol. In this case, once mycophenolate was tolerated, there was no compelling reason to introduce a second agent with a less favorable safety profile.

**Tacrolimus** has evidence supporting its use in dermatomyositis, particularly in refractory ILD, including anti-MDA5–associated rapidly progressive lung disease. However, its side-effect burden — nephrotoxicity, hypertension, neurotoxicity, diabetes, and the need for therapeutic drug monitoring — generally reserves it for cases where other agents have failed or when ILD is the dominant and life-threatening problem.

**Rituximab** is typically reserved for refractory or severe disease, or when rapid B-cell depletion is desired. Of note, this patient did receive rituximab in addition to mycophenolate, reflecting the severity of his presentation with dysphagia, extensive myositis on imaging, and significant functional impairment. Rituximab has particular rationale in antibody-positive dermatomyositis, and some data suggest that anti-SAE1 and anti-Mi-2 subgroups may respond favorably.

The broader teaching point is this: the choice of steroid-sparing agent in dermatomyositis should be individualized based on (1) **disease-specific factors** — particularly the presence or risk of ILD, the myositis-specific antibody subtype, severity of muscle versus cutaneous versus organ involvement, and associated malignancy risk — and (2) **patient-specific factors** — including comorbidities (hepatic, renal, pulmonary), reproductive plans, medication preferences, tolerance, and monitoring feasibility. In this case, the combination of abnormal pulmonary function tests, patient preference for oral therapy, good tolerability of mycophenolate, and disease severity warranting additional rituximab collectively justified the regimen chosen.

### [Management] According to the IMACS International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening, how is malignancy risk stratified in dermatomyositis? What high-risk and intermediate-risk factors were present in this patient, and what surveillance strategy is recommended for patients in the high-risk category?

## Malignancy Risk Stratification in Dermatomyositis: IMACS Framework

The IMACS International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy–Associated Cancer Screening stratifies patients with dermatomyositis into low-, intermediate-, and high-risk categories based on a combination of clinical, serologic, and demographic factors. This risk stratification is critical because dermatomyositis carries one of the strongest associations with malignancy among the autoimmune connective tissue diseases, with reported cancer prevalence ranging from 15–25% in various cohorts, particularly within the first year of diagnosis.

In this patient, two **high-risk factors** were identified: the diagnosis of dermatomyositis itself, and the presence of dysphagia. Dysphagia is recognized as a high-risk feature because it reflects significant pharyngeal and esophageal muscle involvement and has been repeatedly associated with underlying malignancy in the dermatomyositis literature. Two **intermediate-risk factors** were also present: the patient was positive for anti-SAE1 autoantibodies and was male. Anti-SAE1 antibody–positive dermatomyositis has been associated with a malignancy rate of approximately 18% in published cohorts, placing it in an intermediate-risk tier. Male sex is an independent intermediate-risk factor, as male patients with dermatomyositis consistently demonstrate higher cancer rates than female patients across multiple studies.

When high-risk and intermediate-risk factors coexist, as in this case, the patient is classified in the **high-risk category** overall, warranting the most comprehensive surveillance strategy. The recommended approach for high-risk patients includes thorough baseline malignancy screening with cross-sectional imaging — typically CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis — as well as consideration of whole-body FDG-PET imaging to detect occult malignancy not apparent on conventional CT. Tumor marker panels and age- and sex-appropriate endoscopic evaluation, including gastroscopy and colonoscopy, should be performed to exclude gastrointestinal and colorectal malignancy. This is particularly relevant given the known association between dermatomyositis and gastrointestinal cancers. In this patient, CT imaging of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis was performed along with FDG-PET, tumor markers (CEA, CA19-9, CA-125, PSA), and planned upper and lower GI endoscopy — consistent with high-risk category surveillance.

Importantly, high-risk patients require not only comprehensive baseline evaluation but also **ongoing interval surveillance**, as the risk of malignancy persists, particularly during the first three years following dermatomyositis diagnosis. In this case, the plan for continued monitoring based on symptoms, clinical examination, regular blood tests, and serial CT chest imaging reflects the principle that cancer screening in dermatomyositis is not a one-time exercise but a longitudinal commitment. The reassuring findings to date — no metabolically active disease on PET, no radiographic evidence of primary or metastatic malignancy, and normal tumor markers — do not obviate the need for continued vigilance.

A clinically relevant nuance worth emphasizing is that certain myositis-specific antibodies carry particularly strong cancer associations — most notably anti-TIF1-γ and anti-NXP-2 antibodies, which confer the highest malignancy risk and would independently place a patient in a high-risk category. While anti-SAE1 is classified as intermediate-risk in this framework, the presence of additional high-risk clinical features (dermatomyositis diagnosis and dysphagia) in this patient justified escalation to a high-risk surveillance protocol. This case illustrates the practical application of the IMACS guideline: risk is determined by the aggregate burden of individual risk factors rather than any single feature alone, ensuring that patients like this one — with multiple concerning elements even in the absence of the highest-risk autoantibodies — receive the thorough malignancy evaluation that the dermatomyositis diagnosis demands.

### [Pathophysiology] Dysphagia is a significant complication of dermatomyositis, particularly in anti-SAE1-positive disease, and was a prominent feature in this patient's presentation. What is the proposed mechanism of dysphagia in dermatomyositis, and what diagnostic modalities should be used to evaluate its severity and guide nutritional management decisions such as nasogastric tube placement?

**Mechanism of Dysphagia in Dermatomyositis and Diagnostic Evaluation**

Dysphagia in dermatomyositis arises primarily from inflammatory involvement of the striated musculature of the pharynx and upper esophagus. The same complement-mediated microangiopathy that characterizes dermatomyositis—exemplified in this patient by perifascicular endomysial capillary deposition of C5b-9 on muscle biopsy—produces ischemic injury and myonecrosis in a perifascicular distribution. When this process affects the pharyngeal constrictors and strap muscles, it impairs the coordinated muscular contraction necessary for the oral and pharyngeal phases of swallowing. In this case, CT imaging demonstrated diffuse edema and inflammation involving the strap muscles with extension into the retropharyngeal space, providing a structural correlate for the patient's swallowing dysfunction. Notably, flexible nasendoscopy revealed no fixed anatomical obstruction but did show increased secretions and coughing—findings consistent with functional pharyngeal weakness rather than a mechanical lesion.

The severity of dysphagia should be assessed with a **videofluoroscopic (barium) swallow study**, which is the diagnostic standard for characterizing the phases of swallowing affected, identifying residue and aspiration risk, and determining whether dietary modifications or feeding tube placement are necessary. In this patient, the swallow study demonstrated severe pharyngeal dysphagia with pyriform fossae residue and post-swallow penetration of thin and mildly thick fluids to the level of the vocal cords. These objective findings—particularly the risk of frank aspiration, supported by the concurrent CT evidence of peribronchial consolidation attributed to micro-aspiration—provided direct justification for nasogastric tube placement. This approach allowed safe medication and nutritional administration during the prolonged recovery period, with the tube remaining in place for two months until sufficient swallow function returned.

In summary, dysphagia in dermatomyositis reflects inflammatory myopathy of pharyngeal and upper esophageal striated muscle, and its severity should be evaluated with videofluoroscopic swallow studies to objectively quantify aspiration risk and guide decisions regarding enteral feeding support.
