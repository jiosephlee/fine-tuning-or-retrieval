Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

## When Angioedema Isn't: A Framework for Subacute Neck Swelling That Defies Classic Pathways

A man in his 40s walks into your emergency department with three weeks of progressive neck swelling. The first thought? Ludwig's angina or a deep neck space infection. CT neck shows diffuse soft tissue edema involving the strap muscles and extending into the retropharyngeal space — but no abscess, no source. ENT scopes him, finds nothing structural. The baton passes to immunology: "suspected idiopathic angioedema." But here's the catch — he's not on an ACE inhibitor, not on a DPP-4 inhibitor, has no family history, and his complement levels and C1 esterase function come back completely normal. So what do you do when the angioedema pathway doesn't close? This case, published by Chan and colleagues, is a masterclass in what happens when you stop anchoring on the obvious and start looking at what's actually in front of you.

## The Case: What They Almost Missed

This previously well, non-smoking patient presented with subacute neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness in his hip flexors with mild myalgia — a symptom that was initially treated as a footnote rather than a clue. He was afebrile, vitally stable, with a CRP of 55.6 mg/L and a white cell count of 9.1. After ENT excluded a surgical cause, the immunology team took a careful second look. What they found was hiding in plain sight: subtle periungual erythema and nailfold microhaemorrhages on capillaroscopy, generalized facial erythema over the nasolabial folds, a rash on his right lateral thigh, and hip flexor power reduced to 4/5. No heliotrope rash. No Gottron's papules. The classic dermatomyositis textbook features were absent.

The turning point was a creatine kinase level. It came back at 16,184 U/L. That single test reframed the entire presentation.

## Beyond Histamine and Bradykinin: When the Complement Pathway Is Normal

Angioedema is one of those diagnoses that can become a diagnostic wastebasket. When you see neck or facial swelling and the airway is intact, it's easy to default to "idiopathic angioedema" and move on. But this case forces us to think differently. The standard angioedema workup — medication history (ACE inhibitors, DPP-4 inhibitors, NSAIDs), family history, complement C3/C4, C1 esterase inhibitor function and quantification — was unrevealing. No trigger. No pathway. No diagnosis. That should be your signal to stop and reconsider, not to label it idiopathic and discharge.

The key question is: what else causes subacute neck soft tissue swelling? The answer is broader than most of us carry in our default workup. This case reminds us to consider inflammatory myopathies, particularly dermatomyositis, where subcutaneous edema — sometimes called pseudoangioedema — is rare but well described. In a cohort of 121 dermatomyositis patients, only 8 had angioedema-like swelling. In SAE1-positive dermatomyositis specifically, a literature review of 10 cases found only three described edema, and just one mentioned throat-region swelling. It is uncommon, but it happens, and when it does, the stakes are high. This patient had severe pharyngeal dysphagia with post-swallow penetration to the vocal cords on barium swallow, aspiration pneumonitis on CT chest, and active myositis across multiple muscle groups on MRI and PET imaging. Delayed diagnosis could have meant airway compromise or ongoing aspiration.

## The Subtle Signs That Change Everything

What makes this case instructive is how easily the cutaneous and muscular clues could have been overlooked. The patient lacked the hallmark heliotrope eruption and Gottron's papules that most clinicians associate with dermatomyositis. Instead, he had periungual erythema, a positive Holster sign (erythema on the lateral thighs), nailfold microhaemorrhages with avascular areas on capillaroscopy, and proximal weakness. These are the signs that sit in the footnotes of the textbook chapter — present enough to clinch the diagnosis if you look for them, subtle enough to miss entirely if you don't.

The laboratory pattern was equally telling. Beyond the dramatically elevated CK, the patient had elevated transaminases (ALT 143, AST 396) and LDH of 692, reflecting muscle-derived enzyme leakage rather than hepatic dysfunction. His ANA was positive at 1:1280 with a speckled pattern. The myositis antibody panel came back strongly positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies, and the muscle biopsy showed perifascicular necrosis, myophagocytosis, and complement C5b-9 deposition in perifascicular endomysial capillaries — the histological signature of dermatomyositis.

## A Practical Framework for Subacute Neck Swelling

When faced with neck or facial swelling that doesn't fit classic angioedema, a systematic approach is essential. First, confirm the swelling is truly edematous and not infectious — CT imaging and ENT evaluation remain critical first steps. If infection is excluded and the angioedema workup is negative (no offending medications, no family history, normal complement and C1 esterase), the next question should be: are there systemic features? Ask about and examine for proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, dyspnea, skin changes in sun-exposed and extensor surfaces, nailfold abnormalities, and constitutional symptoms. If any of these are present, send a CK. It's cheap, it's fast, and in this case it was the single most important diagnostic test.

The broader differential for non-angioedema neck swelling includes superior vena cava syndrome, orofacial granulomatosis, hypocomplementemic urticarial vasculitis, deep neck space infections that may not be immediately obvious on initial imaging, cellulitis or erysipelas, and drug hypersensitivity reactions such as DRESS. Each of these has a distinct clinical signature, but they share one thing: they won't be found if you stop at "idiopathic angioedema."

## Treatment and the Importance of Speed

This patient received pulse-dose intravenous methylprednisolone (1000 mg daily for 3 days), high-dose prednisone taper, loading IVIG at 2 g/kg over 5 days followed by 5 additional monthly cycles, mycophenolate mofetil, and two doses of rituximab. Methotrexate was deliberately avoided due to concerns about interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis given his abnormal pulmonary function tests. He required nasogastric feeding for persistent dysphagia for two months post-discharge. At three weeks, his CK normalized. At three months, neck swelling fully resolved. At seven months, prednisone was discontinued and dysphagia had fully resolved. This was not a mild case — the speed of immunosuppression mattered.

A critical additional consideration: this patient carried multiple malignancy risk factors. Per IMACS guidelines, dermatomyositis itself and dysphagia are high-risk features, while male sex and SAE1 antibody positivity are intermediate-risk features. He underwent CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, PET scan, tumor markers, and was scheduled for gastroscopy and colonoscopy. In SAE1-positive dermatomyositis, malignancy has been reported in approximately 18% of cases. This is not a diagnosis you make and walk away from — ongoing surveillance is part of the package.

## Key Take-Home Points

- When angioedema workup is negative — no trigger medications, no family history, normal complement and C1 esterase — do not label it idiopathic. Broaden the differential.
- Send a creatine kinase level in any patient with unexplained neck or facial swelling plus weakness, myalgia, dysphagia, or skin changes. It may be the single most important test you order.
- Dermatomyositis can present without classic heliotrope rash or Gottron's papules. Look for periungual erythema, nailfold capillary changes, Holster sign, shawl sign, and subtle proximal weakness.
- Pseudoangioedema in dermatomyositis is rare but real. It reflects muscle and soft tissue inflammation, not histamine or bradykinin-mediated pathways, and it requires immunosuppression, not antihistamines or bradykinin blockers.
- Dysphagia in dermatomyositis is high risk — for aspiration, for malnutrition, and for malignancy. Assess swallowing early and aggressively.
- SAE1-positive dermatomyositis carries a malignancy risk of approximately 18%. Screen comprehensively and surveil longitudinally.
- Treat aggressively and early. This patient needed pulse steroids, IVIG, mycophenolate, and rituximab — and still required NG feeding for two months. Delayed treatment risks irreversible muscle damage and aspiration complications.



# The $7 Test That Reframed the Entire Differential: Why CK Belongs in the Workup of Unexplained Swelling

## The Setup: A Neck That Didn't Fit

A previously well man in his 40s walks into the ED with three weeks of progressive neck swelling. He's afebrile, saturating fine, looks uncomfortable but not toxic. CT neck shows diffuse soft tissue edema involving the strap muscles and extending into the retropharyngeal space. ENT is consulted for possible Ludwig's angina. They scope him — no abscess, no anatomic abnormality, just increased secretions and a surprising amount of coughing. The swelling doesn't look like infection. It doesn't behave like infection either: subacute onset, no fever, no leucocytosis, only a modestly elevated CRP of 55.6 mg/L. So the baton passes to immunology for "suspected angioedema."

Except it doesn't really look like angioedema either. The swelling is erythematous and non-tender. It's been progressing over three weeks — far too slow for hereditary or acquired angioedema, which typically evolve over hours. He's on no ACE inhibitors, no DPP-4 inhibitors, no NSAIDs. No personal or family history of angioedema. Complement studies later come back entirely normal. But here's the thing — nobody notices what doesn't fit until someone takes a really careful look.

## The Pivot: One Creatine Level, One Diagnosis

The immunologist who reviewed this patient noticed three things that the preceding teams hadn't fully connected: subtle periungual erythema, a rash on the right lateral thigh, and hip flexor power that was slightly reduced at 4/5. None of these findings were dramatic. None would have been flagged on a routine ED exam. But together, in the context of neck swelling that clearly wasn't angioedema, they pointed toward muscle. So the immunologist did something elegant in its simplicity: they checked a creatine kinase.

It came back at **16,184 U/L**.

That single result reframed everything. The neck swelling wasn't angioedema at all — it was pseudoangioedema, subcutaneous tissue edema driven by intense underlying muscle inflammation. The patient had anti-SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis, confirmed later by myositis antibody panel, MRI, FDG-PET, and muscle biopsy showing perifascicular necrosis with C5b-9 complement deposition in endomysial capillaries — the histologic signature of dermatomyositis.

## Why CK Matters When Swelling Doesn't Make Sense

The real lesson here isn't about dermatomyositis specifically — it's about the diagnostic habit of reaching for a creatine kinase when you encounter soft tissue swelling that doesn't fit the usual frameworks. Angioedema, whether histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated, evolves over minutes to hours, not weeks. It doesn't come with a CK of 16,000. Deep neck space infections tend to look toxic, or at least show a source on imaging. When swelling is subacute, when it doesn't respond to the usual angioedema workup, and especially when there's even a hint of weakness, myalgia, or dysphagia — a CK level is cheap, fast, and potentially diagnostic.

This patient's CK was over 100 times the upper limit of normal. That's not a borderline result that requires interpretation. That's a result that demands you stop thinking about complement pathways and start thinking about muscle.

## The Differential of a CK Over 10,000

When the CK returns in five figures, the differential narrows considerably. The major categories to consider include inflammatory myopathies (dermatomyositis, polymyositis, immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy), infectious myositis (viral myositis, pyomyositis, rickettsial), medication-induced myotoxicity (statins, particularly in the context of anti-HMGCR antibodies), electrolyte disturbances, and rhabdomyolysis from trauma, seizures, or prolonged immobilization. Malignancy-associated myositis is also a consideration, particularly in older patients and with specific antibody profiles.

In this case, several features pointed specifically toward dermatomyositis rather than other causes of massive CK elevation. The subacute course and prominent cutaneous signs — periungual erythema, a Holster sign, facial erythema over the nasolabial folds — were inconsistent with statin myopathy or traumatic rhabdomyolysis. The patient was on no medications whatsoever, had no trauma, and had no metabolic trigger. The presence of dysphagia, which is reported in roughly 46% of anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis cases, further supported an inflammatory myopathy. And the pattern of muscle involvement on MRI — bilateral obturator internus and externus, hip adductors, gracilis, and distal iliopsoas — was consistent with the proximal-predominant distribution typical of inflammatory myopathy rather than the more focal pattern seen in pyomyositis or the diffuse pattern of rhabdomyolysis.

## Pitfalls That Nearly Cost This Patient Time

Several diagnostic traps are worth highlighting because they nearly delayed this patient's diagnosis.

**Subtle weakness is easy to miss.** This patient had hip flexor power of 4/5 — mildly reduced, but only detectable if you actually test it with resistance. A casual "can you lift your legs?" at the bedside won't catch this. Proximal weakness in dermatomyositis is typically symmetric and slowly progressive, so patients compensate and may not volunteer it as a complaint. In this case, the patient presented with neck swelling; his hip weakness was incidental history that could easily have been overlooked.

**Dysphagia can masquerade as secretion pooling or aspiration risk without an obvious neuromuscular link.** ENT noted increased secretions and coughing on flexible nasendoscopy but attributed this to local pathology. In reality, this patient had severe pharyngeal dysphagia demonstrated on barium swallow, with post-swallow penetration of thin fluids to the vocal cords. Dysphagia in dermatomyositis reflects involvement of the pharyngeal and upper esophageal musculature — muscles that are easy to forget about when you're focused on the neck itself.

**Elevated transaminases don't always mean liver disease.** This patient had an ALT of 143 U/L and an AST of 396 U/L. In isolation, these could prompt a hepatitis workup or imaging for biliary pathology. But AST and ALT are released from skeletal muscle as well as liver, and in the setting of a CK over 16,000, these "liver enzymes" are almost certainly of muscle origin. The AST-to-ALT ratio greater than 3:1 — as seen here — is a classic pattern suggesting a non-hepatic source, particularly muscle. Checking a gamma-glutamyl transferase or fractionated bilirubin can help, but the simplest approach is to recognize that when the CK is sky-high, the transaminases travel with it.

## What Happened Next

This patient received pulse intravenous methylprednisolone at 1 g daily for three days, followed by a prednisone taper. He was loaded with IVIG at 2 g/kg over five days, with five subsequent monthly cycles. Mycophenolate mofetil and two doses of rituximab completed the regimen. The choice to avoid methotrexate was deliberate — the patient had abnormal pulmonary function tests, and methotrexate carries a risk of pulmonary fibrosis that was undesirable in this context.

By three weeks, his CK had normalised to 37 U/L and proximal strength had fully recovered. He needed nasogastric feeding for two months due to persistent dysphagia, but swallowing eventually recovered completely. Cervical swelling fully resolved by three months. Prednisone was discontinued at seven months. At twelve months, he had stable respiratory function and good exercise tolerance.

Given the high-risk malignancy profile — dermatomyositis itself, dysphagia, SAE1 antibody positivity, and male sex — he underwent comprehensive malignancy screening including CT chest, abdomen, and pelvis, FDG-PET, tumor markers, and planned upper and lower GI endoscopy. All imaging and markers have been reassuring to date, but surveillance continues.

## Key Take-Home Points

- **Check a CK when neck or facial swelling doesn't fit angioedema.** Subacute progression, absence of triggers, and normal complement studies should prompt you to think beyond histamine and bradykinin. A CK costs almost nothing and can redirect the entire diagnostic trajectory.

- **Pseudoangioedema is rare but real.** Only about 6–7% of dermatomyositis patients present with angioedema-like swelling. Neck involvement specifically is even less common. But when it happens, it can mimic Ludwig's angina, idiopathic angioedema, or deep neck space infection — and none of those frameworks will explain a CK of 16,000.

- **Test proximal strength properly.** A 4/5 hip flexor can be missed if you don't apply resistance. In any patient with unexplained swelling plus even vague complaints of weakness or myalgia, formal strength testing of proximal muscle groups is essential.

- **AST > ALT with a massive CK means the enzymes are from muscle, not liver.** Don't chase a hepatitis workup when the CK tells you the answer.

- **Dysphagia in dermatomyositis is common and can be severe.** In anti-SAE1 disease, dysphagia occurs in nearly half of patients. It may present as coughing, secretion pooling, or aspiration — symptoms that can be mistaken for ENT pathology if the myopathy isn't recognized.

- **Anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis carries a meaningful malignancy risk.** With multiple risk factors, comprehensive cancer screening and ongoing surveillance are indicated.

- **Subtle skin findings matter.** This patient lacked the classic heliotrope rash and Gottron's papules. His cutaneous signs were periungual erythema, a Holster sign, and facial erythema over the nasolabial folds — findings that are easy to miss but, in context, were diagnostic.



# Anti-SAE1 Dermatomyositis: What This Rare Antibody Tells Us About Phenotype, Prognosis, and Where Myositis Classification Is Heading

## When "Angioedema" Isn't Angioedema

A man in his 40s walks into your emergency department with three weeks of progressive neck swelling. The ENT surgeons get called for Ludwig's angina. The CT shows diffuse soft tissue oedema involving strap muscles and retropharyngeal inflammation — but no abscess, no drainable collection. Flexible nasendoscopy is unremarkable. No airway compromise, no fever, no leukocytosis. So immunology gets consulted for "idiopathic angioedema." But something doesn't fit: he also has proximal hip flexor weakness, subtle periungual erythema, a rash on his lateral thigh, and normal C1 esterase function and complement levels. No ACE inhibitor, no NSAIDs, no family history. This isn't angioedema. A creatine kinase comes back at 16,184 U/L. That single test reframes the entire clinical picture: this is anti-SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis presenting as pseudoangioedema of the neck — a presentation so uncommon that a literature review of 10 published SAE1-positive cases found only one prior description of throat-region swelling.

What makes this case so instructive is not just the diagnostic rarity. It's the fact that the classic cutaneous hallmarks of dermatomyositis — Gottron's papules, heliotrope eruption, shawl sign — were absent. The diagnosis hinged on recognising that muscle inflammation itself can produce subcutaneous tissue oedema, and that myositis-specific autoantibodies identify clinically distinct phenotypes that demand tailored surveillance and treatment strategies.

## The Myositis Antibody Revolution

For decades, inflammatory myopathies were classified by clinical and histopathological criteria: dermatomyositis, polymyositis, inclusion body myositis, immune-mediated necrotising myopathy. That framework is being steadily replaced by antibody-based classification, and for good reason. Myositis-specific antibodies (MSAs) correlate more tightly with clinical phenotype, complication profile, malignancy risk, and prognosis than traditional categories do. They are not just confirmatory tests — they are prognostic tools that should shape your differential, your surveillance strategy, and your counselling from the moment they return.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies target the small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme, a protein involved in post-translational modification via "sumoylation." In the largest published literature review to date encompassing 215 patients with SAE1-positive dermatomyositis, three clinical associations stood out: dysphagia in approximately 46%, interstitial lung disease in approximately 43%, and malignancy in approximately 18%. These are not trivial numbers. Nearly half of these patients will have swallowing dysfunction, nearly half will have pulmonary involvement, and nearly one in five will harbour a malignancy somewhere. When you encounter an anti-SAE1 result, these are the three domains you must actively screen — not passively monitor.

## How Anti-SAE1 Compares to Other Key Antibody Subtypes

Understanding where anti-SAE1 sits among the MSAs helps contextualise the risk profile and guides what you should be looking for.

Anti-MDA5 is the antibody most feared for rapidly progressive interstitial lung disease, particularly in Asian populations, and is also associated with cutaneous ulceration and the amyopathic variant — patients who may have profound skin disease and pulmonary involvement with minimal or no muscle weakness. Anti-TIF1γ carries the strongest malignancy association across the MSA spectrum, particularly in adults over 40, and is also one of the antibodies more commonly linked to subcutaneous oedema and angioedema-like presentations. Anti-Mi-2 tends to produce the "textbook" cutaneous phenotype with prominent Gottron's papules and heliotrope rash, generally responds well to immunosuppression, and carries a more favourable prognosis. Anti-Jo-1, the classic antisynthetase syndrome antibody, is associated with ILD, fever, Raynaud's phenomenon, mechanic's hands, and arthritis — the antisynthetase phenotype triad of myositis, ILD, and arthritis.

Anti-SAE1 occupies a distinct niche. While dysphagia and ILD rates are comparable to or higher than other subtypes, the malignancy association at roughly 18% is substantial enough to warrant structured screening, and the cutaneous presentation can be subtle or atypical — as this case powerfully demonstrates. The patient had a positive Holster sign (erythema on the lateral thighs) and periungual erythema, but no Gottron's papules and no heliotrope eruption. Nailfold capillaroscopy showed microhaemorrhages and avascular areas — findings easily missed without deliberate examination. Without a CK level, this patient could have been treated with antihistamines, bradykinin antagonists, or even intubated for airway protection while the underlying myositis went unrecognised and untreated.

## What Antibody Profiling Means for Malignancy Screening

The International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) uses a risk-stratified approach, and antibody status is central to that stratification. This patient had two high-risk factors (dermatomyositis itself and dysphagia) and two intermediate-risk factors (SAE1 antibody positivity and male sex), placing him squarely in a high-risk category for malignancy. He underwent CT chest, abdomen, and pelvis, PET imaging, tumour markers (CEA, CA19-9, CA-125, PSA), and was scheduled for gastroscopy and colonoscopy — all of which was negative on initial evaluation, but ongoing surveillance is planned based on symptoms, examination, bloods, and serial CT chest imaging.

This is the practical translation of antibody-based classification: anti-TIF1γ positivity should trigger the most aggressive malignancy workup, including age-appropriate endoscopy and cross-sectional imaging, and consideration of repeat screening at intervals. Anti-SAE1 positivity warrants a similarly structured approach given the ~18% malignancy rate. Anti-MDA5 shifts the focus toward ILD surveillance with pulmonary function testing and high-resolution CT, while the malignancy risk is comparatively lower. Anti-Jo-1 demands attention to pulmonary function and ILD progression but carries less malignancy concern. The antibody result does not just confirm a diagnosis — it builds a surveillance roadmap.

## When Classic Signs Are Absent — The Examination That Actually Matters

One of the most important lessons from this case is that dermatomyositis can present without its textbook cutaneous stigmata. The exam findings that ultimately mattered were subtle: periungual erythema, nailfold microhaemorrhages and avascular areas on capillaroscopy, a rash on the right lateral thigh consistent with a Holster sign, proximal weakness graded 4/5 in the hip flexors, and elevated transaminases and LDH that reflected muscle rather than liver pathology. The facial erythema over the nasolabial folds was present but nonspecific. The neck swelling was the dramatic, attention-grabbing feature — but it was the quiet, easy-to-miss findings that pointed toward the correct diagnosis.

The lesson for the bedside clinician: when evaluating unexplained neck or facial swelling that does not fit histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema patterns, deliberately examine the nailfolds, the extensor surfaces of the hands, the upper back, the lateral thighs, and test proximal muscle strength. A CK level is inexpensive and rapidly available. The subcutaneous oedema in dermatomyositis arises from underlying muscle inflammation — it is neither histamine nor bradykinin mediated, and it will not respond to antihistamines, icatibant, or C1 esterase inhibitor.

## Treatment Intensity Matches Phenotype Severity

This patient received aggressive, multi-agent immunosuppression: three days of IV methylprednisolone at 1 gram daily, a prolonged oral prednisone taper, loading and maintenance IVIG at 2 g/kg per cycle, mycophenolate mofetil at 1 gram twice daily, and two doses of rituximab at 1 gram IV two weeks apart. Methotrexate was avoided due to concerns about interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis given his abnormal pulmonary function tests. This is not a mild disease treated with monotherapy — it is a multisystem process with dysphagia severe enough to require nasogastric feeding for two months, aspiration pneumonitis, muscle inflammation confirmed on biopsy and PET imaging, and neck oedema that took three months to fully resolve.

The treatment approach for SAE1-positive dermatomyositis is not standardised, and the largest published cohort showed heterogeneous regimens ranging from steroids alone to multi-agent combinations. But the principle is clear: the phenotype severity should drive treatment intensity. Significant dysphagia, respiratory muscle involvement, or ILD all argue for early, aggressive combination therapy rather than a cautious steroid taper alone.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Check a CK in unexplained neck or facial swelling.** A CK level reframed this entire case. Dermatomyositis-associated subcutaneous oedema is neither histamine nor bradykinin mediated and will not respond to angioedema-directed therapies.
- **Anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis carries a defined phenotype: dysphagia in ~46%, ILD in ~43%, malignancy in ~18%.** Every newly diagnosed patient needs active screening in all three domains — swallow evaluation, pulmonary function testing and CT chest, and structured malignancy workup guided by IMACS risk stratification.
- **Classic cutaneous signs can be absent.** Periungual erythema, nailfold capillary changes, a Holster sign, and proximal weakness may be the only clues. Examine the nailfolds deliberately and test hip flexor and shoulder abductor power.
- **Myositis-specific antibodies are prognostic tools, not just confirmatory tests.** Anti-MDA5 demands aggressive ILD surveillance. Anti-TIF1γ demands the most intensive malignancy screening. Anti-SAE1 demands both dysphagia assessment and malignancy screening. The antibody result should build your surveillance roadmap.
- **Treat according to phenotype severity, not diagnostic label.** Significant dysphagia, aspiration, or ILD warrant early multi-agent immunosuppression, as this case demonstrated with steroids, IVIG, mycophenolate, and rituximab.
- **Subtle transaminitis may be myositis, not hepatitis.** Elevated AST and ALT with an elevated LDH and CK should prompt consideration of muscle inflammation rather than primary hepatic disease.

## The Bottom Line

Myositis classification is moving from clinical-histopathological categories toward antibody-defined phenotypes, and anti-SAE1 is a prime example of why that shift matters. It identifies patients with high rates of dysphagia, ILD, and malignancy who may lack the classic rash, who may present to the emergency department with neck swelling that looks like angioedema, and who need early, aggressive, multi-system evaluation and treatment. The next time you see unexplained facial or neck oedema that doesn't add up, examine the nailfolds, check the CK, and think beyond histamine.



# Dysphagia in Dermatomyositis: More Than Just Proximal Weakness, and Why It Drives Both Morbidity and Malignancy Risk

## The Case That Stops You in Your Tracks

Picture this: a previously well man in his 40s walks into your emergency department with three weeks of progressive neck swelling. ENT is called for suspected Ludwig's angina. The CT shows diffuse neck oedema involving strap muscles and inflammation tracking into the retropharyngeal space. Flexible nasendoscopy is unremarkable. No airway compromise, no abscess, no obvious infection source. The immunology team is consulted for "idiopathic angioedema." And then someone — someone with a sharp eye — notices subtle periungual erythema, a rash on the lateral thigh, and 4/5 hip flexor power. They send a creatine kinase. It comes back at 16,184 U/L.

This was not angioedema. This was anti-SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis presenting with prominent cervical soft tissue swelling — a rare but documented phenomenon where muscle inflammation in the neck produces subcutaneous oedema that mimics bradykinin- or histamine-mediated angioedema. But what makes this case truly instructive is not the neck swelling. It is the dysphagia — severe, persistent, and the single biggest driver of this patient's morbidity over the course of his illness.

## Dysphagia: The Silent Driver of morbidity in Dermatomyositis

When we teach dermatomyositis, we reach for the classic skin signs — Gottron's papules, heliotrope rash, shawl sign — and proximal muscle weakness. What often gets under-emphasised is that the pharynx and upper esophagus are striated muscle too, and they are not spared.

In this case, the patient's video fluoroscopic swallow study revealed severe pharyngeal dysphagia with marked pyriform fossae residue and post-swallow penetration of fluids to the level of the vocal cords. This is high-risk aspiration territory. His CT chest showed patchy peribronchial consolidation, attributed to aspiration pneumonitis — a direct downstream consequence of the swallowing dysfunction. He required nasogastric tube feeding for his entire 21-day inpatient stay and for two full months after discharge.

This is not a minor complication. Dysphagia in dermatomyositis is a direct cause of aspiration pneumonia, malnutrition, prolonged hospitalisation, and significantly impaired quality of life. In the largest published cohort of anti-SAE1-positive dermatomyositis — encompassing 215 patients — dysphagia was present in 46% of cases. Nearly half. That is not a footnote; that is a core feature of the disease phenotype.

## Mechanisms: It Is Not Just Weakness, It Is the Pharynx

The swallowing dysfunction in dermatomyositis is multifactorial but can be broadly understood through two mechanisms. First, inflammatory myopathy directly affects the striated muscles of the pharynx and upper esophagus, producing weakness in the voluntary phase of swallowing — bolus formation, oral transit, and pharyngeal contraction. Second, there may be cricopharyngeal dysfunction, where the upper esophageal sphincter fails to relax adequately, contributing to pooling in the pyriform fossae — exactly what was seen on this patient's barium swallow.

The result is a patient who cannot safely move liquids past the vocal cords without aspiration. In this case, thin and even mildly thickened fluids penetrated to the vocal cords post-swallow, which is why the NG tube was essential rather than optional. Bedside swallowing assessment alone would have been inadequate; the video fluoroscopic study provided the anatomical and functional detail needed to stratify aspiration risk and guide feeding decisions.

## Dysphagia Resolution Lags Behind Everything Else

One of the most striking aspects of this case is the timeline of recovery. Three weeks after discharge, this patient's proximal muscle strength had fully recovered and his creatine kinase had normalised to 37 U/L — a dramatic fall from 16,184. The cervical soft tissue swelling was markedly improved. Yet the nasogastric tube remained in place. Swallowing function had not recovered enough to remove it until two months post-discharge, and full resolution of dysphagia did not occur until seven months after diagnosis.

This temporal dissociation is critical to understand. CK normalises. Strength returns. The face looks better. But the pharyngeal muscles — small, finely coordinated, essential for airway protection — may recover more slowly or require more intensive rehabilitation. Patients and clinicians can be falsely reassured by improving labs and proximal power while the patient remains silently aspirating. Ongoing swallow assessment is not optional in these patients; it is a safety imperative.

## Dysphagia as a Malignancy Risk Marker

Here is where the clinical significance of dysphagia extends beyond the mechanical. Per the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), this patient had two high-risk factors — dermatomyositis itself and dysphagia — plus two intermediate-risk factors: SAE1 antibody positivity and male sex. That placed him in a high-risk category warranting comprehensive malignancy screening.

He underwent CT chest, abdomen, and pelvis, a PET scan, tumour markers (CEA, CA19-9, CA-125, PSA), and was scheduled for upper and lower GI endoscopy. All imaging and markers were reassuring to date, but surveillance is ongoing. The point is this: dysphagia is not just a morbidity marker. In the IMACS framework, it is a signal that should escalate your malignancy screening strategy. If you are not screening these patients comprehensively, you are missing a high-risk subset.

## Practical Approach: What This Means at the Bedside

When you encounter a patient with inflammatory myopathy and any swallowing complaint — even vague, even subtle — do not dismiss it as a minor symptom. Request a video fluoroscopic swallow study rather than relying on bedside assessment alone. The anatomical detail matters: pyriform fossae residue, laryngeal penetration, and cricopharyngeal obstruction each have different management implications. Stratify aspiration risk formally. Consider NG feeding early rather than waiting for a swallow study to confirm what you already suspect clinically.

Assess the chest. This patient's peribronchial consolidation was secondary to micro-aspiration — not interstitial lung disease, not infection — and it resolved with treatment of the underlying myositis and removal of the aspiration source. But aspiration pneumonitis can easily progress to bacterial pneumonia in an immunosuppressed patient on high-dose steroids and rituximab. Pulmonary function tests in this case showed an extrathoracic restrictive pattern, consistent with pharyngeal weakness rather than intrinsic lung disease.

And when the CK comes back normal and the patient says they feel stronger, do not assume the swallow is safe. Reassess it. The pharynx recovers on its own timeline.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Dysphagia in dermatomyositis is common, not rare.** In anti-SAE1-positive disease, it affects nearly half of patients. It reflects striated muscle involvement of the pharynx and upper esophagus, not just proximal limb weakness.
- **Video fluoroscopic swallow study is the assessment tool of choice.** Bedside assessment underestimates aspiration risk. Pyriform fossae residue and laryngeal penetration require formal imaging to identify.
- **Dysphagia drives morbidity.** This patient required NG feeding for over two months, developed aspiration pneumonitis, and had a 21-day inpatient stay. The swallow was the rate-limiting step in recovery, not the limb strength or CK.
- **Dysphagia resolution lags behind other markers.** CK normalised and strength returned within three weeks, but safe swallowing took months. Do not be falsely reassured by improving labs — reassess the swallow formally.
- **Dysphagia escalates malignancy risk under IMACS guidelines.** Dermatomyositis plus dysphagia equals two high-risk factors. These patients warrant comprehensive cancer screening — CT imaging, tumour markers, endoscopy, and ongoing surveillance.
- **Check the nails and the rash.** When neck swelling does not fit angioedema — no medication history, no family history, no complement abnormality — look for periungual erythema, nailfold microhaemorrhages, and subtle cutaneous signs. Then send a CK. It might be 16,000.



# Cancer Screening in Dermatomyositis: Navigating the IMACS Risk Stratification Framework in Practice

A man in his 40s walks in with three weeks of progressive neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness. The ENT surgeons are called for Ludwig's angina. Then immunology is consulted for suspected idiopathic angioedema. The real diagnosis? Anti-SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis — and the moment you make that diagnosis, you've opened a second can of worms entirely: this patient now needs a malignancy workup. Not because he has cancer. Not because he has symptoms of cancer. But because dermatomyositis is one of those rare diseases that carries a paraneoplastic association so significant that ignoring it would be a miss you can't take back.

## The Case That Frames the Problem

This case, published by Chan and colleagues, describes a previously well male in his 40s who presented with severe neck swelling initially misattributed to deep neck space infection and then to idiopathic angioedema. The key to the diagnosis wasn't an advanced imaging modality or a clever biopsy — it was a creatine kinase level, sent because the clinical team noticed that the swelling pattern was atypical for angioedema, that there were subtle nailfold changes, a rash on the lateral thigh, and reduced hip flexor power. The CK came back at 16,184 U/L. A myositis antibody panel returned strongly positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies. Muscle biopsy confirmed perifascicular myonecrosis with C5b-9 deposition in perifascicular endomysial capillaries — classic dermatomyositis.

But here's where the case gets interesting beyond the diagnostic puzzle. Once dermatomyositis was confirmed, the question wasn't just about immunosuppression — it was about cancer screening. This patient was relatively young, had no constitutional symptoms, and yet the framework used by his treating team classified him as high risk for malignancy. That classification drove a comprehensive and ongoing surveillance strategy that deserves unpacking.

## Dermatomyositis and Malignancy: The Scale of the Problem

The association between dermatomyositis and malignancy is one of the strongest and most consistent paraneoplastic relationships in all of medicine. Meta-analyses have consistently shown that cancer is diagnosed in roughly 15–25% of dermatomyositis patients, with most malignancies discovered within the first year of diagnosis — often concurrent with or even preceding the myositis itself. The risk is highest at diagnosis and declines over subsequent years, which is why the intensity of screening is front-loaded.

Not all dermatomyositis patients carry the same risk, however. This is where the IMACS (International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group) framework comes in, and where this case becomes a practical teaching example.

## The IMACS Risk Stratification Framework Applied

The IMACS guideline for idiopathic inflammatory myopathy-associated cancer screening stratifies patients based on risk factors, each assigned as high or intermediate. The idea is straightforward: not every dermatomyositis patient needs a PET-CT and endoscopic evaluation, but those who do should get it promptly.

In this case, the patient had two high-risk factors and two intermediate-risk factors:

- High risk: dermatomyositis itself, and dysphagia
- Intermediate risk: anti-SAE1 antibody positivity, and male sex

With two high-risk and two intermediate-risk factors, the patient was classified as high risk for malignancy overall. This triggered a comprehensive screening protocol.

Let's break down why each factor matters.

**Dysphagia** is one of the most significant predictors of malignancy in dermatomyositis. It reflects more severe disease and is frequently associated with anti-TIF1γ and anti-SAE1 antibodies — both of which carry elevated cancer risk. In the largest published cohort of SAE1-positive dermatomyositis (215 patients reviewed), dysphagia was present in 46% and malignancy in 18%. This patient had severe pharyngeal dysphagia confirmed on videofluoroscopic swallow study, with post-swallow penetration to the vocal cords.

**Male sex** is an independent risk factor. Multiple cohorts have demonstrated that male dermatomyositis patients carry higher malignancy risk than their female counterparts, though the magnitude varies by population and antibody subtype.

**Anti-SAE1 antibodies** target the SUMO activating enzyme involved in post-translational protein modification. Anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis is relatively rare compared to other antibody subtypes and is characterized by prominent cutaneous features, dysphagia, and interstitial lung disease. Malignancy has been reported in approximately 18% of cases. Anti-TIF1γ antibodies are even more strongly associated with cancer, but anti-SAE1 carries enough risk to warrant full screening.

**Age at onset** is also relevant — the classic bimodal distribution peaks at 45–64 years in adults. This patient was in his 40s, placing him at the younger edge of the adult peak but still within it.

## What Screening Looks Like in Practice

For a high-risk patient under the IMACS framework, screening is comprehensive. In this case, the workup included:

- CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis
- FDG-PET scan
- Tumor markers: CEA, CA19-9, CA-125, PSA
- Plans for gastroscopy and colonoscopy to exclude occult gastrointestinal or colorectal malignancy

The CT chest incidentally revealed multiple non-cavitating pulmonary nodules and peribronchial consolidation attributed to aspiration pneumonitis — not malignancy, but findings that necessitate serial follow-up imaging. The PET-CT showed diffuse abnormal FDG accumulation in multiple muscle groups but no metabolically active disease elsewhere to suggest malignancy. All tumor markers were normal. The CT abdomen and pelvis showed no lymphadenopathy or malignancy.

Notably, the PET-CT served a dual purpose here: it confirmed the extent of active muscle inflammation while simultaneously serving as a malignancy screen — a useful efficiency in a disease where both questions need answering.

At 12 months following diagnosis, the patient remained under surveillance. The plan was for continued monitoring based on symptoms, clinical examination, regular blood tests, and serial CT chest imaging, with gastroscopy and colonoscopy still pending to complete the GI workup.

## The Tension: Thoroughness Versus Over-Investigation

Here's where the practical difficulty lies. This is a relatively young patient with no constitutional symptoms, a negative PET-CT, negative tumor markers, and a negative CT chest/abdomen/pelvis. The yield of gastroscopy and colonoscopy in this setting is likely low — but it's not zero. Anti-SAE1 and anti-TIF1γ antibodies are particularly associated with gastrointestinal and colorectal malignancies, and the IMACS framework, applied rigorously, recommends endoscopic evaluation in high-risk patients.

The cost, procedural risk, and patient burden of endoscopy must be weighed against the risk of missing a treatable early-stage GI malignancy in a patient who is about to be heavily immunosuppressed. Once you start high-dose steroids, IVIG, mycophenolate, and rituximab, you've altered the immune landscape in ways that could mask or accelerate an underlying malignancy — making the pre-treatment window the most important opportunity for detection.

There's also the question of interval surveillance. How long do you keep screening? The IMACS framework suggests intensified screening in the first year, with ongoing risk-adapted surveillance thereafter. For this patient, the plan involves serial CT chest imaging and symptom-based monitoring. The challenge is that evidence to guide the optimal duration and frequency of surveillance is limited — there's no randomized trial telling you when to stop.

## Key Take-Home Points

- **Dermatomyositis carries one of the strongest paraneoplastic associations in medicine** — roughly 15–25% of patients have malignancy, most often diagnosed within the first year.
- **The IMACS framework stratifies risk using high and intermediate risk factors** — dermatomyositis itself and dysphagia are high risk; male sex, anti-SAE1 and anti-TIF1γ antibodies, and age at onset are intermediate risk factors.
- **Two high-risk factors alone, or a combination of high and intermediate risk factors, can classify a patient as high risk** — triggering comprehensive malignancy screening.
- **Screening for high-risk patients includes CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, consideration of PET-CT, tumor markers, and endoscopic evaluation** — with endoscopy being particularly relevant for anti-SAE1 and anti-TIF1γ patients given GI malignancy associations.
- **PET-CT can serve double duty** — confirming active myositis while simultaneously screening for malignancy.
- **Dysphagia is not just a symptom to manage** — in dermatomyositis, it's a malignancy risk marker that should escalate your screening strategy.
- **The evidence base for surveillance duration and frequency is limited** — current practice relies on risk-adapted, symptom-based monitoring with serial imaging, and clinicians must balance thoroughness against over-investigation, particularly in younger patients.
- **Complete the workup before heavy immunosuppression when possible** — the pre-treatment window is the best opportunity to detect an underlying malignancy.

## Bottom Line

Cancer screening in dermatomyositis isn't a one-size-fits-all exercise. The IMACS framework gives you a structured way to stratify risk, but applying it to a real patient — especially a younger one with subtle features and a negative initial workup — requires judgment. The framework told this team to screen aggressively, and they did. The PET-CT was negative. The tumor markers were negative. The endoscopy is still pending. Whether that endoscopy finds anything is uncertain, but the decision to pursue it is defensible given the antibody profile and the stakes of missing an early GI malignancy in a patient about to undergo profound immunosuppression.

The next time you diagnose dermatomyositis — whether it presents classically or, as in this case, masquerading as angioedema — your second thought after "start immunosuppression" should be "what's the malignancy risk, and how do I screen for it?" The IMACS framework is the tool that helps you answer both.



# Multi-Agent Immunosuppression in Severe Dermatomyositis: Rationale, Evidence Gaps, and the Art of Drug Selection

## When the Textbook Doesn't Have a Chapter for Your Patient

A man in his 40s walks in with three weeks of progressive neck swelling, proximal weakness, and dysphagia. His CK is over 16,000 U/L. His barium swallow shows severe pharyngeal dysphagia with penetration to the vocal cords. His pulmonary function tests reveal an extrathoracic restrictive pattern. And his antibody panel comes back strongly positive for anti-SAE1 — a subtype of dermatomyositis so rare that the largest published cohort to date describes treatment approaches as simply "heterogeneous." That word — heterogeneous — is doing a lot of heavy lifting. It means nobody really knows the optimal regimen. What follows is a masterclass in assembling a rational immunosuppressive strategy when the evidence base is thin, the stakes are high, and the patient is sitting in front of you with a nasogastric tube and aspiration risk.

## The Backbone: High-Dose Steroids, Then a Thoughtful Taper

The team initiated 1000 mg of intravenous methylprednisolone daily for three days, followed by oral prednisone at 60 mg daily with a slow taper of 5 mg every two to three weeks. This is the foundational move in severe dermatomyositis — rapid, potent anti-inflammatory effect to halt disease progression and protect vulnerable tissues. In this case, the urgency was compounded by significant pharyngeal and cervical muscle involvement creating real aspiration risk, as evidenced by the barium swallow findings and the peribronchial consolidation seen on CT chest attributed to micro-aspiration.

The tapering strategy deserves comment. A 5 mg reduction every two to three weeks is deliberately conservative. In a patient with this degree of muscle involvement and dysphagia, moving too quickly risks a flare that could re-imperil the airway and swallowing function. And indeed, the patient remained on prednisone for seven months before successful discontinuation — a timeline that reflects both the severity of disease and the time required for steroid-sparing agents to reach full effect.

## Mycophenolate Mofetil: The Chosen Steroid-Sparing Agent

Mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) at 1 g twice daily was selected as the primary steroid-sparing agent. The rationale was multifactorial: established use in dermatomyositis, reasonable tolerability, and a daily oral regimen that aligned with patient preference. MMF has a growing evidence base in dermatomyositis, particularly for cutaneous disease and interstitial lung disease associated with the idiopathic inflammatory myopathies, making it an attractive option in a patient with abnormal pulmonary function tests.

But the choice also illuminates what was deliberately left on the table — and why.

## Why Not Methotrexate? The Pulmonary Calculus

Methotrexate was explicitly avoided. The reasoning: this patient had abnormal lung function tests showing a restrictive pattern, and methotrexate carries a well-recognized association with pneumonitis and pulmonary fibrosis. In a patient who already has potential interstitial lung disease from dermatomyositis itself — and anti-SAE1 positivity carries a 43% reported rate of interstitial lung disease — adding a drug that can cause lung injury would be a diagnostic and therapeutic nightmare. If the patient's lung function deteriorated, you would face an impossible clinical question: is this disease progression or drug toxicity? That distinction matters enormously because the management diverges completely.

This is a critical pearl. In dermatomyositis with any respiratory involvement — whether ILD, aspiration pneumonitis, or restrictive physiology on PFTs — methotrexate should give you pause. The risk-benefit calculus shifts.

## Why Not Azathioprine or Tacrolimus?

Azathioprine and tacrolimus were not used, but the stated reason is telling: mycophenolate was well-tolerated. In other words, once the team had a steroid-sparing agent the patient was handling, there was no compelling reason to switch or add either of these alternatives. Azathioprine requires TPMT testing, carries risks of hepatotoxicity and myelosuppression, and has a slower onset of action. Tacrolimus has a role particularly in interstitial lung disease associated with myositis, but its narrow therapeutic window, renal toxicity, and need for drug-level monitoring make it less attractive as a first-line agent when simpler options are available and the patient is tolerating them.

The decision not to escalate or switch agents speaks to a principle that is easy to lose sight of: if your patient is improving and tolerating the regimen, do not reflexively complicate it. The art of immunosuppression is not just knowing which drugs to start — it is knowing when to stop adding.

## Rituximab and IVIG: Heavy Artillery for Severe Disease

The patient received rituximab at 1000 mg intravenously, two doses two weeks apart, and intravenous immunoglobulin at 2 g/kg loading over five days, followed by five additional monthly cycles. These are not first-line agents for uncomplicated dermatomyositis. They were deployed here because the disease was severe: CK above 16,000 U/L, dysphagia severe enough to require nasogastric feeding for months, diffuse muscle inflammation on MRI and PET scan, and involvement of head and neck musculature creating airway concern.

Rituximab targets CD20-positive B cells and has an expanding evidence base in refractory or severe dermatomyositis and polymyositis. The RIM trial — while technically negative on its primary endpoint — showed that a substantial proportion of patients improved, and subsequent real-world data and meta-analyses have supported its use, particularly in patients with myositis-specific antibodies. In anti-SAE1 disease specifically, the evidence is limited to case reports and small case series, but rituximab is increasingly used in severe presentations.

IVIG is a fascinating agent in this context. It has immunomodulatory effects through multiple mechanisms — Fc receptor blockade, modulation of complement, effects on B and T cell function — and has demonstrated benefit in dermatomyositis, particularly for cutaneous disease and refractory cases. Its use here as both loading and maintenance therapy reflects the severity of the presentation and the need for rapid, multi-pronged immunosuppression. The decision to continue monthly IVIG cycles for six months underscores that this was not a "one and done" induction — it was a sustained campaign.

The sequencing matters. Steroids provided immediate anti-inflammatory effect. IVIG likely contributed to rapid disease modulation during the critical early phase. Rituximab offered deeper, longer-lasting B cell depletion. MMF served as the sustained oral maintenance platform. This is multi-layered immunosuppression designed to address disease at multiple immunopathological levels simultaneously.

## The Opportunistic Infection Prophylaxis Problem

Here is where the case takes a turn that every clinician managing heavy immunosuppression should pay attention to. The patient was started on sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim for Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia prophylaxis — standard practice when you are throwing this much immunosuppression at a patient. But he developed hemolytic anemia, and notably, this occurred in the absence of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency. TMP-SMX was ceased, and nebulized pentamidine 300 mg every four weeks was substituted.

This is a crucial practical point. TMP-SMX is the gold standard for PJP prophylaxis, but its adverse effect profile is not trivial — rash, cytopenias, hepatitis, and yes, hemolysis even in patients without G6PD deficiency. When you are already managing a patient on four immunosuppressive agents, a drug-induced hemolytic anemia is a particularly unwelcome complication, both diagnostically and therapeutically. Nebulized pentamidine is the logical alternative, though it is worth noting that it covers only PJP, is less effective than TMP-SMX, and carries its own risks including bronchospasm.

The broader lesson: in patients on aggressive multi-agent immunosuppression, the prophylaxis regimen itself becomes a source of morbidity and requires active surveillance and willingness to adapt. Do not set and forget.

## Sequencing and the Dysphagia Problem

One of the most practical challenges in this case was that the patient had severe dysphagia requiring nasogastric tube feeding for two months. This complicates medication delivery — several immunosuppressive agents are available only in intravenous or specific oral formulations, and the ability to reliably administer oral medications through a nasogastric tube depends on formulation, crushing viability, and absorption. MMF suspension is available and can be given via NG tube. Prednisone tablets can be crushed. But the logistics of coordinating IVIG infusions, rituximab infusions, and oral medications through a feeding tube in an inpatient and early outpatient setting are non-trivial and require active pharmacy and nursing involvement.

The aspiration risk also influenced drug selection. Methotrexate was already excluded for pulmonary reasons, but the broader principle is that any immunosuppressive agent that could worsen respiratory function — through pneumonitis, pulmonary fibrosis, or increased infection risk — was viewed with particular caution in a patient with documented micro-aspiration and abnormal PFTs.

## The Malignancy Surveillance Question

Anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis carries a reported malignancy rate of approximately 18%. This patient had two high-risk factors (dermatomyositis itself and dysphagia) and two intermediate-risk factors (SAE1 positivity and male sex) per the IMACS guideline, placing him in a high-risk category for malignancy screening. He underwent CT chest, abdomen, and pelvis, a PET scan, and tumor marker panels, with plans for endoscopy and colonoscopy. While this is not strictly an immunosuppression decision, it is a critical parallel management stream. The immunosuppressive regimen chosen must not interfere with the ability to conduct ongoing malignancy surveillance — another argument against agents that could obscure imaging or cause organ toxicity that mimics malignancy.

## What This Case Teaches Us About Evidence Gaps

The honest truth is that treatment of anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis is not guideline-driven in any meaningful sense. The largest published cohort showed heterogeneous approaches — most patients received glucocorticoids plus a steroid-sparing agent, with methotrexate being most common, but also MMF, rituximab, azathioprine, and IVIG in selected cases. There are no randomized trials. There are no comparative effectiveness studies. There are no validated treatment algorithms for this antibody subtype.

What this case demonstrates is a rational, principle-based approach to navigating that evidence void: match the intensity of immunosuppression to the severity of disease, avoid agents with overlapping toxicity profiles for vulnerable organs, build in redundancy through multi-agent therapy with different mechanisms of action, anticipate and manage opportunistic infection prophylaxis proactively, and maintain vigilant malignancy surveillance as a parallel stream. The patient achieved full recovery of proximal muscle strength, normalization of CK, complete resolution of cervical swelling, and full recovery of swallowing function by seven months. That outcome supports the strategy — while acknowledging that in the absence of controlled trials, we are building the plane while flying it.

## Key Take-Home Points

- **Severe dermatomyositis warrants multi-agent immunosuppression.** This case used pulse steroids, IVIG, rituximab, and mycophenolate simultaneously — matching intensity to disease severity rather than escalating stepwise while disease progresses unchecked.

- **Avoid methotrexate when pulmonary involvement is present or suspected.** In a patient with abnormal PFTs, potential ILD, and a myositis subtype with a 43% rate of interstitial lung disease, methotrexate's pneumonitis risk creates an unacceptable diagnostic ambiguity if respiratory status worsens.

- **TMP-SMX prophylaxis is not benign.** Hemolytic anemia occurred without G6PD deficiency in this case. Monitor actively and have a backup plan — nebulized pentamidine is the standard alternative but is less effective and covers only PJP.

- **Anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis carries high malignancy risk.** With dysphagia, male sex, and SAE1 positivity, this patient met high-risk criteria per IMACS guidelines, mandating comprehensive malignancy screening as a parallel management stream alongside immunosuppression.

- **The evidence base for antibody-specific dermatomyositis treatment is thin.** Decisions must be principle-driven: severity-matched intensity, organ-specific toxicity avoidance, multi-mechanism redundancy, and proactive complication management.

- **Dysphagia complicates everything.** From medication delivery to aspiration risk to the duration of nasogastric feeding, pharyngeal involvement in dermatomyositis is a marker of severity that should trigger aggressive treatment and multidisciplinary involvement including speech pathology and dietetics.
