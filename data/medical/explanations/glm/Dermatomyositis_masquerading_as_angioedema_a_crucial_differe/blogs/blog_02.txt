Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# The $7 Test That Reframed the Entire Differential: Why CK Belongs in the Workup of Unexplained Swelling

## The Setup: A Neck That Didn't Fit

A previously well man in his 40s walks into the ED with three weeks of progressive neck swelling. He's afebrile, saturating fine, looks uncomfortable but not toxic. CT neck shows diffuse soft tissue edema involving the strap muscles and extending into the retropharyngeal space. ENT is consulted for possible Ludwig's angina. They scope him — no abscess, no anatomic abnormality, just increased secretions and a surprising amount of coughing. The swelling doesn't look like infection. It doesn't behave like infection either: subacute onset, no fever, no leucocytosis, only a modestly elevated CRP of 55.6 mg/L. So the baton passes to immunology for "suspected angioedema."

Except it doesn't really look like angioedema either. The swelling is erythematous and non-tender. It's been progressing over three weeks — far too slow for hereditary or acquired angioedema, which typically evolve over hours. He's on no ACE inhibitors, no DPP-4 inhibitors, no NSAIDs. No personal or family history of angioedema. Complement studies later come back entirely normal. But here's the thing — nobody notices what doesn't fit until someone takes a really careful look.

## The Pivot: One Creatine Level, One Diagnosis

The immunologist who reviewed this patient noticed three things that the preceding teams hadn't fully connected: subtle periungual erythema, a rash on the right lateral thigh, and hip flexor power that was slightly reduced at 4/5. None of these findings were dramatic. None would have been flagged on a routine ED exam. But together, in the context of neck swelling that clearly wasn't angioedema, they pointed toward muscle. So the immunologist did something elegant in its simplicity: they checked a creatine kinase.

It came back at **16,184 U/L**.

That single result reframed everything. The neck swelling wasn't angioedema at all — it was pseudoangioedema, subcutaneous tissue edema driven by intense underlying muscle inflammation. The patient had anti-SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis, confirmed later by myositis antibody panel, MRI, FDG-PET, and muscle biopsy showing perifascicular necrosis with C5b-9 complement deposition in endomysial capillaries — the histologic signature of dermatomyositis.

## Why CK Matters When Swelling Doesn't Make Sense

The real lesson here isn't about dermatomyositis specifically — it's about the diagnostic habit of reaching for a creatine kinase when you encounter soft tissue swelling that doesn't fit the usual frameworks. Angioedema, whether histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated, evolves over minutes to hours, not weeks. It doesn't come with a CK of 16,000. Deep neck space infections tend to look toxic, or at least show a source on imaging. When swelling is subacute, when it doesn't respond to the usual angioedema workup, and especially when there's even a hint of weakness, myalgia, or dysphagia — a CK level is cheap, fast, and potentially diagnostic.

This patient's CK was over 100 times the upper limit of normal. That's not a borderline result that requires interpretation. That's a result that demands you stop thinking about complement pathways and start thinking about muscle.

## The Differential of a CK Over 10,000

When the CK returns in five figures, the differential narrows considerably. The major categories to consider include inflammatory myopathies (dermatomyositis, polymyositis, immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy), infectious myositis (viral myositis, pyomyositis, rickettsial), medication-induced myotoxicity (statins, particularly in the context of anti-HMGCR antibodies), electrolyte disturbances, and rhabdomyolysis from trauma, seizures, or prolonged immobilization. Malignancy-associated myositis is also a consideration, particularly in older patients and with specific antibody profiles.

In this case, several features pointed specifically toward dermatomyositis rather than other causes of massive CK elevation. The subacute course and prominent cutaneous signs — periungual erythema, a Holster sign, facial erythema over the nasolabial folds — were inconsistent with statin myopathy or traumatic rhabdomyolysis. The patient was on no medications whatsoever, had no trauma, and had no metabolic trigger. The presence of dysphagia, which is reported in roughly 46% of anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis cases, further supported an inflammatory myopathy. And the pattern of muscle involvement on MRI — bilateral obturator internus and externus, hip adductors, gracilis, and distal iliopsoas — was consistent with the proximal-predominant distribution typical of inflammatory myopathy rather than the more focal pattern seen in pyomyositis or the diffuse pattern of rhabdomyolysis.

## Pitfalls That Nearly Cost This Patient Time

Several diagnostic traps are worth highlighting because they nearly delayed this patient's diagnosis.

**Subtle weakness is easy to miss.** This patient had hip flexor power of 4/5 — mildly reduced, but only detectable if you actually test it with resistance. A casual "can you lift your legs?" at the bedside won't catch this. Proximal weakness in dermatomyositis is typically symmetric and slowly progressive, so patients compensate and may not volunteer it as a complaint. In this case, the patient presented with neck swelling; his hip weakness was incidental history that could easily have been overlooked.

**Dysphagia can masquerade as secretion pooling or aspiration risk without an obvious neuromuscular link.** ENT noted increased secretions and coughing on flexible nasendoscopy but attributed this to local pathology. In reality, this patient had severe pharyngeal dysphagia demonstrated on barium swallow, with post-swallow penetration of thin fluids to the vocal cords. Dysphagia in dermatomyositis reflects involvement of the pharyngeal and upper esophageal musculature — muscles that are easy to forget about when you're focused on the neck itself.

**Elevated transaminases don't always mean liver disease.** This patient had an ALT of 143 U/L and an AST of 396 U/L. In isolation, these could prompt a hepatitis workup or imaging for biliary pathology. But AST and ALT are released from skeletal muscle as well as liver, and in the setting of a CK over 16,000, these "liver enzymes" are almost certainly of muscle origin. The AST-to-ALT ratio greater than 3:1 — as seen here — is a classic pattern suggesting a non-hepatic source, particularly muscle. Checking a gamma-glutamyl transferase or fractionated bilirubin can help, but the simplest approach is to recognize that when the CK is sky-high, the transaminases travel with it.

## What Happened Next

This patient received pulse intravenous methylprednisolone at 1 g daily for three days, followed by a prednisone taper. He was loaded with IVIG at 2 g/kg over five days, with five subsequent monthly cycles. Mycophenolate mofetil and two doses of rituximab completed the regimen. The choice to avoid methotrexate was deliberate — the patient had abnormal pulmonary function tests, and methotrexate carries a risk of pulmonary fibrosis that was undesirable in this context.

By three weeks, his CK had normalised to 37 U/L and proximal strength had fully recovered. He needed nasogastric feeding for two months due to persistent dysphagia, but swallowing eventually recovered completely. Cervical swelling fully resolved by three months. Prednisone was discontinued at seven months. At twelve months, he had stable respiratory function and good exercise tolerance.

Given the high-risk malignancy profile — dermatomyositis itself, dysphagia, SAE1 antibody positivity, and male sex — he underwent comprehensive malignancy screening including CT chest, abdomen, and pelvis, FDG-PET, tumor markers, and planned upper and lower GI endoscopy. All imaging and markers have been reassuring to date, but surveillance continues.

## Key Take-Home Points

- **Check a CK when neck or facial swelling doesn't fit angioedema.** Subacute progression, absence of triggers, and normal complement studies should prompt you to think beyond histamine and bradykinin. A CK costs almost nothing and can redirect the entire diagnostic trajectory.

- **Pseudoangioedema is rare but real.** Only about 6–7% of dermatomyositis patients present with angioedema-like swelling. Neck involvement specifically is even less common. But when it happens, it can mimic Ludwig's angina, idiopathic angioedema, or deep neck space infection — and none of those frameworks will explain a CK of 16,000.

- **Test proximal strength properly.** A 4/5 hip flexor can be missed if you don't apply resistance. In any patient with unexplained swelling plus even vague complaints of weakness or myalgia, formal strength testing of proximal muscle groups is essential.

- **AST > ALT with a massive CK means the enzymes are from muscle, not liver.** Don't chase a hepatitis workup when the CK tells you the answer.

- **Dysphagia in dermatomyositis is common and can be severe.** In anti-SAE1 disease, dysphagia occurs in nearly half of patients. It may present as coughing, secretion pooling, or aspiration — symptoms that can be mistaken for ENT pathology if the myopathy isn't recognized.

- **Anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis carries a meaningful malignancy risk.** With multiple risk factors, comprehensive cancer screening and ongoing surveillance are indicated.

- **Subtle skin findings matter.** This patient lacked the classic heliotrope rash and Gottron's papules. His cutaneous signs were periungual erythema, a Holster sign, and facial erythema over the nasolabial folds — findings that are easy to miss but, in context, were diagnostic.
