Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

Chapter 5: ## Management, Outcome, and Clinical Lessons

The management of this patient's dermatomyositis required an aggressive, multidisciplinary approach driven by the severity and distribution of his muscle involvement. The decision to pursue intensive immunosuppression was predicated on several alarming features: clinically significant dysphagia with documented aspiration on fluoroscopic swallow study, proximal muscle weakness, and a markedly elevated creatine kinase (CK) of 16,184 U/L. In dermatomyositis, the severity of muscle enzyme elevation generally correlates with the degree of active muscle inflammation, and levels in the tens of thousands indicate widespread myofiber necrosis. The involvement of pharyngeal and cervical musculature was particularly concerning, as these muscles are essential for airway protection and swallowing, and their compromise places the patient at risk for aspiration pneumonitis—a potentially life-threatening complication that this patient had already begun to demonstrate on imaging. This clinical picture demanded rapid suppression of the autoimmune inflammatory process to halt further muscle destruction and prevent irreversible damage or airway compromise.

Corticosteroids form the cornerstone of initial treatment for moderate to severe dermatomyositis, and this patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for three days. Pulse-dose intravenous corticosteroids are favored in severe inflammatory myopathy when rapid, high-level immunosuppression is needed, particularly when bulbar or respiratory muscles are involved. The mechanism is broad suppression of both cellular and humoral immune pathways, reducing the inflammatory infiltrate in muscle tissue and the complement-mediated microangiopathy that characterizes dermatomyositis. After the pulse phase, the patient was transitioned to oral prednisone at 60 mg daily, with a gradual taper of 5 mg every two to three weeks. This tapering schedule reflects the standard approach of starting at a weight-appropriate high dose (typically 0.5–1 mg/kg/day) and reducing slowly over months, balancing the need to maintain disease control against the well-known adverse effects of prolonged glucocorticoid therapy, including osteoporosis, glucose intolerance, adrenal suppression, and increased infection risk.

Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was incorporated into the regimen given the severity of the presentation, particularly the dysphagia and aspiration risk. IVIG is thought to work through multiple immunomodulatory mechanisms, including blockade of Fc receptors on macrophages, modulation of complement activation, neutralization of autoantibodies through anti-idiotypic interactions, and regulation of B- and T-cell function. It is especially valuable in dermatomyositis because the disease involves a complement-mediated microangiopathy, and IVIG can interrupt the deposition of membrane attack complex on endomysial capillaries. The patient received a loading dose of 2 g/kg (165 g total, administered as 35 g daily over five days), followed by maintenance cycles of 2 g/kg every four weeks for five additional cycles. IVIG is particularly beneficial in cases with prominent cutaneous disease, dysphagia, or esophageal involvement, and its relatively rapid onset compared to oral immunosuppressants makes it valuable as bridge therapy while slower-acting agents take effect.

For long-term steroid-sparing immunosuppression, mycophenolate mofetil was selected at a dose of 1 g twice daily. Mycophenolate inhibits inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase, an enzyme critical for de novo guanine nucleotide synthesis in lymphocytes, which are particularly dependent on this pathway. The rationale for choosing mycophenolate over alternative agents involved several case-specific considerations. Methotrexate was avoided due to its known association with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, a concern heightened by this patient's abnormal pulmonary function tests showing a restrictive pattern. This is a critical point: while methotrexate is one of the most commonly used first-line steroid-sparing agents in dermatomyositis, its pulmonary toxicity makes it a poor choice in any patient with existing or suspected lung involvement. Azathioprine and tacrolimus were not pursued because mycophenolate was well tolerated, and switching agents in a clinically stable patient would introduce unnecessary risk. The decision was also informed by patient preference for a daily oral regimen, highlighting the importance of shared decision-making in chronic disease management.

Rituximab, a monoclonal antibody targeting CD20 on B lymphocytes, was administered as two infusions of 1000 mg intravenously, given two weeks apart. This regimen is the standard dosing schedule adapted from rheumatoid arthritis protocols and has been studied extensively in the Rituximab in Myositis (RIM) trial. B-cell depletion is rational in dermatomyositis because B cells contribute to disease pathogenesis through production of autoantibodies—such as the anti-SAE1 antibody in this patient—and through antigen presentation and cytokine secretion that sustain the inflammatory cascade. In severe or refractory disease, rituximab is often combined with other immunosuppressants, and in this case it was used adjunctively with mycophenolate and IVIG to maximize immunosuppressive depth. The limited evidence specific to anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis means that treatment approaches remain heterogeneous, but the combination strategy employed here reflects the general principle that severe, multi-system inflammatory myopathy warrants multi-agent therapy.

Because the patient was receiving high-dose corticosteroids in combination with multiple immunosuppressive agents, prophylaxis against *Pneumocystis jirovecii* pneumonia was indicated. He was initially started on sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim, the standard first-line agent. However, this was complicated by the development of hemolytic anemia, an adverse event that occurred despite normal glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) activity. Drug-induced hemolytic anemia in the absence of G6PD deficiency is a recognized but less common complication of sulfonamide therapy and can arise through immune-mediated mechanisms. The prophylaxis was switched to nebulized pentamidine 300 mg every four weeks, which delivers the drug directly to the pulmonary epithelium and is a reasonable alternative for patients intolerant of systemic prophylactic agents, though it may be less effective against extrapulmonary disease. This complication illustrates the practical challenges of managing immunosuppressed patients, where the prevention of one infection can introduce new toxicities that require ongoing vigilance and flexibility in management.

The patient's inpatient course lasted 21 days, reflecting the complexity of his presentation and the need to coordinate multiple immunosuppressive infusions, diagnostic workup, and rehabilitation. Persistent dysphagia at the time of discharge necessitated nasogastric tube feeding, underscoring that improvement in bulbar muscle function often lags behind that of limb muscles in dermatomyositis. This is clinically important because dysphagia is both a marker of severe disease and a major determinant of quality of life and nutritional status during recovery. The nasogastric tube was required for an additional two months after discharge before swallowing function improved sufficiently for oral intake.

The short-term outcomes demonstrated a meaningful but incomplete response. After three weeks of treatment, the patient showed moderate improvement in hip flexor weakness and marked improvement in cervical soft tissue swelling, though the latter had not fully resolved. This pattern is consistent with the understanding that subcutaneous edema in dermatomyositis, arising from underlying muscle inflammation rather than from histamine- or bradykinin-mediated vascular permeability, responds to immunosuppression on a timescale that differs from true angioedema. The persistence of dysphagia at discharge highlights that pharyngeal muscle recovery can be particularly slow, requiring prolonged supportive care.

By the medium-term follow-up at three weeks post-discharge, proximal muscle strength had fully recovered and CK had normalized to 37 U/L, a dramatic reduction from the initial 16,184 U/L. The normalization of CK is a reliable biochemical marker of disease quiescence in inflammatory myopathy and provides objective evidence that the immunosuppressive regimen has effectively halted active muscle fiber necrosis. At two months, cervical swelling continued to improve, residual facial erythema and periungual changes were the remaining cutaneous findings, and the nasogastric tube was removed as swallowing became adequate for soft solids. By three months, cervical swelling had completely resolved. This trajectory demonstrates that while dermatomyositis can present with dramatic and alarming physical findings, appropriate aggressive treatment can achieve substantial recovery over weeks to months.

Long-term outcomes were favorable. Prednisone was successfully discontinued at seven months, representing effective steroid sparing through the use of mycophenolate and IVIG as maintenance therapy. A follow-up CT chest at 11 months showed near-complete resolution of the basal ground-glass opacities and airspace consolidation that had been attributed to aspiration, and pulmonary function tests showed mild improvement with a residual restrictive pattern thought to reflect the patient's baseline physiology rather than active disease. At 12 months, respiratory function remained stable with good exercise tolerance. The ongoing management plan involves continuation of IVIG, gradual tapering of mycophenolate over the subsequent six months depending on clinical progress, and continued malignancy surveillance. The latter is particularly important given that this patient carries multiple malignancy risk factors under the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) framework: two high-risk factors (dermatomyositis itself and dysphagia) and two intermediate-risk factors (anti-SAE1 antibody positivity and male sex). He will undergo gastroscopy and colonoscopy to exclude occult gastrointestinal malignancy, and ongoing surveillance will be guided by symptoms, examination, blood tests, and serial CT chest imaging. Anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis has been associated with malignancy in approximately 18% of cases, making structured cancer screening essential.

Several clinical lessons emerge from this case that are directly applicable to clinical practice. First, dermatomyositis should be included in the differential diagnosis of unexplained neck swelling, particularly when the swelling is atypical for angioedema in its distribution, duration, or response to therapy. True angioedema—whether histaminergic (allergic) or bradykinin-mediated (hereditary or drug-induced)—typically produces transient, well-demarcated swelling of subcutaneous and submucosal tissues. This patient's swelling was persistent, involved the strap muscles and retropharyngeal space on imaging, and was accompanied by erythema and systemic inflammation, none of which fit neatly into the angioedema framework. Second, a creatine kinase level is a simple, inexpensive, and rapidly available test that should be requested early when the clinical picture is atypical for angioedema, particularly when muscle weakness, skin changes, or dysphagia are present. In this case, the CK result was the pivotal laboratory finding that redirected the diagnostic workup away from angioedema and toward inflammatory myopathy. Third, careful physical examination focusing on subtle cutaneous and nailfold findings—periungual erythema, nailfold microhaemorrhages on capillaroscopy, a rash on the lateral thigh consistent with a Holster sign—provided important diagnostic clues even in the absence of the classic Gottron's papules and heliotrope eruption. These findings should be actively sought in any patient with unexplained soft tissue swelling and systemic symptoms. Fourth, this case demonstrates the value of multidisciplinary collaboration. The patient was initially evaluated by emergency physicians and ENT surgeons, and the eventual diagnosis required input from immunology, radiology, pathology, and speech-language therapy. The transition from an initial impression of Ludwig's angina to a working diagnosis of angioedema and finally to dermatomyositis illustrates how the differential diagnosis evolves as information accumulates, and how each specialty contributes critical data—nasendoscopy excluding structural pathology, capillaroscopy revealing microvascular changes, and muscle biopsy confirming the histologic pattern of dermatomyositis. Finally, the case underscores that when dermatomyositis presents with severe muscle involvement, particularly affecting bulbar and respiratory muscles, prompt and aggressive multidrug immunosuppression is necessary to prevent disease progression and multiorgan damage, and that favorable outcomes are achievable with timely, coordinated care.
