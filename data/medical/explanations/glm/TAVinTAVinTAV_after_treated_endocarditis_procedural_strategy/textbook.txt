Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

## Case Presentation and Clinical Context

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has become a standard treatment for severe aortic stenosis across a wide spectrum of surgical risk, from inoperable patients to those at low surgical risk. As the population of TAVI recipients grows and survival after implantation extends further, clinicians are increasingly encountering structural valve deterioration in previously implanted bioprostheses. When a transcatheter valve fails, a repeat transcatheter procedure — placing a new valve within the existing one, known as TAV-in-TAV — is often preferable to surgical explantation, particularly in patients with prohibitive operative risk. However, each successive reintervention compounds technical challenges related to coronary access, frame overlap, and residual effective orifice area. The concept of lifetime management in structural valve disease emphasizes that the initial valve choice and implantation strategy should anticipate the likely need for future reinterventions, an consideration that becomes especially important in younger patients and those with long projected survival.

The patient in this case is a man in his late 70s who presented to a tertiary cardiac center with progressive exertional dyspnea, a spastic sensation on the left side of the chest during physical exertion, dizziness, and fatigue. His functional capacity had declined to the point where symptoms limited him to walking only 200–300 meters, consistent with New York Heart Association class III functional limitation. These symptoms were the culmination of a decade-long clinical trajectory involving multiple transcatheter valve interventions and a treated episode of prosthetic valve endocarditis.

His medical history was extensive and central to the decision-making for every intervention. He had three-vessel coronary artery disease managed with multiple percutaneous coronary interventions: the right coronary artery in 2009, the left anterior descending (LAD) artery in 2010, a recurrent LAD intervention in 2012, and the circumflex artery in 2014. He developed third-degree atrioventricular block requiring permanent pacemaker implantation in 2014. Additional comorbidities included arterial hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and recurrent episodes of gastrointestinal bleeding beginning in 2022. This combination of cardiac, metabolic, pulmonary, and bleeding comorbidities placed him at high or prohibitive surgical risk throughout his clinical course and was a primary reason that transcatheter strategies were repeatedly favored over surgical valve replacement.

In 2014, the patient developed progressive degeneration of his native aortic valve with severe stenosis. Given his multiple comorbidities and elevated surgical risk, he underwent transcatheter implantation of a 27 mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific). The Lotus valve is a mechanically expandable transcatheter prosthesis characterized by the ability to be repositioned and fully retrieved prior to final release, as well as a design that produces low paravalvular leak rates. At the time of this initial intervention, TAVI was well established for high-risk surgical candidates, and the device selection reflected the goal of achieving a durable seal in a patient with complex anatomy.

By December 2021, the patient experienced worsening exertional dyspnea, and echocardiographic evaluation revealed findings consistent with severe bioprosthetic valve stenosis. Structural valve deterioration in transcatheter bioprostheses involves progressive leaflet calcification, fibrosis, and restriction, leading to reduced leaflet mobility and elevated transvalvular gradients. Over the following months, the mean transvalvular gradient increased dramatically to 83 mm Hg, and left ventricular ejection fraction declined from 54% in December 2021 to 42%, reflecting the hemodynamic consequences of critical prosthetic stenosis on ventricular function. The severity of gradient elevation and the temporal trajectory of deterioration indicated advanced structural failure of the Lotus prosthesis requiring reintervention.

In May 2022, the patient underwent a TAV-in-TAV procedure with implantation of a 23 mm Evolut valve (Medtronic). The Evolut platform is a self-expanding supra-annular bioprosthesis known for favorable hemodynamic performance, particularly low transvalvular gradients, and the ability to be repositioned during deployment. The self-expanding design was advantageous in this setting because it could adapt to the rigid frame of the existing Lotus valve. The procedure was complicated by a pulsatile femoral hematoma and pseudoaneurysm at the access site, necessitating open vascular surgical repair — a reminder that access-related complications remain a significant source of morbidity in TAVI, even as valve delivery systems have become lower-profile. Following the TAV-in-TAV, the patient's left ventricular ejection fraction improved from 42% to 54%, demonstrating a favorable early hemodynamic response to relief of the obstructive gradient. He was subsequently managed with guideline-directed medical therapy for heart failure, including beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, and diuretics as clinically indicated.

Three months later, in September 2022, the patient was hospitalized with persistent fever and worsening heart failure symptoms. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated severe restenosis of the aortic valve prosthesis with a mean gradient of 35 mm Hg. Blood cultures were positive for *Enterococcus faecalis*, establishing a diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). Prosthetic valve endocarditis is a serious complication defined by infection of the valve prosthesis and surrounding cardiac structures; *Enterococcus* species are among the most common enterococcal pathogens in healthcare-associated endocarditis, and *E. faecalis* in particular is known for its ability to form biofilms on prosthetic material. The patient received a full course of targeted antimicrobial therapy consisting of six weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based treatment, followed by oral therapy as recommended by the infectious disease team. Resolution of infection was confirmed through repeated blood cultures, transesophageal echocardiography, and multislice CT (MSCT), all of which demonstrated no evidence of active endocarditis, vegetations, or paravalvular complications. At the time of the subsequent third TAVI, antimicrobial therapy had been fully completed and no long-term suppressive antibiotic regimen was ongoing.

Despite successful treatment of the endocarditis episode, follow-up assessment in January 2024 revealed renewed and progressive prosthetic valve dysfunction. Echocardiography showed severe aortic stenosis with moderate-to-severe regurgitation, a mean transvalvular gradient of 49 mm Hg, and a markedly reduced aortic valve area of 0.5 cm², calculated using the continuity equation. The mechanism of prosthetic failure in this interval was likely multifactorial, involving both structural leaflet degeneration from the prior infection — with fibrosis and probable mechanical restriction of the leaflets — and progressive structural deterioration of the transcatheter prostheses themselves. The left ventricular ejection fraction had recovered to 66%, suggesting that the ventricle had regained contractile reserve after the earlier period of afterload stress, but the severe prosthetic dysfunction again produced symptomatic limitation. On readmission in June 2024, transthoracic echocardiography confirmed persistent grade 2–3 intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation and the severe stenotic gradient. MSCT revealed a minimum lumen diameter of only 13.7 mm within the Evolut prosthesis, confirming severe hemodynamic compromise and providing critical anatomical data for planning a third transcatheter intervention.

This patient's trajectory illustrates the central clinical challenge that motivates this case report: as the population of TAVI recipients grows and patients survive longer with their implants, an increasing number will present with structural valve deterioration requiring repeat intervention. Each additional valve layer compounds the complexity of future procedures, creating a cascading series of technical problems involving coronary access, frame interaction, and effective orifice area. The concept of lifetime management in structural valve disease holds that clinicians must consider the full trajectory of likely reinterventions from the very first valve implantation — anticipating how initial device choices will influence the feasibility, safety, and hemodynamic outcomes of subsequent procedures. This case, involving a third transcatheter valve implanted after two prior TAVI procedures and a treated episode of prosthetic endocarditis, represents an extreme example of this emerging clinical paradigm and underscores the need for detailed preprocedural imaging, multidisciplinary decision-making, and strategy-driven intervention.

## Pathophysiology of Valve Degeneration and Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis

### Structural Valve Deterioration of Bioprosthetic Transcatheter Valves

Bioprosthetic transcatheter valves, like the 27 mm Lotus valve implanted in this patient in 2014, are subject to structural valve deterioration (SVD) over time. The principal mechanisms include cumulative leaflet damage from cyclic mechanical stress, lipid infiltration, and progressive calcification of the xenograft tissue. These processes lead to leaflet thickening, reduced mobility, and eventual stiffening—producing a functional stenosis that mimics the native calcific aortic stenosis the prosthesis was originally implanted to treat. In some cases, superimposed wear and tear can cause leaflet tears or perforations, resulting in concurrent regurgitation. The typical timeframe for clinically significant SVD is approximately 5 to 10 years after implantation, though this varies with patient age, valve design, and hemodynamic loading conditions. In this patient, approximately 7 to 8 years elapsed between the initial Lotus implantation and the emergence of severe bioprosthetic stenosis, consistent with the expected window for SVD. By December 2021, echocardiographic findings confirmed severe stenosis, and by early 2022 the mean transvalvular gradient had risen dramatically to 83 mm Hg—a value indicating critical obstruction. Notably, this deterioration was accompanied by a decline in left ventricular ejection fraction from 54% to 42% over the same period, demonstrating the direct impact of worsening afterload on ventricular performance.

### Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis: Pathogenesis and *Enterococcus faecalis*

Prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) arises when microorganisms colonize the prosthetic valve surface, typically at sites of endothelial disruption or where foreign material creates a favorable environment for bacterial adherence. The formation of a biofilm on prosthetic material renders bacteria relatively protected from both host immune defenses and antibiotic penetration, contributing to the difficulty of eradication. Transcatheter valves carry a generally lower incidence of PVE compared to surgical prostheses, but the risk persists over the device's lifetime. *Enterococcus faecalis*, the organism isolated in this patient's blood cultures three months after the second TAVI, is a Gram-positive enterococcus that is part of the normal gastrointestinal flora. It is a recognized but less common cause of PVE compared to staphylococci or viridans streptococci. Enterococcal endocarditis tends to affect older patients and those with underlying gastrointestinal pathology, and it can produce subacute presentations with persistent fever and gradual hemodynamic compromise. In this case, the patient presented with persistent fever and worsening heart failure symptoms, with echocardiography revealing severe restenosis (mean gradient 35 mm Hg) superimposed on the recently implanted Evolut valve. The temporal relationship between the second TAVI and the development of PVE within three months suggests that the procedure itself, or the perioperative period, may have served as the inciting event for bacteremia and subsequent prosthetic colonization.

### Interaction Between Treated Endocarditis and Structural Degeneration

A critical question in this case is how the episode of treated endocarditis interacted with the ongoing process of structural degeneration to accelerate valve failure. Although the patient completed a full course of targeted antimicrobial therapy—including six weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based treatment followed by oral therapy—and infection was declared resolved based on clinical assessment, repeat blood cultures, transesophageal echocardiography, and multislice CT, the episode likely left lasting structural consequences. Infective endocarditis can produce direct leaflet injury through several mechanisms: destructive lesions from vegetation-related inflammation, microabscess formation in the periannular region, and mechanical disruption of the leaflet tissue itself. Even after successful microbial eradication, the healing process involves fibrotic repair of the damaged leaflets, which can lead to retraction, stiffening, and restricted leaflet motion. These fibrotic changes would compound any preexisting structural degeneration, effectively accelerating the trajectory toward clinical valve dysfunction. In this patient, the transition from a mean gradient of 35 mm Hg at the time of PVE diagnosis to 49 mm Hg by January 2024—despite documented resolution of infection—suggests that the post-infectious fibrotic remodeling contributed to progressive mechanical restriction. Additionally, the emergence of moderate-to-severe regurgitation alongside the stenotic gradient indicates that leaflet coaptation was compromised, potentially through leaflet shortening or perforation related to the prior infectious process. Thus, the mechanism of prosthetic valve failure in this patient was likely multifactorial, combining the expected trajectory of SVD with the additive effects of infection-related leaflet injury and subsequent fibrosis.

### Hemodynamic Consequences of Combined Stenosis and Regurgitation

When a prosthetic valve develops both stenosis and regurgitation simultaneously, the hemodynamic burden on the left ventricle is compounded. Stenosis imposes a pressure load—the ventricle must generate higher systolic pressures to eject blood through the narrowed orifice—increasing afterload, wall stress, and myocardial oxygen demand. Regurgitation imposes a volume load, as a portion of the ejected blood returns to the ventricle during diastole, increasing preload and chamber dimensions. The combination creates a particularly hostile environment for the myocardium: the ventricle must handle both increased preload and increased afterload simultaneously, which can accelerate the development of ventricular dysfunction, dilatation, and heart failure symptoms. In this patient, the severe stenosis (mean gradient 49 mm Hg, AVA 0.5 cm²) combined with grade 2–3 aortic regurgitation produced the clinical picture of exertional dyspnea, chest discomfort, dizziness, and fatigue that prompted re-presentation. The markedly reduced AVA of 0.5 cm², calculated using the continuity equation, confirmed severe prosthetic dysfunction and provided the quantitative basis for the decision to proceed with a third intervention.

### The Patient's Hemodynamic Trajectory

The patient's hemodynamic course across interventions illustrates the dynamic relationship between valve function and ventricular performance. After the initial Lotus implantation in 2014, the ejection fraction was presumably stable or improved, though specific values from that period are not detailed. By December 2021, the EF was 54%, but as the Lotus valve degenerated and the mean gradient climbed to 83 mm Hg by early 2022, the EF declined to 42%—a reduction reflecting the afterload mismatch imposed by critical stenosis. The subsequent TAV-in-TAV with a 23 mm Evolut valve produced a favorable early hemodynamic response, with EF recovering to 54%, demonstrating that ventricular dysfunction in this context was at least partially reversible with relief of the obstructive lesion. The episode of PVE then introduced a new insult, with the mean gradient rising to 35 mm Hg during active infection. After successful antimicrobial treatment and a period of recovery, by January 2024 the EF had rebounded to 66%—possibly reflecting recovery from the infectious insult and the effects of guideline-directed medical therapy for heart failure—while the mean gradient continued to climb to 49 mm Hg, indicating that valve dysfunction was progressing independently of ventricular recovery. This divergence between improving ventricular function and worsening valve function is characteristic of isolated prosthetic failure and provided a clear rationale for reintervention: the ventricle remained capable of benefiting from relief of the valve obstruction, making the patient a suitable candidate for a third transcatheter procedure.

### Resolution of PVE and Implications for Procedural Decision-Making

The confirmation of PVE resolution was a prerequisite for proceeding with the third TAVI. The diagnostic evaluation to establish resolution included repeated blood cultures to document microbiological clearance, transesophageal echocardiography to exclude residual vegetations or paravalvular complications, and multislice CT to assess for structural sequelae such as abscess cavities or pseudoaneurysms. All of these studies confirmed the absence of active infection. This documentation was essential for two reasons. First, implanting a new prosthesis in the setting of active endocarditis would carry a high risk of immediate reinfection of the new device, potentially creating an intractable clinical situation. Second, the standard of care for active PVE—in accordance with European Society of Cardiology guidelines—favors surgical valve replacement, particularly when complicated by heart failure or uncontrolled infection. By establishing that the infection was cured, the clinical team could justify a transcatheter approach based on the same principles that apply to any patient with structural valve deterioration and prohibitive surgical risk.

### Why Management After Cured Endocarditis Aligns With Standard SVD Management

Once prosthetic valve endocarditis has been definitively treated and the patient shows no evidence of active or ongoing infection, the management strategy for subsequent valve dysfunction aligns with that of standard structural valve deterioration. The prior history of infection does not, by itself, alter the fundamental decision-making framework, which is driven by three factors: the hemodynamic severity of the prosthetic dysfunction, the patient's surgical risk profile, and the anatomical feasibility of a transcatheter approach. In this patient, all three factors favored a transcatheter strategy. The hemodynamic severity was well documented (mean gradient 49 mm Hg, AVA 0.5 cm², moderate-to-severe regurgitation). The surgical risk was prohibitive, with a EuroSCORE II of 27.36%, compounded by COPD, diabetes, recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding, and the technical challenges of explanting two deeply integrated transcatheter valve frames—a procedure that would likely require total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement. The anatomical feasibility was confirmed through detailed CT planning. The absence of active infection meant that no additional infection-specific considerations—such as the need for suppressive antibiotic therapy or the selection of a particular valve type based on infection risk—needed to influence the decision. The multidisciplinary heart team therefore approached the case as they would any complex TAV-in-TAV reintervention, with attention to coronary protection, commissural alignment, and optimization of the effective orifice area, rather than tailoring the strategy around the remote infectious history.

## Diagnostic Evaluation and CT-Guided Procedural Planning

The diagnostic evaluation for this patient needed to accomplish two objectives simultaneously: first, to confirm the severity of prosthetic valve dysfunction to justify reintervention, and second, to verify complete resolution of the prior prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) so that a transcatheter strategy could be considered. Both lines of inquiry relied on multimodality imaging and laboratory testing, and the results fed directly into the CT-based procedural planning that determined whether a third transcatheter valve could be implanted safely.

### Echocardiographic Assessment of Prosthetic Valve Dysfunction

By the time of his June 2024 admission, the patient had two overlapping transcatheter prostheses in place—the original 27 mm Lotus valve implanted in 2014 and a 23 mm Evolut valve implanted in 2022—and transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated severe dysfunction of this composite structure. The examination revealed grade 2–3 intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation and a mean transvalvular gradient of 49 mm Hg. The aortic valve area (AVA) was calculated at 0.5 cm² using the continuity equation, a fundamental Doppler-based method that compares stroke volume at the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) to stroke volume through the prosthetic valve. In this method, the LVOT cross-sectional area is derived from a linear diameter measurement, and the LVOT velocity-time integral (VTI) is obtained by pulsed-wave Doppler, while the prosthetic valve VTI is obtained by continuous-wave Doppler. The ratio of the two stroke volumes yields the effective AVA. In the setting of multiple overlapping valve frames, the LVOT measurement can be particularly challenging because the stent struts and frame material create acoustic shadowing and may distort the true annular geometry. Nevertheless, the markedly reduced AVA, combined with a mean gradient of 49 mm Hg, was consistent with severe prosthetic stenosis.

An additional Doppler parameter that can assist in the assessment of prosthetic valve dysfunction is the Doppler velocity index (DVI), which is the ratio of the LVOT peak velocity to the prosthetic valve peak velocity. A DVI below approximately 0.25 suggests significant prosthetic stenosis, as the velocity through the narrowed prosthesis becomes disproportionately high relative to the velocity proximal to it in the LVOT. At the patient's January 2024 follow-up, the mean gradient had already risen to 49 mm Hg with moderate-to-severe regurgitation, and by June 2024 the findings had persisted or progressed, establishing the indication for reintervention. It is worth noting that the combination of stenosis and regurgitation in a bioprosthetic valve typically reflects structural leaflet deterioration—fibrosis, calcification, or tear—which may be accelerated by prior infection-related injury even after the infection itself has been eradicated.

Transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) was performed prior to the planned reintervention and served a dual purpose: it provided a more detailed assessment of the prosthetic valve morphology and, critically, confirmed the absence of active endocarditis or paravalvular complications. TEE is superior to TTE for visualizing vegetations, abscesses, and paravalvular fistulae, particularly in the setting of prosthetic valves where TTE images are often limited by acoustic shadowing from the stent frame.

### Confirmation of Endocarditis Resolution

The patient had been diagnosed with PVE in September 2022, three months after the second TAVI. At that time, blood cultures were positive for *Enterococcus faecalis*, and TTE showed severe restenosis with a mean gradient of 35 mm Hg. He received a full course of targeted antimicrobial therapy consisting of six weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based treatment followed by oral therapy as recommended by the infectious disease team. By the time of the third TAVI procedure, antimicrobial treatment had been completed and no long-term suppressive antibiotic therapy was ongoing.

Before proceeding with reintervention, the clinical team needed to confirm that the infection was fully resolved. This required multiple convergent data streams. Clinical assessment showed no signs of ongoing infection. Repeated blood cultures were negative, excluding persistent bacteremia. TEE demonstrated no vegetations on either prosthesis and no paravalvular complications such as abscess or fistula. Multislice CT (MSCT) corroborated these findings, showing no evidence of perivalvular collections or other imaging signs of active infection. The convergence of negative cultures, clinical stability, and both TEE and MSCT confirmation was essential because performing a transcatheter valve implantation in the setting of active endocarditis could seed the new prosthesis and result in recurrent infection. The 2025 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines for the management of valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis recommend surgical valve replacement as the standard strategy for PVE, particularly when complicated by heart failure or uncontrolled infection. However, these guidelines also emphasize that management should be individualized based on infection control status, surgical risk, and anatomical feasibility. In this patient, the complete resolution of infection, combined with prohibitive surgical risk, supported consideration of a transcatheter approach.

### MSCT for Anatomical Feasibility Assessment

Once the clinical indication for reintervention was established and active infection was excluded, MSCT became the cornerstone of procedural planning. MSCT is the standard imaging modality for TAVI planning because it provides high-resolution three-dimensional data on the aortic root, annular dimensions, coronary ostial heights, and the spatial relationships between existing prosthetic frames and surrounding structures. In a patient with two prior transcatheter valves, the complexity of the CT analysis is substantially greater than in a de novo TAVI.

Several key measurements were obtained. Coronary ostial height was assessed to determine the vertical distance from the aortic annulus (or in this case, from the effective landing zone of the third valve) to the left and right coronary ostia. Low-lying coronary ostia increase the risk of coronary obstruction when a new valve is implanted because the valve's skirt or leaflet tissue may cover the ostial origin. Sinus of Valsalva dimensions were evaluated to determine whether there was adequate space around the existing valve frames for blood to flow around the prosthesis and reach the coronaries—a concept referred to as the "sinus sequestration" risk. The valve-to-coronary distance, which quantifies the clearance between the upper edge of the prosthetic leaflets (or the neo-skirt) and the coronary ostia, was also measured. These measurements collectively determined whether coronary flow could be preserved during and after the third valve implantation.

### Assessment of Frame Interaction and Minimum Lumen Diameter

A critical finding from the MSCT was the measurement of the minimum lumen diameter within the Evolut prosthesis, which was 13.7 mm. This measurement reflected severe haemodynamic compromise and served as a key parameter for selecting the size of the third valve. The minimum lumen diameter represents the narrowest point through which blood must pass and is determined by the interplay between the inner diameter of the Evolut frame and the degree to which its leaflets have thickened, retracted, or been constrained by the underlying Lotus valve. In a TAV-in-TAV configuration, the inner valve is not simply sitting freely within the outer valve; the two frames interact mechanically. The outer frame (Lotus) may constrain the expansion of the inner frame (Evolut), or the inner frame may not fully expand due to the geometry of the outer frame. The result is a reduced effective orifice area, which in this patient was further compounded by the structural deterioration of the leaflets.

The CT analysis also evaluated the interaction between the Lotus and Evolut stent frames—specifically, how the three layers of metal would overlap once the third valve was deployed. Each transcatheter valve has a unique frame design: the Lotus is a mechanically expanded valve with a relatively short, thick frame; the Evolut is a self-expanding nitinol valve with a tall, hourglass-shaped frame; and the Acurate neo2 (the planned third valve) has its own distinctive supra-annular design. Understanding how these three frames would nest within each other was essential for predicting whether the third valve could expand adequately, whether it would be stable, and whether the composite orifice area would be sufficient.

### The Neo-Skirt Concept and Coronary Flow Preservation

A central anatomical concept in TAV-in-TAV procedures is the "neo-skirt." When a new transcatheter valve is implanted inside an existing one, the leaflets of the old prosthesis are pushed outward against the new valve's stent frame. These displaced leaflets, which are typically thickened and fibrotic from structural degeneration, create a circumferential ring of tissue at the level where they are pinned—this is the neo-skirt. The neo-skirt behaves functionally like an extended fabric skirt on the new valve, but it is composed of the old valve's leaflet material. Its clinical significance lies in its potential to obstruct coronary ostia, particularly when it forms at or below the level of the coronary ostia.

In this case, particular attention was paid to the relationship between the anticipated neo-skirt and the coronary ostia. The procedural strategy of high implantation—deploying the third valve at the level of the Evolut valve leaflets rather than lower in the annulus—was chosen specifically to pin the Evolut leaflets against the stent frame above the coronary ostia, thereby minimizing neo-skirt formation below the ostia. This approach had two benefits: it preserved coronary flow during and after the procedure, and it maintained the possibility of future coronary access, which was particularly relevant given the patient's history of three-vessel coronary artery disease and multiple prior percutaneous interventions.

### Coronary Obstruction Risk Stratification

Coronary artery obstruction is one of the most feared complications of TAV-in-TAV procedures. It occurs when the leaflets of the old prosthesis, displaced outward by the new valve, cover the coronary ostia and acutely obstruct blood flow. The risk is higher in women, in patients with low coronary ostial heights, in patients with small sinuses of Valsalva (which limit the space available for the displaced leaflets), and when the existing valve leaflets are tall enough to reach the coronary ostia when folded outward.

Based on the preprocedural CT analysis—incorporating coronary ostial height, sinus dimensions, valve-to-coronary distance, and the predicted position of the neo-skirt—the team determined that the risk of coronary obstruction was sufficiently low to proceed without active coronary protection. Coronary protection during TAV-in-TAV typically involves placing a guidewire and possibly a catheter or stent in the coronary artery at risk before valve deployment, so that if obstruction occurs, immediate intervention can be performed. While protective, this approach adds procedural complexity and time. The CT-based risk stratification in this patient demonstrated adequate coronary clearance, and simultaneous aortography during balloon pre-dilation confirmed that coronary flow remained unobstructed before the valve was deployed, providing an additional layer of real-time safety verification.

### Surgical Risk Assessment

The decision to pursue a transcatheter rather than surgical strategy was heavily influenced by the patient's overall surgical risk profile. His European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation (EuroSCORE II) was 27.36%, a value that places him in the very high-risk category for cardiac surgery. This score incorporates factors such as age, renal function, previous cardiac surgery, and overall clinical status. Beyond the numerical score, the patient's comorbidities further increased the practical risk of surgery: chronic obstructive pulmonary disease would complicate perioperative ventilation and recovery; type 2 diabetes mellitus increased the risk of infection and wound healing complications; recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding since 2022 raised concerns about perioperative anticoagulation and transfusion requirements; and third-degree atrioventricular block with a permanent pacemaker added technical considerations for surgical management.

Anatomical complexity further tipped the balance against surgery. Two previously implanted transcatheter prostheses had become extensively integrated into the aortic wall over the years, with deep fixation of the stent frames. Surgical explantation of these valves would likely have required total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement—a major operation with substantial morbidity and mortality, even in lower-risk patients. The combination of high EuroSCORE II, multiple comorbidities, and prohibitive anatomical complexity from prior valve frames made open surgery an unfavorable option.

### Multidisciplinary Heart Team Decision-Making

The decision to proceed with a third transcatheter intervention was made through a multidisciplinary heart team review, which included interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, imaging specialists, and anesthesiologists. This collaborative approach is the standard for complex structural interventions and is particularly important in cases that fall outside established guidelines or involve off-label device use. The Acurate neo2 prosthesis was used under institutional approval for off-label use, and shared decision-making involved the patient and his family.

The heart team's discussion would have weighed several factors: the severity of prosthetic valve dysfunction and its impact on the patient's quality of life, the absence of active infection, the prohibitive surgical risk, the CT-confirmed feasibility of a transcatheter approach, and the anticipated technical challenges of a triple-valve configuration. The team would also have considered the limitations of a transcatheter strategy—specifically, that if the third prosthesis were to fail prematurely, future options would be extremely limited, as surgical explantation of three overlapping valves would be even more challenging than explantation of two. This "exit strategy" consideration is an important part of the decision-making process in any redo structural intervention, and the patient and family were presumably informed of this limitation.

### Context within the 2025 ESC Guidelines

The 2025 ESC guidelines for the management of valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis provide a framework for this decision. The guidelines recommend surgical valve replacement for PVE, especially in cases with heart failure, uncontrolled infection, abscess, or destructive lesions. However, they emphasize individualized management based on infection control, surgical risk, and anatomical feasibility. In this patient, several factors supported deviation from the standard surgical recommendation: the infection had been fully treated with documented resolution by blood cultures, TEE, and MSCT; no suppressive antibiotic therapy was ongoing; the EuroSCORE II was extremely high; and the anatomical complexity of two deeply integrated stent frames made surgery technically hazardous. Once active infection was excluded, the management strategy for prosthetic valve dysfunction aligned with the approach used for patients without ongoing endocarditis—that is, the decision was driven by anatomical feasibility, haemodynamic severity, and surgical risk rather than by the history of infection alone. This case illustrates how guideline recommendations can be appropriately individualized when the standard approach poses unacceptable risk and when detailed preprocedural evaluation supports the safety of an alternative strategy.

## Procedural Strategy and Technical Execution of TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV

Performing a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation within two existing prosthetic frames represents one of the most technically demanding scenarios in structural heart disease intervention. By the time a patient reaches a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV configuration, the accumulated stent metal, leaflet tissue, and altered annular geometry create constraints that do not exist during first-time or even second-time interventions. This case illustrates how preprocedural computed tomography (CT) modeling can be translated into a deliberate, step-by-step procedural strategy that addresses the principal hazards of triple-valve implantation: coronary obstruction, severe prosthesis-patient mismatch, and loss of future coronary access.

### Access Strategy and Anesthetic Considerations

The procedure was performed under local anesthesia using left radial and left femoral arterial access. This dual-access approach reflects standard practice in complex transcatheter interventions: the femoral artery serves as the primary delivery route for the valve delivery system, which requires a large-bore sheath, while the radial artery provides a secondary access point for aortography and hemodynamic monitoring. Local anesthesia, rather than general anesthesia, was appropriate given the need for real-time patient feedback and the desire to minimize procedural risk in an elderly patient with significant comorbidities, including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and prior gastrointestinal bleeding. The choice of left-sided access for both routes was likely influenced by the patient's prior vascular complications—specifically, the pulsatile femoral hematoma and pseudoaneurysm that complicated the 2022 TAV-in-TAV procedure and required open vascular surgery—making careful selection of access sites essential.

### Valve Selection: Why Acurate neo2?

The choice of prosthesis for a third transcatheter valve is constrained by the internal dimensions of the existing valve stack. MSCT demonstrated a minimum lumen diameter of only 13.7 mm within the Evolut prosthesis, confirming severe narrowing and dictating that any new valve must be deliverable through a critically small orifice. The team selected a size S Acurate neo2 (Boston Scientific), a self-expanding bioprosthetic valve, for several interrelated reasons.

First, the Acurate neo2 features supra-annular leaflet positioning. In a stacked-valve configuration, where the effective orifice area is progressively compromised by each additional layer of metal and tissue, supra-annular leaflets sit above the narrowest portion of the existing valve frames, potentially maximizing the functional opening area. Second, the valve has a relatively low frame height compared with other self-expanding platforms such as the Evolut. A lower frame height reduces the amount of stent material projecting above the aortic annulus, which in turn lessens the risk that the new valve frame will obstruct coronary ostia or create a barrier to future coronary catheter engagement. Third, the Acurate neo2 has demonstrated favorable hemodynamic performance in narrow anatomies, a critical attribute when implanting within the restricted lumen of a degenerated prior prosthesis. Finally, its design supports commissural alignment, a consideration that becomes increasingly important with each successive transcatheter intervention, as discussed below.

### High Implantation Strategy: A Critical Anatomical Decision

Perhaps the most consequential strategic decision in this procedure was the plan for high implantation of the third valve at the level of the Evolut valve leaflets. To understand why this matters, it is necessary to consider what happens mechanically when a new transcatheter valve is deployed inside an existing one.

When a balloon-expandable or self-expanding valve is implanted within a prior bioprosthesis, the frame of the new valve pushes the leaflets of the old valve outward. However, the old leaflets, which are attached at their bases to the old valve frame, can fold inward—toward the center of the flow lumen—if the new valve is implanted too low relative to them. This inward leaflet folding narrows the effective orifice area and can produce or worsen prosthesis-patient mismatch, a condition associated with poorer long-term outcomes, including persistent gradients, left ventricular hypertrophy, and reduced survival. By implanting the Acurate neo2 at the level of the Evolut leaflets, the team aimed to fix the pre-existing leaflets against the stent frame of the new valve, pinning them outward against the aortic wall and preventing them from projecting into the central flow channel.

Equally important, the high implantation strategy was designed to minimize formation of a neo-skirt below the coronary ostia. The term "neo-skirt" refers to the cylindrical structure created when the fabric cuff and frame of a newly implanted transcatheter valve, combined with the displaced leaflets of prior valves, form a barrier that can cover the coronary ostial openings. In a triple-valve configuration, the cumulative effect of multiple fabric cuffs and folded leaflets creates a particularly tall neo-skirt, potentially obstructing coronary inflow. By positioning the third valve high—at the leaflet level of the Evolut—the team reduced the distance between the top of the new valve and the coronary ostia, thereby limiting the height of any obstructive neo-skirt and preserving coronary flow. This approach also facilitated future coronary access, an essential consideration in a patient with known three-vessel coronary disease and multiple prior percutaneous interventions.

### Commissural Alignment: Preserving Future Options

Commissural alignment refers to the rotational orientation of the valve's commissures—the points where the leaflets attach to the frame—relative to the native aortic commissures and the coronary ostia. In a single TAVI procedure, misalignment can narrow the gaps between stent struts through which coronary catheters must pass. In a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedure, three overlapping frames create a dense metallic mesh, and the cumulative effect of misalignment across multiple valves can effectively wall off the coronary ostia, making future percutaneous coronary intervention impossible or necessitating high-risk surgical revascularization.

The team used preprocedural CT to model the interaction between the Evolut and Acurate neo2 frames, determining the optimal rotational alignment that would position the commissural posts of the new valve between, rather than over, the coronary ostia. Fluoroscopic landmarks identified during CT planning were then used to guide deployment orientation in the catheterization laboratory. Although perfect commissural alignment across three valves cannot always be guaranteed, the deliberate effort to achieve it reflects a lifetime management philosophy: each reintervention should be planned not only for immediate hemodynamic success but also to preserve options for future procedures.

### Balloon Pre-dilation and Post-dilation

Balloon pre-dilation was performed using a 22/40 mm catheter under rapid ventricular pacing at 180 beats per minute. Rapid ventricular pacing transiently reduces cardiac output and aortic pressure, preventing the balloon from being ejected during inflation and allowing controlled, stable dilation. Pre-dilation served to fracture any calcific deposits, expand the degenerated leaflets, and create a more receptive landing zone for the self-expanding prosthesis. Simultaneous aortography during pre-dilation confirmed that coronary flow remained unobstructed even under balloon inflation—an important real-time reassurance in a high-risk coronary anatomy.

Post-dilation was performed after valve deployment using a 20/40 mm balloon, again under rapid pacing. The rationale for post-dilation was twofold. First, it optimized expansion of the newly implanted Acurate neo2, ensuring maximal frame circularity and leaflet coaptation. Second, it likely contributed to further radial expansion of the prior Evolut frame. High-pressure balloon dilation can partially expand or even fracture a previously implanted transcatheter valve frame, and although intentional frame fracture was not performed in this case, the post-dilation may have improved leaflet mobility of the Evolut prosthesis and contributed to the overall hemodynamic result. The clinical significance of this mechanism is discussed further in the outcome section, where the dramatic reduction in transvalvular gradient from post-procedural to one-month follow-up suggests ongoing favorable remodeling of the valve complex.

### Intra-procedural Imaging and Step-by-Step Execution

The procedure illustrates a systematic, imaging-guided approach where each step was confirmed before proceeding to the next. The sequence was as follows:

1. **Access and initial angiography**: Left radial and left femoral arterial access was established under local anesthesia. Aortography with a pigtail catheter confirmed severe aortic regurgitation, establishing the baseline hemodynamic pathology.

2. **Guidewire placement**: A Safari guidewire—a pre-shaped, stiff wire designed for stable positioning in the left ventricle—was advanced via the femoral access and positioned in the left ventricle, providing a secure rail over which the delivery system could track.

3. **Balloon pre-dilation**: A 22/40 mm balloon was inflated under rapid ventricular pacing at 180 beats per minute. Simultaneous aortography confirmed preserved coronary flow during inflation, providing real-time reassurance that balloon expansion did not compromise coronary perfusion.

4. **Valve deployment**: The Acurate neo2 (size S) was advanced over the guidewire and deployed in the aortic position, with rotational orientation guided by preprocedural CT modeling and fluoroscopic landmarks to achieve commissural alignment. The valve was implanted high, at the level of the Evolut leaflets, consistent with the preprocedural strategy.

5. **Post-dilation**: A 20/40 mm balloon was inflated under rapid ventricular pacing to optimize valve expansion and potentially expand the prior Evolut frame.

6. **Final aortography**: Contrast injection confirmed correct valve positioning, preserved coronary perfusion, and absence of both perivalvular and transvalvular regurgitation.

The use of simultaneous aortography during critical steps—pre-dilation and final assessment—exemplifies how real-time imaging complements preprocedural CT planning. CT provides the static anatomical roadmap, while intra-procedural angiography provides dynamic confirmation that the strategy is being executed safely.

### Institutional Approval, Multidisciplinary Review, and Shared Decision-Making

The use of the Acurate neo2 in a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV configuration represents an off-label application of the device, as transcatheter heart valves are regulatory-approved for specific indications and anatomical configurations that do not encompass triple-valve stacking. The procedure was performed under institutional approval for off-label use, ensuring appropriate oversight and documentation.

The decision to proceed was made through a multidisciplinary heart team review involving interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, imaging specialists, and anesthesiologists. This collaborative approach is essential in complex structural interventions where no single specialty possesses all the expertise needed to evaluate the full range of surgical risk, anatomical feasibility, hemodynamic implications, and long-term management considerations. Shared decision-making with the patient and his family ensured that they understood the novel nature of the procedure, the limited alternative options, and the uncertainties inherent in a third transcatheter intervention.

The patient's EuroSCORE II of 27.36% reflected prohibitive surgical risk, and the deep fixation of two prior transcatheter valve frames into the aortic wall meant that surgical explantation would likely have required total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement—an operation of extraordinary complexity in a patient of his age and comorbidity profile. These factors collectively justified the transcatheter approach despite its off-label status and technical challenges.

## Outcome, Follow-up, and Clinical Lessons

### Immediate Post-Procedural Assessment

Procedural success was evaluated according to the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 (VARC-3) criteria, which provide a standardized framework for reporting outcomes after transcatheter aortic valve interventions. VARC-3 encompasses assessment of device success, early safety endpoints, and clinical efficacy at follow-up. Device success, in particular, requires confirmation of correct valve deployment, freedom from moderate or severe regurgitation, and acceptable haemodynamic performance without procedure-related complications. In this case, immediate post-procedural transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) confirmed successful deployment of the third transcatheter valve (Acurate neo2, size S). The valve leaflets were mobile with complete opening, and there was no evidence of aortic regurgitation (grade 0)—a critical finding given the moderate-to-severe regurgitation present before the intervention. Final aortography during the procedure had likewise confirmed correct positioning, preserved coronary perfusion, and absence of both perivalvular and transvalvular regurgitation. However, haemodynamic parameters revealed a surprisingly elevated mean transvalvular gradient of 67 mm Hg, a calculated aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.9 cm², and a Doppler velocity index (DVI) of 0.24. These values were consistent with severe patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM) and represented a concerning early result that warranted close surveillance.

### Early Patient-Prosthesis Mismatch and Its Context

Patient-prosthesis mismatch describes a situation in which the effective orifice area of a normally functioning prosthesis is too small relative to the patient's body size, resulting in abnormally high transvalvular gradients. It is most commonly classified using the indexed effective orifice area: severe PPM is generally defined as an indexed EOA of ≤0.65 cm²/m², though thresholds can vary slightly depending on the guideline and the measurement method. In the transcatheter context, PPM is a recognized complication of valve-in-valve procedures, where the existing prosthetic frame and leaflets physically constrain expansion of the new valve, reducing the achievable orifice area. In this patient, the indexed AVA of 0.4 cm²/m² placed him squarely in the severe PPM category. It is important to understand that this finding does not necessarily indicate procedural failure or device malfunction; rather, it reflects the geometric constraints of implanting a third valve within two existing frames. The clinical significance of PPM includes persistent gradients, less complete left ventricular regression, and potentially worse long-term outcomes, making its recognition and monitoring essential after complex valve-in-valve interventions.

### Hemodynamic Evolution and Mechanisms of Gradient Resolution

Despite the concerning immediate post-procedural gradients, follow-up TTE at 1 month demonstrated marked improvement: the mean gradient had decreased to 27 mm Hg, and the AVA had increased to 1.1 cm². Left ventricular remodelling was also observed, with a reduction in chamber volumes. This favorable evolution reflects several mechanisms. First, commissural alignment between the Acurate neo2 and the prior Evolut valve leaflets, guided by preprocedural CT modelling, helped optimize central flow dynamics and the effective orifice area. Second, post-dilatation with a 20/40 mm balloon under rapid ventricular pacing likely contributed to further radial expansion of the Evolut frame, partially improving leaflet mobility. Third, the self-expanding nature of the Acurate neo2 prosthesis allows for continued radial force application over time, which may produce gradual frame expansion and leaflet adaptation in the days to weeks after implantation. While intentional frame fracture was not performed, the combination of high-pressure balloon post-dilation and the self-expanding design likely facilitated the haemodynamic improvement observed at 1 month. This temporal pattern underscores an important clinical point: early gradients after valve-in-valve procedures, particularly with self-expanding prostheses, may overestimate the eventual steady-state haemodynamic performance and should be interpreted with caution.

### Intermediate and Long-Term Follow-Up

At 3.5 months post-implantation, the patient remained asymptomatic with good exercise tolerance and no signs of heart failure or recurrent infection. Valve function remained stable within the moderately stenotic range. At 1-year follow-up (June 2025), the patient was clinically stable and active, without dyspnoea or signs of decompensation. TTE confirmed a mean gradient of 26 mm Hg, AVA of 1.1 cm², and ejection fraction of 65%. No valve-related complications, regurgitation, or evidence of recurrent endocarditis were observed. These findings confirm the durability of the result and demonstrate a sustained functional benefit of the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV intervention. The absence of recurrent endocarditis at 1 year is particularly noteworthy given the patient's prior prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) caused by Enterococcus faecalis, supporting the principle that, once infection is fully treated and confirmed resolved by clinical assessment, repeated blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and CT, subsequent transcatheter management can proceed without the ongoing concern of active infection driving decision-making.

### Challenges of Future Interventions

Despite the favorable outcome, this patient's therapeutic options for any future valve failure are extremely limited. Three overlapping transcatheter valve frames now occupy the aortic annular region, creating a complex stent architecture that poses substantial challenges for both transcatheter and surgical approaches. From a transcatheter perspective, the cumulative mesh burden of three stent frames threatens coronary access, particularly in a patient with known three-vessel coronary artery disease and multiple prior percutaneous coronary interventions. Even with careful commissural alignment performed during each procedure, the increasing density of metal struts may impede catheter engagement of the coronary ostia. From a surgical perspective, the deep fixation of the prior valve frames into the aortic wall makes explantation technically hazardous; surgical removal would likely necessitate total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement, a procedure associated with prohibitive risk in this patient given his EuroSCORE II of 27.36% and comorbidities including COPD, diabetes, and recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding. These constraints highlight the narrow therapeutic margin that accumulates with each successive transcatheter reintervention.

### Emerging Catheter-Based Solutions

Several catheter-based techniques have emerged as potential strategies to address complications in complex valve-in-valve configurations. Bioprosthetic aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction (BASILICA) involves lacerating the leaflets of the existing bioprosthesis to create a pathway for coronary flow when a new valve is deployed, and it has been used to prevent coronary obstruction in high-risk valve-in-valve cases. Bioprosthetic valve fracture, achieved through high-pressure balloon dilation, can fracture the frame of a previously implanted bioprosthesis to allow fuller expansion of a subsequently implanted transcatheter valve, thereby improving the effective orifice area and reducing PPM. While these techniques represent important additions to the structural interventionalist's armamentarium, their feasibility and safety in a triple-stented configuration remain uncertain. The altered mechanics of three overlapping frames, the potential for unpredictable fracture patterns, and the risk of catastrophic coronary obstruction in this anatomy make extrapolation from single valve-in-valve experience difficult. These uncertainties reinforce the need for individualized risk assessment and multidisciplinary decision-making in such complex cases.

### Principles of Lifetime Management

This case illustrates several critical principles for the lifetime management of patients undergoing transcatheter valve interventions. First, with each successive reintervention, considerations such as frame layering, commissural alignment, coronary reaccess, and residual effective orifice area become progressively more complex and constrain future options. Second, long-term planning must begin at the time of the first intervention, particularly in younger or high-risk patients who are likely to outlive their initial prosthesis. The choice of valve type, implantation depth, and commissural orientation at the first procedure can profoundly influence the feasibility and safety of future valve-in-valve procedures. Third, preprocedural CT imaging is indispensable for evaluating coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, valve-to-coronary distance, and the interaction between existing valve frames, all of which inform procedural strategy and risk assessment. Fourth, as TAVI indications expand and patient longevity increases, structural valve deterioration and the need for repeat interventions will become more common, making systematic long-term surveillance essential. This case demonstrates that a third transcatheter intervention after treated PVE is feasible when guided by detailed imaging and a predefined strategy, but it also underscores that the window for intervention narrows with each procedure, making prospective lifetime management planning a clinical necessity rather than a theoretical consideration.
