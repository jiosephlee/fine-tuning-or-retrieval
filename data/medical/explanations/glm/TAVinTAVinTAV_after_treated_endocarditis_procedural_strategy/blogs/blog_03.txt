Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# Preserving Coronary Access Across Three Valve Frames: The Role of CT-Guided Neo-Skirt Planning

## The Triple-Valve Challenge

Here's a case that should make every structural heart team pause and think hard about lifetime valve management. A man in his late 70s presents with severe degeneration of two overlapping transcatheter aortic valves — and a history of treated prosthetic valve endocarditis in between. The question isn't just whether you *can* put a third valve in. It's whether you can do it without occluding the coronaries, without creating a functionally stenotic nightmare, and without foreclosing every future option the patient has. The answer, in this case, hinged almost entirely on one thing: preprocedural CT planning.

This patient's journey is a harbinger of what's coming. He received a 27 mm Lotus valve in 2014, underwent TAV-in-TAV with a 23 mm Evolut in 2022 for structural valve deterioration (mean gradient up to 83 mm Hg, EF dropped to 42%), then developed *Enterococcus faecalis* prosthetic valve endocarditis treated with 6 weeks of IV ampicillin-based therapy. By early 2024, after documented infection resolution, his mean gradient was back up to 49 mm Hg with moderate-to-severe regurgitation and an AVA of 0.5 cm². Two valve frames, three prior coronary stents, COPD, diabetes, recurrent GI bleeding, and a EuroSCORE II of 27.36%. Surgery would have required aortic root and ascending aorta replacement given the depth of stent integration into the aortic wall. The heart team went transcatheter — but the planning was everything.

## The Neo-Skirt Problem: Why Each Valve Makes the Next One Harder

Here is the core anatomical issue that escalates with every successive valve-in-valve. When you implant a new transcatheter valve inside a prior one, the leaflets of the existing prosthesis get pinned outward against its stent frame. Those pinned leaflets form a circular ridge of tissue — what the structural heart community calls the **neo-skirt** — that sits below the coronary ostia. With one valve-in-valve, this is manageable. With two overlapping frames, the neo-skirt becomes taller, wider, and more hemodynamically relevant. With a third valve, you are stacking neoskirts and metal frames in a spatial relationship to the coronaries that becomes increasingly unforgiving.

In this case, the team faced a triple challenge: the Lotus frame from 2014, the Evolut frame from 2022 sitting inside it, and now a third valve going inside both. Each frame adds metal and each set of pinned leaflets adds tissue bulk that can obstruct coronary flow or make future coronary cannulation impossible. The minimum lumen diameter inside the Evolut was 13.7 mm — severely compromised — which tells you how much real estate was already lost.

The critical insight from the CT planning was this: by implanting the third valve *high* — at the level of the Evolut leaflets rather than deeper in the annulus — the team could pin the Evolut leaflets against the Evolut frame rather than letting them fold further inward. This served two purposes. First, it prevented further reduction of the effective orifice area from inward leaflet folding. Second, it minimized the neo-skirt height below the coronary ostia, preserving both immediate coronary flow and future coronary access.

## CT Assessment: What Was Measured and Why It Mattered

The preprocedural multislice CT evaluation was the linchpin of the entire strategy. The team assessed:

- **Coronary ostial height** — How far above the annular plane do the coronary ostia sit, and how does that relate to the anticipated neo-skirt position?
- **Sinus of Valsalva dimensions** — Is there enough space in the sinuses to accommodate three overlapping frames without lateral compression compromising coronary flow?
- **Valve-to-coronary distance** — The straight-line distance from the tips of the pinned leaflets (the neo-skirt) to the coronary ostia. This is the number that tells you whether you are safe, borderline, or in trouble.
- **Frame interaction analysis** — How do the Lotus and Evolut frames overlap, and where will the third frame sit relative to both?
- **Landing zone assessment** — Where inside the Evolut can the Acurate neo2 anchor securely while maintaining optimal leaflet position?

The valve-to-coronary distance measurements, combined with the planned high implantation strategy, indicated sufficient coronary clearance that the procedure was performed **without coronary protection** — no guidewire or chimney stent in either coronary. That is a significant decision in a triple-frame anatomy, and it was justified by CT, not by intuition.

One important limitation: the team did not have access to dedicated 3D expansion analysis software. The CT assessment relied on standard multiplanar reconstructions and measurements rather than simulated three-dimensional valve expansion within the specific anatomical configuration. This is a real-world constraint that many centers face, and it means the planning, while thorough, involved inferential reasoning about how the new valve would expand rather than computational confirmation.

## Valve Selection and Commissural Alignment: The Acurate neo2 Choice

The team selected the Acurate neo2 (size S) for specific design reasons relevant to this anatomy. Its supra-annular leaflet positioning provides favorable hemodynamic performance in narrow anatomies — critical when you're working inside a 13.7 mm lumen. Its relatively low frame height reduces the amount of additional metal that could interfere with coronary access. And its design supports commissural alignment, which was a deliberate goal.

Commissural alignment matters increasingly with each successive valve. When commissures of overlapping valves are aligned, the commissural posts line up and leave the spaces between them — the inter-commissural windows — relatively open. These windows are the corridors through which you can pass a catheter to cannulate the coronaries in the future. When commissures are misaligned, the posts of one valve sit in front of the windows of another, creating a mesh that is extremely difficult to cross with a guide catheter. In a patient with three-vessel disease and four prior PCI interventions, preserving coronary access is not a theoretical concern — it is a clinical necessity.

The team used preprocedural CT modeling and fluoroscopic landmarks to plan alignment during deployment, rather than relying on arbitrary positioning. This is the level of precision that triple-valve procedures demand.

## The Hemodynamic Payoff — and the PPM Reality

The immediate post-procedure echo showed no aortic regurgitation — a technical success by VARC-3 criteria. But the mean gradient was 67 mm Hg with an AVA of 0.9 cm² and a Doppler velocity index of 0.24, consistent with severe prosthesis-patient mismatch. Before you throw your hands up, consider what happened next.

At 1 month, the mean gradient had dropped to 27 mm Hg and the AVA had increased to 1.1 cm². Left ventricular remodeling was already evident. At 1 year, the gradient remained stable at 26 mm Hg, AVA 1.1 cm², EF 65%, and the patient was asymptomatic and active. The early high gradient likely reflected incomplete expansion of the third valve and residual compression from the Evolut frame. The improvement over the first month is attributable to a combination of commissural alignment optimization, effective balloon post-dilation (20/40 mm balloon under rapid pacing), and partial mechanical expansion of the Evolut frame from the high-pressure post-dilation. The Evolut frame likely underwent further radial expansion, improving leaflet coaptation and central flow dynamics.

The take-home here is that early post-procedural gradients in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV may look alarming but can improve substantially as frames settle and expand. Don't judge the result solely on the immediate echo — the 1-month study is more informative.

## Why This Case Matters for Your Practice

This is not an isolated curiosity. With TAVI indications expanding to lower-risk and younger patients, and with structural valve deterioration accumulating over time, TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedures will become more common. The era of thinking about TAVI as a one-and-done intervention is ending. Every first TAVI in a younger patient should trigger a conversation about the lifetime trajectory — what happens at valve 2, valve 3, and beyond.

The history of treated endocarditis in this case is also noteworthy. The 2025 ESC guidelines still recommend surgical valve replacement for PVE, but this patient's anatomy and comorbidities made surgery prohibitive. Once infection was documented as resolved — confirmed by negative blood cultures, TEE without vegetations, and CT without paravalvular complications — the decision-making framework shifted to standard structural valve dysfunction assessment. The prior infection informed the evaluation but did not preclude a transcatheter strategy.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **CT is non-negotiable for TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV.** Valve-to-coronary distance, neo-skirt height estimation, frame interaction analysis, and landing zone planning are the foundation of procedural safety. Without this, you are guessing.
- **High implantation within the prior frame is a deliberate strategy** to pin existing leaflets outward, preserve effective orifice area, and minimize neo-skirt height below the coronies. It is not the default — it is a calculated choice based on CT measurements.
- **Commissural alignment must be planned, not left to chance.** In triple-frame anatomy, misaligned commissures can permanently foreclose coronary access. Use CT modeling and fluoroscopic landmarks.
- **Coronary protection may be avoidable with adequate CT clearance**, but the threshold for deploying protection should be low. This team felt comfortable without it based on their measurements — your threshold may differ depending on anatomy and software availability.
- **Early post-procedural gradients can be misleading.** Severe PPM on the immediate post-procedure echo improved dramatically by 1 month due to frame expansion and settling. The 1-month study is your real baseline.
- **Current CT assessment has limits.** Without dedicated 3D expansion simulation software, planning relies on inference. Advocate for better tools as these procedures become more common.
- **Start planning for valve 3 at valve 1.** Lifetime management of structural valve disease begins with the first intervention. Frame choice, implantation depth, and commissural alignment at the first TAVI all have downstream consequences decades later.
