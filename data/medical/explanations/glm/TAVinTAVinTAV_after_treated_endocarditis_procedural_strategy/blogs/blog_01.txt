Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

## Triple Threat: Navigating the Anatomical Minefield of TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV

Picture this: a man in his late 70s walks into your cath lab with three layers of transcatheter valve frames stacked inside his aortic root, a history of prosthetic valve endocarditis, triple-vessel PCI, a pacemaker, COPD, diabetes, and recurrent GI bleeding. His EuroSCORE II is 27.36%. Surgery? That would mean root and ascending aorta replacement given how deeply those prior valve frames are embedded. Oh, and he's got severe symptoms. What do you do?

This is the world we're heading toward — and frankly, already in. As TAVI indications expand and treated patients live longer, structural valve deterioration and repeat interventions are no longer rare edge cases. They're becoming part of the structural heart landscape. And cases like this one, published by Merkulov and colleagues, give us a window into what the future of redo-redo transcatheter work looks like — the good, the bad, and the anatomically terrifying.

## The Case: A Decade of Valve Disease

Let me walk you through this patient's journey, because it's a timeline that structural heart teams will increasingly recognize.

In 2014, he received a 27 mm Lotus valve for severe aortic stenosis. The Lotus, if you recall, was a mechanically-expanded valve — Boston Scientific's answer to the self-expanding and balloon-expandable platforms. By late 2021, that valve was failing. Mean gradient had climbed to 83 mm Hg by early 2022, and his EF had dropped from 54% to 42%. A TAV-in-TAV was performed with a 23 mm Evolut (Medtronic), and his EF recovered to 54%. So far, a standard redo story.

But three months later, in September 2022, he presented with persistent fever and worsening heart failure. Blood cultures grew *Enterococcus faecalis*. TTE showed severe restenosis with a mean gradient of 35 mm Hg. Prosthetic valve endocarditis. He received six weeks of IV ampicillin-based therapy followed by oral antibiotics, and infection was declared resolved based on clinical assessment, repeat cultures, transesophageal echocardiography, and CT.

Then, in January 2024 — about a year and a half after completing antimicrobial therapy — his valve function worsened again. Mean gradient was 49 mm Hg, AVA had shrunk to 0.5 cm², and he had moderate-to-severe regurgitation. The mechanism was likely multifactorial: leaflet injury from endocarditis with subsequent mechanical restriction, plus progressive structural degeneration of two bioprosthetic valves. By the time of admission in June 2024, he was symptomatic walking 200–300 meters, with dizziness, fatigue, and exertional chest tightness.

The decision was made to proceed with a third transcatheter valve — a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV.

## Why This Case Is Not Just "Another Valve-in-Valve"

Here's what makes this case instructive beyond the novelty of a triple stack. Every layer of valve you add multiplies the technical complexity in ways that aren't simply additive — they're exponential. The challenges cluster around four domains: coronary access, effective orifice area, frame interaction, and infection-related structural change.

Let's take these one at a time.

**Coronary obstruction risk.** With two prior valve frames already in situ, a third prosthesis creates a "neo-skirt" — the folded leaflets of the existing valves get pushed outward against the stent frames and can form a barrier below the coronary ostia. The more valves you stack, the higher this neo-skirt sits relative to the coronaries. In this case, the team used preprocedural CT to measure coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, valve-to-coronary distance, and the interaction between the two existing frames. Based on this analysis, they determined that coronary protection was not necessary — a decision that could easily have gone the other way. The high implantation strategy (deploying the third valve at the level of the Evolut leaflets) was specifically chosen to minimize neo-skirt formation below the coronary ostia and preserve future coronary access.

**Effective orifice area.** Each valve-in-valve procedure narrows the functional orifice. The Lotus had an internal lumen, the Evolut went inside it, and now a third valve was going inside the Evolut. MSCT showed the Evolut's minimum lumen diameter was only 13.7 mm. That's a tight space. Valve selection had to account for this — and this is where the choice of prosthesis becomes critical, which we'll get to in a moment.

**Frame interaction and commissural alignment.** Three overlapping stent frames create a cumulative mesh burden that can make future coronary cannulation a nightmare. The team attempted commissural alignment during deployment of the third valve, guided by CT modeling and fluoroscopic landmarks. But as they honestly acknowledge, even with alignment, the cumulative mesh may still present challenges for future coronary interventions — particularly in a patient with known three-vessel disease and multiple prior PCIs.

**The endocarditis factor.** This is perhaps the most novel aspect. The 2025 ESC guidelines still recommend surgical valve replacement for PVE, particularly with heart failure or uncontrolled infection. But this patient had completed antimicrobial therapy, had no evidence of active infection on TEE or CT, and had prohibitive surgical risk. The team's position was that once infection is resolved, management of prosthetic dysfunction should align with non-endocarditis cases. That's a reasonable and increasingly defensible stance, but the evidence base is thin.

## The Procedural Strategy: CT-Guided Everything

This is where the case becomes a teaching artifact. The team didn't just show up and deploy a valve — they engineered the procedure.

Preprocedural MSCT was the linchpin. It confirmed the severity of stenosis (13.7 mm minimum lumen in the Evolut), assessed coronary obstruction risk, evaluated frame-to-frame interaction, and guided valve selection, implantation depth, and the decision to skip coronary protection. Three-dimensional modeling was used to plan commissural alignment.

The procedure was done under local anesthesia with left radial and left femoral access. After aortography confirmed severe regurgitation, a Safari wire was placed in the LV. Balloon pre-dilation was performed with a 22/40 mm catheter under rapid pacing at 180 bpm, with simultaneous aortography confirming unobstructed coronary flow. This step — checking coronary perfusion during pre-dilation — is a smart move in triple-stack anatomy where you're gambling on coronary obstruction with every maneuver.

The valve chosen was an Acurate neo2, size S. The rationale is worth understanding in detail because it speaks to the broader question of how to select valves for redo-redo procedures.

## Why the Acurate neo2?

The team selected this valve for three specific reasons:

First, supra-annular leaflet positioning. In a triple-stack configuration, the functional orifice is determined not by the annulus but by the narrowest point in the existing frame stack. Supra-annular leaflets sit higher in the flow stream, which can optimize hemodynamics in a constrained anatomy.

Second, relatively low frame height. A lower frame means less metal above the coronary ostia, reducing the mesh burden that could impede future coronary access. In a patient with triple-vessel disease and multiple stents, preserving coronary access isn't a luxury — it's a necessity.

Third, the design supports commissural alignment. The Acurate neo2 has stabilization elements and a top crown that allow controlled deployment, and the team used CT-derived landmarks to align the commissures with the Evolut valve leaflets. This alignment aimed to maximize the effective orifice area and reduce patient-prosthesis mismatch — which, given the 13.7 mm landing zone, was a real risk.

The valve was implanted with high positioning — deliberately at the level of the Evolut leaflets. This served a dual purpose: it pinned the existing leaflets against the stent frame (preventing them from folding inward and further narrowing the orifice) and it minimized neo-skirt formation below the coronaries.

Post-dilation was performed with a 20/40 mm balloon under rapid pacing. Final aortography showed correct positioning, preserved coronary perfusion, and no perivalvular or transvalvular regurgitation.

## The Frame Expansion Hypothesis

Here's a detail that deserves attention. Immediately after the procedure, the mean gradient was 67 mm Hg — actually worse than pre-procedure. The AVA was 0.9 cm² with a DVI of 0.24, consistent with severe patient-prosthesis mismatch. That's not what you want to see after a three-hour procedure in a high-risk patient.

But by one month, the mean gradient had dropped to 27 mm Hg and the AVA had increased to 1.1 cm². That's a substantial improvement that can't be explained by valve acclimatization alone. The authors propose that high-pressure balloon post-dilation contributed to partial mechanical expansion of the prior Evolut frame — essentially, the balloon didn't just expand the new Acurate neo2, it also pushed the Evolut frame outward. This improved leaflet mobility and central flow dynamics. Intentional frame fracture wasn't performed, but the hemodynamic improvement suggests that some degree of frame remodeling occurred.

This is an important observation. In valve-in-valve procedures, the existing frame is often treated as a fixed constraint. But high-pressure post-dilation may modify that constraint, particularly with self-expanding nitinol frames like the Evolut. Whether this is reproducible, safe, or generalizable remains unclear — but it's a hypothesis worth testing.

## The Outcome

At 1-year follow-up (June 2025), the patient remained asymptomatic and active. Mean gradient was 26 mm Hg, AVA 1.1 cm², EF 65%. No regurgitation, no recurrent endocarditis, no valve-related complications. That's a durable result in a patient who had exhausted most of his options.

But let's be clear about the ceiling here. If this third valve fails, the options are bleak. Surgical explantation in a triple-stented, triple-valved aortic root would be an anatomical nightmare. Emerging catheter-based techniques like bioprosthetic valve fracture or BASILICA might be considered, but their feasibility in a triple-stented configuration is entirely speculative. This patient is, in a sense, at the end of the transcatheter runway.

## What This Case Teaches Us

This case is a preview of what's coming. Patients who received TAVI a decade ago are now presenting with valve failure. Some have already had a TAV-in-TAV. As survival improves and indications extend to lower-risk and younger populations, the lifetime management of these patients becomes the central challenge of structural heart disease.

The key lessons here aren't just about technical execution — they're about planning philosophy. Every TAVI should be performed with an awareness of what comes next. Frame design, implantation depth, commissural alignment, and coronary access preservation all matter not just for the index procedure but for every future intervention that may follow. The concept of "lifetime management" isn't aspirational language anymore — it's operational reality.

### Key Take-Home Messages

- **TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV is technically feasible** in selected patients with prohibitive surgical risk, but evidence remains sparse and each case requires individualized, CT-guided planning.
- **Preprocedural CT is non-negotiable** — it drives valve selection, implantation depth, coronary obstruction risk assessment, and commissural alignment strategy.
- **High implantation of the third valve** (at the level of the existing valve leaflets) can pin prior leaflets against the frame, preserve effective orifice area, and minimize neo-skirt formation below the coronaries.
- **Valve selection in narrow stack anatomies should prioritize** supra-annular leaflet positioning, low frame height, and commissural alignment capability — the Acurate neo2 was chosen for exactly these reasons.
- **High-pressure balloon post-dilation may partially expand prior self-expanding frames**, improving hemodynamics beyond what the new valve alone achieves — but this observation needs further investigation.
- **Prior prosthetic valve endocarditis is not an absolute contraindication to transcatheter reintervention** once infection is fully treated and confirmed resolved by imaging and cultures — but multidisciplinary review and shared decision-making are essential.
- **Plan for the runway ending.** In triple-stack configurations, future options are severely limited. Lifetime management thinking must start at the first TAVI, not the third.
