Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Evaluation and CT-Guided Procedural Planning

The diagnostic evaluation for this patient needed to accomplish two objectives simultaneously: first, to confirm the severity of prosthetic valve dysfunction to justify reintervention, and second, to verify complete resolution of the prior prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) so that a transcatheter strategy could be considered. Both lines of inquiry relied on multimodality imaging and laboratory testing, and the results fed directly into the CT-based procedural planning that determined whether a third transcatheter valve could be implanted safely.

### Echocardiographic Assessment of Prosthetic Valve Dysfunction

By the time of his June 2024 admission, the patient had two overlapping transcatheter prostheses in place—the original 27 mm Lotus valve implanted in 2014 and a 23 mm Evolut valve implanted in 2022—and transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated severe dysfunction of this composite structure. The examination revealed grade 2–3 intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation and a mean transvalvular gradient of 49 mm Hg. The aortic valve area (AVA) was calculated at 0.5 cm² using the continuity equation, a fundamental Doppler-based method that compares stroke volume at the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) to stroke volume through the prosthetic valve. In this method, the LVOT cross-sectional area is derived from a linear diameter measurement, and the LVOT velocity-time integral (VTI) is obtained by pulsed-wave Doppler, while the prosthetic valve VTI is obtained by continuous-wave Doppler. The ratio of the two stroke volumes yields the effective AVA. In the setting of multiple overlapping valve frames, the LVOT measurement can be particularly challenging because the stent struts and frame material create acoustic shadowing and may distort the true annular geometry. Nevertheless, the markedly reduced AVA, combined with a mean gradient of 49 mm Hg, was consistent with severe prosthetic stenosis.

An additional Doppler parameter that can assist in the assessment of prosthetic valve dysfunction is the Doppler velocity index (DVI), which is the ratio of the LVOT peak velocity to the prosthetic valve peak velocity. A DVI below approximately 0.25 suggests significant prosthetic stenosis, as the velocity through the narrowed prosthesis becomes disproportionately high relative to the velocity proximal to it in the LVOT. At the patient's January 2024 follow-up, the mean gradient had already risen to 49 mm Hg with moderate-to-severe regurgitation, and by June 2024 the findings had persisted or progressed, establishing the indication for reintervention. It is worth noting that the combination of stenosis and regurgitation in a bioprosthetic valve typically reflects structural leaflet deterioration—fibrosis, calcification, or tear—which may be accelerated by prior infection-related injury even after the infection itself has been eradicated.

Transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) was performed prior to the planned reintervention and served a dual purpose: it provided a more detailed assessment of the prosthetic valve morphology and, critically, confirmed the absence of active endocarditis or paravalvular complications. TEE is superior to TTE for visualizing vegetations, abscesses, and paravalvular fistulae, particularly in the setting of prosthetic valves where TTE images are often limited by acoustic shadowing from the stent frame.

### Confirmation of Endocarditis Resolution

The patient had been diagnosed with PVE in September 2022, three months after the second TAVI. At that time, blood cultures were positive for *Enterococcus faecalis*, and TTE showed severe restenosis with a mean gradient of 35 mm Hg. He received a full course of targeted antimicrobial therapy consisting of six weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based treatment followed by oral therapy as recommended by the infectious disease team. By the time of the third TAVI procedure, antimicrobial treatment had been completed and no long-term suppressive antibiotic therapy was ongoing.

Before proceeding with reintervention, the clinical team needed to confirm that the infection was fully resolved. This required multiple convergent data streams. Clinical assessment showed no signs of ongoing infection. Repeated blood cultures were negative, excluding persistent bacteremia. TEE demonstrated no vegetations on either prosthesis and no paravalvular complications such as abscess or fistula. Multislice CT (MSCT) corroborated these findings, showing no evidence of perivalvular collections or other imaging signs of active infection. The convergence of negative cultures, clinical stability, and both TEE and MSCT confirmation was essential because performing a transcatheter valve implantation in the setting of active endocarditis could seed the new prosthesis and result in recurrent infection. The 2025 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines for the management of valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis recommend surgical valve replacement as the standard strategy for PVE, particularly when complicated by heart failure or uncontrolled infection. However, these guidelines also emphasize that management should be individualized based on infection control status, surgical risk, and anatomical feasibility. In this patient, the complete resolution of infection, combined with prohibitive surgical risk, supported consideration of a transcatheter approach.

### MSCT for Anatomical Feasibility Assessment

Once the clinical indication for reintervention was established and active infection was excluded, MSCT became the cornerstone of procedural planning. MSCT is the standard imaging modality for TAVI planning because it provides high-resolution three-dimensional data on the aortic root, annular dimensions, coronary ostial heights, and the spatial relationships between existing prosthetic frames and surrounding structures. In a patient with two prior transcatheter valves, the complexity of the CT analysis is substantially greater than in a de novo TAVI.

Several key measurements were obtained. Coronary ostial height was assessed to determine the vertical distance from the aortic annulus (or in this case, from the effective landing zone of the third valve) to the left and right coronary ostia. Low-lying coronary ostia increase the risk of coronary obstruction when a new valve is implanted because the valve's skirt or leaflet tissue may cover the ostial origin. Sinus of Valsalva dimensions were evaluated to determine whether there was adequate space around the existing valve frames for blood to flow around the prosthesis and reach the coronaries—a concept referred to as the "sinus sequestration" risk. The valve-to-coronary distance, which quantifies the clearance between the upper edge of the prosthetic leaflets (or the neo-skirt) and the coronary ostia, was also measured. These measurements collectively determined whether coronary flow could be preserved during and after the third valve implantation.

### Assessment of Frame Interaction and Minimum Lumen Diameter

A critical finding from the MSCT was the measurement of the minimum lumen diameter within the Evolut prosthesis, which was 13.7 mm. This measurement reflected severe haemodynamic compromise and served as a key parameter for selecting the size of the third valve. The minimum lumen diameter represents the narrowest point through which blood must pass and is determined by the interplay between the inner diameter of the Evolut frame and the degree to which its leaflets have thickened, retracted, or been constrained by the underlying Lotus valve. In a TAV-in-TAV configuration, the inner valve is not simply sitting freely within the outer valve; the two frames interact mechanically. The outer frame (Lotus) may constrain the expansion of the inner frame (Evolut), or the inner frame may not fully expand due to the geometry of the outer frame. The result is a reduced effective orifice area, which in this patient was further compounded by the structural deterioration of the leaflets.

The CT analysis also evaluated the interaction between the Lotus and Evolut stent frames—specifically, how the three layers of metal would overlap once the third valve was deployed. Each transcatheter valve has a unique frame design: the Lotus is a mechanically expanded valve with a relatively short, thick frame; the Evolut is a self-expanding nitinol valve with a tall, hourglass-shaped frame; and the Acurate neo2 (the planned third valve) has its own distinctive supra-annular design. Understanding how these three frames would nest within each other was essential for predicting whether the third valve could expand adequately, whether it would be stable, and whether the composite orifice area would be sufficient.

### The Neo-Skirt Concept and Coronary Flow Preservation

A central anatomical concept in TAV-in-TAV procedures is the "neo-skirt." When a new transcatheter valve is implanted inside an existing one, the leaflets of the old prosthesis are pushed outward against the new valve's stent frame. These displaced leaflets, which are typically thickened and fibrotic from structural degeneration, create a circumferential ring of tissue at the level where they are pinned—this is the neo-skirt. The neo-skirt behaves functionally like an extended fabric skirt on the new valve, but it is composed of the old valve's leaflet material. Its clinical significance lies in its potential to obstruct coronary ostia, particularly when it forms at or below the level of the coronary ostia.

In this case, particular attention was paid to the relationship between the anticipated neo-skirt and the coronary ostia. The procedural strategy of high implantation—deploying the third valve at the level of the Evolut valve leaflets rather than lower in the annulus—was chosen specifically to pin the Evolut leaflets against the stent frame above the coronary ostia, thereby minimizing neo-skirt formation below the ostia. This approach had two benefits: it preserved coronary flow during and after the procedure, and it maintained the possibility of future coronary access, which was particularly relevant given the patient's history of three-vessel coronary artery disease and multiple prior percutaneous interventions.

### Coronary Obstruction Risk Stratification

Coronary artery obstruction is one of the most feared complications of TAV-in-TAV procedures. It occurs when the leaflets of the old prosthesis, displaced outward by the new valve, cover the coronary ostia and acutely obstruct blood flow. The risk is higher in women, in patients with low coronary ostial heights, in patients with small sinuses of Valsalva (which limit the space available for the displaced leaflets), and when the existing valve leaflets are tall enough to reach the coronary ostia when folded outward.

Based on the preprocedural CT analysis—incorporating coronary ostial height, sinus dimensions, valve-to-coronary distance, and the predicted position of the neo-skirt—the team determined that the risk of coronary obstruction was sufficiently low to proceed without active coronary protection. Coronary protection during TAV-in-TAV typically involves placing a guidewire and possibly a catheter or stent in the coronary artery at risk before valve deployment, so that if obstruction occurs, immediate intervention can be performed. While protective, this approach adds procedural complexity and time. The CT-based risk stratification in this patient demonstrated adequate coronary clearance, and simultaneous aortography during balloon pre-dilation confirmed that coronary flow remained unobstructed before the valve was deployed, providing an additional layer of real-time safety verification.

### Surgical Risk Assessment

The decision to pursue a transcatheter rather than surgical strategy was heavily influenced by the patient's overall surgical risk profile. His European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation (EuroSCORE II) was 27.36%, a value that places him in the very high-risk category for cardiac surgery. This score incorporates factors such as age, renal function, previous cardiac surgery, and overall clinical status. Beyond the numerical score, the patient's comorbidities further increased the practical risk of surgery: chronic obstructive pulmonary disease would complicate perioperative ventilation and recovery; type 2 diabetes mellitus increased the risk of infection and wound healing complications; recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding since 2022 raised concerns about perioperative anticoagulation and transfusion requirements; and third-degree atrioventricular block with a permanent pacemaker added technical considerations for surgical management.

Anatomical complexity further tipped the balance against surgery. Two previously implanted transcatheter prostheses had become extensively integrated into the aortic wall over the years, with deep fixation of the stent frames. Surgical explantation of these valves would likely have required total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement—a major operation with substantial morbidity and mortality, even in lower-risk patients. The combination of high EuroSCORE II, multiple comorbidities, and prohibitive anatomical complexity from prior valve frames made open surgery an unfavorable option.

### Multidisciplinary Heart Team Decision-Making

The decision to proceed with a third transcatheter intervention was made through a multidisciplinary heart team review, which included interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, imaging specialists, and anesthesiologists. This collaborative approach is the standard for complex structural interventions and is particularly important in cases that fall outside established guidelines or involve off-label device use. The Acurate neo2 prosthesis was used under institutional approval for off-label use, and shared decision-making involved the patient and his family.

The heart team's discussion would have weighed several factors: the severity of prosthetic valve dysfunction and its impact on the patient's quality of life, the absence of active infection, the prohibitive surgical risk, the CT-confirmed feasibility of a transcatheter approach, and the anticipated technical challenges of a triple-valve configuration. The team would also have considered the limitations of a transcatheter strategy—specifically, that if the third prosthesis were to fail prematurely, future options would be extremely limited, as surgical explantation of three overlapping valves would be even more challenging than explantation of two. This "exit strategy" consideration is an important part of the decision-making process in any redo structural intervention, and the patient and family were presumably informed of this limitation.

### Context within the 2025 ESC Guidelines

The 2025 ESC guidelines for the management of valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis provide a framework for this decision. The guidelines recommend surgical valve replacement for PVE, especially in cases with heart failure, uncontrolled infection, abscess, or destructive lesions. However, they emphasize individualized management based on infection control, surgical risk, and anatomical feasibility. In this patient, several factors supported deviation from the standard surgical recommendation: the infection had been fully treated with documented resolution by blood cultures, TEE, and MSCT; no suppressive antibiotic therapy was ongoing; the EuroSCORE II was extremely high; and the anatomical complexity of two deeply integrated stent frames made surgery technically hazardous. Once active infection was excluded, the management strategy for prosthetic valve dysfunction aligned with the approach used for patients without ongoing endocarditis—that is, the decision was driven by anatomical feasibility, haemodynamic severity, and surgical risk rather than by the history of infection alone. This case illustrates how guideline recommendations can be appropriately individualized when the standard approach poses unacceptable risk and when detailed preprocedural evaluation supports the safety of an alternative strategy.
