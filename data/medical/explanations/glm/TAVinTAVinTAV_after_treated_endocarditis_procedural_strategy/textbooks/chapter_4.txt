Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

Chapter 4: ## Procedural Strategy and Technical Execution of TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV

Performing a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation within two existing prosthetic frames represents one of the most technically demanding scenarios in structural heart disease intervention. By the time a patient reaches a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV configuration, the accumulated stent metal, leaflet tissue, and altered annular geometry create constraints that do not exist during first-time or even second-time interventions. This case illustrates how preprocedural computed tomography (CT) modeling can be translated into a deliberate, step-by-step procedural strategy that addresses the principal hazards of triple-valve implantation: coronary obstruction, severe prosthesis-patient mismatch, and loss of future coronary access.

### Access Strategy and Anesthetic Considerations

The procedure was performed under local anesthesia using left radial and left femoral arterial access. This dual-access approach reflects standard practice in complex transcatheter interventions: the femoral artery serves as the primary delivery route for the valve delivery system, which requires a large-bore sheath, while the radial artery provides a secondary access point for aortography and hemodynamic monitoring. Local anesthesia, rather than general anesthesia, was appropriate given the need for real-time patient feedback and the desire to minimize procedural risk in an elderly patient with significant comorbidities, including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and prior gastrointestinal bleeding. The choice of left-sided access for both routes was likely influenced by the patient's prior vascular complications—specifically, the pulsatile femoral hematoma and pseudoaneurysm that complicated the 2022 TAV-in-TAV procedure and required open vascular surgery—making careful selection of access sites essential.

### Valve Selection: Why Acurate neo2?

The choice of prosthesis for a third transcatheter valve is constrained by the internal dimensions of the existing valve stack. MSCT demonstrated a minimum lumen diameter of only 13.7 mm within the Evolut prosthesis, confirming severe narrowing and dictating that any new valve must be deliverable through a critically small orifice. The team selected a size S Acurate neo2 (Boston Scientific), a self-expanding bioprosthetic valve, for several interrelated reasons.

First, the Acurate neo2 features supra-annular leaflet positioning. In a stacked-valve configuration, where the effective orifice area is progressively compromised by each additional layer of metal and tissue, supra-annular leaflets sit above the narrowest portion of the existing valve frames, potentially maximizing the functional opening area. Second, the valve has a relatively low frame height compared with other self-expanding platforms such as the Evolut. A lower frame height reduces the amount of stent material projecting above the aortic annulus, which in turn lessens the risk that the new valve frame will obstruct coronary ostia or create a barrier to future coronary catheter engagement. Third, the Acurate neo2 has demonstrated favorable hemodynamic performance in narrow anatomies, a critical attribute when implanting within the restricted lumen of a degenerated prior prosthesis. Finally, its design supports commissural alignment, a consideration that becomes increasingly important with each successive transcatheter intervention, as discussed below.

### High Implantation Strategy: A Critical Anatomical Decision

Perhaps the most consequential strategic decision in this procedure was the plan for high implantation of the third valve at the level of the Evolut valve leaflets. To understand why this matters, it is necessary to consider what happens mechanically when a new transcatheter valve is deployed inside an existing one.

When a balloon-expandable or self-expanding valve is implanted within a prior bioprosthesis, the frame of the new valve pushes the leaflets of the old valve outward. However, the old leaflets, which are attached at their bases to the old valve frame, can fold inward—toward the center of the flow lumen—if the new valve is implanted too low relative to them. This inward leaflet folding narrows the effective orifice area and can produce or worsen prosthesis-patient mismatch, a condition associated with poorer long-term outcomes, including persistent gradients, left ventricular hypertrophy, and reduced survival. By implanting the Acurate neo2 at the level of the Evolut leaflets, the team aimed to fix the pre-existing leaflets against the stent frame of the new valve, pinning them outward against the aortic wall and preventing them from projecting into the central flow channel.

Equally important, the high implantation strategy was designed to minimize formation of a neo-skirt below the coronary ostia. The term "neo-skirt" refers to the cylindrical structure created when the fabric cuff and frame of a newly implanted transcatheter valve, combined with the displaced leaflets of prior valves, form a barrier that can cover the coronary ostial openings. In a triple-valve configuration, the cumulative effect of multiple fabric cuffs and folded leaflets creates a particularly tall neo-skirt, potentially obstructing coronary inflow. By positioning the third valve high—at the leaflet level of the Evolut—the team reduced the distance between the top of the new valve and the coronary ostia, thereby limiting the height of any obstructive neo-skirt and preserving coronary flow. This approach also facilitated future coronary access, an essential consideration in a patient with known three-vessel coronary disease and multiple prior percutaneous interventions.

### Commissural Alignment: Preserving Future Options

Commissural alignment refers to the rotational orientation of the valve's commissures—the points where the leaflets attach to the frame—relative to the native aortic commissures and the coronary ostia. In a single TAVI procedure, misalignment can narrow the gaps between stent struts through which coronary catheters must pass. In a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedure, three overlapping frames create a dense metallic mesh, and the cumulative effect of misalignment across multiple valves can effectively wall off the coronary ostia, making future percutaneous coronary intervention impossible or necessitating high-risk surgical revascularization.

The team used preprocedural CT to model the interaction between the Evolut and Acurate neo2 frames, determining the optimal rotational alignment that would position the commissural posts of the new valve between, rather than over, the coronary ostia. Fluoroscopic landmarks identified during CT planning were then used to guide deployment orientation in the catheterization laboratory. Although perfect commissural alignment across three valves cannot always be guaranteed, the deliberate effort to achieve it reflects a lifetime management philosophy: each reintervention should be planned not only for immediate hemodynamic success but also to preserve options for future procedures.

### Balloon Pre-dilation and Post-dilation

Balloon pre-dilation was performed using a 22/40 mm catheter under rapid ventricular pacing at 180 beats per minute. Rapid ventricular pacing transiently reduces cardiac output and aortic pressure, preventing the balloon from being ejected during inflation and allowing controlled, stable dilation. Pre-dilation served to fracture any calcific deposits, expand the degenerated leaflets, and create a more receptive landing zone for the self-expanding prosthesis. Simultaneous aortography during pre-dilation confirmed that coronary flow remained unobstructed even under balloon inflation—an important real-time reassurance in a high-risk coronary anatomy.

Post-dilation was performed after valve deployment using a 20/40 mm balloon, again under rapid pacing. The rationale for post-dilation was twofold. First, it optimized expansion of the newly implanted Acurate neo2, ensuring maximal frame circularity and leaflet coaptation. Second, it likely contributed to further radial expansion of the prior Evolut frame. High-pressure balloon dilation can partially expand or even fracture a previously implanted transcatheter valve frame, and although intentional frame fracture was not performed in this case, the post-dilation may have improved leaflet mobility of the Evolut prosthesis and contributed to the overall hemodynamic result. The clinical significance of this mechanism is discussed further in the outcome section, where the dramatic reduction in transvalvular gradient from post-procedural to one-month follow-up suggests ongoing favorable remodeling of the valve complex.

### Intra-procedural Imaging and Step-by-Step Execution

The procedure illustrates a systematic, imaging-guided approach where each step was confirmed before proceeding to the next. The sequence was as follows:

1. **Access and initial angiography**: Left radial and left femoral arterial access was established under local anesthesia. Aortography with a pigtail catheter confirmed severe aortic regurgitation, establishing the baseline hemodynamic pathology.

2. **Guidewire placement**: A Safari guidewire—a pre-shaped, stiff wire designed for stable positioning in the left ventricle—was advanced via the femoral access and positioned in the left ventricle, providing a secure rail over which the delivery system could track.

3. **Balloon pre-dilation**: A 22/40 mm balloon was inflated under rapid ventricular pacing at 180 beats per minute. Simultaneous aortography confirmed preserved coronary flow during inflation, providing real-time reassurance that balloon expansion did not compromise coronary perfusion.

4. **Valve deployment**: The Acurate neo2 (size S) was advanced over the guidewire and deployed in the aortic position, with rotational orientation guided by preprocedural CT modeling and fluoroscopic landmarks to achieve commissural alignment. The valve was implanted high, at the level of the Evolut leaflets, consistent with the preprocedural strategy.

5. **Post-dilation**: A 20/40 mm balloon was inflated under rapid ventricular pacing to optimize valve expansion and potentially expand the prior Evolut frame.

6. **Final aortography**: Contrast injection confirmed correct valve positioning, preserved coronary perfusion, and absence of both perivalvular and transvalvular regurgitation.

The use of simultaneous aortography during critical steps—pre-dilation and final assessment—exemplifies how real-time imaging complements preprocedural CT planning. CT provides the static anatomical roadmap, while intra-procedural angiography provides dynamic confirmation that the strategy is being executed safely.

### Institutional Approval, Multidisciplinary Review, and Shared Decision-Making

The use of the Acurate neo2 in a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV configuration represents an off-label application of the device, as transcatheter heart valves are regulatory-approved for specific indications and anatomical configurations that do not encompass triple-valve stacking. The procedure was performed under institutional approval for off-label use, ensuring appropriate oversight and documentation.

The decision to proceed was made through a multidisciplinary heart team review involving interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, imaging specialists, and anesthesiologists. This collaborative approach is essential in complex structural interventions where no single specialty possesses all the expertise needed to evaluate the full range of surgical risk, anatomical feasibility, hemodynamic implications, and long-term management considerations. Shared decision-making with the patient and his family ensured that they understood the novel nature of the procedure, the limited alternative options, and the uncertainties inherent in a third transcatheter intervention.

The patient's EuroSCORE II of 27.36% reflected prohibitive surgical risk, and the deep fixation of two prior transcatheter valve frames into the aortic wall meant that surgical explantation would likely have required total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement—an operation of extraordinary complexity in a patient of his age and comorbidity profile. These factors collectively justified the transcatheter approach despite its off-label status and technical challenges.
