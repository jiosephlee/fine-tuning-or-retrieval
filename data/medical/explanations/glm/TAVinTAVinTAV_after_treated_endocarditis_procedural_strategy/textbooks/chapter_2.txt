Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

Chapter 2: ## Pathophysiology of Valve Degeneration and Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis

### Structural Valve Deterioration of Bioprosthetic Transcatheter Valves

Bioprosthetic transcatheter valves, like the 27 mm Lotus valve implanted in this patient in 2014, are subject to structural valve deterioration (SVD) over time. The principal mechanisms include cumulative leaflet damage from cyclic mechanical stress, lipid infiltration, and progressive calcification of the xenograft tissue. These processes lead to leaflet thickening, reduced mobility, and eventual stiffening—producing a functional stenosis that mimics the native calcific aortic stenosis the prosthesis was originally implanted to treat. In some cases, superimposed wear and tear can cause leaflet tears or perforations, resulting in concurrent regurgitation. The typical timeframe for clinically significant SVD is approximately 5 to 10 years after implantation, though this varies with patient age, valve design, and hemodynamic loading conditions. In this patient, approximately 7 to 8 years elapsed between the initial Lotus implantation and the emergence of severe bioprosthetic stenosis, consistent with the expected window for SVD. By December 2021, echocardiographic findings confirmed severe stenosis, and by early 2022 the mean transvalvular gradient had risen dramatically to 83 mm Hg—a value indicating critical obstruction. Notably, this deterioration was accompanied by a decline in left ventricular ejection fraction from 54% to 42% over the same period, demonstrating the direct impact of worsening afterload on ventricular performance.

### Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis: Pathogenesis and *Enterococcus faecalis*

Prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) arises when microorganisms colonize the prosthetic valve surface, typically at sites of endothelial disruption or where foreign material creates a favorable environment for bacterial adherence. The formation of a biofilm on prosthetic material renders bacteria relatively protected from both host immune defenses and antibiotic penetration, contributing to the difficulty of eradication. Transcatheter valves carry a generally lower incidence of PVE compared to surgical prostheses, but the risk persists over the device's lifetime. *Enterococcus faecalis*, the organism isolated in this patient's blood cultures three months after the second TAVI, is a Gram-positive enterococcus that is part of the normal gastrointestinal flora. It is a recognized but less common cause of PVE compared to staphylococci or viridans streptococci. Enterococcal endocarditis tends to affect older patients and those with underlying gastrointestinal pathology, and it can produce subacute presentations with persistent fever and gradual hemodynamic compromise. In this case, the patient presented with persistent fever and worsening heart failure symptoms, with echocardiography revealing severe restenosis (mean gradient 35 mm Hg) superimposed on the recently implanted Evolut valve. The temporal relationship between the second TAVI and the development of PVE within three months suggests that the procedure itself, or the perioperative period, may have served as the inciting event for bacteremia and subsequent prosthetic colonization.

### Interaction Between Treated Endocarditis and Structural Degeneration

A critical question in this case is how the episode of treated endocarditis interacted with the ongoing process of structural degeneration to accelerate valve failure. Although the patient completed a full course of targeted antimicrobial therapy—including six weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based treatment followed by oral therapy—and infection was declared resolved based on clinical assessment, repeat blood cultures, transesophageal echocardiography, and multislice CT, the episode likely left lasting structural consequences. Infective endocarditis can produce direct leaflet injury through several mechanisms: destructive lesions from vegetation-related inflammation, microabscess formation in the periannular region, and mechanical disruption of the leaflet tissue itself. Even after successful microbial eradication, the healing process involves fibrotic repair of the damaged leaflets, which can lead to retraction, stiffening, and restricted leaflet motion. These fibrotic changes would compound any preexisting structural degeneration, effectively accelerating the trajectory toward clinical valve dysfunction. In this patient, the transition from a mean gradient of 35 mm Hg at the time of PVE diagnosis to 49 mm Hg by January 2024—despite documented resolution of infection—suggests that the post-infectious fibrotic remodeling contributed to progressive mechanical restriction. Additionally, the emergence of moderate-to-severe regurgitation alongside the stenotic gradient indicates that leaflet coaptation was compromised, potentially through leaflet shortening or perforation related to the prior infectious process. Thus, the mechanism of prosthetic valve failure in this patient was likely multifactorial, combining the expected trajectory of SVD with the additive effects of infection-related leaflet injury and subsequent fibrosis.

### Hemodynamic Consequences of Combined Stenosis and Regurgitation

When a prosthetic valve develops both stenosis and regurgitation simultaneously, the hemodynamic burden on the left ventricle is compounded. Stenosis imposes a pressure load—the ventricle must generate higher systolic pressures to eject blood through the narrowed orifice—increasing afterload, wall stress, and myocardial oxygen demand. Regurgitation imposes a volume load, as a portion of the ejected blood returns to the ventricle during diastole, increasing preload and chamber dimensions. The combination creates a particularly hostile environment for the myocardium: the ventricle must handle both increased preload and increased afterload simultaneously, which can accelerate the development of ventricular dysfunction, dilatation, and heart failure symptoms. In this patient, the severe stenosis (mean gradient 49 mm Hg, AVA 0.5 cm²) combined with grade 2–3 aortic regurgitation produced the clinical picture of exertional dyspnea, chest discomfort, dizziness, and fatigue that prompted re-presentation. The markedly reduced AVA of 0.5 cm², calculated using the continuity equation, confirmed severe prosthetic dysfunction and provided the quantitative basis for the decision to proceed with a third intervention.

### The Patient's Hemodynamic Trajectory

The patient's hemodynamic course across interventions illustrates the dynamic relationship between valve function and ventricular performance. After the initial Lotus implantation in 2014, the ejection fraction was presumably stable or improved, though specific values from that period are not detailed. By December 2021, the EF was 54%, but as the Lotus valve degenerated and the mean gradient climbed to 83 mm Hg by early 2022, the EF declined to 42%—a reduction reflecting the afterload mismatch imposed by critical stenosis. The subsequent TAV-in-TAV with a 23 mm Evolut valve produced a favorable early hemodynamic response, with EF recovering to 54%, demonstrating that ventricular dysfunction in this context was at least partially reversible with relief of the obstructive lesion. The episode of PVE then introduced a new insult, with the mean gradient rising to 35 mm Hg during active infection. After successful antimicrobial treatment and a period of recovery, by January 2024 the EF had rebounded to 66%—possibly reflecting recovery from the infectious insult and the effects of guideline-directed medical therapy for heart failure—while the mean gradient continued to climb to 49 mm Hg, indicating that valve dysfunction was progressing independently of ventricular recovery. This divergence between improving ventricular function and worsening valve function is characteristic of isolated prosthetic failure and provided a clear rationale for reintervention: the ventricle remained capable of benefiting from relief of the valve obstruction, making the patient a suitable candidate for a third transcatheter procedure.

### Resolution of PVE and Implications for Procedural Decision-Making

The confirmation of PVE resolution was a prerequisite for proceeding with the third TAVI. The diagnostic evaluation to establish resolution included repeated blood cultures to document microbiological clearance, transesophageal echocardiography to exclude residual vegetations or paravalvular complications, and multislice CT to assess for structural sequelae such as abscess cavities or pseudoaneurysms. All of these studies confirmed the absence of active infection. This documentation was essential for two reasons. First, implanting a new prosthesis in the setting of active endocarditis would carry a high risk of immediate reinfection of the new device, potentially creating an intractable clinical situation. Second, the standard of care for active PVE—in accordance with European Society of Cardiology guidelines—favors surgical valve replacement, particularly when complicated by heart failure or uncontrolled infection. By establishing that the infection was cured, the clinical team could justify a transcatheter approach based on the same principles that apply to any patient with structural valve deterioration and prohibitive surgical risk.

### Why Management After Cured Endocarditis Aligns With Standard SVD Management

Once prosthetic valve endocarditis has been definitively treated and the patient shows no evidence of active or ongoing infection, the management strategy for subsequent valve dysfunction aligns with that of standard structural valve deterioration. The prior history of infection does not, by itself, alter the fundamental decision-making framework, which is driven by three factors: the hemodynamic severity of the prosthetic dysfunction, the patient's surgical risk profile, and the anatomical feasibility of a transcatheter approach. In this patient, all three factors favored a transcatheter strategy. The hemodynamic severity was well documented (mean gradient 49 mm Hg, AVA 0.5 cm², moderate-to-severe regurgitation). The surgical risk was prohibitive, with a EuroSCORE II of 27.36%, compounded by COPD, diabetes, recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding, and the technical challenges of explanting two deeply integrated transcatheter valve frames—a procedure that would likely require total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement. The anatomical feasibility was confirmed through detailed CT planning. The absence of active infection meant that no additional infection-specific considerations—such as the need for suppressive antibiotic therapy or the selection of a particular valve type based on infection risk—needed to influence the decision. The multidisciplinary heart team therefore approached the case as they would any complex TAV-in-TAV reintervention, with attention to coronary protection, commissural alignment, and optimization of the effective orifice area, rather than tailoring the strategy around the remote infectious history.
