Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

Chapter 1: ## Case Presentation and Clinical Context

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has become a standard treatment for severe aortic stenosis across a wide spectrum of surgical risk, from inoperable patients to those at low surgical risk. As the population of TAVI recipients grows and survival after implantation extends further, clinicians are increasingly encountering structural valve deterioration in previously implanted bioprostheses. When a transcatheter valve fails, a repeat transcatheter procedure — placing a new valve within the existing one, known as TAV-in-TAV — is often preferable to surgical explantation, particularly in patients with prohibitive operative risk. However, each successive reintervention compounds technical challenges related to coronary access, frame overlap, and residual effective orifice area. The concept of lifetime management in structural valve disease emphasizes that the initial valve choice and implantation strategy should anticipate the likely need for future reinterventions, an consideration that becomes especially important in younger patients and those with long projected survival.

The patient in this case is a man in his late 70s who presented to a tertiary cardiac center with progressive exertional dyspnea, a spastic sensation on the left side of the chest during physical exertion, dizziness, and fatigue. His functional capacity had declined to the point where symptoms limited him to walking only 200–300 meters, consistent with New York Heart Association class III functional limitation. These symptoms were the culmination of a decade-long clinical trajectory involving multiple transcatheter valve interventions and a treated episode of prosthetic valve endocarditis.

His medical history was extensive and central to the decision-making for every intervention. He had three-vessel coronary artery disease managed with multiple percutaneous coronary interventions: the right coronary artery in 2009, the left anterior descending (LAD) artery in 2010, a recurrent LAD intervention in 2012, and the circumflex artery in 2014. He developed third-degree atrioventricular block requiring permanent pacemaker implantation in 2014. Additional comorbidities included arterial hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and recurrent episodes of gastrointestinal bleeding beginning in 2022. This combination of cardiac, metabolic, pulmonary, and bleeding comorbidities placed him at high or prohibitive surgical risk throughout his clinical course and was a primary reason that transcatheter strategies were repeatedly favored over surgical valve replacement.

In 2014, the patient developed progressive degeneration of his native aortic valve with severe stenosis. Given his multiple comorbidities and elevated surgical risk, he underwent transcatheter implantation of a 27 mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific). The Lotus valve is a mechanically expandable transcatheter prosthesis characterized by the ability to be repositioned and fully retrieved prior to final release, as well as a design that produces low paravalvular leak rates. At the time of this initial intervention, TAVI was well established for high-risk surgical candidates, and the device selection reflected the goal of achieving a durable seal in a patient with complex anatomy.

By December 2021, the patient experienced worsening exertional dyspnea, and echocardiographic evaluation revealed findings consistent with severe bioprosthetic valve stenosis. Structural valve deterioration in transcatheter bioprostheses involves progressive leaflet calcification, fibrosis, and restriction, leading to reduced leaflet mobility and elevated transvalvular gradients. Over the following months, the mean transvalvular gradient increased dramatically to 83 mm Hg, and left ventricular ejection fraction declined from 54% in December 2021 to 42%, reflecting the hemodynamic consequences of critical prosthetic stenosis on ventricular function. The severity of gradient elevation and the temporal trajectory of deterioration indicated advanced structural failure of the Lotus prosthesis requiring reintervention.

In May 2022, the patient underwent a TAV-in-TAV procedure with implantation of a 23 mm Evolut valve (Medtronic). The Evolut platform is a self-expanding supra-annular bioprosthesis known for favorable hemodynamic performance, particularly low transvalvular gradients, and the ability to be repositioned during deployment. The self-expanding design was advantageous in this setting because it could adapt to the rigid frame of the existing Lotus valve. The procedure was complicated by a pulsatile femoral hematoma and pseudoaneurysm at the access site, necessitating open vascular surgical repair — a reminder that access-related complications remain a significant source of morbidity in TAVI, even as valve delivery systems have become lower-profile. Following the TAV-in-TAV, the patient's left ventricular ejection fraction improved from 42% to 54%, demonstrating a favorable early hemodynamic response to relief of the obstructive gradient. He was subsequently managed with guideline-directed medical therapy for heart failure, including beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, and diuretics as clinically indicated.

Three months later, in September 2022, the patient was hospitalized with persistent fever and worsening heart failure symptoms. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated severe restenosis of the aortic valve prosthesis with a mean gradient of 35 mm Hg. Blood cultures were positive for *Enterococcus faecalis*, establishing a diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). Prosthetic valve endocarditis is a serious complication defined by infection of the valve prosthesis and surrounding cardiac structures; *Enterococcus* species are among the most common enterococcal pathogens in healthcare-associated endocarditis, and *E. faecalis* in particular is known for its ability to form biofilms on prosthetic material. The patient received a full course of targeted antimicrobial therapy consisting of six weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based treatment, followed by oral therapy as recommended by the infectious disease team. Resolution of infection was confirmed through repeated blood cultures, transesophageal echocardiography, and multislice CT (MSCT), all of which demonstrated no evidence of active endocarditis, vegetations, or paravalvular complications. At the time of the subsequent third TAVI, antimicrobial therapy had been fully completed and no long-term suppressive antibiotic regimen was ongoing.

Despite successful treatment of the endocarditis episode, follow-up assessment in January 2024 revealed renewed and progressive prosthetic valve dysfunction. Echocardiography showed severe aortic stenosis with moderate-to-severe regurgitation, a mean transvalvular gradient of 49 mm Hg, and a markedly reduced aortic valve area of 0.5 cm², calculated using the continuity equation. The mechanism of prosthetic failure in this interval was likely multifactorial, involving both structural leaflet degeneration from the prior infection — with fibrosis and probable mechanical restriction of the leaflets — and progressive structural deterioration of the transcatheter prostheses themselves. The left ventricular ejection fraction had recovered to 66%, suggesting that the ventricle had regained contractile reserve after the earlier period of afterload stress, but the severe prosthetic dysfunction again produced symptomatic limitation. On readmission in June 2024, transthoracic echocardiography confirmed persistent grade 2–3 intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation and the severe stenotic gradient. MSCT revealed a minimum lumen diameter of only 13.7 mm within the Evolut prosthesis, confirming severe hemodynamic compromise and providing critical anatomical data for planning a third transcatheter intervention.

This patient's trajectory illustrates the central clinical challenge that motivates this case report: as the population of TAVI recipients grows and patients survive longer with their implants, an increasing number will present with structural valve deterioration requiring repeat intervention. Each additional valve layer compounds the complexity of future procedures, creating a cascading series of technical problems involving coronary access, frame interaction, and effective orifice area. The concept of lifetime management in structural valve disease holds that clinicians must consider the full trajectory of likely reinterventions from the very first valve implantation — anticipating how initial device choices will influence the feasibility, safety, and hemodynamic outcomes of subsequent procedures. This case, involving a third transcatheter valve implanted after two prior TAVI procedures and a treated episode of prosthetic endocarditis, represents an extreme example of this emerging clinical paradigm and underscores the need for detailed preprocedural imaging, multidisciplinary decision-making, and strategy-driven intervention.
