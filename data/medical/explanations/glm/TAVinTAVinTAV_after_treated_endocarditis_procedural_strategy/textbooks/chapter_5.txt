Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

Chapter 5: ## Outcome, Follow-up, and Clinical Lessons

### Immediate Post-Procedural Assessment

Procedural success was evaluated according to the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 (VARC-3) criteria, which provide a standardized framework for reporting outcomes after transcatheter aortic valve interventions. VARC-3 encompasses assessment of device success, early safety endpoints, and clinical efficacy at follow-up. Device success, in particular, requires confirmation of correct valve deployment, freedom from moderate or severe regurgitation, and acceptable haemodynamic performance without procedure-related complications. In this case, immediate post-procedural transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) confirmed successful deployment of the third transcatheter valve (Acurate neo2, size S). The valve leaflets were mobile with complete opening, and there was no evidence of aortic regurgitation (grade 0)—a critical finding given the moderate-to-severe regurgitation present before the intervention. Final aortography during the procedure had likewise confirmed correct positioning, preserved coronary perfusion, and absence of both perivalvular and transvalvular regurgitation. However, haemodynamic parameters revealed a surprisingly elevated mean transvalvular gradient of 67 mm Hg, a calculated aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.9 cm², and a Doppler velocity index (DVI) of 0.24. These values were consistent with severe patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM) and represented a concerning early result that warranted close surveillance.

### Early Patient-Prosthesis Mismatch and Its Context

Patient-prosthesis mismatch describes a situation in which the effective orifice area of a normally functioning prosthesis is too small relative to the patient's body size, resulting in abnormally high transvalvular gradients. It is most commonly classified using the indexed effective orifice area: severe PPM is generally defined as an indexed EOA of ≤0.65 cm²/m², though thresholds can vary slightly depending on the guideline and the measurement method. In the transcatheter context, PPM is a recognized complication of valve-in-valve procedures, where the existing prosthetic frame and leaflets physically constrain expansion of the new valve, reducing the achievable orifice area. In this patient, the indexed AVA of 0.4 cm²/m² placed him squarely in the severe PPM category. It is important to understand that this finding does not necessarily indicate procedural failure or device malfunction; rather, it reflects the geometric constraints of implanting a third valve within two existing frames. The clinical significance of PPM includes persistent gradients, less complete left ventricular regression, and potentially worse long-term outcomes, making its recognition and monitoring essential after complex valve-in-valve interventions.

### Hemodynamic Evolution and Mechanisms of Gradient Resolution

Despite the concerning immediate post-procedural gradients, follow-up TTE at 1 month demonstrated marked improvement: the mean gradient had decreased to 27 mm Hg, and the AVA had increased to 1.1 cm². Left ventricular remodelling was also observed, with a reduction in chamber volumes. This favorable evolution reflects several mechanisms. First, commissural alignment between the Acurate neo2 and the prior Evolut valve leaflets, guided by preprocedural CT modelling, helped optimize central flow dynamics and the effective orifice area. Second, post-dilatation with a 20/40 mm balloon under rapid ventricular pacing likely contributed to further radial expansion of the Evolut frame, partially improving leaflet mobility. Third, the self-expanding nature of the Acurate neo2 prosthesis allows for continued radial force application over time, which may produce gradual frame expansion and leaflet adaptation in the days to weeks after implantation. While intentional frame fracture was not performed, the combination of high-pressure balloon post-dilation and the self-expanding design likely facilitated the haemodynamic improvement observed at 1 month. This temporal pattern underscores an important clinical point: early gradients after valve-in-valve procedures, particularly with self-expanding prostheses, may overestimate the eventual steady-state haemodynamic performance and should be interpreted with caution.

### Intermediate and Long-Term Follow-Up

At 3.5 months post-implantation, the patient remained asymptomatic with good exercise tolerance and no signs of heart failure or recurrent infection. Valve function remained stable within the moderately stenotic range. At 1-year follow-up (June 2025), the patient was clinically stable and active, without dyspnoea or signs of decompensation. TTE confirmed a mean gradient of 26 mm Hg, AVA of 1.1 cm², and ejection fraction of 65%. No valve-related complications, regurgitation, or evidence of recurrent endocarditis were observed. These findings confirm the durability of the result and demonstrate a sustained functional benefit of the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV intervention. The absence of recurrent endocarditis at 1 year is particularly noteworthy given the patient's prior prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) caused by Enterococcus faecalis, supporting the principle that, once infection is fully treated and confirmed resolved by clinical assessment, repeated blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and CT, subsequent transcatheter management can proceed without the ongoing concern of active infection driving decision-making.

### Challenges of Future Interventions

Despite the favorable outcome, this patient's therapeutic options for any future valve failure are extremely limited. Three overlapping transcatheter valve frames now occupy the aortic annular region, creating a complex stent architecture that poses substantial challenges for both transcatheter and surgical approaches. From a transcatheter perspective, the cumulative mesh burden of three stent frames threatens coronary access, particularly in a patient with known three-vessel coronary artery disease and multiple prior percutaneous coronary interventions. Even with careful commissural alignment performed during each procedure, the increasing density of metal struts may impede catheter engagement of the coronary ostia. From a surgical perspective, the deep fixation of the prior valve frames into the aortic wall makes explantation technically hazardous; surgical removal would likely necessitate total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement, a procedure associated with prohibitive risk in this patient given his EuroSCORE II of 27.36% and comorbidities including COPD, diabetes, and recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding. These constraints highlight the narrow therapeutic margin that accumulates with each successive transcatheter reintervention.

### Emerging Catheter-Based Solutions

Several catheter-based techniques have emerged as potential strategies to address complications in complex valve-in-valve configurations. Bioprosthetic aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction (BASILICA) involves lacerating the leaflets of the existing bioprosthesis to create a pathway for coronary flow when a new valve is deployed, and it has been used to prevent coronary obstruction in high-risk valve-in-valve cases. Bioprosthetic valve fracture, achieved through high-pressure balloon dilation, can fracture the frame of a previously implanted bioprosthesis to allow fuller expansion of a subsequently implanted transcatheter valve, thereby improving the effective orifice area and reducing PPM. While these techniques represent important additions to the structural interventionalist's armamentarium, their feasibility and safety in a triple-stented configuration remain uncertain. The altered mechanics of three overlapping frames, the potential for unpredictable fracture patterns, and the risk of catastrophic coronary obstruction in this anatomy make extrapolation from single valve-in-valve experience difficult. These uncertainties reinforce the need for individualized risk assessment and multidisciplinary decision-making in such complex cases.

### Principles of Lifetime Management

This case illustrates several critical principles for the lifetime management of patients undergoing transcatheter valve interventions. First, with each successive reintervention, considerations such as frame layering, commissural alignment, coronary reaccess, and residual effective orifice area become progressively more complex and constrain future options. Second, long-term planning must begin at the time of the first intervention, particularly in younger or high-risk patients who are likely to outlive their initial prosthesis. The choice of valve type, implantation depth, and commissural orientation at the first procedure can profoundly influence the feasibility and safety of future valve-in-valve procedures. Third, preprocedural CT imaging is indispensable for evaluating coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, valve-to-coronary distance, and the interaction between existing valve frames, all of which inform procedural strategy and risk assessment. Fourth, as TAVI indications expand and patient longevity increases, structural valve deterioration and the need for repeat interventions will become more common, making systematic long-term surveillance essential. This case demonstrates that a third transcatheter intervention after treated PVE is feasible when guided by detailed imaging and a predefined strategy, but it also underscores that the window for intervention narrows with each procedure, making prospective lifetime management planning a clinical necessity rather than a theoretical consideration.
