Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Management] According to the 2025 ESC guidelines for the management of valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis, what is the recommended treatment strategy for prosthetic valve endocarditis, and under what circumstances might a transcatheter approach be considered instead of surgery in a patient with prior TAVI who develops PVE?

According to the 2025 ESC guidelines for the management of valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis, surgical valve replacement remains the recommended treatment strategy for prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), particularly when complicated by heart failure, uncontrolled infection, or periannular extension. However, the guidelines explicitly emphasize that management must be individualized, taking into account infection control status, surgical risk, and anatomical feasibility of both surgical and transcatheter options.

A transcatheter approach may be considered when three broad conditions are met. First, the infection must be considered cured — confirmed by clinical assessment, repeated blood cultures, and imaging (transthoracic and transesophageal echocardiography, and where appropriate, CT) demonstrating resolution of vegetations and absence of paravalvular complications. Second, the patient must carry prohibitive or high surgical risk, as assessed by a multidisciplinary heart team. Third, detailed preprocedural imaging — particularly contrast CT — must confirm anatomical feasibility, including adequate coronary clearance, acceptable landing zone dimensions, and a viable strategy to preserve coronary access and minimize prosthesis-patient mismatch.

This case illustrates these principles well. The patient had a history of two prior TAVI procedures and developed PVE caused by *Enterococcus faecalis*, which was treated with a complete course of targeted antimicrobial therapy. Resolution of infection was confirmed by repeated blood cultures, transesophageal echocardiography, and CT. When severe prosthetic dysfunction subsequently developed, surgical replacement was deemed exceptionally high-risk: the EuroSCORE II was 27.36%, and the deeply integrated frames of two prior transcatheter prostheses would likely have necessitated total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement. In the absence of active infection and with CT-confirmed feasibility — including favorable valve-to-coronary distances and a planned high-implantation strategy to preserve coronary flow — a third transcatheter valve was implanted successfully.

A critical teaching point emerges from this case: once endocarditis is considered cured and no long-term suppressive therapy is required, the decision-making framework for prosthetic valve dysfunction shifts toward the same principles used for structural valve deterioration in patients without a history of infection — namely, anatomical feasibility, hemodynamic severity, and surgical risk. The prior history of treated endocarditis alone should not, in the absence of active infection, mandate surgical management if the patient's risk profile and anatomy favor a transcatheter approach. Multidisciplinary heart team evaluation and CT-guided procedural planning are essential to optimizing outcomes in these complex cases.
