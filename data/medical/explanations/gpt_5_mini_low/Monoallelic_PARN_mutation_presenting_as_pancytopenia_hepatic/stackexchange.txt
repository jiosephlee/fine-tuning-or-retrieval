Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Clinical Pearls] In a young adult with pancytopenia, premature greying, hepatosplenomegaly, and interstitial lung disease, what clinical clues should prompt evaluation for a telomere biology disorder even in the absence of the classical dyskeratosis congenita mucocutaneous triad?

Bottom line: In any young adult with unexplained cytopenias plus premature ageing or unexplained organ fibrosis, think telomere biology disorder (TBD) even if the classic DC mucocutaneous triad is absent. Early recognition changes diagnostic testing, genetic counselling, surveillance, and transplant planning.

Clinical clues from this case that should prompt evaluation for a TBD
- Age and multisystem premature‑ageing phenotype: striking premature greying, osteoporosis and early functional decline in a patient in her late 20s.
- Pancytopenia or progressive cytopenias out of proportion to an apparent cause: persistent pancytopenia with a low reticulocyte count and marrow showing ineffective erythropoiesis despite correction of severe B12/folate deficiency.
- Unexplained hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis in a young adult: imaging and elastography showing cirrhosis without viral, autoimmune or metabolic explanation.
- Interstitial lung disease / idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis at a young age: CT with diffuse ground‑glass and subpleural fibrosis in a patient decades younger than usual for IPF.
- Hepatosplenomegaly and systemic inflammatory features without another unifying diagnosis.
- Multiorgan involvement that disproportionately exceeds what would be expected from an acquired single cause (for example, severe B12 deficiency produced some marrow findings but not the full constellation of pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis and premature ageing).
- Additional supportive findings: neuropathy, bone marrow FDG uptake without malignancy, normal cytogenetics (argues against myeloid malignancy), family history of early pulmonary or liver disease (may be absent due to de novo variants, variable penetrance, or monoallelic disease).
- Young age with organ fibrosis, bone marrow failure or other “age‑related” diseases—think telomere-driven premature cellular senescence.

Why these clues point to a TBD (pathophysiologic reasoning)
- Telomere maintenance defects preferentially affect highly proliferative tissues (bone marrow, mucosa, hair, lung alveolar epithelium, hepatocytes) leading to early tissue failure and fibrosis. Thus multisystem premature‑ageing features, marrow failure and early fibrotic disease are biologically concordant with telomere dysfunction.
- Variable penetrance and gene-specific phenotypes (monoallelic PARN, TERT, TERC, RTEL1, etc.) mean classical mucocutaneous signs of dyskeratosis congenita may be absent; organ‑predominant presentations (IPF, hepatic fibrosis, bone marrow failure) are well described.

What to do next (diagnostic standard and implications)
- Measure telomere length by a validated laboratory method (flow‑FISH is the diagnostic standard in suspected TBD; relative leukocyte telomere length can also be informative but less standardized).
- Perform germline genetic testing for telomere‑related genes (targeted NGS panel or exome with attention to TERT, TERC, PARN, RTEL1, DKC1 and others). In the case, clinical exome identified a monoallelic PARN variant supporting a TBD diagnosis despite absent mucocutaneous triad.
- If a pathogenic/likely pathogenic variant or very short telomeres are found, provide genetic counselling and cascade testing for at‑risk relatives.
- Management and surveillance implications: avoid cytotoxic exposures when possible, screen for progressive marrow failure, monitor pulmonary and hepatic disease, counsel on malignancy risk, and plan hematopoietic stem cell transplant with telomere‑aware protocols (reduced‑intensity conditioning) when indicated.

Clinical pearl: Do not rely on the mucocutaneous triad to exclude a TBD. In young adults, the combination of unexplained cytopenias (especially with low reticulocyte response), premature greying/other premature‑ageing signs, and early organ fibrosis (lung or liver) should trigger telomere length testing and germline sequencing—as illustrated by this patient with a monoallelic PARN variant whose phenotype united marrow failure, hepatic fibrosis and IPF despite absent classic DC mucocutaneous features.

### [Morning Report] How does a low reticulocyte count with dimorphic anemia, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils help distinguish ineffective erythropoiesis from immune hemolysis, particularly when the direct antiglobulin test is weakly positive?

Direct answer
A low reticulocyte count in the setting of pancytopenia and the specific peripheral‑blood morphologic features described (dimorphic red cells, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, hypersegmented neutrophils) points to ineffective erythropoiesis — intramedullary destruction of developing erythroid precursors — rather than peripheral immune hemolysis, even when the DAT is weakly positive.

Why — clinical reasoning and pathophysiology
- Reticulocyte response: In true peripheral hemolysis the marrow is intact and should mount a brisk compensatory reticulocytosis (absolute or corrected retic index >2, polychromasia on smear). A low reticulocyte percentage (0.39% here) despite anemia indicates the marrow is failing to release mature reticulocytes — either because precursors are dying in the marrow (ineffective erythropoiesis) or because of marrow failure.
- Marrow findings: Bone marrow in this patient showed erythroid hyperplasia with increased iron stores. That pattern is classic for ineffective erythropoiesis — the marrow is hyperactive but the cells are not surviving to circulate. By contrast, autoimmune hemolysis produces marrow erythroid hyperplasia plus robust reticulocytosis and reduced marrow iron stores (because iron is being lost from circulating RBCs and reused).
- Peripheral morphologic clues: Hypersegmented neutrophils and Cabot rings are hallmarks of megaloblastic/intramedullary dyserythropoiesis (eg, severe B12/folate deficiency). Basophilic stippling can be seen with impaired heme synthesis or toxic/metabolic causes and with ineffective erythropoiesis. These findings support a defect in erythroid maturation rather than simple peripheral removal of otherwise normal RBCs.
- Biochemistry/iron studies: Elevated serum iron and ferritin in this case are compatible with ineffective erythropoiesis (recycling iron remains intramedullary/in macrophages and saturates stores). In immune hemolysis one typically sees increased LDH and indirect bilirubin (both can be present in ineffective erythropoiesis as well), but iron indices are usually low/normal as iron is reutilized.
- DAT interpretation: A weakly positive DAT (IgG 2+ here) does not by itself prove clinically significant autoimmune hemolysis. DAT can be positive in low titer after transfusion, in chronic inflammatory states, or be a nonspecific laboratory finding. True warm autoimmune hemolysis usually has a correlate of brisk reticulocytosis, spherocytes on smear, falling hemoglobin with evidence of peripheral RBC destruction, and clinical hemolysis. In this patient the absence of a reticulocyte response, marrow pattern, and smear morphology favor ineffective erythropoiesis as the dominant process despite a positive DAT.

How to apply this at the bedside (practical checklist)
- Always integrate retic count (and corrected retic index) with smear and marrow when available. Low retic + erythroid hyperplasia = ineffective erythropoiesis.
- Use peripheral smear morphology: hypersegmented neutrophils, Cabot rings, basophilic stippling → think megaloblastic/ineffective erythropoiesis.
- Iron studies: high iron/ferritin and marrow iron excess support ineffective erythropoiesis; low marrow iron favors hemolysis or blood loss.
- Reassess the DAT: repeat after strict wash, perform elution studies, and correlate with clinical hemolysis (rapid Hgb fall, jaundice, spherocytes, very high retic). A solitary weak DAT without other supportive evidence should not override the above.
- Treat and reassess: correct reversible causes (here B12/folate); effective therapy should increase retic count if marrow can produce mature RBCs. Persistent low retic after correction suggests underlying marrow failure or inherited dyserythropoiesis (as in this case, raising suspicion for a telomere biology disorder).

Case‑specific synthesis
In this patient the constellation — severe B12/folate deficiency, peripheral smear with hypersegmentation/Cabot rings/stippling, bone marrow erythroid hyperplasia with increased iron stores, low reticulocyte percentage (0.39%) — is classic for ineffective erythropoiesis. Although the DAT was IgG 2+ the lack of an appropriate reticulocyte response, absence of spherocytes or brisk peripheral reticulocytosis, and marrow picture make immune hemolysis unlikely as the primary process. The positive DAT may be an incidental or nonspecific finding; clinical management appropriately focused on correcting nutritional causes and evaluating underlying marrow vulnerability (ultimately identifying a PARN variant and a telomere biology disorder).

### [Pathophysiology] What is the role of PARN in telomere maintenance, and how do monoallelic versus biallelic PARN variants typically differ in phenotype across the spectrum of telomere biology disorders?

Direct answer
- PARN is an essential deadenylase required for the 3′-end maturation and stability of the telomerase RNA component (TERC). By trimming aberrant oligo(A) tails on nascent TERC and other small nuclear RNAs, PARN promotes correct 3′-end formation and protects TERC from exonucleolytic decay. Loss of PARN function therefore reduces mature TERC levels, impairs telomerase activity, accelerates telomere shortening and precipitates the cellular senescence/apoptosis that underlies telomere biology disorders (TBDs).

How monoallelic versus biallelic PARN variants differ clinically (principles and spectrum)
- Allelic dose and timing:
  - Biallelic (recessive) loss-of-function PARN variants produce a more severe, early‑onset telomere disease. Reported phenotypes include Hoyeraal–Hreidarsson–type syndromes and autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6) with the classic mucocutaneous triad, profound marrow failure, neurodevelopmental features and very short telomeres in childhood.
  - Monoallelic (heterozygous) PARN variants most commonly present later and with more organ‑restricted or milder phenotypes. The common manifestations are adult‑onset idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), bone‑marrow failure syndromes (aplastic anaemia or cytopenias) and increasingly recognized hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis. Penetrance is incomplete and expressivity variable.
- Mechanisms:
  - Biallelic disease reflects near‑absent PARN function with severe reduction of mature TERC and marked telomerase deficiency.
  - Heterozygous variants are often consistent with haploinsufficiency: a single defective allele reduces PARN dosage enough in some individuals/tissues to lower TERC and telomerase activity over years, predisposing to organ‑specific failure (lung, liver, marrow) rather than the global, early phenotype seen with biallelic loss.
- Clinical pattern you should expect:
  - Age: biallelic → childhood/young childhood; monoallelic → adulthood (but can present in young adults).
  - Features: biallelic → multisystem DC/HH phenotype (mucocutaneous findings, severe marrow failure, growth/neuro defects); monoallelic → adult fibrotic lung disease, progressive cytopenias or marrow failure, hepatic fibrosis, variable mucocutaneous signs or absent classic triad.
  - Penetrance and severity vary widely even within families; environmental stressors or additional genetic hits (other telomere gene variants, somatic rescue) modify phenotype.

Diagnostics and clinical reasoning (applied to this case)
- Confirmatory approach: measure telomere length (flow-FISH or qPCR in leukocyte subsets) and perform comprehensive genetic testing (next‑generation sequencing panel/exome). Short telomeres plus a PARN variant strengthen causality.
- In the presented case the monoallelic PARN missense (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) lies in a functional domain, is predicted damaging, and the patient has a pattern concordant with monoallelic PARN disease: young adult with IPF, hepatic fibrosis and marrow vulnerability (pancytopenia that persisted despite correction of deficiencies). The absence of the classic DC triad and the adult presentation fit the typical monoallelic phenotype, albeit with substantial multi‑organ involvement—an example of the variable expressivity and incomplete penetrance described above.
- Important caveat: a single monoallelic variant can be difficult to classify; functional assays, telomere length testing, segregation studies and search for additional genetic modifiers help establish pathogenicity.

Management and implications
- Once PARN‑related TBD is suspected: counsel family, institute organ‑specific surveillance (pulmonary function, liver imaging/ elastography, serial blood counts), avoid telomere‑accelerating exposures (smoking, certain chemotherapy), and refer to specialized centres for consideration of hematopoietic stem‑cell transplantation if progressive marrow failure develops.
- Therapeutic note: androgens have been used to elongate telomeres in small studies; HSCT can be curative for marrow disease but requires altered conditioning regimens in TBD and careful pre‑transplant evaluation because of pulmonary/hepatic fibrosis risk.
- In this patient the monoallelic PARN variant provides a unifying explanation for multi‑organ fibrosis and marrow vulnerability and justifies ongoing telomere/TBD surveillance and genetic counselling for relatives.

Take‑home clinical pearl
- PARN maintains telomere integrity by enabling TERC maturation; biallelic PARN loss causes early, severe DC/HH phenotypes, whereas monoallelic PARN variants typically predispose to adult‑onset, organ‑restricted telomere disease (IPF, marrow failure, hepatic fibrosis) with variable penetrance—so a young adult with unexplained pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis plus cytopenias should prompt telomere length testing and PARN-inclusive genetic testing, even if classical mucocutaneous signs are absent.

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a patient with suspected telomere biology disorder presenting with cytopenias, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis, what diagnostic tests beyond routine labs would best establish the diagnosis and assess disease extent?

Direct answer
- Two tests most important to confirm a telomere biology disorder (TBD) and to stage organ involvement are: (1) a validated telomere‑length assay (flow‑FISH) on peripheral blood leukocytes (and if needed skin fibroblasts) and (2) comprehensive germline genetic testing with confirmatory/segregation studies and directed functional testing of the identified PARN variant.

Clinical reasoning and recommended tests (how they apply to this case)
1) Telomere length measurement — flow‑FISH (gold standard for clinical TB D diagnosis)
- Why: TBDs are defined by abnormally short telomeres in actively dividing tissues. Flow‑FISH provides age‑adjusted telomere length percentiles for lymphocyte and granulocyte subsets and is the accepted clinical test to support a genetic TBD.
- Application to the case: the patient has clinical features strongly suggestive of TBD (young age, pancytopenia, hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying) but only a monoallelic PARN variant classified as VUS. Demonstration of very short telomeres (eg, <1st–10th percentile for age, depending on assay) would convert clinical suspicion into objective diagnostic support and strengthen pathogenicity attribution to the PARN variant.

2) Germline genetic work‑up and variant adjudication
- Why: Next‑generation sequencing identified a monoallelic PARN missense change (VUS). To establish causality you need: (a) review with a comprehensive targeted TBD gene panel (or reanalysis of exome) to exclude additional variants in TERT, TERC, RTEL1, DKC1, etc.; (b) familial segregation testing (parents, siblings) to see if variant is de novo or segregates with disease; and (c) if available, functional assays (TERC levels, PARN protein/function assays, telomerase activity assays).
- Application to the case: demonstrating that no other telomerase gene variant is present, that the PARN change is de novo or segregates, or showing reduced telomerase RNA/maturation or impaired PARN function would upgrade the variant from VUS toward pathogenic.

3) Tissue‑specific telomere or mosaicism assessment when results are discordant
- Why: somatic reversion or mosaicism can mask telomere shortening in blood. Measuring telomere length in skin fibroblasts or buccal cells, or repeat flow‑FISH, can uncover tissue‑restricted defects.
- Application: if peripheral blood telomere length is borderline, a fibroblast telomere assay can help.

4) Quantify organ involvement and baseline staging (important for prognosis and management)
- Pulmonary: high‑resolution chest CT (HRCT) with formal radiology staging (usual interstitial pneumonia pattern), pulmonary function tests including DLCO, and 6‑minute walk testing. These assess extent and function of the IPF already suggested on PET–CT and ground‑glass/fibrotic changes in the report.
- Hepatic: transient elastography was already done (liver stiffness 18.1 kPa → cirrhosis). Consider formal hepatology staging (labs for synthetic function, portal hypertension evaluation) and, selectively, liver biopsy if etiology uncertain or to guide management.
- Hematologic: serial complete blood counts with reticulocyte count, repeated bone marrow exam if counts decline, and cytogenetics/MDS panel—already done once but repeat testing is important because TBD patients are at risk for evolving marrow failure or clonal cytogenetic changes.
- Extra‑haematologic baseline: DXA (already showed osteoporosis), dermatology/oral exam, neurologic evaluation for neuropathy, fertility assessment if relevant, and cancer surveillance per TBD guidance.

5) Pre‑transplant and treatment‑planning testing
- If bone marrow transplant is contemplated: HLA typing, evaluation of donor telomere length (related donors may carry the same defect), and tailored transplant conditioning (reduced intensity regimens are recommended in TBD).
- Infectious and malignancy surveillance baseline (eg, HPV, liver cancer screening as indicated).

How these tests change management in this case
- A short telomere result plus supportive genetic/functional data would confirm a TBD diagnosis despite the VUS and direct counseling, family testing, organ surveillance, avoidance of genotoxic exposures, and earlier consideration of transplant with modified conditioning.
- Normal telomere lengths and negative segregation/functional studies would point away from a TBD as the unifying explanation and prioritize other causes and longitudinal observation.

Concise implementation plan for this patient
1. Order flow‑FISH telomere length testing (age‑adjusted percentiles) on peripheral blood; if borderline, add skin fibroblast telomere testing.
2. Re‑review the exome result with a certified molecular geneticist: reflex to a comprehensive TBD gene panel or reanalysis, perform familial segregation testing (parents/siblings), and request functional testing if available (telomerase activity, TERC RNA levels, PARN functional assays).
3. Complete organ staging: formal HRCT chest + PFTs/DLCO and 6MWT; continue hepatology follow‑up given elastography‑documented cirrhosis; repeat bone marrow studies if counts decline; document baseline DXA and targeted specialty evaluations.
4. Provide genetic counseling, discuss implications for family donors, and plan surveillance and precautions (avoid smoking, limit hepatotoxic drugs, vaccination as appropriate).

Key teaching pearl
- In suspected TBD the combination of validated telomere length testing (flow‑FISH) and careful germline genetic adjudication (panel/sequencing, segregation and functional assays) is required to move from suspicion to diagnosis and to guide organ‑specific staging and management — particularly when sequencing yields a monoallelic VUS, as in this patient.

### [Morning Report] When clinical exome sequencing identifies a monoallelic PARN missense variant classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance, what clinical–genetic correlation steps can strengthen confidence that it is disease-relevant?

Short answer
When clinical exome returns a monoallelic PARN missense variant labelled VUS, you strengthen confidence that it is disease‑relevant by integrating (1) detailed phenotype–genotype concordance, (2) objective telomere biology testing, (3) familial and segregation data, (4) targeted functional and molecular assays, and (5) formal reclassification using ACMG criteria plus literature/databank correlation. In this case each of those steps can move the c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg) variant from “uncertain” toward likely pathogenic if they support telomere dysfunction.

What to do and why — practical, case‑grounded steps

1) Rigorously compare the phenotype to known PARN/TBD spectrum
- Why: PARN monoallelic variants classically present with adult‑onset idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) and sometimes bone‑marrow failure or hepatic fibrosis; variable penetrance is common.
- Apply to this case: The patient’s young adult onset of pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and IPF—plus premature greying, osteoporosis and neuropathy—fits the expanding PARN/TBD phenotype and therefore increases prior probability that a rare PARN variant is relevant.

2) Measure telomere length (gold‑standard: flow‑FISH)
- Why: Short leukocyte telomere length is the most direct biological readout of a telomere biology disorder and is recommended when a TBD is suspected.
- Action: Obtain flow‑FISH on peripheral blood (and, if available, granulocytes and lymphocyte subsets). Marked telomere shortening (below the 1st–10th percentile for age) strongly supports pathogenicity.
- Case relevance: The authors emphasize telomere testing as part of the “gold standard”; a short telomere result here would be powerful evidence linking the PARN VUS to disease.

3) Family studies and segregation analysis
- Why: Co‑segregation with disease in affected relatives or de novo occurrence in the proband provides strong evidence. Lack of segregation in healthy older relatives argues against pathogenicity.
- Action: Test first‑degree relatives for the PARN variant and, if available, compare their telomere lengths and clinical findings. Document inheritance (de novo vs inherited).
- Case relevance: The report notes no clear family history; demonstrating de novo status or cosegregation with fibrosis/marrow disease in relatives would materially strengthen pathogenic assignment.

4) Targeted functional assays
- Why: PARN’s role in TERC maturation and RNA deadenylation is mechanistic; functional impairment of PARN or of TERC processing is direct evidence.
- Practical assays:
  - Measure TERC levels and 3′ end maturation in patient cells (RT‑qPCR / northern blot).
  - Assess telomerase activity (TRAP assay) in patient lymphocytes.
  - Complementation assays: express wild‑type PARN in patient cells to see if TERC/telomerase defects are rescued.
  - If available, in vitro biochemical assays of the mutant protein (deadenylase activity) or cell models with the specific missense change.
- Case relevance: The variant lies in the conserved R3H domain (residues 178–245) and in silico tools predict damage—functional impairment of PARN/TERC would upgrade evidence from computational to biological.

5) Population, computational and domain evidence synthesized per ACMG
- Why: Systematic application of ACMG criteria clarifies classification and identifies which lines of evidence are lacking.
- Apply to this variant: The case already documents PM1 (critical domain), PM2 (absent/rare in population databases), PP3 (multiple damaging predictions), PP2 (gene constraint). Adding functional evidence (PS3), cosegregation (PP1), or short telomeres (supporting evidence) can shift the classification.
- Action: Aggregate published PARN variants in ClinVar/literature, submit new data (functional/telomere/family) to ClinVar to update interpretation.

6) Exclude alternate explanations and assess modifiers
- Why: Nutritional deficiencies and autoimmune findings can confound marrow phenotype; proving telomere shortness and PARN dysfunction helps distinguish primary TBD from secondary marrow suppression.
- Case relevance: The patient had severe B12 deficiency explaining some features; demonstration of short telomeres or defective PARN function argues that the PARN variant is an underlying susceptibility that explains multisystem fibrosis and marrow vulnerability.

7) Clinical management consequences and longitudinal correlation
- Why: Demonstrating disease relevance affects counseling, surveillance (pulmonary, hepatic, hematologic, malignancy) and timing/choice of therapies (HSCT donor selection, androgen trials).
- Action: If additional testing supports pathogenicity, institute TBD surveillance and involve genetic counseling; if uncertain, maintain close longitudinal follow‑up with repeat telomere and hematologic assessment, because progressive marrow failure or fibrosis over time would support causality.

Putting it together for this patient
- The c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg) variant already meets PM1/PM2/PP3/PP2 and lies in the R3H domain—a biologically plausible site. The next high‑yield steps are flow‑FISH telomere length measurement, family testing for segregation and targeted functional assays of PARN/TERC processing. Positive results (markedly short age‑adjusted telomeres, cosegregation or de novo status, and demonstrable functional impairment) would collectively allow reclassification toward likely pathogenic and justify TBD‑directed management and surveillance. Negative results (normal telomere length, lack of segregation, normal PARN function) would argue against causality and keep the variant as a VUS.

Final clinical pearl
Genetic variants rarely stand alone—objective measures of the disease mechanism (telomere length, TERC processing, telomerase activity), segregation data and careful phenotype matching are the decisive steps that convert a PARN VUS into a clinically actionable diagnosis.

### [Pathophysiology] Why are hepatic fibrosis and cirrhosis increasingly recognized manifestations of telomere biology disorders, and what mechanisms might link PARN variants to liver disease in addition to pulmonary fibrosis?

Clinical answer (NEJM Morning‑Report style)

Why hepatic fibrosis and cirrhosis are now being recognised as part of telomere biology disorders
- Conceptual link: TBDs cause premature telomere shortening → loss of proliferative reserve and increased cellular senescence/apoptosis in tissues with ongoing turnover or regenerative demand. The liver, although quiescent at baseline, requires robust proliferative capacity for regeneration after injury. When hepatocytes, cholangiocytes and liver progenitors have critically short telomeres they cannot replace lost or injured cells effectively, and repair shifts from regeneration toward fibrogenesis.
- Emerging epidemiology: Earlier descriptions of TBD emphasised mucocutaneous findings and bone‑marrow failure; recent cohort and registry data (and the case series cited in this report) show high rates of clinically significant hepatic fibrosis among adults with telomerase complex mutations (eg, TERT/TERC), implying that liver disease is an under‑recognised, but biologically plausible, manifestation.
- Clinical implication: Young adults with unexplained hepatic fibrosis or cirrhosis—especially when accompanied by cytopenias, premature greying, pulmonary fibrosis or other “premature ageing” features—should raise suspicion for a TBD.

Mechanisms linking PARN variants specifically to liver disease (in addition to pulmonary fibrosis)
1. Telomerase insufficiency via defective TERC maturation
   - PARN is required for 3′‑end maturation and stability of TERC RNA. Loss or reduction of PARN function (haploinsufficiency with monoallelic variants or biallelic loss) reduces mature TERC, impairing telomerase activity.
   - Reduced telomerase → accelerated telomere attrition in dividing hepatic cells → senescence/apoptosis, reduced regenerative capacity, and predisposition to fibrosis after injury. This is the primary mechanism linking PARN variants to organ fibrosis, paralleling the well‑established pathway for TERT/TERC mutations.

2. Consequence of impaired regenerative response → profibrotic healing
   - With impaired hepatocyte replication, injured liver more readily activates stellate cells and myofibroblasts and sustains inflammatory/fibrogenic signalling rather than effective parenchymal regeneration, promoting progressive fibrosis and cirrhosis.

3. Senescence‑associated secretory phenotype (SASP) and chronic inflammation
   - Senescent hepatocytes/cholangiocytes release proinflammatory and profibrotic cytokines (SASP), which amplify stellate cell activation and extracellular matrix deposition. This paracrine effect links cellular telomere dysfunction to organ‑level fibrosis.

4. Extra‑telomeric effects of PARN (mRNA regulation)
   - PARN is an exoribonuclease that deadenylates many RNAs. Dysfunction may dysregulate mRNAs involved in DNA repair, cell cycle control, apoptosis and inflammatory pathways, compounding susceptibility to injury and fibrogenesis independent of telomerase/TERC levels. Thus PARN variants can both shorten telomeres and alter expression of genes that influence fibrotic responses.

5. Multihit model (genetic vulnerability + environmental/physiologic stresses)
   - Monoallelic PARN variants may be insufficient alone; additional stressors (metabolic steatosis, inflammation, infections, nutritional deficiencies, alcohol, toxins) accelerate telomere attrition or provoke repetitive injury. In the presented case, grade II steatosis and systemic inflammation likely acted as additional hits on a genetically susceptible liver, explaining early cirrhosis despite a single PARN allele variant.
   - This model also explains phenotypic heterogeneity (why some PARN carriers develop IPF, others HF, marrow failure, or combinations).

How the case supports these mechanisms
- The patient is young with cirrhosis (transient elastography 18.1 kPa), pulmonary fibrosis, pancytopenia and physical signs of premature ageing—exactly the multisystem phenotype expected from telomere dysfunction.
- Genetic finding: monoallelic PARN missense variant in a conserved domain predicted deleterious; biologically plausible because PARN loss impairs TERC maturation.
- Other causes of liver disease were excluded (viral, autoimmune), but she had hepatic steatosis and systemic inflammation—plausible additional hits on a telomere‑compromised liver.
- The report cites registry data showing high prevalence of HF with TERT/TERC mutations, supporting that telomere defects commonly produce clinically significant liver fibrosis; by extension, PARN defects that reduce telomerase activity can produce the same outcome.

Takeaway points for clinical practice
- Consider a telomere biology disorder in young patients with otherwise unexplained hepatic fibrosis, especially when accompanied by marrow abnormalities, pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying or other premature‑ageing features.
- Pathogenesis in PARN carriers is multifactorial: defective TERC maturation → telomerase insufficiency → telomere shortening → impaired regeneration and SASP‑driven fibrogenesis, possibly compounded by PARN’s broader effects on mRNA regulation and environmental “second hits.”
- Recognising the link alters management: genetic testing and telomere measurement, surveillance for multisystem involvement, avoidance of additional liver insults, and early hematology/genetics referral for consideration of transplant or other targeted therapies.

### [Management] In a young adult with severe vitamin B12 deficiency and a suspected telomere biology disorder, how would you prioritize immediate management and monitoring, and what findings would prompt escalation to consider hematopoietic stem cell transplantation?

Immediate priorities (first 24–72 hours)
- Correct reversible contributors to cytopenia and organ dysfunction. In this case the first and most urgent step was aggressive B12 repletion (IV or IM cyanocobalamin loading then maintenance) plus folate and repletion of vitamin D/electrolytes; this often produces rapid symptomatic and reticulocyte responses and should be done before attributing cytopenias to an inherited marrow failure syndrome. Continue transfusion support for symptomatic anaemia as needed (the authors used whole blood transfusions).
- Supportive care and infection/bleeding precautions: strict neutropenic precautions if neutropenia, platelet support for bleeding or very low counts, treat any active infections promptly, and avoid myelotoxic drugs (chemotherapy, certain immunosuppressants, and excessive alkylators).
- Baseline organ and marrow assessment while deficiencies are corrected: obtain CBC with retic count frequently (eg, twice weekly initially), bone marrow examination (cellularity, dysplasia, cytogenetics), iron studies, hemolysis markers, DAT interpretation in context (as in the case), high-resolution chest CT and pulmonary function tests, liver stiffness assessment (elastography) and hepatic panel, and screen for occult infections. These assessments were performed in the case and helped distinguish ineffective erythropoiesis from immune hemolysis and document pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis.
- Expedite genetic workup and telomere testing: send next‑generation sequencing panel for telomere biology genes and measure telomere length (flow-FISH), because a confirmed pathogenic TBD alters management (HSCT timing, conditioning, donor choice). In the case, clinical exome sequencing identified a monoallelic PARN variant and the clinical picture supported a TBD despite variant classified as VUS—prompting close follow-up.
- Early transplant and specialty referral: involve hematology/transplant, pulmonology and hepatology early. In TBDs, transplant planning and donor selection (avoid related donors who may carry the variant) should begin well before irreversible marrow failure.

Monitoring over weeks–months
- Hematologic surveillance: frequent CBCs with reticulocyte counts to document response after B12 correction. Monitor for progressive cytopenias or transfusion dependence. Repeat bone marrow biopsy if counts worsen, retic remains low, or new cytopenias develop.
- Serial organ surveillance: pulmonary function testing (including DLCO) and HRCT every 6–12 months (or sooner if symptoms worsen) for progression of pulmonary fibrosis; liver synthetic function tests and elastography to track hepatic fibrosis; bone density follow‑up for osteoporosis.
- Telomere length and genetic re-evaluation: if initial genetic result is VUS, consider parental testing, segregation studies, repeat sequencing or functional assays; a short telomere length on flow‑FISH supports a TBD diagnosis and increases urgency of monitoring.
- Risk minimization: vaccination (pneumococcus, influenza), avoid smoking and hepatotoxic agents (alcohol, hepatotoxic drugs), and counsel regarding pregnancy risks and family testing.

When to escalate and consider hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT)
Escalation to HSCT is considered when there is clear, progressive bone marrow failure that is unlikely to be reversed by correcting reversible causes. Specific triggers include:
- Transfusion dependence: need for regular red‑cell or platelet transfusions to maintain acceptable hemoglobin/platelet levels.
- Progressive or severe cytopenias despite correction of deficiencies:
  - ANC persistently <0.5 ×10^9/L with recurrent or severe infections
  - Platelets persistently <20–30 ×10^9/L or clinically significant bleeding
  - Hemoglobin refractory to replacement and supportive measures with poor marrow reserve or symptomatic anemia despite transfusions
- Documentation of bone marrow failure: evolving hypocellularity on marrow biopsy, markedly reduced marrow progenitors, or increasing marrow failure indices (eg, progressive low retic with trilineage decline).
- Clonal evolution / myeloid malignancy: new cytogenetic abnormalities, evolving MDS or transformation to AML—these mandate prompt transplant evaluation.
- Genetic confirmation of a pathogenic telomere gene with progressive haematologic phenotype: a bona fide pathogenic germline variant and short telomeres increase the likelihood that marrow failure will progress and favor earlier transplant referral.
- Failure of medical bridging therapies: lack of durable response to androgens (danazol/nandrolone) or other supportive regimens when used as a bridge.

Practical considerations specific to TBDs
- Timing and candidacy: HSCT in TBD requires individualized timing—early enough to prevent life‑threatening complications but after careful organ assessment because pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis worsens transplant risk. The presence of significant pulmonary fibrosis or advanced cirrhosis may increase transplant morbidity and influence conditioning intensity or make HSCT high risk.
- Conditioning regimens: patients with TBDs tolerate standard myeloablative conditioning poorly; reduced‑intensity or non‑myeloablative regimens and telomere‑sparing approaches are preferred. Discuss these options early with a transplant center experienced in telomere disorders.
- Donor selection: avoid related donors who may harbor the same germline variant; preferentially use an unrelated matched donor or cord blood when appropriate.
- Consider bridging medical therapy: androgens have been used to improve counts in TBDs and can be considered as a temporizing measure while planning HSCT, recognizing limited and variable evidence and potential hepatotoxicity—important in a patient with hepatic fibrosis as in this case.

Bottom line (applied to the presented patient)
- Immediate priority was and remains aggressive correction of severe B12 deficiency and supportive measures; this produced early symptomatic and hematologic improvement in the case.
- Simultaneously, perform baseline telomere testing, marrow evaluation and organ staging (lung and liver), begin genetic counseling, and engage transplant and pulmonology teams early.
- Escalate to HSCT evaluation if the patient develops transfusion dependence, progressive trilineage cytopenias (eg, ANC <0.5, platelets <20–30), marrow hypocellularity or clonal evolution, or if genetic/telomere testing confirms a pathogenic defect with an evolving marrow failure phenotype—balanced against pulmonary/hepatic comorbidity when planning timing and conditioning.

### [Clinical Pearls] What are key counseling and surveillance recommendations for patients and first-degree relatives when a telomere biology disorder is suspected due to a monoallelic PARN variant?

Clinical bottom line
- When a telomere biology disorder (TBD) is suspected in a patient with a monoallelic PARN variant (as in this case with pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis), counseling should emphasize (1) confirmatory testing (telomere length and focused genetic work‑up), (2) cascade testing and reproductive counselling for first‑degree relatives, and (3) targeted organ surveillance and exposure avoidance because of variable penetrance and progressive, multi‑organ risk.

Why this matters (pathophysiology → clinical risk)
- PARN is required for normal TERC maturation; heterozygous (monoallelic) PARN variants can cause haploinsufficiency with accelerated telomere shortening. Short telomeres selectively impair highly proliferative tissues (bone marrow, lung epithelium, liver), producing variable phenotypes (BM failure, IPF, hepatic fibrosis, premature greying, cancer risk). The case illustrates this pleiotropy despite only a monoallelic variant and absence of classic mucocutaneous signs.

Practical recommendations

1) Confirm the diagnosis
- Measure telomere length (flow‑FISH on leukocyte subsets is the diagnostic standard in adults). Markedly short telomeres relative to age supports a TBD and increases the likelihood that the PARN variant is pathogenic.
- Reassess the variant: submit for expert genetics review, consider segregation testing in available relatives, and, if available, functional assays or review of literature/ClinVar to reclassify the VUS.

2) Genetic counselling and testing of first‑degree relatives
- Counsel that monoallelic PARN variants behave most often as autosomal dominant with reduced and age‑dependent penetrance; the empirical recurrence risk to first‑degree relatives is ≈50% if the variant is pathogenic, but expressivity is variable.
- Offer targeted cascade testing for the familial PARN variant and telomere length measurement to adult first‑degree relatives. Testing of asymptomatic children is generally deferred until after counselling and consideration of clinical utility; if performed, discuss the psychosocial and insurance implications and involve genetics.
- Provide reproductive options counselling (preimplantation genetic diagnosis, prenatal testing) if the variant is determined pathogenic and the family desires it.

3) Surveillance for the proband and mutation‑positive relatives
- Hematologic:
  - CBC with differential and reticulocyte count every 3–6 months (sooner if symptomatic). Low threshold to repeat and to refer to hematology.
  - Bone marrow evaluation if cytopenias progress or transform (morphology, cytogenetics/MDS panel).
  - When HSCT is contemplated, coordinate with a centre experienced in TBDs and use reduced‑intensity and organ‑sparing conditioning because standard myeloablative regimens carry excess toxicity.
- Pulmonary:
  - Baseline pulmonary function tests including DLCO and a high‑resolution chest CT (HRCT). Repeat PFTs and chest imaging at least annually or sooner for symptoms; refer early to pulmonary fibrosis specialists.
  - Strongly counsel smoking cessation and avoidance of environmental lung toxins; immunize per guidelines (influenza, pneumococcus).
- Hepatic:
  - Baseline liver biochemical tests, abdominal imaging (ultrasound/transient elastography if available) and hepatology referral for evidence of fibrosis/cirrhosis. Repeat LFTs and elastography at intervals guided by severity (commonly every 6–12 months).
  - Screen and manage complications of portal hypertension/cirrhosis as indicated.
- Cancer surveillance:
  - Maintain routine age‑appropriate cancer screening. Maintain increased vigilance for hematologic malignancies (MDS/AML): promptly investigate new cytopenias or blasts.
  - Low threshold for evaluation of otherwise unexplained lesions (skin, oral leukoplakia, head/neck).
- Bone health and endocrine:
  - Monitor bone density and treat osteoporosis (case had severe osteoporosis); correct vitamin deficiencies and monitor electrolytes.
- Other systems:
  - Clinical exam for mucocutaneous signs; neuro and GI follow‑up as clinically indicated.

4) Management counselling and risk mitigation
- Avoid exposures and therapies that accelerate telomere attrition or cause disproportionate toxicity:
  - Tobacco cessation and avoidance of excessive alcohol.
  - Use caution with alkylating chemotherapy and radiation; discuss alternatives and dose reduction in TBD patients.
  - For cytopenias, favour supportive care and refer early for HSCT planning if progressive marrow failure; when HSCT is required, specialized transplant protocols (reduced‑intensity, organ‑protective) are recommended.
- Discuss potential medical therapies (androgens have shown telomere‑lengthening in small studies) only as experimental/limited evidence; manage within specialty centres.
- Coordinate multidisciplinary care (hematology, pulmonology, hepatology, genetics, transplant teams).

5) Practical follow‑up plan for this patient (case‑based)
- In this patient—already receiving genetic counselling and surveillance—order flow‑FISH to document leukocyte telomere length and pursue familial cascade testing (parents/siblings) if she consents and relatives agree.
- Continue CBC every 3 months, baseline PFT+DLCO and HRCT with yearly reassessment, and hepatology follow‑up with elastography every 6–12 months. Maintain aggressive vitamin repletion and bone protection already started.
- Revisit variant classification as new data appear; if variant is reclassified as pathogenic, ensure relatives are re‑contacted and reproductive counselling updated.

Takeaway
- A monoallelic PARN variant with compatible clinical features should trigger telomere length testing, specialist genetic counselling, and cascade testing of first‑degree relatives. Surveillance must be proactive and multidisciplinary because organ involvement is protean, age‑dependent and may progress despite initial stability—precisely the course illustrated by this young patient.

### [Management] Which medications, procedures, or environmental exposures warrant particular caution in patients with telomere biology disorders due to heightened risk of marrow failure or organ toxicity, and how would this influence management of interstitial lung disease or potential malignancy?

Direct answer
Patients with telomere biology disorders (TBDs) are unusually vulnerable to agents and exposures that injure highly proliferative tissues or that cause DNA damage; in practice you should exercise particular caution with (1) genotoxic chemotherapy and ionizing radiation, (2) certain marrow‑ and liver‑toxic medications, (3) pulmonary toxins and smoking, and (4) some therapies often used for marrow or immune disease. These risks change how you treat interstitial lung disease (ILD), manage suspected or established malignancy, and approach bone‑marrow failure or transplantation.

Why — brief pathophysiology
TBDs (eg, PARN, TERT, TERC mutations) accelerate telomere shortening and cause impaired replicative capacity of stem/progenitor cells. Bone marrow, lung alveolar epithelium and hepatocytes are particularly susceptible: sublethal insults that healthy patients tolerate can precipitate progressive marrow failure, organ fibrosis, or frank organ toxicity in TBD patients.

Specific agents, procedures and exposures to avoid or use with extreme caution
- Alkylating agents and topoisomerase II inhibitors (eg, cyclophosphamide, ifosfamide, melphalan, busulfan, etoposide): high risk of prolonged marrow aplasia and therapy‑related myeloid neoplasms.  
- Ionizing radiation (radiotherapy, high‑dose diagnostic radiation): causes marked marrow and lung injury; minimize exposure.  
- Bleomycin and other pulmonary‑fibrosing chemotherapies: high risk of worsening pulmonary fibrosis.  
- Antimetabolites and classic myelosuppressive chemotherapies (eg, high‑dose methotrexate, cytarabine): use lower doses or avoid if feasible. Methotrexate may worsen lung disease.  
- Bone‑marrow–toxic antibiotics/agents (eg, chloramphenicol, prolonged high‑dose linezolid) and certain antivirals/antifungals with marrow suppression—avoid or monitor closely.  
- Azathioprine, mycophenolate and prolonged cytotoxic immunosuppression: increased risk of cytopenias and secondary malignancy; weigh risks/benefits carefully.  
- Androgens (danazol, nandrolone): can improve blood counts in some TBD patients but carry significant hepatic toxicity—avoid or use cautiously in patients with hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis (as in this case).  
- Growth factor use (G‑CSF/GM‑CSF): may be used for neutropenia but consider theoretical risk of promoting clonal evolution; use judiciously with haematology expertise.  
- Amiodarone, nitrofurantoin and other drugs linked to pulmonary toxicity: avoid because they can worsen ILD.  
- Tobacco, occupational inhalants, and environmental pulmonary toxins: rigorously avoid—smoking accelerates pulmonary disease and risk of fibrosis.  
- High‑dose supplemental oxygen and hyperoxia during procedures should be optimized to need; avoid excessive exposures that might exacerbate lung injury.

How this influences management of ILD and suspected malignancy
- ILD management
  - Avoid lung‑toxic drugs (bleomycin, amiodarone, nitrofurantoin, methotrexate). In this patient with PARN‑associated ILD and hepatic fibrosis, prioritize non‑drug strategies (smoking cessation, immunizations, pulmonary rehabilitation) and early referral to an interstitial lung disease/telomere clinic.  
  - Consider antifibrotic therapy (eg, pirfenidone or nintedanib) in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis after multidisciplinary review; these drugs are not genotoxic but monitor hepatic function closely because this patient already has cirrhosis.  
  - Be cautious with invasive diagnostics (surgical lung biopsy) because of poor wound healing, bleeding risk from cytopenias and poor pulmonary reserve — favor high‑resolution CT and multidisciplinary diagnosis; perform transbronchial cryobiopsy only in centers experienced with TBDs and with hematology input.  
  - For progressive respiratory failure, early discussion of lung transplant referral is appropriate; however, recognize increased post‑transplant risks (bone marrow failure, hepatic disease, increased sensitivity to immunosuppression). Multidisciplinary planning with transplant, hepatology and hematology is required.

- Malignancy management
  - When cancer is suspected or confirmed, avoid standard full‑dose myeloablative regimens if alternative curative approaches exist. Prefer surgical resection where feasible and less‑genotoxic systemic regimens if oncologically reasonable.  
  - If cytotoxic chemotherapy or radiotherapy is required, involve a TBD‑experienced hematology/oncology team to consider dose reduction, alternative agents, or targeted/non‑genotoxic therapies (eg, targeted small molecules, immune therapies where appropriate) and to increase surveillance for prolonged cytopenias and organ toxicity.  
  - For hematologic malignancies requiring transplantation, use reduced‑intensity, non‑myeloablative conditioning regimens tailored to minimize alkylators and radiation; these patients benefit from experienced transplant centers familiar with TBD protocols and long‑term toxicity.  
  - Monitor closely for therapy‑related myeloid neoplasms; counsel patients about higher risk of secondary malignancy.

Practical steps in this patient
- Stop/avoid hepatotoxic agents and androgens given her cirrhosis; danazol would be inadvisable.  
- Avoid pulmonary‑toxic drugs (eg, amiodarone, bleomycin) and counsel on strict smoking avoidance and occupational exposure reduction.  
- If cytopenias worsen or marrow failure progresses, discuss HSCT early with a center that uses reduced‑intensity conditioning designed for TBDs. Pre‑transplant evaluation must include liver and lung assessment and family genetic testing.  
- If malignancy is suspected, coordinate management with centers experienced in TBD so anti‑cancer therapy is individualized and non‑genotoxic options prioritized when possible.  
- Use supportive measures preferentially: transfusions, cautious use of growth factors, vaccination, bone‑protective strategies, and surveillance for progressive organ fibrosis.

Bottom line
Think “telomere hypersensitivity”: avoid genotoxic, marrow‑ and lung‑injuring drugs and exposures; tailor cancer therapy and transplant conditioning to minimize DNA damage; avoid hepatic‑toxic agents (notably androgens) in patients with liver fibrosis. Early involvement of specialized multidisciplinary teams (hematology, pulmonology/ILD, hepatology, oncology, transplant genetics) is essential to balance disease control against the heightened risk of organ toxicity and marrow failure in TBDs.

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a young adult with interstitial lung disease and unexplained cytopenias, what features in the history, family pedigree, and examination increase the pretest probability of a telomere biology disorder compared with autoimmune, infectious, or purely nutritional etiologies?

Short answer
Suspect a telomere biology disorder (TBD) when a young adult has unexplained interstitial lung disease and cytopenias plus multisystem findings of premature ageing or fibrosis, especially when common causes (autoimmune, infectious, nutritional) have been excluded and the hematologic picture is disproportionate to treatable deficiencies. Key features that raise the pretest probability are a specific pattern of organ involvement, early-onset “ageing” stigmata on exam, a suggestive family pedigree (when present), and laboratory/clinical discordance that cannot be explained by acquired disease.

Why — the clinical reasoning
TBDs target tissues with high proliferative demand (bone marrow, lung epithelium, liver, mucosa, hair, bone), so pathogenic germline telomere defects classically produce a constellation of marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis and premature-ageing signs. In contrast, autoimmune, infectious or nutritional causes usually have a more limited distribution, clearer serologic/infectious markers, or correctable hematologic responses once the deficiency/infection is treated.

Findings in history that increase pretest probability
- Young age at onset of fibrotic disease: pulmonary fibrosis or cirrhosis presenting well before the typical age for idiopathic disease (eg, 20s–40s). In the case, progressive dyspnea and radiologic pulmonary fibrosis in a woman in her late 20s is highly atypical and therefore a red flag.  
- Multisystem fibrosis or failure: concurrent or sequential involvement of bone marrow (pancytopenia), lung (ILD/IPF), liver (fibrosis/cirrhosis), and non‑haematologic features (osteoporosis, early greying/alopecia, mucosal atrophy). The case showed pancytopenia, cirrhosis and pulmonary fibrosis together — a pattern characteristic of TBD.  
- Longstanding, insidious course with progressive functional decline disproportionate to single diagnosed cause (eg, cytopenia persisting despite correction of severe B12 deficiency). In the case, correction of nutritional deficiencies did not fully explain persistent multisystem disease.  
- Early-onset complications of replicative failure: premature greying, early osteoporosis with fractures, avascular necrosis or early menopause (if present) — these suggest premature cellular senescence. The case’s premature greying, osteoporosis and mucosal atrophy support this.  
- Lack of an alternative explanatory exposure: absence of toxin exposure, chemotherapy, alcohol/viral hepatitis explaining cirrhosis, or connective-tissue disease explaining ILD increases probability of an inherited cause.

Pedigree/family features that increase pretest probability
- First- or second-degree relatives with early-onset pulmonary fibrosis, unexplained cytopenias or bone marrow failure, early cirrhosis, premature hair greying/alopecia, or unexplained aplastic anemia — especially across generations (compatible with autosomal dominant transmission of many TBD genes such as TERT/TERC/PARN).  
- Pattern of variable penetrance/anticipation: shorter telomeres and earlier more severe disease in successive generations (if available) supports a telomere syndrome.  
- Important caveat: absence of a family history does NOT exclude TBD — incomplete penetrance, variable expressivity, monoallelic mutations, somatic rescue and de novo variants are well described (the presented patient had no clear family history but a monoallelic PARN variant). So a negative pedigree lowers but does not exclude pretest probability.

Examination and bedside clues that increase pretest probability
- Premature ageing signs: premature greying of hair, alopecia, thin or atrophic oral mucosa/atrophic tongue, nail dystrophy, reticular hyperpigmentation — even if only some features present. The patient had premature greying, a bald/atrophic tongue and knuckle hyperpigmentation.  
- Signs of multiorgan fibrosis: hepatosplenomegaly or stigmata of chronic liver disease in a young person; crackles, reduced breath sounds and clubbing or basilar fibrosis on chest exam. The case documented hepatomegaly and basilar changes on lung exam and imaging.  
- Features of marrow failure without classic autoimmune markers: pallor, petechiae/bleeding or recurrent infections with negative autoimmune serologies. The patient had pancytopenia with a low reticulocyte count and a marrow picture inconsistent with pure immune cytopenia (DAT positive but marrow findings and low retic pointed to ineffective erythropoiesis).  
- Discordant exam–lab pattern: severe systemic signs out of proportion to a correctable deficiency (eg, severe organ fibrosis despite correction of B12/folate) — seen in this case.

Laboratory/imaging clues that support TBD over acquired causes
- Multilineage cytopenias with low reticulocyte response (ineffective hematopoiesis) rather than isolated hemolysis or pure peripheral destruction.  
- Radiologic pattern of subpleural/ basilar fibrosis consistent with usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP)/IPF in a young patient.  
- Hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis without viral/autoimmune/metabolic explanation.  
- Negative autoimmune serologies and infectious workup despite systemic inflammation — as in the presented patient.

What to do if you suspect a TBD
- Order telomere length testing (flow-FISH in leukocyte subsets is a standard functional assay) and genetic testing with a multigene panel/next‑generation sequencing for telomere-related genes (TERT, TERC, RTEL1, PARN, DKC1, etc.). The case used clinical exome sequencing and found a monoallelic PARN variant.  
- Counsel that family history may be absent and that genetic results may show variants of uncertain significance; integrate phenotype, telomere length and genetic data.  
- Avoid genotoxic therapies without specialist input (eg, certain conditioning regimens for transplant) and plan multidisciplinary surveillance (hematology, pulmonology, hepatology, genetics).

Bottom line (clinical pearl)
In a young adult with ILD plus unexplained cytopenias, your suspicion for a telomere biology disorder should rise when multiple high‑turnover organs are involved (bone marrow, lung, liver, mucosa, hair, bone), when clinical severity is out of proportion to treatable causes, when autoimmune/infectious workups are unrevealing, and when physical exam shows premature‑ageing stigmata (premature greying, mucosal atrophy, nail/skin changes). A suggestive family history increases pretest probability but its absence does not exclude TBD — proceed to telomere length assessment and targeted genetic testing when the phenotype fits, as illustrated by this patient with PARN-associated TBD.
