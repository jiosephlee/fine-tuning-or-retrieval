Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# When a PARN Variant Is a VUS: Clinically Anchoring Genetics in Suspected Telomere Disease

What grabs you about this case? A woman in her late 20s with progressive pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis — classic “premature ageing” target organs — yet no florid mucocutaneous dyskeratosis. Genetic testing finds a monoallelic PARN missense change (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) reported as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS). How do you turn that ambiguous lab result into actionable clinical reasoning?

This post walks through practical ways to interpret and act on a monoallelic PARN VUS in the setting of a phenotype strongly suggestive of a telomere biology disorder (TBD). I’ll cover how to apply ACMG/AMP thinking in the clinic, the essential next investigations (telomere length testing, segregation, functional data), how to manage patients while uncertainty persists, and common pitfalls.

## Why phenotype matters (phenotype anchoring)
Genetic variants don’t exist in a vacuum. Pathogenicity is a probabilistic statement that combines variant-level evidence and phenotype-level evidence.

- PARN is a known TBD gene. Monoallelic PARN variants have been repeatedly associated with adult-onset pulmonary fibrosis and sometimes bone marrow disease. So a monoallelic PARN change in a patient with IPF + pancytopenia + hepatic fibrosis is biologically plausible.
- This patient’s constellation (young age, cytopenia with trilineage marrow, cirrhosis on elastography, pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying, osteoporosis, neuropathy) is a red flag for a TBD even if the mucocutaneous triad is absent. Clinical suspicion upgrades the pre-test probability that a rare PARN variant is meaningful.

But a VUS is a VUS — it cannot be used as definitive diagnostic evidence on its own. The job is to gather orthogonal evidence to move it toward (or away from) pathogenicity.

## Practical next steps to reclassify or contextualize the VUS

1. Telomere length measurement (high-yield)
   - Send for flow-FISH telomere length testing in leukocyte subsets (gold standard in clinical labs). Short telomeres for age in multiple leukocyte compartments strongly supports a TBD and constitutes powerful phenotypic evidence.
   - If telomeres are short (eg below the 1st–10th centile for age), that increases the likelihood that the PARN variant is pathogenic/haploinsufficient. Normal telomere length argues against a systemic telomere disorder but does not exclude tissue-limited or mosaic disease.

2. Segregation and family studies
   - Test first-degree relatives (ideally affected and unaffected) for the variant and, where possible, measure their telomere length. Co-segregation with disease (or short telomeres in carriers) provides strong supportive evidence.
   - Be cautious: incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity are common in TBDs, so absence of disease in a carrier doesn’t rule out pathogenicity.

3. Reanalysis and orthogonal genetic testing
   - Re-examine the exome for other variants in telomere-related genes (TERT, TERC, RTEL1, DKC1, TINF2 etc.), copy-number variants, or non-coding changes that may explain phenotype or act in digenic fashion.
   - Consider targeted testing of TERC (sometimes missed by exome), and assess for somatic rescue or mosaicism (deep sequencing of blood vs skin fibroblast DNA). If blood has clonal hematopoiesis or somatic variants, germline assessment from skin may help.

4. Functional data
   - Look for published functional assays of the same residue/domain. This variant affects the R3H domain (residues 178–245) — a functionally important region for PARN; that supports PM1-level evidence per ACMG when combined with other data.
   - If no published data, explore collaboration with a research lab for a functional assay (eg PARN activity, TERC stability assays). Functional validation can be decisive but may take time.

5. Population and in silico data review
   - Confirm absence/rarity in population databases (gnomAD) and review in silico predictions — useful but weak evidence alone.
   - Document conservation across species and the physicochemical impact of the amino-acid change.

6. Communicate with the testing lab
   - Provide them with detailed phenotype and results of telomere length testing, family studies, and any functional data — labs will sometimes reclassify a VUS with additional evidence.

## Management you can justify while the VUS remains unresolved

You often must act before definitive classification. When the clinical phenotype strongly suggests TBD, management can be guided by the phenotype and risk, not solely by variant label.

- Surveillance and risk mitigation
  - Start organ surveillance appropriate for TBD: regular pulmonary function testing (including DLCO), high-resolution CT surveillance as clinically indicated, liver monitoring (LFTs, elastography), hematology follow-up with serial CBCs and bone marrow review if counts decline.
  - Avoid risk factors that accelerate telomere attrition: tobacco, excessive alcohol, cytotoxic chemotherapy or radiation unless absolutely needed.
- Hematopoietic decisions
  - If progressive marrow failure develops, discuss hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) early with a centre experienced in transplant for telomere disorders. These patients tolerate standard conditioning poorly — reduced-intensity regimens and tailored donor selection (avoid related donors who may be variant carriers) are critical.
  - Do NOT use a VUS to exclude a potential related donor without further family testing and counseling. If a relative tests negative for the variant and has normal telomere lengths, they may be a safer donor.
- Medical therapies
  - Supportive care: transfusion, vitamin supplementation (as appropriate), osteoporosis management, pulmonary fibrosis therapies as indicated (antifibrotics when appropriate).
  - Consider discussion of androgens (eg danazol) only in specialized centres and in context of clinical trials or where evidence supports benefit — these are not standard first-line therapies and carry risks.
- Genetic counseling and reproduction
  - Offer counseling early. Discuss uncertainty, possible inheritance patterns, reproductive options and need for targeted testing of relatives only in the context of reclassification or robust supporting evidence.
- Documentation and follow-up
  - Document the VUS nature clearly in charts and letters. Set a plan for periodic re-evaluation with the genetics lab (eg annual review), since VUSes are commonly reclassified over time.

## How to talk to patients and families about a VUS
- Be honest and clear: “This variant is of uncertain significance — we can’t say it caused your illness yet, but it’s in a gene that fits your clinical picture.”
- Explain next steps in plain language: telomere length testing, family testing, possible research/functional testing, and why those steps matter.
- Emphasize what will change in care: surveillance, avoidance of certain exposures, and caution around transplant/donor selection — all justified by phenotype and plausible genetic risk, not by the VUS alone.
- Avoid deterministic language: don’t tell family members “you have the disease” based on a VUS; instead offer targeted testing and counseling.

## Common pitfalls and how to avoid them
- Overcalling a VUS as pathogenic because it “fits” — treat variant-level and phenotype-level evidence independently, then integrate. Overcalling risks unnecessary family anxiety and inappropriate donor exclusion.
- Under-calling: dismissing a biologically plausible VUS in the setting of a strong phenotype; that can delay surveillance and transplant planning. If clinical suspicion is high, act on the phenotype while continuing to accrue genetic evidence.
- Relying solely on in silico predictions. Computational tools are supportive but weak evidence by ACMG and should not drive decisions alone.
- Forgetting telomere length. Flow-FISH is a relatively accessible, high-yield test that often clarifies uncertainty.
- Not recontacting. VUS reclassification is common — ensure follow-up with the lab and the patient.

## Applying ACMG/AMP thinking in this case (brief)
- PM1: Variant lies in a critical domain (R3H) — supportive.
- PM2: Absent/rare in population databases — supportive.
- PP3: Multiple in silico tools predict deleteriousness — supporting but weak.
- PP2: Missense mechanism plausible in PARN — supporting.
- Lacking strong functional or segregation data, these collectively justify a VUS classification. However, added evidence — short telomeres, segregation with disease, or functional impairment — could tip toward likely pathogenic.

## Bottom line — clinical pearls
- A monoallelic PARN VUS in a young adult with pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis and bone marrow dysfunction should prompt telomere length testing (flow-FISH) and family studies before accepting or discarding the variant.
- Manage based on phenotype and risk: institute surveillance, avoid telomere-toxic exposures, involve transplant/genetics teams early, and be cautious with donor selection.
- Communicate uncertainty clearly: VUS ≠ “not important” and ≠ “disease-causing” until additional evidence is amassed.
- Reassess periodically — labs reclassify VUSes; new data (functional studies, case reports) may change clinical decision-making.

If you’re caring for a similar patient: get flow-FISH, test relatives selectively, and loop in a geneticist/telomere clinic early. The combination of robust phenotype data and targeted investigations often converts uncertainty into actionable clarity.
