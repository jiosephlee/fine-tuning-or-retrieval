Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# IPF With a Telomere Signature: Clinical Implications of Suspected TBD in Young Fibrosis

What makes this case interesting?
A woman in her late 20s with progressive fatigue, pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and diffuse interstitial lung disease highlights an increasingly recognised clinical pattern: pulmonary fibrosis in a young adult with multisystem premature‑ageing features. Genetic testing identified a monoallelic PARN missense variant (VUS) — not a slam‑dunk pathogenic result, but the phenotype is classic for a telomere biology disorder (TBD). This vignette is a useful springboard to discuss how ILD clinicians should recognise a “telomere signature,” what to do next diagnostically, and how management and transplant planning change when a TBD is suspected.

## Recognising the telomere signature in ILD
Telomere‑related pulmonary fibrosis differs from garden‑variety idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) in a few ways. Key clinical clues in this case that raise the index of suspicion:
- Young age at presentation (often <60, sometimes much younger).
- Extrapulmonary features of premature ageing: premature greying, osteoporosis, thin/atrophic mucosa, nail and skin changes (although the classic DC triad may be absent).
- Haematologic abnormalities (cytopenias, bone marrow failure or poor reticulocyte responses).
- Hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis without other cause.
- Family history of pulmonary fibrosis, bone marrow failure, liver disease, early greying or sudden deaths (but may be absent due to de novo mutation or incomplete penetrance).
- Imaging: subpleural fibrotic change and UIP pattern are common, but ground glass and mixed patterns can be seen early.

Don’t mistake coexisting nutritional deficiencies or autoimmune serologies for the whole story — persistent, multisystem dysfunction despite correction of reversible causes should prompt genetic thinking.

## What to order when you suspect a TBD
A practical stepwise approach for ILD clinics:
1. Phenotype first: detailed family history (three generations), targeted exam for mucocutaneous signs, nails, hair, bone signs, hepatosplenomegaly.
2. Basic screening labs: CBC with reticulocyte count, LFTs, iron studies, B12/folate, bone density as indicated.
3. Pulmonary evaluation: PFTs (including DLCO), HRCT and exercise oximetry. Consider 6‑minute walk.
4. If clinical suspicion persists, send:
   - Telomere length measurement (flow‑FISH is the gold standard for peripheral blood leukocytes).
   - A targeted telomere gene panel (TERT, TERC, PARN, RTEL1, DKC1, etc.) or clinical exome with a telomere panel; interpret variants in context — VUS are common.
5. Multidisciplinary referral early: genetics (for counseling and cascade testing), hematology (bone marrow surveillance), hepatology (fibrosis staging), and transplant team if disease is progressive.

Flow‑FISH results and a pathogenic germline variant confirm a TBD; VUS + short telomeres with compatible phenotype is “functional” evidence to act on clinically.

## Impact on medical therapy and supportive care
Antifibrotics:
- Pirfenidone and nintedanib remain the mainstays for progressive fibrosing ILD, including telomere‑related disease. Data are limited but suggest benefit in slowing decline.
- Expectation management is important: some telomere‑related cases progress faster and may have less predictable responses.

Supportive care:
- Early referral for pulmonary rehabilitation and supplemental oxygen when indicated.
- Vaccination, avoidance of exposures that increase proliferative stress (smoking, occupational dusts), and careful use of cytotoxic drugs (some agents may accelerate marrow/liver injury).
- Screen and treat extrapulmonary problems: bone health, nutritional deficiencies, and liver disease.

Experimental/adjunctive therapies:
- Androgen derivatives (danazol, nandrolone) have shown telomere length gains in small studies for marrow disease; data for lung outcomes are limited and use should be specialist‑led.
- Clinical trials may be appropriate; discuss with genetics/translational teams.

## Transplant implications — be proactive
Lung transplant planning must be tailored when a TBD is present or suspected.

Pre‑transplant assessment:
- Full multisystem evaluation: CBC and bone marrow assessment (if cytopenia), hepatic function and imaging, dental/mucosal assessment, dermatologic exam.
- Identify occult organ dysfunction — even mild marrow or liver disease increases peri‑operative risk.
- Genetic counseling and family testing; identify and exclude related donors who may carry the same mutation.

Peri‑transplant considerations:
- Increased sensitivity to cytotoxic conditioning — higher risk of marrow failure, mucositis, infections and hepatic toxicity. Many centers favour reduced‑intensity conditioning or avoid myeloablative regimens.
- Careful haematology involvement for management of cytopenias and post‑transplant marrow suppression.
- Expect higher risk of bone marrow failure and other extrapulmonary complications after transplant; surveillance is essential.

Post‑transplant surveillance:
- Monitor for late marrow failure, progressive liver disease and malignancies.
- Coordinate long‑term care with hepatology and hematology; immunosuppression may need individualised adjustments.

Donor selection:
- Avoid related donors unless genetic testing excludes the variant/telomere shortening. Unrecognised donor telomere defects have been implicated in poor outcomes.

## Family screening and genetic counseling
- Cascade testing for first‑degree relatives is recommended when a pathogenic variant is identified. With VUS, counseling becomes nuanced — consider telomere length testing in relatives and longitudinal clinical surveillance.
- Reproductive counseling: discussion of inheritance patterns (autosomal dominant with incomplete penetrance vs recessive depending on gene), preimplantation or prenatal options if desired.
- Psychosocial support: uncertainty around VUS and variable penetrance can be distressing; genetics teams are critical.

## Practical algorithm to integrate genetic/telomere testing into ILD clinics
1. Identify red flags in any ILD patient: age <60, cytopenias, premature greying, unexplained liver disease, family history.
2. If any red flags present:
   - Order CBC + retic, LFTs, bone density as indicated.
   - Arrange HRCT/PFTs (standard for ILD).
   - Refer for flow‑FISH telomere length testing and telomere gene panel (or clinical exome) in parallel.
   - Simultaneously refer to genetics and hematology.
3. If telomere length is short and/or a pathogenic variant is found:
   - Initiate cascade family testing, discuss transplant implications, intensify organ surveillance.
   - Start/continue antifibrotic therapy where indicated and expedite transplant evaluation if progression.
4. If tests are negative but clinical suspicion remains high:
   - Periodic reassessment and consider research/expanded testing; manage extrapulmonary disease proactively.
5. Document and communicate restrictions: avoid related living donors without testing; consider reduced‑intensity transplant conditioning.

## Limitations and practical caveats
- Many variants are VUS; integration of phenotype, telomere length and family testing is essential for interpretation.
- Telomere length testing has age‑dependent norms — interpretation requires expertise.
- Not all centers have flow‑FISH; sendouts and delays are common, so start multidisciplinary planning early.
- Evidence base for specific therapies in telomere‑related ILD is limited; much management is extrapolated from IPF and marrow literature.

## Bottom line — clinical pearls and takeaways
- Think telomere biology disorder when fibrosis occurs in young adults, especially with marrow, liver or premature‑ageing features.
- Early genetic and telomere testing changes management: family screening, donor selection, transplant conditioning and multidisciplinary surveillance.
- Antifibrotics, oxygen and rehab still apply, but plan transplant earlier and involve hematology/hepatology/genetics before major interventions.
- Avoid using related donors without genetic/telomere evaluation; anticipate transplant complications and tailor conditioning.
- Even when a variant is a VUS, a compatible phenotype and short telomeres warrant acting clinically to mitigate risk and prepare for progressive disease.

This patient’s story is a reminder: in ILD clinics, the lungs rarely act alone. Finding a telomere signature has real consequences — for treatment, for family members, and for how we plan the safest path to transplant for those who need it.
