Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# Beyond the DC Triad: Spotting Telomere Biology Disorders in Young Adults Without Classic Mucocutaneous Clues

What makes this case striking is how a young woman in her late 20s accumulated progressive multisystem disease—pancytopenia with a low reticulocyte count, premature greying, hepatic cirrhosis, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis changes, osteoporosis with fragility, hepatosplenomegaly and neuropathy—yet lacked the textbook mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita (oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, reticular pigmentation). After years of workup she was found to carry a monoallelic PARN missense variant of uncertain significance, a finding that, together with the clinical picture, pointed clinicians toward a telomere biology disorder (TBD).

Why this matters to the clinician working in the ED, ward or clinic: TBDs are rare, variable in presentation, and commonly under-recognized when the classic mucocutaneous signs are absent. Missing the diagnosis delays appropriate genetic counselling, surveillance for progressive organ failure and early consideration of definitive therapies (for example, tailored transplant planning). The take-home: think telomeres when the pattern of organ dysfunction looks like premature ageing across multiple tissues—especially in younger adults.

## The pattern that should make you pause
TBDs primarily affect tissues with high turnover: bone marrow, mucosa, skin/hair, lung epithelium and liver. The phenotype can be protean and incomplete. In the case presented, the combination of findings is the diagnostic clue:

- Unexplained cytopenias (especially pancytopenia) with low reticulocyte count or poor marrow response to peripheral loss
- Premature greying/alopecia or other signs of early ageing in a young adult
- Organ fibrosis out of proportion to age: idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis
- Osteoporosis/early fragility fractures in a patient without classic risk factors
- Hepatosplenomegaly without clear alternative explanation
- Neuropathy, persistent inflammatory markers or multi-system involvement that doesn’t fit a single acquired disease

If you see several of these features together—especially in someone <50 years old—TBD should be on your differential even if there’s no leukoplakia, nail dystrophy or reticular pigmentation.

## Practical “trigger list” for suspecting a TBD in adults (use clinically)
Consider further evaluation for a TBD when one or more of the following are present without an alternative unifying diagnosis:

- Persistent unexplained cytopenia(s): anemia with low retic, neutropenia or thrombocytopenia, or pancytopenia despite appropriate initial workup
- Early-onset pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) or unexplained restrictive lung disease in a younger adult
- Unexplained liver fibrosis/cirrhosis in absence of viral, alcoholic or autoimmune causes
- Premature greying or alopecia before age 30–40
- Early osteoporosis with fragility fractures or markedly low bone density for age
- Unexplained hepatosplenomegaly
- Multisystem “premature ageing” phenotype: combinations of the above ± neuropathy, endocrinopathies
- Family history of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, aplastic anemia, early cirrhosis, or early unexplained deaths—even if incomplete penetrance is suspected

The more features present, the higher the pre-test probability.

## Initial evaluation — what to order early
Start with a focused but broad screen to rule out common mimics and collect baseline data. In the reported case, conventional causes (severe B12 deficiency, autoimmune serology, viral hepatitis) were addressed but did not fully explain the phenotype.

Key initial labs and tests:
- CBC with differential and reticulocyte count (look for low retic suggesting ineffective erythropoiesis)
- Peripheral blood smear (look for dimorphic anemia, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings)
- B12, folate, iron studies, ferritin, transferrin saturation (correct deficiencies but don’t stop there)
- Hemolysis panel: LDH, haptoglobin, bilirubin, DAT (positive DAT can complicate picture and doesn’t rule out marrow causes)
- LFTs, synthetic liver markers (INR, albumin), abdominal ultrasound and consider transient elastography if there’s hepatomegaly or abnormal LFTs
- Chest imaging: high-resolution CT chest if dyspnea or abnormal CXR (look for ground-glass and subpleural fibrosis)
- Bone marrow biopsy and cytogenetics if unexplained cytopenias persist (to exclude malignancy, MDS, aplastic anemia)
- Vitamin D, calcium, PTH, bone densitometry if bone health is a concern
- Nerve conduction/EMG if neuropathy is present
- Basic infectious and autoimmune screens to exclude common causes (HIV, hepatitis B/C, ANA, autoimmune hepatitis panel)
- Consider PET-CT if systemic inflammatory or infiltrative processes are suspected and malignancy needs exclusion (as done in this case)

Key point: Correct nutritional deficiencies (e.g., severe B12) promptly because they can both cause symptoms and unmask underlying susceptibility, but persistent or disproportionate organ dysfunction after correction should trigger genetic thinking.

## When to send specialized testing for telomere disorders
You don’t need (and shouldn’t order) broad genetic testing for every patient with anemia or IPF. Reserve telomere-specific evaluation for patients with the trigger features above and after exclusion of common acquired causes.

Consider telomere length testing and genetic sequencing when:
- Young age at presentation (<50) with unexplained pulmonary fibrosis, bone marrow failure, or cirrhosis
- Multisystem findings suggesting premature ageing (as above)
- Persistent cytopenias with low reticulocyte count and uninformative marrow/cytogenetics
- Family history suggestive of related conditions (even if incomplete or non-obvious)

Which tests and sequencing:
- Telomere length measurement (flow-FISH on leukocyte subsets is the laboratory gold standard in adults for diagnosing TBD). Short telomeres for age strongly support the diagnosis.
- Targeted gene panel or clinical exome sequencing including telomere-related genes (TERT, TERC, DKC1, PARN, RTEL1, TINF2, etc.). Panels focused on telomere biology or inherited bone marrow failure syndromes are efficient.
- Germline testing is preferred over somatic-only panels; use blood with caution if bone marrow failure or clonal hematopoiesis might confound results—discuss with a geneticist/lab.

Note: Telomere length testing requires age-matched interpretation. A short telomere with a pathogenic variant in a telomere gene clinches the diagnosis; a variant of uncertain significance (VUS), as in this case, may still be clinically meaningful when the phenotype fits—interpret in context and consider segregation studies.

## How to differentiate TBD from common mimics
TBDs can masquerade as nutritional deficiency, autoimmune disease, chronic infection or early-onset primary organ disease. Here’s how to tease them apart:

- Nutritional deficiency (B12/folate, iron): deficits commonly explain cytopenias and neuro features. But in TBD:
  - Deficiencies often coexist (as in the case) and may partially respond to replacement, yet multisystem involvement and persistent cytopenias or organ fibrosis after correction suggest an underlying genetic vulnerability.
  - Look for low retic despite marrow hyperplasia—ineffective erythropoiesis can be a clue.
- Autoimmune disease: autoimmune cytopenias and autoimmune hepatitis can mimic features.
  - Autoimmune serologies, inflammatory markers and response to immunosuppression help differentiate. A positive DAT alone is not diagnostic of primary autoimmune disease—interpret with the overall picture.
- Chronic infection (viral hepatitis, HIV, EBV, CMV): these can cause cytopenias and liver disease.
  - Exclude these with appropriate serologies; negative workup in someone with fibrosis and other age-inappropriate findings should prompt genetic consideration.
- Myelodysplastic syndromes / marrow infiltrative disease:
  - Bone marrow biopsy, cytogenetics and molecular testing primarily differentiate these. In TBD, marrow may show trilineage hematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia but without dysplasia or clonal cytogenetic abnormalities—yet peripheral cytopenias persist because stem cell reserve is reduced.
- Primary IPF, early cirrhosis:
  - Young age, family history and concurrent marrow or hair abnormalities should raise suspicion for TBD rather than isolated idiopathic organ disease.

Bottom line: failure to fully explain the pattern by a single acquired disease and young age are the red flags.

## Management principles and prognostic implications (brief)
- Early recognition changes management: genetic counselling, family screening and tailored surveillance for progressive marrow failure, pulmonary decline and hepatocellular carcinoma risk.
- Treat reversible contributors aggressively (eg, replace B12/folate, optimize nutrition, avoid hepatotoxins, stop smoking).
- Consider hematopoietic stem cell transplantation for progressive bone marrow failure—outcomes depend on recognizing telomere disorders because transplant protocols (reduced-intensity conditioning) and donor selection differ.
- Lung or liver transplantation may be considered in selected cases; telomere status affects perioperative risk and post-transplant complications.
- Experimental therapies such as androgen derivatives have some preliminary data for telomere elongation but require specialist input and long-term monitoring.
- Multidisciplinary care (hematology, pulmonology, hepatology, genetics) is essential.

## Clinical pearls
- Don’t be reassured by absent mucocutaneous signs—up to half of adult TBD presentations lack the classic DC triad.
- A low reticulocyte response in the setting of cytopenia suggests marrow stress/ineffective erythropoiesis and should prompt marrow evaluation and consideration of inherited marrow failure if common causes are excluded.
- Severe nutritional deficiencies can coexist with TBD and partially explain symptoms—document and correct them, then reassess. Persistence or disproportionate organ involvement afterwards is a clue.
- Monoallelic variants in genes like PARN can present primarily with pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis but may also predispose to marrow vulnerability—genotype–phenotype correlations are still evolving.
- Telomere length testing (flow-FISH) plus targeted germline sequencing is the diagnostic pathway; interpret genetic variants in concert with clinical findings and consider specialist genetics input for VUS or complex results.

## Take-home messages
- Consider a telomere biology disorder in younger adults with unexplained multisystem premature-aging phenotypes—particularly when you see combinations of unexplained cytopenias with low retic, premature greying, pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis, early osteoporosis/fragility fractures, hepatosplenomegaly or neuropathy.
- Initial workup should both exclude common mimics (nutritional, autoimmune, infectious, malignant) and gather baseline data (CBC with retic, smear, marrow exam, LFTs, HRCT chest, elastography if liver disease suspected).
- Order telomere length testing (flow-FISH) and targeted germline sequencing when the clinical picture fits; partner with genetics for interpretation and family counselling.
- Early diagnosis matters: it alters surveillance, family screening, transplant planning and avoids misdirected therapies.

If you’re seeing a young patient with “too much ageing” for their years—especially across marrow, lung and liver—add “telomere disorder” to your differential and start the targeted workup.
