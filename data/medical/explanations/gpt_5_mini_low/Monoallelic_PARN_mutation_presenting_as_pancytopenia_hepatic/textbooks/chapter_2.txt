Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

Chapter 2: ## Focused Background: Telomere Biology Disorders and PARN

Telomeres are repetitive DNA–protein structures that cap chromosome ends and preserve genome stability by preventing end-to-end fusions and inappropriate DNA repair. With each cell division telomeres shorten; when they reach a critically short length, cells enter replicative senescence or apoptosis. Telomerase, a ribonucleoprotein complex composed principally of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC), counteracts this shortening by adding telomeric repeats. Tissues with high proliferative demand—haematopoietic stem cells, epithelial surfaces (including oral mucosa and skin), hair follicles, and hepatocytes—are therefore most sensitive to defects in telomere maintenance, explaining the organ predilection seen in telomere biology disorders (TBDs).

Clinically, TBDs span a spectrum. The classic pediatric phenotype, dyskeratosis congenita (DC), is defined by the mucocutaneous triad (nail dystrophy, oral leukoplakia, reticular pigmentation) together with bone marrow failure. However, pathogenic variants in telomere-related genes may present variably: adult-onset isolated organ fibrosis (for example idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, IPF), predominant bone marrow failure, or mixed multisystem disease. Penetrance is often incomplete and age-dependent, so classical features may be absent and presentation can be subtle or restricted to one organ in adulthood. This variable expressivity means a young adult with marrow failure, unexplained hepatic fibrosis or early pulmonary fibrosis should raise suspicion for an underlying TBD even without the full DC phenotype.

PARN (poly(A)-specific ribonuclease) encodes a nuclear exoribonuclease involved in deadenylation—the shortening and removal of poly(A) tails on RNAs—which is a key step in RNA maturation and stability control. Of particular relevance to telomere biology, PARN participates in the 3′-end maturation of TERC by removing aberrant oligo(A) tails that would otherwise target TERC for degradation. Proper maturation and stability of TERC is essential for assembling functional telomerase. Loss or reduction of PARN function therefore leads to decreased mature TERC levels, impaired telomerase activity and accelerated telomere shortening.

Genotype–phenotype correlations in PARN-related disease reflect the degree of functional loss. Biallelic (recessive) loss-of-function PARN variants have been reported in severe, early-onset TBDs such as Hoyeraal–Hreidarsson syndrome and autosomal recessive DC, which often display the full mucocutaneous triad and profound marrow failure. Monoallelic (heterozygous) PARN variants are more commonly associated with adult-onset phenotypes—most notably pulmonary fibrosis and variable bone marrow dysfunction (a pattern sometimes described as PFBMFT4). The biology is consistent with haploinsufficiency: when one allele is defective, reduced PARN dosage may be sufficient over time to lower TERC and telomerase activity below a threshold in certain tissues, producing disease later in life or in a tissue-limited fashion.

Mechanistically, telomere shortening from impaired telomerase preferentially affects stem and progenitor compartments. In the bone marrow, exhausted haematopoietic stem cells lose proliferative reserve, producing progressive cytopenias and predisposition to marrow failure. In the lung, alveolar epithelial progenitors with critically short telomeres have limited capacity for repair after injury, promoting aberrant wound healing and fibrosis (IPF). In the liver, stressed hepatocytes and progenitor cells similarly demonstrate impaired regeneration, predisposing to hepatic fibrosis and eventual cirrhosis. These tissue effects explain how a single underlying telomere maintenance defect—such as PARN dysfunction—can manifest as concurrent marrow cytopenias, pulmonary fibrosis and hepatic fibrosis, as observed in the presented patient.

Clinical expression is further modified by non‑genetic factors. Environmental exposures (smoking, infections), inflammation, metabolic stress and even nutritional deficiencies can increase proliferative demand or DNA damage and thereby accelerate telomere attrition, unmasking or worsening disease in genetically susceptible individuals. In this case, severe vitamin B12 deficiency and systemic inflammation likely compounded ineffective haematopoiesis, precipitating overt cytopenias in a patient already predisposed by a monoallelic PARN variant. Additionally, phenomena such as somatic genetic rescue or clonal evolution can alter tissue‑specific manifestations and contribute to incomplete penetrance, leading to marked heterogeneity even among carriers of the same variant.

Understanding this focused pathophysiology clarifies why a young adult with disproportionate multisystem fibrosis and marrow dysfunction but without classic mucocutaneous findings may nonetheless harbor a telomere biology disorder driven by disrupted PARN function. The finding of a monoallelic PARN variant in such a clinical context therefore provides a plausible mechanistic link and warrants longitudinal surveillance for progressive marrow, lung or liver involvement.
