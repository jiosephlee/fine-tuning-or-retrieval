Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Reasoning: From Ineffective Erythropoiesis to a Telomere Biology Disorder

This patient’s evaluation illustrates stepwise clinical reasoning from a common haematologic presentation—pancytopenia with a low reticulocyte count—toward a rare, unifying genetic diagnosis. The initial laboratory pattern required distinguishing between peripheral destruction of red cells and inadequate production. A low reticulocyte percentage (0.39%) indicates the marrow is not mounting an appropriate regenerative response. Bone marrow examination in this case showed trilineage haematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia and increased iron stores but no dysplasia or infiltrate, a constellation most consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis: the marrow is producing erythroid precursors but they fail to mature or survive to release reticulocytes.

Several peripheral smear and serum findings supported ineffective erythropoiesis rather than pure hemolysis. Dimorphic erythrocytes, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings and hypersegmented neutrophils point toward disordered erythropoiesis and megaloblastic changes. Markers of haemolysis (elevated LDH, indirect bilirubin) were present, and the direct antiglobulin test (DAT) was positive for IgG; however, the absent reticulocytosis and the marrow pattern argue that peripheral immune destruction was not the dominant mechanism. A positive DAT can be incidental or reflect low-grade autoimmune phenomena, but it must be interpreted alongside reticulocytes and marrow findings—here, ineffective erythropoiesis better explains the cytopenias.

Because ineffective erythropoiesis is frequently reversible if due to nutrient deficiency, the next logical step was to identify and correct reversible causes. This patient had severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency—well-known causes of megaloblastic ineffective erythropoiesis and hypersegmented neutrophils—and received standard parenteral B12 repletion. Investigation for malabsorption identified reduced parietal cells on duodenal biopsy and chronic gastritis, a plausible explanation for impaired intrinsic factor production and B12 malabsorption. Nevertheless, the clinical severity and multisystem involvement (hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying, osteoporosis, neuropathy) appeared out of proportion to nutritional deficiency alone, prompting further evaluation for an underlying driver.

Parallel to addressing reversible causes, the team pursued a systematic exclusion of alternate hepatic and pulmonary aetiologies. Hepatic imaging and elastography documented cirrhosis; autoimmune serologies and viral hepatitis panels were negative, and PET–CT did not reveal occult malignancy or granulomatous disease. Pulmonary imaging showed diffuse ground-glass and subpleural fibrotic changes consistent with interstitial fibrosis rather than infection or neoplasm. Excluding common infectious, autoimmune and neoplastic causes is crucial before invoking a unifying inherited syndrome, and in this case those alternative explanations were not supported.

Aggregating clinical clues clarified that this was not purely a haematologic or nutritional disorder. Premature greying of hair, osteoporosis with fractures at a young age, sensorimotor axonal neuropathy, hepatosplenomegaly with established hepatic fibrosis, and early-onset pulmonary fibrosis form a pattern of premature ageing with multisystem fibrotic involvement. When these features coexist with persistent cytopenias, especially in a young adult, one must consider telomere biology disorders (TBDs) even if the classical mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita (oral leukoplakia, nail changes, reticulate pigmentation) is absent.

Deciding when to suspect a TBD rests on recognizing disproportionate, multisystem manifestations of reduced replicative reserve—bone marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, early osteoporosis, and premature hair greying—in the absence of alternative explanations. Incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity are typical for TBDs, so lack of family history or absence of mucocutaneous findings does not exclude the diagnosis. In this patient the persistence of multisystem disease despite correction of B12/folate strengthened the rationale for genetic testing.

Clinical exome sequencing was therefore performed and identified a monoallelic PARN missense variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) within the conserved R3H domain. Choosing clinical exome sequencing is appropriate when the differential is broad and includes multiple potential monogenic causes; for suspected TBDs, targeted panels including TERT, TERC, PARN, RTEL1 and others, or broader exome testing, are commonly used. Detection of a rare variant in a telomere-related gene provided a plausible mechanistic link to the phenotype, given PARN’s role in TERC maturation and telomere maintenance.

Interpreting the variant used ACMG/AMP criteria supplemented by clinical correlation. The variant met PM1 because it affects a critical, well-conserved functional domain (R3H) where pathogenic variants have been reported. PM2 was met by its absence or extreme rarity in population databases. PP2 applies because PARN is intolerant of benign missense variation, and PP3 is supported by multiple in silico tools predicting deleterious impact. Despite these supportive lines of evidence, the variant remained classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS) because definitive functional or segregation data were lacking. This illustrates an important principle: a VUS can be clinically informative when the genotype plausibly explains the phenotype, but it does not constitute a definitive pathogenic call without additional evidence.

When a TBD is suspected but genetic results are inconclusive, ancillary testing can strengthen the diagnostic assessment. Flow-FISH measurement of telomere length in peripheral blood leukocytes is a validated adjunct: markedly shortened telomeres for age support a TBD diagnosis and can help interpret VUS findings. Family testing and segregation analysis—testing parents and available relatives—can clarify whether the variant is de novo or tracks with disease, which is especially helpful for variants in genes with dominant or haploinsufficient effects. Functional assays of telomerase activity or targeted assays for PARN function, if available, can further aid interpretation.

Synthesizing the data leads to a unifying hypothesis for this case: a monoallelic PARN variant reduced telomere maintenance capacity, predisposing replicative tissues (bone marrow, liver, lung, bone) to early senescence and fibrosis. Severe B12 deficiency acted as a compounding stressor on an already vulnerable haematopoietic system, producing the observed ineffective erythropoiesis and cytopenias. The absence of florid mucocutaneous signs reflects the variable expressivity of TBDs; organ-specific manifestations such as pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis can predominate in some patients with monoallelic telomere gene variants.

Finally, there are important pitfalls and practical pearls. Telomere disorders show incomplete penetrance and variable age of onset; therefore, a negative family history does not exclude a genetic telomereopathy. A VUS should not be overinterpreted as causal, but clinical–genetic concordance can justify closer surveillance and targeted family counselling. Ordering telomere length testing and segregation studies can convert a probabilistic diagnosis into a more definitive one. Clinicians should also recognize that treatable contributors (nutrient deficiencies, infections, toxins) must be corrected, because they may unmask or exacerbate a genetic marrow vulnerability. In summary, careful integration of haematologic data, exclusion of reversible and alternative causes, aggregation of systemic clues, and targeted genetic and functional testing allowed clinicians to move from a working diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis to a likely telomere biology disorder in this patient.
