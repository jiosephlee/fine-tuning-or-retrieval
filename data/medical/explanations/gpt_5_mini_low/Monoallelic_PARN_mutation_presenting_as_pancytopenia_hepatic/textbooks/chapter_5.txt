Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

Chapter 5: ## Outcomes, Prognosis, and Key Clinical Lessons

Case outcome
The patient experienced early symptomatic improvement after correction of severe nutritional deficiencies and provision of supportive care. Hemoglobin and other blood counts were stable at the 2‑week follow‑up, fatigue improved, and no new clinical problems were recorded. Because the underlying concern is a telomere biology disorder (TBD) associated with a monoallelic PARN variant, definitive cure was not offered; instead she was placed on longitudinal surveillance for hepatic, pulmonary and haematologic progression and received genetic counselling for herself and her family.

Prognostic considerations in PARN‑related TBD
Prognosis in TBDs is driven by the organs involved, the degree of telomere shortening, the specific gene/variant and exposure to additional stresses (nutritional deficiency, cytotoxic drugs, infections). In PARN‑associated disease the dominant risks are progressive pulmonary fibrosis and, in some patients, progressive bone marrow failure and hepatic fibrosis. Even when cytopenias initially respond to correction of reversible causes (as here with B12 replacement), marrow reserve may be limited; future evolution to chronic marrow failure remains possible and should be anticipated. Pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis—once established—tend to be progressive and are major determinants of morbidity and mortality. Younger age at presentation and multi‑organ involvement generally predict a higher likelihood of progression. Coexisting insults (e.g., persistent nutritional deficits, hepatotoxic or myelotoxic exposures, smoking) accelerate decompensation and should be minimized.

Red flags that should prompt evaluation for a TBD in adults
This case highlights clinical signals that should lower the threshold to evaluate for an inherited telomere disorder:
- Unexplained cytopenias, particularly pancytopenia with a low reticulocyte response and marrow findings consistent with ineffective haematopoiesis rather than isolated peripheral destruction.
- Features of premature ageing (premature greying, early osteoporosis, skin or mucosal atrophy) in a young adult.
- Early‑onset fibrotic disease of organs commonly affected in TBDs—idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis or unexplained hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis—especially when occurring together.
- Multisystem involvement without a unifying acquired explanation, or disproportionate organ dysfunction relative to identified reversible causes (for example, severe B12 deficiency that does not fully explain cytopenia plus organ fibrosis).
- Family history of early pulmonary, hepatic, or haematologic failure, though absence of family history does not exclude de novo mutations or reduced penetrance.

Diagnostic best practices and interpretation of genetic findings
When a TBD is suspected, an integrated diagnostic approach is optimal:
- Measure telomere length (for example by flow‑FISH) when available: markedly short telomeres for age support a TBD diagnosis and strengthen interpretation of sequence variants.
- Use targeted gene panels or exome sequencing focused on telomere‑related genes, with careful annotation of variants.
- Interpret variants (particularly variants of uncertain significance, VUS) in clinical context: a VUS in a gene with strong biological plausibility (PARN here), consistent phenotype, short telomeres and supportive in silico evidence increases the likelihood that the variant is disease‑relevant. Conversely, absence of telomere shortening weakens that inference.
- Re‑assess VUS over time: as population and functional data accumulate, variant classification can change; periodic re‑review and family segregation testing can clarify pathogenicity.
- When feasible, perform family studies (segregation) and functional assays (TERC maturation, PARN activity, or telomerase function) in research or reference labs to help resolve uncertain variants.

Management pearls specific to TBDs and this case
- Address reversible contributors first: correcting nutritional deficiencies, treating infections and avoiding myelotoxic medications can yield clinical benefit and unmask or delay underlying disease progression, as seen here.
- Anticipate and plan for progressive organ dysfunction: enlist a multidisciplinary team (haematology, pulmonology, hepatology, genetics, transplant services, and rehabilitation) early for surveillance and timely intervention.
- When hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) is considered for progressive marrow failure, use protocols tailored for TBD patients (reduced‑intensity conditioning, careful donor selection avoiding related donors with occult TBD, and telomere‑aware risk counselling) because standard myeloablative regimens carry high toxicity in these patients.
- For end‑organ failure (advanced IPF or cirrhosis), referral to lung or liver transplant programs with experience in TBDs is appropriate; candidacy assessment should consider extra‑pulmonary/extra‑hepatic manifestations and overall frailty.
- Risk‑mitigation counseling: avoid tobacco and unnecessary ionizing radiation, minimize hepatotoxic and myelotoxic exposures, and ensure vaccination and prompt treatment of infections.
- Document and communicate the suspected genetic diagnosis to all treating teams so that future therapies (for example chemotherapy or HSCT) are planned with awareness of telomere vulnerability.

Knowledge gaps and areas for future attention
Important uncertainties remain relevant to clinical decision‑making in monoallelic PARN carriers:
- The full spectrum and penetrance of organ involvement with monoallelic PARN variants—particularly the true prevalence and natural history of liver disease—are incompletely defined.
- There are limited data guiding the optimal timing and selection of disease‑modifying therapies (androgens, investigational telomerase‑targeting approaches) and their long‑term benefits and risks in PARN‑related TBD.
- Predictive biomarkers beyond telomere length (for progression risk stratification) are lacking.
- Functional assays that reliably quantify the impact of specific missense PARN variants on telomere biology are not widely available in clinical practice.

Takeaway clinical lessons
In young adults with unexplained multisystem disease—especially the combination of cytopenia with low reticulocyte count, premature ageing signs, and early pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis—think beyond common acquired causes and evaluate for a telomere biology disorder. Correct reversible contributors promptly, but institute long‑term surveillance and multidisciplinary planning because apparent short‑term improvement may mask an underlying progressive genetic predisposition. Genetic findings (including VUS) must be integrated with telomere length testing, clinical phenotype and, where possible, family studies; management decisions (including transplantation) should reflect the unique risks of TBDs.
