Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and Initial Assessment

The patient presented in her late 20s with a 5‑year history of progressive fatigue and declining functional capacity, more recently accompanied by exertional dyspnoea, weight loss and anorexia. A chronic, insidious course over years places her presentation between purely nutritional disease and a more durable systemic disorder; the progressive nature and multisystem complaints prompted evaluation beyond routine nutritional causes. She also reported bone pain with recurrent fractures, a symptom that, together with later imaging, suggested compromised bone integrity rather than isolated musculoskeletal strain.

On examination the most striking features were pallor and icterus, consistent with an anaemic state with some degree of haemolysis or ineffective erythropoiesis. Visible premature greying of hair and knuckle hyperpigmentation imparted a premature‑ageing phenotype that is atypical for her age and raised suspicion for a syndromic process. Oral findings—an atrophic (“bald”) tongue with loss of lingual papillae—can be seen with nutritional deficiency but also occur in inherited marrow‑failure syndromes. Musculoskeletal findings included thoracic scoliosis and bilateral thenar atrophy, while abdominal palpation revealed marked hepatomegaly with splenomegaly on imaging. Respiratory examination showed reduced breath sounds at both lung bases, compatible with lower‑zone parenchymal disease.

Laboratory evaluation established pancytopenia with a low reticulocyte count (0.39%), indicating an inadequate marrow response to peripheral cytopenias. Peripheral blood morphology showed a dimorphic red cell population with basophilic stippling, Cabot rings and hypersegmented neutrophils. This combination points strongly toward ineffective erythropoiesis and disturbed erythroid maturation—hypersegmented neutrophils and macrocytic elements suggest severe cobalamin/folate deficiency, whereas basophilic stippling and Cabot rings reflect disrupted erythropoiesis and may be seen in marrow stress or toxic/metabolic conditions.

Nutritional studies confirmed severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency, which plausibly explains many of the haematologic abnormalities and the atrophic glossitis. Iron studies, however, showed elevated serum iron and ferritin—an “iron‑loaded” pattern paradoxical in anaemia—consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis and impaired iron utilization rather than simple iron excess from intake. This biochemical picture is commonly encountered when marrow erythroid precursors fail to mature and release iron for new haemoglobin synthesis.

Bone marrow examination clarified the marrow substrate: trilineage haematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia and increased iron stores, but no dysplasia, infiltrative disease or malignancy. Normal cytogenetics reduced the likelihood of a primary clonal haematologic malignancy. The erythroid hyperplasia in the marrow, paired with a low reticulocyte count and peripheral features of abnormal erythropoiesis, supports a central (marrow‑based) ineffective erythropoiesis as the dominant mechanism for the anaemia rather than pure peripheral destruction.

A direct antiglobulin test (DAT) was positive for IgG (2+), which raised the possibility of immune‑mediated haemolysis. However, in this patient the overall context—low reticulocyte response, marrow erythroid hyperplasia with ineffective maturation and lack of other haemolytic features—favoured ineffective erythropoiesis over classic autoimmune haemolysis as the principal driver of anaemia. In practice this distinction matters because management differs: autoimmune haemolysis often responds to immunosuppression, whereas ineffective erythropoiesis requires addressing the underlying marrow dysfunction and any reversible causes (eg, nutrient repletion).

Beyond the haematologic system, imaging demonstrated multisystem involvement. Abdominal ultrasound and transient elastography revealed hepatosplenomegaly, steatosis and a liver stiffness (18.1 kPa) consistent with cirrhosis. The degree of hepatic fibrosis was disproportionate for a young person without metabolic syndrome or viral/autoimmune causes on testing, making a secondary or genetic cause more likely. Whole‑body PET‑CT confirmed diffuse ground‑glass opacities with subpleural fibrotic and atelectatic changes in both lung fields, compatible with interstitial lung disease or early pulmonary fibrosis. These pulmonary imaging findings, together with cirrhosis and marrow dysfunction, signalled a multisystem fibrotic process rather than isolated organ disease.

Additional problems identified on baseline assessment included marked osteoporosis on DXA with secondary hyperparathyroidism and vitamin D deficiency—explaining her bone pain and fractures—and electrolyte disturbances (hypocalcaemia, hypokalaemia). Neurophysiologic testing showed an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy with chronic neurogenic changes, accounting for some of her weakness and functional decline. These non‑haematologic features further reinforced the likelihood of a systemic disorder affecting tissues with high proliferative demand or long‑term cellular maintenance needs.

Synthesis of the initial problem list highlights five core issues that directed further workup: (1) pancytopenia driven by ineffective erythropoiesis with low reticulocyte response despite marrow erythroid hyperplasia, (2) severe vitamin B12/folate deficiency partially explaining haematologic and mucosal findings but insufficient to account for the total phenotype, (3) hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis with hepatosplenomegaly and steatosis unexplained by conventional causes, (4) interstitial/pulmonary fibrotic changes on imaging, and (5) extra‑haematologic features of premature ageing (premature greying, skin hyperpigmentation), neuropathy and skeletal fragility. Together, these findings prompted consideration of a unifying diagnosis linking marrow failure, premature ageing and multiorgan fibrosis—most notably a telomere biology disorder—rather than separate, unrelated conditions. Early recognition of this pattern prioritized genetic evaluation and close multidisciplinary surveillance over isolated, short‑term fixes.
