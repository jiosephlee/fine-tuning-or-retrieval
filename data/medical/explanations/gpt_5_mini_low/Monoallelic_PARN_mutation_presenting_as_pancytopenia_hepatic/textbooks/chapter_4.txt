Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

Chapter 4: ## Management Strategy and Longitudinal Care in Suspected PARN-Related TBD

The management of a patient with a suspected PARN-associated telomere biology disorder (TBD) combines immediate correction of reversible contributors to cytopenia and organ dysfunction, organ-specific supportive care, and long-term surveillance and planning for possible progression to bone marrow failure or end‑stage organ disease. In this case the first priorities were to correct nutritional deficiencies and stabilize cytopenias, while initiating surveillance for evolving hematologic, hepatic and pulmonary complications and arranging genetic counselling and specialist referral.

Immediate measures
The patient required prompt parenteral vitamin B12 because of severe deficiency with ineffective erythropoiesis and low reticulocyte response. Standard acute replacement is intramuscular or intravenous cyanocobalamin 1,000 µg daily for 5–7 days (or until clinical/hematologic improvement), then weekly for 4–6 weeks and thereafter monthly maintenance injections; this regimen was used in the case. Concurrent folic acid replacement (eg, 1 mg daily) is appropriate when folate deficiency is present or to support erythropoiesis, and vitamin D repletion (eg, high‑dose weekly cholecalciferol then maintenance) with adequate elemental calcium should be started for documented deficiency and osteoporosis. Electrolyte abnormalities (hypocalcaemia, hypokalaemia) are corrected per standard protocols. Transfusion support is given for symptomatic anemia or hemodynamic instability; use the minimum number of packed red cell transfusions required, and monitor for iron overload if repeated transfusions become necessary.

Rehabilitation and symptom control
Functional decline, fractures and neuropathy require multidisciplinary rehabilitation. Physiotherapy should focus on strength, balance and gait training to reduce falls and preserve independence. Neuropathic pain can be managed with guideline‑based agents (eg, duloxetine, gabapentin) taking renal function and drug interactions into account. For bone health, optimize calcium and vitamin D, assess secondary causes (eg, hyperparathyroidism as in this case) and consider antiresorptive therapy. Bisphosphonate candidacy should be individualized: treat established osteoporosis or fragility fractures after evaluating renal function and life expectancy; ensure adequate calcium/vitamin D and discuss potential risks. Fall prevention strategies and bone-protection measures are essential given early osteoporosis in TBDs.

Hepatic care
Because hepatic fibrosis and cirrhosis were present, routine cirrhosis surveillance and management applies. Enroll the patient in hepatology follow-up with periodic assessment for portal hypertension (clinical exam, platelet count trends, transient elastography as indicated) and hepatocellular carcinoma surveillance (abdominal ultrasound ± alpha‑fetoprotein every 6 months in cirrhosis). Counsel strict alcohol avoidance and treat metabolic contributors: optimise weight, glycaemic control and lipid profile. Vaccinate if not immune against hepatitis A and B and ensure other routine immunizations are up to date. Workup for other causes of liver disease was appropriately completed in the case; ongoing monitoring for progressive liver dysfunction is required.

Pulmonary care
Baseline pulmonary assessment should include high‑resolution CT (HRCT) and pulmonary function tests (PFTs) with diffusion capacity (DLCO) to quantify extent of interstitial lung disease. In TBD patients, serial PFTs and DLCO every 3–6 months initially (then spacing according to stability) are reasonable to detect progression. Repeat HRCT is indicated for clinical or functional decline. For progressive idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) or fibrotic ILD, antifibrotic therapy (nintedanib or pirfenidone) is considered; these agents slow functional decline in fibrotic lung disease and can be used in telomere‑related fibrosis, but risks and benefits must be individualized and coordinated with pulmonology. Infection prevention includes annual influenza vaccination and pneumococcal vaccination as per guidelines; consider prophylactic measures and low threshold for treating respiratory infections. Smoking cessation is mandatory; counsel regarding occupational exposures and travel risks.

Hematology follow‑up
Even when an initial marrow does not show classical aplasia, patients with TBD require close hematologic surveillance because marrow failure can evolve. Regular complete blood counts with reticulocyte counts at intervals of approximately every 3 months (sooner if changes occur) help detect declining hematopoiesis. Worsening cytopenias, rising transfusion requirements, new or progressive neutropenia, or morphologic changes on peripheral smear are triggers for repeating bone marrow evaluation, including morphology, cytogenetics and flow cytometry; consider extended marrow testing (eg, telomere length measurement in leukocyte subsets) if available. Manage infections aggressively and avoid empiric myelosuppressive medications. Erythropoiesis‑stimulating agents or thrombopoietin mimetics are not routinely used in TBD without a clear indication and should be considered cautiously with hematology input.

Transplant considerations
Early engagement of transplant teams experienced in TBD is essential. Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) is the only curative option for progressive marrow failure but carries increased toxicity in TBD patients due to short telomeres; therefore, telomere‑aware approaches are required. Reduced‑intensity conditioning regimens are preferred to limit organ toxicity, and careful selection of immunosuppression and dosing is critical. Donor selection must exclude related donors who may harbour the same germline mutation or short telomeres; ideally use an HLA‑matched unrelated donor or evaluate donors with telomere length/genetic testing when feasible. Timing of HSCT is individualized—consider referral when there are progressive, transfusion‑dependent cytopenias or clonal evolution—balancing decline rate against transplant risks. For end-stage lung or liver disease, transplant candidacy should be discussed early with respiratory and hepatology/transplant teams; multi‑organ involvement and prior HSCT status influence candidacy and outcomes, so coordination among specialties is crucial.

Disease‑modifying options under study
Androgens such as danazol and nandrolone have been reported to increase telomere length and improve blood counts in small studies and case series; danazol may induce hematologic responses in some patients with telomeropathies. However, benefits are variable and risks include hepatic toxicity, lipid abnormalities and virilisation; caution is needed, particularly in patients with hepatic fibrosis as in this case. Enrollment in clinical trials or referral to centres conducting trials of telomere‑targeted therapies (androgens, telomerase modulators, gene therapy) should be offered when available, after a careful risk–benefit discussion.

Genetic counselling
Genetic counselling is essential both at diagnosis and longitudinally. Explain the concept of a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS): it may be reclassified over time as more data accumulate, and family testing can help with interpretation. Discuss inheritance patterns (PARN can be associated with autosomal dominant or recessive presentations depending on variant type and penetrance), the possibilities of de novo mutation, and implications for relatives. Offer cascade testing to first‑degree relatives and consider telomere length testing where available. Reproductive options (preimplantation genetic diagnosis, prenatal testing) should be discussed with a reproductive genetics specialist when a pathogenic variant is confirmed. Provide psychosocial support, as uncertainty about prognosis and familial risk is common.

Preventive care and lifestyle measures
Advise avoidance of known telomere‑accelerating or myelotoxic exposures—chemotherapy with alkylating agents, radiotherapy and certain immunosuppressants—unless absolutely required and after specialist discussion. Vaccinate according to guidelines, avoid live vaccines in the setting of significant immunosuppression, and maintain up‑to‑date cancer screening appropriate for age and organ disease (including skin cancer surveillance given cutaneous manifestations of TBDs). Counsel on smoking cessation, sun protection, prudent alcohol intake (abstinence if hepatic fibrosis), and optimization of metabolic risk factors. Provide clear instructions about when to seek care for fever, bleeding or new respiratory symptoms.

Coordination of care and follow‑up plan
A practical longitudinal plan for this patient includes ongoing monthly B12 injections until stability, then maintenance; hematology follow‑up with CBC and reticulocyte count every 3 months; hepatology surveillance every 3–6 months with imaging and labs dictated by liver stiffness and portal hypertension features; pulmonology follow‑up with PFTs every 3–6 months and HRCT as indicated; bone health monitoring and fracture prevention interventions; and periodic reassessment by genetics for VUS reclassification and family cascade testing. Early referral to specialised centres for consideration of HSCT or transplant evaluation and for clinical trial access is recommended. Documentation of the suspected TBD and counselling about avoidance of myelotoxic exposures should be communicated to all treating teams.

In summary, care for a suspected PARN‑related TBD is multidisciplinary, proactive and anticipatory: correct reversible contributors, provide organ‑specific supportive care, closely monitor for progression, and plan transplant or trial options with teams experienced in telomere disorders while offering ongoing genetic counselling and preventive guidance.
