Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Assessment

The patient presented in her late 20s with a 5‑year history of progressive fatigue and declining functional capacity, more recently accompanied by exertional dyspnoea, weight loss and anorexia. A chronic, insidious course over years places her presentation between purely nutritional disease and a more durable systemic disorder; the progressive nature and multisystem complaints prompted evaluation beyond routine nutritional causes. She also reported bone pain with recurrent fractures, a symptom that, together with later imaging, suggested compromised bone integrity rather than isolated musculoskeletal strain.

On examination the most striking features were pallor and icterus, consistent with an anaemic state with some degree of haemolysis or ineffective erythropoiesis. Visible premature greying of hair and knuckle hyperpigmentation imparted a premature‑ageing phenotype that is atypical for her age and raised suspicion for a syndromic process. Oral findings—an atrophic (“bald”) tongue with loss of lingual papillae—can be seen with nutritional deficiency but also occur in inherited marrow‑failure syndromes. Musculoskeletal findings included thoracic scoliosis and bilateral thenar atrophy, while abdominal palpation revealed marked hepatomegaly with splenomegaly on imaging. Respiratory examination showed reduced breath sounds at both lung bases, compatible with lower‑zone parenchymal disease.

Laboratory evaluation established pancytopenia with a low reticulocyte count (0.39%), indicating an inadequate marrow response to peripheral cytopenias. Peripheral blood morphology showed a dimorphic red cell population with basophilic stippling, Cabot rings and hypersegmented neutrophils. This combination points strongly toward ineffective erythropoiesis and disturbed erythroid maturation—hypersegmented neutrophils and macrocytic elements suggest severe cobalamin/folate deficiency, whereas basophilic stippling and Cabot rings reflect disrupted erythropoiesis and may be seen in marrow stress or toxic/metabolic conditions.

Nutritional studies confirmed severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency, which plausibly explains many of the haematologic abnormalities and the atrophic glossitis. Iron studies, however, showed elevated serum iron and ferritin—an “iron‑loaded” pattern paradoxical in anaemia—consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis and impaired iron utilization rather than simple iron excess from intake. This biochemical picture is commonly encountered when marrow erythroid precursors fail to mature and release iron for new haemoglobin synthesis.

Bone marrow examination clarified the marrow substrate: trilineage haematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia and increased iron stores, but no dysplasia, infiltrative disease or malignancy. Normal cytogenetics reduced the likelihood of a primary clonal haematologic malignancy. The erythroid hyperplasia in the marrow, paired with a low reticulocyte count and peripheral features of abnormal erythropoiesis, supports a central (marrow‑based) ineffective erythropoiesis as the dominant mechanism for the anaemia rather than pure peripheral destruction.

A direct antiglobulin test (DAT) was positive for IgG (2+), which raised the possibility of immune‑mediated haemolysis. However, in this patient the overall context—low reticulocyte response, marrow erythroid hyperplasia with ineffective maturation and lack of other haemolytic features—favoured ineffective erythropoiesis over classic autoimmune haemolysis as the principal driver of anaemia. In practice this distinction matters because management differs: autoimmune haemolysis often responds to immunosuppression, whereas ineffective erythropoiesis requires addressing the underlying marrow dysfunction and any reversible causes (eg, nutrient repletion).

Beyond the haematologic system, imaging demonstrated multisystem involvement. Abdominal ultrasound and transient elastography revealed hepatosplenomegaly, steatosis and a liver stiffness (18.1 kPa) consistent with cirrhosis. The degree of hepatic fibrosis was disproportionate for a young person without metabolic syndrome or viral/autoimmune causes on testing, making a secondary or genetic cause more likely. Whole‑body PET‑CT confirmed diffuse ground‑glass opacities with subpleural fibrotic and atelectatic changes in both lung fields, compatible with interstitial lung disease or early pulmonary fibrosis. These pulmonary imaging findings, together with cirrhosis and marrow dysfunction, signalled a multisystem fibrotic process rather than isolated organ disease.

Additional problems identified on baseline assessment included marked osteoporosis on DXA with secondary hyperparathyroidism and vitamin D deficiency—explaining her bone pain and fractures—and electrolyte disturbances (hypocalcaemia, hypokalaemia). Neurophysiologic testing showed an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy with chronic neurogenic changes, accounting for some of her weakness and functional decline. These non‑haematologic features further reinforced the likelihood of a systemic disorder affecting tissues with high proliferative demand or long‑term cellular maintenance needs.

Synthesis of the initial problem list highlights five core issues that directed further workup: (1) pancytopenia driven by ineffective erythropoiesis with low reticulocyte response despite marrow erythroid hyperplasia, (2) severe vitamin B12/folate deficiency partially explaining haematologic and mucosal findings but insufficient to account for the total phenotype, (3) hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis with hepatosplenomegaly and steatosis unexplained by conventional causes, (4) interstitial/pulmonary fibrotic changes on imaging, and (5) extra‑haematologic features of premature ageing (premature greying, skin hyperpigmentation), neuropathy and skeletal fragility. Together, these findings prompted consideration of a unifying diagnosis linking marrow failure, premature ageing and multiorgan fibrosis—most notably a telomere biology disorder—rather than separate, unrelated conditions. Early recognition of this pattern prioritized genetic evaluation and close multidisciplinary surveillance over isolated, short‑term fixes.

## Focused Background: Telomere Biology Disorders and PARN

Telomeres are repetitive DNA–protein structures that cap chromosome ends and preserve genome stability by preventing end-to-end fusions and inappropriate DNA repair. With each cell division telomeres shorten; when they reach a critically short length, cells enter replicative senescence or apoptosis. Telomerase, a ribonucleoprotein complex composed principally of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC), counteracts this shortening by adding telomeric repeats. Tissues with high proliferative demand—haematopoietic stem cells, epithelial surfaces (including oral mucosa and skin), hair follicles, and hepatocytes—are therefore most sensitive to defects in telomere maintenance, explaining the organ predilection seen in telomere biology disorders (TBDs).

Clinically, TBDs span a spectrum. The classic pediatric phenotype, dyskeratosis congenita (DC), is defined by the mucocutaneous triad (nail dystrophy, oral leukoplakia, reticular pigmentation) together with bone marrow failure. However, pathogenic variants in telomere-related genes may present variably: adult-onset isolated organ fibrosis (for example idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, IPF), predominant bone marrow failure, or mixed multisystem disease. Penetrance is often incomplete and age-dependent, so classical features may be absent and presentation can be subtle or restricted to one organ in adulthood. This variable expressivity means a young adult with marrow failure, unexplained hepatic fibrosis or early pulmonary fibrosis should raise suspicion for an underlying TBD even without the full DC phenotype.

PARN (poly(A)-specific ribonuclease) encodes a nuclear exoribonuclease involved in deadenylation—the shortening and removal of poly(A) tails on RNAs—which is a key step in RNA maturation and stability control. Of particular relevance to telomere biology, PARN participates in the 3′-end maturation of TERC by removing aberrant oligo(A) tails that would otherwise target TERC for degradation. Proper maturation and stability of TERC is essential for assembling functional telomerase. Loss or reduction of PARN function therefore leads to decreased mature TERC levels, impaired telomerase activity and accelerated telomere shortening.

Genotype–phenotype correlations in PARN-related disease reflect the degree of functional loss. Biallelic (recessive) loss-of-function PARN variants have been reported in severe, early-onset TBDs such as Hoyeraal–Hreidarsson syndrome and autosomal recessive DC, which often display the full mucocutaneous triad and profound marrow failure. Monoallelic (heterozygous) PARN variants are more commonly associated with adult-onset phenotypes—most notably pulmonary fibrosis and variable bone marrow dysfunction (a pattern sometimes described as PFBMFT4). The biology is consistent with haploinsufficiency: when one allele is defective, reduced PARN dosage may be sufficient over time to lower TERC and telomerase activity below a threshold in certain tissues, producing disease later in life or in a tissue-limited fashion.

Mechanistically, telomere shortening from impaired telomerase preferentially affects stem and progenitor compartments. In the bone marrow, exhausted haematopoietic stem cells lose proliferative reserve, producing progressive cytopenias and predisposition to marrow failure. In the lung, alveolar epithelial progenitors with critically short telomeres have limited capacity for repair after injury, promoting aberrant wound healing and fibrosis (IPF). In the liver, stressed hepatocytes and progenitor cells similarly demonstrate impaired regeneration, predisposing to hepatic fibrosis and eventual cirrhosis. These tissue effects explain how a single underlying telomere maintenance defect—such as PARN dysfunction—can manifest as concurrent marrow cytopenias, pulmonary fibrosis and hepatic fibrosis, as observed in the presented patient.

Clinical expression is further modified by non‑genetic factors. Environmental exposures (smoking, infections), inflammation, metabolic stress and even nutritional deficiencies can increase proliferative demand or DNA damage and thereby accelerate telomere attrition, unmasking or worsening disease in genetically susceptible individuals. In this case, severe vitamin B12 deficiency and systemic inflammation likely compounded ineffective haematopoiesis, precipitating overt cytopenias in a patient already predisposed by a monoallelic PARN variant. Additionally, phenomena such as somatic genetic rescue or clonal evolution can alter tissue‑specific manifestations and contribute to incomplete penetrance, leading to marked heterogeneity even among carriers of the same variant.

Understanding this focused pathophysiology clarifies why a young adult with disproportionate multisystem fibrosis and marrow dysfunction but without classic mucocutaneous findings may nonetheless harbor a telomere biology disorder driven by disrupted PARN function. The finding of a monoallelic PARN variant in such a clinical context therefore provides a plausible mechanistic link and warrants longitudinal surveillance for progressive marrow, lung or liver involvement.

## Diagnostic Reasoning: From Ineffective Erythropoiesis to a Telomere Biology Disorder

This patient’s evaluation illustrates stepwise clinical reasoning from a common haematologic presentation—pancytopenia with a low reticulocyte count—toward a rare, unifying genetic diagnosis. The initial laboratory pattern required distinguishing between peripheral destruction of red cells and inadequate production. A low reticulocyte percentage (0.39%) indicates the marrow is not mounting an appropriate regenerative response. Bone marrow examination in this case showed trilineage haematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia and increased iron stores but no dysplasia or infiltrate, a constellation most consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis: the marrow is producing erythroid precursors but they fail to mature or survive to release reticulocytes.

Several peripheral smear and serum findings supported ineffective erythropoiesis rather than pure hemolysis. Dimorphic erythrocytes, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings and hypersegmented neutrophils point toward disordered erythropoiesis and megaloblastic changes. Markers of haemolysis (elevated LDH, indirect bilirubin) were present, and the direct antiglobulin test (DAT) was positive for IgG; however, the absent reticulocytosis and the marrow pattern argue that peripheral immune destruction was not the dominant mechanism. A positive DAT can be incidental or reflect low-grade autoimmune phenomena, but it must be interpreted alongside reticulocytes and marrow findings—here, ineffective erythropoiesis better explains the cytopenias.

Because ineffective erythropoiesis is frequently reversible if due to nutrient deficiency, the next logical step was to identify and correct reversible causes. This patient had severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency—well-known causes of megaloblastic ineffective erythropoiesis and hypersegmented neutrophils—and received standard parenteral B12 repletion. Investigation for malabsorption identified reduced parietal cells on duodenal biopsy and chronic gastritis, a plausible explanation for impaired intrinsic factor production and B12 malabsorption. Nevertheless, the clinical severity and multisystem involvement (hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying, osteoporosis, neuropathy) appeared out of proportion to nutritional deficiency alone, prompting further evaluation for an underlying driver.

Parallel to addressing reversible causes, the team pursued a systematic exclusion of alternate hepatic and pulmonary aetiologies. Hepatic imaging and elastography documented cirrhosis; autoimmune serologies and viral hepatitis panels were negative, and PET–CT did not reveal occult malignancy or granulomatous disease. Pulmonary imaging showed diffuse ground-glass and subpleural fibrotic changes consistent with interstitial fibrosis rather than infection or neoplasm. Excluding common infectious, autoimmune and neoplastic causes is crucial before invoking a unifying inherited syndrome, and in this case those alternative explanations were not supported.

Aggregating clinical clues clarified that this was not purely a haematologic or nutritional disorder. Premature greying of hair, osteoporosis with fractures at a young age, sensorimotor axonal neuropathy, hepatosplenomegaly with established hepatic fibrosis, and early-onset pulmonary fibrosis form a pattern of premature ageing with multisystem fibrotic involvement. When these features coexist with persistent cytopenias, especially in a young adult, one must consider telomere biology disorders (TBDs) even if the classical mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita (oral leukoplakia, nail changes, reticulate pigmentation) is absent.

Deciding when to suspect a TBD rests on recognizing disproportionate, multisystem manifestations of reduced replicative reserve—bone marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, early osteoporosis, and premature hair greying—in the absence of alternative explanations. Incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity are typical for TBDs, so lack of family history or absence of mucocutaneous findings does not exclude the diagnosis. In this patient the persistence of multisystem disease despite correction of B12/folate strengthened the rationale for genetic testing.

Clinical exome sequencing was therefore performed and identified a monoallelic PARN missense variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) within the conserved R3H domain. Choosing clinical exome sequencing is appropriate when the differential is broad and includes multiple potential monogenic causes; for suspected TBDs, targeted panels including TERT, TERC, PARN, RTEL1 and others, or broader exome testing, are commonly used. Detection of a rare variant in a telomere-related gene provided a plausible mechanistic link to the phenotype, given PARN’s role in TERC maturation and telomere maintenance.

Interpreting the variant used ACMG/AMP criteria supplemented by clinical correlation. The variant met PM1 because it affects a critical, well-conserved functional domain (R3H) where pathogenic variants have been reported. PM2 was met by its absence or extreme rarity in population databases. PP2 applies because PARN is intolerant of benign missense variation, and PP3 is supported by multiple in silico tools predicting deleterious impact. Despite these supportive lines of evidence, the variant remained classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS) because definitive functional or segregation data were lacking. This illustrates an important principle: a VUS can be clinically informative when the genotype plausibly explains the phenotype, but it does not constitute a definitive pathogenic call without additional evidence.

When a TBD is suspected but genetic results are inconclusive, ancillary testing can strengthen the diagnostic assessment. Flow-FISH measurement of telomere length in peripheral blood leukocytes is a validated adjunct: markedly shortened telomeres for age support a TBD diagnosis and can help interpret VUS findings. Family testing and segregation analysis—testing parents and available relatives—can clarify whether the variant is de novo or tracks with disease, which is especially helpful for variants in genes with dominant or haploinsufficient effects. Functional assays of telomerase activity or targeted assays for PARN function, if available, can further aid interpretation.

Synthesizing the data leads to a unifying hypothesis for this case: a monoallelic PARN variant reduced telomere maintenance capacity, predisposing replicative tissues (bone marrow, liver, lung, bone) to early senescence and fibrosis. Severe B12 deficiency acted as a compounding stressor on an already vulnerable haematopoietic system, producing the observed ineffective erythropoiesis and cytopenias. The absence of florid mucocutaneous signs reflects the variable expressivity of TBDs; organ-specific manifestations such as pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis can predominate in some patients with monoallelic telomere gene variants.

Finally, there are important pitfalls and practical pearls. Telomere disorders show incomplete penetrance and variable age of onset; therefore, a negative family history does not exclude a genetic telomereopathy. A VUS should not be overinterpreted as causal, but clinical–genetic concordance can justify closer surveillance and targeted family counselling. Ordering telomere length testing and segregation studies can convert a probabilistic diagnosis into a more definitive one. Clinicians should also recognize that treatable contributors (nutrient deficiencies, infections, toxins) must be corrected, because they may unmask or exacerbate a genetic marrow vulnerability. In summary, careful integration of haematologic data, exclusion of reversible and alternative causes, aggregation of systemic clues, and targeted genetic and functional testing allowed clinicians to move from a working diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis to a likely telomere biology disorder in this patient.

## Management Strategy and Longitudinal Care in Suspected PARN-Related TBD

The management of a patient with a suspected PARN-associated telomere biology disorder (TBD) combines immediate correction of reversible contributors to cytopenia and organ dysfunction, organ-specific supportive care, and long-term surveillance and planning for possible progression to bone marrow failure or end‑stage organ disease. In this case the first priorities were to correct nutritional deficiencies and stabilize cytopenias, while initiating surveillance for evolving hematologic, hepatic and pulmonary complications and arranging genetic counselling and specialist referral.

Immediate measures
The patient required prompt parenteral vitamin B12 because of severe deficiency with ineffective erythropoiesis and low reticulocyte response. Standard acute replacement is intramuscular or intravenous cyanocobalamin 1,000 µg daily for 5–7 days (or until clinical/hematologic improvement), then weekly for 4–6 weeks and thereafter monthly maintenance injections; this regimen was used in the case. Concurrent folic acid replacement (eg, 1 mg daily) is appropriate when folate deficiency is present or to support erythropoiesis, and vitamin D repletion (eg, high‑dose weekly cholecalciferol then maintenance) with adequate elemental calcium should be started for documented deficiency and osteoporosis. Electrolyte abnormalities (hypocalcaemia, hypokalaemia) are corrected per standard protocols. Transfusion support is given for symptomatic anemia or hemodynamic instability; use the minimum number of packed red cell transfusions required, and monitor for iron overload if repeated transfusions become necessary.

Rehabilitation and symptom control
Functional decline, fractures and neuropathy require multidisciplinary rehabilitation. Physiotherapy should focus on strength, balance and gait training to reduce falls and preserve independence. Neuropathic pain can be managed with guideline‑based agents (eg, duloxetine, gabapentin) taking renal function and drug interactions into account. For bone health, optimize calcium and vitamin D, assess secondary causes (eg, hyperparathyroidism as in this case) and consider antiresorptive therapy. Bisphosphonate candidacy should be individualized: treat established osteoporosis or fragility fractures after evaluating renal function and life expectancy; ensure adequate calcium/vitamin D and discuss potential risks. Fall prevention strategies and bone-protection measures are essential given early osteoporosis in TBDs.

Hepatic care
Because hepatic fibrosis and cirrhosis were present, routine cirrhosis surveillance and management applies. Enroll the patient in hepatology follow-up with periodic assessment for portal hypertension (clinical exam, platelet count trends, transient elastography as indicated) and hepatocellular carcinoma surveillance (abdominal ultrasound ± alpha‑fetoprotein every 6 months in cirrhosis). Counsel strict alcohol avoidance and treat metabolic contributors: optimise weight, glycaemic control and lipid profile. Vaccinate if not immune against hepatitis A and B and ensure other routine immunizations are up to date. Workup for other causes of liver disease was appropriately completed in the case; ongoing monitoring for progressive liver dysfunction is required.

Pulmonary care
Baseline pulmonary assessment should include high‑resolution CT (HRCT) and pulmonary function tests (PFTs) with diffusion capacity (DLCO) to quantify extent of interstitial lung disease. In TBD patients, serial PFTs and DLCO every 3–6 months initially (then spacing according to stability) are reasonable to detect progression. Repeat HRCT is indicated for clinical or functional decline. For progressive idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) or fibrotic ILD, antifibrotic therapy (nintedanib or pirfenidone) is considered; these agents slow functional decline in fibrotic lung disease and can be used in telomere‑related fibrosis, but risks and benefits must be individualized and coordinated with pulmonology. Infection prevention includes annual influenza vaccination and pneumococcal vaccination as per guidelines; consider prophylactic measures and low threshold for treating respiratory infections. Smoking cessation is mandatory; counsel regarding occupational exposures and travel risks.

Hematology follow‑up
Even when an initial marrow does not show classical aplasia, patients with TBD require close hematologic surveillance because marrow failure can evolve. Regular complete blood counts with reticulocyte counts at intervals of approximately every 3 months (sooner if changes occur) help detect declining hematopoiesis. Worsening cytopenias, rising transfusion requirements, new or progressive neutropenia, or morphologic changes on peripheral smear are triggers for repeating bone marrow evaluation, including morphology, cytogenetics and flow cytometry; consider extended marrow testing (eg, telomere length measurement in leukocyte subsets) if available. Manage infections aggressively and avoid empiric myelosuppressive medications. Erythropoiesis‑stimulating agents or thrombopoietin mimetics are not routinely used in TBD without a clear indication and should be considered cautiously with hematology input.

Transplant considerations
Early engagement of transplant teams experienced in TBD is essential. Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) is the only curative option for progressive marrow failure but carries increased toxicity in TBD patients due to short telomeres; therefore, telomere‑aware approaches are required. Reduced‑intensity conditioning regimens are preferred to limit organ toxicity, and careful selection of immunosuppression and dosing is critical. Donor selection must exclude related donors who may harbour the same germline mutation or short telomeres; ideally use an HLA‑matched unrelated donor or evaluate donors with telomere length/genetic testing when feasible. Timing of HSCT is individualized—consider referral when there are progressive, transfusion‑dependent cytopenias or clonal evolution—balancing decline rate against transplant risks. For end-stage lung or liver disease, transplant candidacy should be discussed early with respiratory and hepatology/transplant teams; multi‑organ involvement and prior HSCT status influence candidacy and outcomes, so coordination among specialties is crucial.

Disease‑modifying options under study
Androgens such as danazol and nandrolone have been reported to increase telomere length and improve blood counts in small studies and case series; danazol may induce hematologic responses in some patients with telomeropathies. However, benefits are variable and risks include hepatic toxicity, lipid abnormalities and virilisation; caution is needed, particularly in patients with hepatic fibrosis as in this case. Enrollment in clinical trials or referral to centres conducting trials of telomere‑targeted therapies (androgens, telomerase modulators, gene therapy) should be offered when available, after a careful risk–benefit discussion.

Genetic counselling
Genetic counselling is essential both at diagnosis and longitudinally. Explain the concept of a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS): it may be reclassified over time as more data accumulate, and family testing can help with interpretation. Discuss inheritance patterns (PARN can be associated with autosomal dominant or recessive presentations depending on variant type and penetrance), the possibilities of de novo mutation, and implications for relatives. Offer cascade testing to first‑degree relatives and consider telomere length testing where available. Reproductive options (preimplantation genetic diagnosis, prenatal testing) should be discussed with a reproductive genetics specialist when a pathogenic variant is confirmed. Provide psychosocial support, as uncertainty about prognosis and familial risk is common.

Preventive care and lifestyle measures
Advise avoidance of known telomere‑accelerating or myelotoxic exposures—chemotherapy with alkylating agents, radiotherapy and certain immunosuppressants—unless absolutely required and after specialist discussion. Vaccinate according to guidelines, avoid live vaccines in the setting of significant immunosuppression, and maintain up‑to‑date cancer screening appropriate for age and organ disease (including skin cancer surveillance given cutaneous manifestations of TBDs). Counsel on smoking cessation, sun protection, prudent alcohol intake (abstinence if hepatic fibrosis), and optimization of metabolic risk factors. Provide clear instructions about when to seek care for fever, bleeding or new respiratory symptoms.

Coordination of care and follow‑up plan
A practical longitudinal plan for this patient includes ongoing monthly B12 injections until stability, then maintenance; hematology follow‑up with CBC and reticulocyte count every 3 months; hepatology surveillance every 3–6 months with imaging and labs dictated by liver stiffness and portal hypertension features; pulmonology follow‑up with PFTs every 3–6 months and HRCT as indicated; bone health monitoring and fracture prevention interventions; and periodic reassessment by genetics for VUS reclassification and family cascade testing. Early referral to specialised centres for consideration of HSCT or transplant evaluation and for clinical trial access is recommended. Documentation of the suspected TBD and counselling about avoidance of myelotoxic exposures should be communicated to all treating teams.

In summary, care for a suspected PARN‑related TBD is multidisciplinary, proactive and anticipatory: correct reversible contributors, provide organ‑specific supportive care, closely monitor for progression, and plan transplant or trial options with teams experienced in telomere disorders while offering ongoing genetic counselling and preventive guidance.

## Outcomes, Prognosis, and Key Clinical Lessons

Case outcome
The patient experienced early symptomatic improvement after correction of severe nutritional deficiencies and provision of supportive care. Hemoglobin and other blood counts were stable at the 2‑week follow‑up, fatigue improved, and no new clinical problems were recorded. Because the underlying concern is a telomere biology disorder (TBD) associated with a monoallelic PARN variant, definitive cure was not offered; instead she was placed on longitudinal surveillance for hepatic, pulmonary and haematologic progression and received genetic counselling for herself and her family.

Prognostic considerations in PARN‑related TBD
Prognosis in TBDs is driven by the organs involved, the degree of telomere shortening, the specific gene/variant and exposure to additional stresses (nutritional deficiency, cytotoxic drugs, infections). In PARN‑associated disease the dominant risks are progressive pulmonary fibrosis and, in some patients, progressive bone marrow failure and hepatic fibrosis. Even when cytopenias initially respond to correction of reversible causes (as here with B12 replacement), marrow reserve may be limited; future evolution to chronic marrow failure remains possible and should be anticipated. Pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis—once established—tend to be progressive and are major determinants of morbidity and mortality. Younger age at presentation and multi‑organ involvement generally predict a higher likelihood of progression. Coexisting insults (e.g., persistent nutritional deficits, hepatotoxic or myelotoxic exposures, smoking) accelerate decompensation and should be minimized.

Red flags that should prompt evaluation for a TBD in adults
This case highlights clinical signals that should lower the threshold to evaluate for an inherited telomere disorder:
- Unexplained cytopenias, particularly pancytopenia with a low reticulocyte response and marrow findings consistent with ineffective haematopoiesis rather than isolated peripheral destruction.
- Features of premature ageing (premature greying, early osteoporosis, skin or mucosal atrophy) in a young adult.
- Early‑onset fibrotic disease of organs commonly affected in TBDs—idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis or unexplained hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis—especially when occurring together.
- Multisystem involvement without a unifying acquired explanation, or disproportionate organ dysfunction relative to identified reversible causes (for example, severe B12 deficiency that does not fully explain cytopenia plus organ fibrosis).
- Family history of early pulmonary, hepatic, or haematologic failure, though absence of family history does not exclude de novo mutations or reduced penetrance.

Diagnostic best practices and interpretation of genetic findings
When a TBD is suspected, an integrated diagnostic approach is optimal:
- Measure telomere length (for example by flow‑FISH) when available: markedly short telomeres for age support a TBD diagnosis and strengthen interpretation of sequence variants.
- Use targeted gene panels or exome sequencing focused on telomere‑related genes, with careful annotation of variants.
- Interpret variants (particularly variants of uncertain significance, VUS) in clinical context: a VUS in a gene with strong biological plausibility (PARN here), consistent phenotype, short telomeres and supportive in silico evidence increases the likelihood that the variant is disease‑relevant. Conversely, absence of telomere shortening weakens that inference.
- Re‑assess VUS over time: as population and functional data accumulate, variant classification can change; periodic re‑review and family segregation testing can clarify pathogenicity.
- When feasible, perform family studies (segregation) and functional assays (TERC maturation, PARN activity, or telomerase function) in research or reference labs to help resolve uncertain variants.

Management pearls specific to TBDs and this case
- Address reversible contributors first: correcting nutritional deficiencies, treating infections and avoiding myelotoxic medications can yield clinical benefit and unmask or delay underlying disease progression, as seen here.
- Anticipate and plan for progressive organ dysfunction: enlist a multidisciplinary team (haematology, pulmonology, hepatology, genetics, transplant services, and rehabilitation) early for surveillance and timely intervention.
- When hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) is considered for progressive marrow failure, use protocols tailored for TBD patients (reduced‑intensity conditioning, careful donor selection avoiding related donors with occult TBD, and telomere‑aware risk counselling) because standard myeloablative regimens carry high toxicity in these patients.
- For end‑organ failure (advanced IPF or cirrhosis), referral to lung or liver transplant programs with experience in TBDs is appropriate; candidacy assessment should consider extra‑pulmonary/extra‑hepatic manifestations and overall frailty.
- Risk‑mitigation counseling: avoid tobacco and unnecessary ionizing radiation, minimize hepatotoxic and myelotoxic exposures, and ensure vaccination and prompt treatment of infections.
- Document and communicate the suspected genetic diagnosis to all treating teams so that future therapies (for example chemotherapy or HSCT) are planned with awareness of telomere vulnerability.

Knowledge gaps and areas for future attention
Important uncertainties remain relevant to clinical decision‑making in monoallelic PARN carriers:
- The full spectrum and penetrance of organ involvement with monoallelic PARN variants—particularly the true prevalence and natural history of liver disease—are incompletely defined.
- There are limited data guiding the optimal timing and selection of disease‑modifying therapies (androgens, investigational telomerase‑targeting approaches) and their long‑term benefits and risks in PARN‑related TBD.
- Predictive biomarkers beyond telomere length (for progression risk stratification) are lacking.
- Functional assays that reliably quantify the impact of specific missense PARN variants on telomere biology are not widely available in clinical practice.

Takeaway clinical lessons
In young adults with unexplained multisystem disease—especially the combination of cytopenia with low reticulocyte count, premature ageing signs, and early pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis—think beyond common acquired causes and evaluate for a telomere biology disorder. Correct reversible contributors promptly, but institute long‑term surveillance and multidisciplinary planning because apparent short‑term improvement may mask an underlying progressive genetic predisposition. Genetic findings (including VUS) must be integrated with telomere length testing, clinical phenotype and, where possible, family studies; management decisions (including transplantation) should reflect the unique risks of TBDs.
