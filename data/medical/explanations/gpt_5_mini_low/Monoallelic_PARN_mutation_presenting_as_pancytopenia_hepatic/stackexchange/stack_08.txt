Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Clinical Pearls] What are key counseling and surveillance recommendations for patients and first-degree relatives when a telomere biology disorder is suspected due to a monoallelic PARN variant?

Clinical bottom line
- When a telomere biology disorder (TBD) is suspected in a patient with a monoallelic PARN variant (as in this case with pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis), counseling should emphasize (1) confirmatory testing (telomere length and focused genetic work‑up), (2) cascade testing and reproductive counselling for first‑degree relatives, and (3) targeted organ surveillance and exposure avoidance because of variable penetrance and progressive, multi‑organ risk.

Why this matters (pathophysiology → clinical risk)
- PARN is required for normal TERC maturation; heterozygous (monoallelic) PARN variants can cause haploinsufficiency with accelerated telomere shortening. Short telomeres selectively impair highly proliferative tissues (bone marrow, lung epithelium, liver), producing variable phenotypes (BM failure, IPF, hepatic fibrosis, premature greying, cancer risk). The case illustrates this pleiotropy despite only a monoallelic variant and absence of classic mucocutaneous signs.

Practical recommendations

1) Confirm the diagnosis
- Measure telomere length (flow‑FISH on leukocyte subsets is the diagnostic standard in adults). Markedly short telomeres relative to age supports a TBD and increases the likelihood that the PARN variant is pathogenic.
- Reassess the variant: submit for expert genetics review, consider segregation testing in available relatives, and, if available, functional assays or review of literature/ClinVar to reclassify the VUS.

2) Genetic counselling and testing of first‑degree relatives
- Counsel that monoallelic PARN variants behave most often as autosomal dominant with reduced and age‑dependent penetrance; the empirical recurrence risk to first‑degree relatives is ≈50% if the variant is pathogenic, but expressivity is variable.
- Offer targeted cascade testing for the familial PARN variant and telomere length measurement to adult first‑degree relatives. Testing of asymptomatic children is generally deferred until after counselling and consideration of clinical utility; if performed, discuss the psychosocial and insurance implications and involve genetics.
- Provide reproductive options counselling (preimplantation genetic diagnosis, prenatal testing) if the variant is determined pathogenic and the family desires it.

3) Surveillance for the proband and mutation‑positive relatives
- Hematologic:
  - CBC with differential and reticulocyte count every 3–6 months (sooner if symptomatic). Low threshold to repeat and to refer to hematology.
  - Bone marrow evaluation if cytopenias progress or transform (morphology, cytogenetics/MDS panel).
  - When HSCT is contemplated, coordinate with a centre experienced in TBDs and use reduced‑intensity and organ‑sparing conditioning because standard myeloablative regimens carry excess toxicity.
- Pulmonary:
  - Baseline pulmonary function tests including DLCO and a high‑resolution chest CT (HRCT). Repeat PFTs and chest imaging at least annually or sooner for symptoms; refer early to pulmonary fibrosis specialists.
  - Strongly counsel smoking cessation and avoidance of environmental lung toxins; immunize per guidelines (influenza, pneumococcus).
- Hepatic:
  - Baseline liver biochemical tests, abdominal imaging (ultrasound/transient elastography if available) and hepatology referral for evidence of fibrosis/cirrhosis. Repeat LFTs and elastography at intervals guided by severity (commonly every 6–12 months).
  - Screen and manage complications of portal hypertension/cirrhosis as indicated.
- Cancer surveillance:
  - Maintain routine age‑appropriate cancer screening. Maintain increased vigilance for hematologic malignancies (MDS/AML): promptly investigate new cytopenias or blasts.
  - Low threshold for evaluation of otherwise unexplained lesions (skin, oral leukoplakia, head/neck).
- Bone health and endocrine:
  - Monitor bone density and treat osteoporosis (case had severe osteoporosis); correct vitamin deficiencies and monitor electrolytes.
- Other systems:
  - Clinical exam for mucocutaneous signs; neuro and GI follow‑up as clinically indicated.

4) Management counselling and risk mitigation
- Avoid exposures and therapies that accelerate telomere attrition or cause disproportionate toxicity:
  - Tobacco cessation and avoidance of excessive alcohol.
  - Use caution with alkylating chemotherapy and radiation; discuss alternatives and dose reduction in TBD patients.
  - For cytopenias, favour supportive care and refer early for HSCT planning if progressive marrow failure; when HSCT is required, specialized transplant protocols (reduced‑intensity, organ‑protective) are recommended.
- Discuss potential medical therapies (androgens have shown telomere‑lengthening in small studies) only as experimental/limited evidence; manage within specialty centres.
- Coordinate multidisciplinary care (hematology, pulmonology, hepatology, genetics, transplant teams).

5) Practical follow‑up plan for this patient (case‑based)
- In this patient—already receiving genetic counselling and surveillance—order flow‑FISH to document leukocyte telomere length and pursue familial cascade testing (parents/siblings) if she consents and relatives agree.
- Continue CBC every 3 months, baseline PFT+DLCO and HRCT with yearly reassessment, and hepatology follow‑up with elastography every 6–12 months. Maintain aggressive vitamin repletion and bone protection already started.
- Revisit variant classification as new data appear; if variant is reclassified as pathogenic, ensure relatives are re‑contacted and reproductive counselling updated.

Takeaway
- A monoallelic PARN variant with compatible clinical features should trigger telomere length testing, specialist genetic counselling, and cascade testing of first‑degree relatives. Surveillance must be proactive and multidisciplinary because organ involvement is protean, age‑dependent and may progress despite initial stability—precisely the course illustrated by this young patient.
