Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Morning Report] When clinical exome sequencing identifies a monoallelic PARN missense variant classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance, what clinical–genetic correlation steps can strengthen confidence that it is disease-relevant?

Short answer
When clinical exome returns a monoallelic PARN missense variant labelled VUS, you strengthen confidence that it is disease‑relevant by integrating (1) detailed phenotype–genotype concordance, (2) objective telomere biology testing, (3) familial and segregation data, (4) targeted functional and molecular assays, and (5) formal reclassification using ACMG criteria plus literature/databank correlation. In this case each of those steps can move the c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg) variant from “uncertain” toward likely pathogenic if they support telomere dysfunction.

What to do and why — practical, case‑grounded steps

1) Rigorously compare the phenotype to known PARN/TBD spectrum
- Why: PARN monoallelic variants classically present with adult‑onset idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) and sometimes bone‑marrow failure or hepatic fibrosis; variable penetrance is common.
- Apply to this case: The patient’s young adult onset of pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and IPF—plus premature greying, osteoporosis and neuropathy—fits the expanding PARN/TBD phenotype and therefore increases prior probability that a rare PARN variant is relevant.

2) Measure telomere length (gold‑standard: flow‑FISH)
- Why: Short leukocyte telomere length is the most direct biological readout of a telomere biology disorder and is recommended when a TBD is suspected.
- Action: Obtain flow‑FISH on peripheral blood (and, if available, granulocytes and lymphocyte subsets). Marked telomere shortening (below the 1st–10th percentile for age) strongly supports pathogenicity.
- Case relevance: The authors emphasize telomere testing as part of the “gold standard”; a short telomere result here would be powerful evidence linking the PARN VUS to disease.

3) Family studies and segregation analysis
- Why: Co‑segregation with disease in affected relatives or de novo occurrence in the proband provides strong evidence. Lack of segregation in healthy older relatives argues against pathogenicity.
- Action: Test first‑degree relatives for the PARN variant and, if available, compare their telomere lengths and clinical findings. Document inheritance (de novo vs inherited).
- Case relevance: The report notes no clear family history; demonstrating de novo status or cosegregation with fibrosis/marrow disease in relatives would materially strengthen pathogenic assignment.

4) Targeted functional assays
- Why: PARN’s role in TERC maturation and RNA deadenylation is mechanistic; functional impairment of PARN or of TERC processing is direct evidence.
- Practical assays:
  - Measure TERC levels and 3′ end maturation in patient cells (RT‑qPCR / northern blot).
  - Assess telomerase activity (TRAP assay) in patient lymphocytes.
  - Complementation assays: express wild‑type PARN in patient cells to see if TERC/telomerase defects are rescued.
  - If available, in vitro biochemical assays of the mutant protein (deadenylase activity) or cell models with the specific missense change.
- Case relevance: The variant lies in the conserved R3H domain (residues 178–245) and in silico tools predict damage—functional impairment of PARN/TERC would upgrade evidence from computational to biological.

5) Population, computational and domain evidence synthesized per ACMG
- Why: Systematic application of ACMG criteria clarifies classification and identifies which lines of evidence are lacking.
- Apply to this variant: The case already documents PM1 (critical domain), PM2 (absent/rare in population databases), PP3 (multiple damaging predictions), PP2 (gene constraint). Adding functional evidence (PS3), cosegregation (PP1), or short telomeres (supporting evidence) can shift the classification.
- Action: Aggregate published PARN variants in ClinVar/literature, submit new data (functional/telomere/family) to ClinVar to update interpretation.

6) Exclude alternate explanations and assess modifiers
- Why: Nutritional deficiencies and autoimmune findings can confound marrow phenotype; proving telomere shortness and PARN dysfunction helps distinguish primary TBD from secondary marrow suppression.
- Case relevance: The patient had severe B12 deficiency explaining some features; demonstration of short telomeres or defective PARN function argues that the PARN variant is an underlying susceptibility that explains multisystem fibrosis and marrow vulnerability.

7) Clinical management consequences and longitudinal correlation
- Why: Demonstrating disease relevance affects counseling, surveillance (pulmonary, hepatic, hematologic, malignancy) and timing/choice of therapies (HSCT donor selection, androgen trials).
- Action: If additional testing supports pathogenicity, institute TBD surveillance and involve genetic counseling; if uncertain, maintain close longitudinal follow‑up with repeat telomere and hematologic assessment, because progressive marrow failure or fibrosis over time would support causality.

Putting it together for this patient
- The c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg) variant already meets PM1/PM2/PP3/PP2 and lies in the R3H domain—a biologically plausible site. The next high‑yield steps are flow‑FISH telomere length measurement, family testing for segregation and targeted functional assays of PARN/TERC processing. Positive results (markedly short age‑adjusted telomeres, cosegregation or de novo status, and demonstrable functional impairment) would collectively allow reclassification toward likely pathogenic and justify TBD‑directed management and surveillance. Negative results (normal telomere length, lack of segregation, normal PARN function) would argue against causality and keep the variant as a VUS.

Final clinical pearl
Genetic variants rarely stand alone—objective measures of the disease mechanism (telomere length, TERC processing, telomerase activity), segregation data and careful phenotype matching are the decisive steps that convert a PARN VUS into a clinically actionable diagnosis.
