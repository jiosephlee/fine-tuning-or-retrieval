Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Management] Which medications, procedures, or environmental exposures warrant particular caution in patients with telomere biology disorders due to heightened risk of marrow failure or organ toxicity, and how would this influence management of interstitial lung disease or potential malignancy?

Direct answer
Patients with telomere biology disorders (TBDs) are unusually vulnerable to agents and exposures that injure highly proliferative tissues or that cause DNA damage; in practice you should exercise particular caution with (1) genotoxic chemotherapy and ionizing radiation, (2) certain marrow‑ and liver‑toxic medications, (3) pulmonary toxins and smoking, and (4) some therapies often used for marrow or immune disease. These risks change how you treat interstitial lung disease (ILD), manage suspected or established malignancy, and approach bone‑marrow failure or transplantation.

Why — brief pathophysiology
TBDs (eg, PARN, TERT, TERC mutations) accelerate telomere shortening and cause impaired replicative capacity of stem/progenitor cells. Bone marrow, lung alveolar epithelium and hepatocytes are particularly susceptible: sublethal insults that healthy patients tolerate can precipitate progressive marrow failure, organ fibrosis, or frank organ toxicity in TBD patients.

Specific agents, procedures and exposures to avoid or use with extreme caution
- Alkylating agents and topoisomerase II inhibitors (eg, cyclophosphamide, ifosfamide, melphalan, busulfan, etoposide): high risk of prolonged marrow aplasia and therapy‑related myeloid neoplasms.  
- Ionizing radiation (radiotherapy, high‑dose diagnostic radiation): causes marked marrow and lung injury; minimize exposure.  
- Bleomycin and other pulmonary‑fibrosing chemotherapies: high risk of worsening pulmonary fibrosis.  
- Antimetabolites and classic myelosuppressive chemotherapies (eg, high‑dose methotrexate, cytarabine): use lower doses or avoid if feasible. Methotrexate may worsen lung disease.  
- Bone‑marrow–toxic antibiotics/agents (eg, chloramphenicol, prolonged high‑dose linezolid) and certain antivirals/antifungals with marrow suppression—avoid or monitor closely.  
- Azathioprine, mycophenolate and prolonged cytotoxic immunosuppression: increased risk of cytopenias and secondary malignancy; weigh risks/benefits carefully.  
- Androgens (danazol, nandrolone): can improve blood counts in some TBD patients but carry significant hepatic toxicity—avoid or use cautiously in patients with hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis (as in this case).  
- Growth factor use (G‑CSF/GM‑CSF): may be used for neutropenia but consider theoretical risk of promoting clonal evolution; use judiciously with haematology expertise.  
- Amiodarone, nitrofurantoin and other drugs linked to pulmonary toxicity: avoid because they can worsen ILD.  
- Tobacco, occupational inhalants, and environmental pulmonary toxins: rigorously avoid—smoking accelerates pulmonary disease and risk of fibrosis.  
- High‑dose supplemental oxygen and hyperoxia during procedures should be optimized to need; avoid excessive exposures that might exacerbate lung injury.

How this influences management of ILD and suspected malignancy
- ILD management
  - Avoid lung‑toxic drugs (bleomycin, amiodarone, nitrofurantoin, methotrexate). In this patient with PARN‑associated ILD and hepatic fibrosis, prioritize non‑drug strategies (smoking cessation, immunizations, pulmonary rehabilitation) and early referral to an interstitial lung disease/telomere clinic.  
  - Consider antifibrotic therapy (eg, pirfenidone or nintedanib) in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis after multidisciplinary review; these drugs are not genotoxic but monitor hepatic function closely because this patient already has cirrhosis.  
  - Be cautious with invasive diagnostics (surgical lung biopsy) because of poor wound healing, bleeding risk from cytopenias and poor pulmonary reserve — favor high‑resolution CT and multidisciplinary diagnosis; perform transbronchial cryobiopsy only in centers experienced with TBDs and with hematology input.  
  - For progressive respiratory failure, early discussion of lung transplant referral is appropriate; however, recognize increased post‑transplant risks (bone marrow failure, hepatic disease, increased sensitivity to immunosuppression). Multidisciplinary planning with transplant, hepatology and hematology is required.

- Malignancy management
  - When cancer is suspected or confirmed, avoid standard full‑dose myeloablative regimens if alternative curative approaches exist. Prefer surgical resection where feasible and less‑genotoxic systemic regimens if oncologically reasonable.  
  - If cytotoxic chemotherapy or radiotherapy is required, involve a TBD‑experienced hematology/oncology team to consider dose reduction, alternative agents, or targeted/non‑genotoxic therapies (eg, targeted small molecules, immune therapies where appropriate) and to increase surveillance for prolonged cytopenias and organ toxicity.  
  - For hematologic malignancies requiring transplantation, use reduced‑intensity, non‑myeloablative conditioning regimens tailored to minimize alkylators and radiation; these patients benefit from experienced transplant centers familiar with TBD protocols and long‑term toxicity.  
  - Monitor closely for therapy‑related myeloid neoplasms; counsel patients about higher risk of secondary malignancy.

Practical steps in this patient
- Stop/avoid hepatotoxic agents and androgens given her cirrhosis; danazol would be inadvisable.  
- Avoid pulmonary‑toxic drugs (eg, amiodarone, bleomycin) and counsel on strict smoking avoidance and occupational exposure reduction.  
- If cytopenias worsen or marrow failure progresses, discuss HSCT early with a center that uses reduced‑intensity conditioning designed for TBDs. Pre‑transplant evaluation must include liver and lung assessment and family genetic testing.  
- If malignancy is suspected, coordinate management with centers experienced in TBD so anti‑cancer therapy is individualized and non‑genotoxic options prioritized when possible.  
- Use supportive measures preferentially: transfusions, cautious use of growth factors, vaccination, bone‑protective strategies, and surveillance for progressive organ fibrosis.

Bottom line
Think “telomere hypersensitivity”: avoid genotoxic, marrow‑ and lung‑injuring drugs and exposures; tailor cancer therapy and transplant conditioning to minimize DNA damage; avoid hepatic‑toxic agents (notably androgens) in patients with liver fibrosis. Early involvement of specialized multidisciplinary teams (hematology, pulmonology/ILD, hepatology, oncology, transplant genetics) is essential to balance disease control against the heightened risk of organ toxicity and marrow failure in TBDs.
