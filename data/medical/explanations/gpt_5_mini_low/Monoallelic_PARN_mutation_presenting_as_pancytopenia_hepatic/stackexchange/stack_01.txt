Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Clinical Pearls] In a young adult with pancytopenia, premature greying, hepatosplenomegaly, and interstitial lung disease, what clinical clues should prompt evaluation for a telomere biology disorder even in the absence of the classical dyskeratosis congenita mucocutaneous triad?

Bottom line: In any young adult with unexplained cytopenias plus premature ageing or unexplained organ fibrosis, think telomere biology disorder (TBD) even if the classic DC mucocutaneous triad is absent. Early recognition changes diagnostic testing, genetic counselling, surveillance, and transplant planning.

Clinical clues from this case that should prompt evaluation for a TBD
- Age and multisystem premature‑ageing phenotype: striking premature greying, osteoporosis and early functional decline in a patient in her late 20s.
- Pancytopenia or progressive cytopenias out of proportion to an apparent cause: persistent pancytopenia with a low reticulocyte count and marrow showing ineffective erythropoiesis despite correction of severe B12/folate deficiency.
- Unexplained hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis in a young adult: imaging and elastography showing cirrhosis without viral, autoimmune or metabolic explanation.
- Interstitial lung disease / idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis at a young age: CT with diffuse ground‑glass and subpleural fibrosis in a patient decades younger than usual for IPF.
- Hepatosplenomegaly and systemic inflammatory features without another unifying diagnosis.
- Multiorgan involvement that disproportionately exceeds what would be expected from an acquired single cause (for example, severe B12 deficiency produced some marrow findings but not the full constellation of pulmonary and hepatic fibrosis and premature ageing).
- Additional supportive findings: neuropathy, bone marrow FDG uptake without malignancy, normal cytogenetics (argues against myeloid malignancy), family history of early pulmonary or liver disease (may be absent due to de novo variants, variable penetrance, or monoallelic disease).
- Young age with organ fibrosis, bone marrow failure or other “age‑related” diseases—think telomere-driven premature cellular senescence.

Why these clues point to a TBD (pathophysiologic reasoning)
- Telomere maintenance defects preferentially affect highly proliferative tissues (bone marrow, mucosa, hair, lung alveolar epithelium, hepatocytes) leading to early tissue failure and fibrosis. Thus multisystem premature‑ageing features, marrow failure and early fibrotic disease are biologically concordant with telomere dysfunction.
- Variable penetrance and gene-specific phenotypes (monoallelic PARN, TERT, TERC, RTEL1, etc.) mean classical mucocutaneous signs of dyskeratosis congenita may be absent; organ‑predominant presentations (IPF, hepatic fibrosis, bone marrow failure) are well described.

What to do next (diagnostic standard and implications)
- Measure telomere length by a validated laboratory method (flow‑FISH is the diagnostic standard in suspected TBD; relative leukocyte telomere length can also be informative but less standardized).
- Perform germline genetic testing for telomere‑related genes (targeted NGS panel or exome with attention to TERT, TERC, PARN, RTEL1, DKC1 and others). In the case, clinical exome identified a monoallelic PARN variant supporting a TBD diagnosis despite absent mucocutaneous triad.
- If a pathogenic/likely pathogenic variant or very short telomeres are found, provide genetic counselling and cascade testing for at‑risk relatives.
- Management and surveillance implications: avoid cytotoxic exposures when possible, screen for progressive marrow failure, monitor pulmonary and hepatic disease, counsel on malignancy risk, and plan hematopoietic stem cell transplant with telomere‑aware protocols (reduced‑intensity conditioning) when indicated.

Clinical pearl: Do not rely on the mucocutaneous triad to exclude a TBD. In young adults, the combination of unexplained cytopenias (especially with low reticulocyte response), premature greying/other premature‑ageing signs, and early organ fibrosis (lung or liver) should trigger telomere length testing and germline sequencing—as illustrated by this patient with a monoallelic PARN variant whose phenotype united marrow failure, hepatic fibrosis and IPF despite absent classic DC mucocutaneous features.
