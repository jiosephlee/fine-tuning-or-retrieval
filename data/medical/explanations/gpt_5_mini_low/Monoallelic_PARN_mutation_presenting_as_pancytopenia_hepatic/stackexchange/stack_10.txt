Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a young adult with interstitial lung disease and unexplained cytopenias, what features in the history, family pedigree, and examination increase the pretest probability of a telomere biology disorder compared with autoimmune, infectious, or purely nutritional etiologies?

Short answer
Suspect a telomere biology disorder (TBD) when a young adult has unexplained interstitial lung disease and cytopenias plus multisystem findings of premature ageing or fibrosis, especially when common causes (autoimmune, infectious, nutritional) have been excluded and the hematologic picture is disproportionate to treatable deficiencies. Key features that raise the pretest probability are a specific pattern of organ involvement, early-onset “ageing” stigmata on exam, a suggestive family pedigree (when present), and laboratory/clinical discordance that cannot be explained by acquired disease.

Why — the clinical reasoning
TBDs target tissues with high proliferative demand (bone marrow, lung epithelium, liver, mucosa, hair, bone), so pathogenic germline telomere defects classically produce a constellation of marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis and premature-ageing signs. In contrast, autoimmune, infectious or nutritional causes usually have a more limited distribution, clearer serologic/infectious markers, or correctable hematologic responses once the deficiency/infection is treated.

Findings in history that increase pretest probability
- Young age at onset of fibrotic disease: pulmonary fibrosis or cirrhosis presenting well before the typical age for idiopathic disease (eg, 20s–40s). In the case, progressive dyspnea and radiologic pulmonary fibrosis in a woman in her late 20s is highly atypical and therefore a red flag.  
- Multisystem fibrosis or failure: concurrent or sequential involvement of bone marrow (pancytopenia), lung (ILD/IPF), liver (fibrosis/cirrhosis), and non‑haematologic features (osteoporosis, early greying/alopecia, mucosal atrophy). The case showed pancytopenia, cirrhosis and pulmonary fibrosis together — a pattern characteristic of TBD.  
- Longstanding, insidious course with progressive functional decline disproportionate to single diagnosed cause (eg, cytopenia persisting despite correction of severe B12 deficiency). In the case, correction of nutritional deficiencies did not fully explain persistent multisystem disease.  
- Early-onset complications of replicative failure: premature greying, early osteoporosis with fractures, avascular necrosis or early menopause (if present) — these suggest premature cellular senescence. The case’s premature greying, osteoporosis and mucosal atrophy support this.  
- Lack of an alternative explanatory exposure: absence of toxin exposure, chemotherapy, alcohol/viral hepatitis explaining cirrhosis, or connective-tissue disease explaining ILD increases probability of an inherited cause.

Pedigree/family features that increase pretest probability
- First- or second-degree relatives with early-onset pulmonary fibrosis, unexplained cytopenias or bone marrow failure, early cirrhosis, premature hair greying/alopecia, or unexplained aplastic anemia — especially across generations (compatible with autosomal dominant transmission of many TBD genes such as TERT/TERC/PARN).  
- Pattern of variable penetrance/anticipation: shorter telomeres and earlier more severe disease in successive generations (if available) supports a telomere syndrome.  
- Important caveat: absence of a family history does NOT exclude TBD — incomplete penetrance, variable expressivity, monoallelic mutations, somatic rescue and de novo variants are well described (the presented patient had no clear family history but a monoallelic PARN variant). So a negative pedigree lowers but does not exclude pretest probability.

Examination and bedside clues that increase pretest probability
- Premature ageing signs: premature greying of hair, alopecia, thin or atrophic oral mucosa/atrophic tongue, nail dystrophy, reticular hyperpigmentation — even if only some features present. The patient had premature greying, a bald/atrophic tongue and knuckle hyperpigmentation.  
- Signs of multiorgan fibrosis: hepatosplenomegaly or stigmata of chronic liver disease in a young person; crackles, reduced breath sounds and clubbing or basilar fibrosis on chest exam. The case documented hepatomegaly and basilar changes on lung exam and imaging.  
- Features of marrow failure without classic autoimmune markers: pallor, petechiae/bleeding or recurrent infections with negative autoimmune serologies. The patient had pancytopenia with a low reticulocyte count and a marrow picture inconsistent with pure immune cytopenia (DAT positive but marrow findings and low retic pointed to ineffective erythropoiesis).  
- Discordant exam–lab pattern: severe systemic signs out of proportion to a correctable deficiency (eg, severe organ fibrosis despite correction of B12/folate) — seen in this case.

Laboratory/imaging clues that support TBD over acquired causes
- Multilineage cytopenias with low reticulocyte response (ineffective hematopoiesis) rather than isolated hemolysis or pure peripheral destruction.  
- Radiologic pattern of subpleural/ basilar fibrosis consistent with usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP)/IPF in a young patient.  
- Hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis without viral/autoimmune/metabolic explanation.  
- Negative autoimmune serologies and infectious workup despite systemic inflammation — as in the presented patient.

What to do if you suspect a TBD
- Order telomere length testing (flow-FISH in leukocyte subsets is a standard functional assay) and genetic testing with a multigene panel/next‑generation sequencing for telomere-related genes (TERT, TERC, RTEL1, PARN, DKC1, etc.). The case used clinical exome sequencing and found a monoallelic PARN variant.  
- Counsel that family history may be absent and that genetic results may show variants of uncertain significance; integrate phenotype, telomere length and genetic data.  
- Avoid genotoxic therapies without specialist input (eg, certain conditioning regimens for transplant) and plan multidisciplinary surveillance (hematology, pulmonology, hepatology, genetics).

Bottom line (clinical pearl)
In a young adult with ILD plus unexplained cytopenias, your suspicion for a telomere biology disorder should rise when multiple high‑turnover organs are involved (bone marrow, lung, liver, mucosa, hair, bone), when clinical severity is out of proportion to treatable causes, when autoimmune/infectious workups are unrevealing, and when physical exam shows premature‑ageing stigmata (premature greying, mucosal atrophy, nail/skin changes). A suggestive family history increases pretest probability but its absence does not exclude TBD — proceed to telomere length assessment and targeted genetic testing when the phenotype fits, as illustrated by this patient with PARN-associated TBD.
