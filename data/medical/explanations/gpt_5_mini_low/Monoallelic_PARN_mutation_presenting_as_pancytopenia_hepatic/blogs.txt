Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# Beyond the DC Triad: Spotting Telomere Biology Disorders in Young Adults Without Classic Mucocutaneous Clues

What makes this case striking is how a young woman in her late 20s accumulated progressive multisystem disease—pancytopenia with a low reticulocyte count, premature greying, hepatic cirrhosis, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis changes, osteoporosis with fragility, hepatosplenomegaly and neuropathy—yet lacked the textbook mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita (oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, reticular pigmentation). After years of workup she was found to carry a monoallelic PARN missense variant of uncertain significance, a finding that, together with the clinical picture, pointed clinicians toward a telomere biology disorder (TBD).

Why this matters to the clinician working in the ED, ward or clinic: TBDs are rare, variable in presentation, and commonly under-recognized when the classic mucocutaneous signs are absent. Missing the diagnosis delays appropriate genetic counselling, surveillance for progressive organ failure and early consideration of definitive therapies (for example, tailored transplant planning). The take-home: think telomeres when the pattern of organ dysfunction looks like premature ageing across multiple tissues—especially in younger adults.

## The pattern that should make you pause
TBDs primarily affect tissues with high turnover: bone marrow, mucosa, skin/hair, lung epithelium and liver. The phenotype can be protean and incomplete. In the case presented, the combination of findings is the diagnostic clue:

- Unexplained cytopenias (especially pancytopenia) with low reticulocyte count or poor marrow response to peripheral loss
- Premature greying/alopecia or other signs of early ageing in a young adult
- Organ fibrosis out of proportion to age: idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis
- Osteoporosis/early fragility fractures in a patient without classic risk factors
- Hepatosplenomegaly without clear alternative explanation
- Neuropathy, persistent inflammatory markers or multi-system involvement that doesn’t fit a single acquired disease

If you see several of these features together—especially in someone <50 years old—TBD should be on your differential even if there’s no leukoplakia, nail dystrophy or reticular pigmentation.

## Practical “trigger list” for suspecting a TBD in adults (use clinically)
Consider further evaluation for a TBD when one or more of the following are present without an alternative unifying diagnosis:

- Persistent unexplained cytopenia(s): anemia with low retic, neutropenia or thrombocytopenia, or pancytopenia despite appropriate initial workup
- Early-onset pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) or unexplained restrictive lung disease in a younger adult
- Unexplained liver fibrosis/cirrhosis in absence of viral, alcoholic or autoimmune causes
- Premature greying or alopecia before age 30–40
- Early osteoporosis with fragility fractures or markedly low bone density for age
- Unexplained hepatosplenomegaly
- Multisystem “premature ageing” phenotype: combinations of the above ± neuropathy, endocrinopathies
- Family history of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, aplastic anemia, early cirrhosis, or early unexplained deaths—even if incomplete penetrance is suspected

The more features present, the higher the pre-test probability.

## Initial evaluation — what to order early
Start with a focused but broad screen to rule out common mimics and collect baseline data. In the reported case, conventional causes (severe B12 deficiency, autoimmune serology, viral hepatitis) were addressed but did not fully explain the phenotype.

Key initial labs and tests:
- CBC with differential and reticulocyte count (look for low retic suggesting ineffective erythropoiesis)
- Peripheral blood smear (look for dimorphic anemia, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings)
- B12, folate, iron studies, ferritin, transferrin saturation (correct deficiencies but don’t stop there)
- Hemolysis panel: LDH, haptoglobin, bilirubin, DAT (positive DAT can complicate picture and doesn’t rule out marrow causes)
- LFTs, synthetic liver markers (INR, albumin), abdominal ultrasound and consider transient elastography if there’s hepatomegaly or abnormal LFTs
- Chest imaging: high-resolution CT chest if dyspnea or abnormal CXR (look for ground-glass and subpleural fibrosis)
- Bone marrow biopsy and cytogenetics if unexplained cytopenias persist (to exclude malignancy, MDS, aplastic anemia)
- Vitamin D, calcium, PTH, bone densitometry if bone health is a concern
- Nerve conduction/EMG if neuropathy is present
- Basic infectious and autoimmune screens to exclude common causes (HIV, hepatitis B/C, ANA, autoimmune hepatitis panel)
- Consider PET-CT if systemic inflammatory or infiltrative processes are suspected and malignancy needs exclusion (as done in this case)

Key point: Correct nutritional deficiencies (e.g., severe B12) promptly because they can both cause symptoms and unmask underlying susceptibility, but persistent or disproportionate organ dysfunction after correction should trigger genetic thinking.

## When to send specialized testing for telomere disorders
You don’t need (and shouldn’t order) broad genetic testing for every patient with anemia or IPF. Reserve telomere-specific evaluation for patients with the trigger features above and after exclusion of common acquired causes.

Consider telomere length testing and genetic sequencing when:
- Young age at presentation (<50) with unexplained pulmonary fibrosis, bone marrow failure, or cirrhosis
- Multisystem findings suggesting premature ageing (as above)
- Persistent cytopenias with low reticulocyte count and uninformative marrow/cytogenetics
- Family history suggestive of related conditions (even if incomplete or non-obvious)

Which tests and sequencing:
- Telomere length measurement (flow-FISH on leukocyte subsets is the laboratory gold standard in adults for diagnosing TBD). Short telomeres for age strongly support the diagnosis.
- Targeted gene panel or clinical exome sequencing including telomere-related genes (TERT, TERC, DKC1, PARN, RTEL1, TINF2, etc.). Panels focused on telomere biology or inherited bone marrow failure syndromes are efficient.
- Germline testing is preferred over somatic-only panels; use blood with caution if bone marrow failure or clonal hematopoiesis might confound results—discuss with a geneticist/lab.

Note: Telomere length testing requires age-matched interpretation. A short telomere with a pathogenic variant in a telomere gene clinches the diagnosis; a variant of uncertain significance (VUS), as in this case, may still be clinically meaningful when the phenotype fits—interpret in context and consider segregation studies.

## How to differentiate TBD from common mimics
TBDs can masquerade as nutritional deficiency, autoimmune disease, chronic infection or early-onset primary organ disease. Here’s how to tease them apart:

- Nutritional deficiency (B12/folate, iron): deficits commonly explain cytopenias and neuro features. But in TBD:
  - Deficiencies often coexist (as in the case) and may partially respond to replacement, yet multisystem involvement and persistent cytopenias or organ fibrosis after correction suggest an underlying genetic vulnerability.
  - Look for low retic despite marrow hyperplasia—ineffective erythropoiesis can be a clue.
- Autoimmune disease: autoimmune cytopenias and autoimmune hepatitis can mimic features.
  - Autoimmune serologies, inflammatory markers and response to immunosuppression help differentiate. A positive DAT alone is not diagnostic of primary autoimmune disease—interpret with the overall picture.
- Chronic infection (viral hepatitis, HIV, EBV, CMV): these can cause cytopenias and liver disease.
  - Exclude these with appropriate serologies; negative workup in someone with fibrosis and other age-inappropriate findings should prompt genetic consideration.
- Myelodysplastic syndromes / marrow infiltrative disease:
  - Bone marrow biopsy, cytogenetics and molecular testing primarily differentiate these. In TBD, marrow may show trilineage hematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia but without dysplasia or clonal cytogenetic abnormalities—yet peripheral cytopenias persist because stem cell reserve is reduced.
- Primary IPF, early cirrhosis:
  - Young age, family history and concurrent marrow or hair abnormalities should raise suspicion for TBD rather than isolated idiopathic organ disease.

Bottom line: failure to fully explain the pattern by a single acquired disease and young age are the red flags.

## Management principles and prognostic implications (brief)
- Early recognition changes management: genetic counselling, family screening and tailored surveillance for progressive marrow failure, pulmonary decline and hepatocellular carcinoma risk.
- Treat reversible contributors aggressively (eg, replace B12/folate, optimize nutrition, avoid hepatotoxins, stop smoking).
- Consider hematopoietic stem cell transplantation for progressive bone marrow failure—outcomes depend on recognizing telomere disorders because transplant protocols (reduced-intensity conditioning) and donor selection differ.
- Lung or liver transplantation may be considered in selected cases; telomere status affects perioperative risk and post-transplant complications.
- Experimental therapies such as androgen derivatives have some preliminary data for telomere elongation but require specialist input and long-term monitoring.
- Multidisciplinary care (hematology, pulmonology, hepatology, genetics) is essential.

## Clinical pearls
- Don’t be reassured by absent mucocutaneous signs—up to half of adult TBD presentations lack the classic DC triad.
- A low reticulocyte response in the setting of cytopenia suggests marrow stress/ineffective erythropoiesis and should prompt marrow evaluation and consideration of inherited marrow failure if common causes are excluded.
- Severe nutritional deficiencies can coexist with TBD and partially explain symptoms—document and correct them, then reassess. Persistence or disproportionate organ involvement afterwards is a clue.
- Monoallelic variants in genes like PARN can present primarily with pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis but may also predispose to marrow vulnerability—genotype–phenotype correlations are still evolving.
- Telomere length testing (flow-FISH) plus targeted germline sequencing is the diagnostic pathway; interpret genetic variants in concert with clinical findings and consider specialist genetics input for VUS or complex results.

## Take-home messages
- Consider a telomere biology disorder in younger adults with unexplained multisystem premature-aging phenotypes—particularly when you see combinations of unexplained cytopenias with low retic, premature greying, pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis, early osteoporosis/fragility fractures, hepatosplenomegaly or neuropathy.
- Initial workup should both exclude common mimics (nutritional, autoimmune, infectious, malignant) and gather baseline data (CBC with retic, smear, marrow exam, LFTs, HRCT chest, elastography if liver disease suspected).
- Order telomere length testing (flow-FISH) and targeted germline sequencing when the clinical picture fits; partner with genetics for interpretation and family counselling.
- Early diagnosis matters: it alters surveillance, family screening, transplant planning and avoids misdirected therapies.

If you’re seeing a young patient with “too much ageing” for their years—especially across marrow, lung and liver—add “telomere disorder” to your differential and start the targeted workup.



# When a PARN Variant Is a VUS: Clinically Anchoring Genetics in Suspected Telomere Disease

What grabs you about this case? A woman in her late 20s with progressive pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis — classic “premature ageing” target organs — yet no florid mucocutaneous dyskeratosis. Genetic testing finds a monoallelic PARN missense change (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) reported as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS). How do you turn that ambiguous lab result into actionable clinical reasoning?

This post walks through practical ways to interpret and act on a monoallelic PARN VUS in the setting of a phenotype strongly suggestive of a telomere biology disorder (TBD). I’ll cover how to apply ACMG/AMP thinking in the clinic, the essential next investigations (telomere length testing, segregation, functional data), how to manage patients while uncertainty persists, and common pitfalls.

## Why phenotype matters (phenotype anchoring)
Genetic variants don’t exist in a vacuum. Pathogenicity is a probabilistic statement that combines variant-level evidence and phenotype-level evidence.

- PARN is a known TBD gene. Monoallelic PARN variants have been repeatedly associated with adult-onset pulmonary fibrosis and sometimes bone marrow disease. So a monoallelic PARN change in a patient with IPF + pancytopenia + hepatic fibrosis is biologically plausible.
- This patient’s constellation (young age, cytopenia with trilineage marrow, cirrhosis on elastography, pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying, osteoporosis, neuropathy) is a red flag for a TBD even if the mucocutaneous triad is absent. Clinical suspicion upgrades the pre-test probability that a rare PARN variant is meaningful.

But a VUS is a VUS — it cannot be used as definitive diagnostic evidence on its own. The job is to gather orthogonal evidence to move it toward (or away from) pathogenicity.

## Practical next steps to reclassify or contextualize the VUS

1. Telomere length measurement (high-yield)
   - Send for flow-FISH telomere length testing in leukocyte subsets (gold standard in clinical labs). Short telomeres for age in multiple leukocyte compartments strongly supports a TBD and constitutes powerful phenotypic evidence.
   - If telomeres are short (eg below the 1st–10th centile for age), that increases the likelihood that the PARN variant is pathogenic/haploinsufficient. Normal telomere length argues against a systemic telomere disorder but does not exclude tissue-limited or mosaic disease.

2. Segregation and family studies
   - Test first-degree relatives (ideally affected and unaffected) for the variant and, where possible, measure their telomere length. Co-segregation with disease (or short telomeres in carriers) provides strong supportive evidence.
   - Be cautious: incomplete penetrance and variable expressivity are common in TBDs, so absence of disease in a carrier doesn’t rule out pathogenicity.

3. Reanalysis and orthogonal genetic testing
   - Re-examine the exome for other variants in telomere-related genes (TERT, TERC, RTEL1, DKC1, TINF2 etc.), copy-number variants, or non-coding changes that may explain phenotype or act in digenic fashion.
   - Consider targeted testing of TERC (sometimes missed by exome), and assess for somatic rescue or mosaicism (deep sequencing of blood vs skin fibroblast DNA). If blood has clonal hematopoiesis or somatic variants, germline assessment from skin may help.

4. Functional data
   - Look for published functional assays of the same residue/domain. This variant affects the R3H domain (residues 178–245) — a functionally important region for PARN; that supports PM1-level evidence per ACMG when combined with other data.
   - If no published data, explore collaboration with a research lab for a functional assay (eg PARN activity, TERC stability assays). Functional validation can be decisive but may take time.

5. Population and in silico data review
   - Confirm absence/rarity in population databases (gnomAD) and review in silico predictions — useful but weak evidence alone.
   - Document conservation across species and the physicochemical impact of the amino-acid change.

6. Communicate with the testing lab
   - Provide them with detailed phenotype and results of telomere length testing, family studies, and any functional data — labs will sometimes reclassify a VUS with additional evidence.

## Management you can justify while the VUS remains unresolved

You often must act before definitive classification. When the clinical phenotype strongly suggests TBD, management can be guided by the phenotype and risk, not solely by variant label.

- Surveillance and risk mitigation
  - Start organ surveillance appropriate for TBD: regular pulmonary function testing (including DLCO), high-resolution CT surveillance as clinically indicated, liver monitoring (LFTs, elastography), hematology follow-up with serial CBCs and bone marrow review if counts decline.
  - Avoid risk factors that accelerate telomere attrition: tobacco, excessive alcohol, cytotoxic chemotherapy or radiation unless absolutely needed.
- Hematopoietic decisions
  - If progressive marrow failure develops, discuss hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) early with a centre experienced in transplant for telomere disorders. These patients tolerate standard conditioning poorly — reduced-intensity regimens and tailored donor selection (avoid related donors who may be variant carriers) are critical.
  - Do NOT use a VUS to exclude a potential related donor without further family testing and counseling. If a relative tests negative for the variant and has normal telomere lengths, they may be a safer donor.
- Medical therapies
  - Supportive care: transfusion, vitamin supplementation (as appropriate), osteoporosis management, pulmonary fibrosis therapies as indicated (antifibrotics when appropriate).
  - Consider discussion of androgens (eg danazol) only in specialized centres and in context of clinical trials or where evidence supports benefit — these are not standard first-line therapies and carry risks.
- Genetic counseling and reproduction
  - Offer counseling early. Discuss uncertainty, possible inheritance patterns, reproductive options and need for targeted testing of relatives only in the context of reclassification or robust supporting evidence.
- Documentation and follow-up
  - Document the VUS nature clearly in charts and letters. Set a plan for periodic re-evaluation with the genetics lab (eg annual review), since VUSes are commonly reclassified over time.

## How to talk to patients and families about a VUS
- Be honest and clear: “This variant is of uncertain significance — we can’t say it caused your illness yet, but it’s in a gene that fits your clinical picture.”
- Explain next steps in plain language: telomere length testing, family testing, possible research/functional testing, and why those steps matter.
- Emphasize what will change in care: surveillance, avoidance of certain exposures, and caution around transplant/donor selection — all justified by phenotype and plausible genetic risk, not by the VUS alone.
- Avoid deterministic language: don’t tell family members “you have the disease” based on a VUS; instead offer targeted testing and counseling.

## Common pitfalls and how to avoid them
- Overcalling a VUS as pathogenic because it “fits” — treat variant-level and phenotype-level evidence independently, then integrate. Overcalling risks unnecessary family anxiety and inappropriate donor exclusion.
- Under-calling: dismissing a biologically plausible VUS in the setting of a strong phenotype; that can delay surveillance and transplant planning. If clinical suspicion is high, act on the phenotype while continuing to accrue genetic evidence.
- Relying solely on in silico predictions. Computational tools are supportive but weak evidence by ACMG and should not drive decisions alone.
- Forgetting telomere length. Flow-FISH is a relatively accessible, high-yield test that often clarifies uncertainty.
- Not recontacting. VUS reclassification is common — ensure follow-up with the lab and the patient.

## Applying ACMG/AMP thinking in this case (brief)
- PM1: Variant lies in a critical domain (R3H) — supportive.
- PM2: Absent/rare in population databases — supportive.
- PP3: Multiple in silico tools predict deleteriousness — supporting but weak.
- PP2: Missense mechanism plausible in PARN — supporting.
- Lacking strong functional or segregation data, these collectively justify a VUS classification. However, added evidence — short telomeres, segregation with disease, or functional impairment — could tip toward likely pathogenic.

## Bottom line — clinical pearls
- A monoallelic PARN VUS in a young adult with pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis and bone marrow dysfunction should prompt telomere length testing (flow-FISH) and family studies before accepting or discarding the variant.
- Manage based on phenotype and risk: institute surveillance, avoid telomere-toxic exposures, involve transplant/genetics teams early, and be cautious with donor selection.
- Communicate uncertainty clearly: VUS ≠ “not important” and ≠ “disease-causing” until additional evidence is amassed.
- Reassess periodically — labs reclassify VUSes; new data (functional studies, case reports) may change clinical decision-making.

If you’re caring for a similar patient: get flow-FISH, test relatives selectively, and loop in a geneticist/telomere clinic early. The combination of robust phenotype data and targeted investigations often converts uncertainty into actionable clarity.



# DAT-Positive, Retic-Low: Untangling B12 Deficiency, Ineffective Erythropoiesis, and TBD-Related Marrow Vulnerability

What makes this case interesting
A young woman with 5 years of progressive fatigue presents with pancytopenia and a confusing lab pattern: a positive direct antiglobulin test (IgG 2+), severe vitamin B12/folate deficiency, dimorphic smear with basophilic stippling/Cabot rings and hypersegmented neutrophils, high serum iron and ferritin, low reticulocyte count, and bone marrow showing erythroid hyperplasia without malignancy. On top of that she has hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis and a monoallelic PARN variant suggesting a telomere biology disorder (TBD).

That constellation forces you to reconcile two different mechanisms that can cause anemia: peripheral immune hemolysis and ineffective erythropoiesis from marrow-intrinsic problems — and to do it while keeping an eye on an underlying genetic vulnerability that can blunt marrow reserve and change prognosis/management.

Clinical reasoning: how the pieces fit
- Positive DAT + anemia often screams autoimmune hemolysis. But autoimmune hemolysis classically produces a brisk marrow response (high retic) unless the marrow is failing. Here, the retic index is low (0.39%), arguing against an appropriate compensatory response.
- Peripheral smear shows features of ineffective erythropoiesis (dimorphic cells, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, hypersegmented neutrophils) consistent with severe B12/folate deficiency causing intramedullary destruction or maturation arrest rather than pure peripheral RBC destruction.
- Bone marrow with erythroid hyperplasia and increased iron stores fits with ineffective erythropoiesis — the marrow is trying (hyperplasia) but producing defective RBCs that are destroyed before release. This also explains high serum iron and ferritin (recycled iron not incorporated into mature RBCs).
- The low retic count despite marrow hyperplasia can reflect either severe ineffective erythropoiesis (reticulocytes are not maturing/released) or a marrow that has limited reserve and cannot mount an adequate response — the latter can be seen with telomere-mediated stem cell exhaustion in TBDs.
- Mild laboratory hemolysis markers (LDH, indirect bilirubin) and a positive DAT may coexist with ineffective erythropoiesis — DAT positivity may be incidental or reflect a secondary phenomenon (e.g., autoantibodies in the setting of immune dysregulation). A positive DAT alone does not mandate immunosuppression.

Practical approach to distinguishing immune hemolysis from ineffective erythropoiesis
1. Look at reticulocytes first:
   - High retic (appropriate response) → supports peripheral hemolysis or blood loss.
   - Low/normal retic despite anemia → suggests ineffective erythropoiesis or marrow failure.
2. Smear and marrow clues:
   - Hypersegmented neutrophils, macrocytosis, megaloblastic changes, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings → nutritional/maturation defects.
   - Marrow erythroid hyperplasia with dysplasia and increased iron stores → ineffective erythropoiesis.
3. Hemolysis markers:
   - Peripheral hemolysis usually shows high retic, spherocytes, markedly elevated LDH, low haptoglobin, high indirect bilirubin.
   - In ineffective erythropoiesis you can see elevated LDH/indirect bili but low retic and iron overload pattern.
4. DAT interpretation:
   - Positive DAT (IgG) can indicate warm autoimmune hemolysis but must be interpreted in context. If anemia is severe with appropriate reticulocytosis and hemolytic markers, treat for autoimmune hemolysis.
   - If DAT positive but retic low and marrow pattern points to ineffective erythropoiesis, immunosuppression is not first-line — address underlying cause and observe response.
5. Ancillary tests:
   - Haptoglobin, peripheral smear morphology, bone marrow exam, iron studies, vitamin B12/folate, and hemolysis panel over time. Serial reticulocyte counts after therapy are informative.

Why telomere biology disorders (TBD) matter here
- Telomeropathy (eg, PARN variants) shortens the replicative capacity of hematopoietic stem cells. That can lead to an attenuated reticulocyte response even when the marrow is “trying” (erythroid hyperplasia), making the retic count deceptively low in the face of hemolysis or blood loss.
- TBDs can render the marrow exquisitely vulnerable to stressors: severe B12 deficiency that would be reversible in a healthy marrow may trigger prolonged cytopenias in someone with short telomeres.
- Clinically, TBDs often present with organ fibrosis (lung, liver), premature greying, osteoporosis — the extra-hematologic clues in this case fit the diagnosis and change prognosis and surveillance (eg, need telomere length testing, genetic counselling, consideration of HSCT if progressive marrow failure).

Stepwise management in cases like this (practical, bedside-friendly)
1. Stabilize the patient:
   - Transfuse for symptomatic anemia/hemodynamic instability as needed.
   - Correct electrolytes and treat acute issues (infection, bleeding).
2. Treat clear, reversible causes first:
   - Replace vitamin B12 (eg, intramuscular or IV cyanocobalamin loading, then maintenance) and folate as indicated — watch for reticulocyte response over 1–2 weeks.
   - Address malabsorption causes (eg, reduced parietal cells → decreased intrinsic factor).
3. Reassess response:
   - Expect a reticulocyte rise 3–10 days after B12 repletion in an intact marrow. Lack of retic response suggests marrow limitation.
   - Repeat smear and hemolysis labs. If hemolysis markers and brisk retic response appear → autoimmune hemolysis becomes more likely.
4. Be cautious about immunosuppression:
   - Don’t reflex to steroids solely because DAT is positive. Reserve steroids for clear autoimmune hemolysis (falling Hb with high retic, hemolytic labs, clinical deterioration) or when other causes excluded.
5. Investigate marrow and marrow reserve:
   - Bone marrow biopsy if cytopenias persist, show dysplasia, or to exclude malignancy/infiltration.
   - Telomere length testing (flow-FISH) and targeted genetic testing when clinical features suggest TBD (young age, premature greying, pulmonary/liver fibrosis, family history or multisystem failure).
6. Long-term care and surveillance for TBD:
   - Genetic counselling and family testing.
   - Surveillance for progressive marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis and malignancy.
   - Discuss potential curative therapy (allogeneic HSCT) early if progressive marrow failure develops — timing and conditioning must be tailored in telomeropathies because standard regimens have high toxicity.
   - Consider androgen therapy or clinical trials for telomere elongation cautiously; evidence is limited and individualized.

When to treat as autoimmune hemolysis
- Treat when there is: falling Hb despite supportive care, high retic production index (appropriate marrow response), typical hemolysis labs (very low haptoglobin, very high LDH/indirect bilirubin), spherocytes on smear, and clinical deterioration — even if a background of ineffective erythropoiesis exists.
- If immunosuppression is started, monitor response closely and reassess for underlying marrow disease if response is limited.

Clinical pearls
- A positive DAT is not definitive for immune hemolysis — interpretation must be integrated with reticulocyte response and bone marrow findings.
- High serum iron and ferritin with erythroid hyperplasia point to ineffective erythropoiesis (iron is recycled but not used).
- Severe B12 deficiency can mimic or unmask marrow failure; correct it promptly and watch for retic rise — that response is a functional test of marrow reserve.
- In young patients with multisystem fibrosis, premature greying, and cytopenias, consider telomere biology disorders even if the classical DC mucocutaneous triad is absent.
- Telomeropathies can blunt retic responses and make the marrow less forgiving — a “low retic” in a patient who otherwise should respond should trigger consideration of marrow-intrinsic disease and early genetic evaluation.

Take-home messages
- Don’t treat a positive DAT in isolation. Use the reticulocyte count, smear, hemolysis markers and bone marrow context to decide if this is immune hemolysis or ineffective erythropoiesis.
- Severe B12/folate deficiency causes ineffective erythropoiesis: marrow hyperplasia, high iron/ferritin, dimorphic smear, and low reticulocyte production despite anemia.
- Telomere biology disorders (eg, PARN variants) reduce hematopoietic reserve and can blunt retic responses — they also explain multisystem fibrosis in young adults and change long-term management.
- Correct reversible causes first (B12/folate), re-evaluate reticulocyte response, and if cytopenias persist or clinical features suggest TBD, proceed to telomere testing, genetic counselling, and early specialist referral for hematology and transplant discussion if needed.



# The Liver Is Not an Innocent Bystander: Recognizing and Managing Hepatic Fibrosis in Telomere Syndromes

What makes this case sobering is how a young woman in her late 20s — with progressive fatigue, premature greying, pancytopenia and pulmonary fibrosis — turned out to have cirrhosis on transient elastography and a monoallelic PARN variant. We often think of telomere biology disorders (TBDs) as bone marrow or lung diseases, and most teaching stresses the mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita. This case reminds us that the liver can be an early and clinically silent target, and that hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis in a young adult without usual risk factors should prompt consideration of a telomere syndrome.

Why the liver matters in TBDs
- Telomere-driven replicative senescence most prominently hits highly replicative tissues: marrow, mucosa, lung epithelium — and hepatocytes. Emerging registry data and case series suggest a much higher prevalence of clinically relevant hepatic fibrosis among adults with telomerase-related mutations (TERT/TERC and increasingly PARN) than previously appreciated.
- Hepatic fibrosis in TBDs may be insidious, out of proportion to traditional risk factors, and not accompanied by classic autoimmune or viral markers. In the presented case, imaging and elastography established cirrhosis despite negative autoimmune and viral serologies, supporting a primary telomere-related mechanism.

A practical screening bundle for suspected TBDs
When you have a young adult with unexplained cytopenia, pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying or other premature ageing features — or whenever a telomere syndrome is on the differential — consider an initial liver screen even if LFTs are only mildly abnormal.

Proposed baseline screening bundle:
- Liver ultrasound with Doppler (assess size, surface nodularity, steatosis, splenomegaly, portal flow)
- Transient elastography (FibroScan) with CAP when available (gives quantitative stiffness and steatosis info)
- Basic portal hypertension labs: platelet count, INR, albumin, bilirubin
- Viral hepatitis serologies (HBsAg, anti-HBc, HCV antibody with reflex RNA), and routine metabolic screens (iron studies, ferritin, ceruloplasmin/urinary copper in younger patients as appropriate)
- Autoimmune screen if indicated (ANA, SMA, IgG) — but negative serologies do not exclude TBD-related fibrosis
- Consider noninvasive fibrosis biomarkers if local practice uses them (FIB-4, APRI), but interpret cautiously in cytopenia
- Document alcohol, drug, medication history and screen for metabolic risk factors (BMI, diabetes)
All of the above should be interpreted in the clinical context: in TBDs, stiffness can reflect fibrosis even with modest transaminase abnormalities and absence of serologic etiology.

When serologies are negative — a focused differential
If viral and autoimmune causes are excluded, think broadly:
- Telomere biology disorders (TERT/TERC/RTEL1/PARN and others) — especially when concomitant marrow or pulmonary disease is present
- Metabolic-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) — consider the metabolic profile; steatosis on CAP supports mixed injury
- Drug- or toxin-mediated injury — consider prior chemotherapy, chronic hepatotoxic medications
- Iron overload (less likely if ferritin elevated from ineffective erythropoiesis — interpret iron indices carefully)
- Rare genetic cholestatic or metabolic disorders depending on age and phenotype
Liver biopsy can be considered when the diagnosis is unclear and histology will change management, but weigh procedural risks carefully in cytopenic patients and those with portal hypertension.

Medication cautions and outpatient management nuances
Patients with TBDs and hepatic fibrosis require extra caution with medications:
- Avoid or dose-adjust hepatotoxic drugs. Consider alternatives where possible and consult hepatology/pharmacology for oncology or immunosuppression plans.
- Androgens (danazol, nandrolone) have been trialed to treat telomere shortening in marrow failure. They may help cytopenias but can worsen liver dysfunction (cholestasis, peliosis hepatis, elevated enzymes). In patients with pre-existing fibrosis/cirrhosis, use extreme caution and involve hepatology and hematology; balance marrow benefit against hepatic risk.
- Myelosuppressive agents, cytotoxic chemotherapy, and certain antifibrotic or immunosuppressive drugs used for lung disease may have hepatic metabolism concerns — coordinate care across specialties.
- Vaccinate against hepatitis A and B if seronegative; provide counseling on alcohol avoidance and metabolic risk reduction.

Monitoring frequency and what to watch for
- Clinical follow-up every 3–6 months initially, tailored to severity. Monitor:
  - Complete blood count (watch for evolving marrow failure)
  - Liver panel (AST, ALT, alkaline phosphatase, bilirubin, albumin), INR
  - Renal function and electrolytes
  - Imaging: ultrasound every 6–12 months if cirrhosis; transient elastography periodically (frequency individualized)
  - Screening for portal hypertension: platelet trend, splenomegaly; consider endoscopic variceal screening per local cirrhosis guidelines when portal hypertension suspected
  - Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) surveillance per cirrhosis protocols (ultrasound ± AFP every 6 months) once cirrhosis is established
- Pulmonary surveillance (pulmonary function tests, high-resolution CT) and hematology surveillance should run in parallel because organ involvement can evolve asynchronously.

Referral thresholds and multidisciplinary coordination
- Early hepatology referral: any evidence of significant fibrosis on elastography (e.g., liver stiffness in the cirrhotic range), portal hypertension stigmata, or synthetic dysfunction (hypoalbuminaemia, prolonged INR).
- Early hematology and genetics referral: for marrow dysfunction, progressive cytopenias, consideration of germline testing and family counseling.
- Pulmonology/interstitial lung disease clinic: for coexisting pulmonary fibrosis evaluation and management.
- Transplant steering: discuss potential candidacy early in multidisciplinary meetings. Important nuances:
  - Hematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT) is a potential cure for marrow failure but carries higher risk in telomere syndromes (pulmonary and hepatic comorbidities increase transplant morbidity/mortality). Reduced-intensity conditioning regimens may be used, but outcomes are influenced by extrapulmonary organ disease.
  - Liver transplant candidacy: TBDs complicate candidacy due to coexistent pulmonary fibrosis and marrow dysfunction. Comprehensive assessment must include cardiopulmonary function and marrow reserve. In select patients with isolated progressive liver disease, liver transplant can be considered, but combined-organ considerations (lung, marrow) often require individualized planning.
  - Combined transplants are complex and rare; expert centers and familial/genetic counseling are essential.
- Always include genetic counseling when a germline telomere gene variant is found — implications extend to family screening, reproductive counseling, and long-term surveillance.

Clinical pearls from the PARN case
- Young-onset cirrhosis or significant fibrosis without conventional serologic drivers should raise suspicion for a telomere disorder — especially when there is concurrent marrow dysfunction or pulmonary fibrosis.
- Monoallelic PARN variants, while more commonly linked to pulmonary fibrosis, can associate with hepatic fibrosis and marrow vulnerability — phenotype can be variable and incomplete penetrance is common.
- Severe nutritional deficiencies (eg, vitamin B12) can coexist and precipitate overt cytopenias in a marrow already compromised by short telomeres; correcting reversible contributors is essential but does not exclude an underlying genetic predisposition.
- Negative autoimmune and viral workups do not rule out clinically significant liver disease in TBDs — elastography and imaging provide important diagnostic information.
- Management is multidisciplinary and individualized. Consider risks of androgens and other hepatotoxic therapies in the presence of fibrosis.

Take-home messages
- Consider telomere biology disorders when a young adult has unexplained hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis, especially with marrow or pulmonary involvement.
- Initial liver screening in suspected TBD: ultrasound ± Doppler, transient elastography with CAP, basic portal hypertension labs, and viral/metabolic serologies.
- Negative serologies should prompt consideration of TBD; involve genetics and hepatology early.
- Exercise medication caution (androgens, hepatotoxics). Monitor closely and coordinate among hepatology, hematology, pulmonology and genetics.
- Surveillance: routine labs every 3–6 months, imaging for fibrosis and HCC surveillance per cirrhosis protocols; tailor frequency to disease severity.
- Early multidisciplinary discussion about transplant candidacy is crucial — organ-specific disease, marrow reserve and pulmonary status all influence risk and feasibility.

This case is a useful reminder: the liver is not an innocent bystander in telomere syndromes. Recognizing hepatic involvement early changes surveillance, medication choices, and transplant planning — and it mandates a multidisciplinary approach.



# IPF With a Telomere Signature: Clinical Implications of Suspected TBD in Young Fibrosis

What makes this case interesting?
A woman in her late 20s with progressive fatigue, pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and diffuse interstitial lung disease highlights an increasingly recognised clinical pattern: pulmonary fibrosis in a young adult with multisystem premature‑ageing features. Genetic testing identified a monoallelic PARN missense variant (VUS) — not a slam‑dunk pathogenic result, but the phenotype is classic for a telomere biology disorder (TBD). This vignette is a useful springboard to discuss how ILD clinicians should recognise a “telomere signature,” what to do next diagnostically, and how management and transplant planning change when a TBD is suspected.

## Recognising the telomere signature in ILD
Telomere‑related pulmonary fibrosis differs from garden‑variety idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) in a few ways. Key clinical clues in this case that raise the index of suspicion:
- Young age at presentation (often <60, sometimes much younger).
- Extrapulmonary features of premature ageing: premature greying, osteoporosis, thin/atrophic mucosa, nail and skin changes (although the classic DC triad may be absent).
- Haematologic abnormalities (cytopenias, bone marrow failure or poor reticulocyte responses).
- Hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis without other cause.
- Family history of pulmonary fibrosis, bone marrow failure, liver disease, early greying or sudden deaths (but may be absent due to de novo mutation or incomplete penetrance).
- Imaging: subpleural fibrotic change and UIP pattern are common, but ground glass and mixed patterns can be seen early.

Don’t mistake coexisting nutritional deficiencies or autoimmune serologies for the whole story — persistent, multisystem dysfunction despite correction of reversible causes should prompt genetic thinking.

## What to order when you suspect a TBD
A practical stepwise approach for ILD clinics:
1. Phenotype first: detailed family history (three generations), targeted exam for mucocutaneous signs, nails, hair, bone signs, hepatosplenomegaly.
2. Basic screening labs: CBC with reticulocyte count, LFTs, iron studies, B12/folate, bone density as indicated.
3. Pulmonary evaluation: PFTs (including DLCO), HRCT and exercise oximetry. Consider 6‑minute walk.
4. If clinical suspicion persists, send:
   - Telomere length measurement (flow‑FISH is the gold standard for peripheral blood leukocytes).
   - A targeted telomere gene panel (TERT, TERC, PARN, RTEL1, DKC1, etc.) or clinical exome with a telomere panel; interpret variants in context — VUS are common.
5. Multidisciplinary referral early: genetics (for counseling and cascade testing), hematology (bone marrow surveillance), hepatology (fibrosis staging), and transplant team if disease is progressive.

Flow‑FISH results and a pathogenic germline variant confirm a TBD; VUS + short telomeres with compatible phenotype is “functional” evidence to act on clinically.

## Impact on medical therapy and supportive care
Antifibrotics:
- Pirfenidone and nintedanib remain the mainstays for progressive fibrosing ILD, including telomere‑related disease. Data are limited but suggest benefit in slowing decline.
- Expectation management is important: some telomere‑related cases progress faster and may have less predictable responses.

Supportive care:
- Early referral for pulmonary rehabilitation and supplemental oxygen when indicated.
- Vaccination, avoidance of exposures that increase proliferative stress (smoking, occupational dusts), and careful use of cytotoxic drugs (some agents may accelerate marrow/liver injury).
- Screen and treat extrapulmonary problems: bone health, nutritional deficiencies, and liver disease.

Experimental/adjunctive therapies:
- Androgen derivatives (danazol, nandrolone) have shown telomere length gains in small studies for marrow disease; data for lung outcomes are limited and use should be specialist‑led.
- Clinical trials may be appropriate; discuss with genetics/translational teams.

## Transplant implications — be proactive
Lung transplant planning must be tailored when a TBD is present or suspected.

Pre‑transplant assessment:
- Full multisystem evaluation: CBC and bone marrow assessment (if cytopenia), hepatic function and imaging, dental/mucosal assessment, dermatologic exam.
- Identify occult organ dysfunction — even mild marrow or liver disease increases peri‑operative risk.
- Genetic counseling and family testing; identify and exclude related donors who may carry the same mutation.

Peri‑transplant considerations:
- Increased sensitivity to cytotoxic conditioning — higher risk of marrow failure, mucositis, infections and hepatic toxicity. Many centers favour reduced‑intensity conditioning or avoid myeloablative regimens.
- Careful haematology involvement for management of cytopenias and post‑transplant marrow suppression.
- Expect higher risk of bone marrow failure and other extrapulmonary complications after transplant; surveillance is essential.

Post‑transplant surveillance:
- Monitor for late marrow failure, progressive liver disease and malignancies.
- Coordinate long‑term care with hepatology and hematology; immunosuppression may need individualised adjustments.

Donor selection:
- Avoid related donors unless genetic testing excludes the variant/telomere shortening. Unrecognised donor telomere defects have been implicated in poor outcomes.

## Family screening and genetic counseling
- Cascade testing for first‑degree relatives is recommended when a pathogenic variant is identified. With VUS, counseling becomes nuanced — consider telomere length testing in relatives and longitudinal clinical surveillance.
- Reproductive counseling: discussion of inheritance patterns (autosomal dominant with incomplete penetrance vs recessive depending on gene), preimplantation or prenatal options if desired.
- Psychosocial support: uncertainty around VUS and variable penetrance can be distressing; genetics teams are critical.

## Practical algorithm to integrate genetic/telomere testing into ILD clinics
1. Identify red flags in any ILD patient: age <60, cytopenias, premature greying, unexplained liver disease, family history.
2. If any red flags present:
   - Order CBC + retic, LFTs, bone density as indicated.
   - Arrange HRCT/PFTs (standard for ILD).
   - Refer for flow‑FISH telomere length testing and telomere gene panel (or clinical exome) in parallel.
   - Simultaneously refer to genetics and hematology.
3. If telomere length is short and/or a pathogenic variant is found:
   - Initiate cascade family testing, discuss transplant implications, intensify organ surveillance.
   - Start/continue antifibrotic therapy where indicated and expedite transplant evaluation if progression.
4. If tests are negative but clinical suspicion remains high:
   - Periodic reassessment and consider research/expanded testing; manage extrapulmonary disease proactively.
5. Document and communicate restrictions: avoid related living donors without testing; consider reduced‑intensity transplant conditioning.

## Limitations and practical caveats
- Many variants are VUS; integration of phenotype, telomere length and family testing is essential for interpretation.
- Telomere length testing has age‑dependent norms — interpretation requires expertise.
- Not all centers have flow‑FISH; sendouts and delays are common, so start multidisciplinary planning early.
- Evidence base for specific therapies in telomere‑related ILD is limited; much management is extrapolated from IPF and marrow literature.

## Bottom line — clinical pearls and takeaways
- Think telomere biology disorder when fibrosis occurs in young adults, especially with marrow, liver or premature‑ageing features.
- Early genetic and telomere testing changes management: family screening, donor selection, transplant conditioning and multidisciplinary surveillance.
- Antifibrotics, oxygen and rehab still apply, but plan transplant earlier and involve hematology/hepatology/genetics before major interventions.
- Avoid using related donors without genetic/telomere evaluation; anticipate transplant complications and tailor conditioning.
- Even when a variant is a VUS, a compatible phenotype and short telomeres warrant acting clinically to mitigate risk and prepare for progressive disease.

This patient’s story is a reminder: in ILD clinics, the lungs rarely act alone. Finding a telomere signature has real consequences — for treatment, for family members, and for how we plan the safest path to transplant for those who need it.



# Beyond Blood and Lungs: Bone and Nerve Manifestations in Telomere Disease

What makes this case worth reading? Because it pulls you away from the classic telomere-disease script (bone marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis, mucocutaneous findings) and into two under-appreciated domains: bone fragility and peripheral nerve disease. Our patient — a woman in her late 20s with a monoallelic PARN variant — had osteoporosis with fractures, marked vitamin D deficiency and secondary hyperparathyroidism, plus an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy. These findings remind us that telomere biology disorders (TBDs) are systemic premature‑ageing syndromes: rapidly dividing tissues (bone marrow, gut, skin) get the most attention, but bone remodelling and peripheral nerves are vulnerable too. If you only screen blood counts and CT chests, you can miss important morbidity drivers.

Why bone and nerve involvement in TBDs is biologically plausible
- Telomere shortening accelerates cellular senescence and limits stem/progenitor cell pools. Osteoblast and osteoprogenitor function relies on replicative capacity; premature senescence impairs bone formation and microarchitecture, predisposing to low bone mass and fragility fractures at atypically young ages.
- Hepatic fibrosis and cholestasis — increasingly reported in TBD cohorts — impair 25‑hydroxylation and bile‑acid–dependent absorption of fat‑soluble vitamins, fuelling vitamin D deficiency and secondary hyperparathyroidism that accelerate bone loss.
- Chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and endocrine dysregulation that accompany TBDs (and their comorbidities) further tip bone turnover toward resorption.
- Peripheral nerves may be affected indirectly (nutritional deficiency — B12, vitamin D — and metabolic derangements) and directly via small‑fibre/sensorimotor axonal injury related to chronic cellular stress and impaired Schwann cell/neuronal repair capacity in a setting of telomere dysfunction.

Grounded in the case: what we observed
- Osteoporosis on DEXA with recurrent fragility fractures and bone pain.
- Severe vitamin D deficiency and secondary hyperparathyroidism.
- Multiple electrolyte abnormalities (hypocalcaemia, hypokalaemia) that can compound neuromuscular dysfunction.
- Axonal, asymmetric sensorimotor polyneuropathy on EMG/NCS.
- Hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis on transient elastography — a contributor to vitamin D and metabolic disturbances.
- A PARN monoallelic variant consistent with a telomere biology disorder after exclusion of other causes.

Practical evaluation: what to check when you suspect bone/nerve involvement in TBD
- Bone health:
  - DEXA (hip, lumbar spine) early — don't assume “too young” to scan in TBD.
  - FRAX is of limited use in young patients; rely on absolute BMD and fracture history.
  - Labs: corrected serum calcium, phosphate, albumin, creatinine; 25‑OH vitamin D; PTH; alkaline phosphatase; markers of bone turnover (optional — PINP, CTX) if available.
  - Screen for secondary causes: thyroid function, gonadal status, celiac disease, malabsorption (esp. with GI or prior gastrectomy), and chronic liver disease.
- Neuropathy:
  - Detailed neurological exam focusing on distribution (length‑dependent vs patchy/asymmetric), small vs large fibre features.
  - EMG/NCS to define axonal vs demyelinating, sensory vs motor involvement (as in the case: asymmetric axonal sensorimotor neuropathy).
  - Nutritional screen: B12, folate, thiamine; glucose/HbA1c; renal and hepatic function; and relevant toxins/medications.
- Telomere disease context:
  - Low threshold for telomere length testing (flow FISH) and targeted genetic testing if clinical suspicion.
  - Hepatic evaluation (liver enzymes, transient elastography) since liver disease will affect management choices for bone disease and medications.

Management pearls — tailored to TBD patients with bone and nerve disease
- Correct reversible contributors first:
  - Replete vitamin D (treat deficiency aggressively but safely) and correct calcium; monitor for hypocalcaemia rebound if PTH falls.
  - Replace B12/folate where deficient — both may have contributed to marrow and neuropathic features in this case.
  - Address malabsorption sources (e.g., reduced intrinsic factor or gastroparesis), and coordinate with gastro/hepatology.
- Fracture prevention and osteoporosis therapies:
  - Nonpharmacologic measures: fall‑risk reduction, weight‑bearing/physiotherapy tailored to pulmonary and neurological limits, smoking cessation, alcohol moderation.
  - Antiresorptives (bisphosphonates): generally effective for osteoporosis and not contraindicated purely for liver disease, but use caution if severe renal impairment or ongoing cholestatic liver disease complicated by malabsorption. Ensure vitamin D repletion before initiating bisphosphonates to avoid hypocalcaemia.
  - Anabolic agents (teriparatide) can be considered for severe fragility and multiple fractures; assess contraindications (e.g., prior radiation) and logistic feasibility. Limited data in TBDs — multidisciplinary discussion advised.
  - Denosumab: potent antiresorptive option — remember rebound vertebral fracture risk if therapy is stopped; requires planning for transition to another agent. Consider liver status and infection risk.
  - Choice of agent must factor in hepatic synthetic function, thrombocytopenia, and bleeding risk (common in marrow failure), and potential need for future hematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT) which will influence timing.
- Medication safety in the setting of hepatic involvement:
  - Many osteoporosis/neuropathy medications are hepatically metabolised; check dosing and hepatotoxicity risk.
  - Avoid prolonged systemic glucocorticoids unless essential — they worsen bone loss and can aggravate infection risk in immunocompromised patients.
  - If considering androgen therapy (danazol/nandrolone) or other telomere‑targeted agents, involve hematology and hepatology — androgens may improve telomere biology for marrow disease but carry liver toxicity risk and can affect bone density variably.
  - For neuropathic pain, choose agents mindful of hepatic clearance (e.g., gabapentin/pregabalin are renally cleared and preferable if liver dysfunction present; many antidepressants/anticonvulsants need dose adjustments).
- Rehabilitation and symptom management:
  - Early physiotherapy and occupational therapy to maximise mobility, reduce fall risk and improve ADLs — critical when pulmonary limitation and neuropathy coexist.
  - Orthoses, walking aids, home hazard assessment, and bone health clinics can all reduce fracture risk.
  - Manage neuropathy with a multimodal approach: glucose control if diabetic, vitamin repletion, neuropathic pain agents, and targeted physiotherapy for balance and strength.
- Surveillance and multidisciplinary coordination:
  - Regular reassessment of bone density (intervals individualized — e.g., 1–2 years if rapid change or treatment initiation), calcium–vitamin D–PTH monitoring, and fall/fracture surveillance.
  - Integrate hematology (for marrow surveillance and HSCT planning), pulmonology (IPF follow‑up), hepatology (cirrhosis management and medication implications), endocrinology (bone and mineral metabolism), neurology (neuropathy management), and physiotherapy/OT.
  - Genetic counselling for the patient and family — TBDs can have variable penetrance and implications for relatives.
  - Discuss advanced care planning and transplant options early in progressive marrow or lung disease.

Special considerations tied to this case
- The patient’s severe vitamin B12 deficiency likely amplified marrow failure and neuropathy; correcting nutritional deficits produced symptom improvement but did not obviate the underlying telomere defect. Expect that bone and nerve disease may progress independent of nutritional correction.
- Hepatic fibrosis is well described in TBDs and was present here; it likely contributed to vitamin D deficiency and secondary hyperparathyroidism — both reversible contributors to bone fragility that must be aggressively managed.
- If hematopoietic stem cell transplant becomes necessary for progressive marrow failure, pre‑transplant bone health optimisation is important because transplant conditioning and prolonged immobilisation dramatically increase fracture risk.

Key take‑home points
- Think beyond blood and lungs: telomere biology disorders commonly involve bone (early osteoporosis, fragility fractures) and peripheral nerves (axonal neuropathy), especially when you see premature‑ageing features and multisystem disease.
- Evaluate bone health early: DEXA plus targeted labs (25‑OH D, PTH, calcium, phosphate) and treat vitamin D deficiency before starting anti‑osteoporotic therapies.
- Correct nutritional causes (B12, vitamin D) but recognise they may unmask an underlying TBD that requires ongoing surveillance.
- Management is multidisciplinary: balance fracture prevention, medication safety in hepatic disease, neuropathy treatment, rehab, and the timing/impact of potential curative therapies (HSCT).
- Screen for TBDs (telomere length/genetics) when young patients have unexplained osteoporosis, cirrhosis, pulmonary fibrosis, cytopenias or neuropathy — early diagnosis guides counselling, surveillance and treatment planning.

If you care for a young patient with fragility fractures or neuropathy plus unexplained cytopenia, liver fibrosis or lung fibrosis — don’t assume it’s “just” nutritional. Consider a telomere biology disorder early, and marshal hepatology, hematology, endocrine and neurology colleagues to optimise bone, nerve and systemic outcomes.
