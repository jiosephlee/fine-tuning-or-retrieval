Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Strategy: From Symptoms to Imaging and Sleep Testing

This patient’s diagnostic pathway illustrates how seemingly disparate complaints — long-standing occipital/vertex headache, progressive choking affecting solids and liquids, and unexplained episodes of profound desaturation without dyspnoea — were synthesized into a targeted neurological workup. The strategy relied on recognising “red flags” that suggested a unifying brainstem or craniocervical process, systematically excluding primary ENT and pulmonary causes, and then using imaging and sleep testing to confirm the suspected structural and functional abnormalities.

Red flags prompting neurological unification
Three clinical features in this case pointed away from isolated ENT or pulmonary disease and toward craniocervical pathology. First, the chronic occipital/vertex headaches that began after head trauma were atypical for primary headache disorders and suggested posterior fossa involvement. Second, the dysphagia was neurogenic in pattern — affecting solids and liquids equally and unaccompanied by structural lesions on endoscopy — which raises concern for lower cranial nerve or brainstem dysfunction. Third, recurrent episodes of marked hypoxaemia (SpO2 in the 70s–80s) that occurred without dyspnoea are characteristic of impaired automatic respiratory drive, implicating central respiratory control rather than primary lung disease. When these features coexist, clinicians should suspect a lesion at the cervicomedullary junction, including Chiari malformation type I (CMI).

Historical imaging re-examination: value and findings
A pivotal diagnostic step was retrieval and re-review of the non-contrast CT performed after the patient’s motor vehicle accident. Even though the scan had been originally read as normal, careful inspection demonstrated a crowded foramen magnum with loss of CSF space at the cranio-cervical junction and cerebellar tissue extending to the opisthion. These subtle signs can be overlooked on routine CT reads but are important clues because they frequently prompt dedicated MRI. This case underlines the value of re-examining prior cranial imaging when the clinical picture evolves — a previously “negative” scan may contain missed anatomic abnormalities that explain later symptoms.

Confirmatory MRI and what to evaluate
MRI is the diagnostic standard for CMI. In this patient, sagittal MRI confirmed 11 mm of cerebellar tonsillar descent below the foramen magnum, exceeding the commonly used radiographic threshold of ≥5 mm for adult CMI. Beyond measuring tonsillar descent, MRI assessment should include careful evaluation of the cervicomedullary junction for direct brainstem compression, the configuration of CSF spaces at the foramen magnum (obliteration or crowding supports symptomatic disease), and the spinal cord for associated syringomyelia. In this report, MRI demonstrated the significant descent and crowded CSF spaces consistent with symptomatic CMI; documentation of syringomyelia (if present) would further influence surgical decision-making, but was not a feature emphasised in this case.

Pulmonary workup to exclude intrinsic respiratory disease
Before attributing hypoxaemic episodes to central dysfunction, standard pulmonary investigations were performed and were essentially normal. Spirometry and lung volumes were within predicted ranges, and maximal inspiratory/expiratory pressures (MIP/MEP) were near-normal, arguing against restrictive or neuromuscular respiratory failure at baseline. Arterial blood gas obtained between events showed normal pH, PaCO2 and PaO2, indicating adequate daytime ventilation when awake and cooperative. Normal chest imaging and pulmonary function testing therefore excluded primary pulmonary causes and supported the hypothesis that desaturation events were intermittent and likely neurogenic.

Swallow assessment: role of videofluoroscopy
Given repeated normal upper endoscopy and laryngoscopy despite persistent choking, a videofluoroscopic (modified barium) swallowing study was indicated to evaluate dynamic, neurogenic causes of oropharyngeal dysphagia. Videofluoroscopy allows real-time assessment of bolus flow, timing of airway protection, and identification of penetration or aspiration that may not be apparent on static endoscopy. In neurogenic dysphagia, findings often include delayed swallow initiation, reduced laryngeal elevation, and silent aspiration — patterns that help localise dysfunction to brainstem or cranial nerve pathology, as suspected here.

Polysomnography approach and interpretation in this case
Sleep-disordered breathing was investigated using a type III home sleep study, which recorded airflow, respiratory effort, oxygen saturation and heart rate but lacked EEG-based sleep staging. This study reported an AHI of 6 events/hour with a predominance of central events (72%). Interpreting these results in the context of the patient’s clinical history requires caution: the high proportion of central events aligns with brainstem dysfunction from tonsillar herniation, supporting the diagnosis of acquired central hypoventilation. However, the absolute AHI value is likely an underestimate for two reasons discussed below.

Methodological caveat: limitations of type III studies
Type III devices cannot determine true sleep time because they lack EEG; they calculate AHI by dividing respiratory events by the entire recording time (including wakefulness), which lowers the denominator and underestimates event frequency per hour of sleep. In patients who have frequent arousals or fragmented sleep, this effect can be marked. Additionally, absent EEG prevents correlation of respiratory events with sleep stages (notably REM, when central control is most vulnerable) and prevents detection of associated cortical arousals. Thus, although the type III findings (predominantly central events and documented deep desaturations) strongly suggest central sleep-disordered breathing, the true severity and physiologic consequences are best characterised with full in-laboratory polysomnography.

Planned definitive testing: rationale for type I PSG with capnography and video
A full type I polysomnography is the appropriate next diagnostic step to quantify central hypoventilation and to guide management. Type I PSG provides EEG for accurate sleep staging and total sleep time, electrooculography and chin EMG to differentiate sleep stages, and additional respiratory channels. Adding end-tidal or transcutaneous capnography is essential to detect hypoventilation (sustained hypercapnia) that may not produce discrete apnoeas/hypopnoeas but causes significant ventilatory failure during sleep. Video recording helps correlate clinical events (apnoea without distress, choking episodes) with physiologic data. This combination will provide definitive evidence for automatic respiratory failure, help determine severity, and inform urgency of neurosurgical intervention or non-invasive ventilatory support.

When to suspect CMI in dysphagia or sleep-disordered breathing
This case reinforces that CMI should be considered when neurogenic dysphagia or unexplained central sleep-disordered breathing accompany posterior head pain or craniovertebral junction symptoms. Published series report a substantial prevalence of sleep-related breathing disorders in symptomatic CMI, and guidelines increasingly recommend polysomnography in CMI patients with suspected sleep-disordered breathing. Practically, CMI should be suspected and cranio-cervical imaging obtained when: dysphagia affects all consistencies without endoscopic lesion; lower cranial nerve signs are present; central apnoeas predominate on initial sleep testing; or profound desaturations occur without dyspnoea. Early recognition shortens diagnostic delay and permits timely consideration of posterior fossa decompression, which can reverse CSF flow obstruction and potentially restore automatic respiratory drive.

In summary, the diagnostic strategy in this case combined recognition of neurologic red flags, re-evaluation of prior imaging, confirmatory MRI focused on tonsillar descent and CSF spaces, exclusion of primary pulmonary disease, targeted dynamic swallowing assessment, and staged sleep testing — culminating in a plan for definitive type I polysomnography with capnography to characterise central hypoventilation and guide treatment.
