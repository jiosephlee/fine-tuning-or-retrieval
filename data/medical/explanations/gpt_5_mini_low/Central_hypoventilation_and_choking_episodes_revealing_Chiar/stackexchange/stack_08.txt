Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

### [Clinical Pearls] Which lower cranial nerve deficits are classically associated with symptomatic Chiari I, how do they produce choking and neurogenic dysphagia, and what videofluoroscopic swallow patterns help distinguish neurogenic from structural or reflux-related causes?

Which lower cranial nerves are involved — and why that produces choking and neurogenic dysphagia
- Symptomatic Chiari I commonly affects lower cranial nerve function through direct compression of the medulla and lower cranial nerve roots at the foramen magnum. The clinically relevant nerves are IX (glossopharyngeal), X (vagus, including nucleus ambiguus), and XII (hypoglossal); CN XI may be involved but is less central to swallowing.
- Pathophysiology:
  - CN IX provides pharyngeal sensation and contributes to the afferent limb of the gag and swallow reflex. Loss → delayed or absent swallow trigger and reduced sensation of pharyngeal residue, promoting silent aspiration.
  - CN X (nucleus ambiguus and vagal motor fibers) controls palate elevation, pharyngeal constrictors, laryngeal closure (vocal cord adduction/abduction) and cricopharyngeal relaxation. Dysfunction → palatal weakness (nasal regurgitation), impaired pharyngeal propulsion, incomplete laryngeal closure, cricopharyngeal achalasia or discoordination. These abnormalities produce coughing, choking, aspiration and inefficient bolus clearance.
  - CN XII controls tongue movement and bolus manipulation. Hypoglossal weakness → poor oral containment, posterior spillage and premature leakage into the pharynx, again increasing aspiration risk.
- How these generate the clinical picture in the case:
  - The patient had choking with solids and liquids equally, normal ENT and endoscopic structural exams, and features consistent with brainstem compression on MRI. This pattern (equal-consistency dysphagia, absent structural lesion) points to impaired swallow initiation, reduced pharyngeal contraction and impaired airway protection from IX/X/XII dysfunction caused by tonsillar descent compressing medullary nuclei and roots.

Videofluoroscopic (modified barium swallow) patterns that distinguish neurogenic from structural or reflux-related causes
- Key neurogenic VFSS features
  - Delayed swallow initiation (latency between bolus arrival at the vallecula and pharyngeal swallow).
  - Reduced hyolaryngeal elevation and anterior excursion (impaired airway protection and upper‑esophageal sphincter [UES] opening).
  - Pharyngeal residue in the valleculae and pyriform sinuses (reflects weak pharyngeal contraction).
  - Incomplete/late laryngeal vestibule closure and penetration or frank aspiration; often silent because of impaired sensation (loss of afferent IX/X).
  - Cricopharyngeal dysfunction/achalasia pattern: persistent UES narrowing or “CP bar” with stasis above the UES.
  - Equal impairment for thin liquids and solids (both affected because motor coordination, not luminal calibre, is the problem).
- Features favoring structural (mechanical) obstruction
  - Focal, reproducible bolus hold-up at a specific level (e.g., pharyngeal pouch, web, strictures) with greater difficulty for solids than liquids.
  - Flow-limiting narrowing visualized as a discrete luminal constriction; distal bolus cannot pass until larger intrabolus pressure is generated.
  - Clearance improves with changing bolus consistency (liquids may pass when solids do not).
- Features suggesting reflux‑related laryngopharyngeal disease
  - Laryngeal and pharyngeal mucosal inflammation on endoscopy (arytenoid/interarytenoid edema, posterior laryngitis) rather than a primary motor deficit.
  - On VFSS, you may see normal bolus propulsion pattern but delayed clearance due to mucosal edema, and possible microaspiration with thin liquids; reflux per se is often better demonstrated with pH/impedance testing.
- Practical diagnostic clues (apply to the case)
  - Equal difficulty with solids and liquids, repeated normal laryngoscopy/endoscopy, oxygen desaturations and brainstem compression on MRI together point to neurogenic dysphagia from lower cranial nerve/medullary dysfunction rather than a structural oesophago‑laryngeal lesion or simple reflux — exactly the pattern in this patient.
  - The presence of silent aspiration or poor cough despite significant aspiration on VFSS should raise suspicion for impaired pharyngeal sensation (CN IX/X involvement) from brainstem pathology.

Clinical pearl
- When patients have choking or coughing with both solids and liquids, normal structural studies (flexible laryngoscopy, endoscopy), and additional brainstem signs (headache, respiratory dysregulation, nocturnal desaturation), think neurogenic dysphagia from lower cranial nerve dysfunction (IX/X/XII). A targeted videofluoroscopic swallow study demonstrating delayed swallow initiation, reduced hyolaryngeal excursion, pharyngeal residue and cricopharyngeal dysfunction will distinguish neurogenic failure from mechanical obstruction or reflux-related mucosal disease and should prompt neuroimaging to evaluate for Chiari I or other brainstem pathology.
