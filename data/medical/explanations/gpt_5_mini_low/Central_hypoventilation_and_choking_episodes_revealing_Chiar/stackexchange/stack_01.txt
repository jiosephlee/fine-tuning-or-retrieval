Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

### [Clinical Pearls] How is Chiari malformation type I defined on imaging, what are key MRI sequences and ancillary findings you would obtain (for example, CSF flow studies, syringomyelia), and how can a 'crowded foramen magnum' on CT raise suspicion when MRI is not yet available?

Bottom line
- Chiari I is defined radiographically by caudal displacement of the cerebellar tonsils ≥5 mm below the foramen magnum on midline sagittal MRI. Imaging must be interpreted in clinical context because mild descent can be a normal variant; symptomatic Chiari (Chiari syndrome) requires correlation with signs/symptoms and ancillary findings such as brainstem compression or syrinx.

How to measure and report tonsillar descent
- Use a midline sagittal image and a standard foramen‑magnum reference line (commonly the McRae line: basion → opisthion). Measure the distance from the lowest point of the tonsils to that line.
- Thresholds: ≥5 mm is the classic cutoff for Chiari I; greater descent (as in this case, 11 mm) correlates more strongly with symptomatic compression.
- Also describe tonsil morphology (peg/elongated), degree of crowding at the cisterna magna, and any medullary kinking.

Essential MRI sequences and what to look for
- Sagittal T1 and T2: define tonsillar position, posterior fossa size, cisterna magna effacement, basilar invagination/odontoid retroflexion and gross brainstem compression.
- Axial and parasagittal T2 (or 3D CISS/FIESTA): evaluate tonsils, cerebellar folia, brainstem signal abnormality, lower cranial nerve region, and syrinx in the cervical cord.
- Cervical spine sagittal T2 (extend at least to upper thoracic cord): look carefully for syringomyelia or syringobulbia (a key ancillary finding that changes management urgency).
- Cine phase‑contrast (PC) MRI CSF flow studies: assess CSF flow at the foramen magnum—flow obstruction/abnormal pulsatile dynamics supports pathophysiologic significance and helps surgical planning.
- Optional/adjunct: volumetric posterior fossa measurements when available (helpful in research or complex cases), contrast if concern for other pathology, diffusion or brainstem sequences if ischemia/edema is suspected.

Important ancillary findings to report
- Syringomyelia/syringobulbia and its cranio‑caudal extent.
- Obliteration of the cisterna magna or crowded subarachnoid space at the craniocervical junction.
- Brainstem compression, signal change within the medulla/upper spinal cord (edema/gliosis).
- Bony abnormalities of the craniovertebral junction (occipitalization of C1, platybasia, basilar invagination, retroflexed odontoid) that may coexist.
- Hydrocephalus or other intracranial pathology.

Why these matters for clinical care
- The presence of syrinx, CSF flow obstruction on cine‑PC, or progressive neurological/respiratory symptoms (as in this patient with episodic severe desaturations and bulbar dysfunction) increases urgency for neurosurgical evaluation and often favors posterior fossa decompression.
- Cine‑PC can predict restoration of flow after decompression and is used in preoperative planning.

When MRI is not yet available: what a CT can show and when it should raise suspicion
- CT is insensitive for soft‑tissue detail but may still suggest CMI when there is:
  - Visible cerebellar tissue extending to or below the level of the opisthion/basion on sagittal CT soft‑tissue windows (loss of normal CSF space at the foramen magnum), i.e., a “crowded foramen magnum.”
  - Effacement of the cisterna magna or obliteration of cerebellomedullary cisterns.
  - Small posterior fossa dimensions or reduced posterior fossa volume and displacement of neural tissue toward the foramen magnum.
  - Coexisting bony craniovertebral anomalies (basilar invagination, occipital condyle anomalies, fused atlas) that predispose to tonsillar descent.
- In the presented case a historical noncontrast CT showed a crowded foramen magnum with cerebellar tissue at the level of the opisthion and loss of CSF space—findings that should prompt expedited MRI because they reflect probable significant craniocervical junction crowding and correlate with the later MRI finding of 11 mm tonsillar descent and brainstem compression.

Practical recommendation for work‑up based on this case
- If clinical features suggest Chiari (occipital Valsalva headaches, lower cranial nerve signs, unexplained dysphagia, central sleep‑disordered breathing or unexplained desaturations), obtain MRI brain and cervical spine with:
  - Midline sagittal and axial T1/T2, high‑resolution 3D sequence (CISS/FIESTA) and cine phase‑contrast CSF flow studies.
- If only CT is available and shows crowding/obliteration of cisternal spaces or tonsillar tissue near the opisthion, consider that a red flag—expedite MRI and neurosurgical consultation rather than attributing symptoms to non‑neurologic causes alone (as delayed diagnosis occurred for 14 years in this patient).

Teaching pearl
- Imaging criteria (≥5 mm tonsillar descent) provide a radiologic threshold, but clinical decision‑making depends on symptoms and ancillary findings (syrinx, CSF flow obstruction, brainstem compression). A crowded foramen magnum on CT is a reliable early clue to pursue MRI urgently when clinical features are compatible — exactly what a careful re‑review of this patient’s old CT accomplished.
