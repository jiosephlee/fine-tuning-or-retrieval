Title: Blog about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

## Don’t Trust the First Read: Mining Old Scans for a Crowded Foramen Magnum

What grabbed me about this case was how a 14‑year odyssey of choking, nocturnal desaturation and hair‑trigger occipital pain boiled down to a missed radiologic clue on a trauma CT. A woman in her early 40s developed new headaches after a minor motor vehicle collision and over the next decade developed progressive bulbar symptoms and episodes of profound, asymptomatic desaturation. Repeated ENT and GI evaluations were normal. A home pulse oximeter and an embarrassing airport spell finally pushed clinicians to re‑look at her old non‑contrast CT — and a crowded foramen magnum was hiding in plain sight. MRI confirmed Chiari I with 11 mm tonsillar descent and a sleep study showed predominantly central events. This is the kind of case that should make you reach for old images before ordering more tests.

Why this matters
- Chiari I can be subtle on routine trauma CTs and commonly overlooked when the scan was obtained for something else (head trauma, syncope). The radiologist is often focused on acute hemorrhage or fractures, not posterior fossa crowding.
- The clinical presentations are protean: classic Valsalva‑related occipital headaches are common, but lower cranial nerve dysfunction (dysphagia, choking), sleep‑disordered breathing and even acquired central hypoventilation can be the dominant features.
- Missing a structural cause of progressive bulbar/respiratory dysfunction isn’t just an intellectual miss — it delays potentially disease‑modifying interventions (posterior fossa decompression can reverse symptoms in some patients).

High‑yield radiologic cues to hunt for on legacy imaging
- Tonsillar descent threshold: by convention, ≥5 mm of cerebellar tonsillar descent below the foramen magnum on midline sagittal MRI supports Chiari I. But don’t treat this as an absolute rule — symptomatic patients can have smaller descent and asymptomatic people can have more.
- CSF space effacement at the craniocervical junction: look for loss or narrowing of the subarachnoid CSF cisterns around the foramen magnum on sagittal and axial images. Crowding with obliteration of CSF around the tonsils is a red flag even when descent measurements are borderline.
- Position of the tonsils relative to the opisthion/basion line and clivus: the tonsillar tip at or below the level of the opisthion is suspicious; any descent approaching the level of C1 warrants attention.
- Posterior fossa size and morphology: a small posterior fossa (reduced occipital bone‑to‑foramen magnum volume) predisposes to crowding. On CT you can look qualitatively for a “tight” posterior fossa; on MRI there are validated volumetric measures but these are not needed at the bedside — asymmetry, compression, and effacement are practical signs.
- Ventral brainstem compression and syrinx: inspect the upper cervical cord for syringomyelia and the ventral subarachnoid space for crowding. Brainstem compression explains respiratory and lower cranial nerve findings.
- Dynamic features (if available): cine MRI CSF flow studies can show obstructed CSF pulsatility at the foramen magnum — this strengthens the case for a symptomatic lesion.

Practical approach to re‑reviewing old CT/MRI when the clinical story doesn’t add up
1. Pull the prior images rather than relying on the prior report. You are looking for posterior fossa detail that may have been a low priority at the time of the original read.
2. Reconstruct sagittal images if only axial slices are present (many PACS systems can reformat CTs). The midline sagittal view is essential.
3. Use a systematic checklist while you scroll:
   - Midline sagittal: measure tonsillar tip relative to foramen magnum; assess CSF space anterior and posterior to the tonsils.
   - Axial at the level of foramen magnum: look for circumferential CSF effacement.
   - Upper cervical cord: syrinx? cord signal changes?
   - Bony landmarks: occipital bone morphology, evidence of prior fractures or basilar invagination.
4. Contextualise measurements with symptoms: 5 mm is a guideline. In this case the patient had 11 mm descent and clear physiological consequences (central apnoeas/desats), so the imaging and clinical picture aligned.
5. Don’t forget to compare sides: asymmetry may guide the surgeon and help explain unilateral signs.

How to frame the conversation with radiology (so they actually look)
- Send a focused, brief clinical question: e.g., “Patient with progressive nocturnal choking episodes, unexplained desaturations, and prior head CT after trauma — please re‑review prior CT and current MRI for cerebellar tonsillar descent, effacement of subarachnoid CSF at the foramen magnum, and syrinx. If MRI not yet performed, recommend sagittal T1/T2, axial T2 and cine CSF flow sequences.”
- Include the clinical red flags in one line: “bulbar dysfunction + asymptomatic severe desaturations + chronic occipital/vertex pain” — that pushes posterior fossa assessment up the priority list.
- Ask explicitly for sagittal midline measurements and comment on CSF space (effaced/normal), and request cine CSF flow MRI if surgical planning or physiologic uncertainty exists.
- If the prior report is old and did not comment on the posterior fossa, request a secondary read or neuroradiology overread — many centers will prioritize an overread for potentially surgically relevant findings.

What to request on a targeted MRI when you suspect Chiari I
- Essential sequences: sagittal T1 and T2 of the brain and cervicomedullary junction; axial T2 through the foramen magnum and cervical cord; sagittal/coronal T2 of the posterior fossa.
- Optional but highly useful: cine phase‑contrast CSF flow studies through the foramen magnum; cervical cord T2 for syrinx; high‑resolution 3D T2 or constructive interference in steady state (CISS) for small compressive lesions.
- Sleep/respiratory correlation: if respiratory symptoms are prominent, ask for formal polysomnography with capnography and end‑tidal CO₂ — type III studies can miss central hypoventilation because they lack EEG to define sleep time.

Clinical pearls from this case
- Don’t anchor on negative laryngoscopy/endoscopy for persistent choking; equal difficulty with solids and liquids with normal structural studies suggests a neurogenic cause.
- Asymptomatic desaturation is a game‑changer. “I forgot to breathe” spells, especially without dyspnoea, point toward brainstem dysfunction and mandate neuroimaging review.
- Old trauma imaging isn’t sacrosanct: scans reported “normal” years ago can hide posterior fossa crowding that was outside the original reading scope.
- A low AHI on a type III study does not exclude clinically important central hypoventilation — lack of EEG inflates denominator and lowers the index. If suspicion remains, get a full polysomnogram with capnography.
- Posterior fossa decompression can be reversible for respiratory and bulbar symptoms, so identifying candidates matters.

Take‑home messages
- If the clinical picture (choking, bulbar dysfunction, unexplained nocturnal desaturation, occipital headaches) doesn’t add up, re‑review prior head/neck imaging with a focused posterior fossa checklist rather than accepting the original “trauma CT normal” read.
- On sagittal imaging, look beyond the 5 mm rule: assess CSF effacement at the foramen magnum, posterior fossa crowding and any cord syrinx; any of these with compatible symptoms should prompt MRI with cine CSF flow and neuroradiology input.
- When you contact radiology, be concise and specific: state the red flags, request midline sagittal measurements, comment on CSF spaces, and ask for a neuroradiology overread if needed.
- For respiratory symptoms suspecting central hypoventilation, pursue a type I polysomnogram with CO₂ monitoring — type III studies can substantially underestimate central events.
- Mining old scans is a low‑cost, high‑yield step that can alter diagnosis and management — don’t skip it.

This patient’s story reminds us that the first read is not always the last word. Sometimes the key to a complex clinical puzzle is already sitting in PACS — we just need to know where and how to look.
