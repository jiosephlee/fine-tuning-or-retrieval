Title: Blog about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

## The Silent Desaturator: Central Hypoventilation in Chiari I

What makes this case interesting?
A woman in her early 40s spent 14 years chasing a diagnosis for paroxysmal choking, scalp allodynia and debilitating occipital/vertex pain. She only discovered she was intermittently desaturating to the 70s–80s by chance with a home pulse oximeter during the COVID era and on one occasion at an airport. Multiple ENT and GI exams were normal. A re-review of a 14‑year‑old head CT revealed a crowded foramen magnum; MRI confirmed Chiari I with 11 mm tonsillar descent. Type III sleep testing showed predominantly central events. Clinically she had preserved voluntary breathing but apparently lost her automatic drive and even the sense of air hunger. In short: Chiari I masquerading as acquired central hypoventilation — the “silent desaturator” or an Ondine’s‑curse phenotype caused by brainstem compression.

Why this matters
Most clinicians think about Chiari I as a headache disorder or a cause of syringomyelia and upper‑extremity signs. Respiratory manifestations are less appreciated and can be striking: profound nocturnal or situational hypoxaemia without dyspnoea, central apnoea predominance, and bulbar dysfunction (dysphagia, choking) many years after a seemingly minor head injury. Missing this diagnosis can lead to years of unnecessary testing and social disability. Importantly, posterior fossa decompression can reverse the physiological problem in many patients — so identifying structural causes matters.

Quick primer — brainstem control of automatic breathing (relevant to the case)
Automatic breathing is driven by brainstem respiratory centres (pre‑Bötzinger complex, dorsal and ventral respiratory groups, chemoreceptor integration in the medulla/pons). These centres generate rhythmic output and respond to chemical (PaCO2, PaO2) and mechanical afferents. Voluntary breathing uses cortical pathways and can bypass automatic centres, which is why patients with brainstem dysfunction often can take a deep breath on command yet fail to maintain adequate ventilation during sleep or distraction. Interruption of ascending afferents or of brainstem pattern generators can also blunt the perception of dyspnoea — the patient literally doesn’t “feel” that they’re hypoxic.

How Chiari I produces this phenotype
- Tonsillar descent or crowding at the foramen magnum can compress medullary structures and cranial nerve nuclei, disrupt CSF flow, and injure respiratory pattern generators and chemoreceptor pathways.
- This produces a selective failure of automatic respiration (sleep‑related hypoventilation/central apnoeas) while sparing cortically mediated voluntary breathing.
- Interruption of afferent pathways to cortical interoceptive centres may blunt dyspnoea perception, explaining profound desaturation without subjective distress — as seen in this patient at the airport and on home oximetry.

Distinguishing central hypoventilation from obstructive sleep apnea (clinically and practically)
Clinically: obstructive events are characterized by noisy snoring, witnessed apneas with paradoxical effort, daytime sleepiness and arousals. Central hypoventilation presents with quiet pauses in breathing, fewer arousals, and a mismatch of severe desaturation with minimal sleep fragmentation or subjective sleepiness; there may also be bulbar signs like dysphagia and choking without anatomical airway obstruction.

Investigations: type I polysomnography (full PSG) with EEG, respiratory channels and transcutaneous/end‑tidal CO2 monitoring is essential when you suspect central events. Type III devices (like in this case) can under‑estimate severity because they lack EEG — the AHI will be diluted by recording time that includes wakefulness. Documented profound nocturnal desaturations, a high proportion of central events, and elevated nocturnal CO2 are red flags for central hypoventilation.

Practical detection in real-world settings (lessons from the case)
- Home pulse oximetry can reveal otherwise asymptomatic, dangerous desaturations — a practical first clue. The patient’s repeated 70s–80s values triggered further workup.
- Pay attention to atypical features: choking affecting solids and liquids equally (suggests neurogenic dysphagia), multiple normal ENT/GI studies, and coexisting occipital headaches or scalp allodynia.
- Don’t assume anxiety when patients repeatedly present with low saturations but minimal respiratory distress. Validate objective data and pursue neurological causes if pulmonary/ENT workup is unrevealing.

Essential workup pathway when you suspect brainstem‑mediated central hypoventilation
- Full neurological exam with attention to lower cranial nerve function and signs of myelopathy.
- High‑resolution MRI of the brain and cranio‑cervical junction (sagittal and axial) to assess tonsillar descent, foramen magnum crowding, syrinx, and CSF flow. CT can miss crowding — re‑review old imaging if the history suggests.
- Formal type I polysomnography with EEG and CO2 monitoring (end‑tidal or transcutaneous) to quantify central apnoea index and nocturnal hypoventilation.
- Pulmonary function tests including maximal inspiratory/expiratory pressures to rule out primary respiratory muscle weakness.
- Consider arterial blood gas or daytime CO2 monitoring if hypoventilation suspected.

Management considerations (grounded in the case)
- Treat reversible contributors and optimise safety: nocturnal supplemental oxygen may temporise but can mask hypoventilation and worsen hypercapnia — use with caution and only after respiratory physiology assessment.
- CPAP helps obstructive physiology but may be inadequate for central hypoventilation; adaptive servo‑ventilation or bilevel support with backup rate may be necessary for central hypoventilation pending definitive therapy.
- Neurosurgical evaluation for posterior fossa decompression is indicated when symptomatic brainstem compression is present; many patients have improvement or reversal of respiratory symptoms after decompression.
- When patients defer surgery (as this patient did), implement structured monitoring (regular oximetry/clinical review, expedited PSG/CO2 testing) and safety plans for severe events.

Clinical pearls from this case
- Chiari I can present predominantly with respiratory or bulbar symptoms and cause an acquired central hypoventilation phenotype.
- Patients may desaturate into the 70s–80s without dyspnoea due to disrupted afferent interoceptive signalling — “silent desaturation.”
- Type III sleep studies can substantially underestimate event severity in central disorders; full PSG with CO2 monitoring is the diagnostic gold standard.
- Re‑examine old imaging when the clinical story changes — subtle craniocervical crowding can be missed on initial CT reads.
- Neurogenic dysphagia (difficulty with solids and liquids equally, normal endoscopy) should prompt neurological imaging, including MRI of the cranio‑cervical junction.

Take‑home messages
- Consider Chiari I and other structural brainstem lesions in adults with unexplained nocturnal or episodic severe desaturation, especially when ENT/GI and pulmonary evaluations are unrevealing.
- Preserve suspicion for central hypoventilation when patients have preserved voluntary breathing, bulbar signs (dysphagia/choking), predominance of central events on limited testing, or documented asymptomatic desaturations.
- Use full polysomnography with CO2 monitoring to confirm central hypoventilation; beware of underestimation with home or type III studies.
- Early recognition matters: neurosurgical decompression can be disease‑modifying and, in some cases, life‑saving.

Final thought
This case is a powerful reminder to integrate respiratory physiology, sleep testing limitations and neuroanatomy into our diagnostic thinking. When patients “forget to breathe” without air hunger, think brainstem — and look for Chiari.
