Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

## Case Overview and Clinical Phenotype

This patient’s course is best understood as a slowly evolving, multi‑domain syndrome that links craniocervical pathology to bulbar and respiratory dysfunction rather than as separate ear–nose–throat or primary pulmonary disorders. The timeline is instructive: shortly after a minor motor vehicle collision 14 years ago she developed persistent occipital/vertex headaches and scalp allodynia. Only after several years did intermittent choking episodes begin and then progressively worsen over a seven‑year period, ultimately becoming frequent at night and sometimes associated with profound, asymptomatic oxygen desaturation. This chronology—headache soon after head trauma followed by delayed onset of bulbar and respiratory symptoms—raised the possibility of a structural lesion at the foramen magnum or brainstem that either pre‑existed and was unmasked by trauma or was acquired as a delayed consequence of the injury.

The choking episodes themselves have several features that point away from a primary ENT or mechanical swallowing disorder and towards a neurogenic cause. They are unpredictable, occur both during meals and during sleep, and affect solids and liquids equally. The equal involvement of all consistencies suggests neuromuscular dysphagia rather than obstructive or structural lesions of the oropharynx or oesophagus. The episodes were distressing and socially disabling—she avoided public eating situations and developed significant anxiety and isolation—yet repeated flexible laryngoscopies and upper gastrointestinal endoscopies were normal, making an isolated ENT or GI explanation unlikely and prompting consideration of a central cause.

The most striking physiological finding was intermittent, often silent, hypoxaemia. She documented repeated home pulse oximetry readings into the 70%–80% range and experienced an airport episode with SpO2 78% that promptly normalised with supplemental oxygen and without subjective dyspnoea. These features—profound desaturation without air‑hunger—and the predominance of nocturnal events fit a pattern of impaired automatic respiratory control. Clinically this presents as preserved voluntary breathing (she could breathe normally and perform voluntary respiratory maneuvers when awake and instructed) with failure of the brainstem‑mediated automatic drive, particularly during sleep or when attention lapses. This phenotype resembles acquired central hypoventilation and suggests dysfunction of medullary respiratory centres or their afferent pathways at the craniocervical junction.

Objective testing between events was largely unremarkable, which further supports an intermittent, central problem rather than chronic pulmonary or neuromuscular disease. Baseline vital signs were normal; pulmonary function testing showed near‑normal FEV1 and FVC and only mildly reduced respiratory pressures; an arterial blood gas on room air was within reference limits. These results argue against persistent global ventilatory failure or intrinsic lung disease and are consistent with episodic central hypoventilation superimposed on otherwise preserved respiratory mechanics. The limited sleep study available (type III) reported a low overall AHI but with a predominance of central events; its methodological limitations likely underestimated true sleep‑related respiratory pathology.

Her headache phenotype deserves separate attention because it was the earliest and most chronic symptom. The pain was localized to the occiput and vertex, constant with marked cutaneous allodynia such that even hair weight provoked symptoms; she found symptomatic relief by cutting her hair very short. Differential considerations include nummular headache, occipital neuralgia, or the less well‑characterised burning vertex syndrome. In the context of craniocervical pathology, occipital neuralgia related to posterior fossa crowding or C1‑C2 irritation is plausible, but the precise relationship remains uncertain. Clinically, the presence of a long‑standing, focal occipital/vertex pain with cutaneous hypersensitivity in a patient who later develops bulbar and brainstem signs should raise suspicion for posterior fossa or foramen magnum structural disease.

Putting these elements together yields a concise problem representation: a middle‑aged woman with chronic occipital/vertex headache and scalp allodynia, progressive bulbar dysfunction manifested as neurogenic dysphagia and choking, and episodes of sleep‑related or attention‑dependent automatic breathing failure with preserved voluntary ventilation and intermittent, often silent, hypoxaemia. This cluster of findings is most parsimoniously explained by craniocervical junction pathology causing cerebellar tonsillar descent and brainstem compression (Chiari malformation type I), which can simultaneously produce occipital pain, lower cranial nerve dysfunction, and disruption of medullary respiratory control. Recognising this unified neuroanatomical explanation is critical because it redirects the diagnostic workup from repeated ENT and GI procedures toward neuroimaging of the posterior fossa and, when indicated, neurosurgical consideration for posterior fossa decompression to relieve brainstem compression and potentially reverse the respiratory and bulbar manifestations.

## Anatomy and Pathophysiology: CMI and Neuro-respiratory Control

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is defined radiologically by caudal displacement of the cerebellar tonsils at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum on mid‑sagittal MRI. Contemporary imaging series identify this anatomical finding in roughly 1% of adults, but only a small minority—on the order of 0.1%—become symptomatic. There is a modest female predominance. Recognising the difference between imaging prevalence and symptomatic disease is essential for clinical decision‑making: anatomic tonsillar descent exists on a spectrum from incidental variant to clinically significant malformation, and the presence of symptoms (rather than the degree of descent alone) guides management.

The classic pathophysiological model for symptomatic CMI centres on a volumetric mismatch between posterior fossa contents and the bony posterior fossa. A congenitally small posterior fossa or acquired reductions in available space permit crowding at the foramen magnum, forcing cerebellar tonsils and sometimes other posterior fossa structures caudally. This produces loss of normal subarachnoid cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) spaces at the cranio‑cervical junction and impairs CSF flow dynamics. Importantly, the degree of tonsillar descent correlates imperfectly with symptoms: similar radiological appearances may be clinically silent in some patients but cause marked brainstem dysfunction in others, reflecting individual anatomical variation and dynamic factors (for example, CSF flow obstruction or postural changes).

Anatomically, the craniocervical junction contains structures that are critical for both cranial nerve function and respiratory control. The caudal posterior fossa abuts the dorsal and ventral medulla. Within this region lie the nuclei of lower cranial nerves IX–XII (nucleus ambiguus, hypoglossal nucleus and related motor and sensory nuclei) and key components of the respiratory network: the dorsal respiratory group (including nucleus tractus solitarius, NTS), ventral respiratory group, and elements implicated in rhythm generation such as the pre‑Bötzinger complex. A mass effect or downward traction from tonsillar herniation can compress these nuclei and adjacent white matter pathways, disturb local perfusion, and distort afferent and efferent tracts that subserve bulbar function and respiratory automaticity.

Respiratory dysfunction in CMI arises by several complementary mechanisms. Direct mechanical compression of medullary respiratory centres can reduce the intrinsic rhythmic drive to the spinal motor neurons that control the diaphragm and accessory muscles. Obstruction of CSF flow at the foramen magnum may produce transient pressure gradients and altered intracranial compliance that further stress medullary structures. In addition, distortion of cranial nerve nuclei and their afferent chemoreceptor or mechanoreceptor pathways (for example, carotid body afferents synapsing in the NTS) can blunt ventilatory responses to hypercapnia and hypoxaemia. These combined effects can produce intermittent or sustained failure of automatic respiratory control, particularly during sleep or periods when cortical (voluntary) inputs are reduced.

This pathophysiology explains the characteristic dissociation between voluntary and automatic breathing often seen in CMI. Voluntary breathing is mediated by corticospinal and corticobulbar pathways descending from motor cortex to spinal phrenic motor neurons and cranial nerve motor nuclei; these cortical pathways often remain intact even when the medulla is compressed. Consequently, patients can consciously take deep breaths or maintain minute ventilation while awake and attentive, yet lose the automatic, brainstem‑mediated drive to breathe during sleep or when attention is diverted. Clinically this manifests as preserved voluntary respiratory function with apnoeic or hypoventilatory episodes during sleep or distraction—an acquired phenotype that closely mimics the clinical picture described in classical “Ondine’s curse.”

The absence of dyspnoea during severe desaturation, as occurred in this case, can be understood through interruption of afferent interoceptive pathways. Perception of breathlessness requires intact chemoreceptive signalling and ascending visceral afferents relayed through the NTS and thalamus to higher cortical centres such as the anterior insula and anterior cingulate. Compression or disruption of these brainstem relay pathways can prevent afferent signals from reaching cortical interoceptive networks, producing “silent” hypoxaemia—severe oxygen desaturation without the subjective sensation of air hunger or dyspnoea. This mechanism helps explain why patients with brainstem lesions may not mount an appropriate ventilatory or behavioural response to hypoxaemia.

Bulbar dysfunction in CMI commonly involves the lower cranial nerves and their central nuclei, producing neurogenic dysphagia that typically affects solids and liquids equally. When swallowing failure arises from impairment of the sensorimotor coordination of the pharynx and upper oesophageal sphincter rather than a mechanical obstructive lesion, the clinical pattern is one of global swallowing difficulty across consistencies. This neurogenic pattern aligns with the normal laryngoscopic and endoscopic studies described in the case; structural examinations may be unrevealing because the problem is impaired neural control rather than a visible mucosal or luminal lesion.

It is important to distinguish acquired central hypoventilation secondary to structural brainstem compression from congenital central hypoventilation syndrome (CCHS). CCHS, typically caused by PHOX2B mutations, presents in infancy with lifelong failure of autonomic respiratory drive and a striking absence of chemoreflexes. By contrast, CMI‑related hypoventilation is an acquired, structural disorder that can appear later in life and, crucially, may be reversible with surgical decompression of the posterior fossa. Recognising CMI as a potentially correctable cause of central hypoventilation has practical implications: targeted imaging and neurosurgical consultation are indicated when unexplained central respiratory dysfunction coexists with compatible posterior fossa findings or bulbar signs.

In sum, CMI can produce a constellation of bulbar and respiratory manifestations through direct medullary compression, disturbed CSF flow, and disrupted afferent chemoreceptive pathways. These effects preferentially impair automatic brainstem respiratory control and interoceptive signalling while sparing voluntary cortical breathing, leading to intermittent, and at times life‑threatening, hypoventilation that can masquerade as acquired Ondine’s curse. Awareness of these anatomic and physiologic relationships is essential for interpreting the patient’s choking, asymptomatic desaturation episodes, and selectively abnormal sleep‑study findings, and for considering posterior fossa decompression as a potentially restorative intervention.

## Diagnostic Strategy: From Symptoms to Imaging and Sleep Testing

This patient’s diagnostic pathway illustrates how seemingly disparate complaints — long-standing occipital/vertex headache, progressive choking affecting solids and liquids, and unexplained episodes of profound desaturation without dyspnoea — were synthesized into a targeted neurological workup. The strategy relied on recognising “red flags” that suggested a unifying brainstem or craniocervical process, systematically excluding primary ENT and pulmonary causes, and then using imaging and sleep testing to confirm the suspected structural and functional abnormalities.

Red flags prompting neurological unification
Three clinical features in this case pointed away from isolated ENT or pulmonary disease and toward craniocervical pathology. First, the chronic occipital/vertex headaches that began after head trauma were atypical for primary headache disorders and suggested posterior fossa involvement. Second, the dysphagia was neurogenic in pattern — affecting solids and liquids equally and unaccompanied by structural lesions on endoscopy — which raises concern for lower cranial nerve or brainstem dysfunction. Third, recurrent episodes of marked hypoxaemia (SpO2 in the 70s–80s) that occurred without dyspnoea are characteristic of impaired automatic respiratory drive, implicating central respiratory control rather than primary lung disease. When these features coexist, clinicians should suspect a lesion at the cervicomedullary junction, including Chiari malformation type I (CMI).

Historical imaging re-examination: value and findings
A pivotal diagnostic step was retrieval and re-review of the non-contrast CT performed after the patient’s motor vehicle accident. Even though the scan had been originally read as normal, careful inspection demonstrated a crowded foramen magnum with loss of CSF space at the cranio-cervical junction and cerebellar tissue extending to the opisthion. These subtle signs can be overlooked on routine CT reads but are important clues because they frequently prompt dedicated MRI. This case underlines the value of re-examining prior cranial imaging when the clinical picture evolves — a previously “negative” scan may contain missed anatomic abnormalities that explain later symptoms.

Confirmatory MRI and what to evaluate
MRI is the diagnostic standard for CMI. In this patient, sagittal MRI confirmed 11 mm of cerebellar tonsillar descent below the foramen magnum, exceeding the commonly used radiographic threshold of ≥5 mm for adult CMI. Beyond measuring tonsillar descent, MRI assessment should include careful evaluation of the cervicomedullary junction for direct brainstem compression, the configuration of CSF spaces at the foramen magnum (obliteration or crowding supports symptomatic disease), and the spinal cord for associated syringomyelia. In this report, MRI demonstrated the significant descent and crowded CSF spaces consistent with symptomatic CMI; documentation of syringomyelia (if present) would further influence surgical decision-making, but was not a feature emphasised in this case.

Pulmonary workup to exclude intrinsic respiratory disease
Before attributing hypoxaemic episodes to central dysfunction, standard pulmonary investigations were performed and were essentially normal. Spirometry and lung volumes were within predicted ranges, and maximal inspiratory/expiratory pressures (MIP/MEP) were near-normal, arguing against restrictive or neuromuscular respiratory failure at baseline. Arterial blood gas obtained between events showed normal pH, PaCO2 and PaO2, indicating adequate daytime ventilation when awake and cooperative. Normal chest imaging and pulmonary function testing therefore excluded primary pulmonary causes and supported the hypothesis that desaturation events were intermittent and likely neurogenic.

Swallow assessment: role of videofluoroscopy
Given repeated normal upper endoscopy and laryngoscopy despite persistent choking, a videofluoroscopic (modified barium) swallowing study was indicated to evaluate dynamic, neurogenic causes of oropharyngeal dysphagia. Videofluoroscopy allows real-time assessment of bolus flow, timing of airway protection, and identification of penetration or aspiration that may not be apparent on static endoscopy. In neurogenic dysphagia, findings often include delayed swallow initiation, reduced laryngeal elevation, and silent aspiration — patterns that help localise dysfunction to brainstem or cranial nerve pathology, as suspected here.

Polysomnography approach and interpretation in this case
Sleep-disordered breathing was investigated using a type III home sleep study, which recorded airflow, respiratory effort, oxygen saturation and heart rate but lacked EEG-based sleep staging. This study reported an AHI of 6 events/hour with a predominance of central events (72%). Interpreting these results in the context of the patient’s clinical history requires caution: the high proportion of central events aligns with brainstem dysfunction from tonsillar herniation, supporting the diagnosis of acquired central hypoventilation. However, the absolute AHI value is likely an underestimate for two reasons discussed below.

Methodological caveat: limitations of type III studies
Type III devices cannot determine true sleep time because they lack EEG; they calculate AHI by dividing respiratory events by the entire recording time (including wakefulness), which lowers the denominator and underestimates event frequency per hour of sleep. In patients who have frequent arousals or fragmented sleep, this effect can be marked. Additionally, absent EEG prevents correlation of respiratory events with sleep stages (notably REM, when central control is most vulnerable) and prevents detection of associated cortical arousals. Thus, although the type III findings (predominantly central events and documented deep desaturations) strongly suggest central sleep-disordered breathing, the true severity and physiologic consequences are best characterised with full in-laboratory polysomnography.

Planned definitive testing: rationale for type I PSG with capnography and video
A full type I polysomnography is the appropriate next diagnostic step to quantify central hypoventilation and to guide management. Type I PSG provides EEG for accurate sleep staging and total sleep time, electrooculography and chin EMG to differentiate sleep stages, and additional respiratory channels. Adding end-tidal or transcutaneous capnography is essential to detect hypoventilation (sustained hypercapnia) that may not produce discrete apnoeas/hypopnoeas but causes significant ventilatory failure during sleep. Video recording helps correlate clinical events (apnoea without distress, choking episodes) with physiologic data. This combination will provide definitive evidence for automatic respiratory failure, help determine severity, and inform urgency of neurosurgical intervention or non-invasive ventilatory support.

When to suspect CMI in dysphagia or sleep-disordered breathing
This case reinforces that CMI should be considered when neurogenic dysphagia or unexplained central sleep-disordered breathing accompany posterior head pain or craniovertebral junction symptoms. Published series report a substantial prevalence of sleep-related breathing disorders in symptomatic CMI, and guidelines increasingly recommend polysomnography in CMI patients with suspected sleep-disordered breathing. Practically, CMI should be suspected and cranio-cervical imaging obtained when: dysphagia affects all consistencies without endoscopic lesion; lower cranial nerve signs are present; central apnoeas predominate on initial sleep testing; or profound desaturations occur without dyspnoea. Early recognition shortens diagnostic delay and permits timely consideration of posterior fossa decompression, which can reverse CSF flow obstruction and potentially restore automatic respiratory drive.

In summary, the diagnostic strategy in this case combined recognition of neurologic red flags, re-evaluation of prior imaging, confirmatory MRI focused on tonsillar descent and CSF spaces, exclusion of primary pulmonary disease, targeted dynamic swallowing assessment, and staged sleep testing — culminating in a plan for definitive type I polysomnography with capnography to characterise central hypoventilation and guide treatment.

## Differential Diagnosis and Diagnostic Pitfalls

This patient’s presentation—recurrent choking on solids and liquids, episodic profound oxygen desaturations without dyspnoea, and longstanding vertex/occipital pain—required broad consideration of gastrointestinal, otolaryngological, pulmonary, neuromuscular and primary sleep or headache disorders before a unifying neurological diagnosis (Chiari malformation type I with brainstem compression) became apparent. Below I discuss the key competing diagnoses that were appropriately considered, the evidence used to exclude them in this case, and common diagnostic pitfalls that contributed to the prolonged delay.

Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease and laryngopharyngeal reflux
- Why considered: GERD and laryngopharyngeal reflux commonly produce chronic cough, throat clearing, globus sensation and intermittent choking, and are frequent first-line explanations for oropharyngeal symptoms.
- Evidence against in this case: The patient had multiple normal upper endoscopies, normal pH studies, and no clinical response to empiric acid suppression. Videofluoroscopic swallowing assessment did not reveal a mechanical obstruction to explain equal difficulty with solids and liquids. Dysphagia due to reflux typically fluctuates with meals, acidity and posture and often responds partially to therapy; the lack of such correlations argues against reflux as the primary cause.
- Teaching point: Equal impairment of solids and liquids suggests a neurogenic rather than obstructive or reflux mechanism; persistent symptoms despite normal GI testing should prompt neurological evaluation.

Myasthenia gravis and other neuromuscular junction disorders
- Why considered: Bulbar weakness from myasthenia gravis can cause choking, dysphagia and aspiration, and is an important reversible cause to exclude.
- Evidence against in this case: Serologic tests for acetylcholine receptor and anti-MuSK antibodies were negative, there was no clinical fatigability or diurnal fluctuation of symptoms, and pulmonary function tests did not show the pattern of progressive decrement with sustained effort classically seen in myasthenia. Moreover, voluntary breathing and strength were preserved, whereas myasthenia often shows fluctuating voluntary muscle weakness.
- Teaching point: Negative antibodies do not entirely exclude seronegative myasthenia, but the absence of fatigability, lack of diurnal variation and normal ancillary testing made it unlikely here and focused attention on central causes.

Primary pulmonary disease
- Why considered: Episodes of severe hypoxaemia raise concern for intrinsic lung disease (interstitial lung disease, COPD, pulmonary vascular disease) or upper airway obstruction.
- Evidence against in this case: Chest imaging and comprehensive pulmonary function testing were normal. Resting arterial blood gases were within normal limits. The desaturation episodes occurred unpredictably, sometimes during sleep, without accompanying respiratory effort limitation on daytime evaluations—findings less consistent with chronic parenchymal or obstructive airway disease.
- Teaching point: Normal pulmonary testing and imaging should prompt consideration of central causes of hypoventilation and intermittent hypoxaemia.

Primary sleep-disordered breathing: obstructive vs central events
- Why considered: Sleep apnoea can cause nocturnal choking, gasping and desaturations; distinguishing obstructive from central events is essential for correct management.
- Evidence and interpretation in this case: The home-recorded desaturations and the type III sleep study demonstrated a predominance of central events (72% central), suggesting a central rather than obstructive mechanism. However, the recorded apnea–hypopnea index (AHI 6.0) likely underestimated severity because the type III device lacks EEG and uses total recording time rather than sleep time. The clinical picture—profound nocturnal desaturations without arousal symptoms, lack of typical obstructive features (snoring, witnessed obstructive apnoeas), and concomitant brainstem-compressing lesion on MRI—favoured central hypoventilation related to brainstem dysfunction.
- Teaching point: Event type (central vs obstructive) and clinical context are as important as overall AHI. Limited sleep studies can misclassify severity; full polysomnography with capnography is preferred when central hypoventilation is suspected.

Primary headache disorders: nummular headache, occipital neuralgia and burning vertex syndrome
- Why considered: The patient’s chronic, focal vertex scalp pain with prominent allodynia led to consideration of primary scalp pain syndromes.
- Evidence and interpretation: Early clinicians diagnosed nummular headache based on a small, coin-sized painful area; treatments provided only transient relief. The presence of scalp allodynia and occipital location also overlap with occipital neuralgia, which has been associated with posterior fossa pathology including Chiari malformation. Burning vertex syndrome is a recently described entity with overlapping features. In this case, the scalp pain may represent occipital neuralgia related to hindbrain crowding, an unrelated primary headache, or a coincident burning vertex phenomenon; the evidence is inconclusive.
- Teaching point: Primary headache labels can be applied when structural causes are not sought or identified. Focal scalp pain with cutaneous allodynia should prompt consideration of regional structural pathology, particularly when other neurological signs are present.

Acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse)
- Why considered: The hallmark is failure of automatic (brainstem-mediated) breathing, especially during sleep and periods of distraction, with preservation of voluntary respiratory control—matching this patient’s episodes of severe desaturation without dyspnoea or respiratory distress.
- Alignment with the case: The patient documented repeated asymptomatic oxygen saturations in the 70%–80% range, had preserved daytime voluntary breathing and normal pulmonary mechanics, and MRI showed significant cerebellar tonsillar descent with brainstem compression. The predominance of central events on sleep testing and the clinical scenario of head trauma preceding symptom onset further supported acquired central hypoventilation as the most explanatory diagnosis.
- Teaching point: Acquired central hypoventilation is uncommon but should be suspected when there is a dissociation between voluntary and automatic breathing and when structural brainstem lesions are present.

Diagnostic pitfalls that delayed recognition
- Overreliance on normal ENT/GI testing: Repeated normal laryngoscopy and endoscopy understandably directed clinicians toward non-structural causes, but this led to anchoring on peripheral explanations and missed the central (neurological) origin of dysphagia.
- Misattribution to anxiety: The absence of dyspnoea during profound desaturations led some evaluators to ascribe episodes to anxiety. While anxiety can cause subjective breathlessness, it does not produce objective, reproducible severe hypoxaemia; attributing physiological abnormalities solely to anxiety can dangerously delay investigation of organic causes.
- Failure to re-review prior imaging: The 14-year-old CT that already showed crowding at the foramen magnum was interpreted as normal initially; retrospective review revealed abnormalities. This illustrates the value of re-examining prior imaging (and requesting MRI) when new, unexplained neurological or respiratory symptoms emerge.
- Incomplete sleep evaluation: Use of a type III device without EEG underestimated sleep time and likely the true rate of respiratory events. Limited studies can produce falsely reassuring AHI values and delay definitive diagnosis.
- Narrow diagnostic framing after trauma: The temporal association with a motor vehicle accident raised the question of post-traumatic sequelae versus unmasked congenital Chiari. Without pre-injury imaging, it is difficult to attribute causation; clinicians may either prematurely conclude that trauma explains all subsequent symptoms or dismiss the relevance of old scans, both of which can impede proper diagnosis.

Trauma and the Chiari relationship: unmasking versus causation
- Clinical dilemma: The patient’s symptoms began after a motor vehicle accident and a posttrauma CT already demonstrated hindbrain crowding. Two hypotheses exist—trauma-induced tonsillar descent (acquired Chiari) or trauma that revealed a pre-existing congenital variant by provoking symptoms.
- Diagnostic limitation in this case: Absence of pre-injury imaging prevents definitive attribution. The important practical lesson is to treat the anatomic finding and clinical syndrome together: whether congenital or acquired, significant tonsillar descent with compatible brainstem symptoms warrants consideration of neurosurgical evaluation and appropriate sleep and respiratory testing.
- Teaching point: Clinicians should avoid requiring proof of causation by trauma vs congenital origin when structural compression and compatible symptoms are present; management should be guided by symptom severity, objective physiological impairment and imaging evidence of brainstem compromise.

Summary
The key differential diagnoses—reflux-related dysphagia, myasthenia gravis, primary pulmonary disease, obstructive sleep apnoea and primary headache disorders—were reasonably entertained and largely excluded by targeted testing in this case. The combination of neurogenic-pattern dysphagia, repeated asymptomatic severe desaturations, predominance of central sleep events, and unequivocal MRI evidence of significant tonsillar descent pointed to acquired central hypoventilation from Chiari malformation as the unifying diagnosis. Diagnostic delay was driven by normal ENT/GI studies, misattribution of hypoxaemia to anxiety, limited sleep-study methodology, and missed earlier radiographic signs—pitfalls that clinicians should recognise to avoid prolonged morbidity in similar patients.

## Management Options and Decision-Making

The therapeutic aims in this case are straightforward: relieve the mechanical compression at the craniocervical junction, restore normal CSF flow dynamics, reverse or stabilise bulbar and brainstem-mediated respiratory dysfunction, and improve the patient’s quality of life while minimising risk. Management choices must be driven by the severity and trajectory of neurological and respiratory symptoms, objective evidence of brainstem compromise, and the patient’s preferences after explicit discussion of benefits, risks and uncertainties.

Posterior fossa decompression (PFD): rationale and expected impact
- Rationale: PFD directly addresses the presumed pathophysiology in symptomatic Chiari I — reduced posterior fossa volume with tonsillar descent producing brainstem compression and CSF flow obstruction. Surgical decompression aims to increase the effective space at the foramen magnum, relieve pressure on medullary respiratory and lower cranial nerve centres, and normalise CSF flow.
- Typical technique: most commonly performed procedures include a suboccipital craniectomy (or craniotomy) with removal of part of the posterior arch of C1. Many centres supplement bone removal with duraplasty (opening the dura and placing a patch graft) to expand the subarachnoid space; some surgeons also perform tonsillar reduction or coagulation in selected cases. Technique choice depends on local practice, imaging findings, and surgeon judgement.
- Expected outcomes: improvement in cough/gag dysfunction, dysphagia and sleep-disordered breathing is commonly reported after successful decompression, particularly when symptoms are attributable to direct compressive brainstem dysfunction. Published series and case reports show reversal or marked improvement in central apnoeas, hypoventilation and bulbar symptoms in many patients, although recovery is variable and may be incomplete—especially after long-standing deficits. Risks and benefits must be individualised.

Indications that favour surgical intervention in this case
- Objective anatomical severity: substantial tonsillar descent (11 mm in this patient) and evidence of crowded foramen magnum with loss of CSF space support a structural target for surgery.
- Clinically significant brainstem dysfunction: recurrent neurogenic choking affecting solids and liquids, nocturnal episodes with documented profound oxygen desaturations (to 70%–80%) and a high proportion of central respiratory events on the type III study all point toward medically meaningful compromise.
- Progressive or disabling symptoms: prominent impact on daily life (social withdrawal, safety concerns such as airport episode) and chronic disabling headaches are additional reasons to consider decompression.
- Failure of conservative measures or high risk of complications from untreated disease: when conservative strategies are insufficient to control hypoxaemia or aspiration risk, surgery is more strongly indicated.

Conservative management and structured monitoring
- When the patient elects to delay or decline surgery, conservative care is acceptable with careful surveillance. The case report’s monitoring plan is an appropriate framework: monthly clinical reassessment focused on new or worsening bulbar/respiratory signs; periodic overnight oximetry (eg, quarterly) to screen for recurrent severe desaturations; and interval MRI (commonly annually or sooner if deterioration) to detect progression of tonsillar descent, syrinx formation or other changes.
- Safety counselling is essential: educate the patient about recognition of high-risk situations (unwitnessed nocturnal desaturations, increasing choking frequency, syncope, peri-travel or constrained environments), avoidance of sedatives or opioid medications that depress respiratory drive, and the need for urgent re-evaluation for any acute respiratory complaints.
- Lifestyle adaptations used in the case (dietary moderation at meals, avoiding high-risk situations) are reasonable temporising measures but do not obviate definitive treatment if objective hypoventilation persists or worsens.

Sleep-focused interventions
- Obtain definitive type I polysomnography with full EEG, video, end-tidal or transcutaneous CO2 monitoring to characterise sleep architecture, quantify central hypoventilation versus obstructive events, and accurately determine sleep time. This is crucial before long-term treatment decisions.
- If central hypoventilation is confirmed and surgery is deferred or delayed, non-invasive ventilatory support with a backup-controlled mode should be considered. Options include bilevel positive airway pressure with a backup rate or volume-assured modes that provide a minimum respiratory rate, and in some circumstances domiciliary invasive ventilation is used for severe hypoventilation. Standard CPAP may not be adequate when automatic ventilatory drive is impaired; in pure central hypoventilation, CPAP alone can fail to correct hypoventilation and may worsen hypercapnia in some patients.
- For mixed or predominantly obstructive sleep apnoea, CPAP remains standard. Choice of device must be guided by PSG and capnography data and by respiratory medicine input.
- Short-term supplemental oxygen may correct hypoxaemia but will not treat hypoventilation and can mask hypercapnia; use only with concurrent monitoring and specialist oversight.

Swallowing and aspiration risk mitigation
- Refer to speech–language pathology for comprehensive swallowing assessment (clinical bedside exam and instrumental studies where indicated) and directed therapy. In this patient, the neurogenic pattern (solids and liquids equally affected) warrants swallow rehabilitation and compensatory strategies.
- Implement diet texture modification and supervised eating techniques to reduce aspiration risk; consider thickened liquids and altered food consistencies tailored to assessment findings.
- Advise aspiration precautions (e.g., upright posture while eating, slow small bites, supervision when eating in public initially) and consider repeat modified barium swallow if symptoms progress.
- In severe, persistent aspiration with recurrent pneumonias or demonstrable high-risk physiology, discussion about enteral feeding options may be necessary, ideally in a multidisciplinary context.

Pain management for occipital neuralgia/burning vertex phenotype
- Conservative measures include neuropathic pain agents (eg, gabapentin, pregabalin, certain antidepressants) trialled as tolerated. Local therapies such as greater occipital nerve blocks can provide diagnostic and therapeutic benefit for occipital neuralgia-type pain.
- Coordinate with pain management or neurology for refractory symptoms. Successful control of scalp allodynia can substantially improve quality of life and reduce avoidance behaviours (eg, hair-cutting distress).

Shared decision-making and weighing uncertainty
- Decisions about PFD require honest discussion of expected benefits (potential reversal or improvement of dysphagia, central sleep-disordered breathing and headache), the variability of outcomes, and surgical risks (wound infection, CSF leak, dural complications, cerebellar injury, postoperative bleeding, and, rarely, worsening neurological deficits). Local surgical morbidity and mortality rates should be presented.
- Emphasise the imperfect correlation between imaging severity and symptoms: some patients with pronounced tonsillar descent remain asymptomatic, while others with modest descent are symptomatic. In this case, the combination of objective desaturation, central events and functional impairment increases the probability that surgery would be beneficial, but certainty is not absolute.
- Explore the patient’s values: tolerance for surgical risk versus desire for symptomatic relief, concerns about cultural or personal impacts of current coping strategies, and readiness for postoperative rehabilitation and follow-up. Document the patient’s informed preferences and offer time-limited trial of conservative management only if robust monitoring is acceptable to both clinician and patient.
- Ensure multidisciplinary involvement (neurosurgery, sleep medicine, respiratory medicine, speech pathology, pain management and primary care) to support a coordinated plan and to revisit decisions as new data (eg, definitive PSG results) become available.

In summary, the balance of current evidence and this patient’s clinical picture supports strong consideration of posterior fossa decompression as a potentially disease-modifying intervention, particularly given the documented central respiratory events, severe intermittent desaturations and significant tonsillar herniation. If surgery is deferred, a structured, safety-focused conservative plan with definitive polysomnography and readiness to institute ventilatory support or expedite surgery on clinical deterioration is essential. Shared decision-making grounded in clear explanation of risks, benefits and uncertainties should guide the patient’s chosen path.

## Outcomes, Follow-up, and Clinical Lessons

Outcomes and follow‑up
Over the 8 months since diagnosis the patient elected conservative management while weighing surgical options. She has been followed with a structured monitoring plan: monthly clinic visits for symptom review, quarterly overnight oximetry to screen for recurrent or progressive desaturation, and annual MRI to assess tonsillar position, syrinx development and CSF spaces at the craniocervical junction. Clear escalation criteria were defined: any new daytime hypercapnia or persistent resting hypoxaemia, increasing frequency or severity of nocturnal desaturations (e.g., sustained SpO2 < 88% or repeated drops into the 70s), new or progressive bulbar dysfunction (aspiration, weight loss, recurrent pneumonia), or new neurological deficits would prompt urgent reassessment and expedited referral for posterior fossa decompression. The team also arranged definitive polysomnography (type I study with EEG, video and end‑tidal CO2) to better quantify sleep‑related central hypoventilation once the patient was able to complete testing.

Safety planning
Because the case demonstrated “silent” or unperceived hypoxaemia, a safety plan focused on risk reduction was implemented. The patient was counselled about the possibility of profound desaturation without dyspnoea and advised to carry a pulse oximeter when travelling or when episodes are likely. Temporary supplemental oxygen was prescribed for use during symptomatic episodes or travel if desaturations recur, with instruction to seek emergency care for oxygen requirement that does not rapidly correct with supplemental oxygen. She was given specific travel guidance (avoid long flights without medical clearance, notify carriers if oxygen will be required, obtain fit‑to‑fly assessment) and educated to avoid sedating medications that could further depress automatic respiratory drive. Family members were instructed on recognising behavioral signs of possible hypoventilation (sudden unresponsiveness, cyanosis) and to seek emergency help promptly.

Key clinical lessons from this case
This case emphasizes several practical points for clinicians. First, Chiari I malformation can present predominantly with respiratory dysfunction that mimics acquired central hypoventilation: preserved voluntary breathing with failure of automatic respiratory drive, particularly during sleep or distraction. Second, persistent oropharyngeal choking or dysphagia that affects solids and liquids equally, with normal endoscopic and structural evaluations, should prompt neurological assessment for brainstem or cranio‑cervical junction pathology. Third, historical imaging should be re‑reviewed carefully—findings such as a crowded foramen magnum or loss of CSF space at the craniocervical junction are subtle but diagnostically important and may be missed on initial reads.

Testing strategy and limitations
The case highlights the important limitations of type III sleep studies when central events are suspected. Without EEG, total sleep time cannot be determined and the apnoea–hypopnoea index will be underestimated; absence of EEG also precludes detection of arousals and accurate sleep staging, and limits differentiation of wakeful hypoventilation from sleep‑related events. When clinical suspicion for central hypoventilation or brainstem dysfunction exists, a full type I polysomnography with EEG, video, and continuous CO2 monitoring (end‑tidal or transcutaneous) is the standard test to quantify severity and guide management. In this patient, the predominance of central events on a type III study and dramatic documented desaturations despite a low reported AHI illustrate how limited testing can be misleading.

Imaging lesson
This report underlines the value of targeted review of prior imaging. A non‑contrast head CT performed after trauma may not be reported as abnormal for routine intracranial injury, yet careful scrutiny of the craniocervical junction can reveal posterior fossa crowding and loss of CSF spaces suggestive of Chiari I. When clinical features point to brainstem or lower cranial nerve involvement, obtain or request sagittal MRI sequences that explicitly evaluate tonsillar position relative to the foramen magnum and CSF flow at the cranio‑vertebral junction.

Pathophysiology pearl
The clinical dissociation between intact voluntary respiration and impaired automatic breathing is a key physiologic insight from this case. Voluntary breathing is cortically mediated and often preserved despite medullary compression; automatic respiratory drive depends on brainstem respiratory pattern generators and chemoreceptor afferents that converge in the medulla. Compression or distortion of these structures and their ascending sensory pathways can abolish automatic responses to hypoxia and hypercapnia, producing “silent” hypoventilation that may be intermittent and posture‑ or sleep‑dependent.

Research and knowledge gaps
Several areas require further study. The prevalence, mechanisms and clinical significance of scalp allodynia or vertex pain in Chiari I are poorly characterised; systematic data are lacking and no standardized instrument exists for documenting such sensory features in CMI populations. More broadly, prospective studies are needed to define the true prevalence and natural history of sleep‑disordered breathing in CMI, the predictors of reversibility after decompression, and standardized algorithms for respiratory screening and timing of surgical referral.

Bottom line
In patients with unexplained choking, dysphagia without structural cause, or episodic “silent” hypoxaemia, consider structural lesions at the foramen magnum such as Chiari I. Careful review of historical imaging, appropriate use of full polysomnography with CO2 monitoring, and explicit safety planning are essential while diagnostic and treatment decisions are underway.
