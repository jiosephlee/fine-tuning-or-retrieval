Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

### [Differential Diagnosis] List at least five alternative causes of severe thrombocytopenia in the early post–cardiac surgery period and explain which clinical or laboratory findings in this case help exclude each one.

Direct answer
Five alternative causes of severe thrombocytopenia in the early post–cardiac‑surgery period and why each is unlikely in this case:

1) Hemodilution/CPB‑related platelet consumption
- Rationale: Cardiopulmonary bypass commonly produces an early modest fall in platelets from hemodilution and mechanical platelet activation; nadirs are usually modest and recover within days.
- Why excluded here: The initial decline (210 → 155 on POD1) was modest (expected), but the progressive fall to 13×10^9/L by POD7 is far greater than typical CPB‑related loss and persisted/progressed rather than recovering — arguing against isolated CPB‑related thrombocytopenia.

2) Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) from bleeding/critical illness
- Rationale: DIC produces thrombocytopenia with prolonged PT/APTT, low fibrinogen, high D‑dimer and consumptive coagulopathy.
- Why excluded here: PT/INR and PTT were normal on POD5, fibrinogen was markedly elevated (6.59 g/L) rather than low (early postoperative acute‑phase response explains high fibrinogen), and there was no clinical bleeding or hemodynamic instability initially. These findings make classic consumptive DIC an unlikely primary explanation at the time the thrombocytopenia emerged.

3) Sepsis‑associated thrombocytopenia / critical illness
- Rationale: Severe infection/ sepsis often causes early, progressive thrombocytopenia.
- Why excluded here: The patient was hemodynamically stable, without fever or other signs of systemic infection; blood and urine cultures were negative; WBC count and differential were normal; chest x‑ray unremarkable. Clinical and laboratory data do not support sepsis as the cause.

4) Thrombotic microangiopathy (TTP/HUS) or post‑operative TMA
- Rationale: TMA causes severe thrombocytopenia with microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (schistocytes, low haptoglobin, rising LDH) and often renal/neurologic findings.
- Why excluded (initially): On POD5 there were no schistocytes on film, haptoglobin was elevated (not low), hemoglobin decline was consistent with expected surgical blood loss without ongoing hemolysis, and renal function was normal. (Note: schistocytes and some consumptive features did appear later during the course, but they developed after evidence of severe HIT‑associated coagulopathy rather than preceding the platelet fall.)

5) Post‑transfusion purpura (PTP) or transfusion‑related causes
- Rationale: PTP causes severe delayed thrombocytopenia after transfusion; transfusion reactions or platelet alloimmunization can produce marked drops.
- Why excluded here: The patient received no blood product transfusions during or after surgery, so transfusion‑related etiologies are not applicable.

6) Drug‑induced immune thrombocytopenia (DITP)
- Rationale: Many drugs (e.g., vancomycin, beta‑lactams, heparins, quinines) can cause antibody‑mediated platelet destruction, typically appearing days after drug exposure.
- Why unlikely here: The timing and medication exposures do not fit classic DITP. Perioperative drugs (vancomycin, cefazolin, tranexamic acid) were given only on the day of surgery (too early for a typical new drug‑antibody response), and the progressive decline correlated temporally with preoperative and intraoperative heparin exposure. Additionally, no new offending postoperative drug was identified that would explain the platelet fall.

7) Pseudothrombocytopenia from platelet clumping or laboratory artifact
- Rationale: EDTA‑induced platelet clumping can artifactually lower automated platelet counts.
- Why excluded here: Peripheral smear review confirmed true thrombocytopenia and specifically noted absence of platelet clumps.

Brief synthesis linking to the case
Taken together, common postoperative explanations (CPB‑related dilution/consumption, DIC, sepsis, transfusion reactions, DITP, and spurious lab artifact) were not supported by the clinical course or laboratory data. The presence of a progressive, severe platelet fall beginning soon after documented preoperative heparin exposure, a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, SRA evidence of heparin‑independent platelet activation, and the development of arterial thrombosis (parietal stroke) point to autoimmune HIT (aHIT) as the unifying diagnosis — and explain the otherwise unexplained, severe, and prothrombotic thrombocytopenia in this patient.
