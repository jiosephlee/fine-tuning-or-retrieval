Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

### [Pathophysiology] How does autoimmune HIT (aHIT) differ from classic HIT in terms of antibody behavior and platelet activation, and what serotonin-release assay (SRA) findings support a diagnosis of aHIT?

Direct answer: Autoimmune HIT (aHIT) differs from classic, heparin-dependent HIT because the pathogenic IgG antibodies are often able to activate platelets without heparin (or with only very small amounts) and form platelet‑activating immune complexes that can be unusually strong and long‑lived. In other words, aHIT antibodies are heparin‑independent (or only partially heparin‑dependent) and can produce more intense FcγRIIa‑mediated platelet activation than typical HIT antibodies.

Clinical reasoning / pathophysiology (compact):
- Classic HIT: IgG antibodies recognise complexes of PF4 + polyanion (heparin). Platelet activation is typically heparin‑dependent — strong activation at therapeutic/low heparin concentrations (eg 0.1–0.3 U/mL), little/no activation at 0 U/mL, and inhibition at very high heparin concentrations (eg 100 U/mL). Activation occurs via FcγRIIa cross‑linking on platelets.
- Autoimmune HIT: antibodies either recognise PF4 alone or form ultra‑stable PF4 immune complexes that can activate platelets without exogenous heparin, or at very low heparin levels. These antibodies may produce massive FcγRIIa clustering and sometimes show partial resistance to FcγRIIa blockade. Clinically this produces earlier, deeper thrombocytopenia, persistent hypercoagulability and prolonged seropositivity.

SRA findings that support a diagnosis of aHIT (as illustrated in this case):
- High serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin (eg ~96% in this patient) — a hallmark of heparin‑independent platelet activation and diagnostic of aHIT rather than classic HIT.
- Strong serotonin release at low heparin concentrations (eg 0.1–0.3 U/mL; 97–99% here), with loss of activation at very high heparin (100 U/mL) — the latter still reflects PF4 immune‑complex biology.
- Persistence of platelet‑activating activity over weeks–months supports an autoimmune phenotype.
- In this case a novel supportive finding was only partial inhibition of serotonin release by the FcγRIIa‑blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3) rather than the near‑complete inhibition usually seen in classic HIT; this partial resistance is consistent with unusually potent aHIT antibodies.

Takeaway for practice: when SRA shows strong serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin (and at low heparin) — especially if clinical features are severe (very low nadir platelets, thrombosis, prolonged positivity) — think aHIT. Note that incomplete inhibition by FcγRIIa blockade can occur with particularly aggressive aHIT antibodies and should not exclude the diagnosis.
