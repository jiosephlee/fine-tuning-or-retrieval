Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

### [Clinical Pearls] In a patient with progressive thrombocytopenia after cardiopulmonary bypass, what clinical features and timing would increase your suspicion for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), and how can recent preoperative heparin exposure shift the onset earlier than the usual postoperative day 5–10 window?

Teaching answer — Clinical pearl:

What should raise your suspicion for HIT after cardiopulmonary bypass?
- Timing and magnitude of platelet fall: HIT classically presents with a platelet count fall beginning POD5–10 after intraoperative heparin. Be suspicious when you see a new, progressive platelet decline that is clinically out of proportion to the expected immediate postop drop from CPB (eg, ongoing fall after the initial POD1–2 decrease), especially a fall of ≥50% from baseline or to very low nadirs. In the case, platelets fell from 210×10^9/L preop to 155×10^9/L on POD1 and then steadily to 50×10^9/L by POD5 and 13×10^9/L at nadir — a pattern that should prompt HIT evaluation.
- Thrombosis or new ischemic events: new arterial or venous thrombosis in the setting of thrombocytopenia is a major red flag. The patient developed an acute left parietal infarct concurrent with the platelet fall.
- Lack of alternate explanations: absence of active bleeding, transfusion, thrombotic microangiopathy, sepsis, or drugs known to cause abrupt immune thrombocytopenia supports HIT as the likely cause. In the reported patient, hemodynamics were stable, cultures were negative, and other common causes were excluded.
- Laboratory screening: a strongly positive PF4/polyanion immunoassay (high optical density) reinforces clinical suspicion; confirmatory functional assays (SRA) define pathogenic platelet-activating antibodies. In this case the PF4 ELISA was strongly positive (OD 2.2).

How can recent preoperative heparin exposure shift onset earlier than POD5–10?
- Immunologic basis: the usual POD5–10 window reflects primary IgG formation after a first exposure to heparin–PF4 complexes. If a patient has recent prior exposure to heparin (days to weeks before surgery), memory B cells and preformed anti–PF4/heparin IgG can be present. Re‑exposure (eg, intraoperative UFH) then triggers a rapid anamnestic response or activation by preformed antibodies, producing platelet activation and thrombocytopenia much earlier than POD5.
- Clinical correlate and timing: rapid‑onset or “early” HIT occurs most often when heparin was given within the prior 100 days, especially within the prior 10 days to 2 weeks. In the case, the patient received 2000 U UFH during a cardiac catheterization one week before surgery; this recent exposure explains the unusually early platelet fall beginning before POD5.
- Atypical/autoimmune HIT (aHIT) caveat: some patients develop heparin‑independent, strongly platelet‑activating antibodies (aHIT) that can cause very severe, early, and prolonged thrombocytopenia with pronounced hypercoagulability. The case demonstrated heparin‑independent serotonin release and only partial inhibition by Fc‑blockade — serologic features of aHIT that explain the severe, early presentation.

Practical takeaways for morning report:
- In a post‑CPB patient with progressive thrombocytopenia, ask about any heparin exposure in the preceding days–weeks (catheterization, dialysis, flushes). Recent heparin makes “early” HIT much more likely.
- Red flags that should trigger immediate HIT workup and empiric non‑heparin anticoagulation: ≥50% platelet fall or platelet count <100×10^9/L, new thrombosis or organ ischemia, strong PF4 ELISA, and exclusion of other causes.
- Recognize aHIT when platelet activation is heparin‑independent or especially severe — these cases can have atypical SRA profiles and may require aggressive management (stop all heparin, use direct thrombin inhibitors or fondaparinux, consider IVIG), as in the reported patient.
