Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

Chapter 2: ## Pathophysiology of HIT and Autoimmune HIT (aHIT)

Heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) is an antibody‑mediated prothrombotic disorder in which the immune response is directed against complexes of platelet factor 4 (PF4) and polyanions such as unfractionated heparin (UFH). In classic (heparin‑dependent) HIT, circulating IgG antibodies bind PF4–heparin multimers. These immune complexes simultaneously present Fc portions of IgG that engage and cross‑link FcγRIIa receptors on platelet surfaces. FcγRIIa clustering triggers intracellular signaling in platelets that produces shape change, granule release and expression of procoagulant phospholipid, promoting thrombin generation. Activated platelets and thrombin together drive a hypercoagulable, consumptive state: thrombin converts fibrinogen to fibrin (consuming fibrinogen), produces fibrin degradation products and D‑dimer (so D‑dimer rises), and can cause microangiopathic red‑cell injury visible as schistocytes. Clinically this explains why HIT is paradoxically thrombosis‑predominant despite thrombocytopenia.

Autoimmune HIT (aHIT) describes a subgroup of HIT in which the pathogenic antibodies have broader reactivity and can activate platelets in the absence—or with minimal amounts—of heparin. Mechanistically, aHIT antibodies either recognize PF4 alone, PF4 bound in different conformations, or form very stable immune complexes that do not require heparin to assemble. Because they can activate platelets without heparin, aHIT antibodies produce more severe, more prolonged and sometimes relapsing platelet activation and thrombosis than typical HIT. The laboratory and clinical consequences are consistent with more extensive thrombin generation and a greater consumptive coagulopathy, which may manifest as profoundly low platelet counts, falling fibrinogen, rising D‑dimer and intermittent schistocytosis—features that were seen in this patient.

Laboratory testing reflects these mechanistic differences. The PF4/polyanion ELISA detects antibodies that bind PF4 complexes and is usually strongly positive in both classic HIT and aHIT; very high optical density values (as in this case) raise the likelihood of pathogenic antibodies. Functional platelet activation assays, such as the serotonin‑release assay (SRA), distinguish heparin dependence: typical HIT sera cause strong platelet activation at low therapeutic heparin concentrations (commonly tested at 0.1–0.3 U/mL) and lose activity at very high heparin (100 U/mL) because excess heparin disrupts PF4–heparin lattice formation. In aHIT, sera additionally cause platelet activation in the absence of heparin (0 U/mL), reflecting heparin‑independent immune complexes. This case’s SRA showed exactly that pattern—high release at 0 and at 0.1–0.3 U/mL and inhibition at 100 U/mL—supporting a diagnosis of aHIT.

Platelet activation in HIT is principally mediated by FcγRIIa. Experimentally, platelet activation can be inhibited by monoclonal antibodies (for example IV.3) that block FcγRIIa engagement. In most HIT sera this blockade abrogates SRA serotonin release. The unusual serologic finding in this patient was only partial inhibition of platelet activation by IV.3: serotonin release in the presence of IV.3 remained markedly above baseline. This partial resistance implies unusually potent or atypical immune complexes. Possible explanations include very high antibody avidity or concentration causing strong FcγRIIa clustering that is not fully blocked by the monoclonal antibody dose used, recognition of PF4 epitopes that promote multivalent immune complex formation, or engagement of additional platelet‑activating pathways that are less sensitive to FcγRIIa blockade. Whatever the precise mechanism, incomplete IV.3 inhibition is concordant with the clinically severe, persistent platelet activation and consumptive coagulopathy observed in this patient, and it explains why the initial SRA was labeled “indeterminate” rather than simply negative.

The timing of HIT in relation to heparin exposure also follows immunologic kinetics. Primary immune responses to heparin–PF4 usually take several days to develop, so typical postoperative HIT appears between postoperative day (POD) 5–10. However, if a patient has recent prior exposure to heparin (for example the diagnostic catheterization one week before surgery in this case), memory B cells can drive a more rapid anamnestic response and clinical platelet count fall earlier than POD5. That principle accounts for the unusually early onset here. Over time, as the immune response wanes or the antibody repertoire shifts, serologic profiles may evolve: this patient’s later samples showed diminution of heparin‑independent activity and eventually a pattern more characteristic of heparin‑dependent HIT, consistent with decreasing aHIT antibody titres or loss of the most pathogenic clones over weeks to months.

In summary, classic HIT depends on PF4–heparin immune complexes that activate platelets via FcγRIIa; aHIT represents antibodies capable of heparin‑independent platelet activation, producing more severe and persistent thrombin generation and consumptive laboratory abnormalities. The high ELISA OD, SRA activation at 0 U/mL, and partial resistance to IV.3 blockade in this patient all map to that pathophysiology and explain the patient’s profound thrombocytopenia, biochemical evidence of consumptive coagulopathy, and prolonged clinical course.
