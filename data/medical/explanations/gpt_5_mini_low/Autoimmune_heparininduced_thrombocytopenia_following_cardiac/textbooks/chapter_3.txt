Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Reasoning and Differential in Early Postoperative Thrombocytopenia

Early postoperative thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery presents a diagnostic challenge because multiple common and uncommon causes overlap temporally and because cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) itself produces predictable platelet changes. Effective diagnostic reasoning therefore proceeds stepwise, integrating timing, magnitude of platelet fall, presence or absence of thrombosis, focused laboratory testing (including peripheral smear), and targeted imaging. In this case the diagnostic pathway illustrates how those elements were used to distinguish autoimmune heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT) from other postoperative causes.

Timing as a diagnostic clue
A central principle for HIT diagnosis is the timing of the platelet count fall. Classic HIT usually occurs between postoperative day (POD) 5 and 10 after heparin exposure. A platelet fall reaching nadir before POD5 is therefore atypical and should prompt consideration of alternative causes—unless there was antecedent heparin exposure within the prior 5–10 days. The patient’s platelet decline began immediately after surgery and reached 50×10^9/L by POD5, which at first glance was earlier than typical for HIT after intraoperative UFH. Crucially, review identified a preoperative cardiac catheterization during which unfractionated heparin (UFH) had been given 1 week before surgery. Preoperative heparin exposure explains an “early” onset HIT presentation because antibody formation may have already been initiated; thus timing must be interpreted in the context of all recent heparin exposures.

Applying clinical pretest probability in the post‑CPB patient
In post‑CPB patients, the baseline expected platelet fall is modest (often 30–50%) due to hemodilution and platelet activation during bypass, with recovery thereafter. When applying a clinical pretest probability for HIT, integrate: timing (adjusted for prior UFH exposure), magnitude and trajectory of the fall (progressive decline to very low counts is concerning), and presence of new thrombosis. In this case the progressive fall from 210 to 24×10^9/L, together with a new ischemic stroke on POD6, raised high suspicion for an immune‑mediated process such as HIT rather than routine postoperative thrombocytopenia. Thus the pretest probability was sufficiently high to justify immediate laboratory HIT testing and empiric change in anticoagulation once other causes were deemed unlikely.

Differential diagnosis and why other causes were unlikely
Common postoperative explanations include hemodilution/consumptive loss from surgery and CPB, infection/sepsis with consumptive coagulopathy, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), post‑transfusion purpura, and drug‑induced immune thrombocytopenia. Each was considered and systematically excluded or rendered unlikely by clinical and laboratory findings in this patient: hemodilution/expected postoperative fall was insufficient to explain the progressive and profound thrombocytopenia; there was no hemodynamic instability to suggest cardiogenic shock; blood and urine cultures were negative and there were no clinical features of sepsis; DIC typically produces prolonged PT/PTT and low fibrinogen—here PT/PTT were normal and fibrinogen was elevated consistent with an acute phase response; TTP classically has severe microangiopathic hemolysis with low haptoglobin and abundant schistocytes—initially haptoglobin was elevated and schistocytes were absent on the early smear; post‑transfusion purpura was excluded because no transfusions had been administered; and timing and medication history made drug‑induced immune thrombocytopenia unlikely. The stepwise exclusion of these alternatives strengthened the suspicion for HIT given the antibody‑provoking exposures and the thrombotic complication.

Laboratory evaluation and peripheral smear
The diagnostic workup appropriately included a complete blood count with peripheral smear, hemolysis studies, coagulation panel, and relevant chemistries. The smear excluded pseudothrombocytopenia from platelet clumping and initially showed no schistocytosis, arguing against microangiopathic hemolysis at the outset. Hemolysis labs (normal to elevated haptoglobin) supported absence of hemolytic TTP early on. Coagulation studies showed normal PT/INR and PTT and an elevated fibrinogen, consistent with the early postoperative acute phase rather than DIC. Serial follow‑up labs later demonstrated rising D‑dimer, falling fibrinogen and appearance of some schistocytes, changes interpreted as secondary consumptive coagulopathy related to progressive HIT‑associated thrombosis and fibrin formation rather than primary DIC. Cultures and basic chemistries were unremarkable, further narrowing the differential.

Imaging for thrombosis
Because thrombosis is a common and important manifestation of HIT, particularly when platelet activation is marked, imaging should be prompted by new focal neurologic signs or other ischemic symptoms. In this patient, worsening delirium and further platelet fall triggered CT brain imaging that identified an acute left parietal infarct. The documented thrombotic event substantially increased the clinical probability of HIT in the appropriate temporal and exposure context.

HIT testing strategy and interpretation
HIT diagnosis relies on a two‑step testing approach: a sensitive immunoassay that detects PF4/polyanion antibodies (eg, ELISA) and a functional platelet activation assay (eg, serotonin‑release assay, SRA) that confirms pathogenic, platelet‑activating antibodies. In this case the PF4/polyanion ELISA returned a strong positive (OD 2.2), which is highly supportive but not alone diagnostic because ELISA positivity can occur without clinical HIT. The SRA is considered the gold standard functional test; a typical HIT-positive SRA demonstrates heparin‑dependent platelet activation (high release at low‑to‑therapeutic heparin concentrations and suppression at very high heparin concentrations).

Interpreting an ‘indeterminate’ SRA in aHIT
This patient’s initial SRA was described as “indeterminate” because the serum caused marked serotonin release both in the absence of heparin (0 U/mL) and at low heparin concentrations, and because addition of an FcγRIIA‑blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3) did not fully suppress activation. These features are characteristic of autoimmune HIT (aHIT), a variant in which antibodies can activate platelets without exogenous heparin and may form unusually stable immune complexes. Key interpretive points for clinicians: do not dismiss SRA indeterminate results as non‑HIT without reviewing the activation pattern. Strong activation at 0 U/mL heparin indicates heparin‑independent activating antibodies; lack of complete inhibition by an Fc receptor blocker suggests unusually potent immune complexes that may partially resist blockade in vitro. In such scenarios, the clinical picture (timing adjusted for prior heparin exposure, thrombosis, severe thrombocytopenia) and a strongly positive ELISA support the diagnosis despite SRA atypia. Thus an “indeterminate” SRA in the appropriate context should raise suspicion for aHIT rather than provide reassurance.

Role of serial functional testing
Because antibody characteristics and titers evolve over time, serial functional testing can clarify an initially atypical picture. In this case, subsequent SRA profiles over weeks showed evolution toward patterns more typical of aHIT and later toward heparin‑dependent behavior as the antibody repertoire waned. Serial testing can therefore document persistence of platelet‑activating antibodies, changes in their heparin dependence, and provide laboratory corroboration of the clinical diagnosis made from the constellation of timing, exposure, thrombosis, and initial ELISA positivity.

Putting it together — diagnostic conclusion
By integrating the early but explainable timing (preoperative UFH exposure), progressive and profound platelet fall, new thrombotic stroke, strong positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, and an SRA pattern of heparin‑independent activation with only partial Fc receptor blockade, the diagnostic reasoning converged on autoimmune HIT. Systematic exclusion of competing causes and use of serial functional assays reinforced the diagnosis and justified HIT‑directed management despite the initial “indeterminate” functional test result. This case highlights the necessity of interpreting HIT assays in clinical context, recognizing serologic variants such as aHIT, and avoiding false reassurance from atypical SRA findings.
