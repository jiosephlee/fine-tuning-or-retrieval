Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

Chapter 5: ## Clinical Course, Laboratory Evolution, and Outcomes

After the diagnosis of autoimmune heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT) was suspected, the patient’s clinical course was dominated by profound thrombocytopenia, biochemical evidence of ongoing consumptive coagulopathy, a thrombotic complication (left parietal stroke), and a protracted, ultimately successful hematologic recovery under non‑heparin anticoagulation and adjunctive immunomodulation.

Platelet trajectory and response to therapy
The platelet count nadired at 13 × 10^9/L (POD7) despite prompt cessation of heparins and institution of treatment. High‑dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) was given (three 100 g doses), and therapeutic anticoagulation was started with argatroban. The platelet count showed only a modest and delayed rise after IVIg (to 54 × 10^9/L by about POD14) and then a gradual recovery over weeks, reaching 155 × 10^9/L by POD62 and 202 × 10^9/L by POD87. This slow recovery reflects the unusually severe aHIT phenotype in this patient: autoantibodies with heparin‑independent platelet‑activating capability can produce sustained Fc receptor–mediated platelet consumption and megakaryocyte suppression that is more resistant to immediate reversal than typical heparin‑dependent HIT. The clinical implication is that platelet counts may remain very low for weeks despite appropriate therapy, and clinical decisions (for example, timing of invasive procedures or anticoagulant changes) must account for this protracted course.

Markers of consumptive coagulopathy and relation to anticoagulant choice
Laboratory markers indicated active intravascular coagulation during the early treatment phase. Fibrinogen fell from an elevated postoperative level to a nadir of 2.49 g/L, d‑dimer rose (peaking around POD12), and schistocytes appeared transiently on peripheral smear (POD9–16). Together these findings are consistent with ongoing consumptive coagulopathy driven by antibody‑mediated platelet activation and thrombin generation in aHIT. Although argatroban is an appropriate, short‑acting direct thrombin inhibitor for HIT, the treating team observed progressive biochemical evidence of hypercoagulability while on argatroban (falling fibrinogen, rising d‑dimer, schistocytes). Concern that argatroban was not sufficiently controlling thrombin generation in this severe case led to conversion to therapeutic‑dose fondaparinux on POD14, with an antithrombin concentrate administered because of a mildly reduced AT level. After the switch and continued immunomodulation, the consumptive markers stabilized and the platelet count recovery continued. This sequence illustrates two principles: (1) serial fibrinogen, d‑dimer and smear review provide useful indirect measures of control of HIT‑associated hypercoagulability, and (2) anticoagulant choice in severe aHIT may be guided by clinical and laboratory evidence of ongoing thrombosis rather than by fixed algorithm alone.

Neurologic outcome
The patient developed a left parietal infarct detected after the pronounced platelet fall. Despite this early thrombotic complication, he made a complete neurological recovery by follow‑up. This favorable outcome underscores that timely recognition of HIT/aHIT and rapid institution of non‑heparin anticoagulation and immunotherapy can limit extension of thrombotic injury and permit recovery even in older patients with major perioperative strokes.

Serologic evolution
Serologic testing showed persistent platelet‑activating antibodies for months. Early SRA profiles were characteristic of aHIT: strong heparin‑independent release (high serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin) with activity at low heparin concentrations and loss of activity at very high heparin, and an unusual partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade in vitro. Over the ensuing weeks to months, serial SRA patterns evolved: some later samples continued to show aHIT features, while the last tested sample (POD124) exhibited predominant heparin‑dependent activity (low release at 0 U/mL, marked release at 0.3 U/mL), consistent with waning of the heparin‑independent autoantibody population and transition toward a more conventional HIT seroprofile. Clinically, persistent SRA positivity does not necessarily correlate with ongoing thrombosis risk once the platelet count has recovered, but the prolonged seropositivity seen in aHIT emphasizes the need for caution with future heparin exposure and for individualized timing of anticoagulant changes.

Long‑term anticoagulation plan and rationale
Fondaparinux was continued until POD65, after which apixaban (5 mg twice daily) was resumed for long‑term anticoagulation for atrial fibrillation. The choice to complete a course of fondaparinux and then transition to a direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC) reflects practical balance in HIT management: complete the period of highest thrombotic risk with a non‑heparin parenteral agent that was clinically effective here, then switch to an oral agent for chronic anticoagulation once platelet recovery and clinical stability are achieved. Apixaban is an accepted option after HIT recovery; restarting heparin would be contraindicated while pathogenic antibodies are present.

Follow‑up considerations and implications for future care
This case illustrates several follow‑up issues important for patients recovering from aHIT. First, HIT antibodies (including platelet‑activating antibodies detected by SRA) may persist for many weeks to months; serial testing may show gradual waning, but persistence alone does not mandate indefinite anticoagulation. Second, any future need for heparin exposure (cardiac surgery, cardiopulmonary bypass, vascular procedures) requires careful planning: if serology remains positive or the history is recent, non‑heparin alternatives should be used if feasible; if heparin is unavoidable, specialist input, preprocedural testing and guarded strategies (e.g., use of intraoperative bivalirudin) are required. Third, decisions about the timing of resuming oral anticoagulants and their choice should incorporate platelet recovery, control of any thrombotic process, and the persistence of antibodies; in this patient, apixaban was resumed after stable platelet recovery and clinical improvement. Finally, documentation of the HIT episode and patient education (including medical alert identification and clear instructions to avoid heparins until cleared by a hematologist) are essential to prevent inadvertent heparin re‑exposure.

In summary, the clinical and laboratory course—profound thrombocytopenia with biochemical consumptive features, delayed but eventual platelet recovery under non‑heparin anticoagulation and IVIg, evolving serology from heparin‑independent to predominantly heparin‑dependent activity, and a good neurological outcome—typify a severe aHIT presentation that responded to sustained multidisciplinary management and careful anticoagulant selection.
