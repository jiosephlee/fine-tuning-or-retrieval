Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

## Clinical Context and Presentation After Cardiac Surgery

This patient was an elderly man with longstanding atrial fibrillation on apixaban who underwent diagnostic cardiac catheterization one week before surgery, during which he received a reduced intraprocedural dose of unfractionated heparin (UFH, 2000 U). One week later he underwent complex cardiac surgery on cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) — aortic valve and ascending aorta replacement plus single-vessel coronary bypass and left atrial appendage excision — using the usual intraoperative UFH regimen (total reported ~45,000 U). The operation was described as uneventful, with no major hemorrhage or transfusion requirement and rapid separation from bypass.

Platelet behavior after CPB follows a predictable pattern that is crucial to interpret early postoperative thrombocytopenia. CPB and surgical hemostatic activation typically cause an early, transient fall in platelet count, often most pronounced on day 1, from hemodilution, platelet activation/consumption on extracorporeal surfaces and sequestration. In routine uncomplicated recovery the platelet count then stabilizes and typically begins to recover over the first postoperative week. Against that expected course, this patient’s platelet counts showed only a modest early drop to 155 × 10^9/L on postoperative day (POD) 1 — a change compatible with normal post-CPB kinetics — but then fell progressively and unexpectedly to 50 × 10^9/L by POD5 and reached a nadir of 13 × 10^9/L later in the course. Such a sustained, progressive decline after the initial expected CPB-related fall is not typical and should prompt evaluation for pathological causes rather than attribution to routine postoperative physiology.

Timing and type of perioperative heparin exposures are central to diagnosing heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). Although HIT classically occurs 5–10 days after first heparin exposure, prior heparin exposure in the preceding 100 days can shift onset earlier. In this case, the small UFH dose at catheterization one week prior likely primed antibody formation such that the marked platelet fall began unusually early after the operative heparin exposure. Routine postoperative thromboprophylaxis was started with low-molecular-weight heparin (dalteparin) on POD1 (escalating doses on subsequent days), which is a standard practice but becomes problematic when HIT is developing; recognition of an abnormal platelet trajectory should trigger immediate reconsideration of heparin products. When the progressive decline persisted, prophylactic fondaparinux (a synthetic anti‑Xa agent with very low cross-reactivity in typical HIT) was begun on POD5 as the team moved away from LMWH.

Clinically, the progressive thrombocytopenia was not benign: by POD6 the patient developed worsening delirium and, on imaging, an acute left parietal ischemic infarct was identified. Thrombosis is the major clinical danger of HIT despite thrombocytopenia — antibodies that activate platelets and generate thrombin often produce arterial and venous occlusions. In cardiac surgery patients the overall frequency of HIT is higher than in many other settings (commonly estimated at about 0.5–1.0%, varying with perioperative heparin practice), and when HIT occurs it carries a markedly elevated risk of thrombosis (reported relative risk on the order of 10–20-fold compared with patients without HIT). Thus the combination of an atypical early, progressive platelet fall and a new ischemic stroke strongly raised the index of suspicion for HIT in this postoperative context.

Key clinical points from this presentation:
- Distinguish the expected transient post‑CPB platelet drop (most pronounced immediately post-op with recovery) from a progressive decline beginning within the first week, which is abnormal and should prompt specific evaluation.
- Recent preoperative heparin exposure can accelerate the timing of HIT after surgery, producing an earlier-than-typical onset.
- Initiation or continuation of heparin-based prophylaxis in the setting of unexplained progressive thrombocytopenia risks exacerbating HIT-related hypercoagulability; non‑heparin alternatives should be considered once HIT is suspected.
- New thrombotic events (including arterial strokes) in the setting of thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery are red flags for HIT and warrant urgent diagnostic testing and anticoagulant management changes.

## Pathophysiology of HIT and Autoimmune HIT (aHIT)

Heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) is an antibody‑mediated prothrombotic disorder in which the immune response is directed against complexes of platelet factor 4 (PF4) and polyanions such as unfractionated heparin (UFH). In classic (heparin‑dependent) HIT, circulating IgG antibodies bind PF4–heparin multimers. These immune complexes simultaneously present Fc portions of IgG that engage and cross‑link FcγRIIa receptors on platelet surfaces. FcγRIIa clustering triggers intracellular signaling in platelets that produces shape change, granule release and expression of procoagulant phospholipid, promoting thrombin generation. Activated platelets and thrombin together drive a hypercoagulable, consumptive state: thrombin converts fibrinogen to fibrin (consuming fibrinogen), produces fibrin degradation products and D‑dimer (so D‑dimer rises), and can cause microangiopathic red‑cell injury visible as schistocytes. Clinically this explains why HIT is paradoxically thrombosis‑predominant despite thrombocytopenia.

Autoimmune HIT (aHIT) describes a subgroup of HIT in which the pathogenic antibodies have broader reactivity and can activate platelets in the absence—or with minimal amounts—of heparin. Mechanistically, aHIT antibodies either recognize PF4 alone, PF4 bound in different conformations, or form very stable immune complexes that do not require heparin to assemble. Because they can activate platelets without heparin, aHIT antibodies produce more severe, more prolonged and sometimes relapsing platelet activation and thrombosis than typical HIT. The laboratory and clinical consequences are consistent with more extensive thrombin generation and a greater consumptive coagulopathy, which may manifest as profoundly low platelet counts, falling fibrinogen, rising D‑dimer and intermittent schistocytosis—features that were seen in this patient.

Laboratory testing reflects these mechanistic differences. The PF4/polyanion ELISA detects antibodies that bind PF4 complexes and is usually strongly positive in both classic HIT and aHIT; very high optical density values (as in this case) raise the likelihood of pathogenic antibodies. Functional platelet activation assays, such as the serotonin‑release assay (SRA), distinguish heparin dependence: typical HIT sera cause strong platelet activation at low therapeutic heparin concentrations (commonly tested at 0.1–0.3 U/mL) and lose activity at very high heparin (100 U/mL) because excess heparin disrupts PF4–heparin lattice formation. In aHIT, sera additionally cause platelet activation in the absence of heparin (0 U/mL), reflecting heparin‑independent immune complexes. This case’s SRA showed exactly that pattern—high release at 0 and at 0.1–0.3 U/mL and inhibition at 100 U/mL—supporting a diagnosis of aHIT.

Platelet activation in HIT is principally mediated by FcγRIIa. Experimentally, platelet activation can be inhibited by monoclonal antibodies (for example IV.3) that block FcγRIIa engagement. In most HIT sera this blockade abrogates SRA serotonin release. The unusual serologic finding in this patient was only partial inhibition of platelet activation by IV.3: serotonin release in the presence of IV.3 remained markedly above baseline. This partial resistance implies unusually potent or atypical immune complexes. Possible explanations include very high antibody avidity or concentration causing strong FcγRIIa clustering that is not fully blocked by the monoclonal antibody dose used, recognition of PF4 epitopes that promote multivalent immune complex formation, or engagement of additional platelet‑activating pathways that are less sensitive to FcγRIIa blockade. Whatever the precise mechanism, incomplete IV.3 inhibition is concordant with the clinically severe, persistent platelet activation and consumptive coagulopathy observed in this patient, and it explains why the initial SRA was labeled “indeterminate” rather than simply negative.

The timing of HIT in relation to heparin exposure also follows immunologic kinetics. Primary immune responses to heparin–PF4 usually take several days to develop, so typical postoperative HIT appears between postoperative day (POD) 5–10. However, if a patient has recent prior exposure to heparin (for example the diagnostic catheterization one week before surgery in this case), memory B cells can drive a more rapid anamnestic response and clinical platelet count fall earlier than POD5. That principle accounts for the unusually early onset here. Over time, as the immune response wanes or the antibody repertoire shifts, serologic profiles may evolve: this patient’s later samples showed diminution of heparin‑independent activity and eventually a pattern more characteristic of heparin‑dependent HIT, consistent with decreasing aHIT antibody titres or loss of the most pathogenic clones over weeks to months.

In summary, classic HIT depends on PF4–heparin immune complexes that activate platelets via FcγRIIa; aHIT represents antibodies capable of heparin‑independent platelet activation, producing more severe and persistent thrombin generation and consumptive laboratory abnormalities. The high ELISA OD, SRA activation at 0 U/mL, and partial resistance to IV.3 blockade in this patient all map to that pathophysiology and explain the patient’s profound thrombocytopenia, biochemical evidence of consumptive coagulopathy, and prolonged clinical course.

## Diagnostic Reasoning and Differential in Early Postoperative Thrombocytopenia

Early postoperative thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery presents a diagnostic challenge because multiple common and uncommon causes overlap temporally and because cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) itself produces predictable platelet changes. Effective diagnostic reasoning therefore proceeds stepwise, integrating timing, magnitude of platelet fall, presence or absence of thrombosis, focused laboratory testing (including peripheral smear), and targeted imaging. In this case the diagnostic pathway illustrates how those elements were used to distinguish autoimmune heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT) from other postoperative causes.

Timing as a diagnostic clue
A central principle for HIT diagnosis is the timing of the platelet count fall. Classic HIT usually occurs between postoperative day (POD) 5 and 10 after heparin exposure. A platelet fall reaching nadir before POD5 is therefore atypical and should prompt consideration of alternative causes—unless there was antecedent heparin exposure within the prior 5–10 days. The patient’s platelet decline began immediately after surgery and reached 50×10^9/L by POD5, which at first glance was earlier than typical for HIT after intraoperative UFH. Crucially, review identified a preoperative cardiac catheterization during which unfractionated heparin (UFH) had been given 1 week before surgery. Preoperative heparin exposure explains an “early” onset HIT presentation because antibody formation may have already been initiated; thus timing must be interpreted in the context of all recent heparin exposures.

Applying clinical pretest probability in the post‑CPB patient
In post‑CPB patients, the baseline expected platelet fall is modest (often 30–50%) due to hemodilution and platelet activation during bypass, with recovery thereafter. When applying a clinical pretest probability for HIT, integrate: timing (adjusted for prior UFH exposure), magnitude and trajectory of the fall (progressive decline to very low counts is concerning), and presence of new thrombosis. In this case the progressive fall from 210 to 24×10^9/L, together with a new ischemic stroke on POD6, raised high suspicion for an immune‑mediated process such as HIT rather than routine postoperative thrombocytopenia. Thus the pretest probability was sufficiently high to justify immediate laboratory HIT testing and empiric change in anticoagulation once other causes were deemed unlikely.

Differential diagnosis and why other causes were unlikely
Common postoperative explanations include hemodilution/consumptive loss from surgery and CPB, infection/sepsis with consumptive coagulopathy, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), post‑transfusion purpura, and drug‑induced immune thrombocytopenia. Each was considered and systematically excluded or rendered unlikely by clinical and laboratory findings in this patient: hemodilution/expected postoperative fall was insufficient to explain the progressive and profound thrombocytopenia; there was no hemodynamic instability to suggest cardiogenic shock; blood and urine cultures were negative and there were no clinical features of sepsis; DIC typically produces prolonged PT/PTT and low fibrinogen—here PT/PTT were normal and fibrinogen was elevated consistent with an acute phase response; TTP classically has severe microangiopathic hemolysis with low haptoglobin and abundant schistocytes—initially haptoglobin was elevated and schistocytes were absent on the early smear; post‑transfusion purpura was excluded because no transfusions had been administered; and timing and medication history made drug‑induced immune thrombocytopenia unlikely. The stepwise exclusion of these alternatives strengthened the suspicion for HIT given the antibody‑provoking exposures and the thrombotic complication.

Laboratory evaluation and peripheral smear
The diagnostic workup appropriately included a complete blood count with peripheral smear, hemolysis studies, coagulation panel, and relevant chemistries. The smear excluded pseudothrombocytopenia from platelet clumping and initially showed no schistocytosis, arguing against microangiopathic hemolysis at the outset. Hemolysis labs (normal to elevated haptoglobin) supported absence of hemolytic TTP early on. Coagulation studies showed normal PT/INR and PTT and an elevated fibrinogen, consistent with the early postoperative acute phase rather than DIC. Serial follow‑up labs later demonstrated rising D‑dimer, falling fibrinogen and appearance of some schistocytes, changes interpreted as secondary consumptive coagulopathy related to progressive HIT‑associated thrombosis and fibrin formation rather than primary DIC. Cultures and basic chemistries were unremarkable, further narrowing the differential.

Imaging for thrombosis
Because thrombosis is a common and important manifestation of HIT, particularly when platelet activation is marked, imaging should be prompted by new focal neurologic signs or other ischemic symptoms. In this patient, worsening delirium and further platelet fall triggered CT brain imaging that identified an acute left parietal infarct. The documented thrombotic event substantially increased the clinical probability of HIT in the appropriate temporal and exposure context.

HIT testing strategy and interpretation
HIT diagnosis relies on a two‑step testing approach: a sensitive immunoassay that detects PF4/polyanion antibodies (eg, ELISA) and a functional platelet activation assay (eg, serotonin‑release assay, SRA) that confirms pathogenic, platelet‑activating antibodies. In this case the PF4/polyanion ELISA returned a strong positive (OD 2.2), which is highly supportive but not alone diagnostic because ELISA positivity can occur without clinical HIT. The SRA is considered the gold standard functional test; a typical HIT-positive SRA demonstrates heparin‑dependent platelet activation (high release at low‑to‑therapeutic heparin concentrations and suppression at very high heparin concentrations).

Interpreting an ‘indeterminate’ SRA in aHIT
This patient’s initial SRA was described as “indeterminate” because the serum caused marked serotonin release both in the absence of heparin (0 U/mL) and at low heparin concentrations, and because addition of an FcγRIIA‑blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3) did not fully suppress activation. These features are characteristic of autoimmune HIT (aHIT), a variant in which antibodies can activate platelets without exogenous heparin and may form unusually stable immune complexes. Key interpretive points for clinicians: do not dismiss SRA indeterminate results as non‑HIT without reviewing the activation pattern. Strong activation at 0 U/mL heparin indicates heparin‑independent activating antibodies; lack of complete inhibition by an Fc receptor blocker suggests unusually potent immune complexes that may partially resist blockade in vitro. In such scenarios, the clinical picture (timing adjusted for prior heparin exposure, thrombosis, severe thrombocytopenia) and a strongly positive ELISA support the diagnosis despite SRA atypia. Thus an “indeterminate” SRA in the appropriate context should raise suspicion for aHIT rather than provide reassurance.

Role of serial functional testing
Because antibody characteristics and titers evolve over time, serial functional testing can clarify an initially atypical picture. In this case, subsequent SRA profiles over weeks showed evolution toward patterns more typical of aHIT and later toward heparin‑dependent behavior as the antibody repertoire waned. Serial testing can therefore document persistence of platelet‑activating antibodies, changes in their heparin dependence, and provide laboratory corroboration of the clinical diagnosis made from the constellation of timing, exposure, thrombosis, and initial ELISA positivity.

Putting it together — diagnostic conclusion
By integrating the early but explainable timing (preoperative UFH exposure), progressive and profound platelet fall, new thrombotic stroke, strong positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, and an SRA pattern of heparin‑independent activation with only partial Fc receptor blockade, the diagnostic reasoning converged on autoimmune HIT. Systematic exclusion of competing causes and use of serial functional assays reinforced the diagnosis and justified HIT‑directed management despite the initial “indeterminate” functional test result. This case highlights the necessity of interpreting HIT assays in clinical context, recognizing serologic variants such as aHIT, and avoiding false reassurance from atypical SRA findings.

## Management of aHIT with Thrombosis and Severe Thrombocytopenia

Autoimmune HIT (aHIT) requires prompt, directed management that combines immediate cessation of heparin exposure, effective non‑heparin anticoagulation to prevent and treat HIT‑related thrombosis, adjunctive immunomodulation in severe or refractory cases, and careful laboratory and clinical monitoring for ongoing hypercoagulability or bleeding. The treatment choices in this case illustrate these principles.

Immediate actions
The first and nonnegotiable step is to stop all heparin products (unfractionated heparin and low‑molecular‑weight heparin) and eliminate hidden heparin exposures (eg, heparin‑containing flushes, heparin‑coated catheters). In this patient, prophylactic dalteparin and the earlier intraoperative/small procedural heparin exposures were recognized and discontinued once HIT was suspected. Avoiding further heparin prevents amplification of PF4/heparin immune complexes and is essential even before confirmatory testing returns.

Choice of non‑heparin anticoagulant and initial selection
Because HIT is highly prothrombotic, therapeutic anticoagulation with a non‑heparin agent should be started immediately when HIT is suspected and the bleeding risk allows. Options include parenteral direct thrombin inhibitors (argatroban, bivalirudin) and indirect anti‑Xa agents such as fondaparinux (an antithrombin‑dependent factor Xa inhibitor). Each agent has tradeoffs:

- Argatroban: short half‑life, hepatic clearance, continuous IV infusion with PTT monitoring. Its short half‑life is advantageous when rapid reversal of anticoagulant effect may be needed (eg, new bleeding or urgent procedure). In this case, the treating team selected argatroban initially because the patient had an acute ischemic stroke and severe thrombocytopenia—scenarios in which the ability to rapidly stop anticoagulation is desirable.

- Bivalirudin: another direct thrombin inhibitor with predictable pharmacokinetics used commonly in cardiac surgery settings; renal clearance considerations differ from argatroban.

- Fondaparinux: given subcutaneously once daily and convenient for outpatient transition; however, it is AT‑dependent and has a long half‑life, which makes its anticoagulant effect less rapidly reversible. It is considered an acceptable off‑label option for HIT in many settings. In this patient, fondaparinux was initially given at prophylactic dose before HIT was recognized and was later used therapeutically after reassessment.

The choice should be individualized by weighing thrombotic risk, bleeding risk, organ function (hepatic/renal), need for procedures, and monitoring/logistics. Here argatroban was chosen to allow tight control in the immediate poststroke period and in the setting of very low platelets.

Adjunctive immunomodulation: IVIG
Autoimmune HIT antibodies can activate platelets independent of heparin and can be unusually persistent or aggressive. High‑dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) is used as an adjunct in severe aHIT to block FcγRIIa‑mediated platelet activation. Proposed mechanisms include competitive inhibition at the platelet Fc receptor (FcγRIIa), provision of anti‑idiotypic antibodies that neutralize pathogenic anti‑PF4 antibodies, and modulation of immune effector function.

Dosing strategy and expectations:
- High doses are required in aHIT (typical regimens used include 1–2 g/kg total, often delivered as 0.4 g/kg/day for 5 days or 1 g/kg/day for 2 days). Repeated or additional dosing may be necessary if the clinical response is inadequate.
- Response is variable. In many aHIT cases a brisk platelet rise follows IVIG, but some patients (as in this report) show only a modest or delayed platelet response, reflecting very potent or persistent pathogenic antibodies.
In the case, large cumulative IVIG doses (3 × 100 g) were administered because the platelet response was poor after the initial two doses. The team anticipated variability and monitored clinical and laboratory markers to assess whether immunomodulation plus anticoagulation was controlling hypercoagulability.

Assessing control of hypercoagulability and recognizing consumptive coagulopathy
Because aHIT can produce severe ongoing platelet activation and consumptive coagulopathy despite anticoagulation, clinical and laboratory surveillance focuses on evidence of continued thrombosis or consumption:
- Platelet counts: serial monitoring to document recovery trends.
- D‑dimer: an elevated or rising d‑dimer suggests ongoing thrombosis/ fibrin turnover; falling values after therapy suggest control of thrombogenic activity.
- Fibrinogen: falling fibrinogen in the postoperative setting may indicate consumption from ongoing coagulation; in this patient, fibrinogen fell during initial argatroban therapy, consistent with persistent HIT‑driven coagulopathy.
- Peripheral blood smear: emergence of schistocytes can signal microangiopathic hemolysis/consumptive thrombosis.
These markers guided management in the case: declining fibrinogen, rising d‑dimer and appearance of schistocytes signaled that argatroban plus IVIG had not yet fully controlled hypercoagulability, prompting reconsideration of anticoagulant strategy.

Switching to fondaparinux and the role of antithrombin supplementation
When control of HIT‑related hypercoagulability is inadequate, practical issues and therapeutic effect may prompt switching anticoagulants. In this patient the team switched from argatroban to therapeutic‑dose fondaparinux (10 mg daily) because of concern that argatroban was not sufficiently controlling coagulation (laboratory evidence described above), and because fondaparinux allows once‑daily dosing that is simpler for prolonged therapy.

A specific consideration with fondaparinux is its dependence on endogenous antithrombin (AT) for activity. If AT levels are low—common in consumptive states or postoperatively—fondaparinux efficacy may be reduced. The team measured AT activity (0.70 U/mL) and gave supplemental AT concentrate (1000 U) before starting fondaparinux to optimize anticoagulant effect. This illustrates that when choosing an AT‑dependent agent in a patient with suspected AT deficiency, AT replacement should be considered to ensure therapeutic effect.

Neurologic considerations: balancing anticoagulation intensity after ischemic stroke with severe thrombocytopenia
Managing anticoagulation after an acute ischemic stroke in the setting of aHIT and very low platelets requires balancing the high thrombotic risk of HIT against the risk of hemorrhagic transformation of the cerebral infarct. Practical considerations include:
- Preference for anticoagulants with short half‑lives when immediate cessation may be required (eg, argatroban initially).
- Cautious target anticoagulation intensity (PTT goals individualized) and close neurological monitoring.
- Delay of invasive procedures and avoidance of unnecessary antiplatelet or invasive interventions while platelets are very low.
In this case, argatroban’s short half‑life and PTT titration allowed the team to provide therapeutic anticoagulation while maintaining the option for rapid reversal if intracranial bleeding developed.

Transition of care: duration of parenteral therapy and criteria for switching to oral anticoagulant
Duration of parenteral non‑heparin anticoagulation in HIT is individualized and depends on platelet recovery, clinical stability and resolution of acute thrombotic risk. Common practice is to continue non‑heparin anticoagulation until platelet counts have substantially recovered (often ≥100–150 × 10^9/L) and the patient is clinically stable, then transition to an appropriate oral anticoagulant if long‑term anticoagulation is required.

In this patient, fondaparinux was continued until platelet counts normalized (fondaparinux given until POD65), and apixaban (an oral direct factor Xa inhibitor) was resumed for long‑term anticoagulation once the platelet count had recovered (155 × 10^9/L at POD62) and the patient was stable. This pragmatic approach—transition when platelet recovery and clinical stability are achieved—is consistent with how clinicians move from parenteral agents to oral anticoagulation in HIT/aHIT.

Supportive care and avoidance strategies
- Platelet transfusions: generally avoided in HIT/aHIT because they can fuel thrombosis; reserved for life‑threatening bleeding or urgent procedures where platelet support is unavoidable.
- Documentation: record heparin allergy/HIT diagnosis clearly in the chart and issue allergy bracelets/cards to prevent future heparin exposure.
- Patient education: explain the diagnosis, need to avoid heparin in the future, and the plan for anticoagulation and follow‑up.
- Follow‑up testing: antibody assays (eg, SRA) may remain positive for weeks to months; clinical decisions are guided primarily by platelet recovery and clinical course rather than serology alone.

Clinical lessons from this case
This patient’s management illustrates several key points in aHIT care: immediately stop heparin and start an appropriate non‑heparin anticoagulant (argatroban was chosen here because of stroke and bleeding‑risk considerations); use IVIG for severe aHIT but anticipate variable responses and be prepared to repeat dosing; monitor dynamic laboratory markers (platelet count, fibrinogen, d‑dimer, peripheral smear) to assess ongoing hypercoagulability and treatment efficacy; consider AT supplementation when switching to AT‑dependent agents like fondaparinux if AT activity is low; and defer transition to oral anticoagulants until platelet recovery and clinical stability are achieved. Avoid platelet transfusions except for life‑threatening bleeding, and ensure clear documentation to prevent future heparin exposure.

## Clinical Course, Laboratory Evolution, and Outcomes

After the diagnosis of autoimmune heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT) was suspected, the patient’s clinical course was dominated by profound thrombocytopenia, biochemical evidence of ongoing consumptive coagulopathy, a thrombotic complication (left parietal stroke), and a protracted, ultimately successful hematologic recovery under non‑heparin anticoagulation and adjunctive immunomodulation.

Platelet trajectory and response to therapy
The platelet count nadired at 13 × 10^9/L (POD7) despite prompt cessation of heparins and institution of treatment. High‑dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) was given (three 100 g doses), and therapeutic anticoagulation was started with argatroban. The platelet count showed only a modest and delayed rise after IVIg (to 54 × 10^9/L by about POD14) and then a gradual recovery over weeks, reaching 155 × 10^9/L by POD62 and 202 × 10^9/L by POD87. This slow recovery reflects the unusually severe aHIT phenotype in this patient: autoantibodies with heparin‑independent platelet‑activating capability can produce sustained Fc receptor–mediated platelet consumption and megakaryocyte suppression that is more resistant to immediate reversal than typical heparin‑dependent HIT. The clinical implication is that platelet counts may remain very low for weeks despite appropriate therapy, and clinical decisions (for example, timing of invasive procedures or anticoagulant changes) must account for this protracted course.

Markers of consumptive coagulopathy and relation to anticoagulant choice
Laboratory markers indicated active intravascular coagulation during the early treatment phase. Fibrinogen fell from an elevated postoperative level to a nadir of 2.49 g/L, d‑dimer rose (peaking around POD12), and schistocytes appeared transiently on peripheral smear (POD9–16). Together these findings are consistent with ongoing consumptive coagulopathy driven by antibody‑mediated platelet activation and thrombin generation in aHIT. Although argatroban is an appropriate, short‑acting direct thrombin inhibitor for HIT, the treating team observed progressive biochemical evidence of hypercoagulability while on argatroban (falling fibrinogen, rising d‑dimer, schistocytes). Concern that argatroban was not sufficiently controlling thrombin generation in this severe case led to conversion to therapeutic‑dose fondaparinux on POD14, with an antithrombin concentrate administered because of a mildly reduced AT level. After the switch and continued immunomodulation, the consumptive markers stabilized and the platelet count recovery continued. This sequence illustrates two principles: (1) serial fibrinogen, d‑dimer and smear review provide useful indirect measures of control of HIT‑associated hypercoagulability, and (2) anticoagulant choice in severe aHIT may be guided by clinical and laboratory evidence of ongoing thrombosis rather than by fixed algorithm alone.

Neurologic outcome
The patient developed a left parietal infarct detected after the pronounced platelet fall. Despite this early thrombotic complication, he made a complete neurological recovery by follow‑up. This favorable outcome underscores that timely recognition of HIT/aHIT and rapid institution of non‑heparin anticoagulation and immunotherapy can limit extension of thrombotic injury and permit recovery even in older patients with major perioperative strokes.

Serologic evolution
Serologic testing showed persistent platelet‑activating antibodies for months. Early SRA profiles were characteristic of aHIT: strong heparin‑independent release (high serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin) with activity at low heparin concentrations and loss of activity at very high heparin, and an unusual partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade in vitro. Over the ensuing weeks to months, serial SRA patterns evolved: some later samples continued to show aHIT features, while the last tested sample (POD124) exhibited predominant heparin‑dependent activity (low release at 0 U/mL, marked release at 0.3 U/mL), consistent with waning of the heparin‑independent autoantibody population and transition toward a more conventional HIT seroprofile. Clinically, persistent SRA positivity does not necessarily correlate with ongoing thrombosis risk once the platelet count has recovered, but the prolonged seropositivity seen in aHIT emphasizes the need for caution with future heparin exposure and for individualized timing of anticoagulant changes.

Long‑term anticoagulation plan and rationale
Fondaparinux was continued until POD65, after which apixaban (5 mg twice daily) was resumed for long‑term anticoagulation for atrial fibrillation. The choice to complete a course of fondaparinux and then transition to a direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC) reflects practical balance in HIT management: complete the period of highest thrombotic risk with a non‑heparin parenteral agent that was clinically effective here, then switch to an oral agent for chronic anticoagulation once platelet recovery and clinical stability are achieved. Apixaban is an accepted option after HIT recovery; restarting heparin would be contraindicated while pathogenic antibodies are present.

Follow‑up considerations and implications for future care
This case illustrates several follow‑up issues important for patients recovering from aHIT. First, HIT antibodies (including platelet‑activating antibodies detected by SRA) may persist for many weeks to months; serial testing may show gradual waning, but persistence alone does not mandate indefinite anticoagulation. Second, any future need for heparin exposure (cardiac surgery, cardiopulmonary bypass, vascular procedures) requires careful planning: if serology remains positive or the history is recent, non‑heparin alternatives should be used if feasible; if heparin is unavoidable, specialist input, preprocedural testing and guarded strategies (e.g., use of intraoperative bivalirudin) are required. Third, decisions about the timing of resuming oral anticoagulants and their choice should incorporate platelet recovery, control of any thrombotic process, and the persistence of antibodies; in this patient, apixaban was resumed after stable platelet recovery and clinical improvement. Finally, documentation of the HIT episode and patient education (including medical alert identification and clear instructions to avoid heparins until cleared by a hematologist) are essential to prevent inadvertent heparin re‑exposure.

In summary, the clinical and laboratory course—profound thrombocytopenia with biochemical consumptive features, delayed but eventual platelet recovery under non‑heparin anticoagulation and IVIg, evolving serology from heparin‑independent to predominantly heparin‑dependent activity, and a good neurological outcome—typify a severe aHIT presentation that responded to sustained multidisciplinary management and careful anticoagulant selection.

## Key Lessons, Pitfalls, and Practical Takeaways

This case illustrates several practical points that should change how you think about thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery and how you investigate and manage suspected autoimmune heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT).

First, pay close attention to timing of platelet decline and any recent heparin exposures. Classic postoperative HIT after cardiopulmonary bypass usually presents between postoperative day (POD) 5 and 10. A pronounced platelet fall before POD5 is uncommon and should prompt an active search for preoperative heparin (even small intra‑procedural doses such as those used during a transradial cardiac catheterization). Identifying recent heparin exposure can explain an early onset of HIT and should raise your index of suspicion for HIT or aHIT despite an apparently “too‑early” time course.

Second, interpret laboratory testing in the full clinical context. A very strong PF4/polyanion ELISA (high optical density) together with an atypical or “indeterminate” serotonin‑release assay (SRA) should not be dismissed. Key SRA features that point toward aHIT include robust platelet activation in the absence of added heparin (0 U/mL) and incomplete inhibition of activation by an FcγRIIa‑blocking reagent (IV.3). These findings indicate heparin‑independent, highly activating antibodies — the serologic hallmark of aHIT. Recognize that unusual SRA patterns (partial IV.3 blockade, activation at 0 U/mL heparin) are a pitfall that can lead to false reassurance if one expects only the classic heparin‑dependent profile.

Third, do not be reassured by normal PT/PTT or by postoperative inflammatory changes such as high fibrinogen when considering ongoing hypercoagulability. Early after surgery fibrinogen is often elevated as an acute‑phase reactant, and routine clotting times can remain normal even while HIT‑mediated platelet activation and consumptive coagulopathy are evolving. Instead, follow trends (falling fibrinogen, rising D‑dimer) and peripheral smear findings (new schistocytes) as indirect evidence of uncontrolled thrombin generation; these dynamic measures helped indicate progressive HIT‑related hypercoagulability in this case.

Fourth, severe thrombocytopenia with thrombotic complications and signs of consumption should prompt early, aggressive treatment and awareness that responses can be variable. aHIT is associated with profound platelet falls and a high thrombotic risk; intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) is indicated in severe aHIT because it can block pathogenic Fc‑mediated platelet activation. However, as this case shows, the platelet response to IVIG may be blunted or delayed in particularly severe aHIT phenotypes — anticipate variable efficacy and continue to reassess clinical and laboratory markers of coagulation.

Fifth, select non‑heparin anticoagulation individualized to the clinical situation. Options include parenteral direct thrombin inhibitors (e.g., argatroban) and factor Xa inhibitors such as fondaparinux. In a patient with recent stroke or high bleeding risk, an agent with a short half‑life and easy titration (argatroban) may be preferable initially. If switching to an AT‑dependent agent like fondaparinux, check antithrombin activity and consider AT repletion when levels are low; this case demonstrates that supplemental AT was used before initiating fondaparinux to optimize efficacy. Also plan for practical considerations (continuous IV infusion and PTT monitoring for argatroban versus once‑daily subcutaneous dosing for fondaparinux) when choosing therapy.

Sixth, explicitly document heparin exposure and label heparin as contraindicated for future care. Patients with confirmed HIT/aHIT should have heparin clearly recorded in their chart and their allergy list, and perioperative teams must be warned so that cardiopulmonary bypass or other procedures are planned with non‑heparin strategies. This prevents inadvertent re‑exposure, which can provoke rapid, severe recurrence.

Seventh, use serial functional testing to guide management and to document antibody evolution. Repeated SRAs or other platelet‑activation assays can show changing antibody phenotype over weeks to months (for example, waning of heparin‑independent activity to a more typical heparin‑dependent pattern). These data can help inform duration of non‑heparin anticoagulation and timing of safe re‑exposure to heparin if ever considered (which is rarely done).

Finally, practical bedside points: avoid platelet transfusions unless there is life‑threatening bleeding, since transfused platelets may fuel thrombosis in HIT; maintain a low threshold to escalate or change anticoagulant strategy when laboratory trends suggest ongoing consumption; and coordinate haematology, neurology/neurosurgery, and surgical teams early when thrombotic complications or bleeding risks coexist.

Taken together, these lessons emphasize vigilance for aHIT in atypical presentations, careful interpretation of immunologic and functional assays, early and individualized non‑heparin anticoagulation plus consideration of IVIG, and documented avoidance of future heparin exposure.
