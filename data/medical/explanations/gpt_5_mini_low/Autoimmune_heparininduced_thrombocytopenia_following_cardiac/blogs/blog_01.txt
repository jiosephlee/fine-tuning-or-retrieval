Title: Blog about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

## Early postoperative thrombocytopenia after CPB: when pre-op heparin flips the script

What makes this case interesting
A man in his 70s has uncomplicated CPB surgery, gets the expected early fall in platelets, then by POD5 his platelets are down to 50×10^9/L and on POD6 he’s at 24×10^9/L with a left parietal infarct. A strong PF4/polyanion ELISA and a serotonin‑release assay (SRA) that shows very high platelet activation even with no heparin — plus only partial inhibition with an FcγRIIa‑blocking antibody — point to autoimmune HIT (aHIT). The kicker: the HIT was likely primed by a tiny 2,000 U UFH dose during a transradial diagnostic cath one week before surgery. This case reminds us that the classic POD5–10 window for HIT can be short‑circuited by pre‑operative heparin exposure and that aHIT can be unusually aggressive and serologically atypical.

Why this matters at the bedside
Post‑CPB thrombocytopenia is common and usually benign, so clinicians are tempted to attribute early falls to hemodilution, consumption on the bypass circuit, platelet activation, or drugs. Anchoring on “expected” post‑op thrombocytopenia delays recognition of immune HIT — and in aHIT the thrombotic risk and degree of thrombocytopenia can be profound. Small preoperative UFH exposures (eg, cath lab heparin) can immunologically prime patients so that antibody‑mediated platelet activation appears earlier than the usual POD5–10 window. In this case that earlier onset, combined with a strong immunoassay and a very severe platelet nadir plus cerebral infarct, signaled HIT despite the early timing.

Practical approach — how to think through early post‑CPB thrombocytopenia
1) Don’t use timing in isolation. POD1–POD4 platelet declines are often expected after CPB. But if:
   - the decline is progressive rather than a single nadir followed by recovery,
   - the fall is steep (eg, >50% from baseline),
   - platelet counts continue to drop after POD3–4, or
   - there is new thrombosis (even neurologic signs without overt focal deficits),
then broaden your differential to include HIT/aHIT.

2) Ask about recent heparin exposures before surgery. Small doses given at diagnostic cath, interventional procedures, or even bridging LMWH can prime anti‑PF4 immune responses such that clinical HIT appears earlier after a later heparin re‑exposure (eg, intraop UFH for CPB). In this patient the 2,000 U UFH at cath one week earlier likely accounted for the early presentation.

3) Use the 4Ts cautiously in post‑CPB patients. The 4Ts score (Thrombocytopenia, Timing, Thrombosis, oTher causes) still helps, but the “Timing” component requires nuance after CPB:
   - Classic POD5–10 timing can be shortened if pre‑operative heparin was given.
   - Thrombosis should weigh heavily — any new infarct, limb ischemia, or unexpected clot should raise suspicion.
   - “Other causes” are abundant after cardiac surgery; document why they are less likely (no transfusion, no active infection, stable hemodynamics).

4) Don’t wait for a confirmatory functional assay to act. Send both an immunoassay (PF4 ELISA) and a functional platelet activation test if available, but if the ELISA is strongly positive (high OD) and clinical suspicion is high, stop heparin and switch to a non‑heparin anticoagulant empirically. In this case clinicians changed anticoagulation promptly to argatroban and then to fondaparinux with IVIG when aHIT was suspected.

5) Recognize serologic hallmarks of aHIT. In aHIT you may see:
   - strong ELISA positivity,
   - platelet activation in SRA even without added heparin (0 U/mL),
   - sometimes lack of inhibition at high heparin concentrations,
   - and, rarely, incomplete inhibition with FcγRIIa blockade (this case shows partial IV.3 resistance). These atypical patterns point to very potent, possibly heparin‑independent antibodies that cause fulminant platelet activation.

6) Consider adjunctive therapies earlier in aHIT. High‑dose IVIG can rapidly block Fcγ‑mediated platelet activation in many aHIT patients and is often used when platelet counts are profoundly low or thrombosis is present. Expect variable responses; this patient had only a modest platelet rise after large IVIG doses, emphasizing the heterogeneity and potential severity of aHIT.

Management highlights from the case
- Stop all heparins immediately when HIT/aHIT is suspected.
- Start a non‑heparin anticoagulant (argatroban, bivalirudin, or therapeutic fondaparinux — remember fondaparinux is off‑label for HIT but commonly used). Choose based on bleeding risk, renal/hepatic function, need for rapid reversal, monitoring logistics.
- For severe aHIT, add high‑dose IVIG. Monitor platelet recovery, d‑dimer and fibrinogen trends, and peripheral smear for schistocytes as indicators of consumptive coagulopathy.
- Be prepared to switch anticoagulants if the chosen agent doesn’t control hypercoagulability (in this case argatroban was changed to fondaparinux with supplemental antithrombin).
- Continue non‑heparin anticoagulation until platelet recovery and immunologic testing indicate waning antibody activity; long‑term anticoagulation transition should consider the clinical thrombotic risk (this patient restarted apixaban once platelets recovered).

Clinical pearls
- A small pre‑op heparin dose (eg, 2,000 U at cath) can prime anti‑PF4 antibody formation so that HIT appears early after subsequent intra‑op heparin. Don’t dismiss early POD platelet falls without checking recent heparin exposures.
- In cardiac surgery patients the 4Ts timing element needs reinterpretation: early does not equal “not HIT” if there was recent heparin exposure.
- Very high OD ELISA results plus thrombosis in the setting of falling platelets should prompt immediate non‑heparin anticoagulation, even while awaiting functional testing.
- aHIT can show heparin‑independent platelet activation in the SRA (release at 0 U/mL). Partial resistance to FcγRIIa blockade (IV.3) is rare but possible — treat the patient, not the lab category “indeterminate.”
- Track fibrinogen, d‑dimer, and peripheral film for evidence of consumptive coagulopathy. Falling fibrinogen, rising d‑dimer and schistocytes despite anticoagulation suggest ongoing pathologic platelet activation and thrombosis.

Take‑home messages
- Small, recent preoperative heparin exposures (eg, diagnostic cath) can precipitate early‑onset HIT/aHIT after CPB — timing alone is not enough to exclude HIT.
- In suspected HIT/aHIT after cardiac surgery: stop heparin, start an appropriate non‑heparin anticoagulant, send ELISA and functional testing, and consider early IVIG in severe cases.
- Strong ELISA positivity + thrombosis + progressive platelet fall = act now, even if SRA is atypical or “indeterminate.”
- Treat the clinical syndrome; atypical SRA profiles (eg, heparin‑independent activation or partial IV.3 resistance) can occur in aHIT and should not delay therapy.

This case is a crisp reminder: in post‑CPB thrombocytopenia, look beyond “expected” physiologic drops. Ask about pre‑op heparin, watch the trajectory, and act fast when the pattern — steep fall, strong immunoassay, or thrombosis — screams immune HIT.
