Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

## Presentation and Initial Evaluation of Recurrent Pleural Effusion in a Child

This case illustrates how a predominantly thoracic presentation can obscure an abdominal source. The child presented repeatedly with dyspnea, pleuritic chest pain and a large, unilateral (right-sided) pleural effusion. In pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) the respiratory complaints often dominate; abdominal pain or systemic features of pancreatitis may be absent or mild. Recognizing this pattern — recurrent, large, unilateral effusions with transient clinical improvement after drainage — should prompt consideration of an extrapulmonary cause once routine pulmonary etiologies are excluded.

Focused history is high-yield. In addition to the timeline and character of respiratory symptoms, ask specifically about recent blunt abdominal trauma, even if initially denied; in this case the history of bicycle-handlebar injury emerged only after repeated questioning. Enquire about preceding abdominal pain, vomiting, weight loss, prior pancreatitis, family history of hereditary pancreatitis, cystic fibrosis or known genetic mutations, and use of medications that predispose to pancreatitis. Document tuberculosis exposure because it is a common cause of pediatric pleural effusion in many settings and may bias early management, but keep in mind TB classically causes lymphocyte-predominant effusions and systemic symptoms that may be absent in PPF. Also note any vomiting or severe retching that might suggest oesophageal rupture (Boerhaave’s), which can also produce amylase-rich pleural fluid.

Examination in large pleural effusion is often straightforward: reduced chest expansion and tactile fremitus, stony dullness to percussion and diminished breath sounds over the affected hemithorax, as seen in this child. Assess the patient’s oxygenation and hemodynamic status immediately — tachypnea, hypoxemia and tachycardia may require prompt respiratory support. Look for systemic signs (fever, cachexia, lymphadenopathy) that point toward infection, malignancy or chronic disease; absence of such signs in this case made infective and malignant causes less likely.

Immediate stabilization priorities are oxygenation and relief of respiratory compromise. Supplemental oxygen and analgesia were appropriate initial measures in the child who presented with hypoxemia. Large symptomatic effusions merit prompt diagnostic and therapeutic thoracentesis: bedside ultrasound to confirm and quantify fluid improves safety and success and was used in this case to estimate about one litre of fluid prior to aspiration. Indications for placing an intercostal drain (ICD) include recurrent or large-volume effusions that reaccumulate rapidly, hemothorax or hydropneumothorax on imaging, or need for continuous drainage and monitoring. In this patient repeated large-volume aspirates and recurrence prompted ICD placement.

Early diagnostic tests should be targeted and pragmatic. A chest radiograph establishes laterality and mass effect and, together with lung ultrasound, quantifies the collection and informs procedural planning. Pleural fluid analysis is essential: appearance, cell count and differential, protein, lactate dehydrogenase, Gram stain and culture, cytology and targeted tests (ADA, acid-fast testing or GeneXpert where TB is a concern). Critically, send pleural fluid amylase and lipase when the effusion is large, recurrent, blood-stained, or when there is any history of abdominal trauma or pancreatitis; markedly elevated pleural fluid amylase (often >1,000 U/L) is a key clue to pancreatic origin, as was demonstrated in this case. Imaging beyond CXR — contrast-enhanced CT and MRCP — are indicated when pancreatic fistula is suspected to delineate collections and fistulous tracts.

Be aware of common early pitfalls and anchoring biases. A blood-stained pleural effusion may suggest trauma, malignancy or pulmonary embolism, and a mildly elevated ADA plus TB contact may lead to premature initiation of antitubercular treatment, as occurred here. However, ADA can be nonspecifically elevated and TB should not be assumed without corroborative microbiology or consistent clinical context. Similarly, failure to elicit or to revisit history of abdominal trauma can delay diagnosis. Maintain a broad differential for haemorrhagic effusions and include pancreatic causes in recurrent, amylase-rich pleural effusions; obtain amylase/lipase in pleural fluid early to avoid unnecessary empirical therapies and delayed definitive imaging and management.

## Pathophysiology and Etiology of Pancreatopleural Fistula (PPF) in Children

A pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) results when pancreatic secretions escape the normal ductal system and find a path into the thoracic cavity. Understanding PPF requires following three linked points: how the duct or a pseudocyst ruptures, how pancreatic fluid tracks from the retroperitoneum into the chest, and what the biochemical effects of that fluid are once it reaches the pleural space. In the presented child these mechanisms explain why recurrent, blood‑stained pleural effusions were the dominant clinical problem while abdominal signs were minimal.

Duct disruption and pseudocyst rupture
- A breach in the pancreatic ductal system or rupture of a pancreatic pseudocyst is the initiating event. If the leak opens anteriorly into the peritoneal cavity, pancreatic juice produces chemical peritonitis and ascites. If the disruption is posterior, pancreatic fluid enters the retroperitoneal space. Posterior leaks are the usual precursor of PPF because retroperitoneal fluid can ascend along anatomic planes and pass into the mediastinum and pleural spaces. In this case report the imaging demonstrated a retroperitoneal collection communicating with the distal duct and tracking superiorly — classic for a posterior leak.

Anatomic routes to the chest
- Once in the retroperitoneum, pancreatic fluid follows the path of least resistance upward. The two common natural routes are the aortic and the esophageal hiatuses of the diaphragm. Fluid may ascend through the posterior mediastinum and then breach the pleura to produce unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions. Less commonly, a pseudocyst or enzyme‑rich collection can erode directly through the diaphragm into the pleural space. The CT and MRCP in this child showed a collection tracking through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity, explaining the predominantly right‑sided effusions.

Composition of leaked pancreatic fluid and pleural reaction
- Pancreatic secretions are rich in proteolytic and lipolytic enzymes (amylase, lipase, trypsinogen/trypsin, elastase, phospholipases). When these enzymes contact pleural mesothelium and local tissues they produce chemical inflammation, increased vascular permeability and often hemorrhagic, exudative effusions. High pleural fluid amylase (often >1,000 U/L) and raised lipase are biochemical hallmarks of PPF; the case’s very high pleural amylase and lipase reflect direct contamination of the pleural space by pancreatic juice. Proteolytic enzyme activity also predisposes to loculation, tissue necrosis and the dark red (blood‑stained) appearance frequently reported.

Pediatric etiologies and genetic predisposition
- In children, PPF arises mainly from causes that disrupt the pancreas acutely or chronically. Important pediatric causes include blunt abdominal trauma (classic examples are bicycle handlebar injuries), acute pancreatitis with ductal disruption, and chronic pancreatitis. Unlike adults, where long‑standing alcohol‑related chronic pancreatitis predominates, pediatric chronic pancreatitis is often related to genetic factors. Known gene associations include PRSS1 (cationic trypsinogen mutations), SPINK1 (trypsin inhibitor), CFTR (cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator) and CTRC (chymotrypsin C). These mutations predispose to recurrent ductal injury, early chronic pancreatitis and pseudocyst formation — all of which increase the risk of fistulation. In the present report, blunt trauma preceded the episodes, consistent with trauma being a common non‑hereditary trigger in children; genetic testing is considered when pancreatitis is recurrent or chronic.

Contrast with adults
- In adults, chronic alcoholic pancreatitis is by far the leading cause of PPF because long‑term alcohol use results in ductal disruptions, strictures and pseudocysts. Consequently, adult PPF cases often present in the setting of known alcohol abuse and chronic pancreatic disease. The pediatric pattern differs: fewer alcohol‑related cases, higher relative contribution from trauma and genetic pancreatitis, and therefore younger patients without the typical adult risk profile.

Why thoracic symptoms often predominate — the “pulmonary mask”
- Two factors explain why children with PPF commonly present with chest complaints and may lack abdominal findings. First, the actual leakage and collection of enzyme‑rich fluid occur in the retroperitoneum and mediastinum and then in the pleural space; local pancreatic or peritoneal irritation may be limited, so abdominal pain is absent or mild. Second, even large pleural effusions may produce primarily respiratory signs (dyspnea, chest pain) while systemic symptoms remain muted. Children can compensate using the contralateral lung and partially tolerate slowly accumulating pleural fluid, leading to intermittent or insidious respiratory symptoms rather than acute abdominal distress. In the reported child, repeated thoracic presentations and initial absence of a clear abdominal history produced a “pulmonary mask” that delayed recognition of the pancreatic source.

Clinical relevance to the case
- Linking the history of blunt abdominal trauma, imaging demonstrating retroperitoneal collection tracking into the chest, and the characteristic high pleural amylase/lipase explains the pathogenesis of this patient’s recurrent hemorrhagic pleural effusions. Recognition of these pathophysiologic steps is critical in children because early diagnosis directs appropriate management (drainage, bowel rest, somatostatin analogues, nutrition and, when necessary, endoscopic or surgical ductal interventions) and avoids unnecessary pulmonary treatments.

In summary, PPF in children typically follows posterior ductal disruption or pseudocyst rupture with ascension of enzyme‑rich fluid through diaphragmatic hiatuses into the thorax. The enzymatic injury produces high‑amylase, often hemorrhagic pleural effusions, and pediatric etiologies favor trauma and genetically mediated pancreatitis rather than alcohol. Appreciation of these mechanisms helps explain the frequent predominance of thoracic symptoms and guides targeted diagnostic testing (amylase/lipase in pleural fluid, CT/MRCP, ERCP when indicated).

## Diagnostic Workup and Clinical Reasoning Toward PPF

Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is uncommon in children and often presents with prominent thoracic symptoms, so establishing the link to pancreatic pathology requires a deliberate diagnostic pathway. In any patient—particularly a child—with recurrent, large unilateral (or occasionally bilateral) pleural effusions that do not follow the expected course of infection or malignancy, PPF should be considered. Clinical clues that raised suspicion in this case were the recurrent right-sided effusions despite aspiration, the unusually dark red (blood-stained) fluid, lack of persistent systemic infectious signs, and a delayed but important history of prior blunt abdominal trauma. Recognising these red flags early prevents prolonged pulmonary workup and inappropriate therapy (for example, empiric antitubercular treatment, which initially occurred here).

Pleural fluid analysis is the pivotal first-line test once an effusion is sampled. PPF typically produces an exudative effusion with markedly elevated pancreatic enzymes—pleural fluid amylase and lipase—often with amylase concentrations well above 1,000 IU/L. In this child the pleural amylase (6,118 U/L) and lipase (7,211 U/L) were profoundly elevated and concordant with serum enzyme elevation, which strongly supported pancreatic origin. When available, amylase isoenzyme typing (pancreatic versus salivary type) can be helpful because elevated pleural amylase from PPF is of the pancreatic isoform; other causes with high amylase (for example, from salivary sources) will show the salivary isoform.

When you find amylase-rich pleural fluid, construct a focused differential and use targeted tests to exclude alternatives. Important competing diagnoses include:
- Tuberculous pleuritis: may present with blood-stained or lymphocyte-predominant effusion and mildly raised ADA. Distinguish it by clinical context, positive microbiology (GeneXpert, culture), interferon-gamma assays, and supportive radiographic pulmonary findings. In this case, GeneXpert was negative and the clinical picture (rapid reaccumulation after large-volume aspiration, large enzyme elevations) pointed away from TB.
- Oesophageal rupture (Boerhaave’s syndrome): causes very high pleural amylase of salivary origin, usually with a history of severe vomiting, chest pain, sepsis, and mediastinal emphysema. Absence of those features here made Boerhaave’s unlikely.
- Malignancy (primary or metastatic lung, lymphoma, leukaemia, or pancreatic cancer): can occasionally yield amylase-rich effusions. Cytology, peripheral blood counts, and imaging usually help exclude malignancy; pleural cytology in this case was negative and peripheral studies were unrevealing.
- Empyema: would typically show fever, systemic toxicity, neutrophil-predominant fluid, and microbiologic growth—features absent here.
- Other causes such as hepatic hydrothorax or renal hydrothorax are suggested by liver or renal disease and corresponding imaging/biochemistry; those were not present in this child.

Imaging should proceed in a stepwise fashion, directed by the clinical suspicion. Plain chest radiography and bedside ultrasonography confirm the presence and size of the effusion and guide safe drainage; they do not identify the pancreatic source. Cross-sectional imaging is required when pleural fluid characteristics (high amylase, recurrent reaccumulation) point to a non-pulmonary origin.

- Contrast-enhanced CT of the chest and abdomen is frequently the next step. CT excels at detecting pancreatic inflammation, pseudocysts, peripancreatic fluid collections and their anatomical extent. However, CT visualises the actual fistulous tract in only a minority of cases (roughly one-third to one-half of patients). In this patient, CT demonstrated a multiloculated hypodense collection tracking from the right paraesophageal region down to the tail of the pancreas and associated right hydropneumothorax—an anatomic clue linking thorax and pancreas and prompting further ductal imaging.
- Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) provides noninvasive high-resolution evaluation of pancreatic ductal anatomy and fistulous communications with substantially higher sensitivity for fistula visualisation (around 70–80%). MRCP in this case confirmed tubular cystic collection in the distal body/tail communicating with the distal pancreatic duct and tracking superiorly through the oesophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity, with a smaller connection to the left pleural space—definitive imaging evidence of bilateral (right greater than left) PPFs.
- Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) remains the gold standard for both diagnostic delineation and offers therapeutic intervention (transpapillary stenting or sphincterotomy). ERCP should be considered when conservative therapy fails or when planning ductal intervention; in children it can visualise fistulae in a large proportion of cases and is the route to place stents to reduce intraductal pressure.

In applying this cascade to the presented case, key diagnostic steps converged to confirm PPF: bedside imaging established a large right pleural effusion that recurred after drainage; pleural fluid was sent for analysis and showed very high amylase and lipase concentrations; contrast-enhanced CT revealed a continuous collection from pleura to pancreatic tail; MRCP then mapped the fistulous tracts and ductal narrowing. Other causes of high-amylase effusion—tuberculosis, oesophageal rupture, empyema, malignancy, hepatic or renal causes—were actively considered and ruled out by microbiology (negative GeneXpert), absence of typical history or systemic features, cytology, normal liver/renal testing and relevant imaging.

Putting these elements together—recurrent unilateral large effusions with blood-stained fluid, marked pancreatic enzyme elevation in pleural fluid, imaging showing a retroperitoneal collection tracking through the oesophageal hiatus to the pleural space, and exclusion of other causes—provides a robust, evidence-based path to diagnosing PPF. This integrated diagnostic reasoning also justifies the conservative therapeutic approach (drainage, nil per os, parenteral nutrition, octreotide, and enzyme replacement) undertaken here, reserving ERCP or surgery only if the fistula had failed to close.

## Management Strategy: From Conservative Therapy to Interventions

Management of pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) in children is stepwise and guided by the physiology of pancreatic leakage, the child’s respiratory status, imaging/duct anatomy, and the clinical response to initial conservative measures. The overarching goals are to control respiratory compromise, divert or suppress pancreatic exocrine flow so the fistula can close, and to treat or bypass ductal pathology if conservative measures fail. The clinical decisions in this case exemplify the usual sequence: stabilize the chest, attempt pancreatic “rest” with medical therapy, monitor for closure, and escalate to endoscopic or surgical therapy only when necessary.

Initial respiratory management and drainage
The first priority is respiratory stabilization. Large, symptomatic pleural effusions require prompt drainage. Therapeutic thoracentesis is appropriate for diagnostic relief when a single large effusion is causing dyspnea and oxygen requirement, as was done twice early in this case, producing rapid symptomatic improvement and better oxygenation. An indwelling intercostal drain (ICD) is indicated when effusions are recurrent, large, loculated, or when ongoing drainage and monitoring of output are required; the patient in this report progressed to ICD after multiple recurrences. Monitoring should include daily volume of pleural output, character of fluid, and serial chest imaging or ultrasound to document lung re-expansion and resolution of collections. Persistent high-volume drainage despite conservative measures is a key trigger to escalate therapy.

Pancreatic rest: NPO and parenteral nutrition
Conservative management aims to reduce pancreatic secretion and allow healing of the ductal disruption. This begins with nil per os (NPO) to eliminate enteral stimulation of exocrine secretion. Nutritional needs are met by total parenteral nutrition (TPN) when prolonged enteral restriction is anticipated or when oral intake is inadequate; TPN both supports growth in children and reduces pancreatic stimulation. Typical conservative trials last approximately 2–3 weeks if the child remains stable — enough time for many fistulae to close — with close monitoring of drainage, clinical status, and nutrition. In this case, the team instituted NPO and TPN immediately after MRCP demonstrated the fistulous tract, and the drainage decreased over 10 days.

Somatostatin analogues (Octreotide)
Somatostatin analogues are used adjunctively to suppress exocrine pancreatic secretion, which reduces fistula output and promotes closure. In pediatrics dosing commonly ranges from 10 to 40 μg/kg/day given as a continuous infusion or divided subcutaneous doses; the reported case used 30 μg/kg/day. Mechanistically, octreotide reduces pancreatic enzyme secretion and splanchnic blood flow. Response is assessed by falling pleural drainage and clinical improvement over days to a couple of weeks. Monitoring includes blood glucose (risk of hyper- or hypoglycemia), gallbladder stasis (risk of gallstones with prolonged use), gastrointestinal side effects, and local infusion-site issues. If there is a clear downward trend in drainage and the child’s nutrition can be advanced, the octreotide course can be tapered as recovery proceeds.

Pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT)
PERT is often introduced as the child progresses from NPO toward enteral feeding. Providing exogenous pancreatic enzymes paradoxically reduces endogenous pancreatic stimulation via feedback mechanisms and helps nutrient absorption when oral intake resumes. In this case PERT was started during the conservative phase to support recovery and to facilitate eventual reintroduction of oral feeding.

Criteria to escalate to endoscopic therapy (ERCP)
If conservative therapy (NPO, TPN, octreotide, drainage) fails — typically defined as persistently high output from the ICD over 2–3 weeks, lack of clinical improvement, or progressive malnutrition — endoscopic intervention with ERCP is indicated. ERCP allows direct visualisation of ductal anatomy and can treat ductal leaks or strictures by transpapillary stent placement or balloon dilation. Stenting lowers intraductal pressure and promotes internal drainage, often closing the fistula within 2–3 weeks. In pediatric series ERCP has variable visualization and success rates but can be highly effective; reported closure rates after stenting are substantial, and some series report high technical success in children. Decision factors include location of the leak (proximal vs distal), degree of ductal disruption, and local expertise. ERCP is generally preferred before major surgery when feasible.

Surgical options and indications
Surgery is reserved for children who fail both conservative and endoscopic therapy, when the duct anatomy is not amenable to endoscopic repair (e.g., complete transection, inaccessible distal leaks), or when there are complications such as infected or enlarging pseudocysts. Surgical procedures are tailored to the ductal pathology: distal pancreatectomy for a diseased tail, pancreatojejunostomy (Puestow) for main-duct dilation and stricture, or cystojejunostomy for large pseudocysts communicating with the duct. In pediatric reports surgery has high success rates (reported around 90%+ in small series) but carries the usual operative risks (bleeding, infection, pancreatic fistula, endocrine/exocrine insufficiency) and requires careful long-term follow-up.

Expected timelines and success rates in children
Because pediatric PPF is rare, data are limited and decision-making must be individualized. Published pediatric series suggest conservative therapy succeeds in a minority (roughly 10–15%), ERCP can achieve closure in a substantial proportion of selected patients (some reports show very high technical and clinical success in children), and surgery has the highest likelihood of definitive resolution when indicated (success >90% in selected series). A pragmatic approach is to attempt conservative management for up to 2–3 weeks in stable patients while closely monitoring drainage and nutrition; earlier endoscopy is reasonable when imaging shows a discrete ductal leak or stricture that can be stented.

How this case illustrates the strategy
This case followed the anticipated escalation ladder. The child presented with large, recurrent, dark red pleural effusions causing respiratory compromise and required thoracenteses initially and ultimately an ICD for ongoing drainage and monitoring. MRCP revealed a fistulous tract connecting the distal pancreatic duct to the right pleural space. The team instituted NPO, TPN, octreotide (30 μg/kg/day), PERT, and maintained the ICD. Over ten days the pleural output steadily decreased to none, allowing removal of the ICD and discharge without the need for ERCP or surgery. This favourable result is consistent with successful conservative management in a subset of children, but the team appropriately planned escalation pathways had the drainage persisted or the child’s condition worsened.

Key practical points
- Use therapeutic thoracentesis for immediate relief; place an ICD for recurrent or persistent effusions and to quantify output.  
- Institute NPO and provide TPN when enteral feeding cannot be safely resumed; plan for a 2–3 week conservative trial if the child is stable.  
- Start somatostatin analogues (e.g., octreotide 10–40 μg/kg/day) to reduce pancreatic secretion; monitor for metabolic and GI side effects.  
- Introduce PERT as you advance enteral intake.  
- Escalate to ERCP for persistent high-output fistulae or identifiable ductal lesions amenable to stenting; consider surgery when endoscopic therapy is not feasible or fails.  
- Individualize decisions based on respiratory status, nutrition, ductal anatomy, and local procedural expertise.

This approach balances a non‑operative first line that may succeed — as in this child — with clear, time‑bound criteria and options for escalation to definitive endoscopic or surgical repair when needed.

## Outcomes, Follow-up, and Clinical Lessons

Outcomes in this child were excellent. With conservative management—nil per os, total parenteral nutrition, octreotide, pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy and right-sided chest drainage—the pleural output declined over ten days, the intercostal drain was removed, and the patient was discharged symptom free. On monthly outpatient review he remains well, has returned to full school and play activities without limitation, and has had no recurrence of pleural effusion to date.

Follow-up priorities after a pancreatopleural fistula focus on early recognition of recurrence, monitoring pancreatic function and supporting recovery. Clinically, surveillance begins with symptom checks (dyspnea, chest pain, cough, abdominal pain) and periodic physical examination; parents should be asked to report any new respiratory symptoms promptly. Nutritional status needs attention—ensure adequate caloric intake from a balanced diet while avoiding excessive simple carbohydrates and processed foods that contributed to the patient’s prior obesity. Assess growth and body mass index and provide counseling or referral for weight management when indicated. Because pancreatic injury can affect both exocrine and endocrine function, monitor for signs of malabsorption (steatorrhea, weight loss) and for hyperglycemia; if symptoms or clinical suspicion arise, check fecal elastase and fasting glucose or HbA1c and refer to gastroenterology/endocrinology for further testing and management. Repeat imaging (chest radiograph, ultrasound or MRCP) is reserved for clinical concern—new or recurrent respiratory symptoms, persistent drainage, or biochemical evidence suggesting ongoing pancreatic leak.

Lifestyle and risk-reduction counseling are practical parts of follow-up. Reinforce a balanced, nutrient-rich diet, structured physical activity, and gradual resumption of contact or high-risk activities only after full clinical recovery. Given the precipitating blunt abdominal trauma in this case, advise on safe bicycling practices (helmet use, avoidance of handlebar injuries, protective padding when appropriate) and general injury prevention. Provide family education about signs that warrant urgent review (increasing breathlessness, chest pain, fever, abdominal pain, or steatorrhea).

To reduce diagnostic delay in future patients, maintain a high index of suspicion for a pancreatic source when confronted with recurrent, unilateral or bilateral, large or hemorrhagic pleural effusions—especially if pleural fluid is dark red or blood-stained. Ask specifically about recent blunt abdominal trauma, even remote or apparently minor events; children may underreport injury. Include pancreatopleural fistula in the differential when pleural fluid amylase is markedly elevated.

Practice-minded test stewardship can prevent unnecessary or harmful therapies. In any unexplained, recurrent or blood-stained pleural effusion, measure pleural fluid amylase and lipase early. If amylase is markedly elevated (often >1,000 U/L), consider isoenzyme typing to distinguish pancreatic from salivary sources before committing to prolonged empiric treatments such as antitubercular therapy or extensive oncologic workups. Correlate biochemical results with clinical context and imaging before starting disease‑specific long‑term treatment.

Imaging strategy: obtain MRCP early when clinical or biochemical clues point toward a pancreatic source, because MRCP provides excellent noninvasive delineation of duct anatomy and fistulous tracts and guided diagnosis in this case. Recognize CT’s role in defining collections, pleural complications and pseudocysts, but its lower sensitivity for visualizing the actual fistula. If conservative measures fail (after an appropriate trial, typically days to a few weeks depending on clinical course) or ductal disruption/stenosis is suspected, plan ERCP for both diagnostic clarification and potential therapeutic transpapillary stent placement.

Key clinical pearls and common pitfalls from this case
- Pulmonary mask: pancreatic disease can present predominantly with thoracic symptoms; persistent focus on a primary pulmonary diagnosis can delay correct treatment.
- Dark red, recurrent pleural effusion with very high pleural fluid amylase is a red flag for pancreatopleural fistula.
- Always re-evaluate the differential when large effusions recur despite appropriate thoracic interventions; specifically ask about abdominal trauma and pancreatitis risk factors.
- Early pleural fluid amylase/lipase measurement and timely MRCP are efficient, high-yield steps that can avert prolonged incorrect therapies.
- Conservative management (drainage, bowel rest, somatostatin analogues, nutritional support) can succeed, but plans for ERCP or surgery should be anticipated if there is no clinical improvement.

Taken together, these outcome and follow-up principles emphasize vigilant symptom surveillance, targeted testing and imaging, lifestyle counseling, and a low threshold to re-assess the diagnosis—measures that reduce missed pancreatopleural fistulae and avoid unnecessary treatments.
