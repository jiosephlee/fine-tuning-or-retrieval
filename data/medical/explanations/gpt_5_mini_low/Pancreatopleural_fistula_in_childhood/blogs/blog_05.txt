Title: Blog about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

# TB Trap: Interpreting Pleural ADA and TB Contact in the Era of Pancreatopleural Fistula

What makes this case so instructive is how an apparently straightforward “TB risk + elevated pleural ADA = start empiric anti-TB therapy” pathway nearly blinded the team to a completely different, extra-thoracic diagnosis. A late-childhood boy with recurrent, massive right pleural effusions (each time ~1 L), dark red pleural fluid, and a family TB contact was started on anti-tubercular therapy after a mildly elevated pleural ADA. Only after repeat presentations, careful re‑history (abdominal blunt trauma), CT/MRCP, and pleural fluid enzyme testing did the team land on pancreatopleural fistula (PPF): pleural amylase 6,118 U/L and lipase 7,211 U/L, MRCP-confirmed fistulous tract, and full resolution with chest drainage, bowel rest, TPN and octreotide.

This is the prototypical “pulmonary mask” — a pancreatic problem presenting as a respiratory one — and it shows why over-reliance on a single marker plus epidemiologic risk can cost time, expose kids to unnecessary drugs, and delay definitive care.

Why ADA and TB contact can mislead
Pleural ADA (adenosine deaminase) is a useful test for tuberculous pleural effusion, but it is not perfect. ADA assays are most helpful when combined with pretest probability. Important practical points:

- ADA thresholds: Commonly used cut-offs are 40 U/L (sensitive) and 60–70 U/L (more specific). Performance varies by prevalence and lab method.
- Sensitivity/specificity: In high‑TB prevalence settings, ADA ≥40 U/L has high sensitivity and reasonable specificity for TB pleuritis — but false positives occur (empyema, rheumatoid pleuritis, malignancy, pancreatic disease, parapneumonic effusions).
- Mildly elevated ADA (borderline values) in low-to-intermediate pretest probability should not be taken as definitive for TB.
- TB contact increases pretest probability, but it’s only one data point — a household contact doesn’t confirm disease in the index child.

Confirmatory testing you should get before committing to anti-TB therapy
When pleural fluid ADA is elevated but you have any atypical features, aim to confirm TB rather than treating empirically:

- Microbiology: Xpert MTB/RIF (GeneXpert) on pleural fluid — high specificity, variable sensitivity. A negative GeneXpert reduces but does not exclude TB.
- Mycobacterial culture from pleural fluid or pleural biopsy — gold standard but slow and low sensitivity.
- Pleural biopsy (closed needle or thoracoscopic): histology showing caseating granulomas and positive culture increases diagnostic certainty; medical centers should pursue biopsy when diagnosis is uncertain.
- Clinical trajectory and response: lack of expected improvement after appropriate therapy duration should prompt re-evaluation.

Clues that should pull you away from TB and toward pancreatopleural fistula
Think pancreatic source when a pleural effusion has these features — several of which were present in the reported case:

- Massive, recurrent, unilateral (often left but can be right) pleural effusion that reaccumulates quickly after drainage.
- Pleural fluid is haemorrhagic/dark red or serosanguinous.
- Very high pleural fluid amylase and lipase (amylase ≥1,000 IU/L is strongly suggestive of pancreatic origin; in this child it was >6,000).
- Low-grade or absent systemic infectious signs: lack of fever, non‑neutrophilic pleural fluid, negative cultures/GeneXpert.
- Recent history of pancreatitis or blunt abdominal trauma (may be elicited only after repeated questioning).
- Imaging: CT may show pancreatic collections or track; MRCP is helpful to demonstrate fistula; ERCP can be both diagnostic and therapeutic.

Why pleural amylase matters (and how to use it)
Pleural fluid amylase is an inexpensive test and can be diagnostic in the right context:

- Pancreatic-type amylase (not salivary) in pleural fluid points to PPF or pancreatic disease.
- Thresholds: pleural amylase >1,000 IU/L is classically used to flag pancreatic causes, while lower elevations may be from other causes (e.g., esophageal rupture shows high salivary-type amylase).
- If PPF is suspected, order pleural amylase/lipase and imaging (MRCP) early — it can prevent prolonged and inappropriate TB treatment.

Practical checklist to avoid premature TB treatment when PPF is a contender
Before starting empiric anti‑TB therapy for a pleural effusion with mildly elevated ADA and TB contact, run through this checklist — if any item is positive, pause TB therapy and pursue targeted diagnostics.

- Recurrent or rapidly re‑accumulating effusion after thoracentesis or chest tube?
- Large / massive unilateral effusion (or hydropneumothorax) with respiratory compromise?
- Pleural fluid appearance: grossly bloody or serosanguinous?
- Pleural fluid cell count: non‑neutrophilic exudate without strong septic picture?
- Pleural fluid amylase and lipase ordered? (If not, send them now.)
- Recent history of abdominal pain, pancreatitis, or blunt abdominal trauma (explicitly ask the child/parents again)?
- Chest/abdomen imaging: any signs of pancreatic collections, retroperitoneal tracking, or mediastinal fluid? Consider CT chest+abdomen or MRCP.
- Microbiology results available: GeneXpert on pleural fluid, pleural fluid culture. If negative and clinical suspicion for TB is not compelling, reconsider.
- Consider pleural biopsy if TB remains suspected but microbiology is unrevealing.

Bottom line and clinical pearls
- Don’t let a mildly elevated ADA and a positive household TB contact be the sole reasons to start anti-TB therapy for pleural effusion.
- In any recurrent, large, or haemorrhagic pleural effusion, always include pleural amylase/lipase and look for pancreatic disease — pancreatopleural fistula is rare but classic for very high pleural amylase.
- Use ADA in context: consider pretest probability, confirm with GeneXpert/culture/biopsy when possible, and re-evaluate if the clinical course doesn’t fit TB.
- A quick targeted test (pleural amylase) and focused imaging (MRCP) can change management dramatically — from months of unnecessary ATT to appropriate pancreatic-directed therapy that cures the problem.

Key take-home messages
- Mildly elevated pleural ADA + TB contact is not diagnostic of tuberculous pleuritis; consider pretest probability and confirmatory tests.
- Massive, recurrent, bloody pleural effusion should prompt pleural amylase/lipase testing to exclude pancreatopleural fistula.
- Order GeneXpert and consider pleural biopsy when ADA is elevated but the picture is atypical; a negative GeneXpert should not end your diagnostic thinking.
- A short checklist (recurrent effusion, bloody fluid, high pleural amylase/lipase, trauma history, MRCP findings) can prevent premature TB therapy and expedite correct treatment, as in this child who recovered after pancreatic-directed conservative care.
