Title: Blog about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

# Imaging PPF in Kids: CXR and POCUS to CT, MRCP, then ERCP

What’s striking about pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) in children is how often it wears a “pulmonary mask.” Recurrent, massive, often blood‑stained pleural effusions with chest pain and respiratory distress send clinicians down a thoracic workup — chest X‑rays, antibiotics, even empiric TB therapy — while the real problem sits in the retroperitoneum. The case above is a classic reminder: a late‑childhood boy with recurrent right pleural effusions after a bicycle handlebar injury, dark red effusions, very high pleural amylase/lipase, and an MRCP that mapped a pancreatic collection tracking through the oesophageal hiatus into the chest. With PPF rarity in kids (only a few dozen reported cases) the key is an imaging strategy that gets you from triage to diagnosis to therapy quickly — and with minimal unnecessary radiation.

Here’s a practical, imaging‑first approach I use when PPF is on the differential.

## Step 1 — CXR and POCUS: rapid triage and procedure guidance
- Start with upright chest X‑ray and bedside lung ultrasound (POCUS). Both are fast, low‑cost, and guide immediate management.
  - CXR: documents effusion(s), hydropneumothorax, mediastinal shift, and can suggest recurrence.
  - POCUS: estimates volume, detects loculations, identifies free flow for safe thoracentesis or chest tube placement.
- Use POCUS to guide thoracentesis or initial drainage whenever possible. In the case above, ultrasound-guided aspiration evacuated ~1 L and temporarily improved oxygenation.
- When to suspect a non‑pulmonary origin early: recurrent unilateral effusion, dark/blood‑stained fluid, very large volume, prior abdominal trauma, or pleural fluid with markedly elevated amylase (>1,000 U/L is highly suggestive of PPF).

Bottom line: CXR + POCUS are your first tools — they triage respiratory compromise and safely guide drainage while you plan next imaging.

## Step 2 — When to get cross‑sectional imaging (CT)
- Get contrast‑enhanced CT (abdomen ± chest) when:
  - There is recurrent effusion despite drainage, atypical fluid (blood‑stained), or sustained high output from chest tube.
  - You need to define collections, pseudocysts, retroperitoneal tracking, lung consolidation, or complications (hydropneumothorax, empyema).
  - You suspect trauma‑related pancreatic injury (as in the bicycle handlebar history).
- What CT gives you:
  - Excellent anatomic detail of pancreas, pseudocysts, retroperitoneal collections and thoracic complications.
  - Shows tract/collection tracking in many cases, but visualizes the actual fistulous communication less reliably (only ~33–47% of cases).
- Radiation stewardship:
  - Tailor the study — consider low‑dose pediatric protocols and limit scan length where reasonable (target chest and upper abdomen).
  - If the child is unstable or requires urgent drainage, treat first; CT can follow once stabilized.

Practical rule: use CT to map collections and complications. Don’t expect CT to be your definitive ductal/fistula test.

## Step 3 — MRCP: the non‑invasive ductal roadmap
- MRCP is your next test when CT raises suspicion for a pancreatic source or when pleural fluid amylase/lipase are markedly elevated.
  - Sensitivity for ductal anatomy and fistula visualization in many reports: ~70–80%.
  - MRCP can show pancreatic duct disruption, communication with collections, and the track into the mediastinum/pleura.
  - Secretin‑enhanced MRCP (if available) improves ductal visualization and can be especially helpful in subtle duct disruptions.
- Practical considerations in pediatrics:
  - Coordination: MRCP in children often requires sedation or general anaesthesia — schedule with pediatric radiology/anesthesia early.
  - Timing: aim to perform MRCP before ERCP when the child is stable, because MRCP informs therapeutic planning (site of leak, ductal anatomy, whether stenting is feasible).
  - If chest tube drainage is ongoing and the child is stable, MRCP can usually wait until after initial stabilization/optimization and before invasive endoscopy.
- In the case above MRCP confirmed a tubular collection extending from pancreatic tail through the oesophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity, and showed communication with the distal pancreatic duct — pivotal information that allowed non‑operative management to succeed.

## Step 4 — ERCP: the therapeutic gold standard (and diagnostic adjunct)
- ERCP remains the gold standard for therapeutic management: diagnostic opacification of the pancreatic duct and opportunity for transpapillary stent placement to reduce intraductal pressure and close the leak.
  - Reported visualization rates in children vary (around 38–78% depending on series), but ERCP also allows therapy (stent, sphincterotomy, balloon).
  - Endoscopic stenting often closes fistulae within 2–3 weeks in many patients; stents are left weeks to months as needed.
- When to proceed to ERCP:
  - Failure of conservative therapy (nil per os, TPN, octreotide) after 2–3 weeks, or earlier if ongoing high chest tube output, sepsis, or unfavorable duct anatomy (large leak).
  - If MRCP shows a ductal disruption accessible to endoscopic therapy, consider earlier ERCP in experienced centers.
- Pediatric ERCP logistics:
  - Needs an experienced pediatric endoscopist (or adult endoscopist experienced with children) and appropriate equipment (smaller scopes/catheters when needed).
  - Coordinate timing with anesthesia, MRCP results, and surgical teams. If stenting is feasible, it can often obviate major surgery.
- If ERCP/stenting is not feasible (distal fistula, disrupted duct, severe obstruction), surgical options (distal pancreatectomy, pancreaticojejunostomy, cystojejunostomy) are considered.

## Practical sequencing summary
- Unstable respiratory patient with large effusion: CXR + POCUS → immediate thoracentesis or chest tube under ultrasound guidance → send pleural fluid for amylase/lipase (and routine studies).
- Stable but recurrent unusual effusion (blood‑stained, high volume) or history of trauma: CXR/POCUS → CT chest/abdomen to map collection and complications → MRCP for ductal anatomy/fistula mapping.
- If MRCP demonstrates ductal communication amenable to ERCP: plan ERCP with stent placement (coordinate pediatric anesthesia and endoscopy).
- If MRCP is inconclusive and high clinical suspicion persists: consider ERCP for both diagnostic and therapeutic purposes.
- If conservative measures fail or endoscopic therapy is not feasible: surgical consultation for definitive repair.

## Practical tips for coordinating MRCP and ERCP in kids
- Early multidisciplinary huddle: involve pediatric radiology, anesthesia, pediatric surgery, and a pediatric/experienced ERCP endoscopist as soon as PPF is suspected.
- Send pleural fluid amylase/lipase early — markedly elevated values (pleural amylase >1,000 U/L) should expedite pancreatic imaging.
- Screen for need for sedation/GA for MRCP and ERCP; block time on the same day or consecutive days to shorten hospital stay if anesthesia availability permits.
- If chest tube present, temporarily clamp only under controlled conditions when MRCP is being done if collection visualization is needed — discuss with radiology.
- If transport to MRCP is delayed and ERCP is urgently needed (ongoing leak, sepsis), proceed to ERCP provided ductal therapy is likely and endoscopist is comfortable working without MRCP roadmap.

## Clinical pearls
- Think pancreas when you see recurrent, massive unilateral pleural effusions — especially if dark/bloody and after abdominal trauma.
- Pleural fluid amylase ≥1,000 U/L is highly suggestive of a pancreatic origin; amylase fractionation (pancreatic vs salivary) can help differentiate PPF from other causes (e.g., esophageal rupture, malignancy).
- CT is excellent for collections and complications but frequently misses the fistulous tract; MRCP is the non‑invasive test of choice for visualizing ductal anatomy and the fistula (~70–80% sensitivity).
- ERCP is therapeutic — reduce intraductal pressure with a transpapillary stent to allow fistula closure; in children this requires experienced teams and appropriate equipment.
- Conservative measures (NPO, TPN, octreotide, chest drainage) can work, but success rates in pediatrics are modest — plan imaging and endoscopic options early.

Take‑home messages
- Use CXR + POCUS first to triage and guide safe drainage.
- Order CT when you need to map collections, pseudocysts, or thoracic complications — but don’t rely on CT to show the fistula.
- MRCP is the non‑invasive ductal/fistula study of choice (70–80% sensitivity); plan MRCP before ERCP when feasible.
- ERCP is the therapeutic gold standard — coordinate pediatric anaesthesia and experienced endoscopy early.
- Early pleural fluid amylase testing and a high index of suspicion (trauma, recurrent bloody effusion) will shorten the path from “pulmonary problem” to pancreatic diagnosis and definitive therapy.
