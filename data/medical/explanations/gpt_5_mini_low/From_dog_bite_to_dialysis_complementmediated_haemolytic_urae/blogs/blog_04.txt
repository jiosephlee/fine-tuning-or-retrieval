Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# Pulling the Trigger on Eculizumab: Infection Timing, Vaccines, and Prophylaxis

What makes this case hard and common at the same time is the tug-of-war between two priorities: start eculizumab early to halt complement‑mediated TMA and save kidney function, versus avoid blunting a vital innate defence while the patient still has ongoing or recent bacteraemia. The Capnocytophaga canimorsus case above is a perfect teaching vignette — severe septic presentation with DIC, partial recovery, then delayed MAHA and biopsy‑proven TMA. The team waited until cultures were negative, vaccinated, gave antibiotic prophylaxis, and then started eculizumab — and the patient improved. That sequence illustrates key practical choices you’ll face when considering complement blockade after infection.

Below I summarise the pragmatic, evidence‑informed approach used in real life: when to start eculizumab after bacteremia, what vaccines to give and when, antibiotic prophylaxis options (including how to handle allergies), monitoring, and how to frame the risks with patients.

## The core clinical problem
- Eculizumab blocks terminal complement (C5) — excellent for CM‑HUS, but terminal complement is essential for killing encapsulated organisms (classically Neisseria meningitidis, but also organisms like Capnocytophaga). Starting eculizumab in the face of active bloodstream infection can increase risk of overwhelming infection.
- Delay eculizumab → risk of irreversible kidney injury and worse outcomes. Start too early → infectious complications.
- Practical compromise: clear active bacteremia (negative cultures, clinical control), vaccinate against meningococcus (MenACWY + MenB), and provide antibiotic prophylaxis during the vaccine lead‑in and often while on therapy. Individualise with infectious diseases input.

## Timing: when to pull the trigger
- Ideal scenario: patient is clinically improving, blood cultures are negative, source control achieved, and you can give vaccines ≥14 days before first eculizumab dose. That 2‑week window allows adequate serologic response to meningococcal vaccines.
- Real world: many patients cannot wait 2 weeks because of ongoing TMA. Reasonable strategy used in the presented case and in guidelines:
  - If you cannot wait, give the required meningococcal vaccines immediately and start eculizumab once the team agrees risks/benefits favour treatment.
  - Provide immediate antibiotic prophylaxis and maintain it for the vaccine lead‑in period (see below). Monitor closely and engage ID.
- In the case report, blood cultures had cleared; vaccines were given and antibiotic prophylaxis continued; eculizumab was started once cultures were negative — a conservative, defensible approach.

## Vaccination strategy (what to give)
- Give both:
  - MenACWY conjugate vaccine (covers serogroups A, C, W, Y), AND
  - MenB vaccine.
- Timing:
  - Ideally complete both vaccine series ≥2 weeks before starting complement blockade.
  - If not possible, give vaccines immediately and start antibiotics; vaccinate per schedule thereafter (boosters/second doses as recommended locally).
- Practical notes:
  - MenACWY typically requires a single adult dose for primary immunisation in many settings (boosters as indicated). MenB is usually a multi‑dose series. Check local immunisation schedules.
  - Vaccination does not fully mitigate risk (breakthrough meningococcal disease possible), so antibiotic prophylaxis and education remain essential.

## Antibiotic prophylaxis: who, what, and how long
- Why: until vaccine‑induced protection is expected (commonly 14 days) — and often continued while on eculizumab to reduce risk from meningococcus or other encapsulated organisms, especially in the early months of therapy.
- Commonly used agents:
  - First‑line: oral penicillin V or amoxicillin (covers many meningococci and Capnocytophaga). This was used in the case (Apocillin).
  - If the isolate/susceptibilities warrant (or patient already on IV therapy), IV beta‑lactams may be used while inpatient.
- If true severe beta‑lactam allergy:
  - Discuss options with ID — consider ciprofloxacin (single‑dose is used for meningococcal chemoprophylaxis in exposures; for ongoing prophylaxis, some centres use daily ciprofloxacin) or doxycycline where appropriate, recognising variable data and local resistance patterns.
  - Macrolides (e.g., azithromycin) are less reliable for meningococcus and Capnocytophaga; use only after ID input and susceptibility data.
- Duration:
  - At minimum, continue prophylaxis for 2 weeks after vaccination if vaccination given immediately before eculizumab.
  - Many teams continue a long‑term prophylactic regimen for as long as terminal complement blockade is ongoing, with periodic reassessment (practice varies; involve ID/nephrology).
- Important: antibiotic prophylaxis is not a guarantee. Vaccination + prophylaxis + patient education is layered protection.

## Practical antimicrobial selections (summary for common scenarios)
- No allergy, inpatient/ongoing IV therapy: continue IV beta‑lactam that covers meningococcus and the identified pathogen (eg, ampicillin for Capnocytophaga if susceptible).
- Transition to oral prophylaxis on discharge: oral penicillin V or amoxicillin if susceptible and tolerated.
- Severe penicillin allergy: consult ID. Consider fluoroquinolone (ciprofloxacin) or doxycycline; weigh risks, local resistance, and organism susceptibilities. Do not empirically rely on macrolides for meningococcus.
- Always tailor to culture results and susceptibilities when available.

## Monitoring and safety netting
- Before starting eculizumab:
  - Ensure meningococcal vaccines given (MenACWY + MenB) ideally ≥2 weeks prior; if not, give immediately and start antibiotics.
  - Confirm bacteremia cleared if possible (negative cultures); if ongoing bacteremia, weigh urgency of eculizumab vs infectious risk and get ID involved.
  - Document informed discussion with patient about increased infection risk and prophylaxis plan.
- While on eculizumab:
  - Maintain antibiotic prophylaxis per plan.
  - Monitor for fevers, neck stiffness, photophobia, severe headaches, purpura, or rapidly evolving sepsis — educate patient to seek urgent care.
  - Provide patient with an emergency card/letter stating they are on complement blockade and at risk for meningococcal disease.
  - Consider periodic review of need for ongoing prophylaxis and vaccine boosters.
- If invasive infection is suspected on therapy: treat aggressively with empiric broad therapy and consider stopping eculizumab temporarily only with ID guidance — but usually, treating infection and continuing complement inhibitor is a case‑by‑case decision.

## Communicating risk with patients (how to frame it)
- Be direct: eculizumab can stop the process that is destroying red cells and kidneys, but it increases the risk of serious infections — notably meningococcal disease.
- Explain the mitigation strategy: vaccinations, antibiotic prophylaxis, close monitoring, and an emergency action plan.
- Emphasise symptoms that require immediate attention (fever, severe headache, neck stiffness, petechial rash, high fever, rapid deterioration).
- Document the shared decision and the rationale for timing.

## Clinical pearls from the Capnocytophaga case
- CM‑HUS can present after an initial septic phase — watch for late haemolysis, falling haptoglobin, rising LDH, and schistocytes even as inflammatory markers improve.
- Normal C3/C4 or normal complement pathway assays do NOT exclude CM‑HUS.
- If TTP is unlikely (ADAMTS13 >10%) and TMA is severe, don’t delay attempts at definitive therapy; when feasible, wait for negative cultures and vaccinate, but if that’s not possible start eculizumab with antibiotics and close ID partnership.
- Document vaccine administration and provide patient education and prophylactic antibiotics — these simple steps reduce, but do not eliminate, risk.

## Take‑home messages
- Eculizumab is kidney‑saving in CM‑HUS but increases susceptibility to encapsulated organisms; timing matters.
- Give MenACWY + MenB before starting eculizumab when possible; if not possible, vaccinate immediately and give antibiotic prophylaxis.
- Continue antibiotic prophylaxis at least until vaccine protection is expected (commonly 2 weeks); many centres continue long‑term prophylaxis while on complement blockade — discuss with ID.
- If penicillin allergy exists, involve ID early to choose alternative prophylaxis (e.g., ciprofloxacin or doxycycline in selected situations).
- Always communicate risk clearly with patients, provide an emergency action plan, and monitor closely.

If you’re about to start eculizumab in the setting of a recent bacteremia, get infectious diseases involved, document a plan (vaccination + prophylaxis + monitoring), and balance the immediacy of kidney rescue against infectious risk — the case above shows that with careful sequencing you can both treat the TMA and keep the patient safe.
