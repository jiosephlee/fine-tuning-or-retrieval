Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# Bitten, Septic, and Subtle: Recognizing Capnocytophaga canimorsus Early

What makes this case striking is how a single dog bite in an otherwise healthy woman precipitated a cascade: fulminant sepsis with DIC, profound leukopenia, acral ischemia, acute kidney injury requiring dialysis — and then a delayed, unexpected twist: development of a thrombotic microangiopathy consistent with complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS). Capnocytophaga canimorsus is a rare cause of human invasive disease, but when it shows up it can behave aggressively, masquerade as plain sepsis/DIC, and — as this case shows — act as a “second hit” that unmasks complement‑mediated endothelial catastrophe.

Below I’ll walk through the clinical red flags that should raise suspicion for C. canimorsus, practical empiric antibiotic choices, pitfalls of diagnostics, and the downstream implications when the course doesn’t neatly resolve (including TMA/CM‑HUS and the thorny therapeutic decisions that follow).

## Why suspect Capnocytophaga canimorsus?

C. canimorsus lives in the mouths of dogs and cats. Transmission isn’t just the classic “deep bite” — even licks, scratches or minor breaks in skin can suffice.

Key clinical features (illustrated in the case)
- Rapidly progressive sepsis within days of a dog bite — high fevers, profound malaise.
- Early leukopenia or pancytopenia (this organism is notorious for severe leukopenia in some presentations).
- Coagulopathy that can look like DIC: bleeding, ecchymoses, elevated INR/PT, low fibrinogen.
- Acral ischemia / peripheral mottling and cyanosis (microthrombotic phenomena).
- Multi‑organ dysfunction: hypotension or hypoperfusion, AKI (can progress to oliguria/anuria), hepatic enzyme elevation, refractory hypoglycaemia sometimes seen in severe sepsis.

In the presented patient, these signs occurred rapidly after the dog bite: severe leukopenia, DIC features, mottled cool extremities progressing to toe and finger ischemia, and early anuria leading to dialysis — classic alarm bells for an aggressive zoonotic sepsis.

Clinically: always ask about animal exposure (dog or cat) in rapidly progressive sepsis, especially with DIC, leukopenia or unexplained peripheral ischemia.

## Empiric antibiotic strategy — cover Capnocytophaga up front

Capnocytophaga is generally susceptible to beta‑lactams but can be slow growing and sometimes produces beta‑lactamases. Empiric coverage for severe dog/cat bite–associated sepsis should be broad and tailored to likely pathogens (Pasteurella, Capnocytophaga, anaerobes, oral flora).

Reasonable empiric choices in severe presentations:
- Piperacillin–tazobactam — good initial choice in severe sepsis and was used effectively in this case.
- Carbapenems (eg, meropenem) — excellent broad spectrum if there is concern for beta‑lactamase producing isolates or critical illness.
- For less severe infections or prophylaxis after uncomplicated bite wounds, amoxicillin–clavulanate remains first line (but note access/administration issues may lead to suboptimal prophylaxis — as in this patient who did not start the prescribed oral penicillin).

Cautions
- Simple penicillin V (phenoxymethylpenicillin) or narrow‑spectrum agents are inadequate for severe systemic disease. The patient had been prescribed penicillin V but did not take it; in severe sepsis that would not have been sufficient anyway.
- Some isolates may display variable susceptibility — culture and susceptibility testing remain important.

Duration and de‑escalation
- De‑escalate based on culture results and clinical response.
- Capnocytophaga can be confirmed by blood culture, but it’s slow to grow; if molecular diagnostics (eg, PCR, sequencing) are available they can shorten time to identification.

## Diagnostics: slow cultures, molecular tools, and when to act empirically

Culture characteristics and pitfalls
- Capnocytophaga is fastidious and slow‑growing; routine blood cultures may take several days to flag positive.
- In patients with severe sepsis after an animal bite, don’t wait for culture confirmation to start appropriate empiric therapy.
- A clinical picture of rapid sepsis with leukopenia and DIC in the context of a recent dog/cat exposure should prompt early broad coverage.

Role of molecular diagnostics
- Real‑time PCR and sequencing (including nanopore approaches) can identify Capnocytophaga faster than culture and have increasing availability in tertiary centers.
- Molecular methods are especially helpful when cultures are negative or slow, or when early organism ID would change management (eg, prompt narrowing, public health implications).

Microbiology request tip
- When sending blood cultures, inform the lab about suspected Capnocytophaga — they may extend incubation or use specific methods to increase yield.

## When sepsis improves but anemia and thrombocytopenia persist — think TMA

A crucial learning point: in this case, the initial sepsis and DIC features improved with antibiotics and supportive care, but then the patient developed sudden severe hemolysis, undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH, persistent thrombocytopenia and schistocytes on peripheral smear — a pattern of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA) and TMA. Kidney biopsy confirmed TMA changes.

Diagnostic approach to TMA in this context
- Exclude TTP early: send ADAMTS13 activity. Severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%) supports TTP and mandates urgent plasma exchange.
- Rule out STEC-HUS with stool Shiga toxin PCR.
- If ADAMTS13 is not severely reduced and STEC is negative, consider CM‑HUS (complement‑mediated), especially if there is a triggering infection.
- Complement levels (C3/C4) can be normal in CM‑HUS — normal values do not exclude the diagnosis.
- Peripheral smear for schistocytes is high‑yield and should be done early when hemolysis is suspected.
- Consider renal biopsy when safe/feasible — it can confirm TMA and guide prognosis.

In this patient ADAMTS13 was 43.5% (making TTP unlikely), stool Shiga toxin PCR was negative, and kidney biopsy showed fibrin thrombi / ischemic glomeruli consistent with TMA — clinching the CM‑HUS diagnosis in the clinical context.

## Treatment crossroads: plasma exchange, eculizumab, and infection concerns

Plasma exchange (PEX)
- PEX is life‑saving in TTP, but in CM‑HUS it’s at best temporizing — it may remove circulating factors or antibodies but doesn’t correct underlying complement dysregulation.
- In practice, PEX is often used as bridging therapy while diagnostic tests (ADAMTS13, complement studies) are pending.

Eculizumab (terminal complement blockade)
- Eculizumab transformed CM‑HUS outcomes when started promptly — it halts complement‑mediated endothelial injury and can lead to hematologic remission and renal recovery.
- Timing: balance is critical. In active uncontrolled bacteremia, blocking terminal complement raises concern for worsened infection, especially with encapsulated organisms. In this case clinicians waited until blood cultures were negative and gave meningococcal vaccines and prophylactic antibiotics before dosing.
- Vaccination and prophylaxis: patients need meningococcal vaccination and typically antimicrobial prophylaxis when starting eculizumab due to risk for encapsulated organisms. In the acute setting, vaccinate as early as possible and give antibiotic prophylaxis if vaccinations are recent or before immunity develops.
- Even without an identifiable complement gene mutation, infection‑triggered CM‑HUS can respond well to eculizumab.

Practical decision points illustrated by the case
- The patient received PEX while awaiting ADAMTS13 and complement testing, which is pragmatic when TTP can’t be excluded immediately.
- Eculizumab was started after culture clearance and vaccination, with prophylactic penicillin — a reasonable compromise balancing infection control and need for complement blockade.
- Renal recovery was partial; earlier complement blockade may improve renal outcomes, but real‑world decisions must weigh infectious risks.

## Prognosis and follow‑up considerations

- CM‑HUS outcomes depend heavily on time to eculizumab and degree of renal injury at presentation. Partial recovery with residual CKD, as in this patient, is common when severe AKI and dialysis dependence occur.
- Extended genetic complement testing can detect pathogenic variants that influence duration of eculizumab therapy and relapse risk. Absence of a mutation doesn’t exclude CM‑HUS; it supports an infection‑triggered event and may favor finite therapy, but decisions should be individualized.
- Ongoing nephrology follow‑up, monitoring for relapse, and vaccination/immunization counseling are essential.

## Clinical pearls

- Always ask about recent dog or cat exposure in patients with rapidly progressive sepsis, especially if there’s leukopenia, coagulopathy/DIC or acral ischemia.
- Start broad empiric coverage (eg, piperacillin–tazobactam or a carbapenem) for severe bite‑associated sepsis; do not rely on narrow oral agents for systemic disease.
- Capnocytophaga may take days to grow — consider molecular diagnostics if available and don’t delay empiric therapy.
- If hemolysis and thrombocytopenia develop or persist as sepsis resolves, get a peripheral smear for schistocytes, haptoglobin/LDH, DAT, ADAMTS13 and Shiga toxin testing early.
- Normal C3/C4 does not rule out complement‑mediated HUS.
- PEX is a reasonable bridge while excluding TTP; eculizumab is the definitive therapy for CM‑HUS but requires careful consideration around active infection and appropriate vaccinations/prophylaxis.

Key take‑home messages
- Consider Capnocytophaga canimorsus in severe sepsis after dog/cat exposure — look for rapid deterioration, leukopenia, DIC and acral ischemia.
- Empiric therapy for severe presentations: piperacillin–tazobactam or a carbapenem; de‑escalate when cultures and susceptibilities are available.
- Cultures are slow — use molecular diagnostics when available and start treatment early.
- If hemolysis with schistocytes and persistent thrombocytopenia appear (especially after initial sepsis improvement), evaluate urgently for TMA; distinguish TTP (ADAMTS13 <10%) from CM‑HUS.
- In CM‑HUS, early eculizumab improves outcomes but balance infection risk: ensure culture control, vaccination, and prophylactic antibiotics when initiating complement blockade.

Bottom line: a dog bite is rarely just a minor wound. Keep a high index of suspicion for Capnocytophaga in fulminant sepsis after animal exposure, and be alert for a delayed TMA — because early recognition and timely targeted therapy (whether PEX as a bridge or eculizumab when indicated) can be kidney- and life‑saving.
