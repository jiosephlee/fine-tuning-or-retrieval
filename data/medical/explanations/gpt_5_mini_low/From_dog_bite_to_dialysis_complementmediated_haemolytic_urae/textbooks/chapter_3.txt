Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Reasoning and Differential Diagnosis of TMA

When a patient develops microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) with thrombocytopenia and acute kidney injury (AKI), rapid, stepwise diagnostic reasoning is essential because the differential includes disorders with very different treatments and prognoses. The immediate clinical priority is to identify or exclude thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), because untreated TTP carries a high mortality that is reduced dramatically by urgent plasma exchange. After TTP is addressed (or excluded), attention turns to infection-associated causes (including Shiga toxin-producing E. coli (STEC) HUS and DIC-related MAHA) and to complement-mediated HUS (CM‑HUS), which requires different targeted therapy.

1) Rapid triage to exclude TTP
- Clinical urgency: TTP should be treated empirically with plasma exchange (PEX) when highly suspected because delays are dangerous. Clinical tools such as the PLASMIC score provide a pretest probability while awaiting definitive testing. The PLASMIC score uses seven easily available variables (platelet count, haemolysis evidence, absence of active cancer, absence of transplant, MCV, INR, and creatinine) to stratify risk. In this case the PLASMIC score was 4/7, a low pretest probability, decreasing immediate suspicion for TTP.
- Confirmatory test: ADAMTS13 activity is diagnostic: severe TTP is defined by activity <10% (often with an ADAMTS13 inhibitor). Values >10–20% essentially rule against classic severe TTP as the cause of the syndrome. In this patient an ADAMTS13 activity of 43.5% made TTP unlikely and allowed clinicians to stop treating for TTP and focus on other causes.

2) Evaluation for STEC‑HUS
- Clinical pattern: STEC-HUS typically follows a prodrome of bloody diarrhoea or enteritis. Absence of an enteric prodrome lowers likelihood but does not exclude disease.
- Testing: Stool PCR or culture for Shiga toxin genes (stx1/stx2) is the preferred rapid test. A negative stool PCR in the appropriate clinical context (no diarrhoeal prodrome and negative testing) effectively rules out STEC-HUS. In the reported case, stool PCR was negative and there was no enteric illness, making STEC-HUS unlikely.

3) Distinguishing sepsis/DIC‑associated MAHA from TMA syndromes
- Overlap and clues: Severe sepsis with DIC can produce MAHA-like features (schistocytes, thrombocytopenia, elevated LDH). Clues favouring DIC-related microangiopathy include marked coagulopathy (prolonged PT/aPTT, low fibrinogen, elevated D‑dimer), active bleeding or consumption of clotting factors, and a clinical picture dominated by ongoing septic shock. DIC‑related schistocytosis tends to improve as sepsis resolves.
- Timing and course: In this patient, the initial picture was dominated by sepsis and DIC, but haemolysis and thrombocytopenia reappeared or persisted after the sepsis improved—raising suspicion for a superimposed TMA rather than ongoing DIC alone.

4) Role and interpretation of complement studies in suspected CM‑HUS
- Basic tests: Serum C3 and C4 levels are commonly obtained but have limited sensitivity. Normal C3/C4 do not exclude CM‑HUS because systemic complement consumption may be focal or intermittent; many patients with genetically mediated complement dysregulation have normal routine complement levels at presentation.
- Functional assays: Hemolytic assays (total complement CH50, alternative pathway AH50) and measurements of specific complement components (factor H, factor I, factor B, C5b-9) can provide additional information. However, these assays have interpretive caveats: low AH50/CH50 can reflect consumption from active disease, but can also be altered by recent plasma therapy, transfusions, or complement inhibitors. Anti-factor H autoantibodies should be sought in appropriate age groups.
- Genetic testing: Extended genetic panels for complement-regulatory genes (CFH, CFI, CFB, C3, MCP/CD46, and others) help identify predisposing variants but take time and do not change acute management. A negative genetic panel does not exclude an infection-triggered, complement-mediated process.
- In this case: C3/C4 were normal, but that did not argue strongly against CM‑HUS. A markedly reduced AH50 was later measured, but sampling occurred after PEX and after one dose of eculizumab, and thus most likely reflects treatment effects rather than intrinsic complement deficiency or activation.

5) Impact of interventions on diagnostic testing and optimal timing of samples
- Plasma exchange: PEX removes circulating complement proteins, autoantibodies, and other plasma factors, altering both quantitative and functional complement assays and making interpretation unreliable for some days. It also dilutes ADAMTS13 activity; however, ADAMTS13 sampling should be obtained before the first PEX whenever possible. In emergencies where PEX cannot be delayed, a baseline sample taken before PEX is ideal.
- Eculizumab: As a terminal complement inhibitor (anti‑C5), eculizumab abolishes complement hemolytic function and will markedly lower functional complement assays (CH50/AH50) and increase circulating C5 fragments bound to drug; thus assays after dosing cannot be used to assess the native complement system. Ideally, blood for complement functional assays and samples for genetic testing should be drawn prior to PEX or eculizumab when feasible. In practice, patient safety takes precedence; clinicians must document timing of interventions relative to sampling to interpret results correctly.
- In the presented case, some complement testing was performed before PEX/ecz but extended testing was done after PEX and eculizumab—necessitating interpretation with the understanding that therapy likely depressed functional assay results.

6) Indications and diagnostic yield of renal biopsy in suspected TMA
- When to biopsy: Kidney biopsy is indicated when the diagnosis remains uncertain after noninvasive testing, when there is diagnostic equipoise between glomerular disease and TMA, or when biopsy findings will change management. In dialysis‑dependent AKI with suspected TMA and uncertain etiology, biopsy can demonstrate characteristic microvascular changes that support a TMA diagnosis.
- Expected findings: Light microscopy in TMA shows glomerular or arteriolar endothelial swelling, capillary wall thickening, fibrin thrombi within glomeruli or small arterioles, ischaemic glomerular collapse, and sometimes crescents. Immunofluorescence is usually negative or shows nonspecific entrapment of proteins; electron microscopy may show endothelial detachment and subendothelial expansion.
- In this case, kidney biopsy demonstrated fibrin thrombi, ischaemic glomeruli and acute tubular necrosis—histology consistent with TMA and supportive of CM‑HUS in the appropriate clinical context. The biopsy therefore provided crucial objective evidence of microvascular thrombotic injury in the kidneys and helped support the decision to institute complement blockade.

7) Synthesis: integrating the data to determine CM‑HUS
- Logical sequence: In a patient with MAHA, thrombocytopenia and severe AKI, first obtain an ADAMTS13 activity (before PEX if possible) to exclude TTP; second, evaluate for STEC with stool testing if the history suggests diarrhoeal prodrome; third, assess for sepsis and DIC with coagulation studies and clinical correlation; fourth, obtain complement studies (acknowledging limitations) and send samples for extended complement genetic testing; fifth, consider renal biopsy when noninvasive data are inconclusive or organ‑directed evidence is needed.
- Applying to the case: TTP was effectively excluded by an ADAMTS13 activity of 43.5% (and a low PLASMIC score). STEC‑HUS was excluded by a negative stool PCR and absent enteric prodrome. The initial MAHA-like picture occurred during frank sepsis/DIC, but persistent haemolysis with schistocytes, ongoing thrombocytopenia after normalization of coagulation tests, dialysis‑dependent AKI, and kidney biopsy showing fibrin thrombi and ischaemic glomeruli all pointed to a TMA process beyond sepsis‑related DIC. Complement studies, while not definitive (normal C3/C4), and the clinical response after complement‑directed therapy (PEX as bridging and eculizumab with subsequent haematologic remission and renal recovery) supported the clinical diagnosis of complement‑mediated HUS triggered by Capnocytophaga canimorsus infection.
- Practical takeaways: CM‑HUS is a diagnosis of exclusion that relies on synthesis of clinical course, laboratory testing (with attention to timing and treatment effects), microbiology, and often renal histology. Early ADAMTS13 testing is crucial to exclude TTP; stool testing should be done when STEC is possible; and complement testing and genetic studies are useful but must be interpreted in the context of recent PEX, transfusions, or complement blockade. When clinical suspicion for CM‑HUS is high and reversible causes are excluded, prompt initiation of complement blockade (with appropriate infection control measures) offers the best chance for haematologic remission and renal recovery.
