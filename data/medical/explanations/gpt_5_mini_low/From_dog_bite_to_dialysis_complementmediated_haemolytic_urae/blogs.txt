Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# Bitten, Septic, and Subtle: Recognizing Capnocytophaga canimorsus Early

What makes this case striking is how a single dog bite in an otherwise healthy woman precipitated a cascade: fulminant sepsis with DIC, profound leukopenia, acral ischemia, acute kidney injury requiring dialysis — and then a delayed, unexpected twist: development of a thrombotic microangiopathy consistent with complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS). Capnocytophaga canimorsus is a rare cause of human invasive disease, but when it shows up it can behave aggressively, masquerade as plain sepsis/DIC, and — as this case shows — act as a “second hit” that unmasks complement‑mediated endothelial catastrophe.

Below I’ll walk through the clinical red flags that should raise suspicion for C. canimorsus, practical empiric antibiotic choices, pitfalls of diagnostics, and the downstream implications when the course doesn’t neatly resolve (including TMA/CM‑HUS and the thorny therapeutic decisions that follow).

## Why suspect Capnocytophaga canimorsus?

C. canimorsus lives in the mouths of dogs and cats. Transmission isn’t just the classic “deep bite” — even licks, scratches or minor breaks in skin can suffice.

Key clinical features (illustrated in the case)
- Rapidly progressive sepsis within days of a dog bite — high fevers, profound malaise.
- Early leukopenia or pancytopenia (this organism is notorious for severe leukopenia in some presentations).
- Coagulopathy that can look like DIC: bleeding, ecchymoses, elevated INR/PT, low fibrinogen.
- Acral ischemia / peripheral mottling and cyanosis (microthrombotic phenomena).
- Multi‑organ dysfunction: hypotension or hypoperfusion, AKI (can progress to oliguria/anuria), hepatic enzyme elevation, refractory hypoglycaemia sometimes seen in severe sepsis.

In the presented patient, these signs occurred rapidly after the dog bite: severe leukopenia, DIC features, mottled cool extremities progressing to toe and finger ischemia, and early anuria leading to dialysis — classic alarm bells for an aggressive zoonotic sepsis.

Clinically: always ask about animal exposure (dog or cat) in rapidly progressive sepsis, especially with DIC, leukopenia or unexplained peripheral ischemia.

## Empiric antibiotic strategy — cover Capnocytophaga up front

Capnocytophaga is generally susceptible to beta‑lactams but can be slow growing and sometimes produces beta‑lactamases. Empiric coverage for severe dog/cat bite–associated sepsis should be broad and tailored to likely pathogens (Pasteurella, Capnocytophaga, anaerobes, oral flora).

Reasonable empiric choices in severe presentations:
- Piperacillin–tazobactam — good initial choice in severe sepsis and was used effectively in this case.
- Carbapenems (eg, meropenem) — excellent broad spectrum if there is concern for beta‑lactamase producing isolates or critical illness.
- For less severe infections or prophylaxis after uncomplicated bite wounds, amoxicillin–clavulanate remains first line (but note access/administration issues may lead to suboptimal prophylaxis — as in this patient who did not start the prescribed oral penicillin).

Cautions
- Simple penicillin V (phenoxymethylpenicillin) or narrow‑spectrum agents are inadequate for severe systemic disease. The patient had been prescribed penicillin V but did not take it; in severe sepsis that would not have been sufficient anyway.
- Some isolates may display variable susceptibility — culture and susceptibility testing remain important.

Duration and de‑escalation
- De‑escalate based on culture results and clinical response.
- Capnocytophaga can be confirmed by blood culture, but it’s slow to grow; if molecular diagnostics (eg, PCR, sequencing) are available they can shorten time to identification.

## Diagnostics: slow cultures, molecular tools, and when to act empirically

Culture characteristics and pitfalls
- Capnocytophaga is fastidious and slow‑growing; routine blood cultures may take several days to flag positive.
- In patients with severe sepsis after an animal bite, don’t wait for culture confirmation to start appropriate empiric therapy.
- A clinical picture of rapid sepsis with leukopenia and DIC in the context of a recent dog/cat exposure should prompt early broad coverage.

Role of molecular diagnostics
- Real‑time PCR and sequencing (including nanopore approaches) can identify Capnocytophaga faster than culture and have increasing availability in tertiary centers.
- Molecular methods are especially helpful when cultures are negative or slow, or when early organism ID would change management (eg, prompt narrowing, public health implications).

Microbiology request tip
- When sending blood cultures, inform the lab about suspected Capnocytophaga — they may extend incubation or use specific methods to increase yield.

## When sepsis improves but anemia and thrombocytopenia persist — think TMA

A crucial learning point: in this case, the initial sepsis and DIC features improved with antibiotics and supportive care, but then the patient developed sudden severe hemolysis, undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH, persistent thrombocytopenia and schistocytes on peripheral smear — a pattern of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA) and TMA. Kidney biopsy confirmed TMA changes.

Diagnostic approach to TMA in this context
- Exclude TTP early: send ADAMTS13 activity. Severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%) supports TTP and mandates urgent plasma exchange.
- Rule out STEC-HUS with stool Shiga toxin PCR.
- If ADAMTS13 is not severely reduced and STEC is negative, consider CM‑HUS (complement‑mediated), especially if there is a triggering infection.
- Complement levels (C3/C4) can be normal in CM‑HUS — normal values do not exclude the diagnosis.
- Peripheral smear for schistocytes is high‑yield and should be done early when hemolysis is suspected.
- Consider renal biopsy when safe/feasible — it can confirm TMA and guide prognosis.

In this patient ADAMTS13 was 43.5% (making TTP unlikely), stool Shiga toxin PCR was negative, and kidney biopsy showed fibrin thrombi / ischemic glomeruli consistent with TMA — clinching the CM‑HUS diagnosis in the clinical context.

## Treatment crossroads: plasma exchange, eculizumab, and infection concerns

Plasma exchange (PEX)
- PEX is life‑saving in TTP, but in CM‑HUS it’s at best temporizing — it may remove circulating factors or antibodies but doesn’t correct underlying complement dysregulation.
- In practice, PEX is often used as bridging therapy while diagnostic tests (ADAMTS13, complement studies) are pending.

Eculizumab (terminal complement blockade)
- Eculizumab transformed CM‑HUS outcomes when started promptly — it halts complement‑mediated endothelial injury and can lead to hematologic remission and renal recovery.
- Timing: balance is critical. In active uncontrolled bacteremia, blocking terminal complement raises concern for worsened infection, especially with encapsulated organisms. In this case clinicians waited until blood cultures were negative and gave meningococcal vaccines and prophylactic antibiotics before dosing.
- Vaccination and prophylaxis: patients need meningococcal vaccination and typically antimicrobial prophylaxis when starting eculizumab due to risk for encapsulated organisms. In the acute setting, vaccinate as early as possible and give antibiotic prophylaxis if vaccinations are recent or before immunity develops.
- Even without an identifiable complement gene mutation, infection‑triggered CM‑HUS can respond well to eculizumab.

Practical decision points illustrated by the case
- The patient received PEX while awaiting ADAMTS13 and complement testing, which is pragmatic when TTP can’t be excluded immediately.
- Eculizumab was started after culture clearance and vaccination, with prophylactic penicillin — a reasonable compromise balancing infection control and need for complement blockade.
- Renal recovery was partial; earlier complement blockade may improve renal outcomes, but real‑world decisions must weigh infectious risks.

## Prognosis and follow‑up considerations

- CM‑HUS outcomes depend heavily on time to eculizumab and degree of renal injury at presentation. Partial recovery with residual CKD, as in this patient, is common when severe AKI and dialysis dependence occur.
- Extended genetic complement testing can detect pathogenic variants that influence duration of eculizumab therapy and relapse risk. Absence of a mutation doesn’t exclude CM‑HUS; it supports an infection‑triggered event and may favor finite therapy, but decisions should be individualized.
- Ongoing nephrology follow‑up, monitoring for relapse, and vaccination/immunization counseling are essential.

## Clinical pearls

- Always ask about recent dog or cat exposure in patients with rapidly progressive sepsis, especially if there’s leukopenia, coagulopathy/DIC or acral ischemia.
- Start broad empiric coverage (eg, piperacillin–tazobactam or a carbapenem) for severe bite‑associated sepsis; do not rely on narrow oral agents for systemic disease.
- Capnocytophaga may take days to grow — consider molecular diagnostics if available and don’t delay empiric therapy.
- If hemolysis and thrombocytopenia develop or persist as sepsis resolves, get a peripheral smear for schistocytes, haptoglobin/LDH, DAT, ADAMTS13 and Shiga toxin testing early.
- Normal C3/C4 does not rule out complement‑mediated HUS.
- PEX is a reasonable bridge while excluding TTP; eculizumab is the definitive therapy for CM‑HUS but requires careful consideration around active infection and appropriate vaccinations/prophylaxis.

Key take‑home messages
- Consider Capnocytophaga canimorsus in severe sepsis after dog/cat exposure — look for rapid deterioration, leukopenia, DIC and acral ischemia.
- Empiric therapy for severe presentations: piperacillin–tazobactam or a carbapenem; de‑escalate when cultures and susceptibilities are available.
- Cultures are slow — use molecular diagnostics when available and start treatment early.
- If hemolysis with schistocytes and persistent thrombocytopenia appear (especially after initial sepsis improvement), evaluate urgently for TMA; distinguish TTP (ADAMTS13 <10%) from CM‑HUS.
- In CM‑HUS, early eculizumab improves outcomes but balance infection risk: ensure culture control, vaccination, and prophylactic antibiotics when initiating complement blockade.

Bottom line: a dog bite is rarely just a minor wound. Keep a high index of suspicion for Capnocytophaga in fulminant sepsis after animal exposure, and be alert for a delayed TMA — because early recognition and timely targeted therapy (whether PEX as a bridge or eculizumab when indicated) can be kidney- and life‑saving.



# When DIC Masks a TMA: Unpacking the Sepsis-to-CM‑HUS Transition

This case catches your attention because it looks like a straightforward fulminant sepsis picture from a dog bite — DIC, pancytopenia, multiorgan failure — until the patient begins to improve and then suddenly develops a classic thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA): falling hemoglobin with undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH, new schistocytosis and persistent thrombocytopenia with dialysis‑dependent acute kidney injury (AKI). The pathogen was Capnocytophaga canimorsus, a rare but well‑described zoonotic trigger for severe sepsis and, in selected cases, complement‑mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM‑HUS). This sequence forces clinicians to ask: when should we stop thinking “DIC only” and start thinking TMA and complement blockade?

## Why this is clinically surprising (and important)

Sepsis and DIC are commonly on the same differential as TMA because both can produce thrombocytopenia, hemolysis and organ dysfunction. In early sepsis, coagulopathy dominates the picture; thrombi in the microcirculation may be mediated by tissue factor, cytokine storm and consumption of clotting factors. In CM‑HUS, the driver is endothelial injury from uncontrolled alternative complement pathway activation producing platelet‑rich microthrombi. The initial overlapping laboratory abnormalities (low platelets, elevated LDH, evidence of bleeding or organ ischemia) can delay TMA recognition. In this case, the haemolytic picture only emerged after the sepsis appeared to improve — a key temporal clue suggesting a superimposed, complement‑mediated process rather than persistent DIC.

## Distinguishing sepsis/DIC from a superimposed TMA — practical pointers

Time course and trajectory matter. In DIC from sepsis you expect coagulopathy (prolonged PT/aPTT, low fibrinogen), bleeding manifestations, and clinical improvement of platelet counts and coagulation indices as the infection comes under control. A new, isolated hemolytic anemia (rapid Hb fall), undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH and schistocytes appearing after the coagulopathy normalises should raise suspicion for TMA. Kidney injury that is disproportionate to the septic insult (persistent anuria, rising creatinine despite resolution of hemodynamic instability) is another red flag.

Laboratory features to watch for:
- Rising LDH and undetectable haptoglobin — better markers of hemolysis than a transient anemia.
- Schistocytes on peripheral smear — even 1%–2% is meaningful; the case had 15%–20%, which is striking.
- Platelets that remain low or fall again despite recovery of coagulation parameters (fibrinogen, PT/aPTT) — suggests platelet consumption in microthrombi rather than consumption coagulopathy.
- ADAMTS13 activity — low (<10%) points to TTP; values in the 40% range (as here) argue against it.
- C3/C4 may be normal in CM‑HUS — do not be reassured by normal routine complement levels.

Renal biopsy, when safe and informative, can clinch the diagnosis by showing fibrin thrombi, ischemic glomeruli and TMA changes as in this patient.

## Pathophysiologic contrast: DIC vs CM‑HUS

Sepsis-associated DIC: triggered by systemic inflammation and endothelial/tissue factor activation, with widespread fibrin formation, consumption of clotting factors and platelets, and bleeding tendency. The coagulopathy improves as infection is controlled and inflammatory drive subsides. Hemolysis, if present, is usually modest and secondary.

CM‑HUS: driven by dysregulated alternative complement pathway activation on endothelial surfaces. This produces platelet‑rich, fibrin‑poor microthrombi, prominent MAHA with schistocytes, severe thrombocytopenia and often severe renal involvement. It may be triggered by infections (including C. canimorsus), pregnancy, drugs or other stressors in genetically predisposed patients — but genetic variants are not always demonstrable. Importantly, complement activation is a double‑edged sword: needed to clear organisms but potentially destructive when unrestrained.

## Why C3/C4 are often normal in CM‑HUS

Routine complement components (C3 and C4) are crude snapshots and can be within reference ranges in CM‑HUS. The alternative pathway can be dysregulated locally at the endothelial surface with normal circulating levels. Also, timing matters: levels may normalize or be influenced by plasma exchange and complement inhibitors. Functional assays of the alternative pathway (AH50), factor levels, anti‑factor H antibodies and genetic testing give more depth but have practical delays and interpretation caveats — and they can be affected by therapies (PEX, eculizumab). Thus normal C3/C4 should not stop the clinical workup for CM‑HUS if the phenotype fits.

## When to pivot from DIC‑focused care to a TMA workup and therapy

Be proactive when you see these “tipping points”:
- New or worsening hemolysis (large Hb drop, undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH) that is unexplained by bleeding.
- New or persistent thrombocytopenia once coagulopathy is improving or fibrinogen/PT have normalized.
- Schistocytes on peripheral smear.
- AKI that is severe, progressive or dialysis‑dependent despite improvement in sepsis and haemodynamics.
- ADAMTS13 not severely deficient (so TTP is unlikely) and stool PCR for Shiga toxin negative.
If these occur, start a parallel TMA workup (repeat smear, haptoglobin, LDH, DAT, ADAMTS13, complement studies, urine/stool testing for STEC) and involve nephrology/hematology early. Consider early plasma exchange as a bridge while awaiting ADAMTS13 and complement results — but recognize its temporary nature in CM‑HUS. When diagnosis is strongly suspected, discuss eculizumab promptly; earlier complement blockade correlates with better renal outcomes.

## The therapeutic dilemma: starting eculizumab in the setting of infection

Eculizumab blocks terminal complement (C5) and increases risk of encapsulated bacterial infections, classically meningococci, but also other encapsulated organisms including Capnocytophaga. In practice, balance the risks:
- Prefer to start eculizumab after blood cultures are negative or the infection is controlled where possible.
- If immediate therapy is necessary, vaccinate against Neisseria meningitidis (and other recommended vaccines) as soon as feasible and give appropriate antibiotic prophylaxis until vaccination is protective.
- Recognize PEX can transiently improve parameters but is unlikely to provide sustained recovery without complement blockade in CM‑HUS.
In the reported case, clinicians waited for negative cultures, performed PEX as a bridge, vaccinated and started eculizumab — an approach that achieved hematologic remission and partial renal recovery.

## Practical workflow for the ED/ward when DIC and TMA overlap

1. Identify red flags (see tipping points).
2. Send targeted labs immediately: CBC with smear, haptoglobin, LDH, bilirubin, coagulation panel, fibrinogen, ADAMTS13, C3/C4, creatinine, urine studies, blood cultures, stool Shiga PCR when indicated.
3. Treat sepsis aggressively (antibiotics, source control, supportive care).
4. If ADAMTS13 is pending but clinical suspicion for TTP is low and TMA is suspected, consider PEX while coordinating with hematology — remember PEX is lifesaving in TTP but only temporizing in CM‑HUS.
5. Involve nephrology early for consideration of biopsy and renal replacement therapy.
6. If CM‑HUS is likely and infection is controlled, arrange for vaccination and initiate eculizumab promptly, with prophylactic antibiotics as indicated.

## Clinical pearls from the case

- Don’t be reassured by normal C3/C4 in the face of a TMA phenotype; complement activation can be local and not reflected in routine levels.
- The timing of hemolysis relative to sepsis trajectory is crucial: new MAHA after improvement of the infectious picture suggests a superimposed TMA.
- ADAMTS13 helps exclude TTP; values >10–15% argue against it, shifting the focus to HUS/TMA etiologies.
- Peripheral smear matters — actively look for schistocytes. A formal percentage is useful, but any meaningful schistocytosis in the appropriate context warrants escalation.
- Plasma exchange can bridge but is often insufficient for CM‑HUS; eculizumab is disease‑specific and associated with better renal outcomes when started early.
- Infections can both trigger CM‑HUS and be harder to clear when complement is blocked — time and sequencing of therapy (cultures, antivirals/antibiotics, vaccines, prophylaxis) are important.

## Take‑home messages

- Consider TMA (including CM‑HUS) when hemolysis (undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH), schistocytosis and persistent thrombocytopenia emerge or worsen despite resolution of sepsis/DIC, especially if kidney injury is out of proportion.
- Normal routine complement (C3/C4) does not exclude CM‑HUS; pursue a focused TMA workup and specialist input if clinical suspicion is high.
- Use ADAMTS13 to exclude TTP; use PEX as a bridge in uncertain cases, but plan for eculizumab if CM‑HUS is likely and infection is controlled.
- Early recognition and timely complement blockade improve hematologic and renal outcomes; coordinate vaccinations and prophylactic antibiotics when starting complement inhibitors.

This case is a reminder to keep TMA on your radar even when sepsis and DIC look like the clear story — the evolution of labs over time is often the clue that reveals a second, treatable pathology underneath.



# MAHA + Thrombocytopenia + AKI at the Bedside: A Rapid TMA Algorithm

What makes this case interesting: a previously healthy woman develops fulminant Capnocytophaga canimorsus sepsis after a dog bite, then—after initial improvement—develops classical microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), persistent thrombocytopaenia and dialysis-dependent AKI. Blood cultures grew C. canimorsus and kidney biopsy showed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). ADAMTS13 was not consistent with TTP; eculizumab led to haematologic remission and partial renal recovery. This is a perfect vignette to show how infection can masquerade as DIC early, then unmask a complement-mediated HUS (CM‑HUS), and how to run a rapid, pragmatic bedside TMA workup and treatment algorithm.

Keep in mind: TMA is a syndrome, not a diagnosis. Early decisions save kidneys and lives. The task at the bedside is to rapidly separate TTP (ADAMTS13 deficiency), STEC‑HUS, secondary TMAs and CM‑HUS — because therapy and urgency differ.

Initial bedside assessment (first 0–2 hours)
- Recognize the syndrome: anaemia + schistocytes/MAHA + thrombocytopenia ± AKI ± neurologic signs ± fever.
- Immediately get the core labs you need to decide therapy:
  - CBC with peripheral blood smear (ask lab to do smear now and report schistocyte percentage).
  - LDH, total and indirect bilirubin, haptoglobin, reticulocyte count.
  - Coagulation panel (PT/INR, APTT, fibrinogen) — to help separate DIC.
  - Creatinine, electrolytes, urinalysis.
  - Blood type and hold for transfusion and urgent crossmatch.
  - Blood cultures if infection suspected.
- Send these urgent specialty tests simultaneously (don’t wait for smear):
  - ADAMTS13 activity (stat; state “rule out TTP”).
  - STEC testing (stool PCR for Shiga toxin).
  - Direct antiglobulin test (DAT/Coombs).
  - Complement tests (C3, C4) and, if available locally, alternative pathway assays — but interpret cautiously.

Triage with PLASMIC and pretest probability (minutes)
- Calculate a PLASMIC score while awaiting ADAMTS13 to estimate probability of severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (TTP).
- PLASMIC ≤4 = low pretest probability; 5 = intermediate; ≥6 = high.
- Use this to decide immediate PEX: if high pretest probability (PLASMIC ≥6) or clinical deterioration consistent with TTP, start plasma exchange (PEX) immediately — do not wait for ADAMTS13.

When to start plasma exchange (PEX) as a bridge
- Start PEX emergently if either:
  - High clinical suspicion for TTP (severe neurologic signs, profound thrombocytopenia and MAHA), or
  - PLASMIC score is high.
- If low PLASMIC and ADAMTS13 result is expected quickly, you may defer PEX and focus on other causes — but only if patient is stable.
- PEX removes autoantibodies and replaces ADAMTS13; it can temporize other TMAs too, so it’s a reasonable bridge if you are unsure and the patient is sick.

Interpreting ADAMTS13 and pivot points
- ADAMTS13 <10%: diagnostic of TTP — continue PEX + steroids and add rituximab as per local protocol.
- ADAMTS13 10–30%: grey zone — correlate clinically; consider PEX and hematology input.
- ADAMTS13 >10% (often >20–30%): TTP unlikely — broaden differential to STEC, complement-mediated HUS, secondary TMA (drugs, pregnancy, malignancy, autoimmune, infection/DIC).
- In the presented case ADAMTS13 ≈43.5% — effectively ruled out TTP and prompted pivot to CM‑HUS workup.

Workup while ADAMTS13 and STEC results pending
- If stool PCR for Shiga toxin positive → STEC‑HUS management and supportive care (no eculizumab).
- If STEC negative and ADAMTS13 >10% → consider:
  - Secondary causes (sepsis-related DIC, malignant hypertension, drugs, autoimmune, pregnancy/HELLP, malignancy).
  - Complement-mediated HUS (CM‑HUS), particularly if severe AKI, ongoing MAHA, schistocytes and biopsy consistent with TMA.
- Kidney biopsy can be very informative if diagnosis remains unclear and the patient is stable enough for biopsy. It proved diagnostic in our case (fibrin thrombi, ischaemic glomeruli).

Practical pitfalls and pearls from the case
- DIC can mask or coexist with TMA. Early sepsis may produce coagulopathy, leukopaenia or pancytopenia that obscures schistocytosis; the haemolysis/MAHA may appear only as sepsis improves (as happened here).
- False-positive DATs are common (penicillins, recent transfusion). A positive first DAT here was later negative and probably drug- or transfusion-related — don’t anchor on DAT without smear and schistocyte confirmation.
- Schistocytes are key. A peripheral smear showing schistocytes (≥1%–2% or >1 per high-power field depending on lab) should push you hard toward TMA workup. In this case 15–20% schistocytes clinched the diagnosis of MAHA.
- Complement levels (C3/C4) can be normal in CM‑HUS. Normal C3/C4 does not exclude complement dysregulation; interpret complement assays in context and consider functional testing or genetic studies.
- Timing of tests relative to PEX and eculizumab matters: PEX and eculizumab alter complement assays and may confound interpretation. If possible, draw baseline complement samples before PEX or eculizumab.

When to use eculizumab (complement blockade)
- Strongly consider eculizumab when:
  - TTP and STEC have been reasonably excluded (ADAMTS13 >10%, negative Shiga toxin), AND
  - There is persistent MAHA with thrombocytopenia and severe AKI consistent with TMA, especially with biopsy evidence of TMA.
- Early eculizumab initiation is associated with better renal outcomes. In practice, many centres will start eculizumab once TTP/STEC are unlikely and the patient is improving from any active infection — or after cultures become negative.
- Important infectious risk: terminal complement blockade increases susceptibility to encapsulated organisms (Neisseria, others including Capnocytophaga). Vaccinate (meningococcal ACWY and B) and give antimicrobial prophylaxis if you must start eculizumab before vaccines become protective. In this case, blood cultures were negative before eculizumab and meningococcal vaccines were administered prior to dosing, with antibiotic prophylaxis in place.

Transfusion strategies and supportive care
- Transfuse red cells for symptomatic anaemia or Hb thresholds per local protocol; transfusions may transiently worsen smears/DATs but are life‑saving.
- Platelet transfusions are generally avoided in TTP and other TMAs unless there is life‑threatening bleeding or invasive procedures — because transfused platelets can fuel microthrombi. In DIC with bleeding, platelets may be needed.
- In sepsis/DIC overlapping with TMA, weigh risks: our patient received supportive transfusions during active coagulopathy; decisions must be individualized.
- If PEX is started, remember to transfuse plasma (FFP or Octaplas) per protocol; PEX may transiently correct labs but does not treat CM‑HUS definitively.

Coordination and logistics: who to call and when
- Call hematology urgently for suspected TTP (PEX arrangements) and for guidance on immunosuppression.
- Call nephrology early for AKI/dialysis planning and consideration of biopsy.
- Infectious diseases should be involved if infection is suspected/ongoing and before starting eculizumab.
- Microbiology: flag ADAMTS13 and STEC testing as urgent; some centres offer rapid ADAMTS13 assays.
- If considering complement genetic testing, know that results take weeks — they guide duration of therapy but not the acute decision to treat.

A practical bedside algorithm (summary workflow)
1. Identify MAHA + thrombocytopenia ± AKI.
2. STAT: peripheral smear, LDH, haptoglobin, bilirubin, CBC, coags, creatinine, blood cultures, DAT, stool Shiga PCR, draw ADAMTS13 and complement samples.
3. Calculate PLASMIC. If high or clinical TTP suspected → start PEX immediately and involve hematology.
4. If ADAMTS13 >10% and STEC negative → consider CM‑HUS/secondary TMA. Rule out secondary causes (drugs, pregnancy, autoimmune).
5. If severe AKI and biopsy or clinical picture supports TMA → discuss and consider early eculizumab after weighing infection risks, ensuring cultures are controlled or adequate antibiotics/prophylaxis/vaccination in place.
6. Provide supportive care: transfuse RBCs for symptomatic anaemia; avoid platelets unless bleeding or procedures; start dialysis if indicated.

Key take-home messages
- TMA presentation is time-sensitive. Start the bedside workup immediately (smear, LDH, haptoglobin, bilirubin) and send stat ADAMTS13 and STEC PCR.
- Use PLASMIC to triage urgency for PEX; high score or neurologic compromise → start PEX without waiting for ADAMTS13.
- ADAMTS13 <10% = TTP (PEX ± steroids ± rituximab). ADAMTS13 >10% → pivot to look for STEC, secondary causes and CM‑HUS.
- Normal complement levels do not exclude CM‑HUS. Kidney biopsy can be decisive when safe to perform.
- Eculizumab should be considered early in severe CM‑HUS after excluding TTP/STEC and controlling active infection; vaccinate and give prophylactic antibiotics when starting complement blockade.

This case — dog bite to C. canimorsus sepsis to complement-mediated HUS — reminds us that infectious triggers can cause CM‑HUS even in previously healthy adults. Rapid, parallel processing of labs, clear triage (PLASMIC → ADAMTS13 → STEC), early use of PEX as a bridge, and prompt consideration of complement blockade when appropriate will save kidneys and lives.



# Pulling the Trigger on Eculizumab: Infection Timing, Vaccines, and Prophylaxis

What makes this case hard and common at the same time is the tug-of-war between two priorities: start eculizumab early to halt complement‑mediated TMA and save kidney function, versus avoid blunting a vital innate defence while the patient still has ongoing or recent bacteraemia. The Capnocytophaga canimorsus case above is a perfect teaching vignette — severe septic presentation with DIC, partial recovery, then delayed MAHA and biopsy‑proven TMA. The team waited until cultures were negative, vaccinated, gave antibiotic prophylaxis, and then started eculizumab — and the patient improved. That sequence illustrates key practical choices you’ll face when considering complement blockade after infection.

Below I summarise the pragmatic, evidence‑informed approach used in real life: when to start eculizumab after bacteremia, what vaccines to give and when, antibiotic prophylaxis options (including how to handle allergies), monitoring, and how to frame the risks with patients.

## The core clinical problem
- Eculizumab blocks terminal complement (C5) — excellent for CM‑HUS, but terminal complement is essential for killing encapsulated organisms (classically Neisseria meningitidis, but also organisms like Capnocytophaga). Starting eculizumab in the face of active bloodstream infection can increase risk of overwhelming infection.
- Delay eculizumab → risk of irreversible kidney injury and worse outcomes. Start too early → infectious complications.
- Practical compromise: clear active bacteremia (negative cultures, clinical control), vaccinate against meningococcus (MenACWY + MenB), and provide antibiotic prophylaxis during the vaccine lead‑in and often while on therapy. Individualise with infectious diseases input.

## Timing: when to pull the trigger
- Ideal scenario: patient is clinically improving, blood cultures are negative, source control achieved, and you can give vaccines ≥14 days before first eculizumab dose. That 2‑week window allows adequate serologic response to meningococcal vaccines.
- Real world: many patients cannot wait 2 weeks because of ongoing TMA. Reasonable strategy used in the presented case and in guidelines:
  - If you cannot wait, give the required meningococcal vaccines immediately and start eculizumab once the team agrees risks/benefits favour treatment.
  - Provide immediate antibiotic prophylaxis and maintain it for the vaccine lead‑in period (see below). Monitor closely and engage ID.
- In the case report, blood cultures had cleared; vaccines were given and antibiotic prophylaxis continued; eculizumab was started once cultures were negative — a conservative, defensible approach.

## Vaccination strategy (what to give)
- Give both:
  - MenACWY conjugate vaccine (covers serogroups A, C, W, Y), AND
  - MenB vaccine.
- Timing:
  - Ideally complete both vaccine series ≥2 weeks before starting complement blockade.
  - If not possible, give vaccines immediately and start antibiotics; vaccinate per schedule thereafter (boosters/second doses as recommended locally).
- Practical notes:
  - MenACWY typically requires a single adult dose for primary immunisation in many settings (boosters as indicated). MenB is usually a multi‑dose series. Check local immunisation schedules.
  - Vaccination does not fully mitigate risk (breakthrough meningococcal disease possible), so antibiotic prophylaxis and education remain essential.

## Antibiotic prophylaxis: who, what, and how long
- Why: until vaccine‑induced protection is expected (commonly 14 days) — and often continued while on eculizumab to reduce risk from meningococcus or other encapsulated organisms, especially in the early months of therapy.
- Commonly used agents:
  - First‑line: oral penicillin V or amoxicillin (covers many meningococci and Capnocytophaga). This was used in the case (Apocillin).
  - If the isolate/susceptibilities warrant (or patient already on IV therapy), IV beta‑lactams may be used while inpatient.
- If true severe beta‑lactam allergy:
  - Discuss options with ID — consider ciprofloxacin (single‑dose is used for meningococcal chemoprophylaxis in exposures; for ongoing prophylaxis, some centres use daily ciprofloxacin) or doxycycline where appropriate, recognising variable data and local resistance patterns.
  - Macrolides (e.g., azithromycin) are less reliable for meningococcus and Capnocytophaga; use only after ID input and susceptibility data.
- Duration:
  - At minimum, continue prophylaxis for 2 weeks after vaccination if vaccination given immediately before eculizumab.
  - Many teams continue a long‑term prophylactic regimen for as long as terminal complement blockade is ongoing, with periodic reassessment (practice varies; involve ID/nephrology).
- Important: antibiotic prophylaxis is not a guarantee. Vaccination + prophylaxis + patient education is layered protection.

## Practical antimicrobial selections (summary for common scenarios)
- No allergy, inpatient/ongoing IV therapy: continue IV beta‑lactam that covers meningococcus and the identified pathogen (eg, ampicillin for Capnocytophaga if susceptible).
- Transition to oral prophylaxis on discharge: oral penicillin V or amoxicillin if susceptible and tolerated.
- Severe penicillin allergy: consult ID. Consider fluoroquinolone (ciprofloxacin) or doxycycline; weigh risks, local resistance, and organism susceptibilities. Do not empirically rely on macrolides for meningococcus.
- Always tailor to culture results and susceptibilities when available.

## Monitoring and safety netting
- Before starting eculizumab:
  - Ensure meningococcal vaccines given (MenACWY + MenB) ideally ≥2 weeks prior; if not, give immediately and start antibiotics.
  - Confirm bacteremia cleared if possible (negative cultures); if ongoing bacteremia, weigh urgency of eculizumab vs infectious risk and get ID involved.
  - Document informed discussion with patient about increased infection risk and prophylaxis plan.
- While on eculizumab:
  - Maintain antibiotic prophylaxis per plan.
  - Monitor for fevers, neck stiffness, photophobia, severe headaches, purpura, or rapidly evolving sepsis — educate patient to seek urgent care.
  - Provide patient with an emergency card/letter stating they are on complement blockade and at risk for meningococcal disease.
  - Consider periodic review of need for ongoing prophylaxis and vaccine boosters.
- If invasive infection is suspected on therapy: treat aggressively with empiric broad therapy and consider stopping eculizumab temporarily only with ID guidance — but usually, treating infection and continuing complement inhibitor is a case‑by‑case decision.

## Communicating risk with patients (how to frame it)
- Be direct: eculizumab can stop the process that is destroying red cells and kidneys, but it increases the risk of serious infections — notably meningococcal disease.
- Explain the mitigation strategy: vaccinations, antibiotic prophylaxis, close monitoring, and an emergency action plan.
- Emphasise symptoms that require immediate attention (fever, severe headache, neck stiffness, petechial rash, high fever, rapid deterioration).
- Document the shared decision and the rationale for timing.

## Clinical pearls from the Capnocytophaga case
- CM‑HUS can present after an initial septic phase — watch for late haemolysis, falling haptoglobin, rising LDH, and schistocytes even as inflammatory markers improve.
- Normal C3/C4 or normal complement pathway assays do NOT exclude CM‑HUS.
- If TTP is unlikely (ADAMTS13 >10%) and TMA is severe, don’t delay attempts at definitive therapy; when feasible, wait for negative cultures and vaccinate, but if that’s not possible start eculizumab with antibiotics and close ID partnership.
- Document vaccine administration and provide patient education and prophylactic antibiotics — these simple steps reduce, but do not eliminate, risk.

## Take‑home messages
- Eculizumab is kidney‑saving in CM‑HUS but increases susceptibility to encapsulated organisms; timing matters.
- Give MenACWY + MenB before starting eculizumab when possible; if not possible, vaccinate immediately and give antibiotic prophylaxis.
- Continue antibiotic prophylaxis at least until vaccine protection is expected (commonly 2 weeks); many centres continue long‑term prophylaxis while on complement blockade — discuss with ID.
- If penicillin allergy exists, involve ID early to choose alternative prophylaxis (e.g., ciprofloxacin or doxycycline in selected situations).
- Always communicate risk clearly with patients, provide an emergency action plan, and monitor closely.

If you’re about to start eculizumab in the setting of a recent bacteremia, get infectious diseases involved, document a plan (vaccination + prophylaxis + monitoring), and balance the immediacy of kidney rescue against infectious risk — the case above shows that with careful sequencing you can both treat the TMA and keep the patient safe.



# Complement Testing in the Real World: Normal C3/C4, Post‑PEX Labs, and Genetics

What makes this case interesting: a previously healthy woman with fulminant Capnocytophaga canimorsus sepsis who develops dialysis‑dependent acute kidney injury, then a delayed microangiopathic hemolytic anemia with schistocytes and persistent thrombocytopenia. Basic complement tests (C3/C4) were normal; ADAMTS13 was not consistent with TTP; kidney biopsy showed thrombotic microangiopathy; she improved only after plasma exchange (briefly) and then eculizumab. Extended testing later showed a suppressed AH50 (alternative pathway activity), but no pathogenic complement gene variant — a constellation that highlights the real‑world pitfalls of complement testing during evolving TMA.

Why this matters
- Clinicians often reflexively check C3 and C4 when suspecting complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS), but normal levels do not rule it out.
- Timing of blood draws relative to plasma exchange (PEX) and complement blockade (eculizumab) profoundly alters interpretation of functional assays and antigenic factor levels.
- Genetic testing for complement regulatory variants has imperfect sensitivity and faces interpretation challenges; results influence long‑term therapy decisions but usually arrive late.

Below I’ll walk through practical principles illustrated by the case, and give actionable guidance on what to order and when — and how to read the results you get back.

Short primer: what CM‑HUS tests aim to tell you
- C3/C4 are static serum antigen measurements. Low C3 can reflect ongoing alternative pathway consumption, but many CM‑HUS patients have normal C3/C4.
- Functional assays (AH50, AP activity) test the integrity and activity of the alternative pathway. They are sensitive to prior treatments and plasma replacement.
- Antigenic levels of FH, FI, FB, MCP/CD46 and anti‑FH autoantibodies can identify acquired or inherited regulators of complement dysregulation.
- Genetic sequencing (CFH, CFI, CFB, C3, MCP etc.) looks for germline variants that increase predisposition; absence of variants does not exclude CM‑HUS, and penetrance is incomplete.

Why C3/C4 are often normal in CM‑HUS
- CM‑HUS is a disease of dysregulated amplification at the level of the alternative pathway on endothelial surfaces. This can cause severe local complement activation without large systemic consumption that would depress serum C3/C4.
- Complement components are abundant and acute phase responses may normalise antigen levels.
- Timing matters: C3/C4 measured after recovery or after PEX/IV fluids may have normalised.

Clinical pearl: a normal C3 (or C4) does not exclude complement‑mediated TMA. Use clinical context + biopsy + ADAMTS13 + hemolysis markers to trawl for CM‑HUS.

How PEX (plasma exchange) alters testing and how to account for it
- PEX replaces patient plasma with donor plasma (or Octaplas) and transiently normalises or dilutes complement components, regulatory proteins and function.
- Immediately after PEX, antigenic levels of regulators (FH, FI, etc.) may be near donor levels; AH50 may be reduced if replacement fluid is pathogen‑depleted or if multiple exchanges deplete specific functional activity.
- PEX can also remove anti‑factor H autoantibodies — which is a therapeutic aim if present — making subsequent testing falsely negative.

Practical points about PEX:
- If possible, draw baseline complement antigen and functional studies before the first PEX. That provides the most interpretable snapshot.
- If PEX must be started urgently (e.g., while TTP is being excluded), document timing precisely and expect later assays to be affected.
- When you receive low AH50 after PEX, interpret it as possibly treatment effect rather than intrinsic deficiency unless pre‑PEX samples are available.

Eculizumab and its effects on complement testing
- Eculizumab binds C5 and blocks terminal complement activation. After dosing:
  - CH50 (classical pathway hemolytic assay) and other terminal complement–dependent functional assays become profoundly depressed.
  - Upstream antigen levels (C3/C4, FH) may be unchanged, but functional assays requiring MAC formation will be low/absent.
- Eculizumab does not correct upstream dysregulation; it prevents downstream microvascular damage but will render functional assays that rely on terminal pathway readouts uninterpretable.

Practical takeaways about eculizumab timing:
- Whenever possible, send antigenic and genetic complement studies before the first dose of eculizumab.
- If eculizumab has already been given, functional tests that assess terminal activity (CH50/AH50) will be low — this is expected. Focus instead on antigenic assays and genetic testing (though PEX sequencing can still affect antigenic levels).
- Vaccinate and give prophylactic antibiotics appropriately when initiating eculizumab in the setting of recent bacteremia; the case exemplifies starting eculizumab after cultures turned negative while providing vaccines and prophylaxis.

Genetic testing: what it tells you and limitations
- Molecular sequencing for complement regulators may find pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in ~50% of patients with atypical HUS, depending on cohort and criteria used.
- A negative genetic panel does not exclude CM‑HUS. Penetrance is incomplete, and undiscovered genes or regulatory variants exist.
- Identification of a pathogenic variant (or anti‑FH antibodies) informs prognosis and decisions about duration of eculizumab: variants in CFH/CFI/CFB/C3 or anti‑FH often increase relapse risk and may favor prolonged therapy or close surveillance.
- Genetic results usually take weeks; early therapy decisions will therefore be clinical and biologic rather than genetic.

How to act on the test results in practice — a staged approach (grounded in the case)
1. On initial suspicion of TMA (MAHA + thrombocytopenia ± AKI): draw CBC, peripheral smear, LDH, haptoglobin, creatinine, coagulation panel, blood cultures, C3/C4, ADAMTS13 (stat), and collect serum for extended complement studies and genetics if available. If PEX/eculizumab is being considered, try to get those extended samples before treatment.
2. If ADAMTS13 <10% → treat as TTP (urgent PEX). If ADAMTS13 >10% and Shiga toxin negative and biopsy or clinical course suggests TMA, strongly consider CM‑HUS.
3. If PEX is started emergently (e.g., while ruling out TTP), annotate the timing of sample draws and understand that PEX will alter antigenic/functional assays and may remove autoantibodies.
4. If CM‑HUS is felt likely and the patient is not bacteremic (or blood cultures are clearing), start eculizumab early — don’t wait for genetic results. Vaccinate and provide prophylactic antibiotics as per guidelines.
5. Use genetic and anti‑factor H antibody results to gauge relapse risk and help decide on duration of complement blockade once the acute phase is controlled.

Interpreting the tests you might get back (common scenarios)
- Scenario A: Normal C3/C4, normal CH50/AH50 before any therapy — still could be CM‑HUS. Do not exclude CM‑HUS on the basis of normal static complement levels.
- Scenario B: Low AH50 after PEX but normal antigenic levels — likely PEX effect. If you have pre‑PEX samples, use them; if not, interpret cautiously and plan repeat testing at least 2–4 weeks after stopping PEX (and before eculizumab if possible).
- Scenario C: Suppressed CH50/AH50 after eculizumab — expected drug effect. Do not use these assays to judge intrinsic pathway defects while on complement blockade.
- Scenario D: Anti‑factor H antibodies detected pre‑PEX — strongly suggests an acquired cause and indicates benefit from PEX and immunosuppression; repeat testing after PEX may be negative (treatment effect).
- Scenario E: No pathogenic variants on sequencing — supports infection‑triggered CM‑HUS or non‑genetic trigger. Continue therapy guided by clinical recovery and risk assessment; stopping eculizumab can be considered with close monitoring.

Key clinical pearls
- “Normal C3/C4” is common in CM‑HUS — complement activation on endothelial surfaces may not lower serum C3/C4.
- Draw extended complement tests (FH, FI, FB, MCP, anti‑FH) and genetic samples before PEX or eculizumab if the patient’s condition allows; otherwise document timings.
- PEX can transiently normalise or suppress tests and remove autoantibodies — interpret post‑PEX results as treatment‑affected.
- Eculizumab abolishes terminal pathway functional assays (CH50/AH50). Don’t attempt to use these assays to diagnose upstream defects while the patient is on therapy.
- Genetic testing helps prognosticate and tailor duration of therapy but has limited sensitivity and delayed turnaround.

Take‑home messages
- Normal C3/C4 does not rule out CM‑HUS.
- Obtain ADAMTS13 urgently to exclude TTP; if PEX/eculizumab may be needed, try to draw complement antigenic and functional studies and genetic samples before starting these therapies.
- Interpret AH50/CH50 and antigenic complement levels in the context of recent PEX or eculizumab: PEX and eculizumab both affect assay results, but in different ways.
- Genetic and anti‑factor H testing are valuable for long‑term management and relapse risk stratification but rarely change acute management.
- Clinical judgment — integrating biopsy, hemolysis markers, microbiology, and the time course — remains central. In many cases (as in this Capnocytophaga patient), early complement blockade after reasonable exclusion/management of active infection produces the best renal and hematologic outcomes.

# Kidney Biopsy in TMA: When It Helps, When It Hurts

What makes this case worth reading: a previously well woman goes from a dog bite to fulminant sepsis with DIC, anuric dialysis‑dependent acute kidney injury (AKI), and then—after early improvement—develops a new microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) with persistent thrombocytopaenia. Renal biopsy performed once the platelets rose (to ~77×10^9/L) showed fibrin thrombi, ischemic glomeruli, acute tubular necrosis (ATN) and scattered crescents — classic thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) histology. That biopsy helped move the team from supportive care and plasma exchange to definitive complement blockade with eculizumab, and the patient regained urine output and partial renal recovery.

This is a helpful case to examine the practical question many clinicians face: do you biopsy the anuric, thrombocytopenic patient with suspected TMA? If so, when and how? And what will the histology really change about management?

## The clinical dilemma — why biopsy is controversial in TMA
- TMA syndromes are clinicopathological. In the critically ill adult with MAHA, thrombocytopenia and AKI you must rapidly exclude TTP (severe ADAMTS13 deficiency) and STEC‑HUS, and consider secondary TMAs (sepsis/DIC, malignant hypertension, drugs, pregnancy, autoimmune disease). Decisions about life‑saving therapies (PEX for TTP, eculizumab for complement‑mediated HUS) are often made before biopsy results would be available.
- Kidney biopsy is invasive and carries bleeding risk in thrombocytopenic or coagulopathic patients. Yet the biopsy can:
  - Confirm TMA as the dominant renal lesion (vs isolated ATN or immune complex GN).
  - Demonstrate features that favour complement‑mediated endothelial injury (widespread fibrin thrombi in glomeruli/arterioles, endothelial swelling, ischemic collapse) versus predominantly consumptive coagulopathy/DIC.
  - Provide prognostic information (extent of cortical necrosis, chronicity, crescents).
  - Help justify prolonged targeted therapy (eculizumab) when genetic testing is pending/negative.

In short: biopsy can hurt if done too early or unsafely, but it can help refine diagnosis and treatment decisions—especially when the therapeutic choice carries major risks and costs.

## When a biopsy is most likely to be helpful
Consider biopsy when:
- TTP has been reasonably excluded or already treated (ADAMTS13 activity result available or pretest probability low and PEX started empirically).
- STEC‑HUS is excluded or unlikely.
- Initial infectious trigger is controlled (negative blood cultures or improving infection) and you’re weighing prolonged complement blockade vs transient/secondary TMA management.
- Platelets and coagulation parameters have improved to safer ranges (see thresholds below).
- Kidney involvement is severe (dialysis dependence, anuria, rising creatinine) and you need prognostic information to counsel about recovery or to justify long‑term costly therapy.
In the case above, biopsy was performed after platelets rose to 77×10^9/L and coagulation normalized—timing that allowed obtaining meaningful histology while limiting procedural risk.

## What to expect on histology — and what it means
Typical TMA patterns and their interpretation:
- Acute lesions
  - Fibrin thrombi in glomerular capillaries and arterioles: hallmark of active TMA.
  - Endothelial swelling and mesangiolysis: ongoing endothelial injury.
  - Ischemic glomerular collapse and cortical necrosis: severe ischemia, worse prognosis for recovery.
  - Acute tubular necrosis (ATN): common and may be reversible.
  - Crescents (focal): indicate severe glomerular injury and may reflect prolonged or intense microvascular occlusion.
- What is often absent: immune complex deposition on immunofluorescence (helps exclude immune complex GN).
- Electron microscopy: shows endothelial cell changes and subendothelial widening; not usually diagnostic alone.

How histology changes management:
- Confirmed TMA with active fibrin thrombi plus limited chronic change supports aggressive, targeted therapy (e.g., start/continue eculizumab) when clinical context fits CM‑HUS.
- Predominant ATN with minimal microthrombi or extensive cortical necrosis suggests either secondary injury or irreversible damage—guiding against prolonged complement blockade.
- Immune complex deposition or vasculitis would steer toward alternate immunosuppression.

In the index patient, biopsy showing fibrin thrombi, ischemic glomeruli and ATN (no immune complexes) supported TMA as the driver of AKI and bolstered the decision to commit to complement blockade after infection control.

## Procedural risk mitigation — practical tips
When you decide to biopsy, optimize safety:
- Timing: wait for platelet recovery and correction of coagulopathy if clinically permissible.
- Thresholds commonly used (institutional variation applies):
  - Platelets >50×10^9/L is a commonly accepted minimum for percutaneous biopsy.
  - INR <1.5 and aPTT near normal; correct significant abnormalities with plasma, vitamin K, or PCC as indicated.
- Transfusion strategies:
  - Prophylactic platelet transfusion to reach safe thresholds (keep in mind transfused platelets can transiently obscure underlying thrombocytopenia).
  - Fresh frozen plasma or Octaplas for hypofibrinogenaemia/INR correction if needed.
- Technique:
  - Ultrasound‑guided percutaneous core biopsy is standard.
  - Consider transjugular renal biopsy in high‑risk patients (safer for severe coagulopathy or morbid obesity).
- Post‑procedure monitoring: bed rest, serial haemoglobin checks, early imaging if concern for bleeding.

In the reported case the team waited until platelets rose, and biopsy was performed safely with diagnostic yield.

## Where biopsy fits into the diagnostic cascade
- Immediate/emergent priorities: stabilize patient, start broad antimicrobials for sepsis, obtain blood cultures, correct hypoperfusion, and empirically consider PEX if TTP is suspected while waiting for ADAMTS13.
- If ADAMTS13 is low (<10%): treat as TTP—PEX is lifesaving; renal biopsy generally not necessary acutely.
- If ADAMTS13 is not severely reduced, STEC negative, and infection controlled but renal failure persists or you face a decision about prolonged complement inhibition, renal biopsy can:
  - Strengthen a diagnosis of CM‑HUS if biopsy shows active TMA and absence of immune complexes.
  - Exclude alternate diagnoses that would obviate eculizumab.
- Use biopsy findings together with clinical course, complement testing (C3/C4, functional assays) and genetics to decide duration of complement blockade.

## Clinical pearls from the Capnocytophaga case
- Infectious triggers (including unusual ones like Capnocytophaga canimorsus) may precipitate CM‑HUS even in patients without identified complement gene variants; the trigger may be temporally separated from the haemolysis.
- Normal serum C3/C4 does not exclude complement‑mediated TMA.
- The timing of haemolysis matters: in this patient haemolysis and schistocytosis appeared after the initial septic picture improved—this temporal pattern should raise suspicion for a superimposed TMA rather than ongoing DIC alone.
- Biopsy histology can tip the balance toward eculizumab when infection is controlled and other causes (TTP, STEC) are unlikely.
- When complement blockade is considered in the context of recent or ongoing infection, ensure cultures are negative, vaccinate for meningococcus, and provide antimicrobial prophylaxis as per local protocols.

## Take‑home messages
- Don’t reflexively biopsy every patient with AKI and thrombocytopenia—balance urgency, diagnostic yield and bleeding risk.
- Consider renal biopsy when:
  - TTP and STEC have been reasonably excluded or treated,
  - infection is controlled,
  - the biopsy result would change management (e.g., commit to prolonged eculizumab vs expectant care).
- Aim for safe thresholds (platelets commonly >50×10^9/L, corrected INR), or choose transjugular approach when coagulopathy persists.
- Histology showing fibrin thrombi, ischemic glomeruli and ATN with absent immune complexes supports TMA and strengthens the case for complement blockade; predominant ATN or cortical necrosis suggests a worse prognosis and may argue against long‑term targeted therapy.
- Use biopsy as one data point—integrate clinical course, microbiology, ADAMTS13, complement studies and genetics before committing to long‑term complement inhibition.

Renal biopsy can both hurt and help—done at the right time, with risk mitigation, it often provides the decisive piece of evidence in complex TMA syndromes, as illustrated by this patient who progressed from dog bite to dialysis and ultimately had histology‑guided recovery with complement blockade.
