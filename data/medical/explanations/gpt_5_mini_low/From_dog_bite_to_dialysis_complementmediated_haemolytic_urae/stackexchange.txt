Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Management] A previously healthy woman presents 4 days after a dog bite with fever, leukopenia, coagulopathy, and AKI. What empiric intravenous antibiotic regimen is appropriate to cover Capnocytophaga canimorsus and other dog-associated pathogens, and why is timely initiation critical?

Answer

Empiric therapy for a patient with a dog bite who presents with systemic sepsis should provide reliable coverage for Capnocytophaga canimorsus plus the usual bite-associated organisms (Pasteurella spp., streptococci, staphylococci, anaerobes and mixed oral flora). In a critically ill patient the appropriate initial intravenous choices are:

- Piperacillin–tazobactam IV (broad-spectrum beta‑lactam/beta‑lactamase inhibitor) OR
- Ampicillin–sulbactam IV for less severe illness; or
- Meropenem IV for life‑threatening infection or concern for beta‑lactamase–producing or resistant organisms.

Rationale and practical points
- Capnocytophaga canimorsus is usually susceptible to penicillins but can produce beta‑lactamases and is fastidious and slow-growing in culture. Empiric use of a beta‑lactam combined with a beta‑lactamase inhibitor (piperacillin–tazobactam or ampicillin–sulbactam) reliably covers C. canimorsus and also covers Pasteurella, streptococci, many staphylococci and anaerobes commonly introduced by dog bites. In severely ill patients, a carbapenem (eg, meropenem) gives the broadest early coverage.
- In the case report the team started piperacillin–tazobactam on presentation to cover “dog‑associated pathogens including Capnocytophaga,” and blood cultures later grew C. canimorsus, allowing de‑escalation to ampicillin. That stepwise approach (broad empiric therapy → narrow by culture) is appropriate.
- If MRSA is a concern (eg, severe soft‑tissue infection with high local MRSA prevalence), add vancomycin or daptomycin; for true severe penicillin allergy, consider doxycycline (or a fluoroquinolone) plus metronidazole for anaerobic coverage, recognizing variable susceptibilities and that alternatives may be less well studied for Capnocytophaga.
- Obtain blood and wound cultures before antibiotics if this does not materially delay therapy, but do not delay empiric IV antibiotics in a septic patient.

Why timeliness matters
- C. canimorsus infection can progress very rapidly from a local bite to fulminant sepsis, DIC and multiorgan failure (mortality up to ~30%). Early empiric IV therapy can arrest bacteremia and reduce the endotoxin/complement activation cascade that contributes to endothelial injury.
- In this report the prompt initiation of piperacillin–tazobactam at presentation likely contributed to initial clinical improvement; delayed or inadequate therapy would increase the risk of refractory sepsis, DIC and secondary complications — including triggering complement‑mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (CM‑HUS), which is time‑sensitive in regard to subsequent therapies (plasmapheresis, eculizumab) and renal recovery.
- Because C. canimorsus is slow‑growing and may take days to identify, empiric coverage covering this organism must be started immediately in any patient with systemic manifestations after a dog or cat bite.

Duration and de‑escalation
- Tailor duration to clinical response and culture results; uncomplicated bacteremia often treated for 10–14 days, longer if endovascular or deep‑seated infection. In the reported case, therapy was narrowed to ampicillin once cultures identified C. canimorsus and the patient clinically improved.

Bottom line: start broad empiric IV coverage for bite‑related pathogens immediately in a septic patient—piperacillin–tazobactam (or ampicillin–sulbactam; escalate to meropenem if fulminant)—obtain cultures, then de‑escalate to targeted therapy once C. canimorsus or other pathogens and susceptibilities are known. Timely therapy reduces mortality and may prevent or limit downstream complications such as DIC and infection‑triggered CM‑HUS.

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a septic patient with DIC whose coagulation parameters are improving, what specific clinical and laboratory features should prompt concern for a superimposed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) rather than ongoing DIC?

Direct answer
Be concerned for a superimposed TMA (eg, HUS/CM‑HUS or TTP) when the patient’s global coagulation profile and markers of consumptive coagulopathy are improving but there is new or persistent evidence of microangiopathic haemolysis, thrombocytopenia and end‑organ ischemia that cannot be explained by ongoing DIC. In practice the red flags are a combination of specific clinical and laboratory findings listed below.

Why this distinction matters (brief)
DIC is a consumptive, fibrin‑driven coagulopathy causing prolonged PT/aPTT, low fibrinogen, high D‑dimer, bleeding and fibrin deposition. TMAs are platelet‑rich microvascular thrombotic syndromes driven by endothelial injury and (in CM‑HUS) complement dysregulation; they produce MAHA, schistocytes and organ ischemia (classically kidney and CNS) with relatively preserved routine coagulation tests. Management differs: DIC care is supportive/targeted at the trigger, whereas TMAs may require urgent plasma exchange and/or complement blockade (eculizumab) or targeted treatment for TTP.

Key clinical and laboratory features that should prompt concern for a superimposed TMA
- New or worsening microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) despite resolving sepsis/DIC:
  - Sudden drop in haemoglobin without external bleeding (as in the case: Hb 111 → 68 g/L over 6 days).
  - Rising LDH, undetectable or very low haptoglobin, and increasing indirect bilirubin.
  - Peripheral blood smear with schistocytes (≥1%–2%; in this case 15%–20%).
  - Negative or non‑explanatory DAT (to exclude warm autoimmune hemolysis; initial false positive from penicillin/transfusion may occur).
- Persistent thrombocytopenia out of proportion to residual consumptive coagulopathy:
  - Platelets remain low (eg 30–40 ×10^9/L here) while fibrinogen, PT/aPTT and D‑dimer normalize.
- Worsening or new organ dysfunction typical of TMA:
  - Progressive AKI or oliguria/anuria (this patient was dialysis dependent and remained anuric despite resolving coagulopathy).
  - New neurologic signs (not present in this case but important to watch for).
- Discordance between coagulation tests and haemolysis:
  - Improving PT, aPTT, fibrinogen and D‑dimer but ongoing MAHA and thrombocytopenia strongly suggest TMA rather than ongoing DIC.
- Poor response to therapies aimed at DIC or alternative diagnoses:
  - Failure to respond to transfusions or steroids when autoimmune haemolysis suspected (as occurred here) should prompt re‑evaluation.
- Timing and clinical course:
  - Emergence of haemolysis and TMA features as the septic picture improves (infection is a known trigger for CM‑HUS and often the TMA becomes apparent once overt sepsis/DIC abates, as in this report).

Essential diagnostic steps to confirm/rule out TMA and guide therapy
- Peripheral blood smear for schistocytes (repeat if initial is unrevealing).
- Hemolysis labs: haptoglobin, LDH, bilirubin, reticulocyte count.
- Coagulation panel: PT/INR, aPTT, fibrinogen, D‑dimer (to document improving DIC).
- Direct antiglobulin test (DAT) to exclude autoimmune hemolysis, remembering false positives after certain antibiotics/transfusions.
- ADAMTS13 activity (urgent): severe deficiency (<10%) = TTP → urgent PEX. In this patient ADAMTS13 43.5% made TTP unlikely.
- Complement studies and Shiga‑toxin testing (stool PCR) to distinguish CM‑HUS vs STEC‑HUS; recognize C3/C4 may be normal in CM‑HUS.
- Renal biopsy when diagnosis remains uncertain and safe to perform: histology showing fibrin thrombi, ischemic glomeruli, crescents supports TMA (as in this case).
- Microbiology: ensure bacteremia is controlled before complement blockade when possible.

Immediate management principles (practical)
- While awaiting ADAMTS13, consider urgent plasma exchange if TTP cannot be excluded clinically; PEX may transiently help other TMAs but is definitive for TTP.
- If ADAMTS13 excludes TTP and clinical + biopsy + labs support CM‑HUS, institute complement blockade (eculizumab) promptly once active uncontrolled bacteremia is resolved or adequately treated, because earlier initiation improves renal outcomes (as shown in the case).
- If starting eculizumab, ensure meningococcal vaccination and give appropriate antibiotic prophylaxis when vaccination timing is suboptimal, and monitor closely for infection given the risk with complement blockade.
- Continue to treat the precipitating infection aggressively; in infection‑triggered CM‑HUS, control of infection plus complement inhibition is often necessary.

Takeaway (one‑line)
When routine coagulation parameters normalize in a septic patient but haemolysis, schistocytosis, persistent thrombocytopenia and worsening organ ischemia (especially AKI) emerge or persist, suspect a superimposed TMA—prompt peripheral smear, hemolysis labs, ADAMTS13, complement/STEC testing and early nephrology/hematology input (with biopsy and timely PEX/eculizumab consideration) are indicated.

### [Clinical Pearls] A patient with new hemolysis has a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) for IgG after recent penicillin exposure and transfusions. What mechanisms can cause a false-positive DAT in this setting, and what confirmatory steps help distinguish autoimmune hemolysis from microangiopathic hemolysis?

Direct answer
- False-positive DATs in a patient with recent penicillin exposure and transfusions can result from several non–autoimmune mechanisms: drug-dependent antibody binding or hapten coating (penicillin-type), passive adsorption of donor immunoglobulins or plasma proteins from transfused products, non‑specific adsorption of immunoglobulins or complement during severe inflammation/sepsis, and testing artefacts from high circulating proteins or recent plasma exchange/IV products.  
- To distinguish true warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA) from microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA/TMA), integrate the DAT (including monospecific and eluate testing) with the peripheral smear (schistocytes vs spherocytes), hemolysis markers, response to immunotherapy, and disease‑specific tests (ADAMTS13, complement testing, and tissue biopsy when indicated).

Clinical reasoning and pathophysiology (applied to this case)
1) Why the DAT can be falsely positive after penicillin or transfusion
- Penicillin-type (hapten) mechanism: some β‑lactams can bind to RBC membrane proteins and create a hapten that elicits IgG binding to the drug–RBC complex. This produces a positive DAT without a classical idiopathic warm AIHA; the antibody may be drug‑dependent and disappear when drug exposure ends. In the case report, the patient had recent exposure to phenoxymethylpenicillin and later ampicillin, which can produce this pattern.  
- Passive transfer or adsorption after transfusion/plasma products: transfused plasma or Octaplas may contain immunoglobulins or complement components that coat recipient RBCs transiently, yielding a positive DAT. The patient received multiple blood products and Octaplas, a plausible source of passive coating.  
- Non‑specific adsorption during severe inflammation/sepsis: in sepsis, increased circulating proteins and complement activation can lead to non‑specific Ig or C3 binding to RBCs. This may produce a weak or transient positive DAT that does not reflect pathogenic autoantibodies.  
- Testing and timing effects: recent plasma exchange, IVIG, or high total protein can interfere with DAT reagents and produce false positives. The report notes PEX and Octaplas were used, and the first DAT converted to negative on repeat testing — a pattern consistent with transient, non‑autoimmune coating.

2) How to confirm autoimmune hemolysis versus MAHA (practical, stepwise)
- Peripheral smear morphology: look for schistocytes (fragmented RBCs) — hallmark of MAHA/TMA — versus spherocytes (small, dense RBCs) more typical of warm AIHA. In this case the smear showed 15–20% schistocytes, strongly favouring MAHA.  
- Evaluate hemolysis pattern and marrow response: both AIHA and MAHA have elevated LDH and indirect bilirubin and low haptoglobin, but AIHA usually elicits a brisk reticulocytosis unless marrow is suppressed. The initial lack of reticulocytosis in this patient (during sepsis) complicated interpretation but later undetectable haptoglobin and rising LDH supported active hemolysis.  
- Repeat and refine the DAT:
  - Use monospecific reagents (anti‑IgG vs anti‑C3d). Drug‑hapten reactions often give an IgG‑positive DAT; complement only DAT suggests complement‑mediated processes or immune complex deposition.  
  - Perform eluate testing (acid or heat elution) to recover and identify RBC‑bound antibody specificity. A positive eluate with specificity to red‑cell antigens or drug indicates true pathogenic antibody; a negative eluate despite a positive DAT argues for non‑specific coating. In the case, repeat DAT became negative, arguing against a sustained pathogenic autoantibody.  
  - Drug‑dependent antibody assays: if drug‑induced hemolysis is suspected, test in the presence and absence of the drug to demonstrate drug‑dependent binding.  
- Correlate with clinical course and treatment response:
  - Warm AIHA typically responds to corticosteroids; lack of haemoglobin improvement after high‑dose steroids (as here) makes AIHA less likely.  
  - MAHA/TMA typically does not improve with steroids alone and requires targeted management (PEX bridging, complement blockade if CM‑HUS). This patient did not respond to steroids, platelets remained low, schistocytosis persisted, and kidney biopsy confirmed TMA.  
- Disease‑specific diagnostics:
  - ADAMTS13 activity to rule in/out TTP (severe deficiency <10%). ADAMTS13 was 43.5% here, excluding TTP.  
  - Stool PCR for Shiga toxin to exclude STEC‑HUS (negative here).  
  - Complement testing and renal biopsy: normal C3/C4 do not exclude CM‑HUS; biopsy showing fibrin thrombi and ischaemic injury supported TMA in this case. Extended complement assays and genetics help define CM‑HUS later.  
- Integrate timing and exposures: transient DAT positivity shortly after transfusion or penicillin exposure that becomes negative on repeat testing strongly suggests a non‑autoimmune or drug‑dependent phenomenon. In this report the initial positive IgG DAT that later converted to negative, poor steroid response, and abundant schistocytes pointed away from warm AIHA and toward MAHA from a TMA (ultimately CM‑HUS).

Take‑home clinical pearl
A positive DAT in a septic patient who recently received penicillin or blood products is not definitive proof of warm AIHA. Always correlate the DAT with the peripheral smear (schistocytes vs spherocytes), eluate results, treatment response, and disease‑specific tests (ADAMTS13, stool Shiga toxin, complement studies, and biopsy when indicated). In this case the transient DAT positivity and lack of steroid response, together with marked schistocytosis and biopsy‑proven TMA, distinguished microangiopathic hemolysis (CM‑HUS) from true autoimmune hemolysis.

### [Morning Report] In adults presenting with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, what ADAMTS13 activity threshold effectively rules in thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), how can the PLASMIC score guide urgency, and what immediate therapy should begin while awaiting definitive results?

Key points and clinical approach

1) ADAMTS13 threshold that defines TTP
- Severe ADAMTS13 deficiency — classically <10% activity — is the diagnostic threshold for immune-mediated TTP. Values well above that (for example the 43.5% reported in this case) effectively rule out TTP and prompt evaluation for other TMAs such as HUS/CM‑HUS.

2) How the PLASMIC score guides urgency
- The PLASMIC score is a validated bedside pretest tool to estimate the probability of severe ADAMTS13 deficiency and to triage therapy while awaiting the assay.
  - High risk (PLASMIC 6–7): high pretest probability of TTP → treat empirically as TTP without delay.
  - Intermediate risk (PLASMIC 5): consider early treatment and urgent ADAMTS13 testing; clinical judgment required.
  - Low risk (PLASMIC ≤4): low probability of TTP → less likely to need immediate TTP-directed therapy; continue diagnostic workup for other causes of TMA.
- In the presented case the PLASMIC score was 4/7 (low pretest probability), which corresponded with an ADAMTS13 of 43.5% and appropriately shifted the team away from TTP as the cause.

3) Immediate therapy to start while awaiting definitive results
- If clinical suspicion for TTP is high (especially PLASMIC 6–7 or rapidly progressive MAHA with severe thrombocytopenia and end‑organ ischemia), begin urgent plasma exchange (PEX) plus high‑dose corticosteroids immediately and involve hematology. Consider adding caplacizumab (anti‑vWF nanobody) early when available for immune TTP to inhibit platelet‑vWF aggregation while awaiting ADAMTS13 confirmation.
- If suspicion is low (PLASMIC ≤4), focus the workup on alternative TMAs (STEC PCR, complement studies, ADAMTS13) but still monitor closely; PEX may be used as a bridging measure in unstable patients or when diagnosis is uncertain.
- Do not give platelet transfusions for thrombocytopenia unless there is life‑threatening bleeding or need for an invasive procedure; transfusions can worsen microthrombosis in TTP/HUS.
- In this case team prudently initiated PEX while awaiting ADAMTS13 and complement results — a reasonable bridge given ongoing haemolysis and thrombocytopenia — then stopped pursuing TTP when ADAMTS13 was 43.5% and proceeded to treat CM‑HUS with complement blockade (eculizumab) after infection control and vaccination.

Bottom line: ADAMTS13 activity <10% diagnoses TTP. Use the PLASMIC score to decide how aggressively to treat empirically: high scores mandate immediate PEX ± steroids and caplacizumab; low scores make TTP unlikely and prompt evaluation for alternative TMAs. When TTP is possible, begin PEX and steroids immediately while awaiting ADAMTS13 results; reserve platelet transfusion for bleeding.

### [Pathophysiology] Why do normal C3 and C4 levels not exclude complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS), and how does dysregulation of the alternative pathway explain this laboratory–clinical discrepancy?

Short answer
Normal serum C3 and C4 do not exclude complement‑mediated HUS because CM‑HUS is usually driven by dysregulated activation of the alternative complement pathway at endothelial surfaces (local, cell‑bound activation) rather than by massive, systemic complement consumption that would reliably lower circulating C3/C4. In addition, timing of sampling and effects of treatments (PEX, transfusion, eculizumab) and the biology of the pathways (C4 is not central to the alternative pathway; C3 is an acute‑phase reactant) can mask abnormalities on routine complement testing.

Why this happens — clinical reasoning and pathophysiology
- Pathogenic mechanism: CM‑HUS most commonly reflects failure of surface‑bound regulation of the alternative pathway (eg, defective factor H, factor I, MCP/CD46 or gain‑of‑function in factor B/C3). The alternative pathway undergoes constant low‑level “tick‑over” and is amplified on activator surfaces; when regulation at the endothelial surface is defective, complement is overactivated locally on microvascular endothelium, producing endothelial injury, platelet activation and thrombotic microangiopathy.
- Local versus systemic activation: Because the disease process is focused on the microvasculature, much of the complement activation and fragment deposition occurs at endothelial surfaces and in tissues (kidney glomeruli, arterioles) rather than resulting in large decreases in plasma C3 or C4. Serum levels therefore can remain within reference ranges even while pathogenic complement activity is ongoing in tissues.
- Pathway specificity: C4 is part of the classical and lectin pathways but not required for alternative‑pathway amplification. Many cases of CM‑HUS are driven predominantly by the alternative pathway, so C4 will often be normal. C3 may be consumed, but modest or transient consumption can be masked by acute‑phase synthesis (C3 is an acute‑phase reactant) or by dilution/transfusion/PEX.
- Timing and interventions: Complement levels are time‑dependent. In this case, routine C3/C4 and functional assays were measured early and were normal; extended testing later showed a markedly reduced AH50 (alternative pathway activity) but that result was obtained after plasma exchange and one dose of eculizumab, making interpretation complex. PEX can remove complement proteins and assays can be altered by prior transfusions or complement blockade, so sampling before these interventions is ideal but not always possible in critically ill patients.
- False reassurance from normal routine tests: Routine C3/C4 assays are insensitive to the type of dysregulation in CM‑HUS. A normal result neither rules out ongoing alternative pathway‑mediated endothelial injury nor excludes a genetic predisposition.

Diagnostic implications and standards
- CM‑HUS is a clinicopathological diagnosis of exclusion: correlate MAHA, thrombocytopenia, AKI, biopsy features of TMA, absence of severe ADAMTS13 deficiency and absence of STEC.
- More informative tests: pathway‑specific functional assays (eg, AH50 for alternative pathway), measurement of regulators (factor H, factor I, factor B), testing for anti‑factor H antibodies, and genetic testing for complement regulatory or effector gene variants. Tissue (renal) biopsy showing TMA and a clinical response to eculizumab also support the diagnosis.
- Practical point from the case: this patient had normal routine C3/C4 early, yet developed MAHA, had biopsy‑proven TMA and responded to eculizumab — a classic example that normal complement levels do not exclude CM‑HUS. Later reduced AH50 likely reflected treatment effects (PEX, eculizumab) rather than straightforward depletion from disease.

Takeaway for clinical practice
- Do not use normal C3/C4 to rule out CM‑HUS. If the clinical picture suggests complement‑mediated TMA, pursue pathway‑specific testing, evaluate regulators and genetics, and consider early specialist input and empiric therapy (PEX/anti‑C5) when clinically indicated, recognizing how treatments and timing affect interpretation of complement assays.

### [Clinical Pearls] What classic kidney biopsy findings support a diagnosis of TMA (as in CM-HUS) and help distinguish it from immune complex–mediated glomerulonephritides on light microscopy and immunofluorescence/electron microscopy?

Direct answer
Classic renal biopsy features that support a diagnosis of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) — and help distinguish TMA (as in complement‑mediated HUS) from immune complex–mediated glomerulonephritides — are:

Light microscopy
- Acute TMA: fibrin‑rich thrombi in glomerular capillaries and small arterioles/arteries (microvascular occlusion).  
- Endothelial cell swelling and detachment, capillary lumen narrowing or occlusion, and ischemic collapse of glomerular tufts (sometimes with segmental necrosis or crescents).  
- Acute tubular injury/acute tubular necrosis from cortical ischemia is common.  
- Chronic/organized TMA: subendothelial "onion‑skin" intimal thickening, duplication (double contour) of the glomerular basement membrane (GBM), and intimal fibrosis.

Immunofluorescence (IF)
- Typically negative or shows only nonspecific trapping of IgM and/or C3 in areas of injury.  
- Crucially, IF lacks the granular or linear deposition patterns of immune complexes (eg, granular IgG/C3 along capillary walls and mesangium typical of immune complex GN).

Electron microscopy (EM)
- No electron‑dense immune complex deposits (no subendothelial or subepithelial immune complex deposits that characterize immune complex GN).  
- Acute changes may show endothelial swelling and subendothelial widening with fluffy/electron‑lucent material (reflecting thrombotic/endothelial injury). Chronic TMA can show GBM duplication from repeated endothelial injury.

Why this distinguishes TMA from immune complex GN
- Immune complex GN shows granular (or linear, depending on disease) immune reactants on IF and electron‑dense deposits on EM located in characteristic sites (subendothelial in membranoproliferative/acute proliferative GN; subepithelial “humps” in postinfectious GN). TMA lacks those immune complex signatures and instead shows thrombi, endothelial injury and ischemic changes as the dominant lesion.

Case correlation
- In the presented patient the kidney biopsy demonstrated ischemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi and scattered crescents on light microscopy, immunofluorescence showed no immune complex deposition, and electron microscopy showed no primary glomerulopathy — findings that are classic for TMA and argue strongly against an immune complex–mediated glomerulonephritis. These pathological results, together with the clinical picture of MAHA, thrombocytopenia and schistocytosis, support the diagnosis of complement‑mediated HUS rather than an immune complex GN.

### [Differential Diagnosis] How do you differentiate STEC-HUS from CM-HUS at the bedside and with testing (history, stool PCR, hematologic/renal patterns), and how do these distinctions influence initial management?

Direct answer
- At the bedside, STEC-HUS is suspected when there is a diarrheal prodrome (often bloody) with onset of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia and AKI shortly after the enteric illness; CM‑HUS is suggested when TMA appears without a Shiga-toxin diarrheal prodrome, when an alternative trigger (infection, pregnancy, drugs) precedes TMA, or when TMA develops after systemic infection/sepsis is improving. In practice, timing, exposure history and the clinical course are key discriminators.
- On testing, the single most useful immediate tests are stool Shiga-toxin PCR/culture (to confirm STEC) and ADAMTS13 activity (to exclude TTP). Complement studies (C3/C4, functional assays), anti–factor H antibodies and extended genetics help support CM‑HUS but are not required to make the diagnosis; normal C3/C4 does not exclude CM‑HUS.
- The distinction matters because initial management differs: STEC‑HUS is managed mainly with supportive care (fluid balance, dialysis, transfusions) and antibiotics are generally avoided for Shiga‑toxin E. coli; CM‑HUS is treated with targeted complement blockade (eculizumab) ± bridging plasma exchange, with vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis when eculizumab is used — but in infection‑associated CM‑HUS you balance treating the infection and delaying complement blockade until bacteremia is controlled if possible.

Clinical reasoning and supporting details from the case
1) History and timing
- STEC-HUS classically follows a prodromal diarrheal illness (often bloody) by days. In contrast, this patient had no prodromal enteritis; she had a dog bite followed by fulminant Capnocytophaga canimorsus sepsis. Importantly, the MAHA and thrombocytopenia became most prominent after the septic picture began to improve — a pattern consistent with an infection‑triggered CM‑HUS rather than STEC‑HUS.

2) Microbiologic testing
- Stool PCR for Shiga toxin is the standard bedside/laboratory test to confirm/rule out STEC-HUS. In this case stool PCR was negative, arguing against STEC-HUS.
- Blood cultures grew C. canimorsus, providing a clear alternative trigger for complement dysregulation.

3) Hematologic and renal patterns
- Both STEC-HUS and CM-HUS produce MAHA with schistocytes, elevated LDH, low haptoglobin and thrombocytopenia plus AKI. Thus laboratory overlap requires other data to distinguish them.
- ADAMTS13 activity is used to exclude TTP (severe deficiency <10%). Here ADAMTS13 was 43.5%, effectively ruling out TTP and directing attention to HUS etiologies.
- Complement tests (C3/C4) are often obtained early in suspected CM‑HUS, but normal levels do not exclude it. In this patient basic complement tests were normal, yet clinical course, kidney biopsy showing TMA, poor renal function and response to complement blockade supported CM‑HUS. Extended functional testing later showed a reduced alternative pathway (likely influenced by plasma exchange and eculizumab), illustrating that timing of complement assays and treatments affects interpretation.
- Renal course: severe AKI with dialysis dependence and biopsy showing fibrin thrombi/ischaemic glomeruli point to a severe TMA phenotype that often prompts consideration of CM‑HUS and urgent therapy.

4) Therapeutic implications and timing
- STEC‑HUS: primarily supportive (fluid management, careful transfusion, dialysis as needed). Antibiotics for STEC are controversial because some antibiotics may increase Shiga toxin release and worsen outcome; therefore, empiric antibiotics are not standard for suspected STEC.
- CM‑HUS: eculizumab (anti‑C5) is the targeted therapy and improves hematologic parameters and renal outcome when started early. Plasma exchange (PEX) is not curative for CM‑HUS but can be used as a bridge while awaiting confirmatory tests or if autoantibodies are suspected; it is lifesaving in TTP but only temporary in CM‑HUS.
- Infection‑associated CM‑HUS (as in this case) raises a management dilemma: terminal complement blockade increases risk of overwhelming infection with encapsulated organisms. In this patient team waited until blood cultures were negative, gave meningococcal vaccines and prophylactic antibiotic coverage, and used PEX as a bridge — then started eculizumab, after which sustained hematologic and renal improvement occurred. This sequence illustrates practical balancing of risks: treat the active infection first when feasible, but avoid undue delay in complement blockade if organ failure is progressing.

Practical bedside approach (algorithmic)
1. Rapid bedside assessment
   - Ask about diarrheal prodrome, animal exposures, recent drugs/pregnancy.
   - Check CBC with smear (schistocytes), LDH, haptoglobin, creatinine, urinalysis, coagulation panel.

2. Simultaneous urgent tests
   - ADAMTS13 activity (to rule out TTP).
   - Stool Shiga‑toxin PCR/culture (for STEC).
   - Blood cultures if sepsis suspected.
   - Basic complement tests (C3/C4) and send samples for extended complement testing/anti‑factor H if CM‑HUS suspected (note results may be normal early).

3. Initial management while awaiting results
   - Supportive care: fluid balance, dialysis if needed, transfuse PRBCs/platelets only for bleeding or procedures.
   - If TTP suspected (high pretest probability or ADAMTS13 not immediately available): start urgent PEX.
   - If STEC strongly suspected: avoid antibiotics generally; provide supportive care.
   - If CM‑HUS suspected and organ failure progressing: consider PEX as bridge and plan prompt initiation of eculizumab — but if active bacteremia present, treat infection first, obtain cultures, consider delaying eculizumab until infection is controlled and give vaccines/prophylactic antibiotics as appropriate.

Bottom line
- Use history (diarrhea vs other triggers), stool Shiga PCR, ADAMTS13 activity and the temporal relation between infection and TMA to distinguish STEC‑HUS from CM‑HUS. Normal complement levels do not exclude CM‑HUS. Management diverges: STEC is supportive; CM‑HUS requires complement blockade (eculizumab) often with PEX as a bridge — but in infection‑triggered CM‑HUS balance between infection control and timely complement inhibition is crucial, as illustrated by this patient whose TMA improved only after eculizumab was started once bacteremia was controlled.

### [Management] Outline the roles of plasma exchange (PEX) versus eculizumab in TTP and CM-HUS: when is PEX lifesaving, when is its benefit transient, and why does terminal complement blockade improve outcomes in CM-HUS?

Answer

Key distinction (clinical and therapeutic)
- TTP is driven by severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (usually <10%) with accumulation of ultra‑large von Willebrand multimers that produce widespread platelet microthrombi. Plasma exchange (PEX) is lifesaving in TTP because it rapidly supplies functional ADAMTS13 and removes pathogenic anti‑ADAMTS13 autoantibodies and prothrombotic multimers.
- Complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS) is driven by uncontrolled activation of the alternative complement pathway at the endothelial surface, producing terminal complement effectors that injure endothelium, trigger platelet activation and form thrombotic microangiopathy. Terminal complement blockade with eculizumab (anti‑C5) stops formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC) and downstream endothelial injury; this addresses the pathogenic mechanism and improves haematologic and renal outcomes.

When PEX is lifesaving
- In suspected TTP PEX should be started immediately (before ADAMTS13 results) because TTP progresses rapidly and mortality is high without prompt exchange. PEX removes the circulating autoantibody and replenishes ADAMTS13, reversing the process in most patients.
- The clinical trigger to start PEX is an adult with MAHA + severe thrombocytopenia ± neurologic symptoms ± fever ± renal injury when pretest probability for TTP is high (eg, PLASMIC score), even while awaiting ADAMTS13. In TTP, PEX is disease‑directed and often definitive when combined with immunosuppression.

When PEX benefit is transient (and why)
- In CM‑HUS PEX may transiently improve laboratory or clinical parameters by diluting circulating complement activation fragments, inflammatory mediators, or autoantibodies, and by transiently supplying complement regulators. However, PEX does not correct the underlying dysregulation of the alternative pathway (genetic deficiency, gain‑of‑function, or sustained trigger), so any benefit is usually short lived.
- This case illustrates that pattern: the patient had improving diuresis after several PEX sessions (transient stabilization) but sustained haematologic remission and renal recovery only after eculizumab was started. ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5% (not consistent with TTP), making the PEX effect mechanistic but not corrective.

Why terminal complement blockade (eculizumab) improves outcomes in CM‑HUS
- Eculizumab binds C5 and prevents its cleavage to C5a and C5b, blocking generation of C5b‑9 (MAC). That halts the terminal effector arm causing endothelial injury, microvascular thrombosis and red‑cell fragmentation.
- By stopping ongoing complement‑mediated endothelial damage, eculizumab allows platelet counts to recover, haemolysis to stop, and renal microcirculatory injury to stabilize—improving both haematologic indices and renal outcomes. Earlier initiation is associated with better renal recovery; delayed therapy risks irreversible kidney injury.

Practical and safety considerations (drawn from the case)
- Distinguish TTP from CM‑HUS rapidly: measure ADAMTS13 (severe deficiency <10% strongly favors TTP). If TTP unlikely (as here: ADAMTS13 43.5%), shift therapeutic focus to complement‑mediated causes.
- In infection‑triggered CM‑HUS there is a dilemma: complement blockade may impair host defence against encapsulated organisms. In this patient eculizumab was started after blood cultures were negative and after meningococcal vaccination (and with antibiotic prophylaxis), reducing infectious risk—an approach commonly used when urgent blockade is needed.
- PEX can be used as a bridge while diagnostics are pending or until safe to initiate complement blockade, but expect transient effects unless eculizumab is given to address the underlying complement activation.
- Vaccinate against Neisseria (and consider prophylactic antibiotics) before or when starting eculizumab; monitor carefully for recurrent infection.

Bottom line
- Start PEX emergently when TTP is strongly suspected because it is lifesaving and disease‑specific. In CM‑HUS PEX may help temporarily but does not treat the underlying complement dysregulation. Eculizumab, by blocking terminal complement (C5 → C5b‑9), targets the fundamental mechanism of CM‑HUS and produces durable haematologic and renal improvement, as demonstrated in this case where PEX produced transient gains but sustained recovery followed complement blockade.

### [Management] In CM-HUS triggered by bacteremia, when should eculizumab be initiated relative to infection control, what vaccines and antibiotic prophylaxis are required, and which pathogens pose heightened risk during complement blockade?

Short answer
- Start eculizumab for infection‑triggered CM‑HUS as soon as the TMA diagnosis is secure and the benefit outweighs the infectious risk — ideally after active bacteraemia is controlled (blood cultures negative and the patient clinically improving). If TMA is rapidly progressive and life‑ or kidney‑threatening, do not delay complement blockade: treat the infection aggressively (appropriate antibiotics ± PEX) and start eculizumab while taking measures to mitigate infection risk.
- Before or at initiation, give meningococcal vaccination (quadrivalent A/C/Y/W and serogroup B) and provide immediate antibiotic prophylaxis if vaccination cannot be completed ≥2 weeks before complement blockade.
- Complement blockade markedly increases risk from encapsulated organisms — especially Neisseria meningitidis — and also raises concern for other capsulated gram‑negative and gram‑positive pathogens (eg, Capnocytophaga canimorsus, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae). Close monitoring and prompt empiric antibiotics for fever are mandatory.

Why — clinical reasoning and evidence from the case
- Rationale: Eculizumab (anti‑C5) prevents terminal complement–mediated endothelial injury and is the standard therapy for CM‑HUS; earlier initiation is associated with better hematologic and renal recovery. But terminal complement is essential for killing encapsulated organisms, so blockade increases the risk of fulminant infections, particularly meningococcal disease.
- Timing in practice: In the reported case the team waited until the bacteremia was controlled (daily clinical improvement and negative blood cultures for Capnocytophaga canimorsus) and administered meningococcal vaccines before eculizumab. They gave antibiotic prophylaxis (phenoxymethylpenicillin/“Apocillin”) because the vaccines had not been given ≥2 weeks earlier. After these precautions they started induction eculizumab and achieved sustained hematologic remission and partial renal recovery. This illustrates the recommended compromise: control the infection when possible, vaccinate, provide short‑term antibiotic cover if vaccines are recent or absent, and then institute complement blockade.
- When to not delay: If the TMA is fulminant and delay will cause irreversible organ damage (rapidly worsening anemia, thrombocytopenia, dialysis dependence), the balance can favour immediate eculizumab while continuing appropriate antimicrobial therapy and using PEX as a bridge — with contemporaneous vaccination and prophylactic antibiotics.

Vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis — practical points
- Mandatory vaccines before or at start:
  - Meningococcal quadrivalent (A, C, W, Y) and meningococcal B (MenB). In the case they gave Menveo (A/C/Y/W) and Bexsero (B).
  - Consider pneumococcal vaccination (PCV followed by PPSV23 per local schedule) and Haemophilus influenzae type b vaccine if not previously immunised, particularly in patients at increased risk of encapsulated infections.
  - Seasonal influenza vaccine as appropriate.
- If vaccination cannot be completed ≥2 weeks before eculizumab, give immediate antibiotic prophylaxis and continue until adequate immune response is expected (commonly at least 2 weeks after vaccination). In the case report, prophylactic phenoxymethylpenicillin was used because vaccination had not been given 2 weeks prior.
- Choice of prophylaxis:
  - Oral penicillin V or amoxicillin is commonly used (phenoxymethylpenicillin in the case). For penicillin allergy, alternatives include ciprofloxacin or azithromycin guided by local resistance.
  - Duration: short‑term until 2 weeks post‑vaccination response; some centers use longer or indefinite prophylaxis for select high‑risk patients (eg, asplenia, recurrent meningococcal disease, ongoing complement inhibitor therapy without adequate vaccination response) — tailor to risk and local guidance.
- Counseling: patients must be educated to seek urgent medical care for fever or signs of sepsis despite prophylaxis/vaccination; clinicians should have a low threshold to empirically start broad‑spectrum antibiotics.

Which pathogens are highest risk on complement blockade
- Highest: Neisseria meningitidis (all serogroups). Most feared complication is fulminant meningococcemia; meningococcal infection risk is substantially increased with eculizumab.
- Also important: encapsulated bacteria in general — Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae — and encapsulated gram‑negative organisms (Capnocytophaga species are encapsulated and were the inciting organism in this case). Eculizumab can impair clearance of these organisms and has been associated with severe infections beyond Neisseria.
- Less common but relevant: other Neisseria species and some unusual encapsulated or complement‑sensitive organisms; vigilance for unusual pathogens is warranted.

Bottom line
- Aim to control active infection and obtain negative cultures before starting eculizumab when feasible. If TMA is life‑threatening, begin eculizumab without undue delay but concurrently treat the infection aggressively, vaccinate as soon as possible, and give immediate antibiotic prophylaxis until vaccine protection is established. Educate the patient and maintain close infectious surveillance because complement blockade predisposes most strongly to meningococcal disease and other infections by encapsulated organisms (including the Capnocytophaga that triggered disease in this patient).

### [Clinical Pearls] What clinical features, risk factors, and diagnostic challenges are characteristic of Capnocytophaga canimorsus infection after dog exposure, and why might diagnosis be delayed despite severe presentations such as sepsis, DIC, and TMA?

Clinical pearl — Capnocytophaga canimorsus after dog exposure: what to look for, who is at risk, and why diagnosis is often delayed

Key clinical features
- Highly variable presentation: ranges from minor local infection to fulminant sepsis with multiorgan failure. In this case the patient developed septic shock physiology, refractory hypoglycaemia, marked systemic inflammation, pancytopenia, coagulopathy (DIC) and rapidly progressive AKI requiring dialysis.
- Early signs may be non‑specific: fever, malaise, skin mottling, hypoglycaemia and leukopaenia can dominate and point clinicians toward overwhelming sepsis rather than a zoonotic pathogen.
- Later complications may emerge after initial sepsis control: new‑onset microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), persistent thrombocytopenia and worsening renal failure should raise concern for superimposed TMA/CM‑HUS, as occurred when this patient developed schistocytes, undetectable haptoglobin and rising LDH after apparent improvement in inflammatory markers.

Risk factors (classically and in practice)
- Classic predispositions: splenectomy, alcoholism, chronic liver disease, immunosuppression increase risk of severe C. canimorsus disease.
- Important caveat from this and other reports: severe, invasive disease can occur in otherwise healthy, immunocompetent adults after bites, scratches or close contact with dogs/cats. Thus absence of comorbidity does not exclude the diagnosis.
- Microbial factors: encapsulated or complement‑resistant strains can evade host defence and produce more severe disease; complement activation by the organism can paradoxically precipitate endothelial injury and TMA in susceptible hosts.

Diagnostic challenges that delay recognition
1. Fastidious, slow‑growing organism
   - C. canimorsus often grows slowly and may require prolonged culture or special methods; routine cultures can be negative or take days. In the case, clinical suspicion was present but confirmation took 7 days by blood culture. This delay is common and spurs increased use of molecular methods (eg, real‑time sequencing) when available.

2. Overlap with common, immediately apparent syndromes (sepsis/DIC)
   - Early sepsis and DIC produce fever, coagulopathy, thrombocytopenia, organ dysfunction and schistocyte‑poor peripheral smears; these findings direct attention toward managing sepsis and DIC and can mask a developing TMA. In this patient the initial picture was judged to be DIC from bacteraemia, and only later did schistocytes and frank MAHA appear.

3. Laboratory pitfalls and timing of abnormalities
   - Complement tests (C3/C4) may be normal in CM‑HUS; normal levels do not exclude complement‑mediated disease. In this case C3/C4 were within reference ranges even as TMA was developing.
   - ADAMTS13 testing and other specialized assays are required to separate TTP from other TMAs; results take time. Empiric bridging (PEX) is often used while awaiting results.
   - Hemolysis/TMA markers may appear late—DAT may be falsely positive after penicillin exposure or transfusions, reticulocytosis may be blunted early, and schistocytes can be missed unless specifically sought and quantified.

4. Therapeutic dilemma complicates timing of definitive therapy
   - Complement blockade (eculizumab) is the effective therapy for CM‑HUS but carries infection risks during active bacteraemia; clinicians may defer it until cultures are negative, further delaying definitive treatment. In this case eculizumab was started only after blood cultures were negative and vaccinations/prophylaxis addressed.

Practical takeaways for the clinical team
- Always elicit recent animal exposure in patients with sepsis, especially bites/scratches or close contact; early suspicion permits targeted empiric coverage (eg, beta‑lactam agents active against Capnocytophaga) and alerts lab to prolong cultures or use molecular diagnostics.
- Reassess diagnosis over time: persistent or new thrombocytopenia, rising LDH, falling hemoglobin without bleeding, and appearance of schistocytes should prompt immediate consideration of TMA even if an initial interpretation favored DIC.
- Order the right tests and interpret them in context: send blood cultures (and consider molecular methods if available), obtain ADAMTS13, serial peripheral smears, hemolysis labs, and complement studies—but remember that normal C3/C4 do not exclude CM‑HUS.
- Balance risks when treating: start appropriate antibiotics early; use PEX pragmatically while excluding TTP; consider eculizumab promptly once active bacteremia is controlled and after appropriate vaccination/prophylaxis, because delay in complement blockade is associated with worse renal outcomes.

In short: C. canimorsus produces a protean early sepsis picture and is slow to culture, and TMA/CM‑HUS may evolve later or be masked by DIC. Maintain a high index of suspicion after dog exposure, repeat targeted hematologic and peripheral smear assessments, and escalate diagnostic and therapeutic steps quickly when MAHA/TMA emerges.
