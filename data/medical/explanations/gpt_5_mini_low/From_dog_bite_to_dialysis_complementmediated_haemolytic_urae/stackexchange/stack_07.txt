Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Differential Diagnosis] How do you differentiate STEC-HUS from CM-HUS at the bedside and with testing (history, stool PCR, hematologic/renal patterns), and how do these distinctions influence initial management?

Direct answer
- At the bedside, STEC-HUS is suspected when there is a diarrheal prodrome (often bloody) with onset of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia and AKI shortly after the enteric illness; CM‑HUS is suggested when TMA appears without a Shiga-toxin diarrheal prodrome, when an alternative trigger (infection, pregnancy, drugs) precedes TMA, or when TMA develops after systemic infection/sepsis is improving. In practice, timing, exposure history and the clinical course are key discriminators.
- On testing, the single most useful immediate tests are stool Shiga-toxin PCR/culture (to confirm STEC) and ADAMTS13 activity (to exclude TTP). Complement studies (C3/C4, functional assays), anti–factor H antibodies and extended genetics help support CM‑HUS but are not required to make the diagnosis; normal C3/C4 does not exclude CM‑HUS.
- The distinction matters because initial management differs: STEC‑HUS is managed mainly with supportive care (fluid balance, dialysis, transfusions) and antibiotics are generally avoided for Shiga‑toxin E. coli; CM‑HUS is treated with targeted complement blockade (eculizumab) ± bridging plasma exchange, with vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis when eculizumab is used — but in infection‑associated CM‑HUS you balance treating the infection and delaying complement blockade until bacteremia is controlled if possible.

Clinical reasoning and supporting details from the case
1) History and timing
- STEC-HUS classically follows a prodromal diarrheal illness (often bloody) by days. In contrast, this patient had no prodromal enteritis; she had a dog bite followed by fulminant Capnocytophaga canimorsus sepsis. Importantly, the MAHA and thrombocytopenia became most prominent after the septic picture began to improve — a pattern consistent with an infection‑triggered CM‑HUS rather than STEC‑HUS.

2) Microbiologic testing
- Stool PCR for Shiga toxin is the standard bedside/laboratory test to confirm/rule out STEC-HUS. In this case stool PCR was negative, arguing against STEC-HUS.
- Blood cultures grew C. canimorsus, providing a clear alternative trigger for complement dysregulation.

3) Hematologic and renal patterns
- Both STEC-HUS and CM-HUS produce MAHA with schistocytes, elevated LDH, low haptoglobin and thrombocytopenia plus AKI. Thus laboratory overlap requires other data to distinguish them.
- ADAMTS13 activity is used to exclude TTP (severe deficiency <10%). Here ADAMTS13 was 43.5%, effectively ruling out TTP and directing attention to HUS etiologies.
- Complement tests (C3/C4) are often obtained early in suspected CM‑HUS, but normal levels do not exclude it. In this patient basic complement tests were normal, yet clinical course, kidney biopsy showing TMA, poor renal function and response to complement blockade supported CM‑HUS. Extended functional testing later showed a reduced alternative pathway (likely influenced by plasma exchange and eculizumab), illustrating that timing of complement assays and treatments affects interpretation.
- Renal course: severe AKI with dialysis dependence and biopsy showing fibrin thrombi/ischaemic glomeruli point to a severe TMA phenotype that often prompts consideration of CM‑HUS and urgent therapy.

4) Therapeutic implications and timing
- STEC‑HUS: primarily supportive (fluid management, careful transfusion, dialysis as needed). Antibiotics for STEC are controversial because some antibiotics may increase Shiga toxin release and worsen outcome; therefore, empiric antibiotics are not standard for suspected STEC.
- CM‑HUS: eculizumab (anti‑C5) is the targeted therapy and improves hematologic parameters and renal outcome when started early. Plasma exchange (PEX) is not curative for CM‑HUS but can be used as a bridge while awaiting confirmatory tests or if autoantibodies are suspected; it is lifesaving in TTP but only temporary in CM‑HUS.
- Infection‑associated CM‑HUS (as in this case) raises a management dilemma: terminal complement blockade increases risk of overwhelming infection with encapsulated organisms. In this patient team waited until blood cultures were negative, gave meningococcal vaccines and prophylactic antibiotic coverage, and used PEX as a bridge — then started eculizumab, after which sustained hematologic and renal improvement occurred. This sequence illustrates practical balancing of risks: treat the active infection first when feasible, but avoid undue delay in complement blockade if organ failure is progressing.

Practical bedside approach (algorithmic)
1. Rapid bedside assessment
   - Ask about diarrheal prodrome, animal exposures, recent drugs/pregnancy.
   - Check CBC with smear (schistocytes), LDH, haptoglobin, creatinine, urinalysis, coagulation panel.

2. Simultaneous urgent tests
   - ADAMTS13 activity (to rule out TTP).
   - Stool Shiga‑toxin PCR/culture (for STEC).
   - Blood cultures if sepsis suspected.
   - Basic complement tests (C3/C4) and send samples for extended complement testing/anti‑factor H if CM‑HUS suspected (note results may be normal early).

3. Initial management while awaiting results
   - Supportive care: fluid balance, dialysis if needed, transfuse PRBCs/platelets only for bleeding or procedures.
   - If TTP suspected (high pretest probability or ADAMTS13 not immediately available): start urgent PEX.
   - If STEC strongly suspected: avoid antibiotics generally; provide supportive care.
   - If CM‑HUS suspected and organ failure progressing: consider PEX as bridge and plan prompt initiation of eculizumab — but if active bacteremia present, treat infection first, obtain cultures, consider delaying eculizumab until infection is controlled and give vaccines/prophylactic antibiotics as appropriate.

Bottom line
- Use history (diarrhea vs other triggers), stool Shiga PCR, ADAMTS13 activity and the temporal relation between infection and TMA to distinguish STEC‑HUS from CM‑HUS. Normal complement levels do not exclude CM‑HUS. Management diverges: STEC is supportive; CM‑HUS requires complement blockade (eculizumab) often with PEX as a bridge — but in infection‑triggered CM‑HUS balance between infection control and timely complement inhibition is crucial, as illustrated by this patient whose TMA improved only after eculizumab was started once bacteremia was controlled.
