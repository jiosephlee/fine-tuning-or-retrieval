Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a septic patient with DIC whose coagulation parameters are improving, what specific clinical and laboratory features should prompt concern for a superimposed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) rather than ongoing DIC?

Direct answer
Be concerned for a superimposed TMA (eg, HUS/CM‑HUS or TTP) when the patient’s global coagulation profile and markers of consumptive coagulopathy are improving but there is new or persistent evidence of microangiopathic haemolysis, thrombocytopenia and end‑organ ischemia that cannot be explained by ongoing DIC. In practice the red flags are a combination of specific clinical and laboratory findings listed below.

Why this distinction matters (brief)
DIC is a consumptive, fibrin‑driven coagulopathy causing prolonged PT/aPTT, low fibrinogen, high D‑dimer, bleeding and fibrin deposition. TMAs are platelet‑rich microvascular thrombotic syndromes driven by endothelial injury and (in CM‑HUS) complement dysregulation; they produce MAHA, schistocytes and organ ischemia (classically kidney and CNS) with relatively preserved routine coagulation tests. Management differs: DIC care is supportive/targeted at the trigger, whereas TMAs may require urgent plasma exchange and/or complement blockade (eculizumab) or targeted treatment for TTP.

Key clinical and laboratory features that should prompt concern for a superimposed TMA
- New or worsening microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) despite resolving sepsis/DIC:
  - Sudden drop in haemoglobin without external bleeding (as in the case: Hb 111 → 68 g/L over 6 days).
  - Rising LDH, undetectable or very low haptoglobin, and increasing indirect bilirubin.
  - Peripheral blood smear with schistocytes (≥1%–2%; in this case 15%–20%).
  - Negative or non‑explanatory DAT (to exclude warm autoimmune hemolysis; initial false positive from penicillin/transfusion may occur).
- Persistent thrombocytopenia out of proportion to residual consumptive coagulopathy:
  - Platelets remain low (eg 30–40 ×10^9/L here) while fibrinogen, PT/aPTT and D‑dimer normalize.
- Worsening or new organ dysfunction typical of TMA:
  - Progressive AKI or oliguria/anuria (this patient was dialysis dependent and remained anuric despite resolving coagulopathy).
  - New neurologic signs (not present in this case but important to watch for).
- Discordance between coagulation tests and haemolysis:
  - Improving PT, aPTT, fibrinogen and D‑dimer but ongoing MAHA and thrombocytopenia strongly suggest TMA rather than ongoing DIC.
- Poor response to therapies aimed at DIC or alternative diagnoses:
  - Failure to respond to transfusions or steroids when autoimmune haemolysis suspected (as occurred here) should prompt re‑evaluation.
- Timing and clinical course:
  - Emergence of haemolysis and TMA features as the septic picture improves (infection is a known trigger for CM‑HUS and often the TMA becomes apparent once overt sepsis/DIC abates, as in this report).

Essential diagnostic steps to confirm/rule out TMA and guide therapy
- Peripheral blood smear for schistocytes (repeat if initial is unrevealing).
- Hemolysis labs: haptoglobin, LDH, bilirubin, reticulocyte count.
- Coagulation panel: PT/INR, aPTT, fibrinogen, D‑dimer (to document improving DIC).
- Direct antiglobulin test (DAT) to exclude autoimmune hemolysis, remembering false positives after certain antibiotics/transfusions.
- ADAMTS13 activity (urgent): severe deficiency (<10%) = TTP → urgent PEX. In this patient ADAMTS13 43.5% made TTP unlikely.
- Complement studies and Shiga‑toxin testing (stool PCR) to distinguish CM‑HUS vs STEC‑HUS; recognize C3/C4 may be normal in CM‑HUS.
- Renal biopsy when diagnosis remains uncertain and safe to perform: histology showing fibrin thrombi, ischemic glomeruli, crescents supports TMA (as in this case).
- Microbiology: ensure bacteremia is controlled before complement blockade when possible.

Immediate management principles (practical)
- While awaiting ADAMTS13, consider urgent plasma exchange if TTP cannot be excluded clinically; PEX may transiently help other TMAs but is definitive for TTP.
- If ADAMTS13 excludes TTP and clinical + biopsy + labs support CM‑HUS, institute complement blockade (eculizumab) promptly once active uncontrolled bacteremia is resolved or adequately treated, because earlier initiation improves renal outcomes (as shown in the case).
- If starting eculizumab, ensure meningococcal vaccination and give appropriate antibiotic prophylaxis when vaccination timing is suboptimal, and monitor closely for infection given the risk with complement blockade.
- Continue to treat the precipitating infection aggressively; in infection‑triggered CM‑HUS, control of infection plus complement inhibition is often necessary.

Takeaway (one‑line)
When routine coagulation parameters normalize in a septic patient but haemolysis, schistocytosis, persistent thrombocytopenia and worsening organ ischemia (especially AKI) emerge or persist, suspect a superimposed TMA—prompt peripheral smear, hemolysis labs, ADAMTS13, complement/STEC testing and early nephrology/hematology input (with biopsy and timely PEX/eculizumab consideration) are indicated.
