Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Clinical Pearls] What classic kidney biopsy findings support a diagnosis of TMA (as in CM-HUS) and help distinguish it from immune complex–mediated glomerulonephritides on light microscopy and immunofluorescence/electron microscopy?

Direct answer
Classic renal biopsy features that support a diagnosis of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) — and help distinguish TMA (as in complement‑mediated HUS) from immune complex–mediated glomerulonephritides — are:

Light microscopy
- Acute TMA: fibrin‑rich thrombi in glomerular capillaries and small arterioles/arteries (microvascular occlusion).  
- Endothelial cell swelling and detachment, capillary lumen narrowing or occlusion, and ischemic collapse of glomerular tufts (sometimes with segmental necrosis or crescents).  
- Acute tubular injury/acute tubular necrosis from cortical ischemia is common.  
- Chronic/organized TMA: subendothelial "onion‑skin" intimal thickening, duplication (double contour) of the glomerular basement membrane (GBM), and intimal fibrosis.

Immunofluorescence (IF)
- Typically negative or shows only nonspecific trapping of IgM and/or C3 in areas of injury.  
- Crucially, IF lacks the granular or linear deposition patterns of immune complexes (eg, granular IgG/C3 along capillary walls and mesangium typical of immune complex GN).

Electron microscopy (EM)
- No electron‑dense immune complex deposits (no subendothelial or subepithelial immune complex deposits that characterize immune complex GN).  
- Acute changes may show endothelial swelling and subendothelial widening with fluffy/electron‑lucent material (reflecting thrombotic/endothelial injury). Chronic TMA can show GBM duplication from repeated endothelial injury.

Why this distinguishes TMA from immune complex GN
- Immune complex GN shows granular (or linear, depending on disease) immune reactants on IF and electron‑dense deposits on EM located in characteristic sites (subendothelial in membranoproliferative/acute proliferative GN; subepithelial “humps” in postinfectious GN). TMA lacks those immune complex signatures and instead shows thrombi, endothelial injury and ischemic changes as the dominant lesion.

Case correlation
- In the presented patient the kidney biopsy demonstrated ischemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi and scattered crescents on light microscopy, immunofluorescence showed no immune complex deposition, and electron microscopy showed no primary glomerulopathy — findings that are classic for TMA and argue strongly against an immune complex–mediated glomerulonephritis. These pathological results, together with the clinical picture of MAHA, thrombocytopenia and schistocytosis, support the diagnosis of complement‑mediated HUS rather than an immune complex GN.
