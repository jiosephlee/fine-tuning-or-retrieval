Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Clinical Pearls] What clinical features, risk factors, and diagnostic challenges are characteristic of Capnocytophaga canimorsus infection after dog exposure, and why might diagnosis be delayed despite severe presentations such as sepsis, DIC, and TMA?

Clinical pearl — Capnocytophaga canimorsus after dog exposure: what to look for, who is at risk, and why diagnosis is often delayed

Key clinical features
- Highly variable presentation: ranges from minor local infection to fulminant sepsis with multiorgan failure. In this case the patient developed septic shock physiology, refractory hypoglycaemia, marked systemic inflammation, pancytopenia, coagulopathy (DIC) and rapidly progressive AKI requiring dialysis.
- Early signs may be non‑specific: fever, malaise, skin mottling, hypoglycaemia and leukopaenia can dominate and point clinicians toward overwhelming sepsis rather than a zoonotic pathogen.
- Later complications may emerge after initial sepsis control: new‑onset microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), persistent thrombocytopenia and worsening renal failure should raise concern for superimposed TMA/CM‑HUS, as occurred when this patient developed schistocytes, undetectable haptoglobin and rising LDH after apparent improvement in inflammatory markers.

Risk factors (classically and in practice)
- Classic predispositions: splenectomy, alcoholism, chronic liver disease, immunosuppression increase risk of severe C. canimorsus disease.
- Important caveat from this and other reports: severe, invasive disease can occur in otherwise healthy, immunocompetent adults after bites, scratches or close contact with dogs/cats. Thus absence of comorbidity does not exclude the diagnosis.
- Microbial factors: encapsulated or complement‑resistant strains can evade host defence and produce more severe disease; complement activation by the organism can paradoxically precipitate endothelial injury and TMA in susceptible hosts.

Diagnostic challenges that delay recognition
1. Fastidious, slow‑growing organism
   - C. canimorsus often grows slowly and may require prolonged culture or special methods; routine cultures can be negative or take days. In the case, clinical suspicion was present but confirmation took 7 days by blood culture. This delay is common and spurs increased use of molecular methods (eg, real‑time sequencing) when available.

2. Overlap with common, immediately apparent syndromes (sepsis/DIC)
   - Early sepsis and DIC produce fever, coagulopathy, thrombocytopenia, organ dysfunction and schistocyte‑poor peripheral smears; these findings direct attention toward managing sepsis and DIC and can mask a developing TMA. In this patient the initial picture was judged to be DIC from bacteraemia, and only later did schistocytes and frank MAHA appear.

3. Laboratory pitfalls and timing of abnormalities
   - Complement tests (C3/C4) may be normal in CM‑HUS; normal levels do not exclude complement‑mediated disease. In this case C3/C4 were within reference ranges even as TMA was developing.
   - ADAMTS13 testing and other specialized assays are required to separate TTP from other TMAs; results take time. Empiric bridging (PEX) is often used while awaiting results.
   - Hemolysis/TMA markers may appear late—DAT may be falsely positive after penicillin exposure or transfusions, reticulocytosis may be blunted early, and schistocytes can be missed unless specifically sought and quantified.

4. Therapeutic dilemma complicates timing of definitive therapy
   - Complement blockade (eculizumab) is the effective therapy for CM‑HUS but carries infection risks during active bacteraemia; clinicians may defer it until cultures are negative, further delaying definitive treatment. In this case eculizumab was started only after blood cultures were negative and vaccinations/prophylaxis addressed.

Practical takeaways for the clinical team
- Always elicit recent animal exposure in patients with sepsis, especially bites/scratches or close contact; early suspicion permits targeted empiric coverage (eg, beta‑lactam agents active against Capnocytophaga) and alerts lab to prolong cultures or use molecular diagnostics.
- Reassess diagnosis over time: persistent or new thrombocytopenia, rising LDH, falling hemoglobin without bleeding, and appearance of schistocytes should prompt immediate consideration of TMA even if an initial interpretation favored DIC.
- Order the right tests and interpret them in context: send blood cultures (and consider molecular methods if available), obtain ADAMTS13, serial peripheral smears, hemolysis labs, and complement studies—but remember that normal C3/C4 do not exclude CM‑HUS.
- Balance risks when treating: start appropriate antibiotics early; use PEX pragmatically while excluding TTP; consider eculizumab promptly once active bacteremia is controlled and after appropriate vaccination/prophylaxis, because delay in complement blockade is associated with worse renal outcomes.

In short: C. canimorsus produces a protean early sepsis picture and is slow to culture, and TMA/CM‑HUS may evolve later or be masked by DIC. Maintain a high index of suspicion after dog exposure, repeat targeted hematologic and peripheral smear assessments, and escalate diagnostic and therapeutic steps quickly when MAHA/TMA emerges.
