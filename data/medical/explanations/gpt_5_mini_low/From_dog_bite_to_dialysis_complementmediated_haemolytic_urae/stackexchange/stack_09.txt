Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Management] In CM-HUS triggered by bacteremia, when should eculizumab be initiated relative to infection control, what vaccines and antibiotic prophylaxis are required, and which pathogens pose heightened risk during complement blockade?

Short answer
- Start eculizumab for infection‑triggered CM‑HUS as soon as the TMA diagnosis is secure and the benefit outweighs the infectious risk — ideally after active bacteraemia is controlled (blood cultures negative and the patient clinically improving). If TMA is rapidly progressive and life‑ or kidney‑threatening, do not delay complement blockade: treat the infection aggressively (appropriate antibiotics ± PEX) and start eculizumab while taking measures to mitigate infection risk.
- Before or at initiation, give meningococcal vaccination (quadrivalent A/C/Y/W and serogroup B) and provide immediate antibiotic prophylaxis if vaccination cannot be completed ≥2 weeks before complement blockade.
- Complement blockade markedly increases risk from encapsulated organisms — especially Neisseria meningitidis — and also raises concern for other capsulated gram‑negative and gram‑positive pathogens (eg, Capnocytophaga canimorsus, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae). Close monitoring and prompt empiric antibiotics for fever are mandatory.

Why — clinical reasoning and evidence from the case
- Rationale: Eculizumab (anti‑C5) prevents terminal complement–mediated endothelial injury and is the standard therapy for CM‑HUS; earlier initiation is associated with better hematologic and renal recovery. But terminal complement is essential for killing encapsulated organisms, so blockade increases the risk of fulminant infections, particularly meningococcal disease.
- Timing in practice: In the reported case the team waited until the bacteremia was controlled (daily clinical improvement and negative blood cultures for Capnocytophaga canimorsus) and administered meningococcal vaccines before eculizumab. They gave antibiotic prophylaxis (phenoxymethylpenicillin/“Apocillin”) because the vaccines had not been given ≥2 weeks earlier. After these precautions they started induction eculizumab and achieved sustained hematologic remission and partial renal recovery. This illustrates the recommended compromise: control the infection when possible, vaccinate, provide short‑term antibiotic cover if vaccines are recent or absent, and then institute complement blockade.
- When to not delay: If the TMA is fulminant and delay will cause irreversible organ damage (rapidly worsening anemia, thrombocytopenia, dialysis dependence), the balance can favour immediate eculizumab while continuing appropriate antimicrobial therapy and using PEX as a bridge — with contemporaneous vaccination and prophylactic antibiotics.

Vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis — practical points
- Mandatory vaccines before or at start:
  - Meningococcal quadrivalent (A, C, W, Y) and meningococcal B (MenB). In the case they gave Menveo (A/C/Y/W) and Bexsero (B).
  - Consider pneumococcal vaccination (PCV followed by PPSV23 per local schedule) and Haemophilus influenzae type b vaccine if not previously immunised, particularly in patients at increased risk of encapsulated infections.
  - Seasonal influenza vaccine as appropriate.
- If vaccination cannot be completed ≥2 weeks before eculizumab, give immediate antibiotic prophylaxis and continue until adequate immune response is expected (commonly at least 2 weeks after vaccination). In the case report, prophylactic phenoxymethylpenicillin was used because vaccination had not been given 2 weeks prior.
- Choice of prophylaxis:
  - Oral penicillin V or amoxicillin is commonly used (phenoxymethylpenicillin in the case). For penicillin allergy, alternatives include ciprofloxacin or azithromycin guided by local resistance.
  - Duration: short‑term until 2 weeks post‑vaccination response; some centers use longer or indefinite prophylaxis for select high‑risk patients (eg, asplenia, recurrent meningococcal disease, ongoing complement inhibitor therapy without adequate vaccination response) — tailor to risk and local guidance.
- Counseling: patients must be educated to seek urgent medical care for fever or signs of sepsis despite prophylaxis/vaccination; clinicians should have a low threshold to empirically start broad‑spectrum antibiotics.

Which pathogens are highest risk on complement blockade
- Highest: Neisseria meningitidis (all serogroups). Most feared complication is fulminant meningococcemia; meningococcal infection risk is substantially increased with eculizumab.
- Also important: encapsulated bacteria in general — Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae — and encapsulated gram‑negative organisms (Capnocytophaga species are encapsulated and were the inciting organism in this case). Eculizumab can impair clearance of these organisms and has been associated with severe infections beyond Neisseria.
- Less common but relevant: other Neisseria species and some unusual encapsulated or complement‑sensitive organisms; vigilance for unusual pathogens is warranted.

Bottom line
- Aim to control active infection and obtain negative cultures before starting eculizumab when feasible. If TMA is life‑threatening, begin eculizumab without undue delay but concurrently treat the infection aggressively, vaccinate as soon as possible, and give immediate antibiotic prophylaxis until vaccine protection is established. Educate the patient and maintain close infectious surveillance because complement blockade predisposes most strongly to meningococcal disease and other infections by encapsulated organisms (including the Capnocytophaga that triggered disease in this patient).
