Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

## Clinical Presentation and Early Sepsis Management after Dog Bite

The patient’s illness began four days after a seemingly minor dog bite to the upper lip. She had been prescribed oral phenoxymethylpenicillin at the time of the injury but was unable to fill the prescription, so no prophylactic antibiotics were taken. This gap in early antimicrobial prophylaxis is important in the clinical narrative: while most dog bites do not lead to invasive disease, omission of timely antibiotics increases the risk that organisms from canine oral flora will establish infection and, in rare cases, produce fulminant systemic illness.

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is a gram‑negative, fastidious rod that colonizes the oropharynx of dogs and cats. Transmission occurs most commonly by bites or scratches, but close contact and licking of broken skin can also transmit the organism. Clinical manifestations range from mild local infection to life‑threatening sepsis with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), multiorgan failure and, rarely, thrombotic microangiopathy. Virulence relates in part to its capsule and variable susceptibility to serum complement; some strains resist complement‑mediated killing and have the capacity to provoke severe systemic inflammatory responses. Recognition that a dog bite preceded the illness should raise suspicion for C. canimorsus as a possible pathogen, even in patients who are not classically immunocompromised.

On presentation the patient had a fulminant systemic syndrome: high fever, profound malaise, rapidly progressive hypoglycaemia refractory to standard measures, mottled and cool lower extremities, and laboratory evidence of pancytopenia, acute kidney injury (AKI), elevated liver enzymes and lactic acidaemia. These features are consistent with severe sepsis and early circulatory collapse. Of note, hypoglycaemia is an established but less common manifestation of severe sepsis and may reflect impaired gluconeogenesis, increased metabolic demand and hepatic dysfunction; its presence in this patient signalled systemic metabolic compromise and helped justify urgent escalation of care.

The combination of bleeding manifestations, thrombocytopenia, prolongation of coagulation times and microthrombotic skin changes (mottling, ecchymoses, distal cyanosis) pointed to DIC occurring in the context of sepsis. DIC in sepsis represents dysregulated global activation of coagulation with consumption of platelets and clotting factors, microvascular fibrin deposition and secondary bleeding. Clinically the diagnosis is made by integrating laboratory data (falling platelets, prolonged PT/aPTT, low fibrinogen, elevated D‑dimer) with the clinical context. The patient’s rapid development of anuric renal failure alongside coagulopathy and lactic acidaemia met criteria for severe sepsis with organ dysfunction; a bedside organ‑dysfunction score such as qSOFA can be used to screen patients, but definitive management decisions depend on the overall clinical picture rather than a single score.

Early in the evaluation it was important to exclude surgical emergencies that can produce similar peripheral ischemia—most notably necrotising soft‑tissue infection. In this case, clinical examination and radiology were used to rule out necrotising fasciitis, allowing clinicians to focus on medical resuscitation rather than immediate operative intervention.

Empiric antimicrobial choice must cover the most likely pathogens from animal bites. Given the suspicion for dog‑associated organisms (including Pasteurella, Capnocytophaga, and various anaerobes), the team initiated broad‑spectrum intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam. This agent provides aerobic and anaerobic coverage including many gram‑negative rods and is a standard empiric choice when severe infection following an animal bite is suspected. Because C. canimorsus can be highly virulent and patients can deteriorate rapidly, starting empiric therapy before culture confirmation is appropriate; once blood cultures identified the organism, therapy was de‑escalated to agents tailored to the isolate’s susceptibilities.

A practical challenge in diagnosing Capnocytophaga sepsis is its fastidious, slow growth in routine cultures, which can delay microbiological confirmation. In this case blood cultures later grew C. canimorsus after several days — a typical timeline — illustrating why clinicians must often make early therapeutic decisions on clinical grounds. Increasingly, molecular methods can shorten the diagnostic window, but these are not universally available.

The patient’s ICU course exemplified the severity of C. canimorsus sepsis. She progressed to complete anuria with rapidly worsening azotaemia, unresponsive to diuretics or fluid resuscitation, and developed septic cardiomyopathy leading to fluid overload. Continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) was started because of hemodynamic instability and the need for ongoing solute and fluid control. In severe sepsis with AKI and haemodynamic compromise, CRRT is preferred over intermittent haemodialysis because it provides gentler, continuous fluid removal and metabolic control.

Supportive haematological management included blood product administration and coagulation support. Because DIC involves consumption of platelets and clotting factors, transfusions of platelets, fibrinogen (or cryoprecipitate), and plasma are given as indicated by bleeding, invasive procedures, or critically low laboratory values. Haematology input guided the evaluation of pancytopenia and consideration of alternative diagnoses (for example, acute leukaemia or haemophagocytic syndromes) while transfusions addressed immediate bleeding risk and facilitated invasive diagnostics when required.

In summary, the early management was driven by recognition of severe sepsis after a dog bite, prompt empiric broad‑spectrum antibiotics targeting canine oral flora, aggressive supportive care for multiorgan dysfunction including CRRT for refractory AKI, and targeted haemostatic support for DIC. The delay inherent in culture confirmation of Capnocytophaga underscores the need for early empiric therapy guided by exposure history and clinical severity.

## Transition from Sepsis/DIC to Thrombotic Microangiopathy

In this patient the key clinical inflection occurred after the initial septic picture and coagulopathy were improving: inflammatory markers fell, coagulation parameters normalised and she remained haemodynamically stable, yet she developed a sudden, isolated fall in haemoglobin without external bleeding. This paradox—improvement in systemic sepsis but new-onset anaemia and persistent thrombocytopenia—should prompt consideration of a superimposed microangiopathic process rather than ongoing consumptive coagulopathy from DIC alone.

Laboratory evidence clarified the mechanism of the anaemia. Haptoglobin became undetectable and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) rose, indicating intravascular haemolysis. Reticulocyte response had been blunted initially (consistent with sepsis), but the combination of a brisk fall in haemoglobin, very low haptoglobin and rising LDH is classic for haemolysis. Platelet counts, which had recovered somewhat from the nadir during DIC but remained persistently low (30–46 × 10^9/L), pointed away from isolated post‑sepsis recovery and toward an ongoing consumptive process. In DIC one expects coagulation tests to remain abnormal and for haemolysis to be less prominent once the sepsis resolves; persistent isolated microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) therefore raises concern for thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

A common diagnostic pitfall is a positive direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test (DAT) in this setting. The patient’s initial DAT was IgG‑positive, which prompted empiric corticosteroids for presumed autoimmune haemolysis. However, penicillin-class antibiotics and recent transfusions can cause false‑positive DAT results, and transfusion or drug‑related antibodies may not be the primary driver of haemolysis. The lack of clinical response to high‑dose steroids and ongoing laboratory evidence of red‑cell fragmentation argued against immune‑mediated haemolysis and made a TMA much more likely.

Definitive recognition of MAHA came from the peripheral blood smear, which showed abundant schistocytes (fragmented red cells), in this case 15–20%. Schistocytosis at that level is a sensitive marker of mechanical red‑cell destruction and is a required element for diagnosing MAHA/TMA in conjunction with thrombocytopenia and organ dysfunction. The appearance of schistocytes in a patient who is improving from sepsis is a red flag for TMA rather than recurrent DIC.

Because the kidney was the primary organ failure and because histology can distinguish microvascular thrombotic injury from other causes of acute kidney injury (AKI), a renal biopsy was pursued once it was reasonably safe. Procedural bleeding risk is the main limitation for biopsy in thrombocytopenic patients; a pragmatic platelet threshold is often used, and in this case the team waited until the platelet count had increased to 77 × 10^9/L after plasma exchange before performing the biopsy. This approach balances diagnostic yield against bleeding risk and allowed tissue diagnosis without major complication.

The biopsy showed features characteristic of TMA superimposed on ischaemic injury: severe acute tubular necrosis consistent with the period of oligo‑anuria, glomeruli with ischaemic collapse and fibrin thrombi occluding capillary lumina, and scattered crescents reflecting focal severe glomerular injury. The absence of immune complex deposition on immunofluorescence and the lack of electron‑microscopic evidence of a primary immune complex glomerulopathy support a non‑immune, microvascular thrombotic process—i.e., TMA—rather than immune‑complex mediated glomerulonephritis. These histologic findings explain the persistent haemolysis (mechanical fragmentation across fibrin‑filled microvessels), thrombocytopenia (ongoing platelet consumption in microthrombi), and dialysis‑dependent AKI (microvascular occlusion and ischemia).

Taken together, the clinical course—improvement in systemic inflammatory indices but new MAHA and stable low platelets—coupled with haemolytic laboratory markers, abundant schistocytes, and biopsy‑proven microvascular thrombosis, supports the interpretation that a TMA emerged on the background of resolving sepsis/DIC. In this case the TMA was the dominant driver of ongoing haemolysis and renal failure, prompting targeted therapy (plasma exchange as a bridge and ultimately complement blockade) rather than continued focus on sepsis management alone.

## Diagnostic Reasoning and Differential Diagnosis of TMA

When a patient develops microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) with thrombocytopenia and acute kidney injury (AKI), rapid, stepwise diagnostic reasoning is essential because the differential includes disorders with very different treatments and prognoses. The immediate clinical priority is to identify or exclude thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), because untreated TTP carries a high mortality that is reduced dramatically by urgent plasma exchange. After TTP is addressed (or excluded), attention turns to infection-associated causes (including Shiga toxin-producing E. coli (STEC) HUS and DIC-related MAHA) and to complement-mediated HUS (CM‑HUS), which requires different targeted therapy.

1) Rapid triage to exclude TTP
- Clinical urgency: TTP should be treated empirically with plasma exchange (PEX) when highly suspected because delays are dangerous. Clinical tools such as the PLASMIC score provide a pretest probability while awaiting definitive testing. The PLASMIC score uses seven easily available variables (platelet count, haemolysis evidence, absence of active cancer, absence of transplant, MCV, INR, and creatinine) to stratify risk. In this case the PLASMIC score was 4/7, a low pretest probability, decreasing immediate suspicion for TTP.
- Confirmatory test: ADAMTS13 activity is diagnostic: severe TTP is defined by activity <10% (often with an ADAMTS13 inhibitor). Values >10–20% essentially rule against classic severe TTP as the cause of the syndrome. In this patient an ADAMTS13 activity of 43.5% made TTP unlikely and allowed clinicians to stop treating for TTP and focus on other causes.

2) Evaluation for STEC‑HUS
- Clinical pattern: STEC-HUS typically follows a prodrome of bloody diarrhoea or enteritis. Absence of an enteric prodrome lowers likelihood but does not exclude disease.
- Testing: Stool PCR or culture for Shiga toxin genes (stx1/stx2) is the preferred rapid test. A negative stool PCR in the appropriate clinical context (no diarrhoeal prodrome and negative testing) effectively rules out STEC-HUS. In the reported case, stool PCR was negative and there was no enteric illness, making STEC-HUS unlikely.

3) Distinguishing sepsis/DIC‑associated MAHA from TMA syndromes
- Overlap and clues: Severe sepsis with DIC can produce MAHA-like features (schistocytes, thrombocytopenia, elevated LDH). Clues favouring DIC-related microangiopathy include marked coagulopathy (prolonged PT/aPTT, low fibrinogen, elevated D‑dimer), active bleeding or consumption of clotting factors, and a clinical picture dominated by ongoing septic shock. DIC‑related schistocytosis tends to improve as sepsis resolves.
- Timing and course: In this patient, the initial picture was dominated by sepsis and DIC, but haemolysis and thrombocytopenia reappeared or persisted after the sepsis improved—raising suspicion for a superimposed TMA rather than ongoing DIC alone.

4) Role and interpretation of complement studies in suspected CM‑HUS
- Basic tests: Serum C3 and C4 levels are commonly obtained but have limited sensitivity. Normal C3/C4 do not exclude CM‑HUS because systemic complement consumption may be focal or intermittent; many patients with genetically mediated complement dysregulation have normal routine complement levels at presentation.
- Functional assays: Hemolytic assays (total complement CH50, alternative pathway AH50) and measurements of specific complement components (factor H, factor I, factor B, C5b-9) can provide additional information. However, these assays have interpretive caveats: low AH50/CH50 can reflect consumption from active disease, but can also be altered by recent plasma therapy, transfusions, or complement inhibitors. Anti-factor H autoantibodies should be sought in appropriate age groups.
- Genetic testing: Extended genetic panels for complement-regulatory genes (CFH, CFI, CFB, C3, MCP/CD46, and others) help identify predisposing variants but take time and do not change acute management. A negative genetic panel does not exclude an infection-triggered, complement-mediated process.
- In this case: C3/C4 were normal, but that did not argue strongly against CM‑HUS. A markedly reduced AH50 was later measured, but sampling occurred after PEX and after one dose of eculizumab, and thus most likely reflects treatment effects rather than intrinsic complement deficiency or activation.

5) Impact of interventions on diagnostic testing and optimal timing of samples
- Plasma exchange: PEX removes circulating complement proteins, autoantibodies, and other plasma factors, altering both quantitative and functional complement assays and making interpretation unreliable for some days. It also dilutes ADAMTS13 activity; however, ADAMTS13 sampling should be obtained before the first PEX whenever possible. In emergencies where PEX cannot be delayed, a baseline sample taken before PEX is ideal.
- Eculizumab: As a terminal complement inhibitor (anti‑C5), eculizumab abolishes complement hemolytic function and will markedly lower functional complement assays (CH50/AH50) and increase circulating C5 fragments bound to drug; thus assays after dosing cannot be used to assess the native complement system. Ideally, blood for complement functional assays and samples for genetic testing should be drawn prior to PEX or eculizumab when feasible. In practice, patient safety takes precedence; clinicians must document timing of interventions relative to sampling to interpret results correctly.
- In the presented case, some complement testing was performed before PEX/ecz but extended testing was done after PEX and eculizumab—necessitating interpretation with the understanding that therapy likely depressed functional assay results.

6) Indications and diagnostic yield of renal biopsy in suspected TMA
- When to biopsy: Kidney biopsy is indicated when the diagnosis remains uncertain after noninvasive testing, when there is diagnostic equipoise between glomerular disease and TMA, or when biopsy findings will change management. In dialysis‑dependent AKI with suspected TMA and uncertain etiology, biopsy can demonstrate characteristic microvascular changes that support a TMA diagnosis.
- Expected findings: Light microscopy in TMA shows glomerular or arteriolar endothelial swelling, capillary wall thickening, fibrin thrombi within glomeruli or small arterioles, ischaemic glomerular collapse, and sometimes crescents. Immunofluorescence is usually negative or shows nonspecific entrapment of proteins; electron microscopy may show endothelial detachment and subendothelial expansion.
- In this case, kidney biopsy demonstrated fibrin thrombi, ischaemic glomeruli and acute tubular necrosis—histology consistent with TMA and supportive of CM‑HUS in the appropriate clinical context. The biopsy therefore provided crucial objective evidence of microvascular thrombotic injury in the kidneys and helped support the decision to institute complement blockade.

7) Synthesis: integrating the data to determine CM‑HUS
- Logical sequence: In a patient with MAHA, thrombocytopenia and severe AKI, first obtain an ADAMTS13 activity (before PEX if possible) to exclude TTP; second, evaluate for STEC with stool testing if the history suggests diarrhoeal prodrome; third, assess for sepsis and DIC with coagulation studies and clinical correlation; fourth, obtain complement studies (acknowledging limitations) and send samples for extended complement genetic testing; fifth, consider renal biopsy when noninvasive data are inconclusive or organ‑directed evidence is needed.
- Applying to the case: TTP was effectively excluded by an ADAMTS13 activity of 43.5% (and a low PLASMIC score). STEC‑HUS was excluded by a negative stool PCR and absent enteric prodrome. The initial MAHA-like picture occurred during frank sepsis/DIC, but persistent haemolysis with schistocytes, ongoing thrombocytopenia after normalization of coagulation tests, dialysis‑dependent AKI, and kidney biopsy showing fibrin thrombi and ischaemic glomeruli all pointed to a TMA process beyond sepsis‑related DIC. Complement studies, while not definitive (normal C3/C4), and the clinical response after complement‑directed therapy (PEX as bridging and eculizumab with subsequent haematologic remission and renal recovery) supported the clinical diagnosis of complement‑mediated HUS triggered by Capnocytophaga canimorsus infection.
- Practical takeaways: CM‑HUS is a diagnosis of exclusion that relies on synthesis of clinical course, laboratory testing (with attention to timing and treatment effects), microbiology, and often renal histology. Early ADAMTS13 testing is crucial to exclude TTP; stool testing should be done when STEC is possible; and complement testing and genetic studies are useful but must be interpreted in the context of recent PEX, transfusions, or complement blockade. When clinical suspicion for CM‑HUS is high and reversible causes are excluded, prompt initiation of complement blockade (with appropriate infection control measures) offers the best chance for haematologic remission and renal recovery.

## Pathophysiology: Complement-Mediated HUS and Infection Triggers

Complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) is a thrombotic microangiopathy driven by dysregulated activation of the alternative complement pathway at the endothelial surface, most prominently affecting renal microvasculature. Understanding CM-HUS requires familiarity with how the alternative pathway is normally regulated, how loss of control produces endothelial injury and microthrombi, and how infections may act as the precipitating “second hit.”

The alternative pathway is constitutively active at a low level through spontaneous hydrolysis of C3 (so-called “tick-over”), producing C3b that can deposit on cell surfaces. If deposited on host endothelium, C3b is inactivated by surface and fluid-phase regulators to prevent bystander injury. Key regulators include factor H (a fluid-phase regulator that preferentially binds host polyanions and accelerates decay of C3 convertase and acts as a cofactor for factor I), factor I (a serine protease that cleaves C3b to inactive fragments), complement factor B (which associates with C3b to form the C3bBb convertase and thus is essential for amplification), and membrane cofactor protein (CD46) on host cells which serves as a cofactor for factor I–mediated C3b cleavage. When these regulators function normally, complement activation is targeted to pathogens and limited on host cells.

In CM-HUS, genetic variants or acquired autoantibodies impair one or more of these regulatory mechanisms, tipping the balance toward uncontrolled alternative pathway amplification at endothelial surfaces. Persistent C3b-mediated amplification leads to generation of C5 convertase and terminal pathway activation, producing C5a and membrane attack complex (C5b-9). On renal microvascular endothelium this translates into endothelial activation/dysfunction, loss of antithrombotic properties, exposure of subendothelial matrix, platelet aggregation and fibrin deposition — the histologic and clinical hallmarks of TMA (microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia, thrombocytopenia and organ ischemia, classically acute kidney injury).

Genetic contributors and autoantibodies
Approximately half of patients diagnosed with CM-HUS will have identifiable genetic variants in complement regulators or activators (for example, loss-of-function variants in CFH, CFI, or CD46, or gain-of-function variants in CFB or C3). The penetrance of these variants is incomplete: many carriers never develop disease unless exposed to a triggering event. In addition to genetic defects, acquired anti–factor H autoantibodies can produce a functional deficiency of factor H and phenocopy genetic loss-of-function. Because of incomplete penetrance and the frequent absence of detectable variants, the diagnosis of CM-HUS remains a clinicopathological one, integrating presentation, exclusion of other TMAs (notably severe ADAMTS13 deficiency and STEC-HUS), biopsy findings, and response to complement blockade.

Infectious and other triggers
Although CM-HUS is often labelled “atypical,” its clinical emergence nearly always requires a trigger. Common triggers include bacterial and viral infections, pregnancy and the postpartum state, autoimmune disease flares, certain medications, and other physiologic stresses. Infection is a frequent and biologically plausible trigger: pathogen-associated molecular patterns and endothelial insults increase local complement activation and can overwhelm regulatory capacity, particularly in genetically predisposed individuals. Shiga-toxin–associated HUS (STEC-HUS) is the best-known infection-related TMA but operates through distinct mechanisms; other bacterial pathogens have been implicated as triggers for CM-HUS in case reports and series.

Capnocytophaga canimorsus and complement
Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, gram-negative commensal of dog and cat oropharynx that can cause fulminant sepsis after bites or close contact. Interaction between C. canimorsus and the human complement system is variable and relevant to pathogenesis. Many isolates are complement sensitive and are cleared efficiently in serum, but capsule-expressing strains may achieve partial resistance to complement-mediated killing. This variable serum susceptibility means that the organism both stimulates complement (as an innate immune response) and, in some strains, can survive despite complement activation.

Clinical paradox: defence versus injury
This duality creates a clinical paradox central to CM-HUS pathophysiology. On one hand, intact complement activity—especially the alternative and terminal pathways—is essential for opsonization and killing of encapsulated bacteria, so systemic complement activation is a protective response during infection. On the other hand, excessive or dysregulated activation at the endothelial surface leads to C5 cleavage and C5b-9 formation, endothelial injury and TMA. Thus an infection that provokes strong complement activation (or that directly injures endothelium) can precipitate CM-HUS in a host with subclinical defects in regulation, or even uncommonly in a host without identifiable genetic predisposition.

Interpreting the findings in this case
The patient’s illness illustrates these mechanisms. Severe C. canimorsus sepsis provided a potent inflammatory and complement-activating milieu. The delayed onset of frank microangiopathic haemolysis after initial improvement from sepsis fits the typical pattern of a postinfectious trigger precipitating a dysregulated complement response rather than direct bacterial microangiopathy. Extended complement testing in this patient did not identify pathogenic variants nor anti–factor H antibodies; locally available C3/C4 and pathway screens were normal. The markedly reduced AH50 (alternative pathway haemolytic assay) measured after plasma exchange and after initiation of eculizumab is most consistent with treatment effects: plasma exchange removes complement components and regulators, and eculizumab blocks terminal pathway activity and will reduce functional assay readouts. Therefore, the laboratory constellation supports an infection-triggered CM-HUS (no chronic complement deficiency demonstrated), rather than pre-existing, severe regulatory protein loss.

Comparison with prior reports
Published reports link C. canimorsus to CM-HUS in a small number of cases. Some patients have had identifiable complement gene variants—illustrating the canonical “genetic predisposition plus trigger” model—while others, like the present case, have had no detectable pathogenic variants despite a clinically typical course and good response to complement blockade. Both patterns are described in the literature and reinforce that absence of a genetic abnormality does not exclude CM-HUS, nor does its presence prove causality without compatible clinical features.

Takeaway
CM-HUS represents uncontrolled alternative pathway amplification at endothelial surfaces, typically precipitated by an external trigger such as infection. The balance between complement-mediated host defence and bystander endothelial injury explains why pathogens that strongly engage complement (or can partially resist it) can both provoke sepsis and, in susceptible hosts, initiate TMA. In clinical practice, normal routine complement levels and absence of identifiable genetic variants do not exclude CM-HUS; diagnosis relies on the clinical picture, kidney biopsy findings, exclusion of other TMAs and response to targeted therapy.

## Therapeutic Strategy, Outcomes, and Clinical Lessons

Initial management focused on standard sepsis care with early broad-spectrum antibiotics and supportive measures, then rapid de-escalation to targeted therapy once blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus. In this case piperacillin/tazobactam was started empirically to cover dog-associated pathogens and subsequently narrowed to intravenous ampicillin and finally oral phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) once susceptibilities and culture results were available. Alongside antimicrobials, the team prioritized source control (clinical and radiological exclusion of focal infection) and organ support (fluid resuscitation, correction of coagulopathy, blood-product support) in the ICU.

Renal support was dictated by the rapid progression to anuria and refractory fluid overload. Continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) was used acutely for volume control and metabolic derangements. The patient’s course demonstrates a common pattern in infection-associated TMA: an initial dialysis‑dependent phase followed by gradual return of diuresis as microvascular thrombosis and systemic inflammation are treated. In this patient spontaneous diuresis returned after plasma exchange and before definitive complement blockade, and she ultimately became dialysis independent with a residual creatinine of about 175 µmol/L. Continued outpatient monitoring of creatinine, urine output, fluid status and blood pressure is essential to assess longer‑term renal recovery and guide decisions about ongoing therapies.

When a thrombotic microangiopathy is suspected but the precise subtype is not yet confirmed, plasma exchange (PEX) remains a pragmatic bridging therapy. PEX was used here while awaiting ADAMTS13 activity and complement results. In CM-HUS PEX may transiently improve laboratory parameters by removing circulating factors, cytokines or autoantibodies, but it rarely provides durable control because it does not correct the underlying complement dysregulation. This case illustrates that PEX produced temporary stabilisation (including return of some urine output) but only after targeted complement blockade did sustained haematologic remission and greater renal recovery occur.

Eculizumab was used as the definitive therapy for complement-mediated HUS. The induction regimen employed was standard: 900 mg intravenously once weekly for four weeks, followed by maintenance dosing of 1,200 mg every two weeks. This regimen produced prompt haematological and renal improvement in the patient. Earlier initiation of eculizumab in CM-HUS is associated with better renal outcomes; therefore, once TTP and STEC‑HUS are reasonably excluded and CM‑HUS is strongly suspected, timely initiation should be considered. In practice this requires balancing diagnostic certainty against risks of immune modulation during active infection.

Complement blockade raises specific infectious risks because terminal complement components are important in defence against encapsulated organisms. Before starting eculizumab the team planned and began meningococcal vaccination (quadrivalent ACWY and group B vaccines) and used antibiotic prophylaxis because vaccines had not been administered ≥2 weeks prior to therapy. Importantly, in this patient eculizumab initiation occurred after blood cultures had turned negative, minimising the risk of exacerbating active bacteraemia. When vaccination cannot precede therapy by two weeks, short‑course antimicrobial prophylaxis and close monitoring are recommended; this case followed that precautionary approach.

Clinical response to therapy was monitored by standard hematologic and biochemical markers: rising platelet count, falling lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), recovery of haptoglobin and stabilization of haemoglobin, together with renal trajectory (return of urine output, falling creatinine and reduced dialysis dependence). In this patient platelet recovery and LDH normalisation paralleled clinical improvement after PEX and eculizumab, and haemolysis resolved. Decisions about duration of eculizumab were guided by disease severity, renal recovery and genetic testing. Extended genetic testing in this patient did not reveal pathogenic complement variants; nonetheless, because of the severity of presentation and partial renal recovery, continuation of maintenance eculizumab was chosen with planned reassessment. In general, considerations for tapering or stopping complement blockade include sustained clinical and laboratory remission, absence of pathogenic complement variants, and close outpatient surveillance with a plan for rapid re‑initiation if relapse occurs.

Key practice points illustrated by this case:
- Consider CM‑HUS when new microangiopathic haemolysis and thrombocytopenia emerge as sepsis appears to improve; the timing can help distinguish infection‑related DIC from a superimposed complement‑mediated TMA.
- Normal C3 and C4 do not exclude CM‑HUS; complement levels may be normal even with active disease.
- Rapid ADAMTS13 testing is crucial to exclude TTP and avoid unnecessary delay in targeted therapy; a level well above 10% makes TTP unlikely.
- PEX is an important short‑term bridge in undifferentiated TMA but is not definitive for CM‑HUS.
- Eculizumab is the effective therapy for CM‑HUS; earlier initiation is linked to better renal outcomes but must be balanced against infectious risks.
- Mitigate infectious risk from complement blockade by vaccinating against meningococcus (ACWY and B) and providing antibiotic prophylaxis when vaccination cannot precede therapy by ≥2 weeks; ideally begin eculizumab after blood cultures are negative.
- Kidney biopsy can support the diagnosis when safe and expedient, showing TMA changes that reinforce the urgency of complement‑directed therapy.

Outcome in this case combined haematologic remission with partial renal recovery and dialysis independence. The case underscores the need for vigilance for TMA during and after severe infection, a pragmatic approach to bridging therapies, and timely use of complement inhibitors when CM‑HUS is the leading diagnosis.
