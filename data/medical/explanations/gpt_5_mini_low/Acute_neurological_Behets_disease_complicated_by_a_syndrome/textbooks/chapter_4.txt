Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

Chapter 4: ## Management Strategy and Rationale

This patient’s management balanced three simultaneous priorities: exclude and treat potential CNS infection, control the inflammatory disorder (Behçet’s disease with acute neuro‑BD), and safely correct SIADH‑related hyponatraemia while minimising iatrogenic harm. Each decision was guided by the initial presentation (fever, headache, mucocutaneous lesions, hyponatraemia), evolving diagnostic data (negative microbiology, aseptic CSF with lymphocytic pleocytosis, normal MRI) and the patient’s comorbidities.

Empiric therapy and prompt de‑escalation
Given the combination of fever, meningeal symptoms and hyponatraemia, early empiric antimicrobial coverage was appropriate. Broad‑spectrum antibiotics (cefepime) were started after blood cultures to cover common bacterial pathogens while diagnostic studies were pending. Because viral encephalitis (HSV/VZV) remained a realistic early concern, empiric acyclovir was added when CNS involvement progressed. When CSF PCRs and cultures were repeatedly negative and clinical data pointed away from infectious aetiologies, antimicrobials were discontinued. This approach follows antimicrobial stewardship principles: start early when delay risks harm, but de‑escalate promptly when objective data (cultures, PCR, imaging, clinical trajectory) exclude infection to avoid unnecessary drug exposure and toxicity.

Rationale for colchicine monotherapy in this case
Colchicine was introduced once systemic BD and acute neuro‑BD were diagnosed. Its mechanisms—impairing neutrophil chemotaxis and activation and downregulating proinflammatory cytokine production (including IL‑1β and downstream IL‑6)—make it effective for mucocutaneous BD manifestations and potentially helpful in limiting inflammatory mediator–driven effects in CNS inflammation. In routine practice colchicine is standard for oral/genital ulcers and erythema/folliculitis in BD; it is not considered first‑line for parenchymal neuro‑BD, but it is commonly used adjunctively.

In this patient, several features supported a trial of colchicine as monotherapy rather than immediate high‑dose corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants: the neurological syndrome resembled aseptic meningitis without focal deficits, MRI was normal (no parenchymal brainstem lesions), CSF inflammation was mild, and the diagnostic picture for BD had just emerged. Given the risks of systemic corticosteroids and immunosuppressants—particularly in a patient with poorly controlled diabetes—the team elected a conservative, steroid‑sparing approach. The rapid clinical and biochemical improvement after colchicine initiation (resolution of fever and mucocutaneous lesions, gradual improvement in neurological symptoms and normalization of sodium) supports that colchicine either treated the inflammatory process or the disease followed a self‑limited course; spontaneous recovery can occur in meningoencephalitis‑type presentations of neuro‑BD, so causality cannot be proven here.

Standard care for acute neuro‑BD and how this case differs
Standard management for acute parenchymal neuro‑BD typically involves high‑dose corticosteroids (for example, IV methylprednisolone pulses followed by an oral taper) often combined with steroid‑sparing immunosuppressants (azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, biologics) for severe or relapsing disease. This strategy is based on observational data showing improved neurologic outcomes and reduced relapse risk with aggressive immunosuppression. The present case is atypical because the neuro‑inflammatory manifestations were relatively mild, MRI was negative, and the team successfully avoided systemic steroids and other immunosuppressants. This highlights that therapy must be individualized: milder neuro‑BD presentations may respond to less intensive treatment, but clinicians should be prepared to escalate promptly if there is neurological progression or imaging/paraclinical evidence of parenchymal involvement.

SIADH management and precautions for sodium correction
The diagnosis of SIADH (euvolemic hyponatraemia, low plasma osmolality, inappropriately concentrated urine and elevated urine sodium) requires careful correction to avoid neurologic complications of both hyponatraemia and overly rapid correction (osmotic demyelination). Conservative management was appropriate here because the patient was clinically euvolemic, had no severe neurologic deterioration, and sodium was modestly reduced (124 mmol/L). Measures employed included fluid restriction to <800 mL/day, close daily (or more frequent) serum sodium monitoring, and avoidance of rapid correction.

Principles applied (and generally recommended limits): aim for a safe rise in serum sodium of no more than 8–10 mmol/L in the first 24 hours (some guidelines recommend ≤8 mmol/L) and ≤18 mmol/L over 48 hours, adjusting the plan if neurologic symptoms worsen. Indications for more aggressive therapy—hypertonic (3%) saline boluses—include severe or life‑threatening neurologic symptoms (seizures, obtundation, coma). Vasopressin receptor antagonists (vaptans) can be considered in chronic or euvolemic SIADH that is symptomatic and refractory to fluid restriction, but they require careful monitoring because they can produce rapid sodium rises and are relatively contraindicated in certain settings; they were not necessary here. The team avoided hypertonic saline and vaptans because the patient improved with conservative measures and immunomodulation.

Monitoring for complications related to hyponatraemia and inflammation
The clinical team monitored for known complications of hyponatraemia and systemic inflammation. Notably, the patient had elevated creatine kinase consistent with rhabdomyolysis, which may be triggered or exacerbated by severe hyponatraemia. Recognising this association prompted serial CK measurements, renal function monitoring and appropriate supportive care to reduce risk of acute kidney injury. Persistent hiccups in this case were an important clinical clue suggesting significant hyponatraemia and/or brainstem irritation; clinicians should view otherwise nonspecific symptoms (hiccups, nausea, malaise) in the context of electrolyte disturbances. Daily neurologic assessments and frequent electrolyte checks allowed safe titration of therapy and early detection of deterioration.

Supportive care and management of comorbidities
Local treatments were used for mucocutaneous disease: topical corticosteroid ointment for oral aphthae and silver sulfadiazine for genital ulcers to speed symptomatic recovery and prevent secondary infection. Rehabilitation addressed deconditioning and functional decline during the hospital stay. Given the patient’s poorly controlled diabetes (glucose 14.2 mmol/L, HbA1c 8.2%), metabolic control was deferred until the acute inflammatory and hyponatraemic phases were stabilised; after recovery from the acute episode, appropriate antidiabetic therapy (biguanide) was initiated to reduce infection risk and improve long‑term outcomes. Throughout care, the team balanced infection risk from immunosuppression against the need to control inflammation.

Summary
The management combined early empiric antimicrobial therapy with rapid de‑escalation when infection was excluded, conservative but vigilant SIADH treatment (fluid restriction and close monitoring) to avoid rapid sodium correction, and a trial of colchicine monotherapy aimed at treating BD‑related inflammation while avoiding corticosteroids in a patient with diabetes and a relatively mild neuro‑inflammatory phenotype. Frequent reassessments allowed safe avoidance of aggressive immunosuppression; had the patient’s neurologic status or imaging shown progression, escalation to corticosteroids and additional immunosuppressants would have been indicated. The case illustrates patient‑tailored decision making grounded in diagnostic data, risk–benefit assessment, and close monitoring for complications.
