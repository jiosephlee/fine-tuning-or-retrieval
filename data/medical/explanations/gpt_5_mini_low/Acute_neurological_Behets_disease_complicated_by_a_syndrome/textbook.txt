Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

## Clinical Background and Pathophysiology Relevant to the Case

Behçet’s disease (BD) is a chronic, relapsing, multisystem inflammatory disorder classically recognised by recurrent oral and genital aphthous ulcers and variable skin lesions (the mucocutaneous triad). Pathologically it is best conceptualised as a systemic vasculitis that can involve vessels of various sizes and types, with secondary inflammatory damage in affected organs. Clinical manifestations extend beyond the mucocutaneous features to include ocular inflammation (uveitis), arthropathy, gastrointestinal ulcers and less commonly major organ involvement such as the central nervous system (CNS) and large vessels. The clinical course is heterogeneous: many patients have prolonged periods of mucocutaneous flares punctuated by less frequent but potentially severe organ‑threatening episodes. In practice, recognition of the characteristic mucocutaneous lesions is central to diagnosis, and scoring systems (eg, International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease) are used to classify disease rather than a single confirmatory test.

HLA-B51 is the single strongest genetic association described for BD, with population studies showing increased prevalence among affected patients, particularly in regions along the historic Silk Road. However, HLA-B51 is neither necessary nor sufficient for diagnosis: it is a susceptibility marker that increases disease risk at the population level but does not have diagnostic specificity for an individual patient. Thus a positive HLA-B51, as in this case, supports epidemiologic predisposition but should not be used in isolation to establish BD.

Neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) refers to CNS involvement and can present in two broad phenotypic patterns. The parenchymal form typically produces meningoencephalitis with focal inflammatory lesions, most characteristically affecting the brainstem, basal ganglia and deep white matter. The non‑parenchymal form involves vascular complications such as cerebral venous sinus thrombosis and aseptic intracranial hypertension. Clinically, acute parenchymal neuro‑BD commonly presents with fever, headache, behavioural or cognitive changes, cranial nerve findings, long‑tract signs or cerebellar/brainstem syndromes; meningoencephalitic presentations may be subacute and are sometimes self‑limited. Neurological involvement occurs in approximately 10–20% of BD patients, but most series report that neuro‑BD typically develops several years after the onset of mucocutaneous disease. Synchronous presentation of systemic BD and first‑episode neuro‑BD, as in this report, is uncommon but well described.

Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and neuroimaging findings in acute neuro‑BD are variable. CSF commonly shows aseptic inflammation — mild to moderate lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein — reflecting meningoencephalitis rather than bacterial infection. These CSF changes are non‑specific and serve mainly to demonstrate CNS inflammation and exclude infectious or malignant causes. Brain MRI often reveals T2/FLAIR hyperintense parenchymal lesions in the brainstem, basal ganglia or deep white matter in parenchymal disease; however, MRI can be normal early in the disease course or in mild meningeal‑predominant forms. Therefore, a normal early MRI does not exclude neuro‑BD when the clinical picture and CSF support CNS inflammation in the context of BD. This principle is directly relevant to the present patient, who had CSF evidence of aseptic meningitis despite unremarkable MRI.

Understanding the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) requires a brief review of ADH (vasopressin) physiology. ADH is synthesised in the hypothalamus and released from the posterior pituitary in response to increased plasma osmolality and, to a lesser extent, hypovolaemia or hypotension. ADH acts on renal V2 receptors to promote water reabsorption, lowering plasma osmolality and sodium concentration. SIADH is defined by hypotonic hyponatraemia with inappropriately concentrated urine (high urine osmolality) and elevated urine sodium in a clinically euvolemic patient, with exclusion of adrenal, thyroid and renal causes and of diuretic use. Common CNS triggers include infections (meningitis, encephalitis), intracranial haemorrhage, neoplasms and inflammatory processes; pulmonary disease and certain medications are other frequent causes. In the case presented, the constellation of low serum osmolality, high urine osmolality and urine sodium in an euvolemic patient fulfilled diagnostic criteria for SIADH.

An important mechanistic link between CNS inflammation and inappropriate ADH release is the effect of inflammatory cytokines on neuroendocrine regulation. Interleukin‑6 (IL‑6) and other proinflammatory mediators can stimulate ADH secretion either directly at the hypothalamic‑pituitary axis or indirectly via altered osmoreceptor sensitivity. IL‑6 is frequently elevated in neuroinflammatory conditions and has been detected in the CSF of patients with active neuro‑BD. Although direct measurements were not available in this report, elevated systemic inflammatory markers (eg, CRP) and the temporal association between CNS inflammation and hyponatraemia support the plausibility that cytokine‑mediated ADH dysregulation contributed to SIADH in this patient.

The clinical consequences of hyponatraemia are varied and particularly relevant in neuroinflammatory settings. Even modest decreases in serum sodium can provoke non‑specific neurological symptoms (headache, malaise, confusion); more severe or rapid hyponatraemia may cause focal deficits, seizures or altered consciousness. Some manifestations can be atypical clues: persistent hiccups, as observed in this patient, have been described in association with severe hyponatraemia and SIADH secondary to CNS disease. Hyponatraemia can also lead to muscle cell injury and rhabdomyolysis, likely related to osmotic shifts and energy failure in muscle fibres; elevated creatine kinase should therefore prompt consideration of electrolyte causes in the appropriate clinical context. Recognising these links is important because correcting the underlying inflammatory trigger and cautiously managing the sodium disturbance together influence clinical recovery.

In summary, this case illustrates how systemic BD can present with concurrent aseptic CNS inflammation (neuro‑BD) and SIADH. The diagnosis rests on integrating characteristic mucocutaneous findings with CSF evidence of aseptic meningitis and typical laboratory criteria for SIADH, recognising that early brain MRI can be normal. Pathophysiologically, CNS inflammation with cytokine release (notably IL‑6) provides a biologically plausible mechanism for inappropriate ADH release, and the resulting hyponatraemia can both reflect and amplify neurological symptoms while producing systemic complications such as rhabdomyolysis.

## From Presentation to Provisional Diagnoses

The patient presented acutely with systemic and neurological symptoms that appropriately prompted a broad, time-sensitive differential. His constellation of fever, headache, general malaise, persistent hiccups and new arthralgia raised immediate concern for central nervous system (CNS) involvement—most importantly infectious meningoencephalitis—while the simultaneous presence of mucocutaneous lesions (oral aphthae and folliculitis‑like rash) suggested a systemic inflammatory process. Vital signs showed fever and marked tachycardia; clinical assessment of volume status was euvolemic, a key observation when interpreting his hyponatraemia. Early recognition that the hyponatraemia was hypotonic and the patient clinically euvolemic framed the endocrine evaluation from the outset.

Given the acuity and potential for rapid deterioration, the team prioritized life‑threatening and treatable causes. Infectious etiologies were highest on the initial list: HSV and VZV encephalitis because of fever and headache with CNS symptoms; bacterial meningitis despite the subacute picture because it demands urgent empiric therapy; and tuberculous meningitis as a consideration in indolent aseptic presentations. Noninfectious causes were also considered early—systemic inflammatory disorders (including vasculitides and sarcoidosis), paraneoplastic or primary CNS malignancy (notably lymphoma, which can present with nonspecific neurologic symptoms and elevated inflammatory markers), and metabolic or endocrine contributors to neurological complaints and hyponatraemia (hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency, or SIADH from CNS pathology or drugs).

Baseline laboratory testing helped focus the workup. Markedly elevated inflammatory markers (CRP and ESR) supported an inflammatory or infectious process. Critically, serum sodium was 124 mmol/L with low serum osmolality and inappropriately concentrated urine (urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg and urine sodium 88 mmol/L), while renal function, thyroid studies and morning serum cortisol were within acceptable ranges. This biochemical pattern—hypotonic hyponatraemia, euvolemia clinically, elevated urine osmolality and urine sodium, with preserved renal, thyroid and adrenal function—met diagnostic criteria for the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). The presence of elevated ADH in the setting of hypotonic hyponatraemia further corroborated SIADH. Recognising SIADH early was important because it both guided immediate management (fluid restriction, avoidance of rapid sodium correction) and provided a clue to CNS or systemic inflammation as an underlying driver.

Imaging and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis were performed promptly to evaluate suspected CNS infection or inflammation. Brain MRI obtained early in the course was normal, which did not exclude CNS pathology: acute neuroinflammatory processes—particularly early aseptic meningitis—can have normal neuroimaging. CSF analysis showed a mildly elevated opening pressure, lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein without neutrophilic predominance; cultures and targeted PCR/antigen tests for bacterial pathogens, HSV and VZV were negative. This CSF profile was most consistent with aseptic meningitis. In the clinical context, aseptic meningitis raised the differential toward viral causes (although HSV/VZV tests were negative), autoimmune or inflammatory meningoencephalitis, and neuroinflammatory manifestations of systemic diseases.

While diagnostic testing was in progress, the team aligned interim management with the highest‑risk provisional diagnoses. Empiric broad‑spectrum antibacterial coverage (cefepime) was started after blood cultures to cover potential bacterial meningitis or severe systemic bacterial infection. When the patient’s headache and vertigo worsened, empiric acyclovir was added to cover possible HSV encephalitis until negative CSF viral studies allowed discontinuation. These empiric steps reflect standard practice: when CNS infection is suspected, prompt empiric antimicrobial therapy should not be delayed pending confirmatory tests.

A pivotal evolution in the clinical picture occurred with the appearance on day 5 of a painful scrotal ulcer. Coupled with the existing oral aphthae and folliculitis‑like rash, this finding shifted diagnostic emphasis toward Behçet’s disease (BD). The genital ulcer is relatively specific for BD and, when considered alongside the mucocutaneous lesions and systemic inflammation, raised the likelihood of systemic BD with neurological involvement (acute neuro‑BD). Reframing the case in this way made the previously noted aseptic CSF findings and the SIADH more readily reconciled as manifestations of an inflammatory CNS process rather than primary infectious, neoplastic, or metabolic disorders.

In summary, the early phase of this case illustrates a methodical approach: rapid stabilisation and empiric therapy aimed at the most dangerous and treatable causes (bacterial and herpetic CNS infection), targeted investigations to distinguish infectious from inflammatory or malignant etiologies (MRI, CSF studies, focused serologies), and careful interpretation of hyponatraemia in the context of volume status and laboratory indices that pointed to SIADH. The later emergence of a genital ulcer and the aggregation of mucocutaneous features permitted refinement of the provisional diagnosis to systemic BD with acute neuro‑BD, explaining the aseptic meningitis pattern and offering a unifying explanation for the SIADH.

## Nailing the Diagnoses: Applying Criteria and Diagnostic Reasoning

Establishing the three linked diagnoses in this patient required integrating objective diagnostic criteria with exclusion of mimics and a temporal synthesis of clinical, laboratory and imaging data. Each diagnosis — definite Behçet’s disease (BD), acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD), and the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) — was supported by specific findings in the case and strengthened by the absence of alternative explanations.

Definite Behçet’s disease: application of formal criteria
The diagnosis of systemic BD was made using the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, which assigns weighted points to characteristic manifestations. This patient had recurrent oral aphthae (2 points), later-developed genital aphthosis (2 points) and folliculitis‑type skin lesions (1 point), giving a total of 5 points (≥4 required for “definite” BD). These clinical signs are the cornerstone of the diagnosis; ancillary tests such as HLA‑B51 can be supportive but are neither required nor diagnostic. In this case the mucocutaneous findings were temporally linked to the acute presentation and satisfied the scoring threshold, allowing a confident classification of definite BD.

Acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease: pattern recognition and exclusion
Diagnosis of neuro‑BD relies on demonstrating neurological involvement in a patient with BD and excluding alternative causes. The patient presented with headache, worsening vertigo and other non‑focal CNS symptoms in the setting of newly recognized BD. Although parenchymal brain lesions (classically brainstem involvement) are common in neuro‑BD, normal early MRI does not rule it out: acute neuro‑BD can present as aseptic meningitis or meningoencephalitis without visible MRI abnormalities, particularly in the early phase. Here, CSF showed a sterile inflammatory pattern — mildly elevated opening pressure, protein elevation and lymphocytic pleocytosis — consistent with aseptic meningitis and supportive of CNS inflammation attributable to neuro‑BD. Crucially, a systematic exclusion of infectious, neoplastic and alternative autoimmune causes (detailed below) strengthened the interpretation that the CSF inflammation reflected neuro‑BD rather than another process.

SIADH: confirming the endocrinologic diagnosis
The laboratory and clinical data satisfied standard diagnostic elements for SIADH. The patient had true hypotonic hyponatraemia (serum Na 124 mmol/L with low serum osmolality), inappropriately concentrated urine (very high urine osmolality of 900 mOsm/kg) and elevated urinary sodium (88 mmol/L), indicating impaired free water excretion despite hypo‑osmolality. Clinically he was euvolemic, and other endocrine and renal causes were excluded: thyroid function tests were normal, morning serum cortisol was adequate, and renal function was preserved. ADH (vasopressin) level was elevated in the setting of hyponatraemia, consistent with inappropriate antidiuretic hormone activity. No diuretic use or other common drug triggers were identified. The combination of hypotonic hyponatraemia, concentrated urine, natriuresis, euvolemia and exclusion of thyroid/adrenal/renal causes meets conventional criteria for SIADH.

Excluding mimics: stepwise elimination of alternative explanations
A rigorous exclusion process was essential because infections, malignancy and other inflammatory conditions can mimic or cause similar findings. CNS infection was the first concern given fever and meningeal signs; CSF PCR and cultures were negative for HSV, VZV, bacterial pathogens and mycobacteria, and CSF cytology showed no malignant cells, making infectious meningitis and leptomeningeal malignancy unlikely. Neuroimaging (head CT and brain MRI) showed no mass lesions or characteristic findings to suggest primary CNS tumour or parenchymal neurosarcoidosis. Serum studies were non‑supportive of sarcoidosis (normal ACE, low‑normal lysozyme) and only a mild, non‑specific elevation of soluble IL‑2 receptor was found, which alone does not establish lymphoma. Autoimmune serologies were unrevealing. The absence of identifiable infectious agents, negative cytology, and non‑contributory imaging made alternative diagnoses less likely and increased the pretest probability that the neurological inflammation was related to BD.

Pathophysiologic synthesis: linking neuroinflammation and SIADH
Once SIADH and aseptic CNS inflammation were established and other causes excluded, a unifying pathophysiologic explanation became plausible. Neuroinflammatory states can stimulate inappropriate ADH release; inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin‑6 (IL‑6) are known to promote nonosmotic ADH secretion. Behçet’s disease, particularly during acute neuro‑inflammatory phases, is associated with elevated IL‑6 production in the CNS. Although IL‑6 was not measured in this patient, the marked systemic inflammatory response (very high CRP) and the temporal association — onset of hyponatraemia concurrent with CNS symptoms that improved as inflammatory symptoms resolved — support a model in which neuro‑BD inflammatory activity drove inappropriate ADH release and resultant SIADH.

Bringing it together: diagnostic confidence and clinical reasoning
In sum, the diagnosis of definite BD rested on validated clinical criteria. The diagnosis of acute neuro‑BD was supported by compatible neurological symptoms, CSF evidence of aseptic meningitis and careful exclusion of infectious, neoplastic and other autoimmune causes, recognizing that early MRI can be normal. SIADH was confirmed by standard biochemical criteria and exclusion of alternative endocrine or drug‑related causes. The timing and resolution of hyponatraemia in parallel with treatment of BD and improvement in CNS symptoms provided further circumstantial evidence linking neuroinflammation to ADH dysregulation. This structured approach — apply formal criteria where available, document objective evidence of organ involvement, and systematically exclude mimics — is the framework that allowed confident synthesis of the disparate data into the three final diagnoses in this case.

## Management Strategy and Rationale

This patient’s management balanced three simultaneous priorities: exclude and treat potential CNS infection, control the inflammatory disorder (Behçet’s disease with acute neuro‑BD), and safely correct SIADH‑related hyponatraemia while minimising iatrogenic harm. Each decision was guided by the initial presentation (fever, headache, mucocutaneous lesions, hyponatraemia), evolving diagnostic data (negative microbiology, aseptic CSF with lymphocytic pleocytosis, normal MRI) and the patient’s comorbidities.

Empiric therapy and prompt de‑escalation
Given the combination of fever, meningeal symptoms and hyponatraemia, early empiric antimicrobial coverage was appropriate. Broad‑spectrum antibiotics (cefepime) were started after blood cultures to cover common bacterial pathogens while diagnostic studies were pending. Because viral encephalitis (HSV/VZV) remained a realistic early concern, empiric acyclovir was added when CNS involvement progressed. When CSF PCRs and cultures were repeatedly negative and clinical data pointed away from infectious aetiologies, antimicrobials were discontinued. This approach follows antimicrobial stewardship principles: start early when delay risks harm, but de‑escalate promptly when objective data (cultures, PCR, imaging, clinical trajectory) exclude infection to avoid unnecessary drug exposure and toxicity.

Rationale for colchicine monotherapy in this case
Colchicine was introduced once systemic BD and acute neuro‑BD were diagnosed. Its mechanisms—impairing neutrophil chemotaxis and activation and downregulating proinflammatory cytokine production (including IL‑1β and downstream IL‑6)—make it effective for mucocutaneous BD manifestations and potentially helpful in limiting inflammatory mediator–driven effects in CNS inflammation. In routine practice colchicine is standard for oral/genital ulcers and erythema/folliculitis in BD; it is not considered first‑line for parenchymal neuro‑BD, but it is commonly used adjunctively.

In this patient, several features supported a trial of colchicine as monotherapy rather than immediate high‑dose corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants: the neurological syndrome resembled aseptic meningitis without focal deficits, MRI was normal (no parenchymal brainstem lesions), CSF inflammation was mild, and the diagnostic picture for BD had just emerged. Given the risks of systemic corticosteroids and immunosuppressants—particularly in a patient with poorly controlled diabetes—the team elected a conservative, steroid‑sparing approach. The rapid clinical and biochemical improvement after colchicine initiation (resolution of fever and mucocutaneous lesions, gradual improvement in neurological symptoms and normalization of sodium) supports that colchicine either treated the inflammatory process or the disease followed a self‑limited course; spontaneous recovery can occur in meningoencephalitis‑type presentations of neuro‑BD, so causality cannot be proven here.

Standard care for acute neuro‑BD and how this case differs
Standard management for acute parenchymal neuro‑BD typically involves high‑dose corticosteroids (for example, IV methylprednisolone pulses followed by an oral taper) often combined with steroid‑sparing immunosuppressants (azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, biologics) for severe or relapsing disease. This strategy is based on observational data showing improved neurologic outcomes and reduced relapse risk with aggressive immunosuppression. The present case is atypical because the neuro‑inflammatory manifestations were relatively mild, MRI was negative, and the team successfully avoided systemic steroids and other immunosuppressants. This highlights that therapy must be individualized: milder neuro‑BD presentations may respond to less intensive treatment, but clinicians should be prepared to escalate promptly if there is neurological progression or imaging/paraclinical evidence of parenchymal involvement.

SIADH management and precautions for sodium correction
The diagnosis of SIADH (euvolemic hyponatraemia, low plasma osmolality, inappropriately concentrated urine and elevated urine sodium) requires careful correction to avoid neurologic complications of both hyponatraemia and overly rapid correction (osmotic demyelination). Conservative management was appropriate here because the patient was clinically euvolemic, had no severe neurologic deterioration, and sodium was modestly reduced (124 mmol/L). Measures employed included fluid restriction to <800 mL/day, close daily (or more frequent) serum sodium monitoring, and avoidance of rapid correction.

Principles applied (and generally recommended limits): aim for a safe rise in serum sodium of no more than 8–10 mmol/L in the first 24 hours (some guidelines recommend ≤8 mmol/L) and ≤18 mmol/L over 48 hours, adjusting the plan if neurologic symptoms worsen. Indications for more aggressive therapy—hypertonic (3%) saline boluses—include severe or life‑threatening neurologic symptoms (seizures, obtundation, coma). Vasopressin receptor antagonists (vaptans) can be considered in chronic or euvolemic SIADH that is symptomatic and refractory to fluid restriction, but they require careful monitoring because they can produce rapid sodium rises and are relatively contraindicated in certain settings; they were not necessary here. The team avoided hypertonic saline and vaptans because the patient improved with conservative measures and immunomodulation.

Monitoring for complications related to hyponatraemia and inflammation
The clinical team monitored for known complications of hyponatraemia and systemic inflammation. Notably, the patient had elevated creatine kinase consistent with rhabdomyolysis, which may be triggered or exacerbated by severe hyponatraemia. Recognising this association prompted serial CK measurements, renal function monitoring and appropriate supportive care to reduce risk of acute kidney injury. Persistent hiccups in this case were an important clinical clue suggesting significant hyponatraemia and/or brainstem irritation; clinicians should view otherwise nonspecific symptoms (hiccups, nausea, malaise) in the context of electrolyte disturbances. Daily neurologic assessments and frequent electrolyte checks allowed safe titration of therapy and early detection of deterioration.

Supportive care and management of comorbidities
Local treatments were used for mucocutaneous disease: topical corticosteroid ointment for oral aphthae and silver sulfadiazine for genital ulcers to speed symptomatic recovery and prevent secondary infection. Rehabilitation addressed deconditioning and functional decline during the hospital stay. Given the patient’s poorly controlled diabetes (glucose 14.2 mmol/L, HbA1c 8.2%), metabolic control was deferred until the acute inflammatory and hyponatraemic phases were stabilised; after recovery from the acute episode, appropriate antidiabetic therapy (biguanide) was initiated to reduce infection risk and improve long‑term outcomes. Throughout care, the team balanced infection risk from immunosuppression against the need to control inflammation.

Summary
The management combined early empiric antimicrobial therapy with rapid de‑escalation when infection was excluded, conservative but vigilant SIADH treatment (fluid restriction and close monitoring) to avoid rapid sodium correction, and a trial of colchicine monotherapy aimed at treating BD‑related inflammation while avoiding corticosteroids in a patient with diabetes and a relatively mild neuro‑inflammatory phenotype. Frequent reassessments allowed safe avoidance of aggressive immunosuppression; had the patient’s neurologic status or imaging shown progression, escalation to corticosteroids and additional immunosuppressants would have been indicated. The case illustrates patient‑tailored decision making grounded in diagnostic data, risk–benefit assessment, and close monitoring for complications.

## Outcomes, Follow-up, and Key Clinical Lessons

Clinical trajectory and in-hospital outcome
The patient experienced a rapid clinical response after initiation of colchicine on hospital day 8. Fever, appetite, mucocutaneous lesions and constitutional symptoms began improving within 24 hours of therapy, and neurological complaints (headache, vertigo, persistent hiccups) gradually resolved over the following two weeks. Serum sodium rose from 124 mmol/L to a normal value (136 mmol/L) by day 16 alongside a marked fall in measured ADH (from 22.4 pg/mL to 1.8 pg/mL). The elevated muscle enzymes (CK) and liver enzymes returned to normal as hyponatraemia corrected and systemic inflammation subsided. No hypertonic saline, vasopressin antagonists or systemic corticosteroids were required. After approximately one month of inpatient care (during which conservative fluid restriction and careful sodium monitoring were maintained), he was discharged with continued colchicine.

Long-term outcome
On follow-up, the patient remained clinically well on colchicine monotherapy for roughly four years without recurrence of mucocutaneous or neurological manifestations of Behçet’s disease. During this period his diabetes was later managed with a biguanide, achieving good glycaemic control. No subsequent episodes of hyponatraemia or neuro-BD relapse were reported in the follow-up interval described in the case.

Follow-up priorities for similar patients
After an acute episode of systemic BD complicated by neuro-BD and SIADH, follow-up should be structured and symptom-directed:

- Monitor for BD relapse: regular assessment for recurrent oral or genital ulcers, new skin lesions, or systemic symptoms. Frequency should be individualized but initially more frequent (eg, every 1–3 months), then spaced if stable.
- Neurological surveillance: periodic neurological exams and re-evaluation if new or worsening headaches, cognitive/behavioural changes, focal deficits, dizziness, or persistent hiccups occur. Repeat MRI and CSF studies are indicated if the clinical picture suggests relapse or new focal signs.
- Electrolyte monitoring: check serum sodium promptly when CNS symptoms recur or if there are suggestive features (nausea, confusion, hiccups, weakness). Early screening urine osmolality and urine sodium should be obtained to evaluate suspected hyponatraemia.
- Ophthalmology screening: baseline and interval ophthalmologic exams are recommended even when uveitis is absent at presentation because ocular involvement can develop later.
- Medication review and adherence: ensure ongoing colchicine adherence and evaluate need to escalate therapy if disease activity recurs.

Teaching points and common pitfalls
- Consider neuro-Behçet’s disease in any patient with BD who presents with non-specific CNS symptoms (headache, behavioural change, vertigo, persistent hiccups), even when such neurological manifestations are uncommon at initial diagnosis. Mucocutaneous findings may be evolving—repeated skin/genital examinations can be diagnostic.
- A normal early brain MRI does not exclude acute neuro-BD. Parenchymal lesions, particularly in the brainstem or hypothalamic regions, may be absent early or when presentation is limited to aseptic meningitis; CSF analysis remains important.
- Apply the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease promptly as mucocutaneous signs appear. Clinical scoring avoids overreliance on ancillary tests (eg, HLA-B51 is supportive but not diagnostic).
- Recognise SIADH using the classic diagnostic pattern: hypotonic hyponatraemia with euvolemia, low serum osmolality, inappropriately elevated urine osmolality and urine sodium, and exclusion of adrenal/thyroid dysfunction or diuretic use. In this case the elevated ADH level supported the diagnosis.
- Be alert for clues to significant hyponatraemia beyond cognitive symptoms—persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis (marked CK elevation) can be manifestations of profound hyponatraemia and should prompt electrolyte evaluation.
- Avoid indiscriminate testing: use targeted investigations guided by clinical likelihood. Non-specific elevations (eg, sIL-2R) are commonly seen with inflammation and can mislead if interpreted out of context.

When to escalate therapy beyond colchicine
Colchicine is effective for mucocutaneous BD and may be sufficient in mild, self-limited neuro-BD as illustrated here; however, escalation to systemic corticosteroids and additional immunosuppressants is indicated when the neurological disease is more severe or progressive. Specific triggers to escalate include:

- New or worsening focal neurological deficits (motor weakness, cranial nerve palsies, visual loss).
- Radiographic evidence of parenchymal CNS lesions on MRI, particularly progressive or mass-like lesions.
- Rapidly progressive clinical course, severe encephalopathy, or significant functional decline.
- Failure to improve or clinical deterioration despite initial therapy.
- Recurrent neuro-BD relapses while on appropriate first-line therapy.

In those situations, high-dose corticosteroids with or without steroid-sparing immunosuppressants (eg, azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, anti-TNF therapy depending on severity and phenotype) are commonly used; choice should be individualized and guided by multidisciplinary discussion.

Bottom line
This case demonstrates that careful clinical assessment, timely recognition of evolving mucocutaneous signs, and targeted management of SIADH permit successful recovery from an early episode of neuro-BD without immediate need for corticosteroids. Close follow-up with attention to neurologic symptoms, ocular screening, and sodium monitoring is essential to detect relapse or complications that would mandate escalation of immunosuppressive therapy.
