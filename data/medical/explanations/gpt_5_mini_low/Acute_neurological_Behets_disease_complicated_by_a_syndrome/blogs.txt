Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# Two Diagnoses, One Presentation: Recognizing Concurrent Behçet’s and Neuro‑Behçet’s at the Front Door

What makes this case surprising? A man in his 60s walks in with fever, headache and non‑specific constitutional symptoms and ends up diagnosed with definite Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro‑Behçet’s (neuro‑BD) at the same time — complicated by frank SIADH. Most patients develop neurologic BD years after mucocutaneous disease; seeing both appear concurrently is uncommon. Even more striking: brain MRI was normal, CSF showed only mild aseptic meningitis, and colchicine alone (no systemic steroids or other immunosuppression) was followed by resolution of mucocutaneous lesions, neurologic symptoms and hyponatraemia.

This vignette is a great reminder for ED and admitting teams: don’t get tunnel‑visioned into “infectious meningitis only” when you have fever + headache + hyponatraemia. Look for dermatologic and mucosal clues, apply the BD criteria, remember that neuro‑BD can present with minimal imaging findings, and think about SIADH as a downstream complication of CNS inflammation.

## Why BD and neuro‑BD rarely debut together — and why that matters
- Typical natural history: recurrent oral/genital ulcers and skin lesions precede major organ involvement; neuro‑BD often appears years after the initial mucocutaneous phase. So when neuro symptoms appear early, clinicians often default to infection, malignancy or other autoimmune diagnoses, and BD is missed.
- Clinical consequence: missed diagnosis delays targeted therapy and a focused search for associated problems (ocular involvement, vascular lesions, and — relevant here — endocrine complications like SIADH).

## Bedside clues that should pivot you from infection‑first to an inflammatory BD frame
- Genital aphthae: highly specific for BD. In this case a painful scrotal ulcer appeared during admission and was pivotal for clinching the diagnosis.
- Oral aphthae + folliculitis‑like papules: the pattern of recurrent oral ulcers plus acneiform/folliculitis lesions should raise BD suspicion.
- Systemic inflammatory markers out of proportion: very high CRP/ESR without clear bacterial focus should broaden the differential to include autoinflammatory causes.
- Neurologic features of neuro‑BD can be non‑specific: headache, vertigo, behavioral changes, persistent hiccups — the latter can also be a red flag for severe hyponatraemia.
- Hyponatraemia with low serum osmolality, inappropriately concentrated urine and elevated urine sodium — think SIADH, especially with concurrent CNS inflammation.

## Applying the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease at the bedside
- The International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD) are clinical and additive. Key items and points (practical summary):
  - Oral aphthosis = 2 points
  - Genital aphthosis = 2 points (very specific)
  - Skin lesions (erythema nodosum, pseudofolliculitis, papulopustular lesions) = 1 point
  - Eye lesions, vascular lesions, and positive pathergy each score as well
  - Total ≥4 points = definite BD
- In this case: oral aphthae (2), genital ulcer (2), folliculitis‑like rash (1) = 5 points → definite BD. Use the score early when mucocutaneous findings are present alongside neurologic symptoms.

## Neuro‑Behçet’s — the diagnostic traps
- MRI can be normal early. Parenchymal lesions classically involve brainstem, basal ganglia and diencephalon, but acute meningoencephalitic presentations may have only CSF abnormalities (mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, raised protein).
- CSF: mild lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein are compatible with neuro‑BD but are non‑specific. Exclude infectious causes (HSV/VZV, TB, bacteria) before attributing to BD.
- HLA‑B51 supports but is neither necessary nor sufficient — the diagnosis is clinical.

## SIADH in CNS inflammation — spot the connection
- SIADH is a well‑recognized cause of euvolemic hyponatraemia. CNS inflammation (meningitis, encephalitis, neurosarcoid, malignancy) can drive ADH release.
- Clues in this case consistent with SIADH:
  - Hypoosmolar hyponatraemia with high urine osmolality (900 mOsm/kg) and high urine sodium (88 mmol/L)
  - Elevated ADH level in the setting of euvolemia
  - Clinical symptoms included persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis (CK elevation), both can be seen with profound hyponatraemia.
- Mechanistic thought: inflammatory cytokines (eg, IL‑6) can stimulate ADH secretion. While causality cannot be proven here, the temporal relationship and resolution with anti‑inflammatory therapy support an association.

## Treatment and practical management points for ED/admitting teams
- Initial approach is pragmatic: when febrile headache + meningitic signs = start empirical antimicrobials until infection is excluded. This case did that (cefepime, then acyclovir empirically).
- Don’t delay dermatologic/genital exam. New or evolving mucocutaneous findings may reframe the diagnosis rapidly.
- Manage SIADH supportively unless severe/quadriplegic symptoms or seizures:
  - Fluid restriction (eg <800 mL/day here) and close sodium monitoring may suffice.
  - Avoid overly rapid correction; hypertonic saline or vaptans reserved for severe/symptomatic hyponatraemia or failure of conservative measures.
- Neuro‑BD treatment standards:
  - High‑dose corticosteroids are the typical mainstay for acute neuro‑BD; additional immunosuppressants may be required for parenchymal disease or relapsing disease.
  - Colchicine is established for mucocutaneous BD but is typically not considered effective for major organ (CNS) disease. In this case, colchicine monotherapy was followed by clinical improvement; spontaneous resolution cannot be excluded. Use colchicine for mucocutaneous disease, but be cautious about relying on it alone for neuro‑BD without neurology/rheumatology input.
- Always involve neurology and rheumatology/vasculitis teams early for confirmed or strongly suspected neuro‑BD.

## When to pivot from infection‑first to inflammatory CNS thinking — a checklist
- Fever + headache + CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis, but:
  - Negative PCR/antigen tests for common viral etiologies and negative cultures
  - No focal imaging lesion or normal early MRI
- Presence of mucocutaneous signs: oral ulcers, genital ulcers, folliculitis/papulopustular lesions
- Marked systemic inflammation without a focal infectious source
- New hyponatraemia with SIADH physiology or unexplained persistent hiccups/rhabdomyolysis
- If several of the above are present → add BD/neuro‑BD to your differential and consult specialty teams

## Clinical pearls
- Genital aphthae have high diagnostic specificity for Behçet’s — actively look for them (ask and exam).
- Normal brain MRI does not rule out acute neuro‑Behçet’s; CSF can show only mild aseptic inflammation.
- SIADH can be a complication of CNS inflammation including neuro‑BD; watch for concentrated urine and persistent hiccups as clinical clues.
- Colchicine is effective for mucocutaneous BD and may help inflammatory cytokine‑mediated phenomena, but acute neuro‑BD usually requires corticosteroids/immunosuppression — consult specialists before relying on colchicine alone.
- Apply ICBD scoring early when mucocutaneous lesions are present — it can change your diagnostic trajectory.

Take‑home messages
- Always examine mucosa and skin in febrile headache presentations — oral and genital ulcers can be the diagnostic pivot toward BD.
- A normal MRI does not exclude neuro‑Behçet’s; integrate clinical findings and CSF results.
- Consider SIADH in patients with CNS inflammation and hyponatraemia; manage conservatively while treating the underlying inflammatory process.
- Early specialty involvement (neurology/rheumatology) is key when BD or neuro‑BD is suspected — treatment can require steroids and steroid‑sparing agents despite sometimes apparent improvement with colchicine.

This case is a practical lesson: two diagnoses can present as one syndrome at the front door. Look beyond infection, do a focused dermatologic exam, interpret CSF/MRI in context, and be alert for endocrine complications like SIADH.



# Normal MRI, Abnormal Patient: Diagnosing Acute Neuro‑Behçet’s When Imaging Is Silent

What makes this case worth bookmarking? A man in his 60s came in with fever, headache, persistent hiccups, mucocutaneous ulcers and marked hyponatraemia. Brain MRI was normal. CSF showed aseptic meningitis. He met criteria for systemic Behçet’s disease and was diagnosed with acute neuro‑Behçet’s (neuro‑BD) complicated by SIADH. Colchicine alone — no steroids or other immunosuppressants — coincided with clinical recovery and normalization of sodium. This is a neat reminder that a “clean” MRI does not rule out clinically important CNS inflammation, and that extra‑CNS clues (skin/genital ulcers, hyponatraemia patterns) can be the key to the diagnosis.

Why this matters
- Neuro‑Behçet’s often crops up years after mucocutaneous disease, but it can present concurrently — and sometimes only as aseptic meningitis without parenchymal lesions. Early MRI may be entirely normal.
- Anchoring on a negative MRI risks missing treatable inflammatory CNS disease. The history, exam (including careful mucocutaneous inspection), CSF, and targeted labs are crucial.
- Hyponatraemia in this case was due to SIADH likely driven by CNS inflammation. Recognising SIADH and its clues (hiccups, rhabdomyolysis) prevented mismanagement.

Clues from the case you can use tomorrow
- Presentation: fever, headache, non‑focal meningitic symptoms (hiccups, malaise, vertigo), acute onset mucocutaneous lesions (oral aphthae, folliculitis‑like rash, later genital ulcer).
- CSF pattern supportive of neuro‑inflammation: mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL), elevated protein (0.97 g/L), mildly raised opening pressure (17 cm H₂O), sterile cultures and negative viral PCRs. This is compatible with aseptic meningitis and, in the appropriate clinical context, neuro‑BD.
- SIADH diagnostic features in this patient: hypotonic hyponatraemia (Na 124 mmol/L, serum osmolality low), concentrated urine (urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg), urine sodium high (88 mmol/L), euvolaemic on exam, normal thyroid and adrenal testing, elevated ADH (22.4 pg/mL) that later fell with clinical improvement.
- Imaging: normal brain MRI does not exclude neuro‑BD. Parenchymal lesions (brainstem, basal ganglia) are common but not universal — aseptic meningitis is a known presentation.

When to suspect neuro‑Behçet’s despite a normal MRI
- New CNS symptoms in a patient with mucocutaneous features consistent with BD (recurrent oral/genital ulcers, folliculitis‑like skin lesions), especially when other causes are excluded.
- CSF showing lymphocytic pleocytosis ± elevated protein with negative microbiology.
- Systemic inflammatory markers are high (CRP, ESR) and alternative explanations (infection, malignancy) have been reasonably excluded.
- Concurrent endocrine changes suggesting central involvement (SIADH) that correlate temporally with CNS symptoms.

How to structure your work‑up and not miss the diagnosis
1. Do a focused history and exam looking for mucocutaneous BD features — they may evolve over days (genital ulcers in this case appeared later).
2. Early lumbar puncture: cell count and differential, protein, glucose, opening pressure, bacterial cultures, HSV/VZV PCR, fungal/TB tests when indicated, and cytology if malignancy suspected.
3. Baseline labs to evaluate hyponatraemia: serum/urine osmolality, urine sodium, TSH, cortisol. Consider measuring ADH if available.
4. Use MRI, but know its limits. If initial MRI is normal but clinical suspicion remains high:
   - Repeat MRI with contrast in 1–2 weeks (or sooner if worsening) — parenchymal lesions can be delayed.
   - Consider spinal imaging if focal signs or spinal symptoms develop.
5. Exclude mimics: HSV/VZV encephalitis, bacterial meningitis, TB, neurosarcoidosis (ACE/sIL‑2R may help but have limited sensitivity/specificity), leptomeningeal malignancy (CSF cytology, MRI with contrast), autoimmune encephalitis (antibody testing guided by phenotype).
6. HLA‑B51 can support the diagnosis but is not required — rely on clinical International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease.

Therapeutic implications — practical points
- Acute neuro‑BD is often treated with high‑dose corticosteroids and, when indicated, additional immunosuppression. However, aseptic meningitis‑predominant presentations can sometimes be self‑limited or responsive to less aggressive therapy.
- Colchicine is standard for mucocutaneous BD but is not generally considered sufficient for major organ involvement; this case responded to colchicine monotherapy, but that may not be generalisable.
- Treat SIADH conservatively first: fluid restriction, monitor sodium closely, avoid rapid correction. Hypertonic saline or vasopressin antagonists reserved for severe or refractory cases.
- Watch for hyponatraemia complications — hiccups, rhabdomyolysis (elevated CK in this patient), altered mental status — and manage accordingly.

Red flags that should push you away from benign neuro‑inflammation and toward alternative diagnoses
- Rapidly progressive focal deficits, seizures, severe consciousness impairment — think HSV, bacterial abscess, large parenchymal lesions, or extensive autoimmune encephalitis.
- Neutrophilic CSF pleocytosis, very low CSF glucose, or positive Gram stain/culture → bacterial meningitis or TB.
- Recurrent or progressive lymphocytic pleocytosis with mass lesions, systemic B symptoms or markedly raised sIL‑2R → consider lymphoma/leptomeningeal malignancy; pursue repeat imaging and CSF cytology.
- Pulmonary findings, hilar adenopathy or markedly raised ACE/sIL‑2R → neurosarcoidosis (MRI can be normal early; consider PET, biopsy if suspected).
- No mucocutaneous features and negative CSF infectious workup — broaden autoimmune/paraneoplastic workup as indicated.

Clinical pearls
- “Normal MRI” is not a clean bill of neurological health. In early neuro‑inflammatory disease, MRI can lag behind clinical and CSF findings.
- Always pair imaging with CSF when CNS infection/inflammation is on the differential; CSF can clinch the diagnosis when imaging is unrevealing.
- Look for non‑neurological clues — skin and genital exams are high‑yield. In BD, genital ulcers are particularly specific.
- Hyponatraemia with concentrated urine and euvolaemia = SIADH until proven otherwise; CNS inflammation is a well‑known cause.
- Avoid premature closure: repeat MRI and broaden testing only when guided by evolving signs/symptoms, not reflexively.

Key take‑home messages
- Acute neuro‑Behçet’s can present as aseptic meningitis with a normal early MRI. Diagnosis rests on the clinical picture plus supportive CSF findings and exclusion of mimics.
- Always examine for mucocutaneous BD features and use the International Criteria to guide diagnosis; HLA‑B51 is supportive but not required.
- SIADH may complicate neuro‑BD — recognise the lab pattern (low serum osmolality, inappropriately concentrated urine, high urine Na) and manage conservatively while treating the underlying inflammation.
- Don’t anchor on a negative MRI: repeat imaging, serial CSF, and careful clinical reassessment are your tools when CNS inflammation is suspected.

If you see a patient with fever, headache, sterile lymphocytic CSF and mucocutaneous ulcers — treat the patient, not the scan.



# Hyponatremia With Hiccups: SIADH as a Neuro‑Inflammatory Signal in Behçet’s

What makes this case interesting: an older man presenting with fever, headache and persistent hiccups who is ultimately diagnosed with concurrent systemic Behçet’s disease (BD), acute neuro‑Behçet’s (aseptic meningitis) and SIADH. Hyponatraemia here is not a red herring — it’s a potential clue to CNS inflammation. The patient’s hyponatraemia (Na 124 mmol/L) with high urine osmolality and high urine sodium, euvolemia on exam, and an elevated ADH concentration supported SIADH. All of this corrected alongside clinical improvement after starting colchicine — no steroids or other immunosuppression — suggesting a temporal link between CNS inflammation and ADH dysregulation.

Why this matters at the bedside
- Hyponatraemia is common and nonspecific. In a patient with fever/headache and mucocutaneous signs of BD, think neuro‑BD as a cause of SIADH rather than attributing low sodium solely to medications, dehydration or primary endocrine disease.
- Small, easily missed clues matter: persistent hiccups and unexplained rhabdomyolysis (CK elevation) in this case were manifestations related to severe hyponatraemia and systemic inflammation.
- Early recognition of SIADH in neuro‑inflammatory states guides safe, pragmatic management and avoids dangerous sodium swings.

Pathophysiology — plausible mechanism (grounded in the report)
- CNS inflammation (meningitis/meningoencephalitis) can provoke inappropriate ADH release. Interleukin‑6 (IL‑6) is a likely mediator: IL‑6 can stimulate ADH secretion and is often elevated in neuroinflammatory disorders. In BD, dysregulated monocyte activation causes IL‑6 production and IL‑6 has been measured in CSF of neuro‑BD patients in prior reports.
- The present case did not measure IL‑6, so causation is inferred from timing (neuro symptoms + SIADH) and CRP elevation as a proxy for active systemic inflammation.
- Bottom line: inflammation → cytokines (eg, IL‑6) → increased ADH → water retention and hyponatraemia (SIADH).

How to distinguish SIADH from other causes of hyponatraemia (practical, test‑driven)
- Clinical volume status: SIADH is euvolemic. Look for absence of edema, orthostatic changes, or signs of hypovolaemia.
- Essential lab pattern for SIADH:
  - Hypotonic hyponatraemia (low serum osmolality)
  - Inappropriately concentrated urine (urine osmolality >100–300 mOsm/kg; often >300 mOsm/kg)
  - Elevated urine sodium (>20–40 mmol/L on normal salt intake)
  - Normal adrenal and thyroid function (rule out cortisol deficiency and hypothyroidism)
- Exclude renal failure and diuretic use. Review meds (SSRIs, carbamazepine, some anticancer agents) and recent chemotherapeutics which can cause SIADH.
- In neuro‑BD specifically: consider CSF findings of aseptic meningitis and MRI (may be normal in early disease).

Bedside clues that should raise your index of suspicion
- Persistent hiccups unexplained by other causes — can be a clue to meningeal irritation and/or hyponatraemia severity.
- New or disproportionate lethargy, nausea, headache in a patient with mucocutaneous BD signs.
- Rhabdomyolysis (marked CK rise) without clear traumatic or metabolic cause — severe hyponatraemia can precipitate muscle injury.
- High ADH level (if available) in the context of hypoosmolar hyponatraemia supports SIADH.

Practical management strategy (what was done in the case and generalizable approach)
1. Immediate assessment and safety:
   - Check serum sodium, serum osmolality, urine osmolality and urine sodium.
   - Assess for seizures, profound somnolence, or severe neurologic deficit — treat as severe symptomatic hyponatraemia.
2. Initial conservative measures (appropriate for mild–moderate, non‑severe symptoms):
   - Fluid restriction (typical target <800–1000 mL/day). This worked in the case.
   - Treat the underlying cause (in this case, treating BD/neuro‑BD with colchicine coincided with improvement). Treating CNS inflammation may reduce cytokine‑driven ADH release.
   - Monitor serum sodium frequently (at least every 4–6 hours initially until stable).
3. When to escalate:
   - Severe neurological symptoms (seizure, obtundation, coma) → hypertonic saline (3%) bolus protocol per local guideline.
   - If sodium is falling or not improving with fluid restriction and patient is clinically at risk → consider hypertonic saline or vasopressin receptor antagonists (vaptans) guided by expertise and availability.
   - In neuroinflammatory disease, correcting the underlying inflammation (eg, corticosteroids or other immunosuppression) is often necessary for long‑term control. In this case, colchicine alone correlated with improvement; however corticosteroids are standard for many acute neuro‑BD presentations.
4. Avoid overcorrection (critical pitfall):
   - Aim for cautious correction: general targets are ≤8 mmol/L rise in serum Na over 24 hours (many recommend even ≤6–8 mmol/L/day in high‑risk patients), and ≤18 mmol/L over 48 hours is commonly cited as the upper limit. Patients at highest risk for osmotic demyelination (alcoholism, malnutrition, liver disease, very chronic hyponatraemia) warrant even more conservative correction.
   - If overcorrection occurs (eg, sodium rise >8–10 mmol/L in 24 h), consider relowering with desmopressin and free water (D5W) infusion per protocol.
5. Other therapies:
   - Vaptans (tolvaptan) can be effective for chronic SIADH but are generally avoided in severe acute neurological hyponatraemia where rapid shifts risk demyelination. Use specialist guidance.
   - Demeclocycline is not appropriate in acute settings and has renal adverse effects.

Pitfalls and pearls specific to inflammatory CNS settings
- Don’t assume normal MRI excludes neuroinflammatory disease. In acute neuro‑BD, MRI can be normal early on despite CSF pleocytosis and symptoms.
- High CRP alone is nonspecific; absence of IL‑6 measurement does not negate inflammatory causation.
- Overzealous correction of sodium in CNS disease is dangerous — osmotic demyelination risk is real. Monitor closely and have a plan to re‑lower sodium if needed.
- Treat the underlying inflammation—sometimes that’s the cure for the SIADH. In this case, colchicine monotherapy coincided with rapid improvement of symptoms and normalization of sodium and ADH. This is not proof that colchicine is universally adequate for neuro‑BD, but it illustrates that targeted anti‑inflammatory therapy can resolve inflammation‑driven SIADH.
- Look for “secondary” clues of hyponatraemia severity: persistent hiccups, nausea, muscle pain and elevated CK.

Concise clinical take‑home points
- In a patient with fever/headache and mucocutaneous findings of Behçet’s, hyponatraemia should prompt evaluation for SIADH and possible acute neuro‑Behçet’s.
- SIADH features: hypotonic euvolemic hyponatraemia, low serum osmolality, concentrated urine (high urine osmolality) and high urine sodium; exclude adrenal and thyroid causes.
- Persistent hiccups and unexplained rhabdomyolysis can be clinical clues to significant hyponatraemia in neuroinflammatory disease.
- Initial management: fluid restriction, treat underlying inflammation, frequent sodium monitoring. Escalate to hypertonic saline for severe symptoms; avoid rapid overcorrection (aim ≤6–8 mmol/L/24 h in high‑risk patients).
- Treat the CNS inflammation — resolving the trigger often normalises ADH. Colchicine worked in this case, but corticosteroids/immunosuppression remain mainstays for many acute neuro‑BD presentations.

Closing thought
Hyponatraemia is often dismissed as mundane, but in the right clinical context it’s a valuable signal. When fever, headache, mucocutaneous ulcers and hiccups walk into the ED together, think beyond infection and drugs — consider neuro‑Behçet’s with SIADH, treat cautiously, monitor aggressively, and address the inflammation driving the electrolyte disturbance.



# Targeted Testing, Not Shotgunning: Diagnostic Stewardship in Febrile Neuro‑Inflammation

What makes this case worth your attention? A man in his 60s arrived with fever, headache and mucocutaneous lesions — then developed aseptic meningitis and marked hyponatraemia from SIADH. The final diagnosis was systemic Behçet’s disease with acute neuro‑Behçet’s (neuro‑BD), made clinically once genital ulcers appeared. The team ran a battery of tests (ACE, sIL‑2R, broad autoimmune panels, HLA‑B51) that added little to diagnostic certainty, while the high‑value workup (CSF analysis, targeted PCRs, serial exams and basic endocrine testing) guided management. Colchicine alone — no high‑dose steroids or aggressive immunosuppression — coincided with clinical and biochemical recovery.

This is the kind of case that should make us pause before ordering “everything but the kitchen sink.” In febrile patients with neurological symptoms, it’s easy to reflexively shotgun labs and serologies. But indiscriminate testing breeds false positives, drives unnecessary downstream investigations, and rarely changes acute management. Let’s walk through a pragmatic, FOAM‑friendly approach to diagnostic stewardship in neuro‑inflammatory presentations — rooted in the case above.

Why indiscriminate testing is tempting — and risky
- Diagnostic uncertainty is uncomfortable. Clinicians often hope a broad panel will reveal the unexpected.
- Rare diseases (neurosarcoid, CNS lymphoma, paraneoplastic syndromes) are on every long differential for aseptic meningitis, tempting us to order ACE, sIL‑2R, paraneoplastic antibodies and imaging.
- But low‑prevalence conditions have low positive predictive value for screening tests. A mildly elevated sIL‑2R, for example, is nonspecific and can reflect general inflammation. ACE is neither sensitive nor specific for neurosarcoidosis. HLA‑B51 is supportive but not diagnostic of Behçet’s and won’t change acute management.

High‑value testing: what changes management in the ED/ward
- CSF basic analysis (opening pressure, cell count/differential, protein, glucose): immediate discriminator between bacterial, viral, TB and aseptic processes. In this case the CSF showed mild lymphocytic pleocytosis and raised protein — consistent with aseptic meningitis.
- CSF PCRs for HSV and VZV (and enterovirus if relevant): these are time‑sensitive. HSV encephalitis mandates immediate acyclovir; PCR results guide when to stop it.
- Blood and CSF cultures, Gram/cell stains: essential to rule out pyogenic infection before de‑escalation.
- Basic neuroimaging (CT head for safety if LP considered, MRI brain when available): look for focal parenchymal lesions, abscess, hemorrhage or mass. Note: early neuro‑BD can have normal MRI.
- Routine metabolic and endocrine tests: electrolytes (spot the hyponatraemia), serum and urine osmolality, urine sodium — these diagnose SIADH and change immediate management (fluid restriction vs hypertonic saline).
- ECG and basic labs (glucose, renal function, LFTs, CK): guide safe therapy and identify complications like rhabdomyolysis from severe hyponatraemia.

Tests that are low‑yield acutely (use selectively, not reflexively)
- ACE and sIL‑2R: consider only if neurosarcoidosis is a reasonable pretest diagnosis (consistent imaging, pulmonary sarcoid history, lymphadenopathy). Remember ACE lacks sensitivity; sIL‑2R can be raised in many inflammatory states.
- Broad autoimmune/paraneoplastic panels: appropriate when a subacute/chronic presentation, focal deficits, or neuroimaging strongly suggest autoimmune encephalitis or paraneoplastic process — not routine early in febrile aseptic meningitis.
- HLA‑B51: useful epidemiologically and supportive for Behçet’s, but it is neither necessary nor sufficient — and it rarely alters acute management.
- Extensive malignancy screening (CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, PET) without focal signs or persistently unexplained inflammatory markers: low pretest probability means low yield and can distract from treating the acute problem.

A practical framework for empiric therapy and de‑escalation
1. Initial assessment and safety
   - Stabilise ABCs, assess airway/neurologic status.
   - Rapidly check glucose, electrolytes, renal/hepatic function and coagulation (for LP safety).
   - Start empiric therapy if life‑threatening bacterial or viral CNS infection is possible.

2. Start targeted empiric antimicrobials when indicated
   - If suspicion for bacterial meningitis: empiric IV antibiotics ± adjunctive dexamethasone per local guidelines — do not delay antibiotics for imaging or LP if clinically suspected.
   - If focal encephalitis or altered mental state suspicious for HSV/VZV: start IV acyclovir immediately. CSF PCR will guide discontinuation.
   - Avoid broad immunosuppression (steroids, cyclophosphamide) until infection reasonably excluded or when there is clear indication (and ideally after microbiology results/consensus).

3. Parallel high‑value diagnostics
   - Obtain urgent CSF studies including HSV/VZV PCR and cultures.
   - Obtain MRI brain unless contraindicated; if normal, remember early neuro‑inflammatory disease can still be present.
   - Check serum and urine osmolality/urine Na if hyponatraemia is present — treat hyponatraemia appropriately while searching for cause.

4. Daily reassessment and early de‑escalation
   - Reassess empiric antimicrobials at 48–72 hours when microbiology and PCR results return. If CSF PCRs and cultures negative and a non‑infectious inflammatory diagnosis is emerging, stop antimicrobials.
   - Use evolution of clinical signs, serial exams and targeted test results to pivot towards immune‑directed therapy when warranted.
   - In the presented case, acyclovir was stopped after negative PCRs and colchicine initiated once Behçet’s became evident — a good example of de‑escalation guided by evolving clinical data.

Recognising when systemic features point the way
- Mucocutaneous signs are diagnostic shortcuts. Oral aphthae + genital ulcers + folliculitis‑like rash = Behçet’s in the right context. These findings changed the pretest probabilities and allowed focused therapy (colchicine).
- Don’t miss endocrine clues: persistent hiccups, anorexia, weakness and euvolemic hyponatraemia should prompt evaluation for SIADH. In neuroinflammation, treating the underlying inflammation often corrects the SIADH; meanwhile, standard hyponatraemia measures apply (fluid restriction, avoid rapid correction).

Clinical pearls from the case
- Normal MRI does not exclude acute neuro‑Behçet’s. Early parenchymal lesions can be absent; CSF and clinical context matter.
- Measure urine osmolality and urine sodium in any patient with hyponatraemia and suspected CNS inflammation — SIADH is a common mechanism and is treatable conservatively in many cases.
- Avoid reflexive ordering of ACE, sIL‑2R and broad autoimmune panels unless your pretest probability is reasonably high; these tests are better as confirmatory tests guided by imaging/clinical signs than as screening tools.
- Empiric antivirals (acyclovir) should be started early for suspected viral encephalitis, but stop once high‑quality testing (PCR) excludes HSV/VZV to reduce harm and cost.
- Management decisions should be driven by tests that will alter acute care. Ask with every test: “If this is positive, will I manage the patient differently today?”

Take‑home messages
- In febrile neuro‑inflammation, prioritise high‑value diagnostics: CSF basic studies and PCRs, targeted imaging, and basic metabolic/endocrine testing (electrolytes, serum/urine osmolality).
- Don’t shotgun low‑yield serologies (ACE, sIL‑2R, broad autoimmune panels, HLA‑B51) unless clinical features or imaging raise the specific suspicion; these tests rarely change immediate management and often lead to confusion.
- Start empiric antimicrobials when life‑threatening infection is plausible, but commit to daily reassessment and early de‑escalation using microbiology and evolving clinical data.
- Look for systemic clues (mucocutaneous lesions, endocrine disturbances like SIADH) — these may pivot the differential rapidly and allow focused therapy.
- Diagnostic stewardship is not about doing less care; it’s about doing the right tests at the right time so your decisions today are evidence‑based and patient‑centered.

Bottom line: in acute neuro‑inflammation, targeted testing guided by pretest probability, rapid high‑yield diagnostics and disciplined reassessment beat the shotgun approach every time.



# Colchicine Alone in Acute Neuro‑Behçet’s: Fluke or Future?

What’s surprising
A man in his 60s presented with fever, headache, mucocutaneous Behçet’s features and aseptic meningitis. He had SIADH with hyponatraemia, no focal deficits and a normal brain MRI. After diagnostic workup that excluded infection and malignancy, colchicine monotherapy (no systemic corticosteroids or other immunosuppression) was started and the patient improved quickly: fever and neurologic symptoms settled, mucocutaneous lesions resolved, ADH and sodium normalized, and no relapse was reported over years of follow‑up. This challenges the usual reflex to give steroids for acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) and prompts the question: is colchicine ever sufficient for early neuro‑BD (and its complications like SIADH)?

Why this case matters
- Neuro‑BD is usually treated aggressively with systemic corticosteroids ± other immunosuppressants because parenchymal CNS involvement can cause lasting deficits. Yet acute neuro‑BD can present as aseptic meningitis without MRI lesions, and the natural history may be self‑limited in some patients.
- Colchicine is well established for mucocutaneous BD but is not considered disease‑modifying for CNS involvement in guideline literature. Here colchicine alone coincided with remission of CNS inflammation and SIADH, suggesting a plausible steroid‑sparing role in selected presentations.
- The case is a reminder to integrate clinical phenotype (severity, focal deficits, imaging, CSF) into treatment decisions rather than automatic high‑dose steroids for every CNS complaint in BD.

Mechanistic plausibility for colchicine in neuro‑BD
Colchicine’s mechanisms align with key pathophysiology in BD:
- Inhibits microtubule polymerization → impaired neutrophil chemotaxis and activation. Neutrophilic overactivity is central to BD mucocutaneous lesions and likely contributes to inflammatory cascades in neuro‑BD.
- Downregulates inflammasome signalling and reduces IL‑1β and IL‑6 production. IL‑6 is implicated in CNS inflammation and stimulates ADH release — a plausible link to SIADH seen in this case.
- Alters endothelial and leukocyte interactions, which could blunt perivascular inflammation relevant to BD vasculitis.

So, although colchicine is not conventionally used as monotherapy for neuro‑BD, the pharmacology gives a biologic rationale for benefit in early, neutrophil/IL‑6–driven inflammation.

What the evidence says (short)
- Strong evidence supports colchicine for mucocutaneous and some articular BD manifestations.
- For neuro‑BD the literature is sparse: most series and reviews recommend high‑dose corticosteroids for acute parenchymal disease, often with additional immunosuppression (azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, TNF blockers) for severe or refractory cases.
- Colchicine has been reported as adjunctive therapy for BD but randomized or robust data showing colchicine monotherapy is effective for neuro‑BD are lacking. This case is an illustrative data point but not definitive proof.

When a colchicine‑first (or steroid‑sparing) approach might be reasonable
Consider colchicine monotherapy or a monitored steroid‑sparing trial only when the patient meets a combination of the following low‑risk features:
- Presentation consistent with acute aseptic meningitis: headache, fever, mild CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis, mildly elevated protein, without neutrophilic purulent picture.
- No focal neurological deficits, no altered consciousness, no seizures and no rapidly progressive signs.
- Brain MRI without parenchymal lesions, mass effect, brainstem involvement, or evidence of major vasculitic lesions.
- Hemodynamically stable and able to be admitted/observed closely.
- Reasonable exclusion of infectious etiologies (CSF studies negative or pending-but-low-suspicion; empirical antimicrobials handled per local practice).
- Patient factors that favour avoiding steroids (e.g., poorly controlled diabetes where steroids would cause harm) and when colchicine is not contraindicated.

Important caveat: this approach requires close inpatient monitoring with a low threshold for escalation to high‑dose corticosteroids and other immunosuppression if any of the following occur: new focal deficits, worsening headache/mental status, new MRI abnormalities, increasing CSF pleocytosis or metabolic encephalopathy from hyponatraemia despite correction.

How to identify patients who likely still need early steroids or additional immunosuppression
Immediate corticosteroids (and likely additional immunosuppression) are indicated when:
- Parenchymal neuro‑BD is present on imaging (brainstem, deep grey matter, large lesions).
- Focal neurological deficits, decreased level of consciousness, seizures, or rapidly progressive course.
- High CSF cellularity/protein with signs of significant intracranial inflammation or high opening pressure.
- Ocular involvement threatening vision (uveitis with vision loss), arterial aneurysm, or major organ involvement requiring systemic control.
- Failure to improve or clinical deterioration on a colchicine‑first plan within 24–72 hours.

Safety, monitoring and practical dosing matters with colchicine
Dose and duration
- Typical dosing for BD mucocutaneous disease: colchicine 0.5–1 mg/day (often 0.5 mg twice daily depending on tolerance and local practice). The exact dosing in the case report isn’t specified; use local BD guidance.
- Adjust dose for renal impairment and with significant drug interactions.

Baseline and follow‑up monitoring
- Baseline: renal function (creatinine), liver function tests (ALT/AST/bilirubin), CBC, baseline creatine kinase (especially if myalgia).
- Ongoing: check renal and hepatic function and CBC periodically (frequency individualized; sooner if symptoms), monitor for GI intolerance (nausea, diarrhoea), myopathy (muscle pain/weakness), and neuropathy.
- Monitor for drug interactions: major concerns with strong CYP3A4 inhibitors (e.g., clarithromycin, certain azoles, some protease inhibitors) and P‑glycoprotein inhibitors — these markedly increase colchicine toxicity risk.
- Be cautious with combined statin therapy; colchicine with statins may increase risk of myopathy/rhabdomyolysis, particularly in renal impairment.
- Watch closely for signs of bone marrow suppression (rare) or severe diarrhoea leading to volume/electrolyte problems.

Managing SIADH in neuro‑BD
- Treat the underlying inflammation; in this case correction occurred with colchicine + fluid restriction.
- Acute severe symptomatic hyponatraemia still mandates standard emergency therapy (hypertonic saline) regardless of etiology.
- Monitor sodium closely to avoid osmotic demyelination with overly rapid correction.
- Consider measuring and monitoring ADH/urine osmolality and sodium to guide fluid restriction and need for further therapy.

Clinical pearls
- Neuro‑Behçet’s is heterogeneous: aseptic meningitis without MRI lesions can behave differently from parenchymal brain disease. Tailor immunotherapy to phenotype and severity.
- Colchicine has biologic plausibility for helping neuro‑BD via neutrophil and cytokine modulation; it’s proven for mucocutaneous BD but unproven as sole therapy for CNS disease.
- A carefully selected, closely monitored steroid‑sparing trial may be reasonable in mild aseptic neuro‑BD—especially when steroids carry high risk—but requires clear criteria and low threshold for escalation.
- Always exclude infection before withholding steroids in suspected neuroinflammatory disease; start empiric antimicrobials if there’s diagnostic uncertainty.
- Monitor for colchicine toxicity: renal/hepatic dysfunction and interacting drugs raise the stakes.

Bottom line (takeaway bullets)
- Colchicine monotherapy led to clinical and biochemical recovery in one well‑worked‑up case of acute neuro‑BD presenting as aseptic meningitis with SIADH — intriguing but not practice‑changing alone.
- Consider colchicine as part of early management for selected, low‑risk neuro‑BD presentations (aseptic meningitis, normal MRI, no focal deficits) when close monitoring and infectious exclusion are possible.
- Do NOT substitute colchicine for steroids in moderate‑to‑severe neuro‑BD (parenchymal lesions, focal deficits, rapid progression, vision‑threatening disease).
- If pursuing a steroid‑sparing plan, have predetermined clinical triggers for escalation, and monitor sodium, renal/hepatic function, CBC and CK while watching for drug interactions.

This case opens the door to rethinking tailored immunotherapy for the milder end of the neuro‑BD spectrum. It should prompt caution, not complacency — colchicine alone may be an option in a narrow window, but steroids remain the mainstay for serious CNS involvement until more robust evidence emerges.



# Oral and Genital Ulcers in the Febrile Patient With Headache: Building the Right Differential

What makes this case interesting
A man in his 60s presents with fever, headache, oral aphthae, later a scrotal ulcer, aseptic meningitis on CSF, marked inflammatory markers, hyponatraemia from SIADH — ultimately diagnosed with definite Behçet’s disease (BD) with acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD). MRI was normal. Colchicine alone led to resolution of mucocutaneous lesions, neurological symptoms and SIADH without corticosteroids or other immunosuppression. This scenario is a great reminder that oral/genital ulcers in the febrile patient with neurological symptoms force you to do two things at once: (1) broaden the differential to include infectious and inflammatory mimics and (2) actively look for concurrent organ involvement (eye, CNS, vessels) that changes management urgency.

Clinical context and the diagnostic challenge
Patients with fever + headache + mucocutaneous ulcers sit at the crossroads of infectious neurology and systemic inflammatory disease. Early on, infection (HSV/VZV meningoencephalitis, disseminated HSV with mucosal lesions, primary syphilis, HIV seroconversion, bacterial meningitis with secondary mucocutaneous findings) usually leads the priority list because missing treatable CNS infection is catastrophic. But inflammatory multisystem diseases (Behçet’s, SLE, inflammatory bowel disease–related aphthae, Sweet’s syndrome, reactive arthritis) can present similarly and need a different treatment pathway. The featured case underscores that neuro‑inflammatory disease can provoke endocrine complications — SIADH — so be attuned to hyponatraemia and non‑specific clues like hiccups or altered appetite.

A practical approach to the febrile patient with oral/genital ulcers and headache

1) Immediate priorities (ED / early inpatient)
- Assess airway/breathing/circulation and neurological status. Look for meningism, focal deficits, seizures, reduced consciousness.
- Send “don’t miss” tests: blood cultures, CBC, electrolytes (sodium!), glucose, CRP/ESR, HIV, syphilis serology (RPR/TPPA), HSV/VZV PCR (CSF and/or swabs), consider NAAT from mucosal lesions if present.
- If meningitis/encephalitis suspected: perform urgent LP unless contraindicated. Start empiric antimicrobials/antivirals (eg, acyclovir + broad spectrum antibiotics) while awaiting results.
- Check serum osmolality and urine osmolality/urine sodium if hyponatraemia present — differentiate SIADH vs hypovolaemia.

2) Focused exam for diagnostic clues
- Inspect mucosal lesions: oral aphthae of BD tend to be painful, recurrent, shallow, round/oval with clean base (but are indistinguishable from aphthous ulcers clinically). Genital ulcers in BD are often deep and heal with scarring (classically scrotal or vulvar).
- Look for skin lesions: folliculitis‑like papules, erythema nodosum‑like lesions, pustules after needle prick (pathergy) suggest BD. Sweet’s shows tender neutrophilic plaques/pustules; drug eruptions may be morbilliform or fixed.
- Eye exam: anterior or posterior uveitis (painful red eye, photophobia, blurred vision) strongly points toward BD — ophthalmology urgently if suspected.
- Joints/GI/vascular: arthritis, abdominal pain/diarrhoea, and thromboses are systemic clues for IBD or vasculitis.
- Lymphadenopathy, weight loss, B symptoms, or persistent atypical lesions → consider malignancy or HIV.

3) How to distinguish the major mimics (practical signals)
- HSV 1/2
  - Lesions: grouped vesicles that ulcerate; can be painful; may have mucosal lesions with temporal lobe encephalitis (HSV‑1).
  - Tests: PCR from lesion or CSF; rapid response to acyclovir.
  - Clues against BD: episodic grouped vesicles, typical dermatomal or labial distribution, classic encephalitic MRI pattern.

- Syphilis
  - Lesions: primary chancre (painless), secondary mucocutaneous lesions are polymorphic (condyloma lata, mucous patches).
  - Tests: RPR/TPPA; CSF VDRL in neurosyphilis.
  - Clues against BD: painless primary ulcers, systemic rash including palms/soles, positive serology.

- HIV seroconversion
  - Lesions: aphthous ulcers can occur; systemic viral prodrome, diffuse rash, lymphadenopathy.
  - Tests: HIV p24/antibody, viral load.
  - Consider if risk factors present.

- Reactive arthritis
  - Lesions: mucocutaneous ulcers can occur, keratoderma blennorrhagicum; arthritis prominent; history of GI or GU infection.
  - Hx and cultures helpful.

- IBD‑associated aphthae
  - Lesions: oral ulcers may accompany chronic diarrhoea, weight loss, perianal disease.
  - GI symptoms and colonoscopy aid diagnosis.

- Sweet’s syndrome
  - Lesions: tender, erythematous papules/nodules/plaques often with fever; mucosal involvement less common.
  - Peripheral neutrophilia, brisk response to steroids.

- SLE
  - Lesions: painless mucosal ulcers, malar rash, multisystem features; serologies (ANA, dsDNA, complement) guide diagnosis.

- Drug eruptions (eg, Stevens‑Johnson)
  - Lesions: widespread mucocutaneous necrosis; usually clear drug trigger and systemic toxicity.

- Malignancy (eg, lymphoma)
  - Look for systemic B symptoms, persistent atypical lesions, abnormal imaging or cytology.

Behçet’s disease — what to look for
- Core features: recurrent oral aphthae (required in many criteria), genital ulcers, eye inflammation (uveitis), characteristic skin lesions (folliculitis‑like, erythema nodosum), pathergy positivity.
- Neuro involvement: occurs in ~10–20% of patients, usually years after diagnosis, but can present concurrently. Neuro‑BD commonly causes meningoencephalitis and parenchymal lesions (brainstem preference), but MRI can be normal early, and CSF may show aseptic meningitis.
- Extra tip: HLA‑B51 is associated but not diagnostic; the International Criteria rely on clinical features.
- Complications: neuro‑BD can provoke hyponatraemia via SIADH (CNS inflammation → ADH dysregulation; cytokines like IL‑6 implicated). Check sodium routinely in neuro presentations.

When to involve specialists early
- Infectious disease / neurology: any patient with fever + headache + mucosal ulcers who has CSF abnormalities, focal neuro signs, seizures, or reduced consciousness — start empiric therapy and involve neurologists/infectious disease early.
- Rheumatology: when systemic inflammatory disease is suspected (recurrent oral/genital ulcers + skin lesions + high inflammatory markers) or when diagnosis is uncertain after excluding infection. Early rheumatology input helps plan immunosuppression if required.
- Dermatology: useful for biopsy of atypical lesions, assessment of skin morphology (eg, pathergy testing, Sweet’s vs BD), and guidance on topical/systemic therapies.
- Ophthalmology: urgent if eye symptoms or if BD suspected — posterior or anterior uveitis requires prompt treatment to prevent vision loss.
- Endocrinology: if hyponatraemia is severe, refractory, or complicated (osmotic demyelination risk, refractory SIADH); otherwise general medical teams can manage standard SIADH protocols.

Management pearls from the case and general practice
- Don’t anchor on mucosal ulcers as “just aphthae” — in the right context they are a red flag for systemic disease.
- Always check sodium in CNS/inflammatory presentations. Hyponatraemia may be a clue to CNS inflammation (SIADH) and cause unexpected manifestations (hiccups, rhabdomyolysis).
- Early LP is essential when meningitis/encephalitis is in the differential — CSF guides therapy and excludes treatable infectious causes.
- MRI can be normal in early neuro‑BD — normal imaging does not exclude neuro involvement if CSF and clinical picture suggest CNS inflammation.
- Colchicine is effective for mucocutaneous BD and may help systemic inflammation (it modulates neutrophils and cytokines). However, for severe neuro‑BD most guidelines favour corticosteroids and additional immunosuppression. The presented patient improved on colchicine alone, but this is not the typical course and should not delay escalation when clinically indicated.
- Always avoid rapid sodium correction. In SIADH manage with fluid restriction, monitor sodium closely, and use hypertonic saline or vaptans only when severe neurologic symptoms or failure of conservative measures.

Key take‑home messages
- In a febrile patient with headache and oral/genital ulcers you must simultaneously consider CNS infection and systemic inflammatory disease; rapid LP and empiric coverage may be lifesaving.
- Behçet’s disease can present with mucocutaneous lesions and aseptic meningitis/neuro‑inflammation even when MRI is normal; look for characteristic skin/eye findings and recurrent ulcers.
- Hyponatraemia/SIADH can be a complication of neuroinflammation (including neuro‑BD); check serum and urine osmolality and monitor sodium carefully.
- Involve neurology/infectious disease promptly for suspected CNS infection; involve rheumatology, dermatology and ophthalmology early if BD or other multisystem inflammatory disease is suspected.
- Treat the immediate threats (infection, severe hyponatraemia, sight‑threatening uveitis) first; tailor immunosuppression after excluding infection.

Clinical pearl: recurrent, painful oral ulcers + deep genital ulcers + pustular/follicular skin lesions ± pathergy = Behçet’s until proven otherwise — always look for ocular or CNS involvement.
