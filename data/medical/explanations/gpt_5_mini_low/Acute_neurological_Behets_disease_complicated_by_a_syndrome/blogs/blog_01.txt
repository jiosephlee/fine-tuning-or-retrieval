Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# Two Diagnoses, One Presentation: Recognizing Concurrent Behçet’s and Neuro‑Behçet’s at the Front Door

What makes this case surprising? A man in his 60s walks in with fever, headache and non‑specific constitutional symptoms and ends up diagnosed with definite Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro‑Behçet’s (neuro‑BD) at the same time — complicated by frank SIADH. Most patients develop neurologic BD years after mucocutaneous disease; seeing both appear concurrently is uncommon. Even more striking: brain MRI was normal, CSF showed only mild aseptic meningitis, and colchicine alone (no systemic steroids or other immunosuppression) was followed by resolution of mucocutaneous lesions, neurologic symptoms and hyponatraemia.

This vignette is a great reminder for ED and admitting teams: don’t get tunnel‑visioned into “infectious meningitis only” when you have fever + headache + hyponatraemia. Look for dermatologic and mucosal clues, apply the BD criteria, remember that neuro‑BD can present with minimal imaging findings, and think about SIADH as a downstream complication of CNS inflammation.

## Why BD and neuro‑BD rarely debut together — and why that matters
- Typical natural history: recurrent oral/genital ulcers and skin lesions precede major organ involvement; neuro‑BD often appears years after the initial mucocutaneous phase. So when neuro symptoms appear early, clinicians often default to infection, malignancy or other autoimmune diagnoses, and BD is missed.
- Clinical consequence: missed diagnosis delays targeted therapy and a focused search for associated problems (ocular involvement, vascular lesions, and — relevant here — endocrine complications like SIADH).

## Bedside clues that should pivot you from infection‑first to an inflammatory BD frame
- Genital aphthae: highly specific for BD. In this case a painful scrotal ulcer appeared during admission and was pivotal for clinching the diagnosis.
- Oral aphthae + folliculitis‑like papules: the pattern of recurrent oral ulcers plus acneiform/folliculitis lesions should raise BD suspicion.
- Systemic inflammatory markers out of proportion: very high CRP/ESR without clear bacterial focus should broaden the differential to include autoinflammatory causes.
- Neurologic features of neuro‑BD can be non‑specific: headache, vertigo, behavioral changes, persistent hiccups — the latter can also be a red flag for severe hyponatraemia.
- Hyponatraemia with low serum osmolality, inappropriately concentrated urine and elevated urine sodium — think SIADH, especially with concurrent CNS inflammation.

## Applying the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease at the bedside
- The International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD) are clinical and additive. Key items and points (practical summary):
  - Oral aphthosis = 2 points
  - Genital aphthosis = 2 points (very specific)
  - Skin lesions (erythema nodosum, pseudofolliculitis, papulopustular lesions) = 1 point
  - Eye lesions, vascular lesions, and positive pathergy each score as well
  - Total ≥4 points = definite BD
- In this case: oral aphthae (2), genital ulcer (2), folliculitis‑like rash (1) = 5 points → definite BD. Use the score early when mucocutaneous findings are present alongside neurologic symptoms.

## Neuro‑Behçet’s — the diagnostic traps
- MRI can be normal early. Parenchymal lesions classically involve brainstem, basal ganglia and diencephalon, but acute meningoencephalitic presentations may have only CSF abnormalities (mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, raised protein).
- CSF: mild lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein are compatible with neuro‑BD but are non‑specific. Exclude infectious causes (HSV/VZV, TB, bacteria) before attributing to BD.
- HLA‑B51 supports but is neither necessary nor sufficient — the diagnosis is clinical.

## SIADH in CNS inflammation — spot the connection
- SIADH is a well‑recognized cause of euvolemic hyponatraemia. CNS inflammation (meningitis, encephalitis, neurosarcoid, malignancy) can drive ADH release.
- Clues in this case consistent with SIADH:
  - Hypoosmolar hyponatraemia with high urine osmolality (900 mOsm/kg) and high urine sodium (88 mmol/L)
  - Elevated ADH level in the setting of euvolemia
  - Clinical symptoms included persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis (CK elevation), both can be seen with profound hyponatraemia.
- Mechanistic thought: inflammatory cytokines (eg, IL‑6) can stimulate ADH secretion. While causality cannot be proven here, the temporal relationship and resolution with anti‑inflammatory therapy support an association.

## Treatment and practical management points for ED/admitting teams
- Initial approach is pragmatic: when febrile headache + meningitic signs = start empirical antimicrobials until infection is excluded. This case did that (cefepime, then acyclovir empirically).
- Don’t delay dermatologic/genital exam. New or evolving mucocutaneous findings may reframe the diagnosis rapidly.
- Manage SIADH supportively unless severe/quadriplegic symptoms or seizures:
  - Fluid restriction (eg <800 mL/day here) and close sodium monitoring may suffice.
  - Avoid overly rapid correction; hypertonic saline or vaptans reserved for severe/symptomatic hyponatraemia or failure of conservative measures.
- Neuro‑BD treatment standards:
  - High‑dose corticosteroids are the typical mainstay for acute neuro‑BD; additional immunosuppressants may be required for parenchymal disease or relapsing disease.
  - Colchicine is established for mucocutaneous BD but is typically not considered effective for major organ (CNS) disease. In this case, colchicine monotherapy was followed by clinical improvement; spontaneous resolution cannot be excluded. Use colchicine for mucocutaneous disease, but be cautious about relying on it alone for neuro‑BD without neurology/rheumatology input.
- Always involve neurology and rheumatology/vasculitis teams early for confirmed or strongly suspected neuro‑BD.

## When to pivot from infection‑first to inflammatory CNS thinking — a checklist
- Fever + headache + CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis, but:
  - Negative PCR/antigen tests for common viral etiologies and negative cultures
  - No focal imaging lesion or normal early MRI
- Presence of mucocutaneous signs: oral ulcers, genital ulcers, folliculitis/papulopustular lesions
- Marked systemic inflammation without a focal infectious source
- New hyponatraemia with SIADH physiology or unexplained persistent hiccups/rhabdomyolysis
- If several of the above are present → add BD/neuro‑BD to your differential and consult specialty teams

## Clinical pearls
- Genital aphthae have high diagnostic specificity for Behçet’s — actively look for them (ask and exam).
- Normal brain MRI does not rule out acute neuro‑Behçet’s; CSF can show only mild aseptic inflammation.
- SIADH can be a complication of CNS inflammation including neuro‑BD; watch for concentrated urine and persistent hiccups as clinical clues.
- Colchicine is effective for mucocutaneous BD and may help inflammatory cytokine‑mediated phenomena, but acute neuro‑BD usually requires corticosteroids/immunosuppression — consult specialists before relying on colchicine alone.
- Apply ICBD scoring early when mucocutaneous lesions are present — it can change your diagnostic trajectory.

Take‑home messages
- Always examine mucosa and skin in febrile headache presentations — oral and genital ulcers can be the diagnostic pivot toward BD.
- A normal MRI does not exclude neuro‑Behçet’s; integrate clinical findings and CSF results.
- Consider SIADH in patients with CNS inflammation and hyponatraemia; manage conservatively while treating the underlying inflammatory process.
- Early specialty involvement (neurology/rheumatology) is key when BD or neuro‑BD is suspected — treatment can require steroids and steroid‑sparing agents despite sometimes apparent improvement with colchicine.

This case is a practical lesson: two diagnoses can present as one syndrome at the front door. Look beyond infection, do a focused dermatologic exam, interpret CSF/MRI in context, and be alert for endocrine complications like SIADH.
