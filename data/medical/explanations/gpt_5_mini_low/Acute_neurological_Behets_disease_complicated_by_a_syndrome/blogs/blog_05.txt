Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# Colchicine Alone in Acute Neuro‑Behçet’s: Fluke or Future?

What’s surprising
A man in his 60s presented with fever, headache, mucocutaneous Behçet’s features and aseptic meningitis. He had SIADH with hyponatraemia, no focal deficits and a normal brain MRI. After diagnostic workup that excluded infection and malignancy, colchicine monotherapy (no systemic corticosteroids or other immunosuppression) was started and the patient improved quickly: fever and neurologic symptoms settled, mucocutaneous lesions resolved, ADH and sodium normalized, and no relapse was reported over years of follow‑up. This challenges the usual reflex to give steroids for acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) and prompts the question: is colchicine ever sufficient for early neuro‑BD (and its complications like SIADH)?

Why this case matters
- Neuro‑BD is usually treated aggressively with systemic corticosteroids ± other immunosuppressants because parenchymal CNS involvement can cause lasting deficits. Yet acute neuro‑BD can present as aseptic meningitis without MRI lesions, and the natural history may be self‑limited in some patients.
- Colchicine is well established for mucocutaneous BD but is not considered disease‑modifying for CNS involvement in guideline literature. Here colchicine alone coincided with remission of CNS inflammation and SIADH, suggesting a plausible steroid‑sparing role in selected presentations.
- The case is a reminder to integrate clinical phenotype (severity, focal deficits, imaging, CSF) into treatment decisions rather than automatic high‑dose steroids for every CNS complaint in BD.

Mechanistic plausibility for colchicine in neuro‑BD
Colchicine’s mechanisms align with key pathophysiology in BD:
- Inhibits microtubule polymerization → impaired neutrophil chemotaxis and activation. Neutrophilic overactivity is central to BD mucocutaneous lesions and likely contributes to inflammatory cascades in neuro‑BD.
- Downregulates inflammasome signalling and reduces IL‑1β and IL‑6 production. IL‑6 is implicated in CNS inflammation and stimulates ADH release — a plausible link to SIADH seen in this case.
- Alters endothelial and leukocyte interactions, which could blunt perivascular inflammation relevant to BD vasculitis.

So, although colchicine is not conventionally used as monotherapy for neuro‑BD, the pharmacology gives a biologic rationale for benefit in early, neutrophil/IL‑6–driven inflammation.

What the evidence says (short)
- Strong evidence supports colchicine for mucocutaneous and some articular BD manifestations.
- For neuro‑BD the literature is sparse: most series and reviews recommend high‑dose corticosteroids for acute parenchymal disease, often with additional immunosuppression (azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, TNF blockers) for severe or refractory cases.
- Colchicine has been reported as adjunctive therapy for BD but randomized or robust data showing colchicine monotherapy is effective for neuro‑BD are lacking. This case is an illustrative data point but not definitive proof.

When a colchicine‑first (or steroid‑sparing) approach might be reasonable
Consider colchicine monotherapy or a monitored steroid‑sparing trial only when the patient meets a combination of the following low‑risk features:
- Presentation consistent with acute aseptic meningitis: headache, fever, mild CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis, mildly elevated protein, without neutrophilic purulent picture.
- No focal neurological deficits, no altered consciousness, no seizures and no rapidly progressive signs.
- Brain MRI without parenchymal lesions, mass effect, brainstem involvement, or evidence of major vasculitic lesions.
- Hemodynamically stable and able to be admitted/observed closely.
- Reasonable exclusion of infectious etiologies (CSF studies negative or pending-but-low-suspicion; empirical antimicrobials handled per local practice).
- Patient factors that favour avoiding steroids (e.g., poorly controlled diabetes where steroids would cause harm) and when colchicine is not contraindicated.

Important caveat: this approach requires close inpatient monitoring with a low threshold for escalation to high‑dose corticosteroids and other immunosuppression if any of the following occur: new focal deficits, worsening headache/mental status, new MRI abnormalities, increasing CSF pleocytosis or metabolic encephalopathy from hyponatraemia despite correction.

How to identify patients who likely still need early steroids or additional immunosuppression
Immediate corticosteroids (and likely additional immunosuppression) are indicated when:
- Parenchymal neuro‑BD is present on imaging (brainstem, deep grey matter, large lesions).
- Focal neurological deficits, decreased level of consciousness, seizures, or rapidly progressive course.
- High CSF cellularity/protein with signs of significant intracranial inflammation or high opening pressure.
- Ocular involvement threatening vision (uveitis with vision loss), arterial aneurysm, or major organ involvement requiring systemic control.
- Failure to improve or clinical deterioration on a colchicine‑first plan within 24–72 hours.

Safety, monitoring and practical dosing matters with colchicine
Dose and duration
- Typical dosing for BD mucocutaneous disease: colchicine 0.5–1 mg/day (often 0.5 mg twice daily depending on tolerance and local practice). The exact dosing in the case report isn’t specified; use local BD guidance.
- Adjust dose for renal impairment and with significant drug interactions.

Baseline and follow‑up monitoring
- Baseline: renal function (creatinine), liver function tests (ALT/AST/bilirubin), CBC, baseline creatine kinase (especially if myalgia).
- Ongoing: check renal and hepatic function and CBC periodically (frequency individualized; sooner if symptoms), monitor for GI intolerance (nausea, diarrhoea), myopathy (muscle pain/weakness), and neuropathy.
- Monitor for drug interactions: major concerns with strong CYP3A4 inhibitors (e.g., clarithromycin, certain azoles, some protease inhibitors) and P‑glycoprotein inhibitors — these markedly increase colchicine toxicity risk.
- Be cautious with combined statin therapy; colchicine with statins may increase risk of myopathy/rhabdomyolysis, particularly in renal impairment.
- Watch closely for signs of bone marrow suppression (rare) or severe diarrhoea leading to volume/electrolyte problems.

Managing SIADH in neuro‑BD
- Treat the underlying inflammation; in this case correction occurred with colchicine + fluid restriction.
- Acute severe symptomatic hyponatraemia still mandates standard emergency therapy (hypertonic saline) regardless of etiology.
- Monitor sodium closely to avoid osmotic demyelination with overly rapid correction.
- Consider measuring and monitoring ADH/urine osmolality and sodium to guide fluid restriction and need for further therapy.

Clinical pearls
- Neuro‑Behçet’s is heterogeneous: aseptic meningitis without MRI lesions can behave differently from parenchymal brain disease. Tailor immunotherapy to phenotype and severity.
- Colchicine has biologic plausibility for helping neuro‑BD via neutrophil and cytokine modulation; it’s proven for mucocutaneous BD but unproven as sole therapy for CNS disease.
- A carefully selected, closely monitored steroid‑sparing trial may be reasonable in mild aseptic neuro‑BD—especially when steroids carry high risk—but requires clear criteria and low threshold for escalation.
- Always exclude infection before withholding steroids in suspected neuroinflammatory disease; start empiric antimicrobials if there’s diagnostic uncertainty.
- Monitor for colchicine toxicity: renal/hepatic dysfunction and interacting drugs raise the stakes.

Bottom line (takeaway bullets)
- Colchicine monotherapy led to clinical and biochemical recovery in one well‑worked‑up case of acute neuro‑BD presenting as aseptic meningitis with SIADH — intriguing but not practice‑changing alone.
- Consider colchicine as part of early management for selected, low‑risk neuro‑BD presentations (aseptic meningitis, normal MRI, no focal deficits) when close monitoring and infectious exclusion are possible.
- Do NOT substitute colchicine for steroids in moderate‑to‑severe neuro‑BD (parenchymal lesions, focal deficits, rapid progression, vision‑threatening disease).
- If pursuing a steroid‑sparing plan, have predetermined clinical triggers for escalation, and monitor sodium, renal/hepatic function, CBC and CK while watching for drug interactions.

This case opens the door to rethinking tailored immunotherapy for the milder end of the neuro‑BD spectrum. It should prompt caution, not complacency — colchicine alone may be an option in a narrow window, but steroids remain the mainstay for serious CNS involvement until more robust evidence emerges.
