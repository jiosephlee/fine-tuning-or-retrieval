Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

## Case Presentation and Initial Assessment

This patient was a young adult whose hyperglycaemia was first identified incidentally during an annual health examination, when routine testing revealed both elevated blood glucose and glycosuria. The demographic context and mode of detection are important: the patient was not acutely ill, and the abnormality was discovered on screening rather than emergently, which is a common scenario in some forms of monogenic diabetes such as HNF1A-MODY.

Clinically he reported classic hyperglycaemic symptoms — increased thirst, increased appetite and recent weight loss — but notably there were no features of ketoacidosis at presentation. The absence of diabetic ketoacidosis argues against fulminant type 1 diabetes at that time. On examination there was no acanthosis nigricans, a sign often associated with insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes. Together, symptomatic hyperglycaemia without DKA and without stigmata of marked insulin resistance should raise consideration of non–type 1, non–type 2 causes of early-onset diabetes, including MODY.

Family history provided a strong diagnostic clue. Several first- and second-degree relatives had diabetes diagnosed at young ages (maternal grandmother in her 30s, mother in her late 20s, and a younger brother at age 20). This pattern of multiple affected relatives across successive generations is consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance, which is characteristic of most MODY subtypes (including HNF1A-MODY). Family clustering with early onset is one of the most helpful historical features distinguishing monogenic diabetes from typical type 1 or type 2 diabetes, where family patterns are more heterogeneous.

Anthropometric and vital signs were reassuring for the absence of severe obesity or hemodynamic instability: BMI was 24.6 kg/m2 (near the upper limit of normal to overweight range) and blood pressure 112/70 mmHg. A modestly elevated BMI does not exclude MODY; rising population rates of overweight and obesity make BMI less discriminating. In HNF1A-MODY, patients are frequently non-obese or only mildly overweight, and they typically lack the marked insulin resistance phenotype seen in many type 2 patients.

Baseline glycaemic indices documented substantial hyperglycaemia. HbA1c was 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), fasting plasma glucose 12.4 mmol/L, and the 2-hour value after a 75 g OGTT was 19.8 mmol/L. These values confirm diabetes with marked postprandial and fasting hyperglycaemia. The prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia (high 2-hour OGTT value) is consistent with the early β-cell secretory defect typical of HNF1A-MODY, in which progressive loss of regulated insulin secretion leads to exaggerated postprandial excursions before absolute insulin deficiency develops.

The presence of glycosuria despite only modestly elevated BMI is a noteworthy diagnostic clue in HNF1A-MODY. HNF1A regulates SGLT2 expression in renal proximal tubules; pathogenic HNF1A variants can lower the renal threshold for glucose reabsorption, producing glycosuria at lower plasma glucose levels than would be expected. In practice, unexpected glycosuria in a non-ketotic young adult with preserved weight is one of the features that should prompt consideration of HNF1A-MODY.

Lipid testing in this patient showed a borderline low HDL and elevated triglycerides, which can suggest components of metabolic syndrome. However, interpretation must be cautious: as the Discussion in this report notes, the increasing prevalence of overweight and dyslipidaemia in the general population blunts the ability of metabolic features to reliably separate MODY from type 2 diabetes. Thus, metabolic abnormalities do not exclude MODY and may coexist.

An autonomic neuropathy screen performed early in the assessment detected severely reduced sudomotor function over the hands, measured by electrochemical skin conductance testing. Sudomotor testing assesses small fibre autonomic nerve function and can be an early indicator of diabetic autonomic neuropathy. In a young person with a relatively recent diagnosis of diabetes, finding significant sudomotor dysfunction is atypical and warrants careful interpretation: it may reflect longer-standing hyperglycaemia prior to detection, or variability in test sensitivity and specificity. It does not point to a specific diabetes subtype but underscores the need for prompt and effective glycaemic control and baseline complication screening.

At presentation, a pragmatic initial working diagnosis was type 2 diabetes (given the absence of ketoacidosis and some metabolic derangement) with consideration for non–type 1 causes because of family history and clinical features suggestive of impaired insulin secretion. Because of the degree of hyperglycaemia (HbA1c >10%) and symptomatic presentation, the team elected to begin combination therapy comprising basal insulin (degludec 8 units/day) to rapidly control glucose and reduce glucotoxicity, together with metformin (1500 mg/day) to target hepatic glucose output and a DPP-4 inhibitor (saxagliptin 5 mg/day) to augment incretin-mediated insulin secretion with low hypoglycaemia risk. Starting insulin while further evaluation proceeds is a common and safe approach when glucose is severe; it can preserve β-cell function and allow more accurate later assessment of endogenous insulin secretion. This initial regimen was reasonable pending results of autoimmune antibody testing, stimulated C‑peptide measurement, and genetic testing for MODY, which would refine the diagnosis and long-term therapy.

In summary, the combination of screening-detected hyperglycaemia with glycosuria, symptomatic but non-ketotic presentation, lack of marked insulin resistance, a clear autosomal-dominant family history of early-onset diabetes, and marked postprandial hyperglycaemia on OGTT together raised strong suspicion for HNF1A-MODY and guided the team to pursue biochemical and genetic confirmatory testing while initiating effective glucose-lowering therapy.

## Differential Diagnosis and Diagnostic Workup for Early-Onset Nonautoimmune Diabetes

When a young adult presents with marked hyperglycaemia but without clear ketoacidosis or classic type 1 phenotypes, the clinician must systematically consider three main diagnostic groups: autoimmune type 1 diabetes (T1D), type 2 diabetes (T2D), and monogenic diabetes (MODY and related single‑gene disorders). In this case, the diagnostic pathway combined targeted history, focused biochemical testing and functional assessment of β‑cell reserve to distinguish HNF1A‑MODY from the more common types.

Key differentials and clinical clues
Type 1 diabetes typically presents with rapid onset of symptomatic hyperglycaemia, often with ketosis or ketoacidosis, markedly reduced endogenous insulin secretion and positive islet autoantibodies in most patients. Type 2 diabetes usually has slower onset, is strongly associated with insulin resistance (obesity, acanthosis nigricans, dyslipidaemia) and initially preserved or increased insulin secretion. MODY comprises several autosomal dominant, non‑autoimmune β‑cell disorders (most commonly HNF1A, GCK, HNF4A, and less commonly HNF1B and others), which typically present at a young age, often with a strong multigenerational family history and variable degrees of insulin secretory defect without evidence of autoimmunity. Less common monogenic forms (for example, neonatal diabetes genes) are considered when onset is extremely early or the phenotype is unusual.

In the reported patient several features supported MODY over T1D or typical T2D: disease onset in young adulthood with a striking multigenerational history of early‑onset diabetes (maternal grandmother, mother, brother), absence of diabetic ketoacidosis, glycosuria at relatively modest plasma glucose levels (consistent with reduced renal glucose reabsorption seen in some HNF1A cases), and lack of physical stigmata of insulin resistance (no acanthosis nigricans). Although the patient’s BMI was in the upper normal/overweight range and triglycerides were elevated, these findings do not exclude MODY and have become less discriminatory as obesity prevalence rises.

Autoantibody testing: strategy and interpretation
Testing for pancreatic islet autoantibodies is a first‑line step when distinguishing autoimmune from non‑autoimmune diabetes. The most clinically useful assays are glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen‑2 antibodies (IA‑2A), and zinc transporter 8 antibodies (ZnT8). The presence of one or more of these antibodies in a compatible clinical context strongly supports T1D. Conversely, persistently negative antibody testing increases the likelihood of non‑autoimmune diabetes, including MODY, but is not definitive. Caveats include that some patients with MODY may occasionally have positive autoantibodies (rare seropositive outliers), and that antibody titres can wane over time in T1D—so timing relative to onset matters. In this case both GADA and IA‑2A were negative (ZnT8 was not reported), which, along with clinical features, raised suspicion for MODY and argued against classic T1D.

Assessing β‑cell reserve: the glucagon stimulation test
Functional assessment of endogenous insulin secretion helps distinguish insulin‑deficient T1D from MODY or T2D. The glucagon stimulation test measures C‑peptide before and after intravenous glucagon; a stimulated C‑peptide >0.60 nmol/L (≈0.6 nmol/L) is commonly interpreted as evidence of preserved β‑cell reserve, whereas lower values suggest significant insulin deficiency. In this patient the C‑peptide rose from 0.39 nmol/L fasting to 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes. The stimulated value (>0.60 nmol/L) indicated residual insulin secretion inconsistent with absolute insulin deficiency of established T1D and supportive of non‑autoimmune diabetes such as MODY. Importantly, clinicians must recognize limitations: early after T1D onset C‑peptide can be preserved, and inter‑assay units or cutoffs vary between centres—so results are supportive but not definitive in isolation.

Using probability tools and when to pursue genetic testing
Clinical algorithms, such as the Exeter MODY probability calculator, use features (age at diagnosis, BMI, family history, treatment) to estimate the pretest probability of MODY and guide genetic testing. These tools are useful as a pretest filter but have limitations: they were derived in predominantly White European cohorts and may over‑ or under‑estimate probability in other ethnic groups. In this case, entering the patient’s data gave an estimated MODY probability of about 62%, a value that, together with negative autoantibodies and preserved stimulated C‑peptide, supported proceeding to genetic testing.

Deciding when to order genetic testing involves balancing clinical urgency and the need for reasonably stable metabolic conditions. If the clinical picture strongly suggests MODY (early onset, multigenerational diabetes, negative autoantibodies, preserved C‑peptide), testing should be arranged promptly because a confirmed genetic diagnosis alters therapy and family counselling. It is reasonable, as in this report, to stabilize hyperglycaemia first (to facilitate testing logistics and avoid confounding acute illness effects on C‑peptide), but not to delay testing until insulin is discontinued. Next‑generation sequencing (NGS) panels that include the common MODY genes (HNF1A, GCK, HNF4A, HNF1B, and others) are the preferred initial approach because they efficiently screen multiple candidate genes; variant interpretation should follow established guidelines (ACMG), integrating segregation, in silico prediction, population frequency and functional data where available.

Ancillary testing and supportive evaluations
Routine assessment for diabetes complications—urine albumin, retinal screening—are important baseline investigations but are not diagnostic for MODY. In this case microalbuminuria was absent and there were no retinal findings reported. The report did include autonomic evaluation by sudomotor testing, which showed markedly reduced conductance in the hands. Autonomic neuropathy is not specific to MODY and may reflect longstanding hyperglycaemia; it is a supportive finding of diabetes‑related complications rather than a diagnostic discriminator. Glycosuria, noted at presentation, can be especially helpful: HNF1A mutations reduce SGLT2 expression and lower the renal threshold for glucose, so glycosuria at lower plasma glucose levels can point toward HNF1A‑MODY.

Common pitfalls and interpretive cautions
Several pitfalls can mislead clinicians. A normal or only mildly reduced C‑peptide early after onset does not exclude evolving T1D; repeat testing and antibody assessment may be necessary if clinical evolution suggests autoimmune destruction. Family history can be under‑reported or obscured by misclassification of relatives as T2D. Rising obesity rates reduce the discriminatory power of BMI and metabolic syndrome features. Rarely, patients with MODY have measurable autoantibodies; conversely, some T1D patients are antibody‑negative. Therefore, diagnosis rests on integrating history, phenotype, antibody status and functional testing, with genetic testing as the definitive step when suspicion is high.

How the diagnostic workup led to the HNF1A diagnosis in this case
Applying these principles to the reported patient: a young adult with substantial hyperglycaemia but no ketoacidosis, a strong autosomal dominant family history, negative islet autoantibodies, a preserved stimulated C‑peptide (0.83 nmol/L) and glycosuria prompted targeted genetic testing. An NGS MODY panel identified a novel heterozygous HNF1A variant, clinching the diagnosis of HNF1A‑MODY. This genetic confirmation permitted de‑escalation from insulin and selection of an oral/GLP‑1 receptor agonist‑led regimen more appropriate to the underlying beta‑cell defect, illustrating how an evidence‑based diagnostic workup guides precision management.

In summary, distinguishing MODY from T1D and T2D in early‑onset diabetes requires careful synthesis of family history and phenotype, antibody testing, objective assessment of β‑cell reserve, and selective use of probability tools to decide on genetic testing. Genetic confirmation is central because it establishes the diagnosis, permits targeted therapy and enables family screening.

## HNF1A-MODY: Molecular Pathophysiology and Genetic Confirmation

HNF1A (hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha) is a transcription factor encoded on chromosome 12q24. The gene spans 10 exons and encodes a protein of 631 amino acids that is expressed in several metabolically important tissues, most notably pancreatic β-cells, liver and renal tubular cells. Its domain structure includes an N‑terminal dimerization and DNA‑binding domain and a C‑terminal transactivation domain; many disease‑causing variants cluster in the DNA‑binding region, where they impair target gene regulation.

Pathophysiologically, HNF1A controls expression of multiple genes that are critical for normal glucose handling and β‑cell function. In pancreatic β‑cells it directly or indirectly regulates insulin gene expression and proteins involved in glucose sensing and metabolism such as GLUT2 and enzymes like pyruvate kinase. Loss‑of‑function variants reduce glucose‑stimulated insulin secretion by impairing β‑cell transcriptional programs and metabolic responsiveness. Clinically this produces a characteristic natural history: early abnormalities in postprandial glucose (often identified on 2‑hour OGTT) that progress over years to fasting hyperglycaemia as β‑cell reserve declines. This progressive β‑cell failure distinguishes HNF1A‑MODY from stable defects such as GCK‑MODY, which typically produces mild, nonprogressive fasting hyperglycaemia.

HNF1A expression in renal proximal tubular cells explains another distinctive clinical feature: reduced expression of the sodium–glucose co‑transporter SGLT2 lowers the renal threshold for glucose reabsorption, producing glycosuria at lower plasma glucose concentrations than expected. This mechanism explains the glycosuria observed in many HNF1A‑MODY patients and cautions about the use of SGLT2 inhibitors in this group because of potential dehydration or exaggerated glucosuric effects.

The present case harbors a novel heterozygous variant, c.809A>G, predicting p.Asn270Ser (p.N270S), located in exon 4 within the DNA‑binding domain. Localization to the DNA‑binding domain is biologically important because variants here commonly disrupt HNF1A’s ability to bind promoter sequences and regulate downstream genes, producing a molecular phenotype consistent with reduced insulin secretion and altered renal glucose handling—the clinical features seen in this patient (early onset hyperglycaemia, glycosuria, progressive β‑cell insufficiency).

Establishing pathogenicity for a novel missense variant follows combined genetic, computational and clinical lines of evidence, aligned with American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) guidelines. For this variant the evidence supporting pathogenicity included: (1) absence from population and disease databases (e.g., not reported in ClinVar or VarSome; corresponding to ACMG criterion PM2, supporting rarity in controls); (2) cosegregation with early‑onset diabetes in multiple affected family members (PP1), strengthening the link between genotype and phenotype; (3) location within a functionally critical region (the DNA‑binding domain) near residues previously reported as pathogenic (PM1); and (4) strong computational prediction of deleterious effect (high AlphaMissense score of 0.995 and other in‑silico tools, meeting PP3 as supporting evidence). Taken together—multiple moderate (PM1, PM2) and supporting (PP1, PP3) criteria—this composite evidence supports classification as likely pathogenic under ACMG rules. Functional studies demonstrating loss of transcriptional activity would provide additional definitive (strong) evidence, but even without those data the genetic and clinical concordance in this family is sufficient to justify clinical management decisions tailored for HNF1A‑MODY.

HNF1A sits within a broader spectrum of MODY genes with distinct clinical implications. For example, GCK‑MODY produces mild, nonprogressive fasting hyperglycaemia typically not requiring pharmacologic therapy, whereas HNF4A‑MODY has clinical overlap with HNF1A but may show neonatal hypoglycaemia and different extra‑pancreatic features. HNF1B mutations commonly cause renal malformations and hypoplastic kidneys in addition to diabetes. Recognizing these genotype–phenotype differences is crucial because genetic confirmation alters therapy (for HNF1A, sulfonylureas or incretin‑based agents may replace insulin) and informs surveillance for extrapancreatic manifestations.

Finally, HNF1A‑MODY is still under‑recognized, particularly outside populations of European descent where most early studies were performed. The reported case underscores that MODY can be present in diverse ethnic groups and that reliance on phenotype alone will miss cases. When young onset diabetes is accompanied by negative islet autoantibodies, preserved but declining C‑peptide, glycosuria, and a compatible family history, targeted genetic testing (now feasible via NGS panels) is indicated because confirming an HNF1A variant directly informs precision therapy, prognosis and family counselling.

## Therapeutic Strategy and Course in HNF1A-MODY

At presentation this patient had marked hyperglycaemia (HbA1c 10.2%, fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L) and symptoms of hyperglycaemia but no ketoacidosis. The immediate therapeutic goal was rapid glycaemic stabilization to reduce glucotoxicity and relieve symptoms while further diagnostic workup proceeded. A short-term regimen of basal insulin (degludec 8 units/day), metformin (1,500 mg/day) and a DPP‑4 inhibitor (saxagliptin 5 mg/day) was chosen. This approach provides complementary mechanisms: basal insulin supplies exogenous insulin to control fasting hyperglycaemia and protect β cells from glucotoxicity, metformin reduces hepatic glucose production and improves peripheral insulin sensitivity, and a DPP‑4 inhibitor augments endogenous incretin-stimulated insulin secretion without causing substantial hypoglycaemia. Beginning insulin before genetic results is a pragmatic choice in severe hyperglycaemia to ensure safety and rapid glycaemic control.

Reassessment after the diagnostic workup guided a shift in therapy. The patient had negative islet autoantibodies, a positive family history, a glucagon-stimulated C‑peptide that indicated partially preserved β-cell reserve (stimulated 0.83 nmol/L), and genetic confirmation of an HNF1A pathogenic variant. These findings supported a diagnosis of HNF1A‑MODY and made continued long‑term insulin unnecessary. Once short‑term glycaemic control was achieved (HbA1c fell to 9.1% at one month), insulin was discontinued and replaced with a GLP‑1 receptor agonist (dulaglutide 1.5 mg weekly) combined with metformin. This transition exemplifies precision medicine: genetic diagnosis identified a form of monogenic, non‑autoimmune diabetes in which insulin dependence is not inevitable and where insulin-sparing oral/injectable agents can be effective.

Therapeutic options in HNF1A‑MODY have clinical rationales tied to pathophysiology. Historically, low‑dose sulfonylureas have been the first‑line therapy because HNF1A mutations primarily impair glucose‑stimulated insulin secretion rather than causing insulin resistance. Sulfonylureas directly close pancreatic β‑cell KATP channels to increase insulin release, often achieving excellent glycaemic responses at lower doses than in typical type 2 diabetes. The main limitation is the risk of hypoglycaemia, which can be pronounced in some patients because of the preserved but abnormal β‑cell function.

GLP‑1 receptor agonists are an attractive alternative or successor to sulfonylureas in many HNF1A‑MODY patients, particularly when hypoglycaemia risk is a concern or weight benefits are desired. Small randomized crossover trials in patients with HNF1A‑MODY have shown that GLP‑1 receptor agonists (for example liraglutide) and sulfonylureas (for example glimepiride) produce similar improvements in fasting and postprandial glucose, but GLP‑1 receptor agonists are associated with less hypoglycaemia. The incretin class acts by glucose‑dependent augmentation of insulin secretion, slowing gastric emptying and reducing appetite — mechanisms that both respect residual β‑cell glucose sensing and reduce hypoglycaemia risk compared with sulfonylureas. In this patient, dulaglutide achieved marked improvement (HbA1c fell from 9.1% at one month to 6.0% at six months), supporting the clinical efficacy of GLP‑1 receptor agonist therapy in HNF1A‑MODY with moderate β‑cell reserve.

Metformin remains useful as part of combination therapy in HNF1A‑MODY because it addresses hepatic glucose overproduction and insulin resistance components that may coexist (especially with higher BMI or dyslipidaemia). Although metformin does not correct the primary β‑cell secretory defect, it complements secretion‑targeted agents and is well tolerated. DPP‑4 inhibitors, which prolong endogenous incretin action, can be used for maintenance when GLP‑1 receptor agonist injections are not preferred; they enhance glucose‑dependent insulin secretion and have low hypoglycaemia risk. In this case, after achieving glycaemic stability with dulaglutide, therapy was simplified to metformin plus saxagliptin for longer‑term maintenance with continued good control (HbA1c <6.5%).

A specific caution in HNF1A‑MODY concerns SGLT2 inhibitors. HNF1A regulates expression of SGLT2 in the proximal tubule, and HNF1A‑MODY patients frequently have a reduced renal threshold for glucose and glycosuria at lower plasma glucose concentrations. Because of this baseline reduction in SGLT2 activity, pharmacologic SGLT2 inhibition may produce exaggerated glucosuria, volume depletion and, in rare circumstances, predispose to euglycaemic diabetic ketoacidosis and genitourinary infections. In the reported case, the treating team avoided SGLT2 inhibitors, a reasonable precaution based on the disorder’s pathophysiology and potential safety concerns.

The patient’s clinical course illustrates therapeutic principles: initial insulin temporarily to control severe hyperglycaemia and prevent acute metabolic complications, then withdrawal of insulin after establishing HNF1A‑MODY with adequate stimulated C‑peptide and transition to targeted, insulin‑sparing therapy. The regimen of GLP‑1 receptor agonist plus metformin produced rapid and substantial HbA1c reduction to 6.0% within six months, after which maintenance with metformin and a DPP‑4 inhibitor preserved glycaemic targets.

Ongoing management in HNF1A‑MODY requires routine monitoring for microvascular complications (microalbuminuria, retinopathy, neuropathy) despite often earlier onset of hyperglycaemia, and attention to lifestyle measures (diet, exercise, weight management) and patient education about hypoglycaemia risk and symptom recognition. Regular follow‑up to reassess β‑cell function and adjust therapy is important because progressive β‑cell decline may eventually necessitate escalation of therapy. In summary, genetic diagnosis enabled a tailored therapeutic plan that avoided unnecessary long‑term insulin, minimized hypoglycaemia risk, and achieved durable glycaemic control in this patient with HNF1A‑MODY.

## Clinical Implications, Pitfalls, and Precision Medicine Lessons

This case of HNF1A-MODY underscores practical lessons for clinicians evaluating young adults with atypical diabetes. Recognizing red flags and following a stepwise diagnostic approach can prevent misclassification as type 1 or type 2 diabetes, allow targeted therapy changes (often avoiding lifelong insulin), and enable cascade testing and counseling of relatives.

Red flags for MODY in clinic
The clinical features in this patient illustrate classic “red flags” that should prompt consideration of MODY. These include diabetes onset at a young age (<35 years), an autosomal dominant pattern of early-onset diabetes in multiple generations, absence of islet autoantibodies (GADA, IA-2A, ZnT8), glycosuria out of proportion to glycaemia (related to reduced renal SGLT2 expression in HNF1A defects), and measurable but inadequate stimulated C‑peptide indicating preserved yet failing β‑cell function. Note that modest or absent features of insulin resistance (no acanthosis nigricans here) also support non‑typical T2D. These features should raise suspicion even when BMI is in the overweight range, as rising population BMI has reduced the discriminative power of weight alone.

A practical, stepwise diagnostic algorithm
A pragmatic workflow applied to this case begins with clinical triage (young onset, family history, clinical phenotype), followed by laboratory exclusion of type 1 diabetes with autoantibodies. Assessment of endogenous insulin secretion with fasting and stimulated C‑peptide (for example, glucagon stimulation) helps distinguish absolute insulin deficiency from preserved β‑cell reserve. Probability calculators (eg, Exeter MODY calculator) can aid pretest risk estimation but have limitations outside the populations in which they were derived; use them as adjuncts rather than gatekeepers. When clinical suspicion remains high, targeted genetic testing with an NGS monogenic diabetes panel is the next step. In this patient the sequence of antibody testing → C‑peptide assessment → targeted NGS permitted confirmation of HNF1A-MODY and supported therapeutic de-escalation.

Interpreting variants beyond database entries
Genetic results must be interpreted in context. Many clinically relevant HNF1A variants are novel and absent from public repositories. Applying the ACMG framework—integrating segregation with disease in the family, location in a functional domain (this case: DNA‑binding domain), computational predictions (eg, AlphaMissense), population frequency, and functional or published evidence—strengthens variant classification. Segregation of the variant with early-onset diabetes in relatives (as reported) added weight to pathogenicity. Clinicians should collaborate with geneticists or molecular laboratories when a novel variant is returned to ensure appropriate classification and counseling.

Therapeutic personalization based on genotype and physiology
Diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY directly informed treatment changes in this case. Traditionally, low‑dose sulfonylureas are first‑line for many HNF1A‑MODY patients because they amplify insulin secretion from residual β‑cells. However, GLP‑1 receptor agonists (GLP‑1RA) are an increasingly rational alternative: they can improve glucose control with lower hypoglycaemia risk and may support β‑cell function. In this patient, transitioning from insulin to a GLP‑1RA plus metformin achieved marked HbA1c reduction and durable control, illustrating that insulin can often be deprescribed after genetic confirmation when C‑peptide is preserved. Choice of agent should consider glycaemic severity at presentation, hypoglycaemia risk, comorbidities, availability, and patient preference. Note also that SGLT2 inhibitors may have particular safety considerations in HNF1A‑MODY because of baseline reduced renal glucose reabsorption and theoretical risk of volume depletion and euglycaemic ketoacidosis; such risks should be weighed carefully.

Family implications: cascade testing and counseling
A confirmed monogenic diagnosis has immediate family implications. First‑degree relatives should be offered predictive genetic testing to identify affected but asymptomatic carriers and guide early screening and management. Counseling should include inheritance patterns (autosomal dominant, 50% risk per offspring), variable age of onset, implications for pregnancy planning and neonatal risk when relevant, and the impact on therapy choices. Documentation of a pathogenic variant also facilitates targeted testing rather than broad, repeated metabolic screens for multiple family members.

Health‑system and equity considerations
This case highlights underdiagnosis of MODY, particularly in non‑European populations. Relying solely on calculators or restricted testing criteria derived from White cohorts can miss cases. Health systems should strive to increase access to affordable targeted NGS panels and genetic counseling, prioritize testing for people with clinical red flags, and educate providers about monogenic diabetes to avoid unnecessary lifelong insulin or inappropriate therapies. Cost-effectiveness improves when testing leads to treatment changes (e.g., stopping insulin) and informs family testing, but equitable access remains a challenge.

Common clinical pitfalls to avoid
Several pitfalls are exemplified by this and similar cases. Do not exclude MODY solely because BMI is elevated—obesity prevalence can obscure monogenic presentations. Avoid over-reliance on a single normal C‑peptide measurement; early type 1 diabetes may transiently preserve C‑peptide, so clinical context and serial measurements can help. Do not assume that negative autoantibodies completely rule out type 1 disease in unusual cases; rather, use them as part of the broader evaluation. Finally, do not defer genetic testing simply because a variant is not found in databases—novel variants can be pathogenic and require integrated interpretation.

Key teaching points and exam pearls
- Strong suspicion for MODY should arise with diabetes onset <35 years, autosomal dominant family history, negative islet autoantibodies, and preserved/stimulatable C‑peptide.
- Use a stepwise approach: clinical triage → autoantibodies → C‑peptide → calculate probability → targeted NGS when indicated.
- A pathogenic HNF1A diagnosis frequently permits shift from insulin to oral agents or GLP‑1RA, improving quality of life and reducing hypoglycaemia risk.
- Variant interpretation requires ACMG criteria, segregation data, functional domain knowledge, and computational predictions—absence from ClinVar is not dispositive.
- Cascade testing and genetic counseling are integral to care after monogenic diagnosis.
- Be mindful of health‑equity issues: MODY is underrecognized outside primarily studied populations; broaden testing access and clinician awareness.

In sum, this case demonstrates how integrating careful clinical phenotyping with judicious use of biochemical testing and targeted genetic sequencing enables precision medicine: correct molecular diagnosis, personalized therapy, family risk assessment, and avoidance of unnecessary long‑term insulin therapy.
