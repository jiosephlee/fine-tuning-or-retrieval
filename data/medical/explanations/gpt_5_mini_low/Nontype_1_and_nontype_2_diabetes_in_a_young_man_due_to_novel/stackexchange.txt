Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Differential Diagnosis] A 20-something man presents with hyperglycaemia, marked postprandial glucose on OGTT (2-hour 19.8 mmol/L), glycosuria without ketoacidosis, BMI 24.6 kg/m2, negative GAD and IA-2 antibodies, and a family history of early-onset diabetes across three generations. His glucagon-stimulated C-peptide is 0.83 nmol/L. What diagnosis best fits these data and why is it favored over type 1 and type 2 diabetes?

Most likely diagnosis: HNF1A-MODY (maturity‑onset diabetes of the young due to an HNF1A mutation).

Why this fits and why it is favored over type 1 and type 2 diabetes

1) Key clinical pattern pointing to MODY
- Young adult with diabetes onset before age 25 and a clear autosomal‑dominant family history (maternal grandmother in her 30s, mother in her late 20s, younger brother at 20) — this three‑generation vertical transmission strongly favors monogenic MODY over polygenic T2D or autoimmune T1D.  
- Prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia (2‑hour OGTT 19.8 mmol/L) with glycosuria despite absence of ketoacidosis is characteristic of HNF1A‑MODY (progressive β‑cell secretory defect with reduced renal glucose reabsorption).  
- Genetic confirmation: a novel heterozygous pathogenic HNF1A variant (p.N270S) — DNA sequencing is the diagnostic standard for MODY and makes the diagnosis definitive in this case.

2) Features arguing against type 1 diabetes
- Negative islet autoantibodies (GAD and IA‑2). While antibodies can be absent early in some T1D or in seronegative T1D, the combination of negative antibodies plus a stimulated C‑peptide that is preserved argues against classic autoimmune destruction.  
- Preserved stimulated C‑peptide (glucagon‑stimulated 0.83 nmol/L) indicates residual endogenous insulin secretion; in established T1D C‑peptide is usually very low. The patient also had no ketoacidosis at presentation.

3) Features arguing against typical type 2 diabetes
- BMI 24.6 kg/m2 (not obese) and absence of stigmata of insulin resistance (no acanthosis nigricans) — although later‑stage obesity can blur distinctions, the weight and phenotype here are not typical of early T2D.  
- Strong multigenerational early‑onset diabetes is atypical for polygenic T2D.  
- The pattern of marked postprandial hyperglycaemia and glycosuria from a lowered renal threshold (HNF1A controls SGLT2 expression) is a recognized feature of HNF1A‑MODY rather than T2D physiology.

4) Pathophysiology that unifies the findings
- HNF1A is a transcription factor expressed in β‑cells and renal tubules. Pathogenic variants cause progressive β‑cell dysfunction (reduced insulin and GLUT2 expression → impaired glucose‑stimulated insulin secretion, hence pronounced postprandial spikes) and reduced SGLT2 expression → lower renal glucose threshold and glycosuria. This explains the clinical triad seen in the report: postprandial hyperglycaemia, glycosuria without DKA, and preserved but insufficient C‑peptide.

5) Practical diagnostic hierarchy and management implications
- In a young patient with negative autoantibodies, preserved stimulated C‑peptide, and dominant family history, genetic testing for MODY (NGS panel or targeted HNF1A sequencing) is the appropriate diagnostic standard — exactly what was performed here and which identified the pathogenic p.N270S variant.  
- Correct genetic diagnosis changes therapy: HNF1A‑MODY patients often respond to low‑dose sulfonylureas and, increasingly, GLP‑1 receptor agonists (used successfully in this case). Insulin can often be discontinued if β‑cell function is adequate. Avoiding or using caution with SGLT2 inhibitors is prudent because of exaggerated glycosuria and dehydration risk.

Takeaway clinical pearl: In young patients with early‑onset diabetes, strong multigenerational transmission, negative islet autoantibodies, preserved stimulated C‑peptide, marked postprandial hyperglycaemia and glycosuria, think HNF1A‑MODY and pursue genetic testing — because the diagnosis alters prognosis, family counseling, and therapy.

### [Pathophysiology] In HNF1A-MODY, why can glycosuria appear at comparatively lower plasma glucose levels, and what renal transporter and transcriptional relationship explain this finding?

Short answer
Glycosuria at relatively low plasma glucose in HNF1A‑MODY reflects a reduced renal threshold for glucose reabsorption caused by decreased expression of the sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2). HNF1A is a transcription factor that directly regulates the SLC5A2 gene (which encodes SGLT2); heterozygous loss‑of‑function HNF1A mutations lead to haploinsufficiency, lower SGLT2 levels in proximal tubule cells, impaired glucose reuptake and glycosuria at lower plasma glucose concentrations.

Clinical reasoning and mechanism
- Normal physiology: the bulk of filtered glucose is reabsorbed in the early proximal tubule by SGLT2 (high‑capacity, low‑affinity). When filtered load exceeds reabsorptive capacity the renal threshold is reached and glucosuria appears.
- In HNF1A‑MODY: HNF1A is expressed in the kidney and transcriptionally activates SLC5A2. Heterozygous HNF1A mutations reduce SGLT2 expression → decreased proximal tubule reabsorptive capacity → lower renal threshold for glucose → glycosuria at plasma glucose levels that would not cause glucosuria in people with normal SGLT2.
- Clinical consequence: urinary glucose loss can cause osmotic diuresis and dehydration; the case report notes this mechanism and cautions about additional SGLT2 inhibition (which could precipitate volume depletion or euglycaemic DKA).

Teaching pearl
Detection of glycosuria out of proportion to measured hyperglycaemia in a young patient with family history of early‑onset diabetes should raise suspicion for HNF1A‑MODY—because HNF1A haploinsufficiency lowers SGLT2 expression (SLC5A2), producing a reduced renal glucose threshold that is a useful clinical clue.

### [Clinical Pearls] List three to five clinical and laboratory clues that should prompt consideration of MODY genetic testing in a young adult with new-onset diabetes, and name one limitation of the commonly used MODY probability calculator in diverse populations.

Direct answer
Consider MODY testing when a young adult with new‑onset diabetes has (choose 3–5 of the following salient clues):  
1) Strong autosomal‑dominant family history of early‑onset diabetes (multiple affected first‑degree relatives diagnosed at young ages).  
2) Negative pancreatic islet autoantibodies (GADA, IA‑2, ZnT8) despite hyperglycaemia.  
3) Preserved or only moderately reduced endogenous insulin secretion (measurable fasting or stimulated C‑peptide), inconsistent with classic insulin‑deficient type 1 disease.  
4) Clinical phenotype inconsistent with typical type 2 diabetes — onset at young age (often <25–35), minimal insulin resistance stigmata (no acanthosis nigricans), glycosuria out of proportion to blood glucose (suggesting a low renal glucose threshold).  
5) Marked postprandial hyperglycaemia with relatively low early insulin requirements and good response to non‑insulin agents (sulfonylureas, GLP‑1 RAs) or atypical features for T2D (e.g., absence of DKA).

Clinical reasoning and pathophysiology (brief)
- MODY is a monogenic β‑cell disorder with autosomal‑dominant transmission; family clustering of early‑onset diabetes is therefore a key clue.  
- Most MODY subtypes are non‑autoimmune: negative GADA/IA‑2/ZnT8 reduces the likelihood of type 1 autoimmune diabetes.  
- Mutations (eg, HNF1A) cause progressive β‑cell dysfunction but often leave measurable C‑peptide early on; thus preserved or only partially reduced stimulated C‑peptide argues against immediate insulin‑open type 1 disease.  
- HNF1A mutations specifically reduce expression of renal SGLT2 and pancreatic genes (GLUT2, insulin), which can produce glycosuria at relatively lower glucose levels and prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia rather than severe insulin resistance.  
- Early good response to secretagogues or incretin therapies and low insulin needs further support a non‑T1/T2 mechanism amenable to targeted therapy.

How the case supports these clues
- The patient was a young adult with multiple first‑degree/second‑degree relatives diagnosed in their 20s–30s (strong autosomal‑dominant history).  
- Islet autoantibodies (GADA, IA‑2) were negative.  
- Glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide was present (0.83 nmol/L), indicating residual β‑cell function.  
- BMI was not markedly elevated and no acanthosis nigricans was noted; glycosuria was present and marked postprandial hyperglycaemia was demonstrated on OGTT.  
- Genetic testing confirmed a pathogenic HNF1A variant and treatment was successfully de‑escalated from insulin to GLP‑1 RA and oral agents, consistent with a MODY diagnosis.

One limitation of the commonly used MODY probability calculator
- The Exeter MODY probability calculator was derived from White European cohorts; it may be less accurate in non‑White populations and can overestimate (or otherwise misestimate) the probability of MODY when applied to ethnically diverse patients. In practice this means the calculator’s output must be interpreted in the context of ancestry, rising obesity prevalence, and local disease patterns rather than used as an absolute gatekeeper for genetic testing.

### [Morning Report] How do you interpret a glucagon-stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L (with a practical threshold of >0.60 nmol/L indicating preserved endogenous insulin), and how should this influence initial therapy when HbA1c is approximately 10% at diagnosis?

Interpretation and clinical implications

A glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide of 0.83 nmol/L (above the practical cutoff of ~0.60 nmol/L) indicates preserved endogenous insulin secretion. In other words, this patient has functioning β‑cells despite hyperglycaemia; the result argues against absolute insulin deficiency from autoimmune type 1 diabetes and supports non‑type 1 etiologies (including MODY such as HNF1A).

Why that matters (pathophysiology and diagnostic reasoning)
- HNF1A‑MODY is a primary β‑cell secretory defect with progressive but initially partial loss of insulin secretion. A stimulated C‑peptide in the range reported shows residual β‑cell capacity consistent with HNF1A‑MODY rather than established type 1 diabetes.
- The glucagon stimulation test is supportive but not definitive: it must be interpreted with the clinical picture (age at onset, family history, negative islet autoantibodies) and, when available, genetic testing. The case provides exactly that concordance (negative GADA/IA‑2, strong family history, later confirmed HNF1A mutation).

How this should influence initial therapy when HbA1c ≈10%
- Immediate goal: rapidly lower glucose and reverse glucotoxicity. With symptomatic hyperglycaemia and HbA1c ≈10%, a short course of insulin—often basal insulin or basal‑bolus depending on severity—is reasonable to achieve prompt control. The authors started basal degludec with metformin and a DPP‑4 inhibitor; this is an appropriate initial pragmatic approach while awaiting definitive diagnosis.
- Early preserved C‑peptide shifts medium‑term strategy away from lifelong insulin replacement toward β‑cell–stimulating or insulin‑sparing therapies once glycaemic control is achieved and diagnosis clarifies:
  - First‑line therapy for HNF1A‑MODY classically: low‑dose sulfonylurea (sensitivity to secretagogues is a hallmark).  
  - Alternatives and increasingly preferred options: GLP‑1 receptor agonists (or DPP‑4 inhibitors) with metformin—these may lower HbA1c effectively, carry less hypoglycaemia risk than sulfonylureas, and in this case produced a rapid and durable response (HbA1c fell from 10.2% to 6.0% at 6 months after switching to dulaglutide + metformin).
- Practical pathway to follow in a similar patient:
  1. Start short‑term insulin (often basal) to control marked hyperglycaemia and relieve glucotoxicity while arranging antibody testing and genetic testing.
  2. Interpret stimulated C‑peptide (>0.6 nmol/L) as evidence that insulin discontinuation may be feasible once glucose is controlled.
  3. If MODY (HNF1A) is confirmed or highly likely: transition off insulin to sulfonylurea or GLP‑1 RA + metformin, tailoring choice to age, hypoglycaemia risk, comorbidities and patient preference. Monitor closely for glycaemic control and hypoglycaemia during the transition.
  4. Avoid routine use of SGLT2 inhibitors without careful consideration in HNF1A‑MODY because of pronounced glycosuria and potential dehydration/DKA risk noted in the literature and discussed in the case.
  5. Continue follow‑up with serial HbA1c and consider repeat stimulated C‑peptide over time to track β‑cell decline.

Takeaway (concise)
A stimulated C‑peptide of 0.83 nmol/L indicates preserved endogenous insulin and supports an insulin‑sparing strategy once acute hyperglycaemia is controlled. With HbA1c ≈10% at diagnosis, short‑term insulin to rapidly reduce glucose is appropriate, followed—after confirming MODY or once glycaemia improves—by transition to sulfonylurea or GLP‑1 RA plus metformin. The reported case illustrates this: initial basal insulin for control, genetic confirmation of HNF1A‑MODY, then successful transition to GLP‑1 RA + metformin with sustained glycaemic remission.

### [Management] What is the first-line pharmacotherapy traditionally recommended for HNF1A-MODY, and under what clinical circumstances might a GLP-1 receptor agonist be preferred?

First-line pharmacotherapy historically recommended for HNF1A‑MODY
- Low‑dose sulfonylureas (eg, gliclazide, glimepiride) are the traditional first‑line oral therapy. Rationale: HNF1A mutations produce a progressive β‑cell secretory defect but intact insulin responsiveness to KATP‑channel–mediated stimulation; sulfonylureas augment insulin release directly from residual β‑cells and often achieve excellent glycaemic control at low doses.

When a GLP‑1 receptor agonist may be preferred
- Clinical circumstances favoring a GLP‑1 receptor agonist (GLP‑1 RA) over a sulfonylurea include:
  - Declining or marginal β‑cell function with higher baseline HbA1c (as in this case: HbA1c 10.2% and stimulated C‑peptide 0.83 nmol/L), when insulin‑sparing but more potent secretagogues and β‑cell‑preserving strategies are desirable.
  - High risk of hypoglycaemia or prior hypoglycaemia on sulfonylureas (GLP‑1 RAs have lower hypoglycaemia risk).
  - Need for weight loss or avoidance of weight gain (GLP‑1 RAs promote weight loss; sulfonylureas can cause weight gain).
  - Intolerance or contraindication to sulfonylureas.
  - Patient preference for injectable therapy with cardiovascular or metabolic benefits where applicable.
- Evidence and case support: small crossover trials in HNF1A‑MODY show liraglutide and glimepiride have similar glucose‑lowering effects but GLP‑1 RAs have less mild hypoglycaemia. In the reported patient, genetic confirmation of HNF1A‑MODY, moderately preserved but insufficient β‑cell function, and the goal to discontinue insulin led clinicians to start dulaglutide (1.5 mg weekly) with metformin; HbA1c fell from 10.2% to 6.0% over 6 months, illustrating that a GLP‑1 RA can be an effective and safer alternative to sulfonylurea or ongoing insulin in selected HNF1A‑MODY patients.

Practical point
- Use genotype and β‑cell reserve (C‑peptide), glycaemic severity, hypoglycaemia risk, weight goals and patient preference to individualize therapy. Sulfonylureas remain first‑line historically, but GLP‑1 RAs are an appropriate and increasingly used alternative in patients like this one.

### [Management] Why might SGLT2 inhibitors carry disproportionate risks (for example, dehydration and ketoacidosis) in patients with HNF1A-MODY, and how does the disease biology underpin this concern?

Short answer
HNF1A controls SGLT2 expression in the renal proximal tubule. In HNF1A‑MODY patients SGLT2 expression is reduced at baseline, producing spontaneous glycosuria. Pharmacologic SGLT2 inhibition therefore suppresses already diminished renal glucose reabsorption and can produce disproportionately large glucosuric/diuretic effects. Combined with the hallmark β‑cell insulin secretory defect of HNF1A‑MODY (relative insulinopenia), this creates a perfect storm for volume depletion, hypotension and increased ketogenesis — including euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA). For these reasons SGLT2 inhibitors carry higher and clinically important risks in HNF1A‑MODY and are generally avoided or used only with extreme caution.

Pathophysiology and clinical reasoning (teaching points)
- HNF1A regulates genes in pancreas, liver and kidney. One important renal target is SLC5A2, which encodes SGLT2. Loss‑of‑function HNF1A mutations reduce SGLT2 expression → lower renal glucose reabsorption and baseline glycosuria (explains the glycosuria seen in the case).
- SGLT2 inhibitors further block SGLT2 activity. In a patient who already has reduced SGLT2, pharmacologic inhibition can produce exaggerated glucosuria → marked osmotic diuresis, polyuria, salt and water loss, and dehydration. Case reports (and the Discussion in this report) document severe dehydration and even DKA after profound SGLT2 inhibition in HNF1A‑MODY.
- HNF1A‑MODY causes progressive β‑cell dysfunction with inadequate insulin secretion. Lower circulating insulin (and sometimes relative hyperglucagonemia) lowers the insulin:glucagon ratio, favouring lipolysis and hepatic ketogenesis.
- SGLT2 inhibitors themselves alter metabolic signalling: they decrease plasma glucose, often reduce insulin levels and raise glucagon, which together promote ketone production. In a patient with limited insulin reserve (HNF1A‑MODY) this effect is magnified, increasing the risk of (often euglycaemic) DKA.
- Additional risks: more glycosuria → higher risk of genital mycotic infections; marked diuresis → orthostasis and vascular collapse in vulnerable patients.

Implications for diagnosis and management
- Recognize the clue: unexplained glycosuria with modest hyperglycaemia (as in this patient) suggests a reduced renal threshold and should raise suspicion for HNF1A‑MODY.
- Therapeutic preference: HNF1A‑MODY patients are classically very sulfonylurea‑responsive early on; GLP‑1 receptor agonists, metformin and DPP‑4 agents are reasonable alternatives as β‑cell function declines (as done in this case). These agents avoid the disproportionate dehydration/ketoacidosis risk conferred by SGLT2 inhibitors.
- If an SGLT2 inhibitor is considered (rarely), counsel vigorously, ensure euvolemia, check for preserved insulin secretion, teach sick‑day rules (stop the drug with reduced oral intake/illness), monitor ketones and stop the drug with any ketosis — but in most HNF1A‑MODY patients avoidance is prudent.
- Bottom line: the disease biology — reduced SGLT2 expression plus impaired insulin secretion — explains why standard SGLT2 inhibition can be hazardous in HNF1A‑MODY. This pharmacogenetic interaction is a practical example of why genetic diagnosis (as in the reported case) should guide precision diabetes therapy.

### [Pathophysiology] Describe the key tissues in which HNF1A is expressed and outline at least two downstream targets or processes relevant to the diabetic phenotype (for example, insulin/GLUT2 expression in β-cells and renal SGLT2 regulation).

HNF1A expression — where it matters in diabetes
- The HNF1A transcription factor is highly expressed in three key tissues relevant to the MODY phenotype described in this case: pancreatic β‑cells, hepatocytes (liver), and renal proximal tubular cells (kidney). The case report explicitly notes expression in liver, kidney and pancreas and locates the patient’s mutation to the DNA‑binding domain of HNF1A.

Two (actually three) downstream targets/processes that produce the diabetic phenotype

1) β‑cell genes: insulin secretion machinery (insulin, GLUT2, pyruvate kinase)
- What HNF1A normally does: it directly or indirectly promotes transcription of genes required for glucose sensing and stimulus–secretion coupling in β‑cells — notably the glucose transporter GLUT2, insulin gene expression and enzymes of glycolysis (eg, pyruvate kinase).
- Pathophysiology: loss‑of‑function HNF1A mutations → reduced GLUT2 and glycolytic flux → impaired glucose uptake/sensing and reduced ATP generation → defective closure of KATP channels and diminished glucose‑stimulated insulin secretion. Clinically this produces progressive β‑cell dysfunction with postprandial hyperglycaemia and an inappropriately low insulin response, consistent with the patient’s elevated OGTT and reduced stimulated C‑peptide.
- Therapeutic relevance: preserved but impaired β‑cell function explains why low‑dose sulfonylureas (increase insulin release) or incretin‑based agents (GLP‑1 receptor agonists, DPP‑4 inhibitors) can be effective alternatives to insulin, as used in this case.

2) Renal SGLT2 and renal glucose handling → glycosuria/low renal threshold
- What HNF1A normally does: drives expression of SGLT2 in proximal tubular epithelium.
- Pathophysiology: HNF1A mutations reduce SGLT2 expression → lower renal threshold for glucose and prominent glycosuria even at modest hyperglycaemia (explains the glycosuria in this patient at presentation). The report also highlights that reduced SGLT2 activity has clinical consequences — patients with HNF1A‑MODY may be prone to marked dehydration and, paradoxically, to DKA when SGLT2 activity is low or further inhibited.
- Therapeutic relevance: this mechanistic link cautions about routine use of SGLT2 inhibitors in HNF1A‑MODY (risk of volume depletion, infections, DKA), and supports tailoring therapy away from agents that further inhibit renal glucose reabsorption.

3) Hepatic targets that influence glucose and lipid metabolism
- What HNF1A normally does: regulates many hepatic genes (gluconeogenic and lipid‑related genes and apolipoproteins). The report cites hepatic expression and notes reduced pyruvate kinase activity; HNF1A mutations can alter hepatic glucose output and lipid handling.
- Clinical impact: contributes to fasting and postprandial hyperglycaemia and may modulate lipid profile; this patient had elevated triglycerides and modestly low HDL, which may reflect part of the metabolic milieu though obesity and other factors also contribute.

Concise synthesis
- HNF1A mutation causes a combined phenotype from loss of transcriptional control in β‑cells (reduced GLUT2, insulin, glycolytic enzyme expression → impaired glucose‑stimulated insulin secretion) and in renal proximal tubule (reduced SGLT2 → low renal glucose threshold and glycosuria), with hepatic effects that further influence glucose and lipid metabolism. These molecular consequences explain the family history, early-onset postprandial hyperglycaemia, glycosuria, preserved but inadequate C‑peptide, and guide precision therapy (sulfonylureas or incretin agents favored; caution with SGLT2 inhibitors), as illustrated in this case.

### [Morning Report] In a patient with suspected HNF1A-MODY and severe hyperglycaemia, what is a reasonable sequence of therapy from presentation through stabilization and confirmation of diagnosis, and what factors justify de-escalation off insulin after genetic confirmation?

Bottom line
- Early severe hyperglycaemia without ketoacidosis in a young adult with negative islet autoantibodies and a strong family history is best managed initially like other severe hyperglycaemias—rapid glucose control (often with short‑term insulin) while you complete diagnostic evaluation. After genetic confirmation of HNF1A‑MODY, de‑escalation off insulin is reasonable when there is objective evidence of residual β‑cell function plus clinical improvement in glucose control and no ketogenesis; transition to oral/GLP‑1–based therapy (or low‑dose sulfonylurea) then provides effective, safer long‑term management.

How to sequence therapy (presentation → stabilization → confirmation)
1. Immediate management at presentation
   - Treat the hyperglycaemia promptly to relieve osmotic symptoms and reduce glucotoxicity. In this case the patient had HbA1c 10.2% and fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L without DKA — so initiation of basal insulin for rapid control (eg, small‑to‑moderate basal dose) plus metformin and lifestyle counselling was reasonable.
   - Rationale: insulin corrects severe hyperglycaemia quickly and is the safest initial choice when the diagnosis (type 1 vs MODY vs type 2) is uncertain and β‑cell reserve is unknown.

2. Parallel diagnostic workup while treating
   - Obtain diabetes autoantibodies (GADA, IA‑2, ZnT8) and assess endogenous insulin secretion (fasting and/or stimulated C‑peptide; here glucagon stimulation gave a stimulated C‑peptide 0.83 nmol/L).
   - Take a careful family history, age at onset, BMI and clinical features (glycosuria, lack of acanthosis) to raise suspicion for MODY.
   - Send molecular genetic testing (MODY panel/NGS) when clinical features support it. These steps should proceed while glycaemia is being controlled.

3. Early reassessment after partial glycaemic response
   - Reassess glucose control and C‑peptide after short stabilization (weeks). In the case, HbA1c fell from 10.2% to 9.1% after 1 month on basal insulin + metformin + saxagliptin and stimulated C‑peptide indicated preserved but impaired secretion.
   - If there is preserved endogenous insulin secretion (stimulated C‑peptide generally >0.6 nmol/L or >200–300 pmol/L depending on assay) and negative autoantibodies, probability of non‑autoimmune causes (including HNF1A‑MODY) is high and you can plan for de‑escalation when genetic results return.

4. Transition after genetic confirmation of HNF1A‑MODY
   - Once a pathogenic HNF1A variant is confirmed, transition off insulin is reasonable if:
     a) There is demonstrable residual β‑cell function (stimulated C‑peptide supportive),
     b) The patient’s glucose control is improving on current therapy (falling HbA1c, no ketosis), and
     c) There is close follow‑up and the patient understands hypoglycaemia/ketosis warning signs.
   - Preferred long‑term options for HNF1A‑MODY: low‑dose sulfonylurea (historically first‑line) or GLP‑1 receptor agonists (safer re: hypoglycaemia and beneficial for weight/metabolic profile). In this case the team used dulaglutide + metformin with excellent response (HbA1c 6.0% at 6 months), then simplified to metformin + DPP‑4 inhibitor for maintenance.

Factors that justify de‑escalation off insulin after genetic confirmation (apply these criteria)
- Objective evidence of endogenous insulin production:
  - Stimulated C‑peptide in the preserved range (in this report 0.83 nmol/L) supports ability to secrete insulin and reduces the likelihood of absolute insulin deficiency.
- Absence of autoimmune markers:
  - Negative GADA/IA‑2/ZnT8 makes type 1 diabetes unlikely.
- Clinical trajectory:
  - Glycaemic improvement on minimal insulin/oral agents (drop in HbA1c from 10.2% to 9.1% in one month), absence of ketosis/DKA during course.
- Genetic confirmation of HNF1A pathogenic variant:
  - Confirms a progressive β‑cell secretory defect that is often responsive to insulin secretagogues/GLP‑1 agents rather than lifelong insulin.
- Capacity for close monitoring:
  - Reliable outpatient follow‑up, ability to check glucose frequently and re‑introduce insulin promptly if glycaemia deteriorates.
- Consideration of risks/benefits of alternatives:
  - Sulfonylureas are effective but carry hypoglycaemia risk; GLP‑1 RAs (used in this case) provide glucose lowering with lower hypoglycaemia risk and may preserve β‑cell function. Avoid routine use of SGLT2 inhibitors in HNF1A‑MODY without caution because reduced SGLT2 expression and marked glycosuria may increase dehydration/DKA risk.

Practical monitoring and safety nets after stopping insulin
- Check fasting and postprandial glucose and HbA1c frequently in the first weeks to months.
- Reassess C‑peptide if clinical course is atypical or glycaemia worsens.
- Educate patient on hyperglycaemia/DKA symptoms and provide plan to restart insulin if needed.
- Screen for complications as usual.

Pathophysiologic rationale linking diagnosis to therapy
- HNF1A mutations cause progressive β‑cell secretory failure; patients often have marked postprandial hyperglycaemia but some retained insulin secretion early on. Sulfonylureas and incretin‑based therapies enhance endogenous insulin release and blunt postprandial excursions. Genetic confirmation predicts response to secretagogues/GLP‑1–based therapy and allows stopping insulin when endogenous insulin is adequate.

Application to the presented case
- The treating team followed this reasonable sequence: start basal insulin + metformin + DPP‑4 inhibitor to control severe hyperglycaemia; perform antibody testing and glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide; obtain genetic testing; after genetic confirmation of HNF1A‑MODY and evidence of residual insulin secretion and improving HbA1c, they stopped insulin and successfully transitioned to dulaglutide + metformin (then simplified to metformin + saxagliptin) with sustained HbA1c <6.5%.

Takeaway
- Use insulin acutely to stabilize significant hyperglycaemia when diagnostic uncertainty exists; pursue autoantibodies, C‑peptide and genetic testing in parallel. After genetic confirmation of HNF1A‑MODY, de‑escalate from insulin when there is preserved stimulated C‑peptide, improving glycaemia, negative autoimmunity and the ability to monitor closely — then switch to targeted therapies (low‑dose sulfonylurea or GLP‑1 RA ± metformin) tailored to efficacy and safety for the patient.

### [Clinical Pearls] How would you counsel this patient’s family about inheritance risk, who should be offered testing, and what surveillance is appropriate for relatives found to carry an HNF1A variant?

Key points first: HNF1A-MODY is an autosomal‑dominant disorder — each first‑degree relative of this patient has a 50% chance of carrying the same pathogenic HNF1A variant. Cascade genetic testing of at‑risk relatives, directed clinical surveillance for carriers, and genetic counselling (including discussion of reproductive implications) are the priorities.

Counselling about inheritance risk
- Mode: autosomal dominant. For this family, the pattern (maternal grandmother, mother, younger brother with early‑onset diabetes) is classic vertical transmission.
- Risk to relatives: each child of an affected person has a 50% risk of inheriting the variant. Siblings of the proband have up to a 50% risk if the mother is a carrier (and the family history suggests the mutation is on the maternal side).
- Variable expressivity and age‑dependent penetrance: carriers may present in adolescence or young adulthood, and degree/timing of hyperglycaemia can vary even within the same family.

Who should be offered testing
- Offer targeted genetic testing for the known familial variant (p.N270S / c.809A>G) to:
  - All first‑degree relatives (parents, siblings, children) regardless of whether they already have a diabetes diagnosis.
  - Second‑degree relatives (grandparents, aunts/uncles, nieces/nephews) and more distant relatives if they are in the direct maternal pedigree or if identifying carriers would change management.
- Prioritize testing of relatives with early‑onset diabetes (onset <25–35 years), persistent non‑insulin‑dependent diabetes with negative diabetes autoantibodies and preserved C‑peptide, or those with a family history suggestive of MODY.
- Use targeted single‑variant testing for family members once the pathogenic variant is established in the proband (faster and less expensive than panel testing). Refer to a genetics service for pre‑ and post‑test counselling.

Surveillance and management for relatives found to carry an HNF1A variant
- For asymptomatic carriers (no diabetes):
  - Begin metabolic surveillance in adolescence or earlier if clinically indicated. Practical approach:
    - Annual fasting plasma glucose and HbA1c starting around puberty (many centres begin screening from age 10–12 years) or sooner if symptoms develop.
    - Consider periodic 2‑hour OGTT if there is clinical concern about postprandial hyperglycaemia (HNF1A often shows prominent postprandial glucose excursions).
    - Educate about symptoms of hyperglycaemia and glycosuria; urine glucose testing can be a sensitive clue because of the lowered renal threshold in HNF1A carriers.
- For carriers who develop diabetes:
  - Recognize the typical natural history: progressive β‑cell dysfunction with relatively low insulin resistance.
  - First‑line pharmacotherapy historically: low‑dose sulfonylureas (high sensitivity). However, GLP‑1 receptor agonists and other agents (as in this case) are reasonable alternatives, especially to avoid hypoglycaemia or where sulfonylurea side‑effects are a concern.
  - Avoid routine use of SGLT2 inhibitors with caution — HNF1A carriers have reduced SGLT2 expression and pronounced glycosuria; SGLT2 inhibitors may increase risk of volume depletion and ketoacidosis (as discussed in the case).
  - Monitor and treat according to glycaemic control; refer to endocrinology for therapy tailoring (this case shows good response to GLP‑1 RA with maintenance on metformin ± DPP‑4 inhibitor).
- Complication screening once diabetes is diagnosed:
  - Standard microvascular and cardiovascular surveillance schedules: annual retinal screening, annual urine albumin-to‑creatinine ratio and eGFR, foot care and neuropathy assessment. Start at diagnosis and follow usual diabetes guidelines because complication risk is linked to glycaemic exposure.
- Lifestyle advice: healthy diet, physical activity, weight management if relevant; smoking cessation and cardiovascular risk factor control as for other forms of diabetes.

Reproductive and pregnancy considerations
- Women who are carriers have high risk of diabetes in pregnancy and should receive preconception counselling and early pregnancy metabolic monitoring.
- There is an important fetal consideration: if the fetus inherits the HNF1A variant, in some forms of monogenic diabetes fetal growth can be affected — discuss pregnancy management with maternal‑fetal medicine/genetics specialists. Offer genetic counselling for family planning.

Practical next steps for this family (based on the case)
1. Arrange genetics referral and offer targeted cascade testing for the known HNF1A p.N270S variant to all first‑degree relatives (mother, brother, any children) and other symptomatic maternal relatives.
2. For relatives who test positive but are asymptomatic: start annual fasting glucose and HbA1c from adolescence, consider OGTT if concerns about postprandial hyperglycaemia, and educate about glycosuria and hyperglycaemic symptoms.
3. For relatives with diabetes who test positive: adjust therapy according to HNF1A‑MODY principles (consider low‑dose sulfonylurea or GLP‑1 RA; avoid empiric SGLT2 inhibitors without specialist input), and institute routine complication screening.

How these pieces combine to “likely pathogenic”

A plausible combination here is: PM1 (moderate) + PM2_supporting + PP1 (supporting) + PP3 (supporting) + PP4 (supporting) → accumulation of one moderate + multiple supporting criteria typically yields a Likely Pathogenic classification. Why absence from ClinVar/VarSome or literature does not equal benign
- Rarity of pathogenic missense variants: Many pathogenic variants are private or recently discovered and therefore absent from databases that depend on published or submitted annotations.
- Curation lag and incomplete submission: ClinVar/VarSome are incomplete; pathogenic calls require clinical labs or authors to submit them.
- Population frequency context matters: A truly benign interpretation requires demonstration of appreciable allele frequency in appropriate population datasets incompatible with dominant disease penetrance. Simple absence is neutral-to-supporting rather than refuting pathogenicity.
- Functional and segregation evidence can outweigh novelty: Well‑demonstrated segregation, domain localization, concordant in silico prediction, and a highly specific phenotype provide independent lines of evidence that can classify novel variants as likely pathogenic even if not previously reported.

Practical caveats and next steps (teaching points)
- Functional assay: A functional study demonstrating impaired HNF1A transcriptional activity would upgrade confidence (could provide PS3, strong evidence).
- Segregation quantification: The strength of PP1 depends on number of segregations; formal LOD scoring increases rigor.
- Population checks: Confirm absence/very low frequency in large databases (gnomAD) and adjust evidence weight if any low-frequency allele is found.
- Clinical reporting: Label as “likely pathogenic” with recommendation for cascade testing of at‑risk relatives and consideration of phenotype‑driven management (as in this case). Reclassify if new evidence (functional data, additional segregation) emerges.

Bottom line
In this case the combination of location in the DNA‑binding domain (PM1), absence from databases (PM2_supporting), segregation with autosomal dominant early‑onset diabetes (PP1), strong computational deleteriousness (PP3), and a classical HNF1A‑MODY phenotype (PP4) supports a Likely Pathogenic designation per ACMG logic.
