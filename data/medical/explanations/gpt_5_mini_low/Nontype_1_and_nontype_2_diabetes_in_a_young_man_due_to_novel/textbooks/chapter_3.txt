Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

Chapter 3: ## HNF1A-MODY: Molecular Pathophysiology and Genetic Confirmation

HNF1A (hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha) is a transcription factor encoded on chromosome 12q24. The gene spans 10 exons and encodes a protein of 631 amino acids that is expressed in several metabolically important tissues, most notably pancreatic β-cells, liver and renal tubular cells. Its domain structure includes an N‑terminal dimerization and DNA‑binding domain and a C‑terminal transactivation domain; many disease‑causing variants cluster in the DNA‑binding region, where they impair target gene regulation.

Pathophysiologically, HNF1A controls expression of multiple genes that are critical for normal glucose handling and β‑cell function. In pancreatic β‑cells it directly or indirectly regulates insulin gene expression and proteins involved in glucose sensing and metabolism such as GLUT2 and enzymes like pyruvate kinase. Loss‑of‑function variants reduce glucose‑stimulated insulin secretion by impairing β‑cell transcriptional programs and metabolic responsiveness. Clinically this produces a characteristic natural history: early abnormalities in postprandial glucose (often identified on 2‑hour OGTT) that progress over years to fasting hyperglycaemia as β‑cell reserve declines. This progressive β‑cell failure distinguishes HNF1A‑MODY from stable defects such as GCK‑MODY, which typically produces mild, nonprogressive fasting hyperglycaemia.

HNF1A expression in renal proximal tubular cells explains another distinctive clinical feature: reduced expression of the sodium–glucose co‑transporter SGLT2 lowers the renal threshold for glucose reabsorption, producing glycosuria at lower plasma glucose concentrations than expected. This mechanism explains the glycosuria observed in many HNF1A‑MODY patients and cautions about the use of SGLT2 inhibitors in this group because of potential dehydration or exaggerated glucosuric effects.

The present case harbors a novel heterozygous variant, c.809A>G, predicting p.Asn270Ser (p.N270S), located in exon 4 within the DNA‑binding domain. Localization to the DNA‑binding domain is biologically important because variants here commonly disrupt HNF1A’s ability to bind promoter sequences and regulate downstream genes, producing a molecular phenotype consistent with reduced insulin secretion and altered renal glucose handling—the clinical features seen in this patient (early onset hyperglycaemia, glycosuria, progressive β‑cell insufficiency).

Establishing pathogenicity for a novel missense variant follows combined genetic, computational and clinical lines of evidence, aligned with American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) guidelines. For this variant the evidence supporting pathogenicity included: (1) absence from population and disease databases (e.g., not reported in ClinVar or VarSome; corresponding to ACMG criterion PM2, supporting rarity in controls); (2) cosegregation with early‑onset diabetes in multiple affected family members (PP1), strengthening the link between genotype and phenotype; (3) location within a functionally critical region (the DNA‑binding domain) near residues previously reported as pathogenic (PM1); and (4) strong computational prediction of deleterious effect (high AlphaMissense score of 0.995 and other in‑silico tools, meeting PP3 as supporting evidence). Taken together—multiple moderate (PM1, PM2) and supporting (PP1, PP3) criteria—this composite evidence supports classification as likely pathogenic under ACMG rules. Functional studies demonstrating loss of transcriptional activity would provide additional definitive (strong) evidence, but even without those data the genetic and clinical concordance in this family is sufficient to justify clinical management decisions tailored for HNF1A‑MODY.

HNF1A sits within a broader spectrum of MODY genes with distinct clinical implications. For example, GCK‑MODY produces mild, nonprogressive fasting hyperglycaemia typically not requiring pharmacologic therapy, whereas HNF4A‑MODY has clinical overlap with HNF1A but may show neonatal hypoglycaemia and different extra‑pancreatic features. HNF1B mutations commonly cause renal malformations and hypoplastic kidneys in addition to diabetes. Recognizing these genotype–phenotype differences is crucial because genetic confirmation alters therapy (for HNF1A, sulfonylureas or incretin‑based agents may replace insulin) and informs surveillance for extrapancreatic manifestations.

Finally, HNF1A‑MODY is still under‑recognized, particularly outside populations of European descent where most early studies were performed. The reported case underscores that MODY can be present in diverse ethnic groups and that reliance on phenotype alone will miss cases. When young onset diabetes is accompanied by negative islet autoantibodies, preserved but declining C‑peptide, glycosuria, and a compatible family history, targeted genetic testing (now feasible via NGS panels) is indicated because confirming an HNF1A variant directly informs precision therapy, prognosis and family counselling.
