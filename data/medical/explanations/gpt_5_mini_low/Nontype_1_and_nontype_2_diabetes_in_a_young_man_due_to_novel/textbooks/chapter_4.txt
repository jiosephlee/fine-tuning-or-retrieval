Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

Chapter 4: ## Therapeutic Strategy and Course in HNF1A-MODY

At presentation this patient had marked hyperglycaemia (HbA1c 10.2%, fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L) and symptoms of hyperglycaemia but no ketoacidosis. The immediate therapeutic goal was rapid glycaemic stabilization to reduce glucotoxicity and relieve symptoms while further diagnostic workup proceeded. A short-term regimen of basal insulin (degludec 8 units/day), metformin (1,500 mg/day) and a DPP‑4 inhibitor (saxagliptin 5 mg/day) was chosen. This approach provides complementary mechanisms: basal insulin supplies exogenous insulin to control fasting hyperglycaemia and protect β cells from glucotoxicity, metformin reduces hepatic glucose production and improves peripheral insulin sensitivity, and a DPP‑4 inhibitor augments endogenous incretin-stimulated insulin secretion without causing substantial hypoglycaemia. Beginning insulin before genetic results is a pragmatic choice in severe hyperglycaemia to ensure safety and rapid glycaemic control.

Reassessment after the diagnostic workup guided a shift in therapy. The patient had negative islet autoantibodies, a positive family history, a glucagon-stimulated C‑peptide that indicated partially preserved β-cell reserve (stimulated 0.83 nmol/L), and genetic confirmation of an HNF1A pathogenic variant. These findings supported a diagnosis of HNF1A‑MODY and made continued long‑term insulin unnecessary. Once short‑term glycaemic control was achieved (HbA1c fell to 9.1% at one month), insulin was discontinued and replaced with a GLP‑1 receptor agonist (dulaglutide 1.5 mg weekly) combined with metformin. This transition exemplifies precision medicine: genetic diagnosis identified a form of monogenic, non‑autoimmune diabetes in which insulin dependence is not inevitable and where insulin-sparing oral/injectable agents can be effective.

Therapeutic options in HNF1A‑MODY have clinical rationales tied to pathophysiology. Historically, low‑dose sulfonylureas have been the first‑line therapy because HNF1A mutations primarily impair glucose‑stimulated insulin secretion rather than causing insulin resistance. Sulfonylureas directly close pancreatic β‑cell KATP channels to increase insulin release, often achieving excellent glycaemic responses at lower doses than in typical type 2 diabetes. The main limitation is the risk of hypoglycaemia, which can be pronounced in some patients because of the preserved but abnormal β‑cell function.

GLP‑1 receptor agonists are an attractive alternative or successor to sulfonylureas in many HNF1A‑MODY patients, particularly when hypoglycaemia risk is a concern or weight benefits are desired. Small randomized crossover trials in patients with HNF1A‑MODY have shown that GLP‑1 receptor agonists (for example liraglutide) and sulfonylureas (for example glimepiride) produce similar improvements in fasting and postprandial glucose, but GLP‑1 receptor agonists are associated with less hypoglycaemia. The incretin class acts by glucose‑dependent augmentation of insulin secretion, slowing gastric emptying and reducing appetite — mechanisms that both respect residual β‑cell glucose sensing and reduce hypoglycaemia risk compared with sulfonylureas. In this patient, dulaglutide achieved marked improvement (HbA1c fell from 9.1% at one month to 6.0% at six months), supporting the clinical efficacy of GLP‑1 receptor agonist therapy in HNF1A‑MODY with moderate β‑cell reserve.

Metformin remains useful as part of combination therapy in HNF1A‑MODY because it addresses hepatic glucose overproduction and insulin resistance components that may coexist (especially with higher BMI or dyslipidaemia). Although metformin does not correct the primary β‑cell secretory defect, it complements secretion‑targeted agents and is well tolerated. DPP‑4 inhibitors, which prolong endogenous incretin action, can be used for maintenance when GLP‑1 receptor agonist injections are not preferred; they enhance glucose‑dependent insulin secretion and have low hypoglycaemia risk. In this case, after achieving glycaemic stability with dulaglutide, therapy was simplified to metformin plus saxagliptin for longer‑term maintenance with continued good control (HbA1c <6.5%).

A specific caution in HNF1A‑MODY concerns SGLT2 inhibitors. HNF1A regulates expression of SGLT2 in the proximal tubule, and HNF1A‑MODY patients frequently have a reduced renal threshold for glucose and glycosuria at lower plasma glucose concentrations. Because of this baseline reduction in SGLT2 activity, pharmacologic SGLT2 inhibition may produce exaggerated glucosuria, volume depletion and, in rare circumstances, predispose to euglycaemic diabetic ketoacidosis and genitourinary infections. In the reported case, the treating team avoided SGLT2 inhibitors, a reasonable precaution based on the disorder’s pathophysiology and potential safety concerns.

The patient’s clinical course illustrates therapeutic principles: initial insulin temporarily to control severe hyperglycaemia and prevent acute metabolic complications, then withdrawal of insulin after establishing HNF1A‑MODY with adequate stimulated C‑peptide and transition to targeted, insulin‑sparing therapy. The regimen of GLP‑1 receptor agonist plus metformin produced rapid and substantial HbA1c reduction to 6.0% within six months, after which maintenance with metformin and a DPP‑4 inhibitor preserved glycaemic targets.

Ongoing management in HNF1A‑MODY requires routine monitoring for microvascular complications (microalbuminuria, retinopathy, neuropathy) despite often earlier onset of hyperglycaemia, and attention to lifestyle measures (diet, exercise, weight management) and patient education about hypoglycaemia risk and symptom recognition. Regular follow‑up to reassess β‑cell function and adjust therapy is important because progressive β‑cell decline may eventually necessitate escalation of therapy. In summary, genetic diagnosis enabled a tailored therapeutic plan that avoided unnecessary long‑term insulin, minimized hypoglycaemia risk, and achieved durable glycaemic control in this patient with HNF1A‑MODY.
