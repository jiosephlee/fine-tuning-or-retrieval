Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

Chapter 5: ## Clinical Implications, Pitfalls, and Precision Medicine Lessons

This case of HNF1A-MODY underscores practical lessons for clinicians evaluating young adults with atypical diabetes. Recognizing red flags and following a stepwise diagnostic approach can prevent misclassification as type 1 or type 2 diabetes, allow targeted therapy changes (often avoiding lifelong insulin), and enable cascade testing and counseling of relatives.

Red flags for MODY in clinic
The clinical features in this patient illustrate classic “red flags” that should prompt consideration of MODY. These include diabetes onset at a young age (<35 years), an autosomal dominant pattern of early-onset diabetes in multiple generations, absence of islet autoantibodies (GADA, IA-2A, ZnT8), glycosuria out of proportion to glycaemia (related to reduced renal SGLT2 expression in HNF1A defects), and measurable but inadequate stimulated C‑peptide indicating preserved yet failing β‑cell function. Note that modest or absent features of insulin resistance (no acanthosis nigricans here) also support non‑typical T2D. These features should raise suspicion even when BMI is in the overweight range, as rising population BMI has reduced the discriminative power of weight alone.

A practical, stepwise diagnostic algorithm
A pragmatic workflow applied to this case begins with clinical triage (young onset, family history, clinical phenotype), followed by laboratory exclusion of type 1 diabetes with autoantibodies. Assessment of endogenous insulin secretion with fasting and stimulated C‑peptide (for example, glucagon stimulation) helps distinguish absolute insulin deficiency from preserved β‑cell reserve. Probability calculators (eg, Exeter MODY calculator) can aid pretest risk estimation but have limitations outside the populations in which they were derived; use them as adjuncts rather than gatekeepers. When clinical suspicion remains high, targeted genetic testing with an NGS monogenic diabetes panel is the next step. In this patient the sequence of antibody testing → C‑peptide assessment → targeted NGS permitted confirmation of HNF1A-MODY and supported therapeutic de-escalation.

Interpreting variants beyond database entries
Genetic results must be interpreted in context. Many clinically relevant HNF1A variants are novel and absent from public repositories. Applying the ACMG framework—integrating segregation with disease in the family, location in a functional domain (this case: DNA‑binding domain), computational predictions (eg, AlphaMissense), population frequency, and functional or published evidence—strengthens variant classification. Segregation of the variant with early-onset diabetes in relatives (as reported) added weight to pathogenicity. Clinicians should collaborate with geneticists or molecular laboratories when a novel variant is returned to ensure appropriate classification and counseling.

Therapeutic personalization based on genotype and physiology
Diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY directly informed treatment changes in this case. Traditionally, low‑dose sulfonylureas are first‑line for many HNF1A‑MODY patients because they amplify insulin secretion from residual β‑cells. However, GLP‑1 receptor agonists (GLP‑1RA) are an increasingly rational alternative: they can improve glucose control with lower hypoglycaemia risk and may support β‑cell function. In this patient, transitioning from insulin to a GLP‑1RA plus metformin achieved marked HbA1c reduction and durable control, illustrating that insulin can often be deprescribed after genetic confirmation when C‑peptide is preserved. Choice of agent should consider glycaemic severity at presentation, hypoglycaemia risk, comorbidities, availability, and patient preference. Note also that SGLT2 inhibitors may have particular safety considerations in HNF1A‑MODY because of baseline reduced renal glucose reabsorption and theoretical risk of volume depletion and euglycaemic ketoacidosis; such risks should be weighed carefully.

Family implications: cascade testing and counseling
A confirmed monogenic diagnosis has immediate family implications. First‑degree relatives should be offered predictive genetic testing to identify affected but asymptomatic carriers and guide early screening and management. Counseling should include inheritance patterns (autosomal dominant, 50% risk per offspring), variable age of onset, implications for pregnancy planning and neonatal risk when relevant, and the impact on therapy choices. Documentation of a pathogenic variant also facilitates targeted testing rather than broad, repeated metabolic screens for multiple family members.

Health‑system and equity considerations
This case highlights underdiagnosis of MODY, particularly in non‑European populations. Relying solely on calculators or restricted testing criteria derived from White cohorts can miss cases. Health systems should strive to increase access to affordable targeted NGS panels and genetic counseling, prioritize testing for people with clinical red flags, and educate providers about monogenic diabetes to avoid unnecessary lifelong insulin or inappropriate therapies. Cost-effectiveness improves when testing leads to treatment changes (e.g., stopping insulin) and informs family testing, but equitable access remains a challenge.

Common clinical pitfalls to avoid
Several pitfalls are exemplified by this and similar cases. Do not exclude MODY solely because BMI is elevated—obesity prevalence can obscure monogenic presentations. Avoid over-reliance on a single normal C‑peptide measurement; early type 1 diabetes may transiently preserve C‑peptide, so clinical context and serial measurements can help. Do not assume that negative autoantibodies completely rule out type 1 disease in unusual cases; rather, use them as part of the broader evaluation. Finally, do not defer genetic testing simply because a variant is not found in databases—novel variants can be pathogenic and require integrated interpretation.

Key teaching points and exam pearls
- Strong suspicion for MODY should arise with diabetes onset <35 years, autosomal dominant family history, negative islet autoantibodies, and preserved/stimulatable C‑peptide.
- Use a stepwise approach: clinical triage → autoantibodies → C‑peptide → calculate probability → targeted NGS when indicated.
- A pathogenic HNF1A diagnosis frequently permits shift from insulin to oral agents or GLP‑1RA, improving quality of life and reducing hypoglycaemia risk.
- Variant interpretation requires ACMG criteria, segregation data, functional domain knowledge, and computational predictions—absence from ClinVar is not dispositive.
- Cascade testing and genetic counseling are integral to care after monogenic diagnosis.
- Be mindful of health‑equity issues: MODY is underrecognized outside primarily studied populations; broaden testing access and clinician awareness.

In sum, this case demonstrates how integrating careful clinical phenotyping with judicious use of biochemical testing and targeted genetic sequencing enables precision medicine: correct molecular diagnosis, personalized therapy, family risk assessment, and avoidance of unnecessary long‑term insulin therapy.
