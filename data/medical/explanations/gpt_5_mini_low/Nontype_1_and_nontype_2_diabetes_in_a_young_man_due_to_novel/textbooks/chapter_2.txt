Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

Chapter 2: ## Differential Diagnosis and Diagnostic Workup for Early-Onset Nonautoimmune Diabetes

When a young adult presents with marked hyperglycaemia but without clear ketoacidosis or classic type 1 phenotypes, the clinician must systematically consider three main diagnostic groups: autoimmune type 1 diabetes (T1D), type 2 diabetes (T2D), and monogenic diabetes (MODY and related single‑gene disorders). In this case, the diagnostic pathway combined targeted history, focused biochemical testing and functional assessment of β‑cell reserve to distinguish HNF1A‑MODY from the more common types.

Key differentials and clinical clues
Type 1 diabetes typically presents with rapid onset of symptomatic hyperglycaemia, often with ketosis or ketoacidosis, markedly reduced endogenous insulin secretion and positive islet autoantibodies in most patients. Type 2 diabetes usually has slower onset, is strongly associated with insulin resistance (obesity, acanthosis nigricans, dyslipidaemia) and initially preserved or increased insulin secretion. MODY comprises several autosomal dominant, non‑autoimmune β‑cell disorders (most commonly HNF1A, GCK, HNF4A, and less commonly HNF1B and others), which typically present at a young age, often with a strong multigenerational family history and variable degrees of insulin secretory defect without evidence of autoimmunity. Less common monogenic forms (for example, neonatal diabetes genes) are considered when onset is extremely early or the phenotype is unusual.

In the reported patient several features supported MODY over T1D or typical T2D: disease onset in young adulthood with a striking multigenerational history of early‑onset diabetes (maternal grandmother, mother, brother), absence of diabetic ketoacidosis, glycosuria at relatively modest plasma glucose levels (consistent with reduced renal glucose reabsorption seen in some HNF1A cases), and lack of physical stigmata of insulin resistance (no acanthosis nigricans). Although the patient’s BMI was in the upper normal/overweight range and triglycerides were elevated, these findings do not exclude MODY and have become less discriminatory as obesity prevalence rises.

Autoantibody testing: strategy and interpretation
Testing for pancreatic islet autoantibodies is a first‑line step when distinguishing autoimmune from non‑autoimmune diabetes. The most clinically useful assays are glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen‑2 antibodies (IA‑2A), and zinc transporter 8 antibodies (ZnT8). The presence of one or more of these antibodies in a compatible clinical context strongly supports T1D. Conversely, persistently negative antibody testing increases the likelihood of non‑autoimmune diabetes, including MODY, but is not definitive. Caveats include that some patients with MODY may occasionally have positive autoantibodies (rare seropositive outliers), and that antibody titres can wane over time in T1D—so timing relative to onset matters. In this case both GADA and IA‑2A were negative (ZnT8 was not reported), which, along with clinical features, raised suspicion for MODY and argued against classic T1D.

Assessing β‑cell reserve: the glucagon stimulation test
Functional assessment of endogenous insulin secretion helps distinguish insulin‑deficient T1D from MODY or T2D. The glucagon stimulation test measures C‑peptide before and after intravenous glucagon; a stimulated C‑peptide >0.60 nmol/L (≈0.6 nmol/L) is commonly interpreted as evidence of preserved β‑cell reserve, whereas lower values suggest significant insulin deficiency. In this patient the C‑peptide rose from 0.39 nmol/L fasting to 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes. The stimulated value (>0.60 nmol/L) indicated residual insulin secretion inconsistent with absolute insulin deficiency of established T1D and supportive of non‑autoimmune diabetes such as MODY. Importantly, clinicians must recognize limitations: early after T1D onset C‑peptide can be preserved, and inter‑assay units or cutoffs vary between centres—so results are supportive but not definitive in isolation.

Using probability tools and when to pursue genetic testing
Clinical algorithms, such as the Exeter MODY probability calculator, use features (age at diagnosis, BMI, family history, treatment) to estimate the pretest probability of MODY and guide genetic testing. These tools are useful as a pretest filter but have limitations: they were derived in predominantly White European cohorts and may over‑ or under‑estimate probability in other ethnic groups. In this case, entering the patient’s data gave an estimated MODY probability of about 62%, a value that, together with negative autoantibodies and preserved stimulated C‑peptide, supported proceeding to genetic testing.

Deciding when to order genetic testing involves balancing clinical urgency and the need for reasonably stable metabolic conditions. If the clinical picture strongly suggests MODY (early onset, multigenerational diabetes, negative autoantibodies, preserved C‑peptide), testing should be arranged promptly because a confirmed genetic diagnosis alters therapy and family counselling. It is reasonable, as in this report, to stabilize hyperglycaemia first (to facilitate testing logistics and avoid confounding acute illness effects on C‑peptide), but not to delay testing until insulin is discontinued. Next‑generation sequencing (NGS) panels that include the common MODY genes (HNF1A, GCK, HNF4A, HNF1B, and others) are the preferred initial approach because they efficiently screen multiple candidate genes; variant interpretation should follow established guidelines (ACMG), integrating segregation, in silico prediction, population frequency and functional data where available.

Ancillary testing and supportive evaluations
Routine assessment for diabetes complications—urine albumin, retinal screening—are important baseline investigations but are not diagnostic for MODY. In this case microalbuminuria was absent and there were no retinal findings reported. The report did include autonomic evaluation by sudomotor testing, which showed markedly reduced conductance in the hands. Autonomic neuropathy is not specific to MODY and may reflect longstanding hyperglycaemia; it is a supportive finding of diabetes‑related complications rather than a diagnostic discriminator. Glycosuria, noted at presentation, can be especially helpful: HNF1A mutations reduce SGLT2 expression and lower the renal threshold for glucose, so glycosuria at lower plasma glucose levels can point toward HNF1A‑MODY.

Common pitfalls and interpretive cautions
Several pitfalls can mislead clinicians. A normal or only mildly reduced C‑peptide early after onset does not exclude evolving T1D; repeat testing and antibody assessment may be necessary if clinical evolution suggests autoimmune destruction. Family history can be under‑reported or obscured by misclassification of relatives as T2D. Rising obesity rates reduce the discriminatory power of BMI and metabolic syndrome features. Rarely, patients with MODY have measurable autoantibodies; conversely, some T1D patients are antibody‑negative. Therefore, diagnosis rests on integrating history, phenotype, antibody status and functional testing, with genetic testing as the definitive step when suspicion is high.

How the diagnostic workup led to the HNF1A diagnosis in this case
Applying these principles to the reported patient: a young adult with substantial hyperglycaemia but no ketoacidosis, a strong autosomal dominant family history, negative islet autoantibodies, a preserved stimulated C‑peptide (0.83 nmol/L) and glycosuria prompted targeted genetic testing. An NGS MODY panel identified a novel heterozygous HNF1A variant, clinching the diagnosis of HNF1A‑MODY. This genetic confirmation permitted de‑escalation from insulin and selection of an oral/GLP‑1 receptor agonist‑led regimen more appropriate to the underlying beta‑cell defect, illustrating how an evidence‑based diagnostic workup guides precision management.

In summary, distinguishing MODY from T1D and T2D in early‑onset diabetes requires careful synthesis of family history and phenotype, antibody testing, objective assessment of β‑cell reserve, and selective use of probability tools to decide on genetic testing. Genetic confirmation is central because it establishes the diagnosis, permits targeted therapy and enables family screening.
