Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

Chapter 1: ## Case Presentation and Initial Assessment

This patient was a young adult whose hyperglycaemia was first identified incidentally during an annual health examination, when routine testing revealed both elevated blood glucose and glycosuria. The demographic context and mode of detection are important: the patient was not acutely ill, and the abnormality was discovered on screening rather than emergently, which is a common scenario in some forms of monogenic diabetes such as HNF1A-MODY.

Clinically he reported classic hyperglycaemic symptoms — increased thirst, increased appetite and recent weight loss — but notably there were no features of ketoacidosis at presentation. The absence of diabetic ketoacidosis argues against fulminant type 1 diabetes at that time. On examination there was no acanthosis nigricans, a sign often associated with insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes. Together, symptomatic hyperglycaemia without DKA and without stigmata of marked insulin resistance should raise consideration of non–type 1, non–type 2 causes of early-onset diabetes, including MODY.

Family history provided a strong diagnostic clue. Several first- and second-degree relatives had diabetes diagnosed at young ages (maternal grandmother in her 30s, mother in her late 20s, and a younger brother at age 20). This pattern of multiple affected relatives across successive generations is consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance, which is characteristic of most MODY subtypes (including HNF1A-MODY). Family clustering with early onset is one of the most helpful historical features distinguishing monogenic diabetes from typical type 1 or type 2 diabetes, where family patterns are more heterogeneous.

Anthropometric and vital signs were reassuring for the absence of severe obesity or hemodynamic instability: BMI was 24.6 kg/m2 (near the upper limit of normal to overweight range) and blood pressure 112/70 mmHg. A modestly elevated BMI does not exclude MODY; rising population rates of overweight and obesity make BMI less discriminating. In HNF1A-MODY, patients are frequently non-obese or only mildly overweight, and they typically lack the marked insulin resistance phenotype seen in many type 2 patients.

Baseline glycaemic indices documented substantial hyperglycaemia. HbA1c was 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), fasting plasma glucose 12.4 mmol/L, and the 2-hour value after a 75 g OGTT was 19.8 mmol/L. These values confirm diabetes with marked postprandial and fasting hyperglycaemia. The prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia (high 2-hour OGTT value) is consistent with the early β-cell secretory defect typical of HNF1A-MODY, in which progressive loss of regulated insulin secretion leads to exaggerated postprandial excursions before absolute insulin deficiency develops.

The presence of glycosuria despite only modestly elevated BMI is a noteworthy diagnostic clue in HNF1A-MODY. HNF1A regulates SGLT2 expression in renal proximal tubules; pathogenic HNF1A variants can lower the renal threshold for glucose reabsorption, producing glycosuria at lower plasma glucose levels than would be expected. In practice, unexpected glycosuria in a non-ketotic young adult with preserved weight is one of the features that should prompt consideration of HNF1A-MODY.

Lipid testing in this patient showed a borderline low HDL and elevated triglycerides, which can suggest components of metabolic syndrome. However, interpretation must be cautious: as the Discussion in this report notes, the increasing prevalence of overweight and dyslipidaemia in the general population blunts the ability of metabolic features to reliably separate MODY from type 2 diabetes. Thus, metabolic abnormalities do not exclude MODY and may coexist.

An autonomic neuropathy screen performed early in the assessment detected severely reduced sudomotor function over the hands, measured by electrochemical skin conductance testing. Sudomotor testing assesses small fibre autonomic nerve function and can be an early indicator of diabetic autonomic neuropathy. In a young person with a relatively recent diagnosis of diabetes, finding significant sudomotor dysfunction is atypical and warrants careful interpretation: it may reflect longer-standing hyperglycaemia prior to detection, or variability in test sensitivity and specificity. It does not point to a specific diabetes subtype but underscores the need for prompt and effective glycaemic control and baseline complication screening.

At presentation, a pragmatic initial working diagnosis was type 2 diabetes (given the absence of ketoacidosis and some metabolic derangement) with consideration for non–type 1 causes because of family history and clinical features suggestive of impaired insulin secretion. Because of the degree of hyperglycaemia (HbA1c >10%) and symptomatic presentation, the team elected to begin combination therapy comprising basal insulin (degludec 8 units/day) to rapidly control glucose and reduce glucotoxicity, together with metformin (1500 mg/day) to target hepatic glucose output and a DPP-4 inhibitor (saxagliptin 5 mg/day) to augment incretin-mediated insulin secretion with low hypoglycaemia risk. Starting insulin while further evaluation proceeds is a common and safe approach when glucose is severe; it can preserve β-cell function and allow more accurate later assessment of endogenous insulin secretion. This initial regimen was reasonable pending results of autoimmune antibody testing, stimulated C‑peptide measurement, and genetic testing for MODY, which would refine the diagnosis and long-term therapy.

In summary, the combination of screening-detected hyperglycaemia with glycosuria, symptomatic but non-ketotic presentation, lack of marked insulin resistance, a clear autosomal-dominant family history of early-onset diabetes, and marked postprandial hyperglycaemia on OGTT together raised strong suspicion for HNF1A-MODY and guided the team to pursue biochemical and genetic confirmatory testing while initiating effective glucose-lowering therapy.
