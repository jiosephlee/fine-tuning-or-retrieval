Title: Blog about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

# Precision de-escalation: When and how to stop insulin in HNF1A‑MODY

What makes this case interesting: a young man with marked hyperglycaemia (HbA1c 10.2%), glycosuria and a strong family history was started on insulin while investigations were underway — a common, pragmatic reflex — but ultimately had a previously unreported HNF1A mutation. After genetic confirmation, his team successfully withdrew insulin and landed on GLP‑1 receptor agonist + metformin with durable control (HbA1c fell to 6.0% at 6 months). That sequence — “stabilize with insulin, confirm subtype, then tailor therapy” — is a practical template for MODY clinics. This post gives a stepwise, physiology‑anchored approach to stopping insulin in HNF1A‑MODY and choosing the ideal oral/ injectable alternative.

Context you must keep in mind
- HNF1A‑MODY is a progressive β‑cell secretory defect. Patients typically have preserved but insufficient insulin secretion early on and get worse over years.
- Many are mislabelled as type 1 or type 2 diabetes; genetic confirmation changes therapy and prognosis.
- HNF1A mutations lower SGLT2 expression -> glycosuria is common. This affects symptoms and means we should be cautious with SGLT2 inhibitors.

When to consider de‑escalation: the key signals
- Genetic confirmation of HNF1A pathogenic/likely pathogenic variant (this changes the pretest probability and management).
- Evidence of residual β‑cell function: stimulated C‑peptide is most useful. In this report, a glucagon stimulation test showed stimulated C‑peptide 0.83 nmol/L (supporting preserved function). A common pragmatic threshold is a stimulated C‑peptide >0.6 nmol/L to suggest the patient can be managed without insulin in many contexts.
- Clinical trajectory: falling HbA1c on current therapy, no severe hyperglycaemia or DKA, and patient symptoms improving.
- Absence of type 1 autoimmunity (GADA, IA‑2, ZnT8 negative).
- Patient factors: risk of hypoglycaemia, lifestyle, work/school constraints, preferences.

A stepwise strategy to withdraw insulin (practical bedside sequence)
1. Stabilize first (what was done here)
   - If patient presents with very high glucose or symptomatic hyperglycaemia, short course of insulin to rapidly correct and relieve catabolism is reasonable while you work up the etiology.
   - Start background metformin as tolerated (was used here at 1500 mg/day) unless contraindicated.

2. Do the diagnostic workup early
   - Autoantibodies (GADA, IA‑2, ZnT8).
   - Measure endogenous secretion: fasting and stimulated C‑peptide (glucagon stimulation or mixed‑meal tolerance test).
   - Family history and, where available, MODY probability tools to prioritize genetic testing.
   - Obtain genetic testing promptly; NGS panels now make this feasible.

3. Reassess at the first follow‑up after genetic result (the moment for shared decision)
   - If HNF1A confirmed AND stimulated C‑peptide comfortably >0.6 nmol/L AND HbA1c improving (or acceptable), plan stepwise withdrawal.
   - If stimulated C‑peptide low (<0.3–0.4 nmol/L) or patient had DKA, continue insulin and reassess.

4. Practical insulin taper (example used in practice and reflected in the case)
   - Basal insulin only (avoid stopping rapid insulin abruptly if on multiple daily injections — but in this case only basal degludec 8 units/day was used).
   - Reduce basal by ~20–30% and add or uptitrate non‑insulin therapy (see options below). Monitor self‑glucose (pre‑meal and bedtime) for 3–7 days.
   - If glucose stable or improved, stop basal. If hyperglycaemia recurs, step back to last tolerated dose and reassess beta‑cell function and adherence.
   - Always have a safety plan for hyperglycaemia/DKA signs and clear thresholds for restarting insulin.

Choosing the replacement regimen: where the agents fit

Sulfonylureas (classic first‑line for HNF1A‑MODY)
- Mechanism: enhance insulin secretion by closing KATP channels — directly augment the defect in HNF1A.
- Pros: often very effective at low dose in early HNF1A‑MODY; oral, inexpensive.
- Cons: higher risk of hypoglycaemia, weight gain, and possible earlier β‑cell exhaustion with long term use. Less attractive in patients who have experienced hypoglycaemia, those with erratic meals, or with occupations where hypoglycaemia is high risk.
- Practical tip: If chosen, start low and titrate cautiously; counsel on hypoglycaemia recognition and supply glucose.

GLP‑1 receptor agonists (why they were used in this case)
- Mechanism: glucose‑dependent insulin secretion + appetite suppression + potential β‑cell preservation (clinical trials in MODY are small but suggest benefit).
- Pros: low hypoglycaemia risk (glucose‑dependent), weight neutral or weight loss, robust HbA1c lowering — attractive when hypoglycaemia risk is a concern or sulfonylureas avoided.
- Cons: injectable (though once‑weekly options simplify this), cost, GI side effects.
- In this case the team chose dulaglutide after genetic confirmation, allowing insulin cessation and achieving HbA1c 6.0% at 6 months — a tidy example of GLP‑1RA utility in HNF1A‑MODY when β‑cell function is moderate.

DPP‑4 inhibitors
- Mechanism: modest incretin enhancement, oral.
- Pros: safe, well tolerated, small hypoglycaemia risk.
- Cons: modest efficacy vs GLP‑1RA and sulfonylurea. May be useful as adjunct or in patients unwilling/unable to use injectables or sulfonylureas.
- In the case, saxagliptin was used alongside metformin early on; after stable control on GLP‑1RA, regimen simplified to metformin + saxagliptin for maintenance with good effect.

Metformin
- Mechanism: reduces hepatic glucose output and improves insulin sensitivity.
- Role: standard adjunctive therapy in MODY when insulin resistance features or overweight are present; also beneficial for cardiovascular risk profile.
- Continue unless contraindicated. In this case metformin (1500 mg/day) remained a backbone of therapy.

SGLT2 inhibitors — think twice
- HNF1A variants reduce SGLT2 expression → baseline glycosuria and higher risk of dehydration/DKA in some reports.
- Use cautiously in HNF1A‑MODY; the case discussion flags reports of severe dehydration and DKA when SGLT2 inhibition is superimposed. Avoid routine use unless a compelling reason and with careful patient selection and education.

Monitoring after de‑escalation
- Self‑monitoring: frequent capillary glucose checks during taper (pre‑meal and bedtime) and symptoms review.
- Clinic checks: HbA1c at ~3 months; sooner if glucose unstable.
- Repeat C‑peptide is not mandatory but may be helpful if control deteriorates.
- Surveillance for microvascular complications per usual guidelines.

Putting it together for the patient in this report
- Presented with HbA1c 10.2% and symptomatic hyperglycaemia → short‑term basal insulin + metformin + DPP‑4 inhibitor to stabilize.
- Workup: negative autoantibodies, glucagon stimulated C‑peptide 0.83 nmol/L, strong family history → high suspicion for MODY; genetic testing confirmed novel HNF1A variant.
- After confirmation and reassessment (HbA1c improving to 9.1%), insulin was withdrawn and dulaglutide introduced; metformin continued. This allowed safe insulin cessation and excellent glycaemic control (HbA1c 6.0% at 6 months). Later simplified to metformin + saxagliptin for maintenance while monitoring.

Clinical pearls
- Don’t reflexively lock a young adult into lifelong insulin before completing the autoantibody/C‑peptide/genetic assessment; short‑term insulin to stabilize is reasonable, but plan for reassessment.
- Stimulated C‑peptide >0.6 nmol/L is a practical threshold suggesting enough residual β‑cell function for many non‑insulin regimens. Use it, not just clinical impression.
- Sulfonylureas remain a valid first‑line for HNF1A‑MODY, but GLP‑1 receptor agonists are excellent alternatives when hypoglycaemia risk or weight loss is desirable.
- Avoid SGLT2 inhibitors without careful consideration in HNF1A‑MODY due to baseline SGLT2 downregulation and reports of dehydration/DKA.
- Shared decision making is key: the patient’s work, hypoglycaemia tolerance, and preferences will decide sulfonylurea vs GLP‑1RA vs DPP‑4 strategy.

Take‑home messages
- If you start insulin while investigating young‑onset diabetes, have a clear plan to reassess once antibody and C‑peptide results arrive and genetic testing is available.
- Consider insulin withdrawal when HNF1A‑MODY is genetically confirmed, stimulated C‑peptide is preserved (>0.6 nmol/L), HbA1c is improving, and there’s no DKA history.
- Sulfonylureas are effective first‑line for HNF1A‑MODY but carry hypoglycaemia risk; GLP‑1RAs offer potent glucose lowering with less hypoglycaemia and may be preferable as β‑cell function declines or hypoglycaemia risk is high.
- Metformin is a useful adjunct; DPP‑4 inhibitors can be used for modest effect; avoid routine SGLT2 inhibitor use without careful risk assessment.
- Monitor closely during the taper with frequent glucose checks, early HbA1c, and a plan to restart insulin if glycaemic control deteriorates.

This case is a neat demonstration of precision de‑escalation: stabilize, confirm genotype and physiology, then tailor therapy to the molecular defect and the patient — not the label.
