Title: Blog about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

# The sweet-pee signal: Using glycosuria as a clue to HNF1A‑MODY

What makes this case interesting
A young man with new, marked hyperglycaemia (A1c 10.2%) and striking glycosuria was initially treated as type 2 diabetes with insulin, metformin and a DPP‑4 inhibitor. He had negative islet autoantibodies, a preserved but insufficient stimulated C‑peptide (0.83 nmol/L), a family history of early‑onset diabetes, and—after genetic testing—a novel heterozygous HNF1A mutation (p.N270S). Within months he was transitioned off insulin to a GLP‑1RA + metformin with excellent glycaemic response (A1c down to 6.0%). The “odd” finding that helped point away from routine type 1/2 labels was disproportionate glycosuria for the degree of hyperglycaemia — a classic, underused clue for HNF1A‑MODY.

Why glycosuria matters in HNF1A‑MODY
HNF1A is a transcription factor expressed in pancreas, liver and kidney. One downstream effect is regulation of SGLT2 expression in proximal renal tubules. Pathogenic HNF1A variants commonly reduce SGLT2 expression, lowering the renal threshold for glucose reabsorption. Clinically that means:

- Glycosuria occurs at lower plasma glucose levels than expected.
- Patients can show impressive urinary glucose despite only moderate hyperglycaemia.
- Glycosuria may lead to polyuria, dehydration risk, and in rare situations predispose to euglycaemic DKA if precipitated (and SGLT2 inhibitors might amplify these risks).

In short: unexpected glycosuria = red flag for HNF1A involvement.

Putting the urinalysis into clinical context
A urinalysis is cheap, fast and widely available — but under-valued. Use it as a data point, not a diagnosis. When you see glycosuria that seems “too much” for the measured plasma glucose, think beyond measurement error and consider renal threshold abnormalities such as HNF1A‑MODY.

Key contextual tests and features that strengthen suspicion:
- Family history of early‑onset diabetes with autosomal dominant pattern (this case: mother, maternal grandmother, brother).
- Negative beta‑cell autoantibodies (GADA, IA‑2, ZnT8).
- Preserved but inadequate endogenous insulin secretion (stimulated C‑peptide ~0.83 nmol/L in this patient).
- OGTT showing exaggerated postprandial hyperglycaemia (2‑hour glucose 19.8 mmol/L here) — typical for HNF1A where postprandial excursions can be prominent.
- Absence of ketoacidosis at presentation (unlike classic type 1).
- BMI not strongly suggestive of insulin resistance (this patient BMI 24.6, some metabolic features but not frank obesity).

How to interpret OGTT + glycosuria + no ketones
- OGTT with a large postprandial rise supports a primary β‑cell secretory defect (MODY phenotype) rather than pure insulin resistance.
- Glycosuria at lower glycaemic thresholds suggests reduced SGLT2-mediated reabsorption — classic for HNF1A.
- Lack of ketones and negative autoantibodies argues against type 1 autoimmune diabetes.
Putting these together increases the pretest probability of HNF1A‑MODY and should lower the threshold to pursue genetic testing.

Diagnostic pathway in practice
1. If you find disproportionate glycosuria, confirm plasma glucose and consider repeating urinalysis to exclude lab error or contamination.
2. Take a focused family history for early-onset diabetes.
3. Order: fasting glucose, A1c, OGTT if needed to document postprandial excursion, urine ketones, urinary glucose if available, stimulated C‑peptide and diabetes autoantibodies.
4. Use the clinical picture (negative antibodies, preserved C‑peptide, family pattern, glycosuria) to decide on gene testing — consider an NGS monogenic diabetes panel rather than single‑gene tests in many settings.
5. Apply variant interpretation using ACMG criteria and correlate genotype with phenotype (as in this case, a novel HNF1A variant was classified pathogenic using multiple lines of evidence).

Therapeutic implications (why it matters)
A molecular diagnosis changes treatment and prognosis:
- HNF1A‑MODY often responds well to low‑dose sulfonylureas initially (they boost insulin secretion) — but consider hypoglycaemia risk as β‑cell function wanes.
- GLP‑1 receptor agonists are an effective alternative and may be safer with lower hypoglycaemia risk (this patient did well on dulaglutide).
- Insulin can often be withdrawn if β‑cell function is adequate and glycaemia controlled after diagnosis.
- Be cautious with SGLT2 inhibitors: baseline reduced SGLT2 activity in HNF1A may increase risk of dehydration, genital infections and euglycaemic DKA—use with caution and individual risk assessment.
- Family screening is appropriate once a pathogenic variant is identified.

Clinical pearls
- Don’t ignore the urinalysis. Glycosuria disproportionate to blood glucose is a practical bedside clue to HNF1A‑MODY.
- Negative autoantibodies + preserved stimulated C‑peptide + family history = high yield for genetic testing even if BMI is intermediate or metabolic features are mixed.
- OGTT is helpful: pronounced postprandial hyperglycaemia fits HNF1A pathophysiology.
- A genetic diagnosis allows precision therapy (sulfonylurea or GLP‑1RA instead of lifelong insulin), avoids unnecessary treatments, and prompts family counselling.

Take‑home messages
- Unexpected glycosuria should prompt consideration of HNF1A‑MODY, especially with early‑onset diabetes and family history.
- Integrate urinalysis with OGTT, C‑peptide and antibody testing to build the case for genetic testing.
- A genetic diagnosis changes management: consider sulfonylureas or GLP‑1RAs and be cautious with SGLT2 inhibitors.
- Low cost, high yield: use the simple urine dipstick as a nudge toward precision medicine in young adults with atypical diabetes.

This patient’s story is a reminder: sometimes a cheap urine dipstick—paired with a good history and a few targeted tests—can steer you from broad labels to a precise diagnosis and much better, tailored care.
