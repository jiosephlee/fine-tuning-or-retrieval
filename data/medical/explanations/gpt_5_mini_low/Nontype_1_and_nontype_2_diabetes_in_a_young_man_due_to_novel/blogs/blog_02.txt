Title: Blog about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

## From Variant to Verdict: Applying ACMG to a novel HNF1A mutation

What makes this case interesting? A young man with early-onset hyperglycaemia, glycosuria, negative islet autoantibodies, a strong maternal family history of early diabetes and a glucagon-stimulated C‑peptide consistent with residual β-cell function — classic red flags for MODY. Genetic testing returned a previously unreported heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G; p.N270S). The challenge: how do you move from “novel variant” to a clinically actionable diagnosis? This case is a neat, real-world demonstration of applying ACMG variant-interpretation logic at the bedside to guide precision therapy (insulin withdrawal, GLP‑1 RA + metformin, cascade testing).

Why the verdict matters clinically
MODY is often mislabelled as type 1 or type 2 diabetes. Getting the genetic diagnosis right changes therapy (sulfonylureas, GLP‑1 RAs, avoid unnecessary lifelong insulin), informs prognosis, and drives family cascade testing. In this patient, confirmation of HNF1A-MODY allowed cessation of insulin and maintenance of glycaemic control with GLP‑1 RA and metformin — a tangible example of precision medicine.

Translating ACMG into bedside steps using p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G)

1) Start with the phenotype: is it a fit?
Before you touch the sequence, ask whether the clinical picture fits the gene. HNF1A-MODY typically presents with:
- Early-onset diabetes (often before 25 but can be later),
- Negative pancreatic autoantibodies,
- Preserved but progressively failing β-cell function (C‑peptide not absent),
- Glycosuria from a low renal glucose threshold,
- Autosomal dominant family history of diabetes.

Our case ticks these boxes: young adult, negative GADA/IA‑2, stimulated C‑peptide 0.83 nmol/L (suggesting residual function), glycosuria, and multiple affected maternal relatives. Phenotype is supportive evidence.

2) Is the variant novel or reported?
The variant c.809A>G (p.N270S) was not present in ClinVar or VarSome and had not been reported in the literature. Absence from population and clinical databases is an important datapoint (it argues against benign common polymorphism), but it’s not decisive alone.

3) Location matters — domain and function
The substitution lies in exon 4 within the DNA‑binding domain of HNF1A. Functional domains matter: pathogenic variants cluster in the DNA‑binding domain for HNF1A, where disruption is more likely to affect transcriptional regulation of insulin and GLUT2. ACMG considers “critical domain” location as moderate evidence for pathogenicity (PM1). This variant’s domain location therefore increases pretest probability that it is deleterious.

4) In silico predictions — supportive, not definitive
This variant has a very high AlphaMissense score (0.995 in the report). High-confidence computational predictions that a missense change is damaging provide supporting ACMG evidence (PP3). But remember: algorithms can’t replace functional assays or segregation data — they strengthen your impression but don’t clinch the diagnosis.

5) Segregation with disease — powerful real-world evidence
Segregation with disease across affected family members moves the needle substantially. In the case, the variant segregated with diabetes in several maternal relatives (maternal grandmother, mother, younger brother with early-onset diabetes). Co-segregation is strong evidence (PP1) under ACMG when seen in multiple affected family members. The more informative the pedigree (multiple meioses, documented phenotypes and genotypes), the stronger this evidence becomes.

6) Aggregate the lines of evidence per ACMG
Putting it together for p.N270S:
- PM1: Location in a critical DNA‑binding domain (moderate)
- PP3: Strong in silico deleterious prediction (supporting)
- PM2: Absence from population/clinical databases (moderate)
- PP1: Co-segregation with disease in the family (supporting to moderate depending on numbers)
- Phenotype match: Fits HNF1A clinical picture (supporting)

Combining these criteria (two moderate + multiple supporting lines plus phenotype fit) is consistent with a likely pathogenic/pathogenic classification by ACMG rules in this clinical context — which is what the authors report.

Practical tips for clinicians ordering and interpreting MODY NGS panels

- Pick the right test for the clinical question: use a targeted monogenic diabetes panel (includes HNF1A, GCK, HNF4A, HNF1B, etc.) or exome sequencing if the phenotype is atypical. Panels are faster, cheaper, and often return actionable, curated results.
- Provide good clinical metadata with the test request: age at onset, autoantibody status, C‑peptide, BMI, family history and any atypical features. Variant interpretation improves with phenotype correlation.
- Expect variants of uncertain significance (VUS): many novel missense changes will land here unless you can demonstrate segregation or functional impact. Be prepared to act on “likely pathogenic” but cautious with VUS.
- Ask about lab reporting standards: ensure the lab applies ACMG/AMP guidelines and provides the evidence used for classification. If the report lacks segregation or domain data, request reanalysis if additional family testing becomes available.

Communicating uncertainty — how to talk to patients
Be transparent. Explain that genetic testing often yields three classes: pathogenic/likely pathogenic (actionable), benign/likely benign, and VUS (uncertain). For a likely pathogenic HNF1A variant in a patient with matching phenotype and family history, you can explain that the results are clinically meaningful: they explain the diabetes, change therapy options, and indicate autosomal dominant inheritance. For VUS results, explain that they don’t confirm or exclude a genetic diagnosis and that further steps (family testing, research assays) may reclassify the variant.

Cascade testing — a must in MODY families
When you identify a likely pathogenic HNF1A variant:
- Offer targeted testing (single-site testing is cheap and quick) to first-degree relatives, especially those with early-onset diabetes or suspected dysglycaemia.
- Use results to tailor therapy: family members with the variant may avoid unnecessary insulin and benefit from sulfonylureas/GLP‑1 RAs or specific surveillance.
- Provide genetic counselling: discuss reproductive risks (50% transmission), implications for insurance (jurisdictions differ), and psychosocial aspects.

When to do functional studies or reclassification
If uncertainty persists (e.g., segregation data incomplete), consider:
- Recruiting additional affected/unaffected relatives for targeted testing (often the quickest way to strengthen evidence).
- Contacting the reporting lab to see if functional assays are available or if they will periodically re-evaluate the variant as new data accumulates.
- Collaborating with research groups studying HNF1A to access functional testing (transcriptional assays), if clinically relevant.

Pitfalls and cautions
- Don’t overcall pathogenicity based on in silico tools or domain location alone.
- Beware phenocopies: common T2D can coexist in families; segregation must be interpreted in the context of age at onset and phenotype.
- Avoid reflexively stopping insulin in all genetically confirmed MODY cases — consider current glycaemic control and residual β‑cell function; this patient’s insulin was withdrawn after improvement and genetic confirmation with preserved stimulated C‑peptide.

Clinical pearls
- Remember the red flags for MODY: young-onset diabetes, negative autoantibodies, preserved C‑peptide, glycosuria and a dominant family history.
- Domain matters: missense variants in HNF1A’s DNA‑binding domain are more likely to be pathogenic.
- Co-segregation is often the single most persuasive clinical evidence; get family samples if possible.
- Genetic diagnosis is actionable: it can change therapy, prognosis and prompt cascade testing.
- Labs vary — insist on ACMG-based classification and transparent evidence summaries.

Take-home points
- The c.809A>G (p.N270S) HNF1A variant illustrates how combining phenotype fit, domain location (DNA‑binding), absence from databases, strong in silico prediction (AlphaMissense 0.995) and family co‑segregation yields a likely pathogenic classification under ACMG — and a clinically actionable diagnosis of HNF1A‑MODY in this patient.
- When you suspect MODY, order a targeted NGS panel, include thorough clinical data, and be ready to pursue cascade testing — genetic results change management.
- Use a multidisciplinary approach: clinician + genetic counsellor + diagnostic lab to interpret variants, communicate uncertainty, and implement family testing.

If you’re seeing young patients with atypical diabetes — think beyond the type 1/type 2 binary. Genetic testing guided by ACMG reasoning turns variants into verdicts that matter at the bedside.
