Title: Blog about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

# Beyond Type 1 vs Type 2: Recognizing HNF1A‑MODY at the bedside

This case grabs attention because it’s not the usual “type 1 vs type 2” story. A young man with marked hyperglycaemia (HbA1c 10.2%), glycosuria but no ketoacidosis, negative islet autoantibodies, a strong multigenerational history of early‑onset diabetes, and a novel pathogenic HNF1A variant. He avoided lifelong insulin after genetic confirmation and treatment was rationalised to a GLP‑1 RA + metformin with excellent glycaemic response (HbA1c fell to 6.0% at 6 months). That sequence — clinical suspicion → targeted testing → precision therapy — is the whole point: recognising HNF1A‑MODY changes management.

Below I walk through a pragmatic, bedside approach to young‑onset, non‑ketotic hyperglycaemia with glycosuria and autosomal‑dominant family history, focusing on the tests that help separate MODY from type 1 and type 2 diabetes, practical thresholds, common pitfalls and treatment implications.

## Why suspect HNF1A‑MODY?
- Age: presentation typically <25 years, but can be later. Family patterns often show autosomal‑dominant transmission across generations (multiple affected relatives, early onset).
- Clinical phenotype: prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia, glycosuria out of proportion to plasma glucose (lowered renal glucose threshold), absence of obesity or insulin resistance signs is common but not universal.
- Lab clues: negative pancreatic autoantibodies (GAD, IA‑2, ZnT8), preserved but inadequate insulin secretion (C‑peptide present), no DKA. Genetic testing confirms diagnosis.

This case checks these boxes — and importantly, the HNF1A mutation found was novel, emphasising that absence from databases (ClinVar/VarSome) doesn’t mean non‑pathogenic if clinical and segregation data fit.

## Quick pragmatic diagnostic algorithm at the bedside
1. Initial triage (young patient with hyperglycaemia)
   - Confirm hyperglycaemia: HbA1c, fasting glucose, consider OGTT if presentation subtle but suspicion high.
   - Look for DKA: presence argues for type 1 (but not absolute).
   - Take a focused family history: at least 3 generations? early‑onset diabetes in multiple relatives? autosomal dominant pattern raises MODY probability.

2. First-line laboratory tests to distinguish types
   - Islet autoantibodies: GAD, IA‑2, ZnT8. Negative results make type 1 less likely (especially if all are negative), but positive autoantibodies don’t completely exclude a monogenic cause in rare cases.
   - C‑peptide (fasting and/or stimulated): preserved endogenous secretion supports non‑type 1. Practical thresholds:
     - Stimulated C‑peptide >0.6 nmol/L (≈1.8 ng/mL) suggests residual β‑cell function; values <0.3 nmol/L support insulin deficiency.
     - In this case glucagon stimulation raised C‑peptide from 0.39 → 0.83 nmol/L, indicating insufficient but present β‑cell function — typical for MODY.
   - OGTT: marked postprandial hyperglycaemia (2‑hr glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L) with relatively lower fasting hyperglycaemia is a pattern seen in HNF1A‑MODY.

3. Additional supportive clues
   - Glycosuria disproportionate to plasma glucose — think lowered renal threshold (HNF1A regulates SGLT2).
   - Lack of insulin resistance stigmata (no acanthosis nigricans) helps but isn’t definitive — rising obesity blurs this.
   - Young age at diagnosis and family clustering are big clues.

4. When to order genetic testing
   - Strong clinical suspicion: early onset, autosomal dominant family history, negative autoantibodies and preserved C‑peptide.
   - Use a MODY gene panel (NGS). Remember: many HNF1A variants are private/novel — interpret variant pathogenicity using ACMG criteria plus clinical segregation.

## Interpreting the key tests — pitfalls and timing
- Autoantibodies: positive GAD/IA‑2/ZnT8 support type 1 but seroconversion can vary; in long‑standing type 1 antibodies may fall. Conversely, a small proportion of MODY patients may have autoantibodies — interpret in context.
- C‑peptide: single fasting samples are helpful but stimulated tests (glucagon or mixed meal) are more informative of reserve. Early type 1 disease may have preserved C‑peptide — recheck if clinical course suggests otherwise.
- OGTT: useful to unmask postprandial hyperglycaemia in HNF1A; a very high 2‑hour glucose with relatively modest fasting hyperglycaemia should raise suspicion.
- BMI/metabolic syndrome: historically lower in MODY than T2D, but rising global obesity makes BMI unreliable. Don’t exclude MODY simply because BMI is ≥25 or features of metabolic syndrome are present.
- Prediction tools (Exeter MODY calculator): helpful but derived from European cohorts and may miscalibrate in non‑White populations — treat probability estimates as guides, not absolutes.

## Treatment implications — why the diagnosis matters
- HNF1A‑MODY is classically sulfonylurea‑sensitive (low doses often effective), because the defect is in insulin secretion rather than insulin resistance.
- GLP‑1 receptor agonists are an attractive alternative: effective, lower hypoglycaemia risk than sulfonylureas, and may help preserve β‑cell function. This case shows durable response to a GLP‑1 RA + metformin after stopping insulin.
- Insulin: may be required initially when hyperglycaemia is severe, but genetic diagnosis often allows down‑titration or cessation if β‑cell reserve exists.
- SGLT2 inhibitors: theoretically risky in HNF1A‑MODY because baseline SGLT2 expression is reduced — reports describe severe glycosuria, dehydration and even euglycaemic DKA. Use with caution; weigh risks vs benefits.
- Family screening and counselling: once a pathogenic variant is found, cascade testing and tailored counselling are high yield.

## The genetic testing landscape — practical notes
- NGS panels are now feasible and can detect novel variants. Novel ≠ benign: pathogenicity assessment uses ACMG criteria, functional domain (DNA‑binding domain variants are common), in‑silico scores, and family segregation.
- Don’t delay testing when it will change management (e.g., allow stopping insulin, selecting oral therapy).
- If a variant of uncertain significance (VUS) is found, manage based on clinical phenotype and consider familial segregation studies to strengthen interpretation.

## Clinical pearls
- Think MODY when a young patient has significant hyperglycaemia but:
  - No DKA, negative islet autoantibodies, and
  - Preserved stimulated C‑peptide, and
  - Strong autosomal dominant family history.
- Glycosuria out of proportion to plasma glucose is a red flag for HNF1A‑MODY (SGLT2 downregulation).
- BMI and metabolic syndrome are imperfect discriminators now — don’t rule out MODY in overweight patients.
- The Exeter MODY calculator is useful but may over/under‑estimate risk in non‑European populations — use clinical judgement.
- Genetic diagnosis changes therapy: many HNF1A patients can be managed without long‑term insulin; sulfonylureas or GLP‑1 RAs are reasonable options; be cautious with SGLT2 inhibitors.

## Take‑home messages
- Consider MODY (especially HNF1A) in young, non‑ketotic hyperglycaemia with negative autoantibodies, preserved C‑peptide and a dominant family history.
- Use stimulated C‑peptide, OGTT, and autoantibodies together; none alone is definitive.
- Genetic testing is key — it guides therapy (often allows stopping insulin), family screening and prognosis.
- BMI alone is a poor exclusion criterion; interpret MODY probability tools with caution in non‑White patients.
- Treatment options include low‑dose sulfonylureas or GLP‑1 RAs; avoid routine SGLT2 use without careful risk assessment in HNF1A‑MODY.

If you see a young patient with high glucose, glycosuria, no DKA and multiple affected relatives — pause and ask: could this be MODY? A timely genetic diagnosis can spare lifelong insulin and unlock precision therapy.



## From Variant to Verdict: Applying ACMG to a novel HNF1A mutation

What makes this case interesting? A young man with early-onset hyperglycaemia, glycosuria, negative islet autoantibodies, a strong maternal family history of early diabetes and a glucagon-stimulated C‑peptide consistent with residual β-cell function — classic red flags for MODY. Genetic testing returned a previously unreported heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G; p.N270S). The challenge: how do you move from “novel variant” to a clinically actionable diagnosis? This case is a neat, real-world demonstration of applying ACMG variant-interpretation logic at the bedside to guide precision therapy (insulin withdrawal, GLP‑1 RA + metformin, cascade testing).

Why the verdict matters clinically
MODY is often mislabelled as type 1 or type 2 diabetes. Getting the genetic diagnosis right changes therapy (sulfonylureas, GLP‑1 RAs, avoid unnecessary lifelong insulin), informs prognosis, and drives family cascade testing. In this patient, confirmation of HNF1A-MODY allowed cessation of insulin and maintenance of glycaemic control with GLP‑1 RA and metformin — a tangible example of precision medicine.

Translating ACMG into bedside steps using p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G)

1) Start with the phenotype: is it a fit?
Before you touch the sequence, ask whether the clinical picture fits the gene. HNF1A-MODY typically presents with:
- Early-onset diabetes (often before 25 but can be later),
- Negative pancreatic autoantibodies,
- Preserved but progressively failing β-cell function (C‑peptide not absent),
- Glycosuria from a low renal glucose threshold,
- Autosomal dominant family history of diabetes.

Our case ticks these boxes: young adult, negative GADA/IA‑2, stimulated C‑peptide 0.83 nmol/L (suggesting residual function), glycosuria, and multiple affected maternal relatives. Phenotype is supportive evidence.

2) Is the variant novel or reported?
The variant c.809A>G (p.N270S) was not present in ClinVar or VarSome and had not been reported in the literature. Absence from population and clinical databases is an important datapoint (it argues against benign common polymorphism), but it’s not decisive alone.

3) Location matters — domain and function
The substitution lies in exon 4 within the DNA‑binding domain of HNF1A. Functional domains matter: pathogenic variants cluster in the DNA‑binding domain for HNF1A, where disruption is more likely to affect transcriptional regulation of insulin and GLUT2. ACMG considers “critical domain” location as moderate evidence for pathogenicity (PM1). This variant’s domain location therefore increases pretest probability that it is deleterious.

4) In silico predictions — supportive, not definitive
This variant has a very high AlphaMissense score (0.995 in the report). High-confidence computational predictions that a missense change is damaging provide supporting ACMG evidence (PP3). But remember: algorithms can’t replace functional assays or segregation data — they strengthen your impression but don’t clinch the diagnosis.

5) Segregation with disease — powerful real-world evidence
Segregation with disease across affected family members moves the needle substantially. In the case, the variant segregated with diabetes in several maternal relatives (maternal grandmother, mother, younger brother with early-onset diabetes). Co-segregation is strong evidence (PP1) under ACMG when seen in multiple affected family members. The more informative the pedigree (multiple meioses, documented phenotypes and genotypes), the stronger this evidence becomes.

6) Aggregate the lines of evidence per ACMG
Putting it together for p.N270S:
- PM1: Location in a critical DNA‑binding domain (moderate)
- PP3: Strong in silico deleterious prediction (supporting)
- PM2: Absence from population/clinical databases (moderate)
- PP1: Co-segregation with disease in the family (supporting to moderate depending on numbers)
- Phenotype match: Fits HNF1A clinical picture (supporting)

Combining these criteria (two moderate + multiple supporting lines plus phenotype fit) is consistent with a likely pathogenic/pathogenic classification by ACMG rules in this clinical context — which is what the authors report.

Practical tips for clinicians ordering and interpreting MODY NGS panels

- Pick the right test for the clinical question: use a targeted monogenic diabetes panel (includes HNF1A, GCK, HNF4A, HNF1B, etc.) or exome sequencing if the phenotype is atypical. Panels are faster, cheaper, and often return actionable, curated results.
- Provide good clinical metadata with the test request: age at onset, autoantibody status, C‑peptide, BMI, family history and any atypical features. Variant interpretation improves with phenotype correlation.
- Expect variants of uncertain significance (VUS): many novel missense changes will land here unless you can demonstrate segregation or functional impact. Be prepared to act on “likely pathogenic” but cautious with VUS.
- Ask about lab reporting standards: ensure the lab applies ACMG/AMP guidelines and provides the evidence used for classification. If the report lacks segregation or domain data, request reanalysis if additional family testing becomes available.

Communicating uncertainty — how to talk to patients
Be transparent. Explain that genetic testing often yields three classes: pathogenic/likely pathogenic (actionable), benign/likely benign, and VUS (uncertain). For a likely pathogenic HNF1A variant in a patient with matching phenotype and family history, you can explain that the results are clinically meaningful: they explain the diabetes, change therapy options, and indicate autosomal dominant inheritance. For VUS results, explain that they don’t confirm or exclude a genetic diagnosis and that further steps (family testing, research assays) may reclassify the variant.

Cascade testing — a must in MODY families
When you identify a likely pathogenic HNF1A variant:
- Offer targeted testing (single-site testing is cheap and quick) to first-degree relatives, especially those with early-onset diabetes or suspected dysglycaemia.
- Use results to tailor therapy: family members with the variant may avoid unnecessary insulin and benefit from sulfonylureas/GLP‑1 RAs or specific surveillance.
- Provide genetic counselling: discuss reproductive risks (50% transmission), implications for insurance (jurisdictions differ), and psychosocial aspects.

When to do functional studies or reclassification
If uncertainty persists (e.g., segregation data incomplete), consider:
- Recruiting additional affected/unaffected relatives for targeted testing (often the quickest way to strengthen evidence).
- Contacting the reporting lab to see if functional assays are available or if they will periodically re-evaluate the variant as new data accumulates.
- Collaborating with research groups studying HNF1A to access functional testing (transcriptional assays), if clinically relevant.

Pitfalls and cautions
- Don’t overcall pathogenicity based on in silico tools or domain location alone.
- Beware phenocopies: common T2D can coexist in families; segregation must be interpreted in the context of age at onset and phenotype.
- Avoid reflexively stopping insulin in all genetically confirmed MODY cases — consider current glycaemic control and residual β‑cell function; this patient’s insulin was withdrawn after improvement and genetic confirmation with preserved stimulated C‑peptide.

Clinical pearls
- Remember the red flags for MODY: young-onset diabetes, negative autoantibodies, preserved C‑peptide, glycosuria and a dominant family history.
- Domain matters: missense variants in HNF1A’s DNA‑binding domain are more likely to be pathogenic.
- Co-segregation is often the single most persuasive clinical evidence; get family samples if possible.
- Genetic diagnosis is actionable: it can change therapy, prognosis and prompt cascade testing.
- Labs vary — insist on ACMG-based classification and transparent evidence summaries.

Take-home points
- The c.809A>G (p.N270S) HNF1A variant illustrates how combining phenotype fit, domain location (DNA‑binding), absence from databases, strong in silico prediction (AlphaMissense 0.995) and family co‑segregation yields a likely pathogenic classification under ACMG — and a clinically actionable diagnosis of HNF1A‑MODY in this patient.
- When you suspect MODY, order a targeted NGS panel, include thorough clinical data, and be ready to pursue cascade testing — genetic results change management.
- Use a multidisciplinary approach: clinician + genetic counsellor + diagnostic lab to interpret variants, communicate uncertainty, and implement family testing.

If you’re seeing young patients with atypical diabetes — think beyond the type 1/type 2 binary. Genetic testing guided by ACMG reasoning turns variants into verdicts that matter at the bedside.



# Precision de-escalation: When and how to stop insulin in HNF1A‑MODY

What makes this case interesting: a young man with marked hyperglycaemia (HbA1c 10.2%), glycosuria and a strong family history was started on insulin while investigations were underway — a common, pragmatic reflex — but ultimately had a previously unreported HNF1A mutation. After genetic confirmation, his team successfully withdrew insulin and landed on GLP‑1 receptor agonist + metformin with durable control (HbA1c fell to 6.0% at 6 months). That sequence — “stabilize with insulin, confirm subtype, then tailor therapy” — is a practical template for MODY clinics. This post gives a stepwise, physiology‑anchored approach to stopping insulin in HNF1A‑MODY and choosing the ideal oral/ injectable alternative.

Context you must keep in mind
- HNF1A‑MODY is a progressive β‑cell secretory defect. Patients typically have preserved but insufficient insulin secretion early on and get worse over years.
- Many are mislabelled as type 1 or type 2 diabetes; genetic confirmation changes therapy and prognosis.
- HNF1A mutations lower SGLT2 expression -> glycosuria is common. This affects symptoms and means we should be cautious with SGLT2 inhibitors.

When to consider de‑escalation: the key signals
- Genetic confirmation of HNF1A pathogenic/likely pathogenic variant (this changes the pretest probability and management).
- Evidence of residual β‑cell function: stimulated C‑peptide is most useful. In this report, a glucagon stimulation test showed stimulated C‑peptide 0.83 nmol/L (supporting preserved function). A common pragmatic threshold is a stimulated C‑peptide >0.6 nmol/L to suggest the patient can be managed without insulin in many contexts.
- Clinical trajectory: falling HbA1c on current therapy, no severe hyperglycaemia or DKA, and patient symptoms improving.
- Absence of type 1 autoimmunity (GADA, IA‑2, ZnT8 negative).
- Patient factors: risk of hypoglycaemia, lifestyle, work/school constraints, preferences.

A stepwise strategy to withdraw insulin (practical bedside sequence)
1. Stabilize first (what was done here)
   - If patient presents with very high glucose or symptomatic hyperglycaemia, short course of insulin to rapidly correct and relieve catabolism is reasonable while you work up the etiology.
   - Start background metformin as tolerated (was used here at 1500 mg/day) unless contraindicated.

2. Do the diagnostic workup early
   - Autoantibodies (GADA, IA‑2, ZnT8).
   - Measure endogenous secretion: fasting and stimulated C‑peptide (glucagon stimulation or mixed‑meal tolerance test).
   - Family history and, where available, MODY probability tools to prioritize genetic testing.
   - Obtain genetic testing promptly; NGS panels now make this feasible.

3. Reassess at the first follow‑up after genetic result (the moment for shared decision)
   - If HNF1A confirmed AND stimulated C‑peptide comfortably >0.6 nmol/L AND HbA1c improving (or acceptable), plan stepwise withdrawal.
   - If stimulated C‑peptide low (<0.3–0.4 nmol/L) or patient had DKA, continue insulin and reassess.

4. Practical insulin taper (example used in practice and reflected in the case)
   - Basal insulin only (avoid stopping rapid insulin abruptly if on multiple daily injections — but in this case only basal degludec 8 units/day was used).
   - Reduce basal by ~20–30% and add or uptitrate non‑insulin therapy (see options below). Monitor self‑glucose (pre‑meal and bedtime) for 3–7 days.
   - If glucose stable or improved, stop basal. If hyperglycaemia recurs, step back to last tolerated dose and reassess beta‑cell function and adherence.
   - Always have a safety plan for hyperglycaemia/DKA signs and clear thresholds for restarting insulin.

Choosing the replacement regimen: where the agents fit

Sulfonylureas (classic first‑line for HNF1A‑MODY)
- Mechanism: enhance insulin secretion by closing KATP channels — directly augment the defect in HNF1A.
- Pros: often very effective at low dose in early HNF1A‑MODY; oral, inexpensive.
- Cons: higher risk of hypoglycaemia, weight gain, and possible earlier β‑cell exhaustion with long term use. Less attractive in patients who have experienced hypoglycaemia, those with erratic meals, or with occupations where hypoglycaemia is high risk.
- Practical tip: If chosen, start low and titrate cautiously; counsel on hypoglycaemia recognition and supply glucose.

GLP‑1 receptor agonists (why they were used in this case)
- Mechanism: glucose‑dependent insulin secretion + appetite suppression + potential β‑cell preservation (clinical trials in MODY are small but suggest benefit).
- Pros: low hypoglycaemia risk (glucose‑dependent), weight neutral or weight loss, robust HbA1c lowering — attractive when hypoglycaemia risk is a concern or sulfonylureas avoided.
- Cons: injectable (though once‑weekly options simplify this), cost, GI side effects.
- In this case the team chose dulaglutide after genetic confirmation, allowing insulin cessation and achieving HbA1c 6.0% at 6 months — a tidy example of GLP‑1RA utility in HNF1A‑MODY when β‑cell function is moderate.

DPP‑4 inhibitors
- Mechanism: modest incretin enhancement, oral.
- Pros: safe, well tolerated, small hypoglycaemia risk.
- Cons: modest efficacy vs GLP‑1RA and sulfonylurea. May be useful as adjunct or in patients unwilling/unable to use injectables or sulfonylureas.
- In the case, saxagliptin was used alongside metformin early on; after stable control on GLP‑1RA, regimen simplified to metformin + saxagliptin for maintenance with good effect.

Metformin
- Mechanism: reduces hepatic glucose output and improves insulin sensitivity.
- Role: standard adjunctive therapy in MODY when insulin resistance features or overweight are present; also beneficial for cardiovascular risk profile.
- Continue unless contraindicated. In this case metformin (1500 mg/day) remained a backbone of therapy.

SGLT2 inhibitors — think twice
- HNF1A variants reduce SGLT2 expression → baseline glycosuria and higher risk of dehydration/DKA in some reports.
- Use cautiously in HNF1A‑MODY; the case discussion flags reports of severe dehydration and DKA when SGLT2 inhibition is superimposed. Avoid routine use unless a compelling reason and with careful patient selection and education.

Monitoring after de‑escalation
- Self‑monitoring: frequent capillary glucose checks during taper (pre‑meal and bedtime) and symptoms review.
- Clinic checks: HbA1c at ~3 months; sooner if glucose unstable.
- Repeat C‑peptide is not mandatory but may be helpful if control deteriorates.
- Surveillance for microvascular complications per usual guidelines.

Putting it together for the patient in this report
- Presented with HbA1c 10.2% and symptomatic hyperglycaemia → short‑term basal insulin + metformin + DPP‑4 inhibitor to stabilize.
- Workup: negative autoantibodies, glucagon stimulated C‑peptide 0.83 nmol/L, strong family history → high suspicion for MODY; genetic testing confirmed novel HNF1A variant.
- After confirmation and reassessment (HbA1c improving to 9.1%), insulin was withdrawn and dulaglutide introduced; metformin continued. This allowed safe insulin cessation and excellent glycaemic control (HbA1c 6.0% at 6 months). Later simplified to metformin + saxagliptin for maintenance while monitoring.

Clinical pearls
- Don’t reflexively lock a young adult into lifelong insulin before completing the autoantibody/C‑peptide/genetic assessment; short‑term insulin to stabilize is reasonable, but plan for reassessment.
- Stimulated C‑peptide >0.6 nmol/L is a practical threshold suggesting enough residual β‑cell function for many non‑insulin regimens. Use it, not just clinical impression.
- Sulfonylureas remain a valid first‑line for HNF1A‑MODY, but GLP‑1 receptor agonists are excellent alternatives when hypoglycaemia risk or weight loss is desirable.
- Avoid SGLT2 inhibitors without careful consideration in HNF1A‑MODY due to baseline SGLT2 downregulation and reports of dehydration/DKA.
- Shared decision making is key: the patient’s work, hypoglycaemia tolerance, and preferences will decide sulfonylurea vs GLP‑1RA vs DPP‑4 strategy.

Take‑home messages
- If you start insulin while investigating young‑onset diabetes, have a clear plan to reassess once antibody and C‑peptide results arrive and genetic testing is available.
- Consider insulin withdrawal when HNF1A‑MODY is genetically confirmed, stimulated C‑peptide is preserved (>0.6 nmol/L), HbA1c is improving, and there’s no DKA history.
- Sulfonylureas are effective first‑line for HNF1A‑MODY but carry hypoglycaemia risk; GLP‑1RAs offer potent glucose lowering with less hypoglycaemia and may be preferable as β‑cell function declines or hypoglycaemia risk is high.
- Metformin is a useful adjunct; DPP‑4 inhibitors can be used for modest effect; avoid routine SGLT2 inhibitor use without careful risk assessment.
- Monitor closely during the taper with frequent glucose checks, early HbA1c, and a plan to restart insulin if glycaemic control deteriorates.

This case is a neat demonstration of precision de‑escalation: stabilize, confirm genotype and physiology, then tailor therapy to the molecular defect and the patient — not the label.



# When SGLT2 bites back: Why SGLT2 inhibitors may be hazardous in HNF1A‑MODY

This is one of those “aha” clinical links that feels obvious in retrospect but gets missed at the bedside: HNF1A mutations (the commonest form of MODY) lower renal SGLT2 expression → a chronically reduced renal glucose threshold and often marked glycosuria at relatively modest blood glucose levels. Add a pharmacologic SGLT2 inhibitor and you can push patients into profound osmotic diuresis, dehydration and — in some reports — diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA). The case above illustrates the phenotype well: a young man with HNF1A‑MODY, prominent glycosuria and high glucose levels who responded beautifully to GLP‑1RA + metformin after genetic diagnosis. It’s a useful springboard to discuss why SGLT2 inhibitors deserve caution (or avoidance) in this genotype.

## Mechanistic primer: HNF1A → SGLT2 → renal glucose threshold
HNF1A is a transcription factor expressed in liver, pancreas and kidney. Among its renal targets is the SLC5A2 gene encoding SGLT2, the proximal tubule sodium–glucose cotransporter responsible for the bulk of filtered glucose reabsorption.

- Loss‑of‑function HNF1A variants reduce SGLT2 expression. Clinically this lowers the renal threshold for glucose reabsorption — patients develop glycosuria at lower plasma glucose levels than typical type 2 diabetes.
- Baseline glycosuria in HNF1A‑MODY is therefore a disease trait, not (only) a treatment side effect.
- Pharmacologic SGLT2 inhibition on top of genetically reduced SGLT2 can produce exaggerated glucosuria and osmotic diuresis.

Put simply: the kidney is already “half‑blocked” by genetics; an SGLT2 inhibitor can push glucose loss and diuresis to an extreme.

## Clinical consequences observed and plausibility for harm
The case report notes glycosuria as a feature of HNF1A‑MODY and cites concerns that heavy inhibition of residual SGLT2 activity may precipitate severe dehydration and DKA. Mechanistically this makes sense:

- Marked glucosuria → high osmotic diuresis → volume depletion, hypotension, acute kidney injury risk.
- Volume depletion plus reduced insulin secretion (HNF1A causes progressive β‑cell dysfunction) → increased lipolysis and ketogenesis risk.
- SGLT2 inhibitors can also cause euglycaemic DKA in other settings; with HNF1A where baseline glycosuria and reduced insulin reserve coexist, the DKA risk plausibly rises.

There are published case reports and series (not reproduced here) describing severe dehydration and ketoacidosis in HNF1A patients exposed to SGLT2 inhibitors. The case above reinforces the physiology: prominent glycosuria was present prior to therapy, and the team avoided SGLT2 use in favor of GLP‑1RA + metformin once MODY was diagnosed.

## Risk–benefit calculus: when (if ever) to consider SGLT2 inhibitors in HNF1A‑MODY
SGLT2 inhibitors have proven cardio‑renal benefits in high‑risk populations. That benefit is attractive, but the balance shifts in HNF1A‑MODY:

- Against routine use: baseline low renal glucose threshold, glycosuria, reduced insulin secretory reserve and documented reports of dehydration/DKA argue for avoidance as first‑line add‑on therapy.
- Possible exceptions: older HNF1A patients with established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease or heart failure and preserved hydration status who have been counselled about risks — but even here evidence is scant and caution is advised.
- There is no high‑quality randomized evidence supporting safety or benefit of SGLT2 inhibitors specifically in genetically confirmed HNF1A‑MODY.

My practical stance: preferentially avoid SGLT2 inhibitors in younger HNF1A‑MODY patients and use alternatives for glucose and cardio‑metabolic risk reduction wherever possible. Consider SGLT2 inhibitors only after careful individualized assessment and explicit shared decision making.

## Safer alternatives for glycaemic control and cardio‑metabolic risk in HNF1A‑MODY
Treatment should be genotype‑guided. From the case and broader literature the common, safer options are:

- Low‑dose sulfonylureas (classically effective in HNF1A‑MODY) — but watch for hypoglycaemia as β‑cell function declines.
- GLP‑1 receptor agonists — useful for glucose lowering, weight and cardiovascular risk reduction; the case showed excellent response to dulaglutide and later simplification to oral therapy.
- Metformin — standard first‑line for insulin‑sensitive benefits and weight neutrality.
- DPP‑4 inhibitors — reasonable adjuncts when GLP‑1RA is not tolerated.
- Insulin — reserved for those with substantial β‑cell failure or severe hyperglycaemia; genetic diagnosis may allow earlier de‑escalation if appropriate.

Cardio‑renal risk reduction: if SGLT2 agents are being considered purely for cardiorenal indications in an HNF1A patient, consider alternative proven strategies (GLP‑1RA for ASCVD risk reduction, optimal BP control, statins, RAAS blockade for albuminuria) before SGLT2 use.

## If an SGLT2 inhibitor is contemplated despite the risks — mitigation strategies
If, after multidisciplinary review and shared decision making, an SGLT2 inhibitor is still considered (rare), use a strict protocol to minimize harm:

- Ensure genetic diagnosis and document counselling about DKA and dehydration risk.
- Exclude active volume depletion, low oral intake, recent illness or surgery.
- Avoid in patients with low BMI, frailty or reduced insulin reserve (assess C‑peptide when helpful).
- Start at the lowest effective dose; ensure close early follow‑up (within days) and frequent monitoring of volume status, renal function and ketones.
- Educate patient to measure blood ketones and seek urgent care for nausea, malaise, or breathlessness; stop SGLT2 immediately for vomiting/illness and maintain hydration.
- Consider temporary discontinuation around acute illnesses, reduced oral intake or procedures.
- Consider co‑prescription of carbohydrate source and have low threshold for stopping the drug if marked glycosuria, hypotension or rising urea/creatinine occur.

Even with mitigation, know that a cautious approach is warranted — the physiology remains a mismatch between drug mechanism and genetic defect.

## Clinical pearls
- HNF1A‑MODY commonly causes a lower renal glucose threshold and prominent glycosuria due to reduced SGLT2 expression — glycosuria in a young, non‑obese patient with strong family history should raise suspicion for MODY.
- SGLT2 inhibitors can amplify genetically reduced glucose reabsorption, producing excessive osmotic diuresis and potentially precipitating dehydration and DKA in HNF1A‑MODY.
- Genetic confirmation of MODY matters — it permits treatment de‑escalation from insulin and selection of GLP‑1RA or sulfonylureas rather than risky SGLT2 use.
- If SGLT2 inhibitors are considered for cardiorenal indications in HNF1A, do so only after careful individualized risk assessment, robust counselling and safety monitoring; in most cases choose alternatives.

Key takeaways
- HNF1A mutations downregulate SGLT2 → low renal glucose threshold and baseline glycosuria.
- Adding an SGLT2 inhibitor risks exaggerated glucosuria, dehydration and DKA in HNF1A‑MODY.
- Prefer genotype‑appropriate therapies (low‑dose sulfonylurea, GLP‑1RA, metformin, DPP‑4) for glucose and cardiovascular risk reduction in HNF1A‑MODY.
- If an SGLT2 inhibitor is ever used, apply strict mitigation strategies and close monitoring; generally avoid in young HNF1A‑MODY patients.

Bottom line: Know your genotype. In HNF1A‑MODY the kidneys are already primed to spill glucose — don’t reflexively add a drug that worsens that physiology without a very careful plan.



# The sweet-pee signal: Using glycosuria as a clue to HNF1A‑MODY

What makes this case interesting
A young man with new, marked hyperglycaemia (A1c 10.2%) and striking glycosuria was initially treated as type 2 diabetes with insulin, metformin and a DPP‑4 inhibitor. He had negative islet autoantibodies, a preserved but insufficient stimulated C‑peptide (0.83 nmol/L), a family history of early‑onset diabetes, and—after genetic testing—a novel heterozygous HNF1A mutation (p.N270S). Within months he was transitioned off insulin to a GLP‑1RA + metformin with excellent glycaemic response (A1c down to 6.0%). The “odd” finding that helped point away from routine type 1/2 labels was disproportionate glycosuria for the degree of hyperglycaemia — a classic, underused clue for HNF1A‑MODY.

Why glycosuria matters in HNF1A‑MODY
HNF1A is a transcription factor expressed in pancreas, liver and kidney. One downstream effect is regulation of SGLT2 expression in proximal renal tubules. Pathogenic HNF1A variants commonly reduce SGLT2 expression, lowering the renal threshold for glucose reabsorption. Clinically that means:

- Glycosuria occurs at lower plasma glucose levels than expected.
- Patients can show impressive urinary glucose despite only moderate hyperglycaemia.
- Glycosuria may lead to polyuria, dehydration risk, and in rare situations predispose to euglycaemic DKA if precipitated (and SGLT2 inhibitors might amplify these risks).

In short: unexpected glycosuria = red flag for HNF1A involvement.

Putting the urinalysis into clinical context
A urinalysis is cheap, fast and widely available — but under-valued. Use it as a data point, not a diagnosis. When you see glycosuria that seems “too much” for the measured plasma glucose, think beyond measurement error and consider renal threshold abnormalities such as HNF1A‑MODY.

Key contextual tests and features that strengthen suspicion:
- Family history of early‑onset diabetes with autosomal dominant pattern (this case: mother, maternal grandmother, brother).
- Negative beta‑cell autoantibodies (GADA, IA‑2, ZnT8).
- Preserved but inadequate endogenous insulin secretion (stimulated C‑peptide ~0.83 nmol/L in this patient).
- OGTT showing exaggerated postprandial hyperglycaemia (2‑hour glucose 19.8 mmol/L here) — typical for HNF1A where postprandial excursions can be prominent.
- Absence of ketoacidosis at presentation (unlike classic type 1).
- BMI not strongly suggestive of insulin resistance (this patient BMI 24.6, some metabolic features but not frank obesity).

How to interpret OGTT + glycosuria + no ketones
- OGTT with a large postprandial rise supports a primary β‑cell secretory defect (MODY phenotype) rather than pure insulin resistance.
- Glycosuria at lower glycaemic thresholds suggests reduced SGLT2-mediated reabsorption — classic for HNF1A.
- Lack of ketones and negative autoantibodies argues against type 1 autoimmune diabetes.
Putting these together increases the pretest probability of HNF1A‑MODY and should lower the threshold to pursue genetic testing.

Diagnostic pathway in practice
1. If you find disproportionate glycosuria, confirm plasma glucose and consider repeating urinalysis to exclude lab error or contamination.
2. Take a focused family history for early-onset diabetes.
3. Order: fasting glucose, A1c, OGTT if needed to document postprandial excursion, urine ketones, urinary glucose if available, stimulated C‑peptide and diabetes autoantibodies.
4. Use the clinical picture (negative antibodies, preserved C‑peptide, family pattern, glycosuria) to decide on gene testing — consider an NGS monogenic diabetes panel rather than single‑gene tests in many settings.
5. Apply variant interpretation using ACMG criteria and correlate genotype with phenotype (as in this case, a novel HNF1A variant was classified pathogenic using multiple lines of evidence).

Therapeutic implications (why it matters)
A molecular diagnosis changes treatment and prognosis:
- HNF1A‑MODY often responds well to low‑dose sulfonylureas initially (they boost insulin secretion) — but consider hypoglycaemia risk as β‑cell function wanes.
- GLP‑1 receptor agonists are an effective alternative and may be safer with lower hypoglycaemia risk (this patient did well on dulaglutide).
- Insulin can often be withdrawn if β‑cell function is adequate and glycaemia controlled after diagnosis.
- Be cautious with SGLT2 inhibitors: baseline reduced SGLT2 activity in HNF1A may increase risk of dehydration, genital infections and euglycaemic DKA—use with caution and individual risk assessment.
- Family screening is appropriate once a pathogenic variant is identified.

Clinical pearls
- Don’t ignore the urinalysis. Glycosuria disproportionate to blood glucose is a practical bedside clue to HNF1A‑MODY.
- Negative autoantibodies + preserved stimulated C‑peptide + family history = high yield for genetic testing even if BMI is intermediate or metabolic features are mixed.
- OGTT is helpful: pronounced postprandial hyperglycaemia fits HNF1A pathophysiology.
- A genetic diagnosis allows precision therapy (sulfonylurea or GLP‑1RA instead of lifelong insulin), avoids unnecessary treatments, and prompts family counselling.

Take‑home messages
- Unexpected glycosuria should prompt consideration of HNF1A‑MODY, especially with early‑onset diabetes and family history.
- Integrate urinalysis with OGTT, C‑peptide and antibody testing to build the case for genetic testing.
- A genetic diagnosis changes management: consider sulfonylureas or GLP‑1RAs and be cautious with SGLT2 inhibitors.
- Low cost, high yield: use the simple urine dipstick as a nudge toward precision medicine in young adults with atypical diabetes.

This patient’s story is a reminder: sometimes a cheap urine dipstick—paired with a good history and a few targeted tests—can steer you from broad labels to a precise diagnosis and much better, tailored care.



# Early nerves, early warnings? Sudomotor dysfunction in young-onset diabetes

What jumps off this case is not just a young man with a novel HNF1A mutation and marked hyperglycaemia, but the striking finding of severely reduced sudomotor function in the hands at presentation. In a patient with negative islet autoantibodies, preserved (but insufficient) C‑peptide, glycosuria and a strong family history pointing to HNF1A‑MODY, the presence of early autonomic small‑fiber dysfunction feels out of step with the “textbook” timeline of diabetic complications. That discordance makes this a useful springboard to discuss what sudomotor testing (electrochemical skin conductance) can — and can’t — tell us in young‑onset, non–type 1 diabetes, and what we should do about abnormal results.

## Why is early sudomotor dysfunction interesting here?
- Sudomotor testing detects small‑fiber autonomic dysfunction (sympathetic cholinergic fibers innervating sweat glands). Classically, autonomic neuropathy is a late complication of long‑standing diabetes. Seeing severe reduction at initial presentation in a young adult raises immediate questions: is this driven by hyperglycaemia alone, by genetic/phenotype‑specific factors (HNF1A effect?), or both?
- In HNF1A‑MODY we know there is impaired insulin secretion, often postprandial hyperglycaemia, and glycosuria (lower renal glucose threshold). These metabolic features could plausibly accelerate microvascular or small‑fiber injury, but the natural history of autonomic involvement in MODY is poorly defined.
- Clinically, an abnormal sudomotor test in a young person should prompt action — but what action is evidence‑based? Below I outline test characteristics, biological plausibility, and practical next steps grounded in this case.

## Quick primer: what is electrochemical skin conductance (Sudoscan) testing?
- The device measures electrochemical skin conductance (ESC) by applying a low voltage to palms and soles and quantifying chloride ion flow generated by sweat gland activity. Results are reported as microSiemens (or qualitative normal/abnormal ranges).
- It’s noninvasive, fast (minutes), painless and easy to perform at the bedside/clinic — attractive for screening.
- What it measures: integrity/function of small sympathetic cholinergic fibers and sweat gland response — a surrogate for small‑fiber autonomic dysfunction.
- Test performance: ESC correlates reasonably with other small‑fiber tests (QSART, skin biopsy intraepidermal nerve fiber density) and with composite autonomic testing, but it is not the gold standard. Sensitivity and specificity vary across studies and populations, and values depend on cutoffs, disease duration and glycaemic control.
- Limitations: results can be influenced by skin temperature, hydration, recent glucose levels, local skin conditions, and testing conditions. It cannot localize lesions precisely, nor replace comprehensive autonomic testing when clinically indicated.

## How hyperglycaemia and metabolic context can affect sudomotor function
- Hyperglycaemia — both acute and chronic — injures small fibres via well‑described metabolic pathways (polyol flux, oxidative stress, glycation, microvascular compromise). Severe hyperglycaemia at presentation may therefore already produce measurable small‑fiber dysfunction.
- Postprandial glucose excursions — prominent in HNF1A‑MODY — may be especially injurious even when fasting numbers are less dramatic. OGTT in this case showed marked postprandial hyperglycaemia (2‑hr 19.8 mmol/L).
- Glycosuria and volume depletion: glycosuria (and any osmotic diuresis) can cause dehydration and electrolyte shifts that transiently reduce sweat responses. In HNF1A‑MODY, reduced SGLT‑2 expression is reported; clinically this can increase glycosuria at lower glucose thresholds. This can theoretically modulate sudomotor testing — so interpret results in light of hydration status and recent diuresis.
- Other metabolic contributors: dyslipidaemia (elevated triglycerides in this patient) and insulin deficiency/resistance milieu can augment small‑fiber risk.

## Could genotype (HNF1A) directly influence autonomic/sudomotor function?
- HNF1A is a transcription factor expressed in pancreas, liver and kidney; direct expression in peripheral nerves has not been established as a clear mechanism causing autonomic neuropathy.
- Genetic effects may be indirect: lifelong subtle alterations in glycaemic patterns, renal glucose handling, or other metabolic pathways could increase vulnerability of small fibres over decades. At present, there is not robust evidence that HNF1A mutations independently cause autonomic neuropathy separate from glycaemic exposure.
- Bottom line: plausible mechanistic links exist, but causality between HNF1A genotype and early autonomic dysfunction remains unproven and an area for research.

## What should an abnormal Sudoscan result trigger clinically?
Abnormal sudomotor testing is a flag — it does not diagnose definitive autonomic neuropathy on its own. In this case (severely reduced hand conductance at presentation) practical steps are:

1. Confirm and contextualise
   - Repeat testing if conditions may have affected results (temperature, dehydration, recent exercise or alcohol).
   - Check for reversible contributors: acute hyperglycaemia, dehydration from osmotic diuresis, medications (anticholinergics), topical agents on hands/feet.

2. Broaden autonomic evaluation when clinically warranted
   - If Sudoscan abnormality is confirmed, perform symptom screening for autonomic dysfunction: orthostatic lightheadedness, syncope, resting tachycardia, exercise intolerance, gastroparesis symptoms, erectile dysfunction, urinary dysfunction, and abnormal sweating patterns.
   - Proceed to objective testing if symptoms/signs present or if the Sudoscan abnormality is marked: orthostatic blood pressure/heart rate measurements, heart rate variability with deep breathing, Valsalva manoeuvre, tilt‑table testing and, if needed, quantitative sudomotor axon reflex test (QSART) or skin biopsy for small‑fiber density.
   - Consider referral to autonomic neurology or clinical neurophysiology for comprehensive testing.

3. Intensify surveillance and preventive care
   - Optimize glycaemic control aggressively: this case demonstrates large HbA1c improvement with GLP‑1 RA + metformin (10.2% to 6.0% over months). Early and durable glycaemic improvement is the clearest intervention to slow progression of small‑fiber and autonomic neuropathy.
   - Reinforce foot care education and regular neuropathy screening (monofilament, vibration, temperature discrimination) even in young patients when small‑fiber dysfunction is detected.
   - Screen for other microvascular complications (retinopathy, nephropathy) earlier/frequently if autonomic dysfunction is present.

4. Medication considerations in HNF1A‑MODY with abnormal sudomotor testing
   - GLP‑1 receptor agonists and metformin proved effective in this case; they have low hypoglycaemia risk and may be preferable to sulfonylureas once genetic diagnosis is known.
   - Be cautious with SGLT‑2 inhibitors in HNF1A‑MODY due to increased glycosuria and dehydration risk (the case discussion highlights potential for severe dehydration and euglycaemic DKA in some HNF1A patients). Severe autonomic dysfunction (impaired sympathetic responses) could theoretically increase orthostatic intolerance risk with volume depletion — weigh risks and benefits.
   - Avoid agents that worsen autonomic symptoms (e.g., medications causing orthostatic hypotension) when autonomic dysfunction is present.

## Does an abnormal sudomotor result change long‑term prognosis or management?
- Prognosis: an early abnormal sudomotor test signals increased risk for progressive small‑fiber neuropathy and broader autonomic involvement, but the magnitude of prognostic change depends on subsequent glycaemic control and comorbid risk factor modification.
- Management: yes — it should lead to earlier and more intensive monitoring and treatment efforts:
  - Faster escalation of glycaemic control strategies and closer follow‑up.
  - Low threshold for formal autonomic testing if symptoms or orthostatic signs develop.
  - Educate about fall risk, orthostatic precautions, fluid status and genital/urinary infection risks.
  - Regular surveillance for other microvascular endpoints.

## Clinical pearls from this case
- Sudomotor dysfunction can be detected early in young patients with non–type 1 diabetes; an abnormal ESC is a red flag, not a final diagnosis.
- Interpret ESC in clinical context — consider acute hyperglycaemia, hydration state and local factors before concluding irreversible neuropathy.
- In HNF1A‑MODY, genotype awareness changes therapy: insulin was safely withdrawn in this patient after genetic diagnosis and glycaemia improved on GLP‑1 RA + metformin; medication choices should consider hypoglycaemia risk and the unique renal glucose handling of HNF1A.
- Abnormal sudomotor testing should prompt systematic autonomic symptom screening and targeted objective testing when indicated; it also justifies intensified glycaemic and risk‑factor management.
- SGLT‑2 inhibitors in HNF1A‑MODY need caution given enhanced glycosuria and theoretical dehydration/ketosis risks — particularly relevant if autonomic dysfunction is present.

## Bottom line (take‑home messages)
- Electrochemical skin conductance (Sudoscan) is a quick, noninvasive screen for small‑fiber autonomic dysfunction, but it is not diagnostic alone.
- Severe sudomotor impairment in a young person with early‑onset non–type 1 diabetes should trigger:
  - confirmation and contextualisation of the test,
  - symptom screening and, if warranted, formal autonomic testing,
  - earlier, aggressive optimisation of glycaemic control and preventive microvascular surveillance.
- In monogenic diabetes (HNF1A‑MODY), genetic diagnosis changes therapy choices and may alter risk profiles (e.g., glycosuria), so abnormal autonomic findings should be integrated into a precision medicine plan.
- Finally: abnormal sudomotor tests are an early warning — act on them. Further research is needed to define the natural history of autonomic involvement in MODY and whether genotype modifies neuropathy risk independent of glycaemia.
