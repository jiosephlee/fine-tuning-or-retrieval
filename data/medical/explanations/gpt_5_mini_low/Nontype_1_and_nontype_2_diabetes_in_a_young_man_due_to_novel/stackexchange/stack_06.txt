Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Management] Why might SGLT2 inhibitors carry disproportionate risks (for example, dehydration and ketoacidosis) in patients with HNF1A-MODY, and how does the disease biology underpin this concern?

Short answer
HNF1A controls SGLT2 expression in the renal proximal tubule. In HNF1A‑MODY patients SGLT2 expression is reduced at baseline, producing spontaneous glycosuria. Pharmacologic SGLT2 inhibition therefore suppresses already diminished renal glucose reabsorption and can produce disproportionately large glucosuric/diuretic effects. Combined with the hallmark β‑cell insulin secretory defect of HNF1A‑MODY (relative insulinopenia), this creates a perfect storm for volume depletion, hypotension and increased ketogenesis — including euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA). For these reasons SGLT2 inhibitors carry higher and clinically important risks in HNF1A‑MODY and are generally avoided or used only with extreme caution.

Pathophysiology and clinical reasoning (teaching points)
- HNF1A regulates genes in pancreas, liver and kidney. One important renal target is SLC5A2, which encodes SGLT2. Loss‑of‑function HNF1A mutations reduce SGLT2 expression → lower renal glucose reabsorption and baseline glycosuria (explains the glycosuria seen in the case).
- SGLT2 inhibitors further block SGLT2 activity. In a patient who already has reduced SGLT2, pharmacologic inhibition can produce exaggerated glucosuria → marked osmotic diuresis, polyuria, salt and water loss, and dehydration. Case reports (and the Discussion in this report) document severe dehydration and even DKA after profound SGLT2 inhibition in HNF1A‑MODY.
- HNF1A‑MODY causes progressive β‑cell dysfunction with inadequate insulin secretion. Lower circulating insulin (and sometimes relative hyperglucagonemia) lowers the insulin:glucagon ratio, favouring lipolysis and hepatic ketogenesis.
- SGLT2 inhibitors themselves alter metabolic signalling: they decrease plasma glucose, often reduce insulin levels and raise glucagon, which together promote ketone production. In a patient with limited insulin reserve (HNF1A‑MODY) this effect is magnified, increasing the risk of (often euglycaemic) DKA.
- Additional risks: more glycosuria → higher risk of genital mycotic infections; marked diuresis → orthostasis and vascular collapse in vulnerable patients.

Implications for diagnosis and management
- Recognize the clue: unexplained glycosuria with modest hyperglycaemia (as in this patient) suggests a reduced renal threshold and should raise suspicion for HNF1A‑MODY.
- Therapeutic preference: HNF1A‑MODY patients are classically very sulfonylurea‑responsive early on; GLP‑1 receptor agonists, metformin and DPP‑4 agents are reasonable alternatives as β‑cell function declines (as done in this case). These agents avoid the disproportionate dehydration/ketoacidosis risk conferred by SGLT2 inhibitors.
- If an SGLT2 inhibitor is considered (rarely), counsel vigorously, ensure euvolemia, check for preserved insulin secretion, teach sick‑day rules (stop the drug with reduced oral intake/illness), monitor ketones and stop the drug with any ketosis — but in most HNF1A‑MODY patients avoidance is prudent.
- Bottom line: the disease biology — reduced SGLT2 expression plus impaired insulin secretion — explains why standard SGLT2 inhibition can be hazardous in HNF1A‑MODY. This pharmacogenetic interaction is a practical example of why genetic diagnosis (as in the reported case) should guide precision diabetes therapy.
