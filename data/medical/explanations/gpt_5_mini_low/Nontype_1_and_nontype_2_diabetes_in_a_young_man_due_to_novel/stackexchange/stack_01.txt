Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Differential Diagnosis] A 20-something man presents with hyperglycaemia, marked postprandial glucose on OGTT (2-hour 19.8 mmol/L), glycosuria without ketoacidosis, BMI 24.6 kg/m2, negative GAD and IA-2 antibodies, and a family history of early-onset diabetes across three generations. His glucagon-stimulated C-peptide is 0.83 nmol/L. What diagnosis best fits these data and why is it favored over type 1 and type 2 diabetes?

Most likely diagnosis: HNF1A-MODY (maturity‑onset diabetes of the young due to an HNF1A mutation).

Why this fits and why it is favored over type 1 and type 2 diabetes

1) Key clinical pattern pointing to MODY
- Young adult with diabetes onset before age 25 and a clear autosomal‑dominant family history (maternal grandmother in her 30s, mother in her late 20s, younger brother at 20) — this three‑generation vertical transmission strongly favors monogenic MODY over polygenic T2D or autoimmune T1D.  
- Prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia (2‑hour OGTT 19.8 mmol/L) with glycosuria despite absence of ketoacidosis is characteristic of HNF1A‑MODY (progressive β‑cell secretory defect with reduced renal glucose reabsorption).  
- Genetic confirmation: a novel heterozygous pathogenic HNF1A variant (p.N270S) — DNA sequencing is the diagnostic standard for MODY and makes the diagnosis definitive in this case.

2) Features arguing against type 1 diabetes
- Negative islet autoantibodies (GAD and IA‑2). While antibodies can be absent early in some T1D or in seronegative T1D, the combination of negative antibodies plus a stimulated C‑peptide that is preserved argues against classic autoimmune destruction.  
- Preserved stimulated C‑peptide (glucagon‑stimulated 0.83 nmol/L) indicates residual endogenous insulin secretion; in established T1D C‑peptide is usually very low. The patient also had no ketoacidosis at presentation.

3) Features arguing against typical type 2 diabetes
- BMI 24.6 kg/m2 (not obese) and absence of stigmata of insulin resistance (no acanthosis nigricans) — although later‑stage obesity can blur distinctions, the weight and phenotype here are not typical of early T2D.  
- Strong multigenerational early‑onset diabetes is atypical for polygenic T2D.  
- The pattern of marked postprandial hyperglycaemia and glycosuria from a lowered renal threshold (HNF1A controls SGLT2 expression) is a recognized feature of HNF1A‑MODY rather than T2D physiology.

4) Pathophysiology that unifies the findings
- HNF1A is a transcription factor expressed in β‑cells and renal tubules. Pathogenic variants cause progressive β‑cell dysfunction (reduced insulin and GLUT2 expression → impaired glucose‑stimulated insulin secretion, hence pronounced postprandial spikes) and reduced SGLT2 expression → lower renal glucose threshold and glycosuria. This explains the clinical triad seen in the report: postprandial hyperglycaemia, glycosuria without DKA, and preserved but insufficient C‑peptide.

5) Practical diagnostic hierarchy and management implications
- In a young patient with negative autoantibodies, preserved stimulated C‑peptide, and dominant family history, genetic testing for MODY (NGS panel or targeted HNF1A sequencing) is the appropriate diagnostic standard — exactly what was performed here and which identified the pathogenic p.N270S variant.  
- Correct genetic diagnosis changes therapy: HNF1A‑MODY patients often respond to low‑dose sulfonylureas and, increasingly, GLP‑1 receptor agonists (used successfully in this case). Insulin can often be discontinued if β‑cell function is adequate. Avoiding or using caution with SGLT2 inhibitors is prudent because of exaggerated glycosuria and dehydration risk.

Takeaway clinical pearl: In young patients with early‑onset diabetes, strong multigenerational transmission, negative islet autoantibodies, preserved stimulated C‑peptide, marked postprandial hyperglycaemia and glycosuria, think HNF1A‑MODY and pursue genetic testing — because the diagnosis alters prognosis, family counseling, and therapy.
