Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Clinical Pearls] How would you counsel this patient’s family about inheritance risk, who should be offered testing, and what surveillance is appropriate for relatives found to carry an HNF1A variant?

Key points first: HNF1A-MODY is an autosomal‑dominant disorder — each first‑degree relative of this patient has a 50% chance of carrying the same pathogenic HNF1A variant. Cascade genetic testing of at‑risk relatives, directed clinical surveillance for carriers, and genetic counselling (including discussion of reproductive implications) are the priorities.

Counselling about inheritance risk
- Mode: autosomal dominant. For this family, the pattern (maternal grandmother, mother, younger brother with early‑onset diabetes) is classic vertical transmission.
- Risk to relatives: each child of an affected person has a 50% risk of inheriting the variant. Siblings of the proband have up to a 50% risk if the mother is a carrier (and the family history suggests the mutation is on the maternal side).
- Variable expressivity and age‑dependent penetrance: carriers may present in adolescence or young adulthood, and degree/timing of hyperglycaemia can vary even within the same family.

Who should be offered testing
- Offer targeted genetic testing for the known familial variant (p.N270S / c.809A>G) to:
  - All first‑degree relatives (parents, siblings, children) regardless of whether they already have a diabetes diagnosis.
  - Second‑degree relatives (grandparents, aunts/uncles, nieces/nephews) and more distant relatives if they are in the direct maternal pedigree or if identifying carriers would change management.
- Prioritize testing of relatives with early‑onset diabetes (onset <25–35 years), persistent non‑insulin‑dependent diabetes with negative diabetes autoantibodies and preserved C‑peptide, or those with a family history suggestive of MODY.
- Use targeted single‑variant testing for family members once the pathogenic variant is established in the proband (faster and less expensive than panel testing). Refer to a genetics service for pre‑ and post‑test counselling.

Surveillance and management for relatives found to carry an HNF1A variant
- For asymptomatic carriers (no diabetes):
  - Begin metabolic surveillance in adolescence or earlier if clinically indicated. Practical approach:
    - Annual fasting plasma glucose and HbA1c starting around puberty (many centres begin screening from age 10–12 years) or sooner if symptoms develop.
    - Consider periodic 2‑hour OGTT if there is clinical concern about postprandial hyperglycaemia (HNF1A often shows prominent postprandial glucose excursions).
    - Educate about symptoms of hyperglycaemia and glycosuria; urine glucose testing can be a sensitive clue because of the lowered renal threshold in HNF1A carriers.
- For carriers who develop diabetes:
  - Recognize the typical natural history: progressive β‑cell dysfunction with relatively low insulin resistance.
  - First‑line pharmacotherapy historically: low‑dose sulfonylureas (high sensitivity). However, GLP‑1 receptor agonists and other agents (as in this case) are reasonable alternatives, especially to avoid hypoglycaemia or where sulfonylurea side‑effects are a concern.
  - Avoid routine use of SGLT2 inhibitors with caution — HNF1A carriers have reduced SGLT2 expression and pronounced glycosuria; SGLT2 inhibitors may increase risk of volume depletion and ketoacidosis (as discussed in the case).
  - Monitor and treat according to glycaemic control; refer to endocrinology for therapy tailoring (this case shows good response to GLP‑1 RA with maintenance on metformin ± DPP‑4 inhibitor).
- Complication screening once diabetes is diagnosed:
  - Standard microvascular and cardiovascular surveillance schedules: annual retinal screening, annual urine albumin-to‑creatinine ratio and eGFR, foot care and neuropathy assessment. Start at diagnosis and follow usual diabetes guidelines because complication risk is linked to glycaemic exposure.
- Lifestyle advice: healthy diet, physical activity, weight management if relevant; smoking cessation and cardiovascular risk factor control as for other forms of diabetes.

Reproductive and pregnancy considerations
- Women who are carriers have high risk of diabetes in pregnancy and should receive preconception counselling and early pregnancy metabolic monitoring.
- There is an important fetal consideration: if the fetus inherits the HNF1A variant, in some forms of monogenic diabetes fetal growth can be affected — discuss pregnancy management with maternal‑fetal medicine/genetics specialists. Offer genetic counselling for family planning.

Practical next steps for this family (based on the case)
1. Arrange genetics referral and offer targeted cascade testing for the known HNF1A p.N270S variant to all first‑degree relatives (mother, brother, any children) and other symptomatic maternal relatives.
2. For relatives who test positive but are asymptomatic: start annual fasting glucose and HbA1c from adolescence, consider OGTT if concerns about postprandial hyperglycaemia, and educate about glycosuria and hyperglycaemic symptoms.
3. For relatives with diabetes who test positive: adjust therapy according to HNF1A‑MODY principles (consider low‑dose sulfonylurea or GLP‑1 RA; avoid empiric SGLT2 inhibitors without specialist input), and institute routine complication screening.
4. Provide written information and arrange genetic counselling to discuss inheritance, implications for other relatives, and reproductive options.

Bottom line: because this patient has a confirmed pathogenic HNF1A variant with clear familial transmission, cascade genetic testing of first‑degree relatives is indicated; carriers require regular biochemical surveillance beginning in adolescence and, if they develop diabetes, tailored therapy and standard complication screening. Genetic counselling and coordination with endocrinology/genetics teams will ensure appropriate testing, surveillance and treatment choices.
