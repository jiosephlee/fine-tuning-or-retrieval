Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Morning Report] In a patient with suspected HNF1A-MODY and severe hyperglycaemia, what is a reasonable sequence of therapy from presentation through stabilization and confirmation of diagnosis, and what factors justify de-escalation off insulin after genetic confirmation?

Bottom line
- Early severe hyperglycaemia without ketoacidosis in a young adult with negative islet autoantibodies and a strong family history is best managed initially like other severe hyperglycaemias—rapid glucose control (often with short‑term insulin) while you complete diagnostic evaluation. After genetic confirmation of HNF1A‑MODY, de‑escalation off insulin is reasonable when there is objective evidence of residual β‑cell function plus clinical improvement in glucose control and no ketogenesis; transition to oral/GLP‑1–based therapy (or low‑dose sulfonylurea) then provides effective, safer long‑term management.

How to sequence therapy (presentation → stabilization → confirmation)
1. Immediate management at presentation
   - Treat the hyperglycaemia promptly to relieve osmotic symptoms and reduce glucotoxicity. In this case the patient had HbA1c 10.2% and fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L without DKA — so initiation of basal insulin for rapid control (eg, small‑to‑moderate basal dose) plus metformin and lifestyle counselling was reasonable.
   - Rationale: insulin corrects severe hyperglycaemia quickly and is the safest initial choice when the diagnosis (type 1 vs MODY vs type 2) is uncertain and β‑cell reserve is unknown.

2. Parallel diagnostic workup while treating
   - Obtain diabetes autoantibodies (GADA, IA‑2, ZnT8) and assess endogenous insulin secretion (fasting and/or stimulated C‑peptide; here glucagon stimulation gave a stimulated C‑peptide 0.83 nmol/L).
   - Take a careful family history, age at onset, BMI and clinical features (glycosuria, lack of acanthosis) to raise suspicion for MODY.
   - Send molecular genetic testing (MODY panel/NGS) when clinical features support it. These steps should proceed while glycaemia is being controlled.

3. Early reassessment after partial glycaemic response
   - Reassess glucose control and C‑peptide after short stabilization (weeks). In the case, HbA1c fell from 10.2% to 9.1% after 1 month on basal insulin + metformin + saxagliptin and stimulated C‑peptide indicated preserved but impaired secretion.
   - If there is preserved endogenous insulin secretion (stimulated C‑peptide generally >0.6 nmol/L or >200–300 pmol/L depending on assay) and negative autoantibodies, probability of non‑autoimmune causes (including HNF1A‑MODY) is high and you can plan for de‑escalation when genetic results return.

4. Transition after genetic confirmation of HNF1A‑MODY
   - Once a pathogenic HNF1A variant is confirmed, transition off insulin is reasonable if:
     a) There is demonstrable residual β‑cell function (stimulated C‑peptide supportive),
     b) The patient’s glucose control is improving on current therapy (falling HbA1c, no ketosis), and
     c) There is close follow‑up and the patient understands hypoglycaemia/ketosis warning signs.
   - Preferred long‑term options for HNF1A‑MODY: low‑dose sulfonylurea (historically first‑line) or GLP‑1 receptor agonists (safer re: hypoglycaemia and beneficial for weight/metabolic profile). In this case the team used dulaglutide + metformin with excellent response (HbA1c 6.0% at 6 months), then simplified to metformin + DPP‑4 inhibitor for maintenance.

Factors that justify de‑escalation off insulin after genetic confirmation (apply these criteria)
- Objective evidence of endogenous insulin production:
  - Stimulated C‑peptide in the preserved range (in this report 0.83 nmol/L) supports ability to secrete insulin and reduces the likelihood of absolute insulin deficiency.
- Absence of autoimmune markers:
  - Negative GADA/IA‑2/ZnT8 makes type 1 diabetes unlikely.
- Clinical trajectory:
  - Glycaemic improvement on minimal insulin/oral agents (drop in HbA1c from 10.2% to 9.1% in one month), absence of ketosis/DKA during course.
- Genetic confirmation of HNF1A pathogenic variant:
  - Confirms a progressive β‑cell secretory defect that is often responsive to insulin secretagogues/GLP‑1 agents rather than lifelong insulin.
- Capacity for close monitoring:
  - Reliable outpatient follow‑up, ability to check glucose frequently and re‑introduce insulin promptly if glycaemia deteriorates.
- Consideration of risks/benefits of alternatives:
  - Sulfonylureas are effective but carry hypoglycaemia risk; GLP‑1 RAs (used in this case) provide glucose lowering with lower hypoglycaemia risk and may preserve β‑cell function. Avoid routine use of SGLT2 inhibitors in HNF1A‑MODY without caution because reduced SGLT2 expression and marked glycosuria may increase dehydration/DKA risk.

Practical monitoring and safety nets after stopping insulin
- Check fasting and postprandial glucose and HbA1c frequently in the first weeks to months.
- Reassess C‑peptide if clinical course is atypical or glycaemia worsens.
- Educate patient on hyperglycaemia/DKA symptoms and provide plan to restart insulin if needed.
- Screen for complications as usual.

Pathophysiologic rationale linking diagnosis to therapy
- HNF1A mutations cause progressive β‑cell secretory failure; patients often have marked postprandial hyperglycaemia but some retained insulin secretion early on. Sulfonylureas and incretin‑based therapies enhance endogenous insulin release and blunt postprandial excursions. Genetic confirmation predicts response to secretagogues/GLP‑1–based therapy and allows stopping insulin when endogenous insulin is adequate.

Application to the presented case
- The treating team followed this reasonable sequence: start basal insulin + metformin + DPP‑4 inhibitor to control severe hyperglycaemia; perform antibody testing and glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide; obtain genetic testing; after genetic confirmation of HNF1A‑MODY and evidence of residual insulin secretion and improving HbA1c, they stopped insulin and successfully transitioned to dulaglutide + metformin (then simplified to metformin + saxagliptin) with sustained HbA1c <6.5%.

Takeaway
- Use insulin acutely to stabilize significant hyperglycaemia when diagnostic uncertainty exists; pursue autoantibodies, C‑peptide and genetic testing in parallel. After genetic confirmation of HNF1A‑MODY, de‑escalate from insulin when there is preserved stimulated C‑peptide, improving glycaemia, negative autoimmunity and the ability to monitor closely — then switch to targeted therapies (low‑dose sulfonylurea or GLP‑1 RA ± metformin) tailored to efficacy and safety for the patient.
