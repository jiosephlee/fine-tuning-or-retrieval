Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Clinical Pearls] List three to five clinical and laboratory clues that should prompt consideration of MODY genetic testing in a young adult with new-onset diabetes, and name one limitation of the commonly used MODY probability calculator in diverse populations.

Direct answer
Consider MODY testing when a young adult with new‑onset diabetes has (choose 3–5 of the following salient clues):  
1) Strong autosomal‑dominant family history of early‑onset diabetes (multiple affected first‑degree relatives diagnosed at young ages).  
2) Negative pancreatic islet autoantibodies (GADA, IA‑2, ZnT8) despite hyperglycaemia.  
3) Preserved or only moderately reduced endogenous insulin secretion (measurable fasting or stimulated C‑peptide), inconsistent with classic insulin‑deficient type 1 disease.  
4) Clinical phenotype inconsistent with typical type 2 diabetes — onset at young age (often <25–35), minimal insulin resistance stigmata (no acanthosis nigricans), glycosuria out of proportion to blood glucose (suggesting a low renal glucose threshold).  
5) Marked postprandial hyperglycaemia with relatively low early insulin requirements and good response to non‑insulin agents (sulfonylureas, GLP‑1 RAs) or atypical features for T2D (e.g., absence of DKA).

Clinical reasoning and pathophysiology (brief)
- MODY is a monogenic β‑cell disorder with autosomal‑dominant transmission; family clustering of early‑onset diabetes is therefore a key clue.  
- Most MODY subtypes are non‑autoimmune: negative GADA/IA‑2/ZnT8 reduces the likelihood of type 1 autoimmune diabetes.  
- Mutations (eg, HNF1A) cause progressive β‑cell dysfunction but often leave measurable C‑peptide early on; thus preserved or only partially reduced stimulated C‑peptide argues against immediate insulin‑open type 1 disease.  
- HNF1A mutations specifically reduce expression of renal SGLT2 and pancreatic genes (GLUT2, insulin), which can produce glycosuria at relatively lower glucose levels and prominent postprandial hyperglycaemia rather than severe insulin resistance.  
- Early good response to secretagogues or incretin therapies and low insulin needs further support a non‑T1/T2 mechanism amenable to targeted therapy.

How the case supports these clues
- The patient was a young adult with multiple first‑degree/second‑degree relatives diagnosed in their 20s–30s (strong autosomal‑dominant history).  
- Islet autoantibodies (GADA, IA‑2) were negative.  
- Glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide was present (0.83 nmol/L), indicating residual β‑cell function.  
- BMI was not markedly elevated and no acanthosis nigricans was noted; glycosuria was present and marked postprandial hyperglycaemia was demonstrated on OGTT.  
- Genetic testing confirmed a pathogenic HNF1A variant and treatment was successfully de‑escalated from insulin to GLP‑1 RA and oral agents, consistent with a MODY diagnosis.

One limitation of the commonly used MODY probability calculator
- The Exeter MODY probability calculator was derived from White European cohorts; it may be less accurate in non‑White populations and can overestimate (or otherwise misestimate) the probability of MODY when applied to ethnically diverse patients. In practice this means the calculator’s output must be interpreted in the context of ancestry, rising obesity prevalence, and local disease patterns rather than used as an absolute gatekeeper for genetic testing.
