Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Workup and Reasoning

The diagnostic approach in this case combined serial laboratory data, targeted serologic testing, multimodality imaging, tissue histopathology and a structured assessment of causality to distinguish immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) from other causes of muscle injury and to evaluate a possible link with semaglutide exposure.

Laboratory evaluation
The patient’s serum creatine kinase (CK) showed a clear and progressive rise, from a modest elevation (337 U/L) at the initial hospitalization to a peak of 3992 U/L four months after the single semaglutide dose. This temporal pattern—progressive increase over months rather than an immediate massive spike—argued against classic acute toxic rhabdomyolysis and pointed toward an ongoing myopathic process. Inflammatory markers were mildly elevated (ESR 22 mm/hr, CRP 22.8 mg/L), supporting an inflammatory or immune-mediated etiology rather than an isolated metabolic muscle injury. Thyroid function was normal, excluding hypothyroid myopathy as a contributor. Transaminases (AST 191, ALT 165 U/L) were elevated, but these values were interpreted in the context of known non‑alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD): she had prior liver biopsy–proven steatohepatitis. Because AST and ALT can be increased by both liver and muscle injury, the persistence of transaminitis after CK normalization and the historical liver biopsy supported a hepatic—not purely muscular—origin for her baseline transaminase abnormalities.

Autoantibody testing and implications of seronegativity
A comprehensive autoimmune panel was negative: ANA, anti‑SSA/SSB (except a low‑titer isolated SS‑B of 2.4, discussed below), anti‑Smith, anti‑U1 RNP, ANCA and the myositis-specific/associated antibodies including anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP. Anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP antibodies are strongly associated with IMNM subtypes; their absence places the case in the seronegative IMNM category. It is important for learners to recognize that ~25–35% of IMNM patients can be seronegative, and seronegativity does not exclude the diagnosis when clinical, imaging and pathologic data are concordant. The isolated low‑titer SS‑B without ANA or SS‑A is of uncertain significance here—there is no well-established link between isolated SS‑B and IMNM, and no other features of Sjögren’s syndrome or systemic autoimmune disease were present to suggest an alternative diagnosis.

Muscle MRI
Bilateral thigh MRI showed symmetric proximal muscle edema (most prominent in the vastus lateralis and quadriceps), a pattern typical of inflammatory myopathies. MRI is sensitive for detecting active muscle inflammation and guides biopsy site selection. The symmetric proximal distribution supported an inflammatory/immune myopathy rather than a focal or neurogenic process.

Cardiac assessment
Cardiac testing revealed transient abnormalities: a normal LV ejection fraction on echocardiography, but right ventricular systolic pressure elevation, and cardiac MRI with regional hypokinesis and myocardial edema without late gadolinium enhancement (no definitive fibrosis or infarction). These findings are compatible with evolving myocarditis or a focal stress cardiomyopathy; absence of scarring and subsequent normalization argue against chronic ischemic injury. Cardiac involvement can accompany inflammatory myopathies, so these data neither prove nor disprove IMNM but prompted monitoring and helped exclude an alternate cardiomyopathic cause for weakness or CK elevation.

Muscle biopsy — decisive histopathology
The diagnostic weight in this case rests on muscle histology. The right vastus lateralis biopsy demonstrated dispersed necrotic and regenerating myofibers, prominent macrophage (CD68+) myophagocytosis, membrane attack complex deposition on myofibers, and only rare CD3+ T‑cells with some perivascular inflammation. This combination—marked necrosis with macrophage predominance, complement deposition and relative paucity of lymphocytic infiltrate—is the classical pattern of IMNM and distinguishes it from polymyositis (which shows dense endomysial T‑cell infiltrates) and dermatomyositis (which shows perifascicular atrophy and capillary complement deposition). The membrane attack complex indicates an antibody‑ or complement‑mediated process targeting muscle fibers, consistent with immune‑mediated necrotising pathology even in the absence of identified circulating myositis antibodies.

Liver biopsy — separating hepatic disease from muscle contribution
A repeat liver biopsy confirmed progressive NAFLD with macrovesicular steatosis and minimal portal inflammation, but no features of autoimmune hepatitis. This supported the interpretation that the patient’s baseline transaminase elevations were hepatic in origin and not predominantly from muscle breakdown, particularly because transaminases remained abnormal after CK normalized.

Excluding rhabdomyolysis and toxic myopathy
Several clinical and temporal factors argued against simple drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis or a purely toxic myopathy from semaglutide. Classic drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis produces an abrupt, often very high CK rise shortly after exposure, frequently with myalgias, myoglobinuria (cola‑colored urine), and rapid CK decline after drug cessation and supportive care. In contrast, this patient had only a modest CK initially, developed progressive weakness and a 12‑fold CK increase over months after drug exposure, lacked myoglobinuria or pronounced myalgias, and required immunosuppression for recovery. These features are more consistent with an immune‑mediated process triggered or unmasked by a drug rather than direct myotoxicity that resolves after withdrawal.

Causality assessment using WHO‑UMC criteria
Applying the WHO‑Uppsala Monitoring Centre causality framework, the relationship between semaglutide and IMNM was judged probable. The elements supporting this conclusion included a reasonable temporal association (symptoms and progressive CK elevation began after the semaglutide dose), absence of an alternative explanation despite thorough evaluation (negative myositis serologies, no statin exposure, and no other drug or metabolic cause identified), and clinical improvement after withdrawal of the agent combined with immunotherapy (corticosteroids and IVIG), with sustained remission off immunosuppression. The lack of definitive proof—no autoantibody linking semaglutide to a known mechanism, and inability to exclude a smouldering pre‑existing myopathy fully due to absent pre‑exposure CK measurements—precludes labeling the relationship as certain. Rechallenge was ethically contraindicated given severity, so confirmatory evidence could not be obtained.

Summary of diagnostic reasoning
Taken together, the progressive and sustained CK elevation, proximal symmetric muscle edema on MRI, and—critically—the muscle biopsy findings of necrosis with macrophage predominance and complement deposition established IMNM as the diagnosis. Seronegativity did not exclude IMNM because histopathology and clinical course matched the entity. Other causes (acute rhabdomyolysis, hypothyroid myopathy, statin‑associated HMGCR myopathy, autoimmune hepatitis) were reasonably excluded by history, testing and biopsy. The temporal relationship to semaglutide, lack of alternative triggers, and clinical improvement after stopping the drug plus immunosuppression supported a probable drug‑associated IMNM per WHO‑UMC criteria, while acknowledging that causality cannot be proven without rechallenge or additional cases demonstrating the same pattern.
