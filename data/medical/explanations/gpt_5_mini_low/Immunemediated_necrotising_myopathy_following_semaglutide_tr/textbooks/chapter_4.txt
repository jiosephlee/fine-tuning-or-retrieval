Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

Chapter 4: ## Management Strategy and Clinical Course

The treating team approached this patient’s presentation as an immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) with severe features (profound proximal weakness, dysphagia/dysphonia, and markedly elevated creatine kinase), and management combined prompt, high-intensity immunosuppression with close physiologic monitoring and supportive care.

Initial immunosuppression
Given the severity and rapid progression of weakness, the patient received pulse corticosteroid therapy with intravenous methylprednisolone 1000 mg daily for 5 days. High‑dose IV methylprednisolone is a standard initial strategy in severe inflammatory myopathies to produce rapid anti‑inflammatory effects and limit ongoing muscle necrosis. After the pulse course she was transitioned to oral prednisone at approximately 1 mg/kg daily to maintain immunosuppression while the subsequent adjunctive therapy took effect. Using a high-dose steroid bridge minimizes delay in disease control, recognizing that prolonged sole reliance on steroids is often inadequate for IMNM and carries well‑known adverse effects.

Adjunctive therapy: IVIG for severe features
Because of persistent severe weakness and bulbar involvement, the team administered intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) at 2 g/kg divided over 4 days. IVIG is an established adjunct in IMNM, particularly when rapid functional recovery is needed or when patients have dysphagia, respiratory compromise, or poor response to steroids alone. In this case IVIG was used early to accelerate improvement and to allow earlier steroid tapering. The choice and timing were driven by clinical severity rather than serologic status (the patient was seronegative), reflecting the clinical-priority principle in acute management.

Monitoring response: laboratory and functional markers
The patient’s response was monitored with serial CK measurements and clinical assessment of strength and swallowing. A marked decline in CK occurred over approximately 2 weeks after initiation of combined therapy, correlating with progressive functional gains. This temporal pattern — rapid CK fall followed by gradual restoration of strength and bulbar function — is consistent with effective suppression of immune-mediated muscle injury. Serial clinical examinations (motor strength testing, evaluation of ambulation and swallowing) guided decisions about ongoing therapy intensity and timing of steroid taper.

Steroid tapering strategy
Because she experienced sustained clinical and biochemical improvement, clinicians pursued an accelerated steroid taper, successfully discontinuing prednisone over roughly two months. In suspected drug-associated or monophasic immune myopathies that respond briskly to treatment and to withdrawal of the offending agent, shorter steroid courses and more rapid tapers can be appropriate to reduce steroid toxicity. Tapering should be individualized and based on objective recovery (CK normalization, resolving weakness) and absence of relapse. In contrast, idiopathic or refractory IMNM often requires prolonged, multiagent immunosuppression.

Complications and mitigation
The case illustrates important risks of immunosuppressive therapy: during convalescence the patient developed septic shock from a Pseudomonas urinary tract infection requiring readmission. This highlights the necessity for infection vigilance whenever high‑dose steroids and IVIG are used. Mitigation strategies include baseline infection screening, early treatment of suspected infections, patient education on warning signs, and balancing immunosuppression intensity against infection risk. In practice, clinicians should have a low threshold to investigate febrile or systemic symptoms and to provide appropriate cultures and empiric therapy while reassessing immunosuppression.

Cardiac evaluation and follow-up
Because cardiac involvement can accompany inflammatory myopathies, the team obtained echocardiography and cardiac MRI. Initial imaging showed focal myocardial edema and mild wall motion abnormality without late gadolinium enhancement, and the patient had transient troponin elevation and paroxysmal atrial fibrillation. These findings prompted cardiology surveillance. On follow‑up imaging after treatment and clinical recovery, the prior cardiac abnormalities resolved. This course supports routine cardiac screening in IMNM (ECG, troponin, echocardiography; consider cardiac MRI if abnormalities are suspected) and repeat evaluation to document resolution or progression. Detecting cardiac involvement early influences monitoring, supportive care, and may modify immunosuppressive choices.

Medication stewardship and secondary prevention
A central management decision was permanent avoidance of GLP‑1 receptor agonists after a close temporal association between semaglutide exposure and symptom onset. In suspected drug‑associated IMNM, cessation of the offending agent is a key intervention and may permit shorter immunosuppression courses. For this patient’s metabolic needs (obesity and NAFLD), the team opted non‑GLP‑1 alternatives emphasizing lifestyle modification and close hepatology/metabolic follow‑up rather than rechallenge; pharmacologic alternatives should be chosen cautiously and discussed with the patient given the uncertain causality. Documentation of the adverse reaction and communication to outpatient providers are critical for future medication safety.

Long‑term outcome
The patient achieved sustained remission: CK remained within normal limits and she returned to baseline functional status, off immunosuppression for many months. Cardiac abnormalities and arrhythmia resolved without further intervention. This favorable trajectory—early aggressive therapy, withdrawal of the suspected trigger, and close monitoring—supports the possibility that some drug‑associated IMNM cases can be monophasic and responsive to a limited course of immunotherapy. Continued outpatient surveillance is appropriate, with periodic clinical exams and CK checks to detect relapse early.

Clinical lessons applicable to suspected drug-associated IMNM
- Treat severe, rapidly progressive myopathy empirically and promptly with high‑dose corticosteroids and add IVIG when bulbar involvement or severe weakness is present.  
- Monitor both laboratory (CK) and clinical (strength, swallowing, respiratory) endpoints to guide treatment intensity and tapering speed.  
- Actively search for and stop potential offending drugs; a clear temporal relationship supports this step even if autoantibodies are negative.  
- Maintain high vigilance for infections during and after immunosuppression, and screen for cardiac involvement at baseline and during follow‑up.  
- Individualize duration of immunosuppression: some drug‑associated cases may remit after short courses, but ongoing monitoring for relapse is required.

Overall, the management in this case illustrates a principle-driven approach: early aggressive therapy to prevent irreversible muscle damage, targeted adjunctive treatment for severe features, careful evaluation for complications (infectious and cardiac), and conservative long‑term immunosuppression when clinical and biochemical remission is achieved after removal of the suspected trigger.
