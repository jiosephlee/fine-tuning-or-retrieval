Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

Chapter 1: ## Case Presentation and Clinical Context

This patient is a woman in her 60s whose decline unfolded over months after a single dose of semaglutide and whose pattern of symptoms and investigations prompted evaluation for an inflammatory myopathy. Her baseline medical problems—obesity, non‑alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) with prior liver biopsy, hypertension, and treated hypothyroidism—provide important context because they explain prior transaminase abnormalities and the clinical rationale for starting a GLP‑1 receptor agonist, and because some comorbid conditions and drugs can confound interpretation of muscle enzymes and weakness. Her NAFLD had been documented on imaging and liver biopsy years before the current illness, with modest chronic transaminase elevations (ALT > AST) attributed to hepatic steatosis; this history makes it less likely that early transaminase abnormalities represented occult myopathy before the acute episode.

Approximately 2.5 years after the liver diagnosis she received a single 0.25 mg subcutaneous dose of semaglutide. Within hours she developed severe gastrointestinal intolerance—nausea and vomiting—that progressed over 48 hours to hospitalisation for hypovolaemic complications: poor oral intake, dyspnoea, acute kidney injury (AKI), and a high anion‑gap metabolic acidosis. These acute complications are consistent with dehydration and preservervation injury from persistent vomiting. On that admission a serum creatine kinase (CK) was checked and modestly elevated at 337 U/L. Although this is above the reference range, it is far lower than levels seen in frank rhabdomyolysis and there were no clinical features typical of rhabdomyolysis (no generalized severe myalgias, cola‑coloured urine, or marked CK elevations), supporting the interpretation that the initial CK rise was mild and non‑specific in the setting of systemic illness and AKI. During that hospital stay she also developed paroxysmal atrial fibrillation (pAF); serial cardiac troponins were normal, arguing against acute myocardial infarction as the cause of the arrhythmia.

After discharge she did not receive additional semaglutide but over the next 4 months experienced a progressive, subacute decline characterized by worsening proximal limb weakness, dysphagia for liquids and solids, and dysphonia. These features are clinically important because they indicate involvement of bulbar and axial musculature in addition to limb girdle muscles. Dysphagia and dysphonia suggest weakness of oropharyngeal and laryngeal muscles and raise concern for a myopathic process with risk for aspiration and respiratory compromise, which are red flags that typically prompt expedited inpatient evaluation in neuromuscular disease.

On representation to a community hospital, her CK was markedly higher at approximately 3992 U/L, reflecting ongoing muscle injury developing over weeks to months rather than the rapid peak and fall seen with isolated toxic rhabdomyolysis. She had no cutaneous findings (no heliotrope rash or Gottron papules) and no synovitis, making classic dermatomyositis or an inflammatory arthritis less likely clinically. The combination of severe proximal weakness, bulbar involvement, and CK in the thousands raised concern for an inflammatory myopathy—specifically a necrotising myopathy or immune‑mediated myositis—rather than isolated deconditioning. The presence of pAF again was noted but with normal troponin at that time; cardiac arrhythmia can be associated with systemic illness and is also reported in inflammatory myopathies when myocarditis is present, so it remained an important comorbidity to monitor.

Given the severity of weakness and elevated CK, she received high‑dose intravenous methylprednisolone (1 g daily for 5 days) at the outside centre as empiric therapy for suspected inflammatory myopathy before transfer. This is a common pragmatic approach when an aggressive immune myopathy is suspected and there is concern for rapidly progressive weakness or bulbar dysfunction. She was then transferred to a tertiary centre for rheumatology and neuromuscular evaluation, where further investigation (imaging and muscle biopsy) confirmed a necrotising inflammatory myopathy. The temporal relationship—symptom onset within hours of semaglutide exposure, an initial mild CK rise during the acute illness, followed by progressive weakness and dramatic CK elevation over months despite discontinuation of the drug—helped frame the clinical reasoning. The delayed and progressive course, bulbar signs, lack of classical dermatomyositis skin findings, and biopsy showing necrosis with macrophage predominance pointed clinicians away from simple toxic rhabdomyolysis and toward immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy as the working diagnosis requiring immunosuppressive therapy.
