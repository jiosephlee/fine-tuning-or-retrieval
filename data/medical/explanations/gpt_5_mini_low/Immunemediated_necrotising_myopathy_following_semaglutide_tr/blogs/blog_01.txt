Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# GLP‑1 Agents and Muscle Injury: IMNM vs Toxic Rhabdo at the Bedside

What makes this case interesting: a woman in her 60s developed a progressive, severe proximal myopathy with dysphagia and very high CK over months after a single 0.25 mg dose of semaglutide. Muscle biopsy showed necrotic and regenerating fibers with macrophage/myophagocytosis, membrane attack complex deposition and scant T cells — features consistent with immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). She was seronegative for the usual IMNM antibodies, improved with pulse steroids and IVIG, and remained well off immunosuppression after avoiding GLP‑1 therapy. This contrasts with recently published reports of GLP‑1/GIP agonist–associated rhabdomyolysis where CK spikes massively during active therapy and falls quickly after stopping the drug and hydration.

Why this matters in the ED/wards: GLP‑1 receptor agonists (semaglutide, tirzepatide and others) are now ubiquitous for diabetes and weight loss. When a patient on or recently exposed to a GLP‑1 agonist presents with weakness and CK elevation, clinicians must distinguish two very different processes — an acute toxic rhabdomyolysis that usually resolves with supportive care versus a subacute/ progressive autoimmune necrotising myopathy that may need immunosuppression. Management and prognosis diverge dramatically.

Key clinical differences — how to think through the problem

Tempo and timing
- Toxic rhabdomyolysis: usually acute onset, often during ongoing drug exposure. Symptoms (myalgias, dark urine, weakness) and CK rise are rapid (hours–days). Stopping the drug + aggressive IV fluids typically produces a marked CK decline over days.
- IMNM (drug‑triggered or idiopathic): classically presents with progressively worsening symmetric proximal weakness over weeks–months. CK can be markedly elevated but the rise is slower and sustained even after drug cessation. Dysphagia and dysphonia are more common with IMNM. The semaglutide case developed GI intolerance and AKI within hours of a single dose, but the disabling myopathy evolved over 4 months with a 12‑fold CK increase — a tempo more consistent with IMNM than simple toxic rhabdo.

CK trajectory and systemic features
- Toxic rhabdo: massive CK elevations possible (often tens of thousands). Rapid fall after volume resuscitation and stopping offending agent. Often accompanied by myalgias, cola‑coloured urine, and complications of myoglobinuria/AKI.
- IMNM: CK can be high (thousands) but often remains persistently elevated despite stopping an offending drug. Muscle weakness is prominent and progressive; systemic inflammatory markers may be modestly up. Extramuscular involvement (dysphagia, possible cardiac inflammation) can occur.

Serology and biopsy
- IMNM is associated with autoantibodies (anti‑HMGCR, anti‑SRP) in many cases but up to ~30% are seronegative. A negative myositis panel does not exclude IMNM.
- Muscle biopsy in IMNM: necrotic and regenerating fibers, macrophage-rich myophagocytosis, membrane attack complex deposition on non‑necrotic fibers, sparse lymphocytic infiltrate. These findings contrast with predominantly myofiber disruption and diffuse necrosis without the inflammatory pattern typical of pure toxic rhabdo.
- In the semaglutide case the vastus lateralis biopsy showed dispersed necrotic/regenerating fibers, CD68+ macrophages with myophagocytosis, MAC deposition and rare CD3 cells — classic IMNM histology despite seronegativity.

Response to therapy
- Toxic rhabdo: mainstays are IV fluids, electrolyte management, stopping the offending agent. Immunosuppression is not indicated.
- IMNM: often requires immunosuppression (high‑dose corticosteroids, IVIG, steroid‑sparing agents). Drug‑triggered IMNM can sometimes improve after stopping the trigger plus a short immunosuppressive course, but idiopathic and antibody‑positive cases frequently need prolonged therapy.
- In the presented case, the patient received pulse methylprednisolone and IVIG with clinical and biochemical recovery and was able to taper off steroids and remain well months later.

Practical bedside approach when you see a patient on a GLP‑1 agent with elevated CK and weakness

1. Rapid assessment: check vitals, urine colour, renal function, electrolytes (K+, Ca2+, phosphate), and urine output. Toxic rhabdo carries immediate risk of AKI and electrolyte derangement — manage aggressively.
2. Timecourse and symptom pattern: ask specifically when the drug started, whether symptoms began acutely while on therapy or insidiously after exposure, whether there are progressive proximal weaknesses, dysphagia, or dysphonia (these point toward IMNM).
3. CK trend: serial CKs are informative. A dramatic early spike with quick decline after fluids/support suggests toxic rhabdo. A rising or persistently high CK over weeks despite stopping the drug should raise concern for IMNM.
4. Look for red flags for IMNM: progressive symmetric proximal weakness, dysphagia, respiratory compromise, cardiac symptoms, and MRI showing muscular edema in proximal muscle groups.
5. Early specialty input: involve neurology/rheumatology and consider myositis serologies but don’t wait for results to act if the patient is deteriorating. Muscle MRI and biopsy are diagnostic tools; biopsy is definitive for IMNM histology.
6. Treatment decisions:
   - If toxic rhabdo likely: stop offending meds, aggressive IV fluids, monitor/ treat electrolytes and renal dysfunction.
   - If IMNM likely or biopsy‑proven: immunosuppression (high‑dose steroids ± IVIG); escalate to steroid‑sparing agents per rheumatology guidance.
7. Drug safety counseling: for suspected drug‑triggered IMNM, avoid rechallenge with the same class — in this case clinicians advised the patient to avoid GLP‑1 receptor agonists going forward.

Limitations and uncertainties
- Causality is often probabilistic. The semaglutide case fulfills many criteria for a probable drug‑triggered IMNM (reasonable temporal relationship, lack of alternative explanations, response after withdrawal and immunosuppression) but a definitive causal link cannot be proven without rechallenge (which would be unethical).
- Seronegativity does not exclude IMNM. Up to a third of IMNM patients lack known autoantibodies.
- The biological mechanism by which GLP‑1 receptor agonists might trigger an autoimmune myopathy is unknown; observational vigilance is needed as GLP‑1 use rises.

Clinical pearls
- Don’t assume every elevated CK in a patient with recent GLP‑1 exposure is toxic rhabdomyolysis; consider IMNM when weakness is progressive, proximal, or accompanied by dysphagia/dysphonia and CK remains elevated despite stopping the drug.
- A negative myositis panel does not rule out IMNM — muscle biopsy is the diagnostic gold standard when the clinical picture suggests autoimmune necrotising myopathy.
- Toxic rhabdo tends to have an acute onset and rapid resolution after stopping the offending agent and supportive care; IMNM shows a subacute–chronic progressive course and often requires immunosuppression.
- Early multidisciplinary involvement (rheumatology/neurology) expedites diagnosis and appropriate therapy; delay in treating IMNM can lead to prolonged disability.

Take‑home messages
- Consider two distinct patterns of GLP‑1–associated muscle injury: acute toxic rhabdomyolysis (rapid onset, resolves with stopping drug/fluids) versus immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (subacute progressive weakness, biopsy with necrosis + macrophages/MAC, often needs immunosuppression).
- Use the clinical tempo, CK trajectory, presence of progressive proximal weakness/dysphagia, MRI and biopsy findings, and response to stopping the drug to differentiate them at the bedside.
- If IMNM is suspected, involve specialists early and consider muscle biopsy and prompt immunosuppression; if toxic rhabdo is likely, prioritize fluids and renal protection.
- Be vigilant: with wider GLP‑1 use, rare immune‑mediated complications may become more visible — report suspected cases and discuss risks with patients.

Acknowledgment: the clinical details summarized here are grounded in the published case of probable IMNM following a single semaglutide dose (Lam et al.), contrasted with prior reports of GLP‑1/GIP agonist–associated rhabdomyolysis.
