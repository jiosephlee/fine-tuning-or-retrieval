Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# Seronegative IMNM: Diagnosing the Antibody‑Negative Myopathy

What makes this case interesting
A woman in her 60s developed progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia and a dramatic CK rise to ~4,000 U/L over 4 months after a single dose of semaglutide. Myositis panels (including anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP) were negative, but muscle biopsy showed necrosis, regenerating fibres, membrane attack complex (MAC) deposition, CD68+ myophagocytosis and only rare T cells — the histologic signature of immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). She improved quickly with pulse steroids and IVIG and remained well off immunosuppression after stopping the drug. This case is a useful reminder that an autoimmune necrotising myopathy can be seronegative, may mimic toxic or deconditioning states, and requires a different diagnostic mindset and workup.

Why seronegative IMNM matters
- Approximately one‑third of IMNM patients have no identifiable autoantibody. Relying on serology alone delays diagnosis and treatment.
- IMNM is a clinicopathologic diagnosis: characteristic clinical phenotype + supportive MRI/biopsy + exclusion of mimics.
- Distinguishing IMNM from drug‑toxic myopathy or simple deconditioning is crucial because IMNM often needs immunotherapy (and timely therapy improves outcomes).

A practical diagnostic pathway for suspected seronegative IMNM
1. Start with the clinical phenotype
   - Rapidly progressive proximal weakness (shoulders, hips), difficulties with sit/stand, decline in function over weeks–months.
   - Bulbar signs (dysphagia, dysphonia) and very high CK (>1,000s) favor inflammatory necrotising myopathy over pure sarcopenia or deconditioning.
   - Evaluate for systemic features (rash, ILD, Raynaud, sicca) — absence does not exclude IMNM.

2. First‑line labs and exclusion of common mimics
   - CK, AST/ALT (remember muscle raises transaminases), renal function, electrolytes.
   - TSH (hypothyroidism can mimic myopathy) and cortisol if indicated.
   - Basic autoimmune screen: ANA and myositis panel (including SRP, HMGCR) — but don’t stop if negative.
   - Medications and toxin review: statins, herbal supplements (eg, red yeast rice), recent immunotherapy, or new drugs (note: GLP‑1 RAs are emerging as potential triggers in case reports).
   - Infectious workup as indicated.
   - Consider CK trend: in drug‑toxic rhabdomyolysis CK usually falls after stopping the agent; progressive CK rise despite withdrawal suggests autoimmune process.

3. Electrodiagnostics and imaging — when to use them
   - EMG: useful if available and will often show myopathic motor unit potentials with early recruitment; helps localize and justify biopsy site if MRI is not diagnostic.
   - MRI of muscles (STIR/T2 and T1): look for symmetric proximal muscle edema, especially in quadriceps and pelvic girdle muscles. MRI guides biopsy to active areas and can help distinguish inflammatory myopathy from chronic fatty replacement or disuse atrophy.
   - In this case MRI showed symmetric oedema of proximal vastus lateralis and quadriceps — a pattern consistent with inflammatory necrotising myopathy.

4. Muscle biopsy — the diagnostic anchor (when and where)
   - Indications: progressive weakness with high CK, non‑diagnostic labs/EMG/MRI, or to distinguish IMNM from other myopathies.
   - Timing: biopsy early (don’t wait for serology) if clinical suspicion is high and treatment decisions depend on results.
   - Target active, edematous areas on MRI; avoid end‑stage fatty muscles.
   - Key histopathologic features of IMNM:
     - Scattered necrotic and regenerating fibres (prominent necrosis out of proportion to inflammatory infiltrate).
     - MAC (C5b‑9) deposition on sarcolemma of necrotic and non‑necrotic fibres.
     - Prominent myophagocytosis with macrophages (CD68+).
     - Relative paucity of lymphocytic (CD3+) infiltrate compared with polymyositis.
     - Complement activation and macrophage‑mediated fibre clearance are characteristic.
   - These biopsy features were present in the case and clinched the diagnosis despite negative myositis antibodies.

5. Exclude connective tissue disease and other systemic causes
   - Full rheumatologic review for features of systemic autoimmune disease. Screen for malignancy as clinically indicated (some myopathies associate with occult cancer).
   - Cardiac evaluation: troponin, ECG, echo and consider cardiac MRI if troponin or symptoms suggest myocarditis — IMNM can have cardiac involvement.
   - Review history of NAFLD or transaminitis carefully — muscle disease can raise AST/ALT; baseline liver disease may coexist and confuse interpretation.

6. Deciding on treatment while serology is pending or negative
   - If clinical picture, imaging and biopsy support IMNM, start immunotherapy rather than observing for prolonged time.
   - Typical initial approach for severe IMNM: high‑dose steroids (eg, pulse methylprednisone) plus steroid‑sparing/immunomodulatory therapy (IVIG is effective in many IMNM cases). Tailor to comorbidities and infection risk.
   - In this case pulse steroids + IVIG led to prompt CK normalization and functional recovery, and immunotherapy could be tapered early after clinical and biochemical response — a pattern more consistent with drug‑triggered IMNM than idiopathic disease requiring prolonged therapy.

Coordinating care: practical tips
- Early multidisciplinary collaboration speeds diagnosis: involve neurology (EMG/muscle biopsy), rheumatology (autoimmune workup, immunotherapy planning), and pathology (neuromuscular histopathology).
- Use MRI to guide biopsy — ask radiology to flag the best biopsy target.
- If EMG is delayed, don’t delay biopsy if MRI and clinical picture are convincing.
- Monitor closely for infections if you start high‑dose immunosuppression — this patient developed a pseudomonal UTI while steroid‑treated.
- Discuss potential drug triggers with the patient and advise discontinuation. Rechallenge generally not advised if a severe autoimmune reaction is suspected.

Avoiding common pitfalls
- Don’t assume negative myositis serology equals no autoimmune myopathy. Up to ~30% of IMNM are seronegative.
- Don’t label progressive proximal weakness with high CK as “deconditioning” or “statin intolerance” without targeted evaluation — biopsy can be diagnostic and therapy lifesaving.
- Don’t rely only on CK magnitude: the trend, clinical progression, and distribution of weakness matter.
- Remember muscle can raise AST/ALT — don’t reflexively attribute mild transaminitis solely to liver disease when muscle disease is present.

Clinical pearls
- IMNM histology is distinctive: necrosis + regeneration + MAC deposition + macrophage myophagocytosis with scarce lymphocytes.
- MRI muscle edema in proximal thigh muscles (eg, vastus lateralis) is a helpful noninvasive clue and biopsy guide.
- A brisk clinical and CK response to immunotherapy after drug withdrawal suggests a drug‑triggered autoimmune myopathy rather than self‑limited toxic rhabdomyolysis.
- Seronegative IMNM is a real entity — treat the patient, not the panel.

Key take‑home points
- Consider IMNM in patients with subacute–acute progressive proximal weakness and markedly elevated CK even when myositis antibodies are negative.
- Use MRI to localize active muscle for biopsy; biopsy findings (necrosis, MAC+, CD68+ myophagocytosis, paucity of lymphocytes) make the diagnosis.
- Exclude mimics (thyroid disease, infections, toxins/drugs, malignancy, CTD) but don’t delay biopsy and treatment when clinical suspicion is high.
- Coordinate early between neurology, rheumatology and pathology; start immunotherapy promptly for severe disease — steroids + IVIG are effective choices.
- Report possible drug triggers and counsel patients to avoid rechallenge if drug‑related IMNM is suspected.

This case reminds us: negative antibodies don’t rule out an aggressive autoimmune myopathy. Keep the diagnosis in mind, use MRI and biopsy smartly, and act early — the muscle (and the patient’s function) depends on it.
