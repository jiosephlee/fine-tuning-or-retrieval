Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# Steroids, IVIG, and the Exit Strategy: Managing Severe IMNM Without Lifelong Immunosuppression

What makes this case interesting? A woman in her 60s developed a fulminant immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) with dysphagia, dysphonia, and CK into the thousands after a single dose of semaglutide. Muscle biopsy showed necrosis with macrophage/myophagocytic inflammation and membrane attack complex deposition — classic for IMNM — yet she improved with a short course of high‑dose methylprednisolone and one admission course of IVIG and was off immunosuppression and back to baseline at 10 months. That clinical course looks more like a drug‑triggered IMNM with a finite immunologic insult than the protracted, antibody‑positive IMNM syndromes we usually fear. This raises a practical question EDs, hospitalists, rheumatologists and neurologists increasingly face: when can you be aggressive up front, and when can you plan for a short, finite course and early exit from chronic immunosuppression?

Below I summarize a pragmatic management pathway inspired by this case — when to pulse, when to add IVIG early, how to monitor, how to manage infection/VTE/swallow/respiratory risk, and reasonable criteria to consider a rapid taper and stopping therapy when a drug trigger is suspected.

## Rapid assessment on presentation — the priorities
- Establish the phenotype: rapidly progressive proximal weakness, bulbar symptoms (dysphagia/dysphonia), respiratory compromise and CK elevation point toward a myopathic process that may be immune‑mediated. IMNM often has marked weakness and high CK; biopsy classically shows necrosis > inflammation.
- Identify red flags that push you to urgent treatment: airway threat or aspiration risk from bulbar weakness; respiratory muscle weakness (vital capacity fall, orthopnea, rising PaCO2); rapidly rising CK with renal risk; inability to maintain nutrition or secretions.
- Do targeted baseline tests immediately: CK, CMP (including renal function and transaminases), TSH, troponin/BNP if concern for myocarditis, CBC, CRP/ESR, urinalysis (infection), and a myositis antibody panel (SRP, HMGCR, myositis panel) — but remember seronegative IMNM is common.
- Imaging/diagnostics: MRI muscle is helpful to map inflammation/edema; early muscle biopsy confirms IMNM features and can guide prognosis. Bedside swallow assessment and measures of respiratory strength (vital capacity, negative inspiratory force) are essential if bulbar/respiratory symptoms present.

In the reported case the patient had progressive proximal weakness with dysphagia, CK rising to ~4,000, MRI showing proximal muscle edema, and biopsy consistent with IMNM. She had an early severe phenotype (bulbar involvement) — reason enough to treat promptly.

## When to pulse methylprednisolone
Pulse steroids (eg, methylprednisolone 1 g IV daily for 3–5 days) are indicated for life‑ or function‑threatening inflammatory myopathies: severe bulbar involvement causing aspiration risk, rapid respiratory decline, very high CK with renal threat, or rapidly progressive weakness resulting in loss of mobility. The goal is rapid disease control while other treatments take effect.

In this case: the team gave 1 g IV methylprednisolone daily for 5 days given progressive bulbar symptoms and severe weakness — a reasonable approach.

Practical considerations:
- Pulse first when risk of severe morbidity is high. Use 3–5 days of 1 g/day methylprednisolone (shorter if infection risk is significant), then transition to oral prednisone 1 mg/kg (or equivalent) if improvement occurs.
- Screen and treat latent infections as feasible before prolonged steroids (eg, TB history). If immediate pulse is required, do baseline infection surveillance (urine culture, chest imaging, consider blood cultures in unstable patients).

## Early IVIG — when to add it
IVIG should be considered early in IMNM when:
- There is severe weakness (inability to ambulate, bulbar dysfunction) despite steroids, or
- Bulbar/respiratory involvement is present (higher aspiration and ventilation risk), or
- The clinical picture suggests seronegative IMNM where steroid monotherapy often underperforms, or
- Rapid steroid‑sparing is desirable (eg, in patients with infection risk or comorbidity).

Dosing and timing:
- Typical inpatient regimen: 2 g/kg total, often given over 2–5 days (the case used 2 g/kg split over 4 days). Start as early as practical when severe features exist.
- IVIG is particularly attractive when you suspect a drug‑triggered, finite auto‑immune hit: it can rapidly neutralize pathogenic autoantibody and dampen complement activation (note: muscle biopsy in this patient showed membrane attack complex).

In the case, early IVIG plus pulse steroids was associated with a rapid CK fall and functional improvement, supporting early combined therapy for severe presentations.

## Monitoring: labs and function — the practical rhythm
- CK: baseline and then serially (eg, every 48–72 hours inpatient). Expect CK to lag clinical improvement; falling CK supports response.
- Strength/function: perform daily bedside strength exams (MRC grades for key muscle groups), timed sit‑to‑stand, and document swallow/voice changes.
- Respiratory: serial vital capacity (sitting and supine if available), oxygenation, and consider capillary/arterial blood gas if hypercapnia suspected.
- Cardiac monitoring: troponin/BNP if symptoms or MRI/echo abnormalities — myocarditis can coexist.
- Infection surveillance: daily temp/clinical exam; urine cultures in older or catheterised patients; chest imaging if pulmonary symptoms.

In our case CK declined markedly over 2 weeks and function improved; serial monitoring allowed the team to confidently taper steroids.

## The exit strategy — when to taper and stop immunosuppression
If you suspect a drug-triggered IMNM (recent temporal association with a new agent, e.g., semaglutide in this case) and the patient demonstrates clinical and biochemical response, you can plan an early, relatively rapid taper — but do it with criteria and safety nets.

Reasonable criteria to attempt tapering and consider stopping immunosuppression:
- Sustained clinical improvement: return toward baseline strength and resolution of bulbar symptoms such that airway/aspiration risk is mitigated.
- Objective improvement in function: ability to ambulate or perform baseline ADLs, stable/falling timed functional tests.
- CK normalization or sustained trend toward normal range for several weeks (exact timing varies; the case achieved normal CK and sustained remission).
- No new symptoms suggesting relapse (new weakness, dysphagia, dyspnea).
- Absence of high‑risk autoantibodies (HMGCR or SRP positive disease often requires prolonged therapy; in drug‑induced cases antibodies may be absent).
- Close outpatient follow‑up guaranteed with repeat CK and early access to clinic or re‑admission if relapse occurs.

Taper approach:
- After pulse → prednisone 1 mg/kg daily, reassess within 1–2 weeks.
- If rapid clinical and CK response, consider a faster taper over 4–8 weeks tailored to recovery. The case we reviewed successfully tapered off prednisone within ~2 months.
- If relapse or incomplete recovery, broaden therapy (repeat IVIG, add steroid‑sparing agents: rituximab, mycophenolate, azathioprine, methotrexate) per IMNM protocols.

Document and avoid rechallenge with the suspected offending drug. In this case the patient avoided future GLP‑1 receptor agonists and remained well off immunosuppression at 10 months.

## Infection risk mitigation — learning from the septic UTI
High‑dose steroids and IVIG increase infection risk. Our patient developed a Pseudomonas septic UTI after discharge.

Practical steps to reduce infection morbidity:
- Screen and treat active infections before or during immunosuppression if possible (eg, treat asymptomatic bacteriuria in high‑risk bulbar patients? Use clinical judgement).
- Minimize invasive devices (avoid unnecessary catheters; if required, remove ASAP).
- Vaccination history review (influenza, pneumococcal) — give vaccines when safe and not during high‑dose steroids/IVIG (live vaccines contraindicated).
- Patient education about early signs of infection and low threshold for evaluation.
- Consider prophylaxis for opportunistic infections only when long‑term immunosuppression is planned (eg, PCP prophylaxis), not for short courses.

The take‑home: short courses of high‑dose steroids + IVIG still carry infection risk; anticipate it, minimize exposures (urinary catheters), and counsel patients.

## VTE considerations
This patient had a remote DVT/PE history. Immobility from severe weakness plus high‑dose steroids and acute illness increases VTE risk.

Practical steps:
- Pharmacologic prophylaxis (eg, low molecular weight heparin) unless contraindicated, continued until ambulatory.
- For patients with prior VTE, consider individualized peri‑hospital anticoagulation strategy — consult hematology if complex.
- Encourage early mobilization and physical therapy once safe.

## Bulbar and respiratory care — don’t miss aspiration and ventilatory failure
Bulbar involvement in IMNM is a predictor of morbidity. The case had dysphagia and dysphonia initially.

Actions:
- Formal swallow evaluation early (speech and language pathology), NPO if unsafe.
- Consider nasogastric or percutaneous feeding options if prolonged dysphagia is anticipated.
- Monitor respiratory function closely; admit to monitored settings if vital capacity is falling or if hypercapnia develops.
- Early involvement of ICU and respiratory therapy if there is any hint of impending ventilatory failure.

## When to call second opinions and escalate
- Lack of early improvement or worsening despite pulse steroids + IVIG — consider escalation: second IVIG course, add steroid‑sparing agents (rituximab is often effective for refractory IMNM), and discuss plasmapheresis in select cases.
- Positive HMGCR or SRP antibodies with severe/persistent disease — anticipate longer, multi‑agent immunosuppression.
- Ongoing diagnostic uncertainty — consider repeat biopsy or specialized referral centers.

## Clinical pearls from the case
- Not all necrotising myopathies require lifelong immunosuppression. A drug‑triggered IMNM phenotype (clear temporal relation, biopsy‑proven, seronegative, marked recovery after drug withdrawal + short immunosuppression) can remit after a finite course.
- Treat severe presentations aggressively up front (pulse steroids ± early IVIG) to prevent permanent weakness, aspiration, or respiratory failure.
- Monitor CK trends but prioritize clinical function and respiratory/bulbar status when making acute decisions; CK lags clinical change.
- Proactively manage infection and VTE risk — short, intense immunosuppression still carries complications.
- Avoid rechallenge with the suspected offending agent.

## Take‑home messages
- Consider pulse methylprednisolone (1 g daily × 3–5 days) for severe, rapidly progressive IMNM phenotypes (bulbar/respiratory threat, inability to mobilize).
- Add early IVIG (2 g/kg total) when bulbar/respiratory involvement is present, when steroid response is inadequate, or when you suspect a drug‑triggered IMNM that may respond to a finite immunologic reset.
- Monitor CK, bedside strength, swallow and respiratory function serially; prioritize objective function and respiratory measures over isolated CK numbers.
- If drug‑induced IMNM is suspected and the patient shows sustained clinical and biochemical improvement, a planned rapid taper and stopping of immunosuppression (with close follow‑up) is reasonable.
- Anticipate and mitigate infection and VTE risks during and after immunosuppression; educate patients about early signs of relapse or infection and ensure close outpatient follow‑up.

This case is a useful reminder: be aggressive to save function up front, but if the trigger is removable and recovery is robust, an exit strategy aimed at minimizing chronic immunosuppression can succeed — provided you monitor closely and are ready to escalate if needed.
