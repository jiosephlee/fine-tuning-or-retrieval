Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# From Signal to Causality: Applying WHO‑UMC to Suspected GLP‑1–Triggered Autoimmunity

What makes this case interesting
A woman in her 60s developed a progressive, biopsy‑proven immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) with severe proximal weakness, dysphagia and a 12‑fold rise in CK over 4 months — beginning after a single dose of semaglutide. She improved with high‑dose steroids and IVIG and remained well off immunosuppression after avoiding GLP‑1 therapy. This is the first reported IMNM with a close temporal relationship to a GLP‑1 receptor agonist, raising an important pharmacovigilance question: is this coincidence, direct toxicity, or drug‑triggered autoimmunity?

Use this case as a worked example of structured causality assessment using the WHO‑Uppsala Monitoring Centre (WHO‑UMC) framework, and for practical, bedside steps clinicians can take in the increasingly common setting of GLP‑1 use.

## Quick reminder: what is IMNM?
IMNM is an aggressive autoimmune myopathy characterised by prominent muscle fibre necrosis with macrophage/complimentary activation on biopsy, often severe proximal weakness, markedly elevated CK, and association with autoantibodies (anti‑HMGCR, anti‑SRP) in many — but not all — patients. A substantial minority are seronegative. Idiopathic IMNM often needs prolonged immunosuppression; drug‑associated forms can sometimes remit after withdrawal and limited immunotherapy.

## Applying WHO‑UMC to this patient — the logic ladder
WHO‑UMC uses criteria that you can apply at the bedside to move a signal toward (or away from) probable causality. Main domains are temporal relationship, alternative explanations, dechallenge/rechallenge, known pharmacology, and objective evidence.

1) Temporal relationship
- Signal: symptoms began immediately (hours) after the first semaglutide dose (nausea, vomiting, hypovolaemia), then progressive weakness and large CK rise over 4 months.
- Interpretation: reasonable time relationship — acute intolerance followed by delayed autoimmune phenotype is biologically plausible for an immune‑triggered process.

2) Alternative explanations
- The patient had pre‑existing NAFLD with mild AST/ALT elevation long before semaglutide; no prior statin or known myotoxic exposures; no family history; myositis serology (anti‑HMGCR, anti‑SRP, Jo‑1 etc.) was negative. Cardiac workup had transient abnormalities but ultimately normalised.
- Interpretation: reasonable exclusion of common alternative causes. Absence of HMGCR/SRP antibodies doesn't exclude IMNM — about one‑third are seronegative.

3) Dechallenge (withdrawal) and response
- Semaglutide was not continued; after high‑dose steroids + IVIG (short course) CK normalised and function recovered, and the patient remained well off immunosuppression for months.
- Interpretation: positive dechallenge with sustained clinical remission favors a drug‑related trigger rather than persistent idiopathic IMNM.

4) Rechallenge
- Ethical constraints: rechallenge with the same or another GLP‑1 would be high risk and unjustified given severity; therefore unavailable.
- Interpretation: lack of rechallenge limits definitive proof, but rechallenge is often not required for a “probable” causality determination in severe reactions.

5) Objective evidence
- Muscle biopsy showed necrotic/regenerating fibers, CD68 macrophages, membrane attack complex deposition — classic IMNM histology. CK reached 3992 U/L. These objective data strengthen causality assessment.

Putting it together: WHO‑UMC category
- The case fits “probable/likely” causality: reasonable time relationship, no alternative explanations, positive dechallenge with clinical improvement, objective supporting data, and rechallenge not done (and unethical).

## Practical pharmacovigilance in the GLP‑1 era — what to do in clinic or ED

Counseling before starting GLP‑1 receptor agonists
- Tell patients the common side effects (GI upset, nausea), and rare but important signals (muscle symptoms, myalgias, CK elevation reported in case reports).
- Advise patients to report new profound weakness (especially proximal), difficulty swallowing, new dysphonia, dark urine or inability to arise from a chair.

What to monitor and when to check CK
- Routine CK monitoring for all patients is not currently standard. Consider baseline CK in patients with prior myopathy, unexplained transaminase elevations where muscle source hasn't been excluded, or when patients are on other myotoxic meds.
- Check CK promptly if a patient develops:
  - New or progressive proximal muscle weakness
  - Myalgias with functional decline
  - New dysphagia, dysphonia, or respiratory symptoms suggesting bulbar/respiratory involvement
  - Dark urine or other signs of rhabdomyolysis
- In mild isolated myalgias without weakness, consider CK and clinical reassessment; mild CK elevations alone do not mandate stopping therapy unless symptomatic or high.

Initial evaluation of suspected GLP‑1–related myopathy
- Focused history: timing of symptom onset relative to GLP‑1 exposure, prior statin/supplement exposure (red yeast rice, mushrooms), family history, alcohol/illicit use, prior transaminase elevations.
- Exam: look for proximal weakness, bulbar involvement, skin findings, respiratory compromise.
- Initial labs: CK, AST/ALT (note muscle can raise transaminases), renal function, electrolytes, TSH, inflammatory markers.
- If CK elevated and clinical concern high: neurology/rheumatology consult, EMG ± MRI to localise, and consider muscle biopsy for definitive diagnosis.
- Early involvement of specialists facilitates decisions about immunosuppression.

Reporting and documentation
- Document the timeline carefully: drug start date, dose, single vs repeated exposure, symptom onset and progression, CK values, treatments, and outcomes.
- Report suspected serious adverse reactions to local/national pharmacovigilance systems (e.g., FDA MedWatch in the US, Yellow Card in the UK) — case reports matter. Include biopsy results, labs, and follow‑up.
- Consider institutional review or adverse event committee notification if the patient was started on GLP‑1 in an inpatient setting.

Class re‑exposure: avoid with caution
- Given plausibility of class effects (another GLP‑1 agonist and tirzepatide have been implicated in necrotising/rhabdomyolysis‑type injury), avoid re‑exposure to the same class unless compelling reasons and with informed consent and close monitoring.
- If weight/diabetes therapy is needed, discuss alternative classes and involve endocrinology.

## Clinical pearls
- A temporal association plus objective evidence (biopsy + CK) and improvement after withdrawal/immunotherapy can support a “probable” drug‑cause even without a positive autoantibody or rechallenge.
- Not all IMNM patients are antibody positive — seronegativity does not rule out immune‑mediated disease.
- Acute GI‑mediated hypovolaemia and AKI after GLP‑1 dosing can be the first sign of intolerance; persistent or progressive weakness after that should prompt myopathy evaluation.
- Distinguish direct toxic rhabdomyolysis (rapid CK spike, quick recovery after withdrawal/hydration) from immune‑mediated myopathy (progressive weakness, histologic necrosis with inflammatory features, and need for immunosuppression).

## Take‑home messages
- This semaglutide‑associated IMNM case meets criteria for probable causality per WHO‑UMC: reasonable timing, exclusion of alternatives, objective biopsy confirmation, and positive dechallenge with sustained recovery.
- Be vigilant for new proximal weakness, bulbar symptoms or marked CK rise in patients on GLP‑1 receptor agonists; act early with CK testing and specialist input.
- Document and report suspected serious adverse drug reactions — single case reports can be the seed that identifies a true rare adverse effect.
- Avoid class re‑exposure after a severe suspected immune‑mediated reaction; manage onward metabolic care with alternative strategies and specialist collaboration.

If you see a similar case in your ED or clinic: stabilise the patient, measure CK, involve neurology/rheumatology, document the timeline, stop the GLP‑1 agent, treat IMNM if indicated, and report the event. In the GLP‑1 era, vigilance + structured causality thinking = better patient safety and better science.
