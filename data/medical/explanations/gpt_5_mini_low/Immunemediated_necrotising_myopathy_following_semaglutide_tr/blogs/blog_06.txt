# Cardiac Involvement in Inflammatory Myopathies: Reading the Biomarkers and MRI

What makes this case notable? A woman in her 60s with immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) had modest hs‑troponin elevation, a markedly raised pro‑BNP, normal LVEF on echo but elevated RVSP, and cardiac MRI showing focal myocardial edema and mild regional hypokinesis without late gadolinium enhancement (LGE). Her cardiac abnormalities normalised after treatment for IMNM (steroids + IVIG) and clinical recovery. This is a real‑world example of how cardiac markers, echo and cardiac MRI can sit on the fence between early myocarditis and other causes such as stress cardiomyopathy — and how to manage the diagnostic uncertainty.

Why this matters: inflammatory myopathies often have cardiac involvement. Missing it risks heart failure, arrhythmia or myocarditis. Overcalling it can lead to unnecessary invasive testing and delay in treating the primary myopathy. So how do you read the labs and imaging — and when do you escalate?

## Start with the pre‑test probability and clinical context
Always interpret hs‑troponin, BNP, echo and cMRI in the clinical picture.

- In IMNM and other inflammatory myopathies, cardiac involvement can range from subclinical troponin rise to fulminant myocarditis. Symptoms such as chest pain, new dyspnoea out of proportion to muscle weakness, syncope, palpitations or new conduction disease raise the pre‑test probability of clinically important cardiac disease.
- Consider non‑cardiac contributors to biomarkers: chronic renal dysfunction, sepsis, pulmonary hypertension, PE, and skeletal muscle disease can affect troponin and BNP interpretation. In this case the patient had acute kidney injury initially, sepsis later, and severe myopathy — all potential confounders.

## How to interpret troponin and BNP in suspected inflammatory myopathy
- High‑sensitivity troponin (hs‑Tn) is sensitive for myocardial injury but not specific for etiology. A mild elevation (eg, 62 pg/mL vs normal ≤53 pg/mL here) may reflect small‑volume myocardial injury, demand ischemia, or early inflammatory involvement.
- BNP/pro‑BNP reflects myocardial stretch; very high values (proBNP 4358 pg/mL here) indicate increased wall stress and are more suggestive of clinically relevant cardiac dysfunction (volume/pressure overload) even if the echo LVEF is preserved.
- Patterns that tip you toward myocarditis: rising troponin over serial measurements, symptoms of chest pain/dyspnoea out of proportion, ECG changes, new arrhythmia, regional wall motion abnormalities on echo, and cMRI features of edema/LGE.
- If troponin is only mildly above reference and clinical exam/ECG/echo are reassuring, consider close observation with serial biomarkers rather than immediate invasive workup.

## Echocardiography — what to look for and limitations
- Echo is the first‑line imaging to assess cardiac function and estimate filling pressures and pulmonary pressures.
- LVEF may be normal even with myocardial inflammation (small focal involvement). Regional wall motion abnormalities are important; they can point to myocarditis but mimic ischemia or stress cardiomyopathy.
- Elevated RV systolic pressure (RVSP ~46 mmHg in this case) suggests pulmonary hypertension or increased pulmonary vascular resistance and can contribute to BNP elevation.
- Echo is limited in tissue characterization — it won’t tell you if edema or active inflammation is present.

## Cardiac MRI — the problem‑solver (when used appropriately)
- cMRI provides tissue characterization: T2‑weighted or newer T2 mapping identifies edema; T1 mapping and LGE identify necrosis/fibrosis. The revised Lake Louise Criteria use T1/T2 mapping and LGE to support myocarditis.
- Findings in this case: focal myocardial edema with mild regional hypokinesis and no LGE. That pattern can be seen in early/limited myocarditis (edema without fibrosis yet) or early stress‑induced changes. Absence of LGE makes irreversible injury less likely.
- cMRI timing matters: early in the disease you may see edema without LGE. LGE may take time to become apparent; conversely, LGE can persist after clinical recovery.
- cMRI is most helpful when it changes management: if myocarditis is suspected and the result would alter treatment (immunosuppression, activity restriction, need for biopsy), then cMRI is appropriate.

## Myocarditis vs stress (Takotsubo) cardiomyopathy — distinguishing features
- Myocarditis: often presents with chest pain, elevated troponin, ECG ST‑T changes, variable LV function; cMRI shows edema ± LGE in non‑coronary distributions. Can be global or regional.
- Stress cardiomyopathy: typically follows a catecholaminergic/stress trigger, presents with regional wall motion abnormality that doesn’t match a single coronary territory (apical ballooning or variants), troponin modestly elevated, BNP often high. cMRI may show edema and typically lacks LGE or shows limited LGE.
- In inflammatory myopathy patients, either process is possible: systemic inflammation can provoke myocarditis; the stress of severe systemic illness or sepsis can provoke stress cardiomyopathy. The focality and lack of LGE in this case could fit either.

## When to involve cardiology and when to do more testing
Involve cardiology early if any of the following are present:
- Troponin rising on serial measurements or significantly elevated.
- BNP markedly elevated with symptoms of heart failure (orthopnea, pulmonary edema).
- New ECG changes (ST segment, new conduction disease) or arrhythmias.
- New or worsening dyspnoea out of proportion to neuromuscular weakness.
- Regional wall motion abnormality on echo or unexplained hypotension.

If cardiology is involved, next steps commonly include:
- Serial hs‑troponin and BNP measurements.
- Continuous ECG monitoring if arrhythmia or troponin rise.
- cMRI for tissue characterization unless immediate coronary angiography is indicated (eg, ischemic pattern or high suspicion for ACS).
- Consider endomyocardial biopsy only when results would change therapy (diagnostic uncertainty with suspected fulminant myocarditis, refractory cardiogenic shock, suspected giant cell myocarditis).

## Timing of follow‑up imaging and biomarkers
- Serial biomarkers: repeat hs‑troponin and BNP every 24–48 hours in the acute phase until a trend is established, then less frequently as clinically indicated.
- Repeat echo: at least once during hospitalization if initial echo abnormal; repeat at 1–3 months outpatient to document recovery, sooner if clinical deterioration.
- Repeat cMRI: reasonable at 3–6 months if initial cMRI showed myocarditis features to document resolution of edema/LGE and to guide long‑term prognosis (persistent LGE predicts worse outcomes). Earlier repeats (4–12 weeks) can be considered if clinical course is unclear or if treatment decisions hinge on imaging.
- In this case the patient had normalisation of cardiac MRI and echo on follow‑up, concordant with recovery from IMNM — which is a reassuring pattern.

## How cardiac recovery tracks with control of the myopathy
- When cardiac involvement is immune‑mediated (true myocarditis related to systemic autoimmune disease), effective control of systemic inflammation with immunosuppression (steroids, IVIG, other agents) often results in improvement in cardiac biomarkers and imaging findings.
- In drug‑induced or parainfectious myocarditis, removing the offending trigger and supportive care may be sufficient; immunosuppression is considered on a case‑by‑case basis.
- Correlation is imperfect: cardiac injury can lag behind skeletal muscle recovery or recover independently. Persistence of troponin elevation or LGE despite clinical muscle improvement should prompt cardiology follow‑up and risk stratification.

## Practical screening pearls for suspected inflammatory myopathy
- Baseline screening for new or suspected inflammatory myopathy: ECG, hs‑troponin, BNP, and an echo if symptoms or biomarkers abnormal.
- Any troponin elevation should prompt serial measurements and consideration of cMRI if clinical suspicion for myocarditis is moderate to high.
- Don’t assume a normal LVEF excludes myocardial involvement — use cMRI if symptoms/biomarkers suggest inflammation.
- Avoid routine endomyocardial biopsy unless the diagnosis is uncertain and the result would change therapy.
- Engage cardiology early for significant biomarker abnormalities, arrhythmia, heart failure, or abnormal imaging.

## Putting it into practice — applying this case
- This patient had small troponin rise but very high proBNP and regional hypokinesis with myocardial edema on cMRI. That constellation justified cardiology involvement and cMRI rather than assuming no cardiac disease because LVEF was preserved.
- The imaging pattern (edema without LGE) suggested potentially reversible inflammatory injury or stress‑related change; the decision to treat the systemic IMNM and provide supportive cardiac care was reasonable.
- Her cardiac abnormalities resolved alongside successful treatment of the IMNM — a pattern consistent with immune‑mediated or stress‑related myocardial injury that recovered with control of the systemic disease.

Key take‑home messages
- In inflammatory myopathies, even modest hs‑troponin elevation or a very high BNP warrants careful evaluation; combine biomarkers, ECG, echo and cMRI for decision making.
- Echo is first line for function; cMRI adds tissue characterization that helps distinguish myocarditis from other causes and guide prognosis.
- Absence of LGE does not exclude myocarditis (early disease can show edema only); persistent LGE carries worse prognosis.
- Involve cardiology early for rising biomarkers, arrhythmia, heart failure, or abnormal imaging; reserve biopsy for select cases where the result will alter management.
- Coordinate treatment of the underlying myopathy and cardiac care — cardiac recovery often follows control of systemic inflammation but may lag or require separate management.
