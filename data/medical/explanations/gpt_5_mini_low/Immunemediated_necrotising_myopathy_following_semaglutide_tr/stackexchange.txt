Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Morning Report] A woman in her 60s developed progressive proximal weakness with dysphagia and dysphonia over 4 months after a single dose of semaglutide; her CK peaked near 4000 U/L. What specific clinical, laboratory, and temporal features in this scenario favor immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) over drug-induced rhabdomyolysis from a GLP-1 receptor agonist?

Direct answer: Several linked features in this case point to immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) rather than an acute, direct drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis from a GLP‑1 receptor agonist.

Why — the key distinguishing features (clinical, laboratory, temporal, pathologic, and therapeutic):

- Temporal pattern: onset and progressive course over months after a single dose.  
  - Drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis from a direct toxic effect typically presents acutely while on the drug and improves rapidly (days–weeks) after drug cessation and supportive care. In this patient CK rose progressively over ~4 months with worsening weakness despite having received only a single semaglutide dose, which is far more consistent with a subacute autoimmune process.

- Pattern of weakness and systemic involvement: proximal symmetric weakness with bulbar features (dysphagia, dysphonia).  
  - IMNM classically produces severe proximal limb weakness and can involve bulbar muscles. Typical toxic rhabdomyolysis causes myalgias and weakness but less commonly progressive, prominent bulbar dysfunction.

- CK kinetics and magnitude relative to course: markedly elevated CK (peak ~3992 U/L) developing and persisting over weeks–months rather than a single abrupt huge spike that then falls rapidly.  
  - Acute toxic rhabdomyolysis often causes very large acute CK elevations (often >10–20× ULN) that fall after hydration/drug withdrawal; here CK rose 12‑fold over months and remained elevated until immunotherapy.

- Imaging and biopsy consistent with inflammatory necrotising myopathy: MRI showed symmetric proximal muscle edema; muscle biopsy showed dispersed necrotic and regenerating fibres, macrophage (CD68) myophagocytosis, membrane‑attack‑complex deposition and only rare T cells/perivascular inflammation.  
  - This histologic pattern—prominent necrosis/regeneration with macrophage predominance, MAC deposition, and only sparse lymphocytes—is the hallmark of IMNM. Drug‑induced toxic necrosis / rhabdomyolysis typically shows widespread necrosis without the specific inflammatory and complement features described.

- Response to immunotherapy and clinical course after withdrawal: rapid clinical and CK improvement after high‑dose steroids + IVIG, durable recovery off immunosuppression and without re‑exposure to semaglutide.  
  - IMNM usually responds to immunosuppression (steroids, IVIG) whereas simple toxic rhabdomyolysis improves with removal of the offending agent and supportive care alone and does not require or characteristically respond to immune therapy.

- Absence of alternative causes and supportive ancillary testing: no statin exposure (common trigger for HMGCR IMNM), negative myositis antibody panel (not excluding seronegative IMNM), and supportive MRI/normalization of CK after immune therapy argue for autoimmune trigger rather than metabolic/toxic cause.  
  - Note: up to ~1/3 of IMNM cases are seronegative, so negative SRP/HMGCR panels do not rule out IMNM.

Put together for this patient: the delayed, progressive proximal weakness with bulbar involvement, CK rising over months despite drug discontinuation, muscle MRI and biopsy findings characteristic of necrotising autoimmune myopathy (MAC, macrophages, necrosis/regeneration), and a clear, durable response to immunosuppression/IVIG favor IMNM triggered by (or temporally associated with) semaglutide rather than an acute drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis.

### [Pathophysiology] Muscle biopsy in IMNM typically shows necrotic and regenerating fibers with macrophage-rich myophagocytosis, membrane attack complex (MAC) deposition on myofibers, and a relative paucity of lymphocytes. How do these histopathologic features distinguish IMNM from polymyositis and dermatomyositis?

Direct answer
The muscle biopsy pattern you describe—widespread necrotic and regenerating myofibers with prominent macrophage-rich myophagocytosis, membrane attack complex (MAC) deposition on myofibers, and only a sparse T‑lymphocytic infiltrate—is characteristic of immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) and helps distinguish it from polymyositis and dermatomyositis.

Why these findings distinguish IMNM (pathophysiology and diagnostic reasoning)

- Predominant myofiber necrosis with macrophages:
  - IMNM: The dominant histologic lesion is myofiber necrosis and regeneration with abundant CD68+ macrophages actively phagocytosing fibers. Inflammation is focused on necrotic fibers and is macrophage‑predominant rather than lymphocyte‑predominant.
  - Polymyositis: Characterized by endomysial, predominantly CD8+ T‑cell infiltrates that invade otherwise non‑necrotic muscle fibers (true myocyte invasion), reflecting a T‑cell–mediated cytotoxic process.
  - Dermatomyositis: Shows relatively less myofiber necrosis centrally and instead a perifascicular pattern—perifascicular atrophy and fiber dropout—often with perivascular/perimysial inflammation rather than diffuse macrophage myophagocytosis.

- Composition and location of immune cells:
  - IMNM: Sparse lymphocytes; macrophages predominate and are centered on necrotic fibers. The case biopsy demonstrated CD68+ macrophages with myophagocytosis and only rare CD3+ cells, fitting IMNM.
  - Polymyositis: Dense endomysial CD8+ T cells that invade intact fibers; macrophages are not the dominant cell type.
  - Dermatomyositis: Perivascular and perimysial CD4+ T cells and B cells, often with plasmacytoid dendritic cell signatures.

- Complement (MAC) deposition pattern:
  - IMNM: MAC (C5b‑9) is deposited on the sarcolemma of myofibers (on necrotic and non‑necrotic fibers), consistent with antibody‑ and complement‑mediated myofiber injury. The case shows MAC staining on myofibers—supporting an antibody/complement mechanism typical of IMNM.
  - Dermatomyositis: Classical MAC deposition occurs on endomysial capillaries and is associated with microvascular injury and resultant perifascicular ischemic changes; sarcolemmal MAC on fibers is not the defining feature.
  - Polymyositis: MAC deposition on fibers is not the dominant or defining feature; the cytotoxic mechanism is T‑cell mediated.

- Distribution and special features:
  - IMNM: Diffuse necrosis/regeneration, often with MHC‑I upregulation, but without the perifascicular atrophy of dermatomyositis or the classical fiber invasion by CD8+ cells of polymyositis.
  - Dermatomyositis: Perifascicular atrophy, capillary loss, and ischemic changes reflect small vessel injury.
  - Polymyositis: Fiber invasion by CD8+ T cells and more diffuse endomysial inflammation.

Clinical and diagnostic implications
- Muscle biopsy is the diagnostic standard for distinguishing these entities when clinical features overlap. In this case the biopsy’s necrosis/regeneration, macrophage myophagocytosis, MAC on myofibers, and relative paucity of lymphocytes pointed away from polymyositis (which requires CD8+ invasion of non‑necrotic fibers) and dermatomyositis (which shows perifascicular atrophy and capillary MAC), and supported IMNM.
- Corroborating data often include serologies (anti‑HMGCR or anti‑SRP in many IMNM patients), MRI showing muscle edema, and the clinical phenotype of marked proximal weakness with high CK. This patient was seronegative but had the classic biopsy pattern and clinical course responsive to immunotherapy—again typical for IMNM.

Takeaway pearl
When muscle biopsy shows macrophage‑rich necrosis with sarcolemmal MAC and few lymphocytes, think IMNM—an antibody/complement‑driven necrotizing process—rather than polymyositis (T‑cell–mediated fiber invasion) or dermatomyositis (microvascular injury with perifascicular atrophy and capillary MAC). The case’s biopsy (CD68+ myophagocytosis, MAC on myofibers, rare CD3+ cells) is a prototypical example supporting the IMNM diagnosis.

### [Clinical Pearls] In patients with both elevated CK and elevated transaminases, what clinical and laboratory approach helps differentiate muscle-derived AST/ALT elevations from hepatic sources such as nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (e.g., use of CK, GGT, bilirubin, INR, imaging, and trend discordance)?

Direct answer
When both CK and transaminases (AST, ALT) are elevated, use the pattern and additional liver- vs muscle‑specific tests and imaging, plus serial trends, to distinguish muscle-derived transaminase release from bona fide liver injury. The key discriminators are: (1) CK as the most specific marker of muscle injury and its temporal correlation with AST/ALT; (2) cholestatic and synthetic liver tests (GGT, ALP, bilirubin, INR, albumin, platelets) that point to hepatic disease when abnormal; (3) AST-versus-ALT pattern and AST:ALT relationship to CK; and (4) imaging or biopsy when chronic liver disease is suspected.

Practical clinical approach (stepwise)
1. Confirm the CK level and clinical context
- A markedly elevated CK (hundreds to many thousands U/L) with proximal weakness, myalgias, or rhabdomyolysis strongly suggests muscle as the source. In muscle injury AST tends to rise proportionally with CK; ALT may rise less.
- In the presented case CK rose to ~4,000 U/L and tracked with muscle weakness and biopsy‑proven IMNM, supporting a muscle source for much of the transaminase elevation.

2. Check liver‑specific enzymes that help localize injury
- GGT and alkaline phosphatase (ALP): elevations support hepatic (or biliary) origin. Normal GGT/ALP argues against a primary hepatocellular process.
- Bilirubin, INR, albumin, platelets: abnormalities indicate impaired hepatic synthetic function or advanced liver disease (not expected from isolated muscle injury).
- In the case, GGT/ALP and synthetic markers were not notably deranged and bilirubin/INR were normal historically, arguing against acute hepatic failure as the cause of the transaminase rise.

3. Assess AST:ALT pattern and relationship to CK
- Muscle injury often raises AST more than ALT (AST relatively higher), and AST frequently parallels CK. An AST that falls as CK falls supports muscle origin.
- If ALT > AST and transaminases remain elevated after CK normalizes, a hepatic source is more likely.
- In this patient she had baseline mild ALT>AST from NAFLD before semaglutide; during the acute myopathy both AST and ALT rose but then transaminases persisted mildly after CK normalized — supporting chronic hepatic (NAFLD) contribution plus an acute muscle component.

4. Use additional muscle biomarkers if needed
- Aldolase, LDH, and myoglobin may support muscle injury but are less specific than CK.
- Urine myoglobin may be present in rhabdomyolysis.

5. Serial measurements and trend discordance
- Temporal concordance: if AST/ALT track closely with CK (rise and fall together), muscle is likely the dominant source.
- Discordance: if transaminases remain elevated or worsen while CK normalizes, think liver disease (e.g., NAFLD, viral, autoimmune, drug‑induced).
- The case illustrates this principle: CK normalized with immunosuppression while mild transaminase elevation persisted consistent with her preexisting NAFLD.

6. Imaging and histology when indicated
- Liver ultrasound (or MRI) to evaluate steatosis, biliary obstruction, focal lesions. In the case, ultrasound/MRI showed steatotic hepatomegaly consistent with NAFLD.
- If noninvasive workup is inconclusive or chronic disease needs staging, consider liver biopsy (as was done and showed steatohepatitis).
- Muscle MRI and muscle biopsy are indicated when myopathy is suspected (in the case MRI showed muscle edema and biopsy confirmed IMNM).

Putting it together — an algorithm you can use at the bedside
- Step 1: Assess symptoms: myalgias, weakness, dark urine → think muscle; jaundice, pruritus, RUQ pain → think liver.
- Step 2: Measure CK, AST, ALT, GGT, ALP, bilirubin, INR, albumin, platelets.
- Step 3: Compare kinetics: do AST/ALT track with CK? If yes → muscle. If no → pursue liver evaluation.
- Step 4: If liver tests (GGT/ALP/bilirubin/INR) abnormal or imaging suggests liver disease → hepatology workup (viral serologies, autoimmune markers, imaging, ± biopsy).
- Step 5: If muscle suspected → muscle enzymes, EMG/MRI, neuromuscular referral and muscle biopsy as indicated.

Clinical pearl from the case
This patient had both chronic, biopsy‑proven NAFLD (explaining mild baseline transaminase elevations) and a superimposed IMNM with large CK elevations and AST/ALT rises. The rapid improvement and normalization of CK after immunotherapy with persistent mild transaminase elevation thereafter illustrate the diagnostic value of trend concordance: muscle-derived AST/ALT fell with CK, while the residual ALT/AST that persisted reflected her underlying fatty liver. Use CK trends plus GGT/ALP and synthetic markers, and image/biopsy when uncertainty remains, to localize the source reliably.

### [Clinical Pearls] Anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP antibodies are strongly associated with IMNM, yet up to one-third of patients are seronegative. In a seronegative patient with compatible clinical features and biopsy findings, what is the diagnostic significance and how should this influence confidence in the diagnosis and subsequent management?

Short answer
Absence of anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP does not exclude immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). When the clinical syndrome (subacute severe proximal weakness, markedly elevated CK, MRI muscle edema) and a muscle biopsy show the characteristic necrosis with macrophage‑predominant inflammation and membrane‑attack‑complex deposition, you can be confident in the diagnosis and should treat as IMNM while also searching for alternative triggers and monitoring for relapse.

Clinical reasoning and pathophysiology
- Anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP are strongly associated with IMNM and often guide diagnosis, prognosis, and sometimes therapy. However, ~25–33% of patients with clinicopathologic IMNM are seronegative — implying either undiscovered autoantibodies or seronegative autoimmune biology. Seronegativity therefore lowers the “helpfulness” of serology but not the diagnostic weight of compatible clinical and histopathologic findings.
- IMNM is defined pathologically by scattered necrotic and regenerating fibers, myophagocytosis (CD68+ macrophages), sparse lymphocytes, and often complement (C5b‑9/MAC) deposition on muscle fibres — findings that reflect antibody‑ and complement‑mediated myofibre injury even when the circulating autoantibody is not detected.
- In the presented case the combination of progressive proximal weakness, very high CK, MRI showing proximal muscle edema, and muscle biopsy demonstrating necrotic/regenerating fibres, macrophage infiltration, myophagocytosis and MAC staining is diagnostic of IMNM despite negative myositis panel.

Diagnostic approach in seronegative patients
- Treat the biopsy and clinical phenotype as the gold standard when serology is negative. A classic clinicopathologic picture (as in this case) is sufficient for diagnosis.
- Consider repeat or alternative serologic testing at a reference lab or using different assay platforms (ELISA, immunoprecipitation) because low‑titer or assay‑dependent antibodies can be missed.
- Continue routine evaluation for secondary causes/triggers: recent drugs (statins, now GLP‑1 RA in this case), malignancy screening as indicated, infections, and other connective‑tissue disease features.
- Evaluate for extramuscular involvement (cardiac MRI or troponin, pulmonary imaging) because IMNM can involve heart/lungs even if seronegative.

Management implications
- Management should be guided by disease severity and biopsy confirmation, not seronegativity alone. Severe presentations (marked weakness, dysphagia, very high CK, respiratory or bulbar involvement) warrant prompt immunotherapy — high‑dose corticosteroids, frequently combined early with IVIG and/or a steroid‑sparing agent.
- In suspected drug‑triggered cases (this patient’s clear temporal relationship to semaglutide and recovery after withdrawal), remove the offending agent and treat; drug‑associated IMNM may respond to shorter immunosuppression courses than idiopathic, antibody‑positive IMNM, but close follow‑up is essential.
- Monitor clinical strength, functional status, and CK serially. If improvement is sustained off therapy (as here, normal CK and recovery at 10 months), prolonged aggressive immunosuppression may not be necessary. If relapse or refractory disease occurs, escalate therapy (repeat IVIG, rituximab, mycophenolate, methotrexate, etc.) and reconsider alternative diagnoses or occult triggers.
- Maintain vigilance for cardiac involvement (this patient had transient troponin/proBNP and MRI changes) and manage accordingly.

Bottom line
In a patient with the classic clinical syndrome and supportive muscle biopsy, seronegativity reduces neither the likelihood of true IMNM nor the need for prompt treatment. Use serology as complementary information, attempt repeat/specialized testing if helpful, withdraw any suspect offending drug, initiate appropriate immunotherapy based on severity, and follow closely for relapse or refractory disease. The presented case is a paradigmatic example: biopsy‑proven IMNM with negative SRP/HMGCR antibodies that responded to corticosteroids + IVIG and withdrawal of semaglutide.

### [Management] For severe IMNM with bulbar involvement (dysphagia/dysphonia) and marked weakness, what initial immunosuppressive regimen is recommended (including typical pulse-dose IV methylprednisolone, transition to high-dose oral prednisone, and the role and dosing of IVIG), and how should potential triggers (e.g., GLP-1 receptor agonists) be addressed?

Direct answer
- Initial immunosuppressive approach for severe IMNM with bulbar involvement is aggressive and early: pulse‑dose IV methylprednisolone (typically 1 g IV daily for 3–5 days), immediately followed by high‑dose oral prednisone ~1 mg/kg/day (commonly 60–80 mg/day), with early addition of IVIG at 2 g/kg total dose (given as 2 g/kg over 2–5 days, e.g. 0.5–1 g/kg/day ×2–4 days). IVIG should be given promptly when there is bulbar or respiratory compromise or marked weakness because it often produces a relatively rapid clinical and CK response.

Why this regimen (clinical reasoning & pathophysiology)
- IMNM is a necrotizing, antibody‑mediated myopathy with myofiber necrosis, macrophage/myophagocytic infiltration and complement activation. High‑dose steroids suppress broad inflammation quickly; IVIG provides immune modulation (neutralizes pathogenic antibodies, interferes with complement and Fc receptor–mediated injury) and is particularly effective in severe phenotypes (anti‑SRP, anti‑HMGCR) and when bulbar/respiratory muscles are involved.
- In practice for severe disease one combines rapid pulse steroids to arrest ongoing injury and IVIG to accelerate recovery and reduce steroid dependence; steroid‑sparing agents (rituximab, methotrexate, azathioprine, mycophenolate, or cyclophosphamide) are added early if response is incomplete or if longer‑term immunosuppression is anticipated.

Practical dosing and monitoring points
- IV methylprednisolone: 1 g IV daily ×3–5 days. Shorter courses (3 days) are common, but many teams use 5 days for fulminant disease.
- Transition: switch to oral prednisone ~1 mg/kg/day (round to practical dose, max ~60–80 mg/day) after pulses. Reassess clinically and by CK, then taper guided by improvement; taper is individualized—idiopathic IMNM often requires many months of therapy while drug‑related cases may permit faster taper.
- IVIG: 2 g/kg total. Common regimens: 1 g/kg IV ×2 days or 0.4 g/kg IV daily ×5 days, or 0.5 g/kg ×4 days as used in the presented case. Give early when bulbar/respiratory involvement or severe weakness is present.
- Add steroid‑sparing agent early (within weeks) if severe disease, antibody‑positive IMNM, incomplete response, or need to limit steroid toxicity. Rituximab is commonly used for anti‑SRP disease; methotrexate/azathioprine/mycophenolate are alternatives. Cyclophosphamide may be considered for life‑threatening or refractory disease.
- Monitoring: daily assessment of swallowing/airway and respiratory function (spirometry/negative inspiratory force if worried), CK trends, strength, infection surveillance. Provide prophylaxis for steroid complications (bone protection, gastric protection as indicated, consider PJP prophylaxis if additional immunosuppression is started).

Addressing potential triggers (GLP‑1 receptor agonists and others)
- Stop the suspected offending agent immediately. In this case semaglutide was discontinued; the patient improved with immunotherapy and remained off immunosuppression while avoiding GLP‑1 RA exposure—consistent with a drug‑associated course.
- Do not rechallenge the same agent (or closely related agents) after a probable severe immune adverse event. Document the adverse reaction clearly in the chart and communicate to the patient and their outpatient providers.
- Report suspected drug‑induced IMNM to pharmacovigilance systems (FDA MedWatch or local/national reporting systems) and consider referral to a specialty center if uncertainty remains.
- Continue standard evaluation for other triggers and associations (stop statins if present, test for anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP, screen for occult malignancy where indicated) because IMNM may be idiopathic, paraneoplastic, or drug‑associated.

How the present case supports this approach
- The patient received pulse methylprednisolone 1 g IV daily ×5 days, transitioned to prednisone 1 mg/kg/day, and received IVIG 2 g/kg split over 4 days because of severe weakness and bulbar signs. She had rapid CK improvement and progressive clinical recovery, permitting relatively rapid prednisone wean and no further IVIG—consistent with a drug‑triggered IMNM that responded to early aggressive therapy and avoidance of rechallenge with the suspected agent.

Bottom line
- For severe IMNM with bulbar involvement: give IV methylprednisolone 1 g/day ×3–5 days, promptly start oral prednisone ≈1 mg/kg/day, and add IVIG 2 g/kg (divided over 2–5 days). Stop the suspected trigger (here, semaglutide/GLP‑1 RA), avoid rechallenge, report the adverse event, and tailor further immunosuppression based on clinical response and antibody status.

### [Morning Report] In suspected inflammatory myopathy presenting with progressive proximal weakness and elevated CK, which diagnostic studies are highest-yield to establish diagnosis and assess extent of involvement (e.g., myositis-specific antibodies, thigh MRI for edema, EMG, muscle biopsy, pulmonary screening for ILD, echocardiography/cardiac MRI), and why?

Direct answer
- Highest-yield studies to establish the diagnosis of an inflammatory myopathy and define extent are: (1) a myositis autoantibody panel (myositis-specific and -associated antibodies), (2) MRI of the thighs to document muscle edema and guide biopsy, (3) muscle biopsy for definitive histopathology, and (4) targeted organ screening for extramuscular involvement — specifically pulmonary evaluation (high-resolution chest CT and PFTs if ILD suspected) and cardiac evaluation (ECG, troponin/NT-proBNP, transthoracic echo ± cardiac MRI). EMG is useful adjunctive testing but is lower yield than MRI/biopsy for diagnosis and extent assessment.

Why — clinical reasoning and pathophysiology
- Myositis autoantibodies: Certain antibodies (eg, anti-HMGCR, anti-SRP, Jo-1 and other antisynthetase antibodies, MDA5, TIF1-γ, NXP2) point to specific clinicopathologic subtypes, predict associations (statin exposure, ILD, cancer risk, prognosis) and influence treatment and monitoring. A positive antibody can make the diagnosis more secure and orient extramuscular surveillance. However, ~20–30% (and higher in IMNM subsets) can be seronegative, so a negative panel does not exclude disease — as demonstrated in this case (negative SRP and HMGCR and a broad myositis panel yet biopsy-proven IMNM).
- Thigh MRI: MRI is highly sensitive for active inflammation (T2/STIR or fat-suppressed sequences show muscle edema), maps distribution (proximal symmetric vs focal), detects chronic fatty replacement, and importantly guides the biopsy to an affected muscle (avoiding end-stage fatty tissue). In this case MRI showed symmetric proximal thigh edema that corroborated the clinical pattern and guided biopsy.
- Muscle biopsy: Histopathology is the diagnostic gold standard when clinical/imaging/serology are inconclusive. Patterns (necrotic fibers with macrophage predominance and membrane attack complex deposition in IMNM; perifascicular atrophy in dermatomyositis; endomysial T-cell invasion in polymyositis) establish the subtype and inform prognosis and therapy. In the reported patient, biopsy demonstrated dispersed necrotic/regenerating fibers, CD68+ myophagocytosis and membrane attack complex — classic for IMNM and decisive for management despite negative serologies.
- EMG: Electrodiagnostic testing can document myopathic motor-unit changes and irritability (fibrillations), help separate neurogenic mimics, and support the diagnosis when biopsy is deferred. It is less specific than biopsy and less helpful for biopsy targeting than MRI.
- Pulmonary screening for ILD: Many myositis subtypes, especially antisynthetase and MDA5-associated disease, carry significant ILD risk; early detection (HRCT and PFTs) changes management (aggressive immunosuppression) and prognosis. In this case HRCT was negative, appropriately excluding ILD at presentation.
- Cardiac evaluation: Subclinical myocardial involvement is common and may be life‑threatening. Screening with ECG and troponin/BNP identifies those who need echo and, if findings are equivocal or myocarditis suspected, cardiac MRI. This patient had elevated hs‑troponin and markedly elevated pro‑BNP and underwent echo and cardiac MRI; MRI showed transient myocardial edema without late gadolinium enhancement and subsequent normalization — illustrating why cardiac imaging is high yield.

Putting it together in practice (how to apply to a patient like the case)
1. Obtain baseline CK, AST/ALT, ESR/CRP and a comprehensive myositis antibody panel early. A positive antibody can obviate immediate biopsy in some contexts and direct extramuscular screening.
2. Do thigh MRI to localize active disease and to plan the biopsy site.
3. Perform muscle biopsy when noninvasive studies are nondiagnostic or when a tissue diagnosis will change management — especially if serologies are negative or if necrotizing myopathy is suspected. Biopsy is the diagnostic anchor in IMNM, as seen here.
4. Screen for ILD (HRCT ± PFTs) and assess cardiac involvement (ECG, troponin/BNP, echo; cardiac MRI if concern persists). These tests determine monitoring frequency and urgency of immunosuppression.
5. Use EMG as an adjunct when diagnostic uncertainty remains or to evaluate difficult cases or when biopsy is not feasible.

Bottom line
- Serology, MRI, and biopsy form the core diagnostic triad: antibodies for disease classification, MRI to detect active inflammation and guide biopsy, and biopsy to confirm histopathologic subtype. Concurrent pulmonary and cardiac screening are required because extramuscular involvement is common and alters therapy and prognosis — the case illustrates this approach: negative serologies but diagnostic MRI changes and biopsy-proven IMNM, with dedicated cardiac imaging prompted by abnormal troponin/BNP and clinical concern.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Shortly after semaglutide exposure, the patient had GI losses, AKI, and a modest CK elevation that later rose significantly over months. When evaluating an elevated CK in this setting, what alternative etiologies should be systematically excluded before attributing to IMNM (e.g., statins or red yeast rice, hypothyroidism, exertional injury, infections, toxins, metabolic myopathies, connective tissue diseases)?

Short answer
Before labeling a CK rise as immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM), systematically exclude more common and reversible causes: drugs/supplements (especially statins and red‑yeast rice), endocrine disease (hypothyroidism), exertional or ischemic muscle injury, infectious and toxic causes, metabolic/genetic myopathies, connective‑tissue and inflammatory mimics, and electrolyte/renal contributors. In this case many of those were actively considered and excluded (no statin/red‑yeast intake, normal TSH, no antecedent excessive exertion, supportive MRI/biopsy for IMNM), which strengthens the attribution to a drug‑triggered autoimmune myopathy after semaglutide.

Clinical approach and rationale (what to exclude and how)
1) Medication and supplement toxicity
- Why: Statins are the classic trigger for HMGCR‑associated IMNM; other agents and supplements (red‑yeast rice, some antivirals, immune checkpoint inhibitors, illicit drugs) can cause toxic myopathy or trigger autoimmune myositis.
- How: Thorough medication/supplement history (prescription, over‑the‑counter, herbal). Review timing of exposure relative to CK rise.
- Case: Patient denied prior statin or red‑yeast rice use.

2) Acute exertional, traumatic, ischemic or postictal rhabdomyolysis
- Why: Intense or prolonged exercise, crush injury, seizures, prolonged immobility or ischemia produce large CK elevations that can persist transiently.
- How: History of exertion, seizures, trauma; exam for focal tenderness/compartment signs; urine myoglobin; rapid time course of CK rise/fall.
- Case: No recent excessive activity or trauma; initial modest CK (337 U/L) with later progressive rise over months—less consistent with isolated exertional rhabdo.

3) Endocrine causes (hypothyroidism, adrenal disease)
- Why: Hypothyroidism commonly causes mild–moderate CK elevations and myalgias; adrenal disease and electrolyte disturbances can also contribute.
- How: Check TSH, free T4, cortisol as indicated.
- Case: TSH was normal.

4) Infectious myositis and postinfectious rhabdomyolysis
- Why: Viral (influenza, HIV, EBV, SARS‑CoV‑2), bacterial sepsis or parasitic infections can elevate CK.
- How: Review prodrome/fever, targeted serologies/PCR, cultures if systemic infection suspected.
- Case: No prodrome; later pseudomonal UTI occurred after immunosuppression and CK was normal then.

5) Toxins and substances of abuse
- Why: Alcohol, cocaine, amphetamines, statin interactions, certain antibiotics (e.g., some macrolides with statins), and other toxins can provoke rhabdo.
- How: Social history, toxicology testing as indicated, drug‑interaction review.
- Case: Patient denied alcohol/illicit drug use.

6) Metabolic and hereditary myopathies
- Why: Glycogen storage diseases, lipid metabolism disorders, mitochondrial myopathies can present with chronic or exercise‑induced CK elevations.
- How: Family history, pattern of symptoms, exercise testing, specialized enzymatic/genetic testing.
- Case: No family history or lifelong pattern suggesting inherited myopathy.

7) Connective‑tissue and inflammatory myopathies (dermatomyositis, polymyositis, IMNM)
- Why: Autoimmune myopathies cause persistent proximal weakness and CK elevation; dermatomyositis has skin findings; IMNM has necrosis with relatively sparse lymphocytes.
- How: Autoantibody panels (myositis panel including anti‑SRP and anti‑HMGCR), ANA, inflammatory markers, EMG, MRI for muscle edema, and—definitive—muscle biopsy.
- Case: Myositis panel negative for SRP/HMGCR (seronegative IMNM possible); MRI showed symmetric proximal muscle edema; biopsy showed necrosis, macrophages, membrane‑attack complex → diagnostic for IMNM.

8) Electrolyte abnormalities and renal impairment
- Why: Hypokalemia/hypophosphatemia can cause muscle injury; AKI can follow or exacerbate rhabdomyolysis.
- How: Check basic metabolic panel, phosphate, potassium, creatinine; follow CK trend with fluid resuscitation.
- Case: She had AKI early with hypovolemia from vomiting, but initial CK was only modest and CK continued to rise over months despite recovery—argues against isolated AKI‑related rhabdo.

Diagnostics to order as you exclude alternatives
- Detailed medication/supplement review
- TSH, free T4 (endocrine)
- Viral serologies/PCR if infectious suspicion
- Toxicology screen if indicated
- Electrolytes, phosphate, renal function, urine myoglobin
- CK trend, AST/ALT interpretation (distinguish hepatic vs muscle source)
- Autoantibodies/myositis panel, ANA, connective‑tissue serologies
- Muscle MRI and EMG to localize active inflammation
- Muscle biopsy when noninvasive testing is inconclusive or when autoimmune myopathy is suspected (biopsy is diagnostic for IMNM)

Putting it together for this case
- The time course (single semaglutide dose → GI losses/AKI → modest CK, then progressive proximal weakness and a 12‑fold CK rise over 4 months), absence of statin or supplement exposure, normal TSH, lack of exertional/toxic history, MRI pattern, and muscle biopsy showing necrosis with macrophages and membrane‑attack complex argue against isolated toxic rhabdomyolysis or endocrine/metabolic causes and support IMNM likely triggered by semaglutide. Negative myositis serologies do not exclude IMNM because ~1/3 of IMNM patients are seronegative; biopsy remains the diagnostic standard when clinical ambiguity exists.

Clinical pearl
Always exclude common, reversible causes first (drugs/supplements, hypothyroidism, exertional or toxic rhabdomyolysis, infections, metabolic or electrolyte disturbances) with targeted history, labs and imaging; if uncertainty persists or if clinical features suggest autoimmune myopathy, obtain autoantibodies and a muscle biopsy—biopsy findings are decisive for IMNM.

### [Clinical Pearls] This patient had a mildly elevated high-sensitivity troponin and markedly elevated proBNP with cardiac MRI suggesting myocardial edema but no late gadolinium enhancement. How do you differentiate stress-induced cardiomyopathy from inflammatory myocarditis in inflammatory myopathy patients, and what are the management implications?

Direct answer
- Distinguishing stress‑induced (takotsubo) cardiomyopathy from inflammatory myocarditis rests on pattern recognition (clinical trigger, wall‑motion abnormality distribution, biomarker profile), cardiac MRI tissue characterization (Lake Louise criteria and mapping), and, when needed for management, endomyocardial biopsy. The distinction matters because myocarditis from an autoimmune myopathy may prompt immunosuppression and activity restriction, whereas takotsubo is managed supportively and immunosuppression is not indicated.

Clinical reasoning applied to this case
- Clinical context and biomarkers: this patient had a modest high‑sensitivity troponin elevation (62 pg/mL) with a markedly elevated pro‑BNP (4,358 pg/mL). That pattern — relatively small troponin rise with large natriuretic peptide elevation — is classically seen in stress‑induced cardiomyopathy but can also occur in myocarditis. The presence of an acute systemic illness and ICU course around the time of semaglutide exposure provides a plausible stressor for takotsubo.
- Echocardiography/CMR pattern: echocardiogram showed preserved LVEF (65–70%) with mild RV pressure elevation; cardiac MRI showed regional mild hypokinesis with myocardial edema but no definite late gadolinium enhancement (LGE). On CMR, myocarditis is usually diagnosed when edema (T2) is accompanied by nonischemic LGE (T1/ECV abnormalities) per updated Lake Louise criteria; absence of LGE makes definitive myocarditis less certain, especially if mapping is not diagnostic. Takotsubo commonly shows myocardial edema without LGE and regional wall‑motion abnormalities that may not fit a single coronary territory.
- Natural history in this patient: rapid clinical and imaging normalization on follow‑up (repeat CMR and echocardiogram normalized) favors stress‑induced cardiomyopathy or a transient, mild inflammatory process rather than fulminant autoimmune myocarditis, which more often shows persistent LGE or progressive dysfunction and would frequently warrant ongoing cardiac‑directed immunosuppression.

Diagnostic approach in inflammatory myopathy patients
- Start with noninvasive testing: ECG, serial troponin, pro‑BNP, transthoracic echo. Obtain CMR with T2 mapping and T1/ECV mapping and LGE — this best distinguishes edema versus necrosis/fibrosis. In myocarditis both edema and LGE (nonischemic pattern) are expected; isolated edema without LGE is equivocal.
- Consider PET if CMR equivocal and high suspicion for active inflammation.
- Reserve endomyocardial biopsy for: hemodynamic compromise, rapidly progressive dysfunction, life‑threatening arrhythmia, or when biopsy results would change management (e.g., to justify immunosuppression or exclude alternative diagnoses). Inflammatory myopathy patients with clinical heart failure or high pretest probability of autoimmune myocarditis are more likely to benefit from early biopsy.
- Correlate with systemic disease activity: active IMNM (very high CK, biopsy evidence of muscle inflammation) increases pretest probability of true myocarditis.

Management implications
- Stress‑induced cardiomyopathy: supportive care — treat heart‑failure physiology (diuretics, afterload reduction as tolerated), monitor for arrhythmias and thromboembolism if apical akinesis, treat precipitants, and expect recovery over days to weeks. Immunosuppression is not indicated.
- Autoimmune/inflammatory myocarditis: in addition to standard heart‑failure and arrhythmia management, consider immunosuppression when myocarditis is proven and systemic autoimmune disease activity is present — steroids ± steroid‑sparing agents or IVIG depending on the pathogenetic context. Close cardiology/rheumatology coordination is required. Activity restriction and serial imaging/biomarker surveillance are important.
- In practice for this patient: given modest troponin, marked proBNP, CMR showing edema without LGE, preserved EF, and rapid clinical/imaging recovery, the working diagnosis of stress‑induced cardiomyopathy or a mild, transient myocardial inflammation was reasonable. Conservative cardiac management plus close follow‑up was appropriate; empiric escalation to cardiac immunosuppression was not required. If the patient had ongoing dysfunction, arrhythmias, persistent troponin rise, or CMR with LGE, endomyocardial biopsy and targeted immunosuppression would be indicated.

Practical recommendations (teaching points)
- Use CMR (edema + LGE or abnormal mapping) to distinguish myocarditis from takotsubo when possible; absence of LGE reduces sensitivity for myocarditis but does not entirely exclude early disease.
- Let clinical severity drive invasive testing — biopsy when results will change therapy.
- In inflammatory myopathy patients maintain a low threshold to evaluate cardiac symptoms, but avoid reflexive cardiac immunosuppression unless there is convincing evidence of myocardial inflammation or biopsy confirmation.
- Follow up with serial ECGs, troponin, BNP, and repeat echo/CMR to document recovery or progression; persistent abnormalities should prompt biopsy and consideration of immunosuppressive therapy.

In this case, the constellation of mild troponin rise, very elevated proBNP, myocardial edema without LGE on CMR, preserved LVEF, and normalization on follow‑up favors stress‑induced cardiomyopathy or transient myocarditis that resolved — supporting supportive cardiac care and close surveillance rather than immediate cardiac‑directed immunosuppression.

### [Morning Report] Using the WHO–Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) causality framework, what elements in this case support a 'probable' causal association between semaglutide and IMNM, and why would a rechallenge be considered unethical?

Direct answer: Using the WHO–Uppsala Monitoring Centre causality framework, this case meets the key elements for a "probable/likely" causal assignment because (1) there is a plausible and reasonably specific temporal relationship between semaglutide exposure and onset/progression of disease; (2) objective diagnostic evidence links the clinical syndrome to an immune-mediated process; (3) alternative causes were investigated and are unlikely; and (4) there was a clinically plausible response after withdrawal and immunotherapy (dechallenge). A deliberate rechallenge would be unethical because it risks provoking a recurrent, potentially more severe immune-mediated myopathy with life‑threatening complications when safer alternatives and diagnostic criteria already support the association.

Clinical reasoning tied to the case

1) Time relationship
- Semaglutide was given once; within hours the patient developed severe gastrointestinal intolerance and hypotension leading to hospitalization. Over the next 4 months she developed progressive proximal weakness and a 12‑fold rise in CK (peak 3992 U/L) that was not present prior to the drug exposure. A reasonable latency from exposure to an autoimmune myopathy is recognized; the sequence here (single exposure → acute systemic reaction → subacute progressive myopathy) is temporally coherent with a drug‑triggered immune event.

2) Objective evidence of immune-mediated disease
- Muscle biopsy demonstrated necrotic and regenerating fibers, myophagocytosis with CD68+ macrophages, membrane attack complex deposition, rare CD3+ cells and perivascular inflammation — histology diagnostic of immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). Imaging (MRI) and markedly elevated CK corroborate active myonecrosis. The disease was immunotherapy‑responsive: high‑dose steroids + IVIG produced marked clinical and biochemical improvement and sustained remission off immunosuppression.

3) Exclusion of alternative explanations
- Extensive serologic myositis panel was negative for SRP and HMGCR (and other myositis antibodies); there was no statin exposure, no other implicated supplements, no clear connective‑tissue disease, and prior transaminase elevations were shown on liver biopsy to be longstanding NAFLD rather than muscle breakdown. Although pre‑existing smouldering disease cannot be absolutely excluded (no pre‑drug CK available), the abrupt clinical decline after semaglutide and subsequent recovery after withdrawal and immunotherapy make alternative causes less likely.

4) Response to withdrawal (dechallenge)
- The patient improved substantially after cessation of semaglutide combined with immunosuppression; CK normalized and functional status returned to baseline, allowing cessation of prednisone and no recurrence for many months while the drug was avoided. WHO‑UMC places weight on a reasonable positive dechallenge when assessing causality.

Why this fits WHO‑UMC "probable/likely" rather than "certain"
- A "certain" classification usually requires re‑exposure (rechallenge) or incontrovertible evidence that excludes all other plausible causes. Because rechallenge was not performed and idiopathic or pre‑existing IMNM cannot be formally excluded, "probable/likely" is the most appropriate category.

Why rechallenge would be unethical in this patient
- IMNM is an immune‑mediated disease that caused severe proximal weakness, dysphagia, markedly elevated CK, and possible myocardial involvement in this patient. Re‑exposure to the suspected trigger risks:
  - Rapid recurrence of myonecrosis, potentially more fulminant than the initial episode.
  - Life‑threatening complications (airway compromise from dysphagia, respiratory failure from diaphragmatic involvement, severe rhabdomyolysis, myocarditis).
  - Need for intensive immunosuppression or prolonged therapies with attendant morbidity.
- Ethical principles (nonmaleficence) and standard pharmacovigilance practice do not support intentionally re‑exposing a patient to a drug suspected of causing a severe immune adverse event when (a) alternative therapies exist, (b) objective diagnostic evidence supports the diagnosis, and (c) a positive dechallenge already occurred. Thus, the risks of purposeful rechallenge outweigh any scientific gain in this case.

Takeaway for learners
- WHO‑UMC "probable/likely" relies on timing, objective evidence, exclusion of alternatives, and positive dechallenge. In immune‑mediated toxicities that are severe, a confirmatory rechallenge is not required and is ethically contraindicated — clinical judgment and patient safety trump pursuit of absolute certainty.

### [Pathophysiology] Contrast the expected CK trajectory and clinical course after stopping the offending drug in toxic rhabdomyolysis versus autoimmune IMNM, and explain how the CK time course in this case supports an immune-mediated process.

Teaching point — key distinction in temporal behavior of CK and clinical course after stopping a putative offending drug:

- Drug/toxin-induced rhabdomyolysis (direct toxic myocyte injury)
  - Pathophysiology: direct myofiber necrosis from a toxic or metabolic insult (dehydration, ischemia, statin toxicity in some cases), without a sustained adaptive immune driver.
  - Expected CK trajectory after withdrawal and supportive care (hydration, correction of precipitant): rapid fall over days to a few weeks. The CK typically peaks early and declines steadily once the offending exposure is removed and perfusion/renal function are restored. Clinical weakness and myalgias usually improve in parallel.
  - Management: supportive care; immunosuppression is not required.

- Immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM)
  - Pathophysiology: an auto‑immune process (often anti‑HMGCR or anti‑SRP positive but can be seronegative) with ongoing immune‑mediated myofiber necrosis, complement activation (membrane attack complex), and macrophage myophagocytosis that continues despite removal of any triggering drug.
  - Expected CK trajectory after stopping a suspected trigger: CK does not promptly normalize and may remain elevated or even increase over weeks to months because the immune process becomes self‑sustaining. Improvement in CK and strength usually requires directed immunotherapy (corticosteroids, IVIG, other immunosuppressants), and normalization may take weeks to months after effective immunosuppression.
  - Management: immunosuppression (often combination therapy) with longer courses typically required.

How the CK time course in this case supports an immune‑mediated process
- Timeline from the case: the patient had an acute intolerance to a single semaglutide dose with modest CK (337 U/L) in the initial admission, then over the subsequent 4 months developed progressive proximal weakness and a marked rise in CK to 3992 U/L — a ~12‑fold increase from baseline. Crucially, this large CK rise and clinical deterioration occurred while the patient had been off semaglutide for months.
- Interpretation: a direct toxic rhabdomyolysis would be expected to peak acutely and then improve within days–weeks after drug cessation and volume resuscitation (as in the tirzepatide case cited, where CK fell rapidly without immunosuppression). Instead, this patient’s CK rose progressively after drug withdrawal, consistent with a sustained immune process rather than a resolving toxin effect.
- Corroborating evidence: muscle biopsy showed dispersed necrotic/regenerating fibers, macrophage myophagocytosis, and membrane‑attack‑complex deposition — histologic features characteristic of IMNM. The marked improvement in CK over 2 weeks after high‑dose steroids and IVIG, and durable normal CK off immunosuppression thereafter, further supports an immune‑mediated mechanism that responded to immunotherapy rather than simple removal of a toxin.

Bottom line: toxic rhabdomyolysis → early CK peak with rapid decline after stopping the drug/supportive care; IMNM → persistent or progressive CK elevation despite drug withdrawal and clinical improvement only after immunosuppression. The delayed and progressive CK rise months after a single semaglutide dose, the biopsy findings, and the prompt CK fall with steroids+IVIG together argue for an immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy in this patient rather than simple drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis.
