Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Morning Report] In suspected inflammatory myopathy presenting with progressive proximal weakness and elevated CK, which diagnostic studies are highest-yield to establish diagnosis and assess extent of involvement (e.g., myositis-specific antibodies, thigh MRI for edema, EMG, muscle biopsy, pulmonary screening for ILD, echocardiography/cardiac MRI), and why?

Direct answer
- Highest-yield studies to establish the diagnosis of an inflammatory myopathy and define extent are: (1) a myositis autoantibody panel (myositis-specific and -associated antibodies), (2) MRI of the thighs to document muscle edema and guide biopsy, (3) muscle biopsy for definitive histopathology, and (4) targeted organ screening for extramuscular involvement — specifically pulmonary evaluation (high-resolution chest CT and PFTs if ILD suspected) and cardiac evaluation (ECG, troponin/NT-proBNP, transthoracic echo ± cardiac MRI). EMG is useful adjunctive testing but is lower yield than MRI/biopsy for diagnosis and extent assessment.

Why — clinical reasoning and pathophysiology
- Myositis autoantibodies: Certain antibodies (eg, anti-HMGCR, anti-SRP, Jo-1 and other antisynthetase antibodies, MDA5, TIF1-γ, NXP2) point to specific clinicopathologic subtypes, predict associations (statin exposure, ILD, cancer risk, prognosis) and influence treatment and monitoring. A positive antibody can make the diagnosis more secure and orient extramuscular surveillance. However, ~20–30% (and higher in IMNM subsets) can be seronegative, so a negative panel does not exclude disease — as demonstrated in this case (negative SRP and HMGCR and a broad myositis panel yet biopsy-proven IMNM).
- Thigh MRI: MRI is highly sensitive for active inflammation (T2/STIR or fat-suppressed sequences show muscle edema), maps distribution (proximal symmetric vs focal), detects chronic fatty replacement, and importantly guides the biopsy to an affected muscle (avoiding end-stage fatty tissue). In this case MRI showed symmetric proximal thigh edema that corroborated the clinical pattern and guided biopsy.
- Muscle biopsy: Histopathology is the diagnostic gold standard when clinical/imaging/serology are inconclusive. Patterns (necrotic fibers with macrophage predominance and membrane attack complex deposition in IMNM; perifascicular atrophy in dermatomyositis; endomysial T-cell invasion in polymyositis) establish the subtype and inform prognosis and therapy. In the reported patient, biopsy demonstrated dispersed necrotic/regenerating fibers, CD68+ myophagocytosis and membrane attack complex — classic for IMNM and decisive for management despite negative serologies.
- EMG: Electrodiagnostic testing can document myopathic motor-unit changes and irritability (fibrillations), help separate neurogenic mimics, and support the diagnosis when biopsy is deferred. It is less specific than biopsy and less helpful for biopsy targeting than MRI.
- Pulmonary screening for ILD: Many myositis subtypes, especially antisynthetase and MDA5-associated disease, carry significant ILD risk; early detection (HRCT and PFTs) changes management (aggressive immunosuppression) and prognosis. In this case HRCT was negative, appropriately excluding ILD at presentation.
- Cardiac evaluation: Subclinical myocardial involvement is common and may be life‑threatening. Screening with ECG and troponin/BNP identifies those who need echo and, if findings are equivocal or myocarditis suspected, cardiac MRI. This patient had elevated hs‑troponin and markedly elevated pro‑BNP and underwent echo and cardiac MRI; MRI showed transient myocardial edema without late gadolinium enhancement and subsequent normalization — illustrating why cardiac imaging is high yield.

Putting it together in practice (how to apply to a patient like the case)
1. Obtain baseline CK, AST/ALT, ESR/CRP and a comprehensive myositis antibody panel early. A positive antibody can obviate immediate biopsy in some contexts and direct extramuscular screening.
2. Do thigh MRI to localize active disease and to plan the biopsy site.
3. Perform muscle biopsy when noninvasive studies are nondiagnostic or when a tissue diagnosis will change management — especially if serologies are negative or if necrotizing myopathy is suspected. Biopsy is the diagnostic anchor in IMNM, as seen here.
4. Screen for ILD (HRCT ± PFTs) and assess cardiac involvement (ECG, troponin/BNP, echo; cardiac MRI if concern persists). These tests determine monitoring frequency and urgency of immunosuppression.
5. Use EMG as an adjunct when diagnostic uncertainty remains or to evaluate difficult cases or when biopsy is not feasible.

Bottom line
- Serology, MRI, and biopsy form the core diagnostic triad: antibodies for disease classification, MRI to detect active inflammation and guide biopsy, and biopsy to confirm histopathologic subtype. Concurrent pulmonary and cardiac screening are required because extramuscular involvement is common and alters therapy and prognosis — the case illustrates this approach: negative serologies but diagnostic MRI changes and biopsy-proven IMNM, with dedicated cardiac imaging prompted by abnormal troponin/BNP and clinical concern.
