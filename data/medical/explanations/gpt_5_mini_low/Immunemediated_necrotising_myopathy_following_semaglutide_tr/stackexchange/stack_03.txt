Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Clinical Pearls] In patients with both elevated CK and elevated transaminases, what clinical and laboratory approach helps differentiate muscle-derived AST/ALT elevations from hepatic sources such as nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (e.g., use of CK, GGT, bilirubin, INR, imaging, and trend discordance)?

Direct answer
When both CK and transaminases (AST, ALT) are elevated, use the pattern and additional liver- vs muscle‑specific tests and imaging, plus serial trends, to distinguish muscle-derived transaminase release from bona fide liver injury. The key discriminators are: (1) CK as the most specific marker of muscle injury and its temporal correlation with AST/ALT; (2) cholestatic and synthetic liver tests (GGT, ALP, bilirubin, INR, albumin, platelets) that point to hepatic disease when abnormal; (3) AST-versus-ALT pattern and AST:ALT relationship to CK; and (4) imaging or biopsy when chronic liver disease is suspected.

Practical clinical approach (stepwise)
1. Confirm the CK level and clinical context
- A markedly elevated CK (hundreds to many thousands U/L) with proximal weakness, myalgias, or rhabdomyolysis strongly suggests muscle as the source. In muscle injury AST tends to rise proportionally with CK; ALT may rise less.
- In the presented case CK rose to ~4,000 U/L and tracked with muscle weakness and biopsy‑proven IMNM, supporting a muscle source for much of the transaminase elevation.

2. Check liver‑specific enzymes that help localize injury
- GGT and alkaline phosphatase (ALP): elevations support hepatic (or biliary) origin. Normal GGT/ALP argues against a primary hepatocellular process.
- Bilirubin, INR, albumin, platelets: abnormalities indicate impaired hepatic synthetic function or advanced liver disease (not expected from isolated muscle injury).
- In the case, GGT/ALP and synthetic markers were not notably deranged and bilirubin/INR were normal historically, arguing against acute hepatic failure as the cause of the transaminase rise.

3. Assess AST:ALT pattern and relationship to CK
- Muscle injury often raises AST more than ALT (AST relatively higher), and AST frequently parallels CK. An AST that falls as CK falls supports muscle origin.
- If ALT > AST and transaminases remain elevated after CK normalizes, a hepatic source is more likely.
- In this patient she had baseline mild ALT>AST from NAFLD before semaglutide; during the acute myopathy both AST and ALT rose but then transaminases persisted mildly after CK normalized — supporting chronic hepatic (NAFLD) contribution plus an acute muscle component.

4. Use additional muscle biomarkers if needed
- Aldolase, LDH, and myoglobin may support muscle injury but are less specific than CK.
- Urine myoglobin may be present in rhabdomyolysis.

5. Serial measurements and trend discordance
- Temporal concordance: if AST/ALT track closely with CK (rise and fall together), muscle is likely the dominant source.
- Discordance: if transaminases remain elevated or worsen while CK normalizes, think liver disease (e.g., NAFLD, viral, autoimmune, drug‑induced).
- The case illustrates this principle: CK normalized with immunosuppression while mild transaminase elevation persisted consistent with her preexisting NAFLD.

6. Imaging and histology when indicated
- Liver ultrasound (or MRI) to evaluate steatosis, biliary obstruction, focal lesions. In the case, ultrasound/MRI showed steatotic hepatomegaly consistent with NAFLD.
- If noninvasive workup is inconclusive or chronic disease needs staging, consider liver biopsy (as was done and showed steatohepatitis).
- Muscle MRI and muscle biopsy are indicated when myopathy is suspected (in the case MRI showed muscle edema and biopsy confirmed IMNM).

Putting it together — an algorithm you can use at the bedside
- Step 1: Assess symptoms: myalgias, weakness, dark urine → think muscle; jaundice, pruritus, RUQ pain → think liver.
- Step 2: Measure CK, AST, ALT, GGT, ALP, bilirubin, INR, albumin, platelets.
- Step 3: Compare kinetics: do AST/ALT track with CK? If yes → muscle. If no → pursue liver evaluation.
- Step 4: If liver tests (GGT/ALP/bilirubin/INR) abnormal or imaging suggests liver disease → hepatology workup (viral serologies, autoimmune markers, imaging, ± biopsy).
- Step 5: If muscle suspected → muscle enzymes, EMG/MRI, neuromuscular referral and muscle biopsy as indicated.

Clinical pearl from the case
This patient had both chronic, biopsy‑proven NAFLD (explaining mild baseline transaminase elevations) and a superimposed IMNM with large CK elevations and AST/ALT rises. The rapid improvement and normalization of CK after immunotherapy with persistent mild transaminase elevation thereafter illustrate the diagnostic value of trend concordance: muscle-derived AST/ALT fell with CK, while the residual ALT/AST that persisted reflected her underlying fatty liver. Use CK trends plus GGT/ALP and synthetic markers, and image/biopsy when uncertainty remains, to localize the source reliably.
