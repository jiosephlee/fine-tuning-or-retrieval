Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Clinical Pearls] Anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP antibodies are strongly associated with IMNM, yet up to one-third of patients are seronegative. In a seronegative patient with compatible clinical features and biopsy findings, what is the diagnostic significance and how should this influence confidence in the diagnosis and subsequent management?

Short answer
Absence of anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP does not exclude immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). When the clinical syndrome (subacute severe proximal weakness, markedly elevated CK, MRI muscle edema) and a muscle biopsy show the characteristic necrosis with macrophage‑predominant inflammation and membrane‑attack‑complex deposition, you can be confident in the diagnosis and should treat as IMNM while also searching for alternative triggers and monitoring for relapse.

Clinical reasoning and pathophysiology
- Anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP are strongly associated with IMNM and often guide diagnosis, prognosis, and sometimes therapy. However, ~25–33% of patients with clinicopathologic IMNM are seronegative — implying either undiscovered autoantibodies or seronegative autoimmune biology. Seronegativity therefore lowers the “helpfulness” of serology but not the diagnostic weight of compatible clinical and histopathologic findings.
- IMNM is defined pathologically by scattered necrotic and regenerating fibers, myophagocytosis (CD68+ macrophages), sparse lymphocytes, and often complement (C5b‑9/MAC) deposition on muscle fibres — findings that reflect antibody‑ and complement‑mediated myofibre injury even when the circulating autoantibody is not detected.
- In the presented case the combination of progressive proximal weakness, very high CK, MRI showing proximal muscle edema, and muscle biopsy demonstrating necrotic/regenerating fibres, macrophage infiltration, myophagocytosis and MAC staining is diagnostic of IMNM despite negative myositis panel.

Diagnostic approach in seronegative patients
- Treat the biopsy and clinical phenotype as the gold standard when serology is negative. A classic clinicopathologic picture (as in this case) is sufficient for diagnosis.
- Consider repeat or alternative serologic testing at a reference lab or using different assay platforms (ELISA, immunoprecipitation) because low‑titer or assay‑dependent antibodies can be missed.
- Continue routine evaluation for secondary causes/triggers: recent drugs (statins, now GLP‑1 RA in this case), malignancy screening as indicated, infections, and other connective‑tissue disease features.
- Evaluate for extramuscular involvement (cardiac MRI or troponin, pulmonary imaging) because IMNM can involve heart/lungs even if seronegative.

Management implications
- Management should be guided by disease severity and biopsy confirmation, not seronegativity alone. Severe presentations (marked weakness, dysphagia, very high CK, respiratory or bulbar involvement) warrant prompt immunotherapy — high‑dose corticosteroids, frequently combined early with IVIG and/or a steroid‑sparing agent.
- In suspected drug‑triggered cases (this patient’s clear temporal relationship to semaglutide and recovery after withdrawal), remove the offending agent and treat; drug‑associated IMNM may respond to shorter immunosuppression courses than idiopathic, antibody‑positive IMNM, but close follow‑up is essential.
- Monitor clinical strength, functional status, and CK serially. If improvement is sustained off therapy (as here, normal CK and recovery at 10 months), prolonged aggressive immunosuppression may not be necessary. If relapse or refractory disease occurs, escalate therapy (repeat IVIG, rituximab, mycophenolate, methotrexate, etc.) and reconsider alternative diagnoses or occult triggers.
- Maintain vigilance for cardiac involvement (this patient had transient troponin/proBNP and MRI changes) and manage accordingly.

Bottom line
In a patient with the classic clinical syndrome and supportive muscle biopsy, seronegativity reduces neither the likelihood of true IMNM nor the need for prompt treatment. Use serology as complementary information, attempt repeat/specialized testing if helpful, withdraw any suspect offending drug, initiate appropriate immunotherapy based on severity, and follow closely for relapse or refractory disease. The presented case is a paradigmatic example: biopsy‑proven IMNM with negative SRP/HMGCR antibodies that responded to corticosteroids + IVIG and withdrawal of semaglutide.
