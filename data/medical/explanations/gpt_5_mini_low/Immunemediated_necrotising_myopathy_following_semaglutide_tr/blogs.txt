Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# GLP‑1 Agents and Muscle Injury: IMNM vs Toxic Rhabdo at the Bedside

What makes this case interesting: a woman in her 60s developed a progressive, severe proximal myopathy with dysphagia and very high CK over months after a single 0.25 mg dose of semaglutide. Muscle biopsy showed necrotic and regenerating fibers with macrophage/myophagocytosis, membrane attack complex deposition and scant T cells — features consistent with immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). She was seronegative for the usual IMNM antibodies, improved with pulse steroids and IVIG, and remained well off immunosuppression after avoiding GLP‑1 therapy. This contrasts with recently published reports of GLP‑1/GIP agonist–associated rhabdomyolysis where CK spikes massively during active therapy and falls quickly after stopping the drug and hydration.

Why this matters in the ED/wards: GLP‑1 receptor agonists (semaglutide, tirzepatide and others) are now ubiquitous for diabetes and weight loss. When a patient on or recently exposed to a GLP‑1 agonist presents with weakness and CK elevation, clinicians must distinguish two very different processes — an acute toxic rhabdomyolysis that usually resolves with supportive care versus a subacute/ progressive autoimmune necrotising myopathy that may need immunosuppression. Management and prognosis diverge dramatically.

Key clinical differences — how to think through the problem

Tempo and timing
- Toxic rhabdomyolysis: usually acute onset, often during ongoing drug exposure. Symptoms (myalgias, dark urine, weakness) and CK rise are rapid (hours–days). Stopping the drug + aggressive IV fluids typically produces a marked CK decline over days.
- IMNM (drug‑triggered or idiopathic): classically presents with progressively worsening symmetric proximal weakness over weeks–months. CK can be markedly elevated but the rise is slower and sustained even after drug cessation. Dysphagia and dysphonia are more common with IMNM. The semaglutide case developed GI intolerance and AKI within hours of a single dose, but the disabling myopathy evolved over 4 months with a 12‑fold CK increase — a tempo more consistent with IMNM than simple toxic rhabdo.

CK trajectory and systemic features
- Toxic rhabdo: massive CK elevations possible (often tens of thousands). Rapid fall after volume resuscitation and stopping offending agent. Often accompanied by myalgias, cola‑coloured urine, and complications of myoglobinuria/AKI.
- IMNM: CK can be high (thousands) but often remains persistently elevated despite stopping an offending drug. Muscle weakness is prominent and progressive; systemic inflammatory markers may be modestly up. Extramuscular involvement (dysphagia, possible cardiac inflammation) can occur.

Serology and biopsy
- IMNM is associated with autoantibodies (anti‑HMGCR, anti‑SRP) in many cases but up to ~30% are seronegative. A negative myositis panel does not exclude IMNM.
- Muscle biopsy in IMNM: necrotic and regenerating fibers, macrophage-rich myophagocytosis, membrane attack complex deposition on non‑necrotic fibers, sparse lymphocytic infiltrate. These findings contrast with predominantly myofiber disruption and diffuse necrosis without the inflammatory pattern typical of pure toxic rhabdo.
- In the semaglutide case the vastus lateralis biopsy showed dispersed necrotic/regenerating fibers, CD68+ macrophages with myophagocytosis, MAC deposition and rare CD3 cells — classic IMNM histology despite seronegativity.

Response to therapy
- Toxic rhabdo: mainstays are IV fluids, electrolyte management, stopping the offending agent. Immunosuppression is not indicated.
- IMNM: often requires immunosuppression (high‑dose corticosteroids, IVIG, steroid‑sparing agents). Drug‑triggered IMNM can sometimes improve after stopping the trigger plus a short immunosuppressive course, but idiopathic and antibody‑positive cases frequently need prolonged therapy.
- In the presented case, the patient received pulse methylprednisolone and IVIG with clinical and biochemical recovery and was able to taper off steroids and remain well months later.

Practical bedside approach when you see a patient on a GLP‑1 agent with elevated CK and weakness

1. Rapid assessment: check vitals, urine colour, renal function, electrolytes (K+, Ca2+, phosphate), and urine output. Toxic rhabdo carries immediate risk of AKI and electrolyte derangement — manage aggressively.
2. Timecourse and symptom pattern: ask specifically when the drug started, whether symptoms began acutely while on therapy or insidiously after exposure, whether there are progressive proximal weaknesses, dysphagia, or dysphonia (these point toward IMNM).
3. CK trend: serial CKs are informative. A dramatic early spike with quick decline after fluids/support suggests toxic rhabdo. A rising or persistently high CK over weeks despite stopping the drug should raise concern for IMNM.
4. Look for red flags for IMNM: progressive symmetric proximal weakness, dysphagia, respiratory compromise, cardiac symptoms, and MRI showing muscular edema in proximal muscle groups.
5. Early specialty input: involve neurology/rheumatology and consider myositis serologies but don’t wait for results to act if the patient is deteriorating. Muscle MRI and biopsy are diagnostic tools; biopsy is definitive for IMNM histology.
6. Treatment decisions:
   - If toxic rhabdo likely: stop offending meds, aggressive IV fluids, monitor/ treat electrolytes and renal dysfunction.
   - If IMNM likely or biopsy‑proven: immunosuppression (high‑dose steroids ± IVIG); escalate to steroid‑sparing agents per rheumatology guidance.
7. Drug safety counseling: for suspected drug‑triggered IMNM, avoid rechallenge with the same class — in this case clinicians advised the patient to avoid GLP‑1 receptor agonists going forward.

Limitations and uncertainties
- Causality is often probabilistic. The semaglutide case fulfills many criteria for a probable drug‑triggered IMNM (reasonable temporal relationship, lack of alternative explanations, response after withdrawal and immunosuppression) but a definitive causal link cannot be proven without rechallenge (which would be unethical).
- Seronegativity does not exclude IMNM. Up to a third of IMNM patients lack known autoantibodies.
- The biological mechanism by which GLP‑1 receptor agonists might trigger an autoimmune myopathy is unknown; observational vigilance is needed as GLP‑1 use rises.

Clinical pearls
- Don’t assume every elevated CK in a patient with recent GLP‑1 exposure is toxic rhabdomyolysis; consider IMNM when weakness is progressive, proximal, or accompanied by dysphagia/dysphonia and CK remains elevated despite stopping the drug.
- A negative myositis panel does not rule out IMNM — muscle biopsy is the diagnostic gold standard when the clinical picture suggests autoimmune necrotising myopathy.
- Toxic rhabdo tends to have an acute onset and rapid resolution after stopping the offending agent and supportive care; IMNM shows a subacute–chronic progressive course and often requires immunosuppression.
- Early multidisciplinary involvement (rheumatology/neurology) expedites diagnosis and appropriate therapy; delay in treating IMNM can lead to prolonged disability.

Take‑home messages
- Consider two distinct patterns of GLP‑1–associated muscle injury: acute toxic rhabdomyolysis (rapid onset, resolves with stopping drug/fluids) versus immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (subacute progressive weakness, biopsy with necrosis + macrophages/MAC, often needs immunosuppression).
- Use the clinical tempo, CK trajectory, presence of progressive proximal weakness/dysphagia, MRI and biopsy findings, and response to stopping the drug to differentiate them at the bedside.
- If IMNM is suspected, involve specialists early and consider muscle biopsy and prompt immunosuppression; if toxic rhabdo is likely, prioritize fluids and renal protection.
- Be vigilant: with wider GLP‑1 use, rare immune‑mediated complications may become more visible — report suspected cases and discuss risks with patients.

Acknowledgment: the clinical details summarized here are grounded in the published case of probable IMNM following a single semaglutide dose (Lam et al.), contrasted with prior reports of GLP‑1/GIP agonist–associated rhabdomyolysis.



# Seronegative IMNM: Diagnosing the Antibody‑Negative Myopathy

What makes this case interesting
A woman in her 60s developed progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia and a dramatic CK rise to ~4,000 U/L over 4 months after a single dose of semaglutide. Myositis panels (including anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP) were negative, but muscle biopsy showed necrosis, regenerating fibres, membrane attack complex (MAC) deposition, CD68+ myophagocytosis and only rare T cells — the histologic signature of immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). She improved quickly with pulse steroids and IVIG and remained well off immunosuppression after stopping the drug. This case is a useful reminder that an autoimmune necrotising myopathy can be seronegative, may mimic toxic or deconditioning states, and requires a different diagnostic mindset and workup.

Why seronegative IMNM matters
- Approximately one‑third of IMNM patients have no identifiable autoantibody. Relying on serology alone delays diagnosis and treatment.
- IMNM is a clinicopathologic diagnosis: characteristic clinical phenotype + supportive MRI/biopsy + exclusion of mimics.
- Distinguishing IMNM from drug‑toxic myopathy or simple deconditioning is crucial because IMNM often needs immunotherapy (and timely therapy improves outcomes).

A practical diagnostic pathway for suspected seronegative IMNM
1. Start with the clinical phenotype
   - Rapidly progressive proximal weakness (shoulders, hips), difficulties with sit/stand, decline in function over weeks–months.
   - Bulbar signs (dysphagia, dysphonia) and very high CK (>1,000s) favor inflammatory necrotising myopathy over pure sarcopenia or deconditioning.
   - Evaluate for systemic features (rash, ILD, Raynaud, sicca) — absence does not exclude IMNM.

2. First‑line labs and exclusion of common mimics
   - CK, AST/ALT (remember muscle raises transaminases), renal function, electrolytes.
   - TSH (hypothyroidism can mimic myopathy) and cortisol if indicated.
   - Basic autoimmune screen: ANA and myositis panel (including SRP, HMGCR) — but don’t stop if negative.
   - Medications and toxin review: statins, herbal supplements (eg, red yeast rice), recent immunotherapy, or new drugs (note: GLP‑1 RAs are emerging as potential triggers in case reports).
   - Infectious workup as indicated.
   - Consider CK trend: in drug‑toxic rhabdomyolysis CK usually falls after stopping the agent; progressive CK rise despite withdrawal suggests autoimmune process.

3. Electrodiagnostics and imaging — when to use them
   - EMG: useful if available and will often show myopathic motor unit potentials with early recruitment; helps localize and justify biopsy site if MRI is not diagnostic.
   - MRI of muscles (STIR/T2 and T1): look for symmetric proximal muscle edema, especially in quadriceps and pelvic girdle muscles. MRI guides biopsy to active areas and can help distinguish inflammatory myopathy from chronic fatty replacement or disuse atrophy.
   - In this case MRI showed symmetric oedema of proximal vastus lateralis and quadriceps — a pattern consistent with inflammatory necrotising myopathy.

4. Muscle biopsy — the diagnostic anchor (when and where)
   - Indications: progressive weakness with high CK, non‑diagnostic labs/EMG/MRI, or to distinguish IMNM from other myopathies.
   - Timing: biopsy early (don’t wait for serology) if clinical suspicion is high and treatment decisions depend on results.
   - Target active, edematous areas on MRI; avoid end‑stage fatty muscles.
   - Key histopathologic features of IMNM:
     - Scattered necrotic and regenerating fibres (prominent necrosis out of proportion to inflammatory infiltrate).
     - MAC (C5b‑9) deposition on sarcolemma of necrotic and non‑necrotic fibres.
     - Prominent myophagocytosis with macrophages (CD68+).
     - Relative paucity of lymphocytic (CD3+) infiltrate compared with polymyositis.
     - Complement activation and macrophage‑mediated fibre clearance are characteristic.
   - These biopsy features were present in the case and clinched the diagnosis despite negative myositis antibodies.

5. Exclude connective tissue disease and other systemic causes
   - Full rheumatologic review for features of systemic autoimmune disease. Screen for malignancy as clinically indicated (some myopathies associate with occult cancer).
   - Cardiac evaluation: troponin, ECG, echo and consider cardiac MRI if troponin or symptoms suggest myocarditis — IMNM can have cardiac involvement.
   - Review history of NAFLD or transaminitis carefully — muscle disease can raise AST/ALT; baseline liver disease may coexist and confuse interpretation.

6. Deciding on treatment while serology is pending or negative
   - If clinical picture, imaging and biopsy support IMNM, start immunotherapy rather than observing for prolonged time.
   - Typical initial approach for severe IMNM: high‑dose steroids (eg, pulse methylprednisone) plus steroid‑sparing/immunomodulatory therapy (IVIG is effective in many IMNM cases). Tailor to comorbidities and infection risk.
   - In this case pulse steroids + IVIG led to prompt CK normalization and functional recovery, and immunotherapy could be tapered early after clinical and biochemical response — a pattern more consistent with drug‑triggered IMNM than idiopathic disease requiring prolonged therapy.

Coordinating care: practical tips
- Early multidisciplinary collaboration speeds diagnosis: involve neurology (EMG/muscle biopsy), rheumatology (autoimmune workup, immunotherapy planning), and pathology (neuromuscular histopathology).
- Use MRI to guide biopsy — ask radiology to flag the best biopsy target.
- If EMG is delayed, don’t delay biopsy if MRI and clinical picture are convincing.
- Monitor closely for infections if you start high‑dose immunosuppression — this patient developed a pseudomonal UTI while steroid‑treated.
- Discuss potential drug triggers with the patient and advise discontinuation. Rechallenge generally not advised if a severe autoimmune reaction is suspected.

Avoiding common pitfalls
- Don’t assume negative myositis serology equals no autoimmune myopathy. Up to ~30% of IMNM are seronegative.
- Don’t label progressive proximal weakness with high CK as “deconditioning” or “statin intolerance” without targeted evaluation — biopsy can be diagnostic and therapy lifesaving.
- Don’t rely only on CK magnitude: the trend, clinical progression, and distribution of weakness matter.
- Remember muscle can raise AST/ALT — don’t reflexively attribute mild transaminitis solely to liver disease when muscle disease is present.

Clinical pearls
- IMNM histology is distinctive: necrosis + regeneration + MAC deposition + macrophage myophagocytosis with scarce lymphocytes.
- MRI muscle edema in proximal thigh muscles (eg, vastus lateralis) is a helpful noninvasive clue and biopsy guide.
- A brisk clinical and CK response to immunotherapy after drug withdrawal suggests a drug‑triggered autoimmune myopathy rather than self‑limited toxic rhabdomyolysis.
- Seronegative IMNM is a real entity — treat the patient, not the panel.

Key take‑home points
- Consider IMNM in patients with subacute–acute progressive proximal weakness and markedly elevated CK even when myositis antibodies are negative.
- Use MRI to localize active muscle for biopsy; biopsy findings (necrosis, MAC+, CD68+ myophagocytosis, paucity of lymphocytes) make the diagnosis.
- Exclude mimics (thyroid disease, infections, toxins/drugs, malignancy, CTD) but don’t delay biopsy and treatment when clinical suspicion is high.
- Coordinate early between neurology, rheumatology and pathology; start immunotherapy promptly for severe disease — steroids + IVIG are effective choices.
- Report possible drug triggers and counsel patients to avoid rechallenge if drug‑related IMNM is suspected.

This case reminds us: negative antibodies don’t rule out an aggressive autoimmune myopathy. Keep the diagnosis in mind, use MRI and biopsy smartly, and act early — the muscle (and the patient’s function) depends on it.



# Steroids, IVIG, and the Exit Strategy: Managing Severe IMNM Without Lifelong Immunosuppression

What makes this case interesting? A woman in her 60s developed a fulminant immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) with dysphagia, dysphonia, and CK into the thousands after a single dose of semaglutide. Muscle biopsy showed necrosis with macrophage/myophagocytic inflammation and membrane attack complex deposition — classic for IMNM — yet she improved with a short course of high‑dose methylprednisolone and one admission course of IVIG and was off immunosuppression and back to baseline at 10 months. That clinical course looks more like a drug‑triggered IMNM with a finite immunologic insult than the protracted, antibody‑positive IMNM syndromes we usually fear. This raises a practical question EDs, hospitalists, rheumatologists and neurologists increasingly face: when can you be aggressive up front, and when can you plan for a short, finite course and early exit from chronic immunosuppression?

Below I summarize a pragmatic management pathway inspired by this case — when to pulse, when to add IVIG early, how to monitor, how to manage infection/VTE/swallow/respiratory risk, and reasonable criteria to consider a rapid taper and stopping therapy when a drug trigger is suspected.

## Rapid assessment on presentation — the priorities
- Establish the phenotype: rapidly progressive proximal weakness, bulbar symptoms (dysphagia/dysphonia), respiratory compromise and CK elevation point toward a myopathic process that may be immune‑mediated. IMNM often has marked weakness and high CK; biopsy classically shows necrosis > inflammation.
- Identify red flags that push you to urgent treatment: airway threat or aspiration risk from bulbar weakness; respiratory muscle weakness (vital capacity fall, orthopnea, rising PaCO2); rapidly rising CK with renal risk; inability to maintain nutrition or secretions.
- Do targeted baseline tests immediately: CK, CMP (including renal function and transaminases), TSH, troponin/BNP if concern for myocarditis, CBC, CRP/ESR, urinalysis (infection), and a myositis antibody panel (SRP, HMGCR, myositis panel) — but remember seronegative IMNM is common.
- Imaging/diagnostics: MRI muscle is helpful to map inflammation/edema; early muscle biopsy confirms IMNM features and can guide prognosis. Bedside swallow assessment and measures of respiratory strength (vital capacity, negative inspiratory force) are essential if bulbar/respiratory symptoms present.

In the reported case the patient had progressive proximal weakness with dysphagia, CK rising to ~4,000, MRI showing proximal muscle edema, and biopsy consistent with IMNM. She had an early severe phenotype (bulbar involvement) — reason enough to treat promptly.

## When to pulse methylprednisolone
Pulse steroids (eg, methylprednisolone 1 g IV daily for 3–5 days) are indicated for life‑ or function‑threatening inflammatory myopathies: severe bulbar involvement causing aspiration risk, rapid respiratory decline, very high CK with renal threat, or rapidly progressive weakness resulting in loss of mobility. The goal is rapid disease control while other treatments take effect.

In this case: the team gave 1 g IV methylprednisolone daily for 5 days given progressive bulbar symptoms and severe weakness — a reasonable approach.

Practical considerations:
- Pulse first when risk of severe morbidity is high. Use 3–5 days of 1 g/day methylprednisolone (shorter if infection risk is significant), then transition to oral prednisone 1 mg/kg (or equivalent) if improvement occurs.
- Screen and treat latent infections as feasible before prolonged steroids (eg, TB history). If immediate pulse is required, do baseline infection surveillance (urine culture, chest imaging, consider blood cultures in unstable patients).

## Early IVIG — when to add it
IVIG should be considered early in IMNM when:
- There is severe weakness (inability to ambulate, bulbar dysfunction) despite steroids, or
- Bulbar/respiratory involvement is present (higher aspiration and ventilation risk), or
- The clinical picture suggests seronegative IMNM where steroid monotherapy often underperforms, or
- Rapid steroid‑sparing is desirable (eg, in patients with infection risk or comorbidity).

Dosing and timing:
- Typical inpatient regimen: 2 g/kg total, often given over 2–5 days (the case used 2 g/kg split over 4 days). Start as early as practical when severe features exist.
- IVIG is particularly attractive when you suspect a drug‑triggered, finite auto‑immune hit: it can rapidly neutralize pathogenic autoantibody and dampen complement activation (note: muscle biopsy in this patient showed membrane attack complex).

In the case, early IVIG plus pulse steroids was associated with a rapid CK fall and functional improvement, supporting early combined therapy for severe presentations.

## Monitoring: labs and function — the practical rhythm
- CK: baseline and then serially (eg, every 48–72 hours inpatient). Expect CK to lag clinical improvement; falling CK supports response.
- Strength/function: perform daily bedside strength exams (MRC grades for key muscle groups), timed sit‑to‑stand, and document swallow/voice changes.
- Respiratory: serial vital capacity (sitting and supine if available), oxygenation, and consider capillary/arterial blood gas if hypercapnia suspected.
- Cardiac monitoring: troponin/BNP if symptoms or MRI/echo abnormalities — myocarditis can coexist.
- Infection surveillance: daily temp/clinical exam; urine cultures in older or catheterised patients; chest imaging if pulmonary symptoms.

In our case CK declined markedly over 2 weeks and function improved; serial monitoring allowed the team to confidently taper steroids.

## The exit strategy — when to taper and stop immunosuppression
If you suspect a drug-triggered IMNM (recent temporal association with a new agent, e.g., semaglutide in this case) and the patient demonstrates clinical and biochemical response, you can plan an early, relatively rapid taper — but do it with criteria and safety nets.

Reasonable criteria to attempt tapering and consider stopping immunosuppression:
- Sustained clinical improvement: return toward baseline strength and resolution of bulbar symptoms such that airway/aspiration risk is mitigated.
- Objective improvement in function: ability to ambulate or perform baseline ADLs, stable/falling timed functional tests.
- CK normalization or sustained trend toward normal range for several weeks (exact timing varies; the case achieved normal CK and sustained remission).
- No new symptoms suggesting relapse (new weakness, dysphagia, dyspnea).
- Absence of high‑risk autoantibodies (HMGCR or SRP positive disease often requires prolonged therapy; in drug‑induced cases antibodies may be absent).
- Close outpatient follow‑up guaranteed with repeat CK and early access to clinic or re‑admission if relapse occurs.

Taper approach:
- After pulse → prednisone 1 mg/kg daily, reassess within 1–2 weeks.
- If rapid clinical and CK response, consider a faster taper over 4–8 weeks tailored to recovery. The case we reviewed successfully tapered off prednisone within ~2 months.
- If relapse or incomplete recovery, broaden therapy (repeat IVIG, add steroid‑sparing agents: rituximab, mycophenolate, azathioprine, methotrexate) per IMNM protocols.

Document and avoid rechallenge with the suspected offending drug. In this case the patient avoided future GLP‑1 receptor agonists and remained well off immunosuppression at 10 months.

## Infection risk mitigation — learning from the septic UTI
High‑dose steroids and IVIG increase infection risk. Our patient developed a Pseudomonas septic UTI after discharge.

Practical steps to reduce infection morbidity:
- Screen and treat active infections before or during immunosuppression if possible (eg, treat asymptomatic bacteriuria in high‑risk bulbar patients? Use clinical judgement).
- Minimize invasive devices (avoid unnecessary catheters; if required, remove ASAP).
- Vaccination history review (influenza, pneumococcal) — give vaccines when safe and not during high‑dose steroids/IVIG (live vaccines contraindicated).
- Patient education about early signs of infection and low threshold for evaluation.
- Consider prophylaxis for opportunistic infections only when long‑term immunosuppression is planned (eg, PCP prophylaxis), not for short courses.

The take‑home: short courses of high‑dose steroids + IVIG still carry infection risk; anticipate it, minimize exposures (urinary catheters), and counsel patients.

## VTE considerations
This patient had a remote DVT/PE history. Immobility from severe weakness plus high‑dose steroids and acute illness increases VTE risk.

Practical steps:
- Pharmacologic prophylaxis (eg, low molecular weight heparin) unless contraindicated, continued until ambulatory.
- For patients with prior VTE, consider individualized peri‑hospital anticoagulation strategy — consult hematology if complex.
- Encourage early mobilization and physical therapy once safe.

## Bulbar and respiratory care — don’t miss aspiration and ventilatory failure
Bulbar involvement in IMNM is a predictor of morbidity. The case had dysphagia and dysphonia initially.

Actions:
- Formal swallow evaluation early (speech and language pathology), NPO if unsafe.
- Consider nasogastric or percutaneous feeding options if prolonged dysphagia is anticipated.
- Monitor respiratory function closely; admit to monitored settings if vital capacity is falling or if hypercapnia develops.
- Early involvement of ICU and respiratory therapy if there is any hint of impending ventilatory failure.

## When to call second opinions and escalate
- Lack of early improvement or worsening despite pulse steroids + IVIG — consider escalation: second IVIG course, add steroid‑sparing agents (rituximab is often effective for refractory IMNM), and discuss plasmapheresis in select cases.
- Positive HMGCR or SRP antibodies with severe/persistent disease — anticipate longer, multi‑agent immunosuppression.
- Ongoing diagnostic uncertainty — consider repeat biopsy or specialized referral centers.

## Clinical pearls from the case
- Not all necrotising myopathies require lifelong immunosuppression. A drug‑triggered IMNM phenotype (clear temporal relation, biopsy‑proven, seronegative, marked recovery after drug withdrawal + short immunosuppression) can remit after a finite course.
- Treat severe presentations aggressively up front (pulse steroids ± early IVIG) to prevent permanent weakness, aspiration, or respiratory failure.
- Monitor CK trends but prioritize clinical function and respiratory/bulbar status when making acute decisions; CK lags clinical change.
- Proactively manage infection and VTE risk — short, intense immunosuppression still carries complications.
- Avoid rechallenge with the suspected offending agent.

## Take‑home messages
- Consider pulse methylprednisolone (1 g daily × 3–5 days) for severe, rapidly progressive IMNM phenotypes (bulbar/respiratory threat, inability to mobilize).
- Add early IVIG (2 g/kg total) when bulbar/respiratory involvement is present, when steroid response is inadequate, or when you suspect a drug‑triggered IMNM that may respond to a finite immunologic reset.
- Monitor CK, bedside strength, swallow and respiratory function serially; prioritize objective function and respiratory measures over isolated CK numbers.
- If drug‑induced IMNM is suspected and the patient shows sustained clinical and biochemical improvement, a planned rapid taper and stopping of immunosuppression (with close follow‑up) is reasonable.
- Anticipate and mitigate infection and VTE risks during and after immunosuppression; educate patients about early signs of relapse or infection and ensure close outpatient follow‑up.

This case is a useful reminder: be aggressive to save function up front, but if the trigger is removable and recovery is robust, an exit strategy aimed at minimizing chronic immunosuppression can succeed — provided you monitor closely and are ready to escalate if needed.



# From Signal to Causality: Applying WHO‑UMC to Suspected GLP‑1–Triggered Autoimmunity

What makes this case interesting
A woman in her 60s developed a progressive, biopsy‑proven immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) with severe proximal weakness, dysphagia and a 12‑fold rise in CK over 4 months — beginning after a single dose of semaglutide. She improved with high‑dose steroids and IVIG and remained well off immunosuppression after avoiding GLP‑1 therapy. This is the first reported IMNM with a close temporal relationship to a GLP‑1 receptor agonist, raising an important pharmacovigilance question: is this coincidence, direct toxicity, or drug‑triggered autoimmunity?

Use this case as a worked example of structured causality assessment using the WHO‑Uppsala Monitoring Centre (WHO‑UMC) framework, and for practical, bedside steps clinicians can take in the increasingly common setting of GLP‑1 use.

## Quick reminder: what is IMNM?
IMNM is an aggressive autoimmune myopathy characterised by prominent muscle fibre necrosis with macrophage/complimentary activation on biopsy, often severe proximal weakness, markedly elevated CK, and association with autoantibodies (anti‑HMGCR, anti‑SRP) in many — but not all — patients. A substantial minority are seronegative. Idiopathic IMNM often needs prolonged immunosuppression; drug‑associated forms can sometimes remit after withdrawal and limited immunotherapy.

## Applying WHO‑UMC to this patient — the logic ladder
WHO‑UMC uses criteria that you can apply at the bedside to move a signal toward (or away from) probable causality. Main domains are temporal relationship, alternative explanations, dechallenge/rechallenge, known pharmacology, and objective evidence.

1) Temporal relationship
- Signal: symptoms began immediately (hours) after the first semaglutide dose (nausea, vomiting, hypovolaemia), then progressive weakness and large CK rise over 4 months.
- Interpretation: reasonable time relationship — acute intolerance followed by delayed autoimmune phenotype is biologically plausible for an immune‑triggered process.

2) Alternative explanations
- The patient had pre‑existing NAFLD with mild AST/ALT elevation long before semaglutide; no prior statin or known myotoxic exposures; no family history; myositis serology (anti‑HMGCR, anti‑SRP, Jo‑1 etc.) was negative. Cardiac workup had transient abnormalities but ultimately normalised.
- Interpretation: reasonable exclusion of common alternative causes. Absence of HMGCR/SRP antibodies doesn't exclude IMNM — about one‑third are seronegative.

3) Dechallenge (withdrawal) and response
- Semaglutide was not continued; after high‑dose steroids + IVIG (short course) CK normalised and function recovered, and the patient remained well off immunosuppression for months.
- Interpretation: positive dechallenge with sustained clinical remission favors a drug‑related trigger rather than persistent idiopathic IMNM.

4) Rechallenge
- Ethical constraints: rechallenge with the same or another GLP‑1 would be high risk and unjustified given severity; therefore unavailable.
- Interpretation: lack of rechallenge limits definitive proof, but rechallenge is often not required for a “probable” causality determination in severe reactions.

5) Objective evidence
- Muscle biopsy showed necrotic/regenerating fibers, CD68 macrophages, membrane attack complex deposition — classic IMNM histology. CK reached 3992 U/L. These objective data strengthen causality assessment.

Putting it together: WHO‑UMC category
- The case fits “probable/likely” causality: reasonable time relationship, no alternative explanations, positive dechallenge with clinical improvement, objective supporting data, and rechallenge not done (and unethical).

## Practical pharmacovigilance in the GLP‑1 era — what to do in clinic or ED

Counseling before starting GLP‑1 receptor agonists
- Tell patients the common side effects (GI upset, nausea), and rare but important signals (muscle symptoms, myalgias, CK elevation reported in case reports).
- Advise patients to report new profound weakness (especially proximal), difficulty swallowing, new dysphonia, dark urine or inability to arise from a chair.

What to monitor and when to check CK
- Routine CK monitoring for all patients is not currently standard. Consider baseline CK in patients with prior myopathy, unexplained transaminase elevations where muscle source hasn't been excluded, or when patients are on other myotoxic meds.
- Check CK promptly if a patient develops:
  - New or progressive proximal muscle weakness
  - Myalgias with functional decline
  - New dysphagia, dysphonia, or respiratory symptoms suggesting bulbar/respiratory involvement
  - Dark urine or other signs of rhabdomyolysis
- In mild isolated myalgias without weakness, consider CK and clinical reassessment; mild CK elevations alone do not mandate stopping therapy unless symptomatic or high.

Initial evaluation of suspected GLP‑1–related myopathy
- Focused history: timing of symptom onset relative to GLP‑1 exposure, prior statin/supplement exposure (red yeast rice, mushrooms), family history, alcohol/illicit use, prior transaminase elevations.
- Exam: look for proximal weakness, bulbar involvement, skin findings, respiratory compromise.
- Initial labs: CK, AST/ALT (note muscle can raise transaminases), renal function, electrolytes, TSH, inflammatory markers.
- If CK elevated and clinical concern high: neurology/rheumatology consult, EMG ± MRI to localise, and consider muscle biopsy for definitive diagnosis.
- Early involvement of specialists facilitates decisions about immunosuppression.

Reporting and documentation
- Document the timeline carefully: drug start date, dose, single vs repeated exposure, symptom onset and progression, CK values, treatments, and outcomes.
- Report suspected serious adverse reactions to local/national pharmacovigilance systems (e.g., FDA MedWatch in the US, Yellow Card in the UK) — case reports matter. Include biopsy results, labs, and follow‑up.
- Consider institutional review or adverse event committee notification if the patient was started on GLP‑1 in an inpatient setting.

Class re‑exposure: avoid with caution
- Given plausibility of class effects (another GLP‑1 agonist and tirzepatide have been implicated in necrotising/rhabdomyolysis‑type injury), avoid re‑exposure to the same class unless compelling reasons and with informed consent and close monitoring.
- If weight/diabetes therapy is needed, discuss alternative classes and involve endocrinology.

## Clinical pearls
- A temporal association plus objective evidence (biopsy + CK) and improvement after withdrawal/immunotherapy can support a “probable” drug‑cause even without a positive autoantibody or rechallenge.
- Not all IMNM patients are antibody positive — seronegativity does not rule out immune‑mediated disease.
- Acute GI‑mediated hypovolaemia and AKI after GLP‑1 dosing can be the first sign of intolerance; persistent or progressive weakness after that should prompt myopathy evaluation.
- Distinguish direct toxic rhabdomyolysis (rapid CK spike, quick recovery after withdrawal/hydration) from immune‑mediated myopathy (progressive weakness, histologic necrosis with inflammatory features, and need for immunosuppression).

## Take‑home messages
- This semaglutide‑associated IMNM case meets criteria for probable causality per WHO‑UMC: reasonable timing, exclusion of alternatives, objective biopsy confirmation, and positive dechallenge with sustained recovery.
- Be vigilant for new proximal weakness, bulbar symptoms or marked CK rise in patients on GLP‑1 receptor agonists; act early with CK testing and specialist input.
- Document and report suspected serious adverse drug reactions — single case reports can be the seed that identifies a true rare adverse effect.
- Avoid class re‑exposure after a severe suspected immune‑mediated reaction; manage onward metabolic care with alternative strategies and specialist collaboration.

If you see a similar case in your ED or clinic: stabilise the patient, measure CK, involve neurology/rheumatology, document the timeline, stop the GLP‑1 agent, treat IMNM if indicated, and report the event. In the GLP‑1 era, vigilance + structured causality thinking = better patient safety and better science.



# AST/ALT in Myopathy and NAFLD: Don’t Let the Liver Take the Fall

This case is a great teaching vignette because it forces you to disentangle two very common causes of mild-to-moderate transaminase elevations — fatty liver and muscle injury — in the same patient. A woman in her 60s with known NAFLD received a single dose of semaglutide, acutely decompensated with vomiting, hypovolaemia and a modest CK elevation, and then over the next four months developed progressive proximal weakness with CK peaking at ~4,000 U/L and biopsy‑proven immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). Her AST/ALT were mildly elevated before semaglutide (consistent with known NAFLD), rose in the setting of muscle injury, then remained mildly abnormal even after CK and strength normalized — raising the question: which organ is responsible for the transaminitis at each point in time, and when is a liver biopsy still useful?

If you take one thing away from this case it’s this: AST and ALT are not liver-specific. Muscle disease can drive AST and ALT up (often AST>ALT), and coexisting hepatic pathology can muddy the picture. A structured, temporally aware approach helps avoid misattribution and unnecessary testing — or worse, missed liver disease.

## Why AST/ALT rise in myopathy
- AST is abundant in skeletal muscle; ALT is present in muscle but at lower concentrations. Significant muscle injury (rhabdomyolysis, necrotising myopathy, polymyositis) often elevates AST more than ALT, and both can rise proportionally with CK.
- Transaminase elevations from muscle usually parallel CK and clinical muscle signs (weakness, myalgia, myoglobinuria sometimes).
- CK is your best discriminator: when CK and AST/ALT rise together, think muscle as a major source.

In this patient: prior to semaglutide she had mild AST/ALT elevations (AST≈ALT up to ~2x ULN) consistent with NAFLD documented on ultrasound and liver biopsy. After the index dose she developed acute illness with CK 337 U/L (mild), then over months CK rose to 3,992 U/L with profound proximal weakness. During that interval the AST rose into the hundreds. The temporal association and biopsy-proven IMNM argue that much of the AST/ALT elevation during the acute myopathy was muscle-derived.

## Practical, stepwise approach when NAFLD and myopathy coexist

1. Anchor to time and symptoms
   - Always map labs to the clinical timeline. Did transaminases rise before muscle symptoms/CK? Or in parallel? In this case chronic mild transaminase elevation preceded the myopathy, then rose markedly with CK during the myopathy.

2. Check CK (and repeat it)
   - If CK is normal or near‑normal, muscle is less likely the culprit.
   - If CK is elevated, expect AST (and to a lesser extent ALT) to be elevated too. Track trends — CK should fall if muscle injury resolves; transaminases that follow CK down suggest muscle source.

3. Look at patterns and ratios
   - AST>ALT and AST disproportionately high vs ALT supports muscle contribution, though not diagnostic.
   - Very high ALT out of proportion to CK suggests hepatic source.

4. Use other labs to help localize
   - Myoglobinuria, elevated LDH, aldolase can support muscle injury.
   - GGT and alkaline phosphatase tend not to be elevated from muscle — elevation points toward hepatic/biliary source.
   - Synthetic function (albumin, INR) and bilirubin help assess liver disease severity; normal synthetic markers argue against severe hepatic injury.

5. Imaging
   - Liver ultrasound can document steatosis (as it did here). It won’t tell you whether current transaminase elevation is hepatic vs muscle, but it screens for structural liver disease.
   - Muscle MRI can localize and quantify muscle inflammation/edema, as in this patient’s thigh MRI showing proximal muscle edema.

6. Biopsy decisions
   - Muscle biopsy: indicated if muscle disease is suspected clinically and serology/imaging are inconclusive. In this case, thigh muscle biopsy clinched IMNM with necrosis, macrophage infiltration and complement deposition.
   - Liver biopsy: reserve for situations where hepatic disease must be confirmed or staging will change management — persistent transaminitis after CK normalizes, unexplained progressive liver dysfunction, suspected other causes (autoimmune hepatitis, Wilson’s, etc.), or when histology will inform therapy. This patient had prior liver biopsy confirming NAFLD and later biopsy showed progression (NAS 5/8, stage 1) — useful to separate chronic hepatic disease from acute muscle-related transaminase rises.

7. Serologies and panels — interpret cautiously
   - Myositis panels and autoantibodies help subtype inflammatory myopathies, but up to one-third of IMNM patients are seronegative. Negative myositis panel does not exclude IMNM; muscle biopsy remains diagnostic in many seronegative cases.
   - Liver autoantibodies (ANA, SMA, AMA) and viral hepatitis testing help exclude alternative hepatic causes.

8. Follow trends after treatment/withdrawal
   - If you treat the myopathy (or stop an offending drug) and CK normalizes with concurrent fall in AST/ALT, muscle was likely the dominant contributor. In this patient, CK normalized and muscle strength returned after steroids and IVIG and stopping semaglutide; residual mild transaminitis persisted consistent with chronic NAFLD.

## How this case illustrates the approach
- Baseline: patient had NAFLD with mild chronic AST/ALT elevation documented on biopsy years before semaglutide.
- Acute/subacute: after semaglutide she had an initial CK 337 U/L with nausea and hypovolaemic renal injury — likely early muscle injury but not rhabdomyolysis. Over four months she developed progressive weakness and CK rose to ~4,000 U/L; AST/ALT rose in parallel.
- Localization: thigh MRI showed muscle edema; muscle biopsy confirmed IMNM. Myositis autoantibodies were negative (seronegative IMNM), illustrating that biopsy may be necessary when serology is unrevealing.
- Treatment and follow-up: corticosteroids + IVIG produced clinical and CK improvement. After recovery, CK remained normal but transaminases remained mildly elevated — consistent with chronic NAFLD rather than ongoing muscle injury. A follow-up liver biopsy again showed steatohepatitis, supporting hepatic origin of the persistent mild transaminitis.

## Clinical pearls
- CK is the single most useful test to determine whether AST/ALT elevations are muscle-derived. Always check CK when unexplained transaminase elevation is present, especially with weakness or muscle symptoms.
- AST often rises more than ALT in muscle disease, but patterns overlap — use trends, not a single snapshot.
- Normal GGT and alkaline phosphatase with elevated AST/ALT and CK supports muscle source; abnormal cholestatic enzymes point to hepatic/biliary origin.
- A negative myositis panel does not exclude inflammatory myopathy; muscle biopsy can be diagnostic, especially for IMNM.
- If a patient has preexisting liver disease (eg, NAFLD), document baseline labs and/or liver histology if available — this helps interpretation later if muscle disease occurs.
- Consider liver biopsy when: transaminases remain persistently elevated after CK normalizes, liver synthetic dysfunction is present, or when histologic staging will change management.

## Take‑home messages
- Always consider muscle as a source when AST/ALT are elevated — check CK early.
- Interpret transaminase abnormalities in the context of the timeline, CK trends, other liver tests (GGT, ALP, bilirubin, INR), and imaging.
- Muscle biopsy is often required to diagnose seronegative IMNM; liver biopsy is helpful when persistent hepatic transaminase elevation remains after muscle recovery or when management depends on hepatic histology.
- In patients with coexisting NAFLD and new muscle disease, don’t assume the liver is the automatic culprit — let CK trends and focused testing guide you.

This case reminds clinicians to let physiology and timelines drive interpretation of transaminases. Don’t let the liver take the fall when muscle disease is in the room.



# Cardiac Involvement in Inflammatory Myopathies: Reading the Biomarkers and MRI

What makes this case notable? A woman in her 60s with immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) had modest hs‑troponin elevation, a markedly raised pro‑BNP, normal LVEF on echo but elevated RVSP, and cardiac MRI showing focal myocardial edema and mild regional hypokinesis without late gadolinium enhancement (LGE). Her cardiac abnormalities normalised after treatment for IMNM (steroids + IVIG) and clinical recovery. This is a real‑world example of how cardiac markers, echo and cardiac MRI can sit on the fence between early myocarditis and other causes such as stress cardiomyopathy — and how to manage the diagnostic uncertainty.

Why this matters: inflammatory myopathies often have cardiac involvement. Missing it risks heart failure, arrhythmia or myocarditis. Overcalling it can lead to unnecessary invasive testing and delay in treating the primary myopathy. So how do you read the labs and imaging — and when do you escalate?

## Start with the pre‑test probability and clinical context
Always interpret hs‑troponin, BNP, echo and cMRI in the clinical picture.

- In IMNM and other inflammatory myopathies, cardiac involvement can range from subclinical troponin rise to fulminant myocarditis. Symptoms such as chest pain, new dyspnoea out of proportion to muscle weakness, syncope, palpitations or new conduction disease raise the pre‑test probability of clinically important cardiac disease.
- Consider non‑cardiac contributors to biomarkers: chronic renal dysfunction, sepsis, pulmonary hypertension, PE, and skeletal muscle disease can affect troponin and BNP interpretation. In this case the patient had acute kidney injury initially, sepsis later, and severe myopathy — all potential confounders.

## How to interpret troponin and BNP in suspected inflammatory myopathy
- High‑sensitivity troponin (hs‑Tn) is sensitive for myocardial injury but not specific for etiology. A mild elevation (eg, 62 pg/mL vs normal ≤53 pg/mL here) may reflect small‑volume myocardial injury, demand ischemia, or early inflammatory involvement.
- BNP/pro‑BNP reflects myocardial stretch; very high values (proBNP 4358 pg/mL here) indicate increased wall stress and are more suggestive of clinically relevant cardiac dysfunction (volume/pressure overload) even if the echo LVEF is preserved.
- Patterns that tip you toward myocarditis: rising troponin over serial measurements, symptoms of chest pain/dyspnoea out of proportion, ECG changes, new arrhythmia, regional wall motion abnormalities on echo, and cMRI features of edema/LGE.
- If troponin is only mildly above reference and clinical exam/ECG/echo are reassuring, consider close observation with serial biomarkers rather than immediate invasive workup.

## Echocardiography — what to look for and limitations
- Echo is the first‑line imaging to assess cardiac function and estimate filling pressures and pulmonary pressures.
- LVEF may be normal even with myocardial inflammation (small focal involvement). Regional wall motion abnormalities are important; they can point to myocarditis but mimic ischemia or stress cardiomyopathy.
- Elevated RV systolic pressure (RVSP ~46 mmHg in this case) suggests pulmonary hypertension or increased pulmonary vascular resistance and can contribute to BNP elevation.
- Echo is limited in tissue characterization — it won’t tell you if edema or active inflammation is present.

## Cardiac MRI — the problem‑solver (when used appropriately)
- cMRI provides tissue characterization: T2‑weighted or newer T2 mapping identifies edema; T1 mapping and LGE identify necrosis/fibrosis. The revised Lake Louise Criteria use T1/T2 mapping and LGE to support myocarditis.
- Findings in this case: focal myocardial edema with mild regional hypokinesis and no LGE. That pattern can be seen in early/limited myocarditis (edema without fibrosis yet) or early stress‑induced changes. Absence of LGE makes irreversible injury less likely.
- cMRI timing matters: early in the disease you may see edema without LGE. LGE may take time to become apparent; conversely, LGE can persist after clinical recovery.
- cMRI is most helpful when it changes management: if myocarditis is suspected and the result would alter treatment (immunosuppression, activity restriction, need for biopsy), then cMRI is appropriate.

## Myocarditis vs stress (Takotsubo) cardiomyopathy — distinguishing features
- Myocarditis: often presents with chest pain, elevated troponin, ECG ST‑T changes, variable LV function; cMRI shows edema ± LGE in non‑coronary distributions. Can be global or regional.
- Stress cardiomyopathy: typically follows a catecholaminergic/stress trigger, presents with regional wall motion abnormality that doesn’t match a single coronary territory (apical ballooning or variants), troponin modestly elevated, BNP often high. cMRI may show edema and typically lacks LGE or shows limited LGE.
- In inflammatory myopathy patients, either process is possible: systemic inflammation can provoke myocarditis; the stress of severe systemic illness or sepsis can provoke stress cardiomyopathy. The focality and lack of LGE in this case could fit either.

## When to involve cardiology and when to do more testing
Involve cardiology early if any of the following are present:
- Troponin rising on serial measurements or significantly elevated.
- BNP markedly elevated with symptoms of heart failure (orthopnea, pulmonary edema).
- New ECG changes (ST segment, new conduction disease) or arrhythmias.
- New or worsening dyspnoea out of proportion to neuromuscular weakness.
- Regional wall motion abnormality on echo or unexplained hypotension.

If cardiology is involved, next steps commonly include:
- Serial hs‑troponin and BNP measurements.
- Continuous ECG monitoring if arrhythmia or troponin rise.
- cMRI for tissue characterization unless immediate coronary angiography is indicated (eg, ischemic pattern or high suspicion for ACS).
- Consider endomyocardial biopsy only when results would change therapy (diagnostic uncertainty with suspected fulminant myocarditis, refractory cardiogenic shock, suspected giant cell myocarditis).

## Timing of follow‑up imaging and biomarkers
- Serial biomarkers: repeat hs‑troponin and BNP every 24–48 hours in the acute phase until a trend is established, then less frequently as clinically indicated.
- Repeat echo: at least once during hospitalization if initial echo abnormal; repeat at 1–3 months outpatient to document recovery, sooner if clinical deterioration.
- Repeat cMRI: reasonable at 3–6 months if initial cMRI showed myocarditis features to document resolution of edema/LGE and to guide long‑term prognosis (persistent LGE predicts worse outcomes). Earlier repeats (4–12 weeks) can be considered if clinical course is unclear or if treatment decisions hinge on imaging.
- In this case the patient had normalisation of cardiac MRI and echo on follow‑up, concordant with recovery from IMNM — which is a reassuring pattern.

## How cardiac recovery tracks with control of the myopathy
- When cardiac involvement is immune‑mediated (true myocarditis related to systemic autoimmune disease), effective control of systemic inflammation with immunosuppression (steroids, IVIG, other agents) often results in improvement in cardiac biomarkers and imaging findings.
- In drug‑induced or parainfectious myocarditis, removing the offending trigger and supportive care may be sufficient; immunosuppression is considered on a case‑by‑case basis.
- Correlation is imperfect: cardiac injury can lag behind skeletal muscle recovery or recover independently. Persistence of troponin elevation or LGE despite clinical muscle improvement should prompt cardiology follow‑up and risk stratification.

## Practical screening pearls for suspected inflammatory myopathy
- Baseline screening for new or suspected inflammatory myopathy: ECG, hs‑troponin, BNP, and an echo if symptoms or biomarkers abnormal.
- Any troponin elevation should prompt serial measurements and consideration of cMRI if clinical suspicion for myocarditis is moderate to high.
- Don’t assume a normal LVEF excludes myocardial involvement — use cMRI if symptoms/biomarkers suggest inflammation.
- Avoid routine endomyocardial biopsy unless the diagnosis is uncertain and the result would change therapy.
- Engage cardiology early for significant biomarker abnormalities, arrhythmia, heart failure, or abnormal imaging.

## Putting it into practice — applying this case
- This patient had small troponin rise but very high proBNP and regional hypokinesis with myocardial edema on cMRI. That constellation justified cardiology involvement and cMRI rather than assuming no cardiac disease because LVEF was preserved.
- The imaging pattern (edema without LGE) suggested potentially reversible inflammatory injury or stress‑related change; the decision to treat the systemic IMNM and provide supportive cardiac care was reasonable.
- Her cardiac abnormalities resolved alongside successful treatment of the IMNM — a pattern consistent with immune‑mediated or stress‑related myocardial injury that recovered with control of the systemic disease.

Key take‑home messages
- In inflammatory myopathies, even modest hs‑troponin elevation or a very high BNP warrants careful evaluation; combine biomarkers, ECG, echo and cMRI for decision making.
- Echo is first line for function; cMRI adds tissue characterization that helps distinguish myocarditis from other causes and guide prognosis.
- Absence of LGE does not exclude myocarditis (early disease can show edema only); persistent LGE carries worse prognosis.
- Involve cardiology early for rising biomarkers, arrhythmia, heart failure, or abnormal imaging; reserve biopsy for select cases where the result will alter management.
- Coordinate treatment of the underlying myopathy and cardiac care — cardiac recovery often follows control of systemic inflammation but may lag or require separate management.
