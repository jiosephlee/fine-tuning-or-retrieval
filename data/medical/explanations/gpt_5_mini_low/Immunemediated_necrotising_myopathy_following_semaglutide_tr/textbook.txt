Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

## Case Presentation and Clinical Context

This patient is a woman in her 60s whose decline unfolded over months after a single dose of semaglutide and whose pattern of symptoms and investigations prompted evaluation for an inflammatory myopathy. Her baseline medical problems—obesity, non‑alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) with prior liver biopsy, hypertension, and treated hypothyroidism—provide important context because they explain prior transaminase abnormalities and the clinical rationale for starting a GLP‑1 receptor agonist, and because some comorbid conditions and drugs can confound interpretation of muscle enzymes and weakness. Her NAFLD had been documented on imaging and liver biopsy years before the current illness, with modest chronic transaminase elevations (ALT > AST) attributed to hepatic steatosis; this history makes it less likely that early transaminase abnormalities represented occult myopathy before the acute episode.

Approximately 2.5 years after the liver diagnosis she received a single 0.25 mg subcutaneous dose of semaglutide. Within hours she developed severe gastrointestinal intolerance—nausea and vomiting—that progressed over 48 hours to hospitalisation for hypovolaemic complications: poor oral intake, dyspnoea, acute kidney injury (AKI), and a high anion‑gap metabolic acidosis. These acute complications are consistent with dehydration and preservervation injury from persistent vomiting. On that admission a serum creatine kinase (CK) was checked and modestly elevated at 337 U/L. Although this is above the reference range, it is far lower than levels seen in frank rhabdomyolysis and there were no clinical features typical of rhabdomyolysis (no generalized severe myalgias, cola‑coloured urine, or marked CK elevations), supporting the interpretation that the initial CK rise was mild and non‑specific in the setting of systemic illness and AKI. During that hospital stay she also developed paroxysmal atrial fibrillation (pAF); serial cardiac troponins were normal, arguing against acute myocardial infarction as the cause of the arrhythmia.

After discharge she did not receive additional semaglutide but over the next 4 months experienced a progressive, subacute decline characterized by worsening proximal limb weakness, dysphagia for liquids and solids, and dysphonia. These features are clinically important because they indicate involvement of bulbar and axial musculature in addition to limb girdle muscles. Dysphagia and dysphonia suggest weakness of oropharyngeal and laryngeal muscles and raise concern for a myopathic process with risk for aspiration and respiratory compromise, which are red flags that typically prompt expedited inpatient evaluation in neuromuscular disease.

On representation to a community hospital, her CK was markedly higher at approximately 3992 U/L, reflecting ongoing muscle injury developing over weeks to months rather than the rapid peak and fall seen with isolated toxic rhabdomyolysis. She had no cutaneous findings (no heliotrope rash or Gottron papules) and no synovitis, making classic dermatomyositis or an inflammatory arthritis less likely clinically. The combination of severe proximal weakness, bulbar involvement, and CK in the thousands raised concern for an inflammatory myopathy—specifically a necrotising myopathy or immune‑mediated myositis—rather than isolated deconditioning. The presence of pAF again was noted but with normal troponin at that time; cardiac arrhythmia can be associated with systemic illness and is also reported in inflammatory myopathies when myocarditis is present, so it remained an important comorbidity to monitor.

Given the severity of weakness and elevated CK, she received high‑dose intravenous methylprednisolone (1 g daily for 5 days) at the outside centre as empiric therapy for suspected inflammatory myopathy before transfer. This is a common pragmatic approach when an aggressive immune myopathy is suspected and there is concern for rapidly progressive weakness or bulbar dysfunction. She was then transferred to a tertiary centre for rheumatology and neuromuscular evaluation, where further investigation (imaging and muscle biopsy) confirmed a necrotising inflammatory myopathy. The temporal relationship—symptom onset within hours of semaglutide exposure, an initial mild CK rise during the acute illness, followed by progressive weakness and dramatic CK elevation over months despite discontinuation of the drug—helped frame the clinical reasoning. The delayed and progressive course, bulbar signs, lack of classical dermatomyositis skin findings, and biopsy showing necrosis with macrophage predominance pointed clinicians away from simple toxic rhabdomyolysis and toward immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy as the working diagnosis requiring immunosuppressive therapy.

## Pathophysiology and Differential Diagnosis Framework

Immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is a distinct clinicopathologic form of inflammatory myopathy characterized clinically by subacute-to-rapid onset, severe proximal muscle weakness and markedly elevated serum creatine kinase (CK). Pathologically the hallmark is widespread myofibre necrosis with regeneration and relatively sparse lymphocytic infiltrates; instead, macrophage-mediated myophagocytosis, complement deposition (membrane attack complex) and necrosis predominate. In this case the patient’s progressive proximal weakness, very high CK, MRI evidence of proximal muscle oedema, and muscle biopsy showing dispersed necrotic/regenerating fibres with CD68+ macrophages and membrane attack complex are all concordant with IMNM rather than a primarily neuropathic or metabolic myopathy.

IMNM is heterogeneous by serology. Two well‑described autoantibody-defined subsets are anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP myopathy. Anti‑HMGCR is strongly associated with prior statin exposure in many patients, and anti‑SRP tends to associate with more severe weakness and refractory disease. Both subtypes often require prolonged immunosuppression. Importantly, up to roughly one‑third of IMNM patients are seronegative for known myositis autoantibodies. Seronegativity complicates diagnosis and suggests additional pathogenic triggers or antibodies remain undiscovered. In the present patient the myositis panel was negative for HMGCR and SRP, placing her in the seronegative group; the biopsy and clinical course remain the principal evidence supporting an IMNM diagnosis.

Drug‑associated IMNM is best exemplified by the statin‑associated anti‑HMGCR syndrome. Key features that differentiate drug‑triggered autoimmune necrotising myopathy from simple toxic myositis or transient rhabdomyolysis are (1) progressive weakness and sustained CK elevation despite withdrawal of the offending agent, and (2) objective improvement with immunosuppression. In statin‑linked IMNM many patients do not recover simply after stopping the drug and instead require steroids, IVIG and/or steroid‑sparing agents. This paradigm frames the evaluation of other medications as potential triggers: an immediate adverse toxicity would usually show rapid CK decline on drug cessation, whereas an immune‑mediated process may continue or progress and respond to immune therapy.

Distinguishing IMNM from drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis has practical and diagnostic implications. Rhabdomyolysis is an acute myofibre breakdown syndrome that often presents with severe myalgia, very high CK (can be tens of thousands), myoglobinuria (dark urine), electrolyte disturbances, and acute kidney injury. When caused by a direct toxin or metabolic insult, CK typically falls promptly after removal of the insult and supportive care (hydration, correction of electrolytes). IMNM often shows more gradual onset of weakness, ongoing CK elevation that can persist or even rise after stopping a trigger, and histology dominated by necrosis with inflammatory features rather than diffuse acute sarcolemmal disruption alone. In this case the patient had an immediate GI/volume‑depletion illness after semaglutide with a modest CK elevation, followed by progressive weakness and rising CK over months—behavior more consistent with an evolving immune process than a single episode of toxic rhabdomyolysis.

GLP‑1 receptor agonists (GLP‑1 RAs) are widely used for type 2 diabetes and weight management. Common adverse effects are gastrointestinal (nausea, vomiting) and less commonly pancreatitis or gallbladder disease. Emerging reports have noted decreased skeletal muscle mass with chronic GLP‑1 RA use, and isolated case reports have described rhabdomyolysis temporally related to GLP‑1 RA drugs. Mechanistically, direct myotoxicity from GLP‑1 RAs has not been established; plausible hypotheses for muscle effects include metabolic shifts in nutrient partitioning, weight loss–related muscle wasting, or unrecognized idiosyncratic immune responses in predisposed individuals. In the present case the temporal relationship (symptoms beginning immediately after the first dose, then progressive deterioration) raises the possibility that semaglutide acted as a trigger for an immune‑mediated process rather than causing transient toxic rhabdomyolysis; however, a causal link cannot be proven from a single case.

When approaching the differential diagnosis in a patient like this, several alternative etiologies must be considered and excluded by clinical context, laboratory testing, imaging and biopsy. Endocrine myopathies—most importantly hypothyroid myopathy—can cause proximal weakness and CK elevation; a normal TSH in this patient argues against that cause. Other inflammatory myopathies (dermatomyositis, overlap syndromes) are usually suggested by characteristic skin findings, specific autoantibodies, or interstitial lung disease, none of which were present here. Neuromuscular junction disorders (e.g., myasthenia gravis) produce fatigable weakness rather than predominant CK elevation and show different electrophysiologic and serologic signatures. Critical illness myopathy can follow prolonged ICU stays and neuromuscular blockade, but the timing, biopsy features and outpatient recovery pattern in this case are inconsistent with that diagnosis. Elevated transaminases may reflect muscle breakdown as well as liver disease; this patient had a prior biopsy‑proven hepatic steatosis predating semaglutide, and her transaminases persisted after CK normalized, supporting a hepatic rather than purely muscle source. Finally, cardiac involvement (myocarditis or stress cardiomyopathy) can accompany some myositides; the patient’s transient cardiac MRI abnormalities were non‑specific and later normalized, and they did not change the central myopathy diagnosis.

In sum, the combination of characteristic biopsy features, the clinical course of progressive weakness with sustained CK elevation, and prompt response to immunosuppression after withdrawal of semaglutide supports an immune‑mediated necrotising process most consistent with IMNM. The precedent of drug‑triggered IMNM (notably statins) provides a mechanistic and clinical framework for considering GLP‑1 RAs as potential, though unproven, triggers in susceptible hosts. Clinicians should use the temporal pattern of symptoms and CK dynamics, targeted serologies, imaging and muscle histology to distinguish IMNM from acute toxic rhabdomyolysis and other mimics, because treatment and prognosis differ substantially.

## Diagnostic Workup and Reasoning

The diagnostic approach in this case combined serial laboratory data, targeted serologic testing, multimodality imaging, tissue histopathology and a structured assessment of causality to distinguish immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) from other causes of muscle injury and to evaluate a possible link with semaglutide exposure.

Laboratory evaluation
The patient’s serum creatine kinase (CK) showed a clear and progressive rise, from a modest elevation (337 U/L) at the initial hospitalization to a peak of 3992 U/L four months after the single semaglutide dose. This temporal pattern—progressive increase over months rather than an immediate massive spike—argued against classic acute toxic rhabdomyolysis and pointed toward an ongoing myopathic process. Inflammatory markers were mildly elevated (ESR 22 mm/hr, CRP 22.8 mg/L), supporting an inflammatory or immune-mediated etiology rather than an isolated metabolic muscle injury. Thyroid function was normal, excluding hypothyroid myopathy as a contributor. Transaminases (AST 191, ALT 165 U/L) were elevated, but these values were interpreted in the context of known non‑alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD): she had prior liver biopsy–proven steatohepatitis. Because AST and ALT can be increased by both liver and muscle injury, the persistence of transaminitis after CK normalization and the historical liver biopsy supported a hepatic—not purely muscular—origin for her baseline transaminase abnormalities.

Autoantibody testing and implications of seronegativity
A comprehensive autoimmune panel was negative: ANA, anti‑SSA/SSB (except a low‑titer isolated SS‑B of 2.4, discussed below), anti‑Smith, anti‑U1 RNP, ANCA and the myositis-specific/associated antibodies including anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP. Anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP antibodies are strongly associated with IMNM subtypes; their absence places the case in the seronegative IMNM category. It is important for learners to recognize that ~25–35% of IMNM patients can be seronegative, and seronegativity does not exclude the diagnosis when clinical, imaging and pathologic data are concordant. The isolated low‑titer SS‑B without ANA or SS‑A is of uncertain significance here—there is no well-established link between isolated SS‑B and IMNM, and no other features of Sjögren’s syndrome or systemic autoimmune disease were present to suggest an alternative diagnosis.

Muscle MRI
Bilateral thigh MRI showed symmetric proximal muscle edema (most prominent in the vastus lateralis and quadriceps), a pattern typical of inflammatory myopathies. MRI is sensitive for detecting active muscle inflammation and guides biopsy site selection. The symmetric proximal distribution supported an inflammatory/immune myopathy rather than a focal or neurogenic process.

Cardiac assessment
Cardiac testing revealed transient abnormalities: a normal LV ejection fraction on echocardiography, but right ventricular systolic pressure elevation, and cardiac MRI with regional hypokinesis and myocardial edema without late gadolinium enhancement (no definitive fibrosis or infarction). These findings are compatible with evolving myocarditis or a focal stress cardiomyopathy; absence of scarring and subsequent normalization argue against chronic ischemic injury. Cardiac involvement can accompany inflammatory myopathies, so these data neither prove nor disprove IMNM but prompted monitoring and helped exclude an alternate cardiomyopathic cause for weakness or CK elevation.

Muscle biopsy — decisive histopathology
The diagnostic weight in this case rests on muscle histology. The right vastus lateralis biopsy demonstrated dispersed necrotic and regenerating myofibers, prominent macrophage (CD68+) myophagocytosis, membrane attack complex deposition on myofibers, and only rare CD3+ T‑cells with some perivascular inflammation. This combination—marked necrosis with macrophage predominance, complement deposition and relative paucity of lymphocytic infiltrate—is the classical pattern of IMNM and distinguishes it from polymyositis (which shows dense endomysial T‑cell infiltrates) and dermatomyositis (which shows perifascicular atrophy and capillary complement deposition). The membrane attack complex indicates an antibody‑ or complement‑mediated process targeting muscle fibers, consistent with immune‑mediated necrotising pathology even in the absence of identified circulating myositis antibodies.

Liver biopsy — separating hepatic disease from muscle contribution
A repeat liver biopsy confirmed progressive NAFLD with macrovesicular steatosis and minimal portal inflammation, but no features of autoimmune hepatitis. This supported the interpretation that the patient’s baseline transaminase elevations were hepatic in origin and not predominantly from muscle breakdown, particularly because transaminases remained abnormal after CK normalized.

Excluding rhabdomyolysis and toxic myopathy
Several clinical and temporal factors argued against simple drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis or a purely toxic myopathy from semaglutide. Classic drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis produces an abrupt, often very high CK rise shortly after exposure, frequently with myalgias, myoglobinuria (cola‑colored urine), and rapid CK decline after drug cessation and supportive care. In contrast, this patient had only a modest CK initially, developed progressive weakness and a 12‑fold CK increase over months after drug exposure, lacked myoglobinuria or pronounced myalgias, and required immunosuppression for recovery. These features are more consistent with an immune‑mediated process triggered or unmasked by a drug rather than direct myotoxicity that resolves after withdrawal.

Causality assessment using WHO‑UMC criteria
Applying the WHO‑Uppsala Monitoring Centre causality framework, the relationship between semaglutide and IMNM was judged probable. The elements supporting this conclusion included a reasonable temporal association (symptoms and progressive CK elevation began after the semaglutide dose), absence of an alternative explanation despite thorough evaluation (negative myositis serologies, no statin exposure, and no other drug or metabolic cause identified), and clinical improvement after withdrawal of the agent combined with immunotherapy (corticosteroids and IVIG), with sustained remission off immunosuppression. The lack of definitive proof—no autoantibody linking semaglutide to a known mechanism, and inability to exclude a smouldering pre‑existing myopathy fully due to absent pre‑exposure CK measurements—precludes labeling the relationship as certain. Rechallenge was ethically contraindicated given severity, so confirmatory evidence could not be obtained.

Summary of diagnostic reasoning
Taken together, the progressive and sustained CK elevation, proximal symmetric muscle edema on MRI, and—critically—the muscle biopsy findings of necrosis with macrophage predominance and complement deposition established IMNM as the diagnosis. Seronegativity did not exclude IMNM because histopathology and clinical course matched the entity. Other causes (acute rhabdomyolysis, hypothyroid myopathy, statin‑associated HMGCR myopathy, autoimmune hepatitis) were reasonably excluded by history, testing and biopsy. The temporal relationship to semaglutide, lack of alternative triggers, and clinical improvement after stopping the drug plus immunosuppression supported a probable drug‑associated IMNM per WHO‑UMC criteria, while acknowledging that causality cannot be proven without rechallenge or additional cases demonstrating the same pattern.

## Management Strategy and Clinical Course

The treating team approached this patient’s presentation as an immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) with severe features (profound proximal weakness, dysphagia/dysphonia, and markedly elevated creatine kinase), and management combined prompt, high-intensity immunosuppression with close physiologic monitoring and supportive care.

Initial immunosuppression
Given the severity and rapid progression of weakness, the patient received pulse corticosteroid therapy with intravenous methylprednisolone 1000 mg daily for 5 days. High‑dose IV methylprednisolone is a standard initial strategy in severe inflammatory myopathies to produce rapid anti‑inflammatory effects and limit ongoing muscle necrosis. After the pulse course she was transitioned to oral prednisone at approximately 1 mg/kg daily to maintain immunosuppression while the subsequent adjunctive therapy took effect. Using a high-dose steroid bridge minimizes delay in disease control, recognizing that prolonged sole reliance on steroids is often inadequate for IMNM and carries well‑known adverse effects.

Adjunctive therapy: IVIG for severe features
Because of persistent severe weakness and bulbar involvement, the team administered intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) at 2 g/kg divided over 4 days. IVIG is an established adjunct in IMNM, particularly when rapid functional recovery is needed or when patients have dysphagia, respiratory compromise, or poor response to steroids alone. In this case IVIG was used early to accelerate improvement and to allow earlier steroid tapering. The choice and timing were driven by clinical severity rather than serologic status (the patient was seronegative), reflecting the clinical-priority principle in acute management.

Monitoring response: laboratory and functional markers
The patient’s response was monitored with serial CK measurements and clinical assessment of strength and swallowing. A marked decline in CK occurred over approximately 2 weeks after initiation of combined therapy, correlating with progressive functional gains. This temporal pattern — rapid CK fall followed by gradual restoration of strength and bulbar function — is consistent with effective suppression of immune-mediated muscle injury. Serial clinical examinations (motor strength testing, evaluation of ambulation and swallowing) guided decisions about ongoing therapy intensity and timing of steroid taper.

Steroid tapering strategy
Because she experienced sustained clinical and biochemical improvement, clinicians pursued an accelerated steroid taper, successfully discontinuing prednisone over roughly two months. In suspected drug-associated or monophasic immune myopathies that respond briskly to treatment and to withdrawal of the offending agent, shorter steroid courses and more rapid tapers can be appropriate to reduce steroid toxicity. Tapering should be individualized and based on objective recovery (CK normalization, resolving weakness) and absence of relapse. In contrast, idiopathic or refractory IMNM often requires prolonged, multiagent immunosuppression.

Complications and mitigation
The case illustrates important risks of immunosuppressive therapy: during convalescence the patient developed septic shock from a Pseudomonas urinary tract infection requiring readmission. This highlights the necessity for infection vigilance whenever high‑dose steroids and IVIG are used. Mitigation strategies include baseline infection screening, early treatment of suspected infections, patient education on warning signs, and balancing immunosuppression intensity against infection risk. In practice, clinicians should have a low threshold to investigate febrile or systemic symptoms and to provide appropriate cultures and empiric therapy while reassessing immunosuppression.

Cardiac evaluation and follow-up
Because cardiac involvement can accompany inflammatory myopathies, the team obtained echocardiography and cardiac MRI. Initial imaging showed focal myocardial edema and mild wall motion abnormality without late gadolinium enhancement, and the patient had transient troponin elevation and paroxysmal atrial fibrillation. These findings prompted cardiology surveillance. On follow‑up imaging after treatment and clinical recovery, the prior cardiac abnormalities resolved. This course supports routine cardiac screening in IMNM (ECG, troponin, echocardiography; consider cardiac MRI if abnormalities are suspected) and repeat evaluation to document resolution or progression. Detecting cardiac involvement early influences monitoring, supportive care, and may modify immunosuppressive choices.

Medication stewardship and secondary prevention
A central management decision was permanent avoidance of GLP‑1 receptor agonists after a close temporal association between semaglutide exposure and symptom onset. In suspected drug‑associated IMNM, cessation of the offending agent is a key intervention and may permit shorter immunosuppression courses. For this patient’s metabolic needs (obesity and NAFLD), the team opted non‑GLP‑1 alternatives emphasizing lifestyle modification and close hepatology/metabolic follow‑up rather than rechallenge; pharmacologic alternatives should be chosen cautiously and discussed with the patient given the uncertain causality. Documentation of the adverse reaction and communication to outpatient providers are critical for future medication safety.

Long‑term outcome
The patient achieved sustained remission: CK remained within normal limits and she returned to baseline functional status, off immunosuppression for many months. Cardiac abnormalities and arrhythmia resolved without further intervention. This favorable trajectory—early aggressive therapy, withdrawal of the suspected trigger, and close monitoring—supports the possibility that some drug‑associated IMNM cases can be monophasic and responsive to a limited course of immunotherapy. Continued outpatient surveillance is appropriate, with periodic clinical exams and CK checks to detect relapse early.

Clinical lessons applicable to suspected drug-associated IMNM
- Treat severe, rapidly progressive myopathy empirically and promptly with high‑dose corticosteroids and add IVIG when bulbar involvement or severe weakness is present.  
- Monitor both laboratory (CK) and clinical (strength, swallowing, respiratory) endpoints to guide treatment intensity and tapering speed.  
- Actively search for and stop potential offending drugs; a clear temporal relationship supports this step even if autoantibodies are negative.  
- Maintain high vigilance for infections during and after immunosuppression, and screen for cardiac involvement at baseline and during follow‑up.  
- Individualize duration of immunosuppression: some drug‑associated cases may remit after short courses, but ongoing monitoring for relapse is required.

Overall, the management in this case illustrates a principle-driven approach: early aggressive therapy to prevent irreversible muscle damage, targeted adjunctive treatment for severe features, careful evaluation for complications (infectious and cardiac), and conservative long‑term immunosuppression when clinical and biochemical remission is achieved after removal of the suspected trigger.

## Clinical Pearls, Pitfalls, and Future Directions

This case highlights practical lessons for clinicians encountering patients who develop muscle symptoms after exposure to glucagon‑like peptide‑1 receptor agonists (GLP‑1 RAs). While causality cannot be proven from a single report, the temporal relationship, clinicopathologic correlation, and treatment response provide a useful framework for recognition and management.

When to suspect IMNM after GLP‑1 RA exposure
Suspect immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) when a patient exposed to a GLP‑1 RA develops progressive, predominantly proximal limb weakness over weeks to months, particularly if accompanied by bulbar features such as dysphagia or dysphonia. A rising creatine kinase (CK) that progresses rather than rapidly normalizes after drug withdrawal is an important red flag. Mild CK elevation at the time of an acute intolerance reaction (for example with nausea, vomiting, volume depletion) does not exclude subsequent development of an IMNM if weakness and CK continue to worsen after the acute event, as in this case.

Early tests that change management
Initial investigations that influence early decisions include a serum CK to quantify muscle injury, basic inflammatory markers (ESR/CRP), and a targeted myositis autoantibody panel. Muscle MRI of the thighs is helpful to document proximal muscle edema and to guide biopsy site selection. Importantly, a timely muscle biopsy is often decisive: histology demonstrating myofibre necrosis with macrophage infiltration, membrane attack complex deposition, and paucity of dense lymphocytic infiltrates supports IMNM and informs need for immunosuppression. These investigations should be pursued quickly in a patient with progressive weakness and rising CK because biopsy results and imaging can change the trajectory from supportive care to immunotherapy.

Distinguishing toxic rhabdomyolysis from IMNM
Distinction between a direct toxic rhabdomyolysis and IMNM is clinically important because management differs. Toxic rhabdomyolysis typically presents abruptly with very high CK, myalgias, and often cola‑coloured urine; CK usually falls promptly with hydration and drug cessation. IMNM characteristically shows progressive proximal weakness, often with bulbar involvement, and CK that may continue to rise over weeks to months despite stopping an offending drug. Response to immunosuppression (steroids, IVIG) rather than to hydration and simple withdrawal also supports IMNM. Use the temporal course of symptoms and CK kinetics, presence of inflammatory features on MRI, and biopsy findings to distinguish the two.

Remember seronegative IMNM
A substantial minority of IMNM cases are seronegative for established autoantibodies (eg, anti‑HMGCR, anti‑SRP). Negative myositis panels do not exclude IMNM. In seronegative patients, diagnosis rests on the combination of clinical phenotype, imaging, and muscle histopathology. Therefore, do not delay biopsy or empiric treatment solely because autoantibodies are absent when the overall picture is convincing.

Treatment principles
When IMNM is suspected and disease is severe—particularly with bulbar dysfunction or rapidly progressive weakness—initiate high‑dose corticosteroids promptly and consider adjunctive IVIG early, as was effective in this patient. IVIG is appropriate when there is substantial functional impairment, respiratory or bulbar involvement, or concern for steroid‑resistant disease. After clinical and biochemical improvement (improving strength and falling CK), tailor the steroid taper individually and reassess need for further steroid‑sparing agents; some drug‑associated cases may remit with a shorter immunosuppressive course than idiopathic IMNM. Decisions about prolonged immunosuppression should be driven by relapse or persistent activity on clinical exam, CK, and, when indicated, repeat imaging or biopsy.

Safety considerations during immunosuppression
High‑dose steroids and IVIG carry risks that require active mitigation. Screen and monitor for infection; counsel patients about infection signs and consider prophylaxis according to local protocols (eg, Pneumocystis prophylaxis when prolonged high‑dose steroids or additional immunosuppressants are used). Evaluate cardiac status early—baseline ECG and consideration of echocardiography or cardiac MRI—because myocarditis or cardiomyopathy can accompany immune myopathies. Monitor metabolic complications of steroids (glucose, blood pressure) and the hepatic status already present in many patients treated with GLP‑1 RAs. Educate patients about red flags (worsening shortness of breath, rapid swallowing difficulty, new arrhythmia, fevers) and advise urgent reassessment if these occur.

Pharmacovigilance and patient counseling
If IMNM is suspected after exposure to a GLP‑1 RA, report the event to local pharmacovigilance systems and document the suspected association in the medical record. Avoid rechallenge with the same agent; given potential for severe immune‑mediated reactions, rechallenge is generally inadvisable. When counseling patients considering GLP‑1 RAs who have unexplained prior CK elevations or a history of myopathy, discuss uncertainty about rare immune complications and weigh risks and benefits. Baseline CK or symptom screening is reasonable in patients with prior myopathy, but routine screening in all patients is not supported by current evidence.

Research gaps and future directions
This case points to several areas for investigation. Mechanistic studies are needed to determine whether GLP‑1 RAs can trigger autoimmunity in skeletal muscle and, if so, by what pathways (eg, immune modulation, molecular mimicry, or unmasking of subclinical disease). Genetic or immunologic host factors that predispose to such reactions should be sought. Biomarkers that predict risk or early transition from transient muscle injury to IMNM would be clinically useful. Finally, prospective data are needed to define optimal immunotherapy duration for drug‑associated IMNM and to clarify whether outcomes differ from idiopathic IMNM.

Bottom line
Be alert to progressive proximal weakness, bulbar symptoms, and rising CK after GLP‑1 RA exposure. Rapid evaluation with CK, myositis serologies, thigh MRI, and prompt muscle biopsy when indicated can distinguish IMNM from toxic rhabdomyolysis and guide timely immunosuppressive therapy. Report suspected cases, avoid rechallenge, and balance treatment benefits with infection and cardiac monitoring. More research is required to establish causality, risk factors, and best management strategies for potential GLP‑1 RA–associated immune myopathies.
