Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# Getting the Tryptase Right: Sampling Windows, Interpretation, and the Mastocytosis Connection

What makes this case interesting
This patient had multiphasic anaphylaxis after a single clarithromycin dose, required repeated IM epinephrine, and—critically—no tryptase was drawn during the initial episodes. Later testing showed persistently elevated tryptase and a high probability of systemic mastocytosis, though formal diagnostic criteria weren’t fully met because of limited bone marrow sampling. That sequence is all too common: a dramatic anaphylaxis triggers the question “do they have a mast cell disorder?” — but the opportunity to capture the acute tryptase peak is missed, complicating interpretation and downstream work-up.

Why tryptase matters
- Tryptase is the most widely used biomarker of mast cell activation in the acute setting.
- It helps distinguish true anaphylaxis/mast cell degranulation from mimics and is part of the toolkit for identifying underlying clonal mast cell disease (systemic mastocytosis), a known risk factor for severe and recurrent anaphylaxis.
- But interpretation depends entirely on timing and context. A poorly timed sample is worse than no guidance at all unless you know how to recover from that mistake.

Optimal sampling windows — practical, evidence-based
- Acute (peak) sample: as soon as feasible once anaphylaxis is suspected — ideally within the first hour, but definitely obtain a sample within 1–2 hours of symptom onset (peaks typically 1–2 hours) and no later than 4 hours.
- Second (confirmatory) acute sample: if possible, draw a second sample at 1–2 hours after onset to capture the peak if the first was very early.
- Baseline sample: obtain at least one sample ≥24 hours after complete clinical resolution to establish baseline tryptase. For definitive baseline, repeat at 4–6 weeks if there’s any concern for persistent elevation or to document stability.

The 1.2 × baseline + 2 rule — how to confirm mast cell activation
- Use this formula to decide whether an acute rise represents true mast cell activation:
  acute tryptase > (1.2 × baseline tryptase) + 2 ng/mL
- Example: baseline 10 ng/mL → threshold = (1.2 × 10) + 2 = 14 ng/mL. An acute value >14 ng/mL is consistent with mast cell activation.
- This rule is more sensitive and specific than relying on an arbitrary “absolute” acute value alone.

What to do when the first sample is late or missed (like in the presented case)
- If you missed the ideal acute window and only get a sample later during the hospital course, do the following:
  - Obtain a tryptase immediately (this will be interpreted as a “late acute” value).
  - Obtain a true baseline tryptase ≥24 hours after full symptom resolution (and consider repeating at 4–6 weeks to confirm baseline stability).
  - Apply the 1.2 × baseline + 2 calculation using the highest available acute value (even if late) versus the confirmed baseline. A sufficiently large difference still supports activation.
- If the initial acute sample is completely absent, don’t abandon the work-up. Baseline elevation (especially persistent >20 ng/mL) itself raises strong suspicion for clonal mast cell disease and should prompt further evaluation.

When baseline tryptase should make you worry about mastocytosis
- Baseline tryptase >20 ng/mL is one of the WHO minor diagnostic criteria for systemic mastocytosis and should trigger specialist referral.
- Persistent baseline elevations between the normal range upper limit and 20 ng/mL are common and nonspecific; trends matter. Documented, stable elevation above reference range warrants further assessment.
- In the case presented, baseline and peri-event tryptases were elevated and remained high on follow-up — a red flag even though full WHO criteria weren’t demonstrable on limited marrow sampling.

Practical approach to screening for clonal mast cell disease after severe or recurrent anaphylaxis
1. Acute care (ED/ICU)
   - Treat first—don’t wait to draw blood to treat anaphylaxis.
   - Draw a tryptase as soon as it’s safe and feasible (note the clock from symptom onset). If multiple samples are possible, get one within 1–2 hours and another at 24+ hours.
2. Short-term follow-up (first clinic visit / allergist)
   - If acute sampling was suboptimal or absent: obtain a baseline tryptase ≥24 hours after resolution. Repeat baseline at 4–6 weeks if needed.
   - If baseline ≥20 ng/mL or if baseline is persistently elevated, refer to hematology/allergology for further work-up.
3. Specialist work-up (hematology/allergology)
   - Peripheral blood testing for KIT D816V mutation can be a noninvasive first step (sensitivity variable but helpful).
   - Bone marrow biopsy with morphology, immunohistochemistry (CD25, CD2), and molecular testing remains the gold standard to diagnose systemic mastocytosis.
   - Consider abdominal ultrasound for organomegaly, history of suspicious mediator-related symptoms (recurrent flushing, unexplained anaphylaxis, neuropsychiatric symptoms), and allergy testing where safe.
4. Management implications
   - If clonal mast cell disease is found (or strongly suspected), counsel on avoidance, early epinephrine use, and consider referral to a mast cell specialist for risk stratification and preventive strategies (eg, venom immunotherapy if relevant, H1/H2 blockade, bisphosphonates if bone involvement, etc.).
   - Provide an epinephrine auto-injector and clear anaphylaxis action plan. Patients with mast cell disorders have higher risk of severe/fatal anaphylaxis despite low population mortality overall.

Pitfalls and pearls from the case
- Pearl: Don’t delay epinephrine for labs. But do prioritize at least one acute tryptase sample early in the course when practical.
- Pearl: The 1.2 × baseline + 2 rule is your friend — it helps distinguish significant mast cell activation from a mildly elevated absolute tryptase.
- Pitfall: Relying on a single late tryptase without a confirmed baseline can mislead. Always obtain a baseline ≥24 hours after symptoms resolve.
- Pearl: Recurrent, severe, or multiphasic anaphylaxis should raise suspicion for an underlying mast cell disorder even if the WHO criteria aren’t immediately fulfilled. Persistently elevated baseline tryptase or supportive clinical features (hepatomegaly, bone symptoms, neuropsychiatric symptoms) justify specialist referral.
- Operational pearl: In many EDs, preprinted anaphylaxis order sets should include “tryptase x1 now, repeat at 1–2 h if possible, baseline ≥24 h” to avoid missed diagnostic windows.

Take-home messages
- In suspected anaphylaxis draw tryptase ASAP (ideally within 1–2 hours, no later than 4 hours), and obtain a baseline ≥24 hours after resolution.
- Use the 1.2 × baseline + 2 formula to define significant mast cell activation.
- If the initial acute sample was missed, get an immediate tryptase and then a confirmed baseline; persistent baseline elevation (especially >20 ng/mL) mandates further work-up for systemic mastocytosis.
- Recurrent or multiphasic anaphylaxis is an indication to screen for clonal mast cell disease and to refer to allergy/hematology for targeted investigation and management.

This case is a tidy reminder: treating anaphylaxis comes first, but getting the tryptase timing right when you can is the difference between an unanswered question and a clear path toward diagnosis and safer long-term care.
