Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

## Beyond Biphasic: Making Sense of Biphasic, Multiphasic, and Refractory Anaphylaxis

What makes this case interesting
A woman in her 60s developed classic anaphylaxis one hour after her first dose of clarithromycin and then had three further discrete relapses over the first 12 hours — four treated anaphylactic episodes in total. Each relapse required intramuscular epinephrine and the intensity waned with each episode. Workup showed persistently raised tryptase and features suspicious for systemic mast cell disease, though formal diagnostic criteria were not met. This is a useful real-world prompt to clarify terminology (biphasic, multiphasic, sustained, refractory), give practical bedside clues that change management, and highlight documentation and follow-up priorities.

Why the words matter
- Biphasic anaphylaxis: initial reaction → complete/near-complete clinical resolution → recurrence of symptoms hours later without re-exposure. Classically “two phases.”
- Multiphasic anaphylaxis: more than one recurrence (3+ total phases), as in this case. Rare, poorly characterized.
- Sustained anaphylaxis: ongoing mediator activity where symptoms wax and wane or never fully resolve despite therapy (not true resolution between episodes).
- Refractory anaphylaxis: persistent hypotension/airway compromise despite adequate doses of epinephrine (i.e., not responding to treatment).

These labels aren’t just semantics — they influence monitoring intensity, disposition decisions, and therapeutic choices (e.g., escalation to continuous IV epinephrine).

Practical bedside clues: ongoing absorption vs true recurrence
When a patient re-deteriorates after apparent improvement, ask: is this persistent absorption of antigen, sustained mediator release, or a true delayed secondary wave of mediator release?

Clues suggesting ongoing absorption or ongoing exposure
- Known ingested or depot source that can keep releasing allergen (oral drug with slow GI absorption, extended-release formulations, transdermal patches, parenteral depot).
- Symptoms that relapse very quickly after stopping epinephrine and before any appreciable asymptomatic interval.
- Progressive or fluctuating course without a clear symptom-free period.
- Persistent high or rising tryptase in early serial samples (if available).
- Ongoing GI symptoms that suggest continued absorption.

Clues suggesting true biphasic/multiphasic recurrence
- A clear asymptomatic or near-asymptomatic interval (hours) between episodes.
- Episodes separated by several hours with gradually decreasing severity (as in this case).
- No ongoing source of allergen identified and no further exposures.
- Response to standard intramuscular epinephrine with return of vitals to baseline, followed by a delayed recurrence.
- Tryptase: ideally early sample near peak (1–4 hours from onset) and a baseline ≥24 hours later — biphasic recurrences may have transient peaks with return toward baseline between episodes.

Clues suggesting refractory anaphylaxis
- Failure to respond to appropriately dosed intramuscular epinephrine (0.01 mg/kg, up to 0.5 mg adults).
- Ongoing profound hypotension, poor perfusion, worsening oxygenation despite repeated IM doses.
- Need for vasopressor infusion, airway intervention, or ICU-level support.

How the distinction changes what you do next
Monitoring and disposition
- Single, uncomplicated anaphylaxis that fully resolves with a single IM epinephrine dose: many guidelines support observation (4–6 hours) before discharge, risk-stratified by patient factors.
- Biphasic or suspected multiphasic courses: extend observation — at minimum 12–24 hours depending on severity, number of epinephrine doses given, comorbidities, and potential mast cell disease. Consider ICU monitoring for recurrent hypotension, hypoxaemia, or repeated epinephrine needs.
- Sustained or refractory anaphylaxis: admit to ICU, consider invasive monitoring, and move quickly to vasopressor support.

Therapeutic escalation
- Recurrent episodes that respond reliably to IM epinephrine: giving additional IM doses is appropriate. If recurrences occur frequently or are severe, consider continuous IV epinephrine infusion (careful titration in ICU) rather than repeated IM injections—this is particularly relevant if relapses are frequent or hemodynamic instability recurs rapidly.
- Refractory anaphylaxis: early IV epinephrine infusion at low-dose titratable rates + aggressive fluid resuscitation; consider additional measures (H1/H2 antihistamines, inhaled bronchodilators, glucagon for beta-blocked patients).
- Steroids: no high-quality evidence that steroids prevent biphasic reactions; routine corticosteroids are increasingly questioned. They may be used at clinician discretion for prolonged allergic inflammation, but don’t rely on them to prevent relapses.
- Antihistamines are helpful for cutaneous symptoms but are adjunctive — they don’t treat airway compromise or hypotension.

Practical ED/ICU approach when facing recurrent episodes (apply with clinical judgment)
- Reassess airway, breathing, circulation immediately; treat life threats first.
- Give IM epinephrine promptly for each recurrence (0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg adults). If using auto-injectors, remember many are 0.3 mg — underdose for many adults.
- If the patient needs >2–3 IM epinephrine doses within a short timeframe, or relapses persist, consider ICU transfer and early initiation of an IV epinephrine infusion rather than more IM doses.
- Secure IV access and give fluid boluses liberally for hypotension (crystalloid 20 mL/kg or clinician-guided).
- Use adjuncts (oxygen, nebulized bronchodilators, H1/H2 blockers) as indicated.
- Avoid unnecessary delay for confirmatory testing; treat first, test later.

Documentation and follow-up — what to record to guide future care
Good documentation matters more than ever after a multiphasic course. Include:
- Clear chronology: time of exposure, time of symptom onset, times of each epinephrine dose, times of clinical improvement and relapse.
- Exact epinephrine formulation/dose/route (auto-injector dose vs prepared IM or IV dose).
- Vital signs and objective measures at each phase (BP, HR, sat, RR).
- Any possible ongoing antigen source (medication formulation, depot injections, residual food).
- Tryptase sampling: try for 3 samples if possible — earliest feasible (do not delay therapy), 1–4 hours from onset (near peak), and baseline ≥24 hours after full resolution. Document times of draws relative to symptom onset.
- Contact allergist/immunology and arrange outpatient follow-up: consider mast cell disorder evaluation if tryptase is persistently elevated or clinical history suggests (recurrent severe anaphylaxis, atypical triggers, hepatosplenomegaly).
- Prescribe and teach epinephrine auto-injector use. Note device dose. For adults >50 kg, a 0.5 mg IM dose is preferred in many guidelines; current commonly available auto-injectors are 0.3 mg — counsel accordingly and consider local device availability.

Special point: mast cell disease
Systemic mastocytosis or elevated baseline mast cell burden can predispose to severe, recurrent, or atypical anaphylaxis. In this case, persistently elevated tryptase and hepatomegaly increased suspicion though formal criteria were not met. If mast cell disease is suspected:
- Ensure allergy/immunology referral.
- Counsel patient about higher risk of severe reactions, carry epinephrine, and avoid known triggers.
- Avoid unnecessary provocative testing (skin or challenge testing) if risk of severe anaphylaxis is high — weigh risk/benefit.

Clinical pearls / Take-home messages
- Terminology matters: biphasic = one recurrence after resolution; multiphasic = multiple recurrences; sustained = ongoing symptoms without true resolution; refractory = not responding to adequate epinephrine.
- Distinguish ongoing absorption (think source still in place) from true delayed recurrence (clear asymptomatic interval). That distinction helps decide between extended observation, source control, or escalation to infusion therapy.
- Treat each recurrence as anaphylaxis: immediate IM epinephrine is first-line. If multiple IM doses are required in a short time, consider ICU and early IV epinephrine infusion.
- Don’t rely on steroids to prevent relapses — evidence is weak. Use them selectively, not routinely, for biphasic prevention.
- Document timeline, doses, vitals, and tryptase sample times clearly — this data guides future diagnosis and management.
- If baseline or peak tryptase is elevated or clinical suspicion for mast cell disease exists, arrange specialist follow-up rather than trying to “prove” the allergy in the acute setting.

This clarithromycin case reminds us that although multiphasic anaphylaxis is rare, it changes the stakes for monitoring and disposition. Think sequentially: identify source → treat aggressively and promptly → watch for a silent interval → if recurrences are frequent or severe, move from IM rescue to controlled IV therapy and ICU-level care. Document meticulously and arrange specialist follow‑up when the course is atypical or tryptase is elevated.
