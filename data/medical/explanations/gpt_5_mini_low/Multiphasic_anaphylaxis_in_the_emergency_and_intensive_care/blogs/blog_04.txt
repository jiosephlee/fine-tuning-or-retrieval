Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# Steroids in Anaphylaxis: Time to Step Away

What makes this case interesting
A woman in her 60s developed multiphasic anaphylaxis after a single oral dose of clarithromycin. She responded to intramuscular epinephrine each time but had multiple relapses in the first 12 hours. After the first relapse she was given systemic corticosteroids (prednisone IV and a dose of dexamethasone)—reflecting a common, reflexive practice—but her course continued to require additional epinephrine doses before calming down. This is exactly the sort of case that forces clinicians to ask: do steroids actually prevent biphasic (or multiphasic) anaphylaxis, or are we giving a drug that adds risk without benefit?

Short answer: current evidence argues steroids do not prevent biphasic anaphylaxis and may cause harm or unnecessary downstream care. The clarithromycin case illustrates why routine steroid use as “insurance” is increasingly hard to justify.

Why steroids became “standard” — and why that’s likely wrong
- Historical practice: steroids were historically given in an attempt to prevent delayed recurrence of symptoms (biphasic reactions). That practice became entrenched despite weak evidence.
- Pharmacology mismatch: systemic corticosteroids act mainly through genomic mechanisms that take hours to days to alter inflammatory cell function. Anaphylaxis—and early biphasic relapses—are driven by rapid mast cell/basophil degranulation and mediator release (histamine, tryptase, leukotrienes, prostaglandins). Steroids don’t quickly block those mediators or reverse vasodilation/hypotension in the acute phase.
- Evidence collapse: systematic reviews and large series fail to show a protective effect of steroids against biphasic reactions. Some analyses even associate prehospital steroids with higher admission/ICU rates (likely confounding, but concerning). In the presented case, steroids were given after the first relapse with no obvious prevention of the subsequent second relapse—an experience commonly reported in real-world practice.

Clinical consequences of reflex steroid use
- Delays to other care or masking of symptoms (steroids can change skin findings without addressing hemodynamics).
- Side effects: hyperglycemia (relevant in this patient with diabetes), immunosuppression, psychiatric effects, and potential for longer hospital observation "just in case."
- Resource use: steroids may promote longer observation or admission without demonstrable benefit.

What we should focus on instead — a steroid-sparing, practical approach
Grounded in this case and current best practice, prioritize timely epinephrine, appropriate supportive care, and structured observation/discharge planning.

1) Immediate treatment
- Epinephrine is first-line. Adult dosing: 0.01 mg/kg IM up to 0.5 mg (pragmatic adult dose often given as 0.5 mg). Be aware many auto-injectors are 0.3 mg and can underdose adults >50 kg. In this case the first two IM doses were 0.3 mg auto-injectors and subsequent doses were 0.5 mg IM.
- Repeat IM epinephrine every 5–15 minutes as needed for persistent/worsening symptoms. Escalate to intravenous bolus or continuous infusion only for refractory or severe shock under ICU-level care.
- Supplemental oxygen, aggressive IV fluids for hypotension, airway monitoring, and rapid recognition of respiratory compromise. Antihistamines (H1 blockers) and H2 blockers can help cutaneous symptoms but are adjuncts—not substitutes for epinephrine.

2) Observation and monitoring (risk-stratified; use local protocol)
- Because biphasic/multiphasic relapse can occur, observe patients after full clinical resolution. Exact duration is debated; many biphasic events occur within 6–12 hours but can occur later. In multiphasic cases like this one, relapses clustered in the first 12 hours.
- Monitor vitals, oxygenation, and clinical signs of airway compromise. Consider higher-acuity monitoring (telemetry/ICU) if:
  - Severe initial presentation (hypotension, hypoxaemia, respiratory failure)
  - Need for multiple epinephrine doses
  - Ongoing hemodynamic or respiratory instability
  - Concern for poor social support or inability to return for care

3) Use steroids selectively — not routinely
- Avoid routine systemic corticosteroids solely to prevent biphasic reactions.
- Reserve steroids for specific indications (e.g., severe bronchospasm not responding to standard measures, concomitant adrenal insufficiency, or other steroid-responsive comorbidities).
- If given, document the rationale, dose, and monitor for steroid-related complications (notably hyperglycemia in diabetics).

4) Diagnostics and specialist follow-up
- Tryptase testing: obtain samples per guidelines when feasible—one as soon as possible, a second 1–2 hours (but within 4 hours) after symptom onset when peak levels are expected, and a baseline sample ≥24 hours after full resolution. In this case only later samples were obtained; tryptase was elevated and raised concern for an underlying mast cell disorder.
- Avoid routine provocative allergy testing in the acute inpatient period if it risks severe re-exposure; arrange outpatient allergy/immunology follow-up.
- If raised baseline tryptase or clinical features suggest mast cell disorder, refer to allergy/hematology for further workup (bone marrow biopsy, KIT mutation testing as indicated).

5) Discharge planning and patient education
- Prescribe and teach use of an epinephrine auto-injector (dose appropriate for patient weight). In this case she was discharged with a 0.3 mg auto-injector and training—consider 0.5 mg device for heavier adults when available and appropriate.
- Start/continue H1 antihistamine for symptomatic control as needed (e.g., levocetirizine as in the case).
- Provide written action plan, advice on allergen avoidance (clarithromycin noted as likely trigger), and urgent follow-up with allergy/immunology. Advise return for recurrence of symptoms or if new respiratory or circulatory compromise occurs.

Clinical pearls from this case
- Steroids are not a reliable tool to prevent biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis; their onset of action is too slow to affect immediate mediator-driven relapses.
- Epinephrine remains the only intervention shown to rapidly reverse life-threatening features—dose and timely administration matter. Be mindful that 0.3 mg auto-injectors may underdose many adults.
- Recurrent episodes can still occur despite appropriate treatment; consider higher-acuity observation when multiple epinephrine doses are required.
- Elevated baseline tryptase or atypical courses should prompt evaluation for mast cell disorders, which increase risk of severe anaphylaxis.

Key take-home messages
- Do not give systemic corticosteroids routinely to prevent biphasic anaphylaxis.
- Treat acute anaphylaxis promptly with appropriately dosed IM epinephrine (0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg) and supportive care.
- Observe patients after resolution; extend monitoring for high-risk cases (severe initial reaction, multiple epinephrine doses, persistent instability).
- Use steroids only for specific indications, not as routine prophylaxis against relapse.
- Arrange allergy/mast cell disorder follow-up and clear discharge instructions, including epinephrine auto-injector training.

Final thought
Medicine prefers simple “do X to prevent Y” fixes, and steroids felt like one. But the pathophysiology and the evidence don’t support routine steroid use to prevent biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis. This clarithromycin case is a reminder to double down on what works—rapid, correctly dosed epinephrine and thoughtful monitoring—and to retire interventions that add risk without proven benefit.
