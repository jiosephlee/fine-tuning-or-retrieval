Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# Watchful Waiting or Wires and Walls? Risk‑Stratified Observation After Anaphylaxis

What makes this case interesting: a woman in her 60s developed multiphasic anaphylaxis after a single 500 mg oral dose of clarithromycin — four clinically apparent anaphylactic episodes over the first 12 hours, each responding to intramuscular epinephrine. She required escalation from ED to acute ward to ICU monitoring, received repeated IM epinephrine doses (initially 0.3 mg auto‑injector, later 0.5 mg IM), and ultimately stabilized without needing continuous IV epinephrine. Work‑up revealed persistently raised tryptase and possible systemic mast cell disease, but not enough to meet WHO diagnostic criteria.

This case sits at the uncomfortable intersection of three realities:
- Biphasic/multiphasic anaphylaxis exists and can be clinically dramatic despite appropriate immediate management.
- Incidence is low but reported rates vary widely (most series ~4–6%), so routine long admissions for all is impractical.
- Evidence for how long to observe or when to escalate monitoring (telemetry/ICU) is sparse; guidelines differ and steroid use to prevent relapse is not supported.

The clinical conundrum for EDs and ICUs: do you send the patient home after a 4–6 hour observation with no symptoms, admit to a monitored unit, or place them in the ICU with continuous infusion and telemetry? Below I break down the practical risk‑stratified approach I use, anchored to the case and current data.

Why relapse happens (quick primer)
- True biphasic/multiphasic reactions reflect ongoing mediator activity after apparent clinical resolution, not necessarily re‑exposure. Time to recurrence can be within hours up to several days (rare reports).
- Underlying mast cell disorders (elevated baseline tryptase, mastocytosis spectrum) increase susceptibility to severe, recurrent, or idiopathic reactions — as in this patient who had raised tryptase and hepatomegaly suggestive of a mast cell disorder.

Important modifiers that increase relapse risk (drawn from the case and literature signals)
- Severe initial presentation (hypotension, hypoxemia) — present in this case.
- Delay to epinephrine or underdosing initially (this patient received a 0.3 mg auto‑injector initially — likely underdosed for a 104 kg adult).
- Need for multiple epinephrine doses to control symptoms.
- Suspected or known mast cell disorder or persistently elevated tryptase.
- Ongoing absorption risk from ingested or depot agents (oral/IM medications) — in this case, only a single PO dose was taken and stopped, but oral absorption timing may allow delayed mediator triggers.

Practical, risk‑stratified observation framework
Below is a pragmatic triage for disposition and monitoring. Customize by local resources and clinical judgment.

1) Low risk — ED observation 4–6 hours (suitable for many single‑episode anaphylaxis)
- Candidate features: mild symptoms only (urticaria/angioedema without hypotension/respiratory compromise), rapid complete response to a single appropriate IM epinephrine dose, no delay to epinephrine, no comorbidities that increase risk (cardiac disease, pregnancy), normal baseline vitals and exam, reliable outpatient follow‑up and access to care.
- Monitoring: continued clinical reassessment, consider one additional period of observation after last symptom. No routine telemetry.
- Disposition: discharge with epinephrine auto‑injector(s), antihistamines, education, and clear return precautions.

2) Intermediate risk — extended observation or monitored bed (6–24 hours)
- Candidate features: severe initial presentation that responded to epinephrine but had hypotension or hypoxemia; delayed administration of epinephrine; need for more than one epinephrine dose; significant comorbidity (eg, cardiopulmonary disease); high BMI where initial dosing may have been suboptimal; uncertain exposure/ongoing absorption risk.
- Monitoring: step‑down or acute medical ward with frequent vitals (q1–2h) and nursing experienced with anaphylaxis; consider telemetry if arrhythmia risk or ongoing epinephrine administration is anticipated; check tryptase (ideally acutely and a baseline later) unless it would delay care.
- Disposition: admit for 6–24 hours. In the reported case, initial ED improvement followed by two relapses justified escalation to ICU; this aligns with intermediate‑risk patients who relapsed.

3) High risk — ICU-level monitoring and consideration of continuous therapy
- Candidate features: refractory anaphylaxis (fails to respond adequately to repeated IM epinephrine), multiple relapses despite appropriate IM epinephrine, hemodynamic instability requiring pressors/vasopressors, need for airway support, known mast cell disorder with severe prior reactions, recurrent severe episodes in short succession (multiphasic), or arrhythmia risk.
- Monitoring/interventions: ICU with continuous hemodynamic monitoring/telemetry, nursing expertise for anaphylaxis, low threshold for continuous IV epinephrine infusion if frequent IM dosing is required or hypotension is persistent; consider invasive arterial monitoring if unstable; repeat labs/tryptase sampling per allergy protocol.
- In the presented case, the patient’s first relapse after ED prompted ICU admission given recurrent anaphylaxis and significant initial hypoxemia/hypotension — an appropriate choice.

When does telemetry add value?
- Telemetry is most helpful when: high risk of hemodynamic instability, need for continuous or frequent vasoactive agents, underlying cardiac disease, arrhythmia during episodes, or when continuous infusion of epinephrine is used. It’s less necessary for uncomplicated single‑episode anaphylaxis that fully resolves.

Epinephrine dosing points (practical and critical)
- Recommended adult IM dose: 0.01 mg/kg, maximum 0.5 mg (most adults ~0.5 mg). Many auto‑injectors are 0.3 mg — acceptable in some adults but represents underdosing in heavier patients.
- If initial IM epi (correct dose/timing) doesn’t control symptoms, repeat IM every 5–15 minutes as needed. Persistent hypotension or frequent relapses → consider IV epinephrine infusion in monitored setting.
- The case highlights the consequences of underdosing: the first two episodes responded to 0.3 mg but additional episodes required 0.5 mg IM. Ensure ED teams have weight‑based mindset and ready access to appropriate doses.

Steroids and antihistamines — where we stand
- Antihistamines are used for cutaneous symptoms but do not treat airway/cardiovascular collapse.
- Routine systemic corticosteroids to prevent biphasic reactions are not supported by high‑quality evidence. Multiple systematic reviews show no clear reduction in biphasic events; steroids have adverse effects and may increase admissions. Use steroids selectively rather than reflexively.
- In the presented case, corticosteroids were added after relapse — understandable given local practice and uncertainty — but they may not prevent further episodes.

Tryptase testing and mast cell disease
- If severe, unusual, or recurrent anaphylaxis: measure tryptase acutely (ideally within 1–4 hours of symptom onset when feasible) and a baseline sample ≥24 hours after resolution. Elevated baseline tryptase suggests possible underlying mast cell disorder and alters counseling/monitoring.
- This patient had elevated tryptase during later sampling and baseline elevation on follow‑up, raising suspicion for systemic mastocytosis — a plausible explanation for multiphasic course.

Putting it together — suggested checklist for disposition decision in ED
- Was epinephrine given promptly and in an effective dose? (If delayed/underdosed → escalate observation.)
- Was there hypotension, hypoxemia, or respiratory compromise? (If yes → higher observation level.)
- Were multiple epinephrine doses required? (If yes → consider monitored admission.)
- Is there a history or lab evidence suggesting mast cell disorder? (If yes → consider ICU/monitored admission.)
- Is ongoing absorption of allergen likely (oral/depot exposure)? (If yes → extended observation.)
- Are there comorbidities (cardiac disease, pregnancy, poor access to care) that increase risk? (If yes → escalate.)

Closing clinical pearls
- Severe initial anaphylaxis, delayed or underdosed epinephrine, and need for multiple doses predict higher risk of relapse — act accordingly with longer/monitored observation.
- For adults >50 kg, aim for 0.5 mg IM epinephrine when possible; many 0.3 mg auto‑injectors underdose heavier adults and can be associated with need for rescue doses.
- Routine corticosteroids to prevent biphasic reactions are not evidence‑based. Use selectively.
- If recurrent episodes occur, move to monitored setting and consider continuous IV epinephrine rather than repeated frequent IM dosing.
- Measure tryptase when presentation is severe, prolonged, or recurrent — acute and baseline samples improve diagnostic yield and may reveal occult mast cell disorders.

Take‑home messages
- Not all anaphylaxis is the same. Risk‑stratified observation (ED 4–6 h vs monitored 6–24 h vs ICU) balances patient safety and resource use.
- Escalate monitoring if severe initial collapse, delayed/underdosed epinephrine, repeated epinephrine requirements, suspected mast cell disease, or ongoing exposure risk.
- Prioritize correct epinephrine dosing (0.01 mg/kg, up to 0.5 mg IM) and use ICU/continuous infusion for refractory or rapidly relapsing cases.
- Don’t reflexively give steroids to “prevent” biphasic reactions — the evidence is weak.
- Check tryptase in severe or atypical cases; elevated baseline changes future risk counseling and disposition planning.

This multiphasic clarithromycin case reminds us that vigilance, appropriate epinephrine dosing, and thoughtful, risk‑stratified monitoring — not blanket rules — provide the best route to safe, resource‑sensible care.
