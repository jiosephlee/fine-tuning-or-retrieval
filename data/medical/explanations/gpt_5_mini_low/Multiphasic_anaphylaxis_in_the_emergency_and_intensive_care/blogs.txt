Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

## Beyond Biphasic: Making Sense of Biphasic, Multiphasic, and Refractory Anaphylaxis

What makes this case interesting
A woman in her 60s developed classic anaphylaxis one hour after her first dose of clarithromycin and then had three further discrete relapses over the first 12 hours — four treated anaphylactic episodes in total. Each relapse required intramuscular epinephrine and the intensity waned with each episode. Workup showed persistently raised tryptase and features suspicious for systemic mast cell disease, though formal diagnostic criteria were not met. This is a useful real-world prompt to clarify terminology (biphasic, multiphasic, sustained, refractory), give practical bedside clues that change management, and highlight documentation and follow-up priorities.

Why the words matter
- Biphasic anaphylaxis: initial reaction → complete/near-complete clinical resolution → recurrence of symptoms hours later without re-exposure. Classically “two phases.”
- Multiphasic anaphylaxis: more than one recurrence (3+ total phases), as in this case. Rare, poorly characterized.
- Sustained anaphylaxis: ongoing mediator activity where symptoms wax and wane or never fully resolve despite therapy (not true resolution between episodes).
- Refractory anaphylaxis: persistent hypotension/airway compromise despite adequate doses of epinephrine (i.e., not responding to treatment).

These labels aren’t just semantics — they influence monitoring intensity, disposition decisions, and therapeutic choices (e.g., escalation to continuous IV epinephrine).

Practical bedside clues: ongoing absorption vs true recurrence
When a patient re-deteriorates after apparent improvement, ask: is this persistent absorption of antigen, sustained mediator release, or a true delayed secondary wave of mediator release?

Clues suggesting ongoing absorption or ongoing exposure
- Known ingested or depot source that can keep releasing allergen (oral drug with slow GI absorption, extended-release formulations, transdermal patches, parenteral depot).
- Symptoms that relapse very quickly after stopping epinephrine and before any appreciable asymptomatic interval.
- Progressive or fluctuating course without a clear symptom-free period.
- Persistent high or rising tryptase in early serial samples (if available).
- Ongoing GI symptoms that suggest continued absorption.

Clues suggesting true biphasic/multiphasic recurrence
- A clear asymptomatic or near-asymptomatic interval (hours) between episodes.
- Episodes separated by several hours with gradually decreasing severity (as in this case).
- No ongoing source of allergen identified and no further exposures.
- Response to standard intramuscular epinephrine with return of vitals to baseline, followed by a delayed recurrence.
- Tryptase: ideally early sample near peak (1–4 hours from onset) and a baseline ≥24 hours later — biphasic recurrences may have transient peaks with return toward baseline between episodes.

Clues suggesting refractory anaphylaxis
- Failure to respond to appropriately dosed intramuscular epinephrine (0.01 mg/kg, up to 0.5 mg adults).
- Ongoing profound hypotension, poor perfusion, worsening oxygenation despite repeated IM doses.
- Need for vasopressor infusion, airway intervention, or ICU-level support.

How the distinction changes what you do next
Monitoring and disposition
- Single, uncomplicated anaphylaxis that fully resolves with a single IM epinephrine dose: many guidelines support observation (4–6 hours) before discharge, risk-stratified by patient factors.
- Biphasic or suspected multiphasic courses: extend observation — at minimum 12–24 hours depending on severity, number of epinephrine doses given, comorbidities, and potential mast cell disease. Consider ICU monitoring for recurrent hypotension, hypoxaemia, or repeated epinephrine needs.
- Sustained or refractory anaphylaxis: admit to ICU, consider invasive monitoring, and move quickly to vasopressor support.

Therapeutic escalation
- Recurrent episodes that respond reliably to IM epinephrine: giving additional IM doses is appropriate. If recurrences occur frequently or are severe, consider continuous IV epinephrine infusion (careful titration in ICU) rather than repeated IM injections—this is particularly relevant if relapses are frequent or hemodynamic instability recurs rapidly.
- Refractory anaphylaxis: early IV epinephrine infusion at low-dose titratable rates + aggressive fluid resuscitation; consider additional measures (H1/H2 antihistamines, inhaled bronchodilators, glucagon for beta-blocked patients).
- Steroids: no high-quality evidence that steroids prevent biphasic reactions; routine corticosteroids are increasingly questioned. They may be used at clinician discretion for prolonged allergic inflammation, but don’t rely on them to prevent relapses.
- Antihistamines are helpful for cutaneous symptoms but are adjunctive — they don’t treat airway compromise or hypotension.

Practical ED/ICU approach when facing recurrent episodes (apply with clinical judgment)
- Reassess airway, breathing, circulation immediately; treat life threats first.
- Give IM epinephrine promptly for each recurrence (0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg adults). If using auto-injectors, remember many are 0.3 mg — underdose for many adults.
- If the patient needs >2–3 IM epinephrine doses within a short timeframe, or relapses persist, consider ICU transfer and early initiation of an IV epinephrine infusion rather than more IM doses.
- Secure IV access and give fluid boluses liberally for hypotension (crystalloid 20 mL/kg or clinician-guided).
- Use adjuncts (oxygen, nebulized bronchodilators, H1/H2 blockers) as indicated.
- Avoid unnecessary delay for confirmatory testing; treat first, test later.

Documentation and follow-up — what to record to guide future care
Good documentation matters more than ever after a multiphasic course. Include:
- Clear chronology: time of exposure, time of symptom onset, times of each epinephrine dose, times of clinical improvement and relapse.
- Exact epinephrine formulation/dose/route (auto-injector dose vs prepared IM or IV dose).
- Vital signs and objective measures at each phase (BP, HR, sat, RR).
- Any possible ongoing antigen source (medication formulation, depot injections, residual food).
- Tryptase sampling: try for 3 samples if possible — earliest feasible (do not delay therapy), 1–4 hours from onset (near peak), and baseline ≥24 hours after full resolution. Document times of draws relative to symptom onset.
- Contact allergist/immunology and arrange outpatient follow-up: consider mast cell disorder evaluation if tryptase is persistently elevated or clinical history suggests (recurrent severe anaphylaxis, atypical triggers, hepatosplenomegaly).
- Prescribe and teach epinephrine auto-injector use. Note device dose. For adults >50 kg, a 0.5 mg IM dose is preferred in many guidelines; current commonly available auto-injectors are 0.3 mg — counsel accordingly and consider local device availability.

Special point: mast cell disease
Systemic mastocytosis or elevated baseline mast cell burden can predispose to severe, recurrent, or atypical anaphylaxis. In this case, persistently elevated tryptase and hepatomegaly increased suspicion though formal criteria were not met. If mast cell disease is suspected:
- Ensure allergy/immunology referral.
- Counsel patient about higher risk of severe reactions, carry epinephrine, and avoid known triggers.
- Avoid unnecessary provocative testing (skin or challenge testing) if risk of severe anaphylaxis is high — weigh risk/benefit.

Clinical pearls / Take-home messages
- Terminology matters: biphasic = one recurrence after resolution; multiphasic = multiple recurrences; sustained = ongoing symptoms without true resolution; refractory = not responding to adequate epinephrine.
- Distinguish ongoing absorption (think source still in place) from true delayed recurrence (clear asymptomatic interval). That distinction helps decide between extended observation, source control, or escalation to infusion therapy.
- Treat each recurrence as anaphylaxis: immediate IM epinephrine is first-line. If multiple IM doses are required in a short time, consider ICU and early IV epinephrine infusion.
- Don’t rely on steroids to prevent relapses — evidence is weak. Use them selectively, not routinely, for biphasic prevention.
- Document timeline, doses, vitals, and tryptase sample times clearly — this data guides future diagnosis and management.
- If baseline or peak tryptase is elevated or clinical suspicion for mast cell disease exists, arrange specialist follow-up rather than trying to “prove” the allergy in the acute setting.

This clarithromycin case reminds us that although multiphasic anaphylaxis is rare, it changes the stakes for monitoring and disposition. Think sequentially: identify source → treat aggressively and promptly → watch for a silent interval → if recurrences are frequent or severe, move from IM rescue to controlled IV therapy and ICU-level care. Document meticulously and arrange specialist follow‑up when the course is atypical or tryptase is elevated.



## Dose Matters: Why 0.5 mg IM Epinephrine Should Be Standard in Adults

What makes this worth your attention? A recent multiphasic anaphylaxis case highlights a common and correctable blind spot in adult anaphylaxis care: early use of a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto‑injector in a patient >50 kg, followed by recurrent episodes that required additional IM epinephrine doses. The case illustrates two things we see in practice: (1) delay or underdosing of epinephrine increases the chance of needing escalation, and (2) use of convenient 0.3 mg auto‑injectors in adults can be an underdose for many patients. If you care for adults in the ED or ICU, this matters — dose matters.

Why 0.5 mg IM?
- Guideline rationale: Recommended adult IM epinephrine dosing is 0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg. For the majority of adults (weight ≥50 kg) that tops out at 0.5 mg. This dose targets sufficient alpha and beta receptor stimulation to reverse laryngeal edema, bronchospasm and distributive shock.
- Pharmacology in a sentence: IM epinephrine (vastus lateralis) yields rapid systemic vasoconstriction, bronchodilation and improved cardiac output. Under‑dosing risks incomplete reversal and earlier relapse or need for repeat dosing.
- Real world problem: Many settings only stock or routinely use 0.3 mg auto‑injectors as the “default” adult device. For a 70–100+ kg adult, 0.3 mg is below 0.01 mg/kg and may be insufficient as the first therapeutic dose.

What the case teaches us
- In the report, the patient received 0.3 mg IM via auto‑injector during initial ED resuscitation and had an apparent response, but then developed multiple relapses (multiphasic course) requiring further IM epinephrine (0.5 mg) to terminate subsequent episodes. Each relapse required repeat dosing. The authors note that initial use of 0.3 mg is likely an underdose for many adults and can contribute to escalation of care.
- The case also highlights the tension we face: auto‑injectors minimize delay and hesitancy in time‑sensitive settings but many available adult auto‑injectors deliver only 0.3 mg.

Data and outcomes (practical interpretation)
- Observational data and case series (including the series discussed in the case report) show patients who receive an initial epinephrine dose <0.5 mg are more likely to need additional doses or advanced interventions. This is consistent with the dosing logic and with guideline dosing that targets 0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg.
- Safety profile: IM epinephrine at recommended dosing is generally well tolerated. Adverse cardiovascular events are rare relative to the immediate risk of untreated anaphylaxis. In older patients with significant cardiac disease, individualized consideration is reasonable, but this is not a justification for routine underdosing in otherwise appropriate patients.

Operational strategies — how to get to 0.5 mg quickly and safely
You want speed (no delay) + correct dose. Here are tactics that work in EDs and ICUs.

1) Make 0.5 mg the default for adults ≥50 kg
- Protocolize weight threshold (≥50 kg) and make 0.5 mg IM the standard first‑line adult dose in your anaphylaxis order set and resuscitation checklist.

2) Ensure ready availability of 0.5 mg delivery options
- Stock 0.5 mg epinephrine prefilled syringes or ampoules in resuscitation bays and crash carts that are clearly labeled for IM use.
- If 0.5 mg auto‑injectors are commercially available in your region, stock them for adult use. If not available, use prefilled syringes or rapid draw technique.

3) Use auto‑injectors selectively
- Prehospital and non‑acute settings: auto‑injectors save time and reduce hesitation — use what’s available. If only 0.3 mg is on hand and the patient is an adult >50 kg, treat immediately but plan to give a 0.5 mg IM dose on arrival to ED if still symptomatic or as next step in therapy.
- In-hospital emergency care: prefer a 0.5 mg IM prepared syringe when a trained clinician is present to avoid underdosing.

4) Make drawing and giving 0.5 mg simple and fast
- Pre‑label syringes with 0.5 mg IM for adults in resuscitation trolleys.
- Train staff on IM technique (vastus lateralis, needle length appropriate to body habitus) and on the recommended dose per weight.
- Use cognitive aids: posters, EMR order sets, and pre‑populated anaphylaxis kits.

5) Clarify escalation rules
- If symptoms persist after one 0.5 mg IM dose, repeat IM every 5–15 minutes as indicated and escalate to IV epinephrine infusion for refractory shock following local critical care protocols.
- Avoid complacency after a single low dose that produced transient improvement.

Balancing speed vs dose accuracy — a pragmatic algorithm
- Prehospital/first contact: give whatever auto‑injector is immediately available to avoid delay (0.3 mg if that's what you have). For adults ≥50 kg, notify receiving ED that the patient may need an immediate 0.5 mg IM dose on arrival or closer monitoring.
- ED/inpatient acute care: if trained staff and supplies are present, give 0.5 mg IM immediately for adults ≥50 kg rather than relying on a prior 0.3 mg dose alone.
- If older patient or significant cardiac disease: still favor epinephrine but recognize risks — discuss and document rationale while prioritizing reversal of anaphylaxis.

Clinical pearls
- Don’t let “this was given already” be an excuse: if the prior dose was 0.3 mg in a patient ≥50 kg and the patient is not clinically secure, give 0.5 mg IM without delay.
- Auto‑injectors reduce delay and improve administration in chaotic situations; they are not a one‑size‑fits‑all dosing solution for adult ED/ICU care.
- Titrate to clinical response: repeat IM dosing is expected and appropriate; consider IV infusion if repeated IM dosing fails or if there is refractory shock.
- Institutional change wins: making 0.5 mg IM the default in EMR order sets and stocking accessible 0.5 mg preparations will change behavior more reliably than education alone.

Take‑home messages
- For adults ≥50 kg, target 0.5 mg IM epinephrine as the initial dose (0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg).
- Auto‑injectors are invaluable for speed but many adult auto‑injectors deliver only 0.3 mg — recognize this as a potential underdose.
- Operationalize 0.5 mg dosing with stock, protocols, prefilled syringes and training so the correct dose is also the fast dose.
- If a patient received 0.3 mg and remains at risk or symptomatic, give 0.5 mg IM promptly — clinical response trumps adherence to what was previously given.

The case of multiphasic anaphylaxis reminds us: timely, adequate epinephrine dosing is not academic — it changes courses, resource use and probably outcomes. Make 0.5 mg IM the default for adults in your emergency practice.



# Watchful Waiting or Wires and Walls? Risk‑Stratified Observation After Anaphylaxis

What makes this case interesting: a woman in her 60s developed multiphasic anaphylaxis after a single 500 mg oral dose of clarithromycin — four clinically apparent anaphylactic episodes over the first 12 hours, each responding to intramuscular epinephrine. She required escalation from ED to acute ward to ICU monitoring, received repeated IM epinephrine doses (initially 0.3 mg auto‑injector, later 0.5 mg IM), and ultimately stabilized without needing continuous IV epinephrine. Work‑up revealed persistently raised tryptase and possible systemic mast cell disease, but not enough to meet WHO diagnostic criteria.

This case sits at the uncomfortable intersection of three realities:
- Biphasic/multiphasic anaphylaxis exists and can be clinically dramatic despite appropriate immediate management.
- Incidence is low but reported rates vary widely (most series ~4–6%), so routine long admissions for all is impractical.
- Evidence for how long to observe or when to escalate monitoring (telemetry/ICU) is sparse; guidelines differ and steroid use to prevent relapse is not supported.

The clinical conundrum for EDs and ICUs: do you send the patient home after a 4–6 hour observation with no symptoms, admit to a monitored unit, or place them in the ICU with continuous infusion and telemetry? Below I break down the practical risk‑stratified approach I use, anchored to the case and current data.

Why relapse happens (quick primer)
- True biphasic/multiphasic reactions reflect ongoing mediator activity after apparent clinical resolution, not necessarily re‑exposure. Time to recurrence can be within hours up to several days (rare reports).
- Underlying mast cell disorders (elevated baseline tryptase, mastocytosis spectrum) increase susceptibility to severe, recurrent, or idiopathic reactions — as in this patient who had raised tryptase and hepatomegaly suggestive of a mast cell disorder.

Important modifiers that increase relapse risk (drawn from the case and literature signals)
- Severe initial presentation (hypotension, hypoxemia) — present in this case.
- Delay to epinephrine or underdosing initially (this patient received a 0.3 mg auto‑injector initially — likely underdosed for a 104 kg adult).
- Need for multiple epinephrine doses to control symptoms.
- Suspected or known mast cell disorder or persistently elevated tryptase.
- Ongoing absorption risk from ingested or depot agents (oral/IM medications) — in this case, only a single PO dose was taken and stopped, but oral absorption timing may allow delayed mediator triggers.

Practical, risk‑stratified observation framework
Below is a pragmatic triage for disposition and monitoring. Customize by local resources and clinical judgment.

1) Low risk — ED observation 4–6 hours (suitable for many single‑episode anaphylaxis)
- Candidate features: mild symptoms only (urticaria/angioedema without hypotension/respiratory compromise), rapid complete response to a single appropriate IM epinephrine dose, no delay to epinephrine, no comorbidities that increase risk (cardiac disease, pregnancy), normal baseline vitals and exam, reliable outpatient follow‑up and access to care.
- Monitoring: continued clinical reassessment, consider one additional period of observation after last symptom. No routine telemetry.
- Disposition: discharge with epinephrine auto‑injector(s), antihistamines, education, and clear return precautions.

2) Intermediate risk — extended observation or monitored bed (6–24 hours)
- Candidate features: severe initial presentation that responded to epinephrine but had hypotension or hypoxemia; delayed administration of epinephrine; need for more than one epinephrine dose; significant comorbidity (eg, cardiopulmonary disease); high BMI where initial dosing may have been suboptimal; uncertain exposure/ongoing absorption risk.
- Monitoring: step‑down or acute medical ward with frequent vitals (q1–2h) and nursing experienced with anaphylaxis; consider telemetry if arrhythmia risk or ongoing epinephrine administration is anticipated; check tryptase (ideally acutely and a baseline later) unless it would delay care.
- Disposition: admit for 6–24 hours. In the reported case, initial ED improvement followed by two relapses justified escalation to ICU; this aligns with intermediate‑risk patients who relapsed.

3) High risk — ICU-level monitoring and consideration of continuous therapy
- Candidate features: refractory anaphylaxis (fails to respond adequately to repeated IM epinephrine), multiple relapses despite appropriate IM epinephrine, hemodynamic instability requiring pressors/vasopressors, need for airway support, known mast cell disorder with severe prior reactions, recurrent severe episodes in short succession (multiphasic), or arrhythmia risk.
- Monitoring/interventions: ICU with continuous hemodynamic monitoring/telemetry, nursing expertise for anaphylaxis, low threshold for continuous IV epinephrine infusion if frequent IM dosing is required or hypotension is persistent; consider invasive arterial monitoring if unstable; repeat labs/tryptase sampling per allergy protocol.
- In the presented case, the patient’s first relapse after ED prompted ICU admission given recurrent anaphylaxis and significant initial hypoxemia/hypotension — an appropriate choice.

When does telemetry add value?
- Telemetry is most helpful when: high risk of hemodynamic instability, need for continuous or frequent vasoactive agents, underlying cardiac disease, arrhythmia during episodes, or when continuous infusion of epinephrine is used. It’s less necessary for uncomplicated single‑episode anaphylaxis that fully resolves.

Epinephrine dosing points (practical and critical)
- Recommended adult IM dose: 0.01 mg/kg, maximum 0.5 mg (most adults ~0.5 mg). Many auto‑injectors are 0.3 mg — acceptable in some adults but represents underdosing in heavier patients.
- If initial IM epi (correct dose/timing) doesn’t control symptoms, repeat IM every 5–15 minutes as needed. Persistent hypotension or frequent relapses → consider IV epinephrine infusion in monitored setting.
- The case highlights the consequences of underdosing: the first two episodes responded to 0.3 mg but additional episodes required 0.5 mg IM. Ensure ED teams have weight‑based mindset and ready access to appropriate doses.

Steroids and antihistamines — where we stand
- Antihistamines are used for cutaneous symptoms but do not treat airway/cardiovascular collapse.
- Routine systemic corticosteroids to prevent biphasic reactions are not supported by high‑quality evidence. Multiple systematic reviews show no clear reduction in biphasic events; steroids have adverse effects and may increase admissions. Use steroids selectively rather than reflexively.
- In the presented case, corticosteroids were added after relapse — understandable given local practice and uncertainty — but they may not prevent further episodes.

Tryptase testing and mast cell disease
- If severe, unusual, or recurrent anaphylaxis: measure tryptase acutely (ideally within 1–4 hours of symptom onset when feasible) and a baseline sample ≥24 hours after resolution. Elevated baseline tryptase suggests possible underlying mast cell disorder and alters counseling/monitoring.
- This patient had elevated tryptase during later sampling and baseline elevation on follow‑up, raising suspicion for systemic mastocytosis — a plausible explanation for multiphasic course.

Putting it together — suggested checklist for disposition decision in ED
- Was epinephrine given promptly and in an effective dose? (If delayed/underdosed → escalate observation.)
- Was there hypotension, hypoxemia, or respiratory compromise? (If yes → higher observation level.)
- Were multiple epinephrine doses required? (If yes → consider monitored admission.)
- Is there a history or lab evidence suggesting mast cell disorder? (If yes → consider ICU/monitored admission.)
- Is ongoing absorption of allergen likely (oral/depot exposure)? (If yes → extended observation.)
- Are there comorbidities (cardiac disease, pregnancy, poor access to care) that increase risk? (If yes → escalate.)

Closing clinical pearls
- Severe initial anaphylaxis, delayed or underdosed epinephrine, and need for multiple doses predict higher risk of relapse — act accordingly with longer/monitored observation.
- For adults >50 kg, aim for 0.5 mg IM epinephrine when possible; many 0.3 mg auto‑injectors underdose heavier adults and can be associated with need for rescue doses.
- Routine corticosteroids to prevent biphasic reactions are not evidence‑based. Use selectively.
- If recurrent episodes occur, move to monitored setting and consider continuous IV epinephrine rather than repeated frequent IM dosing.
- Measure tryptase when presentation is severe, prolonged, or recurrent — acute and baseline samples improve diagnostic yield and may reveal occult mast cell disorders.

Take‑home messages
- Not all anaphylaxis is the same. Risk‑stratified observation (ED 4–6 h vs monitored 6–24 h vs ICU) balances patient safety and resource use.
- Escalate monitoring if severe initial collapse, delayed/underdosed epinephrine, repeated epinephrine requirements, suspected mast cell disease, or ongoing exposure risk.
- Prioritize correct epinephrine dosing (0.01 mg/kg, up to 0.5 mg IM) and use ICU/continuous infusion for refractory or rapidly relapsing cases.
- Don’t reflexively give steroids to “prevent” biphasic reactions — the evidence is weak.
- Check tryptase in severe or atypical cases; elevated baseline changes future risk counseling and disposition planning.

This multiphasic clarithromycin case reminds us that vigilance, appropriate epinephrine dosing, and thoughtful, risk‑stratified monitoring — not blanket rules — provide the best route to safe, resource‑sensible care.



# Steroids in Anaphylaxis: Time to Step Away

What makes this case interesting
A woman in her 60s developed multiphasic anaphylaxis after a single oral dose of clarithromycin. She responded to intramuscular epinephrine each time but had multiple relapses in the first 12 hours. After the first relapse she was given systemic corticosteroids (prednisone IV and a dose of dexamethasone)—reflecting a common, reflexive practice—but her course continued to require additional epinephrine doses before calming down. This is exactly the sort of case that forces clinicians to ask: do steroids actually prevent biphasic (or multiphasic) anaphylaxis, or are we giving a drug that adds risk without benefit?

Short answer: current evidence argues steroids do not prevent biphasic anaphylaxis and may cause harm or unnecessary downstream care. The clarithromycin case illustrates why routine steroid use as “insurance” is increasingly hard to justify.

Why steroids became “standard” — and why that’s likely wrong
- Historical practice: steroids were historically given in an attempt to prevent delayed recurrence of symptoms (biphasic reactions). That practice became entrenched despite weak evidence.
- Pharmacology mismatch: systemic corticosteroids act mainly through genomic mechanisms that take hours to days to alter inflammatory cell function. Anaphylaxis—and early biphasic relapses—are driven by rapid mast cell/basophil degranulation and mediator release (histamine, tryptase, leukotrienes, prostaglandins). Steroids don’t quickly block those mediators or reverse vasodilation/hypotension in the acute phase.
- Evidence collapse: systematic reviews and large series fail to show a protective effect of steroids against biphasic reactions. Some analyses even associate prehospital steroids with higher admission/ICU rates (likely confounding, but concerning). In the presented case, steroids were given after the first relapse with no obvious prevention of the subsequent second relapse—an experience commonly reported in real-world practice.

Clinical consequences of reflex steroid use
- Delays to other care or masking of symptoms (steroids can change skin findings without addressing hemodynamics).
- Side effects: hyperglycemia (relevant in this patient with diabetes), immunosuppression, psychiatric effects, and potential for longer hospital observation "just in case."
- Resource use: steroids may promote longer observation or admission without demonstrable benefit.

What we should focus on instead — a steroid-sparing, practical approach
Grounded in this case and current best practice, prioritize timely epinephrine, appropriate supportive care, and structured observation/discharge planning.

1) Immediate treatment
- Epinephrine is first-line. Adult dosing: 0.01 mg/kg IM up to 0.5 mg (pragmatic adult dose often given as 0.5 mg). Be aware many auto-injectors are 0.3 mg and can underdose adults >50 kg. In this case the first two IM doses were 0.3 mg auto-injectors and subsequent doses were 0.5 mg IM.
- Repeat IM epinephrine every 5–15 minutes as needed for persistent/worsening symptoms. Escalate to intravenous bolus or continuous infusion only for refractory or severe shock under ICU-level care.
- Supplemental oxygen, aggressive IV fluids for hypotension, airway monitoring, and rapid recognition of respiratory compromise. Antihistamines (H1 blockers) and H2 blockers can help cutaneous symptoms but are adjuncts—not substitutes for epinephrine.

2) Observation and monitoring (risk-stratified; use local protocol)
- Because biphasic/multiphasic relapse can occur, observe patients after full clinical resolution. Exact duration is debated; many biphasic events occur within 6–12 hours but can occur later. In multiphasic cases like this one, relapses clustered in the first 12 hours.
- Monitor vitals, oxygenation, and clinical signs of airway compromise. Consider higher-acuity monitoring (telemetry/ICU) if:
  - Severe initial presentation (hypotension, hypoxaemia, respiratory failure)
  - Need for multiple epinephrine doses
  - Ongoing hemodynamic or respiratory instability
  - Concern for poor social support or inability to return for care

3) Use steroids selectively — not routinely
- Avoid routine systemic corticosteroids solely to prevent biphasic reactions.
- Reserve steroids for specific indications (e.g., severe bronchospasm not responding to standard measures, concomitant adrenal insufficiency, or other steroid-responsive comorbidities).
- If given, document the rationale, dose, and monitor for steroid-related complications (notably hyperglycemia in diabetics).

4) Diagnostics and specialist follow-up
- Tryptase testing: obtain samples per guidelines when feasible—one as soon as possible, a second 1–2 hours (but within 4 hours) after symptom onset when peak levels are expected, and a baseline sample ≥24 hours after full resolution. In this case only later samples were obtained; tryptase was elevated and raised concern for an underlying mast cell disorder.
- Avoid routine provocative allergy testing in the acute inpatient period if it risks severe re-exposure; arrange outpatient allergy/immunology follow-up.
- If raised baseline tryptase or clinical features suggest mast cell disorder, refer to allergy/hematology for further workup (bone marrow biopsy, KIT mutation testing as indicated).

5) Discharge planning and patient education
- Prescribe and teach use of an epinephrine auto-injector (dose appropriate for patient weight). In this case she was discharged with a 0.3 mg auto-injector and training—consider 0.5 mg device for heavier adults when available and appropriate.
- Start/continue H1 antihistamine for symptomatic control as needed (e.g., levocetirizine as in the case).
- Provide written action plan, advice on allergen avoidance (clarithromycin noted as likely trigger), and urgent follow-up with allergy/immunology. Advise return for recurrence of symptoms or if new respiratory or circulatory compromise occurs.

Clinical pearls from this case
- Steroids are not a reliable tool to prevent biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis; their onset of action is too slow to affect immediate mediator-driven relapses.
- Epinephrine remains the only intervention shown to rapidly reverse life-threatening features—dose and timely administration matter. Be mindful that 0.3 mg auto-injectors may underdose many adults.
- Recurrent episodes can still occur despite appropriate treatment; consider higher-acuity observation when multiple epinephrine doses are required.
- Elevated baseline tryptase or atypical courses should prompt evaluation for mast cell disorders, which increase risk of severe anaphylaxis.

Key take-home messages
- Do not give systemic corticosteroids routinely to prevent biphasic anaphylaxis.
- Treat acute anaphylaxis promptly with appropriately dosed IM epinephrine (0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg) and supportive care.
- Observe patients after resolution; extend monitoring for high-risk cases (severe initial reaction, multiple epinephrine doses, persistent instability).
- Use steroids only for specific indications, not as routine prophylaxis against relapse.
- Arrange allergy/mast cell disorder follow-up and clear discharge instructions, including epinephrine auto-injector training.

Final thought
Medicine prefers simple “do X to prevent Y” fixes, and steroids felt like one. But the pathophysiology and the evidence don’t support routine steroid use to prevent biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis. This clarithromycin case is a reminder to double down on what works—rapid, correctly dosed epinephrine and thoughtful monitoring—and to retire interventions that add risk without proven benefit.



# Getting the Tryptase Right: Sampling Windows, Interpretation, and the Mastocytosis Connection

What makes this case interesting
This patient had multiphasic anaphylaxis after a single clarithromycin dose, required repeated IM epinephrine, and—critically—no tryptase was drawn during the initial episodes. Later testing showed persistently elevated tryptase and a high probability of systemic mastocytosis, though formal diagnostic criteria weren’t fully met because of limited bone marrow sampling. That sequence is all too common: a dramatic anaphylaxis triggers the question “do they have a mast cell disorder?” — but the opportunity to capture the acute tryptase peak is missed, complicating interpretation and downstream work-up.

Why tryptase matters
- Tryptase is the most widely used biomarker of mast cell activation in the acute setting.
- It helps distinguish true anaphylaxis/mast cell degranulation from mimics and is part of the toolkit for identifying underlying clonal mast cell disease (systemic mastocytosis), a known risk factor for severe and recurrent anaphylaxis.
- But interpretation depends entirely on timing and context. A poorly timed sample is worse than no guidance at all unless you know how to recover from that mistake.

Optimal sampling windows — practical, evidence-based
- Acute (peak) sample: as soon as feasible once anaphylaxis is suspected — ideally within the first hour, but definitely obtain a sample within 1–2 hours of symptom onset (peaks typically 1–2 hours) and no later than 4 hours.
- Second (confirmatory) acute sample: if possible, draw a second sample at 1–2 hours after onset to capture the peak if the first was very early.
- Baseline sample: obtain at least one sample ≥24 hours after complete clinical resolution to establish baseline tryptase. For definitive baseline, repeat at 4–6 weeks if there’s any concern for persistent elevation or to document stability.

The 1.2 × baseline + 2 rule — how to confirm mast cell activation
- Use this formula to decide whether an acute rise represents true mast cell activation:
  acute tryptase > (1.2 × baseline tryptase) + 2 ng/mL
- Example: baseline 10 ng/mL → threshold = (1.2 × 10) + 2 = 14 ng/mL. An acute value >14 ng/mL is consistent with mast cell activation.
- This rule is more sensitive and specific than relying on an arbitrary “absolute” acute value alone.

What to do when the first sample is late or missed (like in the presented case)
- If you missed the ideal acute window and only get a sample later during the hospital course, do the following:
  - Obtain a tryptase immediately (this will be interpreted as a “late acute” value).
  - Obtain a true baseline tryptase ≥24 hours after full symptom resolution (and consider repeating at 4–6 weeks to confirm baseline stability).
  - Apply the 1.2 × baseline + 2 calculation using the highest available acute value (even if late) versus the confirmed baseline. A sufficiently large difference still supports activation.
- If the initial acute sample is completely absent, don’t abandon the work-up. Baseline elevation (especially persistent >20 ng/mL) itself raises strong suspicion for clonal mast cell disease and should prompt further evaluation.

When baseline tryptase should make you worry about mastocytosis
- Baseline tryptase >20 ng/mL is one of the WHO minor diagnostic criteria for systemic mastocytosis and should trigger specialist referral.
- Persistent baseline elevations between the normal range upper limit and 20 ng/mL are common and nonspecific; trends matter. Documented, stable elevation above reference range warrants further assessment.
- In the case presented, baseline and peri-event tryptases were elevated and remained high on follow-up — a red flag even though full WHO criteria weren’t demonstrable on limited marrow sampling.

Practical approach to screening for clonal mast cell disease after severe or recurrent anaphylaxis
1. Acute care (ED/ICU)
   - Treat first—don’t wait to draw blood to treat anaphylaxis.
   - Draw a tryptase as soon as it’s safe and feasible (note the clock from symptom onset). If multiple samples are possible, get one within 1–2 hours and another at 24+ hours.
2. Short-term follow-up (first clinic visit / allergist)
   - If acute sampling was suboptimal or absent: obtain a baseline tryptase ≥24 hours after resolution. Repeat baseline at 4–6 weeks if needed.
   - If baseline ≥20 ng/mL or if baseline is persistently elevated, refer to hematology/allergology for further work-up.
3. Specialist work-up (hematology/allergology)
   - Peripheral blood testing for KIT D816V mutation can be a noninvasive first step (sensitivity variable but helpful).
   - Bone marrow biopsy with morphology, immunohistochemistry (CD25, CD2), and molecular testing remains the gold standard to diagnose systemic mastocytosis.
   - Consider abdominal ultrasound for organomegaly, history of suspicious mediator-related symptoms (recurrent flushing, unexplained anaphylaxis, neuropsychiatric symptoms), and allergy testing where safe.
4. Management implications
   - If clonal mast cell disease is found (or strongly suspected), counsel on avoidance, early epinephrine use, and consider referral to a mast cell specialist for risk stratification and preventive strategies (eg, venom immunotherapy if relevant, H1/H2 blockade, bisphosphonates if bone involvement, etc.).
   - Provide an epinephrine auto-injector and clear anaphylaxis action plan. Patients with mast cell disorders have higher risk of severe/fatal anaphylaxis despite low population mortality overall.

Pitfalls and pearls from the case
- Pearl: Don’t delay epinephrine for labs. But do prioritize at least one acute tryptase sample early in the course when practical.
- Pearl: The 1.2 × baseline + 2 rule is your friend — it helps distinguish significant mast cell activation from a mildly elevated absolute tryptase.
- Pitfall: Relying on a single late tryptase without a confirmed baseline can mislead. Always obtain a baseline ≥24 hours after symptoms resolve.
- Pearl: Recurrent, severe, or multiphasic anaphylaxis should raise suspicion for an underlying mast cell disorder even if the WHO criteria aren’t immediately fulfilled. Persistently elevated baseline tryptase or supportive clinical features (hepatomegaly, bone symptoms, neuropsychiatric symptoms) justify specialist referral.
- Operational pearl: In many EDs, preprinted anaphylaxis order sets should include “tryptase x1 now, repeat at 1–2 h if possible, baseline ≥24 h” to avoid missed diagnostic windows.

Take-home messages
- In suspected anaphylaxis draw tryptase ASAP (ideally within 1–2 hours, no later than 4 hours), and obtain a baseline ≥24 hours after resolution.
- Use the 1.2 × baseline + 2 formula to define significant mast cell activation.
- If the initial acute sample was missed, get an immediate tryptase and then a confirmed baseline; persistent baseline elevation (especially >20 ng/mL) mandates further work-up for systemic mastocytosis.
- Recurrent or multiphasic anaphylaxis is an indication to screen for clonal mast cell disease and to refer to allergy/hematology for targeted investigation and management.

This case is a tidy reminder: treating anaphylaxis comes first, but getting the tryptase timing right when you can is the difference between an unanswered question and a clear path toward diagnosis and safer long-term care.



# When to Start an Epinephrine Infusion in Relapsing Anaphylaxis

What makes this case interesting: a woman in her 60s had true multiphasic anaphylaxis after a single oral dose of clarithromycin — four separate anaphylactic episodes over about 12 hours that responded to repeated intramuscular (IM) epinephrine. Teams debated whether to persist with intermittent IM dosing (what ultimately worked) or to start a continuous intravenous (IV) epinephrine infusion pre‑emptively. That debate is exactly the practical dilemma frontline clinicians face: when does repeated IM dosing become unsafe, inefficient or harmful and necessitate a titrated IV infusion?

Below I summarize a pragmatic, evidence‑informed approach to the decision, practical infusion starting doses, monitoring and common pitfalls — grounded in the case above and typical emergency/ICU practice.

Why this matters
- Recurrent (biphasic/multiphasic) anaphylaxis is uncommon but potentially severe. Repeated IM injections are safe and effective in many cases, but they can be impractical or inadequate when hemodynamics are unstable, episodes are frequent, or airway/respiratory compromise is evolving.
- An IV epinephrine infusion allows fine titration to physiologic endpoints but carries real risks (arrhythmia, ischemia, dosing errors) and requires ICU‑level monitoring and pump reliability.

When to strongly consider switching from repeated IM epinephrine to a continuous IV infusion
Use clinical judgment — no single rule fits all — but consider starting an infusion when one or more of the following occur:

- Hemodynamic instability despite appropriate IM dosing: persistent hypotension (systolic BP <90 mmHg or MAP <65 mmHg), ongoing need for multiple boluses, or recurrent severe hypoxaemia.
- Frequent redosing: the patient requires repeated IM epinephrine doses (≥2–3 doses within a short window, e.g., the first 1–2 hours) to control recurring anaphylaxis.
- Refractory or progressive respiratory/airway involvement: worsening stridor, increasing upper airway oedema, progressive bronchospasm despite IM epinephrine and supportive therapy — especially if intubation is being contemplated.
- Logistic/practical concerns: repeated IM dosing is causing delays or errors (busy ED, non‑ICU ward without staff experienced with vasopressors), or patient anatomy/weight makes IM dosing less reliable.
- When continuous titration is desired to avoid oscillations between over‑ and under‑treatment (for example, frequent short lived responses with rapid relapses).

What to do before starting an infusion
- Continue/support airway, oxygen, and large‑bore IV access. Consider two IV lines; central access helpful but not mandatory.
- Give adequate fluid resuscitation: initial crystalloid bolus 20–30 mL/kg (in the case presented she received 500 mL initially but may need larger boluses if persistently hypotensive).
- Ensure an initial bolus dose has been given (IM epinephrine 0.3–0.5 mg) and re-dose IM while preparing the infusion if clinically needed. If immediate IV pressor support is required and the patient is profoundly hypotensive, small IV boluses of epinephrine (e.g., 50–100 mcg = 0.05–0.1 mg) can be given cautiously while the infusion is started.
- Remove ongoing exposure and call allergy/immunology early for follow‑up planning (in the case, clarithromycin was stopped and later mast cell disorder workup was initiated).

Suggested IV epinephrine infusion approach (practical hospital dosing)
- Preparation: dilute epinephrine carefully (common preparation: 1 mg epinephrine in 100 mL normal saline = 10 mcg/mL; verify local pharmacy protocols). Use an infusion pump and label clearly. Double‑check dose calculations (micrograms vs milligrams are easy to confuse).
- Starting rate: a reasonable, commonly used starting range is 0.05–0.1 mcg/kg/min. For a typical adult (~70 kg) that equates roughly to 3.5–7 mcg/min. Many clinicians start 0.05–0.1 mcg/kg/min and titrate.
- Alternative simple starting increments (if weight‑based pumps unavailable): start low, e.g., 0.03–0.1 mcg/kg/min or an absolute 5–10 mcg/min and titrate to effect. Avoid starting extremely high without ICU-level monitoring.
- Titration: increase every few minutes (e.g., every 2–5 minutes) to achieve target blood pressure, improved skin perfusion and resolution of bronchospasm. Some patients require higher rates (up to 0.5–1 mcg/kg/min) but that is uncommon and high rates raise ischemic risk.
- Goals: restore/maintain MAP ≥65 mmHg (or individualized BP target), reduce tachypnoea/bronchospasm, stop progressive airway oedema; minimize need for repeated IM boluses.
- Use IV infusion to bridge through the active anaphylactic phase; avoid prolonged unnecessary infusion once patient is stable.

Monitoring and safety
- ICU environment with continuous ECG and frequent BP monitoring. If peripheral infusion is used, inspect infusion site for extravasation.
- Monitor for arrhythmias, ischemic chest pain, severe hypertension and hyperglycaemia. Frequent assessments of airway, work of breathing and oxygenation.
- Attach urinary output monitoring if prolonged infusion anticipated.
- Document all epinephrine doses (IM, IV bolus and infusion) and rationales — dosing errors are a common harm.

Pitfalls and how to avoid them
- Dosing errors: label syringes/infusion bags, use standardized concentrations and double‑check calculations. Avoid verbal orders for epinephrine dosing whenever possible.
- Overdosing: aggressive titration without re‑assessing fluids or ongoing anaphylaxis cause can produce tachyarrhythmias or myocardial ischemia.
- Relying on infusion to replace IM boluses too early: if IV access delays, give IM dose first.
- Peripheral IV extravasation: use proximal large bore cannula and monitor; if extravasation occurs, stop infusion and treat locally per protocol.
- Escalation inertia: if infusion is not achieving hemodynamic targets despite increasing doses, consider concomitant vasopressors (norepinephrine) and advanced support (intubation, ECMO in extreme refractory shock) and consult critical care.

Integration with other therapies
- Fluids: give generous crystalloids early (20–30 mL/kg bolus) — many anaphylactic patients are relatively hypovolaemic due to vasodilation and capillary leak.
- Antihistamines: H1 (eg, clemastine/ diphenhydramine) and H2 blockers are useful for cutaneous symptoms (urticaria, itching) but do not treat life‑threatening airway or circulatory compromise and should not replace epinephrine. In the presented case IV clemastine was used for skin symptoms.
- Steroids: evidence does not support steroids to prevent biphasic reactions. They may be given by habit in some settings, but do not delay or substitute for epinephrine. In the case, steroids were given after recurrence but there is no convincing data they prevented further recurrences.
- Oxygen and bronchodilators: give high‑flow oxygen for hypoxaemia; nebulized beta‑agonists for bronchospasm may be adjunctive.

Escalation and de‑escalation plans
- Escalation: if infusion fails to achieve stability (rising doses without effect), escalate promptly — consider adding norepinephrine, invasive monitoring, and prepare for intubation if airway compromise progresses. Call ICU/anaesthesia early.
- De‑escalation: once the patient is stable without recurrent symptoms and does not require increasing infusion rates, wean infusion slowly (decrease stepwise over hours) rather than abrupt stop. Many centers observe the patient on a weaning infusion for several hours (commonly 4–6 hours of hemodynamic stability and no clinical relapse) before stopping; however, biphasic/multiphasic reactions can recur later — tailor observation to severity and to risk factors (delayed presentation, severe initial reaction, multiple doses required, possible mast cell disorder).
- In the presented case, symptoms attenuated with repeated IM dosing and no further relapses occurred after the fourth episode; the team avoided infusion but admitted the validity of both strategies given limited evidence.

Special considerations from this case
- Obesity and underdosing concerns: many adult auto‑injectors are 0.3 mg — that may underdose adults >50 kg. This patient initially received 0.3 mg then 0.5 mg IM doses later. Consider 0.5 mg IM for adults >50 kg when available, and revert to IV infusion if adequate effect is not sustained.
- Mast cell disorders: high baseline tryptase and recurrent severe reactions increase suspicion for mast cell disease. These patients may have more severe or recurrent episodes — consider lower threshold to observe longer, involve allergy/immunology, and consider earlier infusion if recurrent.
- Documentation & follow‑up: give an epinephrine auto‑injector on discharge, educate on recognition and avoidance, and arrange specialist follow‑up for allergy and mast cell evaluation.

Clinical pearls / take‑home points
- IM epinephrine remains first‑line and is effective in most anaphylaxis episodes; repeated IM dosing is reasonable initially while preparing definitive care.
- Strongly consider an IV epinephrine infusion when there is hemodynamic instability despite IM dosing, frequent redosing (multiple IM doses in a short timeframe), progressive airway/respiratory compromise, or logistical concerns that make repeated IM dosing unsafe.
- Start the infusion at a low weight‑based rate (commonly 0.05–0.1 mcg/kg/min), titrate to effect, use an infusion pump, and monitor continuously in an ICU setting.
- Give generous fluids early; antihistamines are for cutaneous symptoms only and do not replace epinephrine; steroids do not reliably prevent biphasic reactions.
- Prepare for pitfalls (dosing errors, arrhythmia, extravasation) and have an escalation and de‑escalation plan. Individualize observation duration — severe, multiphasic or suspected mast cell disease may warrant prolonged monitoring and specialist follow‑up.

Final practical line: if you find yourself giving repeated IM doses multiple times in a short window and the patient still needs support — don’t hesitate to move to a controlled, titratable IV infusion in the ICU. It’s not failure — it’s appropriate escalation.
