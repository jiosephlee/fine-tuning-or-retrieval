Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

## Case Presentation and Initial Assessment

The patient was a woman in her 60s with several chronic conditions (type 2 diabetes, hypertension, psoriasis, depression) and a history of allergy to beta-lactam antibiotics manifested in adolescence by facial swelling. She had recently been treated for erysipelas with clindamycin for 5 days, after which she developed a localized rash that was judged to be a delayed allergic reaction. Her antibiotic was switched to clarithromycin, to which she had no prior exposure. Approximately one hour after taking the first 500 mg oral dose of clarithromycin she developed progressive pruritus, flushing, diaphoresis, agitation and a subjective sensation of throat swelling. She presented to the ED about an hour after symptom onset.

On arrival she was hypotensive and hypoxaemic: non-invasive blood pressure 74/50 mm Hg and peripheral oxygen saturation 88% on room air, with a respiratory rate of 22 breaths per minute. Examination documented facial swelling, new generalized erythema and urticaria over the trunk and limbs, and marked diaphoresis. There was no observed tongue or obvious oropharyngeal obstruction; cardiac and lung auscultation were reported as normal. Point-of-care glucose was 12.7 mmol/L and ECG showed sinus rhythm.

Diagnostic reasoning and application of criteria
- The presentation fulfilled standard clinical criteria for anaphylaxis: acute onset of illness with skin/mucosal involvement (flushing, urticaria, angioedema) plus involvement of respiratory compromise (hypoxaemia, dyspnoea) and circulatory compromise (hypotension). These features together make anaphylaxis the leading diagnosis.
- The temporal relationship — symptoms beginning about 1 hour after oral clarithromycin — supports an immediate hypersensitivity reaction to a newly introduced medication, although definitive allergen confirmation was not pursued acutely.

Important differential diagnoses considered at presentation
- Sepsis with distributive shock — could produce hypotension and altered perfusion, but the rapid onset after drug exposure and prominent cutaneous urticaria/angioedema point toward anaphylaxis rather than an infectious cause.
- Cardiogenic shock or acute coronary syndrome — less likely given normal cardiac auscultation, sinus rhythm without ischemic ECG changes, and prominent cutaneous and respiratory findings; however, cardiac causes are important to consider in hypotension.
- Pulmonary embolism — can cause hypoxaemia and hypotension, but again the dermatologic findings and rapid post-exposure timeline favor anaphylaxis.
- Anxiety or hyperventilation — may explain dyspnoea but would not explain hypotension, hypoxaemia and urticaria.
In the acute ED setting, these alternatives should be borne in mind but do not supersede treatment for suspected anaphylaxis when clinical criteria are met.

Immediate priorities and rationale for actions taken
- Airway and oxygenation: ensure patency and oxygen supplementation for hypoxaemia. In this case high-flow oxygen (15 L via non-rebreather mask) was provided immediately and could be titrated as the clinical picture changed.
- Epinephrine as first-line therapy: intramuscular epinephrine is the cornerstone of anaphylaxis treatment because of its alpha- and beta-adrenergic effects (vasoconstriction to restore blood pressure, bronchodilation to improve airflow, and decreased mucosal edema). The patient received 0.3 mg IM epinephrine via an auto-injector promptly upon presentation.
- Positioning and circulatory support: recumbent/supine positioning and rapid IV access to deliver crystalloids for volume support are standard. The team started a 500 mL bolus of normal saline during initial stabilization.
- Remove or stop the trigger: clarithromycin was discontinued and no further doses were given.
- Adjunctive therapies: antihistamine (clemastine 2 mg IV) was administered for cutaneous symptoms, recognizing that H1 antagonists do not treat life‑threatening airway or circulatory compromise but may help pruritus and urticaria. Glucocorticoids were not part of the initial ED treatment in this report and were added later after relapse; their routine immediate benefit in preventing biphasic reactions is unproven.

Initial response and short-term course
- The patient showed an early clinical response to the initial measures: blood pressure improved and the facial swelling and generalized erythema decreased, allowing temporary discontinuation of supplemental oxygen. This early improvement confirmed a treatment response consistent with anaphylaxis rather than an alternative etiology requiring a different immediate strategy.
- Despite the initial stabilization, the patient later developed recurrent episodes of hypotension, hypoxaemia and cutaneous symptoms over the first 12 hours, prompting ICU admission and additional intramuscular epinephrine doses. Those relapses define the multiphasic course described later, but at initial presentation the clinical assessment and interventions appropriately prioritized epinephrine, oxygenation, rapid IV fluids, and stopping the offending agent.

## Pathophysiology and Special Clinical Entities

Anaphylaxis is an acute systemic reaction driven primarily by rapid activation of mast cells and basophils with subsequent release of preformed and newly synthesized mediators. The canonical pathway is IgE‑mediated: a sensitized patient has allergen‑specific IgE bound to high‑affinity FcεRI receptors on mast cells and basophils. Re‑exposure to the allergen causes crosslinking of IgE–FcεRI complexes, triggering intracellular signalling that leads to degranulation and the immediate release of preformed mediators such as histamine, tryptase and heparin, together with rapid synthesis and secretion of lipid mediators (prostaglandins, leukotrienes) and cytokines. These mediators produce the clinical features of anaphylaxis: histamine and leukotrienes cause increased vascular permeability and vasodilation (resulting in hypotension and angioedema), bronchial smooth‑muscle constriction (bronchospasm and wheeze), cutaneous flushing and urticaria, and gastrointestinal symptoms. Tryptase is a protease stored in mast cell granules that serves as a useful biochemical marker of mast cell activation; it has limited direct vasoactive effects but reflects the degree of mast cell degranulation.

Drug‑induced anaphylaxis from orally administered agents differs in important kinetic respects from parenteral exposures. Oral drugs must be absorbed before they act as systemic triggers; absorption time, formulation (immediate vs extended release) and enterohepatic circulation influence how long antigenic stimulus persists. A single oral dose can sometimes lead to a prolonged or intermittent presence of antigen in the circulation (so‑called depot or delayed absorption effects), or to ongoing antigen exposure from intestinal processing, which may produce recurrent or protracted symptoms without further doses. In the presented case, clarithromycin was a single oral 500 mg dose and no additional doses were taken; still, oral pharmacokinetics can account for continued antigen availability over hours and therefore contribute to relapsing clinical courses.

It is clinically important to differentiate biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis from refractory (or persistent) anaphylaxis because the expected course and management differ. Biphasic anaphylaxis classically describes an initial anaphylactic episode that resolves (spontaneously or after treatment), followed by a recurrence of signs and symptoms hours later in the absence of further allergen exposure. The recurrence generally occurs within 24 hours and is usually a single secondary wave. Multiphasic anaphylaxis is a less‑well‑defined but accepted descriptor for cases with more than two distinct episodes or repeated relapses after apparent recovery, as seen in this patient who experienced multiple recurrences in the first 12 hours. Refractory anaphylaxis refers to ongoing or progressive shock and airway compromise despite appropriate and timely administration of epinephrine; it implies failure to control the initial episode rather than recurrence after resolution. Clinically, refractory anaphylaxis often requires escalation such as continuous intravenous vasopressors or repeated/continuous epinephrine, whereas biphasic or multiphasic courses raise questions about observation duration and the potential need for prolonged monitoring or preventive strategies.

Several pathophysiologic mechanisms have been proposed to explain biphasic and multiphasic reactions. One model posits a biphasic mediator release pattern: an immediate wave of preformed mediator release followed by a later wave driven by newly synthesized mediators or secondary cellular activation (eg, via recruited inflammatory cells, complement activation or late prostaglandin/leukotriene synthesis). A second mechanism is persistent or intermittent antigen exposure (the depot effect), particularly relevant to oral drugs, insect venom with retained venom in tissue, or ingested foods that are absorbed over time; continued antigen presence can repeatedly trigger mast cells. A third possibility is sustained mast cell activation from underlying disorders (mast cell hyperreactivity) or amplification loops involving cytokines and recruited eosinophils and basophils that produce delayed mediator release. These mechanisms are not mutually exclusive and likely interact in multiphasic cases.

Understanding tryptase kinetics helps interpret laboratory data in suspected anaphylaxis and in screening for underlying mast cell disease. Tryptase is typically measured as total serum tryptase; after an anaphylactic event it usually rises within minutes to a couple of hours, commonly peaking around 1–2 hours after symptom onset, and declines thereafter toward baseline over the next day or more, often returning to near baseline by 24 hours but sometimes later. Guidelines recommend obtaining an acute sample as soon as feasible, a second sample at about 1–2 hours (within 4 hours) after onset to capture the peak, and a baseline sample at least 24 hours after complete clinical resolution to establish resting tryptase. A commonly used criterion for a clinically significant acute rise is an increase of ≥(1.2 × baseline tryptase + 2) μg/L. Persistently elevated baseline tryptase suggests increased mast cell burden or activation and prompts evaluation for mast cell disorders.

Mast cell disorders, particularly systemic mastocytosis, markedly increase the risk and severity of anaphylaxis. Systemic mastocytosis is a clonal proliferation of mast cells in one or more extracutaneous organs (commonly bone marrow, liver, spleen, gastrointestinal tract) often driven by activating KIT mutations (eg, KIT D816V). Patients have increased mast cell numbers and/or aberrant activation thresholds, so relatively small triggers may provoke large mediator releases. Clinically this presents as recurrent hypotension, flushing, syncope, pruritus, and gastrointestinal symptoms; severe anaphylaxis can occur with atypical triggers (including drugs, Hymenoptera stings, or idiopathically). The World Health Organization diagnostic framework uses one major criterion (multifocal dense mast cell infiltrates in bone marrow or other extracutaneous organ) and four minor criteria (abnormal mast cell morphology or immunophenotype, presence of activating KIT mutation, baseline serum tryptase persistently >20 μg/L, and others). A diagnosis requires the major criterion plus one minor, or three minor criteria. In the case presented, baseline and event tryptase values were elevated and imaging showed hepatomegaly; however, the full set of diagnostic criteria was not met on available testing, leaving systemic mastocytosis likely but unconfirmed. This distinction matters because mast cell disorders alter risk stratification and counselling: patients with documented mastocytosis warrant heightened vigilance, referral to specialist care, discussion of venom avoidance strategies, and often lifelong readiness with epinephrine and tailored perioperative or procedural prophylaxis.

In summary, anaphylaxis is mediated by rapid mast cell and basophil activation with an immediate mediator surge; biphasic and multiphasic phenotypes reflect either secondary mediator waves, ongoing antigen exposure, or underlying mast cell hyperactivity. Oral drug triggers and mast cell disorders are both mechanistically relevant to multiphasic courses: the former through absorption and depot effects and the latter by lowering the threshold and amplifying mediator release. Tryptase measurement—timed acutely and at baseline—is useful both to confirm mast cell activation during events and to screen for mast cell disease, which in turn informs prognosis and long‑term management.

## Diagnostic Workup and Clinical Reasoning

Establishing that this patient experienced a multiphasic anaphylactic course depended on careful correlation of clinical timing, symptom pattern, and exclusion of ongoing allergen exposure. The record documents an initial, typical anaphylactic presentation within one hour of the first oral clarithromycin dose, followed by complete or near-complete clinical responses to epinephrine and supportive care, then discrete recurrences of anaphylaxis separated by asymptomatic intervals over the first 12 hours. The relapses occurred without further doses of clarithromycin (only the single 500 mg oral dose was taken), and the intensity of symptoms diminished with each episode (from profound hypoxaemia and hypotension to mainly cutaneous and respiratory symptoms). These features—clear symptom-free intervals between episodes, no documented re-exposure, and stepwise diminution of severity—support the diagnosis of a multiphasic (multiple biphasic) anaphylactic course rather than persistent, unremitting anaphylaxis.

Distinguishing multiphasic anaphylaxis from sustained anaphylaxis or from ongoing antigen absorption is crucial, because management and investigation differ. Sustained anaphylaxis can represent ongoing exposure (continued ingestion, poorly absorbed depot formulations, transdermal sources), slow gastrointestinal absorption, or inadequate initial therapy. In this patient, chart review verified that clarithromycin was given only once and stopped immediately, arguing against repeated exposure. The timing (recurrences clustered in the first 12 hours) and the reproducible prompt responses to intramuscular epinephrine argue for episodic mediator release rather than a continuously smouldering process. There was no suggestion of an unrecognized continuous source (no retained medication depot, no delayed-release formulation) and no clinical signs that would suggest prolonged gastric emptying as the driver of repeated antigen presentation. Thus the working diagnostic framing was true multiphasic anaphylaxis.

Biochemical confirmation with serial tryptase measurements is an important part of evaluating anaphylaxis and of identifying an underlying mast cell disorder. The recommended ideal protocol is three samples: (1) as soon as feasible after onset of symptoms (or at first clinical contact), (2) a second sample at the expected peak (typically 1–2 hours after symptom onset, and within 4 hours), and (3) a baseline sample at least 24 hours after full clinical resolution to establish the patient’s baseline tryptase. In this case the sampling deviated from the ideal sequence: the first tryptase was obtained 1 hour after the third anaphylactic episode (so it captured a post-event elevation, but not samples from earlier episodes), and a second sample was taken 27 hours after the last episode (serving as a baseline). The absence of an immediate sample during the very first episode limits ability to compare peak-to-baseline rises across all events, but the available data still carry diagnostic weight.

Interpretation of the results: the event (post‑episode) tryptase was elevated, and the “baseline” tryptase measured many hours later remained persistently increased above normal. A persistent elevation of baseline tryptase raises concern for an underlying systemic mast cell disorder (for example, systemic mastocytosis or hereditary alpha‑tryptasemia). In emergency practice, an elevated event tryptase supports mast cell activation during the reaction; an elevated baseline tryptase (commonly >20 ng/mL is used as a threshold in many diagnostic algorithms) prompts investigation for chronic mast cell disease.

Given the severe and multiphasic presentation together with the elevated baseline tryptase, screening for systemic mastocytosis was pursued. The standard diagnostic framework follows WHO criteria: one major criterion (multifocal dense aggregates of mast cells in bone marrow or extracutaneous organ) or three minor criteria are required for diagnosis. Minor criteria include: (1) baseline serum tryptase persistently >20 ng/mL (unless there is an associated hematologic disorder), (2) presence of the c-KIT D816V mutation in blood or marrow, (3) atypical mast cell morphology (spindle-shaped) and/or aberrant expression of CD25/CD2 on bone marrow mast cells, and (4) >25% of mast cells in marrow showing atypical morphology. In this patient the workup yielded elevated baseline tryptase and other supportive findings (for example, hepatomegaly on ultrasound and compatible clinical features), but the bone marrow biopsy was limited and did not permit assessment of the major criterion; only two WHO minor criteria were met. Thus formal diagnostic confirmation of systemic mastocytosis could not be established from the available material. This illustrates an important practical point: bone marrow sampling is the definitive step when screening suggests mast cell disease, but suboptimal or limited samples can leave the diagnosis uncertain and require repeat, more extensive sampling or molecular testing (sensitive c-KIT mutation assays) to complete the evaluation.

When interpreting the case and planning investigations it is also important to review potential risk modifiers and interacting medications. The patient’s body weight (104 kg) is clinically relevant because recommended intramuscular epinephrine dosing is 0.01 mg/kg up to a typical adult maximum of 0.5 mg. Many auto-injectors deliver 0.3 mg, which can represent underdosing in heavier adults and may contribute to the need for additional epinephrine doses. In contrast, drugs that can worsen anaphylaxis or blunt response to epinephrine—classically beta‑blockers and ACE inhibitors—were not part of her medication list. Hydrochlorothiazide had been taken more than 12 hours before presentation and was unlikely to explain relapsing symptoms. These details reduce the likelihood that co‑medication effects were the primary driver of the multiphasic course here.

Alternative diagnoses that can mimic relapsing hypotension or flushing were considered and reasonably excluded. Severe sepsis can produce hypotension and tachypnea but typically evolves with fever, leukocytosis, a source of infection, and progressive rather than waxing‑and‑waning cutaneous mast cell–type symptoms; the abrupt onset within one hour of drug ingestion and the reproducible, rapid response to epinephrine favor an IgE‑ or mast cell–mediated process. Carcinoid syndrome or catecholamine‑secreting tumors (pheochromocytoma) produce intermittent flushing and hemodynamic changes, but their presentations and biochemical markers differ, and the temporal relationship to clarithromycin ingestion and the cutaneous urticaria/angioedema pattern are much more consistent with anaphylaxis. Endocrine causes of shock (adrenal crisis) would be expected to produce prolonged, steroid‑responsive hypotension and different laboratory findings. The combination of characteristic cutaneous signs, respiratory symptoms, hypotension, clear trigger exposure, good epinephrine responsiveness, and the timing of relapses together make these alternative explanations unlikely in this case.

In summary, the diagnostic reasoning combined clinical pattern recognition (discrete asymptomatic intervals and diminishing recurrence severity), exclusion of ongoing exposure and other mimics, and targeted laboratory testing. Although ideal serial tryptase sampling was not fully achieved, the elevated event tryptase and persistently raised baseline prompted appropriate mast cell workup. Bone marrow biopsy limitations prevented formal WHO confirmation of systemic mastocytosis, but the constellation of findings supports a high likelihood of an underlying mast cell disorder—information that has implications for counseling, avoidance strategies, and long‑term risk management.

## Acute Management Nuances and Monitoring Strategy

Epinephrine remains the unequivocal first-line treatment for anaphylaxis because it addresses the fundamental pathophysiology—alpha-adrenergic vasoconstriction to restore vascular tone and reduce mucosal oedema, beta-1 inotropy/chronotropy to support cardiac output, and beta-2 bronchodilation and mast cell stabilising effects. The recommended intramuscular (IM) dose is 0.01 mg/kg (maximum 0.5 mg) delivered into the mid-anterolateral thigh; this is fast, reliably absorbed, and associated with fewer adverse events than intravenous administration in non-resuscitation settings. Proper technique—deep IM injection into a well-perfused muscle, with prompt repeat dosing every 5–15 minutes if symptoms persist—is an important practical point to maximise effect.

A common pitfall illustrated by this case is underdosing from use of 0.3 mg auto‑injectors in adults who weigh >30 kg and especially those >50 kg. Although 0.3 mg autoinjectors facilitate rapid treatment and reduce delays, they may be insufficient in heavier adults; retrospective data (noted in the case discussion) associate an initial 0.3 mg dose with greater need for escalation (additional injections, ICU care) compared with a 0.5 mg initial dose. In practice, when only 0.3 mg is available and the patient remains haemodynamically unstable or has progressive respiratory compromise, clinicians should give additional IM epinephrine promptly rather than delay escalation.

Escalation beyond repeated IM injections is indicated when there is persistent or progressive airway compromise, refractory hypotension despite fluid resuscitation and repeated IM doses, or sustained cardiovascular instability. Large-bore IV access and aggressive crystalloid boluses (20 mL/kg in children; in adults titrated to response, often repeated 500–1000 mL boluses) are standard to correct intravascular depletion from vasodilation and increased vascular permeability. If hypotension or anaphylaxis signs persist despite adequate IM dosing and fluids, an intravenous epinephrine infusion may be required to maintain perfusion. IV epinephrine should be started using a controlled infusion (not bolus pushes, except in cardiac arrest) at low starting rates with close monitoring—and only in settings with cardiac monitoring and staff experienced with vasoactive infusions—because of the risk of arrhythmia and myocardial ischaemia. In the presented case the team considered a continuous IV infusion as a pre-emptive strategy because of recurrent relapses, but appropriately weighed the risks of IV vasoactive therapy against the patient’s gradually improving clinical picture.

Adjunctive therapies have a narrower, supportive role. H1-antihistamines (eg, clemastine, cetirizine) and H2 blockers can relieve pruritus and urticaria but do not treat airway oedema, bronchospasm or hypotension and should never delay epinephrine. Inhaled short-acting beta-agonists are useful for bronchospasm that persists after epinephrine. Routine use of corticosteroids to prevent biphasic reactions has been called into question: multiple studies and systematic reviews have failed to demonstrate reduction in biphasic events, and steroids carry potential harms (eg, hyperglycaemia, infection risk, longer hospital stays). Consequently, steroids are no longer recommended as routine therapy to prevent relapse; they may still be considered case-by-case (for example, in patients with comorbid severe asthma or other indications) but should not replace escalation of epinephrine or monitoring when indicated. In this patient, corticosteroids were administered after the first relapse in line with local protocols, reflecting the persisting heterogeneity of practice.

Decisions about level of monitoring—ward versus ICU—should be driven by objective risk factors. Indications for ICU-level care include severe initial presentation (profound hypotension, hypoxaemia, respiratory failure), requirement for multiple doses of epinephrine, ongoing need for vasopressor infusion, airway intervention, and known or suspected disorders that increase anaphylaxis severity or recurrence risk (such as systemic mast cell disease). In this case, the recurrent shock despite initial response, need for repeated IM epinephrine, and later findings suspicious for mast cell disorder justified ICU admission for close monitoring and readiness to initiate vasoactive IV therapy or advanced airway management.

Observation duration after initial resolution must balance the known timing of biphasic events with resource use. Most biphasic recurrences occur within the first 6–12 hours, but documented cases can occur later (up to several days). A risk-stratified approach is pragmatic: short observation (4–6 hours) may be reasonable for low‑risk patients with single mild events who received timely epinephrine and are stable; extended observation (12–24 hours or admission) is appropriate for those with severe initial reactions, delayed epinephrine administration, need for multiple epinephrine doses, comorbid cardiorespiratory disease, or suspected mast cell disorders. Before discharge, patients should be asymptomatic without ongoing need for oxygen or vasopressors, haemodynamically stable off IV fluids, and able to ambulate and tolerate oral intake. They should receive education, an epinephrine auto‑injector with training, and specialist follow‑up arranged. In the case presented, the team observed the patient through multiple relapses, transferred her back to ward only after 24 hours without recurrence, and discharged her after three days with an auto‑injector and allergology follow-up—an approach consistent with risk‑adapted extended observation.

Applying these principles to multiphasic courses requires dynamic reassessment. The team in this report repeatedly used IM epinephrine for each relapse, which aligns with best practice for prompt, effective treatment while minimising risks of IV vasoactive therapy. They reasonably considered, but deferred, a continuous IV infusion because the patient’s symptom severity decreased with each episode and responded to IM doses, reducing the anticipated benefit of the greater risks of IV epinephrine. Expectant monitoring became defensible as the intensity of episodes waned and no further deterioration occurred. This illustrates an important practical point: in multiphasic anaphylaxis, repeated IM epinephrine with vigilant monitoring and readiness to escalate to IV infusions or airway intervention is an appropriate strategy, reserving continuous IV epinephrine for truly refractory or persistently unstable patients under intensive monitoring.

In summary, immediate IM epinephrine at the correct dose and site is the cornerstone of management; clinicians must guard against underdosing from readily available 0.3 mg autoinjectors in heavier adults. Escalation to fluids, airway support, and controlled IV epinephrine infusions is guided by persistence of life‑threatening features despite adequate IM dosing. Adjuncts address symptoms but do not replace epinephrine. Finally, observation and level of care should be risk‑stratified, with ICU admission and prolonged observation for patients with severe initial presentations, multiple epinephrine requirements, or underlying mast cell disorders—exactly the considerations that guided care in this patient.

## Disposition, Long-term Management, and Clinical Lessons

Outcome and immediate disposition
The patient’s multiphasic course terminated after four anaphylactic episodes; no further relapses occurred during intensive monitoring, she was stepped down from the ICU after a symptom-free interval of roughly 24 hours and discharged after a total hospital stay of 3 days. This outcome highlights that even severe, multiphasic anaphylaxis can resolve with supportive care and repeated administration of intramuscular epinephrine, but requires higher-level monitoring until an adequate symptom-free interval is documented.

Discharge planning and emergency preparedness
On discharge the team provided standard secondary prevention measures. The patient received training and a prescription for an epinephrine autoinjector and instruction in its indications and intramuscular technique. For adults weighing more than ~50 kg, clinicians should prefer a 0.5 mg intramuscular dose where available because adult dosing guidelines recommend 0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg and because underdosing with 0.3 mg devices is associated with need for additional escalation. If only 0.3 mg devices are available, explicit counseling about prompt repeat dosing and early emergency activation is essential. A written individualized emergency action plan should accompany the device and training; it should list likely triggers, the steps to take during anaphylaxis (use autoinjector, call emergency services, repeat dosing if no improvement), and contact details for allergy follow-up.

Trigger avoidance and allergy testing decisions
Because clarithromycin was the presumptive trigger and avoidance is practical, the team documented macrolide allergy clearly in the chart and primary care record and advised the patient and clinicians to avoid further macrolide exposure. In this case the allergy team elected not to pursue provocative skin or drug challenge testing because of the high risk of provoking another severe reaction and the feasibility of avoiding the drug; this is an appropriate, patient-centered decision when avoidance is possible and the risks of testing outweigh expected benefits. Documentation should also describe the reaction phenotype (anaphylaxis) so future prescribers weigh alternatives appropriately.

Follow-up with allergy/immunology and mast cell evaluation
Given the atypical multiphasic course and persistently elevated tryptase, specialist follow-up was arranged. Recommended elements of that follow-up include repeat baseline tryptase at least 24–48 hours after all symptoms have resolved to establish a true baseline, and additional investigations for mast cell disorders when baseline tryptase is persistently elevated (bone marrow evaluation and other tests per specialist guidance). The team counseled the patient about potential mast cell disease co-triggers (for example alcohol, heat, exercise, NSAIDs) and advised avoidance or caution with these factors until a clear diagnosis and personalized plan are in place.

Chronic symptom control and medications
The patient was started on a daily non-sedating H1 antihistamine (levocetirizine) to help control cutaneous and mediator-related symptoms and as part of baseline management in suspected mast cell activation. In patients with confirmed or suspected mast cell disorders, antihistamine dosing and additional agents (H2 blockers, leukotriene modifiers, or mast cell stabilizers) are tailored by specialists; routine chronic corticosteroid therapy is not indicated. Patients should be advised about when to escalate care despite prophylactic medications and to carry their autoinjector at all times.

Clinical lessons and practice pearls
This case illustrates several durable clinical habits:

- Prioritize timely, weight-appropriate intramuscular epinephrine as first-line treatment. Delays or underdosing increase risk of persistence or relapse and the need for escalation.
- Do not rely on corticosteroids to prevent biphasic or multiphasic recurrence; they are not routine prophylaxis for relapse and should not delay definitive management.
- Obtain tryptase measurements using recommended timing: an acute sample as soon as feasible, a second sample 1–2 hours (within 4 hours) after symptom onset to capture the peak, and a baseline sample at least 24 hours after complete resolution. Proper timing is essential to interpret whether mast cell activation or an underlying mast cell disorder is present.
- Risk-stratify post-event monitoring. Severe initial presentations, need for multiple epinephrine doses, or atypical/multiphasic courses justify prolonged observation or ICU-level monitoring until a clear, sustained clinical improvement is documented.
- Maintain a low threshold to investigate for underlying mast cell disease when anaphylaxis is unusually severe, recurrent, or accompanied by elevated baseline tryptase, and arrange timely specialist follow-up rather than attempting higher-risk confirmatory testing during the acute admission.
- Document trigger avoidance clearly and provide an individualized emergency action plan and autoinjector training; consider a 0.5 mg adult device when available.

Finally, this case underlines the need for better data and shared registries for biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis to inform observation periods and advanced management strategies. Meanwhile, careful documentation, appropriate specialist referral, patient education, and ready access to correctly dosed epinephrine remain the most important steps for durable, safe outpatient care.
