Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Clinical Pearls] In an adult weighing 104 kg who presents with anaphylactic shock, what is the recommended intramuscular epinephrine dose, and why might a 0.3 mg auto-injector be inadequate in this case?

Clinical answer (clinical pearl):

- Recommended intramuscular dose: epinephrine 0.01 mg/kg IM (preferably into the mid‑anterior lateral thigh), with an absolute adult maximum single IM dose of 0.5 mg. In a 104‑kg adult this guideline gives 1.04 mg by strict weight calculation but current recommendations cap the routine IM dose at 0.5 mg — therefore the correct initial IM dose for this patient is 0.5 mg.

- Why a 0.3‑mg auto‑injector may be inadequate: 0.3 mg in a 104‑kg patient delivers ≈0.003 mg/kg (0.3/104), which is substantially below the recommended 0.01 mg/kg. Underdosing can result in incomplete reversal of vasodilation, bronchospasm and mucosal edema, slower or transient hemodynamic response, and a higher likelihood of needing additional epinephrine doses or escalation of care. In the presented case the patient initially received 0.3 mg IM auto‑injector doses and experienced recurrent anaphylactic episodes that later required 0.5 mg IM injections to control — a real‑world example of clinical underdosing with 0.3 mg in a heavier adult.

Practical points:
- Give 0.5 mg IM into the lateral thigh for most adults >50 kg and repeat every 5–15 minutes as needed based on response.
- Use IV epinephrine only for refractory anaphylaxis in a monitored setting (small aliquots or infusion with cardiac monitoring) because of arrhythmia/hyper‑adrenergic risks.
- Be aware that many commonly available auto‑injectors are 0.3 mg; for larger adults ensure access to 0.5‑mg dosing in the ED or prescribe a 0.5‑mg autoinjector if available and appropriate.

### [Differential Diagnosis] How do you differentiate biphasic anaphylaxis from refractory or protracted anaphylaxis at the bedside, and what historical or treatment-related clues help you make that distinction?

Short answer
At the bedside you distinguish biphasic (or multiphasic) anaphylaxis from refractory/protracted anaphylaxis by the pattern of symptoms over time and the immediate response to correctly administered epinephrine. Biphasic/multiphasic reactions show an initial clinical response with an asymptomatic interval and then recurrence(s) without further exposure; refractory/protracted reactions show ongoing shock or respiratory compromise that fails to respond (refractory) or never fully resolves and persists for many hours to days (protracted). Practical bedside clues — timing of recurrence, reproducible response to bolus epinephrine, need for continuous infusion, evidence of ongoing antigen exposure, and markers of mast cell activation — help you make the distinction.

Clinical reasoning and bedside features
- Response to epinephrine
  - Biphasic/multiphasic: each episode typically reverses promptly with appropriate intramuscular (IM) or IV bolus epinephrine. In the presented case the patient initially improved after IM epinephrine and each subsequent relapse again resolved after IM epinephrine — a pattern that favors recurrent (biphasic/multiphasic) events rather than true refractory shock.
  - Refractory anaphylaxis: persistent hypotension/bradycardia/airway compromise despite correctly dosed epinephrine (0.01 mg/kg IM up to 0.5 mg; IV boluses or infusion when needed). Requirement for escalating vasopressor support or continuous IV epinephrine to maintain hemodynamics is a hallmark of refractory disease.

- Temporal pattern
  - Biphasic: symptom-free interval between initial resolution and recurrence, usually within 1–24 hours (most within 6–12 h). Multiphasic = several such recurrences.
  - Protracted: continuous, relapsing symptoms without a clear asymptomatic interval lasting many hours to days despite treatment.
  - In the case: relapses occurred within the first 12 hours with intervening improvement → fits biphasic/multiphasic rather than protracted continuous course.

- Need for continuous infusion or ongoing organ support
  - If symptoms require continuous IV epinephrine/vasopressors or prolonged ventilatory support despite adequate bolus doses → think refractory/protracted.
  - If intermittent IM/bolus epinephrine repeatedly terminates discrete episodes → think biphasic/multiphasic.

- Evidence of ongoing antigen exposure or absorption
  - Sustained receptor stimulation from continued exposure (e.g., ongoing IV drug infusion, retained food in GI tract, depot/sustained-release formulations) or re-exposure will mimic persistence rather than true biphasic relapse. Ask about timing, route and formulation of the offending agent.
  - In the case the patient had a single oral 500 mg clarithromycin dose with no further exposure, arguing against ongoing exposure as the cause of recurrence.

Helpful historical and treatment-related clues
- Timing of epinephrine administration: delayed initial epinephrine has been associated with higher biphasic risk. Rapid initial treatment that produced clear improvement then later recurrence suggests biphasic.
- Number and dose of epinephrine doses: multiple discrete successful boluses point to recurrent episodes; need for continuous infusion or failure of repeated boluses points to refractory disease. Note also underdosing risk — many adults received only 0.3 mg auto‑injectors when 0.5 mg would be appropriate; underdosing can cause apparent relapse.
- Severity of the initial reaction: severe initial shock or respiratory failure increases risk of biphasic relapse, but does not distinguish refractory from biphasic without response information.
- Medication history and absorption kinetics: oral or depot agents with slow absorption can produce prolonged symptoms; consider whether symptom recurrence could be ongoing absorption rather than a true biphasic immune response.
- Use of adjunctive therapies: routine corticosteroids do not reliably prevent biphasic reactions. Their presence or absence is not diagnostic; they should not substitute for careful observation.
- Underlying mast cell disease or elevated baseline tryptase: high baseline tryptase or features suggesting mastocytosis increase risk of unusually severe, recurrent, or multiphasic anaphylaxis. In this report the patient had persistently elevated tryptase and features suggesting systemic mastocytosis, which plausibly contributed to the multiphasic course.

Putting it together at the bedside
1. Characterize the immediate epinephrine response. Rapid, reproducible reversal of each episode → biphasic/multiphasic. Lack of response or progressive deterioration despite adequate epinephrine → refractory.
2. Review exposure history. A single, no-further-exposure event with an intervening asymptomatic period favors biphasic. Ongoing exposure or slow-release formulation suggests persistent reaction.
3. Assess need for continuous vasopressors/infusions or advanced airway support. Their need suggests refractory/protracted disease and prompts ICU-level care and early consideration of IV epinephrine infusion.
4. Factor in risk modifiers: delayed initial epinephrine, severity of first episode, multiple epinephrine doses, and underlying mast cell disorders increase the risk of biphasic/multiphasic courses and should lower your threshold for prolonged observation or ICU monitoring.

Clinical implications from the case
- The patient’s course — initial good response to IM epinephrine, subsequent discrete relapses within 12 hours that again promptly responded to IM epinephrine, no further exposure to clarithromycin — supports a diagnosis of multiphasic anaphylaxis rather than refractory or continuously protracted anaphylaxis.
- Her elevated tryptase and possible systemic mastocytosis explain susceptibility to severe and repeated mediator release and justify extended monitoring and specialist follow‑up.
- Practically: severe initial presentations, recurrence within 24 hours, need for multiple epinephrine doses, or suspected mast cell disorders warrant prolonged observation (often 12–24 h or ICU monitoring), readiness to give additional epinephrine, and a low threshold for escalation to continuous IV vasopressor support if boluses fail.

Takeaway
Differentiate by the pattern of symptom recurrence and the response to appropriately dosed epinephrine: reproducible, discrete recurrences after an asymptomatic interval point to biphasic/multiphasic anaphylaxis; failure to respond or continuous instability despite epinephrine indicates refractory/protracted anaphylaxis. History (single vs ongoing exposure), timing and adequacy of initial therapy, number of epinephrine doses, and markers of mast cell activation (tryptase, clinical features of mastocytosis) are the bedside clues that guide diagnosis and monitoring.

### [Morning Report] What are the recommended timings for tryptase sampling during and after anaphylaxis, and how do you interpret an acute rise using the ‘20% + 2’ rule versus a persistently elevated baseline?

Direct answer
- Recommended sampling: obtain an initial tryptase sample as soon as feasible without delaying resuscitation, draw a second (peak) sample at ~1–2 hours after symptom onset (acceptable up to 4 hours), and obtain a baseline sample at least 24 hours after full clinical resolution (often 24–48 hours).
- Interpretation: an acute mast cell activation is supported when the post‑event (peak) tryptase rises above the baseline by the “20% + 2” rule — i.e., peak ≥ (baseline × 1.2) + 2 ng/mL. A persistently elevated tryptase measured on the ≥24‑hour sample (or repeatedly elevated baselines) suggests an underlying mast cell disorder (eg, systemic mastocytosis) rather than only an acute release.

Clinical reasoning and pathophysiology
- Tryptase is released from activated mast cells; serum levels usually begin to rise within minutes, peak around 1–2 hours after symptom onset, and fall over the next several hours (often near baseline by ~24 hours). That temporal profile is why the peak and later baseline samples are required to distinguish acute release from chronically elevated levels.
- The 20% + 2 rule is used because absolute tryptase values vary between individuals; a relative rise above baseline plus a small absolute increment improves specificity for true mast cell activation in the setting of suspected anaphylaxis.
- A single elevated tryptase in the acute phase can reflect either (a) mast cell degranulation from anaphylaxis or (b) an elevated baseline from mast cell disease. Comparing peak to a true baseline (≥24 hours after symptoms) is therefore essential.

Applying this to the presented case
- The authors note that tryptase testing during the early episodes was not done per guidelines. They did obtain a sample 1 hour after the third episode (appropriate timing for a peak) and a second sample 27 hours after the last recurrence (acceptable as a baseline). The reported findings were: an increased tryptase during the episode and a persistently increased baseline on follow‑up—compatible with both acute mast cell activation during the anaphylactic episode and an elevated baseline suggesting an underlying mast cell disorder (they subsequently found features raising the probability of systemic mastocytosis, though formal diagnostic criteria were not fully met).
- Had the peak sample been obtained but no baseline been available, one could not confidently distinguish acute release from preexisting elevation; conversely, an elevated baseline alone would prompt evaluation for systemic mastocytosis (hematology/allergy referral, bone marrow studies, KIT mutation testing as indicated).

Practical points for clinicians
- Do not delay lifesaving treatment to draw tryptase; draw the first sample as soon as practical after airway/circulation are secured.
- Aim for: sample 1 (as soon as feasible), sample 2 at ~1–2 h (≤4 h) from onset, sample 3 ≥24 h after resolution as the baseline.
- Use the 20% + 2 rule to define a significant acute rise. Persistent elevation of baseline tryptase (eg, repeatedly >20 ng/mL or clearly above population norms and not returning to baseline) warrants workup for mast cell disease.
- Remember that normal tryptase does not exclude anaphylaxis (especially some food or non‑hypotensive reactions), so interpret results in the clinical context.

### [Pathophysiology] Which clinical features and laboratory findings in this patient should prompt evaluation for a clonal mast cell disorder, and what are the key WHO diagnostic criteria for systemic mastocytosis?

Key features in this case that should prompt evaluation for a clonal mast cell disorder
- Clinical pattern: severe, multiphasic anaphylaxis with hypotension and repeated need for epinephrine despite avoidance of further exposure. Clonal mast cell disease (eg, systemic mastocytosis) commonly presents with severe, recurrent or unexplained anaphylaxis and can predispose to biphasic or refractory courses.
- Historical clues: very pronounced local reaction to a Hymenoptera sting in the past is another red flag for an underlying mast cell disorder.
- Organ/system findings: hepatomegaly on abdominal ultrasound (not explained otherwise) and neuropsychiatric symptoms (depression, memory problems) — extracutaneous organ involvement raises suspicion.
- Laboratory pattern: an elevated acute tryptase during anaphylaxis and an persistently elevated baseline tryptase on follow‑up. In this patient tryptase was increased during the episode and remained raised thereafter — this is a central objective clue.

Practical testing triggers from the case
- Any patient with idiopathic, severe, recurrent, multiphasic or refractory anaphylaxis should have serum tryptase measured (see timing below) and a baseline tryptase checked after recovery.
- If baseline tryptase is persistently >20 ng/mL (or there is an unexplained pattern of high acute and baseline levels) or clinical features above are present, pursue hematologic evaluation for a clonal mast cell disorder.

Recommended tryptase sampling (important for interpretation)
- Obtain an acute sample as soon as feasible (do not delay life‑saving therapy).
- A second acute sample 1–2 hours after symptom onset (within 4 hours) when tryptase typically peaks.
- A baseline sample at least 24 hours (often ≥48 hours) after complete clinical resolution to establish the patient’s baseline tryptase. (The case missed early measurements but had an acute and a later baseline sample showing persistent elevation.)

WHO diagnostic criteria for systemic mastocytosis (how diagnosis is established)
Diagnosis requires either: the major criterion plus one minor criterion, or at least three minor criteria.

- Major criterion
  - Multifocal, dense infiltrates of mast cells (≥15 mast cells per aggregate) in bone marrow and/or extracutaneous organ(s) on histology.

- Minor criteria (each counts as one)
  1. >25% of mast cells in the infiltrate are atypical or spindle‑shaped, or a similar finding in bone marrow smears.  
  2. Detection of a activating KIT mutation at codon 816 (most commonly D816V) in blood, bone marrow or another extracutaneous organ.  
  3. Mast cells in bone marrow or other extracutaneous organs expressing aberrant surface markers CD25 and/or CD2 (by flow cytometry or immunohistochemistry).  
  4. Persistently elevated baseline serum tryptase >20 ng/mL (unless an associated clonal hematologic non‑mast cell lineage disease elevates tryptase).

How this applies to the presented case
- The authors found elevated tryptase during an episode and persistently elevated baseline tryptase on follow‑up (one minor criterion). Imaging showed hepatomegaly (compatible with extracutaneous involvement). Bone marrow sampling was limited and did not allow demonstration of the major criterion; only two WHO minor criteria were documented, so the formal diagnostic threshold for systemic mastocytosis was not met, but the probability remained high — appropriately prompting further hematology workup (KIT testing, repeat bone marrow with adequate sampling and immunophenotyping for CD25/CD2).

Takeaway (clinical pearl)
- Recurrent or unusually severe anaphylaxis (especially idiopathic, multiphasic, or following Hymenoptera stings), hypotension/shock, persistent elevation of baseline tryptase (>20 ng/mL), and signs of organ involvement should trigger evaluation for a clonal mast cell disorder. Definitive diagnosis rests on WHO criteria requiring histologic, molecular and/or immunophenotypic confirmation (major + ≥1 minor, or ≥3 minor).

### [Management] Given the risk of recurrence after apparent resolution, how would you structure a risk-stratified observation plan in the ED/ICU for this patient, and what factors would lead you to extend monitoring beyond 6–12 hours?

Direct answer
Structure observation using a three‑tier, risk‑stratified plan: low‑risk (short observation, discharge if stable), intermediate‑risk (extended observation 6–12 hours with nursing checks and reassessment), and high‑risk (ICU monitoring ≥24 hours with continuous monitoring and readiness for escalation). In this patient—severe initial shock, multiple epinephrine doses, progressive multiphasic relapses and probable mast cell disorder—I would treat her as high‑risk and continue close ICU observation for at least 24 hours and only step down after 24 hours free of recurrence.

Why: clinical reasoning and pathophysiology
- Biphasic/multiphasic anaphylaxis reflects ongoing mediator release or re‑activation of mast cells after initial suppression; timing of relapses is variable (most within 6–12 hours but reported up to 78 hours). Because predictors are imperfect, risk stratification must weight features that correlate with ongoing mediator burden or a higher likelihood of relapse.
- In this case the patient had several high‑risk features: profound hypotension and hypoxaemia on presentation, requirement for repeated intramuscular epinephrine doses (4 total), progressive need for higher epinephrine dosing, multiphasic relapses within the first 12 hours, and elevated tryptase with suspected systemic mastocytosis — all markers of increased mast cell activation and recurrent mediator release. Those features justify prolonged and higher‑acuity monitoring.

How to operationalize monitoring (what to do and for how long)
- Low‑risk (single, mild reaction; rapid complete resolution after one accepted dose of IM epinephrine; no respiratory/cardiovascular compromise; no history of mast cell disorder; no beta‑blocker use): observe 4–6 hours after symptom resolution with periodic vitals and exam; discharge with epinephrine auto‑injector and allergist follow‑up.
- Intermediate‑risk (moderate reaction, comorbid asthma, older age, delayed epinephrine >1 dose, or persistent urticaria/angioedema): observe 6–12 hours with nursing checks every 15–60 minutes initially, then hourly if stable; continuous pulse oximetry; ensure IV access and immediate epinephrine availability.
- High‑risk (any of the following): severe initial shock or hypoxaemia; need for multiple epinephrine doses or escalating doses; refractory or recurrent anaphylaxis (biphasic/multiphasic); suspected or known mast cell disorder (eg, elevated baseline tryptase); delayed presentation with ongoing absorption risk (eg, depot medication/ingested allergen); concurrent beta‑blocker use or significant cardiopulmonary comorbidity. Management: ICU level monitoring for ≥24 hours, continuous cardiac telemetry, continuous pulse oximetry, frequent airway exams, IV access kept patent, ready epinephrine (IM and IV) and fluids. Consider prepared plans for escalation: repeated IM epinephrine, early use of IV epinephrine infusion if recurrent or refractory, and critical care involvement. Only step down when symptom‑free for 24 hours and clinically stable.

Specific triggers to extend monitoring beyond the usual 6–12 hours (apply to this patient)
- Severe initial presentation (SBP 74/50 mm Hg, SpO2 88%) — higher risk of relapse.
- Multiple epinephrine doses required to control episodes — indicates higher mediator load and risk of recurrence.
- Documented multiphasic course already occurring within the admission (this patient had four episodes, two after admission) — proven recurrence demands extended observation.
- Suspected or confirmed mast cell disorder (elevated tryptase and other suggestive findings in this case) — predisposes to recurrent degranulation and severe anaphylaxis.
- Escalating or prolonged symptoms despite therapy, or need for airway support/oxygen during relapses.
- Incomplete access to follow‑up care or inability to guarantee immediate return if relapse occurs.

Ancillary monitoring and diagnostics to guide disposition (drawn from the case)
- Continuous cardiac telemetry and pulse oximetry while at high risk.
- Maintain IV access and crystalloid resuscitation capacity.
- Serial tryptase sampling (as recommended in the report): obtain a sample as soon as feasible during an acute event, a second sample at ~1–2 hours (within 4 hours) to capture the peak, and a baseline sample ≥24 hours after resolution to help diagnose mast cell disorders. In this patient tryptase was elevated and supported the concern for systemic mastocytosis — that finding contributed to prolonged monitoring and specialist consultation.
- Documented capacity to deliver repeat IM epinephrine immediately; have IV epinephrine available if recurrent/refractory events.
- Consider early specialist (allergy/immunology, hematology for mastocytosis) involvement to guide further workup and long‑term risk counselling.

Therapeutic considerations tied to monitoring
- IM epinephrine remains first‑line; adult dose target 0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg (many auto‑injectors are 0.3 mg and may underdose adults >50 kg — relevant in this patient who initially received 0.3 mg then required higher dosing).
- Routine corticosteroids to prevent biphasic reactions are not supported by good evidence; they may be considered case‑by‑case but should not replace appropriate observation and readiness for repeat epinephrine. In the case, steroids were given after recurrence but evidence for prevention is weak.
- For frequent relapses or refractory anaphylaxis consider continuous IV epinephrine infusion in ICU (weigh risks carefully and involve critical care). In the case report this was considered but ultimately not needed.

Disposition and discharge planning
- Before discharge: symptom‑free interval (high‑risk patients: ≥24 hours), want baseline tryptase obtained, ensure patient education, prescription and training with an appropriately dosed epinephrine auto‑injector, antihistamines for cutaneous symptoms, and prompt allergy/immunology follow‑up. For suspected mast cell disorder, expedite specialty workup and counseling.

Bottom line
Use a risk‑stratified approach: low‑risk 4–6 hours, intermediate 6–12 hours, high‑risk ICU monitoring ≥24 hours. Extend observation beyond 6–12 hours for severe initial shock, multiple/ escalating epinephrine doses, documented biphasic/multiphasic relapses, suspected mast cell disorder (as in this patient with elevated tryptase), respiratory compromise, or inability to ensure rapid return to care. In this case the combination of profound initial shock, multiple recurrences and probable systemic mastocytosis justifies ICU monitoring and a 24‑hour symptom‑free window before step‑down.

### [Clinical Pearls] What is the current evidence regarding corticosteroids in acute anaphylaxis, particularly for preventing biphasic reactions, and how should that evidence influence your practice in the ED and ICU?

Bottom line
- High-quality evidence does not support routine corticosteroids to prevent biphasic (or multiphasic) anaphylaxis. Steroids are not a substitute for prompt epinephrine and supportive care and should not be given routinely for the purpose of preventing relapse.
- In practice in the ED and ICU: give immediate epinephrine for acute anaphylaxis, use antihistamines and fluids as adjuncts, and reserve corticosteroids for specific indications (eg, concomitant asthma exacerbation, known adrenal insufficiency, or other clear steroid-responsive comorbidity). Use clinical risk factors — not steroid administration — to guide observation level and duration; consider ICU/continuous monitoring for patients with severe initial reactions, repeated epinephrine doses, delayed treatment, or suspected mast cell disorder.

Why: the evidence and pathophysiology
- Pathophysiology: the acute phase of anaphylaxis is mediated by rapid mast cell/basophil degranulation with release of histamine, tryptase and other mediators. Corticosteroids work by genomic suppression of inflammatory gene expression that takes hours to days to manifest; they do not rapidly block mediator effects and therefore have no plausible mechanism to abort the immediate bronchospasm, vasodilation or hypotension.
- Systematic evidence: a 2017 systematic review (22 studies) found no convincing evidence that corticosteroids reduce the risk of biphasic reactions (21 studies negative, 1 positive). Subsequent observational analyses have not shown benefit and have suggested potential harm — eg, association with increased hospital/ICU admission after prehospital steroid use in some cohorts. There are no randomized trials showing steroids prevent biphasic anaphylaxis.
- Guidelines: most contemporary guidelines no longer recommend routine steroids for prevention of biphasic reactions; instead they emphasize epinephrine as first-line therapy and risk-stratified observation.

How this applies to the case and to bedside decisions
- In the presented case the team administered corticosteroids after the first relapse out of tradition. That practice is common but not evidence-based for preventing further relapses. The patient nevertheless had multiple recurrences and required repeated IM epinephrine; steroids did not demonstrably prevent additional episodes.
- Practical ED/ICU approach:
  - First-line therapy: immediate intramuscular epinephrine (0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg in adults; many adult patients >50 kg benefit from 0.5 mg). Give early; delayed epinephrine is a risk factor for more severe and biphasic courses.
  - Adjunctive therapy: oxygen, IV crystalloids for hypotension, inhaled beta-agonists for bronchospasm, H1/H2 antihistamines for cutaneous symptoms (these are symptomatic; they do not prevent life‑threatening features).
  - Corticosteroids: do not give routinely to prevent biphasic reactions. Consider steroids only if there is another clear indication (severe refractory bronchospasm with suspected late inflammatory component, known adrenal insufficiency, concomitant severe asthma exacerbation, or other comorbidity where steroids are indicated). If used, document the indication and dose — common historical dosing was IV hydrocortisone 100–200 mg or IV methylprednisolone 40–125 mg — but recognize this is for concurrent steroid-responsive disease, not relapse prevention.
  - Observation/monitoring: decide observation location and duration by risk. Factors that increase likelihood of biphasic/multiphasic course include severe initial reaction, need for multiple epinephrine doses, delayed timing of first epinephrine, protracted symptoms, and possible mast cell disorders. In such patients (as in this case, older patient with elevated tryptase and suspected systemic mastocytosis), a lower threshold for ICU monitoring and longer observation (eg, 24 hours) is reasonable. For low‑risk patients with rapid, complete response to a single epinephrine dose, shorter observation (4–6 hours in many guidelines) is acceptable.
  - Recurrent or refractory episodes: if frequent relapses occur, escalate to ICU, consider continuous IV epinephrine infusion rather than repeated IM doses, and involve allergy/immunology and critical care. Evaluate for underlying mast cell disorder (serial tryptase sampling per guideline timing) when indicated.
  - Documentation and patient education: ensure clear discharge instructions, prescription and training in appropriate intramuscular epinephrine auto-injector (appropriate dose for body weight), and allergist follow-up for testing and consideration of mast cell disease.

Takeaway for clinicians
- Stop reflexive use of corticosteroids solely to prevent biphasic anaphylaxis. Treat acutely with epinephrine, provide appropriate supportive care, and tailor observation and level of care to clinical risk factors — especially if the presentation is severe, recurrent, or there is suspicion for an underlying mast cell disorder.

### [Management] In a patient with multiphasic anaphylaxis who repeatedly responds to IM epinephrine but relapses, what are the indications, benefits, and risks of initiating a titrated intravenous epinephrine infusion compared with an expectant approach?

Bottom line
- A titrated intravenous (IV) epinephrine infusion is indicated when anaphylaxis is recurrent, progressive, or hemodynamically unstable despite appropriate IM epinephrine and supportive care — or when repeated IM dosing is impractical or unsafe.  
- An expectant/watchful approach can be reasonable when relapses are few, responses to IM epinephrine are rapid and complete, vitals are stable between episodes, symptom intensity is declining, and close monitoring with rapid re‑dose capability is available.  
- Choice must be individualized and driven by clinical trajectory, comorbid risk (eg, suspected mast cell disorder), and availability of ICU monitoring because IV epinephrine gives better physiologic control but carries nontrivial cardiovascular risk.

Why these options matter (clinical reasoning and pathophysiology)
- Anaphylaxis is driven by massive mediator release (histamine, tryptase, leukotrienes, prostaglandins) causing vasodilation, capillary leak, bronchospasm and mucosal edema. Biphasic/multiphasic courses reflect ongoing mediator activity or secondary waves of activation despite initial reversal.  
- IM epinephrine (0.01 mg/kg, up to 0.5 mg adult) is first‑line because it is safe, rapidly absorbed and reverses most anaphylactic physiology. Recurrent episodes that repeatedly respond to IM epinephrine may represent intermittent mediator bursts that could be suppressed by a continuous low‑dose IV infusion. Conversely, if recurrences are isolated, short, and getting milder, continued observation with prompt IM re‑dosing may avoid the risks of continuous IV vasopressor therapy.

When to favor a titrated IV epinephrine infusion (indications)
Consider starting a carefully titrated IV infusion when one or more of the following apply:
- Refractory or frequent relapses: multiple recurrences within hours despite appropriate IM epinephrine (as in the presented case — four episodes in the first 12 hours), or increasing frequency/need for escalating IM doses.  
- Hemodynamic instability between episodes or impending shock requiring vasopressor support (persistent hypotension, poor perfusion) despite initial doses.  
- Airway or respiratory compromise not fully controlled by IM dosing (progressive airway edema, ongoing severe bronchospasm).  
- High likelihood of continued mediator release (elevated or rising tryptase, history or suspicion of mast cell disorder/systemic mastocytosis). The case’s elevated baseline and episode tryptase increased the pretest probability for ongoing susceptibility.  
- Logistics/safety concerns: inability to monitor closely, or when IM injections are impractical (eg, morbid obesity, poor peripheral perfusion), or when repeated injections are likely to cause delays or errors.

Benefits of a titrated IV infusion
- Continuous, titratable hemodynamic support — smoothes blood pressure and reduces swings that accompany repeated IM boluses.  
- Can prevent further relapse by suppressing ongoing mediator effects rather than repeatedly reversing discrete episodes.  
- May reduce total epinephrine requirements and avoid repeated painful injections, and can be stopped quickly once stable.  
- Facilitates controlled weaning under ICU monitoring when risk of recurrence is high.

Risks and practical downsides of IV epinephrine
- Cardiovascular adverse effects: tachyarrhythmias, myocardial ischemia, hypertension, severe tachycardia, stroke risk in susceptible patients. These risks are greater with higher infusion rates or rapid up‑titration.  
- Local extravasation/catheter complications if peripheral IV used.  
- Metabolic effects: lactic acidosis, hyperglycaemia, hypokalemia.  
- Requires ICU setting with continuous cardiac monitoring, trained staff, and ready access to arterial pressure monitoring if needed — resource intensive.  
- Potential for dosing or infusion pump errors; therefore strict protocols and weight‑based titration are essential.

How to use an infusion safely (practical principles)
- Only initiate in monitored setting (ICU/stepdown) with continuous ECG and frequent BP checks. Consider arterial line if severe instability anticipated.  
- Use weight‑based starting and titration ranges; common pragmatic adult approach: start low and titrate to effect. A typical starting range used in practice is 0.02–0.05 mcg/kg/min and titrate upward (some references use 0.05–0.5 mcg/kg/min depending on severity). Alternatively, many centers use fixed microgram/min regimens (eg, 1–10 mcg/min) titrated to hemodynamics and airway status. Choose the range familiar to your unit and use the lowest effective dose.  
- Have protocols for initial IV bolus in profound hypotension (eg, 50–100 mcg IV bolus) followed by infusion if bolus transiently effective but hypotension recurs.  
- Regularly reassess for resolution of bronchospasm/airway edema and signs of end‑organ perfusion; once stable, wean infusion slowly and observe for recrudescence.  
- Document doses and triggers; involve allergy/immunology early for specialized guidance and downstream evaluation (tryptase sampling, mast cell disorder workup).

When an expectant approach is reasonable (indications and monitoring)
- Single relapse or small number of brief relapses that respond promptly and completely to IM epinephrine, with progressive diminution in severity (as occurred later in this patient).  
- Hemodynamically stable between episodes, normalizing oxygenation, no progressive airway edema, and reliable nursing/monitoring available for ≥24 hours (most biphasic events occur within first 6–12 hours but can occur up to 72 hours).  
- No strong suspicion of mast cell disease or other risk factors for recurrent severe mediator release.  
- The expectant strategy requires a low threshold to escalate to infusion if recurrences increase in frequency or severity.

Applying this to the presented case
- This patient had multiple relapses (four episodes in 12 hours), required repeated IM epinephrine, and had elevated tryptase with high probability of a mast cell disorder — features that would argue in favor of starting a monitored, titrated IV infusion to prevent further episodes.  
- The team instead chose careful ICU monitoring and repeated IM dosing; symptoms diminished and no further relapses occurred. That outcome illustrates that an individualized approach is acceptable: if the clinical course is improving and ICU monitoring with rapid re‑dose ability is available, expectant management can succeed — but in a patient with more frequent relapses, persistent hemodynamic lability, or confirmed mast cell disease, early infusion would be strongly justifiable.

Takeaway
- Use IM epinephrine first and continue to reassess. Escalate to a titrated IV infusion when recurrence is frequent, progressive, or associated with hemodynamic/airway compromise or a high risk of ongoing mediator release (eg, suspected systemic mastocytosis). Balance the benefit of preventing relapse and stabilizing physiology against the cardiovascular risks and resource needs of continuous IV epinephrine; perform infusion only under continuous monitoring with clear titration and weaning protocols.

### [Differential Diagnosis] What alternative explanations should be considered for recurrent symptoms after initial improvement (for example, ongoing allergen absorption or inadequate epinephrine dosing), and how would you investigate and mitigate each possibility?

Direct answer
Recurrent anaphylactic symptoms after an initial apparent response can reflect several distinct problems that require different investigations and treatments: ongoing or delayed allergen absorption, inadequate epinephrine delivery (under‑dosing or inadequate route), sustained mediator release from an unusually active mast cell compartment (including mast cell disease), true biphasic anaphylaxis, refractory anaphylaxis, or alternative/non‑allergic causes mimicking relapse. Below I list each explanation, why it matters, how to investigate it in practice, and how to mitigate it — with reference to features of the presented case.

1) Ongoing or delayed allergen absorption (continued exposure)
- Rationale: If the offending antigen remains in the body (oral drug still in gut, depot injection, transdermal patch, ingested food in stomach) continued absorption can perpetuate mediator release and produce recurrent symptoms rather than a true biphasic phenomenon.
- Clues in this case: Clarithromycin was oral and only a single 500 mg dose was taken; no further doses reported. A late allergic rash to prior clindamycin suggests prior sensitisation, but re‑exposure after ED was excluded.
- Investigation: focused history (timing, formulation, other exposures), inspect for retained material (patches, topical agents), review medications given in hospital for cross‑reactivity, and consider drug kinetics (enteric coating, slow release).
- Mitigation: stop the agent (done here), consider activated charcoal for recent large oral ingestions if within appropriate time window and no contraindication (rarely applicable in typical therapeutic dosing), supportive care and close observation until unlikely ongoing absorption. In practice for single‑dose oral antibiotics like clarithromycin taken >1 hr before presentation, ongoing absorption is unlikely to cause relapses over many hours, making this a less likely cause in this case.

2) Inadequate epinephrine dosing or delivery
- Rationale: Under‑dosing or delay can produce incomplete reversal, transient response, and recurrence when drug effect wanes (sustained anaphylaxis mistaken for biphasic). Recommended IM dose is 0.01 mg/kg (max ~0.5 mg) — many adult auto‑injectors provide only 0.3 mg and may underdose adults >50 kg.
- Clues in this case: Initial management used a 0.3 mg IM auto‑injector (institutional practice) with later episodes requiring additional IM doses; later IM doses were 0.5 mg. The case discusses evidence that 0.3 mg may be insufficient in heavier adults.
- Investigation: confirm timing and dose of each epinephrine administration, patient weight, clinical response pattern after each dose. If response is transient despite correct IM dosing, consider refractory physiology.
- Mitigation: ensure correct weight‑based dosing (aim for 0.5 mg IM in most adults over 50 kg). For recurrent episodes despite repeated IM doses, escalate: establish IV access, give repeated IM doses while preparing for IV epinephrine infusion (titrated vasopressor infusion) if hypotension or respiratory compromise persists or recurs frequently. Use monitoring (ECG, BP, pulse ox) and ICU care for infusion. In the case, escalation to continuous IV infusion was considered but ultimately not needed.

3) Sustained mediator release / mast cell disorder (e.g., systemic mastocytosis)
- Rationale: Patients with elevated baseline mast cell burden or dysregulated mast cell activation can have prolonged, relapsing, or multiphasic anaphylaxis because mediators are released over longer periods or the threshold for re‑activation is low.
- Clues in this case: persistently elevated tryptase (baseline and during episode), hepatomegaly on ultrasound, and the authors’ conclusion of high probability of systemic mastocytosis (though not fulfilling full diagnostic criteria).
- Investigation: measure acute and baseline tryptase correctly (acute sample as soon as feasible, then 1–2 h after onset [within 4 h] to catch peak, and a baseline sample ≥24 h after full resolution). If baseline tryptase is elevated, refer to allergology/hematology for bone marrow aspirate/biopsy, KIT D816V testing, and WHO criteria for systemic mastocytosis. Avoid provocation testing that risks recurrence until risk is mitigated.
- Mitigation: prolonged observation (longer than routine 4–6 hours) is reasonable; low threshold for ICU monitoring. Consider prophylactic H1/H2 antihistamines and leukotriene modifiers for symptom control, carry epinephrine, and specialist‑directed mast cell stabilizers or targeted therapy if diagnosis is confirmed. In this patient, elevated tryptase and subsequent specialist follow‑up appropriately informed discharge planning (antihistamine therapy, epinephrine prescription).

4) True biphasic versus multiphasic anaphylaxis
- Rationale: Biphasic reactions represent a delayed wave of mediator effects after an initial resolution without further exposure. Timing is variable (most within 24 hours, many within 6–12 hours).
- Clues in this case: multiple recurrences within the first 12 hours with progressively diminishing severity — fits multiphasic biphasic pattern.
- Investigation: document timing of symptom resolution and recurrence, correlate with tryptase kinetics if available, and exclude other causes (ongoing exposure, underdosing).
- Mitigation: extended observation duration tailored to risk factors (severe initial reaction, need for multiple epinephrine doses, delayed epinephrine, mast cell disorder). There’s no proven role for routine steroids to prevent biphasic reactions; current evidence does not support their routine use, though they remain commonly used. The team in this case administered steroids after the first recurrence according to local protocol.

5) Refractory anaphylaxis
- Rationale: persistent anaphylaxis not responding adequately to standard epinephrine dosing may need aggressive hemodynamic support (IV epinephrine infusion, vasopressors, intubation) and search for comorbid contributors.
- Clues: this patient DID respond to IM epinephrine each time — so this was not refractory anaphylaxis.
- Investigation: reassess airway, breathing, circulation, check for concomitant beta‑blocker use (can blunt epinephrine response), evaluate for myocardial ischemia or distributive shock mimics.
- Mitigation: for true refractory cases, IV epinephrine infusion under ICU monitoring, consider glucagon if beta‑blocker is present, aggressive fluid resuscitation, and advanced airway management.

6) Alternative diagnoses or contributors (cardiogenic shock, vasovagal reaction, anxiety, medication effects)
- Rationale: hypotension, dyspnoea, or flushing can have non‑allergic causes or be compounded by other conditions (arrhythmia, pulmonary embolus, panic, drug interactions).
- Investigation: ECG, cardiac enzymes, consider imaging if indicated, blood glucose, infection screen as clinically indicated. In this case, initial ECG was sinus rhythm, glucose modestly elevated, and clinical features were typical for anaphylaxis with urticaria and facial swelling.
- Mitigation: treat underlying cause if identified; if features are classic for anaphylaxis, treat per anaphylaxis algorithm.

Practical bedside approach to a patient like the case
- On initial response followed by relapse: first confirm there has been no further exposure and review timing/doses of epinephrine. If initial IM epinephrine was only 0.3 mg and the patient is >50 kg, treat recurrent severe symptoms with 0.5 mg IM (or weight‑based dosing) promptly.
- If relapses are frequent or hemodynamic compromise persists despite repeated appropriate IM doses, escalate to monitored IV epinephrine infusion in ICU.
- Obtain tryptase samples appropriately: as soon as possible, at 1–2 hours (within 4 h) after symptom onset/peak, and a baseline sample ≥24 h after full clinical resolution to assess for mast cell disorder.
- Extend observation beyond routine intervals when risk factors are present (severe initial reaction, multiple epinephrine doses, elevated tryptase or suspected mastocytosis). In this case extended ICU or monitored observation was reasonable and was used.
- Use H1 (and H2) antihistamines for cutaneous symptoms as adjuncts; routine corticosteroids are not proven to prevent biphasic reactions and should not replace epinephrine or be relied on to prevent relapse.
- Arrange allergy/mast cell workup after recovery; avoid provocative testing that risks re‑inducing severe anaphylaxis until specialist assessment.

Takeaway anchored to the case
- In this patient recurrent episodes were most consistent with multiphasic anaphylaxis in the setting of probable mast cell disease rather than ongoing clarithromycin absorption. A likely contributing practical factor was initial underdosing of epinephrine (0.3 mg IM auto‑injector in an adult >50 kg), which can produce transient improvement and need for additional doses. Correct investigation (timely tryptase sampling, specialist referral) and mitigation (use weight‑appropriate epinephrine dosing, prolonged observation/ICU monitoring when risk factors present, prescription of appropriate auto‑injector and antihistamine) were all applied or considered in the case and represent the recommended steps for similar presentations.

### [Morning Report] Which adjunctive therapies (oxygen, IV fluids, H1/H2 blockers, beta-agonists) have roles in anaphylaxis care, and which symptoms do they target versus which outcomes they do not improve?

Teaching point — primary therapy for anaphylaxis is immediate intramuscular epinephrine; the other measures listed are adjuncts that treat specific signs or support physiology but do not substitute for epinephrine and have not been shown to prevent death or reliably prevent biphasic reactions.

Brief practical summary for each adjunct (what it treats, and what it does NOT reliably change):

- Oxygen
  - Targets: hypoxaemia from upper‑airway oedema, severe bronchospasm, or hypoxaemic shock. Use high‑flow oxygen for SpO2 <94% or signs of respiratory compromise (non‑rebreather/NRB in the ED, escalate as needed).
  - Does NOT: replace epinephrine; does not treat the underlying mast cell mediator release; does not prevent recurrence or improve long‑term outcomes by itself.
  - Case tie‑in: oxygen was given initially and was able to be weaned after epinephrine restored physiology, but was restarted during subsequent relapses — illustrates supportive role only.

- Intravenous crystalloids
  - Targets: intravascular volume depletion and distributive component of anaphylactic shock (vasodilation, increased vascular permeability). Give rapid boluses (eg, 20 mL/kg adults up to repeated boluses) when hypotension persists or before/while vasopressors are started.
  - Does NOT: replace the vasoconstrictive, bronchodilatory and anti‑mediator effects of epinephrine; large volumes alone are unlikely to reverse severe hypotension due to mediator‑driven vasodilation.
  - Case tie‑in: the patient received 500 mL initially and another 500 mL during a relapse — helpful as adjunct during hypotension but epinephrine was required to restore and maintain blood pressure.

- H1 (and H2) antihistamines
  - Targets: cutaneous manifestations — urticaria, pruritus, flushing, and may reduce angioedema limited to histamine‑mediated swelling. H2 blockers can augment H1 effect for cutaneous symptoms in some patients.
  - Does NOT: treat or rapidly reverse airway obstruction, bronchospasm, or shock; do not prevent fatality; do not reliably prevent biphasic reactions. Onset is slower than epinephrine.
  - Case tie‑in: clemastine (H1) was given and helped with skin symptoms, but recurrent hypotension and hypoxaemia required repeated epinephrine doses — demonstrating histamine blockade treats cutaneous signs but not the life‑threatening systemic process.

- Inhaled beta‑2 agonists (nebulized albuterol)
  - Targets: bronchospasm and wheeze from bronchial smooth‑muscle constriction; useful as an adjunct when bronchospasm persists after epinephrine or if wheeze is prominent.
  - Does NOT: treat upper‑airway oedema, mucosal angioedema, or cardiovascular collapse; do not substitute for epinephrine (which also provides alpha‑vasoconstriction and cardiac support).
  - Practical note: give after or concomitant with epinephrine if wheeze persists.
  - Case tie‑in: although bronchodilators are not specifically described in this report, the patient’s dyspnoea and hypoxaemia responded to epinephrine — reinforcing that bronchodilators are adjunctive when bronchospasm is a component.

Additional useful clinical points
- None of these adjuncts obviate the need for prompt epinephrine; delays in epinephrine are associated with worse outcomes and are a risk factor for biphasic courses.
- Corticosteroids (not asked but often used) do not have good evidence to prevent biphasic anaphylaxis and are not a substitute for epinephrine; their routine use is increasingly questioned (and in the case, steroids were given after the first relapse without clear preventive effect).
- Monitoring after clinical resolution is required because of the risk of biphasic or multiphasic recurrence; adjunctive therapies may palliate symptoms but do not reliably prevent recurrence.
- In refractory or relapsing cases (as in this patient), repeated intramuscular epinephrine (or, if truly refractory, IV epinephrine infusion under ICU monitoring) is the escalation step — adjuncts support the patient while addressing symptoms but do not replace vasopressor/adrenergic therapy.

Bottom line: oxygen treats hypoxaemia, IV fluids treat volume loss/hypotension, H1/H2 blockers treat cutaneous histamine‑mediated signs, and inhaled beta‑agonists treat bronchospasm — but none substitute for epinephrine, nor do they reliably prevent mortality or biphasic/multiphasic recurrence.

### [Management] At hospital discharge after multiphasic anaphylaxis to clarithromycin with suspected mast cell disease, what elements should be included in the long-term plan (medications, avoidance counseling, auto-injector selection and training, referral and follow-up testing)?

Direct answer:
At discharge you should give (1) a clear medication plan for prevention and rescue, (2) concrete avoidance counselling and documentation, (3) an appropriate epinephrine auto‑injector prescribed plus hands‑on training and an action plan, and (4) expedited specialist referral and testing targeted at confirming/excluding mast cell disease and clarifying the drug allergy.

Why — and what to include (practical clinical plan grounded in this case):

1) Rescue medication and training
- Epinephrine auto‑injector(s): prescribe an appropriate adult dose. For adults >50 kg (this patient 104 kg) recommend 0.5 mg intramuscular dose if a 0.5‑mg auto‑injector is available in your setting; if only 0.3 mg devices are available, prescribe two devices and review need for repeat dosing. Teach immediate intramuscular injection into mid‑anterolateral thigh, repeat every 5 minutes if symptoms persist (up to 2–3 doses while awaiting EMS), call emergency services after first use. Provide hands‑on practice with a trainer device and document competency in the chart.
- Written anaphylaxis action plan: when to use auto‑injector, when to repeat, when to call EMS, and return precautions. Ensure patient/family know to lie supine with legs elevated for hypotension unless breathing difficulty or vomiting.
- Carrying and maintenance: advise carrying device at all times, check expiration dates, store at room temperature, replace after use.

2) Secondary medications (preventive/symptom relief)
- Daily non‑sedating H1 antihistamine: continue levocetirizine as started. For mast cell disorder patients, higher or twice‑daily dosing may be needed under specialist guidance.
- Add H2 blocker (eg, ranitidine alternatives or famotidine) can be helpful for cutaneous/GI symptoms in mast cell activation—consider local practice/patient tolerance.
- Routine systemic corticosteroids are NOT recommended solely to prevent biphasic anaphylaxis; use only for other clear indications or if directed by allergy/immunology. This reflects evolving data and the discussion in the case.
- Avoid routine prescribing of chronic epinephrine infusions or prophylactic systemic steroids at discharge.

3) Avoidance counselling and documentation
- Document clarithromycin as the suspected trigger in hospital and primary care records, and instruct strict avoidance of clarithromycin. Given preceding clindamycin‑associated rash and the severity with clarithromycin, advise avoiding macrolides and possibly inform primary care to avoid empirical macrolide prescribing until specialist review; however, cross‑reactivity between macrolides is uncommon—definitive testing should be performed only in specialist settings.
- Discuss other potential mast cell triggers (heat/cold, alcohol, NSAIDs, opiates, strong odors, emotional stress, exercise) and individual avoidance measures. Encourage carrying a medical alert ID noting anaphylaxis and suspected mast cell disorder.
- Advise about vaccinations/medication administration: inform providers before procedures, give recommendation for perioperative/anesthesia precautions.

4) Referral and follow‑up testing (timing and priorities)
- Urgent outpatient allergy/immunology follow‑up: within 1–2 weeks to review the event, confirm documentation, reinforce education, and plan testing.
- Tryptase testing: obtain baseline serum tryptase at least 24 hours after full resolution (to establish baseline) and review prior elevated values from hospital. If tryptase was not sampled per recommended timeline during the acute episode (ideally within 1–4 hours of onset), note this and attempt to interpret available values in context. Repeat baseline tryptase as outpatient.
- Mast cell disease workup: refer to a center experienced in mast cell disorders/hematology if baseline tryptase is persistently elevated or clinical suspicion is high (as in this case). Workup may include repeat tryptase, KIT D816V testing, bone marrow evaluation and abdominal imaging as indicated.
- Drug allergy evaluation strategy: avoid provocative testing (skin testing or graded challenge) for clarithromycin until specialist assessment because of the severe anaphylactic reaction and the patient’s suspected mast cell disorder. If testing required to guide antibiotic choices, conduct it under specialist supervision in a controlled setting (hospital/clinic with resuscitation capability).
- Coordinate care: notify primary care, update electronic allergy list, and arrange follow‑up appointments with allergist and, if indicated, hematology/mast cell centre.

5) Other practical discharge items
- Supply at least one (ideally two) auto‑injectors on discharge, written instructions, and trainer device for practice.
- Review when to use emergency services after an injection and requirement to go to ED even if symptoms improve.
- Advise family members/caregivers to learn device use.
- Schedule early outpatient review to reassess need for ongoing H1/H2 therapy, to interpret tryptase results, and to plan further diagnostics.

Bottom line: discharge planning after multiphasic anaphylaxis must combine an effective rescue strategy (appropriately dosed epinephrine auto‑injector, training, and action plan), short‑term preventive measures (daily H1 antihistamine ± H2), clear avoidance and documentation of the suspected drug, and expedited specialist follow‑up for tryptase baseline measurement and mast cell disease evaluation. Routine steroids to prevent biphasic reactions are not supported by current evidence and should not be relied upon as part of the long‑term plan. This case illustrates the need for higher adult epinephrine dosing consideration (0.5 mg) and prompt specialist follow‑up given persistently elevated tryptase and probable—but unconfirmed—systemic mastocytosis.
