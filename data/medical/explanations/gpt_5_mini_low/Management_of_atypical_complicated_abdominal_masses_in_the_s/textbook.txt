Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Assessment of Abdominal Masses in Mid-Pregnancy

This case illustrates a typical diagnostic challenge when a pregnant patient presents in the second trimester with focal abdominal pain, pelvic discomfort and dyspnoea. Early assessment must simultaneously (1) stabilise the mother and fetus, (2) screen for immediately life‑threatening cardiopulmonary disease, and (3) use focused obstetric–gynecologic examination and bedside investigations to guide the differential between mass-related mechanical problems, infection and acute mass complications (torsion, degeneration, rupture).

Key presenting symptoms and their mechanisms
The patient reported acute left upper‑quadrant pain, pelvic pain and mild dyspnoea. In pregnancy, large intra‑abdominal masses produce symptoms mainly by mass effect: a suprapubic or subphrenic lesion can distend and displace the gravid uterus, compress the diaphragm and reduce inspiratory capacity, and create traction or ischemia at a tumor pedicle causing focal pain. Dyspnoea in this setting is most often mechanical (reduced diaphragmatic excursion) rather than primary cardiopulmonary disease, but it still requires rapid exclusion of serious causes such as pulmonary embolism (PE) or cardiac dysfunction. Localized severe pain and a tense, taut‑elastic abdominal mass raise concern for torsion, degeneration (ischaemia/necrosis) or impending rupture, all of which can present with fever and leukocytosis.

Cardiopulmonary screening and stabilization priorities
Initial vital signs and monitoring are aimed at identifying instability and excluding cardiopulmonary emergencies. In this patient, oxygen saturation was normal (≈97%), heart rate and blood pressure were within expected ranges and ECG was normal — findings that lower the likelihood of acute PE or cardiomyopathy but do not exclude them. In pregnancy, common tests are: pulse oximetry, 12‑lead ECG, clinical examination for pleuritic pain or calf swelling, and fetal heart rate assessment. D‑dimer is of limited specificity in pregnancy and should be interpreted with caution; imaging for PE (eg, ventilation/perfusion scan or CT pulmonary angiography) is reserved for cases with persistent clinical suspicion. If respiratory compromise, hypoxaemia or hemodynamic instability were present, immediate maternal resuscitation and urgent imaging would be indicated.

Abdominal examination and recognition of limitations
On abdominal exam the uterus appeared larger than expected for gestational age and a taut‑elastic mass occupying the left upper quadrant was palpable (measured clinically at ~18 cm). In pregnant patients, abdominal palpation is limited by the gravid uterus and body habitus (this patient was obese, BMI 33), and distinguishing uterine from adnexal or other intra‑abdominal masses by palpation alone is often unreliable. Nevertheless, a firm, well‑defined, tender and non‑mobile mass suggests a solid organ or pedunculated lesion (eg, a pedunculated leiomyoma) while a fluctuant, cystic feel might suggest an adnexal cyst. Absence of clinical ascites and focal tenderness helped localise the problem and guided early imaging.

Bimanual pelvic examination—what the findings suggest
The bimanual exam revealed a fixed, rounded hard mass of about 9 cm in the lesser pelvis bulging into the posterior fornix. A posterior fornix bulge on vaginal exam can indicate an adnexal mass (eg, ovarian cyst), a uterine posterior wall lesion (subserosal myoma), or an enlarged uterus occupying the pouch of Douglas. Fixed, non‑mobile character increases concern for adhesions, large intramural components or mass infiltration. Importantly, bimanual palpation in pregnancy is constrained by uterine size — distinguishing a uterine myoma that projects posteriorly from an adnexal cyst can be difficult and requires imaging correlation.

Early symptomatic control while the workup proceeds
Immediate symptom control is important for maternal comfort and to avoid reflex fetal stress. Paracetamol is first‑line analgesia in pregnancy and was effective here (1000 mg IV twice daily reduced pain scores). Non‑pharmacologic measures to relieve mechanical dyspnoea, such as semi‑orthostatic or upright positioning to reduce diaphragmatic compression, are useful and safe. Supplemental oxygen or more aggressive respiratory support would be reserved for hypoxaemia. Analgesics beyond acetaminophen, or invasive interventions, are considered only after careful risk–benefit assessment.

Intercurrent fever and distinguishing infection from mass complications
The patient developed a low‑grade fever and was found to have a Klebsiella pneumoniae urinary tract infection. Fever in a pregnant patient with abdominal masses raises two main possibilities: systemic infection (eg, UTI or pelvic infection) and sterile inflammatory reactions from mass degeneration or torsion. Degenerating leiomyomas often produce localized pain, low‑grade fever and mild leukocytosis; torsion of a pedunculated mass causes acute severe pain and can lead to necrosis, fever and peritoneal signs. The identification of a pathogen in the urine supports a concurrent UTI that likely contributed to fever and neutrophilia, but it does not exclude coexisting mass complication — imaging is required to clarify the cause. Cultures (urine and, intraoperatively, peritoneal fluid) and targeted antibiotics guided by sensitivities are appropriate when infection is suspected.

Laboratory interpretation in pregnancy—physiologic versus pathologic changes
Interpreting labs in pregnancy requires recognition of physiologic shifts. Mild leukocytosis (WBC ~11.3×10^3/µL with neutrophilia) and modest CRP elevation can be seen in normal pregnancy, but higher values or trends, especially with focal symptoms, should prompt evaluation for infection or inflammation. In this case CRP was slightly elevated (1.39 mg/dL) and neutrophils were increased, findings consistent with either pregnancy‑related change plus a superimposed UTI or an inflammatory response to an ischemic mass.

Anemia in pregnancy is common and can be dilutional (physiologic hemodilution) or true iron deficiency. The reported hemoglobin of 9.9 g/dL with ferritin 25 µg/L indicates iron deficiency rather than pure dilutional anaemia (ferritin <30 supports iron depletion). This has implications for perioperative planning and transfusion thresholds; preoperative optimisation or intraoperative preparedness for bleeding is important when significant anemia exists.

Putting the pieces together—early diagnostic priorities and next steps
Given the clinical picture — large, palpable upper and pelvic masses; focal pain; mechanical dyspnoea; low‑grade fever with documented UTI; and laboratory evidence of iron‑deficiency anemia and mild inflammation — the immediate priorities were maternal–fetal monitoring, exclusion of cardiopulmonary emergencies, targeted imaging and infection management. Bedside ultrasound is the next essential step to characterise mass size, location, cystic versus solid components and vascularity; it is safe in pregnancy and was indeed diagnostic in this case. If sonography is inconclusive, non‑contrast MRI can refine the differential. Throughout this workup, multidisciplinary input (obstetrics, gynecology, radiology and, if needed, surgery or infectious disease) is necessary to balance the risks of conservative versus surgical management in mid‑pregnancy. In this case, symptomatic severity, mass vascularity and concern for acute complications led to operative exploration after appropriate stabilization and counselling.

## Diagnostic Workup in Pregnancy: Safe Imaging, Tumor Markers, and the Differential

When a pregnant patient presents with atypical or large abdominal masses, the diagnostic workup must balance obtaining sufficient information to guide management with the safety constraints of pregnancy. In this case the team used a stepwise approach beginning with ultrasound and progressing to non‑contrast MRI, with selective use of tumour markers to support—but not determine—the differential diagnosis.

Ultrasound with colour Doppler remains the first‑line imaging modality in pregnancy. It is safe, widely available and effective at characterizing cystic versus solid lesions, estimating size and documenting vascularity. In this patient, transabdominal and transvaginal sonography identified two large, unilocular cystic masses with regular margins and peripheral vascularisation. Importantly, the lower (caudal) lesion was visualized in close relation to a posterior intramural–subserosal uterine myoma that showed cystic degeneration. These features raised two distinct possibilities: an adnexal cyst (for example, an endometrioma that can decidualise in pregnancy and develop vascularised mural nodules) or a uterine lesion with cystic change. Colour Doppler helped by demonstrating peripheral and hypertrophied vessels, a finding that can be seen both with vascularised myomas and with decidualised endometriotic nodules; therefore Doppler alone did not definitively distinguish the origin.

Non‑contrast MRI is the preferred problem‑solving modality when ultrasound is limited by mass size, multiplicity, posterior location or difficulty defining the relationship to the myometrium or uterine cavity. Safety considerations are paramount: routine gadolinium administration is avoided in pregnancy because of theoretical fetal risks, so MRI without contrast is used when additional soft‑tissue characterization is needed. Current practice also favors field strengths of 1.5 T or less after the first trimester when MRI is indicated. In this patient, MRI without contrast was performed to better define the two large lesions and their relationship to the uterus.

The MRI findings provided more anatomical detail but remained equivocal. The masses appeared as thick‑walled cysts with inhomogeneous fluid signal on T1 and T2 fat‑saturated sequences and contained focal solid components that were hyperintense on both T1 and T2 sequences. The caudal lesion had an unclear cleavage plane from the posterior uterine wall. These imaging features are compatible with either decidualised endometriomas (which can develop heterogeneous or bright areas on T1/T2 as decidual tissue forms and haemorrhagic content changes) or cystic degeneration within leiomyomas (which can produce large fluid‑filled spaces, irregular walls and secondary haemorrhage or necrotic appearing foci). Crucially, the apparent continuity of the caudal mass with the posterior myometrium on MRI favoured a myomatous origin for that lesion, whereas the cranial lesion lacked an identifiable stalk or clear uterine attachment on imaging, making an adnexal origin (ovarian endometrioma or cyst) more likely initially.

Anatomic and physiologic factors in pregnancy can make pedunculated or subserosal fibroids masquerade as adnexal masses. The gravid uterus and large intra‑abdominal “steric bulk” displace organs and distort normal relationships; a pedunculated fibroid’s thin stalk may be stretched, rotated or obscured so that neither ultrasound nor non‑contrast MRI visualizes a feeding vessel or stalk. This explains why the cranial mass in this case was initially interpreted as adnexal, yet at surgery proved to be a pedunculated cystic leiomyoma whose pedicle was not evident on preoperative imaging.

Serum tumour markers have limited diagnostic specificity during pregnancy and must be interpreted cautiously and in context. CA‑125 commonly shows mild physiologic elevations in the first trimester and again near term, with lower median values in the midsecond trimester; other markers (CA 19‑9, CA 15‑3, CEA, LDH, β2‑microglobulin) are also of limited discriminatory value in pregnancy. In this patient, most tumour markers were within reference limits but CA‑125 rose modestly from 36 U/mL to 41.8 U/mL. A single mildly elevated CA‑125 in the second trimester is non‑diagnostic; however, observing a trend can contribute to the pretest probability for an adnexal process such as decidualised endometrioma versus a leiomyomatous process. Even so, CA‑125 cannot reliably distinguish benign from malignant disease in pregnancy and should not drive management alone.

Indications to escalate beyond ultrasound to MRI include large or multiple masses that are difficult to localize, posterior or intramural lesions where uterine involvement is uncertain, rapidly changing symptoms suggesting torsion, rupture or degeneration, and the need to plan a surgical approach while minimizing obstetric risk. Conversely, serum markers have limited utility as solitary decision tools because pregnancy itself alters their values. In this case, the combination of ultrasound and MRI findings—especially the unclear cleavage plane of the caudal lesion with the posterior myometrium and the vascular pattern—tilted the differential toward uterine myomas with cystic degeneration for the pelvic mass, while leaving the origin of the upper mass uncertain. That integrated assessment, together with the patient’s worsening pain, dyspnoea from mass effect and a modest but rising CA‑125, justified surgical exploration to obtain a definitive diagnosis and treat a lesion at risk for torsion and leakage. The intraoperative surprise that the cranial mass was a pedunculated, twisted leiomyoma underscores the limits of imaging in pregnancy and the importance of correlating clinical, laboratory and imaging data when planning management.

## From Indication to Incision: Multidisciplinary Decision-Making and Operative Strategy

This patient’s management illustrates how a focused, multidisciplinary process moves from indication to incision in the uncommon situation of large, symptomatic abdominal masses during the second trimester of pregnancy. The decision to operate was guided by a combination of clinical deterioration, imaging uncertainty, and the anticipated obstetric impact of continued expectant management.

Indications that prompted surgical intervention
The team weighed multiple, case-specific indications for surgery rather than pursuing routine conservative care. The patient had persistent, worsening pain and mechanical dyspnoea attributable to mass effect — symptoms that did not resolve with analgesia and positional measures. Imaging suggested large, vascularized cystic lesions with mixed solid components; CA‑125 showed a rising trend. These findings raised reasonable concern for complications (torsion, rupture, infection) and left diagnostic uncertainty about ovarian versus myometrial origin. Finally, ongoing uterine growth with advancing gestation carried a high likelihood of further enlargement and increasing risk of ischemia/necrosis or acute events. In this context the multidisciplinary team judged that the benefits of obtaining a definitive diagnosis and removing the most threatening lesion outweighed the risks of surgery during pregnancy.

Timing: why the second trimester
When surgery in pregnancy is necessary, the second trimester commonly represents the safest window. In this case, intervention at 21 + weeks balanced competing risks: the miscarriage risk associated with first‑trimester surgery was avoided, while the technical and anesthetic challenges of third‑trimester laparotomy (reduced working space, greater uterine size, and more difficult fetal monitoring) were still limited. The team therefore elected to operate in the early second trimester when fetal organogenesis was complete, uterine size allowed access to the lesions, and the probability of maternal complications from the masses was rising.

Selecting the surgical approach
Preoperative planning considered lesion size, vascularity, suspected uterine involvement and technical feasibility. Although laparoscopy is preferred when feasible because of reduced maternal morbidity, the characteristics here—very large masses occupying subphrenic and pelvic compartments, marked vascularisation, and possible intramural continuity—favoured an open approach. Laparotomy provides better exposure, faster control of hemorrhage, and the ability to handle complex vascular pedicles or unexpected findings promptly. Thus, a longitudinal xipho‑pubic incision was chosen to optimize visualization and minimize intraoperative delays.

Obstetric safeguards integrated into planning
Protecting the pregnancy shaped every procedural step. The team obtained informed consent that addressed maternal and fetal risks and alternatives. Perioperative fetal assessment was documented: fetal cardiac activity was checked preoperatively, monitored intermittently during the operation, and rechecked postoperatively, consistent with recommended practice for intraoperative fetal surveillance. Surgical strategy emphasized minimizing uterine manipulation, keeping operative time short, and avoiding unnecessary handling of the gravid uterus. These goals guided the decision to limit the procedure to the most clinically urgent lesion rather than attempt a more extensive cytoreduction that would prolong the operation and increase obstetric risk.

Intraoperative steps and how findings altered management
At laparotomy the operators first collected peritoneal fluid for culture and cytology to address the diagnostic uncertainty. Fetal cardiac activity was confirmed. Exploration revealed that the cranial lesion was not an adnexal ovarian mass but a pedunculated, atypical cystic leiomyoma arising from the uterus; it was highly vascularised, had evidence of initial torsion and leakage, and was likely the source of the patient’s fever and pain. These intraoperative findings validated the decision to intervene.

Surgical technique was deliberately conservative and focused: the pedunculated myoma was excised at its stalk using a double‑clamp technique to control and divide the vascular pedicle safely. After transection, the uterine implantation site was closed with absorbable sutures to secure hemostasis and preserve uterine integrity. The team avoided extensive uterine dissection and limited blood loss by precise clamping, prompt repair of the stalk base, and efficient operative conduct. Measures to reduce uterine irritability and support pregnancy were initiated intraoperatively (see below).

Rationale for leaving the caudal mass in situ
The pelvic (caudal) mass appeared intimately associated with the posterior uterine wall as a fixed intramural–subserosal lesion. Attempting removal would have required deep myometrial dissection, prolonged surgery and carried substantial risk of hemorrhage, uterine rupture, or pregnancy loss. Given the patient’s stability after removal of the acutely problematic cranial lesion, the team elected expectant management for the caudal myoma to minimize obstetric risk. This conservative choice preserved uterine function and allowed later reassessment; the plan for delivery (elective cesarean when fetal maturity allowed) was adjusted to account for the residual pelvic myoma that could obstruct the birth canal.

Adjunctive measures and perioperative management
Several adjuncts supported the surgical strategy and postoperative course. Intraoperative and postoperative antibiotic prophylaxis was administered and tailored to the documented urinary Klebsiella infection, addressing the infectious concern that had arisen preoperatively. Given the risk of preterm uterine activity after abdominal surgery in pregnancy, prophylactic progesterone was given intraoperatively and continued postoperatively as part of a tocolysis‑sparing strategy to support the pregnancy and reduce contraction risk. Hemostasis considerations in a gravid uterus—anticipating increased vascularity—motivated preparation for transfusion and the team’s rapid, hemostatic technique; despite postoperative anemia requiring iron and transfusion, bleeding was managed successfully.

Outcome and clinical lesson
The focused, multidisciplinary approach led to excision of the lesion most likely to produce an acute complication while minimizing fetal and maternal risk. Pathology confirmed a benign leiomyoma with ischemic necrosis near a twisted pedicle, corroborating the intraoperative diagnosis. Conservative management of the residual caudal myoma proved reasonable: the pregnancy continued to term with an elective cesarean and an excellent neonatal outcome. This case highlights that when surgery in pregnancy is indicated, careful case selection, timing in the second trimester, selection of the safest operative approach, strict obstetric safeguards, and a conservative intraoperative strategy tailored to findings can achieve both maternal therapeutic goals and fetal preservation.

## Postoperative Care, Obstetric Follow-up, and Delivery Planning with Residual Myoma

After intra‑abdominal surgery in pregnancy, care focuses on stabilising the mother, minimising risks to the fetus, and planning surveillance and delivery that account for any residual uterine pathology. In this case the immediate postoperative course required attention to anaemia, infection control, confirmation of benign pathology, serial obstetric surveillance, and a considered delivery strategy because a large pelvic myoma was intentionally left in situ.

Managing postoperative anaemia in pregnancy
The patient developed significant postoperative iron‑deficiency anaemia (postop Hb 7.5 g/dL, Hct 24.3%) after myomectomy. Management balanced maternal oxygen carrying capacity needs and fetal safety. She received intravenous iron (ferric carboxymaltose 1 g) to replete iron stores efficiently during pregnancy and two unit blood transfusions, which raised Hb to 10.0 g/dL and Hct to 30.4%. These steps reflect common practice: use IV iron when rapid replenishment is indicated or oral iron is insufficient/tolerated poorly, and transfuse when haemoglobin is very low (many centres use thresholds of Hb ≤7 g/dL in stable patients or higher thresholds in symptomatic patients or those with ongoing blood loss); in pregnancy transfusion decisions are individualised, taking fetal well‑being, symptoms, and haemodynamic status into account. The combination of transfusion for immediate oxygen delivery and IV iron for longer term replenishment led to successful haematologic recovery in this patient.

Pathology and microbiology correlation
Pathology provided definitive reassurance: the excised cranial lesion was a benign uterine leiomyoma with areas of ischaemic necrosis adjacent to a twisted pedicle—findings concordant with the intraoperative appearance of a vascularised, torsed pedunculated myoma and consistent with the preoperative concern for degeneration or infection. Peritoneal fluid cultures and cytology were negative, helping to exclude overt malignancy or disseminated infection. These results justified conservative management of the remaining pelvic lesion and supported the expectation of benign behaviour.

Surveillance after myomectomy in pregnancy
Following surgery the patient entered a structured obstetric surveillance plan. Serial ultrasounds every three weeks documented satisfactory fetal growth (the fetus tracked at the 86th percentile) and normal amniotic fluid indices, and intermittent scans assessed the residual pelvic myoma. Maternal symptoms—abdominal pain and dyspnoea—improved after resection of the torsed pedunculated myoma and supportive measures. Imaging showed partial involution of the residual intramural‑subserosal myoma, which decreased to about 7.5 cm. Surveillance in this setting emphasizes fetal wellbeing (growth, amniotic fluid, placental assessment) and monitoring of the residual myoma for growth, degeneration, or new mass effect that might alter delivery plans.

Delivery planning with a residual pelvic myoma
A large residual pelvic myoma situated at the uterine torus and posterior isthmic region was judged likely to obstruct fetal descent during labour (cephalopelvic obstruction). Given its size and location, and the prior abdominal surgery, the multidisciplinary team planned an elective cesarean delivery at 37 weeks. The operation was scheduled through the existing midline laparotomy scar to minimise operative risks and to reduce the theoretical risk of uterine rupture associated with new or extensive uterine incisions later in pregnancy. Timing at 37 weeks balanced fetal maturity with avoidance of spontaneous labour that could be obstructed. This approach reflects the principle that mode and timing of delivery in the presence of large myomas should be individualized by number, size and location of tumors, maternal symptoms, and obstetric history.

Maternal–neonatal outcomes and postpartum decisions
The outcome was favourable: an uncomplicated postpartum course, a healthy neonate, and successful breastfeeding. The residual myoma showed partial postpartum regression—common for leiomyomas after delivery—and the patient remained asymptomatic. Given the reduction in size and absence of symptoms, she elected to defer further surgical management. This shared decision aligns with the general practice of postponing definitive myoma surgery until postpartum recovery and when symptoms or growth warrant intervention.

Antibiotic stewardship in the perioperative period
Perioperative antimicrobials were tailored to the clinical context and microbiology. Intraoperative prophylaxis with cefazolin (2 g IV) was given, and postoperative therapy combined cefazolin with clarithromycin based on a urine culture positive for Klebsiella pneumoniae highly sensitive to cephalosporins and macrolides. Antibiotic stewardship principles applied: choose agents effective against the identified pathogen, use the narrowest effective spectrum for the shortest appropriate duration, and consider fetal safety when selecting drugs in pregnancy. In this case culture‑guided therapy targeted the urinary pathogen while continuing standard surgical prophylaxis to reduce postoperative infectious complications.

Overall clinical lesson
This case illustrates a pragmatic, multidisciplinary approach after antepartum myomectomy: correct haematinic deficits promptly (IV iron ± transfusion), confirm benign disease and clear infection by directed testing, maintain close obstetric surveillance of fetal growth and residual lesion size, and plan delivery based on realistic assessment of mechanical obstruction and maternal–fetal safety. When residual myomas regress and symptoms are absent postpartum, deferral of further surgery is a reasonable, patient‑centred choice.

## Pathophysiology and Clinical Lessons: Leiomyomas in Pregnancy and How to Avoid Overtreatment

Uterine leiomyomas (fibroids) are common in reproductive‑age women and display characteristic but variable behaviour during pregnancy. Hormonal and hemodynamic changes in pregnancy can accelerate fibroid growth, particularly early in gestation, owing to increased circulating estrogens, progesterone and local growth factors as well as enhanced uterine blood flow. Growth is non‑linear: most rapid enlargement occurs in the first trimester and early second trimester, with slower increases thereafter. This dynamic underlies why a previously small or asymptomatic lesion can become clinically relevant as the uterus expands and the pregnancy advances.

Patterns of degeneration explain many acute clinical presentations. As fibroids enlarge they can outgrow their blood supply and undergo hyaline, cystic or red (ischemic) degeneration. Cystic degeneration produces fluid‑filled spaces and can appear sonographically or on MRI as unilocular or multilocular cystic masses with thin or thickened walls. Red degeneration (veno‑occlusive ischemia) classically causes localized pain, low‑grade fever and leukocytosis — symptoms that may mimic infection or adnexal pathology. A pedunculated subserosal fibroid may undergo torsion on its stalk, producing severe pain and, if vascular compromise is prolonged, focal necrosis and systemic inflammatory signs. These mechanisms explain the combination of pain, low fever and raised inflammatory markers seen in the present case and justify a low threshold for close monitoring when symptoms suggest ischemia, torsion or secondary infection.

Mass effect is another major driver of symptoms in late first to second trimester. Large fibroids or multiple lesions can restrict diaphragmatic excursion and reduce functional residual capacity, producing dyspnoea that is mechanical rather than cardiopulmonary in origin. Pelvic mass effect explains obstructive symptoms, urinary retention, constipation or difficulty with fetal descent at term. Pain from stretching of the serosa, capsular ischaemia or adjacent organ compression is common and should be interpreted in the context of gestational age, fetal status and imaging findings.

Imaging strategy is central for avoiding overtreatment. Transabdominal and transvaginal ultrasound with colour Doppler remain first‑line because they reliably map number, size, vascularity and relationship to the uterine cavity and adnexa. Key descriptive points that guide management are the exact uterine location (submucosal, intramural, subserosal, pedunculated), maximum diameter, distance from the endometrial cavity and proximity to the placenta. Non‑contrast MRI is valuable when ultrasound is inconclusive — for example, when masses are posterior, large, or when sonographic windows are limited by habitus or uterine enlargement. MRI better differentiates cystic degeneration, hemorrhagic (red) change and solid components, and can help demonstrate continuity with uterine tissue (favouring fibroid) versus true adnexal origin. Typical imaging distinctions include: decidualized endometriomas tending to be adnexal, with internal mural nodularity and increased vascularity in pregnancy, while cystic degenerating fibroids more often demonstrate continuity with the myometrium, irregular cystic spaces within a previously solid mass, and variable wall thickness. However, atypical presentations occur; combining clinical signs, serial imaging and multidisciplinary review reduces unnecessary intervention.

Serum tumour markers must be interpreted with caution in pregnancy. CA‑125 commonly rises in the first trimester and near term; modest elevations are therefore nonspecific and occur with benign conditions including endometriosis, adenomyosis, pelvic inflammation and fibroid degeneration. An isolated mildly elevated CA‑125 offers little diagnostic certainty; serial trends may be informative if levels rise rapidly and correlate with suspicious imaging or clinical deterioration, but marker results should never drive surgery in isolation. In the present case a mildly raised CA‑125 supported the differential but did not on its own mandate resection of both masses.

The risk–benefit framework for intervention centres on maternal and fetal safety and on avoiding overtreatment. The default approach for most fibroids discovered in pregnancy is conservative care: analgesia, hydration, observation, and targeted treatment of complications (e.g., antibiotics for proven infection, transfusion for anemia). Clear indications for surgical intervention are limited and well established: torsion of a pedunculated subserosal mass with persistent pain, suspected rupture, intractable pain unresponsive to maximal conservative therapy, active infection not controlled with antibiotics, rapid lesion enlargement with concern for malignancy, or acute compromise of maternal respiratory or hemodynamic status from mass effect. When symptoms are manageable and fetal wellbeing is maintained, expectant management is usually safest because many fibroids partially regress postpartum.

When surgery is necessary, a set of practical surgical principles reduces obstetric risk and avoids overtreatment. Minimize uterine trauma and operative time: restrict resection to the symptomatic or high‑risk lesion(s) and leave non‑obstructive intramural fibroids in situ, as was appropriately done in this case. Achieve meticulous hemostasis using layered closure and suitable suturing to reduce hemorrhage risk. Choose the surgical approach according to lesion size, number and location, and surgeon experience — large, vascular, posterior or multiple fibroids are often managed via laparotomy; selected smaller pedunculated lesions can be removed laparoscopically in early gestation by experienced teams. Intraoperative fetal heart rate monitoring before and after the procedure should be documented; continuous monitoring during abdominal surgery is recommended when feasible. Tocolytic therapy is not routinely required but may be considered selectively; perioperative use of progesterone or other measures to support the pregnancy should follow local protocols and multidisciplinary agreement. Prophylactic antibiotics should be guided by culture results when infection is a concern. Finally, plan for blood loss management including iron repletion and transfusion thresholds prearranged in advance.

Delivery planning must account for the residual fibroid burden. Large lower‑segment or isthmic fibroids, or a pelvic mass obstructing the birth canal, increase the likelihood of cesarean delivery. Conversely, many women with intramural or subserosal fibroids can still attempt vaginal birth if the lesion does not impede fetal descent. Given the tendency for partial postpartum regression of fibroids, definitive elective myomectomy is often deferred until after delivery and after interval imaging, unless symptoms persist. This staged approach preserves pregnancy while avoiding unnecessary antepartum interventions.

In summary, the clinical lessons from this case emphasize careful mapping of fibroid characteristics, conservative management when safe, and selective, targeted surgery only for clear complications. Integrating clinical signs (severity and acuity of pain, fever, respiratory compromise), serial imaging (ultrasound ± non‑contrast MRI), and laboratory trends (including cautious interpretation of CA‑125) allows clinicians to preserve pregnancy, treat complications promptly, and avoid overtreatment of benign uterine disease.
