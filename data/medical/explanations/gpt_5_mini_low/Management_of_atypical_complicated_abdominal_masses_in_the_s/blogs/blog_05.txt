Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

## Operating with a Passenger: Intraoperative Fetal Monitoring, Anesthesia, and Tocolysis

What makes this case worth your attention? A nulliparous woman in her 40s at 21+ weeks gestation came in with two massive, confusing abdominal cystic lesions causing pain and dyspnoea. Imaging and tumour markers were indeterminate. The team proceeded to laparotomy, expecting adnexal masses, and found a highly vascular, twisted pedunculated leiomyoma — they removed the cranial lesion and left the pelvic one in situ. Fetal cardiac activity was preserved and the pregnancy continued to term with an elective caesarean and a healthy neonate. This case is a neat capsule of the dilemmas we face when a pregnant patient needs non-obstetric abdominal surgery: how do we monitor the fetus, choose anesthesia and perioperative meds, manage maternal physiology when there is mass effect, and coordinate a multidisciplinary plan that treats the mother while preserving the pregnancy?

Below I’ll pull practical lessons from this report and the wider clinical context so you can operationalize safe surgery in the second trimester.

## The basic framing: maternal first, fetus supported
Surgery during pregnancy is often unavoidable. The primary principle is that optimal maternal care maximizes fetal outcome. That means treating infections, suspected ischemia or torsion, or masses that threaten respiratory function or future uterine expansion. But the choices we make — timing, approach (laparoscopy vs laparotomy), anesthetic agents, monitoring, and perioperative drugs — must balance maternal benefits and fetal risks.

This patient had worsening pain, dyspnoea from diaphragmatic compromise, a vascular mass with torsion/leak on arrival, and a mild urinary infection with Klebsiella. Those indications justified operative management despite pregnancy.

## Fetal cardiac monitoring: what, when, how often
Key concept: document fetal heart activity before, during (intermittently), and immediately after surgery. That’s what the team did here and is widely recommended.

- Before surgery: confirm fetal viability with documented heart rate. This establishes baseline and helps informed consent counseling.
- Intraoperative: continuous electronic fetal monitoring (EFM) is ideal for viable fetuses typically ≥24 weeks. In mid-second trimester (around 21–22 weeks), continuous monitoring may be technically limited and may not change management, but intermittent auscultation or ultrasound documentation of heart activity at intervals is reasonable and pragmatic. In this case, serial and intermittent transvaginal ultrasound checks were used.
- After surgery: immediate confirmation of fetal heart activity and ongoing obstetric follow-up (in this case every 3 weeks) to detect evolving problems.

Practical points:
- If gestational age is previable (<24 weeks) continuous EFM is generally not feasible or actionable; document heartbeat by Doppler/US pre- and post-op and intermittently during the case when feasible.
- For viable fetuses, if significant intraoperative concerns arise (maternal instability, uterine irritability, fetal distress) involve obstetrics immediately; have tocolytics and neonatal team available if preterm delivery becomes necessary.

## Positioning and ventilation when mass effect threatens respiration
This patient had dyspnoea from large abdominal masses and gravid uterus. Intra- and perioperative positioning matters.

- Semi-recumbent or semi-orthostatic positioning can offload diaphragmatic pressure and help ventilation; the team used a semi-orthostatic position preop with symptomatic benefit.
- During anesthesia, head-up tilt (reverse Trendelenburg) can improve respiratory mechanics when large upper abdominal masses compress the diaphragm — but balance this against uterine perfusion. A modest head-up tilt is typically well tolerated.
- Avoid prolonged supine hypotension: left uterine displacement is critical after mid-pregnancy to avoid aortocaval compression. In second trimester, a left lateral tilt (15°) or manual displacement is recommended when supine.
- Be prepared for difficult ventilation due to decreased pulmonary compliance; preoxygenate thoroughly and plan for aspiration prophylaxis.

## Anesthesia choices and fetal safety
General principles: choose agents and techniques that maintain maternal oxygenation, hemodynamic stability and uteroplacental perfusion. No single anesthetic agent is absolutely contraindicated in pregnancy; when maternal physiology is supported, fetal exposure is acceptable.

- General anesthesia: widely used for non-obstetric laparotomy in pregnancy; rapid-sequence induction and endotracheal intubation are standard because of aspiration risk.
- Regional anesthesia: may be used for some procedures but is limited for major laparotomy.
- Maintain normotension, avoid prolonged maternal hypoxia, and minimize uterine irritability (avoid uterine stimulants unless required).
- Document fetal heart rate before induction and after emergence. If fetal monitoring is available intraoperatively (viable fetus), use it.

The team provided intraoperative progesterone and continuous fetal checks. That’s reasonable as an adjunct though evidence for routine prophylactic tocolysis is mixed (see below).

## Perioperative tocolysis and progesterone — evidence and controversy
Two related but distinct interventions often considered: tocolytics (acute uterine relaxants to stop contractions) and progesterone (used for preterm birth prevention).

- Tocolytics: routine prophylactic tocolysis for non-obstetric surgery is not supported by high-level evidence. Use should be individualized — reserved for cases with sustained uterine contractions or in threat of preterm labour. Common agents: β2-agonists (terbutaline), calcium-channel blockers (nifedipine), or magnesium sulfate depending on local protocols and contraindications. Each has maternal side effects and fetal considerations.
- Progesterone: the team administered intramuscular progesterone intraoperatively and continued post-op. Evidence for routine prophylactic progesterone to prevent miscarriage or preterm labour after abdominal surgery in the second trimester is limited and heterogeneous. Progesterone is widely used in obstetrics for women with a history of preterm birth or short cervix, but its routine use solely as prophylaxis for surgery is not established by robust trials.
- Practical approach: discuss with obstetrics. Consider:
  - If signs of threatened preterm labour or cervical change → use tocolytics per obstetric guidance.
  - If patient has other indications for progesterone (prior preterm birth, short cervix), continue/consider it.
  - If no obstetric risk factors and no contractions, routine pharmacologic tocolysis or progesterone has unclear benefit — but many centers use them empirically with good outcomes. Document the rationale and obtain informed consent.

In this case, prophylactic progesterone was given intraoperatively and continued — the pregnancy progressed to term without preterm labour, but we can’t know whether progesterone changed the outcome.

## Antibiotic selection when there is an intercurrent infection
This patient had a Klebsiella pneumoniae urinary infection on pre-op cultures. Key points:

- Treat maternal infection aggressively — untreated maternal sepsis is a greater fetal threat than appropriate antibiotics.
- Choose pregnancy-safe antibiotics guided by culture and sensitivity. In this case, Klebsiella was highly sensitive to cephalosporins and clarithromycin. The team used perioperative cefazolin (standard surgical prophylaxis) and post-op cefazolin plus clarithromycin per antibiogram — a sensible targeted approach.
- Beta-lactams (penicillins, cephalosporins) are generally safe in pregnancy. Macrolides are used when indicated, though azithromycin is often preferred over clarithromycin because of better safety data in pregnancy; however, clarithromycin can be used if guided by susceptibility and obstetric review.
- Coordinate infectious disease or microbiology input when organisms are resistant or when using agents with fetal safety concerns.

## Surgical approach: laparoscopy vs laparotomy — pragmatism over dogma
Surgery in pregnancy can be done laparoscopically or via open approach. Decision factors: gestational age, size and location of masses, hemodynamic stability, surgical expertise.

- Laparoscopy advantages: less pain, shorter LOS, lower wound morbidity. It is feasible in pregnancy, especially early and mid-gestation, with open (Hasson) entry and uterine protection.
- Laparotomy favored when masses are large, numerous, intramural or with uncertain origin, or when rapid access is needed — as in this case of huge vascularized masses and suspected torsion. Laparotomy can shorten operative time when exposure is difficult and reduces risk of uterine injury when the uterus is large or anatomy distorted.
- The team chose a longitudinal xipho-pubic incision and removed only the pedunculated lesion — a conservative, pregnancy-sparing strategy that minimized operative time and risk.

## Multidisciplinary communication — the critical glue
This case illustrates excellent team functioning: emergency physicians, obstetricians, gynecologic surgeons, anesthesiologists, microbiology and neonatology were all involved. Key steps to emulate:

- Early multidisciplinary discussion with clear documentation of risks, benefits and alternatives.
- Obtain informed consent addressing maternal and fetal risks, including the possibility of preterm delivery.
- Pre-op checklist: maternal optimization (Hb, iron/transfusion if needed), antibiotic plan, fetal monitoring strategy, tocolysis/progesterone plan, blood products available, neonatal team on standby if delivery becomes necessary.
- Intraoperative coordination: obstetrics documenting fetal viability before/after/during surgery; anesthesia focusing on oxygenation and perfusion; surgical team minimizing uterine manipulation and blood loss.
- Post-op plan: ICU/ward monitoring, serial fetal assessments, and follow-up imaging and hematology as needed.

## What went well in this case
- Appropriate multidisciplinary evaluation and imaging (US + non-contrast MRI).
- Timely surgery for worsening symptoms and suspected torsion/leak — maternal indication well justified.
- Documentation of fetal heart activity before, during (intermittent), and after surgery.
- Pragmatic use of laparotomy to secure exposure and shorten operative time.
- Targeted antibiotics based on urine culture.
- Post-op obstetric follow-up and decision for elective CS at term given residual pelvic myoma.

## Clinical pearls / Take-home points
- Maternal stability is fetal stability: treat maternal emergencies promptly — delayed maternal care is the greater fetal threat.
- Document fetal heart activity pre-op, intermittently intra-op when feasible (or continuously for viable fetuses), and immediately post-op.
- In mid-second-trimester surgery, intermittent ultrasound/Doppler fetal checks are a pragmatic approach when continuous EFM is not possible.
- Position to optimize ventilation (head-up tilt) while maintaining uterine perfusion (left lateral tilt); avoid prolonged supine aortocaval compression.
- Choose anesthesia to maintain maternal oxygenation and hemodynamics; rapid-sequence induction and aspiration prophylaxis are standard.
- Routine prophylactic tocolysis or progesterone for non-obstetric surgery is controversial; individualize with obstetrics input. Use tocolytics when contractions or threatened preterm labour occur.
- Treat maternal infections aggressively with pregnancy-safe antibiotics guided by culture; maternal infection control is a priority for fetal well-being.
- Laparoscopy is possible in pregnancy but large, vascular, or posterior uterine masses may necessitate laparotomy for safety and speed.
- Multidisciplinary planning, explicit informed consent, and documentation are essential to optimize maternal and fetal outcomes.

This case reinforces a practical truth: operating with a passenger requires thoughtful monitoring, a maternal-first clinical strategy, good communication, and tailored perioperative choices. The result here — preservation of pregnancy to term and a healthy neonate — shows that with careful planning and execution, we can treat serious maternal pathology without sacrificing fetal outcome.
