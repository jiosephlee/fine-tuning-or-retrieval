Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# Imaging Strategy for Pelvic Masses in Pregnancy: Ultrasound, Doppler, and Non‑Contrast MRI

What makes this case interesting: a woman in her 40s at 21 weeks presented with dyspnoea and pain and was found to have two very large, cystic-appearing abdominal masses. Ultrasound and Doppler suggested adnexal cysts (decidualised endometriomas were high on the list), CA‑125 was mildly raised, and non‑contrast MRI showed thick-walled, inhomogeneous cysts with hypertrophied vessels — findings that could be either decidualised adnexal disease or atypical, cystic-degenerated or pedunculated leiomyomas. At laparotomy the upper lesion turned out to be a huge pedunculated, torsed and leaking uterine leiomyoma — a diagnosis that was difficult to make preoperatively because of gravid anatomy and “steric bulk.” This case nicely illustrates the strengths and limits of ultrasound/Doppler and when non‑contrast MRI changes management in pregnancy.

Below I summarise a pragmatic imaging algorithm for symptomatic or atypical pelvic masses in the second trimester, explain what US + Doppler can and cannot resolve, when to escalate to MRI, and how imaging features influence the decision for surgery versus expectant management.

## First step: clinical triage and initial ultrasound
Always start with a focused clinical assessment: maternal vitals, fetal heart rate, pain pattern, signs of peritonitis, and effect on respiration or bladder/obstructive symptoms. Imaging choice depends on acuity.

Transabdominal + transvaginal ultrasound is the gateway test in pregnancy:
- Strengths: bedside, safe, real‑time, can evaluate mass morphology (unilocular vs multilocular), wall thickness, internal echo pattern, papillary projections, vascularity with colour/Power Doppler, relationship to the uterus and adnexa (if visualised), and free fluid.
- Limitations: in the second and third trimester a large gravid uterus often obscures anatomy; pedicles or small stalks are easily missed; depth/obesity can limit resolution; operator dependence.

In the case described:
- US showed two large, unilocular cystic masses with regular margins and peripheral vascularisation; the caudal lesion appeared adherent to a posterior myoma with cystic degeneration. But the upper mass’s origin was unclear because no stalk or feeding vessel could be identified.

Clinical pearl: when a mass fills the abdomen or lies in an atypical location relative to the gravid uterus, don’t assume adnexal origin from location alone — try to trace continuity with the uterine wall on multiple planes and with gentle patient repositioning.

## Role of colour/Power Doppler
Doppler adds useful clues:
- Hypertrophied peripheral vessels, low‑resistance flow, or a feeding vessel arising from the uterine circulation suggests a uterine source (pedunculated fibroid) rather than a pure ovarian cyst.
- Central papillary vascularity or solid mural nodules with internal flow raise concern for neoplasm (decidualised endometriomas can show increased peripheral/capsular flow).
- Lack of flow does not exclude torsion — early torsion can preserve some flow or have intermittent compromise.

In the presented case Doppler showed peripheral vascularisation and hypertrophied vessels — a finding consistent with both decidualised endometrioma and a vascularised pedunculated myoma. Doppler findings must be interpreted alongside anatomical relationships.

Clinical pearl: document the presumed vascular supply (ovarian vs uterine vessels) if possible — this can be a key discriminator.

## When to escalate to non‑contrast MRI
Indications to perform MRI in pregnancy for pelvic masses:
- Ultrasound is inconclusive regarding organ of origin (uterine vs adnexal), or tissue planes are unclear.
- Suspicion for malignancy cannot be reasonably excluded on ultrasound + labs.
- Patient is symptomatic (pain, obstructive symptoms, respiratory compromise) and precise anatomy would change management.
- Multiple, large or posterior/intramural masses where ultrasound underestimates distance from the endometrial cavity or placental location.

MRI protocol and safety considerations:
- Use a non‑contrast MRI; avoid gadolinium unless absolutely essential (gadolinium crosses the placenta and is associated with small but real fetal risks).
- Prefer 1.5‑T when possible; 3‑T can be used but many centres avoid it in pregnancy unless benefits clearly outweigh theoretical risks.
- Fast sequences (T2, T1 with and without fat saturation) are helpful. Avoid prolonged scans; keep maternal comfort in mind.
- No absolute contraindication to MRI after the first trimester — diagnostic MRI without contrast is considered reasonably safe.

In the case: non‑contrast MRI demonstrated thick‑walled cysts (1.4 cm wall), inhomogeneous T1/T2 signal and focal hyperintense solid components, and unclear cleavage of the caudal mass from the uterus — findings that raised concern and supported proceeding to surgical exploration.

Clinical pearl: MRI is best used to clarify organ of origin and tissue composition when ultrasound cannot — it will frequently change management when masses are large or posterior.

## Interpreting key imaging patterns
Some practical interpretations frequently encountered in pregnancy:

- Thick‑walled cyst with inhomogeneous T1/T2 signal, peripheral vascularity:
  - Differential: decidualised endometrioma, cystic-degenerated leiomyoma, haemorrhagic cyst, abscess, or neoplasm.
  - Clues favouring endometrioma: “shading” on T2 (progressive signal drop on T2), typical location near adnexa, and history or signs of endometriosis.
  - Clues favouring myoma: continuity with myometrium on multiple planes, feeding branch from the uterine artery, stalk or pedicle on dynamic scanning, and location adjacent to the uterine wall.

- Hypertrophied vessels around a mass:
  - Think of highly vascular pedunculated myoma, decidualised tissue, or neoplasm. Use Doppler to try to identify uterine vs ovarian arterial origin.

- Loss of clear cleavage planes between mass and uterus on MRI:
  - Suggests myometrial origin or strong adherence; increases the likelihood that the mass is uterine (intramural/subserosal) rather than an ovarian cyst.

- Small solid mural nodules or papillary projections with internal flow:
  - Raises concern for neoplasm; if present in pregnancy, consider MRI and multidisciplinary discussion.

Clinical pearl: no single sign is diagnostic. Combine anatomy, Doppler vascular pattern, CA‑125 trend (not absolute in pregnancy), and clinical course.

## How imaging informs management: operative vs expectant
Key drivers of management:
- Maternal symptoms and complications: severe pain, torsion, rupture, infection, respiratory compromise from mass effect — favour operative management.
- Imaging suggesting a pedunculated, torsed, leaking or necrotic lesion — increased operative urgency.
- Suspected malignancy after imaging and tumour marker trend — multidisciplinary discussion and lower threshold for surgery.
- If imaging suggests intramural myoma with minimal symptoms, expectant management is reasonable; plan delivery mode based on whether the mass will obstruct labor.

In the reported case the combination of intractable pain, dyspnoea from mass effect, MRI features suggestive of thick walls and vascularity, and a small CA‑125 rise contributed to the decision for laparotomy. Intraoperatively the cranial mass was a torsed, vascular pedunculated leiomyoma — a lesion at real risk of further complications — and was removed. The caudal myoma was left in situ to avoid obstetric risk given its fixed position and the aim to preserve the pregnancy.

Clinical pearl: imaging that suggests a pedicle/stalk and vascular torsion changes management — a torsed pedunculated myoma behaves like a surgical abdomen.

## Practical algorithm (second trimester, symptomatic or atypical pelvic mass)
1. Immediate bedside: maternal vitals, fetal monitoring, brief gen/abdominal exam.
2. First-line imaging: transabdominal + transvaginal US with colour/Power Doppler; document:
   - Size, morphology, wall thickness, internal echoes, papillary nodules
   - Relationship to uterus and ovaries (look for continuity)
   - Vascular supply (uterine vs ovarian)
   - Free fluid
3. Labs: CBC, CRP, targeted tumour markers (CA‑125 interpreted cautiously in pregnancy; serial values more useful than single reading).
4. If US is diagnostic and patient stable with no complications → expectant management + serial US.
5. If US is inconclusive, mass is large, posterior, multiple, or symptoms/markers concerning → non‑contrast MRI (1.5‑T preferred).
6. Multidisciplinary review (OB/Gyn, radiology, maternal–fetal medicine, surgery) to decide expectant vs surgical approach.
7. If surgery is chosen: time procedure to minimise fetal risk (after viability when possible), plan for fetal monitoring perioperatively, consider progesterone prophylaxis per local protocols, and reserve the least invasive approach that achieves goals (laparoscopy for selected lesions; laparotomy for large, multiple or inaccessible masses).

## Safety and documentation points
- Avoid gadolinium unless absolutely necessary; document informed consent if considered.
- Prefer 1.5‑T MRI; use fast protocols and limit scan time.
- During surgery in pregnancy: monitor and document fetal heart rate before/during/after, keep procedures as short as possible, involve obstetrics in planning, and discuss prophylactic measures (e.g., progesterone) according to local practice.
- CA‑125 is unreliable as a standalone discriminator in pregnancy — use trends and the whole clinical picture.

## Take‑home messages
- Ultrasound + colour/Power Doppler is the first-line and often sufficient test, but gravid anatomy and large masses frequently limit its ability to determine organ of origin.
- Doppler patterns (uterine vs ovarian vascular supply, hypertrophied peripheral vessels) provide valuable discriminators — always try to document the feeding vessel.
- Escalate to non‑contrast MRI when ultrasound is inconclusive, the mass is large or posterior, or when MRI findings would change management.
- Thick-walled, inhomogeneous cysts with peripheral vascularity can represent decidualised endometrioma or cystic-degenerated/pedunculated leiomyoma — look for continuity with the myometrium and feeding vessels to help distinguish them.
- Imaging findings should be integrated with symptoms, temporal evolution, and multidisciplinary input — decide surgery only when maternal/fetal risks from the mass outweigh those of intervention.

This case is a reminder that large, atypical pelvic masses in pregnancy are diagnostically challenging and that a stepwise imaging approach — US/Doppler first, then targeted non‑contrast MRI when needed — is practical, safe, and often decisive for planning management.
