Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Clinical Pearls] How should CA-125 be interpreted during pregnancy, particularly when mildly elevated in the second trimester, and what benign and malignant conditions remain on the differential in this context?

Clinical pearl — interpreting CA‑125 in pregnancy

Bottom line
- CA‑125 is unreliable as a stand‑alone discriminator of benign versus malignant pelvic disease in pregnancy. Pregnancy causes physiologic rises (especially in the first and third trimesters), so a mildly elevated value in the second trimester should not by itself prompt radical management. Interpretation must be integrated with symptoms, serial trends, and imaging (US ± non‑contrast MRI). Rapid rises, markedly elevated values, suspicious imaging (solid nodules, papillary projections, thick septations, ascites, adenopathy) or clinical deterioration increase concern for malignancy and warrant multidisciplinary evaluation.

Why: physiology and test characteristics
- CA‑125 is a glycoprotein produced by coelomic‑derived epithelium (peritoneum, pleura, Müllerian epithelium). Levels commonly peak in early pregnancy (first trimester) and again near term (third trimester). Reported medians: ~23 U/mL first trimester, ~14 U/mL second trimester, ~21 U/mL third trimester.
- The conventional oncologic cut‑off (≥35 U/mL) was derived in nonpregnant populations and loses specificity in pregnancy. False‑positive elevations occur with many benign processes that are common or more likely in pregnancy (decidualisation, leiomyoma degeneration, inflammation, recent surgery, infection, endometriosis/adenomyosis, peritoneal irritation).

What a mildly raised CA‑125 in the second trimester means (practical approach)
- Mild isolated elevation (for example CA‑125 in the low 40s as in this case) in the second trimester is nonspecific:
  - Consider benign causes first, especially if imaging suggests a benign lesion (simple or unilocular cyst, classic fibroid appearance) and there is no ascites, adenopathy, or marked solid papillary components.
  - Repeat CA‑125 (serial trend) can be informative: a stable or declining level supports a benign process; a rapid upward trend is more concerning.
  - Correlate with high‑quality ultrasound (morphology, Doppler) and, if needed, non‑contrast MRI for better tissue characterisation. Gadolinium is avoided in pregnancy unless absolutely necessary.
- Don’t let a single mildly elevated CA‑125 drive overtreatment (eg, termination or radical surgery) in an otherwise stable pregnant patient.

Benign conditions to keep on the differential during pregnancy
- Decidualised endometrioma (can increase CA‑125 and mimic malignancy on imaging)
- Uterine leiomyomas with cystic degeneration, torsion or necrosis (can raise CA‑125 and cause fever/pain) — directly relevant to this case, where the cranial mass was a pedunculated cystic leiomyoma with ischemic change and CA‑125 ~41.8 U/mL
- Physiologic pregnancy‑related elevation (first and third trimester peaks)
- Pelvic inflammatory disease or urinary tract infection with peritoneal irritation (this patient had urinary Klebsiella and low‑grade fever)
- Peritoneal irritation, recent surgery, endometriosis/adenomyosis, ascites from benign causes

Malignant conditions to consider (less likely but important)
- Epithelial ovarian carcinoma (including borderline tumours)
- Primary peritoneal carcinoma or metastatic disease to the adnexa
- Germ cell or sex‑cord stromal tumours (less commonly associated with CA‑125 elevations)
- Malignancy is more likely when CA‑125 is markedly elevated, shows a rapid rise, and imaging shows complex solid components, papillary projections, multiloculated thick septa, ascites, or lymphadenopathy.

How this applies to the presented case
- The patient had a mildly elevated CA‑125 (41.8 U/mL), increasing from 36 U/mL over 2 weeks. That degree of elevation in the second trimester is nonspecific but prompted careful workup.
- Imaging (US and non‑contrast MRI) showed large vascularised cystic masses with mixed solid/cystic features; those findings, the patient’s worsening pain, dyspnoea from mass effect, and fever (with evidence of infection and later ischemic necrosis at pathology) raised concern for complication and justified surgical management.
- Final pathology showed benign leiomyomas with ischemic necrosis — a classic benign explanation for both imaging features and the CA‑125 elevation.

Clinical takeaways
- Use CA‑125 in pregnancy only as an adjunct: interpret against gestational age, symptoms, imaging, and serial measurements.
- Mild second‑trimester elevations (low‑40s) are often benign; prioritize ultrasound/MRI and multidisciplinary discussion.
- Operate in pregnancy when clinical complications (torsion, rupture, infection), concerning imaging, or progressive symptoms threaten maternal or fetal health — not on a mildly elevated CA‑125 alone.
- Definitive diagnosis rests on histopathology (and cytology/ascites studies when present).
