Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Morning Report] In the perioperative period, how should iron-deficiency anemia be managed in pregnancy, including thresholds for IV iron versus red blood cell transfusion, and what special considerations apply to ongoing fetal and maternal monitoring?

Direct answer
- Perioperative iron-deficiency anemia in pregnancy is managed pragmatically on two axes: (1) rapid iron repletion when there is time or oral iron has failed, and (2) red blood cell (RBC) transfusion when oxygen delivery is threatened by severe anemia or active/ongoing bleeding. Use intravenous (IV) iron when you need rapid, reliable iron repletion (preoperative, postoperative, or late pregnancy) and reserve RBC transfusion for hemodynamic compromise, symptomatic anemia, or very low Hb with or without ongoing blood loss.

Clinical thresholds and practical rules
- IV iron
  - Indications in pregnancy: documented iron-deficiency (low ferritin, low MCV, low transferrin saturation or typical CBC) with Hb inadequately corrected or when oral therapy is not tolerated or is too slow given timing (eg, planned surgery, short interval to delivery, or severe symptoms).
  - Common numeric triggers used in practice: Hb <10–11 g/dL with ferritin <30 µg/L, or any iron-deficient pregnant patient who requires rapid repletion (eg, perioperative, late third trimester, or before anticipated blood loss). IV iron is preferred if oral therapy is ineffective, not tolerated, or insufficient time remains for adequate erythropoiesis.
  - Dosing: ferric carboxymaltose and iron sucrose are commonly used; ferric carboxymaltose allows single high-dose administrations (eg, 500–1000 mg single dose depending on deficit and product limits). Monitor for hypersensitivity and observe after infusion.
  - Caveat: active, uncontrolled bacterial infection is a relative contraindication to IV iron because iron can enhance microbial growth; treat and control infection before or concurrently when possible. In the case report the patient had a urinary Klebsiella infection that was treated with appropriate antibiotics prior to IV iron administration.

- RBC transfusion
  - Indications: hemodynamic instability, ongoing hemorrhage, or severe symptomatic anemia where immediate increase in oxygen-carrying capacity is necessary.
  - Numeric thresholds: for stable patients without active bleeding a restrictive threshold (Hb 7 g/dL) is generally used outside pregnancy; in pregnancy clinicians commonly use slightly higher individualized thresholds. Practical guidance:
    - Transfuse for Hb ≤6–7 g/dL in most patients.
    - Consider transfusion for Hb 7–8 g/dL if symptomatic (tachycardia, hypotension, ischemic symptoms), if there is ongoing bleeding, or in the perioperative/obstetric setting when anticipating further blood loss.
    - Maintain a pragmatic target post-transfusion of Hb ≥8–10 g/dL depending on maternal symptomatology, gestational age and obstetric plans (eg, imminent delivery or major surgery).
  - In the case presented the patient’s Hb fell to 7.5 g/dL postoperatively with ongoing iron deficiency and the team gave IV iron plus two units of RBCs, achieving Hb 10 g/dL — an approach consistent with correcting a postoperative Hb in the setting of recent surgery, symptomatic anemia and need to optimize maternal oxygen delivery for the remainder of pregnancy.

Maternal and fetal monitoring considerations (perioperative and after treatment)
- Before, during and after any non-obstetric or obstetric surgery document fetal heart activity. For viable fetuses:
  - Continuous fetal heart rate monitoring/CTG is recommended when gestational age and resources permit (commonly applied for ≥24 weeks but practice varies by center). At minimum, document fetal heart rate immediately before and after surgery.
  - Intraoperative fetal monitoring when feasible (intermittent or continuous depending on GA and logistics) is recommended; the case team documented fetal cardiac activity intraoperatively and used serial/transvaginal ultrasound.
- Maternal monitoring:
  - Standard perioperative hemodynamic monitoring (BP, HR, oxygenation, urine output). Maintain maternal oxygenation and perfusion — maternal hypoxia or hypotension directly compromises fetal oxygen delivery.
  - Monitor for transfusion reactions and iron infusion reactions (anaphylaxis is rare but possible). Observe patients for 30–60 minutes after IV iron per institutional protocol.
  - If IV iron is given in the setting of recent/ongoing infection, ensure appropriate antibiotic therapy and clinical improvement before or during iron repletion (as in the case where antibiotics were given according to culture sensitivities).
- Obstetric considerations after correction:
  - Reassess fetal growth and amniotic fluid (anemia and maternal hemodynamic instability can affect fetal growth); the presented case instituted strict obstetrical follow-up every 3 weeks and documented adequate fetal growth.
  - Modify delivery planning according to maternal recovery, residual lesions and likelihood of hemorrhage. If major surgery was recently performed, consider timing of delivery and mode (the team elected cesarean at term because of residual myoma anatomy).
  - If preterm delivery is a possibility, consider antenatal corticosteroids and magnesium for neuroprotection as indicated; document and counsel regarding transfusion/iron therapy implications for breastfeeding if relevant.

Bottom line (clinical pearl)
- In pregnant patients facing surgery, use IV iron to correct iron-deficiency when oral therapy is insufficient or too slow (common trigger: Hb <10–11 g/dL with low ferritin or need for rapid repletion). Reserve RBC transfusion for severe or symptomatic anemia, hemodynamic instability, or ongoing bleeding — individualized thresholds often fall around Hb ≤7 g/dL for stable patients, with liberalization for symptoms or perioperative/obstetric risk. Always coordinate maternal hemodynamic support with fetal monitoring: check and document fetal heart rate before, during (when feasible), and after surgery; treat active infections before or while giving IV iron; and tailor post-treatment surveillance of fetal growth and maternal recovery, as illustrated in the case.
