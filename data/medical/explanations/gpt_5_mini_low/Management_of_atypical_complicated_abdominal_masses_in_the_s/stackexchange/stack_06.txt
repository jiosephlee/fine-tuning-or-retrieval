Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Clinical Pearls] What intraoperative findings suggest torsion or impending rupture of a pedunculated uterine leiomyoma, and how do these findings change immediate surgical priorities in a gravid patient?

Key intraoperative findings that point to torsion or impending rupture of a pedunculated uterine leiomyoma—and that make operative treatment urgent—are those that indicate compromised blood flow, structural failure, or contamination risk. In this case report the team found exactly these features, which guided them to remove the cranial mass and leave the other myoma in situ.

Findings that suggest torsion or imminent rupture
- Twisted pedicle or obvious rotation of the stalk. (Case: “initially twisted” pedicle.)  
- Congestion, cyanosis or dark discoloration of the myoma surface suggesting venous outflow obstruction and ischemia.  
- Taut, tense, cystic or “taut-elastic” consistency with serosanguinous or bloody leakage from the lesion (Case: “taut-elastic…initially twisted and leaking”).  
- Focal areas of softening, thinning of the capsule or visible defects in the wall that risk rupture.  
- Marked hypervascularity of the stalk or tumour-feeding vessels (Case: “highly vascularised”).  
- Perilesional haemorrhage, hemoperitoneum or purulent/serous fluid in the peritoneal cavity.  
- Local inflammatory signs (adhesions, fibrin) or systemic features consistent with necrosis/infection — fever, leukocytosis, elevated CRP (Case: low-grade fever, neutrophilia, CRP rise).  
- Pathology later confirming ischaemic necrosis near the twisted pedicle (Case: pathology showed ischaemic necrosis).

How these findings change immediate surgical priorities in a gravid patient
- Shift from pure expectant/conservative management to targeted surgical intervention when torsion/rupture threatens maternal infection, uncontrolled pain, haemorrhage, or fetal compromise. The goal becomes rapid control of the source while preserving the pregnancy. (Case rationale: worsening pain, dyspnoea, risk of rupture/infection → laparotomy.)
- Minimize uterine manipulation and surgical time. Prioritize a short, controlled procedure focused on the offending lesion; avoid unnecessary additional procedures. (Case: only the cranial pedunculated myoma was excised; caudal mass was left in situ.)
- Secure the blood supply at the stalk before or immediately upon division to prevent major hemorrhage: clamp and ligate the pedicle, transect, then close the uterine defect with absorbable suture. (Case: excision with double clamp, root closed with single absorbable suture.)
- Maintain continuous fetal surveillance when feasible (document fetal heart rate before, during and after surgery) and take measures to reduce preterm labour risk (e.g., intraoperative and postoperative progesterone /tocolysis per local protocol). (Case: fetal cardiac activity verified intraoperatively; progesterone given intraoperatively and continued.)
- Be prepared for hemorrhage: have blood products available, use meticulous haemostasis, and limit entry into the uterine cavity. (Case: postoperative anemia required IV iron and transfusion.)  
- Control infection risk with appropriate perioperative antibiotics and manage any source (e.g., leaking/necrotic tissue). (Case: intraoperative prophylaxis + postoperative antibiotics guided by urine culture.)
- If other masses are present but stable (no torsion, not actively leaking/infected), defer removal to minimize operative time and obstetric risk; plan postpartum reassessment. (Case: caudal mass left in situ because it appeared fixed and less acute.)
- Multidisciplinary coordination and informed consent emphasizing maternal and fetal risks and the plan to preserve pregnancy whenever possible.

Bottom line: twisted, congested, leaking, hypervascular pedunculated myomas with local necrosis or peritoneal contamination indicate torsion/impending rupture and usually mandate prompt, focused excision in pregnancy. The intraoperative priorities are rapid control of the pedicle and haemostasis, minimal uterine trauma and operative time, continuous fetal monitoring, infection control, and a deliberate decision to limit surgery to the lesion causing acute compromise—exactly the approach used in this case.
