Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Pathophysiology] Describe the pathophysiologic changes in uterine leiomyoma size across pregnancy and the puerperium, and explain how these dynamics inform timing of intervention and delivery planning.

Clinical teaching answer — Pathophysiology, temporal dynamics, and how they guide intervention and delivery planning

Key point
- Uterine leiomyomas do not grow linearly in pregnancy: most rapid enlargement occurs early (first → second trimester), growth slows later, and many myomas regress substantially after delivery. Understanding that time course — plus location (submucosal, intramural, subserosal, pedunculated) and complications (red/cystic degeneration, torsion, haemorrhage, infection, mass effect) — is central to deciding whether and when to intervene and how to plan delivery.

Pathophysiology and temporal dynamics
- Hormonal and vascular drivers: rising estrogen and progesterone and increased uterine blood flow in pregnancy stimulate smooth‑muscle hypertrophy and extracellular matrix deposition, promoting myoma enlargement. Early in pregnancy these influences and angiogenesis produce the greatest relative growth; later, mechanical constraints of the expanding uterus and relative changes in blood flow slow growth.
- Quantitative trend (from the literature and cited in the case): overall myoma size increases about 6.9% per week early in pregnancy, with a larger increment from first → second trimester (~6.1% per week) and slower growth from second → third (~3.1% per week). After delivery many myomas regress substantially: mean volume decrease at 6 weeks postpartum ~44% (wide variance).
- Complications reflect physiology: rapid growth and high vascularity predispose to red (ischaemic) degeneration, cystic change, pain, fever, and — for pedunculated subserosal myomas — torsion. Large lesions can compress the diaphragm, bladder, ureters, or displace the uterus and fetal parts, producing dyspnoea, urinary stasis, or obstructed labour.

How these dynamics inform timing of intervention
- Expectant management is preferred when possible: because many myomas stabilize or shrink later in pregnancy and regress postpartum, deferring definitive surgery avoids pregnancy risks (bleeding, pregnancy loss).
- Indications to operate in pregnancy are complication‑driven, not size alone: acute torsion of a pedunculated myoma, persistent severe pain unresponsive to conservative measures, infection, necrosis with sepsis risk, or life‑threatening mass effect (respiratory compromise, bowel obstruction) justify myomectomy in pregnancy. The case demonstrates this principle: the upper pedunculated, highly vascularised, twisted and leaking leiomyoma produced pain, fever, and dyspnoea from mass effect — appropriate indication for laparotomy and excision at 21+6 weeks.
- Timing considerations: if intervention is required, earlier second trimester (after organogenesis) is often preferred over later gestation because the uterus is still manageable and perioperative fetal risk is lower than in the first trimester; however every case requires multidisciplinary risk assessment. When feasible, delay to allow fetal maturity and to exploit the later slower growth phase; when acute complications occur, do not delay.
- Intraoperative and perioperative planning: minimize operative time and uterine manipulation, ensure continuous fetal monitoring when viable, use non‑teratogenic imaging preoperatively (US, non‑contrast MRI), and offer perioperative measures to support the pregnancy (tocolysis or progesterone when indicated, blood products, antibiotic prophylaxis). The case illustrates use of ultrasound/MRI for assessment, intraoperative fetal monitoring, and postoperative progesterone and close follow‑up.

How dynamics inform delivery planning
- Assess size, number and location relative to the cervix/pelvis and placenta: a residual large intramural/subserosal myoma in the low uterine segment or pelvis can physically obstruct the presenting part and make vaginal birth unsafe. In the case, a large posterior intramural‑subserosal myoma in the isthmic region was judged likely to impede head descent → elective cesarean at 37 weeks.
- Expect postpartum change: if the mass is not causing acute symptoms and some postpartum regression is likely, one can elect for cesarean delivery and defer definitive myoma surgery to the postpartum period when regression may obviate or simplify surgery. The patient in the case elected to postpone further surgery because the residual myoma shrank partially and she was asymptomatic.
- Myomectomy at cesarean: possible in selected cases (size/site favourable, experienced surgical team), but carries higher risks of blood loss and prolonged surgery; decision depends on individual anatomy and obstetric considerations.
- Reassessment and counseling: serial imaging during pregnancy documents growth trend; counsel women that many myomas will stabilize or regress postpartum and that delivery route will be individualized based on pelvic obstruction risk and maternal‑fetal safety.

Practical clinical pearls
- Use ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI to define number, size, location and distance from the uterine cavity/placenta; reserve gadolinium for exceptional situations.
- Monitor serially because most growth occurs early; intervene in pregnancy only for complications or when conservative measures fail.
- Plan delivery based on whether the myoma anatomically obstructs the birth canal or presents other obstetric risks; consider postpartum reassessment before elective definitive surgery.
- Intraoperative fetal monitoring and multidisciplinary coordination (obstetrics, gynaecologic surgery, anaesthesia, neonatology) are essential when operating during pregnancy.

In summary: leiomyomas often grow most in early pregnancy, slow growth later, and commonly regress postpartum. This natural history supports conservative management when possible; operate in pregnancy only for true complications (torsion, severe mass effect, infection, refractory pain). Delivery planning should be individualized based on the residual myoma’s location and likelihood of obstructing labour, with many patients managed by elective cesarean or delayed postpartum myomectomy, as in the presented case.
