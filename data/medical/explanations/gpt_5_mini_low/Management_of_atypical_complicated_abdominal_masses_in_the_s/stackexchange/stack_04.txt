Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Management] In a symptomatic pregnant patient at 21–22 weeks with large, vascularized abdominal masses and worsening pain and restrictive dyspnoea, what are the principal indications to proceed with surgical intervention versus expectant management?

Short answer
Proceed to surgery in pregnancy when mass-related maternal or fetal risk is substantial or when the diagnosis cannot safely be deferred — specifically: severe or progressive symptoms not responsive to conservative measures (intractable pain, restrictive dyspnoea), acute complications (torsion, rupture, haemorrhage, infection, ischaemia/necrosis), mass effect limiting uterine growth or fetal well‑being, or strong concern for malignancy. Expectant management is appropriate when symptoms are mild and controlled, imaging and markers lack worrisome features, and close multidisciplinary monitoring is feasible.

Why — clinical reasoning and pathophysiology
- Maternal physiologic reserve in pregnancy is limited by the gravid uterus and altered cardiopulmonary mechanics; very large intra‑abdominal masses can mechanically compress the diaphragm and lungs (causing restrictive dyspnoea), compromise venous return, or produce pain from torsion, ischemia/degeneration, rupture or infection. These complications can threaten maternal hemodynamics and fetal oxygenation, so they justify intervention.
- Pedunculated or highly vascular masses are at higher risk of torsion, congestion, haemorrhage and necrosis; a twisted/ischemic lesion may present with fever, leukocytosis and worsening pain and can rapidly deteriorate.
- Diagnostic uncertainty matters: adnexal vs uterine origin and benign vs malignant cannot always be resolved on ultrasound or non‑contrast MRI in pregnancy. Progressive imaging/marker changes (new solid components, thick walls, increased peripheral vascularity, rising CA‑125 trend) increase the pretest probability of an occult malignancy or complicated mass and lower the threshold for surgery.

Diagnostic standards to guide the decision
- Serial clinical exams, transvaginal/transabdominal ultrasound with colour Doppler and non‑contrast MRI (≤1.5 T) are the cornerstones. Look for: size, number, pedicle, solid vs cystic components, wall thickness, internal septations, papillary projections, abnormal vascular supply, evidence of torsion, adjacent organ involvement.
- Tumour markers (e.g., CA‑125) can be supportive but are unreliable alone in pregnancy; trends and correlation with imaging are more useful than single values.
- Close fetal monitoring (heart rate before/during/after procedure if operating) is mandatory if surgery is undertaken.

Operational indications, summarized (when to operate)
1. Severe, progressive, or refractory maternal symptoms:
   - Intractable abdominal pain despite analgesia and conservative measures.
   - Restrictive dyspnoea or respiratory compromise attributable to the mass.
2. Acute or high‑risk complications:
   - Torsion of a pedunculated mass, rupture, active intra‑abdominal bleeding, sepsis/infected mass, ischemic necrosis.
3. Mass effect threatening pregnancy/fetal status:
   - Space‑occupying lesions that will obstruct uterine growth, impede delivery, or compromise fetal oxygenation.
4. Diagnostic uncertainty with features suspicious for malignancy:
   - New solid components, thick irregular walls, marked vascularity, rapidly rising tumour markers or inability to exclude an ovarian neoplasm.
5. Anticipated progressive worsening with gestation and unacceptably high risk of later emergency surgery.

When to continue expectant management
- Patient clinically stable, symptoms mild and responsive to conservative therapy (analgesia, positioning), no evidence of torsion/rupture/infection.
- Imaging features compatible with benign lesions (small, intramural leiomyomas without suspicious solid nodules or aggressive vascularity; stable size) and tumour markers not concerning or stable.
- Feasible close follow‑up with serial ultrasound/MRI, laboratory checks and multidisciplinary oversight, and contingency plans for urgent surgery if the clinical picture changes.

Practical perioperative principles if operating in second trimester
- Second trimester (ideally 14–23+6 weeks) is the preferred window if non‑elective surgery is needed — lower risk of teratogenicity than first trimester and lower preterm labour risk than later pregnancy.
- Minimize uterine manipulation, shorten operative time, and aim to preserve the pregnancy (remove only the culpable lesion when possible).
- Continuous fetal monitoring before/during/after operation when feasible; involve obstetrics, anesthesia, neonatology and surgery.
- Prophylactic measures per local practice (tocolysis/progesterone was used in this case) and appropriate antibiotic/iron/blood management.
- Decide approach (laparoscopy vs laparotomy) based on mass size, number, location and surgeon experience; very large, multiple, posterior or intramural lesions often require laparotomy.

How the present case illustrates these principles
- This patient had worsening pain and restrictive dyspnoea attributable to two very large vascularized masses at 21 weeks, fever and rising CA‑125, and MRI features (thick walls, solid components, hypertrophied vessels) that left diagnostic uncertainty and suggested risk of complication. Intraoperatively the cranial lesion was a highly vascular pedunculated leiomyoma that had twisted and leaked (ischemic necrosis)—an indication for myomectomy. The team elected to remove only the symptomatic/twisted mass and leave the adherent pelvic myoma in situ to minimize obstetric risk—illustrating targeted surgical intervention guided by complication risk and pregnancy preservation.
