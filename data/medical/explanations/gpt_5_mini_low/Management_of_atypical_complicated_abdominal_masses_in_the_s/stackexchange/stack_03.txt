Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Clinical Pearls] What are the accepted safe imaging modalities for evaluating pelvic or abdominal masses in pregnancy, and under what circumstances, if any, should gadolinium-enhanced MRI be avoided or considered?

Clinical pearl — imaging pelvic or abdominal masses in pregnancy

1) First-line and preferred modalities
- Transabdominal and transvaginal ultrasound (with colour Doppler) is the primary imaging tool in pregnancy: portable, no ionising radiation, excellent for adnexal lesions, uterine myomas, vascularity and follow‑up. In the presented case the team used ultrasound first and it delineated two large cystic masses and vascularity, guiding further management.

2) Second-line: MRI without contrast
- MRI (non‑contrast) is the preferred second‑line modality when ultrasound is insufficient (large, posterior, multiple or complex masses; need to define relation to uterine cavity/placenta; surgical planning). MRI gives superior soft‑tissue characterisation without ionising radiation. In this case a non‑contrast MRI clarified cyst contents, wall thickness and suspected continuity with a uterine myoma and was appropriately used in the second trimester.
- Practical points: use the lowest reasonable field strength (1.5 T is the commonly recommended upper limit in pregnancy) and avoid prolonged scan times; fetal heart monitoring or documentation before/during/after surgery if intervention is planned.

3) Modalities to avoid or use only when essential
- Gadolinium‑enhanced MRI: avoid in pregnancy unless the diagnostic information is essential and cannot be obtained by ultrasound or non‑contrast MRI and the result would materially change management (for example, strong suspicion of malignancy or life‑threatening maternal condition where contrast will alter therapy). Reasoning:
  - Gadolinium crosses the placenta and enters the fetal compartment; free gadolinium chelates have theoretical teratogenic and toxic risk and have been associated in some observational data with increased risk of stillbirth, neonatal death, and a range of inflammatory/infiltrative conditions when given in pregnancy (hence guideline caution).
  - When considered unavoidable, use the lowest effective dose, discuss risks/benefits with the patient, document informed consent, and consult radiology/obstetrics.
- Ionising radiation studies (CT, PET/CT, radiographs): avoid if possible. CT may be performed in emergencies when information cannot be obtained otherwise (trauma, suspected pulmonary embolism; use dose‑minimising protocols and shielding). If iodinated contrast is used, counsel about potential fetal thyroid effects and arrange neonatal thyroid testing if indicated.

4) How to decide in practice
- Start with ultrasound. If uncertainty persists about origin, extent, or surgical planning (as in this case where masses were large and atypical), obtain non‑contrast MRI.
- Reserve gadolinium only when the additional contrast information would change immediate management (e.g., distinguishing malignant from benign process when surgery or chemotherapy decisions hinge on it), after multidisciplinary review and informed consent.
- Document fetal monitoring plans and the timing of imaging relative to gestational age; MRI is considered reasonably safe after the first trimester, and many centers avoid >1.5 T during pregnancy.

Bottom line (case‑based): The team followed accepted practice — ultrasound followed by non‑contrast MRI (≤1.5 T) — and appropriately avoided gadolinium. Gadolinium‑enhanced MRI in pregnancy should be avoided unless absolutely necessary for maternal management and only after multidisciplinary discussion and informed consent.
