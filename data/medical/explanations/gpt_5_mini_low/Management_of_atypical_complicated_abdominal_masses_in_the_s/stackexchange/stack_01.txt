Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a second-trimester pregnant patient with acute abdominal pain, dyspnoea, and two large cystic abdominal masses on ultrasound, what key sonographic and MRI features help distinguish decidualised endometrioma from cystic degenerating uterine leiomyoma, and how does continuity with uterine tissue influence the differential?

Direct answer
- Sonographically and on non‑contrast MRI the key discriminators are (1) internal content and signal of the cyst, (2) presence and appearance of mural/solid nodules, and (3) evidence of continuity with the myometrium (stalk, “bridging vessels” or shared wall). Continuity with uterine tissue strongly favours a leiomyomatous origin (pedunculated, intramural or subserosal fibroid with cystic degeneration); absence of an identifiable uterine connection and classic blood‑product signal favour an ovarian endometrioma that has undergone pregnancy‑associated decidualisation.

Clinical reasoning and imaging features (teaching points)
1) Internal fluid signal / content
- Endometrioma: classically contains blood products. On MRI this produces T1 hyperintensity (methemoglobin) and the so‑called “T2 shading” (reduced T2 signal) from chronic blood. In pregnancy decidualisation may alter appearance but underlying haemorrhagic content often persists.
- Cystic degenerating leiomyoma: fluid is usually serous or necrotic rather than blood‑filled, so MRI shows T2 hyperintense, heterogeneous fluid without the classic T1 blood‑product signal of an endometrioma. On US the contents may appear anechoic or complex but less classically haemorrhagic.

2) Mural/solid nodules and vascularity
- Decidualised endometrioma: pregnancy may induce highly vascular, papillary‑like mural nodules that are soft and vascular on Doppler and can mimic malignancy. These nodules are typically intraluminal, irregular and arise from the cyst wall.
- Degenerating leiomyoma: solid components tend to be contiguous smooth muscle tissue (thick walls, solid areas from myometrium) and may show relatively organized vascular pedicles when pedunculated. Peripheral vascularity can be present in both, so vascularity alone is not definitive.

3) Continuity with uterine tissue — the decisive feature
- Evidence that the mass shares a wall with the uterus, arises from the myometrium, or is fed by “bridging vessels” from the uterus (visible on detailed ultrasound with colour Doppler or on MRI as a connection to the myometrium) strongly supports a leiomyoma with cystic degeneration or a pedunculated subserosal fibroid.
- Lack of a visible uterine connection (isolated adnexal location, separate ovarian pedicle, or classical endometrioma signal) supports an ovarian endometrioma.
- In this case report the caudal cyst appeared in continuity with a posterior intramural–subserosal myoma on both US and MRI, favouring myomatous cystic degeneration; the upper mass initially lacked an identifiable stalk on imaging (because of the large uterine size and displacement), which led to an initial consideration of decidualised endometrioma—yet intraoperative inspection revealed a pedunculated cystic leiomyoma. This underlines that absence of a visible stalk on imaging can be due to mass effect and does not exclude uterine origin.

Practical implications for management
- If imaging shows clear uterine continuity (shared wall, bridging vessels, pedicle), treat presumptively as leiomyoma and manage expectantly unless complications (torsion, rupture, infection, intolerable symptoms) mandate surgery.
- If imaging shows ovarian location with blood‑product signal and decidualised mural nodules, consider decidualised endometrioma in the differential; conservative management and close follow‑up are preferred unless malignancy cannot be excluded.
- Remember pregnancy alters tumour markers (CA‑125 can be mildly elevated) and imaging appearances; use a combination of targeted transvaginal/transabdominal ultrasound with colour Doppler and non‑contrast MRI to assess continuity to the uterus before committing to surgery.

Bottom line
Continuity with uterine tissue (stalk, shared wall, bridging vessels) is the single most useful imaging sign pointing to a cystic degenerating leiomyoma rather than an ovarian decidualised endometrioma. Combine that sign with MRI fluid/signal characteristics (T1 blood signal and T2 shading favour endometrioma) and the morphology of mural nodules to reach the correct preoperative diagnosis — remembering that large gravid anatomy can obscure a uterine connection, as happened in this case.
