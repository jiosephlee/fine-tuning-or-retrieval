Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Management] What factors guide the choice between laparoscopic versus open myomectomy during pregnancy, and how do mass size, number, location, and gestational age affect this decision?

Bottom line
- The decision between laparoscopic and open (laparotomic) myomectomy in pregnancy is individualized and driven by operative safety for mother and fetus: size, number, location (relationship to the uterine wall and cavity), gestational age, vascularity and suspected complication (torsion, degeneration, rupture, infection or malignancy) determine which approach is safer and more likely to preserve the pregnancy.

Clinical reasoning and principles
1) Gestational age
- Laparoscopy is most appropriate in early pregnancy (first and early second trimester) when the uterus is relatively small, abdominal access is easier, and pneumoperitoneum and trocar placement can be performed with lower technical difficulty and less risk of uterine injury.
- As gestation advances and the gravid uterus enlarges, laparoscopic working space narrows and risks (uterine trauma, difficulty controlling bleeding, compromised visualization) increase — favouring laparotomy in mid‑to‑late second and third trimesters.

2) Mass size and number
- Small (<~5–8 cm), solitary lesions (particularly pedunculated or subserosal) are good laparoscopic candidates: extraction and haemostasis are technically easier, operative time is shorter, and minimally invasive benefits (less pain, faster recovery) apply.
- Large masses (the case: 18 × 12 × 7 cm cranial mass; 9.5–8–8 cm pelvic mass) or multiple myomas reduce laparoscopic feasibility because of limited space, higher vascularity and greater blood loss risk. Large size also makes safe in‑abdominal morcellation or extraction difficult; these favour open myomectomy.

3) Location and relationship to the uterine cavity
- Pedunculated subserosal myomas are most amenable to laparoscopy, even when symptomatic, because a stalk can often be clamped and divided with controlled haemostasis.
- Intramural or submucosal myomas, or those tightly adherent to posterior uterine wall or isthmus, risk entering or distorting the uterine cavity, causing bleeding and potential pregnancy loss; these are harder to manage laparoscopically and often warrant open surgery (as in the case, where the caudal mass was adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma and was left in situ to avoid obstetric risk).
- Posteriorly located or isthmic lesions that could obstruct the birth canal may require planned cesarean or staged open surgery postpartum.

4) Vascularity, degeneration, torsion, suspected infection or malignancy
- Highly vascular, degenerated, twisted (torsed) or infected lesions that threaten acute complications are indications for prompt surgical intervention. The approach should prioritize rapid, secure haemostasis — often favouring laparotomy for large, vascular, or necrotic masses (the cranial mass in the case was highly vascularised, initially twisted and leaking → laparotomy and excision).
- Suspicion of malignancy (atypical imaging, rising tumour markers) also tends to favour open surgery to allow safe removal and appropriate staging.

5) Technical and institutional factors
- Surgeon experience with pregnancy laparoscopy, availability of fetal monitoring intra‑/post‑op, anaesthetic expertise, and blood‑bank support are essential. When team experience is limited or complex anatomy is expected, open approach is safer.

Perioperative management principles (to preserve pregnancy)
- Document fetal heart rate before, during and after surgery; involve obstetrics/neonatology.
- Minimize operative time and blood loss; have cross‑matched blood available.
- Use non‑contrast MRI and targeted ultrasound for preoperative planning to define mass‑uterus relationships.
- Consider perioperative tocolysis or progesterone as institutional practice and gestational age dictates (case used intraoperative and ongoing progesterone).
- Multidisciplinary informed consent discussing risk of pregnancy loss, preterm delivery and need for cesarean.

Applying these principles to the presented case
- Two large, vascularized, atypical masses at 21+6 weeks with pain, dyspnoea, fever and imaging ambiguity raised risk of complication and need for definitive diagnosis. The cranial mass proved to be a twisted, highly vascular pedunculated leiomyoma—an indication for excision; open laparotomy allowed rapid control of bleeding and safe removal. The caudal lesion was adherent to a posterior intramural‑subserosal myoma; because of its location, size and potential to compromise pregnancy if manipulated, it was left in situ to avoid obstetric risk, and delivery was planned by elective cesarean when fetal maturity reached 37 weeks.

Practical summary
- Prefer laparoscopy when early gestation, small solitary subserosal/pedunculated myoma, low vascularity, experienced team and adequate working space.
- Prefer laparotomy when later gestation, large or multiple masses, intramural/posterior/isthmic location, high vascularity, suspected infection/torsion/malignancy, or when rapid haemostasis and maximal access are required.
- Individualize decision with multidisciplinary input, imaging (US ± non‑contrast MRI), and explicit counselling about maternal and fetal risks.
