Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a second-trimester pregnant patient with acute abdominal pain, dyspnoea, and two large cystic abdominal masses on ultrasound, what key sonographic and MRI features help distinguish decidualised endometrioma from cystic degenerating uterine leiomyoma, and how does continuity with uterine tissue influence the differential?

Direct answer
- Sonographically and on non‑contrast MRI the key discriminators are (1) internal content and signal of the cyst, (2) presence and appearance of mural/solid nodules, and (3) evidence of continuity with the myometrium (stalk, “bridging vessels” or shared wall). Continuity with uterine tissue strongly favours a leiomyomatous origin (pedunculated, intramural or subserosal fibroid with cystic degeneration); absence of an identifiable uterine connection and classic blood‑product signal favour an ovarian endometrioma that has undergone pregnancy‑associated decidualisation.

Clinical reasoning and imaging features (teaching points)
1) Internal fluid signal / content
- Endometrioma: classically contains blood products. On MRI this produces T1 hyperintensity (methemoglobin) and the so‑called “T2 shading” (reduced T2 signal) from chronic blood. In pregnancy decidualisation may alter appearance but underlying haemorrhagic content often persists.
- Cystic degenerating leiomyoma: fluid is usually serous or necrotic rather than blood‑filled, so MRI shows T2 hyperintense, heterogeneous fluid without the classic T1 blood‑product signal of an endometrioma. On US the contents may appear anechoic or complex but less classically haemorrhagic.

2) Mural/solid nodules and vascularity
- Decidualised endometrioma: pregnancy may induce highly vascular, papillary‑like mural nodules that are soft and vascular on Doppler and can mimic malignancy. These nodules are typically intraluminal, irregular and arise from the cyst wall.
- Degenerating leiomyoma: solid components tend to be contiguous smooth muscle tissue (thick walls, solid areas from myometrium) and may show relatively organized vascular pedicles when pedunculated. Peripheral vascularity can be present in both, so vascularity alone is not definitive.

3) Continuity with uterine tissue — the decisive feature
- Evidence that the mass shares a wall with the uterus, arises from the myometrium, or is fed by “bridging vessels” from the uterus (visible on detailed ultrasound with colour Doppler or on MRI as a connection to the myometrium) strongly supports a leiomyoma with cystic degeneration or a pedunculated subserosal fibroid.
- Lack of a visible uterine connection (isolated adnexal location, separate ovarian pedicle, or classical endometrioma signal) supports an ovarian endometrioma.
- In this case report the caudal cyst appeared in continuity with a posterior intramural–subserosal myoma on both US and MRI, favouring myomatous cystic degeneration; the upper mass initially lacked an identifiable stalk on imaging (because of the large uterine size and displacement), which led to an initial consideration of decidualised endometrioma—yet intraoperative inspection revealed a pedunculated cystic leiomyoma. This underlines that absence of a visible stalk on imaging can be due to mass effect and does not exclude uterine origin.

Practical implications for management
- If imaging shows clear uterine continuity (shared wall, bridging vessels, pedicle), treat presumptively as leiomyoma and manage expectantly unless complications (torsion, rupture, infection, intolerable symptoms) mandate surgery.
- If imaging shows ovarian location with blood‑product signal and decidualised mural nodules, consider decidualised endometrioma in the differential; conservative management and close follow‑up are preferred unless malignancy cannot be excluded.
- Remember pregnancy alters tumour markers (CA‑125 can be mildly elevated) and imaging appearances; use a combination of targeted transvaginal/transabdominal ultrasound with colour Doppler and non‑contrast MRI to assess continuity to the uterus before committing to surgery.

Bottom line
Continuity with uterine tissue (stalk, shared wall, bridging vessels) is the single most useful imaging sign pointing to a cystic degenerating leiomyoma rather than an ovarian decidualised endometrioma. Combine that sign with MRI fluid/signal characteristics (T1 blood signal and T2 shading favour endometrioma) and the morphology of mural nodules to reach the correct preoperative diagnosis — remembering that large gravid anatomy can obscure a uterine connection, as happened in this case.

### [Clinical Pearls] How should CA-125 be interpreted during pregnancy, particularly when mildly elevated in the second trimester, and what benign and malignant conditions remain on the differential in this context?

Clinical pearl — interpreting CA‑125 in pregnancy

Bottom line
- CA‑125 is unreliable as a stand‑alone discriminator of benign versus malignant pelvic disease in pregnancy. Pregnancy causes physiologic rises (especially in the first and third trimesters), so a mildly elevated value in the second trimester should not by itself prompt radical management. Interpretation must be integrated with symptoms, serial trends, and imaging (US ± non‑contrast MRI). Rapid rises, markedly elevated values, suspicious imaging (solid nodules, papillary projections, thick septations, ascites, adenopathy) or clinical deterioration increase concern for malignancy and warrant multidisciplinary evaluation.

Why: physiology and test characteristics
- CA‑125 is a glycoprotein produced by coelomic‑derived epithelium (peritoneum, pleura, Müllerian epithelium). Levels commonly peak in early pregnancy (first trimester) and again near term (third trimester). Reported medians: ~23 U/mL first trimester, ~14 U/mL second trimester, ~21 U/mL third trimester.
- The conventional oncologic cut‑off (≥35 U/mL) was derived in nonpregnant populations and loses specificity in pregnancy. False‑positive elevations occur with many benign processes that are common or more likely in pregnancy (decidualisation, leiomyoma degeneration, inflammation, recent surgery, infection, endometriosis/adenomyosis, peritoneal irritation).

What a mildly raised CA‑125 in the second trimester means (practical approach)
- Mild isolated elevation (for example CA‑125 in the low 40s as in this case) in the second trimester is nonspecific:
  - Consider benign causes first, especially if imaging suggests a benign lesion (simple or unilocular cyst, classic fibroid appearance) and there is no ascites, adenopathy, or marked solid papillary components.
  - Repeat CA‑125 (serial trend) can be informative: a stable or declining level supports a benign process; a rapid upward trend is more concerning.
  - Correlate with high‑quality ultrasound (morphology, Doppler) and, if needed, non‑contrast MRI for better tissue characterisation. Gadolinium is avoided in pregnancy unless absolutely necessary.
- Don’t let a single mildly elevated CA‑125 drive overtreatment (eg, termination or radical surgery) in an otherwise stable pregnant patient.

Benign conditions to keep on the differential during pregnancy
- Decidualised endometrioma (can increase CA‑125 and mimic malignancy on imaging)
- Uterine leiomyomas with cystic degeneration, torsion or necrosis (can raise CA‑125 and cause fever/pain) — directly relevant to this case, where the cranial mass was a pedunculated cystic leiomyoma with ischemic change and CA‑125 ~41.8 U/mL
- Physiologic pregnancy‑related elevation (first and third trimester peaks)
- Pelvic inflammatory disease or urinary tract infection with peritoneal irritation (this patient had urinary Klebsiella and low‑grade fever)
- Peritoneal irritation, recent surgery, endometriosis/adenomyosis, ascites from benign causes

Malignant conditions to consider (less likely but important)
- Epithelial ovarian carcinoma (including borderline tumours)
- Primary peritoneal carcinoma or metastatic disease to the adnexa
- Germ cell or sex‑cord stromal tumours (less commonly associated with CA‑125 elevations)
- Malignancy is more likely when CA‑125 is markedly elevated, shows a rapid rise, and imaging shows complex solid components, papillary projections, multiloculated thick septa, ascites, or lymphadenopathy.

How this applies to the presented case
- The patient had a mildly elevated CA‑125 (41.8 U/mL), increasing from 36 U/mL over 2 weeks. That degree of elevation in the second trimester is nonspecific but prompted careful workup.
- Imaging (US and non‑contrast MRI) showed large vascularised cystic masses with mixed solid/cystic features; those findings, the patient’s worsening pain, dyspnoea from mass effect, and fever (with evidence of infection and later ischemic necrosis at pathology) raised concern for complication and justified surgical management.
- Final pathology showed benign leiomyomas with ischemic necrosis — a classic benign explanation for both imaging features and the CA‑125 elevation.

Clinical takeaways
- Use CA‑125 in pregnancy only as an adjunct: interpret against gestational age, symptoms, imaging, and serial measurements.
- Mild second‑trimester elevations (low‑40s) are often benign; prioritize ultrasound/MRI and multidisciplinary discussion.
- Operate in pregnancy when clinical complications (torsion, rupture, infection), concerning imaging, or progressive symptoms threaten maternal or fetal health — not on a mildly elevated CA‑125 alone.
- Definitive diagnosis rests on histopathology (and cytology/ascites studies when present).

### [Clinical Pearls] What are the accepted safe imaging modalities for evaluating pelvic or abdominal masses in pregnancy, and under what circumstances, if any, should gadolinium-enhanced MRI be avoided or considered?

Clinical pearl — imaging pelvic or abdominal masses in pregnancy

1) First-line and preferred modalities
- Transabdominal and transvaginal ultrasound (with colour Doppler) is the primary imaging tool in pregnancy: portable, no ionising radiation, excellent for adnexal lesions, uterine myomas, vascularity and follow‑up. In the presented case the team used ultrasound first and it delineated two large cystic masses and vascularity, guiding further management.

2) Second-line: MRI without contrast
- MRI (non‑contrast) is the preferred second‑line modality when ultrasound is insufficient (large, posterior, multiple or complex masses; need to define relation to uterine cavity/placenta; surgical planning). MRI gives superior soft‑tissue characterisation without ionising radiation. In this case a non‑contrast MRI clarified cyst contents, wall thickness and suspected continuity with a uterine myoma and was appropriately used in the second trimester.
- Practical points: use the lowest reasonable field strength (1.5 T is the commonly recommended upper limit in pregnancy) and avoid prolonged scan times; fetal heart monitoring or documentation before/during/after surgery if intervention is planned.

3) Modalities to avoid or use only when essential
- Gadolinium‑enhanced MRI: avoid in pregnancy unless the diagnostic information is essential and cannot be obtained by ultrasound or non‑contrast MRI and the result would materially change management (for example, strong suspicion of malignancy or life‑threatening maternal condition where contrast will alter therapy). Reasoning:
  - Gadolinium crosses the placenta and enters the fetal compartment; free gadolinium chelates have theoretical teratogenic and toxic risk and have been associated in some observational data with increased risk of stillbirth, neonatal death, and a range of inflammatory/infiltrative conditions when given in pregnancy (hence guideline caution).
  - When considered unavoidable, use the lowest effective dose, discuss risks/benefits with the patient, document informed consent, and consult radiology/obstetrics.
- Ionising radiation studies (CT, PET/CT, radiographs): avoid if possible. CT may be performed in emergencies when information cannot be obtained otherwise (trauma, suspected pulmonary embolism; use dose‑minimising protocols and shielding). If iodinated contrast is used, counsel about potential fetal thyroid effects and arrange neonatal thyroid testing if indicated.

4) How to decide in practice
- Start with ultrasound. If uncertainty persists about origin, extent, or surgical planning (as in this case where masses were large and atypical), obtain non‑contrast MRI.
- Reserve gadolinium only when the additional contrast information would change immediate management (e.g., distinguishing malignant from benign process when surgery or chemotherapy decisions hinge on it), after multidisciplinary review and informed consent.
- Document fetal monitoring plans and the timing of imaging relative to gestational age; MRI is considered reasonably safe after the first trimester, and many centers avoid >1.5 T during pregnancy.

Bottom line (case‑based): The team followed accepted practice — ultrasound followed by non‑contrast MRI (≤1.5 T) — and appropriately avoided gadolinium. Gadolinium‑enhanced MRI in pregnancy should be avoided unless absolutely necessary for maternal management and only after multidisciplinary discussion and informed consent.

### [Management] In a symptomatic pregnant patient at 21–22 weeks with large, vascularized abdominal masses and worsening pain and restrictive dyspnoea, what are the principal indications to proceed with surgical intervention versus expectant management?

Short answer
Proceed to surgery in pregnancy when mass-related maternal or fetal risk is substantial or when the diagnosis cannot safely be deferred — specifically: severe or progressive symptoms not responsive to conservative measures (intractable pain, restrictive dyspnoea), acute complications (torsion, rupture, haemorrhage, infection, ischaemia/necrosis), mass effect limiting uterine growth or fetal well‑being, or strong concern for malignancy. Expectant management is appropriate when symptoms are mild and controlled, imaging and markers lack worrisome features, and close multidisciplinary monitoring is feasible.

Why — clinical reasoning and pathophysiology
- Maternal physiologic reserve in pregnancy is limited by the gravid uterus and altered cardiopulmonary mechanics; very large intra‑abdominal masses can mechanically compress the diaphragm and lungs (causing restrictive dyspnoea), compromise venous return, or produce pain from torsion, ischemia/degeneration, rupture or infection. These complications can threaten maternal hemodynamics and fetal oxygenation, so they justify intervention.
- Pedunculated or highly vascular masses are at higher risk of torsion, congestion, haemorrhage and necrosis; a twisted/ischemic lesion may present with fever, leukocytosis and worsening pain and can rapidly deteriorate.
- Diagnostic uncertainty matters: adnexal vs uterine origin and benign vs malignant cannot always be resolved on ultrasound or non‑contrast MRI in pregnancy. Progressive imaging/marker changes (new solid components, thick walls, increased peripheral vascularity, rising CA‑125 trend) increase the pretest probability of an occult malignancy or complicated mass and lower the threshold for surgery.

Diagnostic standards to guide the decision
- Serial clinical exams, transvaginal/transabdominal ultrasound with colour Doppler and non‑contrast MRI (≤1.5 T) are the cornerstones. Look for: size, number, pedicle, solid vs cystic components, wall thickness, internal septations, papillary projections, abnormal vascular supply, evidence of torsion, adjacent organ involvement.
- Tumour markers (e.g., CA‑125) can be supportive but are unreliable alone in pregnancy; trends and correlation with imaging are more useful than single values.
- Close fetal monitoring (heart rate before/during/after procedure if operating) is mandatory if surgery is undertaken.

Operational indications, summarized (when to operate)
1. Severe, progressive, or refractory maternal symptoms:
   - Intractable abdominal pain despite analgesia and conservative measures.
   - Restrictive dyspnoea or respiratory compromise attributable to the mass.
2. Acute or high‑risk complications:
   - Torsion of a pedunculated mass, rupture, active intra‑abdominal bleeding, sepsis/infected mass, ischemic necrosis.
3. Mass effect threatening pregnancy/fetal status:
   - Space‑occupying lesions that will obstruct uterine growth, impede delivery, or compromise fetal oxygenation.
4. Diagnostic uncertainty with features suspicious for malignancy:
   - New solid components, thick irregular walls, marked vascularity, rapidly rising tumour markers or inability to exclude an ovarian neoplasm.
5. Anticipated progressive worsening with gestation and unacceptably high risk of later emergency surgery.

When to continue expectant management
- Patient clinically stable, symptoms mild and responsive to conservative therapy (analgesia, positioning), no evidence of torsion/rupture/infection.
- Imaging features compatible with benign lesions (small, intramural leiomyomas without suspicious solid nodules or aggressive vascularity; stable size) and tumour markers not concerning or stable.
- Feasible close follow‑up with serial ultrasound/MRI, laboratory checks and multidisciplinary oversight, and contingency plans for urgent surgery if the clinical picture changes.

Practical perioperative principles if operating in second trimester
- Second trimester (ideally 14–23+6 weeks) is the preferred window if non‑elective surgery is needed — lower risk of teratogenicity than first trimester and lower preterm labour risk than later pregnancy.
- Minimize uterine manipulation, shorten operative time, and aim to preserve the pregnancy (remove only the culpable lesion when possible).
- Continuous fetal monitoring before/during/after operation when feasible; involve obstetrics, anesthesia, neonatology and surgery.
- Prophylactic measures per local practice (tocolysis/progesterone was used in this case) and appropriate antibiotic/iron/blood management.
- Decide approach (laparoscopy vs laparotomy) based on mass size, number, location and surgeon experience; very large, multiple, posterior or intramural lesions often require laparotomy.

How the present case illustrates these principles
- This patient had worsening pain and restrictive dyspnoea attributable to two very large vascularized masses at 21 weeks, fever and rising CA‑125, and MRI features (thick walls, solid components, hypertrophied vessels) that left diagnostic uncertainty and suggested risk of complication. Intraoperatively the cranial lesion was a highly vascular pedunculated leiomyoma that had twisted and leaked (ischemic necrosis)—an indication for myomectomy. The team elected to remove only the symptomatic/twisted mass and leave the adherent pelvic myoma in situ to minimize obstetric risk—illustrating targeted surgical intervention guided by complication risk and pregnancy preservation.

### [Management] During non-obstetric surgery in the second trimester, what intraoperative fetal monitoring and obstetric precautions should be implemented, and what is the role, if any, of perioperative progesterone or tocolysis?

Direct answer
- Intraoperative fetal monitoring: document fetal heart rate (FHR) and fetal viability immediately before incision, intermittently during the procedure and immediately after closure. When fetal heart sounds can be obtained and continuous monitoring is feasible (typically at a gestational age when reliable external monitoring is possible), consider continuous electronic FHR monitoring; otherwise use serial ultrasound/Doppler or transvaginal ultrasound to document fetal cardiac activity. At minimum, record baseline FHR before, during and after surgery.
- Obstetric precautions: multidisciplinary team present or immediately available (obstetrics, anesthesia, surgery, neonatology); left uterine displacement to avoid aortocaval compression; maternal oxygenation, normotension and normothermia; short, efficient operative time and meticulous hemostasis; have uterotonics, tocolytics and neonatal resuscitation available; avoid unnecessary uterine manipulation; document plans for emergency cesarean if maternal or fetal deterioration occurs.
- Role of perioperative progesterone and tocolysis: routine prophylactic tocolysis is not recommended. Tocolytics should be reserved for treatment of uterine contractions or threatened preterm labour that develop perioperatively (nifedipine is a common first-line agent). Routine perioperative progesterone prophylaxis is not evidence-based for preventing surgery-associated pregnancy loss or preterm birth; its use is variable and should be individualized (consider in patients with other indications for progesterone or in those judged high risk by the team). In the reported case the team administered perioperative intramuscular progesterone empirically and continued it postpartum until delivery — a reasonable individualized approach, but not supported as standard of care by high-quality evidence.

Clinical reasoning and evidence applied to the case
- Monitoring: Obstetric societies and large surgical series emphasize confirming and documenting fetal viability before, during and after any non-obstetric operation in pregnancy. In this report the team used serial/intermittent transvaginal ultrasound to verify fetal cardiac activity intraoperatively — an appropriate strategy when continuous external fetal monitoring is impractical because of gestational age or surgical positioning. The cited multicentre study (Zhao et al.) and the case authors specifically state that FHR should be checked and documented before, during and after surgery.
- Maternal optimization and surgical principles: The dominant determinant of fetal outcome is maternal physiology. Maintaining maternal oxygen delivery (adequate O2, blood pressure, volume status and haemoglobin), avoiding aortocaval compression (left tilt), and limiting operative time and blood loss are essential. Having obstetric and neonatal teams immediately available allows rapid intervention (e.g., emergency cesarean) if maternal or fetal compromise occurs.
- Tocolysis: Perioperative uterine irritability or contractions should be treated, not prevented routinely. Prophylactic tocolytics have not been shown to improve outcomes and can have maternal side effects. Therefore, use tocolysis therapeutically for documented contractions or threatened preterm labour.
- Progesterone: Vaginal or injectable progesterone reduces recurrent spontaneous preterm birth in selected high-risk populations (prior spontaneous preterm birth or short cervix). There is no strong data that perioperative progesterone prevents pregnancy loss after non-obstetric abdominal surgery. The team in this case administered intramuscular progesterone intraoperatively and continued it as a precaution; that approach is reasonable on an individualized basis when a multidisciplinary group judges the pregnancy at higher risk, but it should be understood as empirical rather than evidence-based standard practice.

Practical checklist for the operating room (second trimester non‑obstetric surgery)
- Preop: obstetric consult, document FHR, ultrasound for viability and placental location; consent discussing fetal risk.
- Intraop: left uterine displacement, continuous maternal monitoring (SpO2, BP, urine output), keep surgery as brief as possible, blood products available, serial FHR checks (or continuous monitoring if feasible), obstetrician and neonatology on standby.
- Postop: document FHR, monitor for contractions, arrange obstetric follow-up and fetal surveillance; treat uterine activity with tocolytics only if present; consider individualized use of progesterone only when other indications or high perceived risk.

Bottom line: Confirm fetal viability before, during and after surgery (intermittent ultrasound or continuous monitoring when feasible), protect maternal physiology and minimize operative risk, reserve tocolysis for active uterine contractions, and use perioperative progesterone only on a case‑by‑case basis because routine prophylaxis lacks strong supporting evidence. The management reported in this case (serial intraoperative ultrasound checks, multidisciplinary care, short procedure, and empiric perioperative progesterone) is a defensible, individualized strategy but not a universally mandated protocol.

### [Clinical Pearls] What intraoperative findings suggest torsion or impending rupture of a pedunculated uterine leiomyoma, and how do these findings change immediate surgical priorities in a gravid patient?

Key intraoperative findings that point to torsion or impending rupture of a pedunculated uterine leiomyoma—and that make operative treatment urgent—are those that indicate compromised blood flow, structural failure, or contamination risk. In this case report the team found exactly these features, which guided them to remove the cranial mass and leave the other myoma in situ.

Findings that suggest torsion or imminent rupture
- Twisted pedicle or obvious rotation of the stalk. (Case: “initially twisted” pedicle.)  
- Congestion, cyanosis or dark discoloration of the myoma surface suggesting venous outflow obstruction and ischemia.  
- Taut, tense, cystic or “taut-elastic” consistency with serosanguinous or bloody leakage from the lesion (Case: “taut-elastic…initially twisted and leaking”).  
- Focal areas of softening, thinning of the capsule or visible defects in the wall that risk rupture.  
- Marked hypervascularity of the stalk or tumour-feeding vessels (Case: “highly vascularised”).  
- Perilesional haemorrhage, hemoperitoneum or purulent/serous fluid in the peritoneal cavity.  
- Local inflammatory signs (adhesions, fibrin) or systemic features consistent with necrosis/infection — fever, leukocytosis, elevated CRP (Case: low-grade fever, neutrophilia, CRP rise).  
- Pathology later confirming ischaemic necrosis near the twisted pedicle (Case: pathology showed ischaemic necrosis).

How these findings change immediate surgical priorities in a gravid patient
- Shift from pure expectant/conservative management to targeted surgical intervention when torsion/rupture threatens maternal infection, uncontrolled pain, haemorrhage, or fetal compromise. The goal becomes rapid control of the source while preserving the pregnancy. (Case rationale: worsening pain, dyspnoea, risk of rupture/infection → laparotomy.)
- Minimize uterine manipulation and surgical time. Prioritize a short, controlled procedure focused on the offending lesion; avoid unnecessary additional procedures. (Case: only the cranial pedunculated myoma was excised; caudal mass was left in situ.)
- Secure the blood supply at the stalk before or immediately upon division to prevent major hemorrhage: clamp and ligate the pedicle, transect, then close the uterine defect with absorbable suture. (Case: excision with double clamp, root closed with single absorbable suture.)
- Maintain continuous fetal surveillance when feasible (document fetal heart rate before, during and after surgery) and take measures to reduce preterm labour risk (e.g., intraoperative and postoperative progesterone /tocolysis per local protocol). (Case: fetal cardiac activity verified intraoperatively; progesterone given intraoperatively and continued.)
- Be prepared for hemorrhage: have blood products available, use meticulous haemostasis, and limit entry into the uterine cavity. (Case: postoperative anemia required IV iron and transfusion.)  
- Control infection risk with appropriate perioperative antibiotics and manage any source (e.g., leaking/necrotic tissue). (Case: intraoperative prophylaxis + postoperative antibiotics guided by urine culture.)
- If other masses are present but stable (no torsion, not actively leaking/infected), defer removal to minimize operative time and obstetric risk; plan postpartum reassessment. (Case: caudal mass left in situ because it appeared fixed and less acute.)
- Multidisciplinary coordination and informed consent emphasizing maternal and fetal risks and the plan to preserve pregnancy whenever possible.

Bottom line: twisted, congested, leaking, hypervascular pedunculated myomas with local necrosis or peritoneal contamination indicate torsion/impending rupture and usually mandate prompt, focused excision in pregnancy. The intraoperative priorities are rapid control of the pedicle and haemostasis, minimal uterine trauma and operative time, continuous fetal monitoring, infection control, and a deliberate decision to limit surgery to the lesion causing acute compromise—exactly the approach used in this case.

### [Pathophysiology] Describe the pathophysiologic changes in uterine leiomyoma size across pregnancy and the puerperium, and explain how these dynamics inform timing of intervention and delivery planning.

Clinical teaching answer — Pathophysiology, temporal dynamics, and how they guide intervention and delivery planning

Key point
- Uterine leiomyomas do not grow linearly in pregnancy: most rapid enlargement occurs early (first → second trimester), growth slows later, and many myomas regress substantially after delivery. Understanding that time course — plus location (submucosal, intramural, subserosal, pedunculated) and complications (red/cystic degeneration, torsion, haemorrhage, infection, mass effect) — is central to deciding whether and when to intervene and how to plan delivery.

Pathophysiology and temporal dynamics
- Hormonal and vascular drivers: rising estrogen and progesterone and increased uterine blood flow in pregnancy stimulate smooth‑muscle hypertrophy and extracellular matrix deposition, promoting myoma enlargement. Early in pregnancy these influences and angiogenesis produce the greatest relative growth; later, mechanical constraints of the expanding uterus and relative changes in blood flow slow growth.
- Quantitative trend (from the literature and cited in the case): overall myoma size increases about 6.9% per week early in pregnancy, with a larger increment from first → second trimester (~6.1% per week) and slower growth from second → third (~3.1% per week). After delivery many myomas regress substantially: mean volume decrease at 6 weeks postpartum ~44% (wide variance).
- Complications reflect physiology: rapid growth and high vascularity predispose to red (ischaemic) degeneration, cystic change, pain, fever, and — for pedunculated subserosal myomas — torsion. Large lesions can compress the diaphragm, bladder, ureters, or displace the uterus and fetal parts, producing dyspnoea, urinary stasis, or obstructed labour.

How these dynamics inform timing of intervention
- Expectant management is preferred when possible: because many myomas stabilize or shrink later in pregnancy and regress postpartum, deferring definitive surgery avoids pregnancy risks (bleeding, pregnancy loss).
- Indications to operate in pregnancy are complication‑driven, not size alone: acute torsion of a pedunculated myoma, persistent severe pain unresponsive to conservative measures, infection, necrosis with sepsis risk, or life‑threatening mass effect (respiratory compromise, bowel obstruction) justify myomectomy in pregnancy. The case demonstrates this principle: the upper pedunculated, highly vascularised, twisted and leaking leiomyoma produced pain, fever, and dyspnoea from mass effect — appropriate indication for laparotomy and excision at 21+6 weeks.
- Timing considerations: if intervention is required, earlier second trimester (after organogenesis) is often preferred over later gestation because the uterus is still manageable and perioperative fetal risk is lower than in the first trimester; however every case requires multidisciplinary risk assessment. When feasible, delay to allow fetal maturity and to exploit the later slower growth phase; when acute complications occur, do not delay.
- Intraoperative and perioperative planning: minimize operative time and uterine manipulation, ensure continuous fetal monitoring when viable, use non‑teratogenic imaging preoperatively (US, non‑contrast MRI), and offer perioperative measures to support the pregnancy (tocolysis or progesterone when indicated, blood products, antibiotic prophylaxis). The case illustrates use of ultrasound/MRI for assessment, intraoperative fetal monitoring, and postoperative progesterone and close follow‑up.

How dynamics inform delivery planning
- Assess size, number and location relative to the cervix/pelvis and placenta: a residual large intramural/subserosal myoma in the low uterine segment or pelvis can physically obstruct the presenting part and make vaginal birth unsafe. In the case, a large posterior intramural‑subserosal myoma in the isthmic region was judged likely to impede head descent → elective cesarean at 37 weeks.
- Expect postpartum change: if the mass is not causing acute symptoms and some postpartum regression is likely, one can elect for cesarean delivery and defer definitive myoma surgery to the postpartum period when regression may obviate or simplify surgery. The patient in the case elected to postpone further surgery because the residual myoma shrank partially and she was asymptomatic.
- Myomectomy at cesarean: possible in selected cases (size/site favourable, experienced surgical team), but carries higher risks of blood loss and prolonged surgery; decision depends on individual anatomy and obstetric considerations.
- Reassessment and counseling: serial imaging during pregnancy documents growth trend; counsel women that many myomas will stabilize or regress postpartum and that delivery route will be individualized based on pelvic obstruction risk and maternal‑fetal safety.

Practical clinical pearls
- Use ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI to define number, size, location and distance from the uterine cavity/placenta; reserve gadolinium for exceptional situations.
- Monitor serially because most growth occurs early; intervene in pregnancy only for complications or when conservative measures fail.
- Plan delivery based on whether the myoma anatomically obstructs the birth canal or presents other obstetric risks; consider postpartum reassessment before elective definitive surgery.
- Intraoperative fetal monitoring and multidisciplinary coordination (obstetrics, gynaecologic surgery, anaesthesia, neonatology) are essential when operating during pregnancy.

In summary: leiomyomas often grow most in early pregnancy, slow growth later, and commonly regress postpartum. This natural history supports conservative management when possible; operate in pregnancy only for true complications (torsion, severe mass effect, infection, refractory pain). Delivery planning should be individualized based on the residual myoma’s location and likelihood of obstructing labour, with many patients managed by elective cesarean or delayed postpartum myomectomy, as in the presented case.

### [Management] What factors guide the choice between laparoscopic versus open myomectomy during pregnancy, and how do mass size, number, location, and gestational age affect this decision?

Bottom line
- The decision between laparoscopic and open (laparotomic) myomectomy in pregnancy is individualized and driven by operative safety for mother and fetus: size, number, location (relationship to the uterine wall and cavity), gestational age, vascularity and suspected complication (torsion, degeneration, rupture, infection or malignancy) determine which approach is safer and more likely to preserve the pregnancy.

Clinical reasoning and principles
1) Gestational age
- Laparoscopy is most appropriate in early pregnancy (first and early second trimester) when the uterus is relatively small, abdominal access is easier, and pneumoperitoneum and trocar placement can be performed with lower technical difficulty and less risk of uterine injury.
- As gestation advances and the gravid uterus enlarges, laparoscopic working space narrows and risks (uterine trauma, difficulty controlling bleeding, compromised visualization) increase — favouring laparotomy in mid‑to‑late second and third trimesters.

2) Mass size and number
- Small (<~5–8 cm), solitary lesions (particularly pedunculated or subserosal) are good laparoscopic candidates: extraction and haemostasis are technically easier, operative time is shorter, and minimally invasive benefits (less pain, faster recovery) apply.
- Large masses (the case: 18 × 12 × 7 cm cranial mass; 9.5–8–8 cm pelvic mass) or multiple myomas reduce laparoscopic feasibility because of limited space, higher vascularity and greater blood loss risk. Large size also makes safe in‑abdominal morcellation or extraction difficult; these favour open myomectomy.

3) Location and relationship to the uterine cavity
- Pedunculated subserosal myomas are most amenable to laparoscopy, even when symptomatic, because a stalk can often be clamped and divided with controlled haemostasis.
- Intramural or submucosal myomas, or those tightly adherent to posterior uterine wall or isthmus, risk entering or distorting the uterine cavity, causing bleeding and potential pregnancy loss; these are harder to manage laparoscopically and often warrant open surgery (as in the case, where the caudal mass was adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma and was left in situ to avoid obstetric risk).
- Posteriorly located or isthmic lesions that could obstruct the birth canal may require planned cesarean or staged open surgery postpartum.

4) Vascularity, degeneration, torsion, suspected infection or malignancy
- Highly vascular, degenerated, twisted (torsed) or infected lesions that threaten acute complications are indications for prompt surgical intervention. The approach should prioritize rapid, secure haemostasis — often favouring laparotomy for large, vascular, or necrotic masses (the cranial mass in the case was highly vascularised, initially twisted and leaking → laparotomy and excision).
- Suspicion of malignancy (atypical imaging, rising tumour markers) also tends to favour open surgery to allow safe removal and appropriate staging.

5) Technical and institutional factors
- Surgeon experience with pregnancy laparoscopy, availability of fetal monitoring intra‑/post‑op, anaesthetic expertise, and blood‑bank support are essential. When team experience is limited or complex anatomy is expected, open approach is safer.

Perioperative management principles (to preserve pregnancy)
- Document fetal heart rate before, during and after surgery; involve obstetrics/neonatology.
- Minimize operative time and blood loss; have cross‑matched blood available.
- Use non‑contrast MRI and targeted ultrasound for preoperative planning to define mass‑uterus relationships.
- Consider perioperative tocolysis or progesterone as institutional practice and gestational age dictates (case used intraoperative and ongoing progesterone).
- Multidisciplinary informed consent discussing risk of pregnancy loss, preterm delivery and need for cesarean.

Applying these principles to the presented case
- Two large, vascularized, atypical masses at 21+6 weeks with pain, dyspnoea, fever and imaging ambiguity raised risk of complication and need for definitive diagnosis. The cranial mass proved to be a twisted, highly vascular pedunculated leiomyoma—an indication for excision; open laparotomy allowed rapid control of bleeding and safe removal. The caudal lesion was adherent to a posterior intramural‑subserosal myoma; because of its location, size and potential to compromise pregnancy if manipulated, it was left in situ to avoid obstetric risk, and delivery was planned by elective cesarean when fetal maturity reached 37 weeks.

Practical summary
- Prefer laparoscopy when early gestation, small solitary subserosal/pedunculated myoma, low vascularity, experienced team and adequate working space.
- Prefer laparotomy when later gestation, large or multiple masses, intramural/posterior/isthmic location, high vascularity, suspected infection/torsion/malignancy, or when rapid haemostasis and maximal access are required.
- Individualize decision with multidisciplinary input, imaging (US ± non‑contrast MRI), and explicit counselling about maternal and fetal risks.

### [Morning Report] In a pregnancy complicated by a large posterior intramural-subserosal leiomyoma occupying the pelvis, what criteria support planning an elective cesarean delivery, and how does lesion position predict labor obstruction or uterine rupture risk?

Bottom line
- Elective cesarean delivery is indicated when a uterine leiomyoma is large, fixed, and anatomically positioned to obstruct the birth canal or when it meaningfully increases the risk of intrapartum complications (obstructed labour, haemorrhage, or uterine rupture). In practice this decision is based on size, number, location (especially lower-segment/isthmic or posterior torus masses), mobility/adhesion, relationship to the uterine cavity and fetal head, and clinical course—including symptoms and prior uterine surgery.  
- In this case the posterior intramural–subserosal myoma (≈5.8 × 7.1 cm at diagnosis, clinically fixed in the pouch of Douglas, bulging at the posterior fornix) met those criteria and correctly prompted an elective cesarean at 37 weeks because it was predicted to mechanically obstruct fetal head descent and to increase intrapartum risk.

Clinical reasoning and pathophysiology (why these features matter)
- Mechanical obstruction: A lower-pole, posterior intramural or subserosal myoma that occupies the pouch of Douglas or the isthmic/lower uterine segment can physically block engagement and rotation of the fetal head. A fixed mass that bulges into the posterior fornix or reduces pelvic outlet volume is the classic situation leading to obstructed labour. The case demonstrates this: the caudal myoma was adherent, fixed in the lesser pelvis, and judged likely to “represent a huge obstacle for the fetal head progression during the expulsive phase.”  
- Distortion of uterine contractility and lower-segment thinning: Large intramural myomas alter uterine geometry and contraction vectors, which can make labour ineffective and concentrate stress in thinned areas of the uterus. This increases the theoretical risk of uterine rupture, particularly when a myoma occupies or distorts the lower uterine segment.  
- Rupture risk is highest when the myoma (or prior surgery) compromises uterine wall integrity (for example, previous cavity-entering myomectomy, classical cesarean). A subserosal pedunculated lesion by itself is less likely to cause rupture, but a large intramural lesion in the lower segment that impairs descent or causes excessive contractile forces can contribute to rupture risk.

Practical criteria to support planning an elective cesarean
Use a combined clinical and imaging assessment (bimanual exam + ultrasound ± non‑contrast MRI). Consider elective cesarean when one or more of the following apply:
- Size: large myoma (commonly ≥5–8 cm) in or near the lower uterine segment/pelvic inlet that is unlikely to move with uterine growth. (In the report the residual pelvic myoma was ~58×71 mm and clinically fixed.)  
- Location: lower uterine segment, isthmus, posterior torus, or occupying the pouch of Douglas — locations that directly intersect the path of fetal descent. Posterior intramural–subserosal lesions in the isthmic region are particularly likely to obstruct.  
- Mobility/adhesion: fixed or adherent lesion on bimanual exam or imaging (bulging into posterior fornix, adherent to pelvic sidewall) — less likely to displace out of the way during labour. The case had a fixed pelvic mass partially bulging at the posterior fornix.  
- Relationship to uterine cavity/fetal head: imaging shows the mass encroaching on the lower uterine segment or reducing the space available for head engagement; MRI can define distance from cavity and lower segment. The report emphasises MRI to delineate these relationships.  
- Symptomatic course: persistent or worsening symptoms (pain, dyspnoea, infection, bleeding) or complications during pregnancy that portend instability in labour. The patient had pain and mass‑related respiratory compromise prompting earlier surgery and later influenced delivery planning.  
- Prior uterine surgery or other risk factors for rupture: prior classical cesarean or cavity-entering myomectomy increases rupture risk and pushes toward elective repeat cesarean. Even in the absence of prior hysterotomy, a large lower-segment myoma that will force dysfunctional labour may lead to a decision for planned cesarean.  
- Multidisciplinary consensus: obstetrics, maternal–fetal medicine, and gynecologic surgery input weighing maternal and fetal risks; informed patient preference.

How to apply this to timing and technique
- Plan delivery at term once fetal maturity is expected (the team in the report delivered at 37 weeks).  
- Use a surgical approach that minimises additional uterine trauma; they used the same laparotomy scar from the earlier procedure to minimise risk of scar failure.  
- Document fetal heart rate and counselling. The team used close follow-up and chose elective cesarean when predicted obstruction outweighed the risks of vaginal birth.

What the case illustrates
- Imaging (US + non‑contrast MRI) and careful bimanual exam identified a posterior intramural–subserosal myoma fixed in the pelvis that would obstruct the fetal head. Given size (~5.8 × 7.1 cm initially, residual ≈7.5 cm), posterior isthmic location, clinical fixation and prior abdominal intervention, the team appropriately planned an elective cesarean at 37 weeks with an excellent maternal and neonatal outcome.

Takeaway for trainees
- Don’t base delivery mode only on myoma presence; integrate size, exact location (lower segment/isthmus/posterior torus most concerning), mobility, imaging relationship to the cavity and fetal head, symptoms, and prior surgery. When a lesion is large, fixed and anatomically in the path of descent, an elective cesarean is the safest, evidence‑based choice.

### [Morning Report] In the perioperative period, how should iron-deficiency anemia be managed in pregnancy, including thresholds for IV iron versus red blood cell transfusion, and what special considerations apply to ongoing fetal and maternal monitoring?

Direct answer
- Perioperative iron-deficiency anemia in pregnancy is managed pragmatically on two axes: (1) rapid iron repletion when there is time or oral iron has failed, and (2) red blood cell (RBC) transfusion when oxygen delivery is threatened by severe anemia or active/ongoing bleeding. Use intravenous (IV) iron when you need rapid, reliable iron repletion (preoperative, postoperative, or late pregnancy) and reserve RBC transfusion for hemodynamic compromise, symptomatic anemia, or very low Hb with or without ongoing blood loss.

Clinical thresholds and practical rules
- IV iron
  - Indications in pregnancy: documented iron-deficiency (low ferritin, low MCV, low transferrin saturation or typical CBC) with Hb inadequately corrected or when oral therapy is not tolerated or is too slow given timing (eg, planned surgery, short interval to delivery, or severe symptoms).
  - Common numeric triggers used in practice: Hb <10–11 g/dL with ferritin <30 µg/L, or any iron-deficient pregnant patient who requires rapid repletion (eg, perioperative, late third trimester, or before anticipated blood loss). IV iron is preferred if oral therapy is ineffective, not tolerated, or insufficient time remains for adequate erythropoiesis.
  - Dosing: ferric carboxymaltose and iron sucrose are commonly used; ferric carboxymaltose allows single high-dose administrations (eg, 500–1000 mg single dose depending on deficit and product limits). Monitor for hypersensitivity and observe after infusion.
  - Caveat: active, uncontrolled bacterial infection is a relative contraindication to IV iron because iron can enhance microbial growth; treat and control infection before or concurrently when possible. In the case report the patient had a urinary Klebsiella infection that was treated with appropriate antibiotics prior to IV iron administration.

- RBC transfusion
  - Indications: hemodynamic instability, ongoing hemorrhage, or severe symptomatic anemia where immediate increase in oxygen-carrying capacity is necessary.
  - Numeric thresholds: for stable patients without active bleeding a restrictive threshold (Hb 7 g/dL) is generally used outside pregnancy; in pregnancy clinicians commonly use slightly higher individualized thresholds. Practical guidance:
    - Transfuse for Hb ≤6–7 g/dL in most patients.
    - Consider transfusion for Hb 7–8 g/dL if symptomatic (tachycardia, hypotension, ischemic symptoms), if there is ongoing bleeding, or in the perioperative/obstetric setting when anticipating further blood loss.
    - Maintain a pragmatic target post-transfusion of Hb ≥8–10 g/dL depending on maternal symptomatology, gestational age and obstetric plans (eg, imminent delivery or major surgery).
  - In the case presented the patient’s Hb fell to 7.5 g/dL postoperatively with ongoing iron deficiency and the team gave IV iron plus two units of RBCs, achieving Hb 10 g/dL — an approach consistent with correcting a postoperative Hb in the setting of recent surgery, symptomatic anemia and need to optimize maternal oxygen delivery for the remainder of pregnancy.

Maternal and fetal monitoring considerations (perioperative and after treatment)
- Before, during and after any non-obstetric or obstetric surgery document fetal heart activity. For viable fetuses:
  - Continuous fetal heart rate monitoring/CTG is recommended when gestational age and resources permit (commonly applied for ≥24 weeks but practice varies by center). At minimum, document fetal heart rate immediately before and after surgery.
  - Intraoperative fetal monitoring when feasible (intermittent or continuous depending on GA and logistics) is recommended; the case team documented fetal cardiac activity intraoperatively and used serial/transvaginal ultrasound.
- Maternal monitoring:
  - Standard perioperative hemodynamic monitoring (BP, HR, oxygenation, urine output). Maintain maternal oxygenation and perfusion — maternal hypoxia or hypotension directly compromises fetal oxygen delivery.
  - Monitor for transfusion reactions and iron infusion reactions (anaphylaxis is rare but possible). Observe patients for 30–60 minutes after IV iron per institutional protocol.
  - If IV iron is given in the setting of recent/ongoing infection, ensure appropriate antibiotic therapy and clinical improvement before or during iron repletion (as in the case where antibiotics were given according to culture sensitivities).
- Obstetric considerations after correction:
  - Reassess fetal growth and amniotic fluid (anemia and maternal hemodynamic instability can affect fetal growth); the presented case instituted strict obstetrical follow-up every 3 weeks and documented adequate fetal growth.
  - Modify delivery planning according to maternal recovery, residual lesions and likelihood of hemorrhage. If major surgery was recently performed, consider timing of delivery and mode (the team elected cesarean at term because of residual myoma anatomy).
  - If preterm delivery is a possibility, consider antenatal corticosteroids and magnesium for neuroprotection as indicated; document and counsel regarding transfusion/iron therapy implications for breastfeeding if relevant.

Bottom line (clinical pearl)
- In pregnant patients facing surgery, use IV iron to correct iron-deficiency when oral therapy is insufficient or too slow (common trigger: Hb <10–11 g/dL with low ferritin or need for rapid repletion). Reserve RBC transfusion for severe or symptomatic anemia, hemodynamic instability, or ongoing bleeding — individualized thresholds often fall around Hb ≤7 g/dL for stable patients, with liberalization for symptoms or perioperative/obstetric risk. Always coordinate maternal hemodynamic support with fetal monitoring: check and document fetal heart rate before, during (when feasible), and after surgery; treat active infections before or while giving IV iron; and tailor post-treatment surveillance of fetal growth and maternal recovery, as illustrated in the case.
