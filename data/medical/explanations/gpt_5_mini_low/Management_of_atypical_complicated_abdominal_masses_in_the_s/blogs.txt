Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# When an ‘Adnexal Cyst’ Is a Fibroid: Diagnostic Pitfalls in Pregnancy

What makes this case interesting
A woman in her 40s at 21 weeks’ gestation presented with pain and dyspnoea and was found to have two very large, unilocular cystic abdominal masses on ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI. Pre‑op imaging and a mildly raised CA‑125 favoured adnexal pathology (decidualized endometriomas). At laparotomy the cranial lesion was a highly vascularised, pedunculated, atypical cystic leiomyoma that had twisted and started leaking; the caudal cystic mass proved to be contiguous with a posterior intramural‑subserosal myoma and was left in situ. Mom and baby did well after myomectomy and then elective C‑section at term. This case nicely illustrates how large, degenerating or pedunculated fibroids can masquerade as adnexal cysts in pregnancy and why a methodical, multidisciplinary approach matters.

## Why fibroids can look like adnexal cysts in pregnancy
- Pregnancy changes everything: the gravid uterus displaces anatomy, stretches mesentery and adnexal attachments, and alters the usual spatial relationships you expect on transabdominal/transvaginal scanning. A pedunculated uterine myoma can be pulled far from the uterine body and appear “extra‑uterine.”
- Degeneration of leiomyomas (red/haemorrhagic or cystic degeneration) creates complex cystic or mixed cystic‑solid appearance that overlaps with endometriomas or ovarian cystic neoplasms.
- Decidualization of ovarian endometriomas in pregnancy can produce solid, vascularized mural nodules — another mimic.
- Serum tumour markers are unreliable in pregnancy: CA‑125 physiologically peaks in the 1st and 3rd trimesters and can be mildly raised in benign disease (and in this case rose from 36 to 41.8 U/mL), so an isolated modest elevation should not push you straight to oncologic thinking.

## Imaging: strengths and limitations in the pregnant patient
Ultrasound
- Strengths: real‑time, Doppler assessment, dynamic bimanual interrogation possible (with transvaginal exam), excellent first test in pregnancy.
- Pitfalls: very large masses may be hard to fully characterize transvaginally; “stalks” or feeding vessels can be missed when stretched by the enlarging uterus; acoustic windows are altered by body habitus and gravid abdomen (this patient was obese, BMI 33).
- In this case US showed two large unilocular cystic masses with peripheral vascularization — suspicious but not definitive.

MRI (non‑contrast)
- Strengths: multiplanar anatomy, better tissue characterization than US for multiple/large/posterior lesions and for mapping distance from uterine cavity.
- Limitations in pregnancy: no gadolinium (further limits contrast resolution); avoid >1.5 T if possible (though non‑contrast MRI after first trimester is reasonably safe); large uterus and distorted anatomy still make it hard to assign origin when a fat‑sat T1/T2 lesion abuts the uterus.
- Here MRI showed thick walls, inhomogeneous fluid content and focal T1/T2 hyperintense solid components — features that can be seen in both cystic degenerating fibroids and decidualized endometriomas.

Practical point: neither modality is perfect when masses are large and anatomy is distorted. Use both, but integrate with clinical exam and serial imaging.

## The continuing value of the clinical exam — especially bimanual assessment
- A careful bimanual vaginal exam can detect a fixed pelvic mass, estimate mobility, and help judge whether a pelvic lesion is contiguous with the uterus. In the case presented, bimanual exam found a fixed, rounded hard mass in the pouch of Douglas that bulged into the posterior fornix — a clue to uterine origin for at least one lesion.
- Combine bimanual findings with abdominal palpation: size, tenderness, and whether upper abdominal mass moves with the uterus can be helpful.
- Physical exam is cheap, immediate, and particularly useful when imaging is equivocal.

## Interpreting CA‑125 and other lab data in pregnancy
- CA‑125 may be elevated for many benign reasons in pregnancy (decidual reaction, fibroids, endometriosis) and also with peritoneal irritation from torsion/degeneration/infection.
- In this patient the mild increase plus fever and urine culture positive for Klebsiella raised concern but was not diagnostic.
- Serial trend and correlation with imaging and clinical status is more informative than one isolated value.

## When to operate in pregnancy — weighing risks and benefits
- Conservative management is first‑line for asymptomatic leiomyomas in pregnancy because myomectomy carries risks: haemorrhage, placental compromise, preterm labour, pregnancy loss.
- Indications to strongly consider surgery in pregnancy:
  - Complications: torsion of a pedunculated myoma, ongoing severe pain not controlled by conservative therapy, suspected infection or rupture of a mass, mass effect causing respiratory compromise (this patient had dyspnoea due to restrictive physiology), or clear concern for malignancy.
  - Diagnostic uncertainty when malignancy cannot be reasonably excluded and the lesion is amenable to resection.
- Operative approach should be tailored:
  - Laparoscopy (Hasson open entry) can be considered earlier in pregnancy for smaller, well‑situated lesions, but large, numerous, posterior or intramural masses often require laparotomy.
  - Keep the operation as short and blood‑sparing as possible; multidisciplinary planning with obstetrics, gynecologic surgery, anaesthesia and neonatology is essential.
  - Monitor fetal heart rate before, during and after surgery; informed consent must include obstetric risks.
- In this case the team removed the pedunculated, twisted cranial myoma and left the caudal adherent lesion in place to reduce obstetric risk — a pragmatic, uterus‑sparing compromise.

## Intraoperative and postoperative considerations
- Be prepared for bleeding: crossmatch blood, have uterotonics and haemostatic strategies ready.
- Prophylactic perioperative progesterone was used in this case (IM progesterone started intraop and continued) and fetal monitoring was performed perioperatively.
- Antibiotic prophylaxis and targeted therapy for culture results (here Cefazolin and clarithromycin per urine culture) are important when infection is suspected.
- Postoperative anemia may require IV iron and transfusion — the patient needed both.
- Plan delivery mode based on residual myomas: a large posterior isthmic myoma may block labor and was the rationale for elective Caesarean at 37 weeks in this case.

## Putting it together at the bedside: a practical differential framework
When you encounter a large, cystic pelvic/abdominal mass in pregnancy, systematically consider:
- Is the lesion contiguous with the uterus (myoma) or the adnexa (ovary)? Use bimanual exam, transvaginal when possible, and multiplanar MRI to help.
- Imaging characteristics:
  - Uniform, low‑T2 signal with “chocolate” high T1 signal suggests endometrioma; mural nodules with vascularity may be decidualized.
  - Cystic degeneration of fibroid often shows heterogenous signal, thick walls, possible haemorrhage or necrosis; pedunculated myomas may have a stalk with feeding vessel if visible.
- Clinical clues: acute localized pain (torsion/degeneration), fever or leukocytosis (infection/degeneration), respiratory compromise from mass effect.
- Lab clues: CA‑125 is supportive but not diagnostic in pregnancy; interpret in context and consider serial values.

## Key takeaways and clinical pearls
- Large, cystic or degenerating leiomyomas can closely mimic adnexal cysts (including decidualised endometriomas) on both ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI in pregnancy; do not rely on a single modality.
- Bimanual vaginal exam remains valuable — it can provide clues about fixation and uterine continuity that imaging may miss when anatomy is distorted.
- CA‑125 is unreliable in pregnancy; mild elevations are nonspecific and must be interpreted with clinical correlation and trends.
- Indications for surgical intervention during pregnancy include torsion, rupture, infection, intractable pain, respiratory compromise from mass effect, and strong concern for malignancy; keep surgery as targeted and brief as possible.
- When feasible, remove only the lesion that poses the immediate risk and consider leaving adherent intramural masses in situ to minimise obstetric risk.
- Multidisciplinary planning, perioperative fetal monitoring, and clear patient counselling are essential.

Clinical pearls (bullet list)
- Think “fibroid” even when a large cystic mass seems adnexal in a pregnant patient — anatomy can be misleading.  
- Use ultrasound + non‑contrast MRI + bimanual exam together; none is definitive alone when masses are large.  
- CA‑125: interpret trends, not single modest elevations in pregnancy.  
- Strong indications for myomectomy in pregnancy are torsion, rupture, infection, severe mass effect or diagnostic uncertainty for malignancy.  
- If operating, favour the shortest, safest operation that solves the acute problem (remove the offending mass, not every lesion).  
- Plan delivery mode based on residual myoma size/location and obstetric risk; a posterior isthmic myoma may mandate Cesarean.

This case is a nice reminder: keep a broad differential, integrate hands‑on examination with imaging, and tailor management to the mother‑fetus dyad rather than letting a single test dictate an aggressive course.



# Imaging Strategy for Pelvic Masses in Pregnancy: Ultrasound, Doppler, and Non‑Contrast MRI

What makes this case interesting: a woman in her 40s at 21 weeks presented with dyspnoea and pain and was found to have two very large, cystic-appearing abdominal masses. Ultrasound and Doppler suggested adnexal cysts (decidualised endometriomas were high on the list), CA‑125 was mildly raised, and non‑contrast MRI showed thick-walled, inhomogeneous cysts with hypertrophied vessels — findings that could be either decidualised adnexal disease or atypical, cystic-degenerated or pedunculated leiomyomas. At laparotomy the upper lesion turned out to be a huge pedunculated, torsed and leaking uterine leiomyoma — a diagnosis that was difficult to make preoperatively because of gravid anatomy and “steric bulk.” This case nicely illustrates the strengths and limits of ultrasound/Doppler and when non‑contrast MRI changes management in pregnancy.

Below I summarise a pragmatic imaging algorithm for symptomatic or atypical pelvic masses in the second trimester, explain what US + Doppler can and cannot resolve, when to escalate to MRI, and how imaging features influence the decision for surgery versus expectant management.

## First step: clinical triage and initial ultrasound
Always start with a focused clinical assessment: maternal vitals, fetal heart rate, pain pattern, signs of peritonitis, and effect on respiration or bladder/obstructive symptoms. Imaging choice depends on acuity.

Transabdominal + transvaginal ultrasound is the gateway test in pregnancy:
- Strengths: bedside, safe, real‑time, can evaluate mass morphology (unilocular vs multilocular), wall thickness, internal echo pattern, papillary projections, vascularity with colour/Power Doppler, relationship to the uterus and adnexa (if visualised), and free fluid.
- Limitations: in the second and third trimester a large gravid uterus often obscures anatomy; pedicles or small stalks are easily missed; depth/obesity can limit resolution; operator dependence.

In the case described:
- US showed two large, unilocular cystic masses with regular margins and peripheral vascularisation; the caudal lesion appeared adherent to a posterior myoma with cystic degeneration. But the upper mass’s origin was unclear because no stalk or feeding vessel could be identified.

Clinical pearl: when a mass fills the abdomen or lies in an atypical location relative to the gravid uterus, don’t assume adnexal origin from location alone — try to trace continuity with the uterine wall on multiple planes and with gentle patient repositioning.

## Role of colour/Power Doppler
Doppler adds useful clues:
- Hypertrophied peripheral vessels, low‑resistance flow, or a feeding vessel arising from the uterine circulation suggests a uterine source (pedunculated fibroid) rather than a pure ovarian cyst.
- Central papillary vascularity or solid mural nodules with internal flow raise concern for neoplasm (decidualised endometriomas can show increased peripheral/capsular flow).
- Lack of flow does not exclude torsion — early torsion can preserve some flow or have intermittent compromise.

In the presented case Doppler showed peripheral vascularisation and hypertrophied vessels — a finding consistent with both decidualised endometrioma and a vascularised pedunculated myoma. Doppler findings must be interpreted alongside anatomical relationships.

Clinical pearl: document the presumed vascular supply (ovarian vs uterine vessels) if possible — this can be a key discriminator.

## When to escalate to non‑contrast MRI
Indications to perform MRI in pregnancy for pelvic masses:
- Ultrasound is inconclusive regarding organ of origin (uterine vs adnexal), or tissue planes are unclear.
- Suspicion for malignancy cannot be reasonably excluded on ultrasound + labs.
- Patient is symptomatic (pain, obstructive symptoms, respiratory compromise) and precise anatomy would change management.
- Multiple, large or posterior/intramural masses where ultrasound underestimates distance from the endometrial cavity or placental location.

MRI protocol and safety considerations:
- Use a non‑contrast MRI; avoid gadolinium unless absolutely essential (gadolinium crosses the placenta and is associated with small but real fetal risks).
- Prefer 1.5‑T when possible; 3‑T can be used but many centres avoid it in pregnancy unless benefits clearly outweigh theoretical risks.
- Fast sequences (T2, T1 with and without fat saturation) are helpful. Avoid prolonged scans; keep maternal comfort in mind.
- No absolute contraindication to MRI after the first trimester — diagnostic MRI without contrast is considered reasonably safe.

In the case: non‑contrast MRI demonstrated thick‑walled cysts (1.4 cm wall), inhomogeneous T1/T2 signal and focal hyperintense solid components, and unclear cleavage of the caudal mass from the uterus — findings that raised concern and supported proceeding to surgical exploration.

Clinical pearl: MRI is best used to clarify organ of origin and tissue composition when ultrasound cannot — it will frequently change management when masses are large or posterior.

## Interpreting key imaging patterns
Some practical interpretations frequently encountered in pregnancy:

- Thick‑walled cyst with inhomogeneous T1/T2 signal, peripheral vascularity:
  - Differential: decidualised endometrioma, cystic-degenerated leiomyoma, haemorrhagic cyst, abscess, or neoplasm.
  - Clues favouring endometrioma: “shading” on T2 (progressive signal drop on T2), typical location near adnexa, and history or signs of endometriosis.
  - Clues favouring myoma: continuity with myometrium on multiple planes, feeding branch from the uterine artery, stalk or pedicle on dynamic scanning, and location adjacent to the uterine wall.

- Hypertrophied vessels around a mass:
  - Think of highly vascular pedunculated myoma, decidualised tissue, or neoplasm. Use Doppler to try to identify uterine vs ovarian arterial origin.

- Loss of clear cleavage planes between mass and uterus on MRI:
  - Suggests myometrial origin or strong adherence; increases the likelihood that the mass is uterine (intramural/subserosal) rather than an ovarian cyst.

- Small solid mural nodules or papillary projections with internal flow:
  - Raises concern for neoplasm; if present in pregnancy, consider MRI and multidisciplinary discussion.

Clinical pearl: no single sign is diagnostic. Combine anatomy, Doppler vascular pattern, CA‑125 trend (not absolute in pregnancy), and clinical course.

## How imaging informs management: operative vs expectant
Key drivers of management:
- Maternal symptoms and complications: severe pain, torsion, rupture, infection, respiratory compromise from mass effect — favour operative management.
- Imaging suggesting a pedunculated, torsed, leaking or necrotic lesion — increased operative urgency.
- Suspected malignancy after imaging and tumour marker trend — multidisciplinary discussion and lower threshold for surgery.
- If imaging suggests intramural myoma with minimal symptoms, expectant management is reasonable; plan delivery mode based on whether the mass will obstruct labor.

In the reported case the combination of intractable pain, dyspnoea from mass effect, MRI features suggestive of thick walls and vascularity, and a small CA‑125 rise contributed to the decision for laparotomy. Intraoperatively the cranial mass was a torsed, vascular pedunculated leiomyoma — a lesion at real risk of further complications — and was removed. The caudal myoma was left in situ to avoid obstetric risk given its fixed position and the aim to preserve the pregnancy.

Clinical pearl: imaging that suggests a pedicle/stalk and vascular torsion changes management — a torsed pedunculated myoma behaves like a surgical abdomen.

## Practical algorithm (second trimester, symptomatic or atypical pelvic mass)
1. Immediate bedside: maternal vitals, fetal monitoring, brief gen/abdominal exam.
2. First-line imaging: transabdominal + transvaginal US with colour/Power Doppler; document:
   - Size, morphology, wall thickness, internal echoes, papillary nodules
   - Relationship to uterus and ovaries (look for continuity)
   - Vascular supply (uterine vs ovarian)
   - Free fluid
3. Labs: CBC, CRP, targeted tumour markers (CA‑125 interpreted cautiously in pregnancy; serial values more useful than single reading).
4. If US is diagnostic and patient stable with no complications → expectant management + serial US.
5. If US is inconclusive, mass is large, posterior, multiple, or symptoms/markers concerning → non‑contrast MRI (1.5‑T preferred).
6. Multidisciplinary review (OB/Gyn, radiology, maternal–fetal medicine, surgery) to decide expectant vs surgical approach.
7. If surgery is chosen: time procedure to minimise fetal risk (after viability when possible), plan for fetal monitoring perioperatively, consider progesterone prophylaxis per local protocols, and reserve the least invasive approach that achieves goals (laparoscopy for selected lesions; laparotomy for large, multiple or inaccessible masses).

## Safety and documentation points
- Avoid gadolinium unless absolutely necessary; document informed consent if considered.
- Prefer 1.5‑T MRI; use fast protocols and limit scan time.
- During surgery in pregnancy: monitor and document fetal heart rate before/during/after, keep procedures as short as possible, involve obstetrics in planning, and discuss prophylactic measures (e.g., progesterone) according to local practice.
- CA‑125 is unreliable as a standalone discriminator in pregnancy — use trends and the whole clinical picture.

## Take‑home messages
- Ultrasound + colour/Power Doppler is the first-line and often sufficient test, but gravid anatomy and large masses frequently limit its ability to determine organ of origin.
- Doppler patterns (uterine vs ovarian vascular supply, hypertrophied peripheral vessels) provide valuable discriminators — always try to document the feeding vessel.
- Escalate to non‑contrast MRI when ultrasound is inconclusive, the mass is large or posterior, or when MRI findings would change management.
- Thick-walled, inhomogeneous cysts with peripheral vascularity can represent decidualised endometrioma or cystic-degenerated/pedunculated leiomyoma — look for continuity with the myometrium and feeding vessels to help distinguish them.
- Imaging findings should be integrated with symptoms, temporal evolution, and multidisciplinary input — decide surgery only when maternal/fetal risks from the mass outweigh those of intervention.

This case is a reminder that large, atypical pelvic masses in pregnancy are diagnostically challenging and that a stepwise imaging approach — US/Doppler first, then targeted non‑contrast MRI when needed — is practical, safe, and often decisive for planning management.



# CA‑125 in Pregnancy: Red Herring or Helpful Trend?

This case caught my eye because a woman in her 40s at 21 weeks had two very large cystic-appearing abdominal masses, a modestly raised CA‑125 (41.8 U/mL), and imaging that could have pointed toward decidualised endometriomas — yet at surgery the “upper adnexal” lesion was actually a pedunculated, cystic, vascularised uterine leiomyoma (twisted and leaking). The clinical team faced the classic dilemma: does a single mildly elevated CA‑125 in pregnancy push you toward aggressive management for presumed ovarian disease — or can it be used more sensibly as one piece of the puzzle?

Use this post as a pragmatic clinical guide: what CA‑125 means in pregnancy, its pitfalls, and how to integrate it with the history, exam and imaging so you don’t overcall cancer and subject a pregnant patient to unnecessary intervention.

## Why this case matters
- The CA‑125 was only modestly above the usual adult cutoff (35 U/mL), but it nudged the differential toward adnexal/endometriotic disease in a pregnant patient — a setting in which CA‑125 is unreliable.
- Imaging (US and non‑contrast MRI) was ambiguous because large uterine masses and pregnancy anatomy distort relationships. The team opted for surgery because of symptoms (pain, dyspnoea), size, vascularity and concern for complications — not only the marker.
- Final pathology: benign leiomyomas with ischaemic necrosis near a twisted pedicle. CA‑125 was a red herring, not a diagnosis.

If you take only one lesson from this case: a single, modest CA‑125 elevation in pregnancy should not, by itself, trigger definitive oncologic surgery. Context matters.

## What CA‑125 measures and why pregnancy complicates interpretation
- CA‑125 is a glycoprotein expressed by coelomic and Müllerian epithelium; it is widely used as a tumour marker for epithelial ovarian cancer in nonpregnant adults.
- But CA‑125 is non‑specific. It rises with benign processes that irritate peritoneum or endometrium: endometriosis, adenomyosis, pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, pleuroperitoneal inflammation, and uterine fibroids.
- Pregnancy physiology changes CA‑125:
  - Median values are higher in the first trimester (reported medians ~23 U/mL) and again near term (third trimester median ~21 U/mL) compared with the second trimester (~14 U/mL).
  - Absolute values overlap substantially with nonpregnant ranges; single measurements have poor specificity for malignancy.

Bottom line: pregnancy shifts baseline CA‑125 and increases false positives for pathology.

## How to interpret CA‑125 in a pregnant patient
Think trend + context, not a single cutoff.

1. Timing matters
   - First and third trimesters commonly show higher values. A modest elevation in the second trimester is more unusual, but still not diagnostic.
   - In the case above CA‑125 rose from 36 U/mL to 41.8 U/mL over 2 weeks in the second trimester — a small upward trend, but far from establishing malignancy.

2. Magnitude matters
   - Very high CA‑125 (multiple hundreds to thousands) is more concerning, but pregnancy can rarely produce substantial elevations too (and benign causes can cause marked rises). There is no pregnancy‑validated threshold that reliably distinguishes benign from malignant disease.
   - Modest elevations (e.g., 35–60 U/mL) are common in benign conditions and pregnancy.

3. Clinical context is king
   - Symptoms: pain, fever, signs of infection, rapid growth, torsion or leakage — these are reasons to act irrespective of CA‑125.
   - Imaging: ultrasound (with Doppler) and non‑contrast MRI are the cornerstones. Identify origin (ovarian vs uterine), pedicle/stalk, internal solid components, septations, papillary excrescences, vascular flow and adhesions.
   - Lab findings: inflammatory markers, infection, hemoglobin/anaemia, and other tumour markers can help but are rarely definitive.
   - Multidisciplinary input: obstetrics, gynecologic oncology, radiology and surgery should weigh in.

In the presented case, the decision to operate was driven by large symptomatic masses, mass effect (dyspnoea), vascularity and the risk of torsion/rupture — not CA‑125 alone.

## Practical approach to a pregnant patient with adnexal‑appearing masses and elevated CA‑125

- Start with a careful clinical exam and targeted ultrasound (transvaginal ± transabdominal) using Doppler. Try to define whether the mass is uterine (subserosal/pedunculated fibroid) versus adnexal.
- Use non‑contrast MRI if ultrasound cannot establish origin or if posterior structures are poorly seen. Avoid gadolinium unless absolutely essential.
- Interpret CA‑125 as supportive data only:
  - A single, modest elevation in the second trimester is not diagnostic of ovarian malignancy.
  - Serial CA‑125 measurements may help show a trend, but changes over short intervals can still be nonspecific.
- Consider other benign causes of raised CA‑125 (endometriosis, leiomyoma degeneration, infection/inflammation, pleural/peritoneal irritation).
- Let symptoms and complication risk drive management:
  - Asymptomatic, likely benign masses → conservative management and surveillance.
  - Symptomatic masses with complications (torsion, rupture, infection), rapid growth causing mass effect, or strong imaging features of malignancy → consider intervention (timing and approach individualized).
- If surgery is chosen, minimize fetal risk:
  - Coordinate fetal heart monitoring pre/intra/post‑op, use the least invasive approach feasible (laparotomy vs laparoscopy depending on size/location/gestational age), limit operative time and blood loss, and have multidisciplinary anesthesia and obstetric backup.
- Document the decision‑making clearly, and obtain informed consent about the diagnostic uncertainty and risks.

## Clinical pearls from the case
- A pedunculated uterine leiomyoma can masquerade as an adnexal cyst on both US and non‑contrast MRI, particularly when large and cystic.
- CA‑125 rises modestly in pregnancy and with benign pelvic disease; it should not be used in isolation to diagnose ovarian malignancy in pregnant patients.
- Trends (serial CA‑125) add value over a single value but still must be interpreted against symptoms and imaging.
- Indications for surgery in pregnancy should emphasize clinical instability, complication risk (torsion, rupture, infection), or compelling imaging features — not a solitary mildly elevated tumour marker.
- When surgery is performed, consider limiting the procedure to what’s necessary (e.g., remove the symptomatic/twisted pedunculated myoma and leave a stable intramural myoma) to preserve the pregnancy.

## Take‑home messages
- CA‑125 is frequently elevated in pregnancy and with benign pelvic disease; a modest elevation is often a red herring.
- Interpret CA‑125 in pregnancy only as part of a larger clinical and imaging picture — prioritize symptoms, mass effect and imaging findings.
- Serial CA‑125 may be more informative than a single value but still has limited specificity.
- Use ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI to define origin and features; avoid overcalling malignancy and unnecessary surgery based on CA‑125 alone.
- Operate in pregnancy only for maternal/fetal indications (complications, severe symptoms, high suspicion for malignancy on imaging) and tailor the procedure to minimize obstetric risk.

This case is a tidy reminder: tumour markers can steer thinking, but they must never override clinical judgement and multimodal assessment — especially in pregnancy.



# To Cut or Not to Cut: Second-Trimester Myomectomy for Symptomatic Masses

What makes this case interesting
A woman in her 40s at 21 weeks’ gestation presented with severe abdominal pain and dyspnea and was found to have two very large cystic-appearing abdominal masses. Imaging and tumour markers were equivocal — the masses looked like deci­dualised endometriomas on ultrasound and MRI, CA‑125 was mildly elevated, and the caudal lesion seemed contiguous with a posterior leiomyoma. At laparotomy the “upper ovarian cyst” turned out to be a hugely vascular, pedunculated, torsed uterine leiomyoma that was leaking and likely infected. The team removed only that culprit lesion, left the adherent pelvic myoma in situ, gave perioperative progesterone and antibiotics, and followed the pregnancy to term with an elective cesarean. Maternal and neonatal outcomes were excellent.

This is a useful real-world vignette of decision-making under uncertainty in pregnancy: balancing the risks of operative intervention (bleeding, pregnancy loss, preterm labour, uterine rupture) versus the immediate threats posed by mass effect, torsion/necrosis/infection, and an uncertain oncologic picture.

Clinical approach — how this case was worked up
- Clinical: severe localized pain, palpable upper abdominal mass, pelvic fixed mass on bimanual exam, dyspnea from mass effect. Low‑grade fever and positive urine culture (Klebsiella).
- Labs: iron‑deficiency anemia (Hb 9.9 g/dL → post-op nadir 7.5 g/dL), mild CRP/neutrophilia consistent with inflammation; CA‑125 mildly elevated (41.8 U/mL) and rising.
- Imaging: transabdominal/transvaginal ultrasound showed two large, unilocular cystic masses with peripheral vascularity. Non‑contrast MRI (1.5 T) showed thick‑walled cystic lesions with solid components; caudal lesion appeared contiguous with posterior intramural‑subserosal myoma.
- Working differential: decidualised endometriomas vs cystic‑degenerating leiomyomas; imaging could not conclusively exclude neoplasm.
- Decision triggers for surgery: worsening pain and dyspnea from compressive effect, risk of torsion/rupture/infection, imaging suggesting vascularised and possibly necrotic lesion, inability to obtain a conclusive diagnosis that could safely be observed while the uterus grows.

Why operate in the second trimester?
- The second trimester (classically 14–28 weeks) is the window in pregnancy where, if surgery is required, maternal/fetal risks are generally lower than in the first (organogenesis) or late third trimesters (preterm labour). In this case symptoms and signs of complication (torsion/leak/infection, progressive respiratory compromise) prompted intervention at 21+ weeks.
- Indications for myomectomy in pregnancy remain limited, but generally accepted indications include torsion of a pedunculated fibroid, intractable pain not responsive to conservative therapy, suspicion of malignancy that cannot be excluded, rapid growth with compression, or complications such as infection or haemorrhage.

Operative decision-making: targeted versus liberal surgery
- Key philosophy from this case: treat the culprit lesion only. The team removed the pedunculated, highly vascular twisted myoma (the immediate threat), and left the caudal intramural‑subserosal myoma in situ because it was fixed, likely to complicate surgery more, and not causing irretrievable harm if observed.
- Rationale: shorter operative time, less blood loss, fewer uterine manipulations → lower risk of miscarriage/preterm labour and uterine rupture later on.

Laparotomy versus laparoscopy — practical considerations
- Laparoscopy can be used for selected pregnant patients (usually first/early second trimester, smaller lesions, favorable anatomy). Benefits: less postoperative pain, shorter hospital stay.
- Reasons this case favoured laparotomy:
  - Large size of lesions (upper mass 18 × 12 × 7 cm; lower ~9.5 cm + 5.8 × 7.1 cm leiomyoma),
  - Uncertain anatomy and high vascularity,
  - Need for rapid, controlled access in case of hemorrhage,
  - Gestational uterine size and risk of uterine injury with trocars or pneumoperitoneum.
- If laparoscopy is considered: use open (Hasson) entry, minimize pneumoperitoneum pressures, be experienced in pregnant laparoscopy, have low threshold to convert.

Techniques to minimize obstetric risk — pearls from the case and the literature
- Preoperative:
  - Multidisciplinary planning: obstetrics, gynecology, anesthesia, neonatology, blood bank.
  - Detailed ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI to map lesion(s) and relation to uterine cavity/placenta.
  - Informed consent covering risks to pregnancy, bleeding, transfusion, possible hysterectomy.
  - Optimize hemoglobin and correct iron deficiency where possible pre-op.
- Intraoperative:
  - Continuous fetal monitoring when feasible; at minimum document fetal heart rate before, during and after surgery.
  - Keep operative time as short as safely possible.
  - Targeted myomectomy: remove only the lesion causing acute risk.
  - Use meticulous hemostatic technique: double clamps on a pedicle, prompt control of bleeding, judicious use of uterotonics if indicated postpartum.
  - Careful layered uterine closure with absorbable sutures to minimize future scar weakness.
  - Avoid routine irrigation with foreign agents; collect peritoneal fluid for culture/cytology if malignancy is a concern.
  - Consider intraoperative progesterone support (in this case, 100 mg IM started intraop and continued) — teams sometimes use progestin support though definitive evidence is limited; document rationale.
  - Administer perioperative antibiotics guided by cultures (this patient had Klebsiella UTI and received cefazolin plus clarithromycin per sensitivity).
- Postoperative:
  - Monitor for preterm contractions, bleeding, and fetal wellbeing.
  - Early iron repletion and transfusion if needed (this patient required IV iron and 2 units PRBC with recovery).
  - Close obstetric follow‑up (every 3 weeks in this case), and plan delivery according to anatomy and risk (elective cesarean at term when residual myoma would obstruct labor).
  - Pathology confirmation to exclude malignancy — here pathology confirmed benign leiomyoma with ischemic necrosis.

The CA‑125 trap — interpret with caution in pregnancy
- CA‑125 can be elevated physiologically in pregnancy (peaks in first and third trimesters) and in benign conditions (endometriosis, PID, fibroids). A mildly elevated CA‑125 alone should not drive radical decisions; trends and clinical context matter. In this case a rising CA‑125 added weight to the decision to operate but was not definitive.

When to avoid surgery
- Stable, asymptomatic fibroids or masses without signs of complication — conservative management with observation is preferred.
- If imaging and clinical picture strongly favour benign, stable leiomyomas and symptoms are manageable, defer surgery until postpartum.

Take-home messages
- Consider surgery in pregnancy for uterine masses only when there is torsion of a pedunculated fibroid, intractable symptoms unresponsive to conservative care, confirmed or highly suspected infection/necrosis, or oncologic concern that cannot be safely observed.
- Aim to limit the procedure to the culprit lesion to minimize operative time, blood loss and uterine trauma.
- Use ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI (≤1.5 T) to clarify anatomy; interpret tumour markers (e.g., CA‑125) cautiously in pregnancy.
- Plan operations with a multidisciplinary team, document fetal heart rate before/during/after procedure, and have blood products and neonatology support available.
- Choose laparoscopy for selected small, accessible lesions in experienced hands; prefer laparotomy for large, vascular, multiple or posterior lesions in mid‑pregnancy.
- Meticulous hemostasis and uterine closure, perioperative infection control, and close obstetric follow‑up are essential for good maternal and neonatal outcomes.

This case shows that “to cut or not to cut” in pregnancy is not binary — it’s an individualized risk–benefit decision. Removing the immediate threat while preserving the pregnancy, using targeted surgery and multidisciplinary planning, can lead to excellent outcomes.



## Operating with a Passenger: Intraoperative Fetal Monitoring, Anesthesia, and Tocolysis

What makes this case worth your attention? A nulliparous woman in her 40s at 21+ weeks gestation came in with two massive, confusing abdominal cystic lesions causing pain and dyspnoea. Imaging and tumour markers were indeterminate. The team proceeded to laparotomy, expecting adnexal masses, and found a highly vascular, twisted pedunculated leiomyoma — they removed the cranial lesion and left the pelvic one in situ. Fetal cardiac activity was preserved and the pregnancy continued to term with an elective caesarean and a healthy neonate. This case is a neat capsule of the dilemmas we face when a pregnant patient needs non-obstetric abdominal surgery: how do we monitor the fetus, choose anesthesia and perioperative meds, manage maternal physiology when there is mass effect, and coordinate a multidisciplinary plan that treats the mother while preserving the pregnancy?

Below I’ll pull practical lessons from this report and the wider clinical context so you can operationalize safe surgery in the second trimester.

## The basic framing: maternal first, fetus supported
Surgery during pregnancy is often unavoidable. The primary principle is that optimal maternal care maximizes fetal outcome. That means treating infections, suspected ischemia or torsion, or masses that threaten respiratory function or future uterine expansion. But the choices we make — timing, approach (laparoscopy vs laparotomy), anesthetic agents, monitoring, and perioperative drugs — must balance maternal benefits and fetal risks.

This patient had worsening pain, dyspnoea from diaphragmatic compromise, a vascular mass with torsion/leak on arrival, and a mild urinary infection with Klebsiella. Those indications justified operative management despite pregnancy.

## Fetal cardiac monitoring: what, when, how often
Key concept: document fetal heart activity before, during (intermittently), and immediately after surgery. That’s what the team did here and is widely recommended.

- Before surgery: confirm fetal viability with documented heart rate. This establishes baseline and helps informed consent counseling.
- Intraoperative: continuous electronic fetal monitoring (EFM) is ideal for viable fetuses typically ≥24 weeks. In mid-second trimester (around 21–22 weeks), continuous monitoring may be technically limited and may not change management, but intermittent auscultation or ultrasound documentation of heart activity at intervals is reasonable and pragmatic. In this case, serial and intermittent transvaginal ultrasound checks were used.
- After surgery: immediate confirmation of fetal heart activity and ongoing obstetric follow-up (in this case every 3 weeks) to detect evolving problems.

Practical points:
- If gestational age is previable (<24 weeks) continuous EFM is generally not feasible or actionable; document heartbeat by Doppler/US pre- and post-op and intermittently during the case when feasible.
- For viable fetuses, if significant intraoperative concerns arise (maternal instability, uterine irritability, fetal distress) involve obstetrics immediately; have tocolytics and neonatal team available if preterm delivery becomes necessary.

## Positioning and ventilation when mass effect threatens respiration
This patient had dyspnoea from large abdominal masses and gravid uterus. Intra- and perioperative positioning matters.

- Semi-recumbent or semi-orthostatic positioning can offload diaphragmatic pressure and help ventilation; the team used a semi-orthostatic position preop with symptomatic benefit.
- During anesthesia, head-up tilt (reverse Trendelenburg) can improve respiratory mechanics when large upper abdominal masses compress the diaphragm — but balance this against uterine perfusion. A modest head-up tilt is typically well tolerated.
- Avoid prolonged supine hypotension: left uterine displacement is critical after mid-pregnancy to avoid aortocaval compression. In second trimester, a left lateral tilt (15°) or manual displacement is recommended when supine.
- Be prepared for difficult ventilation due to decreased pulmonary compliance; preoxygenate thoroughly and plan for aspiration prophylaxis.

## Anesthesia choices and fetal safety
General principles: choose agents and techniques that maintain maternal oxygenation, hemodynamic stability and uteroplacental perfusion. No single anesthetic agent is absolutely contraindicated in pregnancy; when maternal physiology is supported, fetal exposure is acceptable.

- General anesthesia: widely used for non-obstetric laparotomy in pregnancy; rapid-sequence induction and endotracheal intubation are standard because of aspiration risk.
- Regional anesthesia: may be used for some procedures but is limited for major laparotomy.
- Maintain normotension, avoid prolonged maternal hypoxia, and minimize uterine irritability (avoid uterine stimulants unless required).
- Document fetal heart rate before induction and after emergence. If fetal monitoring is available intraoperatively (viable fetus), use it.

The team provided intraoperative progesterone and continuous fetal checks. That’s reasonable as an adjunct though evidence for routine prophylactic tocolysis is mixed (see below).

## Perioperative tocolysis and progesterone — evidence and controversy
Two related but distinct interventions often considered: tocolytics (acute uterine relaxants to stop contractions) and progesterone (used for preterm birth prevention).

- Tocolytics: routine prophylactic tocolysis for non-obstetric surgery is not supported by high-level evidence. Use should be individualized — reserved for cases with sustained uterine contractions or in threat of preterm labour. Common agents: β2-agonists (terbutaline), calcium-channel blockers (nifedipine), or magnesium sulfate depending on local protocols and contraindications. Each has maternal side effects and fetal considerations.
- Progesterone: the team administered intramuscular progesterone intraoperatively and continued post-op. Evidence for routine prophylactic progesterone to prevent miscarriage or preterm labour after abdominal surgery in the second trimester is limited and heterogeneous. Progesterone is widely used in obstetrics for women with a history of preterm birth or short cervix, but its routine use solely as prophylaxis for surgery is not established by robust trials.
- Practical approach: discuss with obstetrics. Consider:
  - If signs of threatened preterm labour or cervical change → use tocolytics per obstetric guidance.
  - If patient has other indications for progesterone (prior preterm birth, short cervix), continue/consider it.
  - If no obstetric risk factors and no contractions, routine pharmacologic tocolysis or progesterone has unclear benefit — but many centers use them empirically with good outcomes. Document the rationale and obtain informed consent.

In this case, prophylactic progesterone was given intraoperatively and continued — the pregnancy progressed to term without preterm labour, but we can’t know whether progesterone changed the outcome.

## Antibiotic selection when there is an intercurrent infection
This patient had a Klebsiella pneumoniae urinary infection on pre-op cultures. Key points:

- Treat maternal infection aggressively — untreated maternal sepsis is a greater fetal threat than appropriate antibiotics.
- Choose pregnancy-safe antibiotics guided by culture and sensitivity. In this case, Klebsiella was highly sensitive to cephalosporins and clarithromycin. The team used perioperative cefazolin (standard surgical prophylaxis) and post-op cefazolin plus clarithromycin per antibiogram — a sensible targeted approach.
- Beta-lactams (penicillins, cephalosporins) are generally safe in pregnancy. Macrolides are used when indicated, though azithromycin is often preferred over clarithromycin because of better safety data in pregnancy; however, clarithromycin can be used if guided by susceptibility and obstetric review.
- Coordinate infectious disease or microbiology input when organisms are resistant or when using agents with fetal safety concerns.

## Surgical approach: laparoscopy vs laparotomy — pragmatism over dogma
Surgery in pregnancy can be done laparoscopically or via open approach. Decision factors: gestational age, size and location of masses, hemodynamic stability, surgical expertise.

- Laparoscopy advantages: less pain, shorter LOS, lower wound morbidity. It is feasible in pregnancy, especially early and mid-gestation, with open (Hasson) entry and uterine protection.
- Laparotomy favored when masses are large, numerous, intramural or with uncertain origin, or when rapid access is needed — as in this case of huge vascularized masses and suspected torsion. Laparotomy can shorten operative time when exposure is difficult and reduces risk of uterine injury when the uterus is large or anatomy distorted.
- The team chose a longitudinal xipho-pubic incision and removed only the pedunculated lesion — a conservative, pregnancy-sparing strategy that minimized operative time and risk.

## Multidisciplinary communication — the critical glue
This case illustrates excellent team functioning: emergency physicians, obstetricians, gynecologic surgeons, anesthesiologists, microbiology and neonatology were all involved. Key steps to emulate:

- Early multidisciplinary discussion with clear documentation of risks, benefits and alternatives.
- Obtain informed consent addressing maternal and fetal risks, including the possibility of preterm delivery.
- Pre-op checklist: maternal optimization (Hb, iron/transfusion if needed), antibiotic plan, fetal monitoring strategy, tocolysis/progesterone plan, blood products available, neonatal team on standby if delivery becomes necessary.
- Intraoperative coordination: obstetrics documenting fetal viability before/after/during surgery; anesthesia focusing on oxygenation and perfusion; surgical team minimizing uterine manipulation and blood loss.
- Post-op plan: ICU/ward monitoring, serial fetal assessments, and follow-up imaging and hematology as needed.

## What went well in this case
- Appropriate multidisciplinary evaluation and imaging (US + non-contrast MRI).
- Timely surgery for worsening symptoms and suspected torsion/leak — maternal indication well justified.
- Documentation of fetal heart activity before, during (intermittent), and after surgery.
- Pragmatic use of laparotomy to secure exposure and shorten operative time.
- Targeted antibiotics based on urine culture.
- Post-op obstetric follow-up and decision for elective CS at term given residual pelvic myoma.

## Clinical pearls / Take-home points
- Maternal stability is fetal stability: treat maternal emergencies promptly — delayed maternal care is the greater fetal threat.
- Document fetal heart activity pre-op, intermittently intra-op when feasible (or continuously for viable fetuses), and immediately post-op.
- In mid-second-trimester surgery, intermittent ultrasound/Doppler fetal checks are a pragmatic approach when continuous EFM is not possible.
- Position to optimize ventilation (head-up tilt) while maintaining uterine perfusion (left lateral tilt); avoid prolonged supine aortocaval compression.
- Choose anesthesia to maintain maternal oxygenation and hemodynamics; rapid-sequence induction and aspiration prophylaxis are standard.
- Routine prophylactic tocolysis or progesterone for non-obstetric surgery is controversial; individualize with obstetrics input. Use tocolytics when contractions or threatened preterm labour occur.
- Treat maternal infections aggressively with pregnancy-safe antibiotics guided by culture; maternal infection control is a priority for fetal well-being.
- Laparoscopy is possible in pregnancy but large, vascular, or posterior uterine masses may necessitate laparotomy for safety and speed.
- Multidisciplinary planning, explicit informed consent, and documentation are essential to optimize maternal and fetal outcomes.

This case reinforces a practical truth: operating with a passenger requires thoughtful monitoring, a maternal-first clinical strategy, good communication, and tailored perioperative choices. The result here — preservation of pregnancy to term and a healthy neonate — shows that with careful planning and execution, we can treat serious maternal pathology without sacrificing fetal outcome.



# Planning the Delivery Around Big Fibroids: Mapping, Timing, and Route

What makes this case worth your attention? A woman in her 40s at 21 weeks gestation presented with two very large abdominal masses. Imaging suggested possible ovarian endometriomas vs cystic degeneration in fibroids. At laparotomy the cranial mass turned out to be a huge pedunculated, highly vascularised, partially twisted leiomyoma that was resected; a second large posterior intramural-subserosal myoma was left in situ. The pregnancy proceeded to term with serial surveillance and an elective cesarean at 37 weeks because the residual posterior lower-uterine myoma would likely have obstructed the fetal head. Mother and baby did well.

This case highlights the practical problem we face in obstetric care: large, atypical pelvic masses in pregnancy are diagnostically challenging and force us to plan delivery around anatomy that changes with gestation. Below I walk through a pragmatic roadmap — mapping, timing, and decision-making — grounded in this patient’s course.

## 1) Map the anatomy early and serially — ultrasound first, MRI when needed
- Start with high-quality transabdominal and transvaginal ultrasound. Document number of myomas, maximal diameters, exact uterine location (fundal/anterior/posterior/isthmic/lower segment), relationship to the uterine cavity and cervix, and any pedicle or stalk for pedunculated lesions.
- Use Doppler to look for vascularised pedicles or hypertrophied feeding vessels (torsion risk).
- When ultrasound is limited by gravid uterus or body habitus, obtain a non-contrast MRI (≤1.5 T preferred after first trimester) to clarify posterior/intramural lesions, distance from the endometrial cavity, and relation to lower uterine segment and cervix. In this case MRI helped show the caudal lesion’s continuity with the posterior myoma and the cranial lesion’s complex cystic content — even if final diagnosis required surgery.
- Serial imaging matters. Myomas do not behave linearly in pregnancy: they may grow (more rapidly earlier in gestation) and commonly regress postpartum. Repeat imaging provides trend information that informs delivery planning.

Why this matters: Knowing whether a lesion occupies the lower uterine segment or is an intramural posterior myoma predicting obstruction can change route of delivery planning. A pedunculated fundal myoma behaves differently — it can tors and often is approachable for myomectomy if needed.

## 2) Predicting labor obstruction — size, number, and critical location
- The main anatomic predictor of obstructed vaginal delivery is a large mass physically occupying the birth canal or lower uterine segment. Lower-segment or isthmic posterior myomas are particularly problematic because they can block fetal head engagement and descent.
- Size threshold is not absolute. A posterior intramural/subserosal myoma in the isthmic region measuring several centimeters (for example the 5.8 × 7.1 cm myoma in this case) may be enough to obstruct delivery, particularly when there is a second large mass increasing “steric bulk.”
- Multiple large myomas increase the chance of malpresentation, dysfunctional labour, and cesarean.
- Consider dynamic assessment as pregnancy progresses: a myoma that seems borderline at 24 weeks may become a true mechanical obstruction by term as uterine anatomy and fetal size change.

Clinical implication from the case: The residual caudal myoma was judged likely to impede head progression in active labour, and an elective cesarean at term was planned — enabling avoidance of an urgent cesarean in labor and minimizing the risk of uterine scar rupture by using the previous laparotomy incision.

## 3) When to plan a scheduled cesarean versus attempt vaginal birth
Indications to favor planned cesarean include:
- A large myoma occupying or compressing the lower uterine segment or cervix (clear anatomic obstruction on imaging)
- Multiple large myomas with predicted compromise of cervical dilation or fetal descent
- Prior decision to leave a large residual myoma that cannot be safely removed in pregnancy
- Suspicion of uterine incision integrity compromise (prior classical incision, previous extensive myomectomy)

When vaginal birth may be reasonable:
- Myomas are small, fundal, or anterior and not near the lower uterine segment or cervix
- No evidence of malpresentation and fetal head engagement is achievable
- Shared decision-making after counseling about the risk of labor dystocia and emergency cesarean

From the case: Leaving a large posterior lower-uterine myoma in situ made vaginal birth unlikely; elective cesarean at 37 weeks balanced fetal maturity and maternal safety.

## 4) Concurrent myomectomy at cesarean — yes, sometimes; plan carefully
- Historical teaching discouraged myomectomy at cesarean due to hemorrhage risk. Contemporary data show it can be done safely in selected patients, especially when fibroids are subserosal/pedunculated and haemostasis is achievable.
- Key considerations before attempting concurrent myomectomy:
  - Location and number of fibroids: single, pedunculated, or subserosal lesions are more suitable.
  - Hemodynamic stability and availability of blood products.
  - Surgical expertise and readiness for uterine artery ligation or uterine compression techniques.
  - Intraoperative findings: if a myoma is clearly pedunculated with a discrete stalk (like the cranial lesion in this case), removal may be feasible and justifiable earlier in pregnancy; however the surgical team prudently removed the pedunculated leiomyoma at laparotomy in the second trimester to address torsion and sepsis risk.
- If a myoma is intramural and adherent (particularly posterior lower-segment), removal at cesarean may increase blood loss and the risk of scar defects. Many teams elect to defer.

In this case the team resected the twisted pedunculated myoma during pregnancy to address pain, fever, and the risk of torsion/necrosis. The posterior intramural mass was left alone to minimize obstetric risk and later dictated the cesarean decision.

## 5) Timing of intervention in pregnancy — balance risks and benefits
- Intervene during pregnancy only for compelling indications: torsion of a pedunculated myoma, suspected malignancy, intractable pain not controlled conservatively, haemodynamic instability, infection, or rapid growth compressing respiratory function (as happened with dyspnoea in this patient).
- If intervention is chosen, aim for the shortest, safest procedure with fetal monitoring and perioperative prophylaxis (antibiotics, tocolysis/progesterone where used locally, and blood product availability).
- Laparoscopy can be considered in early pregnancy for suitable lesions (small, pedunculated), but many teams prefer laparotomy for large, numerous, or intramural masses — as occurred here.

This case illustrates an appropriate selective surgical approach: remove the acutely symptomatic pedunculated myoma, leave the adherent posterior lesion, and continue pregnancy surveillance.

## 6) Postpartum regression and counseling on delayed surgery
- Many myomas regress postpartum; the third-trimester and early postpartum period often show significant size reduction. The literature and this case both illustrate that residual myomas may shrink and become asymptomatic.
- Counsel patients that:
  - Immediate postpartum myomectomy can be deferred if symptoms are tolerable and the woman desires future fertility or breastfeeding.
  - If postpartum symptoms persist (pain, bulk symptoms, abnormal uterine bleeding), elective myomectomy can be scheduled once the uterus involutes and breastfeeding considerations are discussed.
  - Shared decision-making should consider maternal preference, ongoing symptoms, family plans, and the anatomic change after delivery.
- In the presented case the patient opted to defer further surgery because the residual myoma shrank partly and she was asymptomatic while breastfeeding.

## 7) Practical pearls and monitoring plan
- Obtain baseline and serial ultrasound exams to watch for growth patterns and relation of fibroids to lower uterine segment. Add non-contrast MRI when ultrasound is nondiagnostic or posterior lesions are suspected.
- Document fetal heart rate before, during and after any maternal surgery (Zhao et al. guidance referenced in the case).
- For symptomatic torsion/infection/necrosis, proceed with timely surgical management — that was the correct call here when the cranial mass was twisted and leaking.
- Plan delivery route early (third-trimester planning), but revisit decisions with serial imaging and evolving maternal preferences.
- Discuss blood loss risk and transfusion plan preoperatively. Make plans for uterotonics, uterine compression sutures, arterial ligation or interventional radiology if significant hemorrhage is a concern.
- Offer postpartum follow-up with interval imaging and discuss timing of elective myomectomy only after recovery and uterine involution.

Key takeaways
- Map size, number and, most importantly, location of fibroids with serial ultrasound; add non-contrast MRI when ultrasound is limited.
- Lesions in the lower uterine segment or isthmus — especially large posterior intramural/subserosal fibroids — are the greatest predictors of obstructed labor and favor planned cesarean.
- Operate in pregnancy only for compelling indications (torsion, infection, intractable symptoms, or suspected malignancy). When surgery is needed, limit resections to what’s necessary to preserve the pregnancy.
- Concurrent myomectomy at cesarean is feasible selectively, but intramural lower-segment myomas are often best deferred to the postpartum period.
- Counsel patients about likely postpartum regression and allow maternal preferences to guide deferred surgery once the anatomy and symptoms have evolved.

Bottom line: Don’t guess the delivery route from a single scan. Map the fibroids, watch their behaviour, and plan delivery based on anatomy at term — not on fear. In complicated cases, a multidisciplinary approach and serial imaging (US ± MRI) let you align obstetric safety with patient-centered choices.
