Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Clinical Pearls] How do you calculate and interpret the Doppler Velocity Index (DVI) and indexed aortic valve area (AVAi) after prosthetic valve implantation, and what thresholds support severe stenosis and/or severe prosthesis–patient mismatch in a post-TAVI valve-in-valve scenario?

Direct answer
- Doppler velocity index (DVI) = VTI_LVOT / VTI_jet (or equivalently peak LVOT velocity ÷ peak transvalvular velocity when VTI is not used). Use pulsed‑wave Doppler in the LVOT (proximal to the prosthesis) for VTI_LVOT and continuous‑wave Doppler through the prosthetic orifice for VTI_jet.
- Aortic valve area by continuity equation (AVA) = CSA_LVOT × VTI_LVOT / VTI_jet, and the indexed AVA (AVAi) = AVA / body surface area (BSA).
- Thresholds that support severe prosthetic stenosis / severe prosthesis‑patient mismatch (PPM) in valve‑in‑valve/TAVI settings:
  - DVI ≤ 0.25 is consistent with severe obstruction of the prosthetic valve.
  - AVAi ≤ 0.65 cm2/m2 is used to define severe PPM (moderate PPM 0.65–0.85 cm2/m2). (Note: some centres use slightly lower cutoffs in obese patients, e.g. ≤0.60 cm2/m2.)
  - An AVA (absolute) <1.0 cm2 also indicates severe stenosis by standard criteria, but indexing is crucial for PPM assessment after prosthetic implantation.

Clinical reasoning and pitfalls (why and how)
- Why use DVI: In prosthetic valves LVOT diameter measurement (and thus CSA_LVOT) is often unreliable because the “effective” flow‑bearing LVOT can be altered by the stent frame and acoustic shadowing. DVI is a dimensionless ratio independent of CSA measurement and less sensitive to flow, so it is especially useful to detect prosthetic obstruction and to compare across serial studies. A low DVI implies a disproportionately high jet velocity past the valve compared with the LVOT flow — i.e., a tight orifice.
- Why index AVA: After valve implantation the anatomic orifice may be small relative to patient size; AVAi adjusts for BSA and defines PPM, which is prognostically important.
- Measurement technique: obtain PW Doppler VTI in the LVOT immediately below the prosthesis or at a reproducible site (avoid sampling within the stent); obtain CW Doppler across the prosthesis for the maximal jet VTI. Use multiple windows to align the CW beam with the jet. For AVA, measure LVOT diameter from the true flow lumen (if prosthesis obscures it, rely more on DVI or use CT/3D echo to assess CSA). Reproducible technique and averaging are important because small errors in LVOT diameter produce large AVA errors.
- Interpretation in valve‑in‑valve/TAVI contexts: Layered frames reduce the effective orifice area and increase risk of PPM. A DVI ≤0.25 and AVAi ≤0.65 cm2/m2 together indicate severe obstruction/PPM requiring attention; absolute gradients may be high immediately after implantation but must be correlated with DVI and AVAi and with clinical status.

Apply to this case
- Immediately after the third TAVI the patient had DVI 0.24 and AVA 0.9 cm2 (AVAi 0.4 cm2/m2). These values meet the criteria for severe obstruction/PPM (DVI ≤0.25 and AVAi 0.4 ≤0.65 cm2/m2), explaining the very high mean gradient (67 mm Hg) seen immediately post‑implantation.
- Subsequent early optimisation (post‑dilatation and mechanical expansion of prior frame) produced hemodynamic improvement: at 1 month mean gradient fell to 27 mm Hg and AVA rose to 1.1 cm2 (AVAi no longer in the severe PPM range), consistent with relieved obstruction and better effective orifice area. This illustrates that elevated immediate post‑TAVI gradients in valve‑in‑valve scenarios may reflect underexpansion or frame interaction and can improve after optimisation; nevertheless, the initial DVI and AVAi correctly identified severe functional compromise.

Takeaway pearls
- In prosthetic valves and valve‑in‑valve settings, rely on DVI as a robust, flow‑independent screen for obstruction; compute AVA by continuity and index it for BSA to assess PPM.
- DVI ≤0.25 and AVAi ≤0.65 cm2/m2 are thresholds supporting severe prosthetic stenosis/PPM. Combine Doppler indices with imaging (CT, 3D echo) and clinical context to guide management (eg, post‑dilatation, consideration of reintervention).
