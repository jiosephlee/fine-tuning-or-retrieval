Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Management] In a patient with prior transfemoral access complications (pseudoaneurysm requiring surgery), what preprocedural and intraprocedural steps reduce vascular risk during redo TAVI (e.g., CT iliofemoral sizing and calcium/burden assessment, ultrasound-guided puncture, pre-closure strategies, alternative access planning such as transaxillary/carotid, and bailout readiness)?

Clinical answer — how to minimise vascular risk for a redo transfemoral TAVI after a prior femoral complication

Bottom line
In patients with a prior transfemoral vascular complication (pseudo‑aneurysm requiring surgery in this case), every redo-TAVI must treat the iliofemoral axis as a critical part of the procedure — not an afterthought. Use detailed preprocedural CT iliofemoral mapping to choose access (or an alternative), plan puncture and closure, and enter the lab with a predefined bailout plan (surgical team, covered stents, wires). The case reported here illustrates that careful CT-guided planning and strategy permitted safe femoral access and a successful third TAVI despite prior femoral surgery — but only because those access risks were explicitly addressed.

Why this matters (pathophysiology/clinical reasoning)
Prior puncture, haematoma and pseudoaneurysm indicate diseased, scarred and potentially fragile common femoral artery (CFA) and iliofemoral tract. Scar and prior surgical repair increase risk of recurrent bleeding, pseudoaneurysm, access-site infection and failure of percutaneous closure. A small, calcified, tortuous or stenotic iliofemoral pathway raises the risk of sheath trauma, vessel rupture or inability to deliver the valve. Preprocedure imaging and a staged, guarded intraprocedural strategy substantially lower these risks.

Concrete preprocedural steps (must do)
- Dedicated iliofemoral MSCT with arterial-phase reconstructions and centreline measurements. Assess: minimal lumen diameter, eccentric calcification (location and circumferential burden), tortuosity, prior surgical repair or graft, femoral bifurcation anatomy, and external iliac artery disease. In the case report MSCT was used to guide valve planning; extend the same CT to access planning.
- Measure minimal lumen diameter against the required device sheath/introducer (including expandable sheaths). If lumen < recommended for the chosen sheath, avoid transfemoral or plan adjuncts (angioplasty, conduit, or alternate access).
- Qualitative and quantitative calcium scoring of the CFA and pelvic arteries (extensive circumferential calcium predicts failure of percutaneous access/closure and higher rupture risk).
- Review prior operative notes/imaging (location of prior pseudoaneurysm repair, vessel patching, scarring) and palpation/duplex clinic exam.
- Multidisciplinary decision on access route (heart team + vascular surgery/interventional radiology). If CT shows marginal femoral access, preselect alternatives (transaxillary, transcarotid, transapical/surgical) and discuss with patient.
- Preprocedure vascular planning: book vascular surgery and interventional radiology backup, ensure availability of covered stents, large bore sheaths, wires and percutaneous occlusion tools.
- Antiplatelet/anticoagulant strategy tailored to bleeding risk and to facilitate closure (coordinate with haemostasis policy).

Intraprocedural technical strategies (practical steps)
- Ultrasound-guided puncture of the CFA (real‑time US to identify true CFA above bifurcation, avoid scarred zones, avoid anterior calcified plaque). In previously operated femoral arteries, US can document vessel wall layers and choose a healthier puncture site.
- Use a micropuncture kit first, confirm position angiographically (small contrast injection) before upsizing.
- Pre-close with suture-based closure devices (usually two Perclose ProGlide deployed in preclose fashion) when anatomy allows; place pre-closure sutures before introducing large-bore sheath. If femoral artery significantly scarred or heavily calcified, consider surgical exposure instead of percutaneous pre-close.
- Consider an ipsilateral or contralateral crossover wire strategy: leave a contralateral sheath/wire for emergency balloon tamponade or covered stent delivery; have a stiff safety wire parked across iliac artery to facilitate rapid device delivery for bailout.
- Keep ACT in target range to balance thrombosis vs bleeding; reverse anticoagulation promptly if major bleeding occurs.
- Use the smallest feasible sheath or an expandable low-profile introducer. When anatomy is marginal, consider balloon angioplasty of focal stenosis prior to large-bore sheath passage.
- Controlled serial dilation can reduce traumatic entry; avoid forceful sheath advancement against resistance.
- Routine use of fluoroscopic landmarks and roadmapping angiography at the puncture site (especially when surface anatomy is distorted by prior surgery).
- If transradial or contralateral access is required for pigtail or coronary protection (as in this case they used left radial for aortography), plan that in advance to avoid extra femoral manipulations.
- Immediate assessment after sheath removal: angiography of the puncture site, duplex if needed. If bleeding or contained rupture — rapid balloon tamponade with an occlusive balloon, then covered stent deployment or surgical repair.
- Have vascular surgery scrubbed/available when high-risk access is attempted (history of prior pseudoaneurysm repair), and ensure immediate availability of covered stents (e.g., Viabahn/covered balloon-expandable stents) sized for the iliofemoral segment.

Alternative access planning
- If CT shows inadequate lumen, heavy circumferential calcification, extreme tortuosity, or prior complex surgical repair, favour non-femoral access: transaxillary (surgical cutdown or percutaneous), transcarotid (with neurologic protection and surgical backup), or surgical transapical/transaortic approaches. Each has its own risk profile — choose based on CT, comorbidities, and operator expertise.
- In the presented case, the team elected femoral access guided by CT and performed the TAVI successfully; that decision should only be made after CT confirms adequate lumen and acceptable calcification/tortuosity.

Bailout readiness (must have in room)
- Contralateral sheath/wire for rapid occlusion or crossover.
- Occlusive balloon for temporary haemorrhage control.
- Covered stents in appropriate sizes for external iliac and CFA.
- Surgical team on call/standing by for open repair.
- Hemostasis adjuncts (thrombin for pseudoaneurysm percutaneous injection in controlled scenarios with imaging guidance).
- Blood products available.

Documentation and informed consent
- Explain heightened vascular risk given prior pseudoaneurysm and prior vascular surgery; document alternative access options discussed and the planned bailout steps. In the reported case, the Heart Team and shared decision-making were emphasised — replicate that for access decisions.

How the published case reflects these principles
- The case report emphasised CT-guided planning for valve selection and coronary protection; although not detailed for iliofemoral anatomy, the same MSCT planning principles apply to access. The team used left femoral arterial access successfully after CT evaluation and used left radial access for aortography — an example of multimodality access planning to minimise manipulation. The favourable outcome after a prior transfemoral surgical repair underlines that careful preprocedural imaging, multidisciplinary planning and intraprocedural vigilance can allow safe transfemoral redo-TAVI in selected patients.

Clinical pearl
Treat the iliofemoral axis as a second procedure to be planned with the same rigor as valve sizing — CT mapping, ultrasound-directed puncture, pre-closure (or surgical exposure if anatomy unfavourable), and a ready bailout plan (balloon tamponade, covered stents, vascular surgery) are the interventions that most consistently reduce vascular complications on redo TAVI.
