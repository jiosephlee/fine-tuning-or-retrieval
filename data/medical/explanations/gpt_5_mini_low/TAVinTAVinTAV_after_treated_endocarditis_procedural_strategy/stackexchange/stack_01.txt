Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Management] In planning a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedure, which CT-derived measurements most directly predict coronary obstruction risk (e.g., coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva width, valve-to-coronary [VTC] distance, and anticipated neo-skirt height), and how would each parameter influence the decision to proceed without protection versus perform BASILICA or planned coronary wiring and chimney stenting?

Direct answer
- The CT measurements that most directly predict coronary obstruction in TAV-in-TAV (and especially TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV) are: coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva (SOV) width (and sinus depth), valve-to-coronary (VTC) distance (sometimes called leaflet-to-coronary distance), and the anticipated neo‑skirt (or displaced‑leaflet) height after the new valve is implanted. Each parameter is used together — not in isolation — to estimate whether the displaced bioprosthetic leaflets (or stacked skirt material) will cover a coronary ostium.

How each measurement guides management (practical thresholds and decision implications)
- Coronary ostial height
  - Why it matters: low ostia are closer to displaced leaflets/skirt and therefore more likely to be occluded.
  - Practical guidance: ostial height <10–12 mm is generally considered high risk for obstruction; 12–14 mm is intermediate and needs closer assessment in the context of valve frame position and SOV size.
  - Management: low-height ostia push you toward coronary protection strategies (planned wiring/chimney stent) or BASILICA when feasible; high ostia (>14 mm) make proceeding without protection reasonable if other measurements are favorable.

- Sinus of Valsalva width/depth (and sinotubular junction)
  - Why it matters: a narrow or shallow sinus cannot accommodate the displaced leaflet or skirt; large sinuses can buffer leaflet displacement and preserve flow.
  - Practical guidance: SOV diameter <30–32 mm (or shallow sinus depth) increases obstruction risk; larger sinuses (>32–34 mm) lower risk.
  - Management: small/narrow sinuses favor preemptive protection (wiring/chimney) or BASILICA; roomy sinuses may allow safe no‑protection strategy.

- Valve-to-coronary (VTC) distance (leaflet-skirt to coronary)
  - Why it matters: VTC quantifies the clearance between the intended final skirt/leaflet plane and the coronary ostium — the single best predictor of obstruction risk in many series.
  - Practical guidance: VTC ≤4 mm (some centers use ≤4–6 mm) is high risk for coronary obstruction; 4–8 mm is intermediate; >8 mm is usually low risk.
  - Management: VTC ≤4 mm → strongly consider protection (BASILICA if leaflet laceration is possible and effective, or planned chimney stenting/wiring if BASILICA not feasible); VTC >8 mm → can usually proceed without protection if other factors benign.

- Anticipated neo‑skirt / displaced‑leaflet height (from CT modelling)
  - Why it matters: the neo‑skirt is the new cylinder of leaflet/skirt material that will sit above the old valve frame; if its top lies at or above the coronary ostia, obstruction can occur even with acceptable ostial height.
  - Practical guidance: if anticipated neo‑skirt projects at or above the coronary ostium, obstruction risk is high regardless of ostial height; if neo‑skirt stops well below the ostium, risk is lower.
  - Management: neo‑skirt that threatens coronaries → plan protection. If neo‑skirt will sit below ostia and VTC adequate, may proceed without protection.

Applying these measurements to strategy choice
- Proceed without coronary protection
  - When: ostial heights comfortably high (>12–14 mm), SOV large/deep, VTC >6–8 mm, and CT modelling shows the neo‑skirt will not reach the ostia. Commissural alignment that preserves coronary access supports this choice.
  - Rationale: low predicted risk; avoids complexities/risks of BASILICA or chimney stents.

- Planned coronary wiring ± chimney stenting (prewired coronary protection)
  - When: intermediate risk (e.g., ostial height borderline 10–12 mm, SOV modest, VTC 3–6 mm or uncertain CT modelling), or when anatomy makes BASILICA technically difficult (heavy frame layering, heavy calcification, uncertain leaflet traversal).
  - How it's done: preposition a guidewire and an undeployed stent in the coronary; if flow is compromised after valve deployment, the stent can be rapidly deployed (chimney).
  - Rationale: provides an immediate bailout that is simpler than attempting leaflet laceration in difficult anatomies; favored when obstruction risk is real but some chance exists that obstruction will NOT occur.

- BASILICA (intentional leaflet laceration)
  - When: VTC is low and CT shows that intact leaflets (native or bioprosthetic) will directly obstruct a coronary ostium, but leaflet laceration is technically feasible and likely to create sufficient flow channel. Often used when the offending leaflet is accessible and not excessively thick or restrained by multiple frames.
  - Limitations in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV: multiple overlapping frames and dense frame apposition can make leaflet traversal or laceration unreliable or unsafe; leaflet length, calcification, and frame geometry affect feasibility.
  - Rationale: when feasible, BASILICA prevents leaflet tissue from forming a continuous barrier to coronary flow and avoids the long‑term issues of chimney stents.

How this relates to the presented case
- The authors used MSCT to measure coronary ostial height, SOV dimensions, VTC distance and to model the anticipated neo‑skirt. Their modelling suggested adequate coronary clearance and that a high implantation of the Acurate neo2 would position the neo‑skirt below the coronary ostia while preserving commissural alignment and future coronary access. Because CT parameters were favorable, they proceeded without coronary protection. This strategy was successful: intra‑procedural aortography confirmed preserved coronary perfusion and post‑deployment imaging showed no obstruction.
- Important nuance: in a triple‑stented anatomy, BASILICA may be less feasible (leaflet traversal and effective laceration can be compromised by layered frames). That concern also factors into favoring CT‑based avoidance of protection when measurements are clearly low risk, or choosing prewired/chimney protection when risk is intermediate but BASILICA is judged technically uncertain.

Bottom line (clinical pearl)
- Use the combination of coronary ostial height, SOV size/depth, VTC distance, and predicted neo‑skirt height from CT modelling to stratify risk: VTC ≤4 mm, ostial height <10–12 mm, small SOV, or neo‑skirt reaching the ostium → high risk → plan protection (BASILICA if feasible; otherwise prewired coronary + chimney). Intermediate values → consider planned wiring with bailout chimney. Favor proceeding without protection only when CT shows adequate VTC clearance, high ostia, roomy sinuses, and neo‑skirt below the coronaries — and when commissural alignment and implantation strategy can further reduce risk, as was done successfully in this case.
