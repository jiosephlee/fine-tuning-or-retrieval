Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Management] After treated prosthetic valve endocarditis due to Enterococcus faecalis, what clinical, microbiologic, and imaging criteria should be met before considering repeat TAVI (e.g., symptom status, negative serial blood cultures, absence of vegetations or paravalvular complications on TEE/CT), and how do contemporary guidelines frame the role of TAVI versus surgery in this setting?

Bottom line
- Repeat TAVI after prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) is an option only when infection has been definitively eradicated, paravalvular anatomy is favourable on high‑resolution imaging, and the heart‑team judges surgery to be prohibitively risky. Before re‑implanting a transcatheter valve you need concordant clinical, microbiologic and imaging evidence of cure plus careful CT/TEE planning to exclude residual infectious complications that would mandate surgery.

Clinical, microbiologic and imaging criteria to meet before considering repeat TAVI (practical checklist)
1) Clinical stability
   - Resolution of fever, systemic inflammatory signs, and heart‑failure decompensation attributable to infection.
   - No ongoing signs of sepsis, persistent embolic phenomena, or new conduction disturbances suggesting extension of infection.
   - In the reported case the patient was afebrile, clinically improved after therapy and remained asymptomatic at follow‑up — an important prerequisite.

2) Microbiologic cure
   - Clearance of bacteremia: serial negative blood cultures after completion of targeted therapy. In practice this means at least two to three sets of blood cultures that are repeatedly negative while off bactericidal therapy (or at least after the planned course has been completed), and no recrudescence on short‑term follow‑up.
   - Completion of an appropriate, guideline‑directed antibiotic course for PVE (commonly 6 weeks for Enterococcus faecalis with targeted therapy) and documentation of treatment adherence. In the case report the patient completed a 6‑week IV ampicillin‑based course with subsequent oral therapy and was not on chronic suppressive antibiotics at the time of reintervention.

3) Imaging evidence of eradication and absence of complications
   - Transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE): no mobile vegetations, no persistent or new paravalvular abscess, pseudoaneurysm or fistula, and no ongoing prosthetic dehiscence. TEE should be compared to prior studies to ensure resolution.
   - Multislice CT (MSCT): complementary assessment to TEE for paravalvular complications, abscess cavities, prosthesis fixation, valve‑to‑coronary relationships and sizing for the next valve. CT is essential when multiple frames are present to plan valve selection, implantation depth and coronary protection.
   - If there is any imaging concern for abscess, persistent vegetation, leaflet destruction or paravalvular leak related to infection, surgery is favored.
   - In the described patient both TEE and MSCT confirmed no vegetations or paravalvular complications and demonstrated acceptable valve‑to‑coronary distances and landing‑zone anatomy — enabling CT‑guided planning for a third TAVI.

4) Timing and durability of observation
   - There is no absolute fixed interval, but most teams wait until a full course of antibiotics is complete and the patient is clinically and microbiologically stable for a period (weeks to months), with no relapse on early follow‑up. The timeline should be individualized by organism virulence, complication history, and surgical risk. In the case report the intervening period between treated PVE (Sept 2022) and the redo TAVI (June 2024) was prolonged and uncomplicated, strengthening confidence of cure.

5) Heart‑team decision and individualized risk assessment
   - Multidisciplinary discussion (cardiology, cardiac surgery, infectious diseases, imaging specialists, and anesthesia). Factors that push toward TAVI include prohibitive surgical risk, extensive stent integration making explantation hazardous, and favourable CT/TEE anatomy. Factors that push toward surgery include persistent infection, valve ring/annular abscess, uncontrolled heart failure from paravalvular destruction, or organisms associated with a high failure rate of medical therapy.

How contemporary guidelines frame TAVI versus surgery after PVE
- Contemporary guidelines (ESC 2025 referenced in the report) continue to recommend surgical valve replacement as the standard therapy for PVE in many situations—particularly when infection is uncontrolled, when there are paravalvular complications (abscess, dehiscence, fistula), or when heart failure is present because of structural destruction.
- However, guidelines recognise that management must be individualised: in patients with treated PVE who have documented microbiologic cure, no imaging evidence of residual infectious complications, and prohibitive surgical risk, a transcatheter approach can be considered by a multidisciplinary heart team.
- Key principles reflected in guideline thinking and illustrated by this case:
   - Do not perform a repeat prosthetic implantation into an infected field. Ensure eradication first.
   - Use high‑resolution imaging (TEE + MSCT) to exclude residual infection and to plan the procedure (coronary protection, commissural alignment, residual effective orifice area).
   - Favor surgery when there is structural destruction from infection that cannot be addressed by a transcatheter valve.
   - Where surgery is prohibitive and infection is cured, TAVI may be an acceptable, patient‑centred alternative — provided careful planning and informed shared decision‑making.

Takeaway applied to this case
- The team followed the expected precautions: completion of a 6‑week antibiotic course for Enterococcus faecalis, serial cultures and imaging (TEE and MSCT) demonstrating no vegetations or paravalvular complications, and a heart‑team determination that surgical explant would be prohibitive. Those criteria supported proceeding with CT‑guided TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV, with tailored strategies (coronary assessment, commissural alignment) to minimise procedural risk and preserve future options. The 1‑year uneventful outcome reinforces that, when strict clinical, microbiologic and imaging criteria are met and multidisciplinary judgment concurs, repeat TAVI after treated PVE can be performed safely in select high‑risk patients.
