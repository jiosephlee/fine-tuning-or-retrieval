Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Differential Diagnosis] Immediately after the third TAVI, the mean gradient was 67 mm Hg with an AVA of 0.9 cm² and DVI 0.24. What are the leading causes of an early high transvalvular gradient after valve-in-valve TAVI, and what tests or imaging (e.g., Doppler indices, acceleration time, leaflet-motion CT, anticoagulation trial for suspected leaflet thrombosis) help distinguish prosthesis–patient mismatch from valve underexpansion, malposition, or thrombosis?

Direct answer
- The immediate high transvalvular gradient after valve‑in‑valve TAVI usually reflects one of three mechanisms (often overlapping): (1) true prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM, an effective orifice area too small for the patient’s body size), (2) mechanical reduction of effective orifice area from valve under‑expansion or malposition (including frame underexpansion, leaflet crowding from an inner/outer frame interaction), or (3) early leaflet thrombosis (hypo‑attenuated leaflet thickening with reduced leaflet motion). Less commonly immediately after implantation residual paravalvular obstruction, severe transvalvular regurgitation with low forward flow, or measurement artifact may contribute.

How to distinguish them — practical tests and imaging, and what each result implies
1) Transthoracic Doppler/TTE (first-line, immediate and serial)
- Key numbers: peak velocity, mean gradient, aortic valve area (continuity equation), indexed AVA (iAVA), and Doppler Velocity Index (DVI = VTI_LVOT / VTI_AV). 
  - DVI ≤0.25 and iAVA ≤0.65 cm²/m² (or iAVA ≤0.6–0.65 severe) indicate severe obstruction/PPM.
  - DVI is helpful because it is relatively flow‑independent; low DVI → true stenosis/PPM or underexpansion.
- Acceleration time (AT) from the LVOT to aortic jet:
  - Prolonged AT (>100–110 ms, depending on lab) suggests a fixed transvalvular obstruction (underexpansion/PPM) rather than isolated regurgitation or low flow.
- Trend over time:
  - Immediate very high gradient that falls after post‑dilatation or in days–weeks suggests mechanical underexpansion with delayed frame expansion (or successful post‑dilation).
  - A rise in gradient over days–weeks after an initially acceptable result raises thrombosis higher on the differential.

In the case: immediate post‑implant DVI 0.24, AVA 0.9 cm², mean 67 mm Hg — values compatible with severe obstruction/PPM by Doppler indices.

2) Fluoroscopy and TEE (intra‑ and early post‑procedure)
- Fluoroscopy: evaluate stent frame expansion, symmetric vs. eccentric expansion, and visible leaflet motion. Underexpanded frame or eccentric frame suggests mechanical underexpansion or malposition.
- TEE: assesses leaflet mobility and can help exclude residual paravalvular leak or obstruction; limited for subtle leaflet thrombus but useful for motion.

3) Multislice CT (gated, 4‑D when possible)
- The diagnostic standard to distinguish underexpansion/malposition from leaflet thrombosis:
  - For thrombosis: look for hypo‑attenuated leaflet thickening (HALT) and reduced leaflet motion (RLM) on 4‑D CT. HALT ± RLM is highly suggestive of subacute leaflet thrombosis.
  - For underexpansion/malposition: measure inner stent frame diameter, minimal lumen area, eccentricity, and relationships of overlapping frames. A frame diameter substantially less than the labelled valve size or preprocedural plan indicates underexpansion.
- In valve‑in‑valve situations CT can also document how prior frames limit the achievable EOA (anatomic PPM).

In the case: preprocedural MSCT showed a minimum lumen diameter of the Evolut prosthesis of 13.7 mm, documenting severe anatomic compromise and guiding the team’s choice and strategy.

4) Therapeutic anticoagulation trial
- Indication: when HALT/RLM is seen on CT or when thrombosis is suspected clinically (subacute rise in gradient) and CT is unavailable or equivocal.
- Trial: full therapeutic anticoagulation (usually warfarin or DOAC per institutional practice) for 3 months with repeat imaging (CT and Doppler). Resolution of HALT/RLM and normalization (or marked improvement) of gradients confirms leaflet thrombosis.
- If gradients improve rapidly with anticoagulation, thrombosis is likely; lack of response suggests fixed mechanical constraint (PPM/underexpansion).

5) Hemodynamic manoeuvres and interventional response
- Balloon post‑dilatation and high‑pressure expansion:
  - Immediate intraprocedural post‑dilatation that lowers the gradient or increases frame diameter points to underexpansion as the dominant mechanism.
  - Commissural alignment and successful post‑dilatation that improve gradients also argue against thrombosis.
- If malposition (too high or low): imaging shows eccentric jet or abnormal implantation depth; correcting malposition is rarely possible percutaneously after deployment.

Putting it together — diagnostic algorithm (concise)
1. Document Doppler indices (DVI, iAVA, AT) and compare to pre‑ and intraprocedural baselines.
2. Inspect fluoroscopy/TEE for frame expansion and leaflet motion.
3. If mechanical constraint suspected (underexpansion/malposition): obtain gated MSCT to measure stent geometry; consider balloon post‑dilatation if safe.
4. If thrombosis suspected clinically or CT shows HALT/RLM: start therapeutic anticoagulation and reimage in ~6–12 weeks (or 3 months) to confirm resolution.
5. If CT shows small annular EO area despite full expansion and indexed EOA consistent with severe PPM, accept PPM and manage medically (or consider further intervention only if symptomatic and feasible).

Application to this case
- Immediately after the third TAVI the patient had severe Doppler obstruction (mean 67 mm Hg, AVA 0.9, DVI 0.24) consistent with severe PPM/obstruction. The team performed balloon post‑dilatation and relied on preprocedural CT planning to maximize expansion. Over the first month the mean gradient fell to 27 mm Hg and AVA rose to 1.1 cm². That time course — early very high gradient with marked improvement after optimisation and over weeks — favors mechanical underexpansion and frame interaction (partial radial expansion of the prior Evolut frame plus successful post‑dilation and supra‑annular valve choice) rather than leaflet thrombosis. If thrombosis had been responsible, one would expect CT evidence of HALT/RLM and prompt improvement with anticoagulation rather than delayed improvement after mechanical optimisation.

Bottom line
- Early high gradient after valve‑in‑valve is most commonly PPM or mechanical underexpansion/malposition; thrombosis is an important but usually subacute cause. Use Doppler indices (DVI, iAVA, AT) for immediate discrimination, fluoroscopy/TEE to assess leaflet/frame mechanics, and gated 4‑D CT to confirm HALT/RLM or quantify frame underexpansion. If HALT/RLM is present or strongly suspected, a trial of therapeutic anticoagulation with repeat CT is appropriate; if underexpansion/malposition is identified and safe to treat, balloon post‑dilatation or other mechanical measures are preferred. In this reported patient, the improvement after optimisation supports underexpansion/frame mechanics as the dominant mechanism rather than thrombosis.
