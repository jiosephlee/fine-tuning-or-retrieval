Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

## Case Synopsis and Clinical Trajectory

This case concerns a man in his late 70s with extensive cardiovascular and systemic comorbidity who ultimately required a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV). At baseline he carried multiple high-risk features: three‑vessel coronary artery disease treated with several prior percutaneous coronary interventions, arterial hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and a history of recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding. In 2014 he also developed third‑degree atrioventricular block necessitating permanent pacemaker placement. These comorbid conditions established a high operative risk profile and framed all subsequent therapeutic decisions.

The patient’s aortic valve journey began in 2014 with transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) of a 27 mm Lotus prosthesis for severe native aortic stenosis. Over the following years the valve underwent structural deterioration: by late 2021 his symptoms of exertional dyspnoea progressed and echocardiography showed severe bioprosthetic stenosis with rising transvalvular gradients and a decline in left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) from 54% to 42%. Given his comorbidities and prohibitive surgical risk, a transcatheter redo strategy was pursued.

In May 2022 a valve‑in‑valve procedure was performed with a 23 mm Evolut valve implanted within the degenerate Lotus prosthesis. Hemodynamics improved promptly—the EF recovered to 54%—but the procedure was not without complication: the patient developed a pulsatile femoral hematoma and pseudoaneurysm that required open vascular surgical repair. The early functional improvement suggested successful relief of obstruction from the degenerated first prosthesis.

Three months after the TAV‑in‑TAV, however, the patient presented with fever and recurrent heart failure. Blood cultures grew Enterococcus faecalis and transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated severe restenosis of the transcatheter prosthesis. A diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) was made. Management followed infectious‑disease guidance: the patient completed a full course of targeted intravenous ampicillin‑based therapy for six weeks, with subsequent oral therapy as advised. Serial clinical assessments, repeated blood cultures, transesophageal echocardiography and multislice CT showed resolution of infection with no residual vegetations or paravalvular complications; thus active infection was considered successfully treated.

Despite microbiological and imaging resolution of PVE, valve function again deteriorated over the following 18 months. By January–June 2024 the patient had severe prosthetic valve dysfunction: symptomatic exertional limitation with a mean transvalvular gradient of approximately 49 mm Hg, a calculated aortic valve area of 0.5 cm², and moderate–to‑severe intraprosthetic regurgitation. Notably, left ventricular performance had recovered—EF rose to about 65%—so symptoms reflected critical valve obstruction and regurgitation rather than pump failure.

Faced with this recurrent structural failure in the context of two prior transcatheter frames, prior PVE that had been treated and a markedly elevated surgical risk (EuroSCORE II 27% plus severe comorbidities), the multidisciplinary heart team concluded that another transcatheter approach was the most appropriate option. The decision followed shared decision‑making with the patient and family and relied heavily on detailed preprocedural imaging to assess anatomical feasibility, coronary risk, and the likelihood of achieving an acceptable effective orifice area despite triple stenting. This led to the plan for a CT‑guided, strategy‑driven third TAVI (Acurate neo2) with attention to implantation depth, commissural alignment and post‑dilatation to optimise haemodynamics while preserving coronary access.

## Key Pathophysiology and Procedural Risks Relevant to Triple Transcatheter Valves

Understanding why transcatheter valves fail and how layered prostheses change cardiac anatomy is essential for safe planning of a third TAVI, particularly after a prior episode of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). The decisions made in this case—careful CT modelling, selection of a supra‑annular device, high implantation and thorough infection clearance—follow directly from the pathophysiology and procedural risks summarized below.

Structural valve deterioration (SVD) and interaction with frame geometry
Bioprosthetic transcatheter valves commonly fail over time by progressive structural valve deterioration. The principal mechanisms are leaflet fibrosis and stiffening, calcification, and pannus formation (organized tissue overgrowth at the sewing or leaflet attachment zones). These processes reduce leaflet mobility and effective orifice area (EOA), producing increasing transvalvular gradients and symptoms of stenosis. When a new transcatheter valve is implanted inside a prior frame, the mechanical interaction between the old and new frames and between the new frame and the diseased leaflets is crucial. Rigid or inward-folded leaflets from the failed valve can become pinned between frames, worsening restriction unless the new valve can effectively expand or fix the old leaflets against the stent in a way that restores central orifice. Repeated layering increases radial stiffness and limits how much subsequent devices can expand, altering flow dynamics and sometimes producing a residual functional stenosis despite technically successful deployment.

Impact of prior prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE)
PVE adds specific injury mechanisms and additional uncertainty. Infection can directly damage leaflets (causing perforation, tearing or scarring) and can stimulate pannus or fibrotic healing that further restricts motion. After appropriate, guideline‑directed antimicrobial therapy, proceeding with a reintervention is acceptable only when infection is considered eradicated. In practice this means clinical resolution of sepsis, persistently negative blood cultures, and imaging that shows no vegetations, paravalvular abscesses or dehiscence on transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and multislice CT (MSCT). The case illustrates that thorough reassessment with TEE and MSCT and completion of an adequate antibiotic course were prerequisites before deciding on a third TAVI. Active or complicated PVE (ongoing bacteremia, abscess, fistula or prosthetic dehiscence) remains a surgical indication in most guidelines and is generally a contraindication to percutaneous reintervention.

Hemodynamic constraints of serial valve‑in‑valve procedures and prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM)
Each additional valve layer narrows the internal lumen available for flow. The effective orifice area is determined not only by the nominal valve size but by the final internal diameter after interaction of all frames and leaflets. This can lead to severe prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM), characterized by a small indexed EOA and high residual gradients. Clinically this shows as high mean gradients and low Doppler velocity index (DVI) despite leaflet mobility of the newly implanted device. In serial ViV situations, planning must anticipate the smallest possible residual lumen (measured on MSCT as minimum lumen diameter within previous frame) and estimate expected EOA and gradients. Strategies to mitigate PPM include choosing valves with supra‑annular leaflet position (which place the effective leaflet orifice above the constrained annular lumen), optimising commissural alignment to maximise central flow, aggressive post‑dilation to expand prior frames (and, in selected native surgical bioprostheses, intentional bioprosthetic valve fracture), and selecting the largest feasible device compatible with coronary safety. The case demonstrates these principles: Acurate neo2 was chosen for its supra‑annular design and favorable haemodynamics, and post‑dilation and partial mechanical expansion of the previous frame contributed to improved gradients over time.

Risk of coronary obstruction and neo‑skirt formation
Coronary obstruction is a feared complication in ViV procedures and is amplified with multiple overlapping frames. Two anatomic determinants are particularly predictive: coronary ostial height and sinus of Valsalva size (which affect leaflet clearance), and the valve‑to‑coronary (VTC) distance—the shortest distance from the expected plane of the displaced leaflet or neo‑skirt to the coronary ostium. Small sinuses, low coronary heights, and VTC distances below commonly used safety thresholds increase the risk of obstruction. Although absolute cutoff values vary between centres and valve types, a VTC <4 mm (or coronary height <10–12 mm with narrow sinuses) is often considered high risk. With layered frames, a new “neo‑skirt” of leaflet tissue or trapped bioprosthetic material can form and extend orifice obstruction above the annulus; overlapping stent cells can also limit the outward displacement of native or prior valve leaflets, increasing obstruction risk. Preprocedural CT mapping of sinus dimensions, coronary heights and predicted leaflet displacement is therefore mandatory; in this patient, CT modelling showed acceptable VTC and clearance, which justified foregoing routine coronary protection.

Coronary reaccess challenges and commissural alignment
Even if immediate coronary obstruction is avoided, future coronary access can be compromised. With each transcatheter frame added, the cumulative mesh burden across the sinuses and coronary ostia increases, making catheter passage more difficult and selective engagement less reliable. Commissural misalignment between frames leads to stent posts and leaflet material landing in front of coronary ostia; even with attempted commissural alignment during deployment, perfect alignment across three different frames is unlikely. This case therefore prioritized commissural alignment and selection of a valve with a frame and leaflet geometry thought to favour preserved coronary access, but it also acknowledges that future coronary interventions could be technically challenging.

Guideline context and individualized decision‑making after PVE
Contemporary infective endocarditis and valve guidelines emphasize surgery for active or complicated PVE (heart failure, uncontrolled infection, prosthetic dehiscence or abscess). However, when infection is eradicated, the decision between redo surgery and transcatheter solutions must be individualized: surgical risk, anatomic feasibility on CT, the likelihood of acceptable haemodynamics and patient preferences all weigh heavily. In patients with prohibitive surgical risk and imaging that excludes residual infection or anatomic contraindications, a carefully planned percutaneous redo (including ViV or, in exceptional cases, ViV‑in‑ViV) can be reasonable. The case exemplifies guideline‑concordant individualized care: after documented microbiological cure and imaging confirmation of no active endocarditis, a multidisciplinary heart team accepted the higher long‑term uncertainty in exchange for a lower immediate procedural risk, and used CT‑guided strategy to mitigate known hazards.

Summary as applied to this case
The patient’s valve failure reflected combined SVD and likely infection‑related leaflet injury; MSCT showed a critically narrowed lumen within the Evolut frame, guiding valve choice and implantation depth. Coronary obstruction risk and future reaccess were carefully evaluated by CT (VTC, sinus size, coronary heights) and mitigated by high implantation and commissural alignment. Antimicrobial therapy had been completed and imaging confirmed eradication of PVE, meeting practical criteria to proceed percutaneously. Despite a high immediate post‑procedure gradient consistent with PPM, post‑dilation and partial expansion of the prior frame improved EOA over weeks, yielding acceptable haemodynamics and clinical benefit at 1 year. This sequence illustrates how SVD, prior infection, and layered anatomy drive both the risks of and the planning requirements for triple transcatheter valve interventions.

## Diagnostic Workup and CT-Guided Planning

In a patient with multiple prior transcatheter valves and a recent episode of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), the diagnostic workup serves three linked functions: confirm resolution of infection, precisely quantify the current degree and mechanism of prosthetic dysfunction, and provide detailed anatomic data to predict interactions between a new valve and the existing frames so that device selection, implantation depth and coronary protection strategy can be chosen safely. In this case each of those objectives was addressed using a coordinated, multimodality approach.

Echocardiography — defining haemodynamic severity and ventricular response
Transthoracic and transoesophageal echocardiography were used to quantify stenosis, assess regurgitation, and follow left ventricular (LV) remodelling and function over time. Key parameters reported in the case illustrate typical echocardiographic assessment in prosthetic valve dysfunction:

- Mean transvalvular gradients and effective orifice area (AVA): Serial TTE showed progressive increases in mean gradient (up to 83 mmHg before the 2022 reintervention, then recurrent high gradients with mean 49 mmHg in 2024) and a small calculated AVA of 0.5 cm2 immediately prior to the third procedure. The AVA here was obtained by the continuity equation, which remains the accepted standard for prosthetic aortic valve area: AVA = (LVOT area × VTI_LVOT) / VTI_aortic valve. Accurate LV outflow tract (LVOT) diameter and Doppler velocity-time integrals (VTIs) are essential, and in prosthetic settings careful alignment and multiple windows reduce measurement error.

- Doppler velocity index (DVI): The immediate post-procedure DVI of 0.24 reflected a low ratio of LVOT to transvalvular velocity and is consistent with severe prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM) or obstruction. DVI is a useful adjunct when LVOT measurements are uncertain.

- Grading intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation (AR): TTE documented grade 2–3 intraprosthetic regurgitation before the third TAVI and no residual regurgitation after the procedure. In prosthetic valves AR grading relies on colour Doppler jet extent, vena contracta when feasible, and pressure half-time, supplemented by haemodynamic data.

- LV remodelling and EF: The patient’s EF had declined during progressive stenosis (from 54% to 42% before the 2022 TAV-in-TAV), improved after the second TAVI, and recovered to 66% by 2024. Serial reductions in LV volumes after the third TAVI demonstrated favourable reverse remodelling, consistent with haemodynamic improvement.

Together, these echocardiographic findings documented severe mixed prosthetic dysfunction (high gradients with significant regurgitation), quantified severity for procedural indication, and provided baseline data for postprocedural comparison.

MSCT for anatomic feasibility and procedural planning
Multislice CT (MSCT) was central to planning the third intervention because it allowed precise measurement of the rigid, multilayered anatomy created by two prior transcatheter valves and direct assessment of the relationship between valve frames and the coronary arteries. The case highlights the specific CT-derived data that guided decision-making:

- Lumen within prior valve: MSCT showed a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm inside the existing Evolut frame, confirming severe anatomic narrowing that correlated with the haemodynamic obstruction. This measurement directly influenced valve sizing and the choice of a device with favourable supra‑annular leaflets.

- Coronary ostial height and sinus of Valsalva dimensions: Accurate measurements of coronary ostial height above the annulus and the sinus dimensions were used to estimate the risk of coronary obstruction after adding another prosthetic layer. Adequate sinus width and ostial height reduce the likelihood that a displaced native or prosthetic leaflet (or a neo‑skirt formed by overlapping frames) will cover the coronary ostium.

- Valve-to‑coronary (VTC) distance and anticipated neo‑skirt location: The team measured the expected distance from the anticipated prosthesis skirt (the “neo‑skirt” created by displaced leaflets and frame tissue) to the coronary ostia. A sufficiently large VTC supported the decision to proceed without routine coronary protection. CT also allowed virtual modelling of how the new valve would sit relative to the Evolut leaflets and where the neo‑skirt would project.

- Implantation depth planning and commissural alignment: CT was used to plan a relatively high implantation, aligning the new valve with the Evolut leaflets to avoid creating a large neo‑skirt that could impinge the coronaries and to preserve the largest achievable effective orifice area. The imaging allowed identification of fluoroscopic landmarks for commissural alignment during deployment, an important step to preserve coronary access in a multi‑frame situation.

Frame–frame interaction analysis
A core component of CT planning was assessment of how the new valve would interact mechanically with the existing Lotus and Evolut frames. Important considerations included:

- Impact on orifice area: Overlapping frames and trapped leaflets can fold inward and narrow the valve orifice. The team used CT to select a valve whose supra‑annular leaflet position and lower frame height (Acurate neo2) would maximise effective orifice area when seated at the planned depth and to predict whether partial expansion of the Evolut frame from high‑pressure post‑dilatation could improve orifice geometry.

- Coronary access and flow: CT evaluation of commissural relationships and frame meshes helped estimate the likelihood that the cumulative mesh burden would obscure coronary ostia or complicate future coronary interventions. The planned commissural alignment aimed to position the new leaflets between the coronary ostia.

Exclusion of active infection
Because the patient had recent Enterococcus faecalis PVE, ruling out active infection was mandatory before reintervention. The case used a standard triad:

- Blood cultures: Repeated cultures were negative at the time of planning, supporting microbiological cure.

- Transoesophageal echocardiography: TEE showed no vegetations or paravalvular complications, making residual infective material unlikely.

- MSCT: In addition to anatomic planning, CT helped exclude periannular complications (abscess, pseudoaneurysm) that would favour surgical management. Together these studies provided multidisciplinary reassurance that the infection had resolved and that transcatheter reintervention was reasonable.

Global risk stratification and implications for approach
The patient’s EuroSCORE II of 27.36% and extensive comorbidity (coronary disease with prior PCI, COPD, diabetes, prior GI bleeding and prior pacemaker) quantified prohibitive surgical risk and influenced the heart‑team decision to pursue a transcatheter solution. CT also demonstrated deep integration of prior stent frames into the aortic wall, suggesting that surgical explantation would be technically complex and carry high morbidity, further supporting the transcatheter plan.

Decision regarding coronary protection
A common precaution in TAV‑in‑TAV is routine coronary protection (guidewire and bailout stent or prepositioned catheters) to rapidly restore flow if obstruction occurs. In this case, CT-derived measurements—sufficient sinus dimensions, acceptable coronary ostial heights, and an anticipated neo‑skirt that would not impinge the coronaries—combined with a planned high implantation and commissural alignment strategy led the team to proceed without routine coronary protection. This decision was explicitly based on patient‑specific anatomic data rather than on a fixed protocol.

In summary, the diagnostic workup combined serial echocardiography for haemodynamic quantification with high‑resolution MSCT for three‑dimensional planning. The imaging allowed confirmation of infection resolution, accurate assessment of stenosis and regurgitation, prediction of frame–frame and valve–coronary interactions, and individualized decisions on device selection (Acurate neo2), implantation depth, commissural alignment and the need (or lack thereof) for coronary protection. These steps illustrate how detailed CT‑guided planning converts complex anatomy and high procedural risk into a feasible, strategy‑driven transcatheter approach.

## Third TAVI: Strategy, Device Selection, and Procedural Execution

In this patient with two prior transcatheter valves and a recent, treated episode of prosthetic valve endocarditis, the procedural plan for the third TAVI prioritized three interdependent goals: preserve coronary perfusion and future coronary access, maximize the effective orifice area (EOA) to avoid severe patient–prosthesis mismatch, and deploy the new device without provoking recurrent infection or device-related complications. Achieving those goals in a triple-stented anatomy required device selection driven by anatomic constraints, careful vascular and hemodynamic management, intraprocedural testing of expansion and coronary flow, deliberate implantation depth and orientation, and post‑deployment optimization.

Device choice and rationale
The team selected a self‑expanding Acurate neo2 size S for several case‑specific reasons. Its supra‑annular leaflet position offers better effective orifice area relative to many intra‑annular designs in narrow or constrained landing zones, an important consideration when multiple prior frames reduce the available lumen. The Acurate neo2 has a relatively low stent height and a design amenable to achieving commissural alignment, both of which help reduce the cumulative metal/leaflet mesh that could impede coronary access. In this patient the MSCT demonstrated a very small lumen (minimal diameter 13.7 mm inside the Evolut frame); therefore a supra‑annular valve with favorable hemodynamics in tight anatomies was preferred to maximize transvalvular flow after implantation.

Access, monitoring and hemodynamic control
Given the high procedural complexity but desire to minimize general anesthesia risks, the procedure was performed under local anesthesia with conscious sedation. Left radial arterial access provided a separate arterial route for aortography and potential catheter‑based coronary protection if needed, while left femoral access served as the primary large‑bore approach for valve delivery and a pigtail for angiography. Rapid ventricular pacing was used during balloon predilation and post‑dilation to transiently reduce cardiac output and motion, allowing more controlled balloon expansion and valve positioning. Continuous hemodynamic monitoring and readiness for conversion to more invasive support were part of the plan given the patient’s prior heart failure and the risk of acute hemodynamic deterioration during valve manipulation.

Predilation strategy and coronary safety assessment
A 22/40 mm balloon was used for predilation under rapid pacing. In this context predilation had several purposes: to disrupt rigid or fibrotic leaflets (which in this patient had likely been stiffened by prior degeneration and the prior infective episode), to test the mechanical compliance of the layered frames and landing zone, and to determine whether expansion would impinge on the coronary ostia. Simultaneous aortography during balloon inflation allowed direct visualization of coronary flow and was used to confirm there was no transient obstruction. This intraprocedural test gave real‑time assurance that proceeding without prophylactic coronary protection (wires or snorkel stents) was acceptable for this anatomy.

High implantation aligned to the Evolut leaflets
Implantation depth and axial positioning were planned based on CT modelling and fluoroscopic landmarks to align the new valve commissures with the Evolut valve leaflets and to position the prosthesis relatively high within the frame. The rationale was twofold. First, by seating the Acurate neo2 at the level of the Evolut leaflets, the procedure intended to pinch the restrictive existing leaflets outward against the prior frame, preventing them from prolapsing inward and forming an obstructing neo‑skirt that would reduce EOA. This strategy reduces the amount of leaflet material that contributes to obstruction within the new valve orifice. Second, a higher implant reduces the chance that a bulky neo‑skirt will overhang the coronary ostia, thereby preserving native coronary perfusion and making future coronary access more feasible. Achieving this higher implant required precise depth control at deployment, using fluoroscopic markers correlated with the preprocedural CT plan.

Commissural alignment technique
Because multiple stent frames can create a complex mesh that hinders selective coronary cannulation, commissural alignment was an explicit objective. Preprocedural MSCT was used to identify the rotational orientation of the Evolut frame and the relation of its commissures and leaflets to coronary ostia. During deployment, fluoroscopic landmarks (e.g., specific frame cusps and radiopaque markers on the Acurate delivery system) were used to rotate and orient the valve so that its commissures lay between prior commissures and avoided overlapping the coronary ostia. While perfect alignment is difficult in redo settings, this deliberate orientation reduces the cumulative obstruction of commissural posts across the coronary trajectories and eases future coronary engagement.

Post‑dilation and mechanical interaction with prior frames
After valve release, controlled post‑dilation with a 20/40 mm balloon under rapid pacing was performed to optimize expansion of the Acurate neo2 and improve leaflet coaptation and EOA. In this case, post‑dilation likely produced partial radial expansion of the underlying Evolut frame as well, which can improve leaflet mobility of the prior valve leaflets that had been constrained and thus contribute to a better transvalvular flow profile. The team intentionally avoided aggressive or off‑label maneuvers such as intentional fracture unless clearly necessary, balancing the potential hemodynamic benefit against the risk of structural damage to adjacent aortic tissue or the need for concomitant surgical bailout, particularly in a patient with prior infection and heavily integrated frames.

Immediate procedural endpoints
The procedural goals were confirmed intra‑procedurally and on immediate post‑deployment angiography and echocardiography. Correct positioning was verified fluoroscopically and by the absence of paravalvular leak on aortography. Coronary perfusion was visually confirmed during balloon testing and after valve deployment. No paravalvular or transvalvular regurgitation was seen on final angiography, indicating good leaflet function and adequate sealing. Hemodynamic improvement was anticipated, but high immediate transvalvular gradient can occur due to small residual lumen, incomplete valve expansion, or reactive factors; serial echocardiographic follow‑up was planned to document early remodeling and gradient reduction, which indeed occurred in this case over the subsequent month.

Summary
In summary, the third TAVI in this patient combined CT‑guided device selection and orientation, intraprocedural testing of coronary patency, high supra‑annular implantation aligned to prior leaflets, and careful post‑dilatation to optimize lumen and leaflet motion. The Acurate neo2 was chosen for its supra‑annular design and facilitation of commissural alignment, and the stepwise approach—predilation with angiographic coronary assessment, precise implantation depth and orientation, followed by controlled post‑dilation—addressed the unique challenges of a triple‑stented aortic root while preserving coronary flow and achieving acceptable midterm hemodynamics.

## Outcomes, Hemodynamic Interpretation, and Follow-up

Immediate post‑procedural assessment met key criteria for technical success by Valve Academic Research Consortium‑3 (VARC‑3): the Acurate neo2 was deployed in the intended position, there was no procedural mortality or need for urgent surgery, and there were no major access‑related or coronary complications. Aortography and transoesophageal imaging confirmed preserved coronary perfusion and the absence of paravalvular leak.

However, the first transthoracic echocardiogram performed the day after implantation documented a paradoxical haemodynamic profile: mobile, well‑opening leaflets with no aortic regurgitation (grade 0), but markedly elevated transvalvular gradients (mean gradient 67 mm Hg), calculated aortic valve area (AVA) 0.9 cm² and a Doppler velocity index (DVI) of 0.24. These Doppler indices meet accepted thresholds for severe prosthesis‑patient mismatch (PPM) and severe stenosis by standard echocardiographic criteria despite an apparently well‑seated and competent prosthesis. Under VARC‑3, this constellation is recorded as device success with adverse haemodynamics rather than immediate device failure.

The subsequent haemodynamic trajectory over the following weeks was favourable and instructive. At 1 month the mean gradient fell dramatically to 27 mm Hg and AVA increased to 1.1 cm²; these improvements were sustained at 3.5 months and at 1‑year follow‑up (mean gradient ~26–27 mm Hg, AVA 1.1 cm², LVEF ~65%). Clinically, the patient experienced reverse left ventricular remodelling, improved exercise tolerance and no recurrent heart failure or infective complications.

Interpreting the early high gradient and later improvement requires integrating procedural and mechanical factors specific to a triple‑stented anatomy. In this case the immediate high gradient most likely reflected a combination of transient under‑expansion or suboptimal leaflet opening dynamics within the layered frames and subacute hemodynamic derangements immediately after deployment. Three mechanisms plausibly contributed to the reduction in gradient over time:

- Optimized commissural alignment: preprocedural CT modelling and careful deployment to align the new valve with the native/Evolut commissures reduced leaflet impingement and enabled a more central jet, increasing effective orifice area once leaflet motion settled.

- Effective post‑dilation: high‑pressure balloon post‑dilation performed at the index procedure improved radial expansion of the Acurate neo2 and likely transmitted forces to the adjacent Evolut frame, increasing the functional lumen of the composite construct.

- Partial radial expansion and mechanical adaptation: gradual mechanical relaxation and settling of overlapping stent frames, along with tissue and leaflet conformation changes after initial implantation, can further enlarge the central flow area over days to weeks, lowering transvalvular gradients.

Taken together, these mechanisms explain why an immediate Doppler‑derived appearance of severe PPM does not always predict a persistently poor haemodynamic outcome after complex valve‑in‑valve procedures, particularly when additional optimization (post‑dilatation) and favourable valve design (supra‑annular leaflet position) are employed.

From a safety and durability standpoint, the case demonstrates an encouraging 1‑year outcome. There were no documented procedure‑related complications per VARC‑3 (no coronary obstruction, no major vascular complication after the index procedure, and no need for reintervention), no residual or recurrent regurgitation, and no clinical or microbiological evidence of recurrent prosthetic valve endocarditis. Echocardiographic parameters at 12 months remained stable (mean gradient ~26 mm Hg, AVA 1.1 cm²) with preserved left ventricular function (EF ≈ 65%), supporting sustained functional benefit.

For clinical practice, this case illustrates two practical lessons: first, early post‑implant Doppler measurements in layered transcatheter prostheses may overestimate permanent obstruction—serial imaging and clinical correlation are essential before declaring procedural failure; second, preprocedural CT guidance, deliberate commissural alignment and intra‑procedural optimization (balloon post‑dilation) can convert an initially haemodynamically marginal result into a durable, acceptable outcome as judged by VARC‑3 performance and 1‑year follow‑up.

## Clinical Lessons and Lifetime Valve Management

This case illustrates several practical principles that should guide decision-making for patients who may require serial transcatheter aortic valve interventions. The most important overarching lesson is that management must be individualized and guided by a multidisciplinary heart team that integrates infection status, surgical risk, and detailed anatomic feasibility rather than relying solely on guideline defaults. In this patient, prior infective endocarditis was fully treated and carefully documented as resolved by blood cultures, TEE and MSCT before reintervention; that information, combined with a prohibitive calculated surgical risk and CT evidence supporting coronary safety and adequate landing zones, tipped the balance in favour of a third transcatheter procedure. For learners, the take‑home is that a history of prosthetic valve endocarditis does not automatically preclude transcatheter reintervention, but it does demand rigorous proof of infection eradication and input from infectious disease specialists and the heart team.

Planning for the patient’s lifetime course of structural valve disease should begin at the time of the first TAVI. Each implanted frame becomes a permanent element that constrains future options: frame layering increases metal and fabric burden, can reduce the effective orifice area (EOA), and complicates coronary reaccess. From the outset, therefore, operators should consider strategies that facilitate future procedures when clinically reasonable for that patient—choosing valve types and orientations that favour commissural alignment, avoiding unnecessary deep implantation, and keeping coronary access in mind when anatomy and clinical context allow. In the presented case, attention to commissural alignment and a high implantation strategy during the third procedure were deliberate attempts to preserve coronary flow and maximise EOA despite two prior frames.

Multislice CT is the cornerstone of planning for redo ViV (and ViV-in-ViV) procedures. Preprocedural CT permits quantitative measurements that directly influence device choice and implant strategy: valve-to-coronary (VTC) distance, coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, sinotubular junction calibre, and the internal lumen orifice of the existing prostheses. Beyond linear measurements, CT-based modelling of the anticipated neo-skirt and how the new valve’s leaflets will interact with prior leaflets and frames is essential to assess the risk of coronary obstruction and to decide whether coronary protection or adjunctive techniques will be required. In this report, CT showed a small remaining lumen within the Evolut frame (minimum 13.7 mm) but adequate clearance of the coronary ostia with the planned implantation depth, which supported proceeding without coronary protection. Practically, trainees should understand that CT findings can change the procedural plan: choice of device (supra- vs intra-annular), decision for preventative coronary protection or BASILICA, and the intended depth of implantation.

Device selection in redo ViV scenarios must balance haemodynamic performance, frame height, and the ability to achieve or preserve commissural alignment. Valves with supra‑annular leaflet position often provide better EOA in constrained annuli and were therefore preferred in this case (Acurate neo2 selected for its supra‑annular leaflets and relatively low frame profile). A lower external frame height can reduce the cumulative obstruction to coronary access posed by multiple stents and limit the vertical extent of a neo‑skirt. Conversely, devices that permit predictable radiographic orientation to facilitate commissural alignment are advantageous when future coronary access is a concern. Learners should note that device choice is a trade-off that must be contextualised to the CT anatomy and the anticipated interaction with existing frames.

Certain adjunctive techniques can improve outcomes in redo procedures but have limitations in triple‑frame settings. High implantation—placing the new valve at or slightly above the leaflets of the previous device—can pin old leaflets against the existing frame and minimise inward leaflet folding that would reduce EOA; this was intentionally used in the presented case. Strategic post‑dilation at high pressure can partially expand an under‑expanded prior frame (as occurred here), improving leaflet motion and central flow without performing intentional frame fracture. However, techniques such as bioprosthetic valve fracture and leaflet‑laceration strategies (BASILICA) have limited and uncertain applicability when multiple concentric frames are present. The mechanics of fracturing an inner or outer frame become unpredictable with layered metallic scaffolds, and leaflet‑laceration may not reliably avert coronary obstruction when a neo‑skirt is multi-layered. Thus, while these tools should be considered during planning, operators must recognise their potentially reduced effectiveness and increased procedural uncertainty in triple‑stented anatomies.

Finally, patients and families must be counselled frankly about future constraints and the realistic limits of therapy after multiple transcatheter implants. Although this patient enjoyed a good 1‑year outcome, cumulative frame layering may make future coronary interventions technically difficult and would leave surgical explantation as a high‑risk, complex option if the current prosthesis fails. Shared decision‑making should include discussion of these long‑term implications—specifically the increasing likelihood of prosthesis‑patient mismatch over successive implants, the potential need for complex coronary wiring or access strategies, and the decreasing feasibility of corrective surgical options. Documentation of commissural alignment and CT datasets should be preserved to aid future operators.

In summary, this case underscores that successful serial transcatheter interventions require early lifetime planning, CT‑driven procedural design, careful device selection with attention to supra‑annular performance and frame profile, and honest counselling about future limits. Multidisciplinary evaluation that integrates infectious disease clearance, detailed anatomic imaging and a realistic appraisal of surgical risk is essential to select patients for repeat transcatheter therapy and to optimise both immediate and long‑term outcomes.
