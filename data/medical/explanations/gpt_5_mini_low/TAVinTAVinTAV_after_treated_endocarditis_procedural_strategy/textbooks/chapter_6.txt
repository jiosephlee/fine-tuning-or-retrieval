Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

Chapter 6: ## Clinical Lessons and Lifetime Valve Management

This case illustrates several practical principles that should guide decision-making for patients who may require serial transcatheter aortic valve interventions. The most important overarching lesson is that management must be individualized and guided by a multidisciplinary heart team that integrates infection status, surgical risk, and detailed anatomic feasibility rather than relying solely on guideline defaults. In this patient, prior infective endocarditis was fully treated and carefully documented as resolved by blood cultures, TEE and MSCT before reintervention; that information, combined with a prohibitive calculated surgical risk and CT evidence supporting coronary safety and adequate landing zones, tipped the balance in favour of a third transcatheter procedure. For learners, the take‑home is that a history of prosthetic valve endocarditis does not automatically preclude transcatheter reintervention, but it does demand rigorous proof of infection eradication and input from infectious disease specialists and the heart team.

Planning for the patient’s lifetime course of structural valve disease should begin at the time of the first TAVI. Each implanted frame becomes a permanent element that constrains future options: frame layering increases metal and fabric burden, can reduce the effective orifice area (EOA), and complicates coronary reaccess. From the outset, therefore, operators should consider strategies that facilitate future procedures when clinically reasonable for that patient—choosing valve types and orientations that favour commissural alignment, avoiding unnecessary deep implantation, and keeping coronary access in mind when anatomy and clinical context allow. In the presented case, attention to commissural alignment and a high implantation strategy during the third procedure were deliberate attempts to preserve coronary flow and maximise EOA despite two prior frames.

Multislice CT is the cornerstone of planning for redo ViV (and ViV-in-ViV) procedures. Preprocedural CT permits quantitative measurements that directly influence device choice and implant strategy: valve-to-coronary (VTC) distance, coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, sinotubular junction calibre, and the internal lumen orifice of the existing prostheses. Beyond linear measurements, CT-based modelling of the anticipated neo-skirt and how the new valve’s leaflets will interact with prior leaflets and frames is essential to assess the risk of coronary obstruction and to decide whether coronary protection or adjunctive techniques will be required. In this report, CT showed a small remaining lumen within the Evolut frame (minimum 13.7 mm) but adequate clearance of the coronary ostia with the planned implantation depth, which supported proceeding without coronary protection. Practically, trainees should understand that CT findings can change the procedural plan: choice of device (supra- vs intra-annular), decision for preventative coronary protection or BASILICA, and the intended depth of implantation.

Device selection in redo ViV scenarios must balance haemodynamic performance, frame height, and the ability to achieve or preserve commissural alignment. Valves with supra‑annular leaflet position often provide better EOA in constrained annuli and were therefore preferred in this case (Acurate neo2 selected for its supra‑annular leaflets and relatively low frame profile). A lower external frame height can reduce the cumulative obstruction to coronary access posed by multiple stents and limit the vertical extent of a neo‑skirt. Conversely, devices that permit predictable radiographic orientation to facilitate commissural alignment are advantageous when future coronary access is a concern. Learners should note that device choice is a trade-off that must be contextualised to the CT anatomy and the anticipated interaction with existing frames.

Certain adjunctive techniques can improve outcomes in redo procedures but have limitations in triple‑frame settings. High implantation—placing the new valve at or slightly above the leaflets of the previous device—can pin old leaflets against the existing frame and minimise inward leaflet folding that would reduce EOA; this was intentionally used in the presented case. Strategic post‑dilation at high pressure can partially expand an under‑expanded prior frame (as occurred here), improving leaflet motion and central flow without performing intentional frame fracture. However, techniques such as bioprosthetic valve fracture and leaflet‑laceration strategies (BASILICA) have limited and uncertain applicability when multiple concentric frames are present. The mechanics of fracturing an inner or outer frame become unpredictable with layered metallic scaffolds, and leaflet‑laceration may not reliably avert coronary obstruction when a neo‑skirt is multi-layered. Thus, while these tools should be considered during planning, operators must recognise their potentially reduced effectiveness and increased procedural uncertainty in triple‑stented anatomies.

Finally, patients and families must be counselled frankly about future constraints and the realistic limits of therapy after multiple transcatheter implants. Although this patient enjoyed a good 1‑year outcome, cumulative frame layering may make future coronary interventions technically difficult and would leave surgical explantation as a high‑risk, complex option if the current prosthesis fails. Shared decision‑making should include discussion of these long‑term implications—specifically the increasing likelihood of prosthesis‑patient mismatch over successive implants, the potential need for complex coronary wiring or access strategies, and the decreasing feasibility of corrective surgical options. Documentation of commissural alignment and CT datasets should be preserved to aid future operators.

In summary, this case underscores that successful serial transcatheter interventions require early lifetime planning, CT‑driven procedural design, careful device selection with attention to supra‑annular performance and frame profile, and honest counselling about future limits. Multidisciplinary evaluation that integrates infectious disease clearance, detailed anatomic imaging and a realistic appraisal of surgical risk is essential to select patients for repeat transcatheter therapy and to optimise both immediate and long‑term outcomes.
