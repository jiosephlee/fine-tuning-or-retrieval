Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Workup and CT-Guided Planning

In a patient with multiple prior transcatheter valves and a recent episode of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), the diagnostic workup serves three linked functions: confirm resolution of infection, precisely quantify the current degree and mechanism of prosthetic dysfunction, and provide detailed anatomic data to predict interactions between a new valve and the existing frames so that device selection, implantation depth and coronary protection strategy can be chosen safely. In this case each of those objectives was addressed using a coordinated, multimodality approach.

Echocardiography — defining haemodynamic severity and ventricular response
Transthoracic and transoesophageal echocardiography were used to quantify stenosis, assess regurgitation, and follow left ventricular (LV) remodelling and function over time. Key parameters reported in the case illustrate typical echocardiographic assessment in prosthetic valve dysfunction:

- Mean transvalvular gradients and effective orifice area (AVA): Serial TTE showed progressive increases in mean gradient (up to 83 mmHg before the 2022 reintervention, then recurrent high gradients with mean 49 mmHg in 2024) and a small calculated AVA of 0.5 cm2 immediately prior to the third procedure. The AVA here was obtained by the continuity equation, which remains the accepted standard for prosthetic aortic valve area: AVA = (LVOT area × VTI_LVOT) / VTI_aortic valve. Accurate LV outflow tract (LVOT) diameter and Doppler velocity-time integrals (VTIs) are essential, and in prosthetic settings careful alignment and multiple windows reduce measurement error.

- Doppler velocity index (DVI): The immediate post-procedure DVI of 0.24 reflected a low ratio of LVOT to transvalvular velocity and is consistent with severe prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM) or obstruction. DVI is a useful adjunct when LVOT measurements are uncertain.

- Grading intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation (AR): TTE documented grade 2–3 intraprosthetic regurgitation before the third TAVI and no residual regurgitation after the procedure. In prosthetic valves AR grading relies on colour Doppler jet extent, vena contracta when feasible, and pressure half-time, supplemented by haemodynamic data.

- LV remodelling and EF: The patient’s EF had declined during progressive stenosis (from 54% to 42% before the 2022 TAV-in-TAV), improved after the second TAVI, and recovered to 66% by 2024. Serial reductions in LV volumes after the third TAVI demonstrated favourable reverse remodelling, consistent with haemodynamic improvement.

Together, these echocardiographic findings documented severe mixed prosthetic dysfunction (high gradients with significant regurgitation), quantified severity for procedural indication, and provided baseline data for postprocedural comparison.

MSCT for anatomic feasibility and procedural planning
Multislice CT (MSCT) was central to planning the third intervention because it allowed precise measurement of the rigid, multilayered anatomy created by two prior transcatheter valves and direct assessment of the relationship between valve frames and the coronary arteries. The case highlights the specific CT-derived data that guided decision-making:

- Lumen within prior valve: MSCT showed a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm inside the existing Evolut frame, confirming severe anatomic narrowing that correlated with the haemodynamic obstruction. This measurement directly influenced valve sizing and the choice of a device with favourable supra‑annular leaflets.

- Coronary ostial height and sinus of Valsalva dimensions: Accurate measurements of coronary ostial height above the annulus and the sinus dimensions were used to estimate the risk of coronary obstruction after adding another prosthetic layer. Adequate sinus width and ostial height reduce the likelihood that a displaced native or prosthetic leaflet (or a neo‑skirt formed by overlapping frames) will cover the coronary ostium.

- Valve-to‑coronary (VTC) distance and anticipated neo‑skirt location: The team measured the expected distance from the anticipated prosthesis skirt (the “neo‑skirt” created by displaced leaflets and frame tissue) to the coronary ostia. A sufficiently large VTC supported the decision to proceed without routine coronary protection. CT also allowed virtual modelling of how the new valve would sit relative to the Evolut leaflets and where the neo‑skirt would project.

- Implantation depth planning and commissural alignment: CT was used to plan a relatively high implantation, aligning the new valve with the Evolut leaflets to avoid creating a large neo‑skirt that could impinge the coronaries and to preserve the largest achievable effective orifice area. The imaging allowed identification of fluoroscopic landmarks for commissural alignment during deployment, an important step to preserve coronary access in a multi‑frame situation.

Frame–frame interaction analysis
A core component of CT planning was assessment of how the new valve would interact mechanically with the existing Lotus and Evolut frames. Important considerations included:

- Impact on orifice area: Overlapping frames and trapped leaflets can fold inward and narrow the valve orifice. The team used CT to select a valve whose supra‑annular leaflet position and lower frame height (Acurate neo2) would maximise effective orifice area when seated at the planned depth and to predict whether partial expansion of the Evolut frame from high‑pressure post‑dilatation could improve orifice geometry.

- Coronary access and flow: CT evaluation of commissural relationships and frame meshes helped estimate the likelihood that the cumulative mesh burden would obscure coronary ostia or complicate future coronary interventions. The planned commissural alignment aimed to position the new leaflets between the coronary ostia.

Exclusion of active infection
Because the patient had recent Enterococcus faecalis PVE, ruling out active infection was mandatory before reintervention. The case used a standard triad:

- Blood cultures: Repeated cultures were negative at the time of planning, supporting microbiological cure.

- Transoesophageal echocardiography: TEE showed no vegetations or paravalvular complications, making residual infective material unlikely.

- MSCT: In addition to anatomic planning, CT helped exclude periannular complications (abscess, pseudoaneurysm) that would favour surgical management. Together these studies provided multidisciplinary reassurance that the infection had resolved and that transcatheter reintervention was reasonable.

Global risk stratification and implications for approach
The patient’s EuroSCORE II of 27.36% and extensive comorbidity (coronary disease with prior PCI, COPD, diabetes, prior GI bleeding and prior pacemaker) quantified prohibitive surgical risk and influenced the heart‑team decision to pursue a transcatheter solution. CT also demonstrated deep integration of prior stent frames into the aortic wall, suggesting that surgical explantation would be technically complex and carry high morbidity, further supporting the transcatheter plan.

Decision regarding coronary protection
A common precaution in TAV‑in‑TAV is routine coronary protection (guidewire and bailout stent or prepositioned catheters) to rapidly restore flow if obstruction occurs. In this case, CT-derived measurements—sufficient sinus dimensions, acceptable coronary ostial heights, and an anticipated neo‑skirt that would not impinge the coronaries—combined with a planned high implantation and commissural alignment strategy led the team to proceed without routine coronary protection. This decision was explicitly based on patient‑specific anatomic data rather than on a fixed protocol.

In summary, the diagnostic workup combined serial echocardiography for haemodynamic quantification with high‑resolution MSCT for three‑dimensional planning. The imaging allowed confirmation of infection resolution, accurate assessment of stenosis and regurgitation, prediction of frame–frame and valve–coronary interactions, and individualized decisions on device selection (Acurate neo2), implantation depth, commissural alignment and the need (or lack thereof) for coronary protection. These steps illustrate how detailed CT‑guided planning converts complex anatomy and high procedural risk into a feasible, strategy‑driven transcatheter approach.
