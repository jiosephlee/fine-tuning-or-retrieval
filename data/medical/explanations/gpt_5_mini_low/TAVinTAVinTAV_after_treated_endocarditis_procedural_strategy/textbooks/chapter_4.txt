Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

Chapter 4: ## Third TAVI: Strategy, Device Selection, and Procedural Execution

In this patient with two prior transcatheter valves and a recent, treated episode of prosthetic valve endocarditis, the procedural plan for the third TAVI prioritized three interdependent goals: preserve coronary perfusion and future coronary access, maximize the effective orifice area (EOA) to avoid severe patient–prosthesis mismatch, and deploy the new device without provoking recurrent infection or device-related complications. Achieving those goals in a triple-stented anatomy required device selection driven by anatomic constraints, careful vascular and hemodynamic management, intraprocedural testing of expansion and coronary flow, deliberate implantation depth and orientation, and post‑deployment optimization.

Device choice and rationale
The team selected a self‑expanding Acurate neo2 size S for several case‑specific reasons. Its supra‑annular leaflet position offers better effective orifice area relative to many intra‑annular designs in narrow or constrained landing zones, an important consideration when multiple prior frames reduce the available lumen. The Acurate neo2 has a relatively low stent height and a design amenable to achieving commissural alignment, both of which help reduce the cumulative metal/leaflet mesh that could impede coronary access. In this patient the MSCT demonstrated a very small lumen (minimal diameter 13.7 mm inside the Evolut frame); therefore a supra‑annular valve with favorable hemodynamics in tight anatomies was preferred to maximize transvalvular flow after implantation.

Access, monitoring and hemodynamic control
Given the high procedural complexity but desire to minimize general anesthesia risks, the procedure was performed under local anesthesia with conscious sedation. Left radial arterial access provided a separate arterial route for aortography and potential catheter‑based coronary protection if needed, while left femoral access served as the primary large‑bore approach for valve delivery and a pigtail for angiography. Rapid ventricular pacing was used during balloon predilation and post‑dilation to transiently reduce cardiac output and motion, allowing more controlled balloon expansion and valve positioning. Continuous hemodynamic monitoring and readiness for conversion to more invasive support were part of the plan given the patient’s prior heart failure and the risk of acute hemodynamic deterioration during valve manipulation.

Predilation strategy and coronary safety assessment
A 22/40 mm balloon was used for predilation under rapid pacing. In this context predilation had several purposes: to disrupt rigid or fibrotic leaflets (which in this patient had likely been stiffened by prior degeneration and the prior infective episode), to test the mechanical compliance of the layered frames and landing zone, and to determine whether expansion would impinge on the coronary ostia. Simultaneous aortography during balloon inflation allowed direct visualization of coronary flow and was used to confirm there was no transient obstruction. This intraprocedural test gave real‑time assurance that proceeding without prophylactic coronary protection (wires or snorkel stents) was acceptable for this anatomy.

High implantation aligned to the Evolut leaflets
Implantation depth and axial positioning were planned based on CT modelling and fluoroscopic landmarks to align the new valve commissures with the Evolut valve leaflets and to position the prosthesis relatively high within the frame. The rationale was twofold. First, by seating the Acurate neo2 at the level of the Evolut leaflets, the procedure intended to pinch the restrictive existing leaflets outward against the prior frame, preventing them from prolapsing inward and forming an obstructing neo‑skirt that would reduce EOA. This strategy reduces the amount of leaflet material that contributes to obstruction within the new valve orifice. Second, a higher implant reduces the chance that a bulky neo‑skirt will overhang the coronary ostia, thereby preserving native coronary perfusion and making future coronary access more feasible. Achieving this higher implant required precise depth control at deployment, using fluoroscopic markers correlated with the preprocedural CT plan.

Commissural alignment technique
Because multiple stent frames can create a complex mesh that hinders selective coronary cannulation, commissural alignment was an explicit objective. Preprocedural MSCT was used to identify the rotational orientation of the Evolut frame and the relation of its commissures and leaflets to coronary ostia. During deployment, fluoroscopic landmarks (e.g., specific frame cusps and radiopaque markers on the Acurate delivery system) were used to rotate and orient the valve so that its commissures lay between prior commissures and avoided overlapping the coronary ostia. While perfect alignment is difficult in redo settings, this deliberate orientation reduces the cumulative obstruction of commissural posts across the coronary trajectories and eases future coronary engagement.

Post‑dilation and mechanical interaction with prior frames
After valve release, controlled post‑dilation with a 20/40 mm balloon under rapid pacing was performed to optimize expansion of the Acurate neo2 and improve leaflet coaptation and EOA. In this case, post‑dilation likely produced partial radial expansion of the underlying Evolut frame as well, which can improve leaflet mobility of the prior valve leaflets that had been constrained and thus contribute to a better transvalvular flow profile. The team intentionally avoided aggressive or off‑label maneuvers such as intentional fracture unless clearly necessary, balancing the potential hemodynamic benefit against the risk of structural damage to adjacent aortic tissue or the need for concomitant surgical bailout, particularly in a patient with prior infection and heavily integrated frames.

Immediate procedural endpoints
The procedural goals were confirmed intra‑procedurally and on immediate post‑deployment angiography and echocardiography. Correct positioning was verified fluoroscopically and by the absence of paravalvular leak on aortography. Coronary perfusion was visually confirmed during balloon testing and after valve deployment. No paravalvular or transvalvular regurgitation was seen on final angiography, indicating good leaflet function and adequate sealing. Hemodynamic improvement was anticipated, but high immediate transvalvular gradient can occur due to small residual lumen, incomplete valve expansion, or reactive factors; serial echocardiographic follow‑up was planned to document early remodeling and gradient reduction, which indeed occurred in this case over the subsequent month.

Summary
In summary, the third TAVI in this patient combined CT‑guided device selection and orientation, intraprocedural testing of coronary patency, high supra‑annular implantation aligned to prior leaflets, and careful post‑dilatation to optimize lumen and leaflet motion. The Acurate neo2 was chosen for its supra‑annular design and facilitation of commissural alignment, and the stepwise approach—predilation with angiographic coronary assessment, precise implantation depth and orientation, followed by controlled post‑dilation—addressed the unique challenges of a triple‑stented aortic root while preserving coronary flow and achieving acceptable midterm hemodynamics.
