Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

Chapter 2: ## Key Pathophysiology and Procedural Risks Relevant to Triple Transcatheter Valves

Understanding why transcatheter valves fail and how layered prostheses change cardiac anatomy is essential for safe planning of a third TAVI, particularly after a prior episode of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). The decisions made in this case—careful CT modelling, selection of a supra‑annular device, high implantation and thorough infection clearance—follow directly from the pathophysiology and procedural risks summarized below.

Structural valve deterioration (SVD) and interaction with frame geometry
Bioprosthetic transcatheter valves commonly fail over time by progressive structural valve deterioration. The principal mechanisms are leaflet fibrosis and stiffening, calcification, and pannus formation (organized tissue overgrowth at the sewing or leaflet attachment zones). These processes reduce leaflet mobility and effective orifice area (EOA), producing increasing transvalvular gradients and symptoms of stenosis. When a new transcatheter valve is implanted inside a prior frame, the mechanical interaction between the old and new frames and between the new frame and the diseased leaflets is crucial. Rigid or inward-folded leaflets from the failed valve can become pinned between frames, worsening restriction unless the new valve can effectively expand or fix the old leaflets against the stent in a way that restores central orifice. Repeated layering increases radial stiffness and limits how much subsequent devices can expand, altering flow dynamics and sometimes producing a residual functional stenosis despite technically successful deployment.

Impact of prior prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE)
PVE adds specific injury mechanisms and additional uncertainty. Infection can directly damage leaflets (causing perforation, tearing or scarring) and can stimulate pannus or fibrotic healing that further restricts motion. After appropriate, guideline‑directed antimicrobial therapy, proceeding with a reintervention is acceptable only when infection is considered eradicated. In practice this means clinical resolution of sepsis, persistently negative blood cultures, and imaging that shows no vegetations, paravalvular abscesses or dehiscence on transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and multislice CT (MSCT). The case illustrates that thorough reassessment with TEE and MSCT and completion of an adequate antibiotic course were prerequisites before deciding on a third TAVI. Active or complicated PVE (ongoing bacteremia, abscess, fistula or prosthetic dehiscence) remains a surgical indication in most guidelines and is generally a contraindication to percutaneous reintervention.

Hemodynamic constraints of serial valve‑in‑valve procedures and prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM)
Each additional valve layer narrows the internal lumen available for flow. The effective orifice area is determined not only by the nominal valve size but by the final internal diameter after interaction of all frames and leaflets. This can lead to severe prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM), characterized by a small indexed EOA and high residual gradients. Clinically this shows as high mean gradients and low Doppler velocity index (DVI) despite leaflet mobility of the newly implanted device. In serial ViV situations, planning must anticipate the smallest possible residual lumen (measured on MSCT as minimum lumen diameter within previous frame) and estimate expected EOA and gradients. Strategies to mitigate PPM include choosing valves with supra‑annular leaflet position (which place the effective leaflet orifice above the constrained annular lumen), optimising commissural alignment to maximise central flow, aggressive post‑dilation to expand prior frames (and, in selected native surgical bioprostheses, intentional bioprosthetic valve fracture), and selecting the largest feasible device compatible with coronary safety. The case demonstrates these principles: Acurate neo2 was chosen for its supra‑annular design and favorable haemodynamics, and post‑dilation and partial mechanical expansion of the previous frame contributed to improved gradients over time.

Risk of coronary obstruction and neo‑skirt formation
Coronary obstruction is a feared complication in ViV procedures and is amplified with multiple overlapping frames. Two anatomic determinants are particularly predictive: coronary ostial height and sinus of Valsalva size (which affect leaflet clearance), and the valve‑to‑coronary (VTC) distance—the shortest distance from the expected plane of the displaced leaflet or neo‑skirt to the coronary ostium. Small sinuses, low coronary heights, and VTC distances below commonly used safety thresholds increase the risk of obstruction. Although absolute cutoff values vary between centres and valve types, a VTC <4 mm (or coronary height <10–12 mm with narrow sinuses) is often considered high risk. With layered frames, a new “neo‑skirt” of leaflet tissue or trapped bioprosthetic material can form and extend orifice obstruction above the annulus; overlapping stent cells can also limit the outward displacement of native or prior valve leaflets, increasing obstruction risk. Preprocedural CT mapping of sinus dimensions, coronary heights and predicted leaflet displacement is therefore mandatory; in this patient, CT modelling showed acceptable VTC and clearance, which justified foregoing routine coronary protection.

Coronary reaccess challenges and commissural alignment
Even if immediate coronary obstruction is avoided, future coronary access can be compromised. With each transcatheter frame added, the cumulative mesh burden across the sinuses and coronary ostia increases, making catheter passage more difficult and selective engagement less reliable. Commissural misalignment between frames leads to stent posts and leaflet material landing in front of coronary ostia; even with attempted commissural alignment during deployment, perfect alignment across three different frames is unlikely. This case therefore prioritized commissural alignment and selection of a valve with a frame and leaflet geometry thought to favour preserved coronary access, but it also acknowledges that future coronary interventions could be technically challenging.

Guideline context and individualized decision‑making after PVE
Contemporary infective endocarditis and valve guidelines emphasize surgery for active or complicated PVE (heart failure, uncontrolled infection, prosthetic dehiscence or abscess). However, when infection is eradicated, the decision between redo surgery and transcatheter solutions must be individualized: surgical risk, anatomic feasibility on CT, the likelihood of acceptable haemodynamics and patient preferences all weigh heavily. In patients with prohibitive surgical risk and imaging that excludes residual infection or anatomic contraindications, a carefully planned percutaneous redo (including ViV or, in exceptional cases, ViV‑in‑ViV) can be reasonable. The case exemplifies guideline‑concordant individualized care: after documented microbiological cure and imaging confirmation of no active endocarditis, a multidisciplinary heart team accepted the higher long‑term uncertainty in exchange for a lower immediate procedural risk, and used CT‑guided strategy to mitigate known hazards.

Summary as applied to this case
The patient’s valve failure reflected combined SVD and likely infection‑related leaflet injury; MSCT showed a critically narrowed lumen within the Evolut frame, guiding valve choice and implantation depth. Coronary obstruction risk and future reaccess were carefully evaluated by CT (VTC, sinus size, coronary heights) and mitigated by high implantation and commissural alignment. Antimicrobial therapy had been completed and imaging confirmed eradication of PVE, meeting practical criteria to proceed percutaneously. Despite a high immediate post‑procedure gradient consistent with PPM, post‑dilation and partial expansion of the prior frame improved EOA over weeks, yielding acceptable haemodynamics and clinical benefit at 1 year. This sequence illustrates how SVD, prior infection, and layered anatomy drive both the risks of and the planning requirements for triple transcatheter valve interventions.
