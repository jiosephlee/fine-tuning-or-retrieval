Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Management] In planning a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedure, which CT-derived measurements most directly predict coronary obstruction risk (e.g., coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva width, valve-to-coronary [VTC] distance, and anticipated neo-skirt height), and how would each parameter influence the decision to proceed without protection versus perform BASILICA or planned coronary wiring and chimney stenting?

Direct answer
- The CT measurements that most directly predict coronary obstruction in TAV-in-TAV (and especially TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV) are: coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva (SOV) width (and sinus depth), valve-to-coronary (VTC) distance (sometimes called leaflet-to-coronary distance), and the anticipated neo‑skirt (or displaced‑leaflet) height after the new valve is implanted. Each parameter is used together — not in isolation — to estimate whether the displaced bioprosthetic leaflets (or stacked skirt material) will cover a coronary ostium.

How each measurement guides management (practical thresholds and decision implications)
- Coronary ostial height
  - Why it matters: low ostia are closer to displaced leaflets/skirt and therefore more likely to be occluded.
  - Practical guidance: ostial height <10–12 mm is generally considered high risk for obstruction; 12–14 mm is intermediate and needs closer assessment in the context of valve frame position and SOV size.
  - Management: low-height ostia push you toward coronary protection strategies (planned wiring/chimney stent) or BASILICA when feasible; high ostia (>14 mm) make proceeding without protection reasonable if other measurements are favorable.

- Sinus of Valsalva width/depth (and sinotubular junction)
  - Why it matters: a narrow or shallow sinus cannot accommodate the displaced leaflet or skirt; large sinuses can buffer leaflet displacement and preserve flow.
  - Practical guidance: SOV diameter <30–32 mm (or shallow sinus depth) increases obstruction risk; larger sinuses (>32–34 mm) lower risk.
  - Management: small/narrow sinuses favor preemptive protection (wiring/chimney) or BASILICA; roomy sinuses may allow safe no‑protection strategy.

- Valve-to-coronary (VTC) distance (leaflet-skirt to coronary)
  - Why it matters: VTC quantifies the clearance between the intended final skirt/leaflet plane and the coronary ostium — the single best predictor of obstruction risk in many series.
  - Practical guidance: VTC ≤4 mm (some centers use ≤4–6 mm) is high risk for coronary obstruction; 4–8 mm is intermediate; >8 mm is usually low risk.
  - Management: VTC ≤4 mm → strongly consider protection (BASILICA if leaflet laceration is possible and effective, or planned chimney stenting/wiring if BASILICA not feasible); VTC >8 mm → can usually proceed without protection if other factors benign.

- Anticipated neo‑skirt / displaced‑leaflet height (from CT modelling)
  - Why it matters: the neo‑skirt is the new cylinder of leaflet/skirt material that will sit above the old valve frame; if its top lies at or above the coronary ostia, obstruction can occur even with acceptable ostial height.
  - Practical guidance: if anticipated neo‑skirt projects at or above the coronary ostium, obstruction risk is high regardless of ostial height; if neo‑skirt stops well below the ostium, risk is lower.
  - Management: neo‑skirt that threatens coronaries → plan protection. If neo‑skirt will sit below ostia and VTC adequate, may proceed without protection.

Applying these measurements to strategy choice
- Proceed without coronary protection
  - When: ostial heights comfortably high (>12–14 mm), SOV large/deep, VTC >6–8 mm, and CT modelling shows the neo‑skirt will not reach the ostia. Commissural alignment that preserves coronary access supports this choice.
  - Rationale: low predicted risk; avoids complexities/risks of BASILICA or chimney stents.

- Planned coronary wiring ± chimney stenting (prewired coronary protection)
  - When: intermediate risk (e.g., ostial height borderline 10–12 mm, SOV modest, VTC 3–6 mm or uncertain CT modelling), or when anatomy makes BASILICA technically difficult (heavy frame layering, heavy calcification, uncertain leaflet traversal).
  - How it's done: preposition a guidewire and an undeployed stent in the coronary; if flow is compromised after valve deployment, the stent can be rapidly deployed (chimney).
  - Rationale: provides an immediate bailout that is simpler than attempting leaflet laceration in difficult anatomies; favored when obstruction risk is real but some chance exists that obstruction will NOT occur.

- BASILICA (intentional leaflet laceration)
  - When: VTC is low and CT shows that intact leaflets (native or bioprosthetic) will directly obstruct a coronary ostium, but leaflet laceration is technically feasible and likely to create sufficient flow channel. Often used when the offending leaflet is accessible and not excessively thick or restrained by multiple frames.
  - Limitations in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV: multiple overlapping frames and dense frame apposition can make leaflet traversal or laceration unreliable or unsafe; leaflet length, calcification, and frame geometry affect feasibility.
  - Rationale: when feasible, BASILICA prevents leaflet tissue from forming a continuous barrier to coronary flow and avoids the long‑term issues of chimney stents.

How this relates to the presented case
- The authors used MSCT to measure coronary ostial height, SOV dimensions, VTC distance and to model the anticipated neo‑skirt. Their modelling suggested adequate coronary clearance and that a high implantation of the Acurate neo2 would position the neo‑skirt below the coronary ostia while preserving commissural alignment and future coronary access. Because CT parameters were favorable, they proceeded without coronary protection. This strategy was successful: intra‑procedural aortography confirmed preserved coronary perfusion and post‑deployment imaging showed no obstruction.
- Important nuance: in a triple‑stented anatomy, BASILICA may be less feasible (leaflet traversal and effective laceration can be compromised by layered frames). That concern also factors into favoring CT‑based avoidance of protection when measurements are clearly low risk, or choosing prewired/chimney protection when risk is intermediate but BASILICA is judged technically uncertain.

Bottom line (clinical pearl)
- Use the combination of coronary ostial height, SOV size/depth, VTC distance, and predicted neo‑skirt height from CT modelling to stratify risk: VTC ≤4 mm, ostial height <10–12 mm, small SOV, or neo‑skirt reaching the ostium → high risk → plan protection (BASILICA if feasible; otherwise prewired coronary + chimney). Intermediate values → consider planned wiring with bailout chimney. Favor proceeding without protection only when CT shows adequate VTC clearance, high ostia, roomy sinuses, and neo‑skirt below the coronaries — and when commissural alignment and implantation strategy can further reduce risk, as was done successfully in this case.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Immediately after the third TAVI, the mean gradient was 67 mm Hg with an AVA of 0.9 cm² and DVI 0.24. What are the leading causes of an early high transvalvular gradient after valve-in-valve TAVI, and what tests or imaging (e.g., Doppler indices, acceleration time, leaflet-motion CT, anticoagulation trial for suspected leaflet thrombosis) help distinguish prosthesis–patient mismatch from valve underexpansion, malposition, or thrombosis?

Direct answer
- The immediate high transvalvular gradient after valve‑in‑valve TAVI usually reflects one of three mechanisms (often overlapping): (1) true prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM, an effective orifice area too small for the patient’s body size), (2) mechanical reduction of effective orifice area from valve under‑expansion or malposition (including frame underexpansion, leaflet crowding from an inner/outer frame interaction), or (3) early leaflet thrombosis (hypo‑attenuated leaflet thickening with reduced leaflet motion). Less commonly immediately after implantation residual paravalvular obstruction, severe transvalvular regurgitation with low forward flow, or measurement artifact may contribute.

How to distinguish them — practical tests and imaging, and what each result implies
1) Transthoracic Doppler/TTE (first-line, immediate and serial)
- Key numbers: peak velocity, mean gradient, aortic valve area (continuity equation), indexed AVA (iAVA), and Doppler Velocity Index (DVI = VTI_LVOT / VTI_AV). 
  - DVI ≤0.25 and iAVA ≤0.65 cm²/m² (or iAVA ≤0.6–0.65 severe) indicate severe obstruction/PPM.
  - DVI is helpful because it is relatively flow‑independent; low DVI → true stenosis/PPM or underexpansion.
- Acceleration time (AT) from the LVOT to aortic jet:
  - Prolonged AT (>100–110 ms, depending on lab) suggests a fixed transvalvular obstruction (underexpansion/PPM) rather than isolated regurgitation or low flow.
- Trend over time:
  - Immediate very high gradient that falls after post‑dilatation or in days–weeks suggests mechanical underexpansion with delayed frame expansion (or successful post‑dilation).
  - A rise in gradient over days–weeks after an initially acceptable result raises thrombosis higher on the differential.

In the case: immediate post‑implant DVI 0.24, AVA 0.9 cm², mean 67 mm Hg — values compatible with severe obstruction/PPM by Doppler indices.

2) Fluoroscopy and TEE (intra‑ and early post‑procedure)
- Fluoroscopy: evaluate stent frame expansion, symmetric vs. eccentric expansion, and visible leaflet motion. Underexpanded frame or eccentric frame suggests mechanical underexpansion or malposition.
- TEE: assesses leaflet mobility and can help exclude residual paravalvular leak or obstruction; limited for subtle leaflet thrombus but useful for motion.

3) Multislice CT (gated, 4‑D when possible)
- The diagnostic standard to distinguish underexpansion/malposition from leaflet thrombosis:
  - For thrombosis: look for hypo‑attenuated leaflet thickening (HALT) and reduced leaflet motion (RLM) on 4‑D CT. HALT ± RLM is highly suggestive of subacute leaflet thrombosis.
  - For underexpansion/malposition: measure inner stent frame diameter, minimal lumen area, eccentricity, and relationships of overlapping frames. A frame diameter substantially less than the labelled valve size or preprocedural plan indicates underexpansion.
- In valve‑in‑valve situations CT can also document how prior frames limit the achievable EOA (anatomic PPM).

In the case: preprocedural MSCT showed a minimum lumen diameter of the Evolut prosthesis of 13.7 mm, documenting severe anatomic compromise and guiding the team’s choice and strategy.

4) Therapeutic anticoagulation trial
- Indication: when HALT/RLM is seen on CT or when thrombosis is suspected clinically (subacute rise in gradient) and CT is unavailable or equivocal.
- Trial: full therapeutic anticoagulation (usually warfarin or DOAC per institutional practice) for 3 months with repeat imaging (CT and Doppler). Resolution of HALT/RLM and normalization (or marked improvement) of gradients confirms leaflet thrombosis.
- If gradients improve rapidly with anticoagulation, thrombosis is likely; lack of response suggests fixed mechanical constraint (PPM/underexpansion).

5) Hemodynamic manoeuvres and interventional response
- Balloon post‑dilatation and high‑pressure expansion:
  - Immediate intraprocedural post‑dilatation that lowers the gradient or increases frame diameter points to underexpansion as the dominant mechanism.
  - Commissural alignment and successful post‑dilatation that improve gradients also argue against thrombosis.
- If malposition (too high or low): imaging shows eccentric jet or abnormal implantation depth; correcting malposition is rarely possible percutaneously after deployment.

Putting it together — diagnostic algorithm (concise)
1. Document Doppler indices (DVI, iAVA, AT) and compare to pre‑ and intraprocedural baselines.
2. Inspect fluoroscopy/TEE for frame expansion and leaflet motion.
3. If mechanical constraint suspected (underexpansion/malposition): obtain gated MSCT to measure stent geometry; consider balloon post‑dilatation if safe.
4. If thrombosis suspected clinically or CT shows HALT/RLM: start therapeutic anticoagulation and reimage in ~6–12 weeks (or 3 months) to confirm resolution.
5. If CT shows small annular EO area despite full expansion and indexed EOA consistent with severe PPM, accept PPM and manage medically (or consider further intervention only if symptomatic and feasible).

Application to this case
- Immediately after the third TAVI the patient had severe Doppler obstruction (mean 67 mm Hg, AVA 0.9, DVI 0.24) consistent with severe PPM/obstruction. The team performed balloon post‑dilatation and relied on preprocedural CT planning to maximize expansion. Over the first month the mean gradient fell to 27 mm Hg and AVA rose to 1.1 cm². That time course — early very high gradient with marked improvement after optimisation and over weeks — favors mechanical underexpansion and frame interaction (partial radial expansion of the prior Evolut frame plus successful post‑dilation and supra‑annular valve choice) rather than leaflet thrombosis. If thrombosis had been responsible, one would expect CT evidence of HALT/RLM and prompt improvement with anticoagulation rather than delayed improvement after mechanical optimisation.

Bottom line
- Early high gradient after valve‑in‑valve is most commonly PPM or mechanical underexpansion/malposition; thrombosis is an important but usually subacute cause. Use Doppler indices (DVI, iAVA, AT) for immediate discrimination, fluoroscopy/TEE to assess leaflet/frame mechanics, and gated 4‑D CT to confirm HALT/RLM or quantify frame underexpansion. If HALT/RLM is present or strongly suspected, a trial of therapeutic anticoagulation with repeat CT is appropriate; if underexpansion/malposition is identified and safe to treat, balloon post‑dilatation or other mechanical measures are preferred. In this reported patient, the improvement after optimisation supports underexpansion/frame mechanics as the dominant mechanism rather than thrombosis.

### [Management] After treated prosthetic valve endocarditis due to Enterococcus faecalis, what clinical, microbiologic, and imaging criteria should be met before considering repeat TAVI (e.g., symptom status, negative serial blood cultures, absence of vegetations or paravalvular complications on TEE/CT), and how do contemporary guidelines frame the role of TAVI versus surgery in this setting?

Bottom line
- Repeat TAVI after prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) is an option only when infection has been definitively eradicated, paravalvular anatomy is favourable on high‑resolution imaging, and the heart‑team judges surgery to be prohibitively risky. Before re‑implanting a transcatheter valve you need concordant clinical, microbiologic and imaging evidence of cure plus careful CT/TEE planning to exclude residual infectious complications that would mandate surgery.

Clinical, microbiologic and imaging criteria to meet before considering repeat TAVI (practical checklist)
1) Clinical stability
   - Resolution of fever, systemic inflammatory signs, and heart‑failure decompensation attributable to infection.
   - No ongoing signs of sepsis, persistent embolic phenomena, or new conduction disturbances suggesting extension of infection.
   - In the reported case the patient was afebrile, clinically improved after therapy and remained asymptomatic at follow‑up — an important prerequisite.

2) Microbiologic cure
   - Clearance of bacteremia: serial negative blood cultures after completion of targeted therapy. In practice this means at least two to three sets of blood cultures that are repeatedly negative while off bactericidal therapy (or at least after the planned course has been completed), and no recrudescence on short‑term follow‑up.
   - Completion of an appropriate, guideline‑directed antibiotic course for PVE (commonly 6 weeks for Enterococcus faecalis with targeted therapy) and documentation of treatment adherence. In the case report the patient completed a 6‑week IV ampicillin‑based course with subsequent oral therapy and was not on chronic suppressive antibiotics at the time of reintervention.

3) Imaging evidence of eradication and absence of complications
   - Transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE): no mobile vegetations, no persistent or new paravalvular abscess, pseudoaneurysm or fistula, and no ongoing prosthetic dehiscence. TEE should be compared to prior studies to ensure resolution.
   - Multislice CT (MSCT): complementary assessment to TEE for paravalvular complications, abscess cavities, prosthesis fixation, valve‑to‑coronary relationships and sizing for the next valve. CT is essential when multiple frames are present to plan valve selection, implantation depth and coronary protection.
   - If there is any imaging concern for abscess, persistent vegetation, leaflet destruction or paravalvular leak related to infection, surgery is favored.
   - In the described patient both TEE and MSCT confirmed no vegetations or paravalvular complications and demonstrated acceptable valve‑to‑coronary distances and landing‑zone anatomy — enabling CT‑guided planning for a third TAVI.

4) Timing and durability of observation
   - There is no absolute fixed interval, but most teams wait until a full course of antibiotics is complete and the patient is clinically and microbiologically stable for a period (weeks to months), with no relapse on early follow‑up. The timeline should be individualized by organism virulence, complication history, and surgical risk. In the case report the intervening period between treated PVE (Sept 2022) and the redo TAVI (June 2024) was prolonged and uncomplicated, strengthening confidence of cure.

5) Heart‑team decision and individualized risk assessment
   - Multidisciplinary discussion (cardiology, cardiac surgery, infectious diseases, imaging specialists, and anesthesia). Factors that push toward TAVI include prohibitive surgical risk, extensive stent integration making explantation hazardous, and favourable CT/TEE anatomy. Factors that push toward surgery include persistent infection, valve ring/annular abscess, uncontrolled heart failure from paravalvular destruction, or organisms associated with a high failure rate of medical therapy.

How contemporary guidelines frame TAVI versus surgery after PVE
- Contemporary guidelines (ESC 2025 referenced in the report) continue to recommend surgical valve replacement as the standard therapy for PVE in many situations—particularly when infection is uncontrolled, when there are paravalvular complications (abscess, dehiscence, fistula), or when heart failure is present because of structural destruction.
- However, guidelines recognise that management must be individualised: in patients with treated PVE who have documented microbiologic cure, no imaging evidence of residual infectious complications, and prohibitive surgical risk, a transcatheter approach can be considered by a multidisciplinary heart team.
- Key principles reflected in guideline thinking and illustrated by this case:
   - Do not perform a repeat prosthetic implantation into an infected field. Ensure eradication first.
   - Use high‑resolution imaging (TEE + MSCT) to exclude residual infection and to plan the procedure (coronary protection, commissural alignment, residual effective orifice area).
   - Favor surgery when there is structural destruction from infection that cannot be addressed by a transcatheter valve.
   - Where surgery is prohibitive and infection is cured, TAVI may be an acceptable, patient‑centred alternative — provided careful planning and informed shared decision‑making.

Takeaway applied to this case
- The team followed the expected precautions: completion of a 6‑week antibiotic course for Enterococcus faecalis, serial cultures and imaging (TEE and MSCT) demonstrating no vegetations or paravalvular complications, and a heart‑team determination that surgical explant would be prohibitive. Those criteria supported proceeding with CT‑guided TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV, with tailored strategies (coronary assessment, commissural alignment) to minimise procedural risk and preserve future options. The 1‑year uneventful outcome reinforces that, when strict clinical, microbiologic and imaging criteria are met and multidisciplinary judgment concurs, repeat TAVI after treated PVE can be performed safely in select high‑risk patients.

### [Pathophysiology] What pathophysiologic mechanisms likely contributed to prosthetic valve failure in this patient (post-infectious leaflet injury/fibrosis, progressive structural degeneration, mechanical leaflet restriction), and how can high implantation in a redo TAVI help pin prior leaflets, limit neo-skirt formation, and improve the effective orifice area and coronary flow?

Direct answer — likely contributors to valve failure
- Multifactorial process in this patient. Three mechanisms plausibly acted together:
  1. Post‑infectious leaflet injury and fibrosis: the documented Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) with subsequent healing is specifically called out in the report as a contributor. Infection can cause leaflet cusp damage, fibrosis, scarring and retraction → loss of leaflet compliance and restricted opening, producing stenosis and/or mixed dysfunction even after microbiologic cure.
  2. Progressive structural degeneration of bioprosthetic tissue: the first Lotus valve (2014) and the subsequent Evolut (2022) had already shown structural deterioration pre‑ and post‑TAV‑in‑TAV with rising gradients over time. Ageing, calcification and leaflet wear produce leaflet stiffening and reduced effective orifice area (EOA).
  3. Mechanical leaflet restriction from layered frames and leaflet crowding: with two nested transcatheter frames in place, previously mobile leaflets can be mechanically pinned, folded inward, or prevented from fully opening. The case documents two overlapping valves, a severely narrowed lumen on CT (minimal diameter 13.7 mm) and very small AVA, consistent with mechanical obstruction from frame–leaflet interactions.

How high implantation of the new valve mitigates these problems (mechanical rationale)
- “Pinning” prior leaflets to the stent frame: deploying the new valve higher — at or above the level of the failing Evolut leaflets — forces the old leaflets to lie fixed against the inner surface of the existing stent frames. That prevents inward folding or billowing of degenerated/fibrotic leaflets into the central flow orifice, restoring a more circular, unobstructed flow lumen.
- Limiting neo‑skirt formation below the coronaries: when a valve is implanted deep, the apposed prior leaflet tissue and valve skirt can form a bulky “neo‑skirt” that projects into the sinuses and may extend beneath coronary ostia, reducing valve‑to‑coronary distance and risking coronary obstruction. A higher (supra‑annular) landing positions the new skirt more cranially so less redundant leaflet tissue accumulates in the sinus region, preserving sinus geometry and the native coronary ostia clearance.
- Improving effective orifice area (EOA) and transvalvular flow:
  - By preventing leaflet prolapse/infolding and reducing skirt bulk in the outflow tract, the central EOA increases compared with a situation where prior leaflets fold inward.
  - Choice of a supra‑annular valve (Acurate neo2) gives leaflets a functional orifice located above the annulus where there is more room, yielding better hemodynamics in a constrained annular/lumen space.
  - High implantation combined with targeted post‑dilation can partially expand the prior self‑expanding frame (the Evolut) and optimise the composite stent geometry, further enlarging the lumen and lowering gradients (the authors attribute improved gradients after the third valve to these effects).
- Preserving coronary flow and future access: by minimising neo‑skirt formation and aligning commissures (as the team did), the ostia are less likely to be covered or obstructed and subsequent coronary cannulation is easier despite frame stacking.

How this relates to the presented case
- The report documents resolved infection without active vegetations, severe restenosis with mixed regurgitation and a minimal Evolut lumen on CT. The team deliberately selected a supra‑annular, low‑frame height valve, used CT modelling to plan a high implant at the level of the Evolut leaflets, performed post‑dilation, and achieved restoration of leaflet mobility, elimination of regurgitation and sustained improvement in gradients (mean gradient fell from 67 mm Hg immediately post‑implant to ~26–27 mm Hg at 1 month and 1 year). The authors attribute the haemodynamic improvement to pinning of prior leaflets, minimised neo‑skirt below the coronaries, commissural alignment and partial mechanical expansion of the prior frame by post‑dilation—exactly the mechanisms outlined above.

Clinical pearl
- In multi‑stented TAV‑in‑TAV scenarios, especially after PVE and leaflet fibrosis, careful preprocedural CT planning, selection of a supra‑annular valve and a deliberate high implant can mechanically mitigate leaflet restriction, limit neo‑skirt formation that threatens coronaries, and optimize the composite EOA — but these strategies require individualized modelling and carry trade‑offs for future coronary access and reintervention.

### [Clinical Pearls] Why might a supra-annular, self-expanding valve with relatively low frame height and favorable commissural-alignment capability (e.g., Acurate neo2) be advantageous in a triple-stented anatomy, and how do these design features affect residual effective orifice area, gradients, and the likelihood of maintaining future coronary access?

Direct answer
A supra‑annular, self‑expanding valve with a relatively low frame height and good commissural‑alignment capability (for example, the Acurate neo2 used in this case) is advantageous in triple‑stented anatomy because those design features maximize residual effective orifice area (EOA), minimize transvalvular gradients, and reduce—but do not eliminate—the risk of compromising future coronary access.

Why (clinical reasoning and pathophysiology)
- Supra‑annular leaflet position places the functional valve orifice above the stent frame and above the stacked leaflets of prior prostheses. In a layered (TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV) situation where inner lumen diameter is severely reduced by prior frames and constrained leaflets, a supra‑annular valve creates a larger flow area for the same stent footprint than an intra‑annular design, improving EOA and lowering gradients.
- Self‑expanding nitinol frames exert continuous outward radial force that can partially expand the constrained frame(s) beneath them (as occurred here: post‑implant and post‑dilation improved the Evolut frame expansion). That passive/active radial expansion helps restore leaflet motion of prior valves and increases the final lumen, translating into lower gradients.
- A relatively low frame height limits the vertical extent of a new “neo‑skirt” and the amount of metal/leaflet overlap projecting above the annulus into the sinuses. This reduces coverage of the coronary ostia and lessens obstruction of the sinus, both improving coronary perfusion and reducing the mechanical barrier to future coronary catheter engagement.
- Commissural alignment capability lets the operator orient the new leaflets and posts relative to the existing frames and coronary ostia. Proper alignment minimizes commissure‑to‑ostium overlap and places stent posts away from coronary takeoffs, preserving a clearer path for future coronary access.

How these features affected the reported case
- Valve selection and high implantation strategy (placing the Acurate neo2 at the Evolut leaflet level) were chosen specifically to “fix” the earlier leaflets open against the frame and avoid inward folding that would shrink the orifice. That strategy, combined with the supra‑annular design, produced a sustained improvement in haemodynamics: immediate post‑procedure gradients were high (reflecting early constrained expansion) but after optimisation and partial expansion of the prior frame the mean gradient fell to ~27 mm Hg at 1 month and remained 26 mm Hg at 1 year with AVA ≈1.1 cm² — an acceptable result in this highly constrained anatomy.
- Post‑dilation under high pressure likely augmented the self‑expanding valve’s effect on the underlying Evolut frame, improving lumen diameter and leaflet mobility; this demonstrates how self‑expanding designs can be synergistic with balloon optimisation to restore EOA.
- Commissural alignment was attempted using CT guidance and fluoroscopic landmarks; this helped preserve coronary perfusion during the procedure and reduced the degree of metal/leaflet overlap at the coronaries, supporting preserved coronary flow without prophylactic coronary protection.

Limitations and caveats
- Even with a low‑height, supra‑annular device and careful commissural alignment, cumulative frame mesh and layered leaflets still increase the risk and technical difficulty of future coronary access and interventions. The case explicitly notes the “cumulative mesh burden” may present future challenges.
- Residual prosthesis‑patient mismatch (PPM) remains a real risk in triple‑stented anatomies; supra‑annular and self‑expanding designs mitigate but do not fully eliminate PPM when landing zone diameters are small (the patient’s Evolut minimum lumen diameter was 13.7 mm).
- Commissural alignment is not perfect in all anatomies—CT planning improves odds but cannot guarantee long‑term easy coronary reaccess.

Practical teaching points
- In redo‑TAVI situations with small internal diameters and prior layered frames, prioritize valves whose leaflets sit supra‑annularly and whose frames can impart radial force to expand previous prostheses.
- Choose a device with low frame height and plan commissural alignment when coronary preservation and future reaccess are priorities.
- Combine device selection with CT‑guided implantation strategy and judicious post‑dilation to maximise residual EOA and minimize gradients, accepting that some degree of PPM and future access difficulty may still remain.

### [Management] Which implantation strategies during TAV-in-TAV(-in-TAV) optimize future coronary reaccess (e.g., commissural alignment, high implantation to keep neo-skirt below ostia, orientation to known coronary takeoff, avoidance of commissural posts in front of ostia), and what are the trade-offs with hemodynamics and anchoring?

Answer:

Key implantation strategies that improve coronary reaccess in TAV‑in‑TAV (and TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV) — and the trade‑offs you must weigh — are the following.

1) Preprocedural CT planning and 3‑D modelling first
- Why: defines coronary heights, sinus dimensions, valve‑to‑coronary distance, frame overlap and where a neo‑skirt will sit relative to the ostia.  
- In the case: MSCT modelling guided valve selection, predicted neo‑skirt clearance and permitted proceeding without coronary protection.

2) Commissural alignment of the new valve with prior valves
- Goal: place valve commissures between coronary ostia so commissural posts/struts do not lie in front of the ostia, preserving a direct path for catheter access.  
- How: use fluoroscopic markers, CT-derived angular orientation and manufacturer alignment techniques during deployment.  
- Trade‑offs: precise alignment may be harder with self‑expanding frames or when multiple existing frames distort landmarks; imperfect alignment can still leave mesh over ostia. In the case, alignment was attempted and contributed to preserved coronary perfusion.

3) High implantation to keep the neo‑skirt above/below the coronary ostia (depending on anatomy)
- Goal: implant so the skirt (or leaflets of the newly implanted valve) does not sit in front of the coronary ostia or create a sealed tube that impedes access. High implantation can pin previous leaflets outward and minimize a neo‑skirt below the ostia.  
- Trade‑offs: higher implants sometimes reduce radial anchoring zone, risk valve migration/embolization, can increase paravalvular leak if sealing zone is compromised. In this patient, a deliberately high implant at the Evolut leaflet level helped preserve coronary flow and future access.

4) Choose valve design with coronary access in mind
- Prefer: valves with short frame height, large open cells, supra‑annular leaflets, and predictable commissural markers (facilitates reaccess).  
- Avoid: tall, dense frames with narrow cells that create a “cage” over the sinuses.  
- In the case: Acurate neo2 was chosen for supra‑annular leaflets, relatively low frame height and facilitation of commissural alignment.

5) Orient valve to known coronary takeoff during deployment
- Use CT to mark the leaflet commissure/marker orientation that will place a cell in front of each ostium rather than a post. This is especially important when coronary disease is present or future PCI likely.  
- Trade‑offs: may lengthen deployment time and requires excellent imaging/experience; orientation options are limited by valve delivery system rotation control.

6) Avoid placing commissural posts directly in front of ostia
- When unavoidable, consider adjunctive strategies: coronary protection wires/catheters, ready access for chimney stenting or BASILICA in high‑risk anatomy.  
- Trade‑offs: protective measures add complexity, contrast, procedure time and may have their own risks.

7) Consider post‑dilatation and frame expansion strategies carefully
- Post‑dilatation can improve effective orifice area (EOA) and open the previous frame to facilitate coronary access, as occurred here where ballooning produced partial Evolut expansion and improved gradients.  
- Trade‑offs: aggressive ballooning risks annular injury, coronary obstruction from displaced leaflets, or structural damage to prior frames; intentional valve/frame fracture may help EOA but is not always feasible in layered frames and may worsen coronary access unpredictably.

8) Have coronary protection/BASILICA strategies prepared when anatomy is borderline
- If CT shows small sinuses, low coronary height, or short valve‑to‑coronary distance, plan for guidewire protection or the BASILICA technique to lacerate leaflets that would otherwise obstruct coronaries. In multilayered frames these maneuvers become more complex and may be less reliable.

Practical synthesis and trade‑offs (what you balance):
- Coronary reaccess optimization (alignment, high implant, low frame valve) vs. anchoring/stability: higher or supra‑annular, low‑frame implants may reduce anchoring surface and increase migration risk particularly in heavily calcified or shallow landing zones.  
- Coronary access vs. haemodynamics: choosing a valve to favour access (low frame, alignment) may improve future PCI but could worsen immediate EOA if leaflet position or sealing is suboptimal; conversely, aggressive expansion to maximize EOA (eg, post‑dilation or valve fracture) may push frames/leaflets toward ostia, risking obstruction.  
- Procedural safety vs. future options: taking extra steps (commissural alignment, orientation, CT modelling) lengthens planning and may complicate initial deployment but preserves lifetime management options.

How this played out in the presented case
- The team used CT modelling, selected a low‑frame, supra‑annular Acurate neo2, attempted commissural alignment and performed a high implant to keep the neo‑skirt clear of coronaries. That strategy preserved coronary flow without protection and allowed acceptable haemodynamics after post‑dilatation. Initial post‑op gradients were high (mean 67 mmHg) but improved after further expansion/post‑dilatation and partial mechanical expansion of the Evolut frame (mean 26–27 mmHg at follow‑up). The case illustrates the trade‑off: prioritizing coronary access and preventing neo‑skirt obstruction required careful imaging and a specific valve choice/implant depth, and achieving acceptable EOA required additional post‑dilatation and reliance on partial expansion of prior frame(s).

Bottom line (clinical pearl)
- Use CT‑guided commissural alignment, valve orientation, and a high implant with a valve design that has low frame height and large cells to maximize coronary reaccess. Always balance those steps against the need for secure anchoring and optimal haemodynamics—anticipate the need for post‑dilatation or adjunctive techniques, and plan coronary protection strategies when CT suggests marginal coronary clearance.

### [Clinical Pearls] How do you calculate and interpret the Doppler Velocity Index (DVI) and indexed aortic valve area (AVAi) after prosthetic valve implantation, and what thresholds support severe stenosis and/or severe prosthesis–patient mismatch in a post-TAVI valve-in-valve scenario?

Direct answer
- Doppler velocity index (DVI) = VTI_LVOT / VTI_jet (or equivalently peak LVOT velocity ÷ peak transvalvular velocity when VTI is not used). Use pulsed‑wave Doppler in the LVOT (proximal to the prosthesis) for VTI_LVOT and continuous‑wave Doppler through the prosthetic orifice for VTI_jet.
- Aortic valve area by continuity equation (AVA) = CSA_LVOT × VTI_LVOT / VTI_jet, and the indexed AVA (AVAi) = AVA / body surface area (BSA).
- Thresholds that support severe prosthetic stenosis / severe prosthesis‑patient mismatch (PPM) in valve‑in‑valve/TAVI settings:
  - DVI ≤ 0.25 is consistent with severe obstruction of the prosthetic valve.
  - AVAi ≤ 0.65 cm2/m2 is used to define severe PPM (moderate PPM 0.65–0.85 cm2/m2). (Note: some centres use slightly lower cutoffs in obese patients, e.g. ≤0.60 cm2/m2.)
  - An AVA (absolute) <1.0 cm2 also indicates severe stenosis by standard criteria, but indexing is crucial for PPM assessment after prosthetic implantation.

Clinical reasoning and pitfalls (why and how)
- Why use DVI: In prosthetic valves LVOT diameter measurement (and thus CSA_LVOT) is often unreliable because the “effective” flow‑bearing LVOT can be altered by the stent frame and acoustic shadowing. DVI is a dimensionless ratio independent of CSA measurement and less sensitive to flow, so it is especially useful to detect prosthetic obstruction and to compare across serial studies. A low DVI implies a disproportionately high jet velocity past the valve compared with the LVOT flow — i.e., a tight orifice.
- Why index AVA: After valve implantation the anatomic orifice may be small relative to patient size; AVAi adjusts for BSA and defines PPM, which is prognostically important.
- Measurement technique: obtain PW Doppler VTI in the LVOT immediately below the prosthesis or at a reproducible site (avoid sampling within the stent); obtain CW Doppler across the prosthesis for the maximal jet VTI. Use multiple windows to align the CW beam with the jet. For AVA, measure LVOT diameter from the true flow lumen (if prosthesis obscures it, rely more on DVI or use CT/3D echo to assess CSA). Reproducible technique and averaging are important because small errors in LVOT diameter produce large AVA errors.
- Interpretation in valve‑in‑valve/TAVI contexts: Layered frames reduce the effective orifice area and increase risk of PPM. A DVI ≤0.25 and AVAi ≤0.65 cm2/m2 together indicate severe obstruction/PPM requiring attention; absolute gradients may be high immediately after implantation but must be correlated with DVI and AVAi and with clinical status.

Apply to this case
- Immediately after the third TAVI the patient had DVI 0.24 and AVA 0.9 cm2 (AVAi 0.4 cm2/m2). These values meet the criteria for severe obstruction/PPM (DVI ≤0.25 and AVAi 0.4 ≤0.65 cm2/m2), explaining the very high mean gradient (67 mm Hg) seen immediately post‑implantation.
- Subsequent early optimisation (post‑dilatation and mechanical expansion of prior frame) produced hemodynamic improvement: at 1 month mean gradient fell to 27 mm Hg and AVA rose to 1.1 cm2 (AVAi no longer in the severe PPM range), consistent with relieved obstruction and better effective orifice area. This illustrates that elevated immediate post‑TAVI gradients in valve‑in‑valve scenarios may reflect underexpansion or frame interaction and can improve after optimisation; nevertheless, the initial DVI and AVAi correctly identified severe functional compromise.

Takeaway pearls
- In prosthetic valves and valve‑in‑valve settings, rely on DVI as a robust, flow‑independent screen for obstruction; compute AVA by continuity and index it for BSA to assess PPM.
- DVI ≤0.25 and AVAi ≤0.65 cm2/m2 are thresholds supporting severe prosthetic stenosis/PPM. Combine Doppler indices with imaging (CT, 3D echo) and clinical context to guide management (eg, post‑dilatation, consideration of reintervention).

### [Morning Report] Given the patient’s EuroSCORE II of 27% and prior endocarditis now resolved, what elements of multidisciplinary heart-team reasoning favor a third transcatheter intervention over surgery (e.g., prohibitive surgical anatomy with integrated prior frames, comorbidities like COPD/diabetes/GI bleeding, CT-demonstrated feasibility), and how should lifetime management be incorporated at the time of the first TAVI?

Bottom line
- In this patient a third transcatheter intervention was favored because the heart team judged open redo surgery to be prohibitive: very high operative mortality (EuroSCORE II ≈27%), dense integration of two prior transcatheter frames into the aortic root, and major comorbidities (COPD, diabetes, recurrent GI bleeding) that increase perioperative and recovery risk. CT showed that a further TAV-in-TAV was anatomically feasible with acceptable valve-to-coronary distances and the possibility of high implantation/commissural alignment to preserve coronary flow — making a transcatheter strategy the safer, practicable option once infection was proven eradicated.
Why the team chose transcatheter over surgery (clinical reasoning anchored to the case)
- Surgical risk and anatomy: EuroSCORE II ~27% denotes prohibitive predicted operative mortality. The case documents “extensive stent integration into the aortic wall” after two prior TAVIs; explantation would likely require complex root/ascending aortic replacement with long cardiopulmonary bypass, grafts and re-do sternotomy — much higher morbidity and mortality than repeat TAVI.
- Comorbidities that amplify surgical harm: COPD (higher ventilator/respiratory complications), diabetes (wound/infectious risk), and recurrent GI bleeding (limits transfusion options, complicates anticoagulation) all tilt the risk–benefit ratio toward a less invasive option.
- Infection status and guideline nuance: Active prosthetic valve endocarditis is an indication for surgery in many cases, but here infective endocarditis was treated and follow-up TEE, blood cultures and CT showed no active infection. Guidelines call for individualized decisions when infection is controlled; thus absence of active PVE removed a major barrier to a transcatheter reintervention.
- CT-guided anatomical feasibility: Preprocedural MSCT in this patient demonstrated a minimum inner lumen (Evolut) of 13.7 mm and acceptable coronary heights, valve-to-coronary distances and frame relationships. These CT findings allowed planning for high implantation, commissural alignment and avoidance of coronary protection—practical factors that make TAV-in-TAV technically feasible and safer than open surgery.
- Procedural strategy to mitigate specific TAV-in-TAV risks: The team selected a supra‑annular, low‑frame valve (Acurate neo2), planned high implantation to tack prior leaflets outward (reduce neo‑skirt), performed commissural alignment to preserve future coronary access, and used CT modelling to minimize obstruction risk. These targeted maneuvers address the two major concerns of repeat TAVI — coronary obstruction and severe patient‑prosthesis mismatch — and were feasible in this anatomy.
How lifetime management should be incorporated at the time of the first TAVI
- Think beyond the index procedure: recognize that younger or longer‑lived patients will probably require reinterventions. Decisions at first TAVI should be guided by anticipated lifetime trajectory.
- Valve choice with reintervention in mind:
  - Prefer platforms that facilitate future coronary access and re‑intervention: valves with predictable commissural orientation, fluoroscopic markers for orientation, lower frame heights or designs that allow supra‑annular haemodynamics.
  - For small annuli, avoid choices that will create an irreversibly small final orifice when layered (consider supra‑annular valves or root enlarging strategies if surgery is chosen).
- Commissural alignment and implant technique:
  - Plan and execute commissural alignment at the initial TAVI to preserve coronary access and simplify future V-in-V procedures.
  - Use implantation depth and orientation strategies that minimize the creation of a obstructive neo‑skirt.
- Sizing and annular planning:
  - When feasible, avoid undersizing and plan for maximal effective orifice area (EOA) to reduce future PPM risk. In borderline anatomies, consider surgical AVR with annular enlargement if lifetime risk of multiple VIVs would lead to severe PPM.
- Documentation and imaging:
  - Obtain high‑quality baseline MSCT with detailed measurements (annulus, sinuses, coronary heights, valve-to-coronary distance) and record valve orientation fluoroscopic landmarks at implant for future planning.
- Patient selection and shared decision‑making:
  - For patients with long life expectancy, discuss tradeoffs between surgery (potentially better durability and larger EOAs, but higher upfront risk) versus TAVI and downstream complexity of VIVs.
- Anticipate and plan for future techniques:
  - Consider whether initial strategy will permit leaflet modification techniques (BASILICA) or bioprosthetic valve fracture later; choose devices and implant positions that preserve those options if they may be needed.
- Infection prevention and surveillance:
  - Educate about endocarditis prevention, ensure appropriate dental and infection prophylaxis per guidelines, and maintain a low threshold for surveillance imaging and blood cultures after suspected infections.
Case‑specific summary
- In this reported patient the heart team integrated (1) prohibitive computed surgical risk (EuroSCORE II 27%), (2) prior PVE now resolved, (3) extensive frame integration making explant technically hazardous, (4) major comorbidities increasing perioperative risk, and (5) CT-confirmed feasibility of a strategy (high implant, commissural alignment, valve choice) that minimized coronary obstruction and optimized haemodynamics. Those elements justified a third transcatheter solution, with lifetime management lessons emphasizing valve selection, commissural alignment, documentation and patient counseling at the time of the first TAVI to preserve future options.

### [Management] In a patient with prior transfemoral access complications (pseudoaneurysm requiring surgery), what preprocedural and intraprocedural steps reduce vascular risk during redo TAVI (e.g., CT iliofemoral sizing and calcium/burden assessment, ultrasound-guided puncture, pre-closure strategies, alternative access planning such as transaxillary/carotid, and bailout readiness)?

Clinical answer — how to minimise vascular risk for a redo transfemoral TAVI after a prior femoral complication

Bottom line
In patients with a prior transfemoral vascular complication (pseudo‑aneurysm requiring surgery in this case), every redo-TAVI must treat the iliofemoral axis as a critical part of the procedure — not an afterthought. Use detailed preprocedural CT iliofemoral mapping to choose access (or an alternative), plan puncture and closure, and enter the lab with a predefined bailout plan (surgical team, covered stents, wires). The case reported here illustrates that careful CT-guided planning and strategy permitted safe femoral access and a successful third TAVI despite prior femoral surgery — but only because those access risks were explicitly addressed.

Why this matters (pathophysiology/clinical reasoning)
Prior puncture, haematoma and pseudoaneurysm indicate diseased, scarred and potentially fragile common femoral artery (CFA) and iliofemoral tract. Scar and prior surgical repair increase risk of recurrent bleeding, pseudoaneurysm, access-site infection and failure of percutaneous closure. A small, calcified, tortuous or stenotic iliofemoral pathway raises the risk of sheath trauma, vessel rupture or inability to deliver the valve. Preprocedure imaging and a staged, guarded intraprocedural strategy substantially lower these risks.

Concrete preprocedural steps (must do)
- Dedicated iliofemoral MSCT with arterial-phase reconstructions and centreline measurements. Assess: minimal lumen diameter, eccentric calcification (location and circumferential burden), tortuosity, prior surgical repair or graft, femoral bifurcation anatomy, and external iliac artery disease. In the case report MSCT was used to guide valve planning; extend the same CT to access planning.
- Measure minimal lumen diameter against the required device sheath/introducer (including expandable sheaths). If lumen < recommended for the chosen sheath, avoid transfemoral or plan adjuncts (angioplasty, conduit, or alternate access).
- Qualitative and quantitative calcium scoring of the CFA and pelvic arteries (extensive circumferential calcium predicts failure of percutaneous access/closure and higher rupture risk).
- Review prior operative notes/imaging (location of prior pseudoaneurysm repair, vessel patching, scarring) and palpation/duplex clinic exam.
- Multidisciplinary decision on access route (heart team + vascular surgery/interventional radiology). If CT shows marginal femoral access, preselect alternatives (transaxillary, transcarotid, transapical/surgical) and discuss with patient.
- Preprocedure vascular planning: book vascular surgery and interventional radiology backup, ensure availability of covered stents, large bore sheaths, wires and percutaneous occlusion tools.
- Antiplatelet/anticoagulant strategy tailored to bleeding risk and to facilitate closure (coordinate with haemostasis policy).

Intraprocedural technical strategies (practical steps)
- Ultrasound-guided puncture of the CFA (real‑time US to identify true CFA above bifurcation, avoid scarred zones, avoid anterior calcified plaque). In previously operated femoral arteries, US can document vessel wall layers and choose a healthier puncture site.
- Use a micropuncture kit first, confirm position angiographically (small contrast injection) before upsizing.
- Pre-close with suture-based closure devices (usually two Perclose ProGlide deployed in preclose fashion) when anatomy allows; place pre-closure sutures before introducing large-bore sheath. If femoral artery significantly scarred or heavily calcified, consider surgical exposure instead of percutaneous pre-close.
- Consider an ipsilateral or contralateral crossover wire strategy: leave a contralateral sheath/wire for emergency balloon tamponade or covered stent delivery; have a stiff safety wire parked across iliac artery to facilitate rapid device delivery for bailout.
- Keep ACT in target range to balance thrombosis vs bleeding; reverse anticoagulation promptly if major bleeding occurs.
- Use the smallest feasible sheath or an expandable low-profile introducer. When anatomy is marginal, consider balloon angioplasty of focal stenosis prior to large-bore sheath passage.
- Controlled serial dilation can reduce traumatic entry; avoid forceful sheath advancement against resistance.
- Routine use of fluoroscopic landmarks and roadmapping angiography at the puncture site (especially when surface anatomy is distorted by prior surgery).
- If transradial or contralateral access is required for pigtail or coronary protection (as in this case they used left radial for aortography), plan that in advance to avoid extra femoral manipulations.
- Immediate assessment after sheath removal: angiography of the puncture site, duplex if needed. If bleeding or contained rupture — rapid balloon tamponade with an occlusive balloon, then covered stent deployment or surgical repair.
- Have vascular surgery scrubbed/available when high-risk access is attempted (history of prior pseudoaneurysm repair), and ensure immediate availability of covered stents (e.g., Viabahn/covered balloon-expandable stents) sized for the iliofemoral segment.

Alternative access planning
- If CT shows inadequate lumen, heavy circumferential calcification, extreme tortuosity, or prior complex surgical repair, favour non-femoral access: transaxillary (surgical cutdown or percutaneous), transcarotid (with neurologic protection and surgical backup), or surgical transapical/transaortic approaches. Each has its own risk profile — choose based on CT, comorbidities, and operator expertise.
- In the presented case, the team elected femoral access guided by CT and performed the TAVI successfully; that decision should only be made after CT confirms adequate lumen and acceptable calcification/tortuosity.

Bailout readiness (must have in room)
- Contralateral sheath/wire for rapid occlusion or crossover.
- Occlusive balloon for temporary haemorrhage control.
- Covered stents in appropriate sizes for external iliac and CFA.
- Surgical team on call/standing by for open repair.
- Hemostasis adjuncts (thrombin for pseudoaneurysm percutaneous injection in controlled scenarios with imaging guidance).
- Blood products available.

Documentation and informed consent
- Explain heightened vascular risk given prior pseudoaneurysm and prior vascular surgery; document alternative access options discussed and the planned bailout steps. In the reported case, the Heart Team and shared decision-making were emphasised — replicate that for access decisions.

How the published case reflects these principles
- The case report emphasised CT-guided planning for valve selection and coronary protection; although not detailed for iliofemoral anatomy, the same MSCT planning principles apply to access. The team used left femoral arterial access successfully after CT evaluation and used left radial access for aortography — an example of multimodality access planning to minimise manipulation. The favourable outcome after a prior transfemoral surgical repair underlines that careful preprocedural imaging, multidisciplinary planning and intraprocedural vigilance can allow safe transfemoral redo-TAVI in selected patients.

Clinical pearl
Treat the iliofemoral axis as a second procedure to be planned with the same rigor as valve sizing — CT mapping, ultrasound-directed puncture, pre-closure (or surgical exposure if anatomy unfavourable), and a ready bailout plan (balloon tamponade, covered stents, vascular surgery) are the interventions that most consistently reduce vascular complications on redo TAVI.
