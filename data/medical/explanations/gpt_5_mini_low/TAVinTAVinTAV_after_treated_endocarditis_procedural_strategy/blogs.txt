Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV after treated PVE: When is a third transcatheter valve the right call?

What makes this case striking is its rarity and the tension it exposes between guideline recommendations and real‑world limits. A man in his late 70s—lots of comorbidity, prior pacemaker, multivessel CAD with multiple PCIs, COPD, diabetes and recurrent GI bleeding—had a 2014 Lotus TAVI, a 2022 Evolut TAV‑in‑TAV, a documented Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) treated with a complete antibiotic course, and then severe prosthetic degeneration requiring a third transcatheter implant (Acurate neo2) in 2024. One year out he is asymptomatic with a stable mean gradient ~26–27 mmHg and AVA ~1.1 cm². The case forces us to ask: when is it acceptable to go back through the valve rather than to the OR after prior PVE?

## The central clinical tension
- ESC guidance (2025) sensibly favors surgery for complicated PVE, but recommendations assume acceptable surgical risk and anatomy amenable to explant. This patient’s EuroSCORE II was 27%, prior multiple transcatheter frames were embedded into the aortic wall, and surgical explant would likely have required root/ascending aorta replacement—high morbidity and mortality.
- The counterpoint: endocarditis history heightens concern for residual infection, seeding, and early re‑failure. Reintervening on a recently infected prosthesis risks treating a structural complication before infection is truly eradicated.

This case illustrates a middle path: conservative surgical candidacy + rigorous confirmation of infection resolution + CT‑guided anatomical planning = off‑label, strategy‑driven TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV with good short‑term durability.

## How this team justified a third transcatheter valve
1. Infection control was confirmed before intervention:
   - Completed full, targeted IV antibiotic therapy for E. faecalis.
   - Repeated blood cultures were negative.
   - TEE and MSCT showed no vegetations or paravalvular complications.
   - Multidisciplinary infectious disease input guided stopping suppressive therapy.

2. Surgical risk and technical feasibility favoured transcatheter approach:
   - Extensive comorbidity and high calculated surgical mortality.
   - Prior valves deeply integrated; surgical explant would be complex and high risk.
   - CT showed a minimal Evolut lumen of 13.7 mm but demonstrated feasible valve‑to‑coronary distances with a plan to preserve coronary flow.

3. Strategy was individualized and imaging‑driven:
   - Preprocedural MSCT and 3D considerations directed valve choice (Acurate neo2 for supra‑annular leaflets, lower frame height) and implantation depth.
   - Aim: high implantation aligned with Evolut leaflets to prevent inward leaflet fold, minimise neo‑skirt below coronaries and preserve future coronary access.
   - Commissural alignment and awareness of cumulative mesh burden were explicitly considered.
   - No prophylactic coronary protection was used after CT modelling suggested acceptable risk; intra‑procedural angiography confirmed coronary patency during balloon predilation.

4. Procedural finesse optimised haemodynamics:
   - Balloon predilation and high‑pressure post‑dilation likely partially expanded the Evolut frame and optimised leaflet motion without intentional frame fracture.
   - Immediate post‑procedure gradients were high (mean 67 mmHg) but improved over the following month to the mid‑20s mmHg—consistent with progressive frame expansion and hemodynamic equilibration.

## Practical framework for decision‑making after PVE when facing failed TAVI
The case gives a practical algorithm you can apply in similar patients:

1. Confirm infection control—don’t shortcut this.
   - Documented completion of an appropriate antibiotic course.
   - Repeated negative blood cultures.
   - High‑quality TEE to exclude vegetations, abscess, fistula or dehiscence.
   - MSCT to look for paravalvular extension and to plan anatomy.
   - Infectious disease review and consensus on stopping suppressive therapy if indicated.

2. Multidisciplinary heart team discussion (including ID, imaging, surgery, interventionalists).
   - Emphasise candidacy for surgery vs prohibitive risk.
   - Discuss patient values and tradeoffs; document shared decision‑making.

3. CT‑led anatomical feasibility assessment:
   - Measure valve‑to‑coronary distances, sinus dimensions, anticipated neo‑skirt height.
   - Model frame‑to‑frame interactions and potential for commissural alignment.
   - Consider whether valve choice and implantation depth can preserve coronary flow and acceptable EOA.

4. Procedural planning:
   - Aim for commissural alignment when possible to preserve coronary access.
   - Consider valve type that maximises EOA (supra‑annular designs may help).
   - Plan for balloon optimisation (pre‑ and post‑dilatation) and have bailout strategies ready (coronary protection, BASILICA or surgical backup where feasible).
   - Explicitly discuss the limits of future options if the new valve fails (surgical explant complexity, limited catheter solutions).

5. Timing:
   - Wait until infection is quiescent—no shortcuts. The team waited months after completion of antibiotics with negative cultures and imaging evidence of no active disease.

## What prior PVE should and should not drive in your decision
Should drive:
- A meticulous exclusion of ongoing infection before any reintervention.
- A lower threshold for multidisciplinary review and documented shared decision‑making.
- Close postprocedural surveillance for recurrence of infection.

Should not by itself absolutely preclude TAVI:
- Once infection is demonstrably resolved, PVE history should not automatically force surgery if surgical risk is prohibitive and anatomy lends itself to a planned transcatheter solution.
- Prior PVE does not change the basic mechanical challenges of TAV‑in‑TAV procedures (PPM, coronary obstruction) — those risks must be addressed by imaging and technique rather than by reflexive avoidance.

## Limitations and real‑world caveats
- Triple‑stented anatomy compounds future coronary access and leaves very few rescue options if the new valve fails. This must be discussed with the patient.
- Evidence for TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV—especially after PVE—is sparse; data are case‑based and short to medium term.
- Immediate post‑implant high gradients can improve over weeks as frames expand; treat fluently but watch closely.
- Some strategies used here (valve choice, implantation depth, off‑label device use) require institutional approval and experienced operators.

## Clinical pearls and take‑home points
- Confirm infection is eradicated before reintervention: document completed antibiotics, serial negative blood cultures, negative TEE/MSCT, and ID sign‑off.
- When surgical risk is prohibitive, a carefully planned TAV‑in‑TAV (or even TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV) can be reasonable if CT shows adequate coronary clearance and allows a strategy to preserve EOA and coronary access.
- Use preprocedural MSCT to model frame interactions, valve‑to‑coronary distance and anticipate neo‑skirt height; let CT drive valve choice and implantation depth.
- Aim for commissural alignment and consider supra‑annular valves with lower frame heights to optimise EOA and future coronary access.
- Expect immediate hemodynamics to evolve—high gradients immediately post‑implant may fall over weeks with balloon expansion and frame settling.
- Multidisciplinary shared decision‑making and explicit discussion of limited future options are essential; document consent and rationale.

This case is a reminder that guidelines guide, but clinical reality and individualized imaging‑led planning—combined with rigorous infection control—can justify and safely deliver unconventional solutions in well‑selected, high‑risk patients.



# CT as the playbook: Imaging strategy for TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV

What makes this case interesting
A man in his late 70s with a Lotus valve (2014) followed by a TAV‑in‑TAV with Evolut (2022), then Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis (treated), ultimately received a third transcatheter valve (Acurate neo2, 2024). Preprocedural multislice CT (MSCT) showed a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm through the Evolut frame and helped the team avoid coronary protection, plan a high implant, and optimise commissural alignment. At 1 year the patient is asymptomatic with a mean gradient ≈26 mm Hg and AVA ≈1.1 cm². Triple‑stented anatomies are rare but increasingly encountered; the imaging workup is the difference between feasible, risky, and impossible.

This post walks through a reproducible CT‑centred planning workflow for TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV, anchored to the case above and focused on de‑risking coronary obstruction, maximising effective orifice area (EOA), and preserving coronary access.

## The core problem: what CT must answer before a third TAVI
Each additional valve layer worsens three related problems:
- Creation of a neo‑skirt (cumulative leaflet/frame material) that can seal across coronary ostia.
- Reduction of EOA and increased risk of severe prosthesis‑patient mismatch (PPM).
- Obscuration and displacement of commissures and coronaries, complicating future coronary access.

Preprocedural CT therefore must answer:
1. Are the coronaries safe (or can they be made safe) with another transcatheter implant?
2. What implant depth/valve type will maximise EOA while preserving coronary flow?
3. What is the likelihood that commissural alignment or future coronary access will be achievable?
4. Are there residual signs of infection, paravalvular complications, or thrombus to change the plan?

In this patient the MSCT showed a minimal residual lumen of 13.7 mm through the Evolut—critical data that drove valve selection (Acurate neo2, supra‑annular), a plan for high implantation, and a decision to proceed without upfront coronary protection.

## A reproducible CT workflow for TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV
1. Image acquisition and reconstruction
   - ECG‑gated contrast MSCT with thin slices (≤0.6 mm) and multiphase (systolic/diastolic) reconstructions.
   - Include arch and coronary phases; reconstruct with metal‑artifact reduction if available.
   - If vegetations/endocarditis suspicion, include delayed phase for abscess/thrombosis.

2. Identify and label prior devices
   - Document device types, sizes and radiographic landmarks (seam markers, commissural posts).
   - Localise leaflets of prior valves relative to annulus and coronary ostia.

3. Valve frame lumen assessment (landing zone)
   - Measure true inner diameters at multiple levels through existing frames (annulus plane, mid frame, supra‑annular high points).
   - For this case the Evolut lumen narrowed to 13.7 mm — a key metric informing both valve sizing and expected residual EOA.

4. Coronary assessment
   - Measure coronary ostial heights from the native annulus plane.
   - Measure sinus of Valsalva (SOV) diameters and volumes.
   - Crucially, calculate valve‑to‑coronary (VTC) distances: predicted distance from the final anticipated neo‑skirt/leaflet plane to each coronary ostium.
   - If multiple frames exist, simulate neo‑skirt by projecting the outermost obstructive leaflet plane (not just the nominal annulus) and use 3D tools if available.

5. Frame–frame interaction and commissural relationships
   - Evaluate frame overlap zones and mesh density at coronary levels.
   - Identify prior commissural posts and their angular relation to native coronaries. Plan rotational orientation of the new valve to achieve best possible commissural alignment and avoid covering the ostia.

6. Neo‑skirt simulation and implantation depth planning
   - Model (mentally or with software) where the new leaflets will sit relative to prior leaflets—will they fold inward or form a stent‑leaflet sandwich?
   - Simulate a "high implant" position so the new valve leaflets splay above or at the level of obstructive prior leaflets to minimise neo‑skirt below the coronaries. In the reported case, a high Acurate neo2 implantation fixed prior leaflets against the frame and reduced neo‑skirt formation.

7. Assess the need for coronary protection or BASILICA
   - If VTC ≤4 mm, or sinuses are small, or CT predicts direct leaflet coverage of an ostium, plan upfront coronary protection (guidewire ± stent) or a leaflet laceration strategy (BASILICA). If VTC is larger and the simulated implant preserves clearance (as in this case), coronary protection can be deferred.
   - Remember: cumulative frame mesh may still impede future access even if immediate obstruction risk is low.

8. Plan adjuncts: predilatation, post‑dilatation, frame fracture possibilities
   - Determine balloon sizes for predilatation and post‑dilatation. High‑pressure post‑dilatation may expand prior frames (serendipitous frame expansion in this case improved gradients).
   - Plan for potential bioprosthetic valve fracture if appropriate (be cautious — overlapping frames may prevent effective fracture or make it risky).

9. Document a CT‑driven operative checklist
   - Finalise valve type, size, orientation, intended depth, balloon sizes, and contingency plans (coronary protection, BASILICA, conversion to surgery).

## Practical CT measurement tips and common pitfalls in layered valves
- Pitfall: Blooming and metal artifact → can over‑ or underestimate lumen. Use metal artifact reduction, narrow kernels, and corroborate with fluoroscopy and echo.
- Pitfall: Measuring from native annulus only → in TAV‑in‑TAV there may be neo‑annular planes higher or lower than native annulus. Measure through the narrowest lumen at each prior valve frame level.
- Pitfall: Ignoring leaflet height and outward leaflet displacement → prior leaflet orientation determines the true neo‑skirt. If prior leaflets splay outward, obstruction risk may be less despite small nominal VTC.
- Pitfall: Underappreciating commissural rotation → a new valve deployed without planned rotational alignment can place a commissural post directly in front of a coronary ostium. Use CT to plan rotational markers and fluoroscopic deployment angles.
- Pitfall: Single‑phase CT for coronaries → you may miss dynamic motion—use cardiac‑gated multiphase reconstructions to better estimate minimal VTC.
- Always cross‑check CT predictions with intra‑procedural aortography and test balloon inflation. In this case, simultaneous aortography during balloon pre‑dilatation confirmed coronary patency before valve release.

## When to forgo coronary protection
There is no absolute rule, but CT can make a defensible case to proceed without upfront protection when:
- Predicted VTC comfortably exceeds safety threshold (commonly >4–6 mm depending on valve type and sinus size).
- Sinus of Valsalva dimensions and coronary height can accommodate potential leaflet displacement.
- Simulations (or software) suggest the neo‑skirt will clear the ostia at planned implant depth, especially when high implantation is feasible.
- Commissural alignment can be achieved to avoid placing heavy frame/commissure burden in front of the ostia.
- There is a back‑up plan and the team is prepared for immediate protection or bailout.

In the presented patient, CT supported that a high Acurate neo2 implant would preserve coronary flow and allow the team to proceed without upfront wires or BASILICA; intra‑procedural balloon inflation with aortography confirmed this decision.

## Valve selection and implantation strategy: CT‑driven choices
- Prefer supra‑annular leaflets and low frame height when EOA is a priority in narrow, layered anatomies (Acurate neo2 in this case).
- Consider valve design that supports commissural alignment (marker visibility and rotational deployment control).
- Plan for high implantation to keep the functional leaflet plane above prior leaflet/material whenever possible to limit neo‑skirt formation.
- Anticipate the need for aggressive post‑dilatation to coax radial expansion of prior frames—but be mindful of annular rupture risk and device‑device interactions.

## Realistic expectations after a triple TAV
- Early transvalvular gradients can be high (this case had an immediate mean gradient of 67 mm Hg post‑implant but improved to 27 mm Hg by 1 month). Expect dynamic improvement with frame expansion and remodelling.
- PPM risk is real and must be discussed with the patient. In this case the indexed AVA was small (0.4 cm²/m² immediately after), but haemodynamics improved and symptoms resolved.
- Coronary access may be challenging in the future even if immediate obstruction is avoided—document orientation and imaging to aid future interventions.

## Clinical pearls
- Think “neo‑skirt,” not just “neo‑annulus.” In layered valves the leaflet plane (and how it will fold or be constrained) is the major determinant of coronary obstruction.
- Measure the narrowest residual lumen at each prior frame level—not just at the native annulus.
- Use CT to plan both axial (implant depth) and rotational (commissural alignment) strategies.
- Balloon test inflation with simultaneous aortography is a low‑tech, high‑yield intra‑procedural confirmation of CT predictions.
- High implantation that tacks prior leaflets against a frame can paradoxically increase EOA by preventing inward leaflet folding—if CT shows this is feasible, it can be the winning tactic.
- Even when avoiding upfront coronary protection, have a contingency plan and immediate equipment available for emergent protection or conversion.

## Take‑home messages
- MSCT is indispensable for TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV: it must assess valve lumen at multiple levels, coronary heights, sinus dimensions, valve‑to‑coronary distances and commissural relationships.
- Simulate the neo‑skirt and planned implant depth on CT — this often determines whether coronary protection or BASILICA is required.
- Plan rotational orientation and aim for commissural alignment to preserve coronary access.
- Use balloon test inflation and aortography to confirm CT predictions before committing to valve release.
- Expect early high gradients with potential improvement after post‑dilatation and frame expansion; set realistic expectations with the patient.
- In complex, high‑risk patients (as in this case), a CT‑driven, multidisciplinary plan can make a third TAVI feasible and durable at 1 year.

This case shows that with careful MSCT planning and a defined implantation strategy, even a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV after treated endocarditis can be done safely and effectively. CT isn’t just imaging—it’s the playbook.



# Saving the coronaries in a triple-frame world: Commissural alignment, high implantation, and future access

What makes this case interesting: a man in his late 70s with two prior TAVIs (Lotus → Evolut) who developed Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis and, after successful treatment, underwent a third transcatheter valve (Acurate neo2) — a true TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV. The team used CT-guided planning to intentionally align commissures and implant high, pinning prior leaflets to minimise a neo-skirt and preserve coronary flow and future coronary access. One-year follow-up: the patient is asymptomatic, valve function stable, coronary perfusion intact. This is a neat, practical lesson in how to protect coronaries not only for the index procedure but for the patient’s “lifetime” coronary needs.

Why coronary strategy matters even more in repeat TAVI
- Every additional stent frame and leaflets folded outward/inward increases the risk of coronary obstruction and makes future coronary cannulation harder. In a single TAV, coronary obstruction is uncommon but feared. In double and triple frames, overlayed mesh, displaced leaflets and neo-skirts can create permanent barriers to both flow and catheter access.
- Strategies that prevent immediate obstruction (coronary protection wires, BASILICA, standby surgery) are different from strategies that prioritise preserving future PCI access (commissural alignment, valve choice, implantation depth/height). Ideally you plan for both.

How this case approached the problem (what they did and why it worked)
- Preprocedural MSCT modelling: critical. They measured valve-to-coronary distances, sinus dimensions, and the minimum lumen inside the Evolut (13.7 mm)—this guided valve selection and the decision to avoid upfront coronary protection.
- Valve choice: Acurate neo2 (self‑expanding, supra-annular leaflets, relatively low frame height, favourable mesh geometry) was chosen to maximise effective orifice area and ease commissural alignment in a narrow, triple-stented landing zone.
- Deliberate high implantation: They implanted the neo2 at the level of the Evolut leaflets to “pin” the prior leaflets outward against the existing frame rather than creating an additional neo‑skirt below the coronaries. High implant reduces the skirt that can cover the coronary ostia and helps preserve coronary flow and future access.
- Commissural alignment: Performed using CT-guided fluoroscopic landmarks to orient the neo2 relative to the underlying Evolut commissures. Alignment reduces the chance that frame struts and commissural posts will line up in front of coronary ostia.
- Aggressive balloon optimisation: Pre-dilation and post‑dilation (22/40 pre; 20/40 post) under rapid pacing both tested coronary flow and helped achieve better expansion of the Evolut frame and the new device. No BASILICA or intentional leaflet laceration was used; coronary flow remained unobstructed on intra-procedural aortography.
- No coronary protection: Because MSCT showed adequate coronary clearance and the team planned a high-implant with commissural alignment, they proceeded without prophylactic wires or stents. That reduced procedural complexity & access risks (useful in a patient with prior vascular complications).

Practical commissural alignment principles across platforms
- Know your platform’s radiographic markers and commissural orientation: Some valves (Acurate neo2, Evolut, Sapien, Lotus) have reproducible fluoroscopic markers that predict leaflet/comms orientation. Use these to rotate the delivery system to the desired orientation before deployment.
- Aim to place valve commissures between coronary ostia, not in front of them. In practice this means aligning fluoroscopic markers so commissural posts avoid the 12 and 3 o’clock positions that correspond to coronary ostia on your chosen projection.
- Consider introducing alignment strategy at the index TAVI: in younger patients or known multivessel CAD, initial commissural alignment makes future reinterventions easier.

High implantation: when and how
- Rationale: Pins prior leaflets against the native/previous frame, reduces sub-coronary neo-skirt formation, can preserve flow and access.
- How: Use MSCT to define the level of prior leaflet nadirs and choose a final implant depth that sits the new valve leaflets at or slightly above prior leaflet coaptation. Fluoroscopic landmarks and cusp overlap projections help achieve repeatable height.
- Caveats: High implant can increase paravalvular leak risk if too shallow, and may increase conduction disturbance risk if too deep into the LVOT—balance is essential.

Choosing a platform for a narrow triple-stented landing zone
- Favor supra-annular designs with lower frame height and open-cell geometry if your intent is to minimise mesh burden in front of coronaries and maximise effective orifice area.
- Self-expanding frames can allow some gradual radial expansion of prior frames during post‑dilation; however, beware of incomplete expansion and resultant PPM.
- Consider the known commissural marker characteristics of your chosen valve — some platforms make alignment easier.

Immediate obstruction prevention versus preserving long-term PCI options: contrast
- Immediate protection strategies (when MSCT suggests high risk of obstruction):
  - Coronary wiring and bailout stent pre-positioned
  - BASILICA to lacerate the native/bioprosthetic leaflet(s) and create a channel for coronary flow
  - Surgical backup
- Long-term PCI preservation strategies (best started at index TAVI and continued in reinterventions):
  - Commissural alignment to avoid placing posts in front of coronaries
  - Valve choice with favourable mesh and leaflet position
  - High implantation to avoid creating a neo-skirt over coronary ostia
  - Minimising frame-in-frame obstruction (select devices with larger internal lumen when possible)
- Note: In some cases you need both approaches: use BASILICA or coronary protection for immediate safety and still attempt commissural alignment/high implant to preserve future access.

Practicalities of coronary engagement after triple TAVI
- Expect altered catheter trajectories: conventional Judkins and Amplatz curves may need reshaping or deeper engagement techniques.
- Start with non-selective aortography to identify effective coronary take-off relative to the outer frame.
- Use multipurpose or telescoping guiding catheters and consider using guide extension catheters to reach beyond the most external frame.
- Consider smaller inner catheters and hydrophilic wires to negotiate through mesh.
- If commissural alignment was reasonable, selective engagement may still be straightforward; if not, expect prolonged fluoroscopy and need for multiple catheters.
- Keep radial access available — in this case, left radial arterial access was used alongside femoral access and proved helpful for aortography and support.

What about PPM and haemodynamics? Don’t forget it
- Layering valves risks smaller effective orifice area. In this case, immediate post‑implant gradients were high (mean 67 mm Hg) but improved over 1 month (27 mm Hg) and remained stable at 1 year (26 mm Hg) after optimisation and partial expansion of prior frame.
- Preprocedural CT and realistic expectations about residual gradients are essential; counsel patients that some degree of residual stenosis or PPM may persist despite technical success.

When infection has been treated — is repeat TAVI OK?
- This patient had documented Enterococcus faecalis PVE treated fully with antibiotics and had no clinical/imaging evidence of active infection at the time of reintervention. Their team proceeded with TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after multidisciplinary review.
- Key point: treated, resolved PVE in a prohibitive-surgical-risk patient may be a reasonable context for redo TAVI, but careful assessment for residual infection or paravalvular complications is mandatory.

Take-home messages
- Preprocedural MSCT-driven planning is mandatory in TAV-in-TAV and especially in triple-stented anatomies; measure valve-to-coronary distances, sinus dimensions and internal lumen diameters.
- Prioritise commissural alignment and deliberate high implantation when feasible — these steps reduce coronary obstruction risk and help preserve future coronary access.
- Choose a valve platform with favourable frame height/mesh geometry for reinterventions in narrow or multi-frame landing zones.
- Balance immediate coronary protection strategies (BASILICA, coronary wire/stent) with long-term PCI preservation strategies; sometimes you need both.
- Be realistic about haemodynamics: PPM can occur, and gradients may improve over time after balloon optimisation and frame expansion.
- After successful treated PVE and multidisciplinary clearance, repeat transcatheter intervention can be feasible — but needs rigorous imaging, team discussion, and informed consent about long-term trade-offs.

This case shows that with careful CT-guided planning, valve selection, commissural alignment and high implantation strategy you can safely navigate a triple-frame aortic valve and protect coronary flow — and keep the option of future PCI as open as possible.



# PPM and gradients after redo TAVI: From scary day‑0 numbers to stable mid‑term hemodynamics

What jumps off this case is the dramatic early transvalvular gradient: day‑0 mean gradient 67 mm Hg after a third transcatheter valve (TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV), which fell to 27 mm Hg at 1 month and 26 mm Hg at 1 year with sustained clinical improvement. That trajectory — terrifying on the table, reassuring at follow‑up — is exactly the kind of real‑world phenomenon clinicians will increasingly face as TAVI patients live longer and undergo sequential reinterventions.

This post walks through how to interpret gradients, AVA, indexed AVA and DVI in layered valve anatomies; why immediate post‑implant numbers can be misleading; what procedural maneuvers likely changed the physiology in this patient; and practical tactics to minimise prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM) when planning redo TAVI.

## Why day‑0 gradients can look so bad (and shouldn’t always trigger panic)

In the presented patient the immediate post‑implant TTE showed severe PPM physiology (mean 67 mm Hg, AVA 0.9 cm2, DVI 0.24), yet the valve behaved well clinically and haemodynamics improved markedly over weeks. Factors that explain high early gradients after redo TAVI include:

- Mechanical underexpansion or temporary leaflet restriction. Crimping, interaction of multiple stent frames, and leaflet impingement by prior bioprosthetic leaflets can transiently limit effective orifice.
- Residual frame recoil or incomplete radial expansion of prior devices (especially self‑expanding frames like Evolut) that may yield high gradients until post‑dilatation or delayed remodelling improves expansion.
- Transient oedema, tissue stiffness or inflammation of existing leaflets after manipulation/predilation/post‑dilation.
- Suboptimal Doppler windows or altered flow dynamics immediately after intervention (tachycardia, hemodynamic instability, high cardiac output states) that exaggerate gradients.

Bottom line: very high day‑0 gradients after complex valve‑in‑valve procedures should prompt careful review (rule out malposition, paravalvular leak, valve thrombosis), but do not always mandate immediate re‑intervention if the valve is well seated, there is no regurgitation, and the patient is clinically stable. Early optimisation (balloon post‑dilatation) and short‑term surveillance often change the picture.

## How to interpret the numbers in layered valves: AVA, indexed AVA and DVI

Valve haemodynamics in multi‑stented anatomies demand a multiparameter approach — isolated mean gradient is informative but can be misleading.

- AVA (continuity equation): remains the reference for valve area but is dependent on accurate LVOT measurement. In redo TAVI, LVOT geometry may be atypical and stroke volume changes after valve intervention. Use AVA trends rather than a single value.
- Indexed AVA (AVA/BSA): crucial for PPM assessment. In this case indexed AVA was 0.4 cm2/m2 on day‑0 (severe PPM), but improved to ~0.5–0.6 cm2/m2 as AVA increased to 1.1 cm2. Indexed AVA helps risk‑stratify symptomatic impact.
- DVI (Doppler velocity index = VTI_LVOT / VTI_Valve): less reliant on LVOT diameter and thus helpful when LVOT measurements are unreliable. A DVI <0.25 suggests severe stenosis; in the case DVI was 0.24 on day‑0 — consistent with the high gradient but later improved clinically.
- Use serial studies. Immediate post‑op numbers are a snapshot; trends over days–weeks better reflect functional result, especially after maneuvers like post‑dilation.

Practical tip: when LVOT measurement is uncertain because of prior frames and altered geometry, rely more on DVI and trends in gradient/AVA rather than a single AVA measurement.

## What likely changed between day‑0 and 1 month in this patient

From the case report, several procedural and post‑procedural elements plausibly explain the haemodynamic improvement:

- Balloon post‑dilation (20/40 mm) under rapid pacing was performed. High‑pressure post‑dilation can expand both the newly implanted valve and partially expand an underexpanded prior self‑expanding frame (Evolut), increasing effective orifice.
- High implantation strategy: the Acurate neo2 was positioned to align with Evolut leaflets and to minimise a neo‑skirt that could obstruct flow — this reduces leaflet infolding and preserves flow effective area.
- Commissural alignment: preserving leaflet opening geometry and coronary clearance can also improve central flow dynamics.
- Delayed mechanical settling/remodelling: over days–weeks, frames can seat further, tissue stress relaxes, and leaflets regain motion, reducing gradients.
- No evidence of valve thrombosis or residual infection — ruling these out is essential before attributing gradient changes to redistribution of flow.

So the initial high gradients were not necessarily fixed PPM but a combination of residual underexpansion and transient leaflet restriction that improved after post‑dilation and time.

## Valve fracture: indications and limits in redo TAVI

Balloon‑expandable valve frame fracture (bioprosthetic valve fracture — BVF) can be a powerful tool to enlarge a rigid surgical valve ring, lower residual gradients and alleviate PPM. But in the triple‑stented TAVI context its role is limited:

- BVF is designed to fracture rigid surgical sewing rings or certain surgical bioprostheses — it’s not applicable to all transcatheter frames, and attempting to deliberately "fracture" transcatheter stents is not standard.
- In layered TAVI, you may accomplish effective expansion of prior self‑expanding frames with high‑pressure balloons (as likely occurred here), but true frame fracture is unpredictable and potentially hazardous (risk of aortic injury, coronary compromise).
- Indications for aggressive frame expansion or fracture should consider coronary proximity (risk of moving a neo‑skirt into the coronary ostia), the structural integrity of prior valves, and feasibility of future coronary access.
- In short, BVF is rarely an option in TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV. Careful CT planning must guide whether aggressive balloon expansion is safe; in this case post‑dilation was performed without coronary compromise.

## Tactics to mitigate PPM in redo TAVI — what worked (and generalizable strategies)

From this case and broader practice, the following tactics reduce the risk/severity of PPM and optimise haemodynamics in redo scenarios:

- Preprocedural CT and 3D modelling: essential for assessing residual lumen, valve‑to‑coronary distances, frame overlap, and to pick a valve that maximises EOA.
- Prefer supra‑annular valve designs when feasible (e.g., Acurate neo2 in this case): supra‑annular leaflets often deliver better effective orifice in small landing zones and layered frames.
- Plan commissural alignment to preserve leaflet function and coronary access — aim to avoid creating a neo‑skirt that impedes flow.
- High implantation strategy (when anatomy allows): positions the new valve to avoid folding prior leaflets into the flow orifice.
- Use a deliberate post‑dilation strategy: appropriately sized high‑pressure balloons can expand both the new and prior frames, improving EOA — but always balance with coronary risk.
- Consider valve sizing that optimises EOA rather than simply anatomic fit — in redo cases EOA becomes a key determinant of functional success.
- Multidisciplinary decision‑making and shared consent: patients must understand tradeoffs — acceptable residual gradients may be preferable to high‑risk surgical explant.

## Clinical pearls

- Very high immediate gradients after complex redo TAVI can improve substantially with post‑dilation and time; serial haemodynamic assessment is critical before declaring device failure.
- Use multiple haemodynamic indices — AVA, indexed AVA, DVI and trend data — rather than a single mean gradient to judge PPM in layered valve anatomies.
- Supra‑annular valves and strategic high implantation + commissural alignment are powerful tools to mitigate PPM in small or layered landing zones.
- Valve fracture has limited role in triple‑stent TAVI and can be risky; planned balloon expansion under CT guidance is a more realistic approach.
- Always confirm absence of valve thrombosis or active infection before attributing gradients to mechanical PPM.

## Take‑home messages

- Don’t panic at day‑0: very high post‑implant gradients after TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV may reflect underexpansion or transient leaflet restriction and can improve with post‑dilation and time.
- Assess valve function with a multiparameter approach (AVA, indexed AVA, DVI) and follow trends over days–months.
- Preprocedural CT planning, supra‑annular valve choice, commissural alignment and targeted post‑dilation are key tactics to reduce PPM risk in redo TAVI.
- Valve fracture is rarely applicable in triple transcatheter configurations; proceed cautiously when considering aggressive expansion maneuvers given coronary and aortic risks.

This case is a practical reminder: immediate haemodynamic numbers are only part of the story. Thoughtful procedural strategy and close follow‑up often convert scary day‑0 gradients into excellent mid‑term outcomes.



# Picking the platform and nailing the deployment in tight, layered anatomies

What makes this case worth your coffee break? A man in his late 70s, two prior TAVIs (Lotus in 2014, Evolut TAV-in-TAV in 2022), treated Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis, and then severe restenosis—so the team implanted a third transcatheter valve (Acurate neo2) in 2024. CT showed a 13.7 mm minimum lumen inside the Evolut frame. One year later the patient is asymptomatic with a durable result. This is TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV in real life: a narrow, layered root, prior infection now cleared, and no surgical option that’s reasonable. The takeaway: careful CT-led planning, choice of platform that matches the constrained anatomy, and a deliberate balloon/pacing strategy made a good result possible.

Below I’ll walk through the reasoning that drove the choice of Acurate neo2, how to size and plan when you’re working off an inner-frame lumen, the mechanics of stacking different valve types (Lotus legacy frame, Evolut supra-annular stent, then Acurate), and the pragmatic intra-procedural tactics that mattered in this case.

## Why Acurate neo2 here?
Short answer: supra‑annular leaflets + modest frame height + commissural alignment friendliness = best fit for a tiny, triple‑stented lumen.

Key considerations that pushed the team toward Acurate neo2:
- The inner lumen measured ~13.7 mm on MSCT. That’s very small. You need a valve with good hemodynamics in small effective orifice areas—supra-annular leaflet position helps preserve EOA versus intra-annular designs.
- A lower overall frame height and a design amenable to precise alignment lowers the chance of creating a bulky neo-skirt that could impinge coronaries or further reduce effective orifice area.
- Acurate neo2’s predictable leaflet geometry and radial behavior in constrained spaces favor better leaflet opening when you’re sitting inside another frame (and possibly partially expanding a prior self-expanding frame).
- Commissural alignment capability matters more now than ever when multiple frames threaten coronary access; Acurate’s orientation markers and predictable commissural posts were used to align to the Evolut leaflets and preserve coronary clearance.

Compare that to alternatives:
- Balloon-expandable high‑frame devices (e.g., Sapien family): good radial force but produce a bulky intra-annular skirt and may worsen PPM in a tiny inner lumen; risk of coronary obstruction if skirt migrates above the coronary ostia.
- Self‑expanding tall frames (e.g., Evolut): excellent supra‑annular hemodynamics, but their taller nitinol frames sitting inside an already layered root can create an even higher skirt burden and limit future coronary access. In this patient an Evolut had already been used; re‑deploying another Evolut risks adding more frame height and mesh burden.
- Lotus (legacy): had a different sealing skirt and frame geometry originally implanted; it’s not an option now but its legacy presence matters mechanically for stack interactions.

In short: for this particular anatomy—very small inner diameter, prior self‑expanding Evolut, need to protect coronaries, and goal to maximise instantaneous effective orifice area—Acurate neo2 was a rational, off‑label but strategy-driven choice.

## Sizing: think inner-frame dimensions, not the native annulus
When you’re doing TAV-in-TAV (or TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV), sizing shifts from “annulus-first” to “inner-frame-first.” The inner lumen dictates what the new valve can physically open to.

What this case taught us:
- MSCT gave a minimum lumen of 13.7 mm inside the Evolut. That effectively becomes your landing zone diameter for circularization and leaflet opening.
- Use inner-frame diameter (and its variability across cardiac cycle) to choose a prosthesis size and anticipate residual EOA/PPM. Expect higher gradients and manage patient expectations: immediate post‑implant gradients may be high and then improve (as in this case: mean gradient 67 mm Hg immediately post‑implant, falling to ~26–27 mm Hg at 1 month–1 year).
- When inner lumens are <15 mm, always expect some degree of severe PPM risk. Your goals become: maximise supra‑annular leaflet excursion, avoid further inward folding of old leaflets, and, if feasible, coax radial expansion of prior frames with high‑pressure ballooning—understanding that full frame fracture may not be possible or safe.

Practical sizing steps:
1. Measure internal diameter at the intended landing zone on MSCT (not the outer annulus). Document the shortest cross‑section.
2. Model valve leaflet position relative to previous leaflets and coronary ostia—CT-based virtual valve implantation is gold if available.
3. Choose a valve that provides the best expected EOA for that inner diameter (supra-annular designs typically beat intra-annular ones in tiny lumens).
4. Plan for aggressive post‑dilatation and set expectations for initial high gradients.

## Balloon strategy and rapid pacing — why they matter here
Balloon steps in this case were not ritualistic; they were purposeful.

Pre-dilation:
- A 22/40 mm balloon was used for predilation under rapid pacing (180 bpm). Purpose: test whether a larger orifice could be created, assess interaction with coronaries on aortography, and prepare the layered leaflets to seat the new valve.
- Predilation also provided immediate confirmation that coronary flow remained intact with mechanical displacement of prior leaflets—critical when you’re planning to implant without coronary protection.

Post-dilation:
- Post-dilation with a 20/40 mm balloon at high pressure was used to optimise expansion of the Acurate neo2 and to obtain additional radial expansion of the underlying Evolut stent (frame compliance permitting). This likely contributed to the marked improvement in gradients between immediate post‑implant and 1 month.
- The team did not attempt deliberate bioprosthetic valve fracture (BFx)—likely because the Evolut frame and layered stents may not be amenable to predictable fracture and the risk/benefit in this setting is uncertain.

Rapid pacing:
- High-rate pacing reduces cardiac output and aortic movement, improving balloon stability during pre- and post-dilation and aiding precise deployment of a self-expanding valve when you’re trying to hit an exact implantation depth (level of prior leaflets).
- 180 bpm was used here—choose the minimum rate that achieves sufficient hypotension and stability; in older patients with conduction disease and pacemaker, be mindful of pacing capture and hemodynamic tolerance.

Practical tips for balloon work in layered anatomies:
- Start with cautious predilation to confirm behavior of prior leaflets and coronary flow.
- Use a balloon size that approximates the inner frame lumen — too small is pointless, too big risks aortic injury or coronary compromise.
- Expect post‑dilation to be the workhorse for optimizing frame expansion—use high pressure but weigh the possible benefit of radial expansion of the old frame vs. damage to the aortic root or annulus.
- Have coronary protection plan available (wires, balloons or stents) even if you expect CT to be reassuring; the team here proceeded without protection based on careful preprocedural assessment.

## Anchoring at the level of prior leaflets — the “fix the leaflets open” trick
In layered valve scenarios the old leaflets can fold inward and create a new, smaller orifice or “neo‑skirt.” Anchorage strategy matters.

This case deployed the Acurate neo2 to align with the Evolut valve leaflets—intentionally implanting at the level of the prior leaflets so the new frame holds those leaflets outwards. The mechanics: the new valve’s stent cages the old leaflets against the prior stent frame, preventing leaflet infolding and helping maximize central flow.

Why implant high (at leaflet level)?
- Reduces the creation of a bulky neo‑skirt below the coronary ostia.
- Fixes prior leaflets in an open position rather than allowing them to sag inward and reduce EOA.
- Helps preserve coronary access by reducing the mass of new skirt material that could sit above the sinus level.

Caveats:
- Implanting too high risks valve embolization or paravalvular leak if the lower part of the frame is not well anchored. Balance is critical.
- Precise fluoroscopic and CT-guided depth markers are essential—this was done here using preprocedural modelling and fluoroscopic landmarks.

## How different platforms stack mechanically (Lotus legacy, Evolut, Acurate)
Understanding frame geometry and leaflet position of prior valves is essential when planning the next device.

Lotus (legacy, balloon‑expandable/locking nitinol):
- Shorter frame with an internal sealing cuff. If present underneath another valve, it can act as a rigid inner scaffold. In this patient Lotus was the original valve in 2014; its legacy presence shaped the first reintervention and subsequent frame interactions.

Evolut (self-expanding, tall supra‑annular frame):
- Tall nitinol frame with supra-annular leaflets. It exerts continuous outward radial force, but inside calcified or layered roots its expansion can be limited. Evolut frames can be partially expanded by high-pressure ballooning, improving leaflet motion. Evolut’s tall mesh can threaten coronary access and add mesh burden if another tall frame is added.

Acurate neo2 (self-expanding, supra-annular leaflets, modest frame height):
- Shorter than Evolut, designed for commissural alignment, and with leaflet positioning that tends to preserve EOA in constrained anatomies. When placed inside an Evolut, Acurate’s stent posts can orient to push Evolut leaflets outward at the leaflet level without further raising a neo-skirt high into the sinuses.

Mechanical interactions to anticipate:
- When you place a new valve inside a self‑expanding frame, the new valve must contend with the prior frame’s radial limits. Expect incomplete circularization and accept residual PPM risk.
- Post‑dilation can achieve partial expansion of the older frame (as likely happened here), improving leaflet mobility and gradients, but not necessarily achieving the full diameter of the new valve’s nominal size.
- Commissural alignment between stacked frames is imperfect but matters: try to align new commissures with prior leaflets to reduce mesh overlap over coronary ostia.

## Risk mitigation and the infection angle
This patient had treated prosthetic endocarditis (Enterococcus faecalis) prior to the third TAVI. Important points that guided the decision-making:
- No evidence of active infection on TEE, blood cultures negative, and antimicrobial therapy completed.
- Surgical risk was prohibitive—root replacement would likely have been required due to embedded frames.
- Multidisciplinary heart team discussion and shared decision-making are mandatory in these scenarios.

From a procedural safety standpoint:
- Confirm infection is quiescent with TEE and negative cultures.
- Consider prolonged monitoring for recurrence, but routine long-term suppressive antibiotics were not used here.
- Be cautious: prior endocarditis may have damaged leaflets making them stiffer and less likely to open; this increases the importance of choosing a supra-annular valve and planning for aggressive post‑dilation.

## What actually happened in this case — the important timeline points
- Pre-procedure CT inner lumen: 13.7 mm.
- Predilation with 22/40 mm balloon under rapid pacing; aortography confirmed coronaries OK.
- Acurate neo2 (size S) implanted with commissural alignment to Evolut leaflets; post‑dilated with 20/40 mm balloon under pacing.
- Immediate TTE: no regurgitation but high mean gradient (67 mm Hg) and severe PPM parameters.
- At 1 month mean gradient improved to ~27 mm Hg and AVA increased; at 1 year mean gradient ~26 mm Hg, AVA ~1.1 cm2, EF 65%, patient asymptomatic.

Interpretation: immediate high gradients likely reflect constrained initial expansion and low flow during the procedure; subsequent partial expansion of the Evolut frame and favorable remodeling improved hemodynamics.

## Clinical pearls
- In serial TAV reinterventions, size off the inner-frame lumen, not the native annulus.
- Supra‑annular leaflet valves give better EOA in small inner lumens—consider them preferentially when PPM risk is high.
- Commissural alignment isn’t cosmetic—do it to preserve coronary access and reduce future procedural complexity.
- Predilation is a test: use it to assess leaflet behavior and coronary perfusion under mechanical displacement before committing to valve deployment without protection.
- Post‑dilatation can improve hemodynamics by expanding both the new prosthesis and, to some extent, the older self‑expanding frame—expect gradients to improve over days to weeks.
- In patients with prior PVE, ensure infection is quiescent by TEE and cultures before reintervention; multidisciplinary discussion is essential.

Key takeaways:
- Triple‑stented anatomies are feasible with careful CT‑guided planning and platform selection (Acurate neo2 was chosen here because it offers supra‑annular leaflets with modest frame height and alignment potential).
- Think inner diameter, anticipate PPM, plan for balloon optimization, and prioritize coronary preservation.
- Detailed preprocedural modeling and a clear anchoring strategy (implanting at prior leaflet level) can convert a prohibitive surgical case into a durable transcatheter success.

If you’re about to face a similar triple‑stent scenario: get high‑quality gated CT, model the landing zone virtually if possible, pick the platform that maximises EOA for the inner lumen, plan predilation to test coronaries, and be ready to post‑dilate aggressively—but always with a Coronary Protection Plan on standby.



# Life after serial TAVI and prior PVE: surveillance, prevention, and planning for a limited runway

What makes this case striking is simple — a man in his late 70s with a Lotus valve (2014) → Evolut TAV‑in‑TAV (2022) → Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis treated with a full antibiotic course → Acurate neo2 TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV (2024), and clinically well at 1 year. Triple‑stented anatomies are no longer theoretical: as TAVI volumes expand and patients live longer, we will increasingly face layered prostheses and the hard reality of “limited runway” for future interventions. This demands a proactive, pragmatic follow‑up and shared decision framework that balances surveillance, infection prevention, bleeding risk, and realistic counseling about next options if the valve fails.

Below I outline a practical post‑procedure program — informed by the case above — with what to measure, when to image, how to manage infection prophylaxis and antithrombotic therapy in a patient with prior PVE and recurrent GI bleeding, and how to counsel patients and families about constrained future choices.

## The surveillance backbone: echo cadence and what to track

Serial transthoracic echo is the workhorse. In this case immediate post‑procedure TTE showed no regurgitation but very high gradients (mean 67 mm Hg) that improved to mean 27 mm Hg at 1 month and stabilized around 26 mm Hg at 1 year — a reminder that early hemodynamics can evolve after post‑dilatation and LV remodeling.

Suggested echo schedule after TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV (modify per symptoms):
- Baseline inpatient/early post‑procedure TTE (within 48–72 h).
- Early outpatient TTE at 30 days.
- Repeat at 3–6 months to document remodeling and gradients.
- At 12 months, then annually if stable.
- Sooner if new symptoms, hemoglobin drop, fever, new murmur or changes in exercise tolerance.

What to record and serially compare:
- Mean and peak transvalvular gradients (consistent Doppler windows and VTI technique).
- Aortic valve area (continuity equation) and indexed AVA when possible.
- Doppler velocity index (DVI) — simple, less flow dependent; watch for DVI <0.35 suggesting significant obstruction.
- Presence and severity of transvalvular and paravalvular regurgitation.
- LV size/function (EF, volumes) and signs of reverse remodeling or worsening dysfunction.
- Pulmonary pressures and right heart strain if present.
- Evidence of hemolysis or high‑velocity jets suggesting structural failure.

Clinical pearl: in layered valves, increased gradients may reflect PPM, leaflet restriction from prior frames, incomplete expansion, or thrombosis. Serial trends and complementary imaging are essential — a single elevated mean gradient immediately post‑op may improve (as in this patient).

## Triggers for advanced imaging (TEE, MSCT, or fluoroscopic/angiographic review)

Advanced imaging should be triggered early rather than later if the clinical picture is concerning.

Indications to escalate:
- Fever or bacteremia (any organism) — urgent blood cultures + TEE to exclude recurrent PVE.
- Unexplained rise in gradients (>10–15 mm Hg absolute from prior baseline) or symptoms out of proportion to echo findings.
- New or worsening transvalvular or paravalvular regurgitation.
- Suspicion of leaflet thrombosis, restricted leaflet motion, or abnormal leaflet thickening on TTE.
- Preprocedural planning if thinking reintervention (CT to assess valve‑to‑coronary, frame interaction, minimum lumen diameters). In the presented case MSCT with lumen 13.7 mm guided valve selection and implantation strategy.
- If coronary ischemia is suspected or future coronary access is anticipated — CT to evaluate valve orientation and valve‑to‑coronary distances.

Modality selection:
- TEE: best for vegetations, paravalvular fistula/abscess in suspected endocarditis.
- MSCT: essential for anatomic assessment in reintervention planning (landing zone, frame overlap, coronary clearance, neo‑skirt estimation).
- 4D CT or dedicated software when available to quantify leaflet motion and frame expansion.

Clinical pearl: in serial TAV, CT planning is not a luxury — it is required if considering any further transcatheter intervention.

## Infection prevention and prior PVE — practical approach

Key premise from the case: the patient completed a full curative antibiotic course for Enterococcus faecalis PVE, had negative repeat cultures and imaging, and no chronic suppressive antibiotics were used. That aligns with guideline principles: no routine long‑term antibiotic suppression if infection is eradicated and there is no prosthetic dehiscence/abscess.

Practical measures:
- No routine chronic suppressive antibiotics after documented cure unless specific indications (e.g., uncontrolled infection, high risk of relapse, unresolved perivalvular infection) and ID specialist agreement.
- Low threshold for investigating any fever: obtain blood cultures promptly. In prosthetic valve patients, treat fever seriously.
- For invasive procedures (dental, contaminated soft tissue, urologic/enteric procedures), follow current guideline recommendations for endocarditis prophylaxis — most relevant is dental procedures with manipulation of gingival tissue or periapical region (amoxicillin 2 g PO 30–60 min prior, or alternative regimens if allergic), particularly given prior PVE. Discuss individualization with ID/cardiology.
- Educate the patient on skin/dental hygiene and promptly treating infections (UTI, skin) — Enterococcus can come from GI/GU sources.
- Document prior PVE clearly in medical records and ensure continuity of care: any future clinicians should be aware of the history.

Clinical pearl: prior cured PVE increases vigilance needs, but does not mandate indefinite suppressive antibiotics. Prophylaxis for qualifying procedures should be discussed and documented.

## Antithrombotic strategy in a patient with GI bleeding risk

Balancing stroke prevention, valve thrombosis risk and bleeding is tough — especially with prior GI bleeding (as in this patient since 2022) and multiple prior coronary stents.

Guiding principles:
- Use the least antithrombotic burden compatible with clinical indications.
- Post‑TAVI antithrombotic choices should be individualized: contemporary practice increasingly favors single antiplatelet therapy (SAPT) after TAVI in patients without another indication for OAC or DAPT.
- If the patient has an indication for oral anticoagulation (AF, LV thrombus), use OAC alone rather than OAC + antiplatelet when possible.
- If recent PCI with drug‑eluting stents exists and DAPT is mandated, minimize duration per current PCI/TAVI consensus (short DAPT then de‑escalate), in conjunction with the interventional cardiology and GI teams.

Applying to our case:
- No chronic OAC was described; prior PCI remote (2009–2014) — if no current stent or AF, SAPT (eg, low‑dose aspirin) might be reasonable, but GI bleeding risk may favor no antiplatelet or careful shared decision making.
- Given recurrent GI bleeding, involve GI to optimize bleeding source management (e.g., eradicate lesions, H. pylori if relevant, consider PPI use when on antiplatelet therapy).
- If antiplatelet needed, use lowest effective dose, ear‑marked follow up for recurrent bleeding, and coordinate with GI for surveillance/therapy.

Clinical pearl: in high bleeding risk patients after TAVI without other indications, a strategy of no antiplatelet or single antiplatelet (rather than DAPT) is often favored — personalized after multidisciplinary discussion.

## Counseling about limited future options — be honest and plan early

This is the hardest part. Tell patients and families plainly: every additional valve reduces future options. In the presented case a third transcatheter valve was successful at 1 year, but if it fails, choices are limited and riskier.

Points to discuss and document:
- Explantation/surgical root replacement after multiple TAVIs is technically difficult and high risk — may entail root and ascending aorta replacement, cardiopulmonary bypass, and complex reconstruction. In this patient the EuroSCORE II was 27% and comorbidities made surgery prohibitive.
- Percutaneous bailout strategies exist but have limits:
  - BASILICA (leaflet laceration) is mainly preventive before TAV‑in‑TAV to avoid coronary obstruction; feasibility in triple‑stented anatomy is uncertain and technically more complex.
  - Bioprosthetic valve fracture (BVF) can improve effective orifice in some surgical valves or certain transcatheter frames, but not all frames are susceptible; data on BVF in multiple layered TAVs are limited and may be unsafe in certain configurations.
  - Valve‑in‑valve‑in‑valve interventions may be possible but risk progressive PPM and coronary obstruction.
  - Emerging techniques (leaflet modification, chimney stenting, coronary protection) are case specific and evidence is limited.
- Lifelong planning: document the current valve types, sizes, CT images, and commissural orientation in a durable, shareable format (patient card, regional registry). This aids future teams.
- If the patient is young or likely to need multiple future interventions, discuss this at first TAVI — choose devices, commissural alignment and implantation depths with “future access” in mind.
- Set realistic expectations: short‑ to intermediate‑term functional improvement is likely (as in this case), but long‑term durability beyond a few years is uncertain; prepare for higher risk and fewer interventions later.

Clinical pearl: early documentation (CT, fluoroscopic angles for valve orientation, operative reports) is invaluable. It may be the difference between a feasible future percutaneous strategy and only a high‑risk surgical option.

## Practical checklist for clinics caring for complex serial‑TAVI patients

Before leaving the visit, ensure the following are addressed and documented:
- Baseline TTE copies and clear numeric baselines for gradients, AVA, DVI and LV function.
- CT images and a succinct summary of valve types/sizes/implantation depths and commissural alignment.
- Clear instructions on when to return (fever, new dyspnea, chest pain, syncope, bleeding) and who to call.
- Infection prophylaxis plan for dental/invasive procedures and ID consultation plan if symptoms recur.
- Antithrombotic plan with rationale, GI follow up arranged if prior bleeding.
- Advanced care planning discussion about limited future options and a documented summary of shared decision‑making.

## Take‑home messages
- After TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV, structured surveillance with early (30 day), short‑term (3–6 month) and annual echo is practical; escalate to MSCT or TEE for unexplained gradient change, suspected thrombosis, or fever/bacteremia.
- Track gradients, AVA, DVI, regurgitation and LV remodeling — trend is more informative than a single value.
- Prior cured PVE does not require routine chronic suppressive antibiotics; maintain high vigilance for fever and follow guideline‑based prophylaxis for qualifying procedures.
- Antithrombotic therapy must be individualized in GI‑bleeding‑prone patients — favor the least therapy consistent with thrombotic risk, and coordinate with GI.
- Counsel patients early and clearly: each TAV reduces future options; document valve specifics and CT planning to preserve as many future choices as possible.

This patient’s 1‑year outcome is encouraging, but it’s a reminder that success today increases the responsibility to plan for the harder choices of tomorrow.
