Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# PPM and gradients after redo TAVI: From scary day‑0 numbers to stable mid‑term hemodynamics

What jumps off this case is the dramatic early transvalvular gradient: day‑0 mean gradient 67 mm Hg after a third transcatheter valve (TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV), which fell to 27 mm Hg at 1 month and 26 mm Hg at 1 year with sustained clinical improvement. That trajectory — terrifying on the table, reassuring at follow‑up — is exactly the kind of real‑world phenomenon clinicians will increasingly face as TAVI patients live longer and undergo sequential reinterventions.

This post walks through how to interpret gradients, AVA, indexed AVA and DVI in layered valve anatomies; why immediate post‑implant numbers can be misleading; what procedural maneuvers likely changed the physiology in this patient; and practical tactics to minimise prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM) when planning redo TAVI.

## Why day‑0 gradients can look so bad (and shouldn’t always trigger panic)

In the presented patient the immediate post‑implant TTE showed severe PPM physiology (mean 67 mm Hg, AVA 0.9 cm2, DVI 0.24), yet the valve behaved well clinically and haemodynamics improved markedly over weeks. Factors that explain high early gradients after redo TAVI include:

- Mechanical underexpansion or temporary leaflet restriction. Crimping, interaction of multiple stent frames, and leaflet impingement by prior bioprosthetic leaflets can transiently limit effective orifice.
- Residual frame recoil or incomplete radial expansion of prior devices (especially self‑expanding frames like Evolut) that may yield high gradients until post‑dilatation or delayed remodelling improves expansion.
- Transient oedema, tissue stiffness or inflammation of existing leaflets after manipulation/predilation/post‑dilation.
- Suboptimal Doppler windows or altered flow dynamics immediately after intervention (tachycardia, hemodynamic instability, high cardiac output states) that exaggerate gradients.

Bottom line: very high day‑0 gradients after complex valve‑in‑valve procedures should prompt careful review (rule out malposition, paravalvular leak, valve thrombosis), but do not always mandate immediate re‑intervention if the valve is well seated, there is no regurgitation, and the patient is clinically stable. Early optimisation (balloon post‑dilatation) and short‑term surveillance often change the picture.

## How to interpret the numbers in layered valves: AVA, indexed AVA and DVI

Valve haemodynamics in multi‑stented anatomies demand a multiparameter approach — isolated mean gradient is informative but can be misleading.

- AVA (continuity equation): remains the reference for valve area but is dependent on accurate LVOT measurement. In redo TAVI, LVOT geometry may be atypical and stroke volume changes after valve intervention. Use AVA trends rather than a single value.
- Indexed AVA (AVA/BSA): crucial for PPM assessment. In this case indexed AVA was 0.4 cm2/m2 on day‑0 (severe PPM), but improved to ~0.5–0.6 cm2/m2 as AVA increased to 1.1 cm2. Indexed AVA helps risk‑stratify symptomatic impact.
- DVI (Doppler velocity index = VTI_LVOT / VTI_Valve): less reliant on LVOT diameter and thus helpful when LVOT measurements are unreliable. A DVI <0.25 suggests severe stenosis; in the case DVI was 0.24 on day‑0 — consistent with the high gradient but later improved clinically.
- Use serial studies. Immediate post‑op numbers are a snapshot; trends over days–weeks better reflect functional result, especially after maneuvers like post‑dilation.

Practical tip: when LVOT measurement is uncertain because of prior frames and altered geometry, rely more on DVI and trends in gradient/AVA rather than a single AVA measurement.

## What likely changed between day‑0 and 1 month in this patient

From the case report, several procedural and post‑procedural elements plausibly explain the haemodynamic improvement:

- Balloon post‑dilation (20/40 mm) under rapid pacing was performed. High‑pressure post‑dilation can expand both the newly implanted valve and partially expand an underexpanded prior self‑expanding frame (Evolut), increasing effective orifice.
- High implantation strategy: the Acurate neo2 was positioned to align with Evolut leaflets and to minimise a neo‑skirt that could obstruct flow — this reduces leaflet infolding and preserves flow effective area.
- Commissural alignment: preserving leaflet opening geometry and coronary clearance can also improve central flow dynamics.
- Delayed mechanical settling/remodelling: over days–weeks, frames can seat further, tissue stress relaxes, and leaflets regain motion, reducing gradients.
- No evidence of valve thrombosis or residual infection — ruling these out is essential before attributing gradient changes to redistribution of flow.

So the initial high gradients were not necessarily fixed PPM but a combination of residual underexpansion and transient leaflet restriction that improved after post‑dilation and time.

## Valve fracture: indications and limits in redo TAVI

Balloon‑expandable valve frame fracture (bioprosthetic valve fracture — BVF) can be a powerful tool to enlarge a rigid surgical valve ring, lower residual gradients and alleviate PPM. But in the triple‑stented TAVI context its role is limited:

- BVF is designed to fracture rigid surgical sewing rings or certain surgical bioprostheses — it’s not applicable to all transcatheter frames, and attempting to deliberately "fracture" transcatheter stents is not standard.
- In layered TAVI, you may accomplish effective expansion of prior self‑expanding frames with high‑pressure balloons (as likely occurred here), but true frame fracture is unpredictable and potentially hazardous (risk of aortic injury, coronary compromise).
- Indications for aggressive frame expansion or fracture should consider coronary proximity (risk of moving a neo‑skirt into the coronary ostia), the structural integrity of prior valves, and feasibility of future coronary access.
- In short, BVF is rarely an option in TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV. Careful CT planning must guide whether aggressive balloon expansion is safe; in this case post‑dilation was performed without coronary compromise.

## Tactics to mitigate PPM in redo TAVI — what worked (and generalizable strategies)

From this case and broader practice, the following tactics reduce the risk/severity of PPM and optimise haemodynamics in redo scenarios:

- Preprocedural CT and 3D modelling: essential for assessing residual lumen, valve‑to‑coronary distances, frame overlap, and to pick a valve that maximises EOA.
- Prefer supra‑annular valve designs when feasible (e.g., Acurate neo2 in this case): supra‑annular leaflets often deliver better effective orifice in small landing zones and layered frames.
- Plan commissural alignment to preserve leaflet function and coronary access — aim to avoid creating a neo‑skirt that impedes flow.
- High implantation strategy (when anatomy allows): positions the new valve to avoid folding prior leaflets into the flow orifice.
- Use a deliberate post‑dilation strategy: appropriately sized high‑pressure balloons can expand both the new and prior frames, improving EOA — but always balance with coronary risk.
- Consider valve sizing that optimises EOA rather than simply anatomic fit — in redo cases EOA becomes a key determinant of functional success.
- Multidisciplinary decision‑making and shared consent: patients must understand tradeoffs — acceptable residual gradients may be preferable to high‑risk surgical explant.

## Clinical pearls

- Very high immediate gradients after complex redo TAVI can improve substantially with post‑dilation and time; serial haemodynamic assessment is critical before declaring device failure.
- Use multiple haemodynamic indices — AVA, indexed AVA, DVI and trend data — rather than a single mean gradient to judge PPM in layered valve anatomies.
- Supra‑annular valves and strategic high implantation + commissural alignment are powerful tools to mitigate PPM in small or layered landing zones.
- Valve fracture has limited role in triple‑stent TAVI and can be risky; planned balloon expansion under CT guidance is a more realistic approach.
- Always confirm absence of valve thrombosis or active infection before attributing gradients to mechanical PPM.

## Take‑home messages

- Don’t panic at day‑0: very high post‑implant gradients after TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV may reflect underexpansion or transient leaflet restriction and can improve with post‑dilation and time.
- Assess valve function with a multiparameter approach (AVA, indexed AVA, DVI) and follow trends over days–months.
- Preprocedural CT planning, supra‑annular valve choice, commissural alignment and targeted post‑dilation are key tactics to reduce PPM risk in redo TAVI.
- Valve fracture is rarely applicable in triple transcatheter configurations; proceed cautiously when considering aggressive expansion maneuvers given coronary and aortic risks.

This case is a practical reminder: immediate haemodynamic numbers are only part of the story. Thoughtful procedural strategy and close follow‑up often convert scary day‑0 gradients into excellent mid‑term outcomes.
