Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# CT as the playbook: Imaging strategy for TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV

What makes this case interesting
A man in his late 70s with a Lotus valve (2014) followed by a TAV‑in‑TAV with Evolut (2022), then Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis (treated), ultimately received a third transcatheter valve (Acurate neo2, 2024). Preprocedural multislice CT (MSCT) showed a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm through the Evolut frame and helped the team avoid coronary protection, plan a high implant, and optimise commissural alignment. At 1 year the patient is asymptomatic with a mean gradient ≈26 mm Hg and AVA ≈1.1 cm². Triple‑stented anatomies are rare but increasingly encountered; the imaging workup is the difference between feasible, risky, and impossible.

This post walks through a reproducible CT‑centred planning workflow for TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV, anchored to the case above and focused on de‑risking coronary obstruction, maximising effective orifice area (EOA), and preserving coronary access.

## The core problem: what CT must answer before a third TAVI
Each additional valve layer worsens three related problems:
- Creation of a neo‑skirt (cumulative leaflet/frame material) that can seal across coronary ostia.
- Reduction of EOA and increased risk of severe prosthesis‑patient mismatch (PPM).
- Obscuration and displacement of commissures and coronaries, complicating future coronary access.

Preprocedural CT therefore must answer:
1. Are the coronaries safe (or can they be made safe) with another transcatheter implant?
2. What implant depth/valve type will maximise EOA while preserving coronary flow?
3. What is the likelihood that commissural alignment or future coronary access will be achievable?
4. Are there residual signs of infection, paravalvular complications, or thrombus to change the plan?

In this patient the MSCT showed a minimal residual lumen of 13.7 mm through the Evolut—critical data that drove valve selection (Acurate neo2, supra‑annular), a plan for high implantation, and a decision to proceed without upfront coronary protection.

## A reproducible CT workflow for TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV
1. Image acquisition and reconstruction
   - ECG‑gated contrast MSCT with thin slices (≤0.6 mm) and multiphase (systolic/diastolic) reconstructions.
   - Include arch and coronary phases; reconstruct with metal‑artifact reduction if available.
   - If vegetations/endocarditis suspicion, include delayed phase for abscess/thrombosis.

2. Identify and label prior devices
   - Document device types, sizes and radiographic landmarks (seam markers, commissural posts).
   - Localise leaflets of prior valves relative to annulus and coronary ostia.

3. Valve frame lumen assessment (landing zone)
   - Measure true inner diameters at multiple levels through existing frames (annulus plane, mid frame, supra‑annular high points).
   - For this case the Evolut lumen narrowed to 13.7 mm — a key metric informing both valve sizing and expected residual EOA.

4. Coronary assessment
   - Measure coronary ostial heights from the native annulus plane.
   - Measure sinus of Valsalva (SOV) diameters and volumes.
   - Crucially, calculate valve‑to‑coronary (VTC) distances: predicted distance from the final anticipated neo‑skirt/leaflet plane to each coronary ostium.
   - If multiple frames exist, simulate neo‑skirt by projecting the outermost obstructive leaflet plane (not just the nominal annulus) and use 3D tools if available.

5. Frame–frame interaction and commissural relationships
   - Evaluate frame overlap zones and mesh density at coronary levels.
   - Identify prior commissural posts and their angular relation to native coronaries. Plan rotational orientation of the new valve to achieve best possible commissural alignment and avoid covering the ostia.

6. Neo‑skirt simulation and implantation depth planning
   - Model (mentally or with software) where the new leaflets will sit relative to prior leaflets—will they fold inward or form a stent‑leaflet sandwich?
   - Simulate a "high implant" position so the new valve leaflets splay above or at the level of obstructive prior leaflets to minimise neo‑skirt below the coronaries. In the reported case, a high Acurate neo2 implantation fixed prior leaflets against the frame and reduced neo‑skirt formation.

7. Assess the need for coronary protection or BASILICA
   - If VTC ≤4 mm, or sinuses are small, or CT predicts direct leaflet coverage of an ostium, plan upfront coronary protection (guidewire ± stent) or a leaflet laceration strategy (BASILICA). If VTC is larger and the simulated implant preserves clearance (as in this case), coronary protection can be deferred.
   - Remember: cumulative frame mesh may still impede future access even if immediate obstruction risk is low.

8. Plan adjuncts: predilatation, post‑dilatation, frame fracture possibilities
   - Determine balloon sizes for predilatation and post‑dilatation. High‑pressure post‑dilatation may expand prior frames (serendipitous frame expansion in this case improved gradients).
   - Plan for potential bioprosthetic valve fracture if appropriate (be cautious — overlapping frames may prevent effective fracture or make it risky).

9. Document a CT‑driven operative checklist
   - Finalise valve type, size, orientation, intended depth, balloon sizes, and contingency plans (coronary protection, BASILICA, conversion to surgery).

## Practical CT measurement tips and common pitfalls in layered valves
- Pitfall: Blooming and metal artifact → can over‑ or underestimate lumen. Use metal artifact reduction, narrow kernels, and corroborate with fluoroscopy and echo.
- Pitfall: Measuring from native annulus only → in TAV‑in‑TAV there may be neo‑annular planes higher or lower than native annulus. Measure through the narrowest lumen at each prior valve frame level.
- Pitfall: Ignoring leaflet height and outward leaflet displacement → prior leaflet orientation determines the true neo‑skirt. If prior leaflets splay outward, obstruction risk may be less despite small nominal VTC.
- Pitfall: Underappreciating commissural rotation → a new valve deployed without planned rotational alignment can place a commissural post directly in front of a coronary ostium. Use CT to plan rotational markers and fluoroscopic deployment angles.
- Pitfall: Single‑phase CT for coronaries → you may miss dynamic motion—use cardiac‑gated multiphase reconstructions to better estimate minimal VTC.
- Always cross‑check CT predictions with intra‑procedural aortography and test balloon inflation. In this case, simultaneous aortography during balloon pre‑dilatation confirmed coronary patency before valve release.

## When to forgo coronary protection
There is no absolute rule, but CT can make a defensible case to proceed without upfront protection when:
- Predicted VTC comfortably exceeds safety threshold (commonly >4–6 mm depending on valve type and sinus size).
- Sinus of Valsalva dimensions and coronary height can accommodate potential leaflet displacement.
- Simulations (or software) suggest the neo‑skirt will clear the ostia at planned implant depth, especially when high implantation is feasible.
- Commissural alignment can be achieved to avoid placing heavy frame/commissure burden in front of the ostia.
- There is a back‑up plan and the team is prepared for immediate protection or bailout.

In the presented patient, CT supported that a high Acurate neo2 implant would preserve coronary flow and allow the team to proceed without upfront wires or BASILICA; intra‑procedural balloon inflation with aortography confirmed this decision.

## Valve selection and implantation strategy: CT‑driven choices
- Prefer supra‑annular leaflets and low frame height when EOA is a priority in narrow, layered anatomies (Acurate neo2 in this case).
- Consider valve design that supports commissural alignment (marker visibility and rotational deployment control).
- Plan for high implantation to keep the functional leaflet plane above prior leaflet/material whenever possible to limit neo‑skirt formation.
- Anticipate the need for aggressive post‑dilatation to coax radial expansion of prior frames—but be mindful of annular rupture risk and device‑device interactions.

## Realistic expectations after a triple TAV
- Early transvalvular gradients can be high (this case had an immediate mean gradient of 67 mm Hg post‑implant but improved to 27 mm Hg by 1 month). Expect dynamic improvement with frame expansion and remodelling.
- PPM risk is real and must be discussed with the patient. In this case the indexed AVA was small (0.4 cm²/m² immediately after), but haemodynamics improved and symptoms resolved.
- Coronary access may be challenging in the future even if immediate obstruction is avoided—document orientation and imaging to aid future interventions.

## Clinical pearls
- Think “neo‑skirt,” not just “neo‑annulus.” In layered valves the leaflet plane (and how it will fold or be constrained) is the major determinant of coronary obstruction.
- Measure the narrowest residual lumen at each prior frame level—not just at the native annulus.
- Use CT to plan both axial (implant depth) and rotational (commissural alignment) strategies.
- Balloon test inflation with simultaneous aortography is a low‑tech, high‑yield intra‑procedural confirmation of CT predictions.
- High implantation that tacks prior leaflets against a frame can paradoxically increase EOA by preventing inward leaflet folding—if CT shows this is feasible, it can be the winning tactic.
- Even when avoiding upfront coronary protection, have a contingency plan and immediate equipment available for emergent protection or conversion.

## Take‑home messages
- MSCT is indispensable for TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV: it must assess valve lumen at multiple levels, coronary heights, sinus dimensions, valve‑to‑coronary distances and commissural relationships.
- Simulate the neo‑skirt and planned implant depth on CT — this often determines whether coronary protection or BASILICA is required.
- Plan rotational orientation and aim for commissural alignment to preserve coronary access.
- Use balloon test inflation and aortography to confirm CT predictions before committing to valve release.
- Expect early high gradients with potential improvement after post‑dilatation and frame expansion; set realistic expectations with the patient.
- In complex, high‑risk patients (as in this case), a CT‑driven, multidisciplinary plan can make a third TAVI feasible and durable at 1 year.

This case shows that with careful MSCT planning and a defined implantation strategy, even a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV after treated endocarditis can be done safely and effectively. CT isn’t just imaging—it’s the playbook.
