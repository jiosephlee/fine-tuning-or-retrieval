Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# Picking the platform and nailing the deployment in tight, layered anatomies

What makes this case worth your coffee break? A man in his late 70s, two prior TAVIs (Lotus in 2014, Evolut TAV-in-TAV in 2022), treated Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis, and then severe restenosis—so the team implanted a third transcatheter valve (Acurate neo2) in 2024. CT showed a 13.7 mm minimum lumen inside the Evolut frame. One year later the patient is asymptomatic with a durable result. This is TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV in real life: a narrow, layered root, prior infection now cleared, and no surgical option that’s reasonable. The takeaway: careful CT-led planning, choice of platform that matches the constrained anatomy, and a deliberate balloon/pacing strategy made a good result possible.

Below I’ll walk through the reasoning that drove the choice of Acurate neo2, how to size and plan when you’re working off an inner-frame lumen, the mechanics of stacking different valve types (Lotus legacy frame, Evolut supra-annular stent, then Acurate), and the pragmatic intra-procedural tactics that mattered in this case.

## Why Acurate neo2 here?
Short answer: supra‑annular leaflets + modest frame height + commissural alignment friendliness = best fit for a tiny, triple‑stented lumen.

Key considerations that pushed the team toward Acurate neo2:
- The inner lumen measured ~13.7 mm on MSCT. That’s very small. You need a valve with good hemodynamics in small effective orifice areas—supra-annular leaflet position helps preserve EOA versus intra-annular designs.
- A lower overall frame height and a design amenable to precise alignment lowers the chance of creating a bulky neo-skirt that could impinge coronaries or further reduce effective orifice area.
- Acurate neo2’s predictable leaflet geometry and radial behavior in constrained spaces favor better leaflet opening when you’re sitting inside another frame (and possibly partially expanding a prior self-expanding frame).
- Commissural alignment capability matters more now than ever when multiple frames threaten coronary access; Acurate’s orientation markers and predictable commissural posts were used to align to the Evolut leaflets and preserve coronary clearance.

Compare that to alternatives:
- Balloon-expandable high‑frame devices (e.g., Sapien family): good radial force but produce a bulky intra-annular skirt and may worsen PPM in a tiny inner lumen; risk of coronary obstruction if skirt migrates above the coronary ostia.
- Self‑expanding tall frames (e.g., Evolut): excellent supra‑annular hemodynamics, but their taller nitinol frames sitting inside an already layered root can create an even higher skirt burden and limit future coronary access. In this patient an Evolut had already been used; re‑deploying another Evolut risks adding more frame height and mesh burden.
- Lotus (legacy): had a different sealing skirt and frame geometry originally implanted; it’s not an option now but its legacy presence matters mechanically for stack interactions.

In short: for this particular anatomy—very small inner diameter, prior self‑expanding Evolut, need to protect coronaries, and goal to maximise instantaneous effective orifice area—Acurate neo2 was a rational, off‑label but strategy-driven choice.

## Sizing: think inner-frame dimensions, not the native annulus
When you’re doing TAV-in-TAV (or TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV), sizing shifts from “annulus-first” to “inner-frame-first.” The inner lumen dictates what the new valve can physically open to.

What this case taught us:
- MSCT gave a minimum lumen of 13.7 mm inside the Evolut. That effectively becomes your landing zone diameter for circularization and leaflet opening.
- Use inner-frame diameter (and its variability across cardiac cycle) to choose a prosthesis size and anticipate residual EOA/PPM. Expect higher gradients and manage patient expectations: immediate post‑implant gradients may be high and then improve (as in this case: mean gradient 67 mm Hg immediately post‑implant, falling to ~26–27 mm Hg at 1 month–1 year).
- When inner lumens are <15 mm, always expect some degree of severe PPM risk. Your goals become: maximise supra‑annular leaflet excursion, avoid further inward folding of old leaflets, and, if feasible, coax radial expansion of prior frames with high‑pressure ballooning—understanding that full frame fracture may not be possible or safe.

Practical sizing steps:
1. Measure internal diameter at the intended landing zone on MSCT (not the outer annulus). Document the shortest cross‑section.
2. Model valve leaflet position relative to previous leaflets and coronary ostia—CT-based virtual valve implantation is gold if available.
3. Choose a valve that provides the best expected EOA for that inner diameter (supra-annular designs typically beat intra-annular ones in tiny lumens).
4. Plan for aggressive post‑dilatation and set expectations for initial high gradients.

## Balloon strategy and rapid pacing — why they matter here
Balloon steps in this case were not ritualistic; they were purposeful.

Pre-dilation:
- A 22/40 mm balloon was used for predilation under rapid pacing (180 bpm). Purpose: test whether a larger orifice could be created, assess interaction with coronaries on aortography, and prepare the layered leaflets to seat the new valve.
- Predilation also provided immediate confirmation that coronary flow remained intact with mechanical displacement of prior leaflets—critical when you’re planning to implant without coronary protection.

Post-dilation:
- Post-dilation with a 20/40 mm balloon at high pressure was used to optimise expansion of the Acurate neo2 and to obtain additional radial expansion of the underlying Evolut stent (frame compliance permitting). This likely contributed to the marked improvement in gradients between immediate post‑implant and 1 month.
- The team did not attempt deliberate bioprosthetic valve fracture (BFx)—likely because the Evolut frame and layered stents may not be amenable to predictable fracture and the risk/benefit in this setting is uncertain.

Rapid pacing:
- High-rate pacing reduces cardiac output and aortic movement, improving balloon stability during pre- and post-dilation and aiding precise deployment of a self-expanding valve when you’re trying to hit an exact implantation depth (level of prior leaflets).
- 180 bpm was used here—choose the minimum rate that achieves sufficient hypotension and stability; in older patients with conduction disease and pacemaker, be mindful of pacing capture and hemodynamic tolerance.

Practical tips for balloon work in layered anatomies:
- Start with cautious predilation to confirm behavior of prior leaflets and coronary flow.
- Use a balloon size that approximates the inner frame lumen — too small is pointless, too big risks aortic injury or coronary compromise.
- Expect post‑dilation to be the workhorse for optimizing frame expansion—use high pressure but weigh the possible benefit of radial expansion of the old frame vs. damage to the aortic root or annulus.
- Have coronary protection plan available (wires, balloons or stents) even if you expect CT to be reassuring; the team here proceeded without protection based on careful preprocedural assessment.

## Anchoring at the level of prior leaflets — the “fix the leaflets open” trick
In layered valve scenarios the old leaflets can fold inward and create a new, smaller orifice or “neo‑skirt.” Anchorage strategy matters.

This case deployed the Acurate neo2 to align with the Evolut valve leaflets—intentionally implanting at the level of the prior leaflets so the new frame holds those leaflets outwards. The mechanics: the new valve’s stent cages the old leaflets against the prior stent frame, preventing leaflet infolding and helping maximize central flow.

Why implant high (at leaflet level)?
- Reduces the creation of a bulky neo‑skirt below the coronary ostia.
- Fixes prior leaflets in an open position rather than allowing them to sag inward and reduce EOA.
- Helps preserve coronary access by reducing the mass of new skirt material that could sit above the sinus level.

Caveats:
- Implanting too high risks valve embolization or paravalvular leak if the lower part of the frame is not well anchored. Balance is critical.
- Precise fluoroscopic and CT-guided depth markers are essential—this was done here using preprocedural modelling and fluoroscopic landmarks.

## How different platforms stack mechanically (Lotus legacy, Evolut, Acurate)
Understanding frame geometry and leaflet position of prior valves is essential when planning the next device.

Lotus (legacy, balloon‑expandable/locking nitinol):
- Shorter frame with an internal sealing cuff. If present underneath another valve, it can act as a rigid inner scaffold. In this patient Lotus was the original valve in 2014; its legacy presence shaped the first reintervention and subsequent frame interactions.

Evolut (self-expanding, tall supra‑annular frame):
- Tall nitinol frame with supra-annular leaflets. It exerts continuous outward radial force, but inside calcified or layered roots its expansion can be limited. Evolut frames can be partially expanded by high-pressure ballooning, improving leaflet motion. Evolut’s tall mesh can threaten coronary access and add mesh burden if another tall frame is added.

Acurate neo2 (self-expanding, supra-annular leaflets, modest frame height):
- Shorter than Evolut, designed for commissural alignment, and with leaflet positioning that tends to preserve EOA in constrained anatomies. When placed inside an Evolut, Acurate’s stent posts can orient to push Evolut leaflets outward at the leaflet level without further raising a neo-skirt high into the sinuses.

Mechanical interactions to anticipate:
- When you place a new valve inside a self‑expanding frame, the new valve must contend with the prior frame’s radial limits. Expect incomplete circularization and accept residual PPM risk.
- Post‑dilation can achieve partial expansion of the older frame (as likely happened here), improving leaflet mobility and gradients, but not necessarily achieving the full diameter of the new valve’s nominal size.
- Commissural alignment between stacked frames is imperfect but matters: try to align new commissures with prior leaflets to reduce mesh overlap over coronary ostia.

## Risk mitigation and the infection angle
This patient had treated prosthetic endocarditis (Enterococcus faecalis) prior to the third TAVI. Important points that guided the decision-making:
- No evidence of active infection on TEE, blood cultures negative, and antimicrobial therapy completed.
- Surgical risk was prohibitive—root replacement would likely have been required due to embedded frames.
- Multidisciplinary heart team discussion and shared decision-making are mandatory in these scenarios.

From a procedural safety standpoint:
- Confirm infection is quiescent with TEE and negative cultures.
- Consider prolonged monitoring for recurrence, but routine long-term suppressive antibiotics were not used here.
- Be cautious: prior endocarditis may have damaged leaflets making them stiffer and less likely to open; this increases the importance of choosing a supra-annular valve and planning for aggressive post‑dilation.

## What actually happened in this case — the important timeline points
- Pre-procedure CT inner lumen: 13.7 mm.
- Predilation with 22/40 mm balloon under rapid pacing; aortography confirmed coronaries OK.
- Acurate neo2 (size S) implanted with commissural alignment to Evolut leaflets; post‑dilated with 20/40 mm balloon under pacing.
- Immediate TTE: no regurgitation but high mean gradient (67 mm Hg) and severe PPM parameters.
- At 1 month mean gradient improved to ~27 mm Hg and AVA increased; at 1 year mean gradient ~26 mm Hg, AVA ~1.1 cm2, EF 65%, patient asymptomatic.

Interpretation: immediate high gradients likely reflect constrained initial expansion and low flow during the procedure; subsequent partial expansion of the Evolut frame and favorable remodeling improved hemodynamics.

## Clinical pearls
- In serial TAV reinterventions, size off the inner-frame lumen, not the native annulus.
- Supra‑annular leaflet valves give better EOA in small inner lumens—consider them preferentially when PPM risk is high.
- Commissural alignment isn’t cosmetic—do it to preserve coronary access and reduce future procedural complexity.
- Predilation is a test: use it to assess leaflet behavior and coronary perfusion under mechanical displacement before committing to valve deployment without protection.
- Post‑dilatation can improve hemodynamics by expanding both the new prosthesis and, to some extent, the older self‑expanding frame—expect gradients to improve over days to weeks.
- In patients with prior PVE, ensure infection is quiescent by TEE and cultures before reintervention; multidisciplinary discussion is essential.

Key takeaways:
- Triple‑stented anatomies are feasible with careful CT‑guided planning and platform selection (Acurate neo2 was chosen here because it offers supra‑annular leaflets with modest frame height and alignment potential).
- Think inner diameter, anticipate PPM, plan for balloon optimization, and prioritize coronary preservation.
- Detailed preprocedural modeling and a clear anchoring strategy (implanting at prior leaflet level) can convert a prohibitive surgical case into a durable transcatheter success.

If you’re about to face a similar triple‑stent scenario: get high‑quality gated CT, model the landing zone virtually if possible, pick the platform that maximises EOA for the inner lumen, plan predilation to test coronaries, and be ready to post‑dilate aggressively—but always with a Coronary Protection Plan on standby.
