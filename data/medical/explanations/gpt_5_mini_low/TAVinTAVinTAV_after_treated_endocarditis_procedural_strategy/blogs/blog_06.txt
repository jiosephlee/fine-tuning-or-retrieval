Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# Life after serial TAVI and prior PVE: surveillance, prevention, and planning for a limited runway

What makes this case striking is simple — a man in his late 70s with a Lotus valve (2014) → Evolut TAV‑in‑TAV (2022) → Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis treated with a full antibiotic course → Acurate neo2 TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV (2024), and clinically well at 1 year. Triple‑stented anatomies are no longer theoretical: as TAVI volumes expand and patients live longer, we will increasingly face layered prostheses and the hard reality of “limited runway” for future interventions. This demands a proactive, pragmatic follow‑up and shared decision framework that balances surveillance, infection prevention, bleeding risk, and realistic counseling about next options if the valve fails.

Below I outline a practical post‑procedure program — informed by the case above — with what to measure, when to image, how to manage infection prophylaxis and antithrombotic therapy in a patient with prior PVE and recurrent GI bleeding, and how to counsel patients and families about constrained future choices.

## The surveillance backbone: echo cadence and what to track

Serial transthoracic echo is the workhorse. In this case immediate post‑procedure TTE showed no regurgitation but very high gradients (mean 67 mm Hg) that improved to mean 27 mm Hg at 1 month and stabilized around 26 mm Hg at 1 year — a reminder that early hemodynamics can evolve after post‑dilatation and LV remodeling.

Suggested echo schedule after TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV (modify per symptoms):
- Baseline inpatient/early post‑procedure TTE (within 48–72 h).
- Early outpatient TTE at 30 days.
- Repeat at 3–6 months to document remodeling and gradients.
- At 12 months, then annually if stable.
- Sooner if new symptoms, hemoglobin drop, fever, new murmur or changes in exercise tolerance.

What to record and serially compare:
- Mean and peak transvalvular gradients (consistent Doppler windows and VTI technique).
- Aortic valve area (continuity equation) and indexed AVA when possible.
- Doppler velocity index (DVI) — simple, less flow dependent; watch for DVI <0.35 suggesting significant obstruction.
- Presence and severity of transvalvular and paravalvular regurgitation.
- LV size/function (EF, volumes) and signs of reverse remodeling or worsening dysfunction.
- Pulmonary pressures and right heart strain if present.
- Evidence of hemolysis or high‑velocity jets suggesting structural failure.

Clinical pearl: in layered valves, increased gradients may reflect PPM, leaflet restriction from prior frames, incomplete expansion, or thrombosis. Serial trends and complementary imaging are essential — a single elevated mean gradient immediately post‑op may improve (as in this patient).

## Triggers for advanced imaging (TEE, MSCT, or fluoroscopic/angiographic review)

Advanced imaging should be triggered early rather than later if the clinical picture is concerning.

Indications to escalate:
- Fever or bacteremia (any organism) — urgent blood cultures + TEE to exclude recurrent PVE.
- Unexplained rise in gradients (>10–15 mm Hg absolute from prior baseline) or symptoms out of proportion to echo findings.
- New or worsening transvalvular or paravalvular regurgitation.
- Suspicion of leaflet thrombosis, restricted leaflet motion, or abnormal leaflet thickening on TTE.
- Preprocedural planning if thinking reintervention (CT to assess valve‑to‑coronary, frame interaction, minimum lumen diameters). In the presented case MSCT with lumen 13.7 mm guided valve selection and implantation strategy.
- If coronary ischemia is suspected or future coronary access is anticipated — CT to evaluate valve orientation and valve‑to‑coronary distances.

Modality selection:
- TEE: best for vegetations, paravalvular fistula/abscess in suspected endocarditis.
- MSCT: essential for anatomic assessment in reintervention planning (landing zone, frame overlap, coronary clearance, neo‑skirt estimation).
- 4D CT or dedicated software when available to quantify leaflet motion and frame expansion.

Clinical pearl: in serial TAV, CT planning is not a luxury — it is required if considering any further transcatheter intervention.

## Infection prevention and prior PVE — practical approach

Key premise from the case: the patient completed a full curative antibiotic course for Enterococcus faecalis PVE, had negative repeat cultures and imaging, and no chronic suppressive antibiotics were used. That aligns with guideline principles: no routine long‑term antibiotic suppression if infection is eradicated and there is no prosthetic dehiscence/abscess.

Practical measures:
- No routine chronic suppressive antibiotics after documented cure unless specific indications (e.g., uncontrolled infection, high risk of relapse, unresolved perivalvular infection) and ID specialist agreement.
- Low threshold for investigating any fever: obtain blood cultures promptly. In prosthetic valve patients, treat fever seriously.
- For invasive procedures (dental, contaminated soft tissue, urologic/enteric procedures), follow current guideline recommendations for endocarditis prophylaxis — most relevant is dental procedures with manipulation of gingival tissue or periapical region (amoxicillin 2 g PO 30–60 min prior, or alternative regimens if allergic), particularly given prior PVE. Discuss individualization with ID/cardiology.
- Educate the patient on skin/dental hygiene and promptly treating infections (UTI, skin) — Enterococcus can come from GI/GU sources.
- Document prior PVE clearly in medical records and ensure continuity of care: any future clinicians should be aware of the history.

Clinical pearl: prior cured PVE increases vigilance needs, but does not mandate indefinite suppressive antibiotics. Prophylaxis for qualifying procedures should be discussed and documented.

## Antithrombotic strategy in a patient with GI bleeding risk

Balancing stroke prevention, valve thrombosis risk and bleeding is tough — especially with prior GI bleeding (as in this patient since 2022) and multiple prior coronary stents.

Guiding principles:
- Use the least antithrombotic burden compatible with clinical indications.
- Post‑TAVI antithrombotic choices should be individualized: contemporary practice increasingly favors single antiplatelet therapy (SAPT) after TAVI in patients without another indication for OAC or DAPT.
- If the patient has an indication for oral anticoagulation (AF, LV thrombus), use OAC alone rather than OAC + antiplatelet when possible.
- If recent PCI with drug‑eluting stents exists and DAPT is mandated, minimize duration per current PCI/TAVI consensus (short DAPT then de‑escalate), in conjunction with the interventional cardiology and GI teams.

Applying to our case:
- No chronic OAC was described; prior PCI remote (2009–2014) — if no current stent or AF, SAPT (eg, low‑dose aspirin) might be reasonable, but GI bleeding risk may favor no antiplatelet or careful shared decision making.
- Given recurrent GI bleeding, involve GI to optimize bleeding source management (e.g., eradicate lesions, H. pylori if relevant, consider PPI use when on antiplatelet therapy).
- If antiplatelet needed, use lowest effective dose, ear‑marked follow up for recurrent bleeding, and coordinate with GI for surveillance/therapy.

Clinical pearl: in high bleeding risk patients after TAVI without other indications, a strategy of no antiplatelet or single antiplatelet (rather than DAPT) is often favored — personalized after multidisciplinary discussion.

## Counseling about limited future options — be honest and plan early

This is the hardest part. Tell patients and families plainly: every additional valve reduces future options. In the presented case a third transcatheter valve was successful at 1 year, but if it fails, choices are limited and riskier.

Points to discuss and document:
- Explantation/surgical root replacement after multiple TAVIs is technically difficult and high risk — may entail root and ascending aorta replacement, cardiopulmonary bypass, and complex reconstruction. In this patient the EuroSCORE II was 27% and comorbidities made surgery prohibitive.
- Percutaneous bailout strategies exist but have limits:
  - BASILICA (leaflet laceration) is mainly preventive before TAV‑in‑TAV to avoid coronary obstruction; feasibility in triple‑stented anatomy is uncertain and technically more complex.
  - Bioprosthetic valve fracture (BVF) can improve effective orifice in some surgical valves or certain transcatheter frames, but not all frames are susceptible; data on BVF in multiple layered TAVs are limited and may be unsafe in certain configurations.
  - Valve‑in‑valve‑in‑valve interventions may be possible but risk progressive PPM and coronary obstruction.
  - Emerging techniques (leaflet modification, chimney stenting, coronary protection) are case specific and evidence is limited.
- Lifelong planning: document the current valve types, sizes, CT images, and commissural orientation in a durable, shareable format (patient card, regional registry). This aids future teams.
- If the patient is young or likely to need multiple future interventions, discuss this at first TAVI — choose devices, commissural alignment and implantation depths with “future access” in mind.
- Set realistic expectations: short‑ to intermediate‑term functional improvement is likely (as in this case), but long‑term durability beyond a few years is uncertain; prepare for higher risk and fewer interventions later.

Clinical pearl: early documentation (CT, fluoroscopic angles for valve orientation, operative reports) is invaluable. It may be the difference between a feasible future percutaneous strategy and only a high‑risk surgical option.

## Practical checklist for clinics caring for complex serial‑TAVI patients

Before leaving the visit, ensure the following are addressed and documented:
- Baseline TTE copies and clear numeric baselines for gradients, AVA, DVI and LV function.
- CT images and a succinct summary of valve types/sizes/implantation depths and commissural alignment.
- Clear instructions on when to return (fever, new dyspnea, chest pain, syncope, bleeding) and who to call.
- Infection prophylaxis plan for dental/invasive procedures and ID consultation plan if symptoms recur.
- Antithrombotic plan with rationale, GI follow up arranged if prior bleeding.
- Advanced care planning discussion about limited future options and a documented summary of shared decision‑making.

## Take‑home messages
- After TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV, structured surveillance with early (30 day), short‑term (3–6 month) and annual echo is practical; escalate to MSCT or TEE for unexplained gradient change, suspected thrombosis, or fever/bacteremia.
- Track gradients, AVA, DVI, regurgitation and LV remodeling — trend is more informative than a single value.
- Prior cured PVE does not require routine chronic suppressive antibiotics; maintain high vigilance for fever and follow guideline‑based prophylaxis for qualifying procedures.
- Antithrombotic therapy must be individualized in GI‑bleeding‑prone patients — favor the least therapy consistent with thrombotic risk, and coordinate with GI.
- Counsel patients early and clearly: each TAV reduces future options; document valve specifics and CT planning to preserve as many future choices as possible.

This patient’s 1‑year outcome is encouraging, but it’s a reminder that success today increases the responsibility to plan for the harder choices of tomorrow.
