Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV after treated PVE: When is a third transcatheter valve the right call?

What makes this case striking is its rarity and the tension it exposes between guideline recommendations and real‑world limits. A man in his late 70s—lots of comorbidity, prior pacemaker, multivessel CAD with multiple PCIs, COPD, diabetes and recurrent GI bleeding—had a 2014 Lotus TAVI, a 2022 Evolut TAV‑in‑TAV, a documented Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) treated with a complete antibiotic course, and then severe prosthetic degeneration requiring a third transcatheter implant (Acurate neo2) in 2024. One year out he is asymptomatic with a stable mean gradient ~26–27 mmHg and AVA ~1.1 cm². The case forces us to ask: when is it acceptable to go back through the valve rather than to the OR after prior PVE?

## The central clinical tension
- ESC guidance (2025) sensibly favors surgery for complicated PVE, but recommendations assume acceptable surgical risk and anatomy amenable to explant. This patient’s EuroSCORE II was 27%, prior multiple transcatheter frames were embedded into the aortic wall, and surgical explant would likely have required root/ascending aorta replacement—high morbidity and mortality.
- The counterpoint: endocarditis history heightens concern for residual infection, seeding, and early re‑failure. Reintervening on a recently infected prosthesis risks treating a structural complication before infection is truly eradicated.

This case illustrates a middle path: conservative surgical candidacy + rigorous confirmation of infection resolution + CT‑guided anatomical planning = off‑label, strategy‑driven TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV with good short‑term durability.

## How this team justified a third transcatheter valve
1. Infection control was confirmed before intervention:
   - Completed full, targeted IV antibiotic therapy for E. faecalis.
   - Repeated blood cultures were negative.
   - TEE and MSCT showed no vegetations or paravalvular complications.
   - Multidisciplinary infectious disease input guided stopping suppressive therapy.

2. Surgical risk and technical feasibility favoured transcatheter approach:
   - Extensive comorbidity and high calculated surgical mortality.
   - Prior valves deeply integrated; surgical explant would be complex and high risk.
   - CT showed a minimal Evolut lumen of 13.7 mm but demonstrated feasible valve‑to‑coronary distances with a plan to preserve coronary flow.

3. Strategy was individualized and imaging‑driven:
   - Preprocedural MSCT and 3D considerations directed valve choice (Acurate neo2 for supra‑annular leaflets, lower frame height) and implantation depth.
   - Aim: high implantation aligned with Evolut leaflets to prevent inward leaflet fold, minimise neo‑skirt below coronaries and preserve future coronary access.
   - Commissural alignment and awareness of cumulative mesh burden were explicitly considered.
   - No prophylactic coronary protection was used after CT modelling suggested acceptable risk; intra‑procedural angiography confirmed coronary patency during balloon predilation.

4. Procedural finesse optimised haemodynamics:
   - Balloon predilation and high‑pressure post‑dilation likely partially expanded the Evolut frame and optimised leaflet motion without intentional frame fracture.
   - Immediate post‑procedure gradients were high (mean 67 mmHg) but improved over the following month to the mid‑20s mmHg—consistent with progressive frame expansion and hemodynamic equilibration.

## Practical framework for decision‑making after PVE when facing failed TAVI
The case gives a practical algorithm you can apply in similar patients:

1. Confirm infection control—don’t shortcut this.
   - Documented completion of an appropriate antibiotic course.
   - Repeated negative blood cultures.
   - High‑quality TEE to exclude vegetations, abscess, fistula or dehiscence.
   - MSCT to look for paravalvular extension and to plan anatomy.
   - Infectious disease review and consensus on stopping suppressive therapy if indicated.

2. Multidisciplinary heart team discussion (including ID, imaging, surgery, interventionalists).
   - Emphasise candidacy for surgery vs prohibitive risk.
   - Discuss patient values and tradeoffs; document shared decision‑making.

3. CT‑led anatomical feasibility assessment:
   - Measure valve‑to‑coronary distances, sinus dimensions, anticipated neo‑skirt height.
   - Model frame‑to‑frame interactions and potential for commissural alignment.
   - Consider whether valve choice and implantation depth can preserve coronary flow and acceptable EOA.

4. Procedural planning:
   - Aim for commissural alignment when possible to preserve coronary access.
   - Consider valve type that maximises EOA (supra‑annular designs may help).
   - Plan for balloon optimisation (pre‑ and post‑dilatation) and have bailout strategies ready (coronary protection, BASILICA or surgical backup where feasible).
   - Explicitly discuss the limits of future options if the new valve fails (surgical explant complexity, limited catheter solutions).

5. Timing:
   - Wait until infection is quiescent—no shortcuts. The team waited months after completion of antibiotics with negative cultures and imaging evidence of no active disease.

## What prior PVE should and should not drive in your decision
Should drive:
- A meticulous exclusion of ongoing infection before any reintervention.
- A lower threshold for multidisciplinary review and documented shared decision‑making.
- Close postprocedural surveillance for recurrence of infection.

Should not by itself absolutely preclude TAVI:
- Once infection is demonstrably resolved, PVE history should not automatically force surgery if surgical risk is prohibitive and anatomy lends itself to a planned transcatheter solution.
- Prior PVE does not change the basic mechanical challenges of TAV‑in‑TAV procedures (PPM, coronary obstruction) — those risks must be addressed by imaging and technique rather than by reflexive avoidance.

## Limitations and real‑world caveats
- Triple‑stented anatomy compounds future coronary access and leaves very few rescue options if the new valve fails. This must be discussed with the patient.
- Evidence for TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV—especially after PVE—is sparse; data are case‑based and short to medium term.
- Immediate post‑implant high gradients can improve over weeks as frames expand; treat fluently but watch closely.
- Some strategies used here (valve choice, implantation depth, off‑label device use) require institutional approval and experienced operators.

## Clinical pearls and take‑home points
- Confirm infection is eradicated before reintervention: document completed antibiotics, serial negative blood cultures, negative TEE/MSCT, and ID sign‑off.
- When surgical risk is prohibitive, a carefully planned TAV‑in‑TAV (or even TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV) can be reasonable if CT shows adequate coronary clearance and allows a strategy to preserve EOA and coronary access.
- Use preprocedural MSCT to model frame interactions, valve‑to‑coronary distance and anticipate neo‑skirt height; let CT drive valve choice and implantation depth.
- Aim for commissural alignment and consider supra‑annular valves with lower frame heights to optimise EOA and future coronary access.
- Expect immediate hemodynamics to evolve—high gradients immediately post‑implant may fall over weeks with balloon expansion and frame settling.
- Multidisciplinary shared decision‑making and explicit discussion of limited future options are essential; document consent and rationale.

This case is a reminder that guidelines guide, but clinical reality and individualized imaging‑led planning—combined with rigorous infection control—can justify and safely deliver unconventional solutions in well‑selected, high‑risk patients.
