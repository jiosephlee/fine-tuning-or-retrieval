Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# Saving the coronaries in a triple-frame world: Commissural alignment, high implantation, and future access

What makes this case interesting: a man in his late 70s with two prior TAVIs (Lotus → Evolut) who developed Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis and, after successful treatment, underwent a third transcatheter valve (Acurate neo2) — a true TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV. The team used CT-guided planning to intentionally align commissures and implant high, pinning prior leaflets to minimise a neo-skirt and preserve coronary flow and future coronary access. One-year follow-up: the patient is asymptomatic, valve function stable, coronary perfusion intact. This is a neat, practical lesson in how to protect coronaries not only for the index procedure but for the patient’s “lifetime” coronary needs.

Why coronary strategy matters even more in repeat TAVI
- Every additional stent frame and leaflets folded outward/inward increases the risk of coronary obstruction and makes future coronary cannulation harder. In a single TAV, coronary obstruction is uncommon but feared. In double and triple frames, overlayed mesh, displaced leaflets and neo-skirts can create permanent barriers to both flow and catheter access.
- Strategies that prevent immediate obstruction (coronary protection wires, BASILICA, standby surgery) are different from strategies that prioritise preserving future PCI access (commissural alignment, valve choice, implantation depth/height). Ideally you plan for both.

How this case approached the problem (what they did and why it worked)
- Preprocedural MSCT modelling: critical. They measured valve-to-coronary distances, sinus dimensions, and the minimum lumen inside the Evolut (13.7 mm)—this guided valve selection and the decision to avoid upfront coronary protection.
- Valve choice: Acurate neo2 (self‑expanding, supra-annular leaflets, relatively low frame height, favourable mesh geometry) was chosen to maximise effective orifice area and ease commissural alignment in a narrow, triple-stented landing zone.
- Deliberate high implantation: They implanted the neo2 at the level of the Evolut leaflets to “pin” the prior leaflets outward against the existing frame rather than creating an additional neo‑skirt below the coronaries. High implant reduces the skirt that can cover the coronary ostia and helps preserve coronary flow and future access.
- Commissural alignment: Performed using CT-guided fluoroscopic landmarks to orient the neo2 relative to the underlying Evolut commissures. Alignment reduces the chance that frame struts and commissural posts will line up in front of coronary ostia.
- Aggressive balloon optimisation: Pre-dilation and post‑dilation (22/40 pre; 20/40 post) under rapid pacing both tested coronary flow and helped achieve better expansion of the Evolut frame and the new device. No BASILICA or intentional leaflet laceration was used; coronary flow remained unobstructed on intra-procedural aortography.
- No coronary protection: Because MSCT showed adequate coronary clearance and the team planned a high-implant with commissural alignment, they proceeded without prophylactic wires or stents. That reduced procedural complexity & access risks (useful in a patient with prior vascular complications).

Practical commissural alignment principles across platforms
- Know your platform’s radiographic markers and commissural orientation: Some valves (Acurate neo2, Evolut, Sapien, Lotus) have reproducible fluoroscopic markers that predict leaflet/comms orientation. Use these to rotate the delivery system to the desired orientation before deployment.
- Aim to place valve commissures between coronary ostia, not in front of them. In practice this means aligning fluoroscopic markers so commissural posts avoid the 12 and 3 o’clock positions that correspond to coronary ostia on your chosen projection.
- Consider introducing alignment strategy at the index TAVI: in younger patients or known multivessel CAD, initial commissural alignment makes future reinterventions easier.

High implantation: when and how
- Rationale: Pins prior leaflets against the native/previous frame, reduces sub-coronary neo-skirt formation, can preserve flow and access.
- How: Use MSCT to define the level of prior leaflet nadirs and choose a final implant depth that sits the new valve leaflets at or slightly above prior leaflet coaptation. Fluoroscopic landmarks and cusp overlap projections help achieve repeatable height.
- Caveats: High implant can increase paravalvular leak risk if too shallow, and may increase conduction disturbance risk if too deep into the LVOT—balance is essential.

Choosing a platform for a narrow triple-stented landing zone
- Favor supra-annular designs with lower frame height and open-cell geometry if your intent is to minimise mesh burden in front of coronaries and maximise effective orifice area.
- Self-expanding frames can allow some gradual radial expansion of prior frames during post‑dilation; however, beware of incomplete expansion and resultant PPM.
- Consider the known commissural marker characteristics of your chosen valve — some platforms make alignment easier.

Immediate obstruction prevention versus preserving long-term PCI options: contrast
- Immediate protection strategies (when MSCT suggests high risk of obstruction):
  - Coronary wiring and bailout stent pre-positioned
  - BASILICA to lacerate the native/bioprosthetic leaflet(s) and create a channel for coronary flow
  - Surgical backup
- Long-term PCI preservation strategies (best started at index TAVI and continued in reinterventions):
  - Commissural alignment to avoid placing posts in front of coronaries
  - Valve choice with favourable mesh and leaflet position
  - High implantation to avoid creating a neo-skirt over coronary ostia
  - Minimising frame-in-frame obstruction (select devices with larger internal lumen when possible)
- Note: In some cases you need both approaches: use BASILICA or coronary protection for immediate safety and still attempt commissural alignment/high implant to preserve future access.

Practicalities of coronary engagement after triple TAVI
- Expect altered catheter trajectories: conventional Judkins and Amplatz curves may need reshaping or deeper engagement techniques.
- Start with non-selective aortography to identify effective coronary take-off relative to the outer frame.
- Use multipurpose or telescoping guiding catheters and consider using guide extension catheters to reach beyond the most external frame.
- Consider smaller inner catheters and hydrophilic wires to negotiate through mesh.
- If commissural alignment was reasonable, selective engagement may still be straightforward; if not, expect prolonged fluoroscopy and need for multiple catheters.
- Keep radial access available — in this case, left radial arterial access was used alongside femoral access and proved helpful for aortography and support.

What about PPM and haemodynamics? Don’t forget it
- Layering valves risks smaller effective orifice area. In this case, immediate post‑implant gradients were high (mean 67 mm Hg) but improved over 1 month (27 mm Hg) and remained stable at 1 year (26 mm Hg) after optimisation and partial expansion of prior frame.
- Preprocedural CT and realistic expectations about residual gradients are essential; counsel patients that some degree of residual stenosis or PPM may persist despite technical success.

When infection has been treated — is repeat TAVI OK?
- This patient had documented Enterococcus faecalis PVE treated fully with antibiotics and had no clinical/imaging evidence of active infection at the time of reintervention. Their team proceeded with TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after multidisciplinary review.
- Key point: treated, resolved PVE in a prohibitive-surgical-risk patient may be a reasonable context for redo TAVI, but careful assessment for residual infection or paravalvular complications is mandatory.

Take-home messages
- Preprocedural MSCT-driven planning is mandatory in TAV-in-TAV and especially in triple-stented anatomies; measure valve-to-coronary distances, sinus dimensions and internal lumen diameters.
- Prioritise commissural alignment and deliberate high implantation when feasible — these steps reduce coronary obstruction risk and help preserve future coronary access.
- Choose a valve platform with favourable frame height/mesh geometry for reinterventions in narrow or multi-frame landing zones.
- Balance immediate coronary protection strategies (BASILICA, coronary wire/stent) with long-term PCI preservation strategies; sometimes you need both.
- Be realistic about haemodynamics: PPM can occur, and gradients may improve over time after balloon optimisation and frame expansion.
- After successful treated PVE and multidisciplinary clearance, repeat transcatheter intervention can be feasible — but needs rigorous imaging, team discussion, and informed consent about long-term trade-offs.

This case shows that with careful CT-guided planning, valve selection, commissural alignment and high implantation strategy you can safely navigate a triple-frame aortic valve and protect coronary flow — and keep the option of future PCI as open as possible.
