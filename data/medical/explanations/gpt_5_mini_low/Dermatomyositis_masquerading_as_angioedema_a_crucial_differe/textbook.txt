Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

## Atypical Presentation and Initial Differential: Neck Swelling Beyond Angioedema

This patient’s initial presentation — three weeks of progressive, non-tender neck swelling accompanied by proximal hip‑flexor weakness, mild myalgia and evidence of pharyngeal dysfunction — illustrates how an uncommon manifestation of a systemic inflammatory myopathy can be mistaken for primary otolaryngology or angioedema syndromes. Understanding why the team moved from an initial working diagnosis of Ludwig’s angina or idiopathic angioedema to dermatomyositis requires integrating the history, focused examination, targeted early investigations and recognition of specific “red flags.”

Presenting features and early clues
The dominant patient complaints were progressive cervical soft‑tissue swelling and proximal muscle weakness. During ENT assessment the patient had increased secretions and coughing on flexible nasendoscopy, an early objective sign of bulbar or pharyngeal dysfunction. These neuromuscular features — proximal limb weakness and dysphagia — are not typical of isolated infectious neck processes or simple angioedema and should prompt consideration of systemic neuromuscular disease.

Red flags arguing against angioedema
Several findings in this case were inconsistent with histaminergic or bradykinin‑mediated angioedema. The patient was afebrile and systemically well, had no exposure to common drug triggers (no ACE inhibitor, DPP‑4 inhibitor, NSAIDs) and no personal or family history of hereditary angioedema. The swelling was persistent and non‑tender rather than transient and pruritic, and there were no urticarial lesions. Complement testing (C3/C4) and C1‑esterase inhibitor quantification/function were normal, making classic C1‑INH deficiency or consumption‑related angioedema unlikely. Importantly, the patient had violaceous facial erythema extending into the nasolabial folds rather than an isolated edematous change, which suggested a cutaneous inflammatory process rather than isolated vascular permeability from angioedema.

ENT and infectious differentials: why initial concern for Ludwig’s angina
Given the location and extent of neck swelling, initial consideration of deep neck space infection (including Ludwig’s angina), cellulitis/erysipelas or sialadenitis was appropriate. CT neck showed diffuse edema of the strap muscles and retropharyngeal inflammation without a focal rim‑enhancing collection or abscess. In a true deep neck infection one would expect a localized fluid collection, systemic inflammatory response (fever, often higher WBC/CRP), and localizing signs such as trismus or severe odynophagia. The absence of a drainable collection on imaging and relatively mild systemic findings argued against a surgically treatable deep neck infection.

Neuromuscular and inflammatory differentials to consider early
When cervical swelling is accompanied by proximal weakness or bulbar features, neuromuscular and inflammatory myopathies move up the differential. Idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (dermatomyositis, polymyositis, immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy), inclusion body myositis (usually older patients with distal weakness pattern), and neuromuscular junction disorders such as myasthenia gravis (typically fatigable weakness) are relevant considerations. Other mimics include delayed drug hypersensitivity syndromes (eg, DRESS), orofacial granulomatosis, hypocomplementaemic urticarial vasculitis and superior vena cava syndrome when facial/neck swelling predominates. The distribution of weakness (proximal hip flexor involvement) and dysphagia in this case favored an inflammatory myopathy rather than many of these alternatives.

Bedside clues pointing to dermatomyositis
A careful skin and nailfold examination provided critical diagnostic clues. Periungual erythema with capillary microhemorrhages on capillaroscopy, a violaceous facial rash including involvement of the nasolabial folds, and a Holster sign (erythematous rash on the lateral thigh) are cutaneous features classically associated with dermatomyositis. Even when hallmark signs such as Gottron’s papules or heliotrope rash are absent, these subtler findings should raise suspicion for dermatomyositis. The combination of proximal muscle weakness and cutaneous changes should prompt early serum muscle enzyme testing.

The pivot test: creatine kinase and what it tells you
Recognizing that the presentation did not fit typical angioedema, the team obtained a creatine kinase (CK) level, which proved decisive: CK was markedly elevated at 16,184 U/L. Very high CK values are characteristic of active myositis and necrosis and are not seen in angioedema or isolated soft‑tissue swelling from infection. An elevated CK reoriented the diagnostic pathway toward inflammatory myopathy and warranted further testing (myositis autoantibodies, MRI or PET of muscle, and muscle biopsy). In practice, ordering CK is a low‑cost, high‑yield test whenever unexplained neck or facial swelling is accompanied by weakness, myalgias or swallowing dysfunction.

How the initial differential was narrowed in this case
The combination of non‑tender, persistent neck edema without radiographic abscess, absence of systemic infectious features or angioedema triggers, prominent proximal weakness and dysphagia, subtle dermal signs on exam and a profoundly elevated CK favored dermatomyositis over ENT or primary allergic vascular etiologies. Subsequent confirmatory studies in this case (positive anti‑SAE1 antibody, MRI/PET evidence of muscle inflammation and characteristic muscle biopsy with perifascicular pathology and capillary C5b‑9 deposition) validated the early diagnostic pivot.

Clinical takeaways for learners
- Always integrate neuromuscular symptoms (weakness, dysphagia) with localizing complaints such as neck swelling; discordance between local findings and systemic features should broaden the differential.  
- Examine skin and nailfolds carefully; subtle dermatomyositis signs can be missed but are diagnostically important.  
- When presentation is atypical for angioedema or deep neck infection, obtain a CK early — a very high CK points to myositis and should change diagnostic and management priorities.  
- Imaging that shows diffuse muscular edema without abscess supports inflammatory myopathy over a surgically treatable infection.  

In this patient, these steps avoided unnecessary surgical intervention, led to timely immunologic workup and initiation of appropriate immunosuppressive therapy with ultimately good clinical recovery.

## Pathobiology of Dermatomyositis and the SAE1 Phenotype

Dermatomyositis (DM) is an autoimmune inflammatory myopathy whose hallmark lesion is a complement-mediated microangiopathy affecting skin and muscle. The earliest and most characteristic histopathologic change is injury to the endomysial and perimysial capillary bed. Deposition of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9) on capillaries triggers endothelial cell injury, loss of capillary density and local ischemia. In skeletal muscle this process produces perifascicular atrophy and fibre degeneration concentrated at the edges of fascicles, a pattern seen on muscle biopsy in this case and reflected by the reported C5b-9 staining. Loss of perfusion and secondary hypoxia are central to muscle fibre necrosis, phagocytosis and regeneration that account for the markedly elevated serum muscle enzymes (eg, CK, AST, ALT, LDH) seen in active DM.

How this microangiopathy produces the patient’s clinical features helps explain why dermatomyositis can mimic other disorders. The inflammatory injury extends beyond muscle fibres to involve perimysial connective tissue and adjacent subcutaneous tissue. Active myositis with associated myofasciitis increases vascular permeability and generates cytokine-driven interstitial fluid accumulation within and around affected muscle groups. This mechanism differs from classical angioedema: it is not mediated by mast-cell histamine release nor by bradykinin excess, and therefore antihistamines, epinephrine or C1-esterase–targeted treatments are not effective. Instead, the swelling is driven by muscle-centered inflammation and edema — sometimes termed pseudoangioedema — which, when prominent in the neck and face, can present to ENT or emergency teams as presumed infectious or allergic neck swelling, as occurred in this patient.

Oropharyngeal and swallowing dysfunction in DM reflects involvement of the striated muscles of the pharynx and upper esophageal sphincter (including cricopharyngeus). Inflammation, necrosis and perifascicular atrophy of these muscles cause weakness, incoordination and impaired bolus propulsion. Clinically this manifests as dysphagia, aspiration risk and pooling in the valleculae and pyriform fossae as seen on the patient’s video fluoroscopic swallow. Severe pharyngeal muscle involvement can produce increased secretions and cough during nasendoscopy without anatomic obstruction. Respiratory testing may show an extrathoracic restrictive pattern when upper airway or bulbar muscles are weak; more generalized restrictive physiology can reflect diaphragmatic or intercostal muscle involvement or coexisting interstitial lung disease (ILD).

The SAE1 antibody defines a distinct serologic subgroup within DM. SAE1 is a subunit of the SUMO (small ubiquitin-like modifier) activating enzyme complex that initiates the SUMOylation pathway — a post-translational modification that regulates nuclear transport, transcription, DNA repair and stress responses. Autoantibodies directed at SAE1 are thought to identify an immune phenotype rather than directly demonstrating pathogenicity via blockade of SUMOylation; however, their presence correlates with a characteristic clinical pattern. Patients with anti-SAE1 positivity more often have prominent cutaneous features (eg, diffuse facial erythema, shawl or V-neck signs, heliotrope changes), a relatively high frequency of dysphagia, and a substantial risk of ILD. In the case reported, the combination of prominent facial/neck cutaneous erythema, periungual changes, severe dysphagia and diffuse muscle inflammation on PET/MRI aligns with the SAE1 phenotype. The literature also suggests an intermediate association with malignancy (reported in some series around the range observed here), so patients with anti-SAE1 antibodies are generally managed with appropriate age- and risk-based cancer screening.

Comparing SAE1 to other myositis-specific autoantibodies helps understand expected organ involvement and guides evaluation. Anti-Mi-2 antibodies classically associate with prominent cutaneous DM features and a generally favorable prognosis with good steroid responsiveness. Anti–TIF1-γ antibodies are strongly linked to cancer-associated DM and conspicuous skin disease but also portend higher malignancy risk. Anti-MDA5 (melanoma differentiation–associated gene 5) is associated with clinically amyopathic DM, rapidly progressive ILD and ulcerative skin lesions. Antisynthetase antibodies (eg, anti-Jo-1) characterize the antisynthetase syndrome with mechanic’s hands, myositis, ILD and arthritis. SAE1 seropositivity overlaps with several features of these groups (notably prominent skin disease and ILD tendency), but differs by its particular constellation — frequent dysphagia and cutaneous prominence without the same strength of association with rapidly progressive ILD seen with MDA5 or the very high malignancy risk seen with TIF1-γ in some cohorts. These serologic distinctions are probabilistic rather than absolute, so clinical assessment and targeted investigations remain essential.

Epidemiologically, DM is uncommon (incidence roughly 10 per million) and has a bimodal age distribution with an adult peak in the mid-adult decades — consistent with this patient in his 40s. Generalized subcutaneous edema or pseudoangioedema is rare in DM cohorts, reported in only a small fraction of patients. Because neck and facial swelling are more commonly investigated as infectious deep-neck processes or histaminergic/bradykinin-mediated angioedema, the DM diagnosis may be delayed unless careful skin and neuromuscular examination suggests myositis and testing (eg, serum CK, myositis antibody panel, imaging, biopsy) is pursued. Recognizing that subcutaneous edema can result from muscle inflammation rather than primary vascular leak syndromes prevents misdirected therapies and prompts immunosuppressive treatment to address the underlying microangiopathy and myositis, as illustrated by the favorable response in this case.

## Diagnostic Workup and Reasoning: From Enceinte Neck Edema to Dermatomyositis

The diagnostic challenge in this case was to explain atypical, prominent cervical soft tissue swelling that clinically mimicked angioedema or deep neck infection, while also accounting for the patient’s proximal weakness and subtle cutaneous changes. A stepwise, targeted workup was therefore performed to rule out infectious and angioedema causes, demonstrate active myositis, identify an autoantibody-defined subtype, evaluate organ involvement, and screen for an associated malignancy. Each investigation contributed to shifting the working diagnosis from ENT and allergy differentials toward autoimmune inflammatory myopathy — specifically SAE1 antibody–positive dermatomyositis.

Laboratory evidence of muscle injury was decisive. The creatine kinase (CK) was profoundly elevated (16,184 U/L), with a concordant pattern of transaminase and lactate dehydrogenase elevation (ALT 143 U/L, AST 396 U/L, LDH 692 U/L) that is typical of skeletal muscle injury rather than isolated hepatocellular disease. Normal renal function and a negative urine myoglobin test argued against severe rhabdomyolysis–related renal compromise in this presentation. These results redirected attention away from isolated soft-tissue infection or idiopathic angioedema and toward a primary myopathic process.

Because the initial presentation suggested angioedema, complement and C1-esterase investigations were essential to exclude bradykinin-mediated mechanisms. Normal C3 and C4 levels with normal C1 esterase inhibitor quantification and function effectively excluded hereditary or acquired C1-INH deficiency as the cause of neck swelling, making histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema unlikely in this clinical context. The absence of exposure to common precipitants (eg, ACE inhibitors) and a non-pruritic, non-fluctuating course further decreased the probability of typical allergic angioedema.

Autoimmune serology provided strong disease-specific evidence. A high-titre speckled ANA supported an autoimmune process, and a myositis panel was strongly positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies while negative for other myositis-specific antibodies (Mi-2, TIF1-γ, Jo-1, OJ). The isolated, strong SAE1 positivity both characterises a distinct dermatomyositis subset and aligns with the clinical phenotype here (cutaneous signs, dysphagia, and frequent neck/face involvement in some reported cases). Before instituting intensive immunosuppression, a comprehensive infection screen (viral serologies, HIV, hepatitis, TB testing) was appropriately completed and was reassuring, permitting safe escalation of immunotherapy.

Imaging studies localized and quantified the inflammatory myopathy. MRI of the thighs demonstrated muscle edema and signal changes consistent with active myositis, particularly in pelvic and hip muscles, corroborating the clinical proximal weakness. FDG-PET confirmed diffuse, metabolically active skeletal muscle inflammation with marked uptake in head and neck musculature, explaining the cervical soft-tissue swelling as inflammatory myositis affecting neck muscles and adjacent tissues rather than true angioedema. Importantly, PET and CT imaging did not reveal metabolically active malignancy to account for a paraneoplastic myositis at baseline.

Swallowing and pulmonary assessments were integral because dysphagia and lung involvement materially affect prognosis and management. Videofluoroscopic swallow study showed severe pharyngeal dysphagia with post-swallow penetration, confirming clinically important bulbar involvement that explained aspiration risk and the patient’s cough and secretions. CT chest demonstrated peribronchial consolidation consistent with aspiration pneumonitis and multiple non-cavitating pulmonary nodules, but no radiographic interstitial lung disease (ILD) at presentation. Pulmonary function testing showed an extrathoracic restrictive pattern without bronchodilator responsiveness; together these findings necessitated aspiration management and influenced immunosuppressive choices (avoiding agents with greater pulmonary toxicity risk).

Muscle histopathology provided the final diagnostic confirmation. Biopsy showed perifascicular necrosis, degeneration and regeneration of myofibres with perimysial inflammatory infiltrates and, importantly, deposition of membrane attack complex (C5b-9) in perifascicular capillaries. This pattern is characteristic of dermatomyositis and distinguishes it from other inflammatory myopathies (for example, inclusion body myositis, which has rimmed vacuoles and endomysial CD8+ cells, or immune-mediated necrotising myopathy, which tends to lack the perifascicular capillary pattern). The histologic evidence, together with the serology and imaging, converged to establish SAE1 antibody–positive dermatomyositis as the unifying diagnosis.

Given the known association between dermatomyositis and malignancy, formal cancer risk stratification per IMACS criteria was applied. The patient met high-risk criteria (dermatomyositis plus dysphagia) with additional intermediate risks (male sex and SAE1 positivity), justifying an expedited and broad malignancy screen. Baseline tests — CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, PET scan, and tumour markers (CEA, CA19‑9, CA‑125, PSA) — were negative for occult neoplasm. Nonetheless, because some paraneoplastic associations may be occult initially, a plan for ongoing surveillance including upper and lower endoscopy and interval imaging was appropriately instituted.

Synthesis of the results produced a coherent explanatory model: the prominent neck swelling represented inflammatory edema and muscle enlargement from active myositis of the cervical and pharyngeal muscles, rather than histaminergic or bradykinin-driven angioedema or a deep neck infection. Profound CK elevation and characteristic transaminase/LDH elevations signalled severe myofibre injury. MRI and FDG-PET localized active inflammation predominantly in head/neck and proximal limb muscles, correlating with dysphagia and proximal weakness. SAE1 autoantibody positivity defined the myositis subtype and, together with classic histopathological capillary C5b-9 deposition and perifascicular muscle fiber changes, confirmed dermatomyositis. Negative infection screens permitted timely initiation of high-intensity immunosuppression, and a negative initial malignancy workup established a surveillance-based rather than immediate oncologic management approach.

In summary, targeted laboratory, serologic, imaging, functional and histopathologic investigations sequentially excluded mimics, demonstrated severe and geographically relevant myositis, identified a specific autoantibody subtype, and guided safe, decisive treatment and appropriate cancer surveillance.

## Acute and Longitudinal Management: Airway-Safe Immunosuppression and Supportive Care

This patient’s presentation required simultaneous attention to airway safety, prompt control of systemic inflammation, prevention of complications of immunosuppression, and supportive measures to address dysphagia and muscle weakness. Management priorities were therefore (1) protect the airway and reduce aspiration risk, (2) rapidly suppress the immune-mediated myositis responsible for neck swelling and pharyngeal weakness, (3) introduce steroid-sparing agents to limit long‑term steroid toxicity, and (4) provide prophylaxis and rehabilitation while monitoring response and safety. The measures taken in this case illustrate these principles.

Airway and aspiration management
Neck soft‑tissue swelling in dermatomyositis can reflect underlying muscle inflammation and pharyngeal neuromuscular weakness rather than histaminergic angioedema. Early bedside assessment by ENT, including flexible nasendoscopy where available, is useful to exclude obstructive lesions and to identify pooling of secretions or impaired protective reflexes. In this patient, increased secretions and coughing on nasendoscopy and instrumental swallow testing demonstrating severe pharyngeal dysphagia mandated active airway and aspiration precautions. A nasogastric tube was placed to secure nutrition and avoid oral intake until swallowing improved; this is standard when aspiration risk is high and allows delivery of oral immunosuppressive medications if necessary. Continuous monitoring for progressive airway compromise is essential; escalation to intubation or surgical airway should be considered if there is evidence of rapidly progressive upper airway obstruction or loss of protective airway reflexes.

Induction therapy and steroid strategy
Severe muscle involvement with potential airway compromise warrants rapid high‑dose corticosteroid therapy. The treating team administered intravenous methylprednisolone 1 g daily for 3 days—an induction strategy commonly used for severe or life‑threatening manifestations of idiopathic inflammatory myopathy—to achieve quick anti‑inflammatory effect. After pulse therapy, the patient was converted to an oral prednisone taper beginning at 60 mg daily with gradual dose reduction. This approach balances the need for prompt disease control with subsequent tapering to minimize prolonged high‑dose steroid exposure. Monitoring during this phase includes daily clinical assessment of strength, neck swelling and swallowing, serial CK and transaminases to gauge biochemical response, and vigilance for steroid complications (hyperglycaemia, hypertension, neuropsychiatric effects, and infection).

Steroid‑sparing and adjunctive immunomodulation
Given the severity of disease and the need to limit steroid burden, early addition of steroid‑sparing agents was appropriate. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was given as a loading regimen of 2 g/kg over 5 days followed by monthly cycles; IVIG can produce relatively rapid clinical improvement in dermatomyositis, is useful for severe dysphagia or bulbar involvement, and is often combined with other immunosuppressants in refractory or severe cases. Mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) at 1 g twice daily served as the primary long‑term steroid‑sparing agent; MMF has an established role in dermatomyositis for muscle and cutaneous disease and was chosen here for tolerability and patient preference for an oral regimen. Because the disease was severe and involved critical musculature, rituximab was also administered (1 g two doses, two weeks apart) as additional escalation; rituximab is often used for severe, refractory, or antibody‑mediated inflammatory myopathies and can be effective when rapid disease control or steroid sparing is required. The combined regimen—steroids, IVIG, MMF and rituximab—reflects an aggressive but rational approach for moderate to severe SAE1‑positive dermatomyositis with bulbar/airway involvement.

Rationale for agent selection and avoidance
Agent choices were guided by organ involvement and risk‑benefit considerations. Methotrexate, frequently used in dermatomyositis, was avoided because the patient had abnormal pulmonary function and radiologic findings (aspiration‑related consolidation and restrictive pattern); methotrexate has a known potential to aggravate or confound interstitial lung disease, so it is often avoided when ILD or impaired lung function is present. Azathioprine and tacrolimus remain valid alternatives if MMF is not tolerated or ineffective; in this case MMF was well tolerated so switching was unnecessary. Rituximab was reasonable given severe disease and the need for relatively rapid and durable immunomodulation. These decisions should always weigh comorbidities, infection risk, fertility considerations, and drug adverse‑effect profiles.

Infection prophylaxis and safety monitoring
High‑intensity immunosuppression requires proactive infection prophylaxis and screening. Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) prophylaxis is recommended when combining high‑dose steroids and other immunosuppressives; trimethoprim‑sulfamethoxazole (TMP‑SMX) is first‑line but was stopped in this patient due to hemolytic anemia, after which monthly inhaled pentamidine was used as an alternative. Baseline screening for chronic viral infections (HBV, HCV, HIV) and latent TB is important before rituximab/other biologics; this patient had an indeterminate QuantiFERON and negative AFB sputum; such results require clinical judgment—repeat testing, specialist infectious disease input, or empiric treatment may be considered depending on exposure history and ongoing immunosuppression. VZV seronegativity was noted; live zoster vaccination is contraindicated once high‑dose immunosuppression is initiated, so vaccination status should be reconciled pre‑treatment when feasible and VZV exposure risk mitigated. Regular laboratory monitoring—full blood count, liver function tests (ALT/AST/LDH were elevated at presentation), renal function, and CK—was used to follow disease activity and drug toxicity. For steroid safety, glucose, blood pressure, bone protection (calcium, vitamin D, and consideration of bisphosphonate in prolonged high‑dose use) and gastroprotection are appropriate.

Rehabilitation, dysphagia care and monitoring
Functional recovery requires early multidisciplinary rehabilitation. Speech and language therapy directed at swallow assessment and progressive re‑feeding plans is crucial; in this case, a video fluoroscopic swallow study guided the decision for enteral feeding and later safe advancement of diet. Ongoing swallow therapy and supervised trials facilitate recovery and removal of nasogastric feeding once safe. Physical therapy focused on proximal muscle strengthening and graded mobilization helps restore function and prevent deconditioning. Regular clinical assessment of proximal strength and activities of daily living, combined with biochemical markers (CK) and inflammatory markers, guide response assessment. Imaging or pulmonary follow‑up is indicated if ILD is suspected or if new respiratory symptoms emerge.

Escalation, tapering and long‑term strategy
Tapering steroids should be individualized based on clinical improvement, normalization of CK, and resolution of dysphagia and neck swelling. In this patient, CK normalisation and clinical recovery supported progressive prednisone tapering and eventual discontinuation at seven months. Continuation of monthly IVIG and maintenance MMF was planned with intention to de‑escalate MMF over subsequent months if remission persists. Criteria to consider tapering or stopping therapies include sustained clinical remission off high‑dose steroids, normal or stable CK and imaging, and absence of new organ involvement. Conversely, relapse, persistent or progressive ILD, refractory bulbar disease, or development of malignancy would prompt reassessment and possible escalation (renewed high‑dose steroids, alternative steroid‑sparing agents, or repeat rituximab). Ongoing malignancy surveillance is also part of long‑term care in dermatomyositis, given the associated cancer risk in adult patients.

In summary, management combined timely airway and aspiration protection with aggressive induction immunosuppression (IV methylprednisolone pulses), rapid institution of steroid‑sparing therapies (IVIG, MMF) and targeted biologic therapy (rituximab) appropriate to the severity and organ involvement. Prophylaxis against opportunistic infections, careful pulmonary considerations in agent selection, and coordinated rehabilitation and monitoring were integral to achieving clinical remission while minimizing treatment complications.

## Outcomes, Follow-up, and Prognosis in SAE1-Positive Dermatomyositis

This patient’s course illustrates a rapid and favorable clinical response to early, intensive immunosuppression in SAE1-positive dermatomyositis, with objective and functional milestones that guided ongoing management.

Clinical response timeline
Within the first three weeks of treatment (high‑dose IV methylprednisolone followed by an oral prednisone taper, IVIG, mycophenolate mofetil and two doses of rituximab), the patient experienced a marked reduction in cervical soft‑tissue swelling and a rapid biochemical response: serum creatine kinase (CK) fell from 16,184 U/L to a normal value (37 U/L) by three weeks. Proximal muscle strength returned to normal in the same early period. Dysphagia lagged behind muscle recovery: despite improvement in neck oedema and limb strength, significant pharyngeal dysphagia persisted, requiring nasogastric feeding. Swallowing function improved over the following weeks, permitting nasogastric tube removal at about two months. By three months, cervical swelling had completely resolved and proximal strength remained stable. Prednisone was successfully stopped seven months after discharge, at which point dysphagia had fully resolved.

Pulmonary course
The patient’s respiratory picture was dominated initially by aspiration changes and an extrathoracic restrictive pattern on pulmonary function testing. With immunosuppression and supportive care (including addressing dysphagia), follow-up imaging showed progressive resolution of lung findings: repeat chest CT at approximately 11 months demonstrated near‑complete resolution of the basal ground‑glass opacities and consolidation attributed to aspiration. Pulmonary function and exercise tolerance remained stable at 12 months, with only mild residual restriction presumed to reflect baseline physiology rather than progressive interstitial lung disease.

Functional recovery
Functional endpoints mirrored the objective improvements. Restoration of proximal muscle strength by three weeks allowed recovery of mobility and activities of daily living (ADLs). Return to safe oral intake occurred around two months as swallowing improved, and by three months the patient was eating soft solids and progressing toward normal diet. By one year he reported good exercise tolerance and no residual functional limitations related to myositis.

Long‑term immunosuppression plan and monitoring
Management after discharge emphasized steroid‑sparing maintenance and cautious tapering guided by clinical status and objective measures (symptoms, manual muscle testing, CK levels). In this case, the team continued monthly IVIG and mycophenolate mofetil, with a plan to taper mycophenolate over the subsequent six months if the patient remained clinically well and CK stayed normal. Prednisone was tapered off by seven months. Ongoing decisions about immunosuppression intensity and duration were individualized, based on relapse risk, side‑effects, and recovery of strength and swallowing. Regular clinical reviews, serial CK measurements, and strength testing were used to detect early relapse; imaging or escalation of therapy would be considered for recurrent weakness, rising CK, recurrent or progressive dysphagia, or new organ involvement.

Malignancy surveillance
Because dermatomyositis, and specifically SAE1 positivity in this patient’s risk profile, confer an elevated—but not obligate—malignancy risk, a structured surveillance strategy was adopted. After an initially negative workup (CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, PET, tumour markers), the plan included regular symptom review and targeted clinical examination, periodic laboratory monitoring, interval chest imaging, and completion of endoscopic evaluation (planned gastroscopy and colonoscopy) to exclude occult gastrointestinal malignancy. The patient was counselled that malignancy risk persists despite an initially reassuring screen, and that new systemic symptoms, unexplained weight loss, or focal findings would prompt prompt re‑investigation.

Prognostic considerations
This case demonstrates that prominent cervical or facial oedema in dermatomyositis—though uncommon—can respond well to timely, aggressive immunosuppressive therapy. Prominent subcutaneous oedema has been associated with more severe disease in some series, particularly when accompanied by dysphagia or significant muscle involvement, and therefore warrants prompt recognition and escalation of treatment. In this patient, early diagnosis (prompt CK testing and muscle biopsy), multimodal immunotherapy, and supportive measures produced rapid biochemical and clinical remission with restoration of function and resolution of aspiration‑related lung changes. Longer‑term prognosis remains guarded by the need for ongoing surveillance for relapse and malignancy, and by the potential adverse effects of prolonged immunosuppression; however, his one‑year outcome is favorable and compatible with sustained remission under careful follow‑up.

## Key Clinical Lessons and Decision Algorithms

This case highlights several practical lessons and a stepwise approach to avoid misdiagnosis when patients present with facial or cervical swelling that appears to be angioedema. The central teaching point is to maintain a broad differential when the presentation is atypical for histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema and to rapidly consider inflammatory myopathies such as dermatomyositis when subtle cutaneous or muscular features are present.

When “angioedema” isn’t angioedema
Consider alternatives to angioedema when swelling is persistent (days to weeks), non-urticarial, non-pruritic, non-tender or when there is poor response to antihistamines/epinephrine. Normal complement levels (C3, C4) and normal C1 esterase inhibitor quantification/function make hereditary or complement-mediated angioedema unlikely and should prompt reconsideration of diagnosis. In that setting, look for cutaneous or neuromuscular clues of dermatomyositis. A bedside creatine kinase (CK) is a simple, rapid test that can point toward myositis when markedly elevated, as occurred in this case, and can expedite appropriate specialist input and imaging.

Focused examination checklist
A short structured exam improves detection of subtle dermatomyositis features that may be missed in an ENT or emergency assessment focused on airway and infection. Key elements to examine and document:
- Nailfolds: periungual erythema, capillary dilatation or microhemorrhages on capillaroscopy.
- Face and photosensitive areas: violaceous or dusky erythema over the eyelids, nasolabial folds, cheeks, and shawl/V signs across shoulders/chest or Holster sign on lateral thighs.
- Hands: Gottron’s papules over extensor surfaces (may be absent).
- Muscles: proximal power (hip flexors, shoulder abduction) — compare bilaterally.
- Bulbar/airway function: assess for dysphagia by asking about choking, nasal regurgitation, voice changes; look for wet or gurgly voice, coughing on swallow, pooling of secretions.
These focused signs in a patient with unexplained neck swelling should raise suspicion for dermatomyositis-associated inflammatory edema rather than primary angioedema.

Rapid workup algorithm for suspected myositis presenting with neck/face edema
When clinical suspicion for myositis exists, pursue a targeted, expedited workup to define severity, complications and need for urgent therapy:
1. Immediate bedside tests: serum CK, AST/ALT, LDH, CRP/white cell count, basic metabolic panel and urine myoglobin (if rhabdomyolysis suspected). Markedly elevated CK, as in this case, is a red flag.
2. Myositis autoantibody panel (including MSA and MAA such as SAE1): helps subclassify disease and inform associated risks (eg, dysphagia, ILD, malignancy) though treatment should not be delayed awaiting results.
3. Imaging to localize inflammation and assess complications:
   - MRI of involved muscle groups (eg, neck/thighs) for active myositis distribution.
   - FDG-PET can show multifocal muscle activity and exclude occult malignancy when clinically indicated.
4. Functional assessments:
   - Video fluoroscopic swallow study if dysphagia or signs of aspiration/wet voice are present.
   - Pulmonary function tests and chest CT to evaluate for aspiration changes and interstitial lung disease.
5. Tissue diagnosis: muscle biopsy if the diagnosis is uncertain or pathology will change management; biopsy features in dermatomyositis (eg, perifascicular atrophy, capillary complement deposition) can be diagnostic.
This sequence balances rapid identification (CK, antibodies) with imaging and functional studies to gauge severity and guide therapy.

Risk stratification and cancer screening
Dermatomyositis carries an increased risk of occult malignancy. Use established risk frameworks (eg, IMACS guidance) to stratify patients early: features such as dermatomyositis phenotype, dysphagia, certain myositis antibodies and older age or male sex increase malignancy risk. In patients meeting high-risk criteria, initiate baseline malignancy screening promptly (history/exam, age-appropriate imaging such as CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, PET if available, tumour markers and targeted endoscopies as clinically indicated). Continue interval surveillance guided by symptoms and risk factors. In this case the presence of dermatomyositis with dysphagia and SAE1 positivity appropriately triggered an early, broad malignancy screen.

Therapeutic priorities — align treatment with lung status and dysphagia severity
Treatment choices must balance severity of muscle and bulbar involvement with pulmonary status:
- Severe pharyngeal/airway involvement or rapidly progressive weakness: initiate high-dose intravenous glucocorticoids (eg, methylprednisolone pulses) urgently to reduce inflammation and preserve airway and swallowing function. This case required 3 days of IV methylprednisolone.
- For severe dysphagia or when rapid steroid-sparing effect is desired, add IVIG early; IVIG can produce relatively rapid improvement in bulbar muscle strength and reduce aspiration risk.
- Choose steroid-sparing agents based on comorbidities: mycophenolate mofetil, azathioprine, methotrexate, tacrolimus and rituximab are options. Consider lung disease when choosing agents (avoid or use caution with methotrexate if ILD is present). Early addition of a maintenance agent is advisable to reduce cumulative steroid exposure.
- In refractory or severe disease, biologicals such as rituximab can be used adjunctively.
- Provide appropriate prophylaxis for opportunistic infections when using combination immunosuppression.
The goal is to rapidly suppress immune-mediated muscle inflammation to prevent irreversible necrosis and to control edema that threatens airway or swallowing.

Early immunosuppression preserves function
Delay in recognizing inflammatory causes of neck swelling risks ongoing muscle injury, aspiration, prolonged dysphagia and long hospital stays. Early, aggressive immunosuppression (high-dose steroids plus IVIG in severe bulbar disease) was associated in this case with normalization of CK, recovery of proximal strength and eventual resolution of cervical edema and dysphagia. Prompt escalation to steroid-sparing agents prevents relapse and facilitates steroid tapering.

Interdisciplinary coordination
Optimal care requires a coordinated multidisciplinary team to address airway safety, rehabilitation and complications:
- ENT for airway assessment and to exclude structural causes or need for airway intervention.
- Rheumatology/immunology or neurology for diagnosis, immunotherapy decisions and longitudinal management.
- Speech–language pathology for swallowing assessment and therapy; early involvement reduces aspiration risk and guides nutrition.
- Nutrition support (eg, nasogastric feeding) when dysphagia precludes safe oral intake.
- Pulmonology for evaluation and follow-up of aspiration pneumonitis or ILD, including PFTs and imaging.
- Pathology and radiology for biopsy interpretation and MRI/PET reporting.
Clear communication and joint decision-making expedite appropriate immunosuppression while safeguarding airway and pulmonary function.

Practical decision checklist to avoid missed dermatomyositis in neck swelling
- If neck/face swelling is unexplained by infection or trauma and is non-pruritic/non-urticarial: check CK immediately.
- Perform a focused skin and neuromuscular exam (nailfolds, heliotrope/shawl/Holster signs, proximal strength, signs of dysphagia).
- Order myositis antibodies and baseline infection screen before immunosuppression.
- If CK is markedly elevated or bulbar/respiratory signs are present: obtain MRI of muscles and a swallow study, start high-dose steroids and involve multidisciplinary team; add IVIG early for severe dysphagia.
- Initiate malignancy screening per risk stratification and tailor long-term immunosuppression to pulmonary status and tolerability.
Following this structured approach reduces diagnostic delay, averts inappropriate treatments for presumed angioedema, and improves functional outcomes in patients whose dermatomyositis presents atypically with facial or neck oedema.
