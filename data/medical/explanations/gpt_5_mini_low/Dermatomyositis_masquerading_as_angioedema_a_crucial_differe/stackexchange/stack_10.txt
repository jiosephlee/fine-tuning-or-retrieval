Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Morning Report] What is the significance of nailfold capillaroscopy findings such as periungual erythema, microhemorrhages, and avascular areas in idiopathic inflammatory myopathies, and how can these findings guide the diagnostic approach in atypical presentations like isolated neck swelling?

Clinical pearl — what those nailfold findings mean, and why they matter in an odd presentation

Periungual erythema, nailfold microhemorrhages and avascular (capillary-dropout) areas on nailfold capillaroscopy are not cosmetic findings: they are a direct window into microvascular injury that is characteristic of the idiopathic inflammatory myopathies, particularly dermatomyositis. Pathophysiologically, dermatomyositis produces complement-mediated capillary and endomysial microangiopathy (C5b-9 deposition and endothelial injury), causing dilated/looped capillaries, petechial microhemorrhages and eventual capillary loss. These changes can precede, accompany or outlast overt skin rashes and muscle weakness.

How this guides diagnosis in atypical presentations (for example isolated neck swelling)
- When a patient presents with an unusual, non‑infectious, non‑histaminergic swelling (eg, persistent “angioedema” of the neck) and the history/response to therapy is atypical, finding periungual erythema/microhemorrhages/avascular areas should raise immediate suspicion for an inflammatory myopathy, especially dermatomyositis. In the case report, subtle nailfold microhemorrhages and avascular areas prompted further investigation and ultimately led to the diagnosis of SAE1‑positive dermatomyositis.
- Practical downstream steps triggered by such capillary changes: obtain serum muscle enzymes (CK), myositis autoantibody panel, targeted imaging (muscle MRI or FDG‑PET), and consider early muscle biopsy if needed. These tests were exactly what changed the course in the reported patient (very high CK, positive SAE1 antibody, MRI/FDG‑PET and diagnostic muscle biopsy).
- The findings also help distinguish pathophysiology: capillary abnormalities point to inflammatory microangiopathy and muscle inflammation as the cause of subcutaneous/soft‑tissue oedema—rather than histaminergic or bradykinin‑mediated angioedema—which alters both immediate management (prompt immunosuppression rather than antihistamines or C1‑inhibitor therapies) and diagnostic workup (look for dysphagia, ILD, malignancy risk).

Clinical utility and limitations
- Nailfold capillaroscopy is noninvasive, bedside-accessible, and informative in patients with minimal or absent classic cutaneous stigmata (heliotrope, Gottron’s papules). In this patient, classic rashes were subtle or absent but capillary changes were present.
- The degree of capillary loss and microhemorrhages can correlate with disease activity and vasculopathy risk (and sometimes with ILD), but capillaroscopy is supportive — not diagnostic on its own. Definitive diagnosis still rests on the integration of clinical features, CK, autoantibodies, imaging and, when required, muscle biopsy (as performed here showing perifascicular necrosis and C5b‑9 deposition).
- Not all myositis autoantibodies map to the same capillaroscopic pattern, and capillaroscopy cannot reliably tell you the specific antibody; it signals that you should look for myositis.

Takeaway teaching points
- Always inspect and (when available) perform nailfold capillaroscopy in patients with unexplained soft‑tissue swelling, atypical “angioedema,” new dysphagia, or proximal weakness. Subtle periungual changes can be the clue that converts a missed ENT/infectious diagnosis into dermatomyositis.
- If capillaroscopy shows microhemorrhages, periungual erythema or avascular areas: urgently check CK, request a myositis antibody panel, and arrange muscle imaging/biopsy as indicated — and consider early immunosuppression if clinical severity warrants (airway, dysphagia, marked myositis), as was done successfully in this case.
