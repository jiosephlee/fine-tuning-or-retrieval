Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# When “Angioedema” Isn’t: Recognizing Pseudoangioedema in Dermatomyositis

What makes this case worth reading: a previously well man in his 40s presented with progressive subacute neck swelling that looked like angioedema or a deep neck infection. ENT and imaging found diffuse neck soft‑tissue edema but no source. It was only after a careful bedside re‑exam (subtle periungual changes, thigh rash), a focused neurologic check (hip flexor weakness) and an urgent CK that the team pivoted to dermatomyositis. He proved to be SAE1‑antibody positive with biopsy‑proven dermatomyositis, significant myositis including head/neck muscles and dysphagia — and required urgent immunosuppression and airway‑directed management. The key surprise: dermatomyositis can cause non‑histaminergic, non‑bradykinin “pseudoangioedema” of the face/neck from adjacent muscle inflammation and edema, and that presentation can masquerade as ENT/angioedema emergencies.

Why this matters in the ED/ward
- Neck and facial swelling usually triggers a standard reflex: airway assessment, consider anaphylaxis/angioedema, look for infection (cellulitis, Ludwig’s), get imaging and call ENT. That pathway is right most of the time — but it can lead to diagnostic anchoring when swelling is subacute, non‑tender, unexplained on CT, and accompanied by subtle inflammatory or neuromuscular signs. Missing inflammatory myopathy risks delayed immunosuppression, worsening dysphagia, aspiration, and progressive muscle/airway compromise.
- This case shows how a few quick bedside checks and three simple labs can change the diagnosis and management within hours.

Bedside red flags that should make you stop and think myositis (not histaminergic/bradykinin angioedema)
- Subacute progressive swelling over days to weeks rather than minutes–hours
- Non‑tender, diffuse soft tissue swelling of the face/neck with no clear infectious source on imaging
- Dysphagia, hoarseness, or cough suggesting pharyngeal/laryngeal muscle involvement
- Proximal muscle weakness (difficulty rising from chair, reduced hip flexor power)
- Cutaneous clues: periungual telangiectasia/erythema, Gottron’s signs, heliotrope or violaceous facial rash, Holster sign, or any new dermatosis
- Normal complement and C1‑esterase studies (argues against classic hereditary/acquired angioedema)
- Modest systemic inflammatory markers (CRP may be mildly elevated) without sepsis physiology

Immediate labs to get when pseudoangioedema is on your differential
- Serum creatine kinase (CK) — can be massively elevated (this case: 16,184 U/L)
- AST/ALT and LDH — can be raised with muscle injury (e.g., AST 396 U/L here)
- Basic autoimmune screen (ANA) and targeted myositis panel if available — but don’t wait for panels to start treatment if clinically indicated
- C3/C4 and C1 esterase function/quantification to exclude complement‑mediated angioedema
- Urine myoglobin and renal function (rhabdomyolysis risk)
- Consider urgent bedside capillary nailfold inspection (nailfold hemorrhages/avascular areas were present in this case)

Pragmatic ED → ward algorithm: how to pivot efficiently
1. Airway first
   - Assess airway patency, voice change, stridor, drooling, respiratory distress. Early ENT airway involvement if any concern for compromise.
   - Prepare for intubation if progressive bulbar weakness, severe dysphagia, or inability to protect airway.

2. Standard initial workup while you reassess diagnosis
   - Sepsis/CT neck to exclude abscess, plain labs, blood cultures if febrile.
   - Complement/C1 esterase studies and allergy workup if angioedema suspected.

3. Re‑examine with a neuromuscular and dermatologic lens
   - Rapid strength screen (proximal > distal), swallowing assessment (is there pooling, coughing with secretions?), and inspection for subtle cutaneous signs and nailfold changes.
   - If weakness, dysphagia, or cutaneous signs present — add CK, AST/ALT, LDH immediately.

4. If CK is elevated or clinical suspicion high
   - Notify rheumatology/immunology/neurology and admit.
   - Consider urgent IV methylprednisolone if there is significant bulbar or respiratory muscle involvement (this patient received 3 days of 1 g IV methylprednisolone).
   - Arrange further workup inpatient: myositis antibody panel, MRI pelvis/thighs, EMG/muscle biopsy, swallow studies and chest imaging for aspiration or ILD.

5. Concurrent safety steps
   - Start aspiration precautions, NPO if significant dysphagia and arrange speech/swallow assessment; consider early nasogastric feeding.
   - Monitor CK and renal function for rhabdomyolysis; treat accordingly.
   - Discuss malignancy screening plans early (dermatomyositis carries increased cancer risk).

Treatment principles in the acute setting (illustrated by the case)
- High‑dose IV glucocorticoids are often the first line for threatening myositis with airway or bulbar involvement.
- Early steroid‑sparing or adjunctive therapy (IVIG, mycophenolate, rituximab) is common when severe involvement is present or when rapid steroid taper is desired; choices are individualized (this patient received IVIG, mycophenolate and rituximab with good outcome).
- Protect against opportunistic infections when giving combined immunosuppression (PJP prophylaxis considered; alternatives used if intolerance).

Clinical pearls
- Pseudoangioedema from dermatomyositis is uncommon (reported in ~6–7% of cohorts) but real — SAE1 and other myositis antibodies (eg, MDA‑5, TIF1‑γ) can be associated with facial/neck edema.
- Neck swelling due to myositis reflects muscle inflammation and edema — it is not histamine or bradykinin mediated, so antihistamines/epinephrine/C1 inhibitor therapy won’t help.
- A CK is cheap, fast, and high yield: get it early if the swelling is subacute, non‑tender, or accompanied by weakness or skin findings.
- Dysphagia can be the clue to significant pharyngeal/neck muscle involvement — swallowing studies and early nutrition planning matter.
- Always reassess: if ENT/CT fails to show an infectious cause and the patient is stable, broaden the differential to autoimmune myositis.

Take‑home messages
- Think myositis when neck/facial “angioedema” is subacute, non‑tender, unexplained on imaging, or associated with dysphagia, proximal weakness, or cutaneous changes.
- Send CK, AST/ALT and LDH urgently — early labs can pivot diagnosis and management.
- Protect the airway, involve ENT early if compromise is possible, and get rheumatology/immunology on board when CK is elevated or clinical suspicion is high.
- Treat severe bulbar/airway‑threatening myositis promptly with high‑dose steroids and escalate to IVIG/other immunosuppression as needed; coordinate swallow support and aspiration precautions.

This case is a great reminder: not all “angioedema” is histaminergic or bradykinin‑driven. A quick neuromuscular and skin check — and a CK — can prevent diagnostic drift and get the patient the timely immunotherapy they need.



# Decoding Anti‑SAE1 Dermatomyositis: Phenotype, Pitfalls, and Prognosis

What makes this case interesting?
A previously well man in his 40s presented with progressive, non‑tender neck swelling that looked like angioedema or a deep neck infection. He had no ACE inhibitor exposure, no local infective source on CT or exam, and minimal overt skin findings. A thoughtful exam (subtle periungual changes, Holster sign), a prompted CK drew attention to severe myositis, and serology returned strongly positive for anti‑SAE1. Muscle biopsy and imaging confirmed dermatomyositis. The neck swelling here was not histaminergic or bradykinin‑mediated angioedema but inflammatory soft‑tissue oedema from myositis — a rare but important presentation to recognize.

Why this matters to frontline clinicians
- Dermatomyositis can masquerade as ENT pathology, angioedema, or soft‑tissue infection when head/neck swelling dominates. That leads to delayed rheumatology/immunology input unless you think of myositis.
- Anti‑SAE1 disease has a distinctive phenotype: prominent cutaneous disease, high rates of dysphagia and significant muscle involvement, and frequent lung involvement (including aspiration risk from dysphagia). Malignancy association is real enough to mandate careful screening.
- Serology is helpful but not definitive alone — assay type, signal thresholds and clinical correlation matter.

Quick phenotype summary from the case + literature
- Cutaneous: classic dermatomyositis rashes (heliotrope, Gottron’s) may occur but can be subtle. SAE1 often presents with shawl/V‑neck signs, heliotrope or extensive erythema. In this report the Holster sign and periungual erythema were clues.
- Muscle: can be severe — very high CKs (case CK >16,000 U/L), diffuse myositis on MRI and FDG‑PET, and histology showing perifascicular necrosis and complement deposition.
- Swelling/angioedema phenotype: uncommon overall but described — facial/neck subcutaneous oedema can dominate and be mislabelled as angioedema or infection.
- Dysphagia and aspiration: common in SAE1 disease — the case had severe pharyngeal dysphagia with aspiration changes and required NG feeding for months.
- Lung: SAE1 is associated with ILD in many series; this patient had peribronchial consolidation consistent with aspiration and nodules; no definite fibrosis on baseline CT but abnormal PFTs.
- Malignancy: reported in SAE1 cohorts (~18% in one review). The case was considered high risk (dermatomyositis + dysphagia + SAE1 + male), prompting CT, PET and plans for endoscopy/colonoscopic screening.

Assay and interpretation pitfalls — know your test
- There is assay variability: line blot/Euroimmun, ELISA, immunoprecipitation and other platforms differ in sensitivity, specificity and cutoffs.
- Low or weak line blot bands can be false positives; quantitative signal intensities (where available) and clear thresholds improve interpretation. In this case the Euroline signal was high (173; lab cutoff <35), supporting true positivity.
- Always interpret anti‑SAE1 (and any myositis antibody) in the clinical context: phenotypic concordance (skin changes, myositis, dysphagia/ILD) strengthens confidence; isolated weak seropositivity without compatible phenotype should prompt cautious interpretation and repeat/alternate testing if the result would change management.
- Consider confirmatory testing (different platform or referral lab with immunoprecipitation) when results are unexpected or when malignancy workup will be extensive.

How SAE1 differs from MDA5 and TIF1‑γ (practical comparisons)
- MDA5: often clinically amyopathic or paucimyopathic, strong association with rapidly progressive ILD, skin ulcerations, and a high mortality ILD phenotype in some populations. Facial/hand swelling less prominent as a defining feature than ILD risk.
- TIF1‑γ: strong cancer‑associated dermatomyositis signal, often prominent cutaneous disease and higher malignancy risk (esp. older adults).
- SAE1: prominent skin disease, significant muscle inflammation/dysphagia, frequent ILD but not always the fulminant RP‑ILD seen with MDA5; intermediate malignancy risk (not as high as TIF1‑γ in some series but substantial enough to require formal screening).
- Bottom line: antibody identity helps stratify complications and guide surveillance, but antibody status is only one piece of the puzzle.

Translating a positive SAE1 into practical monitoring and management (how this case did it)
1. Confirm clinical phenotype before over‑reacting to a lone lab result.
   - In this case: compatible exam findings + very high CK + MRI/biopsy concordant = clear clinico‑serologic diagnosis.

2. Immediate management for severe disease
   - High‑dose IV methylprednisolone pulses for airway/bulbar muscle involvement (case: 1 g daily ×3).
   - Early use of steroid‑sparing or additional immunosuppression in severe disease—IVIG, mycophenolate, rituximab were used here with a good outcome.
   - Avoid methotrexate if significant ILD or aspiration risk — illustrated in this case where methotrexate was deferred due to lung concerns.

3. Muscle monitoring
   - Baseline and serial CKs, clinical strength testing, and functional assessment (bulbar function/dysphagia).
   - Cross‑sectional imaging (MRI) or FDG‑PET if distribution or severity unclear.
   - Muscle biopsy reserved where diagnosis is uncertain or to subtype disease (as here).

4. Lung monitoring
   - Baseline PFTs (including DLCO) and high‑resolution chest imaging (HRCT) to look for ILD; repeat periodically or if symptoms change.
   - Low threshold for ENT/SLT assessment and videofluoroscopic swallow for dysphagia (case had severe pharyngeal dysphagia with aspiration).
   - Respiratory physiotherapy, aspiration precautions and consider early IVIG/immunosuppression if aspiration pneumonitis suspected.

5. Malignancy screening strategy
   - Use IMACS‑guided risk stratification: dermatomyositis + dysphagia + SAE1 + male in this patient → high‑risk.
   - Baseline directed imaging: CT chest/abdomen/pelvis and PET where available (case had both and no occult malignancy).
   - Age‑ and sex‑appropriate screening (colonoscopy, PSA, mammography, cervical screening, etc.).
   - Site‑specific endoscopy: in this case plans for gastroscopy/colonoscopy given GI cancer risk and dysphagia.
   - Ongoing vigilance: review symptoms, repeat targeted imaging if new red flags. Frequency individualized (e.g., intense surveillance first 3 years; continue routine population screening thereafter unless new concerns).

Therapeutic takeaways from the case
- Severe bulbar/airway involvement justifies aggressive upfront therapy. This case had pulse steroids, IVIG loading then maintenance, mycophenolate and rituximab — a multi‑modal approach tailored to severity and comorbid lung disease.
- IVIG is useful in refractory or severe disease, especially bulbar dysfunction and dysphagia.
- Choose steroid‑sparing agents considering lung disease, patient preference, and tolerability (mycophenolate chosen here over methotrexate for lung safety and daily oral dosing preference).
- Prophylaxis for opportunistic infection (Pneumocystis) and monitoring for treatment complications is essential; the case illustrates switching prophylaxis strategies when TMP‑SMX intolerance developed.

Clinical pearls
- Don’t accept “idiopathic angioedema” without checking for occult myositis if neck/face swelling is atypical (non‑pruritic, non‑fluctuating with antihistamines, associated with weakness or subtle skin/nail findings). A CK is a cheap, high‑yield test.
- Subcutaneous oedema in dermatomyositis is rare but real; when present in the head/neck it can mimic ENT disease or angioedema.
- A strongly positive SAE1 on a robust assay with compatible phenotype is meaningful and should prompt targeted surveillance and early, aggressive therapy when muscle, bulbar or lung involvement is significant.
- Interpret myositis panel results in the clinical context; weak isolated bands on line blots need confirmatory steps.
- In severe bulbar or respiratory involvement prioritize airway protection and rapid immunomodulation (steroids ± IVIG ± other agents).

Key take‑home messages
- Consider dermatomyositis in unexplained neck/face swelling, especially if there are any skin changes, nailfold abnormalities, muscle weakness or dysphagia — check a CK early.
- Anti‑SAE1 dermatomyositis often has prominent skin disease, frequent dysphagia and meaningful lung involvement; malignancy risk is real and requires structured screening.
- Line‑blot/Euroimmun and other platforms vary — correlate serology with phenotype and confirm unexpected results when important management decisions depend on them.
- Severe bulbar or respiratory involvement requires aggressive immunotherapy (steroids, IVIG, steroid‑sparing agents); select agents with lung safety in mind.
- Use a pragmatic surveillance plan: baseline CT/HRCT + PFTs, PET or focused imaging for malignancy, age‑appropriate cancer screening and repeat assessments guided by symptoms and risk.

Final comment
This case is a neat reminder that autoimmune disease can present to the ED as what looks like ENT disease or angioedema. A quick bedside CK and careful skin/nail exam can change the diagnostic trajectory — and in anti‑SAE1 disease, that change has real consequences for cancer surveillance, airway safety, and choice of immunotherapy.



# Swallow First, Ask Questions Later: Airway and Dysphagia Management in Inflammatory Myopathy

What makes this case interesting
A previously well man in his 40s presented with 3 weeks of progressive neck swelling that looked like angioedema or deep neck infection — but the swelling was actually inflammatory soft‑tissue oedema from dermatomyositis. Crucially, he had severe pharyngeal dysphagia with aspiration risk and prominent muscle involvement (CK >16,000 U/L) requiring aggressive immunosuppression (pulse steroids, IVIG, mycophenolate, rituximab). This vignette illustrates two dangerous traps: (1) misattributing neck swelling to ENT problems and missing an inflammatory myopathy; and (2) under-recognising dysphagia as the organ threat that determines immediate airway, nutritional and immunotherapy decisions.

This post focuses on practical front‑line management: recognising the physiology (PFTs, videofluoroscopy), preventing aspiration pneumonitis, coordinating ENT/speech pathology/immunology, and pragmatic decisions about escalation (pulse steroids/IVIG), nutrition (NG vs PEG), secretion control and timing therapies while protecting the airway.

## Don’t anchor on “angioedema” — look for myopathy clues
In the case above the swelling was non‑tender, progressive, and accompanied by subtle skin/nail changes, proximal weakness and markedly elevated CK. Key hints that should trigger consideration of inflammatory myopathy:
- Neck/facial “pseudo‑angioedema” that is not itching, urticarial or responding to antihistamines/epinephrine.
- Associated bulbar signs (cough with secretions, dysphagia), proximal weakness, skin findings (periungual changes, Holster sign, heliotrope/Gottron’s when present).
- Markedly elevated muscle enzymes—CK checked early can be diagnostic and pivots evaluation/treatment.

If the presentation is atypical for histaminergic/bradykinin angioedema (no ACEi, no family history, normal complements), broaden the differential immediately.

## PFTs — what does an extrathoracic restrictive pattern tell you?
Pulmonary function tests in inflammatory myopathy may show a restrictive pattern due to respiratory muscle weakness (especially diaphragmatic and accessory muscles) or parenchymal ILD. An “extrathoracic restrictive pattern” as described in this case suggests impaired upper airway or bulbar involvement rather than lower airway obstruction.

Practical points:
- Look for reduced forced vital capacity (FVC) and normal/near‑normal spirometry flow pattern but low maximal inspiratory/expiratory pressures (MIP/MEP) if measured — these point to muscle weakness.
- Rapid decline in FVC or low supine FVC (>10–20% fall compared with upright) suggests diaphragmatic weakness and high risk for ventilatory failure.
- Use PFTs as part of baseline assessment and for monitoring response to therapy, but don’t delay treatment while waiting for tests if clinical risk is high.

## Videofluoroscopy (barium swallow) — what to look for and act on
The VFSS in this patient showed severe pharyngeal dysphagia with pyriform fossae residue and post‑swallow penetration to the vocal cords — classic for pharyngeal phase dysfunction from inflammatory myopathy.

How to interpret and act:
- Penetration/aspiration on thin liquids = high aspiration risk. Overt aspiration or silent aspiration require immediate protective measures.
- Significant pharyngeal residue suggests poor bolus clearance and continued risk between swallows — consider frequent suctioning and nil‑by‑mouth.
- Findings that trigger urgent action: aspiration with cough suppression/silent aspiration, recurring aspiration despite compensatory strategies, inability to handle secretions.

Use VFSS not just to grade impairment but to guide swallowing recommendations (NPO vs modified diets, need for enteral access, therapy targets).

## Preventing aspiration pneumonitis — immediate practical measures
In the case, CT chest showed peribronchial consolidation likely from aspiration. Early prevention is essential.

Initial steps in the ED/inpatient:
- Stop oral intake if significant dysphagia/aspiration risk — err on the side of caution.
- Call speech pathology for bedside evaluation and arrange VFSS urgently.
- Provide airway suctioning and aggressive secretion management (see below).
- Consider early ENT involvement if there is concern about upper airway compromise or pooling that might need intervention.
- Antibiotics are indicated when aspiration pneumonitis progresses to infection; avoid reflexive prolonged antibiotics without infectious evidence.

Remember: prevention > treatment of aspiration pneumonitis.

## Secretion control — an underrated airway protection measure
Patients with bulbar dysfunction often aspirate secretions. Measures to reduce secretions and their consequences include:
- Anticholinergic agents (glycopyrrolate preferred when systemic antimuscarinic effects are a concern) to reduce drooling and pooling. Dose and side effects must be considered.
- Botulinum toxin to salivary glands is an option in refractory drooling but not first line in an acute inpatient with evolving myositis.
- Frequent oral suctioning and chest physiotherapy.
- Consider nebulised saline to mobilise secretions; avoid agents that increase secretions.
Balance secretion control against risk of thickened secretions and impaired cough; coordinate with speech pathology.

## Nutrition: NG tube vs PEG — timing and practicalities
This patient required an NG tube for 2 months and then improved enough for oral feeding. Decision points:
- NG tube is the default short‑term solution when dysphagia is expected to improve with immunotherapy (weeks–months). It’s quick, reversible, and allows timely medication administration (as in this case).
- Consider PEG when: prolonged dependence (>4–6 weeks anticipated), recurrent tube dislodgement, high aspiration risk despite optimised care, or when long‑term enteral access simplifies quality of life and rehabilitation.
- Early multi‑disciplinary discussion (speech pathology, nutrition, gastroenterology/ENT, patient preferences) is essential. In inflammatory myopathy, many patients improve with immunosuppression — this favors a temporary NG strategy unless clear reasons for earlier PEG exist.
- Ensure appropriate prophylaxis (skin care, tube position checks) and plan for swallow reassessment.

## When to escalate immunotherapy: pulse steroids, IVIG and beyond
Airway and swallowing involvement mandate rapid escalation of immunosuppression. Pragmatic approach used in this case:
- Start high‑dose glucocorticoids early. In bulbar/airway compromise, pulse methylprednisolone (eg, 500–1000 mg IV daily × 3 days) is reasonable to rapidly blunt inflammation.
- Combine with steroid‑sparing agents early if severe disease or high steroid burden anticipated — mycophenolate, methotrexate (avoid if ILD concern), azathioprine are options tailored to comorbidities.
- Consider early IVIG when bulbar muscles are severely affected, aspiration is ongoing, or there is life‑threatening muscle involvement. IVIG has relatively rapid effects and was used successfully in this case (2 g/kg loading + monthly cycles).
- Rituximab is a reasonable addition for refractory or severe cases and was used here; it takes longer to act but provides disease control in many patients.
- Coordinate with immunology/rheumatology early — do not delay immunotherapy while awaiting antibody confirmation if clinical picture is strongly suggestive.

Escalation triggers (practical):
- Rapidly worsening dysphagia or inability to clear secretions despite supportive measures.
- Respiratory muscle weakness with declining FVC or need for ventilatory support.
- Markedly elevated CK with systemic signs and bulbar involvement.

## Coordinating ENT, Speech Pathology and Immunology
Key coordination principles:
- ENT: assess for obstructive lesions, perform flexible nasendoscopy to evaluate pooling, vocal cord function and potential need for airway procedures. In this case, ENT found increased secretions and cough — important safety data.
- Speech pathology: bedside assessment, VFSS, tailored compensatory strategies, secretion management plans and rehab goals. They guide NPO vs diet modification and timing for trials.
- Immunology/Rheumatology: lead immunosuppressive plan, coordinate infection screening and prophylaxis (eg PCP), and follow antibody/malignancy workup.
Multidisciplinary rounds with a clear airway plan and feeding plan improve safety and expedite recovery.

## Timing therapy while protecting the airway — a pragmatic timeline
From this case and practical experience:
- Day 0–1: recognise dysphagia/airway risk, NPO, bedside speech assessment, ENT review, check CK, start high‑dose steroids if suspicion high.
- Day 1–3: obtain VFSS; if aspiration confirmed or secretions uncontrolled, proceed with NG feeding, initiate IVIG if severe bulbar involvement, and consider pulse steroids if not already given.
- Day 3–14: monitor CK, strengths, PFTs; continue IVIG cycles or steroid‑sparing agent initiation (eg mycophenolate). Reassess swallow weekly with speech pathology.
- 2–6 weeks: expect initial improvement in strength and swallowing with aggressive therapy in many patients; wean steroids accordingly. If no improvement or deterioration, escalate (rituximab, change steroid‑sparing agent or consider ICU support/airway intervention).
Individualise timing; the guiding principle is: protect the airway and nutrition first, then intensify disease‑directed therapies in parallel.

## Clinical pearls
- Always check CK early when neck/facial swelling is atypical for angioedema — it changed the pathway in this case.
- VFSS is the single most useful test to grade pharyngeal dysfunction and guide immediate management: aspiration on thin liquids → high risk.
- NG tube is often the right short‑term choice in inflammatory myopathy because many patients recover with immunotherapy; delay PEG unless long dependence is likely.
- Pulse steroids + IVIG are reasonable early for severe bulbar disease; start them promptly when airway is threatened.
- Control secretions proactively (glycopyrrolate, suctioning) — pooled secretions are the most common immediate mechanism of aspiration.
- Coordinate ENT, speech pathology and immunology early — each contributes essential, non‑redundant information.

## Take‑home messages
- Consider inflammatory myopathy when neck/facial swelling is unexplained and accompanied by weakness, skin changes or high CK.
- Treat dysphagia and secretion control as airway emergencies — NPO, arrange VFSS, and institute NG feeding early when indicated.
- Escalate immunotherapy rapidly for bulbar/airway involvement: pulse steroids and IVIG are reasonable front‑line options; add steroid‑sparing agents and biologics as needed.
- Use multidisciplinary care (ENT + speech + immunology) to time interventions (NG vs PEG, airway procedures) and to monitor recovery.

This case reminds us: in inflammatory myopathy, the throat can be the most dangerous organ. Protect the airway and feed the patient while you treat the disease.



# Lighting Up the Muscles: How MRI and FDG-PET Guide Myositis Diagnosis (and Exclude Mimics)

What makes this case cool and clinically useful is deceptively simple: a patient with progressive neck “angioedema” who didn’t look or behave like classic histaminergic/bradykinin angioedema, and whose diagnosis was clinched and mapped by imaging. MRI localized active myositis in the pelvic/hip muscles; FDG-PET lit up diffuse muscle inflammation — including head/neck muscles explaining the cervical swelling and dysphagia — and importantly found no occult malignancy to explain paraneoplastic myositis. Muscle biopsy then sealed the diagnosis with the classic dermatomyositis findings: perifascicular necrosis and capillary C5b-9 deposition.

Why this matters: dermatologic clues were subtle (periungual changes, Holster sign), CK was massively elevated, and the neck swelling was actually inflammatory muscle- and soft-tissue edema from myositis rather than primary infectious, neoplastic, or classic angioedema. Imaging prevented unnecessary procedures, guided urgency of immunosuppression, and helped target malignancy screening.

## How MRI and FDG-PET complement each other in myositis

MRI and FDG-PET are both valuable in inflammatory myopathies, but they answer slightly different questions. In this patient they were synergistic.

MRI (STIR/T2, sometimes with gadolinium)
- Strengths: excellent anatomical resolution and muscle-specific detail. MRI localized active myositis to the bilateral obturator internus/externus, adductors, gracilis and distal iliopsoas — information that is directly useful for targeted biopsy and for correlating focal weakness (hip flexors 4/5). MRI shows edema, fatty replacement, and chronic changes; it can detect compartmental involvement and guide site selection for biopsy.
- Limitations: MRI is muscle-focused and may miss low-grade diffuse inflammation; it’s less helpful for whole-body assessment unless whole-body sequences are used. MRI cannot reliably exclude occult malignancy outside the imaged field.

FDG-PET (whole-body)
- Strengths: whole-body overview of metabolic activity. In this case PET demonstrated diffuse and abnormal FDG accumulation across muscle groups (head/neck, deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdomen, pelvis) confirming widespread inflammatory activity that explained the neck swelling and dysphagia. Crucially, PET did not reveal a metabolically active neoplasm to suggest paraneoplastic disease, which helped prioritize the diagnostic and surveillance strategy.
- Limitations: FDG uptake is nonspecific — infection, inflammation and malignancy all light up. PET has lower spatial resolution for small muscle groups compared with MRI, and physiologic muscle uptake (recent exercise, shivering) can confound interpretation.

Practical synthesis from the case:
- Use MRI to map focal disease and to choose a biopsy site with active inflammation. The MRI here showed targetable hip/obturator involvement.
- Use FDG-PET when you need whole-body assessment: to confirm diffuse involvement, detect occult malignancy or systemic inflammatory foci, and to survey for aspiration/infective processes (PET in this case showed diffuse muscle activity and no metabolically active malignancy).
- When both are available, use them together: MRI for anatomy and biopsy planning, PET for extent and cancer surveillance.

## Interpreting the neck swelling: myositis-driven subcutaneous edema, not angioedema

This patient presented to ENT with progressive neck swelling and CT neck showing diffuse oedema involving strap muscles and retropharyngeal space. Key clinical and investigational pointers that this was not histaminergic/bradykinin angioedema:
- Atypical clinical context: subacute 3-week progression, no urticaria, no pruritus, no medication trigger (ACEi, DPP-4 inhibitors), no family history.
- Localizing findings: dysphagia, cough with secretions on nasendoscopy, and focal muscle weakness (hip flexors) — arguing for neuromuscular involvement.
- Nailfold capillaroscopy with microhemorrhages and periungual changes hinted at connective tissue disease.
- Dramatic CK elevation (16,184 U/L) and transaminitis pointing to muscle injury rather than isolated vascular permeability from angioedema.
- Imaging concordance: MRI and PET demonstrating active myositis in neck/head muscles explaining soft tissue edema and aspiration risk.

Bottom line: subcutaneous/soft-tissue edema in dermatomyositis tends to be inflammatory (myositis-related), not mediated by histamine/bradykinin; think “pseudoangioedema” when other systemic signs of myositis are present.

## Muscle biopsy: the histopathologic clincher

The biopsy in this case showed:
- Perifascicular necrosis, vacuolated/degenerate myofibers and myophagocytosis — morphology classically seen in dermatomyositis.
- Perimysial and perifascicular inflammation with dispersed histiocytes.
- Complement C5b-9 (membrane attack complex) deposition in perifascicular endomysial capillaries — a hallmark vascular/complement-mediated injury pattern of dermatomyositis.

Interpretation points:
- Perifascicular atrophy/necrosis + capillary C5b-9 deposition = strong support for dermatomyositis (vs immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy or inclusion body myositis).
- Biopsy targeted to an MRI-active site increases diagnostic yield and reduces sampling error.
- Combined with the serology (high-titre ANA, strongly positive anti-SAE1), biopsy completes clinico-pathologic correlation.

## The lungs: nodules and peribronchial consolidation interpreted as aspiration sequelae, not primary ILD or cancer

This patient’s chest CT showed multiple non-cavitating pulmonary nodules (mid–lower zones) and peribronchial consolidation, and PFTs had an extrathoracic restrictive pattern. Important reasoning from the case:
- The presence of severe oropharyngeal dysphagia with video swallow showing aspiration explains peribronchial consolidation consistent with aspiration pneumonitis (and the PET was concordant).
- No radiologic evidence of classic interstitial lung disease (fibrosis) and PET showed no hypermetabolic lesion to suggest pulmonary malignancy. Tumor markers were unremarkable and PET/CT had no other hotspots.
- Nonetheless, patients with dermatomyositis — especially certain myositis autoantibodies — have increased risk of ILD and malignancy. This patient had intermediate/high risk features and appropriate malignancy surveillance was undertaken (PET negative, serial imaging, and planned endoscopy/colonoscopy).

Clinical nuance: pulmonary nodules in myositis patients require context — correlate with aspiration risk, PET activity, clinical course and repeat imaging before labeling as neoplastic.

## Putting it together: how this changed management

- Imaging helped move the team away from unnecessary surgical drainage or prolonged antibiotics for presumed Ludwig’s angina.
- MRI-directed biopsy confirmed dermatomyositis and justified aggressive immunosuppression (pulse steroids, IVIG, mycophenolate and rituximab) because of airway/extrinsic muscle involvement and severe CK elevation.
- FDG-PET provided reassurance by not showing occult malignancy and confirmed widespread inflammatory distribution including the head/neck muscles — explaining the presentation and guiding prognosis and surveillance plans.

## Clinical pearls and take-home points
- Consider inflammatory myopathy (including dermatomyositis) in subacute neck or facial "angioedema" that lacks typical allergic/bradykinin features, especially if there are subtle cutaneous clues, dysphagia, or proximal weakness.
- Use MRI to localize active muscle inflammation and to select the optimal biopsy site; MRI excels at anatomical mapping.
- Use FDG-PET for whole-body assessment of disease extent and for malignancy surveillance — PET can also corroborate that neck/face swelling is due to inflammatory myositis rather than neoplasm.
- Muscle biopsy showing perifascicular necrosis with C5b-9 deposition is highly supportive of dermatomyositis; combine pathology with imaging and serology (eg, anti-SAE1 here) for a robust diagnosis.
- Pulmonary findings in myositis patients need careful interpretation: aspiration pneumonitis may mimic consolidation, and nodules require PET correlation and clinical context before presuming malignancy.
- Early integration of imaging, serology and targeted biopsy can avoid unnecessary procedures and speed initiation of appropriate immunosuppression in severe cases.

This case is an excellent reminder: when the neck “doesn’t look like” angioedema, think muscles. MRI + FDG-PET = anatomy + extent; biopsy = diagnosis. Use them together to convert diagnostic uncertainty into a clear, evidence-based treatment plan.



# Designing the Immunosuppression Playbook: Steroids, IVIG, Mycophenolate, Rituximab—and PJP Prophylaxis

What makes this case worth saving to your mental bookshelf? A previously well man in his 40s presents with progressive neck swelling that looks exactly like angioedema or a deep neck infection — but it’s actually dermatomyositis with severe myositis of the neck and pharyngeal muscles threatening the airway and causing dysphagia. The team avoided an airway catastrophe by thinking beyond histamine/bradykinin angioedema, checking a CK early, recognising subtle cutaneous findings, and launching aggressive immunosuppression: pulse methylprednisolone, early IVIG, and a steroid‑sparing strategy (mycophenolate + rituximab). When TMP‑SMX for PJP prophylaxis caused haemolysis, inhaled pentamidine was used successfully.

This post pulls practical, bedside‑oriented lessons from that case for managing myositis when muscle inflammation threatens the airway — stepwise immunosuppression, how to pick a steroid‑sparing agent when lungs are involved, using CK/strength to guide tapering, and pragmatic PJP prophylaxis options when TMP‑SMX isn’t tolerated.

## Step 1 — Rapid recognition and the early CK check
- If neck/facial swelling is unexplained by infection or classic angioedema triggers, examine carefully for dermatomyositis stigmata (periungual changes, heliotrope/face erythema, Holster sign, subtle rash) and check a CK. In this case CK was 16,184 U/L — diagnostic clue and baseline to follow.
- Early recognition changes everything: myositis involving bulbar/neck musculature can progress rapidly to aspiration and airway compromise; immune suppression should not be delayed.

## Step 2 — Immediate immunosuppression when airway/airway musculature involved
What they did (and why it makes sense)
- Pulse IV methylprednisolone 1 g IV daily × 3 days. Rationale: rapid control of fulminant muscle inflammation and potential to reverse oedema of inflamed muscle that threatens airway.
- Transition to high‑dose oral prednisone (eg, 60 mg/day) with a deliberate taper. The team tapered by 5 mg every 2–3 weeks — reasonable once strength and CK are improving.
- Early IVIG: loading 2 g/kg total (often split over 2–5 days depending on tolerance), then monthly maintenance cycles in selected severe cases. IVIG has a quicker immune‑modulating effect and is particularly useful for severe bulbar disease, dysphagia, and steroid‑refractory myositis.
- Start a steroid‑sparing agent early (see below) — both to permit steroid taper and to sustain control.

Clinical pearl: When bulbar and neck muscles are affected, combine high‑dose steroids + early IVIG rather than waiting for weeks for an oral steroid or steroid‑sparing agent to take effect.

## Choosing the steroid‑sparing agent: practical framework (mycophenolate / azathioprine / tacrolimus / methotrexate / rituximab)
Evidence is limited and practice heterogeneous — choose based on organ involvement, comorbidities, drug side‑effect profile, and speed of expected effect.

Mycophenolate mofetil (MMF)
- Pros: Well tolerated, oral, commonly used in dermatomyositis, good data for ILD-associated myositis and steroid‑sparing effect. Reasonable choice when avoiding methotrexate.
- Monitoring: CBC (for cytopenias), LFTs, GI tolerance. Typical dose: 1 g PO BID (as in this case).
- Good pick when patient prefers oral daily therapy and when methotrexate is contraindicated.

Azathioprine
- Pros: Oral, long experience in myositis.
- Cons: Slow onset (months), needs TPMT or NUDT15 testing before initiation, risk of cytopenias and hepatotoxicity.
- Monitoring: CBC, LFTs; check TPMT/NUDT15 first.
- Consider if MMF not tolerated or cost/access issues.

Tacrolimus
- Pros: Evidence for use in refractory cases and myositis with ILD; can be used in combination with other agents.
- Cons: Nephrotoxicity, drug interactions, requires trough monitoring.
- Use when there is refractory disease or ILD biology that may respond.

Methotrexate
- Pros: Common steroid‑sparing agent in myositis.
- Contraindication/avoidance: Pre‑existing ILD or abnormal pulmonary function is a relative contraindication — increased risk for pulmonary toxicity and potential to worsen fibrosis. In this case, abnormal lung testing and aspiration risk led to avoiding methotrexate.

Rituximab
- Pros: Useful for refractory disease and certain autoantibody‑positive myositides; sometimes used early in severe disease or when rapid B‑cell targeting is desirable.
- Cons: Infection risk, reactivation of hepatitis B is a concern — screen before dosing. May be used in combination with other agents (eg, MMF). Onset is not immediate but useful for sustained control.
- In this case, two doses of rituximab 1,000 mg two weeks apart were given in addition to MMF and IVIG.

Putting it together (practical decision tree)
- Severe bulbar/airway involvement: pulse steroids + early IVIG. Start an oral steroid‑sparing agent (MMF common). Add rituximab if severe, refractory, or specific antibody profile suggests benefit.
- Significant ILD or abnormal pulmonary function: favour MMF or tacrolimus over methotrexate.
- Refractory disease despite steroids + conventional steroid‑sparing: consider rituximab (and continuing IVIG if helpful).

Clinical pearl: There is no single “best” agent — balance speed, organ involvement (esp. lung), side‑effect profile, monitoring logistics, and patient preference. Combining modalities (steroids + IVIG + MMF +/- rituximab) is common in severe cases.

## Using CK and strength to guide tapering and therapy adjustments
- Baseline CK is essential. Use CK trend and serial strength exams (proximal power, swallow function) rather than CK alone to judge response.
- Typical pattern:
  - Rapid clinical improvement (strength, dysphagia) often precedes CK normalisation.
  - Once CK normalises and clinical strength is stable, begin gradual steroid taper (eg, 5 mg reductions every 2–3 weeks as tolerated).
- Frequency of monitoring: CK and strength weekly to every 2 weeks early, then monthly once stable.
- Don’t rush steroid withdrawal if dysphagia or bulbar weakness persists — airway risk is real.

Clinical pearl: Improvement in bulbar function and ability to protect the airway is the critical clinical endpoint; CK is supportive but monitor both.

## PJP prophylaxis — TMP‑SMX first line, but have a plan B
TMP‑SMX (co‑trimoxazole) is the preferred prophylaxis in patients receiving high‑dose steroids and other significant immunosuppression. Typical dosing options:
- One double‑strength tablet (800/160 mg) three times weekly OR
- One single‑strength tablet (400/80 mg) once daily.
Monitor for cytopenias and drug reactions.

What if TMP‑SMX causes problems?
- In this case TMP‑SMX was started but stopped after haemolytic anaemia developed. Alternatives:
  - Dapsone 100 mg PO daily (requires G6PD testing before starting; risks include haemolysis and methemoglobinemia).
  - Atovaquone 1,500 mg PO once daily with food (well tolerated, useful in TMP‑SMX intolerance; more expensive).
  - Inhaled pentamidine 300 mg once monthly via nebuliser — the team used this successfully. It provides good pulmonary prophylaxis but does not protect extrapulmonary sites and has logistical considerations (need for specialized delivery, less systemic protection).
- Choice depends on G6PD status, tolerance, drug availability, and whether systemic (including extrapulmonary) protection is desired.

Monitoring when switching prophylaxis:
- For dapsone: baseline and periodic hemoglobin, methemoglobin symptoms, and G6PD.
- For atovaquone: clinical tolerance and adherence (GI side effects); no routine labs required but monitor for treatment failures.
- For inhaled pentamidine: ensure proper nebuliser delivery; counsel about local bronchospasm risk and need for respiratory monitoring.

Clinical pearl: If TMP‑SMX intolerance occurs, inhaled pentamidine is a practical real‑world alternative that many centres use (300 mg once monthly), though it offers less systemic protection than oral agents.

## Infection screening and safety checks before rituximab and intensive immunosuppression
- Hepatitis B surface antigen and core antibody — treat/reactivate risk if positive; use prophylaxis as indicated.
- HIV, hepatitis C, VZV serologies as applicable.
- Baseline immunoglobulins and consider vaccinating before B‑cell depletion (e.g., pneumococcus, influenza) when feasible.
- Consider TB screening; if QuantiFERON indeterminate, investigate further (sputum AFB as done in the case).
- Baseline CBC, LFTs, renal function.

## Practical inpatient management checklist when myositis threatens the airway
- Airway assessment and ENT review; low threshold for ICU monitoring.
- Baseline CK, AST/ALT, LDH, renal function, myoglobin/urine output monitoring to prevent/treat rhabdomyolysis.
- Pulse methylprednisolone 1 g IV daily × 3 days for severe disease.
- Early IVIG (2 g/kg total loading), particularly for bulbar involvement.
- Start steroid‑sparing agent (MMF 1 g BID is reasonable) — don’t wait weeks to start.
- Consider rituximab in severe or refractory cases; screen for HBV prior.
- Begin PJP prophylaxis with TMP‑SMX unless contraindicated; have alternatives (dapsone with G6PD check, atovaquone, inhaled pentamidine 300 mg monthly) ready.
- Serial strength exams, swallow assessments (VFSS), and CK to guide tapering and discharge planning.
- Nutrition plan (NG feed if dysphagia) and aspiration precautions; refer to speech/swallow therapy early.

## Key takeaways
- Dermatomyositis can masquerade as angioedema or deep neck infection when neck/bulbar muscles are inflamed — check a CK early if the picture is atypical.
- When muscle inflammation threatens the airway: pulse steroids + early IVIG + early initiation of a steroid‑sparing agent is a pragmatic, effective strategy.
- Avoid methotrexate when pulmonary function is abnormal or ILD is suspected; prefer MMF or tacrolimus in that setting. Rituximab is a reasonable addition for severe or refractory disease.
- Use CK trends and serial strength/swallow exams — not CK alone — to guide steroid tapering.
- TMP‑SMX is first‑line PJP prophylaxis; if intolerance occurs, options include dapsone (G6PD‑dependent), atovaquone, or inhaled pentamidine (300 mg monthly) — each has pros/cons and monitoring requirements.
- Pre‑treatment infection screening (HBV, HIV, TB), baseline immunoglobulins, and vaccination planning are essential before B‑cell depleting therapy.

This case is a neat reminder: thinking beyond the obvious, doing a few targeted bedside checks (CK, nailfold exam), and moving quickly to the right immunosuppression cocktail can avert airway compromise and get patients back to function.



# Dermatomyositis and Cancer: Applying IMACS Risk-Stratified Screening at the Bedside

What makes this worth your attention? Because dermatomyositis isn’t just a rash-plus-weakness disease — it’s a paraneoplastic red flag in a measurable way. The case that prompted this post was a previously well man in his 40s who presented with neck “angioedema” and dysphagia and was ultimately diagnosed with SAE1 antibody–positive dermatomyositis with significant myositis. Using IMACS risk factors, he carried multiple features that placed him in a high-risk group for an occult malignancy, and an urgent, pragmatic cancer screen (CT/PET, targeted endoscopy, tumour markers and age-appropriate tests) was performed. This is the sort of clinical situation where a clear, reproducible screening bundle is useful for ED/ENT teams, hospitalists, and outpatient rheumatology/immunology clinics.

Below I translate the IMACS risk-stratified approach into a bedside-friendly pathway you can drop into discharge summaries and clinic letters. I keep it practical: what to order now, what to defer, how to follow, and when to re-image.

## Quick primer: which dermatomyositis features raise cancer risk?
IMACS and cohort data identify several predictors of higher malignancy risk in patients with idiopathic inflammatory myopathy. Think in terms of strong (high) and intermediate risk features:

- High-risk clinical features
  - Dermatomyositis phenotype (vs polymyositis)
  - Dysphagia (predicts occult cancer in multiple series)
- Intermediate-risk features
  - Certain autoantibodies (TIF1-γ, SAE1 among others)
  - Male sex
  - Older age (risk rises with age — particularly >45–50)

The case: dermatomyositis + dysphagia (two high-risk features) + SAE1 antibody positive + male sex (two intermediate risk features) = high-priority cancer screen. That’s exactly why this patient underwent CT/PET and targeted GI evaluation.

## Practical baseline screening bundle (what to order at diagnosis)
Choose one of two baseline imaging strategies — both are defensible; prefer PET-CT when available and local protocol supports it, otherwise do contrast CT chest/abdomen/pelvis.

1) Imaging (pick one)
   - PET-CT whole body (FDG-PET) — single-test, high yield for metabolically active malignancy and useful if you also suspect occult cancer in muscles/soft tissues.
   - OR CT chest/abdomen/pelvis with IV contrast — conventional and widely available; acceptable alternative if PET not available or contraindicated.

2) Targeted endoscopy
   - Upper GI endoscopy (esophagogastroduodenoscopy) if there are symptoms (dysphagia, odynophagia), or if IMACS risk profile is high (eg dermatomyositis + dysphagia).
   - Colonoscopy per age-appropriate screening or earlier if GI symptoms or risk factors present.
   - Rationale from the case: dysphagia and SAE1 positivity prompted plans for gastroscopy and colonoscopy.

3) Tumor markers — know their limits
   - Consider CEA, CA-125, CA19-9, PSA (men), but understand these are neither sensitive nor specific for screening — they are adjuncts, not replacements for imaging/endoscopy.
   - Use tumour markers to help work up suspicious findings, not as a negative screen to reassure.

4) Age-appropriate cancer screening
   - Ensure the patient is up-to-date with breast, cervical, colorectal, prostate screening as locally recommended; expedite if the IMACS score is high.
   - For patients below the typical screening age but with high-risk features (eg SAE1 positivity, dysphagia), consider early targeted endoscopic evaluation.

5) Baseline labs
   - CBC, LFTs, renal function, CK (already indicated), plus any site-specific markers if clinically indicated.

6) Document and counsel
   - Record the risk discussion and the chosen screening bundle in discharge paperwork and clinic letters. Explain limitations (a normal PET/CT or CT does not exclude microscopic or early cancers).

## Follow-up imaging intervals and triggers
There is no perfect evidence-based timetable; use risk stratification and clinical judgement. A pragmatic schedule used by many groups:

- High-risk patients (eg dermatomyositis + dysphagia, positive TIF1-γ/SAE1, age >45):
  - Baseline PET-CT or CT CAP at diagnosis.
  - Repeat imaging at 6–12 months if initial screen negative.
  - If still negative, consider annual clinical review with low threshold to re-image through year 3; many centres perform focused re-imaging up to 3 years.
  - Re-image sooner with any new systemic symptoms: weight loss, night sweats, new focal pain, haemoptysis, persistent fevers, unexplained anaemia, new masses.

- Intermediate/low-risk patients:
  - Baseline screen at diagnosis (CT CAP or PET based on availability).
  - If negative and no additional risk features, consider repeat imaging at 12 months and then symptom-driven thereafter.

Specific triggers to re-image immediately regardless of schedule:
- New or worsening dysphagia, odynophagia, or weight loss
- New focal pain or palpable mass
- New pulmonary symptoms or progressive breathlessness
- New abnormal routine bloods suggesting malignancy (anaemia, rising alkaline phosphatase, abnormal CA-125/CEA where previously normal and unexplained)

## How to integrate into a discharge/clinic template (copy–paste-able)

- Baseline imaging: PET-CT (or CT chest/abdomen/pelvis) performed on [date] — [result summary].
- Tumour markers sent: CEA, CA19-9, CA-125, PSA (if applicable) — [results].
- Planned targeted endoscopy: Gastroscopy and colonoscopy arranged/considered due to dysphagia and high-risk profile — [date/referral].
Outpatient plan:
- Rheumatology/Immunology clinic review at 2–4 weeks post-discharge.
- Repeat clinical review and CK at 4–6 weeks.
- Repeat imaging (PET-CT or CT CAP) planned at 6–12 months if baseline negative, earlier if new symptoms.
- Patient counselled to report A, B, C, D (A = new/unexplained weight loss; B = new or progressive dysphagia/odynophagia; C = new focal pain or palpable mass; D = new respiratory symptoms).

## Clinical pearls from the index case
- Dermatomyositis can rarely present with soft tissue facial/neck oedema that masquerades as angioedema or infectious neck processes — look for subtle cutaneous signs (periungual changes, photosensitive rash, Holster sign) and check a CK when neck swelling is unexplained.
- Dysphagia in dermatomyositis is not only a complication needing swallowing evaluation — it’s also a marker of increased malignancy risk and should prompt earlier and more aggressive cancer screening.
- SAE1 antibody positivity (and other myositis-specific antibodies like TIF1-γ, MDA-5) modifies malignancy risk — factor antibody status into your screening threshold.
- Tumour markers are adjuncts only; negative markers do not rule out cancer. PET-CT or CT CAP and targeted endoscopy remain the backbone of screening.
- If initial workup is negative, maintain structured surveillance for at least 2–3 years with a low threshold to re-image for new symptoms.

## Take-home messages
- Dermatomyositis + dysphagia = high-risk for occult malignancy — act promptly.
- Baseline whole-body PET-CT (preferred) or CT chest/abdomen/pelvis + targeted endoscopy and age-appropriate screening is a practical initial bundle.
- SAE1 (and other myositis antibodies) and male sex increase risk and should lower your threshold for aggressive screening.
- Normal tumor markers do not exclude malignancy; use imaging and endoscopy as primary tools.
- Document the screening plan in discharge and clinic notes, schedule interval review (6–12 months imaging for high-risk), and re-image immediately if concerning new symptoms develop.
