Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

Chapter 2: ## Pathobiology of Dermatomyositis and the SAE1 Phenotype

Dermatomyositis (DM) is an autoimmune inflammatory myopathy whose hallmark lesion is a complement-mediated microangiopathy affecting skin and muscle. The earliest and most characteristic histopathologic change is injury to the endomysial and perimysial capillary bed. Deposition of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9) on capillaries triggers endothelial cell injury, loss of capillary density and local ischemia. In skeletal muscle this process produces perifascicular atrophy and fibre degeneration concentrated at the edges of fascicles, a pattern seen on muscle biopsy in this case and reflected by the reported C5b-9 staining. Loss of perfusion and secondary hypoxia are central to muscle fibre necrosis, phagocytosis and regeneration that account for the markedly elevated serum muscle enzymes (eg, CK, AST, ALT, LDH) seen in active DM.

How this microangiopathy produces the patient’s clinical features helps explain why dermatomyositis can mimic other disorders. The inflammatory injury extends beyond muscle fibres to involve perimysial connective tissue and adjacent subcutaneous tissue. Active myositis with associated myofasciitis increases vascular permeability and generates cytokine-driven interstitial fluid accumulation within and around affected muscle groups. This mechanism differs from classical angioedema: it is not mediated by mast-cell histamine release nor by bradykinin excess, and therefore antihistamines, epinephrine or C1-esterase–targeted treatments are not effective. Instead, the swelling is driven by muscle-centered inflammation and edema — sometimes termed pseudoangioedema — which, when prominent in the neck and face, can present to ENT or emergency teams as presumed infectious or allergic neck swelling, as occurred in this patient.

Oropharyngeal and swallowing dysfunction in DM reflects involvement of the striated muscles of the pharynx and upper esophageal sphincter (including cricopharyngeus). Inflammation, necrosis and perifascicular atrophy of these muscles cause weakness, incoordination and impaired bolus propulsion. Clinically this manifests as dysphagia, aspiration risk and pooling in the valleculae and pyriform fossae as seen on the patient’s video fluoroscopic swallow. Severe pharyngeal muscle involvement can produce increased secretions and cough during nasendoscopy without anatomic obstruction. Respiratory testing may show an extrathoracic restrictive pattern when upper airway or bulbar muscles are weak; more generalized restrictive physiology can reflect diaphragmatic or intercostal muscle involvement or coexisting interstitial lung disease (ILD).

The SAE1 antibody defines a distinct serologic subgroup within DM. SAE1 is a subunit of the SUMO (small ubiquitin-like modifier) activating enzyme complex that initiates the SUMOylation pathway — a post-translational modification that regulates nuclear transport, transcription, DNA repair and stress responses. Autoantibodies directed at SAE1 are thought to identify an immune phenotype rather than directly demonstrating pathogenicity via blockade of SUMOylation; however, their presence correlates with a characteristic clinical pattern. Patients with anti-SAE1 positivity more often have prominent cutaneous features (eg, diffuse facial erythema, shawl or V-neck signs, heliotrope changes), a relatively high frequency of dysphagia, and a substantial risk of ILD. In the case reported, the combination of prominent facial/neck cutaneous erythema, periungual changes, severe dysphagia and diffuse muscle inflammation on PET/MRI aligns with the SAE1 phenotype. The literature also suggests an intermediate association with malignancy (reported in some series around the range observed here), so patients with anti-SAE1 antibodies are generally managed with appropriate age- and risk-based cancer screening.

Comparing SAE1 to other myositis-specific autoantibodies helps understand expected organ involvement and guides evaluation. Anti-Mi-2 antibodies classically associate with prominent cutaneous DM features and a generally favorable prognosis with good steroid responsiveness. Anti–TIF1-γ antibodies are strongly linked to cancer-associated DM and conspicuous skin disease but also portend higher malignancy risk. Anti-MDA5 (melanoma differentiation–associated gene 5) is associated with clinically amyopathic DM, rapidly progressive ILD and ulcerative skin lesions. Antisynthetase antibodies (eg, anti-Jo-1) characterize the antisynthetase syndrome with mechanic’s hands, myositis, ILD and arthritis. SAE1 seropositivity overlaps with several features of these groups (notably prominent skin disease and ILD tendency), but differs by its particular constellation — frequent dysphagia and cutaneous prominence without the same strength of association with rapidly progressive ILD seen with MDA5 or the very high malignancy risk seen with TIF1-γ in some cohorts. These serologic distinctions are probabilistic rather than absolute, so clinical assessment and targeted investigations remain essential.

Epidemiologically, DM is uncommon (incidence roughly 10 per million) and has a bimodal age distribution with an adult peak in the mid-adult decades — consistent with this patient in his 40s. Generalized subcutaneous edema or pseudoangioedema is rare in DM cohorts, reported in only a small fraction of patients. Because neck and facial swelling are more commonly investigated as infectious deep-neck processes or histaminergic/bradykinin-mediated angioedema, the DM diagnosis may be delayed unless careful skin and neuromuscular examination suggests myositis and testing (eg, serum CK, myositis antibody panel, imaging, biopsy) is pursued. Recognizing that subcutaneous edema can result from muscle inflammation rather than primary vascular leak syndromes prevents misdirected therapies and prompts immunosuppressive treatment to address the underlying microangiopathy and myositis, as illustrated by the favorable response in this case.
