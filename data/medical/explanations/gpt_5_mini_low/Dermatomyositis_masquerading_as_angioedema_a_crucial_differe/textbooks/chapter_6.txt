Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

Chapter 6: ## Key Clinical Lessons and Decision Algorithms

This case highlights several practical lessons and a stepwise approach to avoid misdiagnosis when patients present with facial or cervical swelling that appears to be angioedema. The central teaching point is to maintain a broad differential when the presentation is atypical for histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema and to rapidly consider inflammatory myopathies such as dermatomyositis when subtle cutaneous or muscular features are present.

When “angioedema” isn’t angioedema
Consider alternatives to angioedema when swelling is persistent (days to weeks), non-urticarial, non-pruritic, non-tender or when there is poor response to antihistamines/epinephrine. Normal complement levels (C3, C4) and normal C1 esterase inhibitor quantification/function make hereditary or complement-mediated angioedema unlikely and should prompt reconsideration of diagnosis. In that setting, look for cutaneous or neuromuscular clues of dermatomyositis. A bedside creatine kinase (CK) is a simple, rapid test that can point toward myositis when markedly elevated, as occurred in this case, and can expedite appropriate specialist input and imaging.

Focused examination checklist
A short structured exam improves detection of subtle dermatomyositis features that may be missed in an ENT or emergency assessment focused on airway and infection. Key elements to examine and document:
- Nailfolds: periungual erythema, capillary dilatation or microhemorrhages on capillaroscopy.
- Face and photosensitive areas: violaceous or dusky erythema over the eyelids, nasolabial folds, cheeks, and shawl/V signs across shoulders/chest or Holster sign on lateral thighs.
- Hands: Gottron’s papules over extensor surfaces (may be absent).
- Muscles: proximal power (hip flexors, shoulder abduction) — compare bilaterally.
- Bulbar/airway function: assess for dysphagia by asking about choking, nasal regurgitation, voice changes; look for wet or gurgly voice, coughing on swallow, pooling of secretions.
These focused signs in a patient with unexplained neck swelling should raise suspicion for dermatomyositis-associated inflammatory edema rather than primary angioedema.

Rapid workup algorithm for suspected myositis presenting with neck/face edema
When clinical suspicion for myositis exists, pursue a targeted, expedited workup to define severity, complications and need for urgent therapy:
1. Immediate bedside tests: serum CK, AST/ALT, LDH, CRP/white cell count, basic metabolic panel and urine myoglobin (if rhabdomyolysis suspected). Markedly elevated CK, as in this case, is a red flag.
2. Myositis autoantibody panel (including MSA and MAA such as SAE1): helps subclassify disease and inform associated risks (eg, dysphagia, ILD, malignancy) though treatment should not be delayed awaiting results.
3. Imaging to localize inflammation and assess complications:
   - MRI of involved muscle groups (eg, neck/thighs) for active myositis distribution.
   - FDG-PET can show multifocal muscle activity and exclude occult malignancy when clinically indicated.
4. Functional assessments:
   - Video fluoroscopic swallow study if dysphagia or signs of aspiration/wet voice are present.
   - Pulmonary function tests and chest CT to evaluate for aspiration changes and interstitial lung disease.
5. Tissue diagnosis: muscle biopsy if the diagnosis is uncertain or pathology will change management; biopsy features in dermatomyositis (eg, perifascicular atrophy, capillary complement deposition) can be diagnostic.
This sequence balances rapid identification (CK, antibodies) with imaging and functional studies to gauge severity and guide therapy.

Risk stratification and cancer screening
Dermatomyositis carries an increased risk of occult malignancy. Use established risk frameworks (eg, IMACS guidance) to stratify patients early: features such as dermatomyositis phenotype, dysphagia, certain myositis antibodies and older age or male sex increase malignancy risk. In patients meeting high-risk criteria, initiate baseline malignancy screening promptly (history/exam, age-appropriate imaging such as CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, PET if available, tumour markers and targeted endoscopies as clinically indicated). Continue interval surveillance guided by symptoms and risk factors. In this case the presence of dermatomyositis with dysphagia and SAE1 positivity appropriately triggered an early, broad malignancy screen.

Therapeutic priorities — align treatment with lung status and dysphagia severity
Treatment choices must balance severity of muscle and bulbar involvement with pulmonary status:
- Severe pharyngeal/airway involvement or rapidly progressive weakness: initiate high-dose intravenous glucocorticoids (eg, methylprednisolone pulses) urgently to reduce inflammation and preserve airway and swallowing function. This case required 3 days of IV methylprednisolone.
- For severe dysphagia or when rapid steroid-sparing effect is desired, add IVIG early; IVIG can produce relatively rapid improvement in bulbar muscle strength and reduce aspiration risk.
- Choose steroid-sparing agents based on comorbidities: mycophenolate mofetil, azathioprine, methotrexate, tacrolimus and rituximab are options. Consider lung disease when choosing agents (avoid or use caution with methotrexate if ILD is present). Early addition of a maintenance agent is advisable to reduce cumulative steroid exposure.
- In refractory or severe disease, biologicals such as rituximab can be used adjunctively.
- Provide appropriate prophylaxis for opportunistic infections when using combination immunosuppression.
The goal is to rapidly suppress immune-mediated muscle inflammation to prevent irreversible necrosis and to control edema that threatens airway or swallowing.

Early immunosuppression preserves function
Delay in recognizing inflammatory causes of neck swelling risks ongoing muscle injury, aspiration, prolonged dysphagia and long hospital stays. Early, aggressive immunosuppression (high-dose steroids plus IVIG in severe bulbar disease) was associated in this case with normalization of CK, recovery of proximal strength and eventual resolution of cervical edema and dysphagia. Prompt escalation to steroid-sparing agents prevents relapse and facilitates steroid tapering.

Interdisciplinary coordination
Optimal care requires a coordinated multidisciplinary team to address airway safety, rehabilitation and complications:
- ENT for airway assessment and to exclude structural causes or need for airway intervention.
- Rheumatology/immunology or neurology for diagnosis, immunotherapy decisions and longitudinal management.
- Speech–language pathology for swallowing assessment and therapy; early involvement reduces aspiration risk and guides nutrition.
- Nutrition support (eg, nasogastric feeding) when dysphagia precludes safe oral intake.
- Pulmonology for evaluation and follow-up of aspiration pneumonitis or ILD, including PFTs and imaging.
- Pathology and radiology for biopsy interpretation and MRI/PET reporting.
Clear communication and joint decision-making expedite appropriate immunosuppression while safeguarding airway and pulmonary function.

Practical decision checklist to avoid missed dermatomyositis in neck swelling
- If neck/face swelling is unexplained by infection or trauma and is non-pruritic/non-urticarial: check CK immediately.
- Perform a focused skin and neuromuscular exam (nailfolds, heliotrope/shawl/Holster signs, proximal strength, signs of dysphagia).
- Order myositis antibodies and baseline infection screen before immunosuppression.
- If CK is markedly elevated or bulbar/respiratory signs are present: obtain MRI of muscles and a swallow study, start high-dose steroids and involve multidisciplinary team; add IVIG early for severe dysphagia.
- Initiate malignancy screening per risk stratification and tailor long-term immunosuppression to pulmonary status and tolerability.
Following this structured approach reduces diagnostic delay, averts inappropriate treatments for presumed angioedema, and improves functional outcomes in patients whose dermatomyositis presents atypically with facial or neck oedema.
