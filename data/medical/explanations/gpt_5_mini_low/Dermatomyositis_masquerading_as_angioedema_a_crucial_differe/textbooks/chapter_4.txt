Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

Chapter 4: ## Acute and Longitudinal Management: Airway-Safe Immunosuppression and Supportive Care

This patient’s presentation required simultaneous attention to airway safety, prompt control of systemic inflammation, prevention of complications of immunosuppression, and supportive measures to address dysphagia and muscle weakness. Management priorities were therefore (1) protect the airway and reduce aspiration risk, (2) rapidly suppress the immune-mediated myositis responsible for neck swelling and pharyngeal weakness, (3) introduce steroid-sparing agents to limit long‑term steroid toxicity, and (4) provide prophylaxis and rehabilitation while monitoring response and safety. The measures taken in this case illustrate these principles.

Airway and aspiration management
Neck soft‑tissue swelling in dermatomyositis can reflect underlying muscle inflammation and pharyngeal neuromuscular weakness rather than histaminergic angioedema. Early bedside assessment by ENT, including flexible nasendoscopy where available, is useful to exclude obstructive lesions and to identify pooling of secretions or impaired protective reflexes. In this patient, increased secretions and coughing on nasendoscopy and instrumental swallow testing demonstrating severe pharyngeal dysphagia mandated active airway and aspiration precautions. A nasogastric tube was placed to secure nutrition and avoid oral intake until swallowing improved; this is standard when aspiration risk is high and allows delivery of oral immunosuppressive medications if necessary. Continuous monitoring for progressive airway compromise is essential; escalation to intubation or surgical airway should be considered if there is evidence of rapidly progressive upper airway obstruction or loss of protective airway reflexes.

Induction therapy and steroid strategy
Severe muscle involvement with potential airway compromise warrants rapid high‑dose corticosteroid therapy. The treating team administered intravenous methylprednisolone 1 g daily for 3 days—an induction strategy commonly used for severe or life‑threatening manifestations of idiopathic inflammatory myopathy—to achieve quick anti‑inflammatory effect. After pulse therapy, the patient was converted to an oral prednisone taper beginning at 60 mg daily with gradual dose reduction. This approach balances the need for prompt disease control with subsequent tapering to minimize prolonged high‑dose steroid exposure. Monitoring during this phase includes daily clinical assessment of strength, neck swelling and swallowing, serial CK and transaminases to gauge biochemical response, and vigilance for steroid complications (hyperglycaemia, hypertension, neuropsychiatric effects, and infection).

Steroid‑sparing and adjunctive immunomodulation
Given the severity of disease and the need to limit steroid burden, early addition of steroid‑sparing agents was appropriate. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was given as a loading regimen of 2 g/kg over 5 days followed by monthly cycles; IVIG can produce relatively rapid clinical improvement in dermatomyositis, is useful for severe dysphagia or bulbar involvement, and is often combined with other immunosuppressants in refractory or severe cases. Mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) at 1 g twice daily served as the primary long‑term steroid‑sparing agent; MMF has an established role in dermatomyositis for muscle and cutaneous disease and was chosen here for tolerability and patient preference for an oral regimen. Because the disease was severe and involved critical musculature, rituximab was also administered (1 g two doses, two weeks apart) as additional escalation; rituximab is often used for severe, refractory, or antibody‑mediated inflammatory myopathies and can be effective when rapid disease control or steroid sparing is required. The combined regimen—steroids, IVIG, MMF and rituximab—reflects an aggressive but rational approach for moderate to severe SAE1‑positive dermatomyositis with bulbar/airway involvement.

Rationale for agent selection and avoidance
Agent choices were guided by organ involvement and risk‑benefit considerations. Methotrexate, frequently used in dermatomyositis, was avoided because the patient had abnormal pulmonary function and radiologic findings (aspiration‑related consolidation and restrictive pattern); methotrexate has a known potential to aggravate or confound interstitial lung disease, so it is often avoided when ILD or impaired lung function is present. Azathioprine and tacrolimus remain valid alternatives if MMF is not tolerated or ineffective; in this case MMF was well tolerated so switching was unnecessary. Rituximab was reasonable given severe disease and the need for relatively rapid and durable immunomodulation. These decisions should always weigh comorbidities, infection risk, fertility considerations, and drug adverse‑effect profiles.

Infection prophylaxis and safety monitoring
High‑intensity immunosuppression requires proactive infection prophylaxis and screening. Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) prophylaxis is recommended when combining high‑dose steroids and other immunosuppressives; trimethoprim‑sulfamethoxazole (TMP‑SMX) is first‑line but was stopped in this patient due to hemolytic anemia, after which monthly inhaled pentamidine was used as an alternative. Baseline screening for chronic viral infections (HBV, HCV, HIV) and latent TB is important before rituximab/other biologics; this patient had an indeterminate QuantiFERON and negative AFB sputum; such results require clinical judgment—repeat testing, specialist infectious disease input, or empiric treatment may be considered depending on exposure history and ongoing immunosuppression. VZV seronegativity was noted; live zoster vaccination is contraindicated once high‑dose immunosuppression is initiated, so vaccination status should be reconciled pre‑treatment when feasible and VZV exposure risk mitigated. Regular laboratory monitoring—full blood count, liver function tests (ALT/AST/LDH were elevated at presentation), renal function, and CK—was used to follow disease activity and drug toxicity. For steroid safety, glucose, blood pressure, bone protection (calcium, vitamin D, and consideration of bisphosphonate in prolonged high‑dose use) and gastroprotection are appropriate.

Rehabilitation, dysphagia care and monitoring
Functional recovery requires early multidisciplinary rehabilitation. Speech and language therapy directed at swallow assessment and progressive re‑feeding plans is crucial; in this case, a video fluoroscopic swallow study guided the decision for enteral feeding and later safe advancement of diet. Ongoing swallow therapy and supervised trials facilitate recovery and removal of nasogastric feeding once safe. Physical therapy focused on proximal muscle strengthening and graded mobilization helps restore function and prevent deconditioning. Regular clinical assessment of proximal strength and activities of daily living, combined with biochemical markers (CK) and inflammatory markers, guide response assessment. Imaging or pulmonary follow‑up is indicated if ILD is suspected or if new respiratory symptoms emerge.

Escalation, tapering and long‑term strategy
Tapering steroids should be individualized based on clinical improvement, normalization of CK, and resolution of dysphagia and neck swelling. In this patient, CK normalisation and clinical recovery supported progressive prednisone tapering and eventual discontinuation at seven months. Continuation of monthly IVIG and maintenance MMF was planned with intention to de‑escalate MMF over subsequent months if remission persists. Criteria to consider tapering or stopping therapies include sustained clinical remission off high‑dose steroids, normal or stable CK and imaging, and absence of new organ involvement. Conversely, relapse, persistent or progressive ILD, refractory bulbar disease, or development of malignancy would prompt reassessment and possible escalation (renewed high‑dose steroids, alternative steroid‑sparing agents, or repeat rituximab). Ongoing malignancy surveillance is also part of long‑term care in dermatomyositis, given the associated cancer risk in adult patients.

In summary, management combined timely airway and aspiration protection with aggressive induction immunosuppression (IV methylprednisolone pulses), rapid institution of steroid‑sparing therapies (IVIG, MMF) and targeted biologic therapy (rituximab) appropriate to the severity and organ involvement. Prophylaxis against opportunistic infections, careful pulmonary considerations in agent selection, and coordinated rehabilitation and monitoring were integral to achieving clinical remission while minimizing treatment complications.
