Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Clinical Pearls] A middle-aged man presents with progressive, non-tender cervical swelling, facial erythema, subtle periungual changes, and proximal muscle weakness but has normal C1 esterase levels and no ACE-inhibitor exposure. What clinical features should prompt checking a creatine kinase level rather than treating for histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema?

Check a serum creatine kinase (CK) instead of—or in addition to—treating empirically for histaminergic or bradykinin angioedema when the cervical/ facial “angioedema” has features more consistent with inflammatory myositis than with acute allergic or complement/bradykinin‑mediated swelling. From this case, the key red flags are:

- Subacute, progressive neck swelling over weeks rather than sudden-onset episodic swelling (angioedema is usually more abrupt).
- Non‑tender, non‑pruritic, non‑pitting soft‑tissue swelling localized to the neck/face without urticaria or clear allergen exposure.
- Concurrent proximal, symmetric muscle weakness (eg, hip‑flexor weakness) or myalgias—weakness may be subtle early on but is the cardinal clue in dermatomyositis.
- Oropharyngeal signs of bulbar involvement (dysphagia, increased secretions, cough on nasendoscopy) suggesting involvement of pharyngeal/neck musculature.
- Cutaneous findings suggestive of dermatomyositis even if classic lesions are absent: facial erythema (including across nasolabial folds), periungual erythema/telangiectasias or nailfold haemorrhages, shawl/V‑neck/holster distribution erythema, or a subtle rash on the trunk/limbs.
- Absence of usual causes of angioedema: no ACE‑inhibitor/DPP‑4 inhibitor use, normal C1 esterase function/levels and normal C3/C4.
- Laboratory and imaging clues pointing to muscle injury or inflammation: markedly raised transaminases/LDH out of proportion to liver disease, unexplained inflammatory markers, CT/MRI/PET showing diffuse muscle or soft‑tissue inflammation rather than a localized infectious collection.
- Lack of response to antihistamines, epinephrine, or C1‑inhibitor therapy would also argue against histaminergic/bradykinin mechanisms.

In the reported case these features—subacute non‑tender neck swelling, facial erythema and periungual capillary changes, proximal hip‑flexor weakness, dysphagia, normal C1 esterase and no ACE‑inhibitor exposure—prompted measurement of CK and led to the correct diagnosis of SAE1‑positive dermatomyositis. A timely CK will rapidly distinguish myositis (often with marked CK elevation) from primary angioedema and allow prompt initiation of immunosuppressive therapy when indicated.

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a patient with subacute neck swelling, elevated CRP, and CT showing diffuse strap muscle edema extending to the retropharyngeal space, what are the key diagnostic possibilities beyond angioedema (infectious and non-infectious), and which targeted tests help distinguish among deep neck space infection, dermatomyositis, immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy, myasthenia gravis, and cellulitis?

Direct answer
Beyond angioedema, the key diagnostic considerations for subacute neck swelling with raised CRP and CT showing diffuse strap‑muscle oedema extending into the retropharyngeal space are: (1) deep neck‑space infection (including Ludwig’s angina or retropharyngeal abscess), (2) superficial soft‑tissue infection/cellulitis with or without abscess, (3) inflammatory myopathies (dermatomyositis, immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy [IMNM], inclusion body myositis), and (4) neuromuscular junction disorder with bulbar involvement (eg, myasthenia gravis). The clinical and investigative approach is aimed at distinguishing suppurative infection from inflammatory myopathy and neuromuscular causes — because management (urgent surgical drainage + antibiotics versus immunosuppression) is opposite.

How to differentiate them — targeted tests and the rationale
1) Deep neck‑space infection (Ludwig’s angina, retropharyngeal abscess)
- Clinical pointers: fever, toxic appearance, localized severe pain, trismus, muffled voice, rapidly progressive airway compromise, elevated WBC.
- Imaging: contrast CT neck is diagnostic for abscess — look for focal rim‑enhancing fluid collections, gas, fascial plane disruption. Ultrasound can identify superficial abscess.
- Microbiology: blood cultures; pus culture if drainage performed.
- Ancillary: flexible nasendoscopy to assess airway/pooling and oropharyngeal/retropharyngeal bulge.
- In the case: CT showed diffuse oedema without a drainable rim‑enhancing collection and patient was afebrile with only modest leukocytosis → argues against a focal deep abscess.

2) Cellulitis / erysipelas of the neck
- Clinical pointers: erythema, warmth, tenderness, often unilateral and painful; systemic toxicity if severe.
- Diagnosis: clinical; ultrasound/CT to exclude underlying collection.
- Response to empiric antibiotics supports infection.
- In the case: neck was non‑tender, swelling diffuse and non‑erythematous in pattern of classic cellulitis; lack of response to antibiotics and absence of collection makes cellulitis unlikely.

3) Dermatomyositis (inflammatory myopathy causing pseudangioedema/pseudotumor)
- Pathophysiology: muscle inflammation with capillary complement deposition → muscle edema and subcutaneous/soft‑tissue oedema; can produce prominent neck swelling and dysphagia.
- Clinical pointers: proximal muscle weakness, characteristic cutaneous findings (heliotrope, Gottron’s, shawl/V‑sign, periungual changes), dysphagia, interstitial lung disease.
- Targeted tests: serum CK (markedly elevated in active myositis), transaminases and LDH (muscle source), ANA and myositis autoantibody panel (eg, anti‑SAE1, anti‑Mi‑2, anti‑TIF1‑γ, anti‑MDA5, anti‑Jo‑1). MRI of muscle (shows muscle edema), FDG‑PET can show diffuse muscle uptake, EMG (irritative myopathy), and muscle biopsy (definitive — perifascicular atrophy, capillary C5b‑9 deposition in dermatomyositis).
- In the case: CK 16,184 U/L, ANA 1:1280, positive anti‑SAE1, MRI and PET showing diffuse muscle inflammation, and muscle biopsy with perifascicular changes and C5b‑9 deposition established dermatomyositis as the cause.

4) Immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM)
- Clinical pointers: severe proximal weakness, very high CK; may lack prominent rash. Frequently associated with anti‑HMGCR or anti‑SRP antibodies.
- Targeted tests: CK (very high), myositis antibody panel including anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP; MRI of muscle; muscle biopsy shows prominent necrosis with minimal inflammation (contrast with dermatomyositis which shows perifascicular atrophy and capillary complement deposition).
- Distinguishing features: presence of specific antibodies (anti‑HMGCR/anti‑SRP) and biopsy pattern (necrosis > inflammation) point to IMNM rather than dermatomyositis. In this case IMNM was less likely because of classic dermatomyositis biopsy pattern and positive SAE1.

5) Myasthenia gravis (MG) with bulbar/neck muscle involvement
- Clinical pointers: fatigable weakness, diurnal fluctuation, ocular signs (ptosis, diplopia), prominent bulbar symptoms with swallowing/voice change; physical exam typically shows fatigability rather than muscle tenderness or CK elevation.
- Targeted tests: AChR and MuSK antibodies (serologic), edrophonium or ice test ( bedside ), repetitive nerve stimulation, single‑fiber EMG (most sensitive), pulmonary function tests for respiratory compromise.
- Distinguishing features: CK usually normal (muscle membrane not primarily inflamed), neurophysiology shows neuromuscular junction transmission defect. In the case: markedly elevated CK and inflammatory markers and biopsy excluded MG.

Practical diagnostic approach to this clinical scenario
- Initial urgent steps: airway assessment (flexible nasendoscopy, monitor for obstruction), contrast CT neck to look for collections (already done here), blood cultures and empiric antibiotics if any suspicion of bacterial infection while diagnostic evaluation proceeds.
- Concurrently obtain: CBC/CRP, CK, transaminases, LDH, creatinine, urinalysis for myoglobin.
- If CK is markedly elevated or there are dermatologic clues or proximal weakness: request ANA and a myositis antibody panel, muscle MRI and consider early muscle biopsy. Nailfold capillaroscopy and skin exam for dermatomyositis signs are high‑yield.
- If neuromuscular junction disorder suspected: AChR/MuSK antibodies and electrodiagnostics.
- If infection still in differential: repeat imaging or targeted ultrasound to look for developing abscess; ENT consultation and surgical drainage if collection found.

Takeaway tied to the case
This patient’s presentation was atypical for angioedema or focal infection: neck swelling was non‑tender, CT showed diffuse strap‑muscle edema without drainable abscess, he had subtle cutaneous/nailfold findings and proximal weakness, and CK was profoundly elevated. Those findings — plus positive anti‑SAE1, muscle MRI/PET inflammation and dermatomyositis‑typical muscle biopsy with C5b‑9 capillary deposition — confirm inflammatory myositis (dermatomyositis) as the cause of his “pseudoangioedema.” The key lesson: in unexplained neck swelling with muscle edema on imaging, check CK and myositis antibodies early to distinguish inflammatory myopathy from infection, because management (aggressive immunosuppression vs surgical drainage/antibiotics) diverges.

### [Pathophysiology] Explain the pathophysiologic basis for prominent cervical soft tissue edema in dermatomyositis and contrast it with the mechanisms of histaminergic and bradykinin-mediated angioedema.

Direct answer
The prominent cervical “angioedema” in this patient was not true histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema but inflammatory, myositis‑related soft‑tissue edema driven by active dermatomyositis. Pathophysiologically it reflects microvascular injury and active muscle inflammation with secondary capillary leak and interstitial fluid accumulation in the neck musculature and subcutis, rather than mast‑cell degranulation or excess bradykinin.

Why dermatomyositis produces soft‑tissue swelling — key mechanisms (linked to this case)
- Complement‑mediated microvascular injury: the muscle biopsy in this patient showed C5b‑9 (membrane attack complex) deposition in perifascicular endomysial capillaries — a canonical dermatomyositis lesion. MAC deposition injures endomysial capillaries and endothelial cells, increasing capillary permeability and permitting plasma extravasation into interstitial tissues.  
- Intense local inflammation: MRI, FDG‑PET and biopsy documented active myositis of head/neck and other muscles. Inflamed muscle releases cytokines (eg, IL‑1, IL‑6, TNFα) and nitric oxide that increase vascular permeability and recruit inflammatory cells, amplifying oedema. The case’s elevated CRP and markedly raised CK reflect systemic and local inflammatory activity.  
- Tissue compartment effects and lymphatic impairment: swollen muscles within the confined neck compartments can impair local lymphatic/dural drainage and venous return, promoting persistent interstitial fluid accumulation and a “pseudoangioedema” appearance. CT and PET in this patient showed diffuse neck muscle and soft‑tissue involvement rather than isolated superficial urticarial wheals.  
- Subacute time course and steroid responsiveness: the swelling evolved over weeks and improved after high‑dose steroids, IVIG and steroid‑sparing immunosuppression — consistent with inflammation‑driven edema rather than instantaneously reversible mast‑cell or bradykinin edema.

How this differs from histaminergic angioedema
- Mechanism: mast‑cell degranulation with histamine (± other mediators such as leukotrienes). Often IgE‑mediated or non‑IgE triggers.  
- Clinical features: rapid onset (minutes–hours), often itchy, frequently accompanied by urticaria, erythema, bronchospasm, hypotension in anaphylaxis.  
- Lab/response: there may be elevated tryptase acutely; responds to antihistamines, corticosteroids and epinephrine. In this case there was no urticaria/pruritus, time course was subacute, and the distribution/local findings (muscle FDG uptake, CK elevation, periungual changes) pointed away from mast‑cell disease.

How this differs from bradykinin‑mediated angioedema
- Mechanism: excess bradykinin increases vascular permeability via B2 receptors (caused by C1‑INH deficiency/dysfunction, ACE inhibitor use, or acquired C1‑INH inhibitors).  
- Clinical features: non‑pruritic, non‑urticarial, often recurrent, variable onset (hours), commonly involves face, tongue, larynx and GI tract; does not respond to antihistamines/steroids/epinephrine.  
- Lab/response: hereditary/acquired forms often show low C4 and low/abnormal C1‑INH function/quantity. In this patient, C3/C4 and C1‑INH function/quantification were normal and there was no ACEi exposure, making bradykinin‑mediated causes unlikely.

Putting it together for bedside reasoning
- Clues that neck swelling is myositis‑related rather than classic angioedema: subacute progressive course over weeks, accompanying proximal muscle weakness and very high CK, cutaneous signs of dermatomyositis (periungual changes, Holster sign), imaging and PET demonstrating active muscle inflammation in the neck, and muscle biopsy with C5b‑9 deposition.  
- Therapeutic implication: inflammatory oedema improves with immunosuppression (high‑dose steroids, IVIG, steroid‑sparing agents) as seen in this patient; it will not reliably respond to antihistamines or C1‑INH replacement unless a concurrent histaminergic or bradykinin mechanism is present.

Clinical pearl
When “angioedema” is atypical (subacute, associated muscle weakness, markedly elevated CK, absence of urticaria or complement abnormalities), examine for myositis signs and check CK — dermatomyositis with microvascular injury can masquerade as angioedema but requires immunosuppression rather than antihistamines or bradykinin‑directed therapy.

### [Morning Report] Which specific investigations in this case most strongly established the diagnosis of dermatomyositis (e.g., ANA pattern, myositis-specific antibodies, MRI/PET patterns, and muscle biopsy findings), and what histopathologic and immunohistochemical features are characteristic?

Answer: The diagnosis was established by a combination of (1) myositis‑specific serology, (2) muscle imaging showing active, diffuse myositis, and (3) a characteristic muscle biopsy demonstrating the pathognomonic pattern of dermatomyositis with complement-mediated microangiopathy. In this case the single most specific study was the positive anti‑SAE1 myositis antibody together with the typical biopsy features; MRI and FDG‑PET provided strong noninvasive confirmation and the very high CK quantified active muscle injury.

Why these are decisive (clinical reasoning and pathophysiology)
- Myositis‑specific antibody (anti‑SAE1): Anti‑SAE1 is a recognized dermatomyositis (DM)–associated autoantibody. A strongly positive result (signal 173, normal <35) in the appropriate clinical context (cutaneous findings, proximal weakness, dysphagia, markedly elevated CK) is highly supportive of DM and helps classify the subtype and predict extracutaneous features (eg, dysphagia, ILD, cancer risk). By contrast, the positive ANA speckled pattern (1:1280) is nonspecific but concordant with autoimmune myositis.
- Imaging (MRI and FDG‑PET): MRI of the thighs showed active inflammatory changes in multiple pelvic and thigh muscles; FDG‑PET demonstrated diffuse abnormal muscle FDG uptake including head/neck — both corroborate widespread active myositis, explain the cervical swelling (muscle inflammation/edema rather than histaminergic angioedema), and locate sites for biopsy. These modalities are sensitive for inflammatory myopathy and useful when physical signs are subtle or when sampling guidance is needed.
- Muscle biopsy (definitive tissue confirmation): Histopathology provided the diagnostic pattern characteristic of dermatomyositis — perifascicular myofibre necrosis/degeneration and regeneration, myophagocytosis, perimysial inflammation with histiocytes — together with immunohistochemical evidence of complement activation (C5b‑9) deposited in perifascicular endomysial capillaries. This pattern reflects DM’s primary microvascular injury (complement‑mediated capillary destruction) producing ischemic perifascicular atrophy/necrosis, which distinguishes DM from other inflammatory myopathies (eg, polymyositis, immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy, inclusion‑body myositis).

Key histopathologic and immunohistochemical features to recognize
- Perifascicular pattern: preferential involvement of fibers at the periphery of muscle fascicles — perifascicular necrosis/atrophy and regeneration is classic for DM.
- Myophagocytosis and perimysial/perivascular inflammation: macrophage‑rich inflammation in perimysial and perivascular regions (often with sparse endomysial CD8 T cells, unlike polymyositis).
- Complement deposition: membrane attack complex (C5b‑9) deposition on endomysial/perifascicular capillaries — a hallmark of dermatomyositis implicating complement‑mediated microangiopathy.
- Vascular and capillary loss with resultant ischemic changes in perifascicular fibers (explains the perifascicular distribution).
- Relative paucity of major histocompatibility complex I–restricted CD8+ endomysial cytotoxic T‑cell attack (helps distinguish from polymyositis).

Practical diagnostic hierarchy illustrated by the case
1. Clinical suspicion from cutaneous signs, dysphagia and proximal weakness → measured CK (markedly elevated to 16,184 U/L) signaled myonecrosis.
2. Serology: highly positive anti‑SAE1 (myositis‑specific) established disease subtype and increased specificity beyond ANA.
3. Imaging (MRI, FDG‑PET) documented active, anatomically widespread myositis and guided biopsy.
4. Muscle biopsy with C5b‑9 deposition provided histopathologic confirmation of dermatomyositis and excluded alternative myopathies.

Teaching pearl: In suspected dermatomyositis use a combined approach — clinical exam + CK, obtain myositis‑specific antibodies (which can be disease‑defining), use MRI/PET to localize active disease, and obtain muscle biopsy when the diagnosis is uncertain or to document subtype; the biopsy finding of perifascicular necrosis/atrophy with capillary C5b‑9 deposition is the pathological hallmark distinguishing dermatomyositis from other inflammatory myopathies.

### [Clinical Pearls] What clinical phenotype is associated with anti-SAE1 antibody–positive dermatomyositis, including typical cutaneous findings and the reported frequencies of dysphagia, interstitial lung disease, and malignancy—and how does this inform cancer screening using IMACS risk stratification?

Take‑home: Anti‑SAE1 (anti‑SAE) dermatomyositis is a predominantly cutaneous dermatomyositis phenotype with frequent severe bulbar involvement and a measurable risk of interstitial lung disease and cancer — findings that mandate early, systematic malignancy screening per IMACS risk stratification.

Key phenotype and clinical features
- Cutaneous: classically prominent skin findings — heliotrope eruption, Gottron’s papules, shawl sign and V‑neck sign — often with periungual changes (telangiectasias, microhemorrhages) and variable cutaneous erythema. The case illustrates periungual erythema, violaceous facial erythema (Holster/V‑neck distribution) without classic Gottron’s/heliotrope early on, emphasising that skin signs may be subtle.
- Muscle and bulbar: SAE1 disease commonly shows marked myositis with prominent dysphagia/ bulbar dysfunction. In a large review (n≈215) dysphagia was reported in ~46% of SAE1‑positive patients — consistent with this patient’s severe pharyngeal dysphagia and aspiration.
- Pulmonary: interstitial lung disease (ILD) is frequent (reported ≈43% in that series). In this case there was no frank fibrotic ILD but abnormal pulmonary function and aspiration‑related consolidation; SAE1 patients can have varied lung involvement.
- Edema/angioedema: subcutaneous facial/neck swelling is uncommon overall but can occur; in dermatomyositis cohorts only a small fraction (≈6–7%) had angioedema‑like swelling. This case demonstrates myositis‑related neck soft‑tissue oedema masquerading as angioedema.
- Malignancy risk: reported malignancy frequency in SAE1 cohorts ≈18% (range across studies), i.e., higher than background population but lower than some other autoantibody subsets (eg, anti‑TIF1‑γ). The patient in the report had no detectable cancer on comprehensive workup to date.

How this informs cancer screening — applying IMACS risk stratification
- IMACS endorses stratifying patients by risk factors (examples): dermatomyositis phenotype, dysphagia, male sex, myositis‑associated autoantibodies (certain antibodies confer intermediate risk). In the case, the patient had:
  - Two high‑risk features: a diagnosis of dermatomyositis and dysphagia.
  - Two intermediate‑risk features: SAE1 antibody positivity and male sex.
  - IMACS interpretation therefore classifies him as high‑risk for an associated malignancy, prompting comprehensive baseline and interval surveillance.
- Practical screening approach (as illustrated by the case and IMACS guidance):
  - Baseline comprehensive evaluation: history and focused exam, age‑ and sex‑appropriate cancer screening, cross‑sectional imaging (CT chest/abdomen/pelvis), consideration of FDG‑PET (useful when CT equivocal), tumour markers as adjuncts, and organ‑specific endoscopy when indicated (eg, upper + lower GI endoscopy if clinically justified).
  - Early and intensified surveillance in the first 2–3 years (highest risk period after myositis onset) with repeat targeted imaging/clinical reassessment guided by symptoms or new abnormalities. The report shows this strategy: CT, PET, tumour markers, and planned gastroscopy/colonoscopy, with ongoing interval clinical and imaging follow‑up.
  - Tailor additional testing to age, sex and symptoms (mammography/Pelvic exam/HPV screening, PSA/testicular exam, pelvic ultrasound, bronchoscopy if pulmonary nodules suspicious, etc.).
- Rationale: the highest yield for malignancy detection is early after dermatomyositis onset; combining imaging (CT or PET), directed endoscopy and careful clinical surveillance balances detection with resource use.

Clinical pearl from the case
- Don’t dismiss unexplained neck/facial swelling as routine angioedema when accompanied by subtle cutaneous changes, muscle weakness or elevated CK. Prompt recognition of dermatomyositis (and identification of SAE1 antibodies) changes management: urgent immunosuppression for active myositis and structured, IMACS‑guided cancer screening because of the nontrivial (~18%) malignancy risk and the high incidence of dysphagia/ILD in this antibody subset.

### [Management] In a patient with dermatomyositis presenting with severe dysphagia and suspected aspiration, what immediate supportive measures and diagnostic evaluations should be implemented to protect the airway, assess swallowing safety, and mitigate aspiration risk?

Bottom line: treat the airway and swallowing as potentially unsafe immediately — protect the airway, make the patient nil per os (NPO), establish alternative nutrition, and urgently define swallowing physiology with objective studies (FEES or video fluoroscopic swallow study). At the same time treat the underlying myositis promptly because dysphagia in dermatomyositis is due to oropharyngeal and upper‑esophageal striated muscle weakness and can improve with rapid immunotherapy.

Practical immediate steps (prioritized)

1. Airway assessment and monitoring
- Continuous pulse oximetry, frequent respiratory rate and work‑of‑breathing checks, and low threshold for escalation to ICU-level observation if secretions, cough with poor clearance, stridor, progressive neck swelling, or rising oxygen requirement.  
- Urgent ENT or anesthesia review for flexible nasendoscopy if any concern about supraglottic/ glottic compromise. (In this case ENT flexible nasendoscopy already showed increased secretions and cough — a red flag for aspiration risk.)

2. Protect the airway and stop oral intake
- Make patient NPO immediately until speech‑language pathology (SLP) assessment and objective imaging define safety.  
- If the patient cannot protect the airway (ineffective cough, pooling secretions, recurrent penetration/aspiration on bedside testing) or clinical deterioration, promptly secure the airway (endotracheal intubation) — do not delay for imaging.

3. Alternative nutrition and hydration
- Insert a nasogastric tube for short‑term enteral feeding when aspiration risk is high and airway is protected or controllable (as in this case — patient required NG feeds for 2 months).  
- Plan for gastrostomy (PEG) if prolonged dysphagia/aspiration risk is anticipated (>4–6 weeks) or if repeated aspirations occur.

4. Urgent diagnostic swallowing evaluation
- Objective test of swallowing physiology: either video fluoroscopic swallow study (VFSS / barium swallow) or fibreoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing (FEES). Both are complementary:
  - VFSS defines oral and pharyngeal timing, residue, penetration/aspiration and is useful for diet modification decisions (this case used VFSS and demonstrated severe pharyngeal dysphagia with pyriform fossa residue and post‑swallow penetration).  
  - FEES allows direct visualization of secretions, pooling and laryngeal penetration/aspiration at bedside and can be repeated to monitor progress (useful if patient unstable for transport).
- Baseline chest imaging (CXR or CT chest) and inflammatory markers to document aspiration pneumonitis/aspiration pneumonia. (In this case CT showed peribronchial consolidation consistent with micro‑aspiration.)

5. Supportive care to reduce aspiration risk
- Frequent suctioning of oral/pharyngeal secretions, aggressive oral hygiene.  
- Elevate head of bed to ≥30–45°.  
- Consider texture modification (thickened liquids, pureed diet) only after SLP guidance and objective testing; do not empirically give thin fluids if severe pharyngeal dysfunction is suspected.  
- Implement strategies to improve airway clearance (chest physiotherapy, incentive spirometry) as tolerated.

6. Treat suspected aspiration/infection and the underlying myositis
- If there is radiographic consolidation and clinical infection, treat with appropriate antibiotics guided by clinical judgment. Distinguish chemical aspiration pneumonitis (supportive care) from bacterial aspiration pneumonia (antibiotics).  
- Rapid immunotherapy for dermatomyositis to reverse bulbar/upper airway myopathy — high‑dose intravenous corticosteroids, IVIG and steroid‑sparing immunosuppression as indicated. (The case patient received 3 days methylprednisolone, then prednisone taper, IVIG and later mycophenolate and rituximab with subsequent improvement in dysphagia.)

7. Rehabilitation and follow‑up
- Early and repeated SLP‑directed swallowing rehabilitation and re‑assessment with VFSS or FEES to plan diet advancement and safe decannulation/NG removal. (The patient’s NG was removed after ~2 months when swallow improved.)  
- Consider ENT, neurology/rheumatology coordination to monitor for persistent bulbar weakness and plan for long‑term enteral access if needed.

When to escalate to intubation or tracheostomy
- Inability to protect airway (absent/ineffective cough), recurrent aspiration with respiratory compromise, progressive neck/airway swelling, or respiratory failure — intubate early. Tracheostomy may be required for prolonged ventilatory/airway support.

Putting it together with this case
- The team appropriately obtained ENT nasendoscopy (showing secretions/cough), performed VFSS (documenting severe pharyngeal dysphagia with residue and penetration), obtained chest CT showing aspiration changes, and placed an NG tube for nutrition while promptly initiating high‑dose steroids, IVIG and steroid‑sparing therapy. This sequence — urgent airway evaluation, NPO, objective swallow testing, enteral feeding via NG, aggressive immunotherapy and SLP rehabilitation — is the recommended approach to protect the airway, define swallowing safety, and reduce aspiration risk in dermatomyositis with severe dysphagia.

### [Management] Outline the rationale for using pulse methylprednisolone, a prednisone taper, intravenous immunoglobulin, mycophenolate mofetil, and rituximab in severe dermatomyositis with dysphagia. Why might methotrexate be avoided in patients with abnormal pulmonary function, and what are reasonable alternative steroid-sparing agents?

Bottom line
- Severe dermatomyositis with dysphagia and neck/airway muscle involvement is a life‑ and function‑threatening manifestation that requires rapid, layered immunomodulation: high‑dose pulse steroids for immediate control, a prednisone taper to maintain suppression while avoiding long‑term steroid toxicity, and steroid‑sparing/targeted therapies (IVIG, mycophenolate, rituximab) to treat the underlying immune process and allow steroid withdrawal. In this patient the combination produced rapid clinical improvement, resolution of CK, and eventual recovery of swallowing.

Why use each agent in this setting (clinical reasoning linked to the case)
- Pulse intravenous methylprednisolone (1 g daily × 3 days)
  - Rationale: rapid, potent suppression of fulminant immune‑mediated muscle and soft‑tissue inflammation that threatens airway, swallowing muscles and causes myonecrosis. In this case the patient had severe cervical/airway muscle involvement and dysphagia—indications for immediate high‑dose therapy to prevent respiratory failure and further aspiration.
- Prednisone taper (starting 60 mg and slow wean)
  - Rationale: transition from pulse therapy to an oral regimen to sustain disease control while planning steroid‑sparing therapy. A gradual taper reduces relapse risk and allows time for steroid‑sparing agents to take effect. The team tapered over months and successfully stopped steroids by 7 months.
- Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG; loading 2 g/kg then monthly)
  - Rationale/mechanism: immunomodulation via neutralization of pathogenic autoantibodies, Fc receptor blockade, complement inhibition (relevant given C5b‑9 capillary deposition on biopsy), and modulation of cytokines. IVIG has particular utility for dermatomyositis with severe dysphagia and for patients who need relatively rapid clinical benefit or who have contraindications to cytotoxic drugs. In this case IVIG was used early and continued monthly with clinical benefit in swallowing and muscle strength.
- Mycophenolate mofetil (MMF)
  - Rationale/mechanism: an oral antiproliferative agent that inhibits lymphocyte guanosine nucleotide synthesis (blocks B and T cell proliferation). It is widely used as a steroid‑sparing agent in dermatomyositis because of tolerability, effectiveness for muscle and skin disease, and suitability for long‑term maintenance. In this patient MMF was chosen for daily oral dosing and tolerability; it supported steroid withdrawal.
- Rituximab (two 1,000 mg doses 2 weeks apart)
  - Rationale/mechanism: B‑cell depletion reduces autoantibody production and antigen presentation. Rituximab is considered for severe, refractory, or antibody‑positive inflammatory myopathies—particularly when autoantibodies (here anti‑SAE1) are present or when rapid steroid‑sparing is needed. In this case rituximab was used early to target the antibody‑driven process and contributed to remission.

Pathophysiological justification and sequencing
- Severe dermatomyositis–related dysphagia and soft‑tissue oedema reflect active immune attack on muscle and capillaries (biopsy showed perifascicular necrosis and C5b‑9 deposition). Immediate high‑intensity immunosuppression (pulse steroids) is required to halt inflammation and protect airway/aspiration risk. IVIG provides relatively rapid adjunctive modulation—useful when dysphagia/aspiration is present. Steroid‑sparing agents (MMF, rituximab) are added early because steroids alone are inadequate long‑term and because prolonged high‑dose steroids have morbidity. The combination used mirrors guideline and expert practice for severe or life‑threatening myositis.

Why methotrexate was avoided in this patient
- Methotrexate (MTX) is an effective, commonly used steroid‑sparing agent in dermatomyositis but carries a recognized risk of pulmonary toxicity (drug‑induced pneumonitis) and has been associated with development or worsening of interstitial lung disease (ILD) and pulmonary fibrosis in some patients. This patient already had abnormal pulmonary function (extrathoracic restrictive pattern), CT chest showing pulmonary nodules and aspiration‑related consolidation, and concern for ILD in SAE1 dermatomyositis. Given baseline pulmonary abnormality and the potential for MTX to cause or exacerbate lung disease, the treating team judiciously avoided MTX and chose MMF instead.

Reasonable alternative steroid‑sparing agents (when MTX is less desirable)
- Mycophenolate mofetil (MMF): well‑tolerated; effective for muscle and skin disease; preferred when pulmonary concerns or patient preference favor an oral agent (used in this case).
- Azathioprine: traditional oral option for myositis; useful as a steroid‑sparing agent when tolerated.
- Calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus, cyclosporine): effective especially when ILD coexists or for refractory disease; tacrolimus has evidence in myositis‑related ILD.
- Rituximab: B‑cell targeted therapy for refractory or antibody‑positive disease and severe presentations.
- Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG): particularly helpful for severe dysphagia and cutaneous disease; can be used as a maintenance adjunct.
- Cyclophosphamide: considered for severe, rapidly progressive ILD or life‑threatening organ involvement (useful for severe lung disease).
- JAK inhibitors (tofacitinib, baricitinib): emerging evidence, particularly for refractory cutaneous disease or some antibody‑associated phenotypes (evidence still evolving).
- Choice considerations: comorbidities (lung disease, infection risk), urgency of response, route of administration, fertility and long‑term toxicity, patient preference.

Practical monitoring and prophylaxis points illustrated by the case
- Screen for infections and viral hepatitis before B‑cell depletion or other potent immunosuppression (the team did this).
- Provide PJP prophylaxis when using high‑dose steroids plus additional immunosuppression—this patient received TMP‑SMX initially but changed to nebulized pentamidine after hemolysis.
- Monitor pulmonary status closely if using or avoiding MTX—baseline PFTs and chest imaging informed the decision here.
- Individualize therapy based on severity, organ involvement (dysphagia, ILD), antibody profile (anti‑SAE1), and patient factors.

Takeaway for trainees
- In severe dermatomyositis with dysphagia and airway muscle involvement you must act fast: pulse steroids to arrest inflammation, early addition of IVIG for dysphagia and complement/antibody‑mediated injury, and early steroid‑sparing agents (MMF, rituximab or others) to achieve durable remission and permit steroid taper. Avoid methotrexate when baseline pulmonary disease or ILD is present; instead consider MMF, azathioprine, tacrolimus, rituximab, IVIG, or cyclophosphamide depending on the clinical context.

### [Pathophysiology] A patient with dermatomyositis has markedly elevated AST and ALT alongside a creatine kinase >16,000 U/L and normal bilirubin. How do you distinguish muscle-derived transaminase elevations from hepatic injury, and how should this influence further hepatic workup?

Bottom line
- In inflammatory myopathies transaminases often reflect muscle injury, not hepatocellular disease. The key discriminator is the context — a very high CK with disproportionate AST (often >ALT), raised LDH, and normal markers of cholestasis or synthetic dysfunction argue for muscle-derived transaminases. In that situation, an extensive hepatic workup can be deferred or limited to baseline tests needed for immunosuppression safety; pursue liver imaging/serologies only if there are independent signs of liver disease.

Why transaminases rise in muscle disease
- AST and ALT are present in both liver and skeletal muscle (AST more so). Active myocyte necrosis (as in dermatomyositis) releases CK, AST, ALT and LDH. CK is far more specific to muscle; LDH and AST rise in both liver and muscle. Bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), gamma‑glutamyl transferase (GGT) and coagulation factors are not released from injured muscle, so they remain normal in isolated muscle injury.

Applying this to the case
- This patient had CK 16,184 U/L, AST 396 U/L, ALT 143 U/L, LDH 692 U/L and normal bilirubin. That pattern — massive CK elevation, AST > ALT, raised LDH and no hyperbilirubinaemia — strongly points to muscle as the source of the transaminase elevations. The team appropriately checked viral hepatitis serologies before immunosuppression (all negative). Urine myoglobin was negative, renal function was normal, and CK normalised with treatment while AST/ALT fell — further evidence that enzymes were muscle-derived.

Practical diagnostic approach (clinical Pearls)
1. Always interpret transaminases in the clinical context. Ask: is there muscle pain/weakness, exam findings, or an elevated CK?
2. Check CK early when transaminases are elevated with any muscle symptoms or compatible exam. A very high CK (hundreds to tens of thousands) indicates muscle necrosis.
3. Look at the pattern:
   - AST >> ALT, high CK and LDH with normal bilirubin/ALP/GGT → muscle source likely.
   - Elevated GGT or ALP, hyperbilirubinaemia, or abnormal INR/albumin → investigate hepatobiliary disease.
4. Use GGT: it is not elevated in muscle injury; a normal GGT with raised transaminases supports nonhepatic origin.
5. Follow trend: if CK and transaminases fall together with treatment of myositis, that supports muscle origin.
6. Before starting immunosuppression, do baseline liver tests and viral hepatitis screen (HBsAg, anti‑HBc, HCV), because treatment decisions depend on hepatic safety — this was appropriately done in the case.

When to pursue a formal hepatic workup
- Perform a full hepatic workup (imaging, hepatology referral, broader serologies, autoimmune liver panel) if any of the following are present:
  - Abnormal cholestatic markers (elevated ALP or GGT)
  - Hyperbilirubinaemia
  - Impaired synthetic function (INR elevation, low albumin)
  - Transaminase pattern inconsistent with CK trend (eg, transaminases remain elevated or rise while CK falls)
  - Clinical features of liver disease (jaundice, hepatomegaly, stigmata)
  - Significant risk factors for liver disease or abnormal viral serology

How this should influence management in the present case
- Because the clinical picture (severe myositis with CK 16,184 U/L, AST predominance, normal bilirubin) pointed to muscle-derived transaminases, the team focused on treating dermatomyositis. They appropriately obtained baseline hepatitis screening before immunosuppression and monitored CK and LFT trends; no further hepatic imaging or invasive liver testing was indicated unless liver-specific abnormalities emerged.

### [Management] For an immunosuppressed dermatomyositis patient requiring Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia prophylaxis who develops hemolytic anemia on trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, what are appropriate alternative prophylactic options and dosing schedules, and what are key limitations of inhaled pentamidine?

Direct answer
- Reasonable alternatives to trimethoprim‑sulfamethoxazole (TMP‑SMX) for Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) prophylaxis in an immunosuppressed patient who develops hemolytic anemia on TMP‑SMX are:
  - Dapsone 100 mg orally daily (or 50 mg orally twice daily).
  - Atovaquone 1500 mg orally once daily (taken with a fatty meal to optimise absorption).
  - Aerosolized (nebulized) pentamidine 300 mg once every 4 weeks.

Key practical points, dosing and limitations
- Dapsone
  - Dose: 100 mg PO daily (50 mg BID alternative).
  - Important limits: must obtain G6PD level before starting because dapsone causes oxidative haemolysis and methemoglobinemia in G6PD deficiency. Even with normal G6PD it can cause hemolysis and methemoglobinemia — monitor hemoglobin and oxygenation/Co‑oximetry if symptoms. Also watch for sulfone hypersensitivity and peripheral neuropathy with long term use.
- Atovaquone
  - Dose: commonly 1500 mg PO once daily with food (some regimens use 750 mg twice daily).
  - Important limits: absorption is highly food‑dependent (best with fatty meal); GI side effects and cost can limit use; less robust evidence than TMP‑SMX but effective and well tolerated in many patients.
- Aerosolized (nebulized) pentamidine
  - Dose used in the case and commonly used prophylactic regimen: 300 mg by nebulizer every 4 weeks — the patient in this report tolerated nebulized pentamidine 300 mg monthly.
  - Key limitations to counsel and consider:
    - Less effective than TMP‑SMX for prevention of PJP overall and particularly poor at preventing extrapulmonary PJP (disseminated, sinus/liver/bone marrow involvement) because delivery is confined mainly to the central airways and upper lobes.
    - Variable and sometimes inadequate pulmonary deposition depending on nebulizer type and patient factors (ventilation pattern, airway disease). Use of appropriate nebulizers (eg, Respigard II historically) and experienced respiratory therapy increases reliability; not all centers have the devices or expertise.
    - Not suitable for patients with significant airway hyperreactivity (can provoke cough/bronchospasm) or severe obstructive airways disease.
    - Systemic adverse effects can occur (hypoglycaemia/hyperglycaemia, nephrotoxicity, hypotension, pancreatitis, hematologic toxicity) though less common with inhaled dosing; local side effects include cough, bronchospasm, and unpleasant taste.
    - Monthly clinic visits or supervised inhalations are commonly required — adherence/logistics may be more burdensome than a daily oral agent.
    - If the patient is at high risk for extrapulmonary disease (eg, severe cellular immune suppression), inhaled pentamidine is not preferred.

How to apply this to the presented case
- The patient in the case developed hemolytic anemia on TMP‑SMX (with normal G6PD) and was switched to nebulized pentamidine 300 mg every 4 weeks, which he tolerated. That is an acceptable option when oral agents are contraindicated or not tolerated and when the anticipated risk of extrapulmonary PJP is low and inhaled therapy is feasible.
- If logistical issues, airway reactivity, or concern for extrapulmonary disease arise (or if nebulised delivery is not available), choose an oral agent instead: dapsone (after confirming G6PD) or atovaquone (with counselling about taking with a fatty meal and cost/absorption issues).

Bottom line
- Alternatives to TMP‑SMX: dapsone (100 mg daily), atovaquone (1500 mg daily with fatty meal), or inhaled pentamidine (300 mg nebulized monthly). Inhaled pentamidine is convenient and was used successfully in this patient, but it has important limitations — lower efficacy for extrapulmonary PJP, variable lung deposition, potential for bronchospasm, and logistical constraints — which should guide agent selection for individual patients.

### [Morning Report] What is the significance of nailfold capillaroscopy findings such as periungual erythema, microhemorrhages, and avascular areas in idiopathic inflammatory myopathies, and how can these findings guide the diagnostic approach in atypical presentations like isolated neck swelling?

Clinical pearl — what those nailfold findings mean, and why they matter in an odd presentation

Periungual erythema, nailfold microhemorrhages and avascular (capillary-dropout) areas on nailfold capillaroscopy are not cosmetic findings: they are a direct window into microvascular injury that is characteristic of the idiopathic inflammatory myopathies, particularly dermatomyositis. Pathophysiologically, dermatomyositis produces complement-mediated capillary and endomysial microangiopathy (C5b-9 deposition and endothelial injury), causing dilated/looped capillaries, petechial microhemorrhages and eventual capillary loss. These changes can precede, accompany or outlast overt skin rashes and muscle weakness.

How this guides diagnosis in atypical presentations (for example isolated neck swelling)
- When a patient presents with an unusual, non‑infectious, non‑histaminergic swelling (eg, persistent “angioedema” of the neck) and the history/response to therapy is atypical, finding periungual erythema/microhemorrhages/avascular areas should raise immediate suspicion for an inflammatory myopathy, especially dermatomyositis. In the case report, subtle nailfold microhemorrhages and avascular areas prompted further investigation and ultimately led to the diagnosis of SAE1‑positive dermatomyositis.
- Practical downstream steps triggered by such capillary changes: obtain serum muscle enzymes (CK), myositis autoantibody panel, targeted imaging (muscle MRI or FDG‑PET), and consider early muscle biopsy if needed. These tests were exactly what changed the course in the reported patient (very high CK, positive SAE1 antibody, MRI/FDG‑PET and diagnostic muscle biopsy).
- The findings also help distinguish pathophysiology: capillary abnormalities point to inflammatory microangiopathy and muscle inflammation as the cause of subcutaneous/soft‑tissue oedema—rather than histaminergic or bradykinin‑mediated angioedema—which alters both immediate management (prompt immunosuppression rather than antihistamines or C1‑inhibitor therapies) and diagnostic workup (look for dysphagia, ILD, malignancy risk).

Clinical utility and limitations
- Nailfold capillaroscopy is noninvasive, bedside-accessible, and informative in patients with minimal or absent classic cutaneous stigmata (heliotrope, Gottron’s papules). In this patient, classic rashes were subtle or absent but capillary changes were present.
- The degree of capillary loss and microhemorrhages can correlate with disease activity and vasculopathy risk (and sometimes with ILD), but capillaroscopy is supportive — not diagnostic on its own. Definitive diagnosis still rests on the integration of clinical features, CK, autoantibodies, imaging and, when required, muscle biopsy (as performed here showing perifascicular necrosis and C5b‑9 deposition).
- Not all myositis autoantibodies map to the same capillaroscopic pattern, and capillaroscopy cannot reliably tell you the specific antibody; it signals that you should look for myositis.

Takeaway teaching points
- Always inspect and (when available) perform nailfold capillaroscopy in patients with unexplained soft‑tissue swelling, atypical “angioedema,” new dysphagia, or proximal weakness. Subtle periungual changes can be the clue that converts a missed ENT/infectious diagnosis into dermatomyositis.
- If capillaroscopy shows microhemorrhages, periungual erythema or avascular areas: urgently check CK, request a myositis antibody panel, and arrange muscle imaging/biopsy as indicated — and consider early immunosuppression if clinical severity warrants (airway, dysphagia, marked myositis), as was done successfully in this case.
