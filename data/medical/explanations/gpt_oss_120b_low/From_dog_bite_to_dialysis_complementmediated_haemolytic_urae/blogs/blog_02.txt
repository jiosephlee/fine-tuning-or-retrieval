Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# Capnocytophaga canimorsus: The Forgotten Zoonotic Trigger of Complement‑Mediated HUS  

*Why a seemingly “simple” dog bite can launch you into the world of complement‑mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) – and what you need to know to catch it early.*

---  

## The hook – when a bite turns into dialysis  

Four days after a **dog bite to the lip**, a previously healthy woman in her late‑40s walked into the emergency department with fever, hypoglycaemia, and mottled feet. Within a week she was in the ICU on continuous renal replacement therapy, her labs showing **DIC, pancytopenia, and AKI**. Blood cultures finally grew *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*—a notoriously slow‑growing gram‑negative rod.

What was most surprising wasn't the sepsis itself (C. canimorsus can be fulminant), but the **late emergence of isolated haemolysis, persistent thrombocytopenia, and a kidney biopsy consistent with thrombotic microangiopathy**. The work‑up landed on **complement‑mediated haemolytic uremic syndrome (CM‑HUS)**, a subtype of atypical HUS. After plasma exchange and, crucially, eculizumab, the patient regained urine output and her haematology normalized, though she remains dialysis‑dependent for a time.

The case is a vivid reminder that **C. canimorsus isn’t just a cause of sepsis—it can be the “second hit” that flips a patient into complement‑mediated HUS**.

---  

## Microbiology in a nutshell  

| Feature | Why it matters for clinicians |
|---------|------------------------------|
| **Normal flora of dogs/cats** | Any bite, scratch, or even close oral contact can inoculate the organism. |
| **Encapsulated, gram‑negative rod** | Capsule confers variable resistance to serum complement; some strains are complement‑sensitive, others not. |
| **Fastidious growth** | Requires enriched media; colonies appear after 48‑72 h (sometimes longer). |
| **Broad β‑lactam susceptibility** | Penicillins, ampicillin, ceftriaxone work, but early empiric coverage should include a β‑lactam‑β‑lactamase inhibitor (e.g., piperacillin‑tazobactam). |
| **Molecular detection** | Real‑time nanopore sequencing or 16S rRNA PCR can identify the organism within hours, bypassing culture delays. |

Because cultures can be negative for up to a week, **clinical suspicion** (dog exposure + rapid sepsis) often guides early empiric therapy.

## Clinical spectrum – from bite to blast  

*Capnocytophaga* infections are famously variable:

1. **Mild local cellulitis** – may resolve spontaneously or with oral amoxicillin.  
2. **Severe sepsis** – high fevers, hypotension, disseminated intravascular coagulation, multiorgan failure.  
3. **Neurologic involvement** – meningitis, encephalitis.  
4. **Hematologic derangements** – leukopenia, pancytopenia, DIC.  
5. **Thrombotic microangiopathy** – MAHA, thrombocytopenia, AKI, often masquerading as DIC initially.  

Risk factors for the fulminant forms include **splenectomy, alcoholism, diabetes, chronic liver disease, and immunosuppression**—but, as our case shows, *even immunocompetent adults* can succumb to severe disease.

---  

## When sepsis meets TMA – diagnostic pitfalls  

The overlap between **DIC** and **TMA** is a frequent source of confusion:

* **DIC** presents with prolonged PT/aPTT, low fibrinogen, elevated D‑dimer, and bleeding.
* **TMA (CM‑HUS/TTP)** shows *schistocytes*, *undetectable haptoglobin*, *elevated LDH*, and *relatively preserved coagulation*.

In the early septic phase, the TMA signal is often drowned out by overwhelming inflammation. In the reported patient, the “second wave” of isolated hemolysis appeared **after inflammatory markers were falling**, prompting the shift in diagnostic focus.

Key steps to untangle the two:

1. **Serial CBC and peripheral smear** – look for rising schistocytes once coagulopathy improves.  
2. **ADAMTS13 activity** – <10 % points to TTP; >10 % (as in this case) steers toward HUS/TMA.  
3. **Complement testing** – baseline C3/C4 can be normal; functional assays (AH50, alternative pathway) are more informative but may be confounded by plasma exchange or eculizumab.  
4. **Renal biopsy** – when the picture is still murky, histology can confirm TMA (fibrin thrombi, ischemic glomeruli).  

A high **PLASMIC score** should trigger early plasma exchange while awaiting ADAMTS13; a low score (as here) lets you focus on other TMAs.

## Pathophysiology – how a dog bite flips the complement switch  

1. **Capsular polysaccharide** of C. canimorsus evades early opsonophagocytosis, prompting robust complement activation.  
2. In genetically predisposed hosts, **alternative pathway regulation is already fragile** (factor H, I, B, MCP).  
3. The infection serves as a “second hit,” **overwhelming regulatory capacity** → unchecked C3 convertase activity → endothelial injury and microthrombi.  
4. In patients *without* detectable genetic variants (as in our case), the infection alone may transiently tip the balance, but the laboratory‑defined **CM‑HUS** persists until the complement cascade is halted.

## Therapeutic choreography – antibiotics, plasma exchange, and eculizumab  

| Intervention | Timing | Rationale |
|--------------|--------|-----------|
| **Broad‑spectrum β‑lactam/β‑lactamase inhibitor** (e.g., piperacillin‑tazobactam) | Immediately on suspicion of C. canimorsus | Covers gram‑negative rods, *Capnocytophaga* spp. |
| **Targeted β‑lactam (ampicillin/penicillin)** | Once cultures confirm susceptibility | Narrower spectrum, minimizes collateral damage |
| **Plasma exchange (PEX)** | Initiated when TMA suspected but before ADAMTS13 results | Removes circulating complement activation fragments; critical bridge in TTP, modest benefit in CM‑HUS |
| **Eculizumab** (C5 inhibitor) | After blood cultures cleared, after vaccination and prophylaxis | Stops terminal complement cascade, restores endothelial integrity; early initiation (<7 days from TMA onset) predicts better renal recovery |
| **Vaccination & prophylaxis** | 2 weeks before eculizumab (or as soon as feasible) | Meningococcal vaccines + penicillin prophylaxis mitigate infection risk from complement blockade |

The **key clinical dilemma** is starting eculizumab during active bacteremia. In this case, the team waited until cultures were negative, then gave meningococcal vaccines (Menveo & Bexsero) and a short course of prophylactic ampicillin before the first eculizumab dose—balancing infection control with the need for early complement blockade.

---  

## Take‑home pearls  

- **Never dismiss a dog bite** in a febrile patient; if sepsis is disproportionate to the wound, think *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*.  
- **C. canimorsus can masquerade as DIC**, but persistent hemolysis, schistocytes, and AKI after the inflammatory storm warrant a TMA work‑up.  
- **Normal C3/C4 does NOT exclude CM‑HUS**; functional complement assays and a low ADAMTS13 are more definitive.  
- **Early eculizumab = better renal outcomes**; aim to start within a week of TMA onset, after securing adequate vaccination and antimicrobial coverage.  
- **Molecular diagnostics (nanopore, 16S PCR)** can shave days off the time to pathogen identification—critical when you’re chasing a complement‑mediated cascade.  

---  

## Bottom line  

The dog‑bite‑induced sepsis we all see in textbooks can, in rare but catastrophic cases, **trigger complement‑mediated HUS**. Recognizing the *Capnocytophaga* clue early, differentiating DIC from TMA, and moving swiftly to complement inhibition can spell the difference between dialysis‑dependent renal failure and functional recovery. Keep the canine culprit on your differential—your patient’s kidneys may thank you.
