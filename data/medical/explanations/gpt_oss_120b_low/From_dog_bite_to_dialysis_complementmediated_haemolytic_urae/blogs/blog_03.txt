Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# Eculizumab in the Midst of Infection: Balancing Complement Blockade and Host Defense  

## Why This Case Stands Out  

A previously healthy 40‑something woman went from a dog bite to dialysis‑dependent kidney injury in less than two weeks. The culprit? *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteremia that triggered disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) and, puzzlingly, a later‑emerging thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).  When her hemoglobin plunged, schistocytes appeared, and ADAMTS13 was only mildly reduced, the team leapt to a diagnosis of complement‑mediated haemolytic‑uremic syndrome (CM‑HUS).  

What makes this story compelling for the bedside clinician is not just the rarity of *C. canimorsus*–induced CM‑HUS, but the therapeutic crossroads it forced: **Should we start eculizumab—a powerful complement blocker—while the patient still harbored a live Gram‑negative infection?**  

The answer is nuanced, and the case offers a roadmap for safely threading the needle between halting a lethal TMA and preserving the immune mechanisms needed to clear the bacteria.

## The Core Dilemma  

Eculizumab binds complement component C5, preventing formation of the membrane‑attack complex (MAC).  This is lifesaving in CM‑HUS, but it also eliminates a key line of defense against encapsulated organisms (Neisseria, *Haemophilus*, *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, and—relevant here—*Capnocytophaga*).  The consequences can be dramatic: meningococcemia in a previously healthy adult is a classic, feared complication.

When a patient is still bacteremic, initiating eculizumab risks:

1. **Uncontrolled bacterial proliferation** – the MAC is a primary bactericidal mechanism for capsulated pathogens.
2. **Impaired opsonophagocytosis** – complement fragments (C3b, iC3b) that tag bacteria for neutrophil uptake are reduced.
3. **Blunted immune signaling** – downstream cytokine cascades that assist bacterial clearance are altered.

On the flip side, delaying complement inhibition can permit irreversible endothelial injury, worsening renal function, and higher mortality.  The therapeutic window is narrow; early eculizumab (ideally < 48 h from TMA onset) is associated with the best renal outcomes.

## Practical Strategies Illustrated by the Case  

### 1. Confirm Microbiologic Clearance Before the First Dose  

- **Serial blood cultures**: The team waited until all blood cultures were negative (days 4‑5 of ICU stay) before administering the first eculizumab infusion.  
- **Trend of inflammatory markers**: Declining CRP, procalcitonin, and lactate served as ancillary reassurance that the infection was under control.  

*Take‑home*: Aim for at least two consecutive negative cultures drawn 12–24 h apart. If the pathogen is fastidious (as with *C. canimorsus*), consider extended incubation or molecular diagnostics (e.g., nanopore sequencing) to rule out low‑level bacteremia.

### 2. Use Plasma Exchange (PEX) as a Bridge  

PEX removes circulating complement activation products, immune complexes, and, potentially, bacterial toxins. In this case, three daily PEX sessions were carried out **before** the first eculizumab dose:

- **Benefits**: Temporary control of hemolysis and thrombocytopenia, buying time for cultures to sterilize.  
- **Limitations**: PEX does not correct the underlying complement dysregulation; relapse is common once exchange stops.  

*Take‑away*: When cultures are still positive or when the patient is critically unstable, initiate PEX while arranging for rapid culture clearance. Transition to eculizumab as soon as the bacteremia resolves.

### 3. Vaccinate Early, but Respect Timing  

Meningococcal vaccination is mandatory before eculizumab because the drug abolishes the terminal complement pathway. The patient received Menveo® and Bexsero® **after** the first eculizumab dose but before subsequent infusions, followed by a 2‑week interval for adequate antibody development.  

Practical points:

- **If possible, vaccinate *before* eculizumab**: Give conjugate and serogroup B vaccines at least 2 weeks prior to the first dose; this is the ideal scenario.  
- **If urgent therapy is needed**: Administer vaccines **immediately** before the first dose, start prophylactic antibiotics (see below), and acknowledge that protective titers may be suboptimal for the first 2 weeks.  

### 4. Prophylactic Antibiotics During the Immunization Window  

The patient received oral phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin®) as prophylaxis while awaiting the development of protective anti‑meningococcal antibodies. Penicillins are effective against most meningococci and maintain coverage against *Capnocytophaga* (which usually remains susceptible).  

Guideline‑based options:

| Situation | Preferred Agent | Duration |
|-----------|----------------|----------|
| Post‑vaccination, < 2 weeks before eculizumab effect | Oral penicillin V or amoxicillin | Until 2 weeks after the last vaccine dose |
| Penicillin allergy | Azithromycin 500 mg daily (or doxycycline) | Same duration |
| High‑risk setting (ongoing bacteremia) | Broad‑spectrum IV (e.g., ceftriaxone) until cultures clear | Tailor to organism |

### 5. Monitor for Late‑Onset Infections  

Even after the infection appears cleared, complement blockade persists for weeks. Vigilance for meningococcal, pneumococcal, and other encapsulated infections is mandatory:

- Educate patients on early signs of fever, meningismus, or sepsis.  
- Maintain a low threshold for empirical antibiotics if new fever spikes.  
- Consider periodic revaccination (e.g., booster at 5 years) if complement therapy extends beyond 1 year.

## Decision‑Making Algorithm (Simplified)

1. **TMA identified** – MAHA + thrombocytopenia + AKI.  
2. **Exclude TTP** – ADAMTS13 < 10 %? If yes → immediate PEX, plasma, and avoid eculizumab.  
3. **Exclude STEC‑HUS** – stool PCR, history of diarrheal illness.  
4. **Assess infection status**:  
   - Active bacteremia? → Continue broad‑spectrum antibiotics, do PEX, repeat cultures q12‑24 h.  
   - Cultures negative, inflammatory markers down → proceed to step 5.  
5. **Vaccinate (meningococcal A‑C‑W‑Y + B) if not already done** → give today, start prophylactic antibiotics.  
6. **Administer eculizumab** → first dose today (after cultures negative). Continue prophylaxis for at least 2 weeks.  
7. **Follow‑up**: Weekly labs (CBC, LDH, haptoglobin, creatinine), monitor for infection, and schedule eculizumab maintenance dosing.

## Take‑Home Messages  

- **Don’t let fear of infection paralyze you**: Early eculizumab dramatically improves renal recovery in CM‑HUS, but it must be paired with rigorous infection control measures.  
- **PEX is a useful bridge** when cultures are still positive or the patient is hemodynamically unstable; it buys time for definitive complement blockade.  
- **Vaccination timing matters**, but when urgency dictates, give vaccines immediately before the first eculizumab dose and cover the gap with prophylactic antibiotics.  
- **Serial cultures and inflammatory trends are the bedside biomarkers** that tell you when it’s safe to cross the line into complement inhibition.  
- **Maintain a high index of suspicion for atypical infectious triggers**—*Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is a perfect example of a slow‑growing, fastidious organism that can precipitate a complement‑mediated TMA even in immunocompetent hosts.  

In the end, the case teaches us that with a structured, stepwise approach—culture clearance, bridging PEX, timely vaccination, and prophylactic antibiotics—we can safely introduce eculizumab even in the backdrop of a recent severe infection, turning a potentially fatal TMA into a survivable, partially reversible kidney injury.
