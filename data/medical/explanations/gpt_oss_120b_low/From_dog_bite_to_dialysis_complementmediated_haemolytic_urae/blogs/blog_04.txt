Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# Beyond C3/C4: Advanced Complement Testing and Genetic Work‑up in Atypical HUS  

*Why a “normal” complement panel doesn’t rule out complement‑mediated HUS, how plasma‑exchange and eculizumab skew results, and when to call the genetics lab.*

---

## Why This Case Stands Out  

A previously healthy 48‑year‑old woman presented after a dog bite with fulminant sepsis, DIC, and dialysis‑dependent AKI. Four days later she developed an isolated hemolytic anemia, persistent thrombocytopenia, and a kidney biopsy that showed classic thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). The work‑up eventually landed on complement‑mediated hemolytic‑uremic syndrome (CM‑HUS) triggered by *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteremia—a trigger we rarely think of, and a condition that masquerades as “just” sepsis‑related DIC.

The striking teaching point? **Her routine C3/C4 levels were perfectly normal, yet she had a life‑threatening complement‑driven TMA.** This forced us to look beyond the basic complement panel, encounter the pitfalls of plasma exchange (PEX) and eculizumab on laboratory assays, and finally send an extended genetic panel that, in this case, came back negative.  

If you’ve ever felt the frustration of a “normal” C3/C4 result in a patient who looks textbook for atypical HUS, you’ll find the practical pearls below useful.

---

## The “Basic” Complement Tests: What They Tell You (and What They Don’t)  

| Test | What It Measures | Typical Interpretation in CM‑HUS |
|------|------------------|----------------------------------|
| **C3** | Total complement component 3 (classical & alternative pathways) | Often low in active disease, but can be normal (up to 40 % of cases). |
| **C4** | Complement component 4 (classical pathway) | Usually normal in alternative‑pathway driven disease. |
| **CH50** | Total hemolytic activity (classical pathway) | May be normal; not sensitive for alternative‑pathway activation. |
| **AH50** | Alternative pathway hemolytic activity (functional assay) | **Key test**: low AH50 suggests consumption of the alternative pathway, even when C3/C4 are normal. |

**Take‑home:**  
- A normal C3/C4 **does not exclude** CM‑HUS.  
- AH50 (or its newer automated equivalents) catches alternative‑pathway over‑activation that C3/C4 miss.

In our patient, the first C3/C4 were within reference ranges, leading the team to doubt a complement‑mediated process. Only after the hemolysis re‑emerged did an extended panel uncover a markedly reduced AH50 (6 % of control). That was the first biochemical clue that the alternative pathway was being “sucked dry” by uncontrolled activation.

---

## The Full Complement Panel: When to Order It  

1. **Clinical suspicion of TMA** (MAHA + thrombocytopenia + AKI) **and** ADAMTS13 >10 % (i.e., TTP unlikely).  
2. **Negative triggers for STEC‑HUS** (stool PCR, recent diarrheal illness).  
3. **Atypical presentation**: sepsis, pregnancy, drug exposure, or an unusual infectious trigger (e.g., *C. canimorsus*).  

**Order in tandem:**  
- C3, C4, CH50, AH50  
- Factors H, I, B levels (quantitative)  
- Anti‑Factor H antibodies (ELISA)  
- Soluble C5b‑9 (optional, useful for monitoring eculizumab efficacy).  

In most university labs, the AH50 and factor levels are “reflex” tests: if C3/C4 are low, they’ll automatically run the functional assays. In cases like ours, you have to **specifically request** the AH50—otherwise you’ll miss it.

---

## The Effect of Plasma Exchange (PEX) on Complement Assays  

PEX does three things:  

1. **Removes circulating complement fragments** (C3a, C5a, sC5b‑9).  
2. **Replaces deficient regulatory proteins** (e.g., Factor H) in the plasma.  
3. **Dilutes any auto‑antibodies** (anti‑Factor H).  

**Result:**  
- Immediately after a PEX session, AH50 may **spuriously normalize** because you’ve “refilled” the plasma with fresh donor complement.  
- Factor levels (H, I, B) can transiently rise, masking an underlying deficiency.  

In our patient, the first AH50 was drawn **before** the first PEX, showing the true low activity (6 %). We deliberately timed the sample to avoid the “wash‑out” effect. If you let the lab draw after a PEX, you might be reassured falsely and postpone needed eculizumab.

**Practical tip:**  
- **Draw baseline complement studies before the first PEX**.  
- If you need repeat testing, wait at least **24–48 h** after the last exchange.

---

## The Influence of Eculizumab on Laboratories  

Eculizumab binds C5, preventing formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC). The drug does **not** change C3/C4 concentrations, but it can:

- **Lower soluble C5b‑9** (great for monitoring response).  
- **Alter functional assays** that rely on downstream MAC formation (e.g., CH50).  
- **Mask “terminal” complement activity**, making some assays appear normal even when upstream activation persists.

In our case, the patient received her first eculizumab dose **after** the second PEX and after repeat AH50. The subsequent AH50 remained low, reinforcing that the abnormality lay upstream (alternative pathway) and was not corrected by a single dose of eculizumab.

**Bottom line:**  
- **Do not use a single post‑eculizumab AH50 to rule out ongoing activation.** Repeat after the drug has reached steady‑state (usually after 4–5 weekly doses) if you need to confirm adequate blockade.

---

## Genetic Work‑up: When to Send It and How to Use the Results  

### When to Order an Extended Panel  

- **All patients with confirmed or strongly suspected CM‑HUS**, regardless of C3/C4 results.  
- **Age < 60** with a first episode of TMA.  
- **Family history** of TMA, renal disease, or complement disorders.  
- **Relapsing disease** after eculizumab discontinuation.  

### What to Request  

1. **Gene panel for complement regulators**: CFH, CFI, CFB, C3, MCP (CD46), THBD, DGKE, PLG, VTN, and others.  
2. **Copy‑number analysis** (large deletions/duplications).  
3. **Screen for anti‑Factor H autoantibodies** (if not already done).  

### Interpreting the Results  

| Result | Clinical Implication |
|--------|----------------------|
| **Pathogenic variant found** (e.g., CFH loss‑of‑function) | High relapse risk; lifelong eculizumab (or ravulizumab) usually recommended. |
| **Variants of uncertain significance (VUS)** | Discuss with a genetics counselor; may influence decision for prolonged therapy, especially if functional data supports pathogenicity. |
| **No pathogenic variants** | Supports a “pure trigger” scenario (infection‑only). Shorter eculizumab courses may be considered, but decisions should be based on clinical response and renal recovery. |

In our patient, extended testing **did not reveal any pathogenic variants**, placing her in the “infection‑triggered only” group. We continued eculizumab for several months, planning a cautious taper once renal function stabilised and no further hemolysis occurred.

**Key Pearls:**  

- A **negative genetic panel** does **not** rule out future relapses; infection or pregnancy can still act as “second hits.”  
- **Genetic counseling** is essential: patients often have questions about family screening and reproductive implications.  

---

## Putting It All Together: A Practical Workflow  

1. **Suspect TMA** → Order CBC, smear, LDH, haptoglobin, creatinine, coagulation profile.  
2. **Rule out TTP** quickly (PLASMIC score; ADAMTS13). If ADAMTS13 > 10 %, move on.  
3. **Send comprehensive complement work‑up** *before* the first PEX: C3, C4, CH50, AH50, Factors H/I/B, anti‑Factor H antibodies.  
4. **Start empiric PEX** if TTP cannot be excluded (low risk of delay).  
5. **If ADAMTS13 normal and AH50 low**, think CM‑HUS → consider early eculizumab (after blood cultures clear, appropriate vaccination, and prophylactic antibiotics).  
6. **Obtain kidney biopsy** when safe (helps confirm TMA, rules out other glomerulopathies).  
7. **Send extended genetic panel** (ideally before initiating eculizumab, but not mandatory).  
8. **Monitor response**:  
   - Hemoglobin, platelets, LDH, creatinine daily.  
   - AH50 and sC5b‑9 weekly (after eculizumab steady‑state).  
9. **Plan long‑term therapy** based on genetics, renal recovery, and clinical course.  

---

## Take‑Home Messages  

- **Normal C3/C4 ≠ no complement disease.** AH50 is the functional assay that catches alternative‑pathway over‑activation.  
- **Timing matters:** Draw baseline complement studies *before* plasma exchange; beware of post‑PEX “normalization.”  
- **Eculizumab changes downstream assays** but not upstream AH50; use sC5b‑9 if you need a marker of terminal blockade.  
- **Genetics guide duration** of complement blockade: pathogenic variants → lifelong therapy; no variants → consider trial discontinuation after remission, but stay vigilant for triggers.  
- **Infection can be the sole “second hit.”** Keep a broad differential for atypical triggers (dog bites, tick bites, viral illnesses) when evaluating a TMA.  

---

## Final Thought  

The interplay between infection, complement, and the endothelium is a perfect illustration of how a seemingly straightforward sepsis case can evolve into a complex complement‑mediated TMA. By expanding our laboratory toolkit beyond C3/C4 and integrating genetics early, we can move from “watch‑and‑wait” to targeted therapy—saving kidneys, lives, and a lot of downstream paperwork.  

Stay curious, keep your labs ordered in the right sequence, and don’t let a normal C3 lull you into complacency. The next *Capnocytophaga* bite could be the one that forces you to put these pearls into practice.
