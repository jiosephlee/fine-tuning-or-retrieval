Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Differential Diagnosis] What are the key clinical and laboratory features that differentiate disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) from a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) such as complement‑mediated HUS in a patient with severe sepsis?

**Answer – How to distinguish DIC from a complement‑mediated TMA (atypical HUS) in a septic patient**

When a patient with severe sepsis develops thrombocytopenia, anaemia and an acute‑kidney injury, the first instinct is to attribute everything to disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC).  In the case of the 48‑year‑old woman with Capnocytophaga canimorsus sepsis, however, the clinical picture changed after the initial septic phase: a sudden, isolated fall in haemoglobin, persistently low platelets, undetectable haptoglobin and a marked rise in LDH signalled **microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA)** rather than simple consumptive coagulopathy.  The following clinical and laboratory clues help separate DIC from a thrombotic micro‑angiopathy (TMA) such as complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS).

| Feature | DIC (sepsis‑related) | Complement‑mediated TMA (CM‑HUS) |
|--------|----------------------|-----------------------------------|
| **Onset relative to infection** | Present **early** (often within the first 24‑48 h) and parallels the peak of systemic inflammation. | May be **delayed**; in this case haemolysis appeared on day 10 after the septic storm had begun to resolve. |
| **Platelet count** | Moderate‑to‑severe thrombocytopenia **with a rapid fall**; counts often < 50 × 10⁹/L early and then rise as DIC resolves. | Persistent thrombocytopenia despite improvement of inflammatory markers; platelets remain low (30‑40 × 10⁹/L) for days to weeks. |
| **Coagulation tests** | **Prolonged PT/INR, aPTT**, markedly **elevated D‑dimer**, **low fibrinogen** (often < 1 g/L). | PT/aPTT are usually **normal or only mildly prolonged**; fibrinogen is typically **normal** or only modestly reduced; D‑dimer may be modestly ↑ but not to the levels seen in fulminant DIC. |
| **Hemolysis markers** | Mild or absent hemolysis; LDH modestly ↑, haptoglobin usually normal; schistocytes rare (< 1 %). | **Classic MAHA**: LDH markedly ↑, haptoglobin undetectable, indirect bilirubin ↑, **schistocytes ≥ 5 %** on peripheral smear (15‑20 % in the case). |
| **Kidney involvement** | AKI can occur from hypoperfusion, but **proteinuria/hematuria** is usually modest; urine sediment bland. | **Renal TMA**: oliguria/anuria, proteinuria, hematuria, and biopsy showing fibrin thrombi in glomeruli and arterioles (as in the patient). |
| **Complement levels** | C3/C4 may be normal or mildly ↓; no specific pattern. | **C3/C4 often normal** (as in this case), so normal complement does **not** exclude CM‑HUS. Functional assays (AH50) may be low after plasma exchange or eculizumab but are not diagnostic on their own. |
| **ADAMTS13 activity** | Typically **normal** (> 40 %). | Also normal in CM‑HUS; a value < 10 % would point to TTP. The patient’s ADAMTS13 was 43.5 %, effectively ruling out TTP. |
| **Response to plasma‑exchange (PEX)** | Limited benefit; coagulopathy may improve with supportive blood products, but PEX does not correct the underlying consumption. | PEX may transiently improve counts (by removing complement fragments), but sustained remission requires **terminal complement blockade** (eculizumab). The patient’s platelets rose after PEX, yet full remission only followed eculizumab. |
| **Triggers** | Direct activation of coagulation cascade by endotoxin, tissue factor, massive cytokine release. | **Uncontrolled alternative‑pathway activation** in a genetically or acquired susceptible host; often precipitated by infection, pregnancy or drugs. Here the dog‑bite pathogen itself acted as the “second hit.” |

### Practical Step‑wise Approach (illustrated by the case)

1. **Initial assessment (day 0‑2 of sepsis).**  
   - Expect DIC: low fibrinogen, high D‑dimer, prolonged PT/aPTT, falling platelets.  
   - Treat with antibiotics, source control, and replace blood products as needed.

2. **Re‑evaluate when the clinical trajectory diverges.**  
   - In this patient, despite improving inflammatory markers and normalized PT/aPTT, the **haemoglobin dropped sharply**, platelets stayed low, haptoglobin became undetectable, and LDH surged.  
   - Prompt peripheral smear → **schistocytes 15‑20 %** → MAHA.

3. **Order targeted labs.**  
   - **Coagulation panel** (repeat PT/aPTT, fibrinogen, D‑dimer). Normalization favours TMA.  
   - **Hemolysis work‑up** (LDH, haptoglobin, bilirubin, direct antiglobulin test). Positive DAT may be a false‑positive; repeat testing helps.  
   - **ADAMTS13 activity** to exclude TTP.  
   - **Complement studies** (C3/C4, functional AH50) – remember they can be normal in CM‑HUS.

4. **Imaging/biopsy when needed.**  
   - Renal biopsy (when feasible) showing **fibrin‑rich thrombi** in glomeruli and arterioles confirms TMA, as seen in this case.

5. **Therapeutic decision.**  
   - **If ADAMTS13 < 10 % → TTP → emergent PEX + caplacizumab.**  
   - **If ADAMTS13 > 10 % and DIC parameters have normalized → consider complement‑mediated TMA.**  
   - Initiate **PLEX** as a bridge (especially while awaiting complement results) and start **eculizumab** once infection is controlled and meningococcal vaccination is given.

### Bottom line for the bedside clinician

- **DIC** is a consumption coagulopathy that tracks with the severity of sepsis: marked PT/aPTT prolongation, low fibrinogen, high D‑dimer, and only modest hemolysis.
- **CM‑HUS** (or other TMAs) surfaces **after the septic storm**, with **isolated MAHA**, persistent thrombocytopenia, **normal coagulation tests**, and often **significant renal TMA** on biopsy.
- In any septic patient whose laboratory picture evolves from classic DIC to isolated hemolysis with schistocytes, **pivot the differential toward TMA** and obtain ADAMTS13, complement studies, and consider early complement blockade.

This systematic approach allowed the team in the reported case to recognize that the patient’s deteriorating hematology was no longer driven by DIC but by a complement‑mediated TMA, leading to timely plasma exchange, eculizumab therapy, and ultimately hematologic remission with partial renal recovery.

### [Clinical Pearls] How does the PLASMIC score aid in the initial management of an adult with microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, and what would be the score for this patient?

**Clinical Pearl – Using the PLASMIC Score to Prioritise Therapy in an Adult With New‑Onset MAHA**

When an adult presents with the classic triad of **microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury**, the first diagnostic split is “TTP versus other TMAs.”  Because the definitive test—ADAMTS13 activity—is not available for several hours (or days), bedside risk stratification is essential.  

The **PLASMIC score** (Platelets, Lactate dehydrogenase, No Active Cancer, No Transplant, Mean cor**p**us creatinine, INR < 1.5, Co**m**p**o**nent of the C**l**otting cascade) is a 0‑7 point algorithm that predicts severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10 %).   

| Variable (1 point each) | What to Look For | Rationale |
|------------------------|------------------|-----------|
| **P** – Platelet count > 30 × 10⁹/L | >30 × 10⁹/L | Thrombocytopenia in TTP is usually moderate‑to‑severe; a count >30 makes severe deficiency less likely |
| **L** – LDH > upper‑limit‑normal | Markedly elevated LDH | Reflects extensive hemolysis; high values favour TTP |
| **A** – Absence of active **c**ancer | No known malignancy | Cancer‑associated TMAs rarely have ADAMTS13 < 10 % |
| **S** – Absence of solid‑organ **s**tate / transplant | No solid‑organ transplant | Similar to cancer, transplant‑related TMAs have higher ADAMTS13 |
| **M** – **M**ean creatinine ≤ 2.0 mg/dL (≈ 177 µmol/L) | Creatinine ≤2 mg/dL | Severe renal dysfunction is more typical of atypical HUS or DIC; a normal‐range creatinine leans toward TTP |
| **I** – INR < 1.5 | INR < 1.5 | Coagulopathy is prominent in DIC; a normal INR argues against DIC and thus against non‑TTP TMAs |
| **C** – **C**omplement (C3/C4) **C**omponents normal | Not required for PLASMIC (often omitted) | (included here for completeness; normal C3/C4 does not exclude CM‑HUS) |

**Scoring Interpretation**

- **0‑4 points** – Low probability of severe ADAMTS13 deficiency → TTP unlikely; pursue other TMA work‑up (e.g., complement studies, infection screen).  
- **5‑6 points** – Intermediate probability; consider early plasma exchange while awaiting ADAMTS13.  
- **7 points** – High probability; start emergent plasma exchange immediately.

| Variable | Patient’s Data | Point Earned |

**Total PLASMIC score = 4/7**

**Interpretation for this case:** A score of 4 places the patient in the **low‑risk** category for severe ADAMTS13 deficiency. Consequently, TTP was deemed unlikely, and the team correctly pursued a broader TMA work‑up (complement studies, kidney biopsy) rather than committing to prolonged plasma exchange. ADAMTS13 returned 43 % (well above the <10 % threshold), confirming the PLASMIC prediction.

---

### Key Teaching Points

1. **Rapid bedside triage:** The PLASMIC score can be calculated in minutes from routine labs and history, guiding whether emergent plasma exchange (PEX) is mandatory.
2. **Score ≠ diagnosis:** Even a low PLASMIC score does not rule out TTP completely; if clinical suspicion remains high, start PEX while awaiting ADAMTS13. In this patient, the low score aligned with the ADAMTS13 result and helped avoid unnecessary prolonged PEX.
3. **Integration with the broader work‑up:** After a low PLASMIC score, the next steps are (a) ADAMTS13 to exclude TTP, (b) stool PCR for Shiga toxin, (c) complement functional assays, and (d) renal biopsy when the diagnosis remains uncertain. This systematic approach led to the identification of **complement‑mediated HUS** and timely initiation of eculizumab.
4. **Timing matters:** Initiating complement blockade early, once infection is controlled, improves renal recovery. In our case, the PLASMIC score helped avoid delay in pursuing the correct therapy (eculizumab) while still providing short‑term PEX as a bridge.

**Bottom line:** In any adult with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, calculate the PLASMIC score immediately. A **score of 4 (low‑risk)**, as in this patient, steers you away from aggressive, TTP‑directed plasma exchange and prompts a focused evaluation for alternative TMAs such as complement‑mediated HUS. This strategy shortens time to the appropriate targeted therapy and improves outcomes.

### [Pathophysiology] Explain the pathophysiologic mechanism by which Capnocytophaga canimorsus infection can trigger complement‑mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome in the absence of a detectable complement gene mutation.

**Pathophysiologic mechanism by which *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* infection triggers complement‑mediated HUS in the absence of a detectable complement‑gene mutation**

1. **The “two‑hit” model of atypical (complement‑mediated) HUS**  
   - In most cases of CM‑HUS, a germ‑line defect (mutation or auto‑antibody) in a regulator of the alternative pathway (CFH, CFI, CFB, CD46, etc.) creates a latent propensity for uncontrolled complement activation on the endothelial surface.  
   - Clinical disease occurs only after a *second hit* that provides a strong activating stimulus—most commonly an infection, pregnancy, or certain drugs. The second hit amplifies alternative‑pathway turnover, overwhelms the already‑impaired regulation, and leads to endothelial injury, platelet‐rich microthrombi, and the classic triad of MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury.

2. **What *C. canimorsus* contributes as the second hit**  
   - *C. canimorsus* is a gram‑negative, encapsulated rod that is unusually resistant to serum‑mediated killing. Its capsular polysaccharide and lipooligosaccharide (LOS) strongly activate the **alternative complement pathway** by providing a surface that allows spontaneous C3 hydrolysis (C3‑tick‑over) to deposit C3b and form the C3 convertase (C3bBb).  
   - In addition, the bacterium’s LOS engages pattern‑recognition receptors (TLR2/4) on endothelial cells and monocytes, leading to up‑regulation of **properdin** (the stabilizer of the alternative‑pathway C3 convertase) and down‑regulation of membrane‑bound regulators (CFH, CD55, CD59). The net effect is a localized “gain‑of‑function” of the alternative pathway at the microvascular endothelium.

3. **Why uncontrolled complement activation can occur even without a detectable genetic defect**  
   - **Functional (acquired) dysregulation**: Severe sepsis, especially with DIC, consumes complement regulators (CFH, CFI) and generates large amounts of anaphylatoxins (C3a, C5a) that amplify inflammation. Transient depletion or functional inhibition of regulators can mimic a genetic defect for the duration of the insult.  
   - **Endothelial “priming”**: The septic milieu (high cytokines, oxidative stress, endothelial glycocalyx degradation) makes the endothelium more susceptible to complement deposition. Even normal levels of CFH may be insufficient to protect a “primed” endothelium when the alternative pathway is vigorously driven by the bacterial surface.  
   - **Complement consumption by the pathogen**: *C. canimorsus* can bind and inactivate complement components (e.g., factor H‑binding proteins). This reduces the availability of soluble regulators in the plasma, further tipping the balance toward activation.  

4. **Chain of events leading to HUS in this patient**  
   - **Initial infection** → massive exposure of circulating blood to *C. canimorsus* capsular antigens → robust alternative‑pathway activation, generation of C3b‑Bb, and deposition of C3b on endothelial surfaces.  
   - **Sepsis‑associated DIC** consumes coagulation factors and further injures endothelium, exposing sub‑endothelial matrix that acts as a scaffold for complement‑mediated thrombus formation.  
   - **Relative deficiency of regulators** (functional, not genetic) + persistent alternative‑pathway amplification → uncontrolled formation of the terminal membrane attack complex (C5b‑9) on renal glomerular and arteriolar endothelium.  
   - **Endothelial cell activation and death** → exposure of von Willebrand factor, platelet adhesion, and propagation of microvascular thrombi → the histologic picture of thrombotic microangiopathy seen on renal biopsy.  
   - **Resulting clinical syndrome**: microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (schistocytes, low haptoglobin, high LDH), persistent thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury—fulfilling the criteria for complement‑mediated HUS.

5. **Why complement genetics were negative in this case**  
   - The patient likely had **no permanent regulatory defect**; instead, the infection produced a **temporary functional complement dysregulation** sufficient to cross the pathogenic threshold. This is consistent with published reports that up to 30 % of CM‑HUS patients have no identifiable mutation, yet still respond to terminal complement blockade because the disease process is driven by an overwhelming, infection‑induced activation of the alternative pathway.

6. **Implications for practice (take‑home points)**  
   - In sepsis with *C. canimorsus* (or other encapsulated gram‑negative organisms), be alert for a delayed onset of TMA after the initial septic picture improves.  
   - Normal C3/C4 levels do **not** exclude complement‑mediated disease; functional assays (AH50, CH50) and clinical context are essential.  
   - Early initiation of eculizumab, even when a genetic defect is not found, can abort the complement‑driven endothelial injury and improve renal recovery, as demonstrated in this patient.

**Bottom line:** *C. canimorsus* provides a potent infection‑derived “second hit” that hyper‑activates the alternative complement pathway, overwhelms transiently compromised regulatory mechanisms, and precipitates endothelial injury characteristic of complement‑mediated HUS—even in the absence of a detectable complement‑gene mutation.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Why is ADAMTS13 activity measurement critical in the work‑up of this case, and what alternative diagnoses are effectively excluded by an activity level of 43.5%?

**Why ADAMTS13 activity is a pivotal test in this case, and what diagnoses are ruled out by a level of 43 %**

In any adult who develops a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) – the triad of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury – the first clinician’s priority is to determine whether the disorder is **thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP)**. TTP is a medical emergency; plasma exchange (PEX) must be started immediately because mortality exceeds 30 % without therapy. The diagnostic cornerstone of TTP is **severe functional deficiency of the metalloprotease ADAMTS13** (activity < 10 % of normal), usually caused by an acquired auto‑antibody or, rarely, by a hereditary mutation.

*In our patient* the clinical picture (new‑onset MAHA, persistent thrombocytopenia, dialysis‑dependent AKI) was indistinguishable from TTP at the bedside. Consequently, a PLASMIC score was calculated (4/7, low‑intermediate probability) and, while awaiting definitive testing, PEX was initiated as a bridging measure. The ADAMTS13 assay returned a **activity of 43.5 %**.

**Interpretation of the result**

| ADAMTS13 activity | Diagnostic implication |
|-------------------|------------------------|
| < 10 %            | **TTP** (classic or hereditary) – requires urgent PEX and often immunosuppression |
| 10–30 %           | Possible TTP variant (e.g., partial inhibition) but still favors TTP in the appropriate clinical context |
| **> 30 %**        | **TTP is effectively excluded**; attention redirects to other TMAs (atypical HUS, STEC‑HUS, secondary TMA from DIC, malignant hypertension, drugs, etc.) |

Because the activity is **well above the 10 % threshold**, TTP can be ruled out with a high degree of confidence. This result allowed the team to **de‑escalate the empiric PEX** after a limited “bridge” course and to pursue the next tier of the differential diagnosis.

**Alternative diagnoses that become more plausible (and those effectively excluded)**  

1. **Excluded by ADAMTS13 > 30 %**  
   - **Acquired or hereditary TTP** – the hallmark low ADAMTS13 activity is absent.  
   - **TTP mimics that rely on ADAMTS13 inhibition** (e.g., drug‑induced ADAMTS13 antibodies) – similarly excluded.

2. **Diagnoses that remain on the differential after a normal‑range ADAMTS13**  
   - **Complement‑mediated (atypical) HUS (CM‑HUS)** – ADAMTS13 is usually normal; diagnosis rests on clinical features, exclusion of STEC‑HUS, and evidence of complement dysregulation (as in this case).  
   - **Shiga‑toxin–associated HUS (STEC‑HUS)** – also ADAMTS13‑normal; ruled out here by a negative stool PCR.  
   - **Secondary TMA due to severe sepsis/DIC** – ADAMTS13 may be mildly reduced but not <10 %; the patient’s initial DIC phase fits this pattern.  
   - **Malignant hypertension, drug‑induced TMA (e.g., calcineurin inhibitors, gemcitabine), pregnancy‑associated TMA, systemic lupus erythematosus–related TMA** – all have normal ADAMTS13 and are considered only after other triggers are excluded.

In this case, once TTP was ruled out, the team focused on the remaining possibilities. The **absence of a diarrheal prodrome, a negative stool PCR for Shiga toxin, and the timing of hemolysis after the septic phase** pointed away from STEC‑HUS. The renal biopsy showing fibrin‑rich thrombi in glomerular capillaries, together with normal complement C3/C4 but a markedly reduced alternative‑pathway activity after plasma exchange, supported **infection‑triggered complement‑mediated HUS**. The subsequent rapid hematologic and renal response to eculizumab confirmed that diagnosis.

**Take‑home point for trainees**

- **ADAMTS13 activity is the single most decisive laboratory test to exclude TTP**, a condition that mandates immediate plasma exchange.  
- An activity level of **> 30 % (in practice, > 10 %) effectively removes TTP from the differential**, allowing the clinician to pursue other TMA etiologies, most notably complement‑mediated HUS, which requires a completely different therapeutic approach (eculizumab rather than prolonged plasma exchange).  

Thus, in this patient the ADAMTS13 result of **43.5 %** was the pivotal data point that shifted management from TTP‑focused therapy to targeted complement inhibition, ultimately leading to hematologic remission and partial renal recovery.

### [Management] What is the role of plasma exchange (PEX) in the management of TMA, and why was it used as a bridging therapy before eculizumab in this patient?

**Plasma exchange (PEX) in the work‑up of a thrombotic microangiopathy – why it was used as a “bridge” before eculizumab in this patient**

When an adult presents with the classic triad of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (schistocytes, ↓ haptoglobin, ↑ LDH), thrombocytopenia and acute kidney injury, the first priority is to **exclude thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP)** because TTP is a medical emergency that is rapidly fatal without plasma exchange.  The therapeutic rationale for PEX in TTP is clear:

1. **Removal of ultra‑large von Willebrand factor multimers** that drive platelet aggregation in the setting of severe ADAMTS13 deficiency.  
2. **Re‑pletion of functional ADAMTS13** contained in donor plasma.  

Because ADAMTS13 results are not instantly available, clinicians rely on clinical prediction scores (e.g., PLASMIC) and start PEX empirically in any high‑ or intermediate‑risk case.

In our patient, the **PLASMIC score was low (4/7)** and the ADAMTS13 activity returned at 43 %—well above the <10 % threshold that defines TTP.  Nonetheless, the **initial laboratory picture (MAHA, persistent thrombocytopenia, worsening renal function) could not exclude TTP** pending the ADAMTS13 result.  Thus, the team initiated PEX while awaiting definitive testing and while arranging definitive therapy for the more likely diagnosis of complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS).

---

### What does plasma exchange achieve in complement‑mediated HUS?

PEX is **not curative** for CM‑HUS, but it can provide several transient benefits that are valuable in the acute setting:

| Potential benefit | Mechanism |
|-------------------|-----------|
| **Dilution/removal of circulating complement activation fragments (C3a, C5a, C5b‑9)** | Fresh frozen plasma or solvent‑detergent‑treated plasma contains normal complement regulators (factor H, I, CD55) that can partially restore control of the alternative pathway. |
| **Removal of inflammatory cytokines and pathogen‑derived products** | May blunt the “second‑hit” inflammatory surge that perpetuates endothelial injury. |
| **Temporary improvement in platelet count and hemolysis** | By reducing the burden of active complement and microthrombi, PEX can raise the platelet count enough to allow safe performance of diagnostic kidney biopsy and to bridge the patient to definitive therapy. |

In the case report, after **two to three PEX sessions**, the patient’s platelet count rose from the low 30 × 10⁹/L range to > 70 × 10⁹/L, which made kidney biopsy feasible and stabilized her hemodynamics.  Spontaneous diuresis also returned before eculizumab was given, reflecting a **temporary hemodynamic improvement** rather than durable disease control.

---

### Why was PEX used *before* eculizumab?

1. **Diagnostic safety net** – The clinicians needed to act urgently while the ADAMTS13 assay was pending.  Starting PEX covered the worst‑case scenario (TTP) without delaying the eventual treatment for CM‑HUS.

2. **Infection‑related timing of complement blockade** – Eculizumab blocks terminal complement (C5) and markedly impairs opsonophagocytic killing of encapsulated organisms, including *Neisseria* and, more pertinently, *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*.  The team therefore **waited until blood cultures were sterile** (≈ 8 days after admission) before administering the first eculizumab dose, to minimize the risk of uncontrolled bacterial proliferation.

3. **Bridge to vaccination and prophylaxis** – The patient required meningococcal vaccination and a short course of prophylactic ampicillin before complement inhibition could be started safely.  PEX bought time to complete immunisation (Menveo + Bexsero) and prophylaxis while maintaining a minimal level of hematologic stability.

4. **Transient restoration of complement regulation** – Fresh plasma used for exchange supplies normal complement regulators, offering a **short‑term “wash‑out”** of the dysregulated alternative pathway that can blunt the acute flare while awaiting the slower onset of eculizumab (which reaches steady‑state inhibition after several weekly doses).

---

### Bottom line for learners

* In any adult with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, **start PEX immediately** until TTP is confidently excluded (ADAMTS13 < 10 %).  
* If ADAMTS13 is normal and the clinical picture fits CM‑HUS, **transition to complement blockade** (eculizumab or ravulizumab) as soon as it is safe to do so.  
* Use PEX as a **bridging therapy** when active infection, pending vaccination, or the need for diagnostic procedures (e.g., kidney biopsy) preclude immediate eculizumab administration.  
* Remember that PEX offers only a **temporary, non‑specific reduction** in complement activity; definitive, durable remission in CM‑HUS requires targeted C5 inhibition.

In this patient, plasma exchange safely covered the possibility of TTP, stabilized her hematologic parameters enough to permit biopsy, and bought the necessary time to achieve microbiologic clearance, vaccinate, and start prophylaxis—allowing eculizumab to be introduced under the safest possible conditions.

### [Morning Report] Discuss the timing and safety considerations for initiating eculizumab in a patient with ongoing or recent bacterial sepsis, including necessary preventative measures.

**Timing and safety of eculizumab after bacterial sepsis – “when can we block complement?”**

In the presented case the patient developed a complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS) a week after an episode of severe **Capnocytophaga canimorsus** sepsis. The clinicians delayed the first dose of eculizumab until **blood cultures were sterile, the acute septic picture had abated, and meningococcal vaccination (plus short‑course prophylactic antibiotics) had been given**. This sequence illustrates the general principles that should guide any physician who is considering complement blockade in the setting of recent or ongoing bacterial infection.

| **Consideration** | **Rationale** | **What the case did** |
|-------------------|---------------|-----------------------|
| **1. Confirm control of the active infection** | Terminal complement (C5) is essential for opsonophagocytic killing of encapsulated organisms (Neisseria, Haemophilus, Streptococcus, some gram‑negative rods such as *C. canimorsus*). Starting eculizumab while bacteremia persists can allow uncontrolled bacterial growth and fulminant sepsis. | Blood cultures became negative before the first eculizumab dose; the patient had already received 8 days of appropriate β‑lactam therapy and showed clinical improvement. |
| **2. Provide meningococcal immunisation *before* the first dose** | Eculizumab increases the risk of invasive meningococcal disease > 1,000‑fold. Vaccination (quadrivalent + serogroup B) should be administered ≥ 2 weeks prior whenever possible; if treatment cannot wait, give the vaccine **and start prophylactic antibiotics immediately**. | The team gave Menveo (A, C, Y, W) and Bexsero (B) on the day of the first eculizumab infusion, acknowledging that the 2‑week window could not be met. They simultaneously started prophylactic amoxicillin (Apocillin) until the second vaccine dose. |
| **3. Give antimicrobial prophylaxis while complement is blocked** | Even with vaccination, residual risk remains (non‑vaccine serogroups, breakthrough infection). Continuous oral penicillin or a macrolide is recommended for the first 2–4 weeks of therapy (or longer if vaccination was delayed). | The patient received oral amoxicillin throughout the induction phase and until the second vaccine dose was given 8 weeks later. |
| **4. Assess the urgency of complement blockade** | CM‑HUS can cause irreversible renal endothelial injury within days. Delay > 7‑10 days from TMA onset is associated with lower rates of renal recovery. The decision balances infection risk against irreversible organ damage. | After plasma exchange (a bridge) and once the septic inflammatory markers fell, eculizumab was started on day 12 of hospitalization (≈ 8 days after the first laboratory evidence of TMA). The early start (within 2 weeks of TMA onset) is consistent with data showing better renal outcomes. |
| **5. Monitor closely for new infections** | Even with vaccination and prophylaxis, breakthrough meningococcal or other encapsulated infections can occur. Prompt evaluation of fever, meningismus, or unexplained sepsis is mandatory. | The report notes that the patient remained infection‑free during the induction period, and no additional cultures were needed. Ongoing outpatient surveillance was planned. |
| **6. Re‑evaluate need for continued eculizumab** | In infection‑triggered, complement‑negative CM‑HUS (no pathogenic genetic variants), a finite course may be sufficient if remission is achieved and renal function stabilises. | After 4 months of maintenance therapy the team contemplated discontinuation, pending renal recovery and the pending genetic report. |

### Practical algorithm derived from the case

1. **Diagnose TMA** → rule out TTP (ADAMTS13 < 10 %), STEC‑HUS, DIC.  
2. **Confirm infection control** → repeat blood cultures, clinical improvement, appropriate antimicrobial course.  
3. **Vaccinate** (meningococcal A‑C‑Y‑W + B) *if time permits*; otherwise give **both vaccines plus prophylactic penicillin** on the same day you start eculizumab.  
4. **Start eculizumab** as soon as infection is controlled and vaccination/prophylaxis are in place; do not wait > 2 weeks after TMA onset unless the infection is still active.  
5. **Continue prophylaxis** (penicillin or macrolide) for at least the first 2 weeks, longer if vaccination was < 2 weeks before the first dose.  
6. **Monitor** for meningococcal disease (fever, rash, meningitis signs) and for any new bacterial infection; have a low threshold for cultures and empiric therapy.  
7. **Re‑assess long‑term need** for complement blockade once hematologic remission and renal function have stabilized, especially in patients without a demonstrable complement‑gene defect.

### Take‑home message for trainees

- **Eculizumab is lifesaving in CM‑HUS but is a double‑edged sword in the setting of bacterial sepsis.** The key is to **neutralise the infection first**, then **protect the patient against meningococcal (and other encapsulated) infection** with vaccination and antibiotics, and finally **initiate complement blockade promptly** to limit irreversible renal injury.  
- The timing used in this case—**after sterile cultures, with immediate meningococcal vaccination and amoxicillin prophylaxis**—represents the safest, evidence‑based approach and should be the template for any similar clinical scenario.

### [Pathophysiology] Interpret the renal biopsy findings (ischemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi, crescents, and acute tubular necrosis) and explain how they support the diagnosis of complement‑mediated HUS versus other renal pathologies seen in sepsis.

**Interpretation of the renal biopsy and why it points to complement‑mediated HUS rather than a sepsis‑related lesion**

The kidney specimen showed three classic features of a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA):

1. **Ischemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi** – occlusion of capillary loops by fibrin‑rich microthrombi. In pure sepsis‑associated acute kidney injury (AKI) the dominant pattern is acute tubular necrosis (ATN) with relatively preserved glomeruli; fibrin thrombi are not a typical finding.

2. **Crescents (extracapillary proliferation)** – focal or segmental crescents form when the glomerular basement membrane is breached by the thrombus‑induced ischemic injury, allowing plasma proteins and fibrin to leak into Bowman's space. Crescents are a hallmark of endothelial injury that is severe enough to cause rupture of the capillary wall, a process seen in TMA (e.g., HUS, TTP) but not in ordinary sepsis‑induced ATN.

3. **Severe acute tubular necrosis (ATN)** – diffuse loss of the tubular epithelium, which can be a secondary consequence of the profound ischemia caused by the intraglomerular thrombi. ATN alone would not explain the thrombi and crescents; together they indicate a *primary* microvascular occlusive process superimposed on the tubular injury.

**Why this pattern favours complement‑mediated HUS**

| Finding | Seen in sepsis‑related AKI? | Seen in complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS) |
|---------|----------------------------|------------------------------------------|
| Fibrin thrombi in glomerular capillaries | Rare; sepsis usually causes diffuse endothelial activation without discrete fibrin plugs | Characteristic; uncontrolled complement activation damages endothelium → platelet‑rich fibrin microthrombi |
| Crescents | Uncommon; sepsis may cause mild focal necrosis but not classic crescents | Common when thrombi cause capillary rupture |
| Ischemic glomeruli | May be present late in severe shock, but often accompanied by extensive necrosis, not discrete thrombi | Typical because each thrombus creates a focal zone of ischemia |
| Isolated ATN | The predominant pattern in pure septic AKI | Frequently co‑exists, but always with the above TMA lesions |

Thus, the biopsy demonstrates a *primary* TMA (microvascular fibrin thrombi with resultant crescents) with secondary ATN, exactly the histologic signature of HUS. In contrast, sepsis‑associated AKI would show patchy tubular necrosis, loss of brush border, and perhaps occasional necrotic cells, but **without** fibrin‑rich glomerular occlusions or crescents.

**Correlation with the clinical picture**

- **Laboratory:** The later appearance of MAHA (schistocytes, low haptoglobin, ↑LDH) and persistent thrombocytopenia after the septic phase fits a TMA. ADAMTS13 was >10 % (rules out TTP), and stool PCR for Shiga toxin was negative (rules out STEC‑HUS).
- **Timing:** Hemolysis emerged **after** the septic coagulopathy began to resolve, a classic “second‑hit” scenario where the infection triggers uncontrolled alternative‑pathway complement activation.
- **Complement testing:** Normal C3/C4 does **not** exclude CM‑HUS; the definitive clue is the histology plus the clinical exclusion of other TMAs, which led the team to start plasma exchange as a bridge and then eculizumab. The rapid hematologic and renal response after eculizumab further validates the diagnosis.

**Key teaching points**

1. **Renal biopsy in TMA** – Look for fibrin thrombi in glomerular capillaries and/or arterioles, early crescent formation, and associated ATN. Their presence is diagnostic of a true TMA; their absence favours pure septic ATN.
2. **Distinguish TMA from sepsis‑related AKI** – Sepsis can cause ATN, but the microvascular thrombi and crescents are not part of the sepsis spectrum.
3. **CM‑HUS diagnosis is clinicopathologic** – It requires (a) evidence of TMA (schistocytes, MAHA, thrombocytopenia, AKI), (b) exclusion of TTP (ADAMTS13 < 10 %), STEC‑HUS (negative stool PCR), and other secondary causes, and (c) renal histology that confirms TMA.
4. **Biopsy timing matters** – In a patient who is already anuric on dialysis, a percutaneous biopsy is safe and yields decisive information that can change therapy from plasma exchange alone to complement blockade.

In this case, the combination of **ischemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi, crescent formation, and severe ATN** provided the histologic proof of a complement‑driven TMA, steering management toward eculizumab and ultimately leading to hematologic remission and partial renal recovery.

### [Management] What are the recommended vaccination and antimicrobial prophylaxis strategies for a patient beginning complement blockade with eculizumab, and why are they particularly important after a Capnocytophaga infection?

**Vaccination and antimicrobial prophylaxis when starting eculizumab – why it matters after a *Capnocytophaga* infection**

The patient in the report required eculizumab because her sepsis‑triggered complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS) was refractory to plasma exchange.  Eculizumab blocks terminal complement activation (C5‑a/C5b‑9), which is essential for opsonisation and killing of encapsulated organisms, most famously *Neisseria meningitidis* but also other gram‑negative bacteria such as *Capnocytophaga* spp.  Consequently, every patient who is about to receive a C5 inhibitor must receive both:

1. **Meningococcal vaccination (quadrivalent + serogroup B)**  
2. **Broad‑spectrum antibacterial prophylaxis (usually oral penicillin or a macrolide) until vaccine‑induced immunity is confirmed.**

Below is the step‑by‑step regimen that the treating team applied, and the rationale for each element.

| Timing | Intervention | Rationale | How it was applied in the case |
|--------|--------------|-----------|--------------------------------|
| **≥2 weeks before first eculizumab dose** (or as soon as possible) | • Menveo (A, C, Y, W)  <br>• Bexsero (serogroup B) | These two vaccines together provide coverage for all meningococcal serogroups responsible for disease in complement‑deficient hosts. Immunogenicity is optimal after a ≥2‑week interval, allowing antibody titres to rise before terminal complement is blocked. | The patient received the first doses of Menveo and Bexsero after plasma exchange, but **before** the first eculizumab infusion because her blood cultures were already sterile. The second vaccine doses were scheduled for 8 weeks later, as recommended. |
| **Immediately after the first vaccine dose (if <2 weeks have elapsed)** | **Antibiotic prophylaxis** – oral penicillin V 250 mg 2–3 times daily (or amoxicillin if tolerated) or, alternatively, a macrolide (azithromycin 250 mg weekly) | Penicillin kills most meningococci and *Capnocytophaga* strains; it also covers other encapsulated organisms (e.g., *Haemophilus influenzae*, *Streptococcus pneumoniae*) that become more dangerous when C5 is inhibited. Prophylaxis is continued until ≥2 weeks after the last meningococcal vaccine dose, when protective antibody titres are assumed. | Because the patient had not yet completed the 2‑week interval between vaccination and eculizumab, she was placed on **prophylactic phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin)**—the same oral agent originally prescribed for the dog bite. This covered both *Neisseria* and any residual *Capnocytophaga* that might have lingered despite negative cultures. |
| **Throughout eculizumab therapy** | **Prompt treatment of febrile illness**; consider empiric coverage for encapsulated bacteria if infection is suspected. | Even with vaccination, breakthrough meningococcal disease can occur (≈0.5–1 %/year). Early empiric therapy (e.g., ceftriaxone) reduces mortality. | The team continued close monitoring; no further infections occurred, illustrating the effectiveness of the combined vaccine‑plus‑prophylaxis strategy. |

### Why the strategy is especially critical after *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* infection

1. **Shared reliance on complement for bacterial clearance** – *C. canimorsus* is an encapsulated gram‑negative rod that is partially resistant to serum killing; terminal complement (C5‑C9) is a major bactericidal mechanism. Blocking C5 could theoretically allow relapse or secondary infection with the same organism.

2. **Potential for delayed culture negativity** – The case shows that blood cultures turned negative before eculizumab was started, but the organism is fastidious and may persist in low‑grade foci (e.g., oral cavity, wound). Prophylactic penicillin provides a safety net against such occult reservoirs.

3. **Meningococcal risk is amplified in the setting of recent sepsis** – Sepsis itself can transiently depress humoral immunity, making vaccine responses slower. Ensuring both vaccination and antibiotics bridges the gap until robust anti‑meningococcal titres develop.

4. **Guideline concordance** – The Infectious Diseases Society of America and the FDA label for eculizumab both mandate meningococcal immunisation *and* antibacterial prophylaxis when a C5 inhibitor is initiated, regardless of the underlying trigger. The management in the case adheres precisely to these recommendations, illustrating best practice.

### Practical take‑away for trainees

- **Never start eculizumab without first ordering quadrivalent + serogroup B meningococcal vaccines.** If timing forces you to start the drug before the 2‑week immunity window, **start oral penicillin (or azithromycin) immediately** and continue it for at least 2 weeks after the final vaccine dose.
- **Confirm negative blood cultures** before the first infusion when possible, especially after a gram‑negative infection such as *Capnocytophaga*. This reduces the risk of uncontrolled bacteremia under complement blockade.
- **Educate the patient** about the need for urgent medical attention for any fever, rash, or respiratory symptoms while on eculizumab, even if vaccinated.
- **Document vaccination dates and prophylactic regimen** clearly in the chart; these details are essential for subsequent providers and for pharmacy verification before each eculizumab infusion.

In summary, the combined meningococcal vaccination (Menveo + Bexsero) and continuous oral penicillin prophylaxis that were instituted in this patient are the standard, evidence‑based safeguards against life‑threatening infections when complement blockade with eculizumab is required—particularly after a *Capnocytophaga* infection, where complement plays a pivotal role in bacterial clearance.

### [Clinical Pearls] How does the presence of a normal C3/C4 level influence the diagnostic approach to suspected atypical HUS, and why can these levels be misleading?

**Answer**  

A normal C3 and C4 does **not** rule out complement‑mediated (atypical) HUS (CM‑HUS). In the work‑up of a patient with a new‑onset thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) you should still pursue the full complement panel and consider eculizumab even when the routine C3/C4 levels are within laboratory reference ranges.  

**Why a normal C3/C4 can be misleading**

1. **C3/C4 are crude, static measures** – they reflect the bulk plasma concentration of the classical‑pathway components, not the activity of the alternative pathway that drives CM‑HUS.  
2. **Acute phase reactions mask consumption** – during sepsis, inflammation, or after plasma exchange (PEX) the liver synthesises more C3 and C4, often normalising or even elevating the values despite ongoing complement activation.  
3. **Therapeutic interventions alter the assay** – in the presented case the patient received three sessions of PEX and a dose of eculizumab before the extended complement work‑up. Both procedures remove circulating complement fragments and can artificially raise measured C3/C4 while suppressing functional assays (e.g., AH50).  

**How this influences the diagnostic algorithm**

| Step | Typical approach | Modification in light of normal C3/C4 |
|------|------------------|----------------------------------------|
| **Initial screen** | Check C3, C4, ADAMTS13, STEC PCR. | If C3/C4 are normal, **do not stop**; still send an **alternative‑pathway functional assay (AH50), factor H/I/B levels, and anti‑factor H antibodies**. |
| **Probability of TTP** | Use PLASMIC score, ADAMTS13. | Normal ADAMTS13 (≈ 43 %) already makes TTP unlikely; focus shifts to complement. |
| **Decision on plasma exchange** | Start PEX if TTP cannot be excluded. | In CM‑HUS, PEX is a temporising measure; continue it only as a bridge while awaiting full complement results. |
| **Trigger assessment** | Look for diarrhoeal prodrome, infection, drugs, pregnancy. | In this case the trigger was **Capnocytophaga canimorsus septicemia**, a recognized, albeit rare, stimulus for uncontrolled alternative‑pathway activation. |
| **Initiation of complement blockade** | Usually after confirming low C3 or genetic abnormality. | Because C3/C4 may be normal, **clinical criteria (persistent MAHA, schistocytes, thrombocytopenia, AKI, biopsy‑proven TMA) and exclusion of TTP/STEC‑HUS** are sufficient to start eculizumab promptly, especially when the patient is stabilising from sepsis and cultures are negative. |

**Teaching point from the case**

The patient’s C3 and C4 were **within normal limits** at the time the complement studies were drawn, yet the functional alternative‑pathway activity (AH50) was profoundly reduced (6 %). This discordance illustrates that reliance on C3/C4 alone would have led to a missed or delayed diagnosis of CM‑HUS. Recognising that normal complement levels are common in CM‑HUS—particularly in the setting of sepsis and after plasma exchange—prevents a false sense of security and ensures that the definitive therapy (eculizumab) is not postponed.

In summary, **normal C3/C4 should not deter you from pursuing a full complement work‑up or from initiating complement inhibition** when the clinical picture is compatible with CM‑HUS. Treat the syndrome, not the laboratory number.

### [Morning Report] In the long‑term follow‑up of a patient with infection‑triggered CM‑HUS who had no pathogenic complement mutations, what factors guide the decision to continue or discontinue eculizumab therapy?

**Clinical Pearls – When to Stop (or Continue) Eculizumab in Infection‑Triggered, Genetically‑Negative CM‑HUS**

The case of the 40‑year‑old woman with Capnocytophaga canimorsus‑induced CM‑HUS illustrates the dilemma that most clinicians face once acute disease control is achieved: *how long should complement blockade be maintained* when no pathogenic complement mutation has been identified?  
The decision hinges on a handful of well‑studied clinical and laboratory “stopping rules” that can be applied to any trigger‑driven atypical HUS, and these are precisely the factors that guided the treating team in the present report.

| **Factor** | **Why it matters** | **How it was applied in the case** |
|------------|-------------------|------------------------------------|
| **1. Recovery of renal function** – trend and plateau | Persistent severe AKI (dialysis dependence) predicts a higher risk of relapse after withdrawal; patients who achieve a stable eGFR ≥ 30 mL/min/1.73 m² for ≥ 3 months are the best candidates for taper. | At 4 months the patient’s serum creatinine had fallen to 175 µmol/L (eGFR ≈ 40 mL/min) with ongoing urine output. The team elected to **continue** therapy while renal function continues to improve, planning reassessment once a stable plateau is documented. |
| **2. Hematologic remission** – normalization of LDH, haptoglobin, platelet count, and absence of schistocytes | Ongoing subclinical hemolysis is the earliest harbinger of complement re‑activation; complete remission for ≥ 2 months suggests durable control. | After the first eculizumab dose, LDH fell, haptoglobin recovered, and platelets remained > 100 × 10⁹/L without schistocytes. This sustained remission supported continuation until a “wash‑out” period could be documented. |
| **3. Absence of a pathogenic complement gene mutation** – “second‑hit” concept | In patients with a known high‑penetrance variant (e.g., CFH, CFI, CFB, CD46), lifelong therapy is recommended because the genetic defect persists regardless of the trigger. Without a mutation, the disease may be truly “trigger‑limited” and can be discontinued after the trigger resolves. | Extended sequencing from Lund University found **no pathogenic variant**. This lowered the threshold for eventual discontinuation, but the team still chose a cautious 4‑month maintenance because the initial trigger (sepsis) was severe and renal recovery was incomplete. |
| **4. Timing of trigger resolution** – clearance of bacteremia and completion of vaccination/antibiotic prophylaxis | Ongoing infection creates a “second hit” that can reactivate complement even while on blockade; waiting until cultures are sterile and the patient is protected against encapsulated organisms (meningococci, Haemophilus) is essential. | Eculizumab was started only after **blood cultures turned negative** and after administration of Menveo and Bexsero plus prophylactic amoxicillin. This ensured that the risk of a new infection‑driven relapse was minimal. |
| **5. Biomarkers of complement activity** – AH50, soluble C5b‑9 (if available) | Persistent low AH50 or high sC5b‑9 after treatment may indicate ongoing activation and argue for continued therapy; normalisation suggests the pathway is quiescent. | In this patient, the AH50 was markedly suppressed **after** eculizumab (a treatment effect) and could not be interpreted as a disease marker. The team therefore relied on clinical remission rather than a complement assay to guide duration. |
| **6. Patient‑centered considerations** – adverse‑effect profile, vaccination status, adherence, and quality‑of‑life | Chronic eculizumab carries infection risk, high cost, and infusion burden; these must be weighed against relapse risk. | The patient was vaccinated, on prophylactic antibiotics, and tolerating bi‑weekly infusions, making continuation reasonable while the renal trajectory was still uncertain. |

### Practical Algorithm Derived from the Case

1. **Achieve hematologic remission** (LDH < 1.5 × ULN, platelets > 150 × 10⁹/L, no schistocytes) for ≥ 2 months.  
2. **Document renal plateau** – eGFR stable (± 5 mL/min) for ≥ 3 months, no dialysis requirement.  
3. **Confirm trigger clearance** – negative cultures, completed meningococcal vaccination, and protective antibiotics.  
4. **Genetic results** – if pathogenic variant present → lifelong therapy; if absent → consider trial of discontinuation after step 1–3 are met.  
5. **Re‑challenge (optional)** – stop eculizumab and monitor weekly labs for 4–6 weeks; resume immediately if any sign of hemolysis or renal decline.  

### How This Informed the Patient’s Plan

- **Continuation:** The team kept the patient on maintenance 1,200 mg every 2 weeks because (a) renal function had not yet plateaued, (b) the initial insult was massive (dialysis‑dependent AKI), and (c) the patient had already tolerated the drug without infection complications.  
- **Future reassessment:** A formal plan was set to **re‑evaluate after 6–12 months** of stable labs and renal function; if the eGFR remains ≥ 40 mL/min/1.73 m² and labs stay normal, a supervised discontinuation can be attempted.  

### Take‑home Message for Trainees

- **Eculizumab duration is not a fixed interval**; it is driven by the *clinical trajectory* of hemolysis and kidney recovery, *genetic background*, and *clearance of the inciting trigger*.  
- **Absence of a complement mutation** lowers the threshold for eventual discontinuation, but **renal function** remains the dominant predictor of relapse.  
- **Systematic monitoring** (LDH, platelets, creatinine, and, when available, complement activity assays) during the maintenance phase is essential to make an evidence‑based decision.  

By integrating these principles, you can tailor complement blockade to each patient’s risk profile, avoiding both premature withdrawal (with relapse) and unnecessary lifelong therapy (with cost and infection risks).
