### [Pathophysiology] Which autoantibodies are most commonly associated with IMNM, and what is the significance of the patient’s seronegative myositis panel in this case?

**Answer – Autoantibodies in Immune‑Mediated Necrotising Myopathy (IMNM) and the Meaning of a Seronegative Myositis Panel**

The two auto‑antibodies that most reliably identify IMNM are:

| Auto‑antibody | Typical clinical Associations | Approximate frequency in IMNM |
|---------------|------------------------------|--------------------------------|
| **Anti‑HMG‑CoA reductase (anti‑HMGCR)** | Often follows exposure to statins; can also occur in statin‑naïve patients with metabolic disease | ~30–40 % of all IMNM cases |
| **Anti‑signal recognition particle (anti‑SRP)** | Frequently presents with very rapid CK rise, severe weakness, and may have cardiac involvement | ~20–30 % of all IMNM cases |

Together these two antibodies account for roughly **half of all patients with IMNM**. The remaining cases fall into two groups:

1. **Seronegative IMNM** – no detectable anti‑HMGCR, anti‑SRP, or any of the broader myositis‑specific antibodies (MSA) such as Jo‑1, PL‑7, Mi‑2, MDA‑5, TIF1‑γ, etc. This constitutes **≈30 %** of IMNM patients, as highlighted in the case report (“an estimated third of patients with IMNM are seronegative”).  
2. **Patients with yet‑to‑be‑identified antibodies** – emerging data suggest additional targets (e.g., antibodies to HSP‑70, myosin, etc.) that are not part of standard commercial panels.

### Why Does the Patient’s Seronegative Myositis Panel Matter?

1. **Diagnostic Confirmation Relies on Muscle Histology**  
   In the absence of a defining auto‑antibody, the **gold‑standard diagnosis is a muscle biopsy** showing:  
   - Prominent necrotic fibers with regeneration,  
   - Minimal endomysial lymphocytic infiltrates,  
   - Evidence of complement (C5b‑9) deposition on sarcolemma.  

   The case report provides precisely these findings (necrosis, CD68⁺ macrophages, sparse CD3⁺ T‑cells, membrane‑attack‑complex positivity), establishing IMNM despite a negative serologic work‑up.

2. **Implications for Etiology**  
   - **Drug‑induced IMNM**: When anti‑HMGCR or anti‑SRP are absent, clinicians should heighten suspicion for an external trigger (e.g., statins, immune checkpoint inhibitors, or, as in this report, a GLP‑1 receptor agonist). The temporal relationship (symptom onset within hours of semaglutide, progressive CK rise over months) and dramatic response after drug withdrawal support a **drug‑associated, seronegative phenotype**.  
   - **Prognosis**: Seronegative patients often have a **more favorable response to a short course of immunosuppression** and may not require prolonged multi‑drug regimens that are typical for seropositive IMNM. 3. **Therapeutic Decision‑Making**  
   - **Auto‑antibody positivity** (especially anti‑SRP) is associated with more severe disease, higher relapse rates, and may prompt early use of steroid‑sparing agents (e.g., rituximab, methotrexate).  
   - **Seronegative status**, as in this patient, allows clinicians to **opt for a limited immunosuppressive trial** (high‑dose steroids ± IVIG) and monitor closely for relapse, rather than committing to long‑term agents from the outset.

4. **Future Monitoring**  
   - Although the current panel is negative, **repeat serology** can be considered if clinical relapse occurs, because low‑titer antibodies may emerge later.  
   - Emerging next‑generation assays (e.g., line blot arrays, immunoprecipitation with radiolabeled antigens) may uncover novel antibodies not captured in standard panels.

In this case, the absence of anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP reinforced the interpretation that the IMNM was **drug‑induced and seronegative**, guiding a time‑limited immunosuppressive strategy and emphasizing the importance of drug cessation (semaglutide) in achieving durable remission.

### [Management] Outline the recommended initial immunosuppressive regimen for severe IMNM and explain why intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was added in this patient’s management.

**Initial immunosuppressive regimen for severe immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM)**  

1. **High‑dose glucocorticoids** – First‑line therapy is pulse intravenous methyl‑prednisolone (≈1 g daily) for 3–5 days, followed by oral prednisone 1 mg/kg/day (or equivalent) with a rapid taper once clinical response and creatine‑kinase (CK) trends are favorable.  
2. **Early steroid‑sparing agents** – In practice, a second‑line agent (methotrexate, azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil, or tacrolimus) is added within the first few weeks to permit earlier taper of steroids and to reduce long‑term glucocorticoid toxicity. The choice is guided by comorbidities, patient age, and tolerability.  

These steps constitute the “standard” initial regimen for severe IMNM, which is characterised by rapid CK rise, profound proximal weakness, and, often, bulbar involvement (dysphagia/dysphonia). Early aggressive immunosuppression is essential because untreated disease can lead to irreversible muscle fiber loss and persistent disability.

**Why intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was added in this patient**  

- **Persistent, severe weakness despite steroids:** After 5 days of 1 g/day methyl‑prednisolone the patient still had marked proximal weakness and bulbar dysfunction. In IMNM, steroid monotherapy often yields a delayed kinetic; persistent functional deficit after the pulse phase is an indication for adjunct therapy.  
- **High CK burden and rapid rise:** CK peaked at ≈4 000 U/L, reflecting active necrosis. IVIG has demonstrated rapid CK reduction in antibody‑mediated necrotising myopathies, likely via Fc‑receptor blockade, complement inhibition, and neutralisation of pathogenic autoantibodies (even when seronegative, as in this case).  
- **Seronegative disease with a potential drug‑trigger:** The patient was negative for the classic IMNM antibodies (anti‑HMGCR, anti‑SRP). In drug‑associated or seronegative IMNM, clinical experience suggests a higher response rate to IVIG than to steroids alone, probably because the pathogenic immune effector mechanism is more humoral than cellular.  
- **Safety and rapid onset:** IVIG can be administered over 4 days (2 g/kg total) and often produces clinical improvement within 1–2 weeks, as was observed (CK fell markedly, dysphagia improved). This was especially valuable while the patient was recovering from a recent septic shock and needed a temporising measure that did not further immunosuppress her.  

**Take‑away for trainees:**  
- Start severe IMNM with high‑dose intravenous glucocorticoids, then transition to oral prednisone and add a steroid‑sparing agent early.  
- If weakness remains pronounced after the initial steroid pulse, or if CK is markedly elevated, add IVIG (2 g/kg divided over 4–5 days).  
- In seronegative or drug‑triggered IMNM, IVIG can be a decisive adjunct that shortens the course of high‑dose steroids and facilitates early taper, as illustrated in the semaglutide‑associated case where a single course of steroids + IVIG led to full functional recovery and allowed cessation of immunosuppression within months.

### [Pathophysiology] Describe the key histopathologic findings on muscle biopsy that confirm a diagnosis of IMNM and differentiate it from other inflammatory myopathies.

**Key Histopathologic Features that Confirm Immune‑Mediated Necrotising Myopathy (IMNM) and Distinguish It From Other Inflammatory Myopathies**

1. **Predominant Myofibre Necrosis with Regeneration**  
   - *What you see*: Scattered, often confluent necrotic fibers admixed with numerous basophilic, newly‑regenerating fibers (central nuclei, enlarged size, basophilic cytoplasm).  
   - *Why it matters*: In IMNM necrosis is the dominant process; the inflammatory infiltrate is minimal. This contrasts with polymyositis (PM) and inclusion‑body myositis (IBM), where end‑omysial or perimysial T‑cell infiltrates are prominent and necrosis is less extensive.

2. **Sparse Lymphocytic Infiltrate**  
   - *What you see*: Occasional CD3⁺ T‑cells, usually perivascular or in the endomysium, but overall low density (often <10 cells per high‑power field).  
   - *Why it matters*: PM and dermatomyositis (DM) show dense CD8⁺ (PM) or CD4⁺/B‑cell (DM) infiltrates that invade non‑necrotic fibers. The paucity of lymphocytes is a hallmark of IMNM.

3. **Prominent Macrophage‑Dominated Phagocytosis (CD68⁺)**  
   - *What you see*: Numerous CD68⁺ macrophages surrounding and engulfing necrotic fibers (myophagocytosis). Acid phosphatase staining highlights these active phagocytes.  
   - *Why it matters*: Macrophage‑mediated clearance is a characteristic response in IMNM. In DM, macrophages are present but usually perivascular; in PM they are fewer and confined to sites of fiber injury.

4. **Membrane Attack Complex (MAC, C5b‑9) Deposition on Sarcolemma**  
   - *What you see*: Immunohistochemistry shows linear or granular C5b‑9 staining on both necrotic and non‑necrotic fibers.  
   - *Why it matters*: Complement‑mediated membrane injury is a pathogenetic driver in IMNM (especially HMGCR‑positive disease). MAC deposition is less extensive in PM/DM and absent in purely toxic rhabdomyolysis.

5. **Absence of Perifascicular Atrophy and Perimysial Inflammation**  
   - *What you see*: No perifascicular fiber atrophy, no perimysial fibrosis or inflammatory cuffs.  
   - *Why it matters*: These findings are typical of DM (perifascicular atrophy) and to a lesser extent PM (perimysial infiltrates). Their absence supports IMNM.

6. **No Rimmed Vacuoles or Intranuclear Inclusions**  
   - *What you see*: No rimmed vacuoles, β‑amyloid–positive inclusions, or p62 aggregates.  
   - *Why it matters*: These are hallmarks of IBM and are not seen in IMNM.

---

### How These Findings Were Illustrated in the Reported Case

- **Biopsy of the right vastus lateralis** showed **dispersed necrotic and regenerating fibers** (Fig 3A‑B).  
- **Acid phosphatase and CD68 immunostains** highlighted **macrophage infiltrates actively phagocytosing necrotic fibers** (Fig 3D, Fig 4B).  
- **C5b‑9 (MAC) staining** was positive on both necrotic and intact fibers, confirming **complement‑mediated injury** (Fig 4C).  
- **CD3⁺ T‑cells** were **rare** and largely perivascular (Fig 4A), demonstrating the **paucity of lymphocytic infiltrate**.  
- **No perifascicular atrophy, perimysial inflammation, rimmed vacuoles, or inclusion bodies** were reported, effectively **ruling out DM, PM, and IBM**.

These combined features satisfy the pathologic criteria for IMNM as delineated in contemporary consensus definitions (European Neuromuscular Centre, 2022). Recognizing this pattern is essential because it directs treatment toward aggressive immunosuppression (high‑dose steroids ± IVIG) rather than supportive care alone, which would be appropriate for a purely toxic rhabdomyolysis picture.

### [Morning Report] What cardiac complications can accompany IMNM, and how were the patient’s cardiac MRI and biomarkers interpreted in this context?

**Cardiac involvement in immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM)**  

IMNM is a systemic inflammatory myopathy and, like the other idiopathic inflammatory myopathies, can affect the heart. The most frequently reported cardiac manifestations are:

| Manifestation | Typical clinical / imaging features |
|---------------|--------------------------------------|
| **Myocarditis / myocardial inflammation** | Chest pain, dyspnoea, arrhythmias; cardiac MRI shows myocardial oedema, T2‑weighted hyperintensity, and often late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) reflecting necrosis or fibrosis. |
| **Arrhythmias** | A‑fib, ventricular ectopy, heart‑block; may be the first clue to cardiac disease. |
| **Heart‑failure phenotype** | Reduced or, less commonly, preserved LVEF; may be secondary to myocarditis or to a stress‑related cardiomyopathy (“takotsubo‑type” pattern). |
| **Conduction disease / pericardial effusion** | Less common but described in case series of necrotising myopathies. |
| **Coronary‑artery‐spasm–like ischemia** | Usually excluded by normal coronary angiography; considered part of the inflammatory milieu. |

The prevalence of cardiac disease in IMNM is not precisely known because most series are small, but cardiac MRI (CMR) detects subclinical involvement in up to 30 % of patients with other inflammatory myopathies, and a similar frequency is plausible in IMNM.

---

### How the cardiac MRI and biomarkers were interpreted in this patient

**1. Biomarkers (hs‑troponin and pro‑BNP)**  

* **High‑sensitivity troponin I 62 pg/mL (ULN ≤ 53 pg/mL).**  
  - A mild elevation in an otherwise stable patient raises suspicion for myocardial injury, but the value is below the typical range seen in acute myocarditis (often several‑fold above ULN).  
  - In the context of IMNM, low‑grade troponin rises are common and may reflect subtle myocyte inflammation rather than frank infarction.

* **Pro‑BNP 4 358 pg/mL (ULN ≤ 125 pg/mL).**  
  - Markedly elevated, consistent with increased ventricular wall stress.  In this case the elevation could be multifactorial: acute renal dysfunction on admission, volume overload from the initial dehydration episode, and possible early systolic dysfunction.

**Interpretation:** The biomarker profile suggested *myocardial involvement* but was not specific for active myocarditis. The modest troponin rise argued against a large infarct, while the high BNP supported a transient reduction in systolic performance or heightened filling pressures.

**2. Cardiac‑magnetic‑resonance (CMR) findings**  

* **Mild hypokinesis of the mid‑anteroseptal and anterior walls** – a regional wall‑motion abnormality that does not follow a coronary‑artery distribution, typical of inflammatory or stress‑related injury.  

* **Myocardial oedema on T2‑weighted images** – indicates active inflammation or edema.  

* **Absence of definitive late gadolinium enhancement (LGE).**  
  - LGE represents irreversible injury (necrosis/fibrosis). Its absence suggests that the inflammation was early and potentially reversible.

* **No significant scarring, infarction, or valvular disease** – rules out chronic ischemic heart disease as the cause of the wall‑motion defect.

**Interpretation within the IMNM framework:**  
The CMR pattern—regional hypokinesis + edema without LGE—is classic for *early myocarditis* or for a *stress‑induced (catecholamine‑mediated) cardiomyopathy* that can accompany systemic inflammatory states. In IMNM, the most plausible explanation is myocarditis secondary to the same immune process that damages skeletal muscle. Because LGE was lacking, the authors concluded that the changes were *potentially reversible* and not yet fibrotic.

**3. Clinical synthesis**

* The patient’s **paroxysmal atrial fibrillation** during the initial hospitalization could be a manifestation of atrial inflammation or a stress trigger; its resolution with clinical improvement supports a transient inflammatory etiology.  

* Follow‑up imaging showed *normalisation of both wall motion and the absence of edema*, confirming that the cardiac abnormality was *reversible* and likely related to the acute inflammatory phase of IMNM rather than an independent cardiomyopathy.

---

### Take‑home points for residents

1. **Cardiac involvement should be actively sought in IMNM**—any new dyspnoea, chest pain, or arrhythmia warrants ECG, troponin, BNP and, when available, CMR.  
2. **CMR is the preferred modality**: T2‑weighted edema identifies active inflammation; LGE differentiates reversible myocarditis from permanent scar.  
3. **Mild troponin elevation with marked BNP** in this setting points toward *myocardial inflammation with transient systolic dysfunction* rather than coronary ischemia.  
4. **Reversibility is common** when immunosuppression and removal of the trigger (here, semaglutide) are instituted promptly, as demonstrated by the patient’s normalization of MRI findings and biomarkers on follow‑up.  

In summary, the case illustrates that IMNM can present with *myocarditis‑like* cardiac changes—regional wall‑motion abnormality, myocardial edema, and modest troponin rise—yet these findings can be transient and improve with appropriate therapy and removal of the offending agent.

### [Clinical Pearls] Discuss the role of GLP‑1‑receptor agonists in muscle metabolism. Why might semaglutide precipitate muscle injury, and how does this differ from the mechanism of statin‑associated HMGCR‑positive IMNM?

**Clinical Pearls – GLP‑1‑receptor agonists and muscle injury**

**1. GLP‑1‑receptor agonists (GLP‑1 RAs) and skeletal‑muscle metabolism**  
- GLP‑1 receptors are expressed on myocytes and satellite cells. Activation stimulates cyclic‑AMP/PKA pathways that favor **protein synthesis** and inhibits catabolic signaling (e.g., FoxO‑mediated ubiquitin‑proteasome activity). In animal studies, chronic GLP‑1 RA exposure modestly preserves lean mass during caloric restriction.  
- In humans, the most reproducible adverse signal is **loss of lean body mass** with rapid weight loss, especially when calorie intake is aggressively curtailed. The mechanism appears to be an indirect effect of **negative energy balance**, not a direct myotoxicity.  

**2. How semaglutide can precipitate immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM)**  
- The case of a 60‑year‑old woman illustrates a **temporal association**: a single 0.25 mg dose of semaglutide → acute nausea/vomiting → hospitalization, modest CK rise (337 U/L), then a **progressive, sub‑acute rise in CK (up to 4 000 U/L) and proximal weakness over 4 months**.  
- Muscle biopsy showed necrotic/regenerating fibers with **macrophage infiltration (CD68^+), complement (MAC) deposition, and sparse CD3^+ T cells** – the histologic hallmark of IMNM. Autoantibody panels (HMGCR, SRP, Jo‑1, etc.) were negative, placing her in the seronegative IMNM group.  
- The most plausible pathogenesis is **drug‑triggered loss of tolerance**. GLP‑1 RAs can modulate immune function (they are expressed on T‑cells and dendritic cells and can bias cytokine production). In a predisposed host (e.g., underlying sub‑clinical autoimmunity, hepatic steatosis, prior inflammatory stress), a single exposure may have acted as a **hapten or adjuvant**, exposing muscle antigens to the immune system and initiating a complement‑mediated necrotising process.  

**3. Contrast with statin‑associated HMGCR‑positive IMNM**  
| Feature | GLP‑1 RA‑associated IMNM (case) | Statin‑associated HMGCR‑positive IMNM |
|--------|--------------------------------|--------------------------------------|
| **Trigger** | Single semaglutide dose; immune modulation rather than direct enzyme inhibition | Chronic statin exposure → up‑regulation of HMG‑CoA reductase in regenerating muscle |
| **Autoantibody** | Negative for HMGCR, SRP; seronegative IMNM | Positive anti‑HMGCR (high‑titer) |
| **Onset** | Delayed (weeks‑months) after exposure; sub‑acute progressive weakness | Often after months of statin use; can present after discontinuation |
| **CK trajectory** | Gradual rise, peaks months later; normalizes after drug withdrawal + short immunosuppression | Very high CK (often >10 000 U/L) at presentation; may stay elevated despite statin cessation, requiring prolonged immunosuppression |
| **Response to therapy** | Short course of steroids + IVIG led to sustained remission; immunosuppression tapered rapidly | Typically needs long‑term high‑dose steroids, steroid‑sparing agents (azathioprine, methotrexate, rituximab) for months to years |
| **Pathophysiology** | Likely immune dysregulation secondary to GLP‑1 receptor signaling on immune cells → complement‑mediated necrosis | Autoantibody (anti‑HMGCR) formation against over‑expressed enzyme → complement activation and necrosis |

**4. Practical take‑aways for the bedside**  
- **When a patient on a GLP‑1 RA develops unexplained proximal weakness or CK rise, consider IMNM**—especially if CK remains elevated weeks after drug discontinuation and the pattern is not typical for pure rhabdomyolysis (i.e., CK does not fall rapidly with hydration).  
- **Obtain a thorough medication history**, including the date of the first GLP‑1 RA dose, and screen for autoantibodies (HMGCR, SRP, myositis panel). A negative panel does not exclude IMNM; biopsy is the gold standard.  
- **Management** mirrors other drug‑induced IMNMs: stop the offending agent, initiate high‑dose corticosteroids, and consider IVIG early in severe cases. Because the immune trigger is removed, many patients (as in this case) can be weaned off steroids within weeks to months, unlike HMGCR‑positive disease that often requires chronic immunosuppression.  

**Bottom line:** GLP‑1 RAs are generally safe, but they can act as immune triggers leading to seronegative IMNM, a presentation distinct from the classic statin‑related, HMGCR‑positive necrotising myopathy. Recognizing the pattern—sub‑acute CK rise, proximal weakness, biopsy‑proven necrosis, and rapid remission after drug withdrawal and short‑term immunosuppression—allows timely treatment and prevents unnecessary prolonged immunosuppression.

**Morning Report Pearls – Working up Unexplained CK Elevation + Proximal Weakness to Diagnose Immune‑Mediated Necrotising Myopathy (IMNM)**

| Test | Interpretation in IMNM |
|------|------------------------|
| **CK (total)** | Markedly elevated (often > 5 × ULN). In our patient CK peaked at 3 992 U/L. |
| **Aldolase** | Often parallels CK; useful if hemolysis or myocardial injury confounds CK. |
| **AST/ALT** | May be modestly raised due to muscle leakage; differentiate from liver disease (check GGT, ALP). |
| **CBC, CMP, ESR/CRP** | Rule out systemic inflammation or metabolic derangements; ESR/CRP modestly ↑ (22 mm/hr, 22.8 mg/L) in the case. |
| **Renal function & urinalysis** | Exclude rhabdomyolysis‑induced AKI; the case had transient AKI with modest CK rise initially. |
| **Serum myoglobin** (optional) | Helpful if rhabdomyolysis suspected. |

**Key point:** A CK that continues to rise weeks after drug exposure, rather than falling rapidly, raises suspicion for an immune‑mediated process.

### 3. Autoantibody Serology – Myositis Panel  
Order a **comprehensive myositis antibody panel** (e.g., line‑immunoassay or multiplex bead assay) that includes:

- **Anti‑HMGCR** (strongly linked to statin‑associated IMNM)  
- **Anti‑SRP** (classic IMNM)  
- **Anti‑Mi‑2, Jo‑1, PL‑7, PL‑12, EJ, OJ, MDA‑5, TIF1‑γ, NXP‑2, cN‑1A** (other myositis specific antibodies)  
- **ANA, ENA, anti‑SSA/SSB** (to capture overlap syndromes)

*Interpretation*: A positive HMGCR or SRP essentially confirms IMNM. **Negative antibodies do not exclude IMNM**—≈ 30 % are seronegative, as seen in the case (all autoantibodies negative). Persistently negative serology should prompt early muscle imaging/biopsy.

### 4. Muscle Imaging  
**MRI of the symptomatic region** (often thighs or pelvis) performed with STIR or fat‑suppressed T2 sequences.

- **Findings suggestive of IMNM**: Diffuse, symmetric, proximal muscle edema without focal fatty infiltration; modest subcutaneous edema may be present.  
- **Utility**: Guides biopsy site (most inflamed, still viable tissue) and helps rule out structural lesions (e.g., tumors, compartment syndrome).  

In the reported patient, MRI showed symmetric edema of the vastus lateralis and quadriceps, corroborating a proximal inflammatory process.

### 5. Exclusion of Mimics  
Before committing to a biopsy, eliminate common alternatives:

| Condition | Quick “rule‑out” tests |
|-----------|------------------------|
| **Statin‑ or drug‑induced toxic myopathy** | Review medication list, see if CK normalizes after drug withdrawal (usually within 2–4 weeks). |
| **Rhabdomyolysis** | CK > 10 000 U/L, myoglobinuria, acute kidney injury – CK falls rapidly with hydration. |
| **Endocrine myopathies** (hypothyroidism, Cushing) | TSH, cortisol, ACTH. |
| **Metabolic myopathies** (glycogen storage, fatty acid oxidation) | Resting CK modest, exercise‑induced spikes; consider forearm ischemic test or genetics. |
| **Neurological disorders** (motor neuron disease, neuropathy) | EMG/NCS – shows neurogenic pattern vs. myopathic. |
| **Infectious myositis** | Viral serologies (HIV, HTLV, hepatitis), ESR/CRP markedly high, fever. |

In the case, thyroid function was normal, viral serologies were negative, and the CK trajectory did not fit a simple toxic rhabdomyolysis course.

### 6. Electromyography (EMG)  
**Optional but helpful** when diagnosis remains uncertain.

- **Myopathic pattern**: Small, short‑duration, polyphasic motor units, early recruitment.  
- **Fibrillation potentials and positive sharp waves** may be present in active necrotising disease.  

A myopathic EMG supports an inflammatory myopathy and can help exclude neurogenic disorders.

### 7. Muscle Biopsy – The Definitive Test  
**Indications**: Persistent CK elevation > 5 × ULN, proximal weakness, negative serology, or need to differentiate IMNM from other inflammatory myopathies.

**Technical pearls**:

1. **Site selection** – Guided by MRI (most edematous yet non‑fat‑replaced muscle).  
2. **Specimen handling** – Obtain both frozen sections (for enzyme studies) and formalin‑fixed paraffin‑embedded tissue (for immunohistochemistry).  
3. **Core findings in IMNM**:  
   - **Scattered necrotic and regenerating fibers** with abundant macrophages (CD68⁺).  
   - **Sparse CD3⁺ T‑cells**, often perivascular.  
   - **Membrane attack complex (C5b‑9) deposition** on sarcolemma of necrotic and non‑necrotic fibers.  
   - **Minimal endomysial inflammatory infiltrate** (distinguishes from polymyositis).  

The patient's thigh biopsy showed exactly those features—necrosis with macrophage‑dominant infiltrate, MAC deposition, and only rare T‑cells—establishing the diagnosis of IMNM.

### 8. Ancillary Cardiac Evaluation (if indicated)  
Because IMNM can be associated with myocardial inflammation (as in the case’s mild cardiac MRI changes), a **baseline ECG, troponin, and echocardiogram** are reasonable. Persistent cardiac involvement may dictate additional immunosuppression.

---

## Putting It All Together – Practical Algorithm

1. **Proximal weakness + CK↑** → Basic labs + medication review.  
2. **CK > 5 × ULN, progressive, no rapid fall after drug stop** → Order comprehensive myositis antibody panel.  
3. **If antibodies positive** → Diagnose specific IMNM (HMGCR or SRP) → start immunosuppression.  
4. **If antibodies negative** → Obtain MRI of affected muscle → target biopsy.  
5. **Biopsy confirms necrosis with macrophage predominance & MAC** → Diagnosis of seronegative IMNM.  
6. **Initiate therapy** (high‑dose steroids ± IVIG; consider steroid‑sparing agents if refractory).  
7. **Follow CK, strength, and repeat imaging/clinical assessment** to gauge response.

**Take‑home message for the team:**  
- **Never rely on CK alone**; a rising CK over weeks despite drug withdrawal is a red flag for autoimmune necrotising myopathy.  
- **Autoantibody panels are essential but not definitive**; up to one‑third of IMNM patients are seronegative, as illustrated.  
- **MRI guides the biopsy**

### [Differential Diagnosis] Given the patient’s prior history of NAFLD and elevated transaminases, how can clinicians differentiate hepatic versus muscular sources of AST/ALT abnormalities?

**Answer – How to Tell Hepatic from Muscular AST/ALT Elevation in a Patient With NAFLD Who Later Develops Myopathy**

When a patient with known non‑alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) presents with a rise in aminotransferases, the first instinct is to attribute the abnormality to the liver. However, AST and ALT are not liver‑specific; skeletal muscle injury can raise both enzymes, and in the setting of an immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) they may be misleading. The case of the 60‑year‑old woman who developed IMNM after a single dose of semaglutide illustrates the systematic approach we use to separate hepatic from muscular sources.

---

### 1.  Look at the Pattern of Enzyme Elevation

| Feature | Suggests Liver | Suggests Muscle |
|---------|----------------|-----------------|
| **AST : ALT ratio** | >1 (AST predominates) | Often ≤1 (ALT may be equal or higher) |
| **Magnitude** | Mild‑moderate (≤5 × ULN) in NAFLD; >5 × ULN raises concern for acute injury | CK‑driven rises can push AST/ALT into the hundreds‑to‑thousands, but usually proportional to CK |
| **Concurrent enzymes** | ↑Alkaline phosphatase, GGT, bilirubin in cholestasis; synthetic dysfunction (↓albumin, ↑PT/INR) in severe hepatitis | Normal bilirubin, ALP, GGT; isolated transaminase rise |

In the case, the patient’s chronic NAFLD had produced a **steady, mild 2‑fold elevation of ALT > AST** with normal bilirubin, ALP and synthetic function—classic for steatohepatitis. When she later presented with IMNM, her AST (191 U/L) and ALT (165 U/L) were **only modestly above baseline** despite a CK of 3 992 U/L, suggesting that the bulk of the transaminase rise remained hepatic.

---

### 2.  Correlate With a Muscle‑Specific Marker – Creatine Kinase (CK)

CK is the most sensitive laboratory test for skeletal‑muscle injury.  

* **CK > 5 × ULN** in the presence of any transaminase rise strongly points to a muscular component.  
* **Trend comparison:** In pure liver disease CK stays normal; in myopathy CK mirrors the clinical course, falling rapidly with treatment or muscle recovery.

In the reported patient, CK rose from a low‑normal 337 U/L during the initial semaglutide reaction to **3992 U/L** (≈20 × ULN) four months later, temporally linked to the progression of weakness. The parallel drop of CK to normal after IVIG and steroids confirmed that the muscle injury, not the liver, was driving the enzyme abnormalities at that stage.

---

### 3.  Use the AST/ALT : CK Ratio (the “AST/CK index”)

A practical bedside heuristic is the **AST/CK ratio**:

\[
\text{AST/CK index} = \frac{\text{AST (U/L)}}{\text{CK (U/L)}}
\]

* **< 0.01** → Strongly suggests muscle origin (AST is tiny relative to a huge CK).  
* **≥ 0.03** → More compatible with hepatic injury.

In our patient during the IMNM flare:

\[
\frac{191\;U/L}{3992\;U/L} \approx 0.048
\]

This value is **borderline** but, together with the massive CK elevation and clinical myopathy, supports a **muscle contribution** to the transaminase rise. Conversely, three years earlier, CK was unremarkable and the ratio would have been > 0.1, consistent with liver‑derived AST/ALT.

---

### 4.  Assess Clinical Context and Ancillary Testing

| Clinical clue | Hepatic | Muscular |
|---------------|----------|----------|
| **Symptoms** | Right‑upper‑quadrant pain, jaundice, pruritus | Proximal weakness, myalgias, dysphagia, dark urine |
| **Physical exam** | Hepatomegaly, stigmata of chronic liver disease | Muscle tenderness, atrophy, decreased strength |
| **Imaging** | Ultrasound/CT showing steatosis, fibrosis | MRI showing muscle edema (as in the thigh MRIs) |
| **Liver biopsy** | Steatosis, ballooning, lobular inflammation | Normal hepatic architecture; muscle biopsy shows necrosis (as obtained) |

The case report demonstrated **macrovesicular steatosis on liver biopsy** (unchanged from prior) and **muscle biopsy with necrotising fibers and CD68+ macrophages**, confirming two distinct disease processes.

---

### 5.  Practical Algorithm for the Bedside

1. **Identify the presence of muscle symptoms** (weakness, dysphagia, myalgias).  
2. **Order CK** (and possibly aldolase). If CK is normal → hepatic source is most likely.  
3. **Calculate AST/CK index**; a low index points to muscle.  
4. **Review other liver tests** (bilirubin, ALP, GGT, INR). Isolated AST/ALT rise with normal others leans muscle.  
5. **Repeat labs after addressing the obvious cause** (e.g., stop offending drug, hydrate). A rapid fall in CK with persistent ALT suggests residual liver disease; parallel fall in both points to a single source.  
6. **Consider imaging/biopsy** if the diagnosis remains ambiguous.

---

### Bottom Line for This Patient

- **Baseline elevation** of ALT > AST (≈2 × ULN) with normal CK and liver‑specific abnormalities → **NAFLD‑related hepatic injury**.  
- **New, marked CK rise** to > 20 × ULN, concurrent proximal weakness, and MRI/biopsy evidence of necrotising myopathy → **muscle‑derived contribution** to AST/ALT.  
- **Persistent mild ALT/AST after CK normalises** reflects the underlying steatohepatitis, not ongoing muscle damage.

By systematically correlating enzyme patterns, CK, clinical features, and imaging/biopsy data, clinicians can confidently distinguish hepatic from muscular transaminase elevations, even in complex cases where both processes coexist. This approach prevents misattribution of a flare to the liver, prompts timely recognition of life‑threatening myopathies, and guides appropriate treatment—exactly what was required in the semaglutide‑associated IMNM case.

### [Management] What are the considerations for tapering and discontinuing corticosteroids in drug‑induced IMNM, and why was rapid weaning possible in this case?

**Answer – Tapering and Discontinuing Corticosteroids in Drug‑Induced IMNM**

In immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) the default strategy is prolonged high‑dose glucocorticoid therapy (often 1 mg/kg prednisone) followed by slow tapering over many months, because the underlying autoimmune process tends to persist. However, when the necrotising myopathy is *drug‑induced*—as in the semaglutide case we just discussed—several practical considerations allow a much more rapid withdrawal:

| Consideration | Why it matters in drug‑induced IMNM | How it was applied in the case |
|---------------|--------------------------------------|--------------------------------|
| **Clear temporal relationship and drug withdrawal** | Removal of the inciting antigen eliminates the immune stimulus that perpetuates muscle injury. | The patient stopped semaglutide after the single dose; no further exposure occurred. |
| **Limited auto‑antibody repertoire** | Seronegative disease (negative HMGCR, SRP, Jo‑1, etc.) argues against an entrenched, auto‑antibody‑driven process that would relapse on taper. | Comprehensive myositis panel was negative; only a low‑titer SS‑B was present, which is not pathogenic in IMNM. |
| **Robust early response to immunomodulation** | A dramatic fall in CK and rapid clinical recovery suggest that the inflammatory cascade is still reversible and not self‑sustaining. | CK dropped from >3 900 U/L to normal within 2 weeks after IV methylprednisolone + IVIG; strength returned to baseline. |
| **Short duration of immunosuppression needed** | In drug‑induced cases, once the trigger is gone, the immune system often “resets” after a brief course of steroids/IVIG, obviating the need for maintenance therapy. | Only one pulse steroid course (5 days) followed by oral prednisone 1 mg/kg; IVIG was given once (2 g/kg). |
| **Absence of ongoing organ involvement** | Persistent cardiac, pulmonary or cutaneous disease would mandate slower taper; here those systems normalized (echo, cardiac MRI, CT). | Cardiac MRI abnormalities resolved; no interstitial lung disease; no rash or synovitis. |
| **Risk‑benefit balance** | Prolonged high‑dose steroids carry considerable adverse effects (infection, hyperglycaemia, bone loss). When the disease appears self‑limited, the harms of a long taper outweigh the benefits. | The patient experienced a severe septic shock unrelated to steroids after discharge; limiting steroid exposure was prudent. |
| **Monitoring of CK and functional status** | CK is a reliable surrogate for disease activity in IMNM; stable, normal CK permits a faster taper. | Serial CK measurements remained normal after the initial decline, guiding the 2‑month taper. |

**Why rapid weaning was possible in this patient**

1. **Drug‑related trigger removed:** Semaglutide was given only once and then discontinued. Without ongoing antigenic stimulation, the immune response could wane quickly.
2. **Seronegative, non‑persistent autoimmunity:** The lack of HMGCR or SRP antibodies (and the absence of other myositis‐specific antibodies) suggested a transient, drug‑mediated process rather than chronic idiopathic IMNM, which typically requires months of immunosuppression.
3. **Excellent early biochemical and clinical response:** CK fell from nearly 4 000 U/L to the normal range within 2 weeks; the patient regained the ability to stand, sit and swallow, indicating that muscle necrosis had been halted.
4. **No evidence of residual systemic inflammation:** Inflammatory markers (CRP, ESR) normalized, cardiac imaging cleared, and there was no interstitial lung disease—features that usually mandate a slower taper.
5. **Safety concerns:** The patient subsequently suffered a severe pseudomonal urinary‑tract infection; minimizing steroid exposure reduced the risk of further infections.

**Practical take‑home points for clinicians**

- **Confirm drug causality** (temporal association, exclusion of other triggers, improvement on withdrawal) before planning a short taper.
- **Obtain a baseline and serial CK**; a sustained normalization is the most objective gauge that the disease is quiescent.
- **Use a brief pulse of IV methylprednisolone** (e.g., 1 g/day × 3–5 days) followed by a moderate oral dose (≈1 mg/kg) for 2–4 weeks, then taper **by 10–20 % per week** if CK remains normal and strength is stable.
- **Add a single course of IVIG** when weakness is severe, as it can accelerate recovery and may reduce the required steroid dose.
- **Monitor for relapse** during and after taper (CK, strength, dysphagia); any rise should prompt a slower taper or restart of immunotherapy.
- **Educate the patient** to avoid the offending agent (all GLP‑1 receptor agonists in this case) and to report any recurrent muscle symptoms promptly.

In summary, drug‑induced IMNM behaves like a “self‑limited” autoimmune process once the offending medication is removed. The combination of seronegativity, a brisk CK response, and lack of ongoing systemic disease permits a rapid corticosteroid taper—often within 2 months—as demonstrated in this semaglutide‑associated case.
