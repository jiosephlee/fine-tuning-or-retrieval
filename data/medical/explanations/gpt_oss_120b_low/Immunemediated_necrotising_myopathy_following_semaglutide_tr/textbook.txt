Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Evaluation  

The patient was a woman in her early sixties with a background of several chronic disorders that are commonplace in a metabolic‐focused practice. She had longstanding obesity (body mass index >30 kg/m²) with non‑alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) that had progressed to steatohepatitis, essential hypertension, hypothyroidism treated with levothyroxine, and a remote episode of deep‑venous thrombosis/pulmonary embolism for which she was no longer anticoagulated. Apart from these conditions she had been otherwise healthy, independent in all activities of daily living, and was not taking statins, herbal supplements, or other agents that are known triggers of immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM).

### Semaglutide exposure and acute reaction  

Approximately 2½ years after her NAFLD diagnosis, given the persistence of hepatic steatosis and excess weight, her primary care physician initiated therapy with the glucagon‑like peptide‑1 (GLP‑1) receptor agonist semaglutide. The first dose (0.25 mg administered subcutaneously) was given in the outpatient setting. Within a few hours she developed nausea, vomiting, and marked anorexia. Over the next 48 h her oral intake remained poor, she complained of dyspnoea and felt generally weak, prompting an emergency department visit.  

On presentation she was volume‑depleted, with orthostatic hypotension and tachypnoea. Laboratory studies revealed an anion‑gap metabolic acidosis (pH 7.28, bicarbonate 14 mmol/L) and an acute rise in serum creatinine to 2.1 mg/dL (baseline 0.9 mg/dL), consistent with pre‑renal acute kidney injury secondary to hypovolaemia. She required intravenous isotonic fluids, bicarbonate infusion for the acidosis, and brief non‑invasive ventilation for respiratory distress. Her renal function improved rapidly with hydration and did not necessitate renal replacement therapy.  

A creatine kinase (CK) level drawn on admission was modestly elevated at 337 U/L (reference 12–191 U/L). This value was not high enough to fulfil the usual criteria for rhabdomyolysis (CK > 5 × upper limit of normal) and she lacked classic features such as myalgias, dark urine, or a sarcolytic rash. Nonetheless, the CK elevation, together with the systemic stress of severe dehydration, raised the possibility of a subclinical muscle injury. She was discharged after five days once nausea resolved, electrolytes normalized, and her renal indices returned to baseline. At discharge she reported feeling deconditioned but denied any focal weakness, dysphagia, or voice changes.

### Evolution of neuromuscular symptoms  

Over the subsequent four months the patient’s functional status deteriorated progressively. She noted increasing difficulty rising from a chair, climbing stairs, and lifting objects—classic proximal limb weakness that interfered with her independence. Approximately three months after the semaglutide exposure she began to experience dysphagia, initially for liquids and later for solids, as well as a hoarse voice (dysphonia). These bulbar symptoms signaled involvement of the oropharyngeal musculature, a pattern frequently observed in inflammatory myopathies but atypical for pure glucotoxic myopathy. She denied any skin rash, joint pain, fever, or recent infections that might suggest a viral myositis. She also reported no new medications, supplements (including red‑yeast rice or mushroom extracts), or strenuous physical activity.

When her progressive weakness prompted referral to a tertiary centre, the neurological examination demonstrated markedly reduced Medical Research Council (MRC) grades in the proximal muscles (e.g., shoulder abduction 3/5, hip flexion 2/5) with relative preservation of distal strength. Reflexes were brisk but symmetric, and there were no sensory deficits. Of note, there were no cutaneous findings such as heliotrope rash, Gottron papules, or mechanic’s hands that would point toward a dermatomyositis spectrum. Cardiopulmonary and abdominal examinations were unremarkable, and there was no peripheral edema or synovitis.

### Initial laboratory assessment  

A comprehensive laboratory panel was obtained on admission to the tertiary facility (Table 1). The most striking abnormality was a CK that had risen dramatically from the modest 337 U/L measured during the initial hospitalisation to 1 237 U/L at the first rheumatology evaluation, peaking at 3 992 U/L one week later—a twelve‑fold increase relative to baseline. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were mildly elevated (AST 191 U/L, ALT 165 U/L); these transaminases are frequently raised in muscle injury but also reflected her known hepatic steatosis. Thyroid‑stimulating hormone was within normal limits, excluding hypothyroid myopathy as a contributor. Basic metabolic parameters (electrolytes, glucose, renal function) were normalized after the initial volume resuscitation. Inflammatory markers were modestly raised (ESR 22 mm/h, CRP 22.8 mg/L), consistent with an active myopathic process.  

Autoimmune serology, including a broad myositis panel, was negative for the classic IMNM‑associated antibodies (anti‑HMGCR, anti‑SRP) and the antisynthetases (Jo‑1, PL‑7, PL‑12, EJ, OJ), as well as for ANA, anti‑SSA, anti‑SSB (the latter was low‑titer positive at 2.4 U, below the diagnostic threshold), anti‑dsDNA, ANCA, and anti‑mitochondrial antibodies. Complement levels were normal, making immune‑complex mediated disease less likely. Cardiac biomarkers showed a mildly elevated high‑sensitivity troponin (62 pg/mL; upper limit ≤ 53 pg/mL) and a markedly raised NT‑proBNP (4 358 pg/mL), prompting cardiac imaging that ultimately revealed only mild, non‑specific myocardial edema without late gadolinium enhancement.  

Collectively, the chronology—an immediate gastrointestinal intolerance to semaglutide, an early modest CK rise, followed by a delayed but pronounced CK surge and subacute proximal muscle weakness with bulbar involvement—prompted the care team to consider an immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy potentially triggered by the GLP‑1 receptor agonist. The absence of a clear drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis pattern, the lack of a statin or other known myotoxic exposure, and the negative autoantibody screen (a recognized feature in roughly one‑third of IMNM cases) further focused the diagnostic work‑up on tissue confirmation via muscle biopsy, which ultimately established the final diagnosis.

## Differential Diagnosis and Diagnostic Work‑up  

The presentation of a previously healthy woman in her 60s with rapidly progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, dysphonia, and marked creatine kinase (CK) elevation forces the clinician to consider a broad spectrum of disorders that can affect the skeletal musculature. A systematic, step‑wise approach—starting with the most common and potentially life‑threatening entities and proceeding to more specific, less frequent causes—helps to focus investigations and avoid premature closure. In this case, the differential diagnosis was narrowed through careful clinical correlation, targeted serologic testing, advanced imaging, and definitive histopathology, ultimately establishing immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) as the working diagnosis.

### 1. Broad Differential for Acute/Sub‑acute Proximal Weakness  

| Category | Representative Conditions | Typical Clues |
|----------|---------------------------|--------------|
| **Toxic/Drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis** | Statins, fibrates, illicit drugs, severe exertion, electrolyte disturbances | Sudden CK >10 000 U/L, myoglobinuria, dark urine, renal failure, rapid CK decline after removal of offending agent |
| **Inflammatory myopathies** | Polymyositis, dermatomyositis, inclusion‑body myositis, immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy | Sub‑acute onset, proximal > distal weakness, elevated CK (often 1–5 × ULN), inflammatory infiltrates on biopsy, autoantibodies (e.g., anti‑Jo‑1, anti‑SRP, anti‑HMGCR) |
| **Metabolic myopathies** | Glycogen storage disease, mitochondrial myopathy, fatty acid oxidation defects | Exercise‑induced weakness, lactate rise, episodic CK spikes, often childhood or early adulthood |
| **Endocrine disorders** | Hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism, adrenal insufficiency, Cushing’s syndrome | Abnormal thyroid labs, systemic signs (weight change, skin changes), modest CK rise |
| **Neuromuscular junction disorders** | Myasthenia gravis, Lambert‑Eaton syndrome | Fatigable weakness, ocular involvement, abnormal electrophysiology, antibodies (AChR, VGCC) |
| **Structural/Genetic myopathies** | Limb‑girdle muscular dystrophies, facioscapulohumeral dystrophy | Insidious onset, family history, dystrophic changes on MRI/biopsy, CK often chronically elevated |

### 2. Features Argue Against Pure Rhabdomyolysis  

- **CK Kinetics**: The patient’s CK rose from a modest 337 U/L during the initial semaglutide reaction to a peak of 3 992 U/L four months later. Pure rhabdomyolysis typically produces an abrupt CK surge (often >10 000 U/L) within 24–48 h of muscle injury, followed by a rapid decline with hydration. The delayed and progressive rise is inconsistent with a single toxic insult.  
- **Absence of Myoglobinuria**: No cola‑coloured urine or documented urine myoglobin was reported, diminishing the likelihood of massive muscle breakdown.  
- **Renal Failure Mechanism**: The initial acute kidney injury was attributed to hypovolaemia from vomiting, not to myoglobin‑induced tubular obstruction.  
- **Persistent Weakness**: In rhabdomyolysis, weakness resolves as CK normalises; here, weakness progressed despite CK initially being only modestly elevated, indicating an ongoing pathogenic process rather than a one‑time toxin effect.

### 3. Rationale for an Auto‑immune Myopathy Work‑up  

Even in the absence of a classic rash or positive antinuclear antibody (ANA), the constellation of proximal weakness, dysphagia, dysphonia, and sustained CK elevation mandated evaluation for an inflammatory myopathy. The lack of a clear alternative explanation (no recent strenuous activity, no metabolic derangements, normal thyroid studies) increased suspicion for an immune‑mediated process. Moreover, the temporal association with a novel drug (semaglutide) raised the possibility of a drug‑triggered autoimmune reaction, akin to statin‑associated HMGCR‑positive IMNM.

### 4. Serologic Testing: Myositis‑specific Antibodies  

A comprehensive myositis panel was ordered, including:

- **Anti‑HMGCR** (highly associated with statin‑related IMNM) – negative.  
- **Anti‑SRP** – negative.  
- **Anti‑Jo‑1, PL‑7, PL‑12, EJ, OJ, Mi‑2, MDA‑5, TIF1‑γ, NXP‑2, cN‑1A** – all negative.  
- **Extractable nuclear antigens** (SSA/Ro, SSB/La, Sm, RNP) – negative except for a low‑titer isolated SSB (2.4 U; normal <1.0).  

The absence of detectable myositis‑specific antibodies does not exclude IMNM. Approximately one‑third of IMNM patients are seronegative, and diagnosis then hinges on clinical features and muscle biopsy. The negative panel, coupled with the lack of systemic connective‑tissue disease features, reinforced the need for tissue confirmation rather than a diagnosis of a classic dermatomyositis or polymyositis.

### 5. Imaging Studies  

- **MRI of the Thighs**: T2‑weighted fat‑suppressed images demonstrated symmetric, mild to moderate edema of the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps, with subcutaneous edema of the lateral thighs. Muscle edema on MRI is a sensitive, non‑invasive marker of active myositis and helped localize the most affected muscle groups for biopsy.  
- **Cardiac Evaluation**: Because IMNM can co‑occur with myocarditis, an echocardiogram (LVEF 65‑70 %, RV systolic pressure 46 mm Hg) and cardiac MRI were performed. The cardiac MRI showed subtle mid‑anteroseptal hypokinesis, myocardial edema, and no late gadolinium enhancement—findings compatible with transient inflammatory injury rather than irreversible scar. Elevated high‑sensitivity troponin (62 pg/mL) and markedly increased pro‑BNP (4 358 pg/mL) were noted, supporting transient myocardial stress. These studies excluded an overt infarct‑type process while documenting mild cardiac involvement that could be part of a systemic inflammatory response.  

### 6. Muscle Biopsy: Defining Features of IMNM  

A core needle biopsy of the right vastus lateralis provided the decisive evidence:

1. **Necrotic and Regenerating Fibers** – Scattered necrotic fibers with adjacent basophilic regeneration, the hallmark of necrotising myopathy.  
2. **Macrophage Infiltrate** – Immunostaining for CD68 highlighted abundant macrophages engaged in myophagocytosis, reflecting an innate immune response.  
3. **Membrane Attack Complex (C5b‑9) Deposition** – Positive staining on both necrotic and intact fibers demonstrates complement‑mediated injury, a characteristic finding in IMNM.  
4. **Sparse T‑cell Presence** – Few CD3‑positive cells were seen, consistent with the “paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates” that distinguishes IMNM from polymyositis, where dense endomysial CD8⁺ T‑cell infiltrates predominate.  
5. **Perivascular Inflammation** – Minimal perivascular cuffs further support a primarily humoral/innate immune mechanism.  

These histopathologic criteria satisfy the European Neuromuscular Centre (ENMC) definition of IMNM: necrotic fibers with macrophage predominance, complement deposition, and limited lymphocytes, in the appropriate clinical context.

### 7. Exclusion of Multisystem Involvement  

Given the patient’s pre‑existing non‑alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and the potential for systemic drug toxicity, additional organ assessments were undertaken:

- **Liver Biopsy**: Showed macrovesicular steatosis (≈70 % of parenchyma) without significant inflammation or fibrosis, confirming chronic NAFLD rather than drug‑induced hepatitis or myopathy‑related transaminitis.  
- **High‑Resolution Chest CT**: No interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy, thereby excluding an interstitial pneumonitis that can accompany antisynthetase syndrome.  
- **Pulmonary Evaluation**: Normal auscultation and oxygenation; no dyspnoea beyond the initial hospitalization.  

Collectively, these investigations ruled out alternative causes of the elevated transaminases, cardiac biomarkers, and systemic symptoms, reinforcing the muscle disease as the primary pathology.

### 8. Integrating the Data  

The diagnostic algorithm proceeded as follows:

1. **Clinical pattern** → proximal, sub‑acute weakness with bulbar involvement → raises suspicion for inflammatory myopathy.  
2. **Laboratory screen** → CK markedly elevated, ESR/CRP modestly raised, negative ANA, negative myositis‑specific antibodies → points toward seronegative IMNM.  
3. **Imaging** → thigh MRI confirming active muscle edema; cardiac studies identifying only mild, reversible inflammation.  
4. **Biopsy** → necrotising fibers, macrophage predominance, complement deposition, scarce T‑cells → fulfills IMNM diagnostic criteria.  
5. **Exclusion of mimics** → absence of myoglobinuria, delayed CK peak, lack of severe electrolyte or metabolic derangements, negative toxicology → argues against pure rhabdomyolysis or metabolic myopathy.  

The convergence of clinical, serologic, radiologic, and histopathologic data solidified the diagnosis of immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy, most plausibly triggered by the single exposure to semaglutide. This systematic work‑up exemplifies how a broad differential can be narrowed efficiently, ensuring that appropriate immunosuppressive therapy is initiated without missing alternative, treatable mimics.

## Pathophysiology of Immune‑Mediated Necrotising Myopathy and the Role of GLP‑1 Receptor Agonists  

Immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is a distinct clinicopathologic entity that occupies the interface between classic inflammatory myopathies and pure toxic myopathies. Understanding its pathophysiology is essential to appreciate why a drug such as semaglutide—normally prescribed for metabolic disease—might act as a trigger in a susceptible individual.

### Definition of IMNM: clinical phenotype, histologic hallmarks, and serologic subsets  

Patients with IMNM present with sub‑acute to chronic, symmetric proximal weakness that progresses over weeks to months. Dysphagia, dysphonia, and respiratory muscle involvement occur in a minority but signal a severe phenotype. Laboratory testing invariably shows a marked elevation of serum creatine kinase (CK), often in the thousands, reflecting ongoing muscle fibre necrosis.  

Histologically, the disease is characterised by scattered necrotic and regenerating myofibres with minimal end‑stage fibrosis. Unlike polymyositis, the inflammatory infiltrate is sparse; when present it consists chiefly of macrophages (CD68‑positive) and a few CD3‑positive T‑cells confined to perivascular or perimysial regions. A diagnostic hallmark is deposition of the membrane‑attack complex (MAC, C5b‑9) on the sarcolemma of both necrotic and non‑necrotic fibres, indicating that complement activation directly contributes to myofibre injury.  

Serologically, two autoantibodies dominate: anti‑hydroxy‑3‑methylglutaryl‑CoA reductase (anti‑HMGCR) and anti‑signal‑recognition‑particle (anti‑SRP). Approximately two‑thirds of patients are positive for one of these, while a sizable minority are seronegative. The seronegative group—like the patient described—often lacks a clear trigger and may harbour yet‑unidentified autoantibodies.

### Immune mechanisms: complement activation, macrophage‑mediated myophagocytosis, limited T‑cell infiltrate  

The prevailing model integrates innate and adaptive immunity. Binding of an as‑yet‑undefined autoantigen on the myofibre surface (e.g., HMGCR or SRP) by auto‑antibodies leads to classical complement pathway activation. C5b‑9 MAC insertion into the sarcolemma causes sub‑lytic injury that triggers calcium influx, mitochondrial dysfunction, and ultimately necrosis.  

Necrotic fibres release alarmins (e.g., HMGB1, ATP) that attract monocytes. These differentiate into CD68‑positive macrophages which perform myophagocytosis of dead and damaged fibres, a process visualised in the biopsy as acid phosphatase positivity. Macrophages also secrete pro‑inflammatory cytokines (TNF‑α, IL‑1β, IL‑6) that amplify local injury and may recruit the scant T‑cell infiltrate observed. The limited CD3‑positive T‑cells suggest that adaptive cellular immunity plays a secondary, perhaps antigen‑presenting, role rather than being the primary effector.

### Established drug‑associated IMNM: the statin‑HMGCR paradigm  

Statins are the prototypical drugs linked to IMNM. Chronic exposure to HMGCR‑inhibiting agents up‑regulates HMGCR expression in regenerating muscle fibres, creating a neo‑antigen that provokes anti‑HMGCR autoantibody formation. The resulting immune response follows the same complement‑mediated pathway described above, explaining why many statin‑associated cases persist after drug cessation and respond only to immunosuppression.  

Key parallels with the present case are the temporal association between drug exposure and myopathy, the presence of necrotising pathology, and the need for immunosuppressive therapy. Differences include the lack of a known antibody (the patient is seronegative) and the fact that only a single dose of semaglutide preceded the disease, whereas statin‑related IMNM usually follows months of therapy.

### GLP‑1 receptor agonist pharmacology: metabolic actions, gastrointestinal side‑effects, and muscle‑mass reports  

Glucagon‑like peptide‑1 (GLP‑1) receptor agonists (e.g., semaglutide, liraglutide, tirzepatide) mimic the incretin hormone GLP‑1, enhancing glucose‑dependent insulin secretion, suppressing glucagon, delaying gastric emptying, and promoting satiety. Their most frequent adverse effects are nausea, vomiting, and diarrhoea, reflecting the central and peripheral actions on gastrointestinal motility.  

Beyond glycaemic control, GLP‑1 receptors are expressed on skeletal muscle, adipose tissue, and the cardiovascular system. Small observational studies have reported modest reductions in lean body mass with chronic GLP‑1 agonist therapy, possibly secondary to appetite suppression and caloric deficit rather than direct catabolic signaling. However, overt myotoxicity has not been a recognized class effect, and only isolated case reports of rhabdomyolysis (not immune‑mediated) have emerged.

### Potential immunologic pathways linking GLP‑1 agonism to autoimmunity  

The mechanistic bridge between GLP‑1 agonism and IMNM remains speculative, but several plausible immunologic routes can be considered:

1. **Cytokine modulation** – GLP‑1 signaling exerts anti‑inflammatory effects in the vasculature and pancreas, yet in certain contexts it can shift the cytokine milieu toward a Th1‑dominant response, especially when the drug is administered in the setting of acute stress (e.g., severe dehydration and renal failure). Transient rise in IL‑6 or TNF‑α could lower the threshold for auto‑antibody production in a predisposed host.

2. **Molecular mimicry** – The GLP‑1 receptor shares limited peptide homology with proteins expressed on muscle membranes. An immune response initially directed at the exogenous peptide (or associated carrier proteins in the formulation) might cross‑react with native muscle antigens, akin to classical drug‑induced autoimmune phenomena.

3. **Off‑target immune activation** – The semaglutide formulation contains excipients (e.g., phenol, benzyl alcohol) that can act as haptens, binding to muscle proteins and creating neo‑epitopes capable of eliciting auto‑antibody formation. In individuals with pre‑existing subclinical autoimmunity (e.g., low‑titer SS‑B positivity), this could tip the balance toward overt disease.

4. **Altered muscle metabolism** – Acute severe vomiting and resultant hypovolaemia precipitated acute kidney injury in the patient, creating a metabolic milieu of oxidative stress and myocyte injury. Damaged fibres may expose normally sequestered antigens, facilitating auto‑antibody generation and complement activation.

These hypotheses are not mutually exclusive and underscore that a single dose of semaglutide, in the context of profound gastrointestinal toxicity, could have acted as the “second hit” required for clinical IMNM to manifest.

### Evidence from tirzepatide‑related rhabdomyolysis and how the current case diverges  

A recent report described tirzepatide‑associated rhabdomyolysis with CK exceeding 50 000 U/L within days of drug initiation. The CK trajectory was acute, fell rapidly with hydration, and the patient recovered without immunosuppressive therapy. This pattern is typical of a toxic, direct muscle injury—often termed “drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis”—where the offending agent is removed and supportive care suffices.  

In contrast, the present case exhibits:

* **A delayed, progressive CK rise** (from 337 U/L at initial presentation to >3 900 U/L four months later).
* **Persistent proximal weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia**, indicating ongoing myofibre necrosis rather than a transient leak.
* **Histologic evidence of immune‑mediated necrosis** (MAC deposition, macrophage infiltrates) rather than purely necrotic fibre loss.
* **Requirement for corticosteroids and IVIG**, which are ineffective in pure toxic rhabdomyolysis but essential for immune‑mediated disease.

These distinctions support the interpretation that semaglutide acted not as a direct myotoxin but as a trigger for an autoimmune process.

### Application of WHO‑UMC causality criteria to this case  

The World Health Organization‑Uppsala Monitoring Centre (WHO‑UMC) system grades drug‑event relationships as certain, probable/likely, possible, unlikely, conditional/unclassified, or unassessable. Applying the criteria:

1. **Temporal relationship** – The adverse muscle event began within hours of semaglutide exposure (vomiting, AKI) and progressed over the ensuing months, satisfying a reasonable time link.  
2. **De‑challenge** – Withdrawal of semaglutide (the patient received only one dose) coincided with a gradual clinical improvement after immunosuppression; CK normalized and no further muscle injury occurred while off the drug.  
3. **Re‑challenge** – Not performed for ethical reasons given the severity of the reaction.  
4. **Alternative explanations** – Statins, other myotoxic supplements, or a pre‑existing connective‑tissue disease were excluded by history, serology, and biopsy. The seronegative status adds uncertainty, but the absence of other triggers makes them unlikely.  
5. **Known pharmacology** – While GLP‑1 agonists are not established causes of IMNM, emerging case reports of muscle injury provide a plausible mechanistic basis.

Accordingly, the WHO‑UMC framework would categorize the relationship as **probable/likely**: the temporal pattern, positive de‑challenge, and lack of alternative etiology together make semaglutide a plausible precipitant, even though definitive proof (e.g., rechallenge) is unavailable.

---

In summary, IMNM is driven by auto‑antibody–mediated complement activation and macrophage‑directed necrosis, a process that can be initiated by drugs that alter antigen expression or immune tolerance. Statins exemplify this paradigm through the HMGCR pathway. Although GLP‑1 receptor agonists are primarily metabolic agents, the severe gastrointestinal reaction, metabolic stress, and potential immunologic effects of semaglutide may have provided the necessary trigger for immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy in a susceptible host. The case’s clinical course, biopsy findings, and response to immunosuppression distinguish it from pure rhabdomyolysis and align it with drug‑associated IMNM, supporting a probable causal link under WHO‑UMC criteria.

## Therapeutic Approach and Clinical Course

The patient’s management was organized around three core objectives: rapid suppression of the immune‑mediated muscle injury, prevention of treatment‑related complications, and restoration of functional capacity. The therapeutic plan therefore combined high‑dose corticosteroids, adjunctive intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), and vigilant supportive care, followed by a carefully monitored taper of immunosuppression and long‑term surveillance.

### High‑dose Intravenous Methylprednisolone Pulse

On presentation to the tertiary center the patient exhibited severe proximal weakness, dysphagia, dysphonia, and a CK that had risen to almost 4 000 U/L. In immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) the pathogenic process is driven by a brisk, antibody‑mediated muscular necrosis that can progress rapidly to irreversible loss of muscle fibers if not arrested early. Current consensus for severe, rapidly progressive IMNM recommends an initial “pulse” of intravenous methylprednisolone (typically 1 g daily for 3–5 days). The rationale is twofold: (1) supraphysiologic glucocorticoid concentrations achieve a prompt and profound inhibition of cytokine production, complement activation, and macrophage‑mediated myophagocytosis; and (2) the intravenous route guarantees reliable bioavailability in patients who may have impaired oral intake due to dysphagia.

Our patient received 1 g of methylprednisolone daily for five consecutive days. During this period the CK began to decline, and she reported a modest improvement in limb strength, suggesting that the inflammatory cascade was being dampened.

### Transition to Oral Prednisone: Dose and Taper

Following the pulse, oral prednisone was initiated at 1 mg/kg/day (approximately 70 mg daily for this patient). This dosage aligns with guideline‑derived “high‑dose” regimens for active IMNM and serves to maintain immunosuppression while the corticosteroid effect of the pulse wanes. A slow taper was planned to minimize the risk of disease flare and to reduce steroid‑related adverse effects such as hyperglycaemia, hypertension, osteoporosis, and opportunistic infection.

The taper schedule was individualized based on clinical response and CK trends. Over the first two weeks the prednisone dose was reduced by 10 mg every 5–7 days, reaching 30 mg daily by week four. Because the CK normalized and the patient’s muscle strength approached baseline, the taper was accelerated after the fourth week, with reductions of 5 mg every 5 days until cessation at approximately eight weeks from initiation. Throughout the taper, routine monitoring—including weekly CK, fasting glucose, blood pressure, and bone‑protective measures (calcium, vitamin D, and a bisphosphonate) – was performed to detect early steroid toxicity.

### Adjunctive Intravenous Immunoglobulin (IVIG)

Given the severity of the myopathy (CK > 3 000 U/L, marked dysphagia, and evidence of necrotising lesions with complement deposition on biopsy), IVIG was added after the initial steroid pulse. IVIG is recommended in IMNM when (a) there is rapid functional decline, (b) the patient is seronegative for the more readily treatable anti‑SRP or anti‑HMGCR antibodies, or (c) there is an inadequate response to steroids alone. The immunomodulatory effects of IVIG in IMNM include neutralisation of pathogenic autoantibodies, inhibition of complement activation, and modulation of Fc‑γ receptor–mediated macrophage activity.

The patient received a total dose of 2 g/kg, divided over four consecutive days (0.5 g/kg per day). This regimen is standard for autoimmune neuromuscular disorders and is generally well tolerated. Within two weeks of completing IVIG, CK fell to < 200 U/L, and the patient’s dysphagia and dysphonia markedly improved, supporting the therapeutic contribution of IVIG in halting ongoing muscle injury.

### Supportive Measures During the Acute Phase

While immunosuppression targeted the underlying pathology, aggressive supportive care was essential to prevent secondary organ injury:

* **Fluid Resuscitation and Renal Surveillance** – Because CK elevation can precipitate myoglobinuric nephropathy, isotonic intravenous fluids were administered to maintain urine output > 1 mL/kg/h, and serial creatinine and urine dipstick assessments were performed. The patient’s baseline AKI, which had resolved after the initial semaglutide reaction, remained stable throughout her myopathy treatment.

* **Cardiac Monitoring** – Elevated high‑sensitivity troponin and cardiac MRI findings suggested mild myocardial inflammation. Serial ECGs, troponin measurements, and telemetry were used to detect arrhythmias; echocardiography and repeat cardiac MRI performed at 3‑month follow‑up confirmed resolution of myocardial oedema and preserved ejection fraction.

* **Nutritional and Swallowing Support** – Dysphagia placed the patient at risk for aspiration and malnutrition. A speech‑language pathologist performed bedside swallowing assessments, and a soft‑diet protocol with tube feeding was instituted until oral intake was safe.

* **Infection Prophylaxis** – Given the high‑dose steroids and IVIG, prophylactic measures included pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) prophylaxis with trimethoprim‑sulfamethoxazole and strict aseptic technique during IV line placement.

### Complication: Septic Shock from Pseudomonal UTI

Approximately 12 days after discharge, the patient was readmitted with septic shock secondary to a pseudomonal urinary tract infection. The source was likely multifactorial—immunosuppression, indwelling urinary catheter (used during her previous hospitalization), and possible urinary stasis. Management required broad‑spectrum anti‑pseudomonal antibiotics (e.g., meropenem), aggressive fluid resuscitation, and vasopressor support. Importantly, the CK remained normal during this infection, indicating that the underlying myopathy was quiescent.

The septic episode prompted a brief suspension of oral prednisone (held for 5 days) and reinforced the need for infection vigilance during immunosuppression. After clinical stabilization, prednisone was re‑initiated at the previously tapered dose, and the taper continued without further interruption.

### Outcome Metrics and Recovery Trajectory

* **CK Normalization** – CK fell from a peak of 3 992 U/L to < 150 U/L within 14 days of completing IVIG. Serial measurements thereafter remained within the laboratory reference range throughout follow‑up.

* **Functional Recovery** – Muscle strength, assessed using the Medical Research Council (MRC) scale, improved from grade 2/5 in proximal groups at admission to 5/5 by week 6. Dysphagia, evaluated via videofluoroscopic swallow study, resolved by week 8, allowing return to a regular diet. Dysphonia similarly improved, with normal speech intelligibility by week 10.

* **Cardiac Imaging** – Repeat cardiac MRI at three months demonstrated complete resolution of myocardial oedema and no late gadolinium enhancement, correlating with the normalization of troponin and the absence of clinical cardiac symptoms.

### Long‑Term Follow‑Up

At the 10‑month outpatient visit, the patient was off all immunosuppressive agents for 7 months. She remained free of muscle weakness, CK stayed within normal limits, and cardiac imaging was normal. Importantly, the patient avoided any further exposure to GLP‑1 receptor agonists; alternative weight‑management strategies (dietary counseling, structured exercise) were emphasized. Surveillance labs (CK, liver enzymes, renal function) are scheduled every three months for the first year and then semi‑annually. The durability of remission in seronegative IMNM after a brief course of steroids and a single IVIG infusion is encouraging, yet the patient has been counseled that delayed relapse, although uncommon, can occur and would warrant prompt reassessment.

In summary, the therapeutic strategy—high‑dose steroid pulse, rapid transition to oral prednisone with a gentle taper, a single course of IVIG, and meticulous supportive care—achieved complete biochemical and clinical remission of semaglutide‑associated IMNM. The case underscores the importance of early aggressive immunosuppression in severe IMNM, the utility of IVIG in seronegative disease, and the need for vigilant infection monitoring during immunosuppressive therapy.

## Clinical Implications, Pharmacovigilance, and Future Directions  

The case of immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) that followed a single dose of semaglutide illustrates several concepts that are directly relevant to everyday clinical practice and to the broader effort to understand rare but serious adverse drug reactions. Although GLP‑1 receptor agonists are now first‑line agents for obesity and type 2 diabetes, clinicians must remain vigilant for atypical muscle complications that diverge from the classic picture of drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis. The following points synthesize the lessons from this report, provide a pragmatic approach to new‑onset muscle weakness in patients receiving GLP‑1 agonists, and outline priorities for pharmacovigilance and research.

### Recognising atypical drug‑induced myopathy versus classic rhabdomyolysis  

GLP‑1 agonists are well known for causing transient nausea, vomiting, and, in rare reports, acute rhabdomyolysis with a rapid rise and fall of creatine kinase (CK). In rhabdomyolysis the CK often exceeds 10 000 U/L within days of the offending exposure, two‑to‑three‑day hydration leads to a swift decline, and muscle symptoms resolve without the need for immunosuppression. By contrast, IMNM presents with a **sub‑acute, progressive** pattern: CK elevation may be modest initially, rises over weeks to months, and is accompanied by **persistent proximal weakness, dysphagia, or dysphonia**. Laboratory markers of systemic inflammation (ESR, CRP) may be modestly elevated, and standard myositis autoantibodies are frequently absent. The presence of **persistent CK elevation beyond the expected time course for a toxic myopathy** should raise suspicion for an immune‑mediated process.

### Practical algorithm for evaluating new‑onset muscle weakness in patients on semaglutide or related agents  

1. **History and temporal relationship**  
   * Identify the exact timing of GLP‑1 agonist initiation, dose, and any acute gastrointestinal adverse events.  
   * Document the trajectory of symptoms (acute vs. sub‑acute) and associated features (dysphagia, dysphonia, rash, cardiac or pulmonary signs).  

2. **Initial laboratory work‑up**  
   * CK, AST/ALT, aldolase, myoglobin, renal function, electrolytes.  
   * Inflammatory markers (ESR, CRP).  
   * Full myositis autoantibody panel (including HMGCR, SRP, Jo‑1, Mi‑2, MDA‑5, TIF1‑γ, NXP‑2, cN‑1A).  

3. **Distinguish toxic vs. immune‑mediated patterns**  
   * **Toxic rhabdomyolysis:** CK >10 000 U/L within 48 h, rapid decline with hydration, no progressive weakness.  
   * **Possible IMNM:** CK rise that continues or peaks weeks to months later, proximal weakness disproportionate to CK, lack of response to fluids alone.  

4. **Imaging**  
   * MRI of affected muscle groups to detect symmetric edema and to guide biopsy sites.  

5. **Early muscle biopsy**  
   * If CK remains >5 × upper limit of normal after 2–3 weeks of supportive care, serology is negative, and clinical suspicion for IMNM is high, obtain a **timed biopsy** from the most edematous region. Histology demonstrating necrotic fibers with scant inflammatory infiltrate, macrophage (CD68) infiltration, and complement deposition confirms the diagnosis and prevents delays in treatment.  

6. **Management decisions**  
   * **Discontinue the GLP‑1 agonist immediately** – even a single dose may act as a trigger.  
   * Initiate **brief high‑dose glucocorticoids** (e.g., IV methylprednisolone 1 g daily × 3–5 days) followed by an oral taper.  
   * Add **IVIG** (2 g/kg divided over 4–5 days) when weakness is severe or CK fails to fall promptly.  
   * Reserve prolonged immunosuppression (e.g., azathioprine, methotrexate, rituximab) for cases with **persistent disease activity** after drug withdrawal and a short steroid/IVIG course.  

### Guidance on discontinuation of the offending agent and duration of immunosuppression  

The cornerstone of therapy is **prompt drug cessation**. In the reported case, withdrawal of semaglutide combined with a short course of steroids and a single IVIG regimen resulted in complete functional recovery and normalization of CK within months, obviating the need for chronic immunosuppression. Clinicians should therefore:  

* **Avoid rechallenge** under any circumstances; the risk of recurrent, possibly more severe myopathy outweighs any benefit.  
* **Taper steroids rapidly** once CK normalizes and clinical strength improves (typically over 6–8 weeks), monitoring for rebound CK rise.  
* **Reserve maintenance immunosuppression** for seropositive IMNM (e.g., anti‑HMGCR, anti‑SRP) or for patients with persistent weakness after drug withdrawal.  

### Reporting adverse events to pharmacovigilance databases  

Given the rarity of GLP‑1‑agonist‑associated IMNM, each reported case materially contributes to the safety signal. Clinicians should:  

* Submit a detailed adverse event report to **FDA’s FAERS**, **EMA’s EudraVigilance**, and **WHO‑VigiBase**. Include drug name, dose, timing, CK trends, biopsy results, and treatment outcomes.  
* Encourage institutional pharmacovigilance offices to log the case in **internal safety databases** to facilitate future case‑control analyses.  
* When possible, share de‑identified data with collaborative registries (e.g., the International Myositis Registry) to enhance case aggregation.  

### Potential risk factors to investigate  

The index patient’s pre‑existing non‑alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), elevated baseline transaminases, and low‑titer anti‑SS‑B positivity raise the question of **host susceptibility**. Future clinicians should consider:  

* **Baseline liver disease** – may reflect altered drug metabolism or heightened systemic inflammation.  
* **Autoantibody profile** – even isolated, low‑titer antibodies may signal an underlying immune dysregulation that predisposes to drug‑triggered IMNM.  
* **Genetic predisposition** – HLA‑DRB1*07:01 and other alleles have been linked to statin‑associated IMNM; similar associations should be explored for GLP‑1 agonists.  

Collecting these variables prospectively will enable risk‑stratification and possibly guide pre‑treatment screening.  

### Research agenda  

1. **Prospective pharmacovigilance surveillance** – Systematic CK monitoring in the first 12 weeks after GLP‑1 agonist initiation, especially in patients with liver disease or existing autoimmunity.  
2. **Mechanistic studies** – In‑vitro and animal models to examine whether GLP‑1 receptor activation on skeletal muscle or immune cells can trigger complement activation, macrophage infiltration, or neo‑antigen formation.  
3. **Registry development** – A dedicated **GLP‑1‑agonist‑associated myopathy registry** would capture demographic, clinical, serologic, and histologic data, enabling estimation of incidence and identification of phenotypic patterns.  
4. **Biomarker discovery** – Proteomic or transcriptomic profiling of muscle tissue and serum may uncover signatures that differentiate immune‑mediated necrosis from pure toxic injury.  
5. **Therapeutic trials** – Small, open‑label studies comparing short‑course steroids + IVIG versus steroids alone in drug‑triggered IMNM could define the optimal immunomodulatory strategy.  

---

**Take‑home messages for the clinician**  

* In patients receiving semaglutide (or any GLP‑1 receptor agonist) who develop **sub‑acute proximal weakness with a rising CK**, think beyond rhabdomyolysis and consider IMNM, even when standard myositis antibodies are negative.  
* **Early muscle biopsy** is essential when serology is unrevealing; it provides a definitive diagnosis and informs the need for immunosuppression.  
* **Discontinue the GLP‑1 agonist promptly**, initiate a short high‑dose steroid course, and add IVIG for severe presentations; most patients may recover without long‑term immunosuppression.  
* **Report** every suspected case to national and international pharmacovigilance systems to build a safety signal that will guide future prescribing.  

By integrating vigilant clinical assessment, targeted diagnostics, and systematic reporting, clinicians can both safeguard individual patients and contribute to the collective understanding of GLP‑1‑agonist‑related immune myopathies.
