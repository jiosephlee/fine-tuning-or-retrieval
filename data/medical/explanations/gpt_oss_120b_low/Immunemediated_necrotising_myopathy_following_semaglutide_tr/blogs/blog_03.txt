## Why This Case is a Head‑turner  

When you think of immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM), the first things that pop up are the classic “anti‑HMGCR” or “anti‑SRP” antibodies. The textbook algorithm: **CK > 1 000 U/L → auto‑antibody panel → positive → diagnosis.**  

Enter a 60‑year‑old woman who, after a single dose of semaglutide, develops progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, and a CK that climbs from a modest 337 U/L to almost 4 000 U/L over four months. The auto‑antibody work‑up is a **sponge‑sized negative** – ANA, anti‑SRP, anti‑HMGCR, myositis panel, everything is negative. Yet her muscle biopsy tells a different story: necrotic fibers, macrophage infiltrates, complement (MAC) deposition, and only a few CD3⁺ T‑cells.  

In an era where panels of myositis‑specific antibodies are becoming ever more expansive, this case reminds us that **seronegativity does not equal “no disease.”** It also forces us to confront when to stop chasing labs and move on to tissue diagnosis.  

---

## The Clinical Puzzle of Seronegative IMNM  

**What makes seronegative IMNM tricky?**  

1. **Overlap with other myopathies** – Elevated CK, proximal weakness, and MRI edema can be seen in toxic myopathy, polymyositis, steroid‑induced myopathy, or even severe deconditioning.  
2. **Atypical auto‑antibody patterns** – Up to **⅓ of IMNM patients are truly seronegative**, meaning no HMGCR, SRP, or even myositis‑specific antibodies. A low‑titer SS‑B (as in our patient) is often an incidental finding and should not distract from the primary pathology.  
3. **Temporal ambiguity** – In drug‑related cases (GLP‑1‑RA, statins, etc.) the timing of symptom onset can be vague, raising the question: “Is this a direct toxic effect or an autoimmune response?”  

**Key clinical clues that should raise suspicion for IMNM despite a negative panel:**  

- **Rapidly progressive proximal weakness** with bulbar involvement (dysphagia, dysphonia).  
- **CK trajectory**: a sustained rise over weeks to months, not the abrupt peak‑and‑fall typical of pure rhabdomyolysis.  
- **MRI findings**: symmetric, proximal muscle edema with sparing of fascia and a “stippled” pattern on T2/STIR.  
- **Lack of clear alternative cause**: no recent strenuous exercise, no statin exposure, no obvious inflammatory disease.  

If you have two or more of these, it’s time to think “muscle biopsy now.”

## Muscle Biopsy: The Definitive Arbiter  

### What to Look For  

| Histologic Feature | Why It Matters |
|--------------------|----------------|
| **Scattered necrotic fibers with regeneration** | Hallmark of necrotising process; not seen in pure inflammatory myositis. |
| **Macrophage (CD68⁺) infiltrates** | Indicates ongoing phagocytosis of necrotic fibers; supports immune‑mediated injury. |
| **Sparse CD3⁺ T‑cells** | IMNM is characteristically *pauci‑lymphocytic* – a stark contrast to polymyositis (dense endomysial CD8⁺ infiltrate). |
| **Membrane Attack Complex (C5b‑9) staining** | Direct evidence of complement‑mediated fiber injury – a “smoking gun” for autoimmune necrosis. |
| **Fiber type grouping or atrophy?** | May suggest chronicity; not required for diagnosis but helps stage disease. |

In our patient, the biopsy showed **necrotic/regenerating fibers, CD68⁺ macrophages, and MAC deposition**, with only occasional CD3⁺ cells. This pattern is textbook IMNM and essentially clinches the diagnosis regardless of serology.

### When to Biopsy  

- **CK > 1 000 U/L** *and* **proximal weakness** that is not explained by a clear toxic exposure.  
- **Negative myositis panel** (including extended panels for HMGCR, SRP, Mi‑2, MDA‑5, etc.).  
- **MRI** shows **diffuse, symmetric muscle edema** without focal injury.  
- **Clinical course** suggests an autoimmune process (progressive, not self‑limited).  

If you’re still on the fence after these criteria, remember that **biopsy is low‑risk** (usually outpatient, local anesthesia) and can prevent months of diagnostic odyssey.

---

## Integrating Imaging, Labs, and Histology  

1. **Start with the bedside:** thorough neuromuscular exam, CK trend, and review of meds.  
2. **MRI as the gatekeeper:** A STIR‑hyperintense, symmetric pattern in proximal muscles (quadriceps, hip flexors) steers you away from focal injuries or compartment syndromes. Lack of fatty infiltration supports an acute process.  
3. **Serology comes next:** Run the broad myositis panel. If negative, still consider IMNM if the clinical picture fits.  
4. **Biopsy to seal the deal:** The histopathology will answer the “immune” vs “toxic” question definitively.  

In the semaglutide case, the **MRI showed symmetric edema**, the **auto‑antibodies were negative**, and the **biopsy confirmed IMNM** – allowing the team to start targeted immunosuppression (high‑dose steroids + IVIG) and avoid unnecessary prolonged investigations.

---

## Treatment Pearls (When Seronegative Doesn’t Mean “Untreatable”)  

- **Initial high‑dose steroids:** 1 mg/kg prednisone or IV methylprednisolone pulses (1 g/day for 3–5 days).  
- **Adjunct IVIG:** 2 g/kg divided over 4–5 days can be decisive, especially when the disease is fulminant or steroid‑refractory.  
- **Early taper** is possible in drug‑induced IMNM once CK normalises and strength improves – as shown by our patient who was off prednisone within two months.  
- **Avoid the culprit:** Discontinue GLP‑1‑RA (or any suspected drug). Re‑challenge is usually unethical.  

**Take‑home:** Seronegative IMNM often responds to a short, aggressive immunosuppressive course if caught early. Delay can lead to chronic weakness and unnecessary exposure to high‑dose steroids for months.

---

## Bottom Line – Key Take‑Home Messages  

- **Seronegative IMNM is real** and accounts for a substantial minority of necrotising myopathies.  
- **Don’t let a negative antibody panel lull you into complacency**; the clinical picture and imaging are equally, if not more, important.  
- **Muscle biopsy is the gold‑standard** when the diagnosis is in doubt – look for necrosis, macrophages, scant lymphocytes, and complement deposition.  
- **Rapid, targeted immunosuppression** (steroids ± IVIG) can restore function and allow early taper in drug‑associated cases.  
- **Pharmacovigilance matters** – as GLP‑1‑RAs become ubiquitous, clinicians must stay alert to rare but serious muscle toxicities that may masquerade as seronegative IMNM.
