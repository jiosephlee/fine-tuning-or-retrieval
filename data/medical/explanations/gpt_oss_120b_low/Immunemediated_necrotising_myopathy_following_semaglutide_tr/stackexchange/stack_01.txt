Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Differential Diagnosis] What clinical features distinguish immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) from acute rhabdomyolysis, and how did these features manifest in this patient?

**Key clinical features that separate immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) from acute rhabdomyolysis, and how they appeared in this patient**

| Feature | Typical in acute rhabdomyolysis | Typical in IMNM | Patient’s findings |
|--------|--------------------------------|----------------|-------------------|
| **Onset of CK rise** | Sudden, often >10 000 U/L within hours‑days of the precipitant; peaks early and then falls rapidly once the offending agent is removed and hydration is given. | Insidious to sub‑acute rise; CK may be modest initially but climbs over weeks‑months, often reaching several thousand and remaining elevated until immunotherapy is started. | CK was 337 U/L on the first admission (after the single semaglutide dose) – far below the “thousands” typical of rhabdomyolysis. Four months later CK rose to 3 992 U/L, a 12‑fold increase that accrued over weeks, not days. |
| **Temporal relationship to trigger** | CK normalizes within days‑weeks after drug discontinuation and aggressive fluid resuscitation. | CK persists or worsens despite drug withdrawal; improvement only after immunosuppression. | After semaglutide was stopped, CK *continued to climb* for months; only after high‑dose steroids + IVIG did the CK fall dramatically. |
| **Pain & urine colour** | Severe myalgias, often diffuse; “cola‑coloured” or tea‑coloured urine from myoglobinuria is common. | Myalgias may be mild or absent; dysphagia, dysphonia, and focal proximal weakness predominate; urine is usually clear. | The patient denied muscle pain, rash, or dark urine. She presented with progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia—classic for IMNM. |
| **Renal involvement** | AKI is usually secondary to myoglobin‑induced tubular injury; improves with hydration. | AKI may be present from volume depletion but is not driven by myoglobin; renal function often improves with fluids alone. | Initial AKI was related to hypovolaemia from vomiting; renal function recovered with hydration, without a CK‑driven surge. |
| **Systemic inflammation** | Often absent; laboratory inflammatory markers may be normal. | Low‑grade systemic inflammation (elevated ESR/CRP) is typical, reflecting an autoimmune process. | ESR 22 mm/h and CRP 22.8 mg/L were modestly elevated, consistent with an inflammatory myopathy. |
| **Muscle imaging** | Diffuse edema but usually correlates with an acute trauma/toxic pattern; may be limited. | MRI shows symmetric proximal muscle edema, often with sub‑cutaneous involvement, persisting over weeks. | MRI of the thighs showed symmetric proximal muscle and sub‑cutaneous edema, compatible with an ongoing inflammatory process rather than a fleeting toxic injury. |
| **Muscle biopsy** | Necrosis with abundant myoglobin, minimal inflammatory infiltrate; macrophages dominate, but no complement deposition. | Necrotising fibres with scattered macrophages, **membrane attack complex (C5b‑9) deposition**, and sometimes sparse CD3⁺ T cells—hallmarks of IMNM. | Biopsy demonstrated necrotic/regenerating fibres, CD68⁺ macrophages, C5b‑9 positivity, and rare CD3⁺ cells—exactly the pattern of IMNM. |
| **Autoantibodies** | Usually negative; if present, they are unrelated to the myopathy. | May have anti‑SRP, anti‑HMGCR, or be seronegative; a negative panel does not exclude IMNM. | Myositis panel was negative for SRP, HMGCR, Jo‑1, etc., placing her in the seronegative IMNM group (≈⅓ of cases). |
| **Response to immunotherapy** | No benefit; CK falls only with removal of toxin and hydration. | Marked CK and clinical improvement after corticosteroids ± IVIG or other immunosuppressants. | CK fell rapidly after 5 days of IV methylprednisolone followed by oral prednisone and a 2 g/kg course of IVIG; functional status returned to baseline. |

**Why this case fits IMNM rather than rhabdomyolysis**

1. **CK trajectory** – modest elevation early on, then a gradual 12‑fold rise over months, contrary to the brisk peak‑and‑decline pattern of toxin‑mediated rhabdomyolysis.
2. **Lack of myoglobinuria or severe muscle pain** – classic rhabdomyolysis features were absent.
3. **Persistent proximal weakness and bulbar involvement (dysphagia, dysphonia)** – hallmark of IMNM, rarely seen in pure rhabdomyolysis.
4. **Biopsy evidence** – necrotising fibres with C5b‑9 membrane attack complex and scant T‑cell infiltrate, diagnostic of IMNM.
5. **Therapeutic response** – dramatic improvement only after immunosuppression, not after simple hydration.

Thus, the constellation of a sub‑acute CK rise, focal proximal weakness with bulbar signs, inflammatory laboratory/imaging findings, and a biopsy pattern of necrotising myopathy with complement deposition unequivocally distinguishes immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy from acute rhabdomyolysis in this patient.
