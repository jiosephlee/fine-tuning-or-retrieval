Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Morning Report] What cardiac complications can accompany IMNM, and how were the patient’s cardiac MRI and biomarkers interpreted in this context?

**Cardiac involvement in immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM)**  

IMNM is a systemic inflammatory myopathy and, like the other idiopathic inflammatory myopathies, can affect the heart. The most frequently reported cardiac manifestations are:

| Manifestation | Typical clinical / imaging features |
|---------------|--------------------------------------|
| **Myocarditis / myocardial inflammation** | Chest pain, dyspnoea, arrhythmias; cardiac MRI shows myocardial oedema, T2‑weighted hyperintensity, and often late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) reflecting necrosis or fibrosis. |
| **Arrhythmias** | A‑fib, ventricular ectopy, heart‑block; may be the first clue to cardiac disease. |
| **Heart‑failure phenotype** | Reduced or, less commonly, preserved LVEF; may be secondary to myocarditis or to a stress‑related cardiomyopathy (“takotsubo‑type” pattern). |
| **Conduction disease / pericardial effusion** | Less common but described in case series of necrotising myopathies. |
| **Coronary‑artery‐spasm–like ischemia** | Usually excluded by normal coronary angiography; considered part of the inflammatory milieu. |

The prevalence of cardiac disease in IMNM is not precisely known because most series are small, but cardiac MRI (CMR) detects subclinical involvement in up to 30 % of patients with other inflammatory myopathies, and a similar frequency is plausible in IMNM.

---

### How the cardiac MRI and biomarkers were interpreted in this patient

**1. Biomarkers (hs‑troponin and pro‑BNP)**  

* **High‑sensitivity troponin I 62 pg/mL (ULN ≤ 53 pg/mL).**  
  - A mild elevation in an otherwise stable patient raises suspicion for myocardial injury, but the value is below the typical range seen in acute myocarditis (often several‑fold above ULN).  
  - In the context of IMNM, low‑grade troponin rises are common and may reflect subtle myocyte inflammation rather than frank infarction.

* **Pro‑BNP 4 358 pg/mL (ULN ≤ 125 pg/mL).**  
  - Markedly elevated, consistent with increased ventricular wall stress.  In this case the elevation could be multifactorial: acute renal dysfunction on admission, volume overload from the initial dehydration episode, and possible early systolic dysfunction.

**Interpretation:** The biomarker profile suggested *myocardial involvement* but was not specific for active myocarditis. The modest troponin rise argued against a large infarct, while the high BNP supported a transient reduction in systolic performance or heightened filling pressures.

**2. Cardiac‑magnetic‑resonance (CMR) findings**  

* **Mild hypokinesis of the mid‑anteroseptal and anterior walls** – a regional wall‑motion abnormality that does not follow a coronary‑artery distribution, typical of inflammatory or stress‑related injury.  

* **Myocardial oedema on T2‑weighted images** – indicates active inflammation or edema.  

* **Absence of definitive late gadolinium enhancement (LGE).**  
  - LGE represents irreversible injury (necrosis/fibrosis). Its absence suggests that the inflammation was early and potentially reversible.

* **No significant scarring, infarction, or valvular disease** – rules out chronic ischemic heart disease as the cause of the wall‑motion defect.

**Interpretation within the IMNM framework:**  
The CMR pattern—regional hypokinesis + edema without LGE—is classic for *early myocarditis* or for a *stress‑induced (catecholamine‑mediated) cardiomyopathy* that can accompany systemic inflammatory states. In IMNM, the most plausible explanation is myocarditis secondary to the same immune process that damages skeletal muscle. Because LGE was lacking, the authors concluded that the changes were *potentially reversible* and not yet fibrotic.

**3. Clinical synthesis**

* The patient’s **paroxysmal atrial fibrillation** during the initial hospitalization could be a manifestation of atrial inflammation or a stress trigger; its resolution with clinical improvement supports a transient inflammatory etiology.  

* Follow‑up imaging showed *normalisation of both wall motion and the absence of edema*, confirming that the cardiac abnormality was *reversible* and likely related to the acute inflammatory phase of IMNM rather than an independent cardiomyopathy.

---

### Take‑home points for residents

1. **Cardiac involvement should be actively sought in IMNM**—any new dyspnoea, chest pain, or arrhythmia warrants ECG, troponin, BNP and, when available, CMR.  
2. **CMR is the preferred modality**: T2‑weighted edema identifies active inflammation; LGE differentiates reversible myocarditis from permanent scar.  
3. **Mild troponin elevation with marked BNP** in this setting points toward *myocardial inflammation with transient systolic dysfunction* rather than coronary ischemia.  
4. **Reversibility is common** when immunosuppression and removal of the trigger (here, semaglutide) are instituted promptly, as demonstrated by the patient’s normalization of MRI findings and biomarkers on follow‑up.  

In summary, the case illustrates that IMNM can present with *myocarditis‑like* cardiac changes—regional wall‑motion abnormality, myocardial edema, and modest troponin rise—yet these findings can be transient and improve with appropriate therapy and removal of the offending agent.
