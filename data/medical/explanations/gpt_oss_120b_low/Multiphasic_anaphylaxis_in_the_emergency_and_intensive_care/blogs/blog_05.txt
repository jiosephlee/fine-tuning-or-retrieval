Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# Observation Times After Anaphylaxis: How Long Is Long Enough?

## Why This Case Got My Attention  

When a middle‑aged woman with a benign skin reaction to amoxicillin a few decades ago suddenly collapses after a single dose of clarithromycin, you expect a textbook anaphylactic shock: rapid epinephrine, fluids, and a quick discharge after a short observation period. Instead, she rattles through **four distinct episodes of anaphylaxis** over a 12‑hour window, needs repeated intramuscular epinephrine, a brief stint in the ICU, and finally a 24‑hour “clear” period before being sent home.  

Multiphasic anaphylaxis is rare, but it forces us to confront the biggest unanswered question in everyday emergency practice: **how long should we keep patients under watchful eyes after the initial reaction?** ‑ The answer isn’t simple, and the literature is a patchwork of definitions, incidence estimates, and divergent guideline recommendations. This case shines a light on the clinical and logistical tensions that arise when we try to balance patient safety with the reality of a busy ED and limited inpatient beds.

## The Current Landscape – Guideline Recommendations

| Guideline | Recommended Observation after Symptom Resolution | Comments |
|-----------|---------------------------------------------------|----------|
| **Resuscitation Council (UK) 2021** | No fixed duration; suggests *risk‑stratified* approach (high‑risk → ≥ 6 h; low‑risk → 1–2 h) | Emphasises clinical judgment; introduces “three‑tier” risk model. |
| **World Allergy Organization (2020)** | Up to **6 h** for all patients; consider longer for severe presentations | Simple rule of thumb; does not differentiate between severity levels. |
| **Dutch NHG (2022)** | About **half** of biphasic reactions occur within 6–12 h, but no specific mandated period. | Leaves decision to the treating physician. |
| **American College of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology (2021)** | Minimum **4–6 h**, longer if severe or multiple epinephrine doses needed. | Mirrors UK practice but adds “multiple doses” as a trigger for extended monitoring. |
| **EAACI (2023)** | **12 h** if the patient required > 1 epinephrine dose or had hypotension/respiratory compromise. | More aggressive for high‑severity cases. |

The spread is striking—some societies suggest a **single shift (≈ 8 h)**, others advocate **up to a full day** for those needing more than one epinephrine injection. None provide hard data on **resource utilization** vs. **prevention of biphasic events**.

## How Common Is Biphasic (or Multiphasic) Anaphylaxis?

Published incidence rates range from **0.4 % to 23 %**, but the bulk of recent analyses settle around **4–6 %** for biphasic reactions. Multiphasic courses (≥ 3 episodes) are even scarcer; case series report figures < 1 % of all anaphylaxis encounters. The variation stems largely from:

1. **Inconsistent definitions** – some authors count any recurrence within 24 h; others require a symptom‑free interval of ≥ 1 h.
2. **Variable follow‑up** – many studies only monitor for 4–6 h, missing later relapses.
3. **Differences in trigger and patient population** – drug‑induced reactions (particularly antibiotics) seem more prone to biphasic patterns than food‑induced reactions.

Because the denominator (total anaphylaxis cases) is massive and the numerator (biphasic events) small, robust risk predictors have remained elusive.

## What Does the Multiphasic Case Teach Us?

1. **Severity matters** – The patient required **four epinephrine doses** and progressed from hypotension to deep hypoxemia, indicating a high‑risk profile.
2. **Timing of relapses** – All recurrences happened **within the first 12 h**, aligning with the “half occur within 6–12 h” observation from Dutch data.
3. **Diminishing intensity** – Each subsequent episode was less severe, suggesting a possible “exhaustion” of mediator release, but this cannot be assumed for every patient.
4. **ICU observation was pragmatic** – While an ICU bed is costly, it provided immediate access to repeat epinephrine, airway support, and continuous vitals—something a regular ward often cannot guarantee for a patient who may deteriorate rapidly.

## Building a Risk‑Stratified Observation Framework

Given the heterogeneity in recommendations, a pragmatic, **risk‑stratified algorithm** can help clinicians decide *how long* to keep a patient under observation.

### Step 1 – Identify High‑Risk Features (any one warrants extended monitoring)

- **Initial presentation with hypotension (SBP < 90 mm Hg) or hypoxemia (SpO₂ < 92 %).**
- **Need for > 1 epinephrine dose** (including auto‑injector + repeat doses).
- **Involvement of the airway** (stridor, hoarseness, swollen lips/tongue) even if resolved.
- **Underlying mast cell disease suspicion** (elevated baseline tryptase, known systemic mastocytosis, severe cutaneous mastocytosis).
- **Delayed epinephrine administration** (> 15 min from symptom onset).
- **Concurrent β‑blocker therapy** (blunts epinephrine response).
- **Pregnancy** (lower physiologic reserve).

### Step 2 – Assign Observation Duration

| Risk Category | Observation Time (after symptom resolution) | Setting |
|---------------|----------------------------------------------|--------|
| **Low** (no high‑risk features, single epinephrine, stable vitals) | **1–2 h** | ED observation unit or fast‑track ward |
| **Intermediate** (one high‑risk feature, stable after initial treatment) | **6 h** | ED observation or step‑down unit |
| **High** (≥ 2 high‑risk features, recurrent episodes, or need for ICU‑level care) | **12–24 h** (or until 24 h after last epinephrine) | ICU or monitored medical floor with telemetry |

The **24‑hour ceiling** reflects the longest interval reported for biphasic reactions in most series and aligns with the case’s safe discharge after a full day without further events.

### Step 3 – Practical Considerations

- **Document timing of each epinephrine dose** and vital sign trends. This assists in meeting the observation window without unnecessary prolongation.
- **Obtain serum tryptase** promptly (ideally within 1–4 h of a reaction) and schedule a baseline sample ≥ 24 h later. Even if not mandated, it provides valuable information for future risk stratification.
- **Educate the patient** before discharge: A prescribed auto‑injector (preferably 0.5 mg for adults > 50 kg), clear instructions on when to use it, and a follow‑up appointment with allergy/immunology within 1–2 weeks.
- **Consider early ICU transfer** when recurrent epinephrine doses are needed within a short window, especially if airway compromise is a concern. The ICU provides rapid access to repeat dosing, continuous hemodynamic monitoring, and advanced airway tools.

## Resource Implications – Balancing Safety and Throughput

### The Cost of “Too Long”

- **ICU bed days** are expensive (≈ $4,000–$5,000 per day in many US hospitals). Investing 24 h of ICU time for every anaphylaxis patient would be unsustainable.
- **Observation unit overload** – Extending all patients to 12 h can clog fast‑track areas, delaying care for other acute cases.

### The Cost of “Too Short”

- **Missed biphasic reactions** may lead to delayed epinephrine administration, increased morbidity, and rare mortality.
- **Readmissions** – A patient discharged after 2 h who returns in shock imposes a larger cost (repeat imaging, ICU admission, potential litigation).

### A Pragmatic Balance

- **Targeted extended monitoring** for the ~5 % of patients who truly need it (those meeting high‑risk criteria) is likely the most cost‑effective approach.
- **Utilize step‑down units** with telemetry rather than full ICU for intermediate‑risk patients; they provide continuous monitoring without the full ICU overhead.
- **Implement a “watch‑and‑wait” protocol**: If a high‑risk patient stabilizes after 6 h and remains asymptomatic, consider transfer to a regular ward with scheduled vitals every 2 h, rather than keeping them in the ICU for the full 24 h.

## Take‑Home Messages

- **There is no universal “one‑size‑fits‑all” observation time** after anaphylaxis; current guidelines vary from 4 h to 24 h.
- **Risk stratification**—based on initial severity, epinephrine requirements, airway involvement, and comorbid mast cell disease—can guide observation duration and level of care.
- **High‑risk patients (e.g., multiple epinephrine doses, hypotension, possible mastocytosis) merit at least 12–24 h of monitored observation**, preferably in an environment capable of rapid epinephrine administration and airway management.
- **Low‑risk patients can safely be observed for 1–2 h** after symptom resolution, provided vitals are stable and there are no concerning features.
- **Tryptase sampling** should be performed early (within 1 h) and repeated at 24 h to aid future risk assessment, even though it does not affect acute management.
- **Education is non‑negotiable**: Ensure every patient leaves with a correctly‑dosed epinephrine auto‑injector (0.5 mg for adults > 50 kg) and clear instructions on when and how to use it.

In the end, the goal isn’t to keep every anaphylaxis patient under a microscope for a full day, but to **match observation intensity to individual risk**. By doing so, we protect the vulnerable few who will relapse while preserving precious resources for the many who won’t. The multiphasic clarithromycin case underlines both the danger of under‑observation and the feasibility of a structured, risk‑adjusted approach—an approach that can be implemented today in most EDs and hospitals.
