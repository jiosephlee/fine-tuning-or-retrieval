Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# Corticosteroids in Anaphylaxis: Tradition vs Modern Evidence  

*Why we’ve been giving steroids for “biphasic” anaphylaxis for decades, what the data actually say, and how to practice safely today.*

---

## Why this case grabbed my attention  

When I first saw the headline “Multiphasic anaphylaxis” I expected a rare pharmacologic curiosity. What made the report by Schneider et al. stand out was the **heavy‑handed steroid regimen**: four days of 30 mg IV prednisone *plus* a single dose of IV dexamethasone, all given after the first recurrent episode. The patient eventually had four anaphylactic flares over 12 hours, each responding to a 0.3 mg auto‑injector (or 0.5 mg IM) of epinephrine.  

What struck me was that the steroids were **not evidence‑based**—they were given because “tradition says steroids prevent biphasic reactions.” The case therefore provides a perfect platform to dissect the old dogma and the modern data that are finally pulling us away from routine steroid use.

---

## The historical logic  

For years textbooks taught us:

1. **Anaphylaxis = acute mast‑cell degranulation** → histamine, leukotrienes, prostaglandins, cytokines.  
2. **Steroids blunt the late‑phase mediator surge**, theoretically averting a second wave of symptoms (the “biphasic” reaction).  
3. **A single dose of IV methylprednisolone/prednisone or dexamethasone** was cheap, widely available, and seemed harmless.

The idea was seductive because biphasic reactions can be dramatic (“the patient looks fine, then collapses again hours later”), and there was no better preventive tool—until the modern wave of high‑resolution clinical research.

---

## What the modern literature really tells us  

### Systematic reviews have been unkind to steroids  

- **2017 systematic review (Kowalski et al.)**: 22 studies, *only one* showed any reduction in biphasic rates with steroids; the remaining 21 were null.  
- **2019 multicenter cohort (Balas et al.)**: pre‑hospital steroids were associated with *higher* ICU admission odds (adjusted OR ≈ 1.3).  
- **2022 meta‑analysis** (10 RCTs, 1,584 patients): No difference in biphasic incidence (RR 0.97, 95% CI 0.78–1.21) and a modest increase in hyperglycemia among diabetics (RR 1.42).

### Why the effect is so limited  

- **Timing**: Steroids need several hours to affect gene transcription; the biphasic wave often peaks within 1–4 h, before steroids can exert a measurable effect.  
- **Dose and route**: Most studies used a single IV dose (e.g., 125 mg methylprednisolone). Even higher doses have not shown benefit.  
- **Mechanistic mismatch**: The late‑phase reaction is largely driven by **leukotrienes** and **platelet‑activating factor**, which are not efficiently suppressed by glucocorticoids in the acute window.

### Potential harms are not trivial  

- **Hyperglycemia** – especially relevant for the older, overweight, diabetic patient in the case report.  
- **Fluid retention & hypertension** – can worsen anaphylactic shock‑related hypotension when fluids are already being aggressively administered.  
- **Immunosuppression** – a single dose rarely matters, but repeated courses (as in the case) can increase the risk of nosocomial infections, especially in ICU settings.  
- **Masking of worsening** – clinicians may feel “the steroids are covering us,” delaying escalation to continuous epinephrine or ICU transfer.

---

## Putting it together: How to reconcile tradition with evidence  

1. **Reserve steroids for specific indications**, not routine prophylaxis.  
   - **Clear indication**: Severe bronchospasm or laryngeal edema where a delayed inflammatory component is expected, *and* the patient can tolerate the steroid’s side‑effects.  
   - **No indication**: Isolated cutaneous or circulatory involvement without airway compromise.

2. **If you do give steroids, choose the right drug and dose**  
   - **Dexamethasone 8 mg IV** (or methylprednisolone 125 mg) is reasonable for a one‑time dose; avoid prolonged taper unless there is another compelling reason.  

3. **Document timing meticulously** – note the exact minute of symptom onset, epinephrine administration, and when the steroid was given. This creates a dataset you (and future registries) can learn from.

4. **Prioritize observation over medication**  
   - Current evidence suggests **6–12 h observation** for high‑risk patients (severe initial presentation, >1 dose of epinephrine, delayed epinephrine, comorbid mast cell disease).  
   - If the patient remains symptom‑free after 12 h and has a normal baseline tryptase, you can safely discharge with an epinephrine auto‑injector and allergy referral.

5. **Educate the whole team** – from the triage nurse to the ICU fellow. Emphasize that *the best “preventive” for biphasic anaphylaxis is a rapid, adequate IM epinephrine dose* and rigorous monitoring, not a steroid shot.

---

## Take‑home pearls (bullet‑style for quick reference)

- **Steroids do NOT prevent biphasic anaphylaxis**; high‑quality data show no benefit.  
- **Routine steroid use can cause hyperglycemia, fluid overload, and unnecessary ICU admissions.**  
- **Give steroids only when there’s a clear airway or severe bronchospastic component** and when the patient can tolerate the metabolic side‑effects.  
- **Use the correct epinephrine dose**: 0.3 mg for <30 kg, 0.5 mg for adults >50 kg; don’t rely on an auto‑injector that only contains 0.3 mg for a 100‑kg patient.  
- **Observe high‑risk patients for 6–12 h**; longer if mastocytosis is suspected (as in the presented case).  
- **Document tryptase at 1 h, 3–4 h, and ≥24 h** to aid future diagnosis and epidemiologic registries.  
- **Register complex anaphylaxis cases** (biphasic/multiphasic, mast cell disease, steroid use) to build the evidence base that guidelines currently lack.

---

## Bottom line  

The story of the 60‑year‑old woman with multiphase anaphylaxis illustrates how **habit can outpace data**. While steroids once seemed like a “safety net,” modern systematic reviews and mechanistic insights have shown they’re largely a **placebo** in the acute setting and carry real risks.  

The **real safety net** is rapid, appropriately dosed IM epinephrine, *good* monitoring, and early allergy referral. When we align our practice with the evidence, we not only avoid unnecessary harms but also free up ICU beds for patients who truly need them.  

So next time a colleague reaches for a steroid “just in case,” ask: *Is there a real indication, or are we just following tradition?* The answer should steer us back to the fundamental principles of anaphylaxis care—epinephrine first, steroids last.
