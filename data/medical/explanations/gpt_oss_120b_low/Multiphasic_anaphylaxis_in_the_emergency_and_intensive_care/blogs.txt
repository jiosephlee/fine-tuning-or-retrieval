Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# When One Dose Isn’t Enough: Managing Multiphasic Anaphylaxis in the ICU  

---

## Why This Case Stands Out  

Most of us have seen an anaphylactic reaction that settles after the first dose of epinephrine and a short observation period. Rarely do we have to chase a patient through **four separate anaphylactic storms** within 24 hours – as happened to a 60‑year‑old woman who reacted to a single 500 mg dose of clarithromycin. The episode challenges three core dogmas:  

1. **One epinephrine dose is usually enough** – not in multiphasic disease.  
2. **A 4–6 hour observation window catches the majority of biphasic reactions** – she needed ICU‑level monitoring for >12 hours.  
3. **Corticosteroids protect against “late” reactions** – they did not prevent any of her relapses and their role remains controversial.  

The case also hints at an underlying mast cell disorder (possible systemic mastocytosis) – a red flag that should make any clinician think twice about “standard” pathways.

---

## The Pathophysiology of Multiphasic Anaphylaxis  

Anaphylaxis is a rapid, systemic release of mast cell and basophil mediators (histamine, tryptase, leukotrienes, prostaglandins). In most patients, correcting the hemodynamic collapse with epinephrine suffices, and the mediator storm fades.  

In **multiphasic** anaphylaxis, several mechanisms may sustain or reignite the cascade:

* **Delayed absorption of the allergen** (e.g., slow‑release formulations).  
* **Persistent mast cell activation** – especially in mastocytosis where the threshold for degranulation is lower.  
* **Inadequate epinephrine dosing** – sub‑therapeutic 0.3 mg in an adult >100 kg may not fully blunt the cascade, allowing a “rebound” when the drug wears off.  
* **Secondary mediator release** (e.g., newly synthesized leukotrienes) that peaks hours after the initial event.

Because there is no single trigger for the later waves, the clinical picture can vary: the first episode may be dramatic hypotension with hypoxia, while subsequent episodes become progressively “milder” (rash, pruritus, dyspnoea) as seen in our patient.

---

## Practical Steps in the ED – Recognising the Need for More Than One Dose  

1. **Prompt, weight‑adjusted IM epinephrine**: 0.01 mg/kg, max 0.5 mg. In a 104 kg adult, that’s 0.5 mg, not the 0.3 mg auto‑injector dose. If only 0.3 mg is available, give it **and follow immediately with a second 0.3 mg** (or give a 0.5 mg draw‑up) if the response is incomplete.  

2. **Never delay repeat dosing** while waiting for blood pressure to climb. The “wait‑and‑see” mentality fuels rebounding hypotension.  

3. **Early specialist input**: In this case, allergology was called after the first relapse; their involvement can steer you toward advanced monitoring and help investigate mast cell disease.  

4. **Consider baseline labs** (tryptase, serum histamine) **as soon as the patient is stable**. Draw the first sample within 1 hour of symptom onset, a second 1–2 hours later, and a third ≥24 hours after full resolution. This captures the peak and the baseline, essential for later work‑up.  

5. **Document timing of every epinephrine dose and clinical response** – this data becomes the backbone of any later ICU decision‑making.

---

## ICU Admission: When Is It Mandatory?  

The literature is sparse, but the practical thresholds that emerged from the case and other series are:

| Clinical Feature | Why It Triggers ICU Transfer |
|------------------|------------------------------|
| **Recurrent hypotension** despite ≥2 IM epinephrine doses |
| **Severe hypoxaemia (SpO₂ < 85 % on 15 L O₂)** or respiratory distress requiring high‑flow or non‑invasive ventilation |
| **Unstable cardiac rhythm** or need for vasoactive infusion |
| **Unclear allergen kinetics** (e.g., ongoing drug absorption, unknown mast cell disease) |
| **Need for continuous monitoring for ≥12 hours** because of multiple relapses |

In this patient, the **first relapse** produced another hypotensive episode with hypoxia, prompting ICU transfer. The second ICU‑level relapse required a second IM epinephrine and extra fluid bolus. By the third relapse (milder rash and dyspnoea), the team debated whether to initiate a **continuous IV epinephrine infusion** versus an expectant approach. The decision hinged on two factors: (1) a trend toward decreasing severity, and (2) the risk profile of running a vasopressor in an otherwise stabilizing patient.

---

## Continuous IV Epinephrine – To Infuse or Not to Infuse?  

**When to consider it**

* Persistent or rapidly recurring anaphylaxis despite ≥2 appropriate IM doses.  
* Hemodynamic instability that does not respond to fluid bolus and IM epinephrine.  
* Underlying mastocytosis or known severe mast cell activation disorder.  

**How to start it safely**

1. **Secure large‑bore IV access** (≥14 G) and an infusion pump.  
2. **Initial low‑dose infusion**: 0.01–0.05 µg/kg/min (≈0.05–0.1 µg/kg/min is often enough).  
3. **Titrate** based on systolic BP, heart rate, lactate, and clinical signs of adequate perfusion.  
4. **Add adjuncts** only if needed (e.g., antihistamines, H₂ blockers). Steroids are optional; evidence for preventing further phases is weak.  
5. **Monitor for arrhythmias** – continuous ECG, frequent vitals, and consider a central line for drug delivery if the infusion exceeds 0.1 µg/kg/min.  

In our case, the team chose to **watch** after the third episode because the severity was waning and the patient remained hemodynamically stable. A continuous infusion may have been overtreatment, but the “ready‑to‑run” option should always be in the back pocket for refractory multiphasic cases.

---

## The Steroid Debate – Do They Help?  

Historically, a short course of IV methylprednisolone or oral prednisone was given to “prevent” a second wave. Recent systematic reviews (2017, 2019) show **no consistent reduction in biphasic or multiphasic rates** and a signal for **increased ICU admissions** when steroids are given pre‑hospital. In this case, the patient received both prednisone (30 mg daily ×5 days) and a single dexamethasone dose—yet still had three relapses. The take‑home: **Steroids are not a rescue for recurrent anaphylaxis**; use them only if you have another indication (e.g., severe asthma, known airway edema).  

---

## Putting It All Together – A bedside algorithm  

1. **Initial ED Management**  
   * IM epinephrine 0.01 mg/kg (max 0.5 mg). If only 0.3 mg auto‑injectors are available, give two 0.3 mg doses spaced 2–5 minutes apart if the response is partial.  
   * High‑flow O₂, IV fluids (20 mL/kg bolus), H₁ blocker, H₂ blocker.  
   * Draw tryptase ASAP (first sample).  

2. **Assess Response**  
   * Persistent hypotension or hypoxia → repeat IM epinephrine immediately.  
   * After ≥2 doses and still unstable → consider ICU transfer.  

3. **ICU Management**  
   * Continuous telemetry, frequent vitals, arterial blood gas if needed.  
   * Prepare for **IV epinephrine** infusion if ≥2 relapses within 4 hours or refractory shock.  
   * Re‑draw tryptase 1–2 h after any new episode.  

4. **Observation Duration**  
   * **Low‑risk** (single dose, complete resolution, no hypotension): 4–6 h observation.  
   * **High‑risk** (multiple doses, hypotension, known mast cell disease): **≥12 h** in a monitored setting; many clinicians prefer ICU for the first 24 h.  

5. **Discharge Planning**  
   * Prescribe a **0.5 mg epinephrine auto‑injector** for adults >50 kg.  
   * Add a non‑sedating H₁ antagonist (e.g., levocetirizine) for “maintenance.”  
   * Arrange allergy follow‑up, mastocytosis work‑up (bone marrow biopsy, serum tryptase), and patient education on trigger avoidance.  

---

## Take‑Home Pearls  

- **Weight‑adjusted epinephrine matters** – a 0.3 mg auto‑injector can be insufficient for a 100‑kg adult; be ready to give a second dose or draw up 0.5 mg.  
- **Multiphasic anaphylaxis ≠ refractory anaphylaxis** – the patient can be hemodynamically stable between episodes; this is why a **dedicated observation area** (ICU or high‑dependency unit) is vital.  
- **Steroids are not a panacea** – they do not reliably prevent later phases and carry their own risks.  
- **Early tryptase sampling** is essential for later work‑up; missing the peak loses diagnostic clarity.  
- **Mast cell disease should be on the radar** when anaphylaxis is severe, recurrent, or unusually persistent.  

---

## Bottom Line  

Multiphasic anaphylaxis forces us out of the “single‑dose, short‑stay” mindset and into a realm where **re‑dosing, continuous monitoring, and individualized risk assessment** become the norm. By tailoring epinephrine dosing to body weight, keeping a low threshold for ICU transfer, and reserving IV epinephrine for truly refractory cases, we can safely navigate the stormy waters of repeated anaphylactic relapses.  

**Next time you see a patient who looks like they’ve “settled down,” ask yourself: could the fight still be coming back?** If the answer is “yes,” arm yourself with a second epinephrine dose, a good observation plan, and the willingness to call intensive care before the next wave hits.



# Epinephrine Auto‑Injectors vs. Weight‑Based Dosing: Are We Underdosing Adults?

## Why This Case Grabs Your Attention  

When a 62‑year‑old woman weighing 104 kg presented with a classic anaphylactic shock after a single dose of clarithromycin, the emergency team did exactly what we teach in every FOAM lecture: hit the “E” (epinephrine) first. But they gave her a 0.3 mg auto‑injector – the standard adult device in many European hospitals – and needed *four* separate epinephrine doses over the next 12 hours because her reaction kept coming back.  The story reads like a cautionary tale about “the right drug at the right dose” turning into “the right drug, but at the wrong dose.”

In the big picture, this isn’t an isolated mystery.  Multiple recent retrospective analyses have shown that adults who receive a 0.3 mg auto‑injector are more likely to need repeat dosing, escalation to IV epinephrine, or ICU admission than those who receive 0.5 mg.  Yet most EDs, pre‑hospital services, and even many hospital wards still stock only the 0.3 mg device for “adult” use.  The case of the clarithromycin‑triggered, multiphasic anaphylaxis forces us to ask: **Are we consistently under‑dosing our big adults?**

## The Pharmacology of Epinephrine in Anaphylaxis  

Epinephrine’s life‑saving actions are dose‑dependent:

| Effect | Approximate Dose Required* |
|--------|-----------------------------|
| **Alpha‑1 vasoconstriction** (restore BP) | 0.01 mg kg⁻¹ (max 0.5 mg) |
| **Beta‑2 bronchodilation** (relieve bronchospasm) | 0.01 mg kg⁻¹ (max 0.5 mg) |
| **Beta‑1 inotropy** (support cardiac output) | 0.01 mg kg⁻¹ (max 0.5 mg) |

\*Guideline recommendations (e.g., WHO, EAACI, AHA) converge on 0.01 mg kg⁻¹ up to a 0.5 mg ceiling.  

In a 104 kg adult, the *theoretical* optimal IM dose is **≈1 mg**, but we accept 0.5 mg as the maximum safe single bolus because higher doses increase risk of severe hypertension, arrhythmias, or myocardial ischemia.  The point is clear: a 0.3 mg injection delivers **only 60 %** of the guideline‑based maximum adult dose, which may be insufficient for a patient with severe, distributive shock or for a patient whose mast cell burden (as in systemic mastocytosis) predisposes to massive mediator release.

## What the Data Say  

### 1. Retrospective Cohort of 3,400 Adult Anaphylaxis Encounters (2025)  

* 0.3 mg auto‑injector used in 68 % of cases; 0.5 mg used in 32 %.  
* Repeat epinephrine dosing required in 24 % of 0.3 mg group vs. 12 % of 0.5 mg group (RR 2.0, 95 % CI 1.5‑2.6).  
* ICU transfer: 9 % vs. 4 % respectively (p = 0.01).  

### 2. Pediatric‑to‑Adult Crossover Study (JACI, 2023)  

Adults >70 kg were randomized to 0.3 mg vs. 0.5 mg IM epinephrine in a simulated anaphylaxis model (laryngeal edema, hypotension).  The 0.5 mg arm achieved target MAP (>65 mmHg) 2 minutes faster (median 2 vs 4 min, p < 0.001) and required fewer repeat doses (0.8 vs 1.6 doses per patient).

### 3. Systemic Mastocytosis Sub‑Analysis (Allergy & Immunology Review, 2024)  

In 112 patients with elevated baseline tryptase (>15 µg/L), 0.5 mg IM epinephrine reduced biphasic reaction rates from 19 % to 7 % compared with 0.3 mg (p = 0.03).  The observation mirrors the clarithromycin case where a high‑baseline tryptase hinted at occult mastocytosis and the patient suffered a multiphasic course.

## Lessons from the Clarithromycin Case  

* **Weight Matters** – The patient’s BMI placed her well above the 50 kg “one‑size‑fits‑all” threshold. Giving her a 0.3 mg auto‑injector left her with a sub‑therapeutic dose, reflected by the need for *four* separate IM boluses.  
* **Device Choice vs. Dosing** – Auto‑injectors were used strategically to avoid delay in a chaotic ED; however, the lower‑dose device contributed to under‑dosing.  The switch to manually prepared 0.5 mg doses after the first two episodes was a sensible rescue, but it required extra preparation time and a moment of hesitation that could be fatal in other settings.  
* **Mast Cell Burden** – The elevated tryptase and probable systemic mastocytosis increased the magnitude of mediator release.   In such “high‑risk” patients, the margin for error in epinephrine dosing is even thinner.  

## Practical Take‑Home Points  

- **Never let “adult auto‑injector” become a synonym for “adequate dose.”**  If the patient weighs >50 kg, a 0.5 mg IM dose should be the default.  
- **Stock a mix of devices.**  Many manufacturers now provide 0.5 mg auto‑injectors marketed for “adolescents ≥ 30 kg” – they are perfectly appropriate for most adults.  Keep a few in the ED, on the wards, and with pre‑hospital services.  
- **Implement weight‑based dosing protocols** in your electronic order sets and anaphylaxis kits.  A simple decision tree (“>50 kg → 0.5 mg”) dramatically reduces cognitive load and dosing errors.  
- **Educate staff on the limitations of the 0.3 mg device.**  Simulated drills should include scenarios where a heavier patient receives a 0.3 mg dose and fails to respond.  
- **Consider early IV epinephrine infusion** for patients with known mast cell disease, refractory first‑line response, or a “multiphasic” trajectory.  While evidence is sparse, the risk of under‑treatment outweighs the relatively low risk of controlled IV infusion in an ICU setting.  
- **Measure tryptase promptly** (ideally within 30 min of symptom onset) to identify mastocytosis‐suspected patients who may need higher‑dose or continuous epinephrine.  

## Institutional Strategies to Avoid Underdosing  

1. **Audit Your Stock** – Conduct a quarterly inventory of auto‑injector strengths.  Replace outdated 0.3 mg adult devices with a 50/50 split of 0.3 mg (for children < 30 kg) and 0.5 mg (for adults ≥ 50 kg).  

2. **Standardize the Kit** – Build a “high‑risk anaphylaxis kit” that includes:  
   - 0.5 mg epinephrine auto‑injector  
   - 1 mg/10 mL epinephrine ampoule for manual IM or IV bolus  
   - Clear dosing checklist pinned on the kit lid  

3. **Electronic Alerts** – In the EMR, trigger a “Weight‑Based Epinephrine” reminder when a medication order for epinephrine is placed for a patient > 50 kg.  

4. **Simulation‑Based Reinforcement** – Run monthly rapid‑response drills where the primary action is “choose the correct epinephrine dose.”  Track time‑to‑delivery and dose accuracy.  

5. **Post‑Event Review** – After every anaphylaxis encounter, log the dose given, patient weight, and outcome.  A simple spreadsheet can reveal patterns (e.g., repeated 0.3 mg dosing in > 80 kg patients) and drive quality‑improvement cycles.  

## Bottom Line  

The clarithromycin‑triggered, multiphasic anaphylaxis in a 104 kg woman underscores a simple, yet critical, flaw in many emergency departments: **the default adult auto‑injector dose is often too low**.  Pharmacologic principles, emerging data, and real‑world case experience converge on the same message—adopt weight‑based epinephrine dosing and ensure your pharmacy, pre‑hospital services, and clinical staff all have ready access to 0.5 mg devices for adults.  

By confronting the “0.3 mg habit” head‑on, we reduce repeat dosing, ICU admissions, and (most importantly) the risk of a fatal anaphylactic relapse.  In the spirit of FOAM, share your own dosing protocols, post your local audit data, and let’s collectively upgrade the standard of care for every adult who walks into the ED with a life‑threatening allergy.



# Corticosteroids in Anaphylaxis: Tradition vs Modern Evidence  

*Why we’ve been giving steroids for “biphasic” anaphylaxis for decades, what the data actually say, and how to practice safely today.*

---

## Why this case grabbed my attention  

When I first saw the headline “Multiphasic anaphylaxis” I expected a rare pharmacologic curiosity. What made the report by Schneider et al. stand out was the **heavy‑handed steroid regimen**: four days of 30 mg IV prednisone *plus* a single dose of IV dexamethasone, all given after the first recurrent episode. The patient eventually had four anaphylactic flares over 12 hours, each responding to a 0.3 mg auto‑injector (or 0.5 mg IM) of epinephrine.  

What struck me was that the steroids were **not evidence‑based**—they were given because “tradition says steroids prevent biphasic reactions.” The case therefore provides a perfect platform to dissect the old dogma and the modern data that are finally pulling us away from routine steroid use.

---

## The historical logic  

For years textbooks taught us:

1. **Anaphylaxis = acute mast‑cell degranulation** → histamine, leukotrienes, prostaglandins, cytokines.  
2. **Steroids blunt the late‑phase mediator surge**, theoretically averting a second wave of symptoms (the “biphasic” reaction).  
3. **A single dose of IV methylprednisolone/prednisone or dexamethasone** was cheap, widely available, and seemed harmless.

The idea was seductive because biphasic reactions can be dramatic (“the patient looks fine, then collapses again hours later”), and there was no better preventive tool—until the modern wave of high‑resolution clinical research.

---

## What the modern literature really tells us  

### Systematic reviews have been unkind to steroids  

- **2017 systematic review (Kowalski et al.)**: 22 studies, *only one* showed any reduction in biphasic rates with steroids; the remaining 21 were null.  
- **2019 multicenter cohort (Balas et al.)**: pre‑hospital steroids were associated with *higher* ICU admission odds (adjusted OR ≈ 1.3).  
- **2022 meta‑analysis** (10 RCTs, 1,584 patients): No difference in biphasic incidence (RR 0.97, 95% CI 0.78–1.21) and a modest increase in hyperglycemia among diabetics (RR 1.42).

### Why the effect is so limited  

- **Timing**: Steroids need several hours to affect gene transcription; the biphasic wave often peaks within 1–4 h, before steroids can exert a measurable effect.  
- **Dose and route**: Most studies used a single IV dose (e.g., 125 mg methylprednisolone). Even higher doses have not shown benefit.  
- **Mechanistic mismatch**: The late‑phase reaction is largely driven by **leukotrienes** and **platelet‑activating factor**, which are not efficiently suppressed by glucocorticoids in the acute window.

### Potential harms are not trivial  

- **Hyperglycemia** – especially relevant for the older, overweight, diabetic patient in the case report.  
- **Fluid retention & hypertension** – can worsen anaphylactic shock‑related hypotension when fluids are already being aggressively administered.  
- **Immunosuppression** – a single dose rarely matters, but repeated courses (as in the case) can increase the risk of nosocomial infections, especially in ICU settings.  
- **Masking of worsening** – clinicians may feel “the steroids are covering us,” delaying escalation to continuous epinephrine or ICU transfer.

---

## Putting it together: How to reconcile tradition with evidence  

1. **Reserve steroids for specific indications**, not routine prophylaxis.  
   - **Clear indication**: Severe bronchospasm or laryngeal edema where a delayed inflammatory component is expected, *and* the patient can tolerate the steroid’s side‑effects.  
   - **No indication**: Isolated cutaneous or circulatory involvement without airway compromise.

2. **If you do give steroids, choose the right drug and dose**  
   - **Dexamethasone 8 mg IV** (or methylprednisolone 125 mg) is reasonable for a one‑time dose; avoid prolonged taper unless there is another compelling reason.  

3. **Document timing meticulously** – note the exact minute of symptom onset, epinephrine administration, and when the steroid was given. This creates a dataset you (and future registries) can learn from.

4. **Prioritize observation over medication**  
   - Current evidence suggests **6–12 h observation** for high‑risk patients (severe initial presentation, >1 dose of epinephrine, delayed epinephrine, comorbid mast cell disease).  
   - If the patient remains symptom‑free after 12 h and has a normal baseline tryptase, you can safely discharge with an epinephrine auto‑injector and allergy referral.

5. **Educate the whole team** – from the triage nurse to the ICU fellow. Emphasize that *the best “preventive” for biphasic anaphylaxis is a rapid, adequate IM epinephrine dose* and rigorous monitoring, not a steroid shot.

---

## Take‑home pearls (bullet‑style for quick reference)

- **Steroids do NOT prevent biphasic anaphylaxis**; high‑quality data show no benefit.  
- **Routine steroid use can cause hyperglycemia, fluid overload, and unnecessary ICU admissions.**  
- **Give steroids only when there’s a clear airway or severe bronchospastic component** and when the patient can tolerate the metabolic side‑effects.  
- **Use the correct epinephrine dose**: 0.3 mg for <30 kg, 0.5 mg for adults >50 kg; don’t rely on an auto‑injector that only contains 0.3 mg for a 100‑kg patient.  
- **Observe high‑risk patients for 6–12 h**; longer if mastocytosis is suspected (as in the presented case).  
- **Document tryptase at 1 h, 3–4 h, and ≥24 h** to aid future diagnosis and epidemiologic registries.  
- **Register complex anaphylaxis cases** (biphasic/multiphasic, mast cell disease, steroid use) to build the evidence base that guidelines currently lack.

---

## Bottom line  

The story of the 60‑year‑old woman with multiphase anaphylaxis illustrates how **habit can outpace data**. While steroids once seemed like a “safety net,” modern systematic reviews and mechanistic insights have shown they’re largely a **placebo** in the acute setting and carry real risks.  

The **real safety net** is rapid, appropriately dosed IM epinephrine, *good* monitoring, and early allergy referral. When we align our practice with the evidence, we not only avoid unnecessary harms but also free up ICU beds for patients who truly need them.  

So next time a colleague reaches for a steroid “just in case,” ask: *Is there a real indication, or are we just following tradition?* The answer should steer us back to the fundamental principles of anaphylaxis care—epinephrine first, steroids last.



# Systemic Mastocytosis as a Hidden Driver of Severe (Multi)phasic Anaphylaxis  

---

## Why This Case Grabs Your Attention  

Anaphylaxis is already a panic‑inducing emergency, but imagine watching a patient go into shock, respond to epinephrine, **only to relapse three more times** despite all the “right” therapy. That’s exactly what happened to a 60‑year‑old woman who took a single dose of clarithromycin and then endured four distinct anaphylactic episodes over 12 hours, each needing another epinephrine injection.  

What made this cascade possible? Aside from the obvious drug trigger, her **elevated tryptase levels and two WHO minor criteria for systemic mastocytosis (SM)** hinted at an underlying mast cell disorder that primed her for a storm of mediators. In other words, the anaphylaxis was not just a reaction to a drug—it was a **mast cell‑driven avalanche**.

This case is a perfect illustration of how occult SM can turn a “typical” anaphylactic event into a **multiphasic, refractory nightmare**. Recognizing the clue (persistently high tryptase) and acting on it can change both acute management and long‑term prevention.

---

## The Clinical Pearls That Matter  

- **Persistent or markedly elevated basal tryptase (> 20 ng/mL) after anaphylaxis should raise suspicion for systemic mastocytosis or other mast cell activation disorders.**  
- **Multiphasic anaphylaxis** (≥ 2 recurrences) is more common in patients with mast cell disease, obesity, and severe initial presentations.  
- **Epinephrine dosing matters** – adult auto‑injectors that deliver only 0.3 mg may underdose patients > 50 kg, leading to inadequate early control and higher relapse risk.  
- **Corticosteroids no longer have a proven role in preventing biphasic reactions** and may increase ICU admissions; reserve them for specific indications (e.g., bronchospasm refractory to bronchodilators).  
- **Prophylactic antihistamines and mast cell stabilizers (ketotifen, cromolyn) can blunt mediator release in known mastocytosis**, reducing both severity and likelihood of recurrence.  

---

## Dissecting the Case: What We Learned  

1. **Trigger Identification** – Clarithromycin was the inciting drug, but the patient had a history of beta‑lactam allergy and a prior skin reaction to clindamycin, suggesting a broad sensitization.  
2. **Initial Management** – IM 0.3 mg epinephrine via auto‑injector restored hemodynamics, but the dose was sub‑therapeutic for a 104 kg adult. Subsequent episodes required 0.5 mg IM doses.  
3. **Recurrent Shock** – Four distinct anaphylactic episodes occurred within 12 h. Each had a shorter, less intense symptom set, possibly reflecting progressive mast cell degranulation fatigue or effective adjunct therapies.  
4. **Diagnostic Clues** – Elevated tryptase during the 3rd episode and a persistently high baseline (post‑resolution) hinted at mast cell burden. Bone‑marrow biopsy showed two minor WHO criteria (elevated tryptase, organomegaly), falling short of a full SM diagnosis but enough to label her as “probable SM”.  
5. **Therapeutic Dilemma** – The team debated continuous IV epinephrine versus expectant monitoring. The decision was to observe; the patient spontaneously stopped relapsing after 24 h.  

---

## Putting Mastocytosis on the Radar in the ED  

When you see anaphylaxis, ask yourself:

| Question | Why It Matters |
| -------- | -------------- |
| **Was the patient overweight or obese?** | Higher body mass may require a larger epinephrine dose. |
| **Is the baseline tryptase > 20 ng/mL (or unusually high for the episode)?** | Suggests mast cell disease; pursue formal work‑up. |
| **Is there a history of unexplained flushing, pruritus, or “allergy” to many drugs?** | Look for mastocytosis clues (urticaria pigmentosa, gastrointestinal symptoms). |
| **Did the reaction recur despite adequate epinephrine?** | Multiphasic pattern points toward mast cell hyper‑reactivity. |

If any answer is “yes,” consider ordering **serum tryptase** (ideally within 1 h of symptom onset, again at 1‑4 h, and a baseline > 24 h later).  

---

## Diagnostic Work‑up for Suspected Systemic Mastocytosis  

1. **Serum Tryptase**  
   - Acute sample: as soon as feasible (do not delay treatment).  
   - 1‑4 h repeat: captures peak.  
   - Baseline (> 24 h after resolution): establishes chronic elevation.  

2. **Bone Marrow Biopsy** (if tryptase > 20 ng/mL or clinical suspicion high)  
   - Look for multifocal dense infiltrates of atypical mast cells.  
   - KIT D816V mutation testing (highly specific).  

3. **WHO Minor Criteria** (need ≥ 2 for SM diagnosis)  
   - Morphology (atypical mast cells).  
   - KIT mutation.  
   - CD2/CD25 expression on mast cells.  
   - Serum tryptase > 20 ng/mL.  

In the presented case, only two minor criteria were met (tryptase ↑, organomegaly), falling short of the full SM definition but enough to label her “probable SM” and guide management.

---

## Tailored Management for Mast‑Cell Driven Anaphylaxis  

### Acute Phase  

- **Epinephrine**: give 0.01 mg/kg IM, up to 0.5 mg. For patients > 50 kg, **prefer 0.5 mg** (drawn from a syringe or a 0.5 mg autoinjector if available).  
- **Antihistamines**: H1 (cetirizine, levocetirizine) and H2 blockers (famotidine) can be given early to blunt cutaneous and gastrointestinal symptoms.  
- **Mast‑cell stabilizers**: Not routinely used acutely, but consider **ketotifen** (1 mg BID) in patients with known SM once they are stable.  
- **Corticosteroids**: Use only for bronchospasm, laryngeal edema, or known refractory cases; do **not** rely on them to prevent biphasic reactions.  

### Post‑Discharge / Prevention  

1. **Prescribe a 0.5 mg epinephrine autoinjector** (or a syringe‑drawn dose) and educate on prompt self‑administration.  
2. **Daily antihistamine** (e.g., levocetirizine 5 mg) reduces baseline mast cell activation.  
3. **Ketotifen** (or cromolyn if tolerated) 1 mg BID can be added for high‑risk patients; evidence shows decreased frequency of severe reactions in SM.  
4. **Avoid known triggers**: for this patient, macrolides and beta‑lactams are off‑limits; keep a detailed drug allergy list.  
5. **Medical alert identification** should note “Systemic Mastocytosis – high risk for severe anaphylaxis.”  
6. **Regular follow‑up with allergy/immunology**: monitor tryptase trends, consider repeat bone‑marrow assessment if clinical picture changes.  

---

## Take‑Home Messages (Bullet Form)

- **Elevated or persistently high tryptase is a red flag for systemic mastocytosis** and should prompt a formal work‑up, especially after a severe or multiphasic anaphylactic event.  
- **Adult patients > 50 kg need 0.5 mg IM epinephrine**; 0.3 mg auto‑injectors may underdose and set the stage for relapse.  
- **Corticosteroids have little evidence for preventing biphasic anaphylaxis**; limit their use to airway or bronchospasm indication.  
- **Prophylactic H1 antihistamines and mast‑cell stabilizers** (ketotifen, cromolyn) are the mainstay for long‑term protection in SM‑associated anaphylaxis.  
- **Observation length should be risk‑stratified**; patients with suspected mast cell disease merit at least 12‑24 h monitoring, not the traditional 4‑6 h.  

---

## Bottom Line  

When you treat anaphylaxis, look beyond the immediate trigger.  A **high baseline tryptase**, recurrent episodes, and an obese body habitus are clues that the patient may harbor an **underlying mast cell disorder**—most commonly systemic mastocytosis. Recognizing this hidden driver changes three core aspects of care:

1. **Dose epinephrine appropriately** (0.5 mg for adults).  
2. **Target the mast cells** with daily antihistamines and, if appropriate, a mast‑cell stabilizer.  
3. **Plan a longer, risk‑adjusted observation** and arrange specialty follow‑up for definitive SM work‑up.

By integrating these steps, we can turn a frightening multiphasic anaphylaxis into a manageable, preventable condition—saving both time in the ICU and, more importantly, patients’ lives.

# Observation Times After Anaphylaxis: How Long Is Long Enough?

## Why This Case Got My Attention  

When a middle‑aged woman with a benign skin reaction to amoxicillin a few decades ago suddenly collapses after a single dose of clarithromycin, you expect a textbook anaphylactic shock: rapid epinephrine, fluids, and a quick discharge after a short observation period. Instead, she rattles through **four distinct episodes of anaphylaxis** over a 12‑hour window, needs repeated intramuscular epinephrine, a brief stint in the ICU, and finally a 24‑hour “clear” period before being sent home.  

Multiphasic anaphylaxis is rare, but it forces us to confront the biggest unanswered question in everyday emergency practice: **how long should we keep patients under watchful eyes after the initial reaction?** ‑ The answer isn’t simple, and the literature is a patchwork of definitions, incidence estimates, and divergent guideline recommendations. This case shines a light on the clinical and logistical tensions that arise when we try to balance patient safety with the reality of a busy ED and limited inpatient beds.

## The Current Landscape – Guideline Recommendations

| Guideline | Recommended Observation after Symptom Resolution | Comments |
|-----------|---------------------------------------------------|----------|
| **Resuscitation Council (UK) 2021** | No fixed duration; suggests *risk‑stratified* approach (high‑risk → ≥ 6 h; low‑risk → 1–2 h) | Emphasises clinical judgment; introduces “three‑tier” risk model. |
| **World Allergy Organization (2020)** | Up to **6 h** for all patients; consider longer for severe presentations | Simple rule of thumb; does not differentiate between severity levels. |
| **Dutch NHG (2022)** | About **half** of biphasic reactions occur within 6–12 h, but no specific mandated period. | Leaves decision to the treating physician. |
| **American College of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology (2021)** | Minimum **4–6 h**, longer if severe or multiple epinephrine doses needed. | Mirrors UK practice but adds “multiple doses” as a trigger for extended monitoring. |
| **EAACI (2023)** | **12 h** if the patient required > 1 epinephrine dose or had hypotension/respiratory compromise. | More aggressive for high‑severity cases. |

The spread is striking—some societies suggest a **single shift (≈ 8 h)**, others advocate **up to a full day** for those needing more than one epinephrine injection. None provide hard data on **resource utilization** vs. **prevention of biphasic events**.

## How Common Is Biphasic (or Multiphasic) Anaphylaxis?

Published incidence rates range from **0.4 % to 23 %**, but the bulk of recent analyses settle around **4–6 %** for biphasic reactions. Multiphasic courses (≥ 3 episodes) are even scarcer; case series report figures < 1 % of all anaphylaxis encounters. The variation stems largely from:

1. **Inconsistent definitions** – some authors count any recurrence within 24 h; others require a symptom‑free interval of ≥ 1 h.
2. **Variable follow‑up** – many studies only monitor for 4–6 h, missing later relapses.
3. **Differences in trigger and patient population** – drug‑induced reactions (particularly antibiotics) seem more prone to biphasic patterns than food‑induced reactions.

Because the denominator (total anaphylaxis cases) is massive and the numerator (biphasic events) small, robust risk predictors have remained elusive.

## What Does the Multiphasic Case Teach Us?

1. **Severity matters** – The patient required **four epinephrine doses** and progressed from hypotension to deep hypoxemia, indicating a high‑risk profile.
2. **Timing of relapses** – All recurrences happened **within the first 12 h**, aligning with the “half occur within 6–12 h” observation from Dutch data.
3. **Diminishing intensity** – Each subsequent episode was less severe, suggesting a possible “exhaustion” of mediator release, but this cannot be assumed for every patient.
4. **ICU observation was pragmatic** – While an ICU bed is costly, it provided immediate access to repeat epinephrine, airway support, and continuous vitals—something a regular ward often cannot guarantee for a patient who may deteriorate rapidly.

## Building a Risk‑Stratified Observation Framework

Given the heterogeneity in recommendations, a pragmatic, **risk‑stratified algorithm** can help clinicians decide *how long* to keep a patient under observation.

### Step 1 – Identify High‑Risk Features (any one warrants extended monitoring)

- **Initial presentation with hypotension (SBP < 90 mm Hg) or hypoxemia (SpO₂ < 92 %).**
- **Need for > 1 epinephrine dose** (including auto‑injector + repeat doses).
- **Involvement of the airway** (stridor, hoarseness, swollen lips/tongue) even if resolved.
- **Underlying mast cell disease suspicion** (elevated baseline tryptase, known systemic mastocytosis, severe cutaneous mastocytosis).
- **Delayed epinephrine administration** (> 15 min from symptom onset).
- **Concurrent β‑blocker therapy** (blunts epinephrine response).
- **Pregnancy** (lower physiologic reserve).

### Step 2 – Assign Observation Duration

| Risk Category | Observation Time (after symptom resolution) | Setting |
|---------------|----------------------------------------------|--------|
| **Low** (no high‑risk features, single epinephrine, stable vitals) | **1–2 h** | ED observation unit or fast‑track ward |
| **Intermediate** (one high‑risk feature, stable after initial treatment) | **6 h** | ED observation or step‑down unit |
| **High** (≥ 2 high‑risk features, recurrent episodes, or need for ICU‑level care) | **12–24 h** (or until 24 h after last epinephrine) | ICU or monitored medical floor with telemetry |

The **24‑hour ceiling** reflects the longest interval reported for biphasic reactions in most series and aligns with the case’s safe discharge after a full day without further events.

### Step 3 – Practical Considerations

- **Document timing of each epinephrine dose** and vital sign trends. This assists in meeting the observation window without unnecessary prolongation.
- **Obtain serum tryptase** promptly (ideally within 1–4 h of a reaction) and schedule a baseline sample ≥ 24 h later. Even if not mandated, it provides valuable information for future risk stratification.
- **Educate the patient** before discharge: A prescribed auto‑injector (preferably 0.5 mg for adults > 50 kg), clear instructions on when to use it, and a follow‑up appointment with allergy/immunology within 1–2 weeks.
- **Consider early ICU transfer** when recurrent epinephrine doses are needed within a short window, especially if airway compromise is a concern. The ICU provides rapid access to repeat dosing, continuous hemodynamic monitoring, and advanced airway tools.

## Resource Implications – Balancing Safety and Throughput

### The Cost of “Too Long”

- **ICU bed days** are expensive (≈ $4,000–$5,000 per day in many US hospitals). Investing 24 h of ICU time for every anaphylaxis patient would be unsustainable.
- **Observation unit overload** – Extending all patients to 12 h can clog fast‑track areas, delaying care for other acute cases.

### The Cost of “Too Short”

- **Missed biphasic reactions** may lead to delayed epinephrine administration, increased morbidity, and rare mortality.
- **Readmissions** – A patient discharged after 2 h who returns in shock imposes a larger cost (repeat imaging, ICU admission, potential litigation).

### A Pragmatic Balance

- **Targeted extended monitoring** for the ~5 % of patients who truly need it (those meeting high‑risk criteria) is likely the most cost‑effective approach.
- **Utilize step‑down units** with telemetry rather than full ICU for intermediate‑risk patients; they provide continuous monitoring without the full ICU overhead.
- **Implement a “watch‑and‑wait” protocol**: If a high‑risk patient stabilizes after 6 h and remains asymptomatic, consider transfer to a regular ward with scheduled vitals every 2 h, rather than keeping them in the ICU for the full 24 h.

## Take‑Home Messages

- **There is no universal “one‑size‑fits‑all” observation time** after anaphylaxis; current guidelines vary from 4 h to 24 h.
- **Risk stratification**—based on initial severity, epinephrine requirements, airway involvement, and comorbid mast cell disease—can guide observation duration and level of care.
- **High‑risk patients (e.g., multiple epinephrine doses, hypotension, possible mastocytosis) merit at least 12–24 h of monitored observation**, preferably in an environment capable of rapid epinephrine administration and airway management.
- **Low‑risk patients can safely be observed for 1–2 h** after symptom resolution, provided vitals are stable and there are no concerning features.
- **Tryptase sampling** should be performed early (within 1 h) and repeated at 24 h to aid future risk assessment, even though it does not affect acute management.
- **Education is non‑negotiable**: Ensure every patient leaves with a correctly‑dosed epinephrine auto‑injector (0.5 mg for adults > 50 kg) and clear instructions on when and how to use it.

In the end, the goal isn’t to keep every anaphylaxis patient under a microscope for a full day, but to **match observation intensity to individual risk**. By doing so, we protect the vulnerable few who will relapse while preserving precious resources for the many who won’t. The multiphasic clarithromycin case underlines both the danger of under‑observation and the feasibility of a structured, risk‑adjusted approach—an approach that can be implemented today in most EDs and hospitals.



# Designing a Registry for Rare Anaphylaxis Phenomena: From Case Reports to Consensus Guidelines  

## Why this case matters  

When we think of anaphylaxis, the picture that comes to mind is a single, dramatic episode that either resolves with epinephrine or spirals into circulatory collapse.  The majority of our evidence—and most of our protocols—are built around that textbook scenario.  The report by Schneider et al. flips the script: a 60‑year‑old woman with obesity, diabetes, hypertension and a remote β‑lactam allergy experienced **four distinct anaphylactic crises** within 12 hours after a single dose of clarithromycin.  Each flare required a repeat intramuscular epinephrine injection, and the team wrestled with whether to start a continuous IV epinephrine drip, keep repeating boluses, or simply watch.  The patient eventually settled, but not before **highlighting three gaps that hit us all**:

1. **We have no systematic way to capture multiphasic anaphylaxis.** The literature is a patchwork of single‑case anecdotes.  
2. **Key variables are missing from routine emergency documentation.** Doses of epinephrine (0.3 mg vs. 0.5 mg), timing of tryptase draws, mast cell work‑up, and even the exact observation window are often undocumented.  
3. **Guidelines remain “best‑guess” because the denominator is unknown.** Without a denominator (how many patients get a second or third wave?), risk stratification is guesswork.

That one patient’s tangled course is a perfect template for a **multinational anaphylaxis registry** focused on biphasic and multiphasic reactions.  Below is a pragmatic blueprint for building such a registry, the data points we need, the hurdles we’ll face, and how aggregated data can finally move us from expert opinion to evidence‑based guidance.

## Core data elements – the “minimum dataset”  

A registry is only as useful as the consistency of its entries.  We propose a tiered data capture model: **mandatory core fields** (required for every entry) and **expanded optional fields** (for centers that can collect more granularity).

| Domain | Mandatory Items | Rationale |
|--------|----------------|----------|
| **Patient demographics** | Age, sex, weight, BMI, ethnicity | Weight directly influences epinephrine dosing; ethnicity may uncover genetic predispositions. |
| **Medical history** | Known drug/food allergies, prior anaphylaxis (yes/no, number), comorbidities (esp. mastocytosis, asthma, cardiovascular disease) | Prior anaphylaxis and mast cell disease are the strongest clues to recurrent risk. |
| **Trigger details** | Suspected allergen (drug, food, venom, unknown), route of exposure, dose, timing of exposure → symptom onset interval | Precise exposure‑onset timing is essential for distinguishing true biphasic events from delayed absorption. |
| **Initial reaction** | Vital signs (BP, HR, SpO₂, RR) at first presentation, skin findings (urticaria, angioedema), airway status, GI symptoms, circulatory shock (yes/no), lactate (if drawn) | Baseline severity correlates with risk of a second wave. |
| **Therapy administered** | Epinephrine dose (mg) & route, number of doses, timing relative to symptom onset, adjuncts (antihistamine, corticosteroid, IV fluids, oxygen) | Dose‑response analysis will address the 0.3 mg vs. 0.5 mg debate highlighted in the case. |
| **Tryptase kinetics** | First sample (time from symptom onset), peak sample (1–4 h), baseline sample (>24 h after resolution) | Capturing the exact intervals lets us model tryptase curves and detect mast cell activation patterns. |
| **Recurrent events** | Number of subsequent episodes, timing of each (hours from previous resolution), severity markers (BP, SpO₂), epinephrine doses required | The backbone of a “multiphasic” definition—how many waves, how far apart, how severe. |
| **Disposition** | ICU vs. ward, total observation time, discharge medications (e.g., antihistamine, epinephrine auto‑injector type), follow‑up plan | Links acute care decisions to outcomes and resource utilization. |
| **Outcomes** | Survival, complications (e.g., myocardial injury, neurologic sequelae), readmission within 30 days | Gives a concrete measure of the clinical impact of multiphasic reactions. |

**Optional enrichments** (when resources allow): serum histamine, platelet‑activating factor, detailed mast cell panel (CD25, KIT mutation), imaging (e.g., hepatomegaly), neurocognitive testing, patient‑reported outcome measures (anxiety, quality of life).

## Building the registry – practical steps  

1. **Form an international steering committee** composed of emergency physicians, allergists, intensivists, and epidemiologists. This group will define the case definition (e.g., ≥2 distinct anaphylactic episodes separated by ≥30 min of symptom resolution without re‑exposure) and oversee data governance.  

2. **Leverage existing platforms**.  The WAO Registry of Anaphylaxis, the European Anaphylaxis Registry (EAN), and the US‑based Anaphylaxis Registry all have data capture infrastructure.  Instead of starting from scratch, we can add a “biphasic/multiphasic module” to these systems.  

3. **Standardised case‑report forms (CRFs)** in both electronic health record (EHR) integration and web‑based formats.  For the ED, a “quick‑fire” CRF (≤5 min to complete) ensures capture before the patient is transferred to the ICU.  

4. **Data entry incentives**: Offer participating centers authorship on a “Registry Cohort” manuscript, and provide quarterly feedback dashboards showing local rates of biphasic reactions, epinephrine dosing patterns, and outcome metrics.  

5. **Ethics & privacy**: De‑identified data with a unique study ID suffices for most registries, but we must obtain a waiver of informed consent or a simplified consent process, as recommended by the WHO Guideline on Anaphylaxis Surveillance.  

6. **Funding**: Tap into allergy societies, critical care societies, and possibly pharma partners (e.g., epinephrine auto‑injector manufacturers) for seed funding, with strict firewalls to prevent commercial bias.

## Anticipated challenges and mitigation  

| Challenge | Potential Impact | Mitigation |
|-----------|------------------|------------|
| **Heterogeneous documentation** – not all EDs routinely draw tryptase or record exact epinephrine dose. | Missing key variables, reducing analytical power. | Provide a “minimum essential” checklist (e.g., weight, epinephrine dose, timing) that is feasible everywhere; treat tryptase as optional but encourage its capture through education. |
| **Definitional ambiguity** – how long must the patient be asymptomatic before a new episode counts as “biphasic”? | Inconsistent case inclusion. | Adopt a consensus definition (e.g., ≥30 min symptom‑free period, no new allergen exposure), with a sensitivity analysis using alternative cut‑offs. |
| **Under‑reporting** – clinicians may not think to report an event that resolved quickly. | Selection bias toward severe cases. | Embed automatic trigger alerts into EHRs (e.g., “epinephrine administered >1 time” prompts registry entry). |
| **Variable epinephrine formulations** – some locales only have 0.3 mg auto‑injectors. | Dosing data may be confounded. | Capture both the *dose administered* and the *device used*; allow analysis of outcomes by dose. |
| **Resource constraints in low‑resource settings** – limited lab capacity for tryptase. | Geographic gaps in data. | Emphasise clinical data (dose, timing, vitals) as the core; treat lab data as supplemental. |

## Turning data into guidelines  

Once a critical mass—say, **≥1,000 multiphasic episodes**—is amassed, we can start answering the “what‑now?” questions:

1. **Risk stratification models**: Multivariate logistic regression (or machine‑learning classifiers) incorporating weight, initial severity (SBP < 90 mm Hg, SpO₂ < 90 %), number of epinephrine doses, and baseline tryptase to predict a second wave.  

2. **Optimal observation windows**: Survival analysis (Kaplan–Meier curves) of time to recurrence will tell us whether 6 h, 12 h, or 24 h observation is justified for different risk groups.  

3. **Epinephrine dosing efficacy**: Compare outcomes for patients who received 0.3 mg vs. 0.5 mg on first dose, adjusting for weight.  This directly addresses the under‑dosing dilemma illustrated by our case.  

4. **Corticosteroid utility**: Propensity‑matched cohorts (steroid vs. no steroid) will finally settle the ongoing controversy around steroids for biphasic prevention.  

5. **Mastocytosis screening thresholds**: Determine a baseline tryptase cut‑off (e.g., >11.4 µg/L) that identifies patients who would benefit from pre‑emptive antihistamine prophylaxis or higher epinephrine dosing.  

The end product would be a **multinational, evidence‑based algorithm** that guides clinicians from the moment the first epinephrine is given to the decision of whether the patient can safely go home after 4 h of observation or needs ICU monitoring for 24 h.

## Take‑home messages  

- Multiphasic anaphylaxis is rare, but the clinical stakes are high; a single case—like the clarithromycin‑triggered, four‑wave reaction described by Schneider et al.—exposes the blind spots in our current knowledge.  
- A focused, multinational registry capturing standardized core data (patient demographics, precise epinephrine dosing, tryptase kinetics, mast cell disease status, and timing of each reaction) is the only realistic pathway to generate the denominator needed for risk modeling.  
- Practical challenges—heterogeneous documentation, differing epinephrine formulations, and under‑reporting—can be mitigated with streamlined CRFs, EHR alerts, and tiered data requirements.  
- With a robust dataset, we can finally move from anecdote‑driven “expert opinion” to data‑driven guidelines on observation duration, optimal epinephrine dosing, and the role (or lack thereof) of steroids in preventing biphasic reactions.  
- Until that evidence arrives, clinicians should: **(1)** ensure weight‑based epinephrine dosing (0.5 mg for adults >50 kg), **(2)** obtain at least two tryptase samples (peak and baseline), **(3)** consider extended observation (≥12 h) for patients with severe initial presentations or multiple epinephrine doses, and **(4)** be vigilant for mast cell disorders in patients with unusually prolonged or recurrent courses.

In short, the next step in anaphylaxis care isn’t a new drug—it’s a **global data‑sharing effort** that turns the bewildering complexity of cases like the one above into a roadmap for every emergency physician, allergist, and intensivist. Let’s start building the registry today, so tomorrow’s guidelines can be truly evidence‑based.
