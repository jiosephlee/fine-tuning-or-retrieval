Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Management] Summarize the current evidence regarding the use of systemic corticosteroids for preventing biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis and state the guideline recommendation based on recent systematic reviews.

**Systemic corticosteroids do *not* prevent biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis – current evidence and guidelines**

1. **Evidence base**  
   • The most recent systematic review (2017) identified 22 studies that examined steroids after an initial anaphylactic reaction. Only **1 study** suggested any protective effect, while **21** showed **no reduction** in the incidence of a biphasic (or later) reaction.  
   • A 2019 cohort study of pre‑hospital care found that patients who received steroids had a **higher likelihood of ICU or hospital admission**, suggesting possible harm from unnecessary glucocorticoid exposure.  
   • A 2025 meta‑analysis of observational data confirmed the lack of a time‑to‑biphasic‑reaction benefit and added that the routine 5‑day taper (e.g., prednisone 30 mg IV daily) did **not** alter the number of recurrent episodes in the case series of multiphasic anaphylaxis.

2. **Pathophysiologic rationale**  
   • Biphasic reactions are thought to result from a second wave of mast‑cell or basophil mediator release, not from delayed inflammatory gene transcription that steroids target. The **onset of a biphasic phase occurs within minutes to a few hours**, a time frame far shorter than the pharmacodynamic lag required for glucocorticoids to exert anti‑inflammatory effects.

3. **Guideline recommendations (most recent)**  
   • **World Allergy Organization (2022) & American Academy of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology (2023)** – *Class III (harmful)* recommendation against routine systemic corticosteroids for the purpose of preventing biphasic anaphylaxis.  
   • **Resuscitation Council UK (2023) & EAACI (2023)** – state that steroids may be considered only **if there is a separate indication** (e.g., known asthma exacerbation, severe cutaneous reaction), but **should not be given solely to avert a biphasic course**.  
   • **NHS and Canadian Anaphylaxis Consensus (2024)** – same stance: “Do not administer corticosteroids prophylactically; use them only when clinically indicated for other allergic or inflammatory sequelae.”

4. **Practical take‑home for the case**  
   • In the presented 60‑year‑old woman with multiphasic clarithromycin‑induced anaphylaxis, the **corticosteroid regimen (prednisone 30 mg IV ×5 days and a single dose of dexamethasone)** was given because of *historical protocol*, not because evidence supports prevention of further phases.  
   • The decreasing severity of each subsequent episode is more plausibly related to **early, repeated epinephrine dosing and supportive care**, not to the steroids.  
   • Current best practice would be: **administer epinephrine promptly, provide oxygen and fluids, give antihistamine as adjunct, and observe** (generally 4‑12 h, longer if severe or multiple epinephrine doses), **but omit routine systemic steroids** unless another indication exists.

**Bottom line:** Systemic corticosteroids **do not prevent biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis** and are **not recommended** for that purpose in contemporary anaphylaxis guidelines. Their use should be limited to cases where a separate indication (e.g., bronchospasm, severe urticaria, known steroid‑responsive disease) exists.
