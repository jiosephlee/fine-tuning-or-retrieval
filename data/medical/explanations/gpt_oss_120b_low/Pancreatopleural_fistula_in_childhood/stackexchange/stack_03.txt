Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

### [Pathophysiology] Describe the anatomic pathway by which pancreatic secretions from a posterior pancreatic duct disruption can reach the pleural cavity, and explain why the effusion is often right‑sided.

**Pathway of a posterior pancreatic‑duct disruption to the pleural cavity**

1. **Location of the injury** – When trauma (or a ruptured pseudocyst) breaches the **posterior aspect of the pancreatic duct** in the body or tail, pancreatic juice is released directly into the **retro‑peritoneal space** because the pancreas lies largely retroperitoneally.

2. **Retro‑peritoneal tracking** – From the site of disruption the fluid follows the path of least resistance along **loose fascial planes**. It climbs superiorly in the **posterior mediastinum** because the retro‑peritoneal tissue is continuous with the mediastinal connective tissue through the **diaphragmatic hiatuses**.

3. **Hiatus of communication** – The two diaphragmatic apertures that most readily allow upward propagation are:
   - **Esophageal (oesophageal) hiatus** – a muscular opening at T10 that permits the esophagus and vagal trunks to pass. It is relatively wide and lies slightly to the **right of the midline**.
   - **Aortic hiatus** – at T12, posterior to the diaphragm, also providing a conduit for the aorta and thoracic duct.  
   In most reported pediatric PPFs, as in this boy, the fluid preferentially uses the **esophageal hiatus** (MRCP showed a tubular collection extending through this hiatus).

4. **Mediastinal/pleural entry** – Once the fluid reaches the **right pleural space** through the hiatus, it breaches the parietal pleura (often where the pleura is thinnest adjacent to the mediastinum) and accumulates as a **right‑sided pleural effusion**. Because the fluid is rich in pancreatic enzymes, it quickly becomes **hemorrhagic and exudative**, producing the dark‑red thoracentesis described.

**Why the effusion is most often right‑sided**

- **Anatomic asymmetry of the hiatuses** – The esophageal hiatus is located **right‑ward** of the vertebral column; the aortic hiatus lies slightly left of midline but is more posterior and less directly connected to the pleural cavity. Fluid that ascends through the esophageal hiatus therefore reaches the **right pleural cavity first**.
- **Retro‑peritoneal fascial planes** – The right retro‑peritoneal space is less compartmentalised by the posterior parietal peritoneum than the left, allowing easier upward migration on the right side.
- **Liver “shield” effect** – The liver buttresses the right hemidiaphragm, making direct trans‑diaphragmatic erosion less likely; instead the fluid uses the pre‑existing hiatal route, which deposits it on the right.
- **Clinical observation** – In the series cited in the report, 68 % of pancreatic‑pleural fistulas were right‑dominant, matching the described anatomic route.

Thus, a posterior duct disruption dumps pancreatic secretions into the retro‑peritoneum, the fluid ascends through the right‑ward esophageal (or occasionally aortic) hiatus, traverses the mediastinum, and breaches the right pleura, producing the characteristic right‑sided, enzyme‑rich pleural effusion seen in this child.
