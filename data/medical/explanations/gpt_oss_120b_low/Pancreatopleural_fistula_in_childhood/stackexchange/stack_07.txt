Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

### [Differential Diagnosis] Identify three non‑pancreatic conditions that can also produce pleural fluid with markedly elevated amylase, and indicate one clinical or laboratory clue that helps distinguish each from a PPF.

**Non‑pancreatic causes of a markedly amylase‑rich pleural effusion (≥1 000 U/L) and the key distinguishing feature for each**

| Condition | Why amylase is high in the pleura | One practical clue that steers you away from a pancreatopleural fistula |
|-----------|-----------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Esophageal perforation (Boerhaave’s syndrome)** | Leakage of saliva‑rich fluid (salivary‑type amylase) into the mediastinum and pleural space after a full‑thickness tear. | **Acute onset after forceful vomiting or retching** – the patient typically reports a sudden, severe chest/upper‑abdominal pain, often with subcutaneous emphysema. A contrast esophagogram (water‑soluble) will demonstrate extravasation, whereas pancreatic imaging is normal. |
| **Malignancy (lung adenocarcinoma, lymphoma, metastatic pancreatic or ovarian carcinoma)** | Tumor cells can produce amylase (often salivary iso‑enzyme) or cause malignant pleural effusion that becomes secondarily enzyme‑rich. | **Pleural fluid cytology showing malignant cells or a markedly lymphocyte‑predominant exudate with high LDH**. In addition, systemic features such as weight loss, night sweats, or a radiographic mass on chest CT point toward malignancy rather than a retro‑peritoneal pancreatic source. |
| **Tuberculous pleuritis** | Mycobacterial infection can stimulate pleural mesothelial cells to release amylase (usually salivary type) and also raise ADA. | **Pleural fluid ADA > 40 U/L together with a positive interferon‑γ release assay or GeneXpert for MTB**, and a clinical picture of fever, night sweats, and a known TB exposure. The effusion is typically lymphocyte‑rich, and imaging shows pleural thickening without any pancreatic pathology. |

**Teaching point:**  
When you encounter an exudative pleural effusion with a **very high amylase level**, the first step is to determine the **iso‑enzyme pattern** (pancreatic vs. salivary). Pancreatic‑type amylase (P‑amylase) virtually confirms a pancreatopleural fistula, whereas salivary‑type amylase suggests one of the above extra‑pancreatic entities. Correlate the amylase result with the clinical context (vomiting, TB exposure, systemic malignancy signs) and targeted imaging (esophagogram, chest CT, abdominal/pancreatic MRI) to avoid mis‑diagnosis, as illustrated in the presented child whose trauma‑related pancreatic injury was initially obscured by a misleading TB work‑up.
