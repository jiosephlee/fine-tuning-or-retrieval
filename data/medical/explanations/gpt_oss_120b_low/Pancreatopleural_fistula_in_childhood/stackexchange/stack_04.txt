Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

### [Differential Diagnosis] List the imaging modalities used to diagnose pancreatopleural fistula and compare their sensitivity for directly visualizing the fistulous tract.

**Imaging work‑up of a pancreatopleural fistula (PPF): What studies actually show the fistulous tract?**  

| Modality | What it depicts | Reported sensitivity for *direct* fistula visualization* | Practical role in the case |
|----------|----------------|-----------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------|
| **Contrast‑enhanced CT (abdomen‑chest)** | Pancreatic parenchyma, pseudocysts, retro‑peritoneal fluid collections, pleural effusion; can suggest a tract when a low‑density “string” of fluid extends from pancreas to pleura. | **33 %–47 %** – the tract is seen only when the fluid track is large enough to be resolved on axial images. | In the boy, CT demonstrated a multiloculated hypodense collection that *tracked* from the para‑esophageal region to the pancreatic tail, raising suspicion for a PPF, but it did not directly delineate a patent fistulous channel. |
| **Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography (MRCP)** | T2‑weighted pancreatic ductal anatomy and fluid‑filled tracts; excellent for demonstrating communication between a pancreatic duct/pseudocyst and the pleural cavity. | **70 %–80 %** – the fistula is directly visualized in the majority of cases because the high‑contrast fluid on MRCP outlines the narrow ductal‑to‑pleural connection. | MRCP in this patient *confirmed* bilateral (right > left) pancreatopleural fistulas, showing a tubular cystic collection extending through the esophageal hiatus into the pleural space. |
| **Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography (ERCP)** | Direct opacification of the pancreatic duct and any abnormal side‑branches or leaks; allows therapeutic stenting. | **38 %–78 %** (reported in pediatric series). When the leak is active, contrast extravasation into the pleural cavity can be captured, giving the highest anatomic fidelity, but the rate varies with ductal anatomy and technical factors. | ERCP is the *gold‑standard* for therapeutic planning (duct stenting, sphincterotomy) and, when performed, can demonstrate the fistulous communication. In this case it was not required because MRCP already provided a clear anatomic map. |
| **Ultrasound (abdominal or thoracic)** | Detects pancreatic fluid collections, pleural effusion; can guide drainage. | **<20 %** for fistula visualization – limited by acoustic windows and inability to track retro‑diaphragmatic pathways. | Used only to confirm the large pleural effusion and guide thoracentesis; did not contribute to fistula identification. |
| **Fluoroscopic fistulography (percutaneous injection of contrast into a collection)** | Directly outlines the tract when contrast is poured into a known pseudocyst or retro‑peritoneal fluid. | **Variable, 40 %–70 %**; highly operator dependent and rarely performed nowadays. | Not employed in this case. |

\*Sensitivity figures are drawn from the series cited in the case report’s discussion and from contemporary reviews of pediatric PPF imaging. They reflect the proportion of studies that **directly visualized the communicating channel**, not merely indirect signs (e.g., a fluid collection abutting the diaphragm).

**Key teaching points**

1. **CT is often the first cross‑sectional study** because it is readily available and can suggest a PPF by showing a fluid track from the pancreas to the pleural space, but its ability to *directly* depict the narrow fistulous lumen is limited (≈ 1/3 of cases).  
2. **MRCP is the most sensitive non‑invasive test** for direct fistula visualization (≈ 75 %); the high‑contrast fluid column on heavily T2‑weighted sequences makes a thin duct‑to‑pleura connection conspicuous. In our boy, MRCP clinched the diagnosis.  
3. **ERCP remains the reference standard** when therapeutic intervention (ductal stenting) is planned; it can demonstrate a leak in up to three‑quarters of pediatric patients, but its invasive nature and the need for expertise limit its use as a pure diagnostic tool.  

**Bottom line:** When a pancreatopleural fistula is suspected, start with a contrast‑enhanced CT to look for a retro‑diaphragmatic fluid track, then obtain an MRCP for definitive non‑invasive delineation of the fistulous tract. Reserve ERCP for cases that will benefit from endoscopic therapy or when MRCP is equivocal.
