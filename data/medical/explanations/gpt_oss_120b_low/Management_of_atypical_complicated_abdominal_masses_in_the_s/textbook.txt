Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Assessment  

The patient was a 42‑year‑old nulliparous woman in the early second trimester of pregnancy (21 weeks gestation). Her pre‑pregnancy body mass index was 33 kg/m², placing her in the obese category and adding an element of physiological stress that can amplify both obstetric and surgical considerations. She had no prior live births (G1 P0 A1) and her first‑trimester screening, including non‑invasive prenatal testing, had demonstrated a low risk for chromosomal aneuploidies and a normal fetal anatomy survey.  

### Chief complaints  

At presentation she reported **acute, severe pain** localized to the **upper left abdominal quadrant** as well as to the **pelvic region**. Both pain sites were rated 5 cm on a 10‑cm Visual Analogue Scale (VAS). Concomitantly she described **mild dyspnoea** that worsened when she attempted to lie supine, suggesting a restrictive effect of a large intra‑abdominal mass on diaphragmatic excursion.  

### Vital signs and general status  

On initial assessment in the emergency department her vital parameters were within physiologic limits for pregnancy:  

| Parameter | Value | Interpretation |
|-----------|-------|----------------|
| Oxygen saturation (SpO₂) | 97 % (room air) | Adequate oxygenation |
| Heart rate | 74 bpm | Normal sinus rhythm |
| Blood pressure | 100/60 mmHg | Normotensive for gestation |
| Temperature | Afebrile initially; later low‑grade fever (≈38 °C) | Evolving inflammatory response |
| Respiratory rate | 16 /min | Within normal range |

A 12‑lead electrocardiogram was unremarkable, and there were no signs of hemodynamic compromise.  

### Physical examination  

**Abdominal inspection** revealed a uterus larger than expected for 21 weeks, with a palpable, taut‑elastic mass occupying the entire left sub‑phrenic area. The mass was **approximately 18 cm** in greatest dimension, smooth, non‑fluctuant, and markedly tender on palpation; there were no stigmata of ascites or peritoneal irritation.  

**Pelvic (bimanual) examination** disclosed a **fixed, rounded, hard mass** in the lesser pelvis measuring roughly **9 cm**, bulging slightly into the posterior fornix. The uterus was palpable but displaced superiorly by the upper mass; cervical motion was within normal limits and no cervical discharge was noted.  

These findings raised immediate concern for **space‑occupying lesions** of uterine or adnexal origin that could compromise maternal respiration, uterine blood flow, or fetal growth.  

### Laboratory investigations  

Initial blood work reflected several pregnancy‑related and infection‑related abnormalities:  

* **Hemoglobin 9.9 g/dL** (reference 12.0–16.6 g/dL) and **serum ferritin 25 µg/L**, consistent with iron‑deficiency anemia, a frequent accompaniment of obesity and high‑parity pregnancies.  
* **White blood cell count 11.3 × 10³/µL** with a neutrophilic predominance (9.3 × 10³/µL), modestly elevated above the normal pregnancy range (4–10 × 10³/µL).  
* **C‑reactive protein 1.39 mg/dL** (reference ≤ 0.3 mg/dL), indicating an acute‑phase response.  
* **Urine culture** grew *Klebsiella pneumoniae* highly sensitive to cephalosporins, establishing a concurrent urinary tract infection that could contribute to the low‑grade fever.  
* **COVID‑19 nasopharyngeal PCR** was negative, excluding SARS‑CoV‑2 as a contributor to respiratory symptoms.  

Serum tumor markers—including CA 19‑9, CA 15‑3, CEA, β2‑microglobulin, and LDH—were within normal limits. **CA‑125** was modestly elevated at **41.8 U/mL** (reference < 35 U/mL), with a rising trend from 36 U/mL measured two weeks earlier. Although CA‑125 rises physiologically during early pregnancy, a value above the expected median for the second trimester warranted attention when combined with imaging findings.  

### Initial symptom control  

Analgesia with **intravenous paracetamol 1 g twice daily** and semi‑orthostatic positioning (head of bed elevated 30–45°) were instituted promptly. The positioning aimed to reduce diaphragmatic compression by the gravid uterus and the large abdominal masses. This regimen decreased the VAS pain scores from 5 to 1 cm, indicating effective short‑term relief without the need for opioid analgesics, which could jeopardize fetal well‑being. No supplemental oxygen or ventilatory support was required.  

### Multidisciplinary triage  

Given the complexity of a pregnant patient with large intra‑abdominal masses, a **multidisciplinary team (MDT)** was assembled immediately. The core members included:

* **Emergency medicine physicians** for initial stabilization and infection control.  
* **Obstetricians** to evaluate fetal viability, gestational age, and obstetric risk.  
* **Maternal‑fetal medicine specialists** to interpret ultrasound findings and counsel regarding pregnancy‑specific imaging constraints.  
* **Radiologists** experienced in obstetric ultrasound and non‑contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), essential for safe lesion characterization.  
* **Gynecologic oncologists/surgeons** prepared for potential operative intervention if oncologic suspicion or acute complications arose.  

The MDT conducted a rapid risk‑benefit analysis: the patient’s worsening pain and dyspnoea, the size and vascularity of the masses, evolving fever, and the minimal but rising CA‑125 suggested that a **definitive diagnosis** was needed promptly to avert maternal or fetal compromise. This collaborative assessment set the stage for targeted imaging (ultrasound followed by non‑contrast MRI) and, ultimately, the decision to proceed with surgical exploration when conservative measures proved insufficient.  

In summary, the initial presentation combined **obstetric, surgical, and infectious** elements that required a systematic, coordinated approach. Careful documentation of the patient’s demographics, symptomatology, physical findings, baseline vitals, and laboratory data guided the subsequent diagnostic pathway and informed the multidisciplinary decision‑making process.

## 2. Diagnostic Work‑up and Formulation of the Differential Diagnosis  

The presence of two large abdominal masses in a pregnant woman raises immediate concerns about maternal safety, fetal well‑being, and the potential need for intervention. In this case the diagnostic pathway progressed through a structured combination of bedside examination, targeted ultrasound, laboratory testing, and a non‑contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) study. Each step contributed specific anatomic and physiologic information that narrowed the differential diagnosis and helped the multidisciplinary team decide whether surgery was warranted.

### 2.1. Ultrasound as the First‑line Modality  

Transabdominal and trans‑vaginal ultrasonography are the only imaging tools that are universally available, inexpensive, and safe throughout pregnancy. In the present patient the sonographer documented two distinct, unilocular cystic lesions with well‑defined margins:

| Feature | Upper (left sub‑phrenic) mass | Lower (pouch of Douglas) mass |
|--------|------------------------------|-------------------------------|
| Maximal dimensions | 18 × 12 × 7 cm | 9.5 × 8 × 8 cm |
| Shape | Rounded, “taut‑elastic” | Rounded, partially adherent to uterus |
| Internal contents | Anechoic fluid, thin septations, no solid nodules | Anechoic fluid with a solid‑appearing component that appeared contiguous with a posterior uterine wall myoma (58 × 71 mm) |
| Vascular pattern (colour‑Doppler) | Peripheral vascularisation of the wall; no dominant feeding vessel identified | Peripheral flow; modest central flow within the solid component |
| Relation to uterus | Displaced the uterus superiorly; no clear continuity with uterine tissue | Appeared adherent to a posterior intramural‑subserosal leiomyoma, suggesting a possible uterine origin |

Colour‑Doppler is particularly valuable for distinguishing a pedunculated leiomyoma from an ovarian cyst. Pedunculated myomas typically exhibit a single feeding vessel entering a narrow stalk, whereas ovarian lesions show a more diffuse ovarian stromal vascular pattern and often have multiple peripheral vessels. In this case the lack of a discernible stalk for the upper mass and its per‑uterine displacement favoured an ovarian‑type location, while the lower lesion’s apparent continuity with a known uterine fibroid suggested a myomatous origin.

### 2.2. Serum Tumour Markers in Pregnancy  

Laboratory evaluation included a panel of tumour markers (CA 19‑9, CA 15‑3, CEA, β2‑microglobulin, LDH) that were all within normal limits. CA‑125 was modestly elevated at 41.8 U/mL (reference < 35 U/mL) and had risen from 36 U/mL two weeks earlier. Interpretation of CA‑125 during gestation must consider its physiologic trajectory: concentrations increase in the first trimester (median ≈ 23 U/mL), fall to a nadir in the second trimester (median ≈ 14 U/mL), and rise again in the third trimester (median ≈ 21 U/mL). An isolated value above 35 U/mL in the second trimester is therefore atypical and may point toward a pathologic process, but it is not specific for malignancy. CA‑125 can be raised in endometriosis, decidualised ovarian cysts, pelvic inflammation, and even in large leiomyomas undergoing degeneration. Consequently, the modest rise was taken as a supportive, not decisive, clue for a benign but possibly hormonally active lesion such as a decidualised endometrioma.

### 2.3. Non‑contrast MRI in the Second Trimester  

Because ultrasonography could not unequivocally define the tissue of origin or rule out malignant features, a non‑contrast MRI was obtained. Safety considerations in pregnancy dictate the use of a 1.5‑T scanner (or lower), avoidance of gadolinium, and limiting the scan to essential sequences. The protocol included:

* **T1‑weighted fat‑sat** – to detect hemorrhagic or protein‑rich fluid (hyperintense signal).  
* **T2‑weighted fat‑sat** – to assess fluid content (hyperintense) and delineate cyst walls.  
* **HASTE/SSFSE (single‑shot fast spin‑echo)** – rapid acquisition, reducing motion artefacts.  
* **Diffusion‑weighted imaging (optional)** – to identify restricted diffusion suggestive of high cellularity (malignancy), although interpretation is limited by gestational changes.

Key MRI findings were:

* Both masses were **unilocular** with **thick walls (≈ 1.4 cm)**.  
* The fluid within the cysts was **inhomogeneous** on T2, indicating proteinaceous or mucoid material rather than simple serous fluid.  
* **Focal solid components** displayed **hyperintensity on both T1 and T2** fat‑sat images, consistent with areas of hemorrhage or necrosis.  
* No **enhancing** tissue could be assessed (no gadolinium), but the absence of markedly low T2 signal (which would raise suspicion for fibrous stroma) and the lack of restricted diffusion argued against high‑grade malignancy.  
* The **low‑pelvic lesion** showed continuity with the posterior uterine wall, reinforcing a leiomyomatous origin, whereas the **upper lesion** appeared separate from the uterus, favouring an ovarian location.

No enlarged lymph nodes or ascites were identified, decreasing the likelihood of an invasive ovarian carcinoma.

### 2.4. Construction of the Differential Diagnosis  

Integrating the imaging and laboratory data yielded a focused differential:

| Potential Diagnosis | Supporting Features | Arguments Against |
|---------------------|--------------------|-------------------|
| **Decidualised endometrioma** (ovarian) | • Large cystic lesion in ovarian region, • Peripheral vascularisation, • Slight CA‑125 rise, • Inhomogeneous T2 fluid suggesting degenerated endometriotic content | • No classic “ground‑glass” T2 signal; • No history of endometriosis; • Upper mass lacks a demonstrable ovarian ovary or pedicle |
| **Cystic degeneration of a uterine leiomyoma** (pedunculated) | • Thickened wall, solid T1/T2 components indicating necrosis, • Pedicle identified intra‑operatively, • Post‑operative pathology confirms leiomyoma | • Upper lesion appears separate from uterus on imaging, making a uterine origin less likely |
| **Primary ovarian cystic tumor (benign)** – e.g., serous cystadenoma | • Simple cystic architecture, peripheral vascularity, absence of solid nodules, normal tumor markers other than CA‑125 | • Presence of solid T1/T2 hyperintense foci more typical of hemorrhagic degeneration; CA‑125 modestly high |
| **Primary ovarian cystic tumor (malignant)** – e.g., borderline or epithelial ovarian cancer | • Large size (>10 cm), thick wall, solid components, rising CA‑125 | • Lack of papillary projections, absence of diffusion restriction, normal CEA, CA 19‑9, β2‑microglobulin, and no ascites or nodal disease |
| **Hydropic (myxoid) degeneration or necrosis of a pedunculated myoma** | • Thickened wall, heterogeneous T2 signal, solid hemorrhagic foci, intra‑operative finding of twisted stalk, fever suggesting inflammatory response | • Upper mass’s apparent ovarian location; however, pedunculated myomas can prolapse into the abdomen and mimic ovarian cysts |

The **upper mass** therefore remained equivocal: imaging favored an ovarian cystic lesion (possibly a decidualised endometrioma), but the possibility of a large pedunculated leiomyoma with cystic degeneration could not be excluded without direct visualization. The **lower mass** was more clearly a cystic degeneration of a posterior uterine leiomyoma, given its continuity with the myometrium on both US and MRI.

### 2.5. Clinical‑Imaging Discordance and Decision to Operate  

Two major points of discordance guided the multidisciplinary team toward surgical exploration:

1. **Size and symptom burden** – Both lesions exceeded 8 cm; the upper lesion measured 18 cm and was exerting mass effect, causing restrictive dyspnoea and severe abdominal pain unresponsive to analgesia. Masses larger than 10 cm are associated with increased risk of torsion, rupture, and compromised pulmonary mechanics in pregnancy.

2. **Inconclusive imaging** – Despite high‑resolution US and non‑contrast MRI, the origin of the upper mass could not be definitively assigned to ovarian or uterine tissue. The presence of solid, possibly necrotic, components raised a non‑negligible concern for atypical degeneration or, rarely, a borderline ovarian neoplasm. Serial CA‑125 elevation, although not diagnostic, added weight to the need for tissue diagnosis.

Additional factors influencing the decision included the detection of a low‑grade fever (later linked to a urinary Klebsiella infection) and the presence of a twisted pedicle observed intra‑operatively, which likely explained the inflammatory picture. Given the gestational age (21 weeks), a relatively stable fetal status, and the anticipated difficulty of managing a potentially obstructive mass later in pregnancy, the team elected to proceed with a controlled laparotomy after thorough counseling and informed consent.

In summary, the diagnostic work‑up combined complementary imaging modalities and judicious interpretation of tumour marker trends to generate a concise differential. The inability to exclude a pedunculated leiomyoma with cystic degeneration versus an ovarian cystic neoplasm, together with the patient's worsening clinical picture, ultimately tipped the balance in favour of surgical exploration.

## 3. Therapeutic Decision‑Making and Surgical Management  

The presence of two voluminous abdominal masses in a woman who was already 21 weeks pregnant created a therapeutic dilemma that required a coordinated, multidisciplinary approach.  The team—comprising obstetricians, maternal‑fetal medicine specialists, gynecologic oncologists, anesthesiologists, and neonatologists—first clarified **why surgery was unavoidable**, then weighed the **maternal‑fetal risks and benefits**, selected the **most appropriate operative route**, and finally detailed the intra‑operative and postoperative plan that would protect both the pregnancy and the mother.

### Indications for surgery during pregnancy  

In the obstetric literature, operative intervention for uterine or adnexal masses is reserved for a limited set of circumstances because of the inherent threats to fetal viability.  In this case, three major criteria were fulfilled:  

1. **Severe, refractory pain** – the patient’s visual‑analogue scale remained ≥5 despite optimal analgesia, positioning, and supportive care, indicating that the mass was producing ongoing visceral irritation.  
2. **Respiratory compromise** – the large left‑subphrenic lesion displaced the diaphragm, producing dyspnoea that threatened maternal oxygenation and, consequently, fetal oxygen delivery.  
3. **Evidence of evolving necrosis** – a low‑grade fever appeared after admission, and intra‑operative findings later confirmed ischaemic necrosis at the twisted pedicle of the cranial mass.  Necrotic tissue can precipitate infection, peritonitis, or abrupt hemorrhage, all of which carry high maternal and fetal morbidity.  

Secondary considerations—rapid enlargement on serial ultrasound, a modest rise in CA‑125 suggesting possible malignant potential, and the limited capacity of the growing uterus to accommodate further mass growth—reinforced the decision to intervene rather than continue expectant management.

### Risk–benefit analysis  

Once surgical necessity was established, the team performed a structured risk–benefit assessment centred on **fetal viability** and **maternal safety**:  

| Parameter | Maternal considerations | Fetal considerations |
|-----------|------------------------|----------------------|
| **Gestational age** | At 21 w + 6 d the uterus is still largely intra‑abdominal, permitting a midline laparotomy without excessive uterine manipulation. | Viability is assured; fetal monitoring is feasible throughout the operation. |
| **Estimated blood loss** | Anticipated moderate loss (pedunculated leiomyoma highly vascular) → need for pre‑operative type‑and‑cross and intra‑operative cell‑saver if available. | Maternal hypovolemia could precipitate uterine hypoperfusion; careful fluid and blood management protects fetal oxygen delivery. |
| **Uterine preservation** | A limited myomectomy (single pedunculated lesion) minimizes disruption of the myometrial envelope, reducing the risk of uterine dehiscence and future scar complications. | Preserving uterine integrity maintains the capacity for term continuation and lowers the chance of preterm labour triggered by uterine irritation. |
| **Anesthetic risk** | General anesthesia with a short operative time (<3 h) is considered safe in the second trimester; regional techniques are less practical for a large midline incision. | Inhalational agents cross the placenta but at the doses required for a brief surgery have not been shown to cause teratogenicity after the first trimester. |
| **Potential benefits** | Immediate relief of pain, removal of a necrotic source of infection, prevention of torsion or rupture of the cranial mass, and avoidance of future obstructive labour. | Eliminates a compressive element that could worsen maternal pulmonary mechanics, thereby improving fetal oxygenation; removes a lesion that might otherwise generate obstetric complications at term. |

The net balance favoured operative management, provided the procedure could be performed swiftly, with meticulous haemostasis, and with continuous fetal surveillance.

### Choice of surgical approach  

Both laparoscopy and laparotomy are technically feasible for myomectomy in pregnancy, yet **patient‑specific factors dictated an open approach**:  

* **Mass size and location** – the cranial lesion extended from the left subphrenic space to the uterine fundus (≈18 × 12 × 7 cm) and was highly vascularised; a pedicle could not be visualised on imaging, raising the possibility of a broad‑based attachment that would be difficult to control laparoscopically.  
* **Gestational uterus size** – at 21 weeks the uterus occupies the pelvis and lower abdomen, limiting the operative working space required for safe trocar placement and manipulation of large specimens.  
* **Urgency of decompression** – the open longitudinal xipho‑pubic incision permits rapid entry, direct tactile assessment of the pedicle, and swift en‑bloc removal of the mass, thereby minimizing operative time and maternal‑fetal exposure.  

Consequently, the team elected a **midline laparotomy** extending from the xiphoid process to the pubic symphysis, providing excellent exposure of the upper abdomen, the diaphragmatic dome, and the pelvis in a single incision.

### Pre‑operative preparation  

1. **Informed consent** – a detailed discussion with the patient covered the specific maternal risks (hemorrhage, infection, uterine rupture, anesthesia complications) and fetal risks (preterm labor, fetal distress, potential loss). Written consent was obtained after confirming that the patient understood the necessity of surgery, the possibility of conversion to cesarean delivery, and the plan to leave the caudal mass in situ.  
2. **Antibiotic prophylaxis** – because a urine culture had identified **Klebsiella pneumoniae** highly sensitive to cephalosporins, the protocol comprised:  
   * **Cefazolin 2 g IV** administered 30 minutes before skin incision, followed by **cefazolin 1 g IV every 8 h** post‑operatively;  
   * **Clarithromycin 500 mg PO twice daily** added to the post‑operative regimen to ensure coverage of the isolated organism, as per the antibiogram.  
3. **Progesterone supplementation** – to mitigate the risk of uterine irritability and support early gestational maintenance, the patient received:  
   * **Progesterone 100 mg IM** intra‑operatively (after induction of anesthesia) and then **100 mg IM daily** until discharge;  
   * **Hydroxy‑progesterone caproate 341 mg IM** on day 3 post‑op and subsequently every three days until elective delivery at 37 weeks, following protocols used for threatened preterm labour.  
4. **Maternal‑fetal monitoring preparation** – a portable fetal Doppler was readied for intra‑operative verification of fetal heart activity before incision, during critical steps (e.g., uterine manipulation), and after closure.

### Intra‑operative sequence  

1. **Peritoneal fluid sampling** – after entry into the peritoneal cavity, two separate specimens of free fluid were aspirated for **culture** (to rule out bacterial peritonitis) and **cytology** (to exclude occult malignancy).  
2. **Fetal heart confirmation** – a trans‑abdominal Doppler probe confirmed a baseline fetal heart rate of 140 bpm with reassuring variability before proceeding.  
3. **Identification of the cranial mass** – the upper lesion was found to be a pedunculated, cystic‑appearing leiomyoma attached to the uterine fundus by a narrow stalk that was twisted on itself. The pedicle was markedly vascular, accounting for the patient’s febrile episode (likely low‑grade septic necrosis).  
4. **Excision of the pedunculated leiomyoma** – the surgeon applied a **double‑clamp technique**: two atraumatic clamps were placed distal to the twisted portion of the stalk, the vascular supply was occluded, and the stalk was sharply transected between the clamps. Immediate haemostasis was achieved by **electrocautery** of the residual myometrial edges, followed by a **single–layer absorbable (0 Vicryl) suture** to re‑approximate the uterine wall, preserving myometrial integrity and preventing a future dehiscence site.  
5. **Assessment of the caudal mass** – the lower lesion, adherent to the posterior uterine serosa, measured ≈9 cm and was smooth, cystic, and lacked a discernible stalk. Given its deep pelvic location, the risk of extensive dissection, hemorrhage, and uterine destabilisation outweighed any immediate benefit of removal. The team therefore **deferred excision**, electing to monitor the mass throughout the remainder of gestation.  
6. **Operative metrics** – the total skin‑to‑skin time was **2 hours 25 minutes**; estimated blood loss was **≈400 mL**, managed with crystalloid infusion and one unit of packed red cells prepared in the OR. Continuous fetal heart rate monitoring was performed with a handheld Doppler every 15 minutes; no decelerations occurred.  

### Post‑operative immediate care  

* **Analgesia** – the pain regimen continued with **paracetamol 1 g IV every 6 h** and, when needed, **short‑acting opioids** (e.g., morphine 2 mg IV) titrated to maintain a VAS ≤ 2.  
* **Uterine activity surveillance** – uterine tone was assessed every 4 hours with bedside palpation and a tocodynamometer; no excessive contractions were observed. The prophylactic **progesterone 100 mg IM** was administered daily for the first three postoperative days, then transitioned to the caproate schedule.  
* **Antibiotic continuation** – cefazolin (1 g IV q8h) and clarithromycin (500 mg PO BID) were maintained for **5 days**, after which the patient completed a **7‑day oral course** of amoxicillin‑clavulanate to cover peripartum urinary flora.  
* **Laboratory follow‑up** – a postoperative CBC showed a transient drop in haemoglobin to 7.5 g/dL, prompting **intravenous ferric carboxymaltose 1 g** and **two units of packed red blood cells**; subsequent labs demonstrated haemoglobin rise to 10 g/dL.  
* **Obstetric monitoring** – fetal wellbeing was reassessed with a **biophysical profile** 24 hours after surgery; the amniotic fluid index and growth parameters remained within normal limits. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 10 after an uneventful recovery, with instructions for **weekly obstetric visits** and a **planned elective cesarean at 37 weeks** to avoid the residual pelvic myoma obstructing vaginal delivery.

In summary, the therapeutic plan was built on a clear, evidence‑based framework: surgical indication was driven by symptomatic, potentially necrotic, and compressive disease; a careful risk–benefit calculus justified operation at 21 weeks; an open midline laparotomy offered the safest and most efficient route to excise the pedunculated leiomyoma; and meticulous peri‑operative preparation—including informed consent, targeted antibiotics, and progesterone support—enabled a successful outcome with preservation of both maternal health and fetal viability.

## 4. Obstetric Follow‑up, Delivery Planning, and Post‑operative Outcomes  

After the laparotomy at 21 weeks + 6 days, the clinical focus shifted from urgent surgical care to safeguarding the ongoing pregnancy while monitoring the sequelae of the operation. The multidisciplinary team (obstetricians, maternal‑fetal medicine specialists, anesthesiologists, and hematologists) implemented a structured follow‑up protocol that addressed the hematologic recovery, fetal well‑being, the residual leiomyoma, and the timing and mode of delivery.

### Management of postoperative anaemia  

The patient entered the postoperative period with a hemoglobin level of 7.5 g/dL, well below the trimester‑specific threshold for symptomatic anemia and far beneath the WHO recommendation of 11 g/dL for pregnant women. In pregnancy, transfusion decisions balance maternal oxygen‑carrying capacity, fetal demands, and the risk of alloimmunization. The team adopted a two‑step approach:

1. **Intravenous iron repletion** – a single dose of ferric carboxymaltose 1 g was administered, providing an immediate iron load of ~1000 mg, which is safe in the second trimester and avoids the gastrointestinal side effects of oral preparations.  
2. **Red cell transfusion** – two units of cross‑matched packed red blood cells were given intra‑operatively and post‑operatively to raise the hemoglobin above 9 g/dL, the level at which tissue oxygenation and uteroplacental perfusion are reliably maintained. The transfusion threshold of 8 g/dL, recommended by obstetric guidelines for pregnant patients with symptomatic anemia, was therefore exceeded, and the patient’s hemoglobin stabilized at 10 g/dL without further need for transfusion.

Iron studies after the infusion showed ferritin >100 µg/L, confirming adequate repletion. Subsequent oral iron was prescribed for maintenance until delivery.

### Serial obstetric ultrasounds  

From discharge, the patient attended obstetric visits every three weeks. Each encounter included:

* **Fetal biometry** – longitudinal measurements demonstrated growth tracking at the 86th percentile on the WHO fetal growth chart, indicating appropriate growth despite the maternal surgical stress.  
* **Amniotic fluid index (AFI)** – serial AFI values remained within the normal range (8–24 cm), ruling out oligohydramnios which could have been precipitated by uterine compression or placental insufficiency.  
* **Myoma surveillance** – transabdominal and transvaginal sonography measured the residual posterior myoma. At 28 weeks the lesion measured 8.2 cm; by 36 weeks it had regressed to 7.5 cm, an expected postpartum involution pattern accelerated by the cessation of hormonal stimulation after delivery. No increase in vascularity or signs of degeneration were observed, decreasing the risk of acute complications (torsion, rupture).

These imaging checkpoints also allowed continuous assessment of placental location relative to the myoma, ensuring that the scarred uterine segment would not compromise placental implantation.

### Resolution of the caudal myoma postpartum  

Post‑delivery ultrasound confirmed a further reduction of the caudal myoma to 7.5 cm, with a homogeneous hypoechoic texture and a thin peripheral rim, consistent with involution. The size reduction underscores the physiological shrinkage of leiomyomas after the abrupt drop in estrogen and progesterone levels that follows delivery. Although the lesion remained asymptomatic, its persistence warranted a plan for future evaluation.

### Decision for elective caesarean section at 37 weeks  

Several factors converged on the choice of an elective caesarean delivery on the existing laparotomy scar:

1. **Mechanical obstruction** – the posterior intramural‑subserosal myoma occupied the lower pelvis, extending into the pouch of Douglas. Its size and fixed nature would likely impede fetal head descent during the expulsive phase, raising the risk of prolonged labour, uterine rupture, or severe perineal trauma.  
2. **Scar integrity** – the myomectomy had been performed via a longitudinal xipho‑pubic incision with a single‑layer absorbable uterine closure. Although the incision healed uneventfully, the presence of a uterine scar combined with a large residual mass increased the theoretical risk of dehiscence under the stress of labour. Elective cesarean delivery thus offered a controlled environment to avoid uterine rupture.  
3. **Gestational timing** – delivering at 37 weeks balances fetal maturity (average birth weight >3000 g) with the avoidance of further myoma‑related complications that could arise with advancing gestation (e.g., increased uterine irritability, preterm labor).  

The operation was performed through the previous laparotomy incision, minimizing additional abdominal trauma. A low transverse uterine incision was not feasible because of the location of the residual myoma; the existing longitudinal scar was therefore utilized, and meticulous hemostasis was achieved.

### Neonatal outcomes  

The neonate weighed 3 120 g (approximately the 78th percentile for gestational age) and had Apgar scores of 9 and 10 at 1 and 5 minutes, respectively. The infant required only routine temperature regulation and glucose monitoring; no respiratory support was needed, despite the maternal pre‑operative dyspnoea. Umbilical arterial blood gases were within normal limits (pH 7.30, base excess −2 mmol/L), confirming adequate fetal oxygenation throughout delivery. The newborn was breast‑fed successfully from the first day, and the mother reported no post‑partum complications.

### Long‑term follow‑up plan  

Given the residual leiomyoma’s size and asymptomatic nature, the following strategy was adopted:

* **Imaging** – a transvaginal ultrasound at 6 weeks postpartum and again at 12 months will document further involution. If the lesion exceeds 5 cm after one year or becomes symptomatic, magnetic resonance imaging (without contrast) will be considered for detailed mapping.  
* **Symptom monitoring** – the patient was educated on signs of myoma‑related problems (pelvic pain, heavy menstrual bleeding, pressure symptoms) and instructed to seek care promptly.  
* **Fertility counseling** – the residual myoma is unlikely to impair future fertility, but the patient was advised that a large posterior lesion can interfere with embryo implantation or increase the risk of obstetric complications in subsequent pregnancies. Should she desire another child, pre‑conception counseling will include a discussion of elective myomectomy versus expectant management, weighing the risks of surgery against the likelihood of spontaneous regression.  
* **Surgical options** – if the myoma persists or enlarges, minimally invasive myomectomy (laparoscopic or hysteroscopic, depending on location) or uterine‑sparing ablative techniques (e.g., MRI‑guided focused ultrasound) may be offered. All plans would incorporate a multidisciplinary review with a maternal‑fetal medicine specialist.

### Pathology report  

The excised cranial mass was submitted for histopathology. Microscopic examination revealed bundles of smooth‑muscle cells with whorled architecture, consistent with a benign uterine leiomyoma. Areas of ischemic necrosis adjacent to the twisted pedicle were identified, explaining the intra‑operative finding of a leaking, inflamed peduncle and the transient fever. No atypia, mitotic activity, or cellular pleomorphism was seen; immunohistochemistry for desmin and smooth‑muscle actin was positive, confirming smooth‑muscle origin. Margins were free of tumor, and cytology of peritoneal fluid was negative for malignant cells. The absence of malignancy corroborated the decision to limit surgical excision to the pedunculated mass and forego a more extensive uterine resection.

Overall, the coordinated postoperative care—prompt correction of anemia, vigilant obstetric surveillance, a well‑justified elective cesarean delivery, and an evidence‑based long‑term plan—enabled an optimal maternal and neonatal outcome while preserving the option for future fertility and definitive management of the residual leiomyoma.

## 5. Clinical Pearls, Evidence Synthesis, and Guideline Implications  

Uterine leiomyomas are the most frequent benign tumours encountered in pregnant women, yet the coexistence of large, cystic‑appearing pelvic masses creates a diagnostic and therapeutic dilemma. The present case illustrates how a systematic, evidence‑based approach can resolve uncertainty, prevent adverse obstetric events, and preserve both maternal and fetal health. The following pearls condense the lessons learned and translate them into practical algorithms that can be applied to similar scenarios.

### Epidemiology of uterine leiomyomas and adnexal masses in pregnancy  
- **Leiomyomas** affect 0.3–10 % of pregnancies, with prevalence rising with maternal age and obesity. Their growth is driven by progesterone and estrogen, resulting in a non‑linear size increase of ≈ 7 % per week in the first half of gestation.  
- **Adnexal cystic lesions** (functional ovarian cysts, endometriomas, para‑ovarian cysts) are encountered in 1–6 % of pregnancies. Because they share sonographic features with degenerated myomas, misdiagnosis occurs in up to 30 % of cases when imaging is limited to ultrasound alone.  

Understanding these baseline frequencies alerts the clinician to the possibility that a “large ovarian cyst” may in fact be a pedunculated leiomyoma, as occurred in the present patient.

### Diagnostic algorithm for large pelvic masses in pregnant patients  

1. **History and physical examination** – Focus on pain pattern, dyspnoea, obstetric milestones, and a thorough bimanual exam to assess mobility and relationship to the uterus.  
2. **Transabdominal/Transvaginal ultrasound with colour Doppler** – First‑line modality; evaluate size, wall thickness, internal septations, vascular pattern, and continuity with uterine tissue. Presence of a pedicle, a broad base attached to the myometrium, or marked peripheral vascularity favours a myoma.  
3. **Serum tumour markers** – CA‑125, CA‑19‑9, CEA, β‑2‑microglobulin, LDH. In pregnancy, CA‑125 may be modestly elevated (median 14 U/mL in the second trimester) and is not diagnostic; serial trends are more informative than a single value.  
4. **Non‑contrast magnetic resonance imaging** – Indicated when ultrasound is indeterminate or when a large mass threatens maternal or fetal physiology. Use a ≤1.5 T scanner, omit gadolinium, and obtain T1/T2 fat‑sat sequences. Look for:  
   - **Myometrial continuity** (low‑signal uterine wall surrounding the lesion)  
   - **Signal heterogeneity** (cystic degeneration → high T2, low T1; hemorrhagic content → high T1)  
   - **Absence of solid enhancing nodules** (would raise suspicion for malignancy)  
5. **Multidisciplinary review** – Radiology, maternal‑fetal medicine, and surgical teams jointly interpret findings and discuss risks versus benefits of operative intervention.  

Applying this algorithm to the current case clarified that the upper mass originated from the uterus (pedunculated leiomyoma) and that the lower mass represented a cystically degenerated intramural‑subserosal myoma rather than an ovarian tumour.

### Interpretation of CA‑125 trends in pregnancy  
- **Physiological peaks** occur in the first (≈ 23 U/mL) and third trimesters (≈ 21 U/mL). Values > 35 U/mL in the second trimester, as seen in this patient (41.8 U/mL), are atypical but still lack specificity.  
- **Sources of elevation** include decidualised endometriosis, adenomyosis, pelvic inflammation, and leiomyoma degeneration. Hence, CA‑125 should be regarded as an adjunct, not a decisive marker, and only significant, progressive rises should prompt further imaging or operative planning.  

### Safety profile of non‑contrast MRI after the first trimester  
- **Magnetic field strength** ≤ 1.5 T is considered safe; no teratogenic effects have been documented.  
- **Gadolinium** is contraindicated unless the benefit decisively outweighs the potential fetal toxicity (risk of nephrogenic systemic fibrosis, unknown long‑term effects).  
- **Acoustic noise** can be mitigated with ear protection; fetal monitoring is not required for non‑contrast scans but may be prudent when the gestational age exceeds 24 weeks.  

### When to consider myomectomy during pregnancy  
Surgical removal of a leiomyoma is reserved for **complicated** lesions. Indications include:  

| Indication | Typical Threshold |
|------------|-------------------|
| **Size** > 10 cm (or rapid growth causing compression) | > 10 cm or > 5 cm increase within 4 weeks |
| **Pedunculated with torsion** (pain, necrosis, fever) | Any size when torsion is evident |
| **Refractory symptoms** (uncontrolled pain, dyspnoea, obstructive urinary symptoms) despite optimal analgesia and positioning | Persistent VAS ≥ 4 |
| **Imminent obstetric compromise** (mass limiting uterine expansion, threatening fetal head descent) | Mass impinging on uterine cavity or lower segment |

In the presented patient, the combination of a > 10 cm pedunculated leiomyoma, signs of torsion (pain, low‑grade fever, necrotic tissue at surgery), and restrictive lung mechanics satisfied three of the four criteria, justifying operative intervention.

### Laparotomy versus laparoscopic myomectomy in the second trimester  

| Parameter | Laparotomy (open) | Laparoscopic (Hasson) |
|-----------|-------------------|-----------------------|
| **Maternal morbidity** | Higher postoperative pain, longer hospital stay (average 5–7 days) | Lower pain scores, shorter stay (2–3 days) |
| **Operative time** | 120‑150 min (as in case) | 90‑110 min (when feasible) |
| **Blood loss** | 300‑800 mL (higher transfusion risk) | 150‑400 mL (often transfusion‑free) |
| **Fetal safety** | Comparable fetal heart‑rate stability when surgery ≤ 2 h | Similar when CO₂ pressure ≤ 12 mm Hg |
| **Mass characteristics** | Preferred for very large (> 12 cm), multiple, or posterior/intramural lesions | Feasible for ≤ 10 cm, pedunculated, anterior lesions, early second trimester |

The decision to perform a laparotomy in this case was driven by the massive size, posterior location, and suspicion of torsion—factors that would have limited the visual field and maneuverability of laparoscopic instruments.

### Progesterone prophylaxis to reduce pre‑term labour during intra‑operative fetal monitoring  
- **Rationale**: Surgical manipulation and anaesthesia can trigger uterine irritability. A single intramuscular dose of 100 mg 17‑hydroxyprogesterone caproate intra‑operatively, followed by daily dosing, has been shown in small series to lower the incidence of postoperative preterm contractions (relative risk ≈ 0.6).  
- **Practical tip**: Administer the first dose immediately after uterine closure, then continue until delivery or at least 4 weeks postpartum. This regimen was employed successfully in the present case, with no subsequent preterm labour.

### Algorithm for delivery planning in the presence of residual uterine masses  

1. **Assess residual mass size and location** (ultrasound at 34‑36 weeks).  
2. **Determine likelihood of obstructed labour**:  
   - *Posterior or isthmic mass > 8 cm* → high risk of impaction → elective CS.  
   - *Anterior, lateral, or subserosal mass < 5 cm* → consider trial of labour.  
3. **Evaluate uterine scar integrity** (post‑myomectomy). If a full‑thickness myometrial repair was performed, schedule CS at 37‑38 weeks to reduce rupture risk.  
4. **Counsel the patient** on neonatal outcomes, maternal recovery, and future fertility.  

In the reported patient, a 9 cm posterior torus myoma was deemed an obstetric obstacle, prompting an elective repeat laparotomy‑CS at 37 weeks, which resulted in an uncomplicated delivery.

### Future research gaps  

- **Randomised controlled trials on timing of myomectomy** (first vs second trimester) to delineate optimal gestational windows that balance maternal symptom relief against fetal risk.  
- **Standardised MRI protocols** (e.g., diffusion‑weighted imaging without contrast) to improve differentiation between degenerated leiomyoma and ovarian pathology.  
- **Long‑term reproductive outcomes** after intra‑pregnancy myomectomy, particularly regarding subsequent fertility, uterine rupture rates, and placental implantation abnormalities.  

Addressing these gaps will refine guidelines, reduce practice variability, and improve counselling for pregnant patients faced with large pelvic masses.  

---

**Take‑home message:** Large, symptomatic uterine masses in pregnancy demand a step‑wise imaging work‑up (US → non‑contrast MRI), careful interpretation of tumour markers, and a multidisciplinary decision‑making process. Myomectomy should be reserved for lesions that are large, pedunculated with torsion, or threatening maternal‑fetal physiology, with laparotomy preferred for very large or posterior lesions. Progesterone prophylaxis and vigilant intra‑operative fetal monitoring enhance safety, while delivery planning must incorporate residual mass characteristics to decide between vaginal birth and elective caesarean section.
