Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Morning Report] Identify the potential complications of severe hyponatraemia in the setting of SIADH and explain why rhabdomyolysis and persistent hiccups can be presenting clues.

**Potential complications of severe hyponatraemia in SIADH (as illustrated in the Behçet‑related case)**  

1. **Cerebral oedema and neurologic deterioration** – Rapid fall in serum Na⁺ (<125 mmol/L) causes water shift into the intracellular compartment, especially brain cells. Patients can develop headache, confusion, seizures, or coma. In the reported patient the persistent hiccups and worsening vertigo were early neurologic warnings that the hyponatraemia was clinically significant.

2. **Rhabdomyolysis** – Severe hypotonicity impairs Na⁺‑dependent muscle cell homeostasis, leading to intracellular swelling, membrane disruption and leakage of creatine kinase (CK) and myoglobin. The case showed a CK of 1 349 U/L, confirming muscle injury that resolved as the sodium level normalized. Rhabdomyolysis can precipitate acute kidney injury if not recognised promptly.

3. **Cardiopulmonary effects** – Hyponatraemia reduces extracellular osmolarity, which can impair cardiac contractility and predispose to pulmonary oedema, particularly when concurrent volume‑status alterations exist. The patient remained euvolemic, but careful fluid monitoring was essential to avoid over‑correction‑related osmotic demyelination.

4. **Osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS)** – Although ODS is a risk of *rapid* correction, it is a feared complication in any severe hyponatraemia because the brain adapts to chronic low Na⁺. The treating team limited fluid intake to <800 mL/day and avoided hypertonic saline, thereby preventing a rapid rise in Na⁺ and the possibility of ODS.

### Why **persistent hiccups** and **rhabdomyolysis** are useful clues

| Symptom | Pathophysiologic link to hyponatraemia | Clinical relevance in the case |
|---------|----------------------------------------|--------------------------------|
| **Persistent hiccups** | Hiccups arise from irritation of the phrenic‑nerve‑mediated respiratory centre in the medulla. Severe hyponatraemia causes subtle brainstem oedema, which can stimulate the hiccup reflex. Reported in meningitis‑related SIADH, hiccups may be the first or only sign of profound hypo‑osmolarity. | The patient’s three‑day history of “persistent hiccups” preceded the neurological work‑up and, together with headache and vertigo, pointed toward a central electrolyte disturbance rather than a primary gastrointestinal cause. |
| **Rhabdomyolysis (elevated CK)** | Low extracellular Na⁺ diminishes the Na⁺/K⁺‑ATPase gradient, leading to intracellular Na⁺ accumulation, calcium overload, and muscle cell lysis. It is a well‑described, though under‑appreciated, complication of Na⁺ <125 mmol/L. | CK was markedly raised (1 349 U/L) on admission, prompting evaluation for muscle injury. Recognition that severe hyponatraemia can cause rhabdomyolysis guided the team to monitor renal function and avoid nephrotoxic agents while treating the electrolyte abnormality. |

**Take‑home teaching points**

- In any patient with SIADH, **always assess for neurologic signs** (confusion, seizures, hiccups) and **muscle enzyme elevation**; both may herald severe hyponatraemia even when the patient appears clinically stable.
- **Fluid restriction** and **slow, controlled correction** (≤8 mmol/L in 24 h) are mandatory to prevent ODS while allowing the underlying inflammation (neuro‑Behçet’s) to resolve.
- Resolution of the hyponatraemia, hiccups, and CK elevation after colchicine therapy in this case underscores that treating the primary inflammatory driver (neuro‑BD) is as important as the electrolyte management.
