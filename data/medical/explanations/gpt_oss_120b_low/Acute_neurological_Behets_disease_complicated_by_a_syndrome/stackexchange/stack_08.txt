Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Management] How would you monitor and safely correct hyponatraemia in a patient with SIADH secondary to neuro‑inflammation, and what are the risks of overly rapid sodium correction?

**Monitoring and correcting hyponatraemia in SIADH secondary to neuro‑Behçet’s disease**

1. **Initial assessment and goals**  
   * Confirm true hypotonic hyponatraemia – serum Na < 135 mmol/L, serum osmolality < 275 mOsm/kg, inappropriately concentrated urine (> 100 mOsm/kg) with urine Na > 30 mmol/L, as was demonstrated in the case (Na 124 mmol/L, serum osmolality 267 mOsm/kg, urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg, urine Na 88 mmol/L).  
   * Establish volume status (euvolemic in SIADH) and exclude adrenal, thyroid, renal, and drug‑induced causes. In the reported patient this work‑up was already complete.  

2. **Monitoring protocol**  
   * **Serum sodium**: check every 2–4 h while any active correction is being undertaken; after stabilization, daily checks are sufficient.  
   * **Fluid balance**: strict input‑output chart; record oral intake, intravenous fluids, and urine output.  
   * **Weight**: daily weight to detect hidden fluid shifts.  
   * **Neurologic status**: repeat mental‑status exam at each sodium check; any new confusion, seizures, or focal deficit may signal rapid over‑correction.  

3. **Therapeutic hierarchy for SIADH in neuro‑inflammation**  

   | Step | Intervention | Rationale in this case |
   |------|---------------|------------------------|
   | **A. Treat the underlying inflammation** | Initiate disease‑specific therapy (colchicine in this patient). Effective control of neuro‑Behçet’s inflammation reduces ectopic ADH release (IL‑6‑mediated). | The patient’s sodium normalized after colchicine, allowing a conservative approach. |
   | **B. Fluid restriction** | Limit free water to 800–1000 mL/day (≈ 0.5 L/24 h). This is the cornerstone of SIADH management when the underlying cause is being treated. | The team used fluid restriction alone; no hypertonic saline was required. |
   | **C. Pharmacologic adjuncts (if Na < 120 mmol/L or symptomatic)** | • **Oral urea** 15–30 g tid – increases free‑water excretion. <br>• **Vaptans** (tolvaptan) – V2‑receptor antagonists; reserve for refractory cases. | Not needed here because the patient remained asymptomatic and Na rose slowly with fluid restriction. |
   | **D. Hypertonic saline (3 % NaCl)** | Indicated only for severe symptoms (seizures, obtundation) or Na < 115 mmol/L with rapid decline. Give 100 mL bolus over 10 min, then re‑check Na in 30 min; repeat only if Na rise < 4‑6 mmol/L and neurologic status dictates. | The case did not require this; the patient was alert and neurologically intact. |

4. **Rate of correction – safety limits**  
   * **Desired increase**: ≤ 8 mmol/L in any 24‑h period; many centres aim for ≤ 6 mmol/L/24 h in high‑risk patients (e.g., alcohol misuse, malnutrition, chronic hyponatraemia).  
   * **Why the limit?** Rapid Na⁺ shifts create an osmotic gradient that pulls water out of brain cells, precipitating **osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS)** (central pontine and extrapontine myelinolysis). ODS may manifest as dysarthria, dysphagia, quadriplegia, “locked‑in” syndrome, or isolated movement disorders, often with delayed onset (2–6 days after over‑correction).  
   * **Monitoring for over‑correction**: if Na rises > 8 mmol/L in 24 h, promptly re‑infuse **desmopressin (DDAVP) 1–2 µg IV** and give **free water** (e.g., 5 % dextrose) to halt or reverse the rise. In the reported patient, Na rose from 124 to 136 mmol/L over ~2 weeks, far below the safety threshold, obviating the need for rescue therapy.  

5. **Practical flow for this scenario**

   1. **Day 0–1**: Confirm SIADH, start fluid restriction (≤ 800 mL/day), obtain baseline Na, weight, urine output.  
   2. **Day 1‑3**: Check Na q4 h. If rise ≤ 4 mmol/L/24 h, continue fluid restriction; add colchicine for neuro‑Behçet’s, which should curtail ADH drive.  
   3. **Day 3‑7**: If Na still < 130 mmol/L and patient asymptomatic, maintain fluid restriction; consider oral urea if Na < 120 mmol/L persists.  
   4. **Day 7‑14**: As inflammatory markers improve (CRP down, ADH normalizes), anticipate gradual Na rise; continue daily Na checks until ≥ 135 mmol/L.  
   5. **If at any point Na ↑ > 8 mmol/L/24 h**: stop fluid restriction, give DDAVP 1 µg IV, infuse 5 % dextrose, and re‑measure Na in 2 h.  

6. **Take‑home pearls**  

   * **Treat the trigger** – controlling neuro‑Behçet’s inflammation (colchicine in this case) is the most effective way to resolve SIADH.  
   * **Fluid restriction is usually sufficient** when the underlying disease is managed and the patient is neurologically stable.  
   * **Never exceed the 8 mmol/L/24 h sodium‐rise limit**; ODS, though rare, is devastating and preventable.  
   * **Frequent labs and neurologic checks** are the safety net that allows early detection of over‑correction.  

In the presented patient, meticulous fluid restriction, daily sodium surveillance, and prompt anti‑inflammatory therapy led to a safe, stepwise correction of hyponatraemia without the need for hypertonic saline or vaptans, and no neurologic sequelae from rapid correction were observed. This approach exemplifies the standard of care for SIADH secondary to CNS inflammation.
