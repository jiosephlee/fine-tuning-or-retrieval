Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Morning Report] In a patient with fever, headache, hyponatraemia and aseptic meningitis, outline the stepwise differential diagnosis and the key investigations that help distinguish infectious meningitis from acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease.

**Acute fever‑headache‑hyponatraemia with aseptic meningitis – how do we separate an infectious meningitis from acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD)?**  

Below is a step‑wise diagnostic algorithm that can be used at the bedside, illustrated with the case of the 60‑year‑old man who presented with fever, headache, marked hyponatraemia (Na⁺ 124 mmol/L) and a lymphocytic CSF pleocytosis.

---

### 1.  First‑line “rule‑out” for infection (the “infectious‑meningitis” branch)

| Question / Finding | What it tells you | Where it fits in the algorithm |
|--------------------|-------------------|--------------------------------|
| **Rapid onset, high fever, neck stiffness?** | Classic bacterial meningitis (usually >38.5 °C, stiff neck, altered mental status). | If present → start empiric ceftriaxone + vancomycin ± ampicillin, obtain urgent CSF. |
| **CSF opening pressure > 25 cm H₂O, neutrophil predominance, low glucose, high protein?** | Bacterial meningitis (or early tuberculous meningitis). | Positive → treat as bacterial; consider adjunctive dexamethasone. |
| **CSF lymphocytic pleocytosis, normal/ mildly low glucose, protein ↑ modestly?** | Viral or aseptic meningitis, tuberculous meningitis, early neuro‑BD. | Proceed to targeted viral PCR and TB work‑up. |
| **Viral PCR (HSV‑1/2, VZV, enteroviruses, EBV, CMV) negative?** | Decreases likelihood of common viral encephalitis. | In the case, all viral studies were negative. |
| **CSF acid‑fast stain, TB PCR, culture negative?** | Makes tuberculous meningitis unlikely (but remember that TB may be smear‑negative early). | In the case, TB tests were negative. |
| **Blood cultures, urine cultures, chest X‑ray, ENT exam – no focus of infection** | Low pre‑test probability for bacterial sepsis. | Reinforces moving away from infection. |

**Take‑home point:** Once the CSF shows a *lymphocytic* picture, normal glucose and negative bacterial/viral/amoebic studies, infectious meningitis becomes a diagnosis of exclusion.

---

### 2.  Evaluate for **non‑infectious inflammatory meningitis** – the “neuro‑Behçet” branch

| Clinical clue | Why it matters | How it guides testing |
|---------------|----------------|-----------------------|
| **Mucocutaneous lesions (oral aphthae, genital ulcers, folliculitis‑like rash)** | Pathognomonic for Behçet’s disease (BD). | Apply the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD). In the case, oral aphthae (2 pts) + genital ulcers (2 pts) + skin lesions (1 pt) = 5 pts → definite BD. |
| **HLA‑B51 positivity** | Supports BD epidemiologically but not required for diagnosis. | Helpful background but does not change immediate management. |
| **Absence of focal neurologic deficits or parenchymal lesions on MRI** | Acute neuro‑BD can present as “meningoencephalitis‑like” syndrome with a *normal* MRI early on. | Normal brain MRI does **not** exclude neuro‑BD; repeat imaging if symptoms persist. |
| **CSF profile: mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (≤30 cells/µL), protein ↑ modestly, glucose normal** | Typical for acute neuro‑BD meningoencephalitis. | Correlate with systemic BD features. |
| **Elevated inflammatory markers (CRP > 100 mg/L, ESR > 50 mm/hr)** | Reflects systemic vasculitis; also explains IL‑6‑driven SIADH. | No specific cutoff, but helps differentiate from viral meningitis (usually CRP modest). |
| **SIADH (eu‑volemic hyponatraemia, low serum osmolality, urine osmolality >300 mOsm/kg, urine Na⁺ >30 mmol/L, ADH ↑)** | CNS inflammation can trigger ADH release; SIADH is a recognized complication of neuro‑BD. | Confirms a neuro‑inflammatory process; does not distinguish infection vs. BD alone, but supports an inflammatory etiology. |

**Key investigations that tip the balance toward neuro‑BD**

1. **Detailed mucocutaneous exam** – look for *genital ulcers* (high specificity) and *typical skin lesions* (pseudofolliculitis, erythema nodosum‑like).  
2. **ICBD scoring** – ≥4 points = definite BD.  
3. **Brain MRI with contrast** – while early neuro‑BD may be normal, look for characteristic brainstem, basal ganglia or thalamic hyperintensities (if present, strongly supportive).  
4. **CSF analysis** – lymphocytic pleocytosis, protein 0.5‑1.0 g/L, normal glucose, *negative cultures/PCR*.  
5. **Serum IL‑6 (if available)** – markedly elevated IL‑6 favors a cytokine‑driven inflammatory process and can explain SIADH. (Not done in the case but conceptually useful.)  
6. **Exclusion labs** – ACE (sarcoidosis), sIL‑2R (lymphoma), ANA, ANCA, anti‑NMDAR antibodies – performed only if clinical suspicion exists; all were negative in the case.  

---

### 3.  Putting it together – a practical flow‑chart for the presenting triad

```
Fever + Headache + Hyponatraemia
          |
   → Obtain serum electrolytes, osmolality, urine studies (SIADH screen)
          |
   → Perform lumbar puncture (CSF cell count, protein, glucose, cultures,
                              viral PCR, TB PCR)
          |
   ├─ If CSF neutrophilic, low glucose → Bacterial meningitis → treat
   └─ If CSF lymphocytic, cultures/PCR negative → Proceed to non‑infectious work‑up
          |
   → Detailed skin/mucosal exam + ICBD scoring
          |
   ├─ Meets ICBD criteria → Diagnose systemic BD
   │    → Add CSF + MRI → If compatible = acute neuro‑BD
   └─ Does NOT meet BD criteria → Consider other aseptic meningitis causes
          (autoimmune, sarcoid, lymphoma, drug‑induced SIADH)
```

---

### 4.  Take‑home pearls for the morning report

* **Infectious meningitis is the first consideration**, but a *lymphocytic* CSF with negative PCR/cultures should prompt a search for an inflammatory etiology.  
* **Behçet’s disease can present neurologically at the first encounter**; look carefully for oral/genital ulcers and characteristic skin lesions.  
* **A normal brain MRI does not exclude acute neuro‑BD**; the early meningo‑encephalitic phase may be radiologically silent.  
* **SIADH is a valuable physiologic clue**—CNS inflammation (viral, tuberculous, or neuro‑BD) can cause ADH release; hyponatraemia often precedes or accompanies the neurologic picture.  
* **CSF analysis remains the cornerstone**: modest lymphocytic pleocytosis (≤30 cells/µL) with protein up‑trend and sterile cultures leans toward neuro‑BD once systemic BD is established.  
* **Management implications**: If neuro‑BD is diagnosed, immunosuppression (corticosteroids ± colchicine, azathioprine, or cyclophosphamide) is indicated; however, this case shows that colchicine alone can suffice in a mild, self‑limited presentation.  

By following this step‑wise approach, you can efficiently distinguish infectious meningitis from acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease in patients who present with the striking triad of fever, headache, hyponatraemia and aseptic meningitis.
