# When Behçet’s Hits the Brain: Recognizing Acute Neuro‑Behçet’s at First Presentation  

*By [Your Name], MD – EM/Neurology*  

---

## Why This Case Stands Out  

Seeing a patient walk into the ED with fever, headache, a handful of oral ulcers and a serum sodium of 124 mmol/L is already a diagnostic headache. Add in a rash that looks like folliculitis and a genital ulcer that appears *after* admission, and you’ve got a puzzle that most clinicians will first try to solve as infection or malignancy.  

What makes the case described by Kakehi et al. remarkable is that **systemic Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro‑Behçet’s (neuro‑BD) were diagnosed *simultaneously***—a scenario that occurs in less than 5 % of BD patients. Even more intriguing, the neuro‑inflammatory process manifested as **SIADH‑induced hyponatraemia** without any obvious parenchymal lesions on MRI. The whole picture forced the team to think beyond the usual “meningitis = infection” algorithm and ultimately treat the patient with colchicine alone, achieving full recovery.  

If you haven’t encountered neuro‑BD at first presentation, this case is a perfect reminder that BD can be a “great masquerader” of central nervous system (CNS) inflammation.

---

## Quick Refresher: Behçet’s Disease & Neuro‑Behçet’s  

| Feature | Systemic BD | Neuro‑BD |
|---|---|---|
| **Typical age** | 20‑40 y (peak) | 30‑50 y, but can be older |
| **Key clinical hallmarks** | Recurrent oral aphthae, genital ulcers, skin lesions (erythema‑nodosum‑like, papulopustular), uveitis | Parenchymal (brainstem, basal ganglia) or non‑parenchymal (vascular, meningitis) CNS involvement |
| **Epidemiology of neuro‑BD** | 10‑20 % of BD patients develop CNS disease, usually **years** after initial mucocutaneous symptoms | Acute neuro‑BD (meningoencephalitis‑type) may present early, but rare at initial diagnosis |
| **Typical MRI** | Parenchymal hyperintensities in brainstem, thalamus, basal ganglia | Often abnormal; however, early disease can have a normal scan (as in this case) |

## The Clinical Red Flags That Should Prompt Neuro‑BD Consideration  

1. **Concurrent mucocutaneous lesions** – Oral aphthae plus any of: genital ulcer, papulopustular rash, folliculitis‑like lesions. Even a single genital ulcer after admission can tip the scale.  
2. **Non‑specific CNS symptoms in the setting of inflammation** – Fever, headache, vertigo, hiccups, or encephalopathic features without focal deficits.  
3. **Hyponatraemia out of proportion** – Serum Na < 130 mmol/L with euvolemic status, low serum osmolality, high urine osmolality and Na⁺ – classic SIADH pattern. Inflammation of the hypothalamus or brainstem can trigger ADH release.  
4. **CSF picture of aseptic meningitis** – Mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, elevated protein, normal glucose, negative cultures & PCR.  
5. **Absence of definitive infectious or neoplastic findings** – Normal brain MRI, negative CSF PCRs, no systemic signs of malignancy.  

When these pieces come together, think **acute neuro‑Behçet’s** even if the patient has never been diagnosed with BD before.

---

## Work‑up Blueprint  

1. **Detailed skin & mucosal exam** – Document number, size, and evolution of oral/genital ulcers; look for “folliculitis‑like” papules on face/trunk.  
2. **Baseline labs** – CBC, CRP/ESR, CMP (focus on Na⁺, CK), HIV/Hepatitis panel, autoimmune screen *only if* clinical suspicion exists.  
3. **Hyponatraemia algorithm** – Verify euvolemic status, obtain serum/urine osmolality, urinary Na⁺, and measure ADH if available. Apply the classic SIADH criteria.  
4. **Neuroimaging** – MRI with and without contrast; include high‑resolution brainstem and hypothalamic sequences. Remember a normal scan does **not** exclude early neuro‑BD.  
5. **Lumbar puncture** – Opening pressure, cell count, protein, glucose, cultures, viral PCRs, cytology. A modest lymphocytic pleocytosis (≤30 cells/µL) with high protein is typical.  
6. **International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD)** – Score ≥ 4 (oral aphthae = 2, genital ulcer = 2, skin lesions = 1). Positive HLA‑B51 supports but is not required.  

If infection, malignancy, and other autoimmune conditions are reasonably excluded, you have sufficient ground to label the case as **acute neuro‑BD**.

## Therapeutic Nuances: Colchicine as a First‑Line Option?  

Standard teaching: **high‑dose glucocorticoids** ± immunosuppressants (azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, anti‑TNF agents) for neuro‑BD.  

The reported case challenges this paradigm:

* **Colchicine monotherapy** (1.5 mg/day) led to rapid resolution of fever, headache, meningitic CSF changes, and hyponatraemia.  
* No steroids or biologics were used; the patient remained steroid‑free for four years.  

**Mechanistic rationale** – Colchicine blocks neutrophil chemotaxis and dampens IL‑1β/IL‑6 production, cytokines implicated in both BD flares and SIADH‑driven ADH secretion.  

**Take‑home:** While colchicine should *not* replace steroids in severe parenchymal neuro‑BD (e.g., brainstem infarction, large lesions), it may be a reasonable initial agent in **mild, meningoencephalitic presentations** when steroids are contraindicated or when the patient is already euvolemic and hyponatraemic. Close monitoring for neurological deterioration remains mandatory.

---

## Practical Pearls & Take‑Home Messages  

- **Don’t let a normal MRI lull you into complacency.** Early acute neuro‑BD can look radiographically silent; CSF and clinical context are decisive.  
- **Hyponatraemia = red flag.** SIADH in the setting of fever/meningitic symptoms should prompt evaluation for CNS inflammation, including neuro‑BD.  
- **Mucocutaneous clues are worth a full point.** Even a single genital ulcer appearing after admission can push you over the ICBD threshold.  
- **CSF can be the decisive study.** A modest lymphocytic pleocytosis with high protein in an otherwise sterile work‑up is classic for aseptic meningeal involvement in BD.  
- **Colchicine may be more than a mucocutaneous soothing agent.** In selected patients with acute neuro‑BD, it can control inflammation and correct SIADH without steroids.  

---

### Quick Checklist for the Busy Clinician  

- [ ] Fever + headache + oral/genital ulcers → think BD.  
- [ ] Serum Na < 130 mmol/L, euvolemic → run SIADH work‑up.  
- [ ] MRI normal **and** CSF aseptic → consider early neuro‑BD.  
- [ ] ICBD score ≥ 4 → definite Behçet’s diagnosis.  
- [ ] Start colchicine (1.5 mg daily) *if* neurological signs are mild and no contraindications; add steroids only if deterioration or parenchymal lesions appear.  

---

**Bottom line:** Acute neuro‑Behçet’s can present at the very first encounter, masquerading as “just another meningitis.” A high index of suspicion, systematic exclusion of infection, and appreciation of the hyponatraemia‑SIADH link can spare patients from unnecessary antibiotics and aggressive immunosuppression—sometimes a simple colchicine tablet does the trick. Stay vigilant, keep the Behçet’s checklist handy, and remember: the brain sometimes shouts before the skin has finished talking.
