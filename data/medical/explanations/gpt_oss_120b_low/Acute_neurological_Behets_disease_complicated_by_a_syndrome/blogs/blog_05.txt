Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# Beyond the Ulcers: Systemic Behçet’s Disease and Endocrine Complications  

## Why This Case Got My Attention  

When a 60‑year‑old man walks into the ED with fever, a pounding headache, and a handful of oral ulcers, you’re already thinking “Behçet’s disease” – especially if the skin is dotted with folliculitis‑like papules and a painful genital ulcer later shows up.  What makes the story truly **unique** is that, on the same admission, he develops a profound hyponatraemia (Na⁺ 124 mmol/L) that fulfills the classic criteria for **syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH)**.  

Neurological Behçet’s (neuro‑BD) itself is rare (10‑20 % of BD patients) and usually appears years after the mucocutaneous lesions.  Simultaneous diagnosis of systemic BD, acute neuro‑BD, **and** SIADH is practically unheard of.  Even more surprising—he improves dramatically with colchicine alone, without corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants.  

This case forces us to ask:  

* Are endocrine disturbances an under‑appreciated facet of Behçet’s?  
* How often should we be looking for them, and what mechanisms might be driving the link?  

Below we unpack the endocrine spectrum of BD, walk through the pathophysiology, and lay out a pragmatic screening approach for the busy clinician.  

## Quick Recap of the Index Case  

| Feature | Key Findings |
|--------|--------------|
| **Age / Sex** | 60‑year‑old Asian man |
| **Presenting symptoms** | Fever, headache, hiccups, arthralgia, oral aphthae |
| **Mucocutaneous signs** | Oral ulcers, folliculitis‑like rash, genital ulcer (HLA‑B51⁺) |
| **Neurologic work‑up** | Normal brain MRI, CSF: mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, ↑ protein |
| **Endocrine labs** | Hyponatraemia 124 mmol/L, low serum osmolality, high urine osmolality, ↑ urinary Na⁺, ADH 22.4 pg/mL → SIADH |
| **Management** | Colchicine monotherapy, fluid restriction; steroids avoided |
| **Outcome** | Resolution of fever, neurologic symptoms, mucocutaneous lesions, and Na⁺ normalization within 2 weeks; 4‑year remission on colchicine alone |

The take‑home message from this single vignette is that **acute neuro‑BD can trigger SIADH**, and that **colchicine may be sufficient** to tame both the inflammatory and endocrine storm in selected patients.  

## The Broader Endocrine Landscape in Behçet’s  

While the textbook definition of BD centers on oral/genital ulcers, uveitis, and skin lesions, a growing body of case reports and small series points to a surprisingly diverse set of endocrine derangements.  Below is a non‑exhaustive list of the most frequently reported entities, together with a brief mechanistic hypothesis for each.  

### 1. SIADH (the star of our case)  

* **Epidemiology:** < 1 % of BD cohorts, but reported more often in neuro‑BD series.  
* **Mechanism:** CNS inflammation → cytokine surge (IL‑6, IL‑1β) → hypothalamic ADH release. IL‑6 is known to stimulate ADH directly; BD is a prototypical IL‑6‑rich disease.  
* **Clinical clue:** Persistent hiccups, nausea, or unexplained low Na⁺ in a febrile BD patient.  

### 2. Adrenal Insufficiency  

* **Reports:** Isolated case reports of primary adrenal failure secondary to vasculitic infiltration or Addisonian crisis precipitated by infection in BD.  
* **Mechanism:** Small‑vessel vasculitis causing adrenal hemorrhage (Waterhouse‑Friderichsen‑like) or granulomatous destruction.  
* **Red flags:** Hyperpigmentation, orthostatic hypotension, hyponatraemia with hyperkalaemia, refractory fatigue.  

### 3. Thyroiditis (subclinical or overt)  

* **Reports:** A handful of cases of painless thyroiditis or Graves‑type hyperthyroidism.  
* **Mechanism:** Autoimmune cross‑reactivity; BD shares HLA‑B51 with other autoimmune thyroid disorders. Cytokine‑mediated follicular injury may precipitate thyroid hormone leakage.  
* **Red flags:** Tremor, heat intolerance, unexplained tachycardia in a BD patient.

### 4. Diabetes Mellitus (type 2)  

* **Reports:** Not truly “Behçet‑related” but a frequent comorbidity, often exacerbated by glucocorticoid flares.  
* **Mechanism:** Chronic inflammation → insulin resistance; glucocorticoids worsen glycaemia.  

### 5. Hyperparathyroidism & Calcium Dysregulation  

* **Reports:** Rare, but isolated hypercalcaemia described in vasculitic lesions affecting parathyroid glands.  
* **Mechanism:** Granulomatous production of 1,25‑OH‑vitamin D.  

### 6. Pituitary Involvement  

* **Reports:** Case series of pituitary stalk thickening, hypophysitis, and either diabetes insipidus or pan‑hypopituitarism.  
* **Mechanism:** Direct inflammatory infiltration of the pituitary‑hypothalamic axis.  

## Putting Pathophysiology Into Practice  

The common denominator across most of these endocrine hits is **systemic vasculitis plus a cytokine surge** (IL‑1β, IL‑6, TNF‑α).  In the central nervous system, inflammation can hijack the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, leading to either excess (SIADH) or deficiency (adrenal insufficiency).  Peripheral vascular inflammation can compromise glandular perfusion (e.g., adrenal hemorrhage).  

**Key point:** Endocrine manifestations often *precede* obvious organ damage; they may be the first clue that your BD is not just “ulcers on the skin.”  

## Practical Screening Recommendations  

1. **Baseline labs at diagnosis of systemic BD**  
   * CBC, CMP (focus on Na⁺, K⁺, glucose, calcium)  
   * ESR/CRP (to gauge inflammatory load)  
   * Thyroid panel (TSH, free T₄)  
   * Morning cortisol (optional, but low threshold if fatigue, hypotension, or hyponatraemia)  

2. **Targeted endocrine work‑up when neuro‑BD is suspected**  
   * Serum and urine osmolality, urine Na⁺ → SIADH screen  
   * ADH level (if available) – not mandatory, but helpful for academic cases  
   * MRI of hypothalamic‑pituitary region if neurologic signs are focal or persistent  

3. **During acute flares** (especially when high‑dose steroids are used)  
   * Re‑check glucose and electrolytes daily for the first 72 h  
   * Monitor for new‑onset hyponatraemia or hyperkalaemia  

4. **When steroids are prescribed**  
   * Prophylactic bone health assessment (DEXA) – steroids are a known risk factor  
   * Educate patients on symptoms of adrenal crisis (especially if tapering rapidly)  

5. **Long‑term follow‑up** (every 6–12 months)  
   * Repeat thyroid panel; consider anti‑thyroid antibodies if TSH abnormal  
   * Annual fasting glucose/HbA1c, even if previously normoglycaemic  

**Bottom line:** A single set of baseline labs will catch the majority of clinically relevant endocrine issues; repeat testing should be driven by new symptoms or high‑dose steroid exposure.  

## Therapeutic Nuggets from the Index Case  

* **Colchicine as a front‑line agent** – In this patient, colchicine (1.5 mg daily) was enough to quell both the mucocutaneous flare and the neuro‑inflammatory process, leading to rapid Na⁺ correction without the need for steroids.  While colchicine is classically a mucocutaneous therapy, its inhibition of neutrophil chemotaxis and IL‑1β/IL‑6 production may make it a viable “bridge” for mild neuro‑BD.  
* **Fluid restriction remains cornerstone for SIADH** – Target < 800 mL/day, monitor Na⁺ every 6–12 h, avoid rapid shifts (> 8 mmol/L/24 h) to prevent osmotic demyelination.  
* **Avoid unnecessary hypertonic saline** – In the setting of SIADH secondary to inflammation, correction is usually modest; aggressive saline may worsen cerebral edema.  

When steroids are unavoidable (e.g., fulminant parenchymal lesions), taper quickly and consider adding colchicine or azathioprine to limit steroid exposure and the downstream risk of iatrogenic diabetes.  

## Take‑Home Pearls  

* **Think endocrine early.** Fever, headache, and hyponatraemia in a BD patient should immediately raise suspicion for SIADH, especially if CSF shows inflammatory changes.  
* **Neuro‑BD can be a “silent” diagnosis.** Normal MRI does *not* exclude acute meningoencephalitis‑type neuro‑BD; CSF analysis and clinical context are essential.  
* **Colchicine may be more than skin‑deep.** In select patients with mild neuro‑BD and SIADH, colchicine monotherapy can control inflammation and restore electrolyte balance.  
* **Screening is cheap, consequences are not.** A baseline CMP, thyroid panel, and cortisol level at BD diagnosis can prevent missed adrenal insufficiency or thyroid dysfunction down the line.  
* **Cytokines are the bridge.** IL‑6 and IL‑1β link vascular inflammation to ADH release, adrenal cortical suppression, and thyroid disruption—reminding us that Behçet’s is fundamentally an immunologic disease with multisystem sequelae.  

## Final Thoughts  

Behçet’s disease may start as “recurrent mouth ulcers,” but the story often unfolds across organ systems we don’t routinely inspect.  Endocrine complications—SIADH, adrenal insufficiency, thyroiditis—are rare but clinically significant.  By incorporating a **low‑threshold endocrine screen** into the initial work‑up and staying vigilant for atypical neurologic presentations, we can catch these complications early, tailor therapy (perhaps even with colchicine alone), and spare patients from the morbidity of uncontrolled inflammation and electrolyte derangements.  

Stay curious, keep your labs broad, and remember: **the next ulcer might be a hormone.**
