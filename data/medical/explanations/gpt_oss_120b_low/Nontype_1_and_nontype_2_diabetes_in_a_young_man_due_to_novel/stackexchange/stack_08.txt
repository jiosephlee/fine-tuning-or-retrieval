Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Management] What are the implications of identifying a pathogenic HNF1A mutation for the patient’s family members, and what cascade testing strategy would you recommend?

**Answer – Implications of a pathogenic HNF1A mutation for relatives and recommended cascade testing**

The identification of a heterozygous pathogenic HNF1A variant (p.N270S) in this young man confirms autosomal‑dominant HNF1A‑MODY. Because the mutation is transmitted in a 50 % manner to each first‑degree relative, any biologically related adult who carries the same allele is at high risk of developing progressive β‑cell failure and will usually manifest diabetes before the third decade of life.  

**Key implications for family members**

| Issue | Clinical relevance |
|------|---------------------|
| **Risk of diabetes** | ≈ 50 % chance of inheriting the mutation; carriers typically develop diabetes by age 25‑35, often with mild‑to‑moderate hyperglycaemia, post‑prandial excursions, and a low renal glucose threshold (glycosuria). |
| **Therapeutic advantage** | Early molecular diagnosis permits use of low‑dose sulfonylureas or GLP‑1‑RA ± metformin rather than lifelong insulin, dramatically reducing hypoglycaemia risk and improving quality of life (as illustrated by the proband’s HbA1c fall from 10.2 % to 6.0 %). |
| **Surveillance** | For carriers without overt diabetes, yearly fasting glucose or HbA1c beginning at age 10 – 12, with a low‑threshold oral glucose tolerance test (2‑h ≥ 11.1 mmol/L) and periodic C‑peptide assessment to gauge β‑cell reserve. |
| **Reproductive counselling** | The mutation confers a 50 % transmission risk to each offspring; pre‑conception or prenatal genetic counselling is appropriate for couples who wish to know the fetal genotype. |
| **Psychosocial impact** | Knowledge of carrier status may relieve uncertainty, but counseling should address anxiety and the need for lifelong monitoring. |

**Cascade testing strategy**

1. **Identify the pedigree** – Draw a three‑generation family tree, marking all individuals with documented diabetes (maternal grandmother, mother, brother) and those with unknown status.

2. **Test all first‑degree relatives** (parents, siblings, children). In this family:
   * **Mother** – already diabetic; predictive testing will confirm whether her disease is mutation‑linked (informative for future children).
   * **Younger brother** – diabetic; testing clarifies whether his phenotype is HNF1A‑MODY (guides therapy).
   * **Father** – clinically unaffected; a negative test excludes him as a carrier and reduces anxiety for his side of the family.

3. **If a first‑degree relative is positive**, extend testing to their first‑degree relatives (second‑degree relatives of the proband). This includes:
   * Maternal aunt/uncle and their children (maternal cousins).
   * The proband’s own children (once they are ≥ 5 years old, or earlier if there is unexplained hyperglycaemia).

4. **Testing methodology** – Targeted Sanger sequencing or a focused NGS panel for the known HNF1A c.809A>G (p.N270S) variant is sufficient; full‑gene sequencing is not required once the pathogenic allele is defined.

5. **Timing** – Offer testing promptly after the proband’s result is communicated. For asymptomatic minors, testing is justified because early detection changes management (choice of oral agents, avoidance of unnecessary insulin, and lifestyle planning).

6. **Interpretation and follow‑up** –  
   * **Positive result** – initiate MODY surveillance protocol (annual fasting glucose/HbA1c, education on low‑threshold glycosuria, and consider early low‑dose sulfonylurea/GLP‑1‑RA if hyperglycaemia emerges).  
   * **Negative result** – reassure that the risk of MODY is not increased; routine diabetes screening according to population guidelines applies.

7. **Documentation** – Record the variant in the patient’s electronic health record and request inclusion in any national rare‑disease registry to facilitate future genotype‑phenotype studies.

**Bottom line:** Because HNF1A‑MODY is an autosomal‑dominant disease with high penetrance, cascade genetic testing of all first‑degree relatives (and subsequently their first‑degree relatives if positive) is essential. Early identification allows genotype‑directed therapy, prevents unnecessary insulin exposure, and provides accurate prognostic information for the entire extended family.
