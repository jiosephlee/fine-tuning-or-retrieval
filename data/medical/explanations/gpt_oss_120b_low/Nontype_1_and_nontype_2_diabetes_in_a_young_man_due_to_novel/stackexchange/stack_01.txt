Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Clinical Pearls] What clinical features in this patient most strongly suggest a diagnosis of MODY rather than type 1 or type 2 diabetes?

**Key clinical clues that point toward MODY (HNF1A‑type) rather than classic type 1 or type 2 diabetes**

| Feature | Why it favors MODY | How it appears in the case |
|---------|-------------------|----------------------------|
| **Age of onset < 25 y (often in the teens‑early 20 s)** | MODY is defined by diabetes that manifests before the typical age of type 2 disease and without the abrupt β‑cell loss seen in type 1. | The patient was a *young adult* (diagnosed in his early 20 s). |
| **Strong autosomal‑dominant family history of early‑onset diabetes** | Affected first‑degree relatives usually develop diabetes in the same age window; this is uncommon in sporadic type 2 and rare in type 1 (which is usually not familial). | Mother (late‑20 s), maternal grandmother (30 s) and younger brother (20 y) all have diabetes. |
| **Absence of pancreatic auto‑antibodies** | Type 1 diabetes is characteristically GADA, IA‑2A, ZnT8 positive; MODY patients are almost always negative. | Both GADA and IA‑2A were negative. |
| **Preserved (though not robust) C‑peptide** | In early type 1, C‑peptide may be low or falling; in MODY there is generally enough residual β‑cell function to produce measurable C‑peptide. | Glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide rose to 0.83 nmol/L (above the 0.60 nmol/L threshold for preserved secretion). |
| **Lack of marked insulin resistance** | Type 2 diabetes usually accompanies obesity, acanthosis nigricans, dyslipidaemia, or a high BMI; MODY patients often have normal‑weight bodies and no skin signs of insulin resistance. | BMI = 24.6 kg/m² (borderline high but not obese), *no* acanthosis nigricans; lipid profile shows isolated hypertriglyceridaemia but not classic metabolic syndrome. |
| **Presence of glycosuria with only modest hyperglycaemia** | HNF1A‑MODY reduces renal SGLT2 expression, lowering the glucose renal threshold; modest glucose levels can still produce glycosuria—a feature rarely seen in type 1/2. | The patient was identified during a routine exam because of *hyperglycaemia plus glycosuria* despite an HbA1c of 10 % (glucose not extremely high). |
| **No ketoacidosis at presentation** | DKA is typical of untreated type 1; MODY patients develop hyperglycaemia without ketosis. | The case explicitly notes *absence of diabetic ketoacidosis*. |
| **Rapid response to non‑insulin agents (GLP‑1 agonist, metformin)** | HNF1A‑MODY is exquisitely sensitive to agents that amplify residual insulin secretion (sulfonylureas, GLP‑1 RAs). Type 1 patients would remain insulin‑dependent, and many type 2 patients need higher‑dose insulin. | After confirming the HNF1A mutation, insulin was stopped and HbA1c fell from 9.1 % to 6.0 % on dulaglutide + metformin. |

**Putting it together in this patient**

- Early‑onset diabetes with a *clear autosomal‑dominant pedigree*.
- Negative islet autoantibodies and *preserved stimulated C‑peptide* argue against type 1.
- BMI near normal, *absence of acanthosis nigricans* and *no overt insulin‑resistance phenotype* make classic type 2 unlikely.
- Glycosuria at relatively modest glucose levels and *lack of ketosis* are classic “red flags” for HNF1A‑MODY.
- The excellent glycaemic response to a GLP‑1 receptor agonist (and later to metformin) further supports a β‑cell‑defect rather than insulin resistance or absolute insulin deficiency.

These combined clinical pearls should raise a high pre‑test probability for MODY (the Exeter calculator gave a 62 % probability in this case) and prompt targeted genetic testing, which ultimately identified the novel pathogenic HNF1A variant.
