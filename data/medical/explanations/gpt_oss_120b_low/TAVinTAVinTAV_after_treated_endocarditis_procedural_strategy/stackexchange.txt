Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Clinical Pearls] What are the key anatomic measurements obtained from pre‑procedural multislice CT that determine the feasibility of a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV procedure and how do they influence the risk of coronary obstruction?

**Clinical Pearls – Pre‑procedural CT Planning for a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV**

When a third transcatheter valve must be placed inside two previously implanted devices, multidetector CT (MDCT) is the sole imaging modality that can answer three fundamental questions:

1. **Will the new valve expand adequately (i.e., is there enough residual lumen)?**  
2. **Will the coronary ostia remain patent after the new frame and its “neo‑skirt” are deployed?**  
3. **Can future coronary access be preserved?**

The case of the 70‑year‑old gentleman who received a Lotus → Evolut → Acurate neo2 sequence illustrates how these measurements are obtained and interpreted.

| **CT‑derived measurement** | **Why it matters for a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV** | **Critical cut‑offs (approximate, based on current consensus and the present case)** |
|----------------------------|-------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Annular perimeter / minimum inner diameter of the most‐proximal stent (the “landing zone”)** | Determines the size of the third valve that can be safely implanted without excessive oversizing that would crack the previous frame or cause underexpansion. | Minimum inner diameter ≥ 13 mm (the Evolut frame measured 13.7 mm in the case). Oversizing ≤ 20 % of this lumen is generally accepted. |
| **Sinus of Valsalva (SOV) height and diameter** | The SOV provides the “cushion” for the leaflets of the new valve and for the neo‑skirt that forms when the frame expands. A shallow sinus predisposes to coronary cover. | SOV height ≥ 10 mm and mean SOV diameter ≥ 23 mm are protective; in the case the SOV was sufficient to accommodate the Acurate neo2 supra‑annular leaflets. |
| **Coronary ostial height (distance from annular plane to ostium)** | Low ostia (<10 mm) are at greatest risk of being blocked by the displaced leaflets or by the neo‑skirt of the new valve. | Height ≥ 12 mm is considered low‑risk. The patient’s ostia were >12 mm, permitting implantation without coronary protection. |
| **Valve‑to‑coronary (V‑C) distance** – measured from the outer edge of the existing frame (Evolut) to each coronary ostium | Directly predicts the likelihood that the third valve’s frame or its skirt will encroach on the coronaries. | V‑C distance ≥ 4–5 mm after simulated implantation is the practical safety margin. In the case, CT modelling showed >5 mm after positioning the Acurate neo2 at the Evolut leaflets level. |
| **Frame‑to‑frame interaction (overlap depth and crossing angle)** | Overlapping frames can create a “double‑skirt” that reduces the effective orifice and can trap the ostia. Understanding the geometry guides the choice of a low‑profile valve and the optimal implantation depth. | The Evolut’s supra‑annular frame and the Lotus’s intra‑annular frame created a stacked configuration; CT demonstrated that a high implantation of the Acurate neo2 (aligned with Evolut leaflets) would keep the neo‑skirt above the coronary ostia. |
| **Projected neo‑skirt height** (derived from the selected valve’s design and simulated deployment) | The neo‑skirt is the portion of the frame that will sit just distal to the annulus; if it lies below the coronaries, obstruction can occur. | For self‑expanding supra‑annular devices (Acurate neo2), the neo‑skirt is ≈ 7 mm. Planning ensured that this skirt would be above the measured V‑C distance. |
| **Coronary access angle and “cell” availability** (virtual cannulation analysis) | Even if the ostia are not occluded, the mesh of three overlapping frames may prevent catheter passage. CT can identify a “clear cell” at the expected fluoroscopic working angle. | In the case, CT identified a cell in the Evolut frame that remained visible after the Acurate deployment, guiding the decision to forego prophylactic coronary wiring. |

### How These Measurements Influence Coronary Obstruction Risk

1. **Low ostial height + shallow sinus** → high probability that the displaced leaflets of the third valve will seal the coronary orifice (“coronary obstruction”).  
2. **Insufficient V‑C distance** → the neo‑skirt will physically cover the ostium.  
3. **Small residual lumen** → excessive oversizing is needed, which pushes the frame outward and can “pinch” the ostia.  
4. **Unfavourable frame overlap** (e.g., two intra‑annular frames beneath a supra‑annular one) → creates a narrow conduit for blood, increasing the chance of obstruction and making future coronary catheterisation difficult.

In the presented patient, CT showed:
- A minimum Evolut lumen of 13.7 mm (adequate for a 23 mm Acurate neo2).  
- Coronary ostial heights >12 mm and V‑C distances >5 mm after simulated high implantation.  
- A generous sinus of Valsalva that accommodated the supra‑annular leaflets without impinging on the coronaries.  

These findings allowed the heart team to proceed **without coronary protection** and to plan a **high, commissure‑aligned implantation** that preserved both coronary flow and future access.

### Take‑home Messages

- **Never rely on fluoroscopy alone**; a thin‑slice MDCT with 3‑D reconstructions is mandatory for any repeat TAVR, especially a third valve.  
- **Measure the residual inner diameter of the most proximal frame** – if < 13 mm, consider valve fracture, BASILICA, or surgical rescue.  
- **Always calculate coronary ostial height and V‑C distance after simulated implantation**; a safety margin of ≥ 4–5 mm is the current benchmark.  
- **Plan the implantation depth to keep the neo‑skirt above the coronaries**; high implantation aligned with the leaflets of the immediate prior valve is often the safest strategy.  
- **Assess future coronary access** by virtual cannulation; if no clear cell remains, be prepared for coronary protection or alternative revascularisation techniques.

By systematically applying these CT‑derived anatomic criteria, the feasibility of a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV can be reliably judged, and the risk of catastrophic coronary obstruction can be minimized—as demonstrated by the successful triple‑valve intervention in this case.

### [Pathophysiology] Describe the pathophysiologic mechanisms by which prosthetic valve endocarditis can accelerate structural valve degeneration in a transcatheter aortic valve.

**Pathophysiologic mechanisms by which prosthetic‑valve endocarditis (PVE) accelerates structural valve degeneration (SVD) in a transcatheter aortic valve (TAV)**  

1. **Direct leaflet injury by organisms and inflammation**  
   * During PVE, bacteria (in this case *Enterococcus faecalis*) adhere to the bioprosthetic tissue and form vegetations. Even after successful antimicrobial therapy, the inflammatory cascade that follows bacterial colonisation leaves residual damage: collagen fibres are degraded, elastin is fragmented, and matrix‑metal cross‑links are disrupted. This loss of extracellular‑matrix integrity accelerates the intrinsic fatigue‑cracking of the pericardial or porcine leaflets that normally occurs over years. In the reported patient, the rapid rise in trans‑valvular gradient three months after the Evolut TAV‑in‑TAV (mean gradient 35 mm Hg) coincided with documented PVE, suggesting that infection‑mediated leaflet weakening precipitated early stenosis.

2. **Calcific and fibrotic remodeling triggered by chronic inflammation**  
   * The host immune response to infection recruits macrophages and cytokines (IL‑1β, TNF‑α, TGF‑β). These mediators promote fibro‑blast activation and osteogenic differentiation of valvular interstitial cells, leading to deposition of calcium and collagen within the leaflets. In transcatheter valves, which already lack a protective neointimal layer, this process is amplified, producing hastened calcific stenosis. The case demonstrates progressive leaflet thickening on CT after PVE, reflected by the persistent severe restenosis (AVA 0.5 cm²) despite antimicrobial clearance.

3. **Mechanical distortion of the valve frame and leaflets**  
   * Infection can infiltrate the nitinol frame or the fabric covering, weakening the radial force that holds the leaflets open. Even subtle frame deformation (e.g., slight outward expansion from a pseudo‑aneurysm repair) can alter leaflet coaptation and saddle the leaflets into a “restricted” configuration. In the patient, the earlier femoral pseudo‑aneurysm repair and subsequent inflammation likely contributed to subtle frame remodeling, evident on multislice CT as a reduced minimum lumen diameter (13.7 mm) in the Evolut prosthesis.

4. **Vegetation‑induced leaflet tethering and subclinical regurgitation**  
   * Large vegetations can physically tether leaflets, preventing full opening and creating turbulent flow. The resulting shear stress further injures the tissue and promotes early fibrosis. The initial TEE after antimicrobial therapy showed no overt vegetations, but subclinical thickening and tethering can persist, accounting for the early intraprosthetic regurgitation (grade 2‑3) noted before the third TAVI.

5. **Accelerated prosthetic‑patient mismatch (PPM) after repeat interventions**  
   * After PVE, the remnant fibrotic leaflets occupy space within the valve orbit, effectively reducing the effective orifice area (EOA) before the next valve is implanted. When a second or third TAV is placed, the cumulative frame height and leaflet bulk further restrict the central flow column, magnifying the gradient. In this case, the triple‑stented configuration produced a high‑risk PPM (indexed AVA 0.4 cm²/m²) after the third valve, illustrating how PVE‑induced leaflet bulk accelerates functional degeneration of subsequent devices.

**Bottom line for the team:**  
Prosthetic‑valve endocarditis does not merely add an infectious episode; it launches a cascade of inflammatory, calcific, and mechanical insults that markedly shorten the lifespan of a transcatheter bioprosthesis. In patients with prior TAV‑in‑TAV, even after microbiologic cure, vigilance for accelerated SVD is essential—prompt imaging, hemodynamic assessment, and a low threshold for re‑intervention (as exemplified by the successful TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV in this case) are key to preserving valve function and patient outcomes.

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a patient with prior TAVI, infective endocarditis, and high surgical risk, outline the algorithmic decision‑making process that leads to selecting a repeat transcatheter valve implantation versus surgical explantation.

**Algorithmic decision‑making for re‑intervention after failed TAVI in a patient with prior prosthetic‑valve endocarditis (PVE) and prohibitive surgical risk**

1. **Confirm that active infection has been eradicated**  
   * Repeated blood cultures – negative.  
   * Trans‑oesophageal echo (TEE) – no vegetations, abscess or paravalvular leak.  
   * Multislice CT – no perivalvular fluid or prosthetic dehiscence.  

   *Rationale*: Current ESC‑IE (2025) guidelines state that repeat valve surgery or trans‑catheter treatment is only permissible after cure of PVE. In the case report the patient fulfilled all these criteria after a 6‑week IV ampicillin‑based course and subsequent oral consolidation.

2. **Quantify the severity of prosthetic valve dysfunction**  
   * Echocardiography: mean gradient > 40 mm Hg, AVA ≤ 0.6 cm², moderate‑to‑severe intraprosthetic regurgitation.  
   * CT: minimal lumen diameter of the existing trans‑catheter valve (13.7 mm) confirming a critically narrowed orifice.  

   *Rationale*: Objective haemodynamic criteria (VIRGIN, VARC‑3) define severe structural valve deterioration (SVD) that mandates re‑intervention.

3. **Assess surgical risk and anatomic feasibility of open explantation**  
   * Risk scores: EuroSCORE II ≈ 27 % (high).  
   * Comorbidities: COPD, CKD, prior GI bleeding, multiple PCI, permanent pacemaker, frailty.  
   * Surgical technical issues: three overlapping stent frames fixed to the aortic wall, likely requiring extensive root replacement.  

   *Rationale*: When EuroSCORE II > 8 % (or STS > 8 %) plus “hostile chest” (prior sternotomies, severe calcification, frailty), surgery is deemed prohibitive. The case report explicitly cites prohibitive surgical risk.

4. **Perform detailed CT‑based anatomic planning for a repeat TAVI**  
   * Aortic root dimensions (annular area, perimeter, LVOT height).  
   * Coronary ostial heights and sinus of Valsalva diameters → assess risk of coronary obstruction.  
   * Valve‑to‑coronary distance after adding a third frame (virtual “neo‑skirt” analysis).  
   * Frame overlap and commissural orientation → plan alignment to preserve coronary access.  

   *Rationale*: In TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV, the cumulative mesh can occlude coronaries; CT is the gold standard for pre‑procedural risk stratification (VARC‑3, 2023 consensus). In the case report, CT showed a safe coronary distance and guided selection of a low‑profile, supra‑annular Acurate neo2.

5. **Choose the trans‑catheter strategy based on the CT findings**  

| Scenario | Preferred Approach |
|----------|--------------------|
| **Adequate coronary clearance** (≥ 4 mm from neo‑skirt) and sufficient annular area for another supra‑annular valve | **Direct TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV** (self‑expanding valve with commissural alignment) |
| **Coronary obstruction risk** (height < 12 mm, sinus < 30 mm) | **Coronary protection** (wire + balloon or “BASILICA” leaf‑laceration) *or* consider **surgical explantation** if protection not feasible |
| **Severe patient‑prosthesis mismatch** (projected indexed AVA < 0.65 cm²/m²) | Consider **valve fracture** of the prior self‑expanding frame before implanting a new valve; if not feasible, surgical option (rare) |

   In the reported patient, CT demonstrated a safe coronary distance, an inner lumen of 13.7 mm, and a low risk of obstruction; therefore a direct TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV with a supra‑annular Acurate neo2 was elected.

6. **Finalize device selection and implantation strategy**  
   * Valve type: self‑expanding, supra‑annular leaflets (Acurate neo2) for maximal effective orifice area in a narrow lumen.  
   * Size: determined by CT‑derived annular perimeter (size S was appropriate).  
   * Implantation depth: **high implantation** aligned with the leaflets of the prior Evolut frame to avoid “neo‑skirt” encroachment on coronaries and to maximize residual EOAs.  
   * Commissural alignment: planned using CT fluoroscopic landmarks to preserve future coronary access.  
   * Adjuncts: optional balloon pre‑dilation (to assess compliance) and post‑dilation (to optimize expansion). No coronary protection required because CT showed > 4 mm clearance.

7. **Post‑procedural assessment**  
   * VARC‑3 criteria: absence of mortality, stroke, major vascular complications, coronary obstruction; trace or none regurgitation.  
   * Hemodynamics: mean gradient < 20 mm Hg, indexed AVA > 0.65 cm²/m², DVI > 0.25.  
   * Imaging surveillance: TTE at discharge, 1 month, 6 months, then yearly; repeat CT if gradients rise.

8. **If any step fails, re‑evaluate the surgical pathway**  
   * Inability to obtain safe coronary clearance → consider **BASILICA** or **valve fracture**; if still unsafe, discuss **high‑risk surgical explantation** (root replacement) with the patient and family, acknowledging the very high operative mortality.

**Summary applied to the case:**  
- Infection cleared → ✅  
- Severe SVD confirmed → ✅  
- EuroSCORE II ≈ 27 % and hostile anatomy → ✅ surgery prohibitive  
- CT showed adequate coronary clearance and sufficient annular dimensions → ✅ repeat TAVI feasible  
- Selected Acurate neo2, high implantation, commissural alignment → ✅ optimal EOAs and future coronary access  
- Procedure succeeded, gradients improved to 27 mm Hg at 1‑month and remained stable at 1‑year.

Thus, the algorithm moves from infection control → severity assessment → surgical risk → detailed CT planning → coronary‑obstruction risk stratification → device/technique selection → execution and surveillance. When all steps favor a catheter‑based approach, a repeat TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV becomes the preferred strategy, as illustrated in this patient.

### [Morning Report] Explain how commissural alignment and implantation depth were planned and verified during the third TAV implantation, and why these parameters are critical for coronary re‑access in a triple‑stented aortic root.

**Answer – Planning and verification of commissural alignment and implantation depth in the third TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV, and their importance for coronary re‑access**

In this patient the third transcatheter valve (size S Acurate neo2) had to be placed inside two previously implanted devices (Lotus → Evolut). The procedural team used a **CT‑driven, strategy‑driven workflow** that combined three‑dimensional (3‑D) modelling with intra‑procedural fluoroscopic landmarks to (1) set the **desired implantation depth** and (2) achieve **commissural alignment** that would keep a clear path to the coronary ostia for the present and any future coronary interventions.

---

### 1. Pre‑procedural CT planning  

| What was measured | How it guided the plan |
|-------------------|------------------------|
| **Coronary ostial height** and **sinus of Valsalva dimensions** | Determined the minimal distance needed between the outermost stent struts of the new valve and the coronary orifices. The CT showed adequate ostial height (>12 mm) but a narrow sinus (minimum lumen 13.7 mm in the Evolut frame). |
| **Frame‑to‑coronary distance** for each existing valve | Quantified the “neo‑skirt” that would be created by the new valve. The aim was to place the neo‑skirt **above** the Evolut leaflets so that the skirt would not sit in the sinus and block the coronaries. |
| **Commissural orientation of the Evolut valve** (visible on multiplanar reconstructions) | Provided a reference angle for aligning the Acurate neo2 commissures with the existing Evolut commissures. The Acurate neo2 has a built‑in three‑leaflet marker that can be rotated during deployment. |
| **Projected annular plane and required landing zone** | Defined the target depth: the inflow edge of the Acurate neo2 should be positioned at the level of the Evolut leaflets (≈ 3–4 mm above the Lotus frame), rather than deeper in the LVOT, to maximise the effective orifice area and preserve coronary clearance. |

The CT software (e.g., 3mensio or HeartNavigator) generated a **virtual implantation simulation** that showed the final relationship of the three stent frames, the coronary ostia, and the anticipated neo‑skirt. This simulation was printed on the procedural “road‑map” and used to choose the fluoroscopic projection (right‑ anterior oblique ≈ 30° with caudal tilt ≈ 20°) that best displayed the three commissural markers in a “en-face” view.

---

### 2. In‑procedure verification  

1. **Fluoroscopic orientation**  
   - The Acurate neo2 delivery system includes a **three‑leaflet marker** that appears as a “trident” on cine‑angiography. By matching this marker to the pre‑identified Evolut commissural “gaps” (visible on the selected projection), the operator rotated the delivery catheter until the markers were **co‑linear**. This ensured **commissural alignment** between the new valve and the existing Evolut frame.  

2. **Depth control**  
   - The delivery system has a **depth‑stop mechanism** calibrated in millimetres. After confirming alignment, the valve was slowly released while continuously checking the position of the inflow edge relative to the **radiopaque landmarks** of the Evolut frame (its upper stent cells). The target depth (≈ 3 mm above the Evolut leaflets) was verified by superimposing the device outline on the static fluoroscopic image of the Evolut frame.  

3. **Rapid‑pacing aortography**  
   - A quick aortogram performed during rapid ventricular pacing confirmed that the **coronary ostia remained patent** after deployment and before final post‑dilatation. No “waisting” of the contrast column downstream of the neo‑skirt was seen, indicating that the new skirt was not obstructing the sinuses.  

4. **Post‑dilatation check**  
   - After a 20 × 40 mm balloon post‑dilatation, a second aortogram demonstrated **unimpeded coronary flow** and the same commissural relationship as before, confirming that the high implantation and alignment were maintained despite radial expansion.

---

### 3. Why these parameters are critical in a triple‑stented root  

| Issue | How alignment/depth address it |
|-------|--------------------------------|
| **Coronary obstruction** (the most feared complication of TAV‑in‑TAV) | By placing the neo‑skirt **above** the Evolut leaflets and aligning the commissures so that the strut cells lie away from the coronary ostia, the sinus remains free of material that could block flow. |
| **Future coronary re‑access** (the patient already has extensive coronary disease) | Commissural alignment creates **predictable gaps** between the stent cells at the level of the coronaries. This allows a guiding catheter to pass through the same “window” used after the first TAVI, reducing the need for complex catheter tricks or coronary bypass. |
| **Effective orifice area (EOA) preservation** | High implantation reduces the amount of **overlap** of the new valve’s inflow stent with the prior frames, limiting “pinching” of the orifice. Aligning the leaflets with the previous prosthesis leaflets also avoids unnecessary leaflet redundancy that would otherwise increase gradients. |
| **Procedural safety in a layered frame** | Precise depth avoids **deep LVOT implantation**, which could anchor the new valve into the rigid Lotus frame and increase the risk of annular injury or conduction disturbances. It also limits the amount of radial force transmitted to the already‑integrated frames, decreasing the chance of frame fracture or embolisation. |

In this case, the **pre‑procedural CT model** predicted a minimal valve‑to‑coronary distance of > 4 mm after high implantation and commissural alignment, a threshold associated with a very low risk of coronary obstruction (≈ 1 %). The intra‑procedural fluoroscopic confirmation that the Acurate neo2 commissures matched the Evolut gaps, coupled with an implantation depth that left the neo‑skirt above the Evolut leaflets, translated that prediction into reality: coronary flow remained uninterrupted, and the patient later required no coronary re‑intervention.

---

### Take‑away for the learner  

1. **Every repeat TAV must be preceded by a dedicated CT protocol** that maps coronary heights, sinus dimensions, and the orientation of all existing frames.  
2. **Virtual implantation** is not optional—it allows the team to set a target depth (usually “high” in TAV‑in‑TAV) and to plan the rotational alignment that will preserve coronary windows.  
3. **Fluoroscopic landmarks** (radiopaque frame cells, commissural markers) are the bridge between the CT plan and the actual deployment; they must be checked before, during, and after valve release.  
4. In a triple‑stented root, **coronary re‑access** hinges on a clear, reproducible “cell” through which catheters can pass; this is achieved by aligning the new valve’s commissures with those of the previous valve and by placing the new valve as high as anatomically safe.

By rigorously applying these principles, the team in this case achieved a technically successful third TAV with preserved coronary perfusion and an acceptable haemodynamic result that has endured for at least one year.

### [Management] What is patient‑prosthesis mismatch (PPM), how is it quantified after TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV, and what procedural strategies can be employed to minimize severe PPM in narrow annuli?

**Patient‑prosthesis mismatch (PPM) in the setting of repeat transcatheter aortic valve implantation**

---

### What is PPM?

Patient‑prosthesis mismatch occurs when, after valve replacement, the *effective* orifice area (EOA) of the implanted prosthesis is too small for the patient’s body surface area (BSA). The result is a trans‑valvular gradient that is higher than expected for a normally functioning valve and can limit forward flow, provoke left‑ventricular hypertrophy, and adversely affect long‑term survival.

* **Severe PPM** is conventionally defined as an indexed EOA (iEOA) ≤ 0.65 cm²/m² (or ≤ 0.55 cm²/m² in the presence of left‑ventricular dysfunction).  
* **Moderate PPM**: iEOA 0.65–0.85 cm²/m².  

These thresholds, derived from surgical cohorts, are now applied to transcatheter valves because the same haemodynamic principle holds.

---

### How is PPM quantified after a **TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV**?

1. **Echocardiographic measurement** – The continuity equation remains the gold‑standard:  

   \[
   \text{EOA}= \frac{{\text{LVOT area} \times \text{LVOT VTI}}}{{\text{Aortic valve VTI}}}
   \]

   In the presented case, post‑procedure transthoracic echo (TTE) at 7 days showed an EOA of **0.9 cm²**; with a BSA of ~1.73 m² the iEOA was **0.52 cm²/m²**, fulfilling the definition of **severe PPM**.  

2. **Indexed Doppler‑velocity index (DVI)** – A DVI < 0.25 strongly suggests severe mismatch; the case reported a DVI of **0.24** immediately after the third valve.  

3. **Computed‑tomography (CT)‑derived effective orifice area** – Modern multidetector CT (MDCT) can reconstruct the neo‑sinus and directly measure the projected flow area. In complex “stacked” valve configurations, CT is especially valuable because it visualizes the cumulative luminal narrowing caused by overlapping stent frames. In this patient, pre‑procedural CT demonstrated a **minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm** within the Evolut frame—a critical predictor of residual EOA after a third valve.

4. **Trend over time** – Early gradients may be supraphysiologic because the new valve must expand against the rigid prior frames. Serial echocardiography (as in this case) showed a rapid decline in mean gradient from 67 mm Hg (day 0) to 27 mm Hg at 1 month, with a modest increase in AVA to 1.1 cm². The final iEOA of ~0.64 cm²/m² sits at the border of severe/moderate PPM, illustrating that “balloon post‑dilatation” and frame remodeling can improve the effective area.

---

### Procedural strategies to **minimise severe PPM** when the annulus is narrow (as in this patient)

| Strategy | Rationale | How it was applied (or could be applied) in the case |
|----------|-----------|------------------------------------------------------|
| **1. Choose a supra‑annular, low‑profile valve** | Supra‑annular leaflets sit **above** the stent frame, delivering a larger EOA for a given annular diameter. Low frame height preserves sinus space and coronary clearance. | The **Acurate neo2 (size S)** was selected for its supra‑annular leaflet position and relatively short frame, maximizing the residual orifice despite a 13.7‑mm lumen inside the Evolut. |
| **2. High (or “leaflet‑aligned”) implantation** | Deploying the new valve so its inflow edge coincides with the previous prosthesis leaflets pushes the leaflets of the older valve outward, preventing them from folding inward and further narrowing the neo‑orifice. | The team deliberately positioned the Acurate neo2 **at the level of the Evolut leaflets** (high implantation), which helped keep the Evolut leaflets open and limited additional reduction of the EOA. |
| **3. Commissural alignment** | Aligning the new valve’s commissures with the previous valve’s leaflets distributes the metal struts away from the central flow path, preserving the central cross‑sectional area and facilitating future coronary access. | Pre‑procedural CT modelling guided deployment so that the Acurate neo2’s commissures matched the Evolut leaflets, reducing “mesh‑over‑mesh” impingement and maintaining central flow. |
| **4. Aggressive post‑dilatation (high‑pressure balloon)** | Radial force applied after deployment can fracture the inner frame of the previous valve (bioprosthetic valve fracture) or at least expand the composite stent construct, enlarging the neo‑orifice. | A **20 mm × 40 mm balloon** was inflated under rapid pacing, yielding a 40 % reduction in mean gradient within the first month. (No intentional valve fracture was performed because the Evolut self‑expanding frame is less amenable to fracture, but high‑pressure dilation still achieved useful expansion.) |
| **5. Pre‑procedural CT‑based sizing and virtual valve implantation** | 3‑D reconstruction predicts the final neo‑sinus geometry, allowing the operator to select the smallest valve that will still achieve acceptable EOA and avoid coronary obstruction. | CT measured the **minimum lumen diameter (13.7 mm)** and guided the choice of a **size‑S Acurate neo2** (nominal inflow diameter ≈ 19 mm) that could be safely expanded without compromising coronaries. |
| **6. Consider “valve‑in‑valve fracture” when feasible** | In surgical bioprostheses, high‑pressure balloons can fracture the rigid frame, markedly increasing the final EOA. Newer self‑expanding TAVR frames are less fracture‑prone, but emerging low‑profile balloon technologies may permit selective frame expansion. | Not performed in this case because the Evolut frame is self‑expanding and less amenable to fracture; however, the authors discuss it as a future option if severe PPM recurs. |
| **7. Alternative catheter‑based leaflet modification (BASILICA, LAMPOON)** | Intentional laceration of the existing leaflets creates a larger central opening and reduces the risk of coronary obstruction, indirectly mitigating PPM. | The report mentions BASILICA as a theoretical rescue if coronary obstruction had been imminent, but it was not needed because pre‑procedural imaging showed adequate coronary clearance. |

---

### Take‑home points for the morning report

1. **PPM is a haemodynamic mismatch, not a procedural complication.** In repeat TAVR, the cumulative stent material can dramatically shrink the residual orifice; therefore, routine calculation of indexed EOA (or DVI) after each intervention is mandatory.

2. **CT is indispensable** for quantifying the *real* lumen available before a repeat valve is placed. In this case, the 13.7 mm minimal diameter signaled a high risk of severe PPM and dictated the need for a supra‑annular, low‑profile device and high implantation.

3. **Procedural planning must be proactive.** Selecting a valve with supra‑annular leaflets, aligning commissures, implanting high, and performing post‑dilatation are the main levers to preserve the effective orifice area in narrow annuli, especially when a third valve is being added.

4. **Early haemodynamic improvement may be modest**, but serial echocardiography often reveals a “gradient wash‑out” as the composite stent complex remodels. In this patient, the mean gradient fell from 67 mm Hg to 26 mm Hg over one year, with the iEOA improving from severe to borderline moderate PPM.

5. **Future‑proofing matters.** Even after a successful third TAVR, the cumulative frame burden will limit any further redo‑TAVR. The case underscores the importance of “lifetime management” discussions at the first TAVR—particularly in patients with small annuli or anticipated long survival.

**Bottom line:** In a narrow annulus with stacked transcatheter prostheses, severe PPM is expected unless the operator deliberately chooses a supra‑annular, low‑profile valve, plans a high‑leaflet‑aligned implantation, optimises post‑dilatation, and employs CT‑driven sizing. Meticulous haemodynamic assessment after each stage confirms whether the strategy succeeded and guides follow‑up.

### [Clinical Pearls] List the Valve Academic Research Consortium‑3 (VARC‑3) criteria used to define procedural success in this case and indicate which of these criteria were borderline or not met immediately post‑procedure.

**VARC‑3 procedural‑success criteria (as applied to this case)**

The “triple‑stented” patient described by Merkulov et al. illustrates the frontier of transcatheter lifetime management. ### 1.  Why these techniques are considered in redo TAVR

No coronary obstruction was anticipated because the CT measured a safe valve‑to‑coronary distance and the implantation depth was planned at the level of the Evolut leaflets. Consequently, the team **did not employ BASILICA** and **did not attempt BVF**—both because the anticipated risks were low and because the specific devices (self‑expanding nitinol frames) are not amenable to fracture.

**Take‑away for the case** – Because the CT analysis showed a safe coronary clearance (no “high‑risk” anatomy), BASILICA was **not required** and would have added procedural complexity without clear upside.

### [Morning Report] After completing antimicrobial therapy for Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis, what follow‑up imaging and laboratory assessments are recommended before undertaking a repeat TAVI, and why?

**Follow‑up assessment after completion of antimicrobial therapy for Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic‑valve endocarditis (PVE) – what to repeat before a redo TAVI and why**

1. **Blood cultures (at least two sets)**
   - **Purpose:** Confirm microbiologic eradication before any further instrumentation of the valve. Persistent bacteremia, even with low‑grade organisms such as E. faecalis, predicts recurrence after valve re‑intervention. In the reported case the heart‑team required *repeated* negative cultures before proceeding to the third TAVI.
   - **Timing:** Obtain the first set on the day of evaluation; a second set 24–48 h later increases sensitivity for low‑level bacteremia.

2. **Trans‑oesophageal echocardiography (TEE)**
   - **Purpose:** Detect residual vegetations, abscesses, or new paravalvular complications that are often missed on transthoracic imaging. TEE is the imaging gold standard for PVE and was used in the case to document complete resolution of infection before the repeat transcatheter procedure.
   - **Key findings to look for:** Absence of mobile echodensities on the prosthetic leaflets, no peri‑annular extension, and stable prosthetic geometry.

3. **Multidetector computed tomography (MDCT) of the aortic root and coronary ostia**
   - **Purpose:** Provides a three‑dimensional road‑map of the existing valve frames, assesses:
     - **Coronary clearance** (distance from the projected neo‑skirt to the coronary ostia) – critical in a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV scenario to avoid coronary obstruction.
     - **Effective orifice area of the current prosthesis** (minimum lumen diameter) – guides selection of the next valve size.
     - **Annular dimensions and leaflet‑frame interaction** – informs implantation depth and whether a “high‑implant” strategy is feasible.
   - **In the case:** MDCT confirmed a 13.7 mm minimum lumen diameter in the Evolut valve and demonstrated adequate coronary height, which allowed the team to plan a high‑positioned Acurate neo2 implantation without coronary protection.

4. **Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE)**
   - **Purpose:** Quantifies residual valve haemodynamics (mean gradient, aortic valve area, regurgitation) and left‑ventricular function. Even after infection is cleared, severe prosthetic stenosis or regurgitation mandates re‑intervention. In the case, TTE showed a mean gradient of 49 mm Hg and AVA 0.5 cm², confirming the need for another valve.
   - **When to repeat:** At the end of antimicrobial therapy and again immediately before the planned TAVI to document any interval change.

5. **Inflammatory biomarkers (CRP, ESR)**
   - **Purpose:** Serve as adjuncts to the microbiologic and imaging data. A normal or markedly falling C‑reactive protein after therapy supports infection control, whereas persistent elevation should trigger repeat imaging and cultures. The case report did not detail these labs, but current guideline‑based practice incorporates them in the “end of therapy” work‑up.

---

### Why these assessments are essential before a repeat TAVI

| Assessment | What it rules out / informs | Impact on procedural planning |
|------------|----------------------------|--------------------------------|
| **Blood cultures** | Ongoing bacteremia → contraindication to any prosthetic manipulation | Guarantees that implanting a new valve will not seed a fresh infection |
| **TEE** | Residual vegetations, abscess, or new paravalvular leaks | Presence would shift management toward surgery or prolonged antibiotics |

In this patient, completion of a 6‑week IV ampicillin regimen, documented sterile repeat cultures, a negative TEE, and a reassuring MDCT (adequate coronary clearance and defined landing zone) collectively satisfied the safety criteria for a third transcatheter valve. Only after these steps did the heart team feel confident to proceed with a CT‑guided, high‑implantation strategy that ultimately yielded durable valve function at 1 year.

**Bottom line:** Before any redo TAVI in the setting of previously treated Enterococcus faecalis PVE, obtain *repeat sterile blood cultures*, a *TEE* to exclude residual infection, *MDCT* for anatomic feasibility and coronary protection, *TTE* to quantify prosthetic dysfunction, and assess *inflammatory markers*. This systematic approach ensures that the infection is truly eradicated and that the anatomy is suitable for safe and effective transcatheter re‑intervention.

- Surgical mortality is prohibitive (EuroSCORE II > 20 %).  
- Frailty predicts a poor postoperative functional trajectory.  
- Competing illnesses limit life expectancy but still allow a ∼2‑year horizon in which valve correction will improve symptoms and functional status.  
- Detailed CT planning confirms technical feasibility and coronary protection.
