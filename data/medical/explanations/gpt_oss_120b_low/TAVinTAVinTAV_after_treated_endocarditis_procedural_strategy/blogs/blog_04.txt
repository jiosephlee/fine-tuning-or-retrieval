Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# Coronary Access After Multiple TAVR Layers: Imaging, Techniques, and Future Options  

## Why This Case Stands Out  

When you hear “TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV,” the first thought is usually *“how on earth do you keep the coronaries open?”* The recent case from the Chazov Center shows exactly that dilemma. A septuagenarian who already had two transcatheter aortic valve implants—and even survived prosthetic‑valve endocarditis—required a third valve. The team succeeded, but they had to **engineer coronary patency** from the ground up. That’s the sort of scenario that is moving from “once‑in‑a‑blue‑moon” to “keep‑your‑eye‑on‑this‑for‑the‑next‑decade” as TAVR expands into younger, lower‑risk patients who will inevitably outlive their first device.

The take‑home message? **If you’re going to stack valves, you must stack the planning.** Imaging, commissural alignment, and a toolbox of adjunctive techniques become the lifelines that keep the coronary ostia reachable for everything from routine angiography to emergent PCI.

## Imaging the Coronary Landscape Before the Third Valve  

A meticulous CT work‑up is non‑negotiable when you’re contemplating a third TAVR. In the reported case, the team used multi‑slice CT (MSCT) to draw a 3‑D map that answered three pivotal questions:

1. **Coronary Ostial Height** – The distance from the annular plane to the left and right coronary ostia. The rule of thumb remains > 10 mm to avoid obstruction, but with stacked frames you need a *buffer* that accounts for the “neo‑skirt” (the frame‑leaflet complex of the newly implanted valve) plus the residual frame of the previous valve(s).

2. **Sinus of Valsalva Dimensions** – The width of the sinus determines whether there’s enough “room” for the displaced leaflets of the older valve to move aside. A narrow sinus (< 30 mm) is a red flag; you may need to consider coronary protection (balloon‑catheter or wire) or an alternative strategy.

3. **Valve‑to‑Coronary Distance (VTC)** – This is measured from the projected outer edge of the upcoming valve frame to the coronary ostia after deployment. A VTC < 4 mm is associated with a high obstruction risk. In the triple‑layered anatomy, you have to model *each* frame’s impact on VTC, which the team did by overlaying the proposed Acurate neo2 onto the existing Evolut and Lotus scaffolds.

Beyond these static measurements, looking at **commissural orientation** on the CT helps you decide how to orient the new valve’s commissures relative to the coronaries. Modern 3‑D workstations can simulate valve rotation; a few degrees of tweak can shift the “neo‑skirt” away from the ostia dramatically.

### Practical Imaging Checklist  

- **Annular sizing**: still the foundation, but now you’re also sizing the *effective* landing zone after accounting for previous frames.  
- **Coronary height & sinus width**: record both absolute values and the *projected* values after the new valve.  
- **Frame overlap zone**: identify where the prior valve’s cells intersect the coronary ostia; this is where BASILICA or valve fracture might be needed.  
- **Commissural alignment plan**: decide on the target rotation (commonly 0°, 30°, or 60°) that lines the new valve’s commissures away from the ostia.  
- **Coronary protection plan**: if VTC < 4 mm, place a wire or balloon in the at‑risk coronary before deployment.

## Procedural Strategies to Preserve Coronary Access  

### 1. Commissural Alignment  

The Acurate neo2 used in the case is designed for **top‑down alignment**; its “crown” sits just above the annulus, and the three commissural posts can be rotated to line up with the native leaflets. By aligning the new valve’s commissures with the *Evolut* leaflets (instead of the Lotus frame), the operators created a wider central orifice and a more favorable “neo‑skirt” geometry.  

**Key point:** When you know you’ll need future coronary access, **aim for a commissural orientation that points the frame’s cells away from the coronaries**. For self‑expanding valves (Evolut, Acurate), this is achieved by rotating the delivery system under fluoroscopy using the three radiopaque markers.

### 2. High Implantation Technique  

Deploying the third valve **higher** in the aortic root (just above the leaflets of the second valve) serves two purposes:

- It pushes the new valve’s sealing skirt *above* the coronary ostia, reducing the chance that displaced leaflets will occlude them.  
- It preserves a “valve‑to‑coronary” clearance for future catheters.  

In the case report, the team pre‑dilated and post‑dilated under rapid pacing but monitored aortography to confirm that coronaries remained patent throughout.

### 3. Coronary Protection (Balloon/Wire)  

If pre‑procedural CT predicts a tight VTC, you can **pre‑emptively wire the at‑risk coronary** and have a low‑profile balloon ready for rapid inflation (“chimney” technique). Even if you never need to deploy the balloon, the wire itself provides a safety rail for immediate PCI if obstruction occurs.

### 4. BASILICA (Bioprosthetic or Native Aortic Scallop Intentional Laceration to Prevent Iatrogenic Coronary Artery Obstruction)  

BASILICA is most often discussed in valve‑in‑valve scenarios where the leaflets of the surgical prosthesis may obstruct flow. In a triple‑layered setup, the **leaflets of the second‑generation transcatheter valve** (Evolut) become the culprits. Lacerating them before deploying the third valve can create a gap for blood (and catheters) to pass.  

**Practical tip:**  
- Use a 0.014" guidewire looped around the target leaflet, electrify with a RF generator, and cut the leaflet in a controlled fashion.  
- Confirm correct orientation on CT to avoid lacerating the wrong scallop.  

### 5. Valve Fracture  

While usually reserved for surgical bioprostheses (e.g., valve‑in‑valve with a small surgical prosthetic), **bioprosthetic valve fracture (BVF)** with high‑pressure non‑compliant balloons can also expand the metallic frame of a self‑expanding valve if it’s made of a fracture‑prone alloy (e.g., some early‑generation Medtronic devices).  

In the presented case, the team didn’t fracture the Evolut frame, but a high‑pressure post‑dilation likely *expanded* the stent enough to improve the effective orifice area without causing structural failure.

### 6. Dedicated Coronary‑Friendly Devices  

Manufacturers are responding to the “coronary access problem” with new designs:

- **Lotus Edge** (discontinued) and the newer **JenaValve** feature open cells at the sinuses to facilitate coronary cannulation.  
- **Myval** and **Viva** have lower frame heights and larger cell sizes.  

If you anticipate the need for multiple re‑interventions, **choose a valve platform that leaves the sinuses as open as possible** from the start.

## Future Options When Access Becomes Impossible  

Even with the best planning, the cumulative mesh can eventually block coronary catheters. Here’s what the horizon holds:

| Technique | When to Consider | What You Need |
|-----------|------------------|---------------|
| **Retrograde Coronary Access via the Aortic Root** | Extremely narrowed sinus, no antegrade route | Specialized catheters (e.g., Turnpike, Ikari) and 3‑D roadmap |
| **Trans‑septal “Coronary Bypass” (Trans‑caval)** | No percutaneous route | Surgical hybrid deployment of a graft from the femoral vein to the coronary ostium (experimental) |
| **Coronary Stent “Scaffold” Pre‑Implant** | Planned TAVR‑in‑TAVR in tight sinuses | Implant a coronary stent across the ostium into the aortic root before valve deployment to keep a channel open |
| **Laser‑Assisted Frame Modification** | Resistant frames causing obstruction | High‑energy laser catheters to cut frame struts (still experimental) |
| **Surgical Redo with Root Replacement** | All percutaneous options exhausted | Conventional surgery, often requiring a full root/ascending aorta replacement|

Most of these remain in the exploratory phase, but they underline that **coronary access is a lifetime issue**, not just a peri‑procedural concern.

## Take‑Home Points  

- **Pre‑procedure CT is your roadmap.** Measure coronary height, sinus width, and valve‑to‑coronary distance for *each* existing frame; model the planned valve’s impact before you step into the cath lab.  
- **Commissural alignment isn’t optional**—it’s a cornerstone of preserving future coronary access, especially in self‑expanding devices.  
- **High implantation and careful device selection** (supra‑annular leaflets, low frame height, large cells) can give you the extra millimetres needed to keep the coronaries patent.  
- **Have a protection plan** (wire/balloon, BASILICA, BVF) ready for any VTC < 4 mm scenario.  
- **Think ahead**: if the patient has significant coronary disease, plan the initial TAVR with a “future‑access‑friendly” valve to avoid being trapped in a mesh cage later.  

The Chazov case proves that a third TAVR is technically achievable, but it also serves as a reminder that every additional layer makes coronary re‑access exponentially harder. By integrating detailed imaging, intentional commissural alignment, and a versatile toolbox of adjunctive techniques, we can turn a potential catastrophic obstruction into a routine, survivable procedure.  

**Bottom line:** When you stack TAVR valves, *stack* your planning—especially the coronary planning. 🚀
