# When Three Valves Aren’t Enough: Managing TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV Failures  

*Deep dive into the technical and anatomic challenges of a third‑generation transcatheter valve implantation.*

---  

## Why This Case Is Worth Your Attention  

A 70‑plus‑year‑old gentleman has already survived **two transcatheter aortic valve implantations (TAVIs)** and a bout of **prosthetic valve endocarditis**. By 2024 he needed **a third TAVI** – a *TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV* procedure that most of us have only seen in a handful of case reports.  

What makes it compelling isn’t just the “triple‑valve” factoid; it’s the **real‑world problem set** we’ll all face as TAVI expands:

* **Structural valve deterioration (SVD)** is no longer a curiosity – it’s becoming a predictable part of the patient’s life‑time journey.  
* **Endocarditis** adds a layer of infection‑control complexity that many operators are uncomfortable navigating with a percutaneous approach.  
* **Anatomical constraints** – overlapping frames, limited sinus dimensions, low coronary ostia – turn a routine valve deployment into a high‑stakes puzzle.  

The authors’ success (asymptomatic at 1 year, good gradients) offers a practical template for future “third‑generation” redo cases. Let’s unpack the pearls.

## Core Clinical Pearls  

### 1. **Never Let Infection Status Be a Blanket Contra‑Indication**  
* Once blood cultures are negative, TEE shows no vegetations, and CRP normalizes, it is reasonable to consider a redo TAVI if surgical risk is prohibitive.  

### 2. **CT‑Guided Road‑Mapping Is Non‑Negotiable**  
* Measure **coronary ostial height**, **sinus of Valsalva diameter**, and **frame‑to‑coronary distance** after each implanted valve.  
* Re‑create the **virtual landing zone** for the next device; in this case a minimum 13.7 mm lumen in the Evolut frame dictated the need for a low‑profile, supra‑annular valve.  

### 3. **Choose the Valve That Plays Nicely With What’s Already There**  
* **Acurate neo2**: low frame height, supra‑annular leaflets, built‑in commissural alignment markers → preserves coronary access and maximises effective orifice area (EOA).  
* Avoid heavily post‑dilated or tall self‑expanding devices that could bury the coronaries under another “skirt.”  

### 4. **High (or “leaflet‑aligned”) Implantation Can Rescue the Effective Orifice Area**  
* Deploy the new valve **at the level of the prior prosthesis leaflets** (not too deep). This pins the previous leaflets outward, reduces “neo‑skirt” obstruction, and improves downstream flow.  

### 5. **Don’t Forget the “Minor” Details: Commissural Alignment, Post‑Dilatation, and Frame Fracture**  
* Align the neo2 commissures with the Evolut leaflets → opens a corridor for future coronary work.  
* A modest post‑dilatation (20 mm × 40 mm) under rapid pacing helped expand the embedded Evolut frame, lowering the mean gradient from 67 mm Hg immediately post‑implant to 27 mm Hg at 1 month.  
* **Valve fracture** was not needed here but remains an option if gradients stay prohibitive.  

### 6. **Multidisciplinary Heart‑Team Decision‑Making Is the Bedrock**  
* Infectious disease clearance, cardiac imaging, interventional cardiology, cardiac surgery, and anesthesia all contributed. This mitigates the “off‑label” nature of a third TAVI.  

### 7. **Plan for Future Interventions From Day 1**  
* Even if the patient is “cured,” a triple‑stented root can become a **coronary access nightmare** later.  
* Document the commissural orientation, keep a 3‑D CT archive, and discuss potential BASILICA or valve‑in‑valve‑in‑valve strategies should coronary disease progress.

## Procedural Blueprint (Step‑by‑Step)

1. **Pre‑Procedure Imaging**  
   * **MSCT** with thin‑slice reconstructions → assess sinus size, frame overlap, and coronary clearance.  
   * **TEE** to reconfirm infection eradication and rule out paravalvular leak.  

2. **Access Strategy**  
   * Left radial for coronary protection (if needed) and left femoral for valve delivery.  
   * Use a **Safari** or similar stiff wire positioned in the LV.  

3. **Balloon Pre‑Dilate** (22 mm × 40 mm) under rapid pacing (≈180 bpm) to open the stiff Evolut frame and gauge coronary flow.  

4. **Deploy Acurate neo2 (Size S)**  
   * Align neo2 commissures with Evolut leaflets using CT‑derived fluoroscopic landmarks.  
   * Target a **high implantation** just above the Evolut leaflets.  

5. **Post‑Dilatation** (20 mm × 40 mm) under rapid pacing to fine‑tune expansion and improve gradients.  

6. **Immediate Assessment**  
   * Aortography → confirm no coronary obstruction, no perivalvular leak.  
   * Hemodynamics → note initial gradient (expected high), then re‑evaluate after post‑dilatation.  

7. **Post‑Procedural Surveillance**  
   * TTE before discharge → baseline gradients, AVA, regurgitation.  
   * Follow‑up at 1 month, 3 months, and 1 year: watch for gradient normalization, LV remodeling, and infection recurrence.  

---

## Take‑Home Messages (Bullet List)

- **Serial TAVIs are feasible** if infection is cleared, surgical risk is prohibitive, and CT shows adequate coronary clearance.  
- **Pre‑procedural CT is the cornerstone** – precise measurements of lumen diameter, sinus dimensions, and valve‑to‑coronary distances guide valve choice and implantation depth.  
- **Select a low‑profile, supra‑annular valve** (e.g., Acurate neo2) to maximize effective orifice area in a narrow, multilayered annulus.  
- **High, commissure‑aligned implantation** reduces neo‑skirt obstruction, preserves coronary access, and improves hemodynamics.  
- **Post‑dilatation can dramatically lower gradients** by expanding the previously implanted frame; consider it routinely in triple‑stented anatomy.  
- **Multidisciplinary heart‑team review** ensures that infection status, anatomical feasibility, and patient preferences are all reconciled before an off‑label redo.  
- **Future‑proof your plan** – document commissural orientation and keep 3‑D imaging on file; anticipate potential coronary interventions or another valve‑in‑valve down the line.

## Final Thoughts  

As TAVI moves from “high‑risk only” to a **lifetime management strategy**, we must start thinking like architects rather than just surgeons. Every valve we implant is a **structural element** that will influence the next iteration. The case of a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV after treated endocarditis shows that with meticulous CT planning, judicious valve selection, and a heart‑team approach, even the most tangled aortic roots can be successfully navigated.

Keep your imaging stack updated, respect the anatomy, and don’t shy away from a third valve when the patient’s physiology, not the novelty of the procedure, dictates it. The future will likely bring **fourth‑generation** or hybrid solutions, but the lessons from this case—*measure twice, align wisely, and protect the coronaries*—will remain timeless.  

Stay sharp, stay open‑access, and happy valve‑ing!
