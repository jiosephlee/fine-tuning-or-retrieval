Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Management] What are the potential roles and limitations of adjunctive techniques such as BASILICA or bioprosthetic valve fracture in the setting of a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV, especially regarding coronary obstruction and effective orifice area?

**Adjunctive techniques in a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV setting – What can BASILICA and bioprosthetic‑valve fracture (BVF) really do, and where do they fall short?**  

The “triple‑stented” patient described by Merkulov et al. illustrates the frontier of transcatheter lifetime management. After a Lotus valve (2014) → Evolut valve (2022) → Acurate neo2 valve (2024), the operators relied on meticulous CT planning, high‑implantation, and commissural alignment to avoid coronary obstruction and to preserve an acceptable effective orifice area (EOA). The case provides a concrete backdrop for discussing two adjunctive strategies that have gained popularity in isolated TAV‑in‑TAV procedures—BASILICA (leaflet laceration to prevent coronary obstruction) and BVF (high‑pressure balloon fracture of a surgical bioprosthetic frame).  

---

### 1.  Why these techniques are considered in redo TAVR

| Issue in redo TAVR | What the adjunctive technique addresses |
|--------------------|------------------------------------------|
| **Coronary‑ostium obstruction** (neo‑skirt of the newly implanted valve or displaced leaflets covering the coronary orifices) | **BASILICA** – intentionally lacerates the leaflet(s) that would otherwise obstruct flow, creating a “splint” for the coronaries. |
| **Severe prosthesis‑patient mismatch (PPM) / small effective orifice** (multiple frames compressing the flow channel) | **BVF** – high‑pressure balloon fracture of the rigid surgical bioprosthetic frame enlarges the annular diameter, allowing a larger transcatheter valve to be implanted and increasing EOA. |

In the current case, the pre‑procedural CT showed a **minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm** in the Evolut frame, and the heart team deliberately selected a supra‑annular Acurate neo2 and performed high‑pressure post‑dilatation (20 × 40 mm) to “push” the existing stent outward. No coronary obstruction was anticipated because the CT measured a safe valve‑to‑coronary distance and the implantation depth was planned at the level of the Evolut leaflets. Consequently, the team **did not employ BASILICA** and **did not attempt BVF**—both because the anticipated risks were low and because the specific devices (self‑expanding nitinol frames) are not amenable to fracture.

---

### 2.  Potential **roles** of BASILICA in a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV

| Situation where BASILICA could be useful | Feasibility in the triple‑stented scenario |
|-------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------|
| **Projected coronary‑ostium height < 10 mm** *or* sinus of Valsalva diameter < 30 mm after the second valve, and CT predicts the “neo‑skirt” will sit below the coronaries. | The technique can still be performed, but the **lacerated leaflet must be reachable through the existing stent mesh**. With three overlapping frames, catheter navigation becomes technically demanding, and the risk of damaging a prior frame or dislodging a prosthetic leaflet is higher. |
| **Need to preserve future coronary access** (patient has extensive PCI history, as in this case). | BASILICA may **facilitate coronary re‑access** by creating a gap, but the cumulative metal struts of three valves often limit sheath passage irrespective of leaflet laceration. In the present case, the CT‑derived valve‑to‑coronary distance was already adequate, so the incremental benefit would be marginal. |

**Limitations**  

* **Device compatibility** – BASILICA has been validated chiefly with balloon‑expandable or self‑expanding valves that have a clear leaflet‑to‑frame configuration. In a triple‑stented anatomy, the targeted leaflet may be partially constrained by the underlying frame, reducing the efficacy of the laceration.  
* **Procedural risk** – Adding a trans‑septal puncture, rail‑guided electrified guidewire, and rapid pacing prolongs the case and introduces potential for cardiac perforation, especially when the annulus is already heavily calcified or scarred from prior implants.  
* **Uncertain durability** – The lacerated leaflet may become a nidus for thrombus or become trapped within the new frame, an issue not yet captured in long‑term registries.  

**Take‑away for the case** – Because the CT analysis showed a safe coronary clearance (no “high‑risk” anatomy), BASILICA was **not required** and would have added procedural complexity without clear upside.

---

### 3.  Potential **roles** of Bioprosthetic Valve Fracture (BVF) in a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV

| Situation where BVF could be useful | Feasibility in the triple‑stented scenario |
|--------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------|
| **Surgical bioprosthetic frame** (e.g., a failed stented surgical valve) that is the *first* layer, limiting the annular size for subsequent transcatheter valves. | BVF is **only applicable to surgical bioprostheses** constructed of cobalt‑chrome or stainless‑steel stents that fracture at predictable pressures (≈ 16–18 atm). The first valve in this patient was a **Lotus (purely nitinol, non‑fracturable)**, and the second was a **self‑expanding Evolut** (nitinol, also non‑fracturable). Hence, **BVF is not technically possible** in this anatomy. |
| **Severe PPM after TAV‑in‑TAV** where the inner frame can be enlarged to allow a larger transcatheter valve. | Even if a fracture were possible, the **overlying third valve** (Acurate neo2) would already be in place, making any post‑implant fracture risky for device embolisation or frame disruption. |

**Limitations**  

* **Device‑specific** – BVF requires a surgical valve with a metallic frame designed to fracture; most contemporary TAVR devices (Lotus, Evolut, Acurate, Sapien 3) are made of **nitinol** or **polymer** that either does not fracture or fractures unpredictably, risking annular rupture.  
* **Risk of annular injury** – In a heavily stented aorta, high‑pressure balloons can cause dissection, rupture, or damage to previously embedded frames, especially when the aortic wall has already incorporated the prior stents (as seen in the case).  
* **Unclear hemodynamic benefit** – In the present case, high‑pressure post‑dilatation (20 mm balloon) already achieved a **significant reduction of the mean gradient from 67 mm Hg immediately post‑procedure to 27 mm Hg at 1 month**, suggesting that the incremental gain from a formal BVF would be modest, while the procedural hazard would be high.  

**Take‑away for the case** – Because the existing frames are **non‑fracturable nitinol**, BVF is **not an option**; the team instead relied on careful high‑implantation and post‑dilatation to maximize the EOA.

---

### 4.  Practical algorithm for adjunctive technique selection in a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV

1. **CT‑based anatomic risk stratification**  
   * Measure coronary‑to‑annulus height, sinus width, and valve‑to‑coronary distance after each prior valve.  
   * Identify “high‑risk” coronary obstruction (neo‑skirt < 4 mm above coronary ostium).  

2. **If high‑risk coronary obstruction** → consider **BASILICA** **only** when the target leaflet is accessible (i.e., not fully trapped by a preceding nitinol frame).  

3. **If severe PPM (indexed EOA < 0.65 cm²/m²)** and the **underlying valve is a surgical bioprosthesis with a known fracture profile** → consider **BVF** before or after the new TAVR implantation.  

4. **If neither condition is met** (as in the Merkulov case) → proceed with:
   * **High implantation** aligned to the prior valve leaflets,  
   * **Commissural alignment** to preserve coronary access, and  
   * **Aggressive post‑dilatation** to maximize radial expansion of the existing frames.  

---
