### [Management] Outline a multidisciplinary follow‑up plan for a patient with symptomatic Chiari I malformation who elects conservative management, including surveillance imaging, sleep studies, and symptom monitoring.

**Multidisciplinary Follow‑up Plan for a Symptomatic Chiari I Patient Who Chooses Conservative Management**  

The goal is to monitor disease‑related complications (brain‑stem compression, syrinx formation, sleep‑disordered breathing, bulbar dysfunction) while preserving quality of life and intervening promptly if the clinical picture worsens.  The plan below mirrors the surveillance strategy employed in the reported case (Mousa et al., 2024) and incorporates current consensus recommendations for Chiari I malformation.

| **Domain** | **Team Member** | **What to Do** | **Frequency / Timing** | **Key Points for the Clinician** |
|------------|----------------|----------------|------------------------|----------------------------------|
| **Neurology / Neurosurgery** | Neurologist & Chiari‑specialist neurosurgeon | • Review new or worsening symptoms (headache pattern, dizziness, dysphagia, choking, limb weakness, sensory change).  <br>• Perform focused neuro‑exam (cerebellar testing, cranial‑nerve exam, gait). <br>• Decide on need for surgical decompression. | • Clinic visit **every 3 months** for the first year, then **every 6 months** if stable. <br>• Immediate review if any acute neurological change. | • In the case report, progression of choking episodes and new nocturnal desaturation prompted the decision to keep the patient under close observation; similar vigilance is required. |
| **Neuro‑Imaging** | Neuroradiologist | • MRI of brain and cervical spine (sagittal T1/T2, cine phase‑contrast CSF flow) to assess (a) tonsillar descent, (b) CSF flow obstruction, (c) syrinx development or enlargement, (d) posterior‑fossa crowding. | • **Baseline MRI** (already obtained). <br>• **Repeat MRI at 12 months** and thereafter **annually** if stable; sooner (6 months) if new symptoms arise or if prior imaging showed a small syrinx. | • An 11‑mm tonsillar descent was present in the case; follow‑up imaging ensures that any increase in descent or new syrinx is caught early, as surgical thresholds often hinge on these radiologic changes. |
| **Sleep Medicine** | Sleep physician / Pulmonologist | • Full‑night, **type I polysomnography** with EEG, capnography, and video (to capture true sleep time and central events). <br>• If unavailable, continue home‐type III study with oximetry and respiratory effort as interim. | • **Baseline PSG** (arranged for the patient in the case). <br>• **Repeat PSG** annually or sooner if AHI rises >5, central events increase, or the patient reports worsening daytime sleepiness, fatigue, or new nocturnal choking. | • The case’s type III study underestimated the severity of central events; full PSG provides definitive data and helps decide if nocturnal ventilation is needed. |
| **Pulmonary Function & Gas Exchange** | Pulmonologist / Respiratory therapist | • Spirometry, maximal inspiratory/expiratory pressures (to detect bulbar‑muscle weakness). <br>• Arterial blood gas (ABG) or transcutaneous CO₂ monitoring during wakefulness and sleep (if hypoventilation suspected). | • **Every 6 months** while under observation; more often if desaturation episodes become frequent. | • The patient’s baseline PFTs were normal; repeat testing can uncover a subtle decline that may herald progression. |
| **Swallowing / Speech‑Language Pathology** | Speech‑language pathologist (SLP) | • Clinical bedside swallowing screen. <br>• Instrumental evaluation (videofluoroscopic swallow study or fiberoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing) if symptoms change. <br>• Education on safe swallowing techniques, diet modification, and aspiration precautions. | • **Initial SLP assessment** (already done in the case) and **follow‑up every 6 months** or sooner if choking frequency or severity increases. | • Neurogenic dysphagia may evolve; early SLP involvement can reduce aspiration risk and improve quality of life. |
| **Psychological / Psychiatric Support** | Psychologist / Psychiatrist | • Screen for anxiety, depression, and health‑related stress (common in patients with chronic choking and hypoxia). <br>• Provide coping strategies, CBT, or medication as needed. | • **Baseline evaluation** and **interval follow‑up every 6–12 months**, or whenever the patient reports increased distress. | • In the case, social withdrawal and anxiety were major contributors to disability; addressing mental health is integral to a conservative approach. |
| **Primary Care Coordination** | Family physician / Nurse practitioner | • Review vaccination status (influenza, pneumococcal). <br>• Manage comorbidities (e.g., hypertension, sleep hygiene). <br>• Serve as the central point for symptom reporting and referral activation. | • **Every 3 months** (aligned with neurology visits) for medication review, vitals, and reinforcement of follow‑up schedule. | • The family‑medicine clinic was the entry point for the patient; ongoing primary‑care oversight ensures continuity. |
| **Emergency Action Plan** | All team members | • Provide the patient with written instructions: (1) seek emergency care for any sudden severe desaturation (<85 % on pulse oximetry), new weakness, or loss of consciousness; (2) bring a copy of the MRI and recent PSG results; (3) consider portable pulse‑oximeter with alarm. | • Review and update plan **annually** or after any acute event. | • The patient’s airport episode illustrates that life‑threatening hypoxia can occur without dyspnea; an explicit plan reduces delay in definitive care. |

### Practical Workflow

1. **Initial “Conservative‑Management” Visit**  
   *Neurology + Neurosurgery* reviews the MRI, discusses the natural history, and documents baseline symptom scores (headache VAS, choking frequency, Epworth Sleepiness Scale).  
   *Sleep medicine* schedules a full PSG within the next 4–6 weeks.  
   *SLP* performs bedside swallow assessment and provides strategies (e.g., chin‑tuck, thickened liquids).  

2. **Monthly Check‑ins (Telehealth or In‑person)**  
   - Review home‑pulse‑ox logs (frequency of desaturation <90 %).  
   - Update choking diary.  
   - Reinforce adherence to sleep‑study preparation and SLP recommendations.  

3. **Quarterly Oximetry**  
   - Home‑type III study for trend analysis; flag any increase in central events (>5 h⁻¹) or prolonged desaturation (>30 s).  

4. **Annual Imaging & Sleep Study**  
   - Obtain brain‑cervical MRI and repeat PSG.  Compare CSF flow curves; any worsening may tip the balance toward surgery.  

5. **Trigger Points for Surgical Referral**  
   - New or worsening syrinx (≥3 mm increase).  
   - Progressive brain‑stem signs (new weakness, gait disturbance, dysphagia).  
   - Central sleep apnoea becoming moderate–severe (central AHI ≥ 15) or requiring supplemental nocturnal ventilation.  
   - Recurrent hypoxic events despite conservative measures.  

### Summary

A structured, multidisciplinary surveillance program—neurology/neurosurgery, neuro‑imaging, sleep medicine, pulmonology, speech‑language pathology, mental‑health services, and primary care—allows early detection of disease progression while respecting the patient’s current wish to avoid surgery.  The schedule described above reflects the actual follow‑up adopted for the patient in Mousa et al. (2024) and aligns with contemporary Chiari‑I monitoring guidelines.  Prompt escalation to posterior‑fossa decompression should be triggered by objective radiologic or physiologic deterioration, or by a clinically unacceptable increase in respiratory or bulbar symptoms.
