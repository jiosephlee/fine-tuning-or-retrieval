Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

### [Differential Diagnosis] Differentiate acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse) due to Chiari malformation from congenital central hypoventilation syndrome related to PHOX2B mutations in terms of clinical presentation and genetic testing.

**Acquired central hypoventilation (Ondine’s curse) secondary to Chiari I malformation versus congenital central hypoventilation syndrome (CCHS) due to PHOX2B mutations**

| Feature | Acquired CCHS – Chiari I (as in the presented patient) | Congenital CCHS – PHOX2B‑related |
|---------|--------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| **Age of onset** | Typically late childhood to adulthood; in the case report the patient first noticed symptoms in her early‑30 s, 14 years after a minor head trauma. | Neonatal period; most patients present within the first days to weeks of life with apnea or failure to thrive. |
| **Triggering event** | Often preceded by a structural insult (trauma, intracranial hypertension, posterior‑fossa surgery) that precipitates tonsillar herniation. In the case, a motor‑vehicle accident was temporally linked to the appearance of occipital headache and later respiratory symptoms. | No precipitating event; the defect is present from conception. |
| **Clinical phenotype** | • Preserved voluntary ventilation (patient can breathe normally when awake or distracted).  <br>• Loss of automatic drive during sleep or when attention is diverted → nocturnal choking, “forgetting to breathe”, profound desaturations without dyspnoea (airport episode).  <br>• Associated brain‑stem signs are common: occipital/headache, lower‑cranial‑nerve dysfunction (dysphagia, choking), sometimes syringomyelia.  <br>• Sleep study shows a predominance of central events, often with a modest AHI because the study may underestimate events (type‑III study in the case). | • Severe hypoventilation both during sleep *and* wakefulness; patients often require ventilation from birth.  <br>• Autonomic dysregulation (hypotension, temperature instability) and neuro‐endocrine abnormalities (Hirschsprung disease, cardiac dysrhythmias) are frequent.  <br>• Bulbar dysfunction may be present but is usually accompanied by generalized hypotonia rather than isolated choking. |
| **Radiologic hallmark** | MRI demonstrates cerebellar tonsillar descent ≥5 mm (11 mm in the case) with crowding of the foramen magnum, often with CSF flow obstruction or syrinx. The finding is *acquired* or previously unrecognized, and the respiratory phenotype resolves after posterior‑fossa decompression. | Brain MRI is usually normal; the pathology is at the genetic/neuronal‑development level rather than an anatomic compression. |
| **Genetic testing** | Not indicated. PHOX2B sequencing is typically negative; the diagnosis rests on neuro‑imaging and exclusion of other causes. In the case report the authors explicitly contrast the patient’s “acquired” Ondine’s curse with the congenital form and do not mention any PHOX2B mutation. | Mandatory for confirmation. A pathogenic expansion in the polyalanine repeat of **PHOX2B** (most commonly 20/24–20/33 repeat genotypes) is found in >95 % of classic CCHS. Targeted sequencing or repeat‑primed PCR is the standard test. |
| **Management focus** | • Address the structural lesion: posterior‑fossa decompression often restores automatic respiratory drive. <br>• Until surgery, nocturnal ventilatory support (BiPAP, CPAP, or tracheostomy) is used as a bridge. | • Lifelong ventilatory support (non‑invasive ventilation or tracheostomy). <br>• Surveillance for associated autonomic/enteric anomalies. <br>• No surgical cure because there is no compressive lesion. |
| **Prognosis** | Potentially reversible; many patients achieve normal automatic breathing after adequate decompression, as suggested by the case series cited in the report. | Chronic condition; mortality is related to ventilation failure or associated autonomic complications. |

**Key teaching points derived from the case**

1. **Pattern of breathing loss** – The patient’s ability to breathe voluntarily while lacking automatic drive during sleep or distraction is classic for *acquired* central hypoventilation due to brain‑stem compression, distinguishing it from the congenital form where the respiratory drive is defective in both states.

2. **Associated neurologic signs** – Headache, occipital allodynia, and bulbar dysphagia are typical of Chiari‑related brain‑stem irritation; congenital CCHS presents mainly with isolated ventilatory failure and a constellation of autonomic–neurocristopathies.

3. **Diagnostic algorithm** – In any adult or adolescent with unexplained central apnoea, a high‑resolution brain‑MRI of the cranio‑cervical junction should precede genetic testing. A positive Chiari finding obviates the need for PHOX2B analysis.

4. **Genetic testing is not a “rule‑out” for acquired cases** – PHOX2B sequencing should be reserved for patients with the classic neonatal presentation or when imaging is unrevealing. The case report exemplifies that an overlooked Chiari malformation can masquerade as “Ondine’s curse” for years, delaying appropriate neurosurgical treatment.

**Bottom line:**  
Acquired central hypoventilation from Chiari I malformation presents in later life with selective loss of automatic breathing, often after a precipitating insult, and is diagnosed by MRI of the posterior fossa; genetic testing for PHOX2B is unnecessary. Congenital CCHS presents in the newborn period with pervasive hypoventilation, autonomic dysregulation, and a pathogenic PHOX2B expansion, and requires lifelong ventilatory support. Recognizing these distinctions prevents unnecessary genetic work‑up in structural cases and directs patients promptly to the definitive therapy—posterior‑fossa decompression.
