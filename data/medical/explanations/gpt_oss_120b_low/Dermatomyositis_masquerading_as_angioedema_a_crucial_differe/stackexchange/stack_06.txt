Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Management] Discuss the indications for malignancy screening in dermatomyositis patients and outline the screening strategy used for this patient, including its rationale based on risk stratification.

**Malignancy screening in dermatomyositis – When and How?**

Dermatomyositis (DM) is one of the classic “cancer‑associated autoimmune diseases.”  Large registry data (e.g., the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group, IMACS) show that the incidence of an occult solid‑organ malignancy within the first 3 years of DM diagnosis is ≈15–20 %.  Consequently, current practice guidelines recommend *systematic cancer screening* for every adult with new‑onset DM, with the intensity of the work‑up guided by individual risk factors.

---

### 1.  Indications for cancer screening in DM

| **Risk factor** | **Impact on screening intensity** |
|-----------------|------------------------------------|
| **Core diagnosis of DM** (vs. polymyositis) | Mandatory baseline evaluation |
| **Age > 45 yr** (especially > 60 yr) | Increases baseline probability; more extensive imaging |
| **Male sex** | Slightly higher cancer‑risk in DM |
| **Dysphagia** | Strongly associated with underlying malignancy (odds ratio ≈2–3) |
| **Specific myositis antibodies** – **high‑risk** (anti‑TIF1‑γ, anti‑NXP‑2, anti‑Mi‑2) <br> **intermediate‑risk** (anti‑SAE1, anti‑MDA5) | Antibody profile informs the pre‑test probability |
| **B‑symptoms, unexplained weight loss, anemia, or elevated tumor markers** | Prompt targeted investigations |

The IMACS guideline stratifies patients into *low*, *intermediate*, or *high* cancer‑risk groups based on the presence of these variables.  The patient in the present case had **four** risk factors:

* DM diagnosis (core)
* Dysphagia (high‑risk clinical feature)
* Anti‑SAE1 positivity (intermediate‑risk serology)
* Male gender (intermediate‑risk demographic)

Thus he was classified as **high‑risk**, mandating a *comprehensive* baseline screen.

---

### 2.  Screening strategy employed for this patient – Rationale

| **Component** | **Justification in this case** |
|---------------|--------------------------------|
| **Cross‑sectional imaging of thorax, abdomen, pelvis (CT)** | CT chest identified pulmonary nodules and aspiration changes; CT abdomen/pelvis excluded obvious visceral masses.  CT is the backbone of baseline cancer surveillance in DM because it evaluates the most common sites (lung, breast, ovary, pancreas, gastrointestinal tract). |
| **Whole‑body FDG‑PET/CT** | PET adds functional data, increases sensitivity for occult, metabolically active tumors (especially in the head/neck, lymph nodes, and small visceral lesions) that may be missed on CT alone.  In this patient the PET scan showed diffuse muscular inflammation but no hyper‑metabolic focus suggestive of malignancy, effectively “ruling out” a covert tumor at presentation. |
| **Serum tumor markers (CEA, CA‑19‑9, CA‑125, PSA)** | Although not highly specific, they provide a low‑cost adjunct and were normal, supporting the imaging findings.  PSA is especially relevant in a male patient > 40 yr. |
| **Age‑appropriate endoscopic evaluation (gastroscopy & colonoscopy)** | Upper and lower gastrointestinal cancers are the most frequent solid tumors associated with DM in men.  Because endoscopy can detect early mucosal neoplasia not seen on imaging, the plan for both procedures was incorporated even though initial imaging was negative. |
| **Routine laboratory monitoring (CBC, CMP, ESR/CRP, CK)** | Persistent unexplained anemia, liver function abnormalities, or a new rise in inflammatory markers can be a clue to occult malignancy; serial labs are therefore part of the surveillance protocol. |
| **Symptom‑directed follow‑up (clinical exam, repeat imaging at 6–12 months)** | The IMACS guideline advises repeat imaging (usually CT chest/abdomen/pelvis) at 6–12 months if the initial screen is negative, then annually for the first 3 years.  In this patient, the plan includes serial CT chest (given pre‑existing nodules) and ongoing symptom review. |

**Why this approach?**  
The combination of **CT** (high anatomical resolution) plus **FDG‑PET** (functional sensitivity) offers the highest likelihood of detecting a hidden malignancy at baseline.  Adding **tumor markers** and **endoscopy** covers the most common DM‑associated cancers that may be missed on cross‑sectional imaging.  This tiered strategy aligns with IMACS recommendations for *high‑risk* patients: a “full‑body” search at diagnosis followed by scheduled surveillance.

---

### 3.  Take‑home points for the bedside learner

1. **All adults with new‑onset DM need cancer screening**; the intensity is dictated by age, sex, dysphagia, and myositis‑specific antibodies.
2. **Anti‑SAE1 antibodies place the patient in an intermediate‑risk serologic category**, but the presence of dysphagia upgrades the overall risk to *high*.
3. **Baseline work‑up** should include:
   * CT chest/abdomen/pelvis (or MRI if radiation is a concern),
   * Whole‑body FDG‑PET/CT when available,
   * Serum tumor markers appropriate to sex and age,
   * Age‑appropriate endoscopic procedures (EGD, colonoscopy) especially in men.
4. **Follow‑up surveillance** is required for at least 3 years, with repeat imaging at 6–12 months and then annually, plus symptom‑directed evaluation.
5. **Negative baseline studies do not obviate continued vigilance**; 15–20 % of associated cancers appear after the initial screen, underscoring the need for ongoing clinical monitoring.

In this case, the patient’s high‑risk profile prompted a *comprehensive* baseline screen (CT + PET + tumor markers + planned endoscopy) that has so far remained negative, and a surveillance plan has been instituted in accordance with international guidelines.
