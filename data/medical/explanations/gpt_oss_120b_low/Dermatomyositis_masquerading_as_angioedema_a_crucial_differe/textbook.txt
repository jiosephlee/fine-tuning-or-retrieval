Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Differential Diagnosis  

The patient was a previously healthy man in his early forties who arrived at the emergency department after three weeks of a steadily enlarging neck mass.  The swelling was described by the patient as “tight and sore,” and it was accompanied by a vague sense of heaviness in the hips that made climbing stairs and rising from a seated position difficult.  Over the same period he noted increasing difficulty swallowing both liquids and soft foods, which he attributed to a “sticky” sensation in the throat rather than frank choking.  He denied fever, recent upper‑respiratory infection, recent dental work, or any trauma to the head and neck.  There was no history of recent travel, exposure to sick contacts, or animal bites.  

### History of Present Illness  

- **Progressive cervical edema**: began insidiously, localized to the anterior neck, and expanded to involve the whole cervical soft‑tissue envelope.  The edema was non‑tender, non‑fluctuant, and did not exhibit the classic erythema or warmth expected of cellulitis.  
- **Proximal muscle weakness**: the patient reported new‑onset difficulty lifting his hips against gravity (hip‑flexor power 4/5) and mild myalgia of the thighs.  He denied arm weakness, gait instability, or preceding trauma.  
- **Dysphagia**: described as a sensation of food “sticking” in the throat, with occasional coughing during meals, but without overt choking, voice change, or weight loss.  

### Relevant Negative History  

A careful review of systems and medication history yielded several important exclusions:  

- **No recent drug exposures** known to precipitate angioedema, such as angiotensin‑converting‑enzyme (ACE) inhibitors, dipeptidyl‑peptidase‑4 (DPP‑4) inhibitors, or non‑steroidal anti‑inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs).  
- **No prior episodes** of facial or oropharyngeal swelling, ruling against a personal history of hereditary or acquired angioedema.  
- **Absence of infection clues**: no pharyngitis, recent dental infection, odontogenic pain, or skin breakdown that might seed a deep neck space infection.  
- **No systemic autoimmune disease** known to cause vasculitic edema, and no family history suggestive of complement‑mediated angioedema.  

These negative data points narrowed the post‑initial differential by lowering the pre‑test probability for many common causes of rapid neck swelling.  

### Physical Examination Findings  

On examination the patient was afebrile, hemodynamically stable, and maintained saturations of 99 % on room air.  Key findings included:  

- **Neck**: marked, diffuse, non‑tender edema involving the strap muscles.  No overlying erythema, induration, or fluctuance was noted.  No tracheal deviation or stridor was present, suggesting that airway compromise was not yet imminent.  
- **Cutaneous/ nailfold**: capillaroscopy revealed **micro‑haemorrhages** and isolated avascular fields at the nailfolds, a subtle sign of microvascular injury often seen in connective‑tissue disease.  Peri‑ungual erythema was present, and a faint **erythematous rash** was noted on the right lateral thigh.  Classic heliotrope rash or Gottron’s papules were absent, but the “holster sign” (purplish erythema over the upper arms or shoulders) was later identified on detailed inspection.  
- **Neuromuscular**: Manual muscle testing demonstrated hip‑flexor strength 4/5 (Medical Research Council scale) with otherwise preserved strength in the upper limbs.  No facial weakness or ocular involvement was observed.  

These findings presented a constellation that was atypical for pure infectious edema but raised suspicion for a systemic inflammatory process involving muscle and microvasculature.  

### Initial Imaging and Interpretation  

Given the concern for a deep neck space infection, a contrast‑enhanced **CT scan of the neck** was obtained emergently.  The study showed **diffuse edema of the strap muscles** (sternohyoid, sternothyroid, omohyoid) and **increased attenuation within the retropharyngeal space**.  No focal fluid collection, gas formation, or rim‑enhancing abscess was evident.  The radiologist’s impression favored **Ludwig’s angio‑itis** (the radiographic term for early Ludwig’s angina) because of the characteristic involvement of the submandibular and submental soft tissues, but the absence of a clear nidus of infection (e.g., dental abscess) was noted.  

### First‑Line Differential Diagnoses in the Emergency/ENT Setting  

The emergency physician and ENT team therefore entertained a short list of entities that can produce rapid cervical swelling:  

1. **Deep neck space infection (Ludwig’s angina/angio‑itis)** – typically odontogenic, characterized by rapid spread of infection into the submandibular, sublingual, and submental spaces, often with fever, trismus, and a “hot” neck.  The CT findings were partially compatible, but the lack of odontogenic source and systemic signs made this less likely.  

2. **Histamine‑mediated angioedema** – usually associated with urticaria, pruritus, and a rapid onset after an identifiable trigger (food, drug, insect bite).  The patient’s swelling was sub‑cutaneous rather than mucosal, and there were no cutaneous wheals or itching, arguing against this mechanism.  

3. **Bradykinin‑mediated angioedema (hereditary or acquired C1‑esterase inhibitor deficiency)** – classically presents with non‑pruritic, non‑urticarial swelling of the face, lips, tongue, or airway, often without accompanying erythema.  It may be precipitated by ACE inhibitors or occur spontaneously.  The patient denied ACE‑I exposure and had no prior episodes, and laboratory screening for complement abnormalities later proved normal; however, these results were not available at presentation.  

4. **Non‑infectious inflammatory conditions** – including **inflammatory myopathies** (e.g., dermatomyositis, polymyositis), **vasculitis** (e.g., urticarial vasculitis, hypocomplementemic urticarial vasculitis), and **autoimmune edema syndromes** (e.g., systemic lupus erythematosus with serositis).  The presence of proximal muscle weakness, nailfold micro‑haemorrhages, and a thigh rash tilted the balance toward an inflammatory myopathy, despite the rarity of neck edema as an initial manifestation.  

### Rationale for Shifting Focus from Infectious to Immunologic Etiologies  

Two key factors prompted the treating team to broaden the diagnostic lens beyond infection:  

- **Lack of infectious corroboration** – the patient was afebrile, the white‑cell count was within normal limits, and C‑reactive protein, while modestly elevated (55 mg/L), was not at the level typically seen in fulminant deep‑space infections.  Moreover, the CT did not reveal an abscess or gas, which are hallmarks of necrotizing infection.  

- **Presence of systemic, non‑localizing signs** – early proximal muscle weakness, a peripheral rash, and nailfold capillary changes are not explained by a localized neck infection.  These cutaneous and muscular clues are classic for connective‑tissue disease and warranted a targeted evaluation (CK level, auto‑antibody panel).  

Consequently, the team consulted immunology, ordered serum creatine kinase (which later returned markedly elevated), and initiated a broader work‑up for inflammatory myopathies.  This pivot from a purely infectious algorithm to one that incorporated autoimmune mechanisms was pivotal in achieving an early diagnosis of dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema.

## Dermatomyositis: Pathophysiology, Clinical Spectrum, and the Role of Anti‑SAE1 Antibodies  

Dermatomyositis (DM) belongs to the group of idiopathic inflammatory myopathies (IIMs), a heterogeneous family of autoimmune disorders that primarily target skeletal muscle but often involve skin, lung, heart and other organs. Within the IIM classification, DM is distinguished from polymyositis, inclusion‑body myositis, immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy and overlap myositis by a characteristic combination of cutaneous manifestations, a specific microvascular pathology, and a predilection for humoral immune mechanisms.

### Definition and Classification  
DM is defined as an acquired autoimmune disease characterized by (1) chronic inflammation of skeletal muscle with a predominantly **proximal, symmetric weakness**, (2) **typical dermatoses** that involve the hands, face, neck and trunk, and (3) a **histopathologic pattern of complement‑mediated microangiopathy**. The International Myositis Classification Criteria (2022) assign a high diagnostic weight to the presence of the heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules or a biopsy showing perifascicular atrophy with C5b‑9 (membrane attack complex) deposition. Patients are further stratified by myositis‑specific antibodies (MSAs); the anti‑small ubiquitin‑like modifier‑activating enzyme subunit 1 (anti‑SAE1) antibody defines a distinct serologic subgroup that carries particular clinical nuances.

### Key Pathogenic Mechanisms  

#### Complement‑Mediated Microangiopathy  
The hallmark lesion in DM is a **microvascular injury** of the capillaries that perfuse the perifascicular region of skeletal muscle. Autoantibodies and immune complexes activate the classical complement cascade, culminating in **C5b‑9 (membrane attack complex) deposition** on endothelial membranes. This leads to capillary necrosis, hypoperfusion, and secondary perifascicular muscle fibre atrophy. The same process underlies the characteristic Gottron’s papules and heliotrope rash, where dermal vessels are similarly targeted.

#### Autoantibody‑Driven Immune Dysregulation  
Beyond complement activation, a spectrum of **myositis‑specific antibodies** (MSAs) exerts pathogenic influence by shaping the adaptive immune response. MSAs can be directed against cytoplasmic enzymes (e.g., Jo‑1), nuclear helicases (Mi‑2), transcriptional regulators (TIF1‑γ) or, in the case of SAE1, components of the **SUMO (small ubiquitin‑like modifier) pathway**. The presence of a particular MSA correlates with distinct clinical phenotypes, disease severity, and extramuscular involvement, informing both diagnostic suspicion and prognostic counseling.

#### Role of Sumoylation and the SAE Complex  
The **SAE (SUMO‑activating enzyme) heterodimer** comprises SAE1 and SAE2, which together catalyze the first step of sumoylation—a reversible post‑translational modification that modulates protein stability, nuclear‑cytoplasmic transport, and transcriptional activity. Anti‑SAE1 antibodies recognize the catalytic subunit, potentially interfering with normal sumoylation of proteins involved in immune regulation, muscle repair and endothelial integrity. Although the precise mechanistic link remains incompletely elucidated, experimental data suggest that dysregulated sumoylation may augment inflammatory cytokine production and promote the vascular injury characteristic of DM. Consequently, anti‑SAE1 antibodies serve as both a **biomarker of disease** and a **potential contributor to pathogenesis**.

### Clinical Hallmarks  

#### Cutaneous Signs  
Skin disease is often the first clue to DM and follows a characteristic distribution:  

* **Gottron’s papules** – raised violaceous papules over the extensor surfaces of the MCP and PIP joints.  
* **Heliotrope rash** – dusky periorbital erythema with edema, sometimes accompanied by a “shawl” of erythema over the neck and shoulders.  
* **V‑neck/holster sign** – erythematous, edematous rash of the anterior neck and upper chest, mirroring the shape of a V or a holster.  
* **Peri‑ungual telangiectasia** and nailfold capillary changes (microhemorrhages, avascular zones) detectable on capillaroscopy.  

These lesions arise from the same complement‑mediated microangiopathy that harms muscle capillaries.

#### Muscle Involvement  
Patients develop **proximal muscle weakness** (hip flexors, shoulder girdle) that progresses over weeks to months. Laboratory testing typically reveals **marked elevation of serum creatine kinase (CK)**—often >5 × upper limit of normal—as well as raised transaminases and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), reflecting muscle breakdown. Imaging (MRI, FDG‑PET) shows increased signal or metabolic activity in affected muscles, while biopsy confirms perifascicular atrophy, perimysial inflammation, and C5b‑9 deposition.

#### Extramuscular Manifestations  
* **Interstitial lung disease (ILD):** Occurs in ~30‑40 % of adults, presenting as ground‑glass opacities or traction bronchiectasis on CT.  
* **Dysphagia:** Involvement of oropharyngeal and esophageal musculature leads to aspiration, weight loss, and, as illustrated in the case, severe neck swelling that mimics angioedema.  
* **Cardiac involvement:** Arrhythmias, myocarditis or conduction disease may be present but are less common.  
* **Malignancy risk:** Adult DM confers a 15‑20 % cumulative risk of an associated cancer, especially within the first three years after diagnosis. Screening is guided by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) recommendations.

### Epidemiology and Age Distribution  
The overall incidence of DM is approximately **10 cases per million person‑years**. There is a bimodal age pattern: a peak in **children aged 5–14 years** and a second peak in **adults 45–64 years**. The disease is slightly more prevalent in females, but serologic subgroups such as anti‑SAE1 do not show a strong gender bias.

### Anti‑SAE1 Antibody Phenotype  

#### Frequency and Core Clinical Features  
Anti‑SAE1 antibodies are identified in **~5‑10 %** of adult DM cohorts, making them an uncommon but clinically important MSA. The serologic profile is associated with:

* **Prominent cutaneous disease** – often an early, extensive V‑neck/holster rash and peri‑ungual erythema.  
* **Dysphagia** – reported in ~45‑50 % of SAE1‑positive patients, reflecting early involvement of pharyngeal musculature.  
* **Interstitial lung disease** – present in ~40 % of cases, though typically milder than in anti‑MDA5 disease.  
* **Edema/angioedema‑like swelling** – observed in a minority (≈6‑8 % of all DM patients) but more frequently reported among SAE1‑positive individuals compared with other MSAs; however, the prevalence remains low (≈15‑20 % of SAE1‑positive cases).  

#### Edema versus True Angioedema  
In DM, **inflammatory edema** arises from capillary leakage secondary to complement‑mediated endothelial injury and cytokine‑driven increased vascular permeability. The resulting soft‑tissue swelling can be **localized (e.g., neck, face)** and clinically indistinguishable from **histamine‑ or bradykinin‑mediated angioedema**. Key discriminators include:

* **Absence of urticaria, pruritus, or rapid onset after an allergen exposure.**  
* **Persistence despite antihistamines or C1‑esterase inhibitor therapy.**  
* **Concurrent muscle weakness, elevated CK, and cutaneous signs of DM.**  

Understanding this distinction is essential because management diverges dramatically—DM requires high‑dose glucocorticoids and immunosuppression, whereas true angioedema is treated with airway‑protective measures, antihistamines, bradykinin‑targeted agents, or C1‑esterase replacement.

### Integrating Pathophysiology, Clinical Spectrum, and Anti‑SAE1 Antibodies in the Present Case  
The reported patient exemplifies how **anti‑SAE1‑driven dermatomyositis** can manifest with **neck soft‑tissue swelling** that mimics idiopathic angioedema. The underlying complement‑mediated microangiopathy produced capillary leak in the neck strap muscles, generating **inflammatory edema** rather than a histamine‑mediated reaction. Subtle dermatoses (peri‑ungual telangiectasia, holster sign) and a striking CK rise prompted the clinician to consider DM, leading to confirmatory testing for anti‑SAE1 antibodies and a muscle biopsy that demonstrated classic C5b‑9 deposition. Recognizing this serologic subtype informed both prognostication (higher risk of dysphagia and ILD) and therapeutic decisions (early aggressive immunosuppression with steroids, IVIG, mycophenolate and rituximab).  

In sum, dermatomyositis is an immune‑mediated vasculopathy of muscle and skin, with anti‑SAE1 antibodies marking a subgroup prone to prominent cutaneous disease, dysphagia, and occasional inflammatory edema that can be mistaken for angioedema. Awareness of these pathogenetic and phenotypic nuances enables timely diagnosis and appropriate immunomodulatory treatment, as illustrated by the successful outcome of the case.

## Diagnostic Work‑up and Reasoning Process  

The diagnostic pathway in this case illustrates how a systematic combination of laboratory, imaging, and procedural investigations can sift through a wide differential—from infectious deep‑neck space disease to hereditary angioedema—and converge on a unifying diagnosis of anti‑SAE1 antibody–positive dermatomyositis. Each test was chosen to address a specific clinical question, and the sequential results progressively narrowed the possibilities until the constellation of findings satisfied established classification criteria for dermatomyositis.

### Laboratory Panel  

**Creatine kinase (CK) and muscle enzyme trends**  
The first clue that the neck swelling was not a simple angioedema came from the markedly elevated CK (16 184 U/L). CK is released from damaged skeletal muscle, and levels >1 000 U/L are highly suggestive of an active myopathic process. Serial CK measurement is also valuable for monitoring disease activity; in this patient the CK fell to 37 U/L within three weeks of immunosuppression, corroborating clinical improvement.  

**Liver enzymes and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH)**  
AST (396 U/L), ALT (143 U/L), and LDH (692 U/L) were all elevated. In inflammatory myopathies, transaminases rise because these enzymes are present in skeletal muscle as well as hepatocytes; LDH is a nonspecific marker of cellular turnover but is frequently raised in active myositis. The pattern—higher AST than ALT—favours muscle rather than liver injury.  

**Inflammatory markers (CRP, white‑cell count)**  
A modestly raised CRP (55.6 mg/L) with a normal white‑cell count (9.1 × 10⁹/L) suggested systemic inflammation without a clear bacterial infection. In deep neck space infections, CRP is often markedly higher and accompanied by leukocytosis; the relatively modest elevation supported an inflammatory, non‑infectious etiology.  

**Autoimmune serologies**  
An ANA titer of 1:1280 with a speckled pattern indicated a high‑level autoantibody response, a finding common in idiopathic inflammatory myopathies. The myositis‑specific antibody panel identified a strong SAE1 signal (173 IU; normal <35). Anti‑SAE1 antibodies target the SUMO‑activating enzyme and are highly specific for a subset of dermatomyositis that frequently presents with prominent cutaneous disease and dysphagia. The absence of other antibodies (anti‑Mi‑2, anti‑TIF1‑γ, anti‑Jo‑1, anti‑OJ) helped to exclude alternative serologic subtypes that carry different clinical phenotypes or malignancy risks.  

**Complement levels and C1‑esterase inhibitor testing**  
Normal C3, C4, quantitative C1‑esterase inhibitor, and functional C1‑esterase inhibitor levels effectively ruled out hereditary or acquired angioedema, which are mediated by complement or bradykinin pathways. This laboratory exclusion was essential because the initial presentation—rapid, painless neck swelling—fits the classic description of angioedema.  

### Imaging Studies  

**Neck CT**  
The emergent CT demonstrated diffuse edema of the strap muscles and retropharyngeal soft tissue without a fluid collection, gas formation, or rim‑enhancing abscess. The absence of a discrete infectious focus made Ludwig’s angina unlikely and shifted the focus toward a non‑infectious, inflammatory infiltrate.  

**Chest CT and pulmonary function tests (PFTs)**  
Chest CT identified several non‑cavitating nodules and peribronchial consolidation, findings compatible with aspiration pneumonitis secondary to dysphagia rather than primary interstitial lung disease (ILD). The PFTs showed an extrathoracic restrictive pattern, consistent with reduced lung volumes from muscle weakness in the neck and pharynx, further supporting a myopathic process.  

**MRI of the thighs**  
MRI revealed high‑signal intensity on fluid‑sensitive sequences (STIR) within the bilateral obturator internus, externus, adductors, gracilis, and distal iliopsoas muscles, denoting active inflammation. The distribution—predominantly proximal, symmetric, and involving both axial and appendicular muscles—is characteristic of inflammatory myopathy and helped to rule out focal muscular disorders such as inclusion body myositis (which tends to affect distal muscles and is asymmetric).  

**FDG‑PET**  
Whole‑body FDG‑PET demonstrated diffuse, intense uptake in multiple muscle groups, most prominently in the head, neck, and shoulder girdle. This pattern reflects metabolically active inflammation rather than neoplastic uptake. Importantly, PET disclosed no hypermetabolic lesions suggestive of occult malignancy, satisfying the malignancy‑screening component of the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) recommendations.  

### Procedural Diagnostics  

**Nailfold capillaroscopy**  
Capillaroscopic examination disclosed microhemorrhages and avascular areas, hallmarks of microvascular injury seen in connective‑tissue diseases, including dermatomyositis. While not diagnostic on its own, these findings corroborated the systemic autoimmune nature of the disease and reinforced the need for myositis‑specific testing.  

**Muscle biopsy**  
A percutaneous biopsy of the thigh muscle provided definitive histopathologic confirmation. Histology showed perifascicular necrosis, regenerative fibers, and perimysial inflammation with scattered histiocytes. Immunostaining demonstrated C5b‑9 (membrane attack complex) deposition in the endomysial capillaries—a classic feature of dermatomyositis, reflecting complement‑mediated microangiopathy. The combination of perifascicular pathology and complement deposition satisfies the histologic criteria required for a “definite” diagnosis under the 2017 European League Against Rheumatism/American College of Rheumatology (EULAR/ACR) classification system.  

### Integration of Findings  

The diagnostic synthesis was achieved by aligning three pillars:  

1. **Biochemical evidence of muscle injury** – the striking CK elevation, AST/ALT ratio, and LDH rise indicated active myositis.  
2. **Serologic specificity** – a high‑titer anti‑SAE1 antibody pinpointed a dermatomyositis subset known for cutaneous and dysphagic manifestations, and the ANA speckled pattern reinforced an autoimmune basis.  
3. **Morphologic confirmation** – MRI and FDG‑PET localized muscular inflammation; capillaroscopy demonstrated microvascular involvement; and the muscle biopsy provided the pathognomonic perifascicular necrosis and complement deposition.  

Collectively, these data fulfilled the 2017 EULAR/ACR classification criteria for dermatomyositis (score ≥ 9.0), with points contributed by the CK level (>10 000 U/L), anti‑SAE1 positivity, MRI‑demonstrated muscle edema, and characteristic histology.  

### Ruling Out Alternative Diagnoses  

- **Infection**: Lack of fever, modest CRP rise, normal WCC, and CT showing no abscess argued against deep neck space infection.  
- **Hereditary/acquired angioedema**: Normal complement profile and C1‑esterase inhibitor levels excluded bradykinin‑mediated angioedema.  
- **Neuromuscular junction disorders** (e.g., myasthenia gravis): Absence of fluctuating weakness, negative autoantibodies (AChR, MuSK), and presence of CK elevation made this unlikely.  
- **Drug hypersensitivity or contact dermatitis**: No new medications, and the systemic muscle enzyme derangement would not accompany a pure hypersensitivity reaction.  
- **Malignancy**: Comprehensive imaging (CT, PET) and tumor marker panel were negative; ongoing surveillance was instituted per IMACS guidelines owing to the known cancer‑association risk in anti‑SAE1 disease.  

Through a disciplined, step‑wise approach, the team transformed an initially misleading presentation of “angioedema” into a precise diagnosis of anti‑SAE1 antibody‑positive dermatomyositis, allowing rapid initiation of disease‑modifying therapy and averting potentially fatal airway compromise.

## Therapeutic Strategy and Rationale  

The management of this patient required a coordinated approach that addressed three overarching goals: (1) rapid suppression of the fulminant inflammatory process that threatened the airway and caused extensive muscle damage, (2) establishment of a steroid‑sparing regimen that would maintain disease control while minimizing long‑term glucocorticoid toxicity, and (3) prevention of treatment‑related complications, particularly infection and pulmonary toxicity. The therapeutic plan was therefore organised into an acute‑phase protocol, a transition to chronic immunosuppression, and supportive measures that facilitated recovery of swallowing and muscle function.  

### Acute Phase Treatment  

#### High‑dose Intravenous Methylprednisolone (1 g/day × 3)  
Severe dermatomyositis with involvement of neck musculature poses an immediate airway risk because edema of the strap muscles can progress to obstructive respiratory compromise. Intravenous methylprednisolone at 1 g daily for three consecutive days is the standard “pulse” regimen recommended by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) for life‑threatening disease activity. The massive glucocorticoid load produces a rapid decline in pro‑inflammatory cytokines (IL‑6, TNF‑α) and suppresses T‑cell activation, resulting in a reduction of muscle fibre necrosis and capillary deposition of complement (C5b‑9) that characterises dermatomyositis. In this case the pulse achieved a noticeable decrease in the cervical soft‑tissue swelling within 48 h, confirming its efficacy in halting the airway‑threatening process.  

#### Intravenous Immunoglobulin (IVIG) Loading Dose  
IVIG was administered concurrently (2 g/kg over 5 days) to augment the anti‑inflammatory effect and to address the possibility of refractory disease. IVIG exerts multiple mechanisms that are particularly relevant in dermatomyositis: (i) saturation of Fcγ receptors on macrophages, thereby limiting antibody‑mediated clearance of damaged muscle fibres; (ii) inhibition of complement activation and blockade of the membrane attack complex that underlies perifascicular capillary injury; and (iii) neutralisation of pathogenic auto‑antibodies, including anti‑SAE1, through idiotype–anti‑idiotype interactions. Randomised data in adult inflammatory myopathies have shown that IVIG improves muscle strength and CK levels when added to glucocorticoids, especially in patients with severe weakness, dysphagia, or pulmonary involvement. The loading dose therefore served to achieve brisk clinical improvement while the steroid taper was being organised.  

#### Steroid Tapering Protocol  
After the methylprednisolone pulses, oral prednisone was initiated at 60 mg daily (≈1 mg/kg for this patient) to maintain anti‑inflammatory pressure. The taper was planned at a rate of 5 mg every 2–3 weeks, a schedule that balances two competing risks: (a) relapse of muscle inflammation if the dose is reduced too quickly, and (b) cumulative glucocorticoid toxicity (osteoporosis, hyperglycaemia, cataract). CK levels were used as a biochemical marker of disease activity; tapering was paused or slowed whenever CK rose >2‑fold above the nadir or when clinical strength regressed. By 7 months the patient had successfully discontinued prednisone, reflecting a well‑controlled disease state.  

### Steroid‑Sparing Agents  

#### Mycophenolate Mofetil (MMF)  
MMF was chosen as the first‑line steroid‑sparing agent for several reasons. Mycophenolic acid inhibits inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase, thereby suppressing de novo guanosine synthesis and preferentially affecting proliferating lymphocytes. This mechanism curtails both B‑cell antibody production and T‑cell mediated muscle injury. In dermatomyositis, MMF has demonstrated comparable efficacy to methotrexate for improving muscle strength and pulmonary function, with a more favourable safety profile in patients at risk for interstitial lung disease (ILD). The patient already exhibited an extrathoracic restrictive pattern and CT evidence of aspiration‑related consolidation; methotrexate’s known propensity for drug‑induced pneumonitis made MMF the safer choice. The starting dose of 1 g twice daily was titrated to 2 g twice daily as tolerated, with close monitoring of complete blood count, liver enzymes, and serum creatinine.  

#### Rituximab  
Rituximab, a chimeric anti‑CD20 monoclonal antibody, depletes circulating B cells and thereby reduces auto‑antibody production. Evidence for rituximab in refractory dermatomyositis comes primarily from the RIM (Rituximab in Myositis) trial and subsequent observational cohorts, which demonstrated clinically meaningful improvements in muscle strength and CK levels after two 1000‑mg infusions given two weeks apart. In anti‑SAE1 positive disease, B‑cell activity is a plausible driver of pathogenic auto‑antibody generation, supporting rituximab’s use even though the patient was not yet refractory. The decision to add rituximab early was guided by the severe initial presentation (airway edema, dysphagia) and the desire to achieve durable disease control while allowing a rapid steroid taper. B‑cell counts were measured before each infusion to ensure adequate depletion, and hepatitis B screening was performed to mitigate the risk of reactivation.  

#### Agents Deliberately Avoided  

* **Methotrexate** – Although methotrexate is frequently employed as a steroid‑sparing agent in dermatomyositis, it carries a well‑documented risk of pulmonary toxicity, ranging from hypersensitivity pneumonitis to fibrosis. Given the patient’s early ILD‑type changes on PFTs and CT, the team elected to avoid methotrexate to prevent exacerbation of respiratory compromise.  

* **Azathioprine & Tacrolimus** – Both agents are viable alternatives; however, azathioprine’s metabolite 6‑mercaptopurine can cause myelosuppression and hepatic injury, which would compound the elevated transaminases already present. Tacrolimus, a calcineurin inhibitor, has a narrower therapeutic window and requires frequent drug‑level monitoring; the patient preferred a regimen with less laboratory burden. Consequently, these drugs were kept as backup options should MMF or rituximab be intolerable.  

### Infection Prophylaxis  

High‑dose steroids, IVIG, MMF, and rituximab collectively place patients at increased risk for opportunistic infections, particularly Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP). Standard prophylaxis with trimethoprim‑sulfamethoxazole (TMP‑SMX) was initiated, but the patient developed hemolytic anaemia without G6PD deficiency, an idiosyncratic reaction to TMP‑SMX. After discontinuation, nebulised pentamidine (300 mg monthly) was instituted, providing effective PJP prophylaxis with a markedly lower risk of systemic toxicity. Routine screening for viral reactivation (CMV, HBV) was performed before rituximab, and the patient remained negative throughout therapy.  

### Supportive Care  

#### Nutritional Support via Nasogastric Tube  
Severe dysphagia, documented by barium swallow as significant pharyngeal residue and aspiration, mandated enteral feeding. A nasogastric tube allowed delivery of high‑protein, calorie‑dense nutrition while the inflammatory process affecting the pharyngeal musculature settled. Oral medications (prednisone, MMF, etc.) were administered via the tube to maintain therapeutic dosing without interruption. The tube was removed once videofluoroscopic assessment at two months showed safe swallowing of soft solids.  

#### Physical Therapy and Rehabilitation  
Early mobilisation is essential to prevent contractures and to restore muscle strength after an acute myositis flare. The patient engaged in a supervised physiotherapy program beginning with passive range‑of‑motion exercises, progressing to resistance training as CK normalised and pain subsided. Functional outcome measures (Manual Muscle Test, 6‑Minute Walk Test) were recorded at each clinic visit to gauge response and to guide escalation of rehabilitation intensity.  

### Monitoring During Therapy  

A structured monitoring protocol was instituted to detect disease activity, drug toxicity, and infection early:  

| Parameter | Frequency | Rationale |
|-----------|------------|----------|
| Creatine kinase (CK) | Weekly during acute phase, then monthly | Direct marker of muscle inflammation; guides steroid taper |
| Liver enzymes (AST, ALT, ALP) | Bi‑weekly for first 2 months, then monthly | MMF and steroids can cause hepatotoxicity; baseline elevation noted |
| Complete blood count (CBC) | Weekly initially, then monthly | Detects MMF‑related cytopenias and PJP prophylaxis‑related cytopenias |
| Renal function (creatinine, eGFR) | Monthly | MMF dose adjustment in renal impairment |
| Immunoglobulin levels | Every 3 months while on IVIG | Ensures adequate trough IgG and avoids hyperviscosity |
| B‑cell (CD19+) count | Before each rituximab cycle | Confirms B‑cell depletion and informs re‑dosing |
| Pulmonary function tests (PFTs) | Baseline, 3 months, then every 6 months | Monitors ILD progression; informs methotrexate avoidance |
| Imaging (MRI or FDG‑PET if indicated) | As clinically indicated | Detects new muscle involvement or occult malignancy |
| Infection surveillance (clinical review, chest radiograph if symptomatic) | At each visit | Early detection of opportunistic infections, especially in the setting of rituximab and MMF |

The integration of biochemical, functional, and imaging data allowed the team to titrate therapy safely, achieve complete resolution of the cervical edema, restore muscle strength, and wean off glucocorticoids within seven months. This systematic, evidence‑based therapeutic strategy underscores the importance of aggressive early immunosuppression, judicious selection of steroid‑sparing agents tailored to individual organ involvement, and vigilant prophylaxis and monitoring in severe dermatomyositis presenting with pseudo‑angioedema.

## Outcome, Follow‑up, and Clinical Lessons  

The patient’s clinical course illustrates the rapid reversibility of inflammatory‑mediated neck edema once appropriate immunosuppression is instituted, as well as the importance of vigilant, long‑term surveillance for both residual organ involvement and occult malignancy in dermatomyositis.

### Short‑term response  
Within the first three weeks of hospitalisation, the high‑dose intravenous methylprednisolone regimen, followed by a prednisone taper, produced a striking reduction in the diffuse cervical soft‑tissue swelling seen on physical examination and on repeat imaging. Concomitantly, serum creatine kinase (CK) fell from a peak of 16 184 U/L to within the normal range (37 U/L) after 3 weeks, reflecting swift quenching of muscle necrosis. Hip‑flexor strength, which had been 4/5 on presentation, improved to full (5/5) by the end of the admission, allowing the patient to ambulate without assistance.  

Despite these improvements, the severe pharyngeal dysphagia documented on the barium swallow persisted. Oropharyngeal weakness and impaired coordination of the swallowing muscles required nasogastric (NG) tube feeding for an additional two months. This prolonged need for enteral support underscores that, in dermatomyositis, dysphagia may lag behind the biochemical and muscular response to therapy and often dictates the duration of hospitalization and the timing of safe oral intake.

### Long‑term outcome  
At the 2‑month outpatient review, the patient’s cervical edema had largely resolved, leaving only a faint violaceous erythema over the face and peri‑ungual telangiectasias—findings that are typical cutaneous remnants of dermatomyositis. Muscle strength remained normalized, and CK stayed suppressed on serial testing. By seven months, the prednisone taper was completed without disease flare, and the NG tube had been removed at 3 months when swallowing function recovered sufficiently for soft solids.  

Pulmonary function testing performed at 12 months demonstrated a persistent mild restrictive pattern (reduced forced vital capacity with normal or near‑normal diffusion capacity). In the context of normal chest imaging and the absence of progressive interstitial lung disease, this abnormality is most plausibly attributable to residual baseline physiology or deconditioning rather than active myositis. Serial testing at 12 months showed only a modest improvement, reinforcing the need for ongoing respiratory monitoring in patients with prior aspiration‑related lung injury.

### Imaging follow‑up  
A high‑resolution CT of the chest obtained at 11 months revealed near‑complete resolution of the basal ground‑glass opacities and areas of consolidation that had originally been interpreted as aspiration pneumonitis. There was no emergence of fibrotic changes or new nodules, supporting the notion that the inflammatory lung process was secondary to micro‑aspiration from dysphagia and responded well to the combined immunosuppressive and rehabilitative measures.

### Malignancy surveillance strategy  
Given the established association between dermatomyositis and malignancy, the patient was stratified according to the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies (IMACS) cancer‑screening guidelines. He possessed two high‑risk features (dermatomyositis and dysphagia) and two intermediate‑risk features (anti‑SAE1 seropositivity and male sex), classifying him as high‑risk for an occult neoplasm. Consequently, a comprehensive baseline work‑up—including whole‑body PET‑CT, contrast‑enhanced chest, abdomen, and pelvis CT, and tumor marker panel—was performed and yielded no evidence of malignancy.  

Ongoing surveillance follows a symptom‑driven algorithm:  
* **Every 3–6 months:** clinical assessment, complete blood count, comprehensive metabolic panel, CK, and repeat ANA/ myositis panel if clinically indicated.  
* **Annually:** chest CT (or low‑dose CT if radiation exposure is a concern) and repeat PET‑CT if new systemic symptoms arise.  
* **At 12 months:** upper (gastroscopy) and lower (colonoscopy) gastrointestinal endoscopy to exclude occult gastrointestinal cancers, as recommended for high‑risk dermatomyositis patients.  

The plan emphasizes that imaging and invasive investigations are to be repeated only when new clinical clues emerge, minimizing unnecessary radiation and procedural risk while maintaining vigilance for a malignancy that could develop months after disease onset.

### Key educational points  

1. **Edema as a myositis manifestation** – Neck or facial swelling can be an atypical presentation of inflammatory myopathy and should prompt consideration of dermatomyositis when histamine‑ or bradykinin‑mediated causes are excluded.  
2. **Early CK and skin assessment** – A single CK measurement, performed promptly in the emergency setting, can differentiate inflammatory edema from true angioedema. Likewise, a meticulous skin and nailfold examination may reveal subtle heliotrope‑like erythema, peri‑ungual changes, or the “holster sign,” steering the diagnostic pathway toward myositis.  
3. **Anti‑SAE1 serology** – Anti‑SAE1 antibodies are strongly associated with cutaneous disease and dysphagia, but they do not preclude the development of subcutaneous edema; clinicians should therefore not rely on serotype to predict the absence of this manifestation.  
4. **Multimodal immunosuppression** – Aggressive early therapy with high‑dose glucocorticoids, intravenous immunoglobulin, and a steroid‑sparing agent (mycophenolate mofetil) combined with rituximab can induce rapid remission even in severe presentations featuring airway‑compromising edema and dysphagia.  
5. **Mandatory malignancy work‑up** – High‑risk serologic and clinical features necessitate a systematic cancer screening protocol at diagnosis and structured long‑term follow‑up, as the risk of paraneoplastic disease persists beyond the acute phase.

### Future directions  
Emerging targeted therapies—most notably Janus‑kinase (JAK) inhibitors and antagonists of the type I interferon pathway (e.g., anti‑IFN‑α/β monoclonal antibodies)—have shown promise in refractory inflammatory myopathies by attenuating the downstream cytokine cascade implicated in muscle and skin inflammation. Small case series suggest that JAK inhibition may reduce CK levels and improve cutaneous disease more rapidly than conventional agents. Although data specific to anti‑SAE1‑positive dermatomyositis remain scarce, incorporation of these agents into future treatment algorithms could be considered for patients who fail to achieve remission with standard glucocorticoid‑plus‑steroid‑sparing regimens. Ongoing clinical trials will clarify their safety profile and optimal positioning relative to established therapies such as IVIG and rituximab.  

In summary, this case underscores that atypical cervical edema can herald a systemic inflammatory myopathy. Prompt CK testing, careful cutaneous appraisal, and early, aggressive immunosuppression facilitate rapid recovery, while systematic malignancy surveillance remains a cornerstone of long‑term management.
