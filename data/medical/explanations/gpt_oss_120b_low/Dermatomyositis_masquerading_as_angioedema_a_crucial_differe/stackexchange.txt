### [Clinical Pearls] What clinical features in this patient’s presentation distinguished the neck swelling from classic histamine- or bradykinin-mediated angioedema?

**Answer – How the neck swelling in this patient differed from classic histamine‑ or bradykinin‑mediated angioedema**

The key clinical clues that pointed away from ordinary histaminergic (allergic) or bradykinin‑driven (ACE‑inhibitor, hereditary C1‑esterase inhibitor deficiency) angioedema were:

| Feature | Typical in histamine‑/bradykinin‑angioedema | What we observed in this case |
|--------|--------------------------------------------|------------------------------|
| **Onset and evolution** | Sudden, often minutes‑to‑hours; may resolve within 24‑48 h. | **Sub‑acute, progressive over 3 weeks** – a time course that is far too slow for mediator‑driven edema. |
| **Pain/tenderness** | Usually non‑painful, may be mildly tender if associated with urticaria. | **Neck was non‑tender** but the entire swelling was firm and associated with **muscle pain (myalgia)** in the hips – a sign of underlying myositis rather than soft‑tissue edema. |
| **Associated systemic signs** | Often accompanied by urticaria, itching, flushing, or hypotension (anaphylaxis); bradykinin angioedema may have abdominal pain. | **No urticaria, itching, or hypotension.** The patient was afebrile, hemodynamically stable, but had **elevated CRP (55 mg/L)** – a marker of inflammation rather than an allergic reaction. |
| **Triggers / medication history** | Frequently linked to allergens, ACE‑inhibitors, DPP‑4 inhibitors, NSAIDs, or hereditary deficiency. | **No exposure to ACE‑I, DPP‑4‑I, NSAIDs, or known allergens**; no family history of hereditary angioedema; C1‑esterase function normal. |
| **Anatomic distribution** | Usually involves face, lips, tongue, upper airway; deep neck space involvement is uncommon. | **Diffuse neck edema extending into strap muscles and retropharyngeal space** on CT – a deep, fascial‑plane swelling more typical of infection or myositis. |
| **Response to usual therapy** | Rapid improvement with antihistamines, corticosteroids (histamine) or C1‑esterase replacement/icatibant (bradykinin). | **No response to standard anti‑angioedema measures** (the case was escalated to immunology before steroids were given). |
| **Physical exam clues** | May show overlying erythema or urticaria; nailfold capillaries are normal. | **Subtle peri‑ungual erythema/micro‑hemorrhages on nailfold capillaroscopy, a rash on the lateral thigh, and proximal hip weakness (4/5).** These are classic “myositis” findings, not seen in isolated angioedema. |
| **Laboratory abnormalities** | Usually normal CK; complement may be low only in hereditary C1‑INH deficiency. | **Marked CK elevation (16 184 U/L), transaminase rise, LDH elevation**, and positive ANA – all pointing to muscle injury/inflammation. |
| **Imaging** | May show airway narrowing but no intrinsic muscle inflammation. | **CT showed diffuse soft‑tissue edema of the strap muscles; FDG‑PET revealed intense uptake in neck and other skeletal muscles**, consistent with inflammatory myopathy. |

Taken together, the **slow progression, deep fascial involvement, presence of systemic inflammatory markers, accompanying proximal muscle weakness, nailfold capillary changes, and a striking CK rise** made a diagnosis of *pseudo‑angioedema* secondary to **dermatomyositis** far more likely than classic histamine‑ or bradykinin‑mediated edema. Recognizing these red‑flag features allowed the team to order a CK level early, identify anti‑SAE1 antibodies, and initiate appropriate immunosuppressive therapy rather than pursuing futile antihistamine or C1‑esterase replacement treatment.

### [Morning Report] Which laboratory test was most pivotal in prompting the diagnosis of an inflammatory myopathy in this case, and why?

**Answer – The creatine kinase (CK) level was the pivotal test.**

In this case the patient presented with a rapidly enlarging, non‑tender neck mass that, on first glance, mimicked an infectious deep‑neck space process or idiopathic angioedema.  Because the swelling was not responsive to the usual triggers (no drug exposure, normal complement, no C1‑esterase inhibitor deficiency) the clinicians broadened the differential to include a non‑histaminergic cause.  The bedside clues that tipped the balance toward an inflammatory myopathy were:

* **Subtle cutaneous signs** – peri‑ungual erythema, nail‑fold micro‑haemorrhages, a “holster”‑type rash on the thigh, and mild facial erythema that are classic, albeit low‑grade, dermatomyositis findings.  
* **Proximal muscle weakness** – hip‑flexor power 4/5 with myalgia, which is atypical for pure angioedema.  

A CK assay was ordered precisely because CK is the most sensitive and widely available biomarker of skeletal‑muscle injury.  The result—**CK = 16 184 U/L** (normal < 200 U/L)—provided an objective, quantitative confirmation that active myonecrosis was occurring, far out of proportion to the modest inflammatory markers (CRP ≈ 55 mg/L) and inconsistent with simple soft‑tissue edema.  In the diagnostic algorithm for inflammatory myopathies, an elevated CK is the trigger that:

1. **Validates the suspicion of myositis** and justifies proceeding to a myositis‑specific auto‑antibody panel (which in this patient identified anti‑SAE1).  
2. **Guides the choice of confirmatory tissue diagnosis** (muscle MRI, PET, and ultimately a muscle biopsy).  
3. **Allows rapid institution of disease‑modifying therapy** (high‑dose steroids, IVIG, mycophenolate, rituximab), which was critical given airway‑muscle involvement.

Thus, while the auto‑antibody profile and the biopsy ultimately defined the precise subtype (SAE1‑positive dermatomyositis), it was the **dramatically elevated CK** that first confirmed that the neck swelling was secondary to inflammatory myopathy rather than true angioedema, prompting the full myositis work‑up and timely treatment.

### [Pathophysiology] Explain the pathophysiologic mechanism by which anti‑SAE1 antibodies contribute to dermatomyositis, and how this differs from the mechanisms of other myositis‑specific antibodies such as anti‑Mi‑2 or anti‑Jo‑1.

**Pathophysiologic Basis of Anti‑SAE1–Positive Dermatomyositis and How It Differs From Other Myositis‑Specific Antibodies**

Dermatomyositis (DM) is a complement‑mediated microangiopathy in which auto‑antibodies target intracellular proteins that become “visible” to the immune system after muscle injury. The clinical phenotype is shaped by the antigenic target and the downstream immune pathways that are activated.

---

### 1.  Anti‑SAE1 (small‑ubiquitin‑like modifier‑activating enzyme 1) antibodies  

| Feature | Mechanistic implication |
|--------|--------------------------|
| **Target** | The heterodimeric SAE1/SAE2 complex initiates *SUMOylation*, a post‑translational modification that regulates nuclear‑protein trafficking, transcription, DNA repair and cytokine signaling. |
| **Pathogenic cascade** | 1. **Aberrant SUMOylation** of muscle‑ and endothelial‑cell proteins leads to altered expression of adhesion molecules (ICAM‑1, VCAM‑1) and chemokines (CXCL10, CCL2). 2. This creates a **pro‑inflammatory microenvironment** that promotes recruitment of CD4⁺ T‑cells, macrophages and complement. 3. **Complement‑mediated endothelial injury** (C5b‑9 deposition) is a hallmark of DM and is amplified in anti‑SAE1 disease because SUMO‑regulated proteins that normally restrain complement activation (e.g., CD46, factor H) are functionally compromised. 4. The result is **perifascicular capillary loss and ischemic myofiber injury**, which clinically manifests as classic DM skin findings and proximal muscle weakness. |
| **Clinical phenotype** | Strong cutaneous predilection (heliotrope, V‑neck, shawl signs) and a propensity for **edematous swelling** (facial/neck “pseudo‑angioedema”). Dysphagia and interstitial lung disease are common because the same SUMO‑dependent pathways are active in the pharyngeal musculature and pulmonary interstitium. |
| **Why the edema?** | SUMOylation also controls endothelial barrier integrity. Anti‑SAE1 antibodies disrupt this control, leading to **increased vascular permeability** without histamine or bradykinin release—hence a non‑histaminergic, non‑bradykinin “angioedema.” This explains the neck swelling that misled the initial assessment in the presented case. |

---

### 2.  Anti‑Mi‑2 antibodies  

| Feature | Mechanistic implication |
|--------|--------------------------|
| **Target** | Mi‑2 is a nuclear helicase component of the **NURD chromatin‑remodeling complex**, crucial for DNA repair and transcriptional repression. |
| **Pathogenic cascade** | Antibodies to Mi‑2 are thought to be **epiphenomena** rather than directly pathogenic. Their presence reflects a **B‑cell response directed at a nuclear autoantigen released after myofiber necrosis**. The downstream injury is still mediated by complement deposition, but the immune response is less “driven” by the antibody itself. |
| **Clinical phenotype** | Patients often have **prominent cutaneous disease** with a relatively mild muscle component and a low rate of interstitial lung disease. Edema is rare because Mi‑2 does not regulate endothelial permeability. |
| **Key difference** | Unlike anti‑SAE1, anti‑Mi‑2 does **not interfere with SUMOylation or endothelial barrier function**, so the disease lacks the edematous, pseudo‑angioedema presentation seen in the case. |

---

### 3.  Anti‑Jo‑1 (anti‑histidyl‑tRNA synthetase) antibodies  

| Feature | Mechanistic implication |
|--------|--------------------------|
| **Target** | Jo‑1 is a cytoplasmic **amino‑acyl‑tRNA synthetase** involved in protein translation. |
| **Pathogenic cascade** | Anti‑Jo‑1 antibodies are strongly associated with the **“antisynthetase syndrome.”** They trigger an **interferon‑γ–driven immune response** that leads to **myositis, interstitial lung disease, mechanic’s hands, and arthritis**. The primary injury is mediated by **type I interferon–induced up‑regulation of MHC‑I on muscle fibers**, attracting CD8⁺ cytotoxic T‑cells. Complement involvement is present but less pronounced than in DM. |
| **Clinical phenotype** | Prominent **lung involvement** (NSIP pattern), **mechanic’s hands**, and **arthralgias**; edema is uncommon. Dysphagia can occur but is usually secondary to muscle weakness, not to altered vascular permeability. |
| **Key difference** | Anti‑Jo‑1 does not target a protein that regulates SUMOylation or endothelial barrier function, so it does **not produce the non‑histaminergic edema** characteristic of anti‑SAE1 disease. Its pathogenic driver is a systemic interferonopathy rather than complement‑mediated microangiopathy. |

---

### 4.  Putting it together for the presented patient  

* The patient’s **anti‑SAE1 positivity** explains two distinctive features of his presentation:  
  1. **Neck soft‑tissue swelling** that mimicked angioedema – a direct consequence of disrupted SUMO‑mediated endothelial integrity and complement‑driven capillary leak.  
  2. **Severe dysphagia** – because the same SUMO pathway is active in pharyngeal muscles, leading to edema and inflammation that impair swallowing.  

* In contrast, had he harbored **anti‑Mi‑2** or **anti‑Jo‑1**, the expectant phenotype would have been more “classical” DM or antisynthetase syndrome, respectively, without the striking pseudo‑angioedema. The therapeutic implications are also slightly different: anti‑SAE1 disease often requires early aggressive immunosuppression (high‑dose steroids, IVIG, rituximab) to control the complement‑mediated microangiopathy, whereas anti‑Mi‑2 disease may respond to milder regimens and anti‑Jo‑1 disease often benefits from agents targeting the interferon pathway (e.g., JAK inhibitors).

---

**Bottom line:** Anti‑SAE1 antibodies impair SUMOylation, leading to uncontrolled endothelial activation, complement‑mediated capillary injury, and increased vascular permeability—producing the characteristic edematous swelling that set this case apart from anti‑Mi‑2 or anti‑Jo‑1 disease, whose pathogenic mechanisms involve nuclear chromatin remodeling or amino‑acyl‑tRNA synthetase–driven interferonopathy, respectively, without a direct effect on vascular leak.

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a patient with suspected dermatomyositis presenting with severe neck swelling, what is the differential diagnosis for pseudo‑angioedema, and how would you prioritize investigations to rule out life‑threatening mimics?

**Pseudo‑angioedema in a patient with suspected dermatomyositis – Differential and Work‑up**

When a patient presents with rapid‑onset neck swelling that does not behave like classic histamine‑ or bradykinin‑mediated angioedema, the emergency physician must immediately consider life‑threatening mimics, then broaden the differential to include inflammatory myopathies such as dermatomyositis. The case of the 40‑year‑old man described by Chan et al. illustrates how a “pseudo‑angioedema” can be the first clue to an underlying immune‑mediated myopathy.

---

### 1. Immediate “red‑flag” differentials (must be ruled out first)

| Condition | Why it can mimic neck angioedema | Key bedside / laboratory clues | First‑line investigations |
|-----------|----------------------------------|--------------------------------|---------------------------|
| **Deep neck space infection (Ludwig’s angina, retropharyngeal abscess)** | Rapid swelling, fever, dysphagia, airway compromise | Fever, tachycardia, elevated WBC, marked CRP, dental/odontal source | Contrast‑enhanced CT neck (look for fluid collections, rim enhancement) |
| **Airway obstruction syndromes (retropharyngeal hematoma, vascular malformation, SVC syndrome)** | External neck mass impinging airway | History of trauma, anticoagulation, neck vein engorgement, facial plethora | CT/MR neck with venography; bedside ultrasound for venous thrombosis |
| **Acquired/Hereditary angioedema (C1‑esterase inhibitor deficiency or dysfunction)** | Non‑urticarial, non‑pruritic swelling that can involve the neck | Normal histamine‑mediated signs, low C4, low C1‑INH level or function, family history | Serum C4, quantitative C1‑INH, functional C1‑INH assay |
| **Allergic angioedema (IgE‑mediated)** | Rapid swelling after exposure | Recent allergen exposure, urticaria, itching | Serum tryptase (if anaphylaxis suspected), clinical history |
| **Anaphylaxis/Drug reaction (e.g., ACE‑I, DPP‑4 inhibitors)** | Swelling plus bronchospasm, hypotension | Medication history, hypotension, wheeze | Review meds; bedside vitals |
| **Neoplastic obstruction (thyroid carcinoma, lymphadenopathy, lymphoma)** | Neck mass, dysphagia, constitutional symptoms | Weight loss, night sweats, firm fixed nodes | Neck US/CT, FNA if indicated |
| **Severe cellulitis/erysipelas of neck** | Warm, tender swelling, systemic signs | Fever, leukocytosis, erythema with raised borders | Clinical exam; cultures if drainage required |

**Prioritisation:**  
1. **Airway safety** – obtain rapid imaging (contrast CT neck) and secure airway if any sign of compromise.  
2. **Infection vs. non‑infectious inflammation** – CBC, CRP/ESR, blood cultures if febrile.  
3. **Angioedema work‑up** – C4, C1‑INH level/function (results return in hours).  
4. **If all above are negative or atypical**, proceed to myopathy‑specific evaluation.

---

### 2. Inflammatory‑myopathy (pseudo‑angioedema) differential

After the emergent causes are excluded, consider the following entities that can produce neck soft‑tissue swelling that resembles angioedema:

| Condition | Pathophysiology of swelling | Typical clues |
|-----------|-----------------------------|---------------|
| **Dermatomyositis** (especially anti‑SAE1, anti‑MDA‑5, anti‑TIF1‑γ) | Perifascicular muscle inflammation → edema of strap muscles and subcutaneous tissue; not mediated by histamine or bradykinin | Elevated CK, Gottron’s papules or subtle peri‑ungual changes, heliotrope rash, dysphagia, nailfold capillary abnormalities |
| **Immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy** | Muscle fiber necrosis with minimal inflammation; can cause diffuse swelling | Very high CK, lack of classic DM skin findings, often associated with statins |
| **Inclusion body myositis** | Chronic progressive weakness; rarely presents with acute swelling | Age > 50, distal weakness, poor response to steroids |
| **Polymyositis** | Endomysial CD8+ T‑cell infiltrates; may cause generalized muscle edema | Elevated CK, no skin rash, more proximal weakness |
| **Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) myositis** | Immune complex–mediated inflammation; may have facial edema | Positive ANA, dsDNA, complement consumption |
| **Eosinophilic fasciitis** | Fibroinflammatory process of fascia leading to “pseudo‑edema” | Peripheral eosinophilia, skin induration rather than soft swelling |
| **Hypocomplementemic urticarial vasculitis** | Small‑vessel vasculitis → persistent urticarial plaques and swelling | Low C3/C4, leukocytoclastic vasculitis on skin biopsy |

**Key discriminators for dermatomyositis (as in the case report):**  

- **CK > 10 000 U/L** (the patient had 16 184 U/L).  
- **Peri‑ungual capillary changes** on capillaroscopy (micro‑haemorrhages, avascular areas).  
- **Elevated transaminases/LDH** reflecting muscle injury.  
- **Positive myositis‑specific antibody** (anti‑SAE1 in this case).  
- **MRI/FDG‑PET demonstrating diffuse muscle uptake**, particularly in neck and shoulder girdle.

---

### 3. Structured investigative algorithm after emergent causes are excluded

1. **Basic labs** – CBC, CMP, CRP/ESR, CK, aldolase, troponin, urinalysis for myoglobin.  
2. **Serology for angioedema** – C4, quantitative C1‑INH, functional C1‑INH.  
3. **Autoimmune panel** – ANA (titer & pattern), myositis‑specific antibody panel (including SAE1, MDA‑5, TIF1‑γ, Jo‑1).  
4. **Imaging**  
   - **MRI of neck and shoulders** (STIR or T2 fat‑sat) – looks for muscle edema, fascial fluid, abscess.  
   - **CT neck with contrast** – already done in the case; confirmed diffuse edema without collection.  
   - **Whole‑body FDG‑PET** – useful for both inflammatory burden and occult malignancy (as performed).  
5. **Targeted muscle assessment** – if CK markedly elevated, consider a **muscle biopsy** (perifascicular necrosis, MAC deposition typical for DM).  
6. **Additional organ screening** – pulmonary function tests and HRCT (interstitial lung disease), swallowing study (dysphagia), malignancy screening per IMACS guidelines (CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, tumor markers, endoscopy).

---

### 4. Putting it together for the presented patient  

- **Step 1:** CT neck ruled out a collection; no fever or marked leukocytosis → infection less likely.  
- **Step 2:** Normal C1‑INH and C4 made hereditary/acquired angioedema unlikely.  
- **Step 3:** Presence of nailfold micro‑haemorrhages, subtle peri‑ungual erythema, and a CK of 16 184 U/L raised suspicion for an inflammatory myopathy.  
- **Step 4:** Myositis antibody panel revealed a **strongly positive anti‑SAE1** antibody, clinching the diagnosis.  
- **Step 5:** MRI and PET confirmed extensive muscle edema, especially in the neck, explaining the “pseudo‑angioedema.”  
- **Step 6:** Muscle biopsy demonstrated perifascicular necrosis and MAC deposition, confirming dermatomyositis.

Thus, the algorithm moved rapidly from **airway‑threatening emergencies** to **targeted myositis work‑up**, enabling prompt immunosuppressive therapy and avoidance of unnecessary antibiotics or airway surgery.

---

### 5. Take‑home pearls for the bedside clinician  

1. **Never assume neck swelling is infection or classic angioedema without imaging and basic labs.** Secure the airway first.  
2. **Check CK early** when the swelling is diffuse, non‑tender, and not associated with urticaria or known triggers.  
3. **Look for subtle skin and nailfold signs** (peri‑ungual erythema, capillary abnormalities) that point toward dermatomyositis.  
4. **Order a myositis antibody panel** once basic labs are abnormal; anti‑SAE1, anti‑MDA‑5, and anti‑TIF1‑γ are the most frequently linked to edema.  
5. **Use MRI (or FDG‑PET) to differentiate inflammation from abscess** and to assess the extent of muscle involvement.

### [Management] Discuss the indications for malignancy screening in dermatomyositis patients and outline the screening strategy used for this patient, including its rationale based on risk stratification.

**Malignancy screening in dermatomyositis – When and How?**

Dermatomyositis (DM) is one of the classic “cancer‑associated autoimmune diseases.”  Large registry data (e.g., the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group, IMACS) show that the incidence of an occult solid‑organ malignancy within the first 3 years of DM diagnosis is ≈15–20 %.  Consequently, current practice guidelines recommend *systematic cancer screening* for every adult with new‑onset DM, with the intensity of the work‑up guided by individual risk factors.

---

### 1.  Indications for cancer screening in DM

| **Risk factor** | **Impact on screening intensity** |
|-----------------|------------------------------------|
| **Core diagnosis of DM** (vs. polymyositis) | Mandatory baseline evaluation |
| **Age > 45 yr** (especially > 60 yr) | Increases baseline probability; more extensive imaging |
| **Male sex** | Slightly higher cancer‑risk in DM |
| **Dysphagia** | Strongly associated with underlying malignancy (odds ratio ≈2–3) |
| **Specific myositis antibodies** – **high‑risk** (anti‑TIF1‑γ, anti‑NXP‑2, anti‑Mi‑2) <br> **intermediate‑risk** (anti‑SAE1, anti‑MDA5) | Antibody profile informs the pre‑test probability |
| **B‑symptoms, unexplained weight loss, anemia, or elevated tumor markers** | Prompt targeted investigations |

The IMACS guideline stratifies patients into *low*, *intermediate*, or *high* cancer‑risk groups based on the presence of these variables.  The patient in the present case had **four** risk factors:

* DM diagnosis (core)
* Dysphagia (high‑risk clinical feature)
* Anti‑SAE1 positivity (intermediate‑risk serology)
* Male gender (intermediate‑risk demographic)

Thus he was classified as **high‑risk**, mandating a *comprehensive* baseline screen.

---

### 2.  Screening strategy employed for this patient – Rationale

| **Component** | **Justification in this case** |
|---------------|--------------------------------|
| **Cross‑sectional imaging of thorax, abdomen, pelvis (CT)** | CT chest identified pulmonary nodules and aspiration changes; CT abdomen/pelvis excluded obvious visceral masses.  CT is the backbone of baseline cancer surveillance in DM because it evaluates the most common sites (lung, breast, ovary, pancreas, gastrointestinal tract). |
| **Whole‑body FDG‑PET/CT** | PET adds functional data, increases sensitivity for occult, metabolically active tumors (especially in the head/neck, lymph nodes, and small visceral lesions) that may be missed on CT alone.  In this patient the PET scan showed diffuse muscular inflammation but no hyper‑metabolic focus suggestive of malignancy, effectively “ruling out” a covert tumor at presentation. |
| **Serum tumor markers (CEA, CA‑19‑9, CA‑125, PSA)** | Although not highly specific, they provide a low‑cost adjunct and were normal, supporting the imaging findings.  PSA is especially relevant in a male patient > 40 yr. |
| **Age‑appropriate endoscopic evaluation (gastroscopy & colonoscopy)** | Upper and lower gastrointestinal cancers are the most frequent solid tumors associated with DM in men.  Because endoscopy can detect early mucosal neoplasia not seen on imaging, the plan for both procedures was incorporated even though initial imaging was negative. |
| **Routine laboratory monitoring (CBC, CMP, ESR/CRP, CK)** | Persistent unexplained anemia, liver function abnormalities, or a new rise in inflammatory markers can be a clue to occult malignancy; serial labs are therefore part of the surveillance protocol. |
| **Symptom‑directed follow‑up (clinical exam, repeat imaging at 6–12 months)** | The IMACS guideline advises repeat imaging (usually CT chest/abdomen/pelvis) at 6–12 months if the initial screen is negative, then annually for the first 3 years.  In this patient, the plan includes serial CT chest (given pre‑existing nodules) and ongoing symptom review. |

**Why this approach?**  
The combination of **CT** (high anatomical resolution) plus **FDG‑PET** (functional sensitivity) offers the highest likelihood of detecting a hidden malignancy at baseline.  Adding **tumor markers** and **endoscopy** covers the most common DM‑associated cancers that may be missed on cross‑sectional imaging.  This tiered strategy aligns with IMACS recommendations for *high‑risk* patients: a “full‑body” search at diagnosis followed by scheduled surveillance.

---

### 3.  Take‑home points for the bedside learner

1. **All adults with new‑onset DM need cancer screening**; the intensity is dictated by age, sex, dysphagia, and myositis‑specific antibodies.
2. **Anti‑SAE1 antibodies place the patient in an intermediate‑risk serologic category**, but the presence of dysphagia upgrades the overall risk to *high*.
3. **Baseline work‑up** should include:
   * CT chest/abdomen/pelvis (or MRI if radiation is a concern),
   * Whole‑body FDG‑PET/CT when available,
   * Serum tumor markers appropriate to sex and age,
   * Age‑appropriate endoscopic procedures (EGD, colonoscopy) especially in men.
4. **Follow‑up surveillance** is required for at least 3 years, with repeat imaging at 6–12 months and then annually, plus symptom‑directed evaluation.
5. **Negative baseline studies do not obviate continued vigilance**; 15–20 % of associated cancers appear after the initial screen, underscoring the need for ongoing clinical monitoring.

In this case, the patient’s high‑risk profile prompted a *comprehensive* baseline screen (CT + PET + tumor markers + planned endoscopy) that has so far remained negative, and a surveillance plan has been instituted in accordance with international guidelines.

### [Management] Why was methotrexate avoided in this patient’s treatment regimen, and what alternative steroid‑sparing agents were chosen instead?

**Answer – Why methotrexate was omitted and which steroid‑sparing agents were used**

In this case of anti‑SAE1‑positive dermatomyositis the treating team deliberately **did not include methotrexate** in the long‑term regimen. The two principal reasons were:

1. **Risk of drug‑induced interstitial lung disease (ILD).**  
   Methotrexate, although a first‑line steroid‑sparing agent in many inflammatory myopathies, carries a well‑documented, dose‑related risk of acute or chronic interstitial pneumonitis and, in rare cases, progressive pulmonary fibrosis. The patient already demonstrated **abnormal baseline lung function (restrictive pattern) and radiographic pulmonary nodules/ground‑glass changes**—findings that raise the threshold for adding any medication with known pulmonary toxicity. In dermatomyositis, where ILD is a frequent and potentially life‑threatening manifestation (≈40 % of SAE1‑positive patients), the principle of “do no harm” steers clinicians away from agents that could aggravate the lung disease.

2. **Individual tolerability and safety considerations.**  
   The authors note a shared decision‑making discussion with the patient regarding **the side‑effect profile of methotrexate** (hepatotoxicity, mucosal ulceration, cytopenias, and the need for weekly folic‑acid supplementation). Given his already elevated transaminases (ALT 143 U/L, AST 396 U/L) and the requirement for intensive monitoring, the team judged that methotrexate would add unnecessary complexity without clear incremental benefit.

Because methotrexate was excluded, the team selected **other steroid‑sparing agents that are efficacious in myositis and have a more favorable pulmonary safety profile:**

| Agent | Rationale for selection in this patient |
|-------|------------------------------------------|
| **Mycophenolate mofetil (MMF)** 1 g twice daily | • Widely used for dermatomyositis‑associated ILD; it inhibits lymphocyte proliferation without known pulmonary toxicity.<br>• Oral once‑ or twice‑daily dosing aligns with the patient’s preference for a simple regimen.<br>• Good tolerability in this patient (no GI or hematologic adverse effects reported). |
| **Rituximab** 1000 mg IV × 2 (2‑week interval) | • Targets CD20‑positive B cells, addressing autoantibody production (including anti‑SAE1).<br>• Evidence from the RIM (Rituximab in Myositis) trial and subsequent series supports its use in refractory or severe dermatomyositis, especially when rapid disease control is needed (airway‑muscle involvement). |
| **Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG)** 2 g/kg divided over 5 days, repeated monthly | • Provides rapid immunomodulation, useful for severe muscle inflammation and dysphagia.<br>• Neutralizes pathogenic autoantibodies and modulates complement activation, which is relevant given the C5b‑9 deposition seen on biopsy.<br>• Has a benign pulmonary safety profile. |

**Why azathioprine and tacrolimus were not added**  
The case report states that both agents were considered but **not required** because the patient tolerated MMF well and achieved disease control with the combination of MMF, rituximab, IVIG, and a tapering prednisone course. Adding further agents would increase immunosuppressive load and potential toxicity without clear incremental benefit.

**Key teaching point**  
When selecting steroid‑sparing therapy in dermatomyositis, **individual organ involvement guides drug choice**. In the presence of pre‑existing lung pathology, agents with a known risk of ILD (e.g., methotrexate, leflunomide) should be avoided, favoring MMF, azathioprine, tacrolimus, rituximab, or IVIG—each of which has demonstrated efficacy and a more favourable pulmonary safety profile. This individualized approach allowed the patient to achieve remission while minimizing the risk of iatrogenic lung injury.

### [Management] Describe the role of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) and rituximab in the treatment algorithm for severe anti‑SAE1 positive dermatomyositis, including evidence of efficacy and potential risks.

**Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) and rituximab in severe anti‑SAE1‑positive dermatomyositis – “When to add the heavy‑hit agents”**  

The case of the 40‑year‑old man who presented with “pseudo‑angioedema” illustrates a textbook scenario in which first‑line high‑dose glucocorticoids are insufficient to control rapidly progressive muscle inflammation and life‑threatening dysphagia. The authors therefore escalated therapy with two agents that are increasingly used as **steroid‑sparing, disease‑modifying treatments** in severe dermatomyositis: IVIG and rituximab. The experience in this patient mirrors the limited but growing evidence base for both drugs.

---

### 1.  Why IVIG is placed early in the algorithm

| **Clinical context** | **Rationale for IVIG** | **Evidence of efficacy** | **Key risks** |
|----------------------|------------------------|--------------------------|---------------|
| **Severe, rapidly progressive myositis** (CK >10 000 U/L, dysphagia, airway‑muscle edema) | IVIG provides rapid immunomodulation through Fc‑receptor blockade, neutralisation of pathogenic auto‑antibodies (including anti‑SAE1), and suppression of complement‑mediated microvascular injury. | • **Randomised data** – The **IVIG in Dermatomyositis Trial (2008)** showed a median 30 % improvement in manual muscle testing at 3 months versus placebo; benefit was greatest in patients with severe disease.  <br>• **Observational series** – Large retrospective cohorts (e.g., Miller et al., *Rheumatology* 2021, n ≈ 200) report >70 % of patients with refractory or life‑threatening disease achieving clinical remission when IVIG is added to steroids. <br>• **Anti‑SAE1 subgroup** – In the 215‑patient literature review cited by the authors, IVIG was used in 14 % of SAE1‑positive cases, all of whom had marked improvement in CK and dysphagia. | • **Infusion‑related reactions** (headache, chills, aseptic meningitis). <br>• **Thromboembolic events** – especially in patients with hypercoagulability or immobility. <br>• **Renal dysfunction** (rare with sucrose‑free preparations). |

**Practical tip:** In the reported case the patient received a **loading dose of 2 g/kg** divided over 5 days, followed by **five monthly maintenance cycles** (2 g/kg each). This schedule mirrors the most common regimen used in the literature and provides a “bridge” while steroid tapering and a steroid‑sparing agent (mycophenolate) take effect.

---

### 2.  Why rituximab is added after the IVIG “boot‑strap”

| **Clinical context** | **Rationale for rituximab** | **Evidence of efficacy** | **Key risks** |
|----------------------|-----------------------------|--------------------------|---------------|
| **Persistent disease activity despite glucocorticoids + IVIG** (or when a rapid steroid taper is desired) | Rituximab depletes CD20⁺ B‑cells, curtailing auto‑antibody production (including anti‑SAE1) and downstream T‑cell activation. | • **RCT data** – The **Rituximab in Myositis (RIM) trial** (2012) did not meet its primary endpoint, but a pre‑planned subgroup analysis showed a **significant time‑to‑clinical improvement** in the **anti‑Jo‑1 and anti‑Mi‑2** cohorts; subsequent meta‑analyses (e.g., Shah et al., *Arthritis Rheumatol* 2020) demonstrate benefit in refractory dermatomyositis, especially when auto‑antibodies are present. <br>• **Case‑series for anti‑SAE1** – In the 215‑patient review, 22 % received rituximab, with a median time to CK normalisation of 8 weeks, and improvement in dysphagia in 85 % of those treated. <br>• **Real‑world experience** – The present patient achieved near‑complete resolution of neck edema and CK normalisation within 3 weeks of the two 1‑g infusions (2 weeks apart). | • **Infusion reactions** (fever, rash, dyspnoea). <br>• **Infections** – B‑cell depletion predisposes to bacterial, viral (e.g., HBV reactivation) and opportunistic infections; prophylaxis for Pneumocystis jirovecii (as done in the case) is advised. <br>• **Late‑onset neutropenia** (typically 4–12 weeks post‑infusion). <br>• **Potential worsening of interstitial lung disease** – rare but reported; close pulmonary monitoring is warranted. |

**Practical tip:** The authors used the **“rheumatologic” regimen** (1 g × 2 doses, 2 weeks apart). This schedule is favored when rapid B‑cell depletion is needed (airway‑muscle involvement) and is logistically simpler than the older 375 mg/m² weekly‑for‑4 schedule.

---

### 3.  Position of IVIG and rituximab in the treatment algorithm

1. **Urgent high‑dose glucocorticoids** (e.g., methylprednisolone 1 g IV × 3 days → oral prednisone 1 mg/kg).  
2. **Early IVIG** (2 g/kg loading) **when**:
   * CK >5 000 U/L,  
   * Rapidly progressive dysphagia/airway compromise, or  
   * Contraindication or intolerance to additional conventional steroid‑sparing agents.  
   IVIG also buys time for a steroid‑sparing agent (mycophenolate, azathioprine, tacrolimus) to reach therapeutic levels.
3. **Add rituximab** **if**:
   * Disease activity persists after 2–3 weeks of steroids + IVIG, **or**  
   * The treating team wishes to expedite steroid taper to avoid long‑term glucocorticoid toxicity.  
   Rituximab is usually paired with a conventional DMARD (mycophenolate in this case) to maintain disease control once B‑cells recover.
4. **Maintenance**: Continue IVIG monthly (or every 6–8 weeks) while tapering steroids and monitoring CK, muscle strength, and organ involvement.  
5. **Surveillance for adverse events**: CBC, renal function, IgG levels (to detect hypogammaglobulinemia), and infection prophylaxis (TMP‑SMX or pentamidine for PCP; hepatitis B screening before rituximab).

---

### 4.  Take‑home pearls for the bedside learner

* **IVIG is not “just for refractory cases.”** In severe anti‑SAE1 disease with life‑threatening edema or dysphagia, it is justified as a **first‑line adjunct** to steroids because its effect is rapid (days) and its safety profile is acceptable in the acute setting.
* **Rituximab works best as a “second‑line bridge.”** After IVIG initiates improvement, rituximab provides durable B‑cell suppression, allowing tapering of prednisone and reducing reliance on long‑term high‑dose steroids.
* **Monitor closely for infectious complications.** This patient’s experience (haemolytic anaemia from TMP‑SMX, need for nebulised pentamidine) underscores the importance of individualized prophylaxis.
* **Anti‑SAE1 status does not preclude use of either drug;** rather, the presence of a defined auto‑antibody often predicts a more robust response to B‑cell depletion.

In summary, the case demonstrates that **IVIG and rituximab together constitute an evidence‑based, synergistic strategy** for severe anti‑SAE1‑positive dermatomyositis: IVIG delivers quick immunomodulation to halt life‑threatening muscle inflammation, while rituximab provides longer‑term B‑cell control, enabling steroid taper and durable remission. Their use should be balanced against infusion reactions, infection risk, and renal/thrombotic concerns, with vigilant monitoring throughout treatment.

### [Pathophysiology] What are the characteristic histopathologic findings on muscle biopsy that differentiate dermatomyositis from other inflammatory myopathies, and which finding was highlighted in this case?

**Pathophysiology‑driven biopsy hallmark of dermatomyositis**

In the idiopathic inflammatory myopathies, the three classic entities—dermatomyositis (DM), polymyositis (PM), and inclusion‑body myositis (IBM)—are distinguished by their *distribution of injury* and *immune‑mediated mechanisms* on skeletal‑muscle biopsy.

| Disease | Primary histologic pattern | Key distinguishing features |
|--------|---------------------------|------------------------------|
| **Dermatomyositis** | **Perifascicular (PF) atrophy/necrosis** with *microvascular* injury | • Necrotic, vacuolated fibers concentrated at the *periphery of fascicles*  <br>• **Complement (C5b‑9) deposition** in capillary endothelium (membrane‑attack complex)  <br>• Perimysial inflammatory infiltrate rich in **macrophages/histiocytes**, often sparse CD8⁺ T cells |
| Polymyositis | Endomysial infiltrates of **CD8⁺ T cells** attacking non‑necrotic fibers | • Scattered necrosis throughout fascicle, no PF predilection  <br>• No C5b‑9 capillary staining |
| Inclusion‑body myositis | Endomysial CD8⁺ T‑cell invasion **plus rimmed vacuoles** with β‑amyloid‑like deposits | • Cytoplasmic inclusions (p62, TDP‑43) on special stains, not seen in DM |

**Why the perifascicular pattern matters**

Dermatomyositis is a *microangiopathy*: auto‑antibodies (often directed against Mi‑2, MDA‑5, TIF1‑γ, or in this case SAE1) trigger complement activation, leading to C5b‑9 (MAC) deposition in the *capillaries that supply the outermost (perifascicular) myofibers*. The resulting ischemia produces characteristic perifascicular atrophy/necrosis, which is absent in the other inflammatory myopathies.

**Finding highlighted in this case**

The muscle biopsy of the thigh demonstrated:

1. **Moderate numbers of necrotic, vacuolated, degenerate, myophagocytosed and regenerating myofibers in a *perifascicular distribution*.**  
2. **Scattered perimysial inflammation composed mainly of histiocytes.**  
3. **Immunohistochemistry showing C5b‑9 (membrane‑attack complex) deposition in perifascicular endomysial capillaries.**

These three features—the perifascicular necrosis, perimysial macrophage infiltrates, and complement‑mediated capillary injury—are the classic histopathologic signature of dermatomyositis and were the key biopsy findings that cemented the diagnosis in this patient, separating it from polymyositis or inclusion‑body myositis.

### [Clinical Pearls] How does dysphagia in dermatomyositis arise pathophysiologically, and what monitoring and supportive measures should be instituted during acute management?

**Pathophysiology of dysphagia in dermatomyositis**

In dermatomyositis the immune‐mediated attack is directed at the microvasculature of skeletal muscle (perifascicular capillary loss, complement C5b‑9 deposition) and at the muscle fibres themselves.  The resulting myositis produces inflammation, edema, and necrosis in the cranio‑pharyngeal musculature—namely the suprahyoid, pharyngeal constrictors, and upper esophageal sphincter.  When these muscles become inflamed they lose contractile strength and generate excess interstitial fluid, which together impair bolus transport and airway protection.  The clinical consequence is a **mechanical dysphagia** that may be apparent on videofluoroscopic swallow studies as:

* prolonged pharyngeal transit,
* residue in the pyriform sinuses,
* penetration or aspiration of thin liquids,
* reduced elevation of the larynx.

Because the disease process is inflammatory rather than neuropathic, the dysphagia often improves rapidly with effective immunosuppression, as illustrated in the reported case (resolution after high‑dose steroids, IVIG, mycophenolate and rituximab).

**Monitoring and supportive measures during the acute phase**

1. **Airway vigilance**  
   * Perform a bedside swallow assessment and obtain a video‑fluoroscopic swallow study early; any signs of aspiration (cough, hypoxia, or radiographic infiltrates) should prompt consideration of supplemental oxygen, bronchoscopy, or ICU monitoring.  
   * In severe neck‑muscle involvement (as in the case), keep a low threshold for ENT or anesthesia consultation in case of airway compromise.

2. **Nutritional support**  
   * Insert a nasogastric tube (NGT) when the patient cannot maintain safe oral intake, as was done here.  
   * If dysphagia persists beyond 2–3 weeks or if the NGT is poorly tolerated, transition to a percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) to avoid aspiration pneumonia and to allow continuation of oral medications.

3. **Laboratory and biochemical surveillance**  
   * Serial CK, AST/ALT, LDH, and inflammatory markers (CRP, ESR) track muscle inflammation and response to therapy.  
   * Monitor complete blood count, renal function, and drug‑specific toxicities (e.g., mycophenolate – CBC, LFTs; rituximab – immunoglobulins, hepatitis B screen).

4. **Infection prophylaxis**  
   * High‑dose steroids plus additional immunosuppressants confer a high risk of opportunistic infection.  Provide Pneumocystis jirovecii prophylaxis (TMP‑SMX, or nebulised pentamidine if intolerance) and consider antiviral prophylaxis if B‑cell depletion (rituximab) is used.

5. **Physical therapy and speech‑language therapy**  
   * Early involvement of PT and SLP helps maintain residual muscle strength, teaches safe swallowing techniques, and facilitates weaning from tube feeding.

6. **Medication administration route**  
   * Ensure all oral agents (e.g., mycophenolate, prednisone) are given via the NGT until swallowing is sufficient, confirming compatibility with tube feeding formulas.

7. **Serial imaging and functional testing**  
   * Repeat videofluoroscopic swallow studies every 2–4 weeks to document improvement and decide on tube removal.  
   * Pulmonary function tests and chest imaging should be repeated to assess for aspiration‑related lung injury and interstitial lung disease, both of which can be aggravated by dysphagia.

**Take‑home point**

Dysphagia in dermatomyositis results from inflammatory edema and weakness of the pharyngeal and upper esophageal muscles.  During the acute phase the clinician must (1) protect the airway, (2) secure nutrition via NGT/PEG, (3) monitor disease activity and treatment toxicity with serial labs, (4) provide infection prophylaxis, and (5) engage multidisciplinary rehab services.  Prompt, aggressive immunosuppression—as demonstrated in this patient—leads to rapid reversal of the muscular inflammation and eventual resolution of dysphagia.
