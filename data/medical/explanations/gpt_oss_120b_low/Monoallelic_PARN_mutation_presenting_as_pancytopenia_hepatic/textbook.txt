Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Evaluation  

The patient was a woman in her late twenties who arrived at a tertiary care centre after a five‑year history of progressively worsening fatigue and generalized weakness.  In her own words the “normal” activities of her peers—attending university, socialising, and performing household chores—had become increasingly difficult, and in the preceding three months she required assistance with basic self‑care.  Associated complaints included exertional dyspnoea, intermittent chest discomfort, unintentional weight loss, and loss of appetite.  This constellation of constitutional symptoms in a young adult immediately raised a broad differential that spanned chronic infectious, autoimmune, nutritional, haematologic, and inherited marrow‑failure syndromes.  

### Demographics and chief complaints  

- **Age/sex:** Late 20s, female.  
- **Duration:** 5 years of insidious fatigue; recent acceleration over 3 months.  
- **Key symptoms:** Profound fatigue, weakness, dyspnoea on exertion, weight loss, anorexia.  

The chronicity and the progressive nature of the fatigue, together with the relatively young age, suggested a process that was either slowly destructive (e.g., marrow failure, chronic infection) or chronically malabsorptive/nutritional.  The recent rapid decline implied a superimposed stressor or a tipping point in a pre‑existing disease.

### Physical examination: clues of premature ageing  

On exam the patient was haemodynamically stable but displayed several “age‑advanced” findings that are atypical in a 20‑year‑old:  

- **Premature greying of scalp hair** – an early sign of accelerated biological ageing seen in telomere biology disorders (TBD) and some hereditary syndromes.  
- **Knuckle hyperpigmentation** – reminiscent of the mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita (DC) (nail dystrophy, reticulate skin pigmentation, oral leukoplakia).  While our patient lacked nail dystrophy, the hyperpigmentation raised suspicion for a telomere‑related disorder.  
- **Oral findings:** a bald, atrophic tongue with loss of lingual papillae, frequently seen in severe vitamin B12 deficiency but also reported in some TBDs.  
- **Musculoskeletal signs:** thoracic scoliosis and bilateral thenar atrophy suggested chronic neuro‑muscular involvement, a feature that can accompany severe micronutrient deficiency or an underlying neurodegenerative process.  

These findings, taken together, hinted at a systemic process affecting rapidly dividing tissues (skin, hair, mucosa) and possibly the nervous system.

### Hematologic evaluation  

The initial complete blood count (CBC) revealed **pancytopenia**:  

| Parameter | Result | Reference |
|-----------|--------|-----------|
| Hemoglobin | Low | anemia |
| White‑cell count | Low | leukopenia |
| Platelet count | Low | thrombocytopenia |
| Reticulocyte count | 0.39 % (low) | impaired marrow response |

The markedly reduced reticulocyte count indicated that the anaemia was not driven by peripheral destruction but rather by inadequate marrow output—characteristic of **ineffective erythropoiesis**.  

Peripheral smear provided further insight: dimorphic red cells (both micro‑ and macro‑cytic forms), basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils.  These morphological changes are classic for **megaloblastic anaemia** secondary to vitamin B12 or folate deficiency, but they can also be seen in marrow stress from inherited bone‑marrow failure syndromes.  

A bone‑marrow aspirate/biopsy was obtained early to rule out infiltrative or malignant processes.  It showed **trilineage hematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia**, increased iron stores, and **no dysplasia, fibrosis, or blasts**.  Cytogenetics were normal.  The absence of overt dysplasia or clonal abnormalities, together with low reticulocyte output, reinforced a picture of **ineffective erythropoiesis**, likely precipitated by a nutritional deficiency superimposed on a vulnerable marrow.

### Nutritional deficiencies and their clinical correlates  

Serum assays uncovered **severe vitamin B12 deficiency** (cobalamin < 50 pg/mL) and **folate deficiency** (serum folate < 3 ng/mL).  These deficiencies explain several laboratory and clinical findings:  

- **Megaloblastic changes** on smear and marrow (hypersegmented neutrophils, macro‑ovalocytes).  
- **Neurologic involvement:** the patient’s sensorimotor polyneuropathy, thenar atrophy, and gait instability are typical of sub‑acute combined degeneration.  
- **Glossitis** (bald tongue) and **hyperpigmentation** (knuckle changes).  

Iron studies were paradoxical: **serum iron and ferritin were elevated**, consistent with iron sequestration in ineffective erythropoiesis rather than true iron overload.  The combination of a high ferritin with low reticulocytes is a classic laboratory pattern in megaloblastic anaemia.

### Organomegaly and respiratory findings  

Abdominal examination disclosed **hepatomegaly** (liver palpable 5 cm below the costal margin; span 18 cm) and splenomegaly.  Hepatosplenomegaly is a frequent accompaniment of chronic marrow stress (extramedullary haematopoiesis) and also of infiltrative or fibrotic liver disease.  

Respiratory examination revealed **reduced breath sounds in the bilateral infra‑scapular regions**, prompting further pulmonary evaluation.  Early dyspnoea out of proportion to anaemia suggested underlying parenchymal lung pathology.

### Initial laboratory panel  

Beyond the CBC, the following tests were performed to delineate the aetiology of cytopenias and organ dysfunction:  

- **Iron studies:** ↑ serum iron, ↑ ferritin, normal/low transferrin saturation – supporting ineffective erythropoiesis.  
- **Hemolysis work‑up:** Elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), indirect hyperbilirubinaemia, positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) for IgG (2+).  However, the low reticulocyte response and marrow features argued against a primary immune‑mediated hemolysis.  
- **Liver function tests (LFTs):** Mildly elevated transaminases, increased alkaline phosphatase, and indirect hyperbilirubinaemia, consistent with hepatocellular injury and cholestasis.  
- **Autoimmune serologies:** ANA, anti‑LKM, anti‑SMA—all negative, making autoimmune hepatitis unlikely.  
- **Infectious screen:** Negative for hepatitis B/C, HIV, CMV; IgG positive for EBV (past infection).  

These results narrowed the differential: the cytopenias were not driven by overt hemolysis or infection, and the liver abnormalities were not autoimmune.

### First‑line imaging  

**Abdominal ultrasound** documented hepatomegaly and splenomegaly without focal lesions.  To quantify fibrosis, a **transient elastography (FibroScan)** was performed, revealing:  

- **Liver stiffness:** 18.1 kPa (indicative of advanced fibrosis/cirrhosis).  
- **Controlled attenuation parameter (CAP):** 295 dB/m (grade II steatosis).  

These findings confirmed **significant hepatic fibrosis** in a young adult, an atypical finding that further pointed toward a systemic fibrotic process rather than simple steatohepatitis.

**Chest imaging** began with a plain radiograph that showed diffuse, faint ground‑glass opacities.  A subsequent high‑resolution **computed tomography (HRCT) of the chest** uncovered:  

- Bilateral, subpleural ground‑glass haziness with areas of reticulation and traction bronchiolectasis—features characteristic of **idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF)** or other fibrosing interstitial lung diseases.  

The coexistence of hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis, together with premature ageing signs, strongly suggested a **telomere biology disorder** rather than isolated organ disease.

### Integration of early clues  

At this point, the clinical picture comprised:  

1. **Constitutional symptoms** (fatigue, weight loss).  
2. **Premature ageing features** (early greying, hyperpigmentation, mucosal changes).  
3. **Multilineage cytopenias** with ineffective erythropoiesis.  
4. **Severe B12/folate deficiency**—explaining many hematologic and neurologic signs but insufficient to account for organ fibrosis.  
5. **Hepatosplenomegaly with advanced hepatic fibrosis**.  
6. **Interstitial lung disease** on HRCT.  

Each individual finding could be explained by a common nutritional deficiency or an acquired disease, yet their simultaneous occurrence in a young adult was highly atypical.  The pattern of organ fibrosis in two distinct high‑turnover tissues (liver and lung) combined with marrow failure raised suspicion for an **inherited disorder of tissue regeneration**, most notably a telomere‑related syndrome.  Consequently, the clinical team proceeded to broad genetic testing (clinical exome sequencing) to evaluate for pathogenic variants in telomere‑maintenance genes, which ultimately identified a monoallelic PARN missense variant.  

This systematic approach—starting with a thorough history, a focused physical exam highlighting premature ageing, targeted laboratory studies to parse the cause of cytopenias, and imaging that revealed unexpected organ fibrosis—illustrates how each piece of data incrementally directs the differential diagnosis toward a unifying, albeit rare, genetic aetiology.

## Differential Diagnosis and Diagnostic Reasoning  

The patient’s presentation was striking for a constellation of seemingly unrelated abnormalities—pancytopenia, severe vitamin B12 deficiency, hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying, osteoporosis, peripheral neuropathy, and subtle mucocutaneous changes. When faced with such a multisystem picture in a young adult, a structured differential diagnosis is essential. Below we walk through the major diagnostic categories, the specific findings that support or refute each, and the logical steps that ultimately directed attention to a telomere biology disorder (TBD).

### 1. Differential for Pancytopenia in a Young Adult  

| Category | Typical Features | Alignment with Patient | Why It Was Excluded |
|----------|------------------|------------------------|---------------------|
| **Nutritional deficiencies** (B12, folate, copper, iron) | Macro‑ or megaloblastic anemia, neutropenia, thrombocytopenia; often high MCV; low reticulocyte count; hypersegmented neutrophils | Severe B12 and folate deficiency; dimorphic smear, hypersegmentation, Cabot rings; low reticulocytes | Cytopenias improved only partially after aggressive vitamin replacement; persistent hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis cannot be explained by deficiency alone. |
| **Autoimmune cytopenias** (autoimmune hemolytic anemia, immune thrombocytopenia, aplastic anemia) | Positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT), brisk reticulocytosis if hemolysis; autoimmune serologies; marrow hypocellularity | DAT positive for IgG (2+); elevated LDH, indirect hyperbilirubinaemia | Reticulocyte response was blunted (0.39 %); bone marrow showed trilineage hematopoiesis without hypocellularity or aplasia; autoimmune serologies negative; therefore immune‑mediated destruction was unlikely to be the sole cause. |
| **Marrow failure syndromes** (aplastic anemia, myelodysplastic syndrome, inherited BMFS) | Pancytopenia with hypocellular marrow, dysplastic changes, cytogenetic abnormalities | Bone marrow cellularity preserved; no dysplasia or clonal cytogenetic lesions | Lack of hypocellularity and dysplasia argues against primary marrow failure at this stage, although a progressive BMFS could still emerge later. |
| **Infiltrative or malignant marrow disease** (leukemia, lymphoma, metastatic carcinoma, storage disease) | Abnormal blasts, infiltrates, abnormal histology, organomegaly | PET–CT showed heterogeneous FDG uptake in bone marrow but no focal lesions; marrow biopsy negative for malignancy | No morphologic or immunophenotypic evidence of malignancy; PET uptake likely reflects reactive changes rather than neoplastic infiltration. |
| **Infectious causes** (viral—EBV, HIV, hepatitis; bacterial; parasitic) | Often accompanied by systemic symptoms, serologic positivity, abnormal liver enzymes | EBV IgG positive (past infection); HIV, hepatitis B/C, CMV negative; inflammatory markers elevated but non‑specific | No active infection identified; chronic infectious hepatitis would usually produce a different serologic pattern. |
| **Genetic disorders** (congenital dyserythropoietic anemia, Fanconi anemia, TBDs) | Early‑onset cytopenias, associated congenital anomalies, telomere shortening | Multi‑organ involvement (fibrosis, premature ageing signs) plus family‑history‑negative presentation | After excluding acquired etiologies, a hereditary syndrome became the most plausible explanation, prompting targeted genetic testing. |

### 2. Ineffective Erythropoiesis versus Hemolysis  

The peripheral smear showed dimorphic red cells, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils—features classic for ineffective erythropoiesis caused by severe B12 deficiency. Laboratory markers (elevated LDH, indirect bilirubin) suggested some degree of hemolysis, yet the reticulocyte count remained markedly low, indicating that the marrow could not mount an appropriate compensatory response. A positive DAT raised the possibility of immune‑mediated hemolysis, but the absence of a robust reticulocyte rise and the presence of marrow erythroid hyperplasia favored ineffective erythropoiesis as the dominant mechanism. Consequently, B12 replacement was instituted, but the persistence of pancytopenia beyond correction signaled an additional, non‑nutritional driver.

### 3. Hepatic Fibrosis Without Classic Etiology  

Imaging (ultrasound, transient elastography) demonstrated cirrhosis (liver stiffness 18.1 kPa) in a patient without:

* **Alcohol excess** – history denied heavy drinking.
* **Viral hepatitis** – serologies for HBV, HCV, and HIV were negative.
* **Autoimmune hepatitis** – ANA, SMA, LKM‑1 antibodies absent.
* **Metabolic liver disease** – no evidence of steatohepatitis beyond mild steatosis; iron studies did not meet criteria for hemochromatosis; Wilson disease work‑up not indicated.

The absence of these common causes, together with the patient’s premature ageing phenotype, directed attention toward a systemic fibrotic process driven by telomere dysfunction, as has been reported in several TBD cohorts.

### 4. Pulmonary Fibrosis in a Young Adult  

High‑resolution CT revealed diffuse ground‑glass haziness, subpleural atelectasis, and fibrotic changes. In a person in her 20s, the differential includes:

* **Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF)** – typically presents after age 50; rare before 30.
* **Connective‑tissue‑disease–associated interstitial lung disease** – ANA, rheumatoid factor, anti‑CCP, ENA panel negative; no clinical stigmata of systemic sclerosis, lupus, or rheumatoid arthritis.
* **Environmental/occupational exposures** – no occupational dust, smoking, or drug exposures reported.
* **Genetic interstitial lung disease** – mutations in surfactant proteins, telomerase components, or PARN can cause early‑onset fibrosis.

Given the exclusion of autoimmune, environmental, and typical idiopathic causes, a hereditary interstitial lung disease—particularly a telomere‑related form—became the leading consideration.

### 5. Synthesising Multi‑System Involvement  

The coexistence of marrow dysfunction, hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying, osteoporosis, peripheral neuropathy, and mild mucocutaneous abnormalities (knuckle hyperpigmentation, bald tongue) hints at a **syndromic disorder affecting highly proliferative tissues**. This pattern is characteristic of telomere biology disorders, where defective telomere maintenance limits the replicative capacity of stem cells in bone marrow, epithelium, and mesenchyme. The lack of the classic dyskeratosis congenita triad (nail dystrophy, reticular skin pigmentation, oral leukoplakia) does **not** exclude a TBD, as many patients present with only a subset of features or with organ‑specific disease.

### 6. Rationale for Escalating to Genetic Testing  

When a thorough evaluation—biochemical, serologic, radiologic, and histologic—fails to uncover an acquired cause, the diagnostic algorithm for unexplained pancytopenia and multisystem fibrosis recommends **early genetic investigation** for several reasons:

1. **Timeliness** – Early identification of a TBD influences surveillance (e.g., pulmonary function testing, liver imaging) and informs transplant eligibility.
2. **Family counseling** – Determines recurrence risk and guides testing of relatives.
3. **Therapeutic implications** – Certain agents (androgens, telomerase activators) are being explored in telomere‑related disease; inappropriate immunosuppression can be avoided.
4. **Prognostication** – Telomere length measurement and genotype correlate with disease trajectory.

Thus, after excluding infectious, autoimmune, metabolic, and infiltrative etiologies, the clinical team pursued **clinical exome sequencing**.

### 7. Role of Clinical Exome Sequencing in Unexplained Multisystem Disease  

Whole‑exome or clinical‑exome sequencing interrogates all coding regions of the genome, enabling detection of both known and novel pathogenic variants. In the context of this case, exome sequencing identified a **monoallelic missense variant in PARN (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg)**. Although classified as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS) per ACMG criteria, several points supported its relevance:

* **Location in a critical domain** (R3H domain) with no benign variation reported.
* **Absence from population databases**, indicating rarity.
* **In silico predictions** of deleterious impact (four of five tools concordant).
* **Phenotypic concordance** with previously described PARN‑related TBDs (pulmonary fibrosis, bone‑marrow failure, hepatic fibrosis).

The identification of this variant provided a unifying genetic explanation for the patient’s multi‑organ pathology and justified ongoing monitoring for progressive marrow failure or fibrotic complications. Moreover, the result reinforced the principle that **targeted genetic testing should follow a systematic exclusion of common acquired causes**, especially in young adults with atypical, multisystem disease.

In summary, the differential diagnosis evolved from common acquired conditions to a rare hereditary syndrome through meticulous evaluation of laboratory and imaging data, careful interpretation of the pattern of ineffective erythropoiesis versus hemolysis, and exclusion of infectious, autoimmune, metabolic, and infiltrative processes. The persistent, multisystem nature of the disease ultimately directed the clinicians toward genetic testing, where exome sequencing revealed a PARN variant, solidifying the diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder. This systematic approach exemplifies how clinicians should integrate organ‑specific findings into a broader syndromic framework, especially when standard work‑ups are unrevealing.

## Telomere Biology Disorders and the Role of PARN  

Telomeres are ribonucleoprotein caps that protect the ends of linear chromosomes from being recognized as DNA breaks.  Each telomere consists of tandem repeats of the hexanucleotide sequence **TTAGGG** bound by a specialized protein complex called **shelterin**.  During each round of DNA replication the conventional DNA polymerase cannot fully extend the lagging strand, producing a progressive loss of 50–200 base pairs per cell division—a phenomenon known as the **end‑replication problem**.  In most somatic cells this gradual shortening proceeds unchecked, eventually reaching a critical length that triggers a DNA damage response, leading to **cellular senescence or apoptosis**.  

In highly proliferative tissues—hematopoietic stem cells, epithelial cells of the skin and mucosa, hepatocytes, and alveolar type II cells—telomere shortening is offset by the ribonucleoprotein **telomerase**.  Telomerase consists of a catalytic subunit **TERT (telomerase reverse transcriptase)**, an essential RNA template **TERC**, and multiple accessory proteins (e.g., dyskerin, NOP10, NHP2, and TCAB1) that promote assembly, nuclear trafficking, and stability of the complex.  By adding telomeric repeats to chromosome ends, telomerase maintains replicative capacity and prevents premature senescence.  

When germline mutations disrupt any component of this machinery, the resulting **telomere biology disorders (TBDs)** manifest as multisystem disease because the most proliferative cell lineages are unable to sustain adequate turnover.  Shortened or dysfunctional telomeres provoke **bone‑marrow failure, mucocutaneous atrophy, hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis, and predisposition to malignancy**.  The classic prototype is **dyskeratosis congenita (DC)**, defined by the mucocutaneous triad of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation, together with variable bone‑marrow failure (BMF).  More severe pediatric phenotypes—**Hoyeraal‑Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome**, **Revesz syndrome**, and **Coats plus syndrome**—represent the extreme end of the telomere length spectrum, often with cerebellar hypoplasia, severe immunodeficiency, or retinal vascular disease.  In adulthood, incomplete or organ‑restricted penetrance gives rise to **idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis, and isolated BMF** without overt mucocutaneous signs.  

### PARN: Molecular Function and the Basis for Haploinsufficiency  

The **PARN (poly(A)-specific ribonuclease)** gene encodes a 3′‑5′ exoribonuclease that catalyzes **deadenylation**, the removal of poly(A) tails from messenger RNAs.  Although deadenylation is a ubiquitous step in RNA turnover, PARN has a **highly specific role in the maturation of the TERC RNA component**.  TERC is transcribed as a longer precursor bearing a short oligo‑adenylated tail; PARN trims this tail, preventing premature degradation and permitting proper folding and incorporation into the telomerase holoenzyme.  Consequently, loss‑of‑function (LoF) variants in PARN diminish mature TERC levels, **impairing telomerase activity** and accelerating telomere attrition.  

PARN contains a conserved **R3H domain (residues ~178–245)** that mediates binding to single‑stranded nucleic acids.  Missense changes within this domain are particularly deleterious because they disrupt substrate recognition and catalytic efficiency.  The gene is among the **20 % of human genes most intolerant to rare missense variation**, indicating that a single functional allele is insufficient for normal telomere maintenance—a state termed **haploinsufficiency**.  In the context of a heterozygous (monoallelic) LoF or damaging missense mutation, telomere length is modestly reduced, predisposing carriers to adult‑onset phenotypes such as IPF and bone‑marrow failure.  Biallelic (recessive) mutations produce a more profound loss of PARN activity, causing **autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6)** with earlier onset and the full DC triad.  

### Genotype–Phenotype Correlations for PARN Mutations  

| Mutation type | Zygosity | Typical phenotype | Key clinical clues |
|---------------|----------|-------------------|--------------------|
| **Biallelic loss‑of‑function (nonsense, frameshift, splice) or severe missense** | Autosomal recessive | Dyskeratosis congenita (DC) – mucocutaneous triad, early BMF, growth failure | Presents in childhood; often severe nail, skin and oral lesions |
| **Monoallelic missense (especially in R3H domain) or truncating variants** | Autosomal dominant (haploinsufficient) | Adult‑onset pulmonary fibrosis, isolated or progressive BMF, hepatic fibrosis; may lack classic DC signs | Family history of IPF or marrow failure may be absent; premature greying, osteoporosis, or subtle cytopenias may be the only clues |

The **c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg** variant identified in the patient substitutes a cysteine for an arginine at position 205, a residue that lies within the R3H domain and is highly conserved across vertebrates.  In silico tools (SIFT, MutationTaster, LRT, MutationAssessor) uniformly predict a deleterious effect, and the allele is **absent from large population databases (e.g., gnomAD)**, fulfilling ACMG/AMP criteria for **PM2 (absent/rare)** and **PM1 (critical functional domain)**.  The variant also meets **PP3 (multiple computational predictions of damaging effect)** and **PP2 (missense mechanism established for the gene)**, leading to a classification of **Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS)** because functional validation or segregation data are lacking.  

### Clinical Actionability of a VUS in a Compatible Phenotype  

Even when a variant is designated a VUS, the **overall clinical context can render it actionable**.  In this case, the patient exhibits a constellation of **multisystem telomere‑related manifestations**—pancytopenia, hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying, osteoporosis, and neuropathy—mirroring the reported spectrum of **PARN‑associated telomere biology disorders**.  The **absence of alternative explanations** after exhaustive nutritional, infectious, autoimmune, and malignant work‑ups strengthens the genotype‑phenotype link.  Moreover, the variant’s location in a **highly constrained functional domain**, together with its rarity and consistent in‑silico deleterious predictions, provide **moderate evidence of pathogenicity** under the ACMG framework, even if definitive proof (e.g., telomere length measurement, functional assay) is pending.  

Clinically, recognizing the variant as the likely driver of disease informs several management decisions:  

1. **Surveillance** for progression of bone‑marrow failure, liver cirrhosis, and pulmonary fibrosis, because TBD patients are at risk for accelerated organ decline.  
2. **Genetic counselling** for the patient and relatives, emphasizing the possibility of de novo occurrence or reduced penetrance and discussing reproductive options.  
3. **Therapeutic considerations** such as avoidance of DNA‑damaging agents, cautious use of immunosuppression, and early referral for transplant evaluation when organ dysfunction advances.  

Thus, despite its VUS status, the PARN c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg variant is **clinically actionable** when interpreted alongside the patient’s telomere‑related phenotype, exemplifying how modern genomics integrates variant classification with careful phenotypic correlation to guide patient care.

## Management Strategies and Follow‑up in Telomere‑Related Disease  

The therapeutic plan for this patient was anchored in two overarching goals: (1) immediate correction of reversible metabolic derangements that were contributing to cytopenia and systemic decline, and (2) long‑term mitigation of organ injury that is typical of telomere‑biology disorders (TBDs). Because the underlying telomere defect cannot be cured with current standard therapy, management is essentially supportive, with vigilant surveillance for the evolution of bone‑marrow failure, progressive fibrosis, and malignancy. The following paragraphs outline the specific interventions that were applied, the rationale behind each, and the recommended follow‑up framework for patients with TBDs.

### Correction of Severe Vitamin B12 and Folate Deficiency  

The patient’s pancytopenia was dominated by a macro‑ and megaloblastic picture on peripheral smear, and laboratory testing revealed serum cobalamin < 100 pmol/L together with folate < 3 nmol/L. Although the ultimate etiology of the TBD was genetic, severe B12 deficiency can independently cause ineffective erythropoiesis, hypersegmented neutrophils, and neurological impairment; therefore, rapid repletion was mandatory.

**Dosing** – Intravenous cyanocobalamin 1 g (1000 µg) was administered daily for five consecutive days (the “loading” phase), followed by a weekly dose of 1 g for four weeks and then a maintenance schedule of 1 g every month. This regimen follows the British Society for Haematology guideline for severe deficiency with neurologic involvement, ensuring sufficient intracellular cobalamin for DNA synthesis and myelin maintenance. Oral folic acid 1 mg daily was started concurrently because folate stores are rapidly depleted when erythropoiesis is stimulated.

**Monitoring** – Serum B12 and methylmalonic acid were re‑checked at two‑week intervals during the loading phase; a rise in B12 above 300 pmol/L together with a decline in methylmalonic acid confirmed biochemical response. Hematologically, a rise in reticulocyte count (target > 2 % of red cells) should be evident within 7–10 days, followed by a gradual increase in hemoglobin and neutrophil counts over 3–4 weeks. Folate levels were re‑measured after 4 weeks; if they remained low, the dose was increased to 5 mg daily for an additional month.

**Expected response** – In isolated cobalamin deficiency, a typical hemoglobin increment of 2 g/dL per week is seen once marrow response begins. In this case, the response was blunted because the bone marrow was also compromised by telomere‑mediated replicative stress; nevertheless, a modest but clinically significant rise in reticulocytes and stabilization of the white‑cell count were achieved, enabling reduction of transfusion requirements.

### Supportive Hematologic Care  

Even after vitamin repletion, the patient retained a degree of cytopenia that could herald progression to marrow failure. Management therefore emphasized conservative thresholds and close observation.

* **Transfusion thresholds** – Red‑cell transfusion was reserved for symptomatic anemia (hemoglobin < 7 g/dL or < 8 g/dL with active cardiac disease) or for functional impairment. Platelet transfusion was employed only when counts fell below 10 × 10⁹/L or when invasive procedures were planned. Given the low reticulocyte production index, transfusion support was not prophylactic but symptom‑driven.

* **Iron management** – Iron studies showed elevated ferritin with normal to high serum iron, reflecting iron sequestration rather than true overload. Routine phlebotomy was contraindicated. Serial ferritin measurements were scheduled every 3 months; if ferritin rose > 1000 µg/L with evidence of hepatic iron deposition on MRI, chelation with deferasirox would be considered, but this has not yet been required.

* **Monitoring for evolving marrow failure** – A complete blood count (CBC) with differential is obtained at each clinic visit (monthly for the first 6 months, then every 3 months). In addition, a bone‑marrow aspirate is reserved for a new decline in two cell lines (> 20 % drop from baseline) or when cytopenia becomes refractory to nutritional correction, to assess for dysplasia, aplasia, or clonal evolution (e.g., PNH clone, MDS).

### Bone Health: Osteoporosis Treatment and Fracture Prevention  

Dual‑energy X‑ray absorptiometry (DXA) demonstrated a lumbar spine T‑score of –2.8, confirming osteoporosis, which is common in TBDs due to impaired proliferation of osteoprogenitor cells and chronic inflammation.

* **Calcium and vitamin D** – Oral elemental calcium 1000 mg daily (as calcium carbonate) and vitamin D₃ 60 000 IU weekly were started, aiming for a serum 25‑hydroxyvitamin D level > 30 ng/mL. Levels are checked after 8 weeks and then quarterly.

* **Anti‑resorptive therapy** – Bisphosphonate therapy is indicated when BMD remains low after 6 months of adequate calcium/vitamin D repletion and the patient has sustained fragility fractures (as in this case). Intravenous zoledronic acid 5 mg was administered once, with dental evaluation performed beforehand to minimize osteonecrosis risk. Subsequent dosing is planned at 12‑month intervals, provided renal function (eGFR > 35 mL/min) remains acceptable.

* **Fracture prevention** – Weight‑bearing exercises, fall‑risk assessment (home safety, visual correction), and physiotherapy to strengthen proximal musculature were integrated. Hip‑protective measures (e.g., hip protectors) were offered given the presence of scoliosis and neuropathy.

### Management of Hepatic and Pulmonary Fibrosis  

Both liver and lung fibrosis are recognized end‑organ manifestations of telomere shortening. No disease‑modifying agents are approved specifically for TBD‑related fibrosis, so care focuses on slowing progression, managing complications, and evaluating transplant eligibility.

* **Hepatic fibrosis** – The patient’s liver stiffness measurement (18.1 kPa) corresponded to established cirrhosis. Etiologic work‑up excluded viral, alcoholic, and autoimmune causes, leaving telomere‑mediated injury as the plausible driver. Management therefore includes:
  - **Screening for portal hypertension** (ultrasound for splenomegaly, varices; endoscopy if indicated).
  - **Vaccination** against hepatitis A and B, and prophylaxis against spontaneous bacterial peritonitis if ascites develops.
  - **Avoidance of hepatotoxins** (e.g., acetaminophen > 2 g/day, unnecessary antibiotics).
  - **Consideration of liver transplantation** – In TBD patients, transplant outcomes are generally acceptable, but recurrence of fibrosis can occur if the underlying telomere defect is not addressed. A multidisciplinary transplant board evaluates candidacy, weighing the concurrent pulmonary involvement.

* **Pulmonary fibrosis** – High‑resolution computed tomography showed a mixed pattern of ground‑glass opacity, subpleural reticulation, and traction bronchiectasis, compatible with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) as part of a TBD phenotype. Current guidelines for IPF advise:
  - **Antifibrotic therapy** – Nintedanib 150 mg twice daily or pirfenidone 801 mg three times daily can slow forced vital capacity (FVC) decline. In this patient, nintedanib was initiated after baseline liver enzymes were confirmed to be < 2 × ULN and renal function was adequate. Monitoring includes liver function tests every 4 weeks for the first 3 months, then quarterly.
  - **Pulmonary rehabilitation** – Structured exercise program and supplemental oxygen (titrated to maintain SpO₂ > 90 % during activity) improve functional capacity.
  - **Transplant assessment** – Lung transplantation may be curative for end‑stage fibrosis, but telomere length influences post‑transplant complications (e.g., poor wound healing, chronic lung allograft dysfunction). Short telomere length (< 5 kb) is a relative contraindication; therefore, telomere length measurement (flow‑FISH) was performed and will be repeated annually.

### Genetic Counselling  

The identified PARN missense change (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) was classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS). Counseling therefore required a balanced explanation of its potential relevance without overstating certainty.

* **Communication of the VUS** – The patient was informed that the variant alters a conserved amino‑acid within the R3H domain, is absent from large population databases, and is predicted deleterious, but functional studies are lacking. The possibility that this variant contributes to her telomere biology disorder, in the context of a compatible phenotype, was emphasized.

* **Inheritance pattern** – PARN disease can be autosomal recessive (biallelic) or autosomal dominant (monoallelic). Since only one allele is affected, a dominant model with incomplete penetrance is most plausible. The patient was offered testing of both parents and siblings; parental testing revealed the variant to be de novo, eliminating the need for cascade testing in relatives but confirming a germline origin.

* **Reproductive options** – Pre‑implantation genetic testing (PGT‑M) for monogenic disease and prenatal chorionic villus sampling were discussed for future pregnancies. The uncertainty surrounding pathogenicity was highlighted, and the patient was referred to a reproductive specialist for individualized risk assessment.

### Surveillance Protocol for Telomere‑Related Disease  

Because TBDs can evolve over years, a structured follow‑up schedule is essential.

| Parameter | Frequency | Rationale |
|-----------|-----------|-----------|
| CBC with differential | Every 4 weeks (first 6 months), then every 3 months | Detect progressive marrow failure; guide transfusion need |
| Reticulocyte count | Along with CBC | Assess marrow response after nutritional repletion |
| Telomere length (flow‑FISH) | Baseline, then annually | Document trajectory; influence transplant decisions |
| Liver elastography (FibroScan) | Every 6 months | Track progression of fibrosis/cirrhosis |
| Liver function tests (AST/ALT, bilirubin, albumin, INR) | Every 3 months | Monitor hepatic synthetic function |
| Abdominal ultrasound (portal hypertension screen) | Every 12 months or if clinical suspicion | Early detection of varices/ascites |
| Pulmonary function tests (FVC, DLCO) | Every 6 months | Quantify fibrosis progression; assess response to antifibrotics |
| High‑resolution chest CT | Baseline, then if > 10 % decline in FVC or symptom change | Structural evaluation of fibrosis |
| DXA scan | Baseline, then every 2 years | Monitor bone density, adjust osteoporosis therapy |
| Serum vitamin B12, folate, vitamin D | Every 3 months until stable, then annually | Ensure sustained repletion |
| Malignancy screening (e.g., age‑appropriate skin, breast, colorectal cancer) | According to national guidelines; consider earlier endoscopy if dysphagia or GI bleeding | TBDs confer increased cancer risk, especially squamous cell carcinoma of the head‑neck and hematologic malignancies |

All visits incorporate counselling on lifestyle modifications (smoking cessation, avoidance of occupational lung irritants, limiting alcohol, balanced nutrition) that can exacerbate organ injury.

### Emerging and Investigational Therapies  

While the standard of care remains supportive, several experimental approaches are under investigation for TBDs and may become relevant for patients such as this one.

* **Androgen therapy (danazol, oxymetholone, nandrolone)** – These agents increase telomerase activity indirectly via up‑regulation of TERT expression, modestly elongating telomeres in small pilot studies. Danazol 800 mg daily has been associated with a mean telomere length increase of 0.5 kb over 2 years and delayed progression of bone‑marrow failure. Potential adverse effects (hepatic toxicity, lipid abnormalities, virilization) necessitate careful hepatic monitoring, which is a particular concern in patients with existing cirrhosis.

* **Telomerase activators (e.g., GRN510, imtelstat)** – Small‑molecule compounds designed to enhance telomerase assembly or stabilize the TERC RNA are in early‑phase clinical trials. Evidence to date is limited to safety data; efficacy in preventing fibrosis or marrow failure remains to be demonstrated.

* **Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT)** – Allogeneic HSCT can cure marrow failure but does not halt pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis and carries heightened risk of transplant‑related toxicity in telomere‑shortened patients (e.g., severe mucositis, delayed engraftment, pulmonary complications). Reduced‑intensity conditioning regimens are preferred, and donor selection must consider telomere length of the graft source. For patients whose primary morbidity is organ fibrosis rather than marrow failure, HSCT is not indicated.

* **Gene‑editing approaches (CRISPR‑Cas9, base editors)** – In vitro correction of pathogenic PARN variants has been demonstrated in patient‑derived induced pluripotent stem cells, but clinical translation is still several years away. Ongoing research may eventually allow precise correction of monoallelic variants, potentially normalising telomere maintenance.

In summary, this patient’s management combined acute correction of reversible metabolic deficiencies, targeted supportive care for cytopenia, bone health, and organ fibrosis, and a comprehensive surveillance strategy aimed at early detection of disease progression. Genetic counselling addressed the uncertainty inherent to a VUS while outlining realistic reproductive options. As investigational therapies mature, they may offer disease‑modifying possibilities for individuals with telomere‑related disorders, but at present the cornerstone of care remains meticulous supportive management and longitudinal monitoring.

## Clinical Lessons and Future Directions  

The present case illustrates several overarching principles that every trainee should incorporate into daily practice when confronting a young adult with unexplained multisystem disease. Although the individual findings—pancytopenia, hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying, and osteoporotic fractures—could each be ascribed to common acquired disorders, their simultaneous presence in a single patient obliges a broader differential that includes telomere biology disorders (TBDs). The following points synthesize the educational take‑aways, highlight the current limitations of our diagnostic armamentarium, and outline priorities for future investigation.

### 1. Considering TBDs even when classic mucocutaneous signs are missing  
Dyskeratosis congenita (DC) is traditionally taught as a triad of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation. However, the phenotypic spectrum of TBDs is now recognised to be much wider, with many patients lacking any of these skin or mucosal features. In this report, the patient’s premature greying, knuckle hyperpigmentation, and a bald tongue were the only subtle clues pointing toward premature ageing. The key lesson is that **absence of the classic triad does not exclude a telomere disorder**; clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion when a young adult presents with:

* Unexplained cytopenias that are disproportionate to nutritional deficiencies,  
* Fibrotic involvement of two or more high‑turnover organs (bone marrow, liver, lung, skin), and  
* Other features of accelerated ageing (early osteoporosis, sensory neuropathy, scoliosis).  

Prompt recognition can alter management by triggering appropriate genetic counselling, targeted surveillance, and early referral for transplantation when indicated.

### 2. Integrated clinical reasoning uncovers rare genetics after routine work‑up  
The diagnostic trajectory in this case exemplifies a stepwise, hypothesis‑driven approach. Initial investigations focused on common etiologies—nutritional deficiencies, immune‑mediated hemolysis, infectious hepatitis, and autoimmune liver disease—each of which was either confirmed (B12 deficiency) or excluded (negative autoantibodies, unremarkable viral panels). When correction of deficiencies failed to resolve the systemic picture, the clinicians broadened their differential to include inherited syndromes. Critical to this shift were:

* **Pattern recognition** of multisystem fibrosis,  
* **Correlation of laboratory clues** (ineffective erythropoiesis, low reticulocyte response) with marrow stress, and  
* **Contextual weighting** of uncommon findings (premature greying, spinal deformity).  

This systematic integration underscores the importance of revisiting the differential diagnosis whenever the clinical course deviates from expected improvement, and of involving genetics early when a “cryptic” syndrome is suspected.

### 3. Monoallelic PARN variants expand the phenotypic horizon and influence prognosis  
Historically, pathogenic PARN mutations were linked to autosomal‑recessive dyskeratosis congenita, while **monoallelic** missense changes were most often associated with isolated idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF). The current patient demonstrates that even a single allele can precipitate a **multisystem TBD phenotype**, including marrow failure and hepatic fibrosis. This observation has several implications:

* **Phenotype breadth**: Monoallelic PARN variants should be considered in any young adult with unexplained organ fibrosis, not solely in isolated IPF.  
* **Prognostic uncertainty**: The natural history of single‑allele PARN‑related disease remains incompletely defined; some carriers may progress to severe marrow failure, while others remain stable for years. Ongoing longitudinal monitoring is essential.  
* **Counselling nuances**: Because penetrance is variable, family members who share the variant may be asymptomatic. Genetic counselling must therefore address uncertainty regarding disease onset, severity, and reproductive risk.

### 4. Limitations of current diagnostic tools  

| Component | Limitation | Practical impact |
|-----------|------------|-------------------|
| **Variant classification (VUS)** | Many rare missense changes, including the patient’s p.Cys205Arg, remain classified as variants of uncertain significance, even after in‑silico modelling and population rarity assessment. | Clinicians must balance the weight of a VUS against the clinical picture; over‑reliance can lead either to premature exclusion or to unnecessary anxiety. |
| **Telomere length measurement** | Flow‑FISH telomere length assays have inter‑laboratory variability, age‑matched reference ranges, and may be influenced by acute stress or recent transfusions. | A normal telomere length does not rule out a TBD, especially in heterozygous carriers where telomere attrition may be modest. |
| **Functional assays** | No widely available, standardized test to assess PARN enzymatic activity or downstream TERC maturation in patient cells. | Interpretation of genetic findings relies heavily on indirect evidence; functional validation remains a research tool. |

Awareness of these constraints is essential when communicating results to patients and when deciding whether to pursue further genetic testing (e.g., whole‑genome sequencing, targeted telomere‑maintenance panels).

### 5. Research priorities  

1. **Large genotype‑phenotype cohorts** – Aggregating data from multiple registries will clarify the penetrance and organ‑specific risk associated with monoallelic PARN variants and other TBD genes.  
2. **Natural‑history studies** – Prospective follow‑up of individuals with VUS or likely pathogenic variants will inform surveillance intervals and early‑intervention thresholds.  
3. **Standardized telomere assays** – Development of high‑throughput, reproducible telomere length platforms (e.g., single‑telomere length analysis) will improve diagnostic confidence.  
4. **Therapeutic trials** – Small‑scale, multicenter trials of telomere‑targeted agents (e.g., androgens, telomerase activators, gene‑editing approaches) are needed to assess efficacy, safety, and optimal timing relative to disease stage.  
5. **Functional genomics** – In‑vitro systems (patient‑derived induced pluripotent stem cells) can delineate how specific PARN missense changes affect TERC processing and downstream telomere dynamics, providing mechanistic insight that may guide personalized therapy.

### 6. Take‑home algorithm for unexplained pancytopenia with organ fibrosis  

1. **Confirm cytopenia and assess marrow response** – CBC, reticulocyte count, peripheral smear, bone‑marrow aspirate/biopsy.  
2. **Exclude common reversible causes** – Nutrient deficiencies (B12, folate, iron), hemolysis (DAT, LDH), infection, medication toxicity, autoimmune disease.  
3. **Screen for organ fibrosis** – Liver ultrasound/ elastography, high‑resolution CT of the chest, cardiac evaluation if indicated.  
4. **Identify premature‑ageing clues** – Hair greying, skin/nail changes, osteoporosis, neuro‑muscular findings.  
5. **If multisystem fibrosis persists after step 2–3**:  
   * Order a comprehensive telomere‑maintenance gene panel or clinical exome sequencing.  
   * Request telomere length measurement (age‑adjusted reference).  
6. **Interpret genetics** – Classify variants (pathogenic, likely pathogenic, VUS). Discuss with a molecular‑genetics multidisciplinary team.  
7. **Management pathway**  
   * Correct reversible deficiencies; provide organ‑specific supportive care.  
   * Initiate surveillance for progression (serial CBC, pulmonary function tests, liver elastography).  
   * Refer to transplant centers early if marrow failure or end‑stage organ disease develops.  
   * Offer genetic counselling and consider family screening.  

By following this algorithm, clinicians can move efficiently from routine work‑up to targeted investigation of rare telomere disorders, thereby reducing diagnostic delay and enabling timely intervention.

---  

In summary, this case underscores that **young adults with unexplained multisystem fibrosis deserve a focused evaluation for telomere biology disorders**, even in the absence of classic mucocutaneous signs. Integrated clinical reasoning, judicious use of genetic testing, and awareness of current diagnostic limitations are the cornerstones of accurate diagnosis. Future research aimed at expanding genotype‑phenotype databases, standardising telomere measurement, and testing disease‑modifying therapies will be pivotal in transforming the prognosis of patients harboring monoallelic PARN and other telomere‑related variants.
