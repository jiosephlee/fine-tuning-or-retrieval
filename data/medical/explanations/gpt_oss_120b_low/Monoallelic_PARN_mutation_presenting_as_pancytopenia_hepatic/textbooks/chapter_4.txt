Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

Chapter 4: ## Management Strategies and Follow‑up in Telomere‑Related Disease  

The therapeutic plan for this patient was anchored in two overarching goals: (1) immediate correction of reversible metabolic derangements that were contributing to cytopenia and systemic decline, and (2) long‑term mitigation of organ injury that is typical of telomere‑biology disorders (TBDs). Because the underlying telomere defect cannot be cured with current standard therapy, management is essentially supportive, with vigilant surveillance for the evolution of bone‑marrow failure, progressive fibrosis, and malignancy. The following paragraphs outline the specific interventions that were applied, the rationale behind each, and the recommended follow‑up framework for patients with TBDs.

### Correction of Severe Vitamin B12 and Folate Deficiency  

The patient’s pancytopenia was dominated by a macro‑ and megaloblastic picture on peripheral smear, and laboratory testing revealed serum cobalamin < 100 pmol/L together with folate < 3 nmol/L. Although the ultimate etiology of the TBD was genetic, severe B12 deficiency can independently cause ineffective erythropoiesis, hypersegmented neutrophils, and neurological impairment; therefore, rapid repletion was mandatory.

**Dosing** – Intravenous cyanocobalamin 1 g (1000 µg) was administered daily for five consecutive days (the “loading” phase), followed by a weekly dose of 1 g for four weeks and then a maintenance schedule of 1 g every month. This regimen follows the British Society for Haematology guideline for severe deficiency with neurologic involvement, ensuring sufficient intracellular cobalamin for DNA synthesis and myelin maintenance. Oral folic acid 1 mg daily was started concurrently because folate stores are rapidly depleted when erythropoiesis is stimulated.

**Monitoring** – Serum B12 and methylmalonic acid were re‑checked at two‑week intervals during the loading phase; a rise in B12 above 300 pmol/L together with a decline in methylmalonic acid confirmed biochemical response. Hematologically, a rise in reticulocyte count (target > 2 % of red cells) should be evident within 7–10 days, followed by a gradual increase in hemoglobin and neutrophil counts over 3–4 weeks. Folate levels were re‑measured after 4 weeks; if they remained low, the dose was increased to 5 mg daily for an additional month.

**Expected response** – In isolated cobalamin deficiency, a typical hemoglobin increment of 2 g/dL per week is seen once marrow response begins. In this case, the response was blunted because the bone marrow was also compromised by telomere‑mediated replicative stress; nevertheless, a modest but clinically significant rise in reticulocytes and stabilization of the white‑cell count were achieved, enabling reduction of transfusion requirements.

### Supportive Hematologic Care  

Even after vitamin repletion, the patient retained a degree of cytopenia that could herald progression to marrow failure. Management therefore emphasized conservative thresholds and close observation.

* **Transfusion thresholds** – Red‑cell transfusion was reserved for symptomatic anemia (hemoglobin < 7 g/dL or < 8 g/dL with active cardiac disease) or for functional impairment. Platelet transfusion was employed only when counts fell below 10 × 10⁹/L or when invasive procedures were planned. Given the low reticulocyte production index, transfusion support was not prophylactic but symptom‑driven.

* **Iron management** – Iron studies showed elevated ferritin with normal to high serum iron, reflecting iron sequestration rather than true overload. Routine phlebotomy was contraindicated. Serial ferritin measurements were scheduled every 3 months; if ferritin rose > 1000 µg/L with evidence of hepatic iron deposition on MRI, chelation with deferasirox would be considered, but this has not yet been required.

* **Monitoring for evolving marrow failure** – A complete blood count (CBC) with differential is obtained at each clinic visit (monthly for the first 6 months, then every 3 months). In addition, a bone‑marrow aspirate is reserved for a new decline in two cell lines (> 20 % drop from baseline) or when cytopenia becomes refractory to nutritional correction, to assess for dysplasia, aplasia, or clonal evolution (e.g., PNH clone, MDS).

### Bone Health: Osteoporosis Treatment and Fracture Prevention  

Dual‑energy X‑ray absorptiometry (DXA) demonstrated a lumbar spine T‑score of –2.8, confirming osteoporosis, which is common in TBDs due to impaired proliferation of osteoprogenitor cells and chronic inflammation.

* **Calcium and vitamin D** – Oral elemental calcium 1000 mg daily (as calcium carbonate) and vitamin D₃ 60 000 IU weekly were started, aiming for a serum 25‑hydroxyvitamin D level > 30 ng/mL. Levels are checked after 8 weeks and then quarterly.

* **Anti‑resorptive therapy** – Bisphosphonate therapy is indicated when BMD remains low after 6 months of adequate calcium/vitamin D repletion and the patient has sustained fragility fractures (as in this case). Intravenous zoledronic acid 5 mg was administered once, with dental evaluation performed beforehand to minimize osteonecrosis risk. Subsequent dosing is planned at 12‑month intervals, provided renal function (eGFR > 35 mL/min) remains acceptable.

* **Fracture prevention** – Weight‑bearing exercises, fall‑risk assessment (home safety, visual correction), and physiotherapy to strengthen proximal musculature were integrated. Hip‑protective measures (e.g., hip protectors) were offered given the presence of scoliosis and neuropathy.

### Management of Hepatic and Pulmonary Fibrosis  

Both liver and lung fibrosis are recognized end‑organ manifestations of telomere shortening. No disease‑modifying agents are approved specifically for TBD‑related fibrosis, so care focuses on slowing progression, managing complications, and evaluating transplant eligibility.

* **Hepatic fibrosis** – The patient’s liver stiffness measurement (18.1 kPa) corresponded to established cirrhosis. Etiologic work‑up excluded viral, alcoholic, and autoimmune causes, leaving telomere‑mediated injury as the plausible driver. Management therefore includes:
  - **Screening for portal hypertension** (ultrasound for splenomegaly, varices; endoscopy if indicated).
  - **Vaccination** against hepatitis A and B, and prophylaxis against spontaneous bacterial peritonitis if ascites develops.
  - **Avoidance of hepatotoxins** (e.g., acetaminophen > 2 g/day, unnecessary antibiotics).
  - **Consideration of liver transplantation** – In TBD patients, transplant outcomes are generally acceptable, but recurrence of fibrosis can occur if the underlying telomere defect is not addressed. A multidisciplinary transplant board evaluates candidacy, weighing the concurrent pulmonary involvement.

* **Pulmonary fibrosis** – High‑resolution computed tomography showed a mixed pattern of ground‑glass opacity, subpleural reticulation, and traction bronchiectasis, compatible with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) as part of a TBD phenotype. Current guidelines for IPF advise:
  - **Antifibrotic therapy** – Nintedanib 150 mg twice daily or pirfenidone 801 mg three times daily can slow forced vital capacity (FVC) decline. In this patient, nintedanib was initiated after baseline liver enzymes were confirmed to be < 2 × ULN and renal function was adequate. Monitoring includes liver function tests every 4 weeks for the first 3 months, then quarterly.
  - **Pulmonary rehabilitation** – Structured exercise program and supplemental oxygen (titrated to maintain SpO₂ > 90 % during activity) improve functional capacity.
  - **Transplant assessment** – Lung transplantation may be curative for end‑stage fibrosis, but telomere length influences post‑transplant complications (e.g., poor wound healing, chronic lung allograft dysfunction). Short telomere length (< 5 kb) is a relative contraindication; therefore, telomere length measurement (flow‑FISH) was performed and will be repeated annually.

### Genetic Counselling  

The identified PARN missense change (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) was classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS). Counseling therefore required a balanced explanation of its potential relevance without overstating certainty.

* **Communication of the VUS** – The patient was informed that the variant alters a conserved amino‑acid within the R3H domain, is absent from large population databases, and is predicted deleterious, but functional studies are lacking. The possibility that this variant contributes to her telomere biology disorder, in the context of a compatible phenotype, was emphasized.

* **Inheritance pattern** – PARN disease can be autosomal recessive (biallelic) or autosomal dominant (monoallelic). Since only one allele is affected, a dominant model with incomplete penetrance is most plausible. The patient was offered testing of both parents and siblings; parental testing revealed the variant to be de novo, eliminating the need for cascade testing in relatives but confirming a germline origin.

* **Reproductive options** – Pre‑implantation genetic testing (PGT‑M) for monogenic disease and prenatal chorionic villus sampling were discussed for future pregnancies. The uncertainty surrounding pathogenicity was highlighted, and the patient was referred to a reproductive specialist for individualized risk assessment.

### Surveillance Protocol for Telomere‑Related Disease  

Because TBDs can evolve over years, a structured follow‑up schedule is essential.

| Parameter | Frequency | Rationale |
|-----------|-----------|-----------|
| CBC with differential | Every 4 weeks (first 6 months), then every 3 months | Detect progressive marrow failure; guide transfusion need |
| Reticulocyte count | Along with CBC | Assess marrow response after nutritional repletion |
| Telomere length (flow‑FISH) | Baseline, then annually | Document trajectory; influence transplant decisions |
| Liver elastography (FibroScan) | Every 6 months | Track progression of fibrosis/cirrhosis |
| Liver function tests (AST/ALT, bilirubin, albumin, INR) | Every 3 months | Monitor hepatic synthetic function |
| Abdominal ultrasound (portal hypertension screen) | Every 12 months or if clinical suspicion | Early detection of varices/ascites |
| Pulmonary function tests (FVC, DLCO) | Every 6 months | Quantify fibrosis progression; assess response to antifibrotics |
| High‑resolution chest CT | Baseline, then if > 10 % decline in FVC or symptom change | Structural evaluation of fibrosis |
| DXA scan | Baseline, then every 2 years | Monitor bone density, adjust osteoporosis therapy |
| Serum vitamin B12, folate, vitamin D | Every 3 months until stable, then annually | Ensure sustained repletion |
| Malignancy screening (e.g., age‑appropriate skin, breast, colorectal cancer) | According to national guidelines; consider earlier endoscopy if dysphagia or GI bleeding | TBDs confer increased cancer risk, especially squamous cell carcinoma of the head‑neck and hematologic malignancies |

All visits incorporate counselling on lifestyle modifications (smoking cessation, avoidance of occupational lung irritants, limiting alcohol, balanced nutrition) that can exacerbate organ injury.

### Emerging and Investigational Therapies  

While the standard of care remains supportive, several experimental approaches are under investigation for TBDs and may become relevant for patients such as this one.

* **Androgen therapy (danazol, oxymetholone, nandrolone)** – These agents increase telomerase activity indirectly via up‑regulation of TERT expression, modestly elongating telomeres in small pilot studies. Danazol 800 mg daily has been associated with a mean telomere length increase of 0.5 kb over 2 years and delayed progression of bone‑marrow failure. Potential adverse effects (hepatic toxicity, lipid abnormalities, virilization) necessitate careful hepatic monitoring, which is a particular concern in patients with existing cirrhosis.

* **Telomerase activators (e.g., GRN510, imtelstat)** – Small‑molecule compounds designed to enhance telomerase assembly or stabilize the TERC RNA are in early‑phase clinical trials. Evidence to date is limited to safety data; efficacy in preventing fibrosis or marrow failure remains to be demonstrated.

* **Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT)** – Allogeneic HSCT can cure marrow failure but does not halt pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis and carries heightened risk of transplant‑related toxicity in telomere‑shortened patients (e.g., severe mucositis, delayed engraftment, pulmonary complications). Reduced‑intensity conditioning regimens are preferred, and donor selection must consider telomere length of the graft source. For patients whose primary morbidity is organ fibrosis rather than marrow failure, HSCT is not indicated.

* **Gene‑editing approaches (CRISPR‑Cas9, base editors)** – In vitro correction of pathogenic PARN variants has been demonstrated in patient‑derived induced pluripotent stem cells, but clinical translation is still several years away. Ongoing research may eventually allow precise correction of monoallelic variants, potentially normalising telomere maintenance.

In summary, this patient’s management combined acute correction of reversible metabolic deficiencies, targeted supportive care for cytopenia, bone health, and organ fibrosis, and a comprehensive surveillance strategy aimed at early detection of disease progression. Genetic counselling addressed the uncertainty inherent to a VUS while outlining realistic reproductive options. As investigational therapies mature, they may offer disease‑modifying possibilities for individuals with telomere‑related disorders, but at present the cornerstone of care remains meticulous supportive management and longitudinal monitoring.
