Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

## Beyond the Mucocutaneous Triad: Recognizing “Silent” Dyskeratosis Congenita in Adults  

*Why a 28‑year‑old woman with pancytopenia, liver and lung fibrosis, premature greying, and osteoporosis should make you think of a telomere biology disorder—even when the classic skin‑nail‑mouth signs are missing.*

---

### Why this case caught our eye  

When we see a young adult with a cocktail of fatigue, cytopenias, and organ fibrosis we instinctively launch a work‑up for acquired causes—nutritional deficiencies, autoimmune disease, chronic infections, or even occult malignancy. The patient described in the case report, however, kept throwing curveballs:

* **Severe B12 deficiency** explained the macro‑macrocytosis and some neurologic signs, but correcting the deficiency left a sizeable “gap”—persistent pancytopenia, progressive hepatic stiffness, and sub‑clinical interstitial lung disease.
* **Premature greying**, knuckle hyper‑pigmentation, and a bald tongue hinted at accelerated ageing, yet there was **no oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy or reticular skin pigmentation**—the classic triad of dyskeratosis congenita (DC).
* **Bone‑pain, fractures, and osteoporosis** in a 20‑something are unusual unless you think of secondary causes (cortisol excess, chronic steroid use, hyperparathyroidism). The labs showed secondary hyperparathyroidism, but the underlying driver was still missing.

The pattern—multisystem fibrosis plus a subtle ageing phenotype—should raise suspicion for a **telomere biology disorder (TBD)**, even when mucocutaneous hallmarks are absent. In this patient a monoallelic **PARN** missense variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) emerged as the unifying genetic clue.

---

### TL;DR: Take‑home pearls  

- **TBDs can present without the classic DC triad**; look for premature greying, early osteoporosis, organ fibrosis, and cytopenias.  
- **Monoallelic PARN variants** are now recognized as a cause of combined pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, and bone‑marrow stress.  
- **Genetic testing** (clinical exome or targeted panels) plus **telomere length measurement** should be in the work‑up of any young adult with unexplained multisystem disease.  
- Early diagnosis guides **surveillance (pulmonary, hepatic, hematologic)**, informs **family counselling**, and opens the door to specific therapeutic options (androgen therapy, HSCT) when appropriate.  

---

## 1. The expanding phenotype of telomere disorders  

Historically, DC was taught as a triad of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin hyper‑pigmentation, usually surfacing in childhood. Over the past decade, we’ve learned that:

| Classic DC features | Frequently *absent* in adult TBDs |
|----------------------|-----------------------------------|
| Oral leukoplakia     | ✔ (often missing) |
| Nail dystrophy       | ✔ (often missing) |
| Reticular skin pigmentation | ✔ (often missing) |

Instead, adults may display any combination of the following:

- **Bone‑marrow failure:** pancytopenia with low reticulocytes, often precipitated by an additional stressor (e.g., B12 deficiency, chemotherapy).  
- **Pulmonary fibrosis:** frequently presents as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) in the 30‑50 yr age group.  
- **Hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis:** high‑resolution elastography shows stiff liver even before overt portal hypertension.  
- **Premature ageing signs:** early greying, cataracts, osteoporosis, and sometimes dental anomalies.  
- **Neurologic involvement:** peripheral neuropathy or neuro‑cognitive changes are increasingly reported.

PARN, a poly(A) ribonuclease, is required for proper maturation of the telomerase RNA component (TERC). Haploinsufficiency (monoallelic pathogenic variants) leads to subtle telomere shortening—enough to tip the balance in high‑turnover tissues without producing the full‑blown DC skin picture.

---

## 2. When to suspect a “silent” TBD  

A **systematic red‑flag checklist** helps keep TBD on your differential:

1. **Age < 40** with *any* of the following clusters:
   - Unexplained cytopenias (especially low retic count)  
   - Interstitial lung disease / UIP pattern on CT in a non‑smoker  
   - Unexplained hepatic fibrosis or early cirrhosis (no alcohol, viral, autoimmune cause)  
   - Premature greying, loss of hair, or other ageing‑related signs  
   - Early‑onset osteoporosis or fragility fractures  
2. **Family history** may be silent (de novo mutation) but ask about:
   - Early deaths from liver disease, pulmonary disease, or bone‑marrow failure  
   - Unexplained infertility or miscarriages (telomere defects affect gametogenesis)  
3. **Laboratory clues**:
   - Dimorphic anemia, hypersegmented neutrophils (ineffective erythropoiesis)  
   - Elevated ferritin with normal iron studies (ineffective erythropoiesis)  
   - Low or absent telomere length on flow‑FISH (≤1st percentile for age)  

If two or more organ systems are involved, the pre‑test probability for a TBD jumps dramatically.

---

## 3. Practical diagnostic algorithm  

```
Young adult with multisystem fibrosis or cytopenia
│
├─► Exclude common acquired causes first
│    • Nutrient deficiencies (B12, folate, iron)
│    • Autoimmune disease (ANA, anti‑ENA, IgG4)
│    • Viral hepatitis, HIV, EBV, CMV
│    • Alcohol, drugs, environmental exposures
│
├─► If work‑up is negative and ≥2 organ systems affected:
│    • Order quantitative telomere length (flow‑FISH or qPCR)
│    • If telomeres < 1st percentile → proceed to genetic testing
│
├─► Genetic testing options
│    • Targeted TBD panel (TERT, TERC, PARN, RTEL1, DKC1, etc.)
│    • Clinical exome sequencing if panel negative
│
└─► Interpretation
     • Pathogenic/likely pathogenic variant → diagnosis of TBD
     • Variant of uncertain significance (VUS) in a gene with strong phenotype
         → consider functional studies, familial segregation, and close follow‑up
```

**Key tip:** Telomere length measurement is often the cheapest “gatekeeper” test. Values that fall below the 10th percentile for age are suspicious; a result below the 1st percentile is highly suggestive of a pathogenic TBD.

## 4. Where does this leave management?  

### a. Surveillance  

| System | Recommended monitoring |
|--------|------------------------|
| **Pulmonary** | Baseline HRCT, then yearly PFTs (DLCO, FVC) and symptom review |
| **Hepatic** | Ultrasound + elastography every 12‑24 mo; screen for portal hypertension |
| **Hematologic** | CBC every 3‑6 mo; retic count; consider bone‑marrow aspirate if progressive |
| **Skeletal** | DEXA every 2 yr; fall risk assessment |
| **Ophthalmologic** | Annual slit‑lamp exam (cataracts, retinal telangiectasia) |

### b. Therapeutic options  

- **Androgen therapy (danazol, nandrolone)**: modest telomere elongation reported in small series; monitor liver function and testosterone‑related side effects.  
- **Hematopoietic stem‑cell transplantation (HSCT)**: potentially curative for marrow failure but carries high mortality in patients with pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis—requires careful risk‑benefit analysis.  
- **Lung or liver transplantation**: Consider when organ failure is the dominant life‑limiting factor; telomere length may predict post‑transplant complications (e.g., poor wound healing, chronic rejection).  

### c. Lifestyle & preventive care  

- Avoid smoking, vaping, and occupational dust exposures—these accelerate telomere attrition.  
- Minimize alcohol intake to protect the liver.  
- Optimize vitamin D, calcium, and consider anabolic agents for severe osteoporosis.  
- Provide genetic counselling—first‑degree relatives may need testing even if asymptomatic.

---

## 5. A final note on the case  

The patient’s **monoallelic PARN missense variant** (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) is still classified as a VUS, yet the phenotypic fit is compelling. This highlights a broader challenge: our databases lag behind clinical reality. When you encounter a VUS in a gene with a strong phenotype, treat the **clinical picture** as the primary guide and arrange longitudinal monitoring.

The good news is that once a TBD is recognized, you can **proactively surveil** for organ decline, counsel family members, and discuss emerging disease‑modifying therapies before irreversible damage accrues.

---

### Bottom line  

Don’t let the absence of a leukoplakic tongue or dystrophic nails lull you into thinking telomere disease is off the table. In young adults, a **multisystem fibrotic or cytopenic presentation**—especially when accompanied by premature greying or osteoporosis—should prompt a **telomere work‑up**. Early detection changes everything: it sharpens surveillance, informs transplant candidacy, and offers a chance for targeted therapy before the disease runs its full course.  

Keep your eyes on the “silent” DC; its whispers are louder than you think.
