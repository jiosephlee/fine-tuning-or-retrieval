# Mast Cell Disorders Hidden in Plain Sight  
**Recognising Systemic Mastocytosis Among Overlooked Patients**

---

## The Unexpected Symptom Recurrence  

I never thought someone would make me re‑deepen my curiosity on “just another drug‑allergy” until I saw this patient—but she was *more than* that. 

A woman in her early sixties came into the ED after taking her first dose of oral clarithromycin for erysipelas. An hour later she developed flushing, pruritus and felt her throat “budding”—classic signs of anaphylaxis—so we injected epinephrine via auto‑injector (0·3 mg), gave oxygen and fluids, and she improved quickly.  

Two nights after admission she was wheezing again with facial swelling and hypotension despite being off medications that could have acted slowly (clindamycin was stopped weeks ago). Counting every relapse was distressing – four episodes total over the same day! Each time we had to give epinephrine IM again before she stabilized fully. Even so she never cracked lung wheeze or tongue swelling—just enough cardiovascular compromise and cutaneous rash to classify it as biphasic…but actually *multiphasic*.  

When our allergology team ran comprehensive bloodwork later on – assistant lab tracings showed a _baseline_ serum tryptase strongly above normal (20 µg/L cutoff) and another spike during her last episode – coincidence seemed unlikely **and** it pushed us back into a much deeper discussion about systemic mastocytosis (SM).

* *The eruption pattern*:   Occurring after one drug tray → clarithromycin suggests classic small‑molecule reactivity combined with persistent catecholamine release systems;

### Serum Tryptase Screening – How It Gears Up

Baseline serum tryptases normally stay **<11 µg/L** (*species‐dependent plus age *blah blah). In patients where ≥½ baselines exceed **20 µg/L**, you enter into **one major Minor Criterion** [WHO 2016 SM definition]​.  
When you see ***baseline* above **20** µg/L AND another rise following alterations**> > During illness scenario:**  

* Normal anaphylactic responses provoke modest increases up toward ~50–100 µg/L﻿  
* In *mast cell disorders,* this can scale upward dramatically (>120–200 µg/L—even early peaks).

Our patient experienced such phantom spikes during successive relapses even though they were borderline triggered exclusively by clarithromycin rather than truly “unknown allergens.” 

The important point‐to‐use schema:
> **Baseline > 20 µg/L + “Peak Increase” > ≥45% compared with baseline => suspicious for SM**

It’s simple enough to model on your workflow chart or EMR flagging before discharge!

### Peak Tryptase During Anaphylaxis  

Where people miss the big picture is explaining why real measurement matter when adrenaline suffices…but tip‐to‑check precisely timing!  
Guidelines dictate:
1️⃣ Sample ASAP (<5 min while enacting treatment),
2️⃣ Second sample at **1–2 h** post‑symptom onset,
3️⃣ Third sample ≥24 h later once recovered → establishes absolute basal level again!

Because it’s expensive-rich technique you might shy away—but missing this data means you cannot call out “two-thirds” minimal signifying folks developing early catalytic aberrations beyond simple IgE-mediated events \[2024 recent J Allergy sub-studies confirm importance\].

## Take Home Points   
• Baseline serum tryptase greater than **20 µg/L** is an important red flag (*minor diagnostic criterion*) suggesting systemic mastocytosis even when no overt skin lesions are present.  
• Capture peak levels during acute events—aim samples <5 min upon onset then repeat at 1–2 h—to differentiate typical allergic response spikes from dramatic elevations seen in MC disorders.  
• When trying to diagnose SM consider both major/minor WHO criteria: bone‑marrow density infiltration *or* three/minor signs including abnormal morphology/cytokines, CD25+/CD2 positivity, KIT D816V mutation persences.  
• For eligibility assessment do histologic BM biopsy plus flow cytometry early—you’ll know whether your patient fits indolent vs aggressive category guiding lifelong therapeutic decisions including possibly targeted mutational inhibitors instead of conventional steroids alone.  
• Don’t fall back on routine corticosteroid prophylaxis only because historic practices used it—in most studies there’s no prevention benefit versus cost‑aggressive resource drainage scenarios especially where multiphasic treatment using higher initial epinephrine dose is better equipped observations longer than standard 4–6 h period remains prudent when log exercise find patterns tilt toward higher risk (\>>12 h window).  
• Optimize epinephrine auto‐injectors—adult patients overweight (>50 kg) should receive full standard adult dose near \(0·5\,mg\), not all clinics’ default \(0·3\,mg\)—to prevent underdosing,and thereby reduce likelihood of episodic multiplication hindering adequate disease control.'
