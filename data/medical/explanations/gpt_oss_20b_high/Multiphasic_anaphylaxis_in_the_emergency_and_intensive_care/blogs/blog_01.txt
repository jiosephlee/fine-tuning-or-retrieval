Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# Multiphasic Anaphylaxis Unveiled: Why Some Reactions Keep Coming Back

## The “never‑ending” episode

I’ll start with the headline—our patient is a woman in her sixties who walked into our ED one hour after swallowing a single dose of clarithromycin and left four hours later still alive but already on her second dose of epinephrine. In less than 12 hours she had suffered **four distinct anaphylactic episodes**—the first in the ED and three subsequent relapses while in hospital—before finally cooling down enough to be discharged three days later. What makes this story stand out is not just the sheer number of recurrences but also what we learned afterward: her baseline tryptase was elevated, imaging hinted at hepatomegaly, and we eventually considered systemic mastocytosis even though she never met full diagnostic criteria.

In short: *multiphasic anaphylaxis is real,* can happen even when you’ve “treated” the patient adequately, and it demands a different lens than you get from textbook “one‑off” reactions.

---

## Biphasic vs Multiphasic – Definitions that Matter

| Guideline | Definition | Key Points |
|-----------|------------|------------|
| **UK Resuscitation Council** | Recurrence of symptoms **after apparent resolution** without re‑exposure | Uses “biphasic” for one recurrence; no formal term for >2 |
| **Dutch NHG** | Similar concept—symptoms recur after initial improvement | Emphasizes timing window up to 24 h |
| **WAO (World Allergy Organization)** | Recurrence within 72 h after first event | Does not define “multiphasic” explicitly |
| **Our case** | Four distinct episodes over ~12 h | Illustrates “multiphasic” as multiple recurrences |

The terminology is inconsistent because most guidelines were written before the term “multiphasic” entered common usage. In practice we often use it informally when patients have more than one relapse—exactly what our patient did.

---

## Incidence – How Often Do These Come Back?

Reported incidence varies wildly—from **0.4 %** up to **23 %**—but most contemporary series cluster around **4–6 %** for biphasic reactions when strict criteria are applied. Multiphasic cases are even rarer, so estimates are even less reliable because they’re usually anecdotal or single‑center observations like ours. The takeaway? If you see one recurrent episode you might be dealing with a minority—but if you see several it’s likely part of a pattern that many clinicians will only ever encounter once or twice over their careers.

---

## Timing Window – When Do They Return?

The classic teaching is “watch for 4–6 hours.” Reality is messier:

* Most biphasics occur **within 6–12 hours** after symptom resolution.
* Some cases recur as late as **78 hours** (over three days) after initial exposure.
* In our patient all four relapses happened **within the first 12 hours**, but note that she had only taken clarithromycin once—so residual drug absorption was unlikely to explain all episodes.

Because timing can be so variable—and because guidelines don’t give us hard cut‑offs—it’s safer to adopt a risk‑stratified observation strategy rather than rely on a single time window.

---

## Clinical Clues – How Do You Tell It’s Not Residual Exposure?

1. **Timing relative to last dose**  
   * If symptoms return more than a few hours after stopping an oral medication or after finishing a food challenge, think true biphasia rather than lingering allergen absorption.

2. **Severity trajectory**  
   * In our patient each relapse was progressively less severe—a pattern sometimes seen when the body has already mobilized mediators but still has remaining mast cells ready to fire again (especially if there’s an underlying mast cell disorder).

3. **Tryptase sampling**  
   * Guidelines recommend three samples: at presentation, 1–2 h later (peak), and >24 h after resolution (baseline). Our patient’s first sample was taken only during her third episode—a missed opportunity that likely contributed to uncertainty about peak levels versus baseline elevations seen in systemic mastocytosis.

4. **Underlying conditions**  
   * Elevated baseline tryptase (>11 ng/mL) plus hepatomegaly raises suspicion for systemic mastocytosis—a known risk factor for severe and recurrent anaphylaxis.

5. **Auto‑injector dosing issues**  
   * The first two doses were delivered via a 0.3 mg auto‑injector despite her weight (>50 kg). Underdosing can predispose patients to rebound because full therapeutic levels weren’t achieved initially—a possible contributor here.

---

## Management Dilemmas – Observation & Treatment

### Observation
* Traditional guidelines suggest 4–6 hours post‑resolution monitoring on general wards.
* The UK tiered approach recommends longer observation (up to 24 hours) for high‑risk patients—those who received multiple epinephrine doses or have severe initial presentations.
* In our case we escalated her care to ICU after the first relapse because she had two prior doses within hours—an approach consistent with tiered recommendations but lacking firm evidence on optimal duration beyond this point.

### Treatment
* **Epinephrine dosing**  
   * The standard adult dose is **0.01 mg/kg**, capped at 0.5 mg per injection—not the default auto‑injector dose of 0.3 mg often stocked in hospitals or pharmacies.
   * Evidence suggests patients who receive only 0.3 mg are more likely to need additional doses or ICU admission compared with those who receive full adult doses.
* **Corticosteroids**  
   * Historically used prophylactically against biphasia—but systematic reviews show no benefit and possible harm (higher ICU/hospital admission rates). Most current guidelines advise against routine steroids though many clinicians still administer them out of habit.
   * In our patient we gave both prednisone daily for five days and dexamethasone once because institutional protocols differed between ward and ICU—illustrating how practice can vary despite evidence gaps.
* **Continuous IV epinephrine?**  
   * Considered by allergology consult but deemed too risky given lack of clear benefit data versus potential complications from prolonged vasopressor support.
* **Antihistamines & fluids**  
   * We continued clemastine IV during episodes and gave supplemental saline during second relapse—standard supportive care steps that help mitigate cutaneous symptoms and maintain volume status.

---

## Key Take‑Home Pearls

- Multiphasic anaphylaxis can recur up to ~80 hours after initial exposure—even without re‑exposure—so keep vigilance beyond the first day if risk factors exist.
- Use weight‑based epinephrine dosing (0.01 mg/kg) rather than default auto‑injector amounts; underdosing may precipitate rebound reactions.
- Obtain tryptase samples at presentation, ~1–2 h later (peak), and >24 h post‑resolution—missing these intervals reduces diagnostic clarity especially if systemic mastocytosis is suspected.
- Persistent or recurrent reactions raise suspicion for underlying mast cell disorders; consider referral for specialized evaluation when baseline tryptase >11 ng/mL or other clues arise.
- Current evidence does not support routine corticosteroid prophylaxis for biphasia or multipharasia; use them selectively based on individual risk assessment rather than protocol mandates.
- Observation periods should be tailored using risk stratification tools—for example UK tiered approach—rather than fixed times alone; high‑risk patients may warrant ICU monitoring until symptom-free status persists ≥12–24 hours.
- Document every episode meticulously—including timing relative to last drug dose—to aid differential between true recurrence versus residual exposure.
- When encountering multiphasia, consider contributing your case data to registries or consensus forums—collective experience can help shape future evidence‑based guidance where randomized trials are impossible due to rarity.

---

Multiplexing your clinical vigilance with these pearls will help you recognize when a seemingly “resolved” reaction is merely pausing before roaring back again—and ensure you’re prepared not just physically but also protocolually when those roars return."
