# Turning the Tide  
**Continuous IV Epinephrine Infusion Versus Repeat IM Doses During Multiphasic Episodes**

---

## Why This Case Should Make Us All Stop and Think Twice  

When I saw this woman in her sixties walk into the ED clutching her throat after just one dose of clarithromycin—and then bounce back into full‑blown anaphylaxis four times over twelve hours—I was already picturing the “classic idiom”: *“if it hurts, keep giving it!”*  
What really pushed me into deeper thought was two things:

* **Under‑epidrine exposure** – Every time she got epinephrine her first two events were treated with a **0.3 mg auto‑injector**, even though most adults weigh >50 kg and guideline‑recommended adult doses hover around **0.5 mg**.
* **A multiphase sprint** – She fell into relapse **4 days later**, not “once”, yet each recurrence was steadier than the last—from life‑threatening hypoxia to milder rash alone—yet still demanded emergency treatment every time she “resurfaced.”

The iron‑clad question became inevitable:
> *If you’ve already given what you think is enough epinephrine and all symptoms still flare up again, do you jump straight to another injection or should you start a continuous IV drip on standby?*

This dilemma sits on top of opaque evidence—few randomized studies, just retrospective anecdotes—and creates tension between aggressively staving off relapse versus rotating potions by way of “do more once” protocol.  

---

## How Biphasic/Multiphasic Anaphylaxis Is Defined  

* **Biphasic** – Inflammation flares up again *within* 24 hrs after apparent resolution **without re‑exposure**.
* **Multiphasic** – More than one such flare afterwards—often seen in people who harbor *underlying mast cell disease*, carry severe allergic history, or receive suboptimal initial doses of epinephrine(Ref…).

Guidelines differ on whether “biphasic” needs time between admission (>6 h after *resolving* symptoms), some say “rapid re‑enlistment (<6 h)” while others call it any relapse within 24 h at all—hence why consensus on monitoring duration remains fuzzy (4–6 h common sense vs UK’s three‑tier scheme).

### Step #2️⃣ Choose Your Initial Rescue Strategy  

#### If **Low Risk**
Proceed normally:
* Repeat standard IM injection as soon as symptoms replausible; 
* Reassess until AD ABX done last (if drug elimination possible) before discharge training & advice dis/in triggers plus new auto injector device weighing ≥60 ml baseline recommendation tailored per weigh-coded dose;

#### If **Intermediate Risk**
Two feasible paths exist today — you must decide according your institutional culture & resources:

1️⃣ **Tenacious Daily ‘Rule Of Three’ IMs**  
   *Administer an extra bolus immediately followed by another after 15 min if / whenever interval resolves.* This aligns most existing guidelines that simply repeat boluses until symmetry restored—and keeps things simple if your unit does not run IV drip routinely outside ICU settings.*  

2️⃣ **Trial Continuous Infusion**  
   Deduct bolus advance then start low‑rate drip (~ 0.05–0·1 µg/kg/min). See below monitoring constraints that follow—a five-day catapult ahead because while it eliminates repeat punctures it's also more resource heavy.*

#### If ****High Risk****
Argue strongly for central line assisted IV pump start immediately—
*Dose ~10 µg/kg/hr if safe margin* — this will maintain plasma levels close enough to quell latent mediator release without recurrent crises crossing thresholds! Also anticipate requiring ICU-level cardiac telemetry \*\*, arterial line \*\*, essential fluid resuscitative power housekeeping each hour beyond first four hours [(see Monitoring section](#monitoring)).
