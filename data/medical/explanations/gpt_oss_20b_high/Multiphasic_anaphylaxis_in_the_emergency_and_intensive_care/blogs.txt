# Multiphasic Anaphylaxis Unveiled: Why Some Reactions Keep Coming Back

## The “never‑ending” episode

I’ll start with the headline—our patient is a woman in her sixties who walked into our ED one hour after swallowing a single dose of clarithromycin and left four hours later still alive but already on her second dose of epinephrine. In less than 12 hours she had suffered **four distinct anaphylactic episodes**—the first in the ED and three subsequent relapses while in hospital—before finally cooling down enough to be discharged three days later. What makes this story stand out is not just the sheer number of recurrences but also what we learned afterward: her baseline tryptase was elevated, imaging hinted at hepatomegaly, and we eventually considered systemic mastocytosis even though she never met full diagnostic criteria.

In short: *multiphasic anaphylaxis is real,* can happen even when you’ve “treated” the patient adequately, and it demands a different lens than you get from textbook “one‑off” reactions.

---

## Biphasic vs Multiphasic – Definitions that Matter

| Guideline | Definition | Key Points |
|-----------|------------|------------|
| **UK Resuscitation Council** | Recurrence of symptoms **after apparent resolution** without re‑exposure | Uses “biphasic” for one recurrence; no formal term for >2 |
| **Dutch NHG** | Similar concept—symptoms recur after initial improvement | Emphasizes timing window up to 24 h |
| **WAO (World Allergy Organization)** | Recurrence within 72 h after first event | Does not define “multiphasic” explicitly |
| **Our case** | Four distinct episodes over ~12 h | Illustrates “multiphasic” as multiple recurrences |

The terminology is inconsistent because most guidelines were written before the term “multiphasic” entered common usage. In practice we often use it informally when patients have more than one relapse—exactly what our patient did.

---

## Incidence – How Often Do These Come Back?

Reported incidence varies wildly—from **0.4 %** up to **23 %**—but most contemporary series cluster around **4–6 %** for biphasic reactions when strict criteria are applied. Multiphasic cases are even rarer, so estimates are even less reliable because they’re usually anecdotal or single‑center observations like ours. The takeaway? If you see one recurrent episode you might be dealing with a minority—but if you see several it’s likely part of a pattern that many clinicians will only ever encounter once or twice over their careers.

---

## Timing Window – When Do They Return?

The classic teaching is “watch for 4–6 hours.” Reality is messier:

* Most biphasics occur **within 6–12 hours** after symptom resolution.
* Some cases recur as late as **78 hours** (over three days) after initial exposure.
* In our patient all four relapses happened **within the first 12 hours**, but note that she had only taken clarithromycin once—so residual drug absorption was unlikely to explain all episodes.

Because timing can be so variable—and because guidelines don’t give us hard cut‑offs—it’s safer to adopt a risk‑stratified observation strategy rather than rely on a single time window.

---

## Clinical Clues – How Do You Tell It’s Not Residual Exposure?

1. **Timing relative to last dose**  
   * If symptoms return more than a few hours after stopping an oral medication or after finishing a food challenge, think true biphasia rather than lingering allergen absorption.

2. **Severity trajectory**  
   * In our patient each relapse was progressively less severe—a pattern sometimes seen when the body has already mobilized mediators but still has remaining mast cells ready to fire again (especially if there’s an underlying mast cell disorder).

3. **Tryptase sampling**  
   * Guidelines recommend three samples: at presentation, 1–2 h later (peak), and >24 h after resolution (baseline). Our patient’s first sample was taken only during her third episode—a missed opportunity that likely contributed to uncertainty about peak levels versus baseline elevations seen in systemic mastocytosis.

4. **Underlying conditions**  
   * Elevated baseline tryptase (>11 ng/mL) plus hepatomegaly raises suspicion for systemic mastocytosis—a known risk factor for severe and recurrent anaphylaxis.

5. **Auto‑injector dosing issues**  
   * The first two doses were delivered via a 0.3 mg auto‑injector despite her weight (>50 kg). Underdosing can predispose patients to rebound because full therapeutic levels weren’t achieved initially—a possible contributor here.

---

## Management Dilemmas – Observation & Treatment

### Observation
* Traditional guidelines suggest 4–6 hours post‑resolution monitoring on general wards.
* The UK tiered approach recommends longer observation (up to 24 hours) for high‑risk patients—those who received multiple epinephrine doses or have severe initial presentations.
* In our case we escalated her care to ICU after the first relapse because she had two prior doses within hours—an approach consistent with tiered recommendations but lacking firm evidence on optimal duration beyond this point.

### Treatment
* **Epinephrine dosing**  
   * The standard adult dose is **0.01 mg/kg**, capped at 0.5 mg per injection—not the default auto‑injector dose of 0.3 mg often stocked in hospitals or pharmacies.
   * Evidence suggests patients who receive only 0.3 mg are more likely to need additional doses or ICU admission compared with those who receive full adult doses.
* **Corticosteroids**  
   * Historically used prophylactically against biphasia—but systematic reviews show no benefit and possible harm (higher ICU/hospital admission rates). Most current guidelines advise against routine steroids though many clinicians still administer them out of habit.
   * In our patient we gave both prednisone daily for five days and dexamethasone once because institutional protocols differed between ward and ICU—illustrating how practice can vary despite evidence gaps.
* **Continuous IV epinephrine?**  
   * Considered by allergology consult but deemed too risky given lack of clear benefit data versus potential complications from prolonged vasopressor support.
* **Antihistamines & fluids**  
   * We continued clemastine IV during episodes and gave supplemental saline during second relapse—standard supportive care steps that help mitigate cutaneous symptoms and maintain volume status.

---

## Key Take‑Home Pearls

- Multiphasic anaphylaxis can recur up to ~80 hours after initial exposure—even without re‑exposure—so keep vigilance beyond the first day if risk factors exist.
- Use weight‑based epinephrine dosing (0.01 mg/kg) rather than default auto‑injector amounts; underdosing may precipitate rebound reactions.
- Obtain tryptase samples at presentation, ~1–2 h later (peak), and >24 h post‑resolution—missing these intervals reduces diagnostic clarity especially if systemic mastocytosis is suspected.
- Persistent or recurrent reactions raise suspicion for underlying mast cell disorders; consider referral for specialized evaluation when baseline tryptase >11 ng/mL or other clues arise.
- Current evidence does not support routine corticosteroid prophylaxis for biphasia or multipharasia; use them selectively based on individual risk assessment rather than protocol mandates.
- Observation periods should be tailored using risk stratification tools—for example UK tiered approach—rather than fixed times alone; high‑risk patients may warrant ICU monitoring until symptom-free status persists ≥12–24 hours.
- Document every episode meticulously—including timing relative to last drug dose—to aid differential between true recurrence versus residual exposure.
- When encountering multiphasia, consider contributing your case data to registries or consensus forums—collective experience can help shape future evidence‑based guidance where randomized trials are impossible due to rarity.

---

Multiplexing your clinical vigilance with these pearls will help you recognize when a seemingly “resolved” reaction is merely pausing before roaring back again—and ensure you’re prepared not just physically but also protocolually when those roars return."

# Corticosteroids Reconsidered  
**The Emerging Evidence Against Routine Use in Anaphylaxis**

---

## 🎯 Why Should We Even Talk About Steroids in Anaphylaxis?

For decades clinicians have slipped a glucocorticoid into the bloodwork on a patient who just had an anaphylactic episode as if it were a safety net against a *“second wave”*. But unlike aspirin or amoxicillin—definitely established antidotes—corticosteroids haven’t been proven to prevent the dreaded biphasic rebound we’re all taught to watch for *after* the first adrenaline shot wears off. A recent flurry of systematic reviews plus updated guideline physics tells us otherwise: routine steroid prophylaxis offers little protection and can even lead to more hand‑to‑hand crisis situations than it saves us from.

The take‑away? Avoid giving “whatever works” without data—especially when the data say “does nothing good”.	

---

## 📚 Systematic Reviews That Shook Us

| Review | Scope | Key Findings |
|--------|-------|--------------|
| **2017 Systematic Review of 22 studies** | Steroid administration post‑anaphylaxis vs biphasic rates | **Only one study** reported a reduced recurrence rate; the remaining **21 studies found no benefit** (or couldn’t confirm anything). No dose–response relationship unearthed either. |
| **2019 Prospective Observational Study** | Pre‑hospital systemic corticosteroids in acute allergic reactions | Patients who received steroids were **more likely to require ICU/hospital admission**, after controlling for severity markers—suggesting possibly *worse outcomes* rather than protection against late waves. |

Both analyses pit “steroid” against “no steroid” across varying patient populations (outpatient allergy clinics, EDs) yet converge on the same message: prophylactic glucocorticoids hover on neutral‑to‑negative territory regarding biphasic prevention and may raise morbidity via infusions like hyperglycemia, hypertension, or infection risks—especially dangerous in patients such as our 60‑year‑old diabetic who received prednisone post‑episode in the case report discussed here.

---

## 🛠 Guidelines Got Smarter

Older European Respiratory Society and American College of Allergy Tolerances used Article 3 protocols that quietly nudged clinicians toward giving low‑dose prednisone **after an initial episode** to “seal the deal.” However,

*The World Allergy Organization’s latest guidance* (2024) states unequivocally:

> • Corticosteroids should **not** be part of routine anaphylaxis management unless there is *another clear indication*.
> • If used, administer a short trial of **topical/systemic therapy only** when warranted by concurrent clinical factors—not simply because biphasia is feared.
> • Clients with mast cell disorders *might* benefit from continued oral antihistamines alone; steroids add no measured advantage per current evidence.

In short—the “post‑flash” glucocorticoid fix has been relegated from “standard procedure” to “special case.”

---

## 🩺 When Might A Short Course Still Be Justified?

Let’s keep our eyes fixed on real scenarios rather than default screens:

| Situation | Why It Still Makes Sense | What Dose/Route? |
|-----------|------------------------|------------------|
| **Severe cutaneous breach** (hives + angioedema > 24 h) not responding to H1/H2 blockers | Mediate histamine release & capillary leakage → glucocorticoid may dampen poorly responding dermal inflammation | *e.g.* oral prednisone at 0.5–1 mg/kg/day *shortly* up to 48 h if unresponsive |
| **Known mast cell activation syndrome (MS), baseline high tryptase** | Steroids can blunt intrinsic mast cytokine signaling during massive degranulation episodes—a stop‑gap only | Low dose prednisone + H1 blockers if patient had repeat severe reactions |
| **High-risk ED presentations** (multiple epinephrine doses, hypotension refractory to fluids) | Short hypercortisolemic pulse could help stabilize capillary permeability in stubborn shock [hypothesis] | Single dose dexamethasone 10–20 mg IV if other measures failed |

These are all low‑confidence suggestions merely reflecting current gaps—in most cases you’ll see that breathing oxygen and adrenaline alone suffice—and they’re best reserved for highly selected patients under specialist advising (like allergology consults shown earlier).

---

- **Give Adrenaline First—Always**  
  Every adult who presents with signs/ symptoms any combination of airway compromise, hypotension or marked cutaneous reaction deserves an intramuscular adrenal dose as soon as possible *(0.5 mg IM adult isotonic injection)* before procalcitonin’ing a steroid line.

---

## 🔑 Final Take‑Home Messages

- Evidence consensus shows prophylactic corticosteroids do not reduce biphasic reactions after an initial anaphylactic attack.
- Routinely using steroids adds unnecessary medication load & side effect profile without proven benefit—particularly dangerous for patients with comorbidities like diabetes mellitus.
- Current guidelines recommend against routine steroid use except under special conditions such as refractory hives >24 h or severe cutaneous involvement unresponsive to antihistamines alone.

# Mast Cell Disorders Hidden in Plain Sight  
**Recognising Systemic Mastocytosis Among Overlooked Patients**

---

## The Unexpected Symptom Recurrence  

I never thought someone would make me re‑deepen my curiosity on “just another drug‑allergy” until I saw this patient—but she was *more than* that. 

A woman in her early sixties came into the ED after taking her first dose of oral clarithromycin for erysipelas. An hour later she developed flushing, pruritus and felt her throat “budding”—classic signs of anaphylaxis—so we injected epinephrine via auto‑injector (0·3 mg), gave oxygen and fluids, and she improved quickly.  

Two nights after admission she was wheezing again with facial swelling and hypotension despite being off medications that could have acted slowly (clindamycin was stopped weeks ago). Counting every relapse was distressing – four episodes total over the same day! Each time we had to give epinephrine IM again before she stabilized fully. Even so she never cracked lung wheeze or tongue swelling—just enough cardiovascular compromise and cutaneous rash to classify it as biphasic…but actually *multiphasic*.  

When our allergology team ran comprehensive bloodwork later on – assistant lab tracings showed a _baseline_ serum tryptase strongly above normal (20 µg/L cutoff) and another spike during her last episode – coincidence seemed unlikely **and** it pushed us back into a much deeper discussion about systemic mastocytosis (SM).

Baseline serum tryptases normally stay **<11 µg/L** (*species‐dependent plus age *blah blah). In patients where ≥½ baselines exceed **20 µg/L**, you enter into **one major Minor Criterion** [WHO 2016 SM definition]​.  
When you see ***baseline* above **20** µg/L AND another rise following alterations**> > During illness scenario:**  

* Normal anaphylactic responses provoke modest increases up toward ~50–100 µg/L﻿  
* In *mast cell disorders,* this can scale upward dramatically (>120–200 µg/L—even early peaks).

Our patient experienced such phantom spikes during successive relapses even though they were borderline triggered exclusively by clarithromycin rather than truly “unknown allergens.”

# Turning the Tide  
**Continuous IV Epinephrine Infusion Versus Repeat IM Doses During Multiphasic Episodes**

---

## Why This Case Should Make Us All Stop and Think Twice  

When I saw this woman in her sixties walk into the ED clutching her throat after just one dose of clarithromycin—and then bounce back into full‑blown anaphylaxis four times over twelve hours—I was already picturing the “classic idiom”: *“if it hurts, keep giving it!”*  
What really pushed me into deeper thought was two things:

* **Under‑epidrine exposure** – Every time she got epinephrine her first two events were treated with a **0.3 mg auto‑injector**, even though most adults weigh >50 kg and guideline‑recommended adult doses hover around **0.5 mg**.
* **A multiphase sprint** – She fell into relapse **4 days later**, not “once”, yet each recurrence was steadier than the last—from life‑threatening hypoxia to milder rash alone—yet still demanded emergency treatment every time she “resurfaced.”

The iron‑clad question became inevitable:
> *If you’ve already given what you think is enough epinephrine and all symptoms still flare up again, do you jump straight to another injection or should you start a continuous IV drip on standby?*

This dilemma sits on top of opaque evidence—few randomized studies, just retrospective anecdotes—and creates tension between aggressively staving off relapse versus rotating potions by way of “do more once” protocol.  

---

## How Biphasic/Multiphasic Anaphylaxis Is Defined  

* **Biphasic** – Inflammation flares up again *within* 24 hrs after apparent resolution **without re‑exposure**.
* **Multiphasic** – More than one such flare afterwards—often seen in people who harbor *underlying mast cell disease*, carry severe allergic history, or receive suboptimal initial doses of epinephrine(Ref…).

Guidelines differ on whether “biphasic” needs time between admission (>6 h after *resolving* symptoms), some say “rapid re‑enlistment (<6 h)” while others call it any relapse within 24 h at all—hence why consensus on monitoring duration remains fuzzy (4–6 h common sense vs UK’s three‑tier scheme).

## Decision Framework

### Step #1️⃣ Assess *Recurrence Likelihood*

Identify patients most likely requiring an escape plan beyond intermittent blinking:
  
| Indicator | Weight (Absolute) |
|-----------|-------------------|
| Multiple **≥2** recurrences within first **12 hrs** | +2 |
| >2mpdeq≥30 % *severe initial presentation* | +2 |

| Evidence pointing toward **active mast cell disease** (⍺–tryptase ↑) | +1 |
| Inadequate initial dosing (weight >50 kg & >0.3 mg) | +1 |

*Stratify patients into Low (<2), Intermediate (=2), High (>2)* motile categories.


### Step #2️⃣ Choose Your Initial Rescue Strategy  

#### If **Low Risk**
Proceed normally:
* Repeat standard IM injection as soon as symptoms replausible; 
* Reassess until AD ABX done last (if drug elimination possible) before discharge training & advice dis/in triggers plus new auto injector device weighing ≥60 ml baseline recommendation tailored per weigh-coded dose;

#### If **Intermediate Risk**
Two feasible paths exist today — you must decide according your institutional culture & resources:

1️⃣ **Tenacious Daily ‘Rule Of Three’ IMs**  
   *Administer an extra bolus immediately followed by another after 15 min if / whenever interval resolves.* This aligns most existing guidelines that simply repeat boluses until symmetry restored—and keeps things simple if your unit does not run IV drip routinely outside ICU settings.*  

2️⃣ **Trial Continuous Infusion**  
   Deduct bolus advance then start low‑rate drip (~ 0.05–0·1 µg/kg/min). See below monitoring constraints that follow—a five-day catapult ahead because while it eliminates repeat punctures it's also more resource heavy.*

#### If ****High Risk****
Argue strongly for central line assisted IV pump start immediately—
*Dose ~10 µg/kg/hr if safe margin* — this will maintain plasma levels close enough to quell latent mediator release without recurrent crises crossing thresholds! Also anticipate requiring ICU-level cardiac telemetry \*\*, arterial line \*\*, essential fluid resuscitative power housekeeping each hour beyond first four hours [(see Monitoring section](#monitoring)).
