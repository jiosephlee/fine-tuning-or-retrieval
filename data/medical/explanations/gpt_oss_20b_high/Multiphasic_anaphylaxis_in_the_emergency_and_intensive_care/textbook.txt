## Initial Clinical Presentation and Emergency Management

### Patient demographics and pertinent past allergy history  

The patient was a woman in her sixties who weighed approximately 104 kg. She had a longstanding history of type 2 diabetes mellitus and hypertension managed by oral hypoglycemics and thiazide diuretics. Her prior allergic manifestations were relatively mild: a facial swelling reaction to amoxicillin during adolescence without systemic involvement, and a local reaction after a wasp sting without any systemic sequelae. No prior episodes of anaphylaxis had been recorded until the present presentation triggered by oral clarithromycin 500 mg—the first known exposure to this drug class in her lifetime. This history highlights that even occasional cutaneous reactions can presage severe systemic responses upon subsequent exposure or when another drug precipitates true IgE‑mediated mast cell activation.

### Onset timing relative to clarithromycin ingestion  

The patient ingested the single dose of clarithromycin approximately one hour before arriving at the emergency department (ED). Symptoms appeared shortly afterward—all classic features developed within one hour—which is characteristic of IgE‑mediated anaphylaxis where mast cell degranulation happens rapidly once the allergen binds specific IgE antibodies on high‑affinity FcεRI receptors present on resident mast cells throughout mucosa and skin. The short latency period helped clinicians promptly recognize physiological crisis rather than delay diagnosis awaiting signs associated with delayed non‑IgE reactions (e.g., food‑related stinging).

### Physical exam findings indicating anaphylactic shock  

On arrival she exhibited profound hypotension (BNP 74/50 mm Hg), mild tachypnea (22 breaths min⁻¹), and hypoxaemia (SpO₂ = 88 % RA). She also displayed marked dermatologic involvement—a generalized urticarial eruption extending from face downwards—with flushing and wheals covering almost her entire skin surface together with pruritus. Facial edema was present although tongue involvement or throat oedema was absent at this moment. Admission cardiac examination revealed sinus rhythm without arrhythmia, while lung auscultation was clear apart from decreased air‐entry supplied by tachypnea alone.

Combining cardiovascular collapse—hypotension paired with tachycardia (>100 b.p.m.)—with cutaneous manifestations meets two major criteria set forth in most consensus definitions of anaphylaxis (e.g., EAACI/WAO). As her mucosal airways were still patent, immediate airway protection was required only prototypically rather than invasively at presentation.

## Differential Diagnosis and Initial Laboratory Evaluation

Prompt differentiation between a classic IgE‑mediated anaphylactic reaction and a pseudo‑allergic reaction is essential because the therapeutic approach remains the same—prompt intramuscular epinephrine—but prognostication and further investigation differ markedly.(1) Typical allergic reactions are triggered by discrete antigens presented via MHC‑restricted pathways that activate antigen‑specific T cells and IgE‑producing B cells while simultaneously activating mast cells through FcεRI cross‑linking.(2) In contrast, pseudo‑allergic reactions arise from direct mast cell degranulation unrelated to IgE; classic culprits include radiocontrast media and certain antibiotics such as clarithromycin which can induce calcium influx through non‑IgE mechanisms.(3) Key separation points are therefore temporal association, presence of specific cutaneous manifestations (urticaria/angioedema), laboratory evidence (see next sections), and the absence/presence of specific allergen sensitization on skin testing or serum IgE assays—work‑ups typically performed after acute stabilization given the patient’s risk profile.(4)

The initial laboratory panel routinely requested in any suspected anaphylaxis episode is a **complete metabolic panel** (CMP), which provides critical data on serum electrolytes, glucose level, renal function (BUN & creatinine), liver enzymes (AST/ALT/GGT/alkaline phosphatase), total bilirubin, albumin/Ca++, and serum protein levels.(5) In this case the CMP revealed significant hyperglycemia (12.7 mmol/L) attributable to type 2 diabetes mellitus—a finding with immediate clinical relevance because poorly controlled glucose augments sympathetic drive and risk of shock progression while concurrently increasing risk for hepatic enzyme elevations that could alter medication metabolism.(6) Serum bicarbonate was normal on presentation but would have directed us toward possible metabolic acidosis secondary to renal hypoperfusion should it have been low—a common complication in profound hypotension encountered here(7). Additionally, BUN/creatinine levels were checked before fluid resuscitation because precipitous moderate volume replacement can worsen creatinine in patients already at risk for prerenal injury given their history of hypertension and limited cardiopulmonary reserve.(8)

**Serum tryptase** is the biomarker most directly linked with mast cell degranulation triggered by either mechanism outlined above.(9) Current consensus guidelines recommend three timed plasma samples:(a) immediately upon presentation or **within minutes** without delaying needed treatment;(b) a second sample collected **1–2 hours** post‑onset—or whenever concentrations peak (~60 min into the reaction)—as values typically rise to maximum activity during this interval;(c) a third “baseline” sample collected **≥24 hours** after resolution when mediator release has subsided(10). In our patient these samples were delayed until well beyond the third episode making it impossible to delineate the true peak interval curvature graphically, thereby limiting interpretation strength for diagnosing systemic mastocytosis.(11)

Criteria used in interpreting **tryptase levels** hinge upon comparison between basal concentration obtained during remission and the measured peak during an acute episode.(12) A rise exceeding **11.5 µg/L above baseline** is accepted as evidence for significant mast cell activation, whereas baseline values >20 µg/L are considered suggestive, though not diagnostic alone, of clonal disorders such as systemic mastocytosis based on WHO minor criteria selection(13). When no true baseline exists—as in this instance where only one value post‑reaction was available—clinicians often use reference laboratory ranges (<11 µg/L standard adult value); however this loses discriminative power when evaluating multiphasic scenarios where peak magnitudes might differ subtly between episodes(14).

*(Comorbidities)* Diabetes mellitus imposes additional interpretative complexity beyond glucose control alone—in particular autonomic neuropathy secondary to long‑standing hyperglycemic states may blunt compensatory tachycardia responses during hypovolemia that commonly accompany anaphylactic shock(15). This attenuation could mask severity early in presentations where vitals appear deceptively stable despite impending circulatory collapse,(16). Likewise any overlooked gastrointestinal intolerance such as lactose malabsorption must be accounted for when choosing intravenous fluids; infants or patients prone to diarrhea require isotonic crystalloids devoid of significant sugar content to prevent unnecessary osmotic load on kidneys strained by acute blood pressure lability(17).

Should alternative diagnoses remain plausible—for instance infectious etiologies causing sepsis-like presentations or cardiac causes leading to decreased output—the clinical team can order targeted imaging studies immediately alongside labs:(i) **Chest radiography** rules out pulmonary embolism or severe pneumonia,*riobrosis*/(ii) **Abdominal ultrasound** offers rapid assessment for hepatosplenomegaly often seen in clonal mast cell disease,(iii) **CT angiography** may become imperative if suspicion arises regarding vascular catastrophe following severe hypotensive events.* These investigations give definitive data about anatomical causes that might mimic respiratory distress or unexplained tachycardia affecting dye distribution,*[**put together]*[the ellipsis].  

In sum, bridging early physiologic data through CMP parameters with precise timing of serum tryptase acquisition creates a robust framework enabling accurate differential diagnosis between typical allergy-mediated versus pseudo-alergic triggers while providing epidemiologic clues pointing toward underlying predispositions such as systemic mastocytosis—all pivotal steps guiding both immediate therapy and longer-term preventive strategies in complex multiphasic allergic cases.

## Biphasic versus Multiphasic Anaphylaxis: Definitions & Epidemiology  

**Unified definition**  
A standard working definition separates anaphylactic courses according both onset speed and duration until relapse. Biphasia refers to a single recurrence that arises after complete clinical resolution and occurs within a period ranging from less than five hours up to forty‑eight hours following initial symptom disappearance—the window deemed clinically meaningful because most relapses peak prior to the end‑of‑day error boundary documented in most prospective registries. ## Acute Management Strategies in Multiphasic Episodes

The presentation of multiple recurrences within hours of initial resolution challenges conventional anaphylaxis protocols because it raises unresolved questions about drug selection, dosing precision and patient monitoring intensity. In the reported woman’s episode—four independent crises occurring over twelve hours—the team was forced to decide whether continued reliance on intermittent intramuscular injections would suffice or whether a more aggressive approach involving continuous intravenous support or adjunctive pharmacotherapy might have altered outcomes.

### Choice among Injectable versus Intravenous Infusion

Intramuscular epinephrine remains the cornerstone therapy for any acute anaphylactic reaction because it delivers rapid systemic levels with limited systemic side effects compared with intravenous administration. However, when repeated IM injections fail to arrest physiologic collapse—as happened during this patient's second and third crises—options narrow toward either multiple consecutive injections or a transitioning strategy that incorporates intravenous boluses followed by a low‑rate infusion.

The pivot toward IV therapy is justified primarily by pharmacokinetics: IV epinephrine achieves maximal plasma concentration instantly while intramuscular routes require several minutes for absorption—a critical delay when hypotension or hypoxaemia jeopardizes organ perfusion. Yet IV delivery carries its own peril set by its steep concentration–effect curve; uncontrolled spikes can precipitate arrhythmias or severe hypertension—especially problematic in patients weighing above average—as seen here where her total body mass exceeded typical thresholds for standard auto‑injector doses (see Weather below). For these reasons most contemporary courses advocate using intermittent IM injections first while reserving continuous IV infusions only if no adequate response is observed after two or more IM doses within a short timeframe (often five minutes).

### Weight‑adjusted Safety Window Advantage Over Standard Auto‑Injectors

Adult dosing guidelines recommend 0.01 mg/kg of epinephrine, capping total at 0.5 mg per administration regardless of weight—a figure that often exceeds what standard commercial auto‑injectors provide (commonly 0.3 mg). The “safety window” concept highlights the risk that patients exceeding fifty kilograms receive only two‑thirds of the recommended drug amount if they rely solely on a 0.3 mg device—as was initially employed in this case’s first two crises—while remaining underexposed thereafter unless doses are increased manually from vials as clinicians did later in her ICU stay (500 µg per injection). A weighted approach affords greater certainty that each mg administered corresponds proportionally to body mass and thus better stabilizes cardiovascular tone once it falls below normotensive levels unseen for hours post‑initial reaction.

### Stepwise Escalation Algorithm Beyond Initial Failure

Education about systematic escalations benefits trainees by providing both predictability and evidence‐based rationality under high stress:

1\. **First emergency response** – administer IM epinephrine at weight‐adjusted dose (≥500 µg).  
2\. **If symptoms persist** beyond five minutes – repeat dose at higher volume from prepped vials (usually 500 µg for adults >50 kg).  
3\. **If still inadequate** – either shift immediately to IV therapy using a loading dose (e.g., 50–100 µg) followed by titration at 0.01–0.05 µg/kg/min until all features disappear OR maintain higher‑frequency IM administrations separated by five–ten minute intervals while continuously monitoring vitals until clarity emerges—this “expectant” stance reduced sedation need but risked delayed reappearance at any time point until observed otherwise during our patient’s fourth episode within twenty four hours post‑last crisis.
4\. **Continuous reassessment** – repeat arterial pressure checks every two minutes during IV infusion build‑up, thrice per hour thereafter once steady state achieves.
This structured algorithm aligns with recent consensus statements recommending proactive, uniform escalation steps rather than ad hoc decision making when recurring symptoms arise post‑first course of care.

## Investigation for Underlying Mast Cell Disorders  
In cases where anaphylaxis recurs more than twice despite adequate initial therapy—especially when trigger identification fails—contemplating an underlying clonal mast‐cell proliferative disorder becomes essential. Systemic mastocytosis (SM) represents the prototypical disease capable of predisposing patients to frequent biphasic or multiphasic reactions through continuous mediator release from tissue‐infiltrating leukemic‐like mast cells.[^1]  

**WHO Diagnostic Framework – Major/Minor Criteria**  
The World Health Organization classifies SM by combining one major criterion—or at least three minor ones—to establish pathology definitively.[^2]  
*Major Criterion:* Dense aggregates (>15 mast cells per high‑power field) identified by combined morphologic estimation and chromogranin A or tryptase immunostaining in bone marrow aspirates or biopsies.[^3]  
*Minor Criteria Include:*  
1) Serum baseline tryptase level >20 µg/L.[^4]  
2) More than two clonally distinct expressions {\displaystyle CD25} or {\displaystyle CD2} surface markers detectable by flow cytometry.[^5]  
3) Identification of activating KIT mutations (usually D816V).  
4) Presence of lesions showing organ damage attributable to excessive tissue infiltration (e.g., hepatomegaly/splenomegaly).[^6]  

In our patient only two minor clues emerged—a persistently raised basal tryptase (>35 µg/L at multiple points) and focal hepatic enlargement on ultrasound—which place her assumption into the “probable” SM category because neither major criterion was fulfilled nor were three independent minor markers demonstrated simultaneously due to limited biopsy material.*

**Tryptase Threshold > 30 µg/L Indicates Probable Disease**  
While <20 µg/L establishes normalcy according to conventional guidelines, a sustained level exceeding 30 µg/L markedly elevates suspicion for clonal proliferation rather than transient post‑reaction elevation.[^7] Importantly, our measurements were taken moments after each recurrence instead of during immediate crisis peaks (<4 h post‑symptom onset), thereby reflecting true basal secretion rather than spurious transient spikes commonly encountered following acute allergic insults.[^8] A persistent basal level >30 µg/L has been associated with near‑cooperative predictive power across multicenter studies,[^9] suggesting that beyond serving as such a marker it could function prognostically by signifying greater tissue burden susceptible for repeated discharges.*

**Liver Imaging – Hepatomegaly Correlation**  
Extra‑cutaneous organ involvement constitutes one ancillary evidence arm within WHO’s framework. ## Clinical Implications & Recommendations  

The dramatic multiphasic course experienced by this patient illustrates several weaknesses in contemporary anaphylaxis care pathways that warrant systematic adjustment. First is the frequency of inappropriate epinephrine doses delivered through low‑content auto‑injectors commonly stocked in many emergency departments and community settings. Adult patients exceeding five‑hundred gram body weight—most adults do so when overweight—are routinely inadequately treated when a 0.30 mg cartridge supplies only sixty percent of the generally accepted therapeutic quantity of approximately 0.50 mg required by guideline recommendations (10 µg/kg up to a maximum adult dose). Delayed response after underdosing increases both immediate morbidity and the likelihood of further recurrences because remaining mediators persist longer before endogenous absorption is saturated by subsequent doses. The literature demonstrates that receipt of an adequate first dose reduces subsequent rescue injections fivefold relative to cases starting with a lower dosage [pharmacy journal]. Consequently, hospitals should replace low‑dose units with dedicated high‑dose adult cartridges whenever practicable, while clinicians remain trained in manual additions from vial stock if stock status permits expedience during critical pauses such as pre‑hospital scenes where hesitation can be deadly.

Next comes a clinically actionable prediction framework that identifies real time “high‑risk” sufferers who merit prolonged observation beyond standard thresholds historically set at four–six hours post-resolution. This risk model consolidates repeat criteria highlighted in meta‑analyses: delayed administration (>10 min), hypotension requiring ≥2 mg intravenous fluid loading upon arrival, recurrent dyspnea requiring supplemental oxygen beyond one hour, requirement for more than one autoinjector dose during an episode, and elevated baseline serum tryptase signalling a latent mast cell disorder such as systemic mastocytosis—the latter feature was evident in our case despite falling short of formal WHO diagnostic criteria. When two or more predictors coexist patient outcomes curve toward longer onset lag times notable across biphasic episodes observed within twenty‐four hours ‑ with the residual minority occurring beyond ten days rarely enough to dissuade extended admission only when multiple recurrences had already manifested within that window.
