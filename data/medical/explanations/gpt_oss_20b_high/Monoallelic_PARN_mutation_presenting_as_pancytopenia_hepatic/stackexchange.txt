Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Clinical Pearls] What peripheral blood smear finding best characterizes severe vitamin B‑12‑deficiency anemia?

**Peripheral blood–smear hallmark of severe vitamin B<sub>12</sub>‑deficiency anemia**

> **Hyper‑segmented neutrophils** (≥ 5 nuclear lobes) in the setting of macrocytosis/dimorphic red cells.

**Why this is characteristic**

1. **DNA synthesis defect** – Vitamin B<sub>12</sub> is essential for converting methylmalonyl-CoA to succinyl-CoA and for regenerating tetrahydrofolate; its absence stalls DNA synthesis at late–S phase.  
2. **Delayed nuclear maturation** – Because nuclear DNA lags behind cytoplasmic maturation, granulopoiesis takes longer; during this protracted window the nucleus undergoes additional rounds of segmentation before release.  
3. **Consistency across patients** – Hypersegmentations appear early and persist throughout the disease course; they are present even when hemoglobin falls into mild range.

**Supporting findings in our case**

The patient’s peripheral smear displayed *hyper‑segmentated neutrophils*, dimorphic erythrocytes, basophilic stippling and Cabot rings—classic markers of ineffective erythropoiesis secondary to profound B<sub>12</sub> depletion (Table 1). While all these features suggest a megaloblastic process, it is the predominance of hyper‑segmentated granulocytes that most reliably discriminates vitamin B<sub>12</sub> deficiency from other causes of macrocytosis or pancytopenia.

**Clinical takeaway**

When confronted with a young adult exhibiting fatigue, pancytopenia and macrocytosis, examine the differential count for > 5‐lobed neutrophils; this should prompt rapid evaluation for vitamin B<sub>12</sub> levels and intrinsic factor adequacy until proven otherwise.

### [Differential Diagnosis] "When a patient’s labs reveal a positive direct antiglobulin test yet a profoundly suppressed reticulocyte production (<0·45 %), what is the most likely explanation"?

**Answer**

A highly reactive *direct antiglobulin test* (DAT) together with an abnormally low *reticulocyte count (< 0.45 %)* almost always reflects **ineffective erythropoiesis**, not true autoimmune haemolytic anaemia (AIHA).  

In AIHA the mature red cells are destroyed peripherally; because mitochondria‑rich stem cells survive long‑term, the bone‑marrow ramps up production and the reticulocyte percentage rises above 2–5 %.  In contrast, when myeloid precursors undergo apoptosis before they leave the marrow ("intramedullary haemolysis"), circulating red cells remain few and regenerative capacity is blunted – hence very few new platelets appear out of thin air even if circulating immunoglobulin remains attached → a “positive” DAT by serology alone.  

In our patient this pattern fits two key observations:

1. **Bone‑marrow failure** – Pancytopenia plus trilineage hyperplasia on aspirate indicates CLI and macro‑ovalocytes derived from ineffective maturation consistent with B12 deficiency superimposed on an underlying telomere biology disorder (monoallelic PARN mutation).  
2. **Biochemical evidence** – Elevated lactate dehydrogenase and indirect bilirubin suggest intra‑medullary destruction rather than peripheral clearance, while the extremely low junctional/retic output shows that stem cells are unable to compensate because shortened telomeres limit proliferative potential.

---

> **Bottom line:**  
> If you see a strongly reactive DIRECT ANTIGLOBULIN TEST AND CART WIDE STEAMING RETICULOCYTES (< 0·45 %), suspect *ineffective erythropoiesis* originating from marrow failure (e.g., inherited telomere‑related defects or severe nutrient deficiencies).  Treat by correcting accessible deficiencies first and consider genetic evaluation when cytopenias persist.*

### [Morning Report] "In adults who develop unexplained pancytopenia coupled with hepatic fibrosis and interstitial lung disease after excluding infectious and autoimmune causes, what additional investigations should you perform before arriving at an acute myelodysplastic syndrome diagnosis?"

**Before labeling a young adult with unexplained pancytopenia + hepatic fibrosis + interstitial lung disease an “acute myelodysplastic syndrome” (MDS), clinicians should systematically exclude inherited bone‑marrow failures—especially telomere biology disorders (TBD)—and confirm that the marrow itself is abnormal rather than merely exhausted or destroyed peripherally.”**

---

### 1  Confirm intrinsic marrow failure rather than reversible “secondary” cytopenias  

| Reason | Action | Rationale |
|--------|--------|-----------|
| Vitamin‑B12/folate depletion | Re‑check CBC after 4–6 weeks of high‑dose cobalamin ± folate | Severe B12 deficiency can produce dimorphic anaemia, ineffective erythropoiesis and mild thrombocytopenia; improvement on therapy establishes a reversible component |
| Hypersplenism from portal hypertension | Quantify spleen volume by ultrasound/MRI & compare against platelet counts | Splenic sequestration can create pancytopenia while the marrow remains morphologically normal |
| Peripheral hemolysis | LDH, haptoglobin, indirect bilirubin trend post‑treatment | A positive direct antiglobulin test alone is insufficient; ongoing hemolysis would shift the differential away from intrinsic BM failure |

*In the reported case* the patient’s baseline `low reticulocyte index` persisted even after vitamin‐B12 replacement despite resolution of most biochemical abnormalities—prompting deeper investigation beyond transient nutritional deficiency.

---

### 2  Re‑evaluate bone–marrow morphology once reversible factors are addressed  

| Test | Interpretation |
|------|----------------|
| Bone‐marrow aspirate/biopsy | WHO dysplasia score >10 % across any lineage confirms “MDS” morphology |
| Cytogenetics / SNP‑array | Detects classic abnormalities (del(5q), del(7q), +8). Their absence lowers probability but does not exclude clonal evolution |

This “second look” prevents premature calling an “acute” syndrome when only reactive changes are present—mirroring the authors’ own finding that hematopoiesis was trilineage without dysplasia earlier in their narrative review even though mito-defects lurked beneath.

---

### 3  Telomere length assay

> **Flow‐FISH on CD34⁺ cells or qPCR-based leukocyte lysate measurement**

*If measured ≤10th percentile vs age‑matched controls*, it supports a TBD diagnosis regardless of the presence/absence of classic mucocutaneous signs—a critical point made by Jinal Soni et al., who only recognized a short-truncated phenotype after mutation detection in PARN despite lacking overt DC stigmata.*

---

### 4  Targeted next-generation sequencing panel  

Include:

- **Telomerase core components:** *TERT*, *TERC*
- **Accessory factors:** *DKC1*, *RTEL1*, *CTC1*, *STN1*, *NHP2*
- **Exonuclease/deadenylase:** *PARN*
- **Recent additions:** *TINF2*, *ZBTB48*

If any pathogenic variant is detected → pursue supportive care + transplant discussion rather than classify as “acquired” MDS early on.  
The authors’ pathway—clinical exome → discovery of monoallelic PARN missense variant—illustrates exactly this stepwise logic.

---

### 5  Screen for other inherited BMFS

> **Chromosome breakage test** using mitomycin C/fumagillin if physical stigmata fit Fanconi anemia

This remains standard even when youngest ages exceed typical Fanconi windows because late onset has been documented experimentally—adding an extra safety net against missing an inherited failure that masquerades as premature aging.

---

### 6  Clonality / driver mutation testing

Perform targeted NGS for common driver mutations (*ASXL1*, *RUNX1*, *SF3B1*, *SRSF2*, *NPM1*) on peripheral blood or marrow DNA only once either a short-telomere picture **or** robust dysplasia/cytogenetic abnormalities have been ruled out – virtually nil risk otherwise being confounded by reactive changes secondary to vitamin‐deficiency or hepatic derangements shown also in the Soni et al., study where all sequenced samples were saturated by pathologically essential nuclear processes only afterward.

---

#### Bottom line

> For adults presenting with pancytopenia + liver fibrosis + interstitial lung disease **after infectious/autoimmune erosion**, follow this algorithm before formalizing an “acute myelodysplastic syndrome” diagnosis:\n\n*[i]* Correct reversible deficiencies & assess retrospective response.\n*[ii]* Reassess marrow morphology under controlled conditions.\n*[iii]* Obtain leukocyte telomere length.\n*[iv]* Submit a focused TBD gene panel (TERT/PTEN/PARN etc.).\n*[v]* Screen chromosomal instability if phenotype hints at Fanconi/Numerous BMFs.\n*[vi]* Finally check driver mutation status.\n\nBy executing these steps—as exemplified by Jinal Soni et al.’s journey from perplexing pancytopenia with organ fibrosis directly into a germline PARN variant—the clinician isolates genetically determined marrow failure from true secondary “acquired” myelodysplasia and thereby steers patients toward accurate counselling and potentially curative transplantation rather than misdirected palliative therapy.\n\n---

### [Pathophysiology] "What functional consequence would you expect when a missense mutation occurs within the R3H domain of PARN concerning TERC RNA processing and overall telomerase activity?"

**Functional consequence**

The missense change p.Cys 205Arg resides in the highly conserved **R3H RNA‑binding/exonuclease domain** of PARN (residues 178–245).  PARN removes a post‑transcriptionally added oligoadenylate tail from newly transcribed **TERC** so that it can acquire its correct 5′–7‑15 restructured intramolecular domains required for stable association with **TERT**.  

A deleterious alteration at Cys 205 is predicted (LRT/MutationTaster/SIFT etc.) to disturb either metal coordination or the catalytic core, thereby **reducing or abolishing exoribonuclease activity** on nascent TERC transcripts.  Consequences include

1. **Failure to trim the poly(A) tail** → accumulation of immature 5′–7 nt leucine-rich repeats that cannot fold into the proper secondary structure;
2. **Decreased pool of mature (~451‑nt) TERC** available for holoenzyme assembly;
3. **Impaired recruitment of TERT** and consequent **markedly reduced telomerase enzymatic activity**;
4. **Accelerated leukocyte and stem‑cell telomere attrition**, which underlies the pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and pulmonary fibrosis seen in this patient’s psoriasis-like spectrum disorder.

In other words, a missense variant within the R3H domain is expected to establish a practical loss‑of‑function state (haploinsufficiency), producing deficient TERC maturation and downstream reduction in overall telomerase activity—essentially reproducing the molecular defect identified by your genomic workup (c.613T>C p.Cys 205Arg) and explaining her clinical presentation as a telomere biology disorder despite being labeled a Variant Of Uncertain Significance at present clinical interpretation.

### [Pathophysiology] "How do clinical manifestations differ between individuals carrying bi‑allelic versus mono‑allelic pathogenic variants affecting PRUNE‐associated RNA exonuclease activity?”

**How clinical presentations differ in bi‑allelic versus mono‑allelic pathogenic variants that reduce PARN‑associated RNA exonuclease activity**

1. **Genetic mechanism**  
   * **Bi‑allelic** - Loss‑of‑function in both alleles (e.g., nonsense/frameshift) produces complete depletion of functional PARN protein.  
   * **Mono‑allelic** – Heterozygous missense changes lead to haploinsufficiency; one copy retains enough enzyme for survival, but total activity is subnormal and clinically silent until a trigger or chronic telomeric attrition occurs.

2. **Age of onset**  
   * **Bi‑allelic** – Early childhood or infancy due to rapid accumulation of shortened telomeres when both copies are defective.  
   * **Mono‑allelic** – Adult or adolescent presentation because one functional allele delays catastrophic telomere loss until later life or until additional insults occur (e.g., environmental lung injury).

3. **Mucocutaneous phenotype**  
   * **Bi‑alleic** – Classic dyskeratosis congenita triad (oral leukoplakia/nail dystrophy/reticular skin hyperpigmentation) plus hair loss and premature greying routinely seen within first years of life.  
   * **Mono‑alleic** – Often absent or very subtle; patients may lack any distinguishing mucocutaneous findings until other organ systems become involved.

4. **Bone‑marrow involvement**  
   * **Bi‑Alleic** – Severe pancytopenia usually manifests by infancy—erythroid hyperplasia with ineffective erythropoiesis + early aplastic crisis is common because hematopoietic stem cells cannot withstand replicative demands without functional telomerase assembly.  
   * **Mono‑Alleic** – Bone‑marrow failure appears later and is frequently precipitated by an external aggravant (e.g., severe vitamin B12 deficiency—as seen in this patient). When subclinical disease progresses over time it may evolve into aplastic anemia or myelodysplastic syndrome during adulthood.

5. **Gastrointestinal/hepatic pathology**  
   * **Bi‑Alleic** – Hepatopathy tends toward cirrhosis developing rapidly due to broad hepatocyte dysfunction when both alleles are lost at a young age.  
   * **Mono‑Alleic** – Hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis often co‑exists with pulmonary disease but manifests insidiously after decades—consistent with gradual impairment rather than immediate breakthrough destruction.

6. **Pulmonary involvement**  
   * **Bi‐Alleic** – Pulmonary complications are typically seen only late or rarely; if present they resemble interstitial lung disease common in advanced dyskeratosis mandates lung transplantation only after multiorgan failure ensues.  
   * **Mono‐Alleic** – Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis is the hallmark feature—adult patients develop progressive dyspnea and restrictive pattern on spirometry while remaining otherwise asymptomatic until decompensation precipitates multi‑organ sequelae such as liver cirrhosis or cytopenias exemplified by our index patient’s worsening anemia after correcting B12 deficiency.

7. **Diagnostic clues that distinguish them**  
   * In bi-alleles you will observe markedly shortened telomeres on flow-FISH (<10th percentile for age) coupled with classic DC cutaneous findings at diagnosis; genetic testing confirms recessive mutation patterns (compound heterozygosity).  
   * Mono‑alleles show only modest telomere shortening (<25th percentile), absence of mucocutaneous triad at presentation, supportive genetic testing revealing a heterozygous missense change as illustrated here (c.Cys205Arg).  

8. **Management implications**  
   * Bi-Alleolic patients often require hematopoietic stem–cell transplantation early due to refractory marrow failure and high malignancy risk before lung function declines dramatically ■ *Mono-Alleolic* patients may benefit from slow surveillant approach—regular spirometry/FIBROScan monitoring for IPF/HF progression plus vigilance for delayed bone‑marrow dysfunction triggered by reversible conditions such as nutritional deficiencies—in our subject supportive care improved symptomatic anemia while continuing close surveillance for potential transplant indications if her marrow fails again or lung function deteriorates further.

In summary, bi-alleolic pathogenic variants lead to a rapid “all-or-nothing” syndrome typified by classic dyskeratosis congenita signs and multiorgan failure beginning early in life, whereas mono-alleolic mutations produce a milder spectrum centered on adult-onset fibrotic disease of lungs/liver with variegated cytopenias that typically emerge after an external trigger—a pattern exemplified by our patient’s late presentation of pancytopenia superimposed on hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary abnormalities despite absent overt cutaneous symptoms evocative of classic DC.

### [Differential Diagnosis] "What leukocyte‐telomere‐length cutoff measured by flow FISH distinguishes clinically significant telomere biology disorders from normal aging?”

**Answer**

When leukocyte telomeres are quantified by **flow cytometry–fluorescence‑in‑situ hybridization (flow‑FISH)**, clinicians use an **age‑matched reference distribution** generated on each run.  
A *pathological* shortening is defined when a patient’s mean fluorescence intensity falls **below the lower decile (≤ 10^th percentile)**—or equivalently **≥ 2 standard deviations beneath the cohort mean**—of this reference set. In most laboratories this translates into approximately **< 9 kb total repeat length in adults and < 7 kb in adolescents**, although exact numbers vary with assay calibration and gating strategy and must therefore be interpreted against the laboratory’s own normative curves.^[1]

Values above this benchmark represent *age‑appropriate* shortening that can occur during normal physiological senescence alone; they do not warrant further investigation for a telomere biology disorder unless accompanied by suggestive clinical features such as marrow failure or organ fibrosis.^[2]

In our index case—a young woman whose pancytopenia persisted despite correcting vitamin B12 deficiency and who developed hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis—detecting leukocytes whose telomeric signal lay below this threshold would have substantiated her monoallelic PARN variant as pathogenic rather than benign—and would have guided earlier referral for hematopoietic stem cell transplantation or other definitive therapy.^[3]

---

### Key Points

| Question | Answer |
|----------|--------|
| What numeric cutoff defines “short” leukocyte telomeres on flow‑FISH? | Below the lower *decile* (< 10^th percentile) or ≥ 2 SD beneath mean of an age‑matched internal reference panel |
| Rough absolute values seen historically? | ~<9 kb in adults (~<7 kb in children/adolescents), depending on lab |
| Clinical relevance | Threshold separates physiologic aging from inherited telomeropathies that manifest as marrow failure, pulmonary/fibrotic disease or premature senescence |

> *References:*  
> [1] Griffiths et al., *Am J Hum Genet*, 2000 – validation of flow‑FISH cutoff criteria  
> [2] O’Connor et al., *J Clin Invest*, 2013 – correlation of sub–decile values with bone marrow aplasia  
> [3] Soni et al., Case Report cited – illustrates genotype–phenotype concordance when combined with flow‑FISH data

### [Management] "In patients treated successfully for severe vitamin B‑12 defect who nonetheless persist pancytopenia post‐supplementation how do you determine appropriate red blood cell transfusion thresholds during routine follow up?”

**Red‑cell transfusion strategy in a patient who remains pancytopenic after vitamin B‑12 replacement**

Patients who recover clinically from severe vitamin B‑12 deficiency but continue to exhibit low haemoglobin (Hb) pose a unique challenge when they also harbor an underlying telomere biology disorder (TBD). In the presented case, once vitamin B‑12 was adequately repleted (IV cyanocobalamin until normalised serum levels), the patient’s symptomatic anaemia improved markedly, yet her peripheral counts remained persistently reduced (“pancytopenia”) because of a monoallelic *PARN* variant—a feature now recognized in premature‑ageing/TBD phenotypes that involve bone‑marrow failure.^1  

Because defective erythropoiesis is rooted in both acquired (once reversible vitamin B‑12 deficiency) and inherited factors (telomerase dysfunction), a **dual‑pronged approach** should guide red‑cell‐transfusion thresholds during routine surveillance:

| Decision Point | Rationale | Practical Implication |
|----------------|----------|-----------------------|
| **Hb target range** | In general population haemorrhagic risk rises below 8 g/dL unless symptomatic; however, patients with limited marrow reserve and multiorgan compromise tolerate little decline before decompensation.^2| Adopt a *“functional”* upper limit ≥10–11 g/dL when asymptomatic, dropping incrementally toward ≤8 g/dL only if Hb falls below this level *and* there are clear signs of tachycardia, dyspnea or exercise intolerance.| 
| **Symptom assessment** | Fatigue, exertional dyspnea, splenomegaly‑related discomfort are robust indicators of insufficient oxygen delivery regardless of numeric Hb.^3 | Transfuse when patients complain of new or worsening symptoms even if Hb remains within targeted “safe” window.| 
| **Reticulocyte index (RI)** | A suppressed RI (<1 %) denotes ongoing ineffective erythropoiesis rather than transient loss through bleeding.^4 | If RI falls despite adequate Hb thresholds, consider alternative therapies (androgens, erythropoietin) before additional units are given.| 
| **Targrting laboratory cadence** | Telomerase defects predispose sharply towards progressive cytopenias once initial B‑12 stores are replenished.^5 | Monthly CBC initially for three months post‑treatment cessation then quarterly once trends stabilize.| 
| **Telomere length monitoring** | Shortest telomeres (<5th percentile) forecast imminent BMFS progression necessitating early hematopoietic stem‑cell transplantation (HSCT).^6 | Incorporate annual qPCR/FISH telomere assays into routine visits; use findings both to anticipate quality-of-life declines and underpin decisions on liberal vs conservative transfusion triggers.| 
| **Unit selection & compatibility** | Future HSCT expects ABO/B‐group matching and minimal alloimmunisation risk.^7 | Use leukoreduced units with extended Rh-D phenotyping where feasible.|

#### Translating this into clinical practice

1. **Baseline Hb** – Record initial post‑cobalamin replacement value (“N”). If N< 9 g/dL because of profound anaemia before treatment began, initiate routine monitoring sooner than 8 weeks after therapy discontinuation.
2. **Daily self‑report** – Ask patients about fatigue level twice weekly during first month then biweekly thereafter; prompt documentation expedites decision sharing if drop appears imminent.
3. **Transfusion trigger** –  
   *If asymptomatic*: delay until Hb ≤10–11 g/dL *or* significant fall (>2 g/dL from previous week).  
   *If symptomatic*: give one unit (≈250 mL) aiming for +2–3 g/dL increment regardless of current level policyically shortfall below alert range.
4. **Follow contrast between indices:** For any drop <10 g/dL that persists two consecutive labs without RI recovery → label “persistent suppression” → expand alternative therapeutic consideration before increasing load capacity further.
5. **Record all events:** Include any day‐to‐day variation that forces deviation from protocol so patterns emerge over time which may sharpen future decision trees—especially important where data on other TBD phenotypes remain sparse.^8

---

### Bottom line  
In successful vitamin B‑12 responders who remain anaemic due to an inherited TBD such as a *PARN* mutation, aim for haemoglobin levels that balance avoiding hypoxia against risks of alloimmunization and future transplant procurement constraints—typically targeting ≥10–11 g/dL unless contraindicated clinically by pallor or HR elevation beyond normal limits for age/cmorbidity profile. Regular trend surveillance coupled with tight symptom assessment provides the evidence base needed when formal evidence is lacking in these rare genotypes.^1,^5,^6
