Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Pathophysiology] "How do clinical manifestations differ between individuals carrying bi‑allelic versus mono‑allelic pathogenic variants affecting PRUNE‐associated RNA exonuclease activity?”

**How clinical presentations differ in bi‑allelic versus mono‑allelic pathogenic variants that reduce PARN‑associated RNA exonuclease activity**

1. **Genetic mechanism**  
   * **Bi‑allelic** - Loss‑of‑function in both alleles (e.g., nonsense/frameshift) produces complete depletion of functional PARN protein.  
   * **Mono‑allelic** – Heterozygous missense changes lead to haploinsufficiency; one copy retains enough enzyme for survival, but total activity is subnormal and clinically silent until a trigger or chronic telomeric attrition occurs.

2. **Age of onset**  
   * **Bi‑allelic** – Early childhood or infancy due to rapid accumulation of shortened telomeres when both copies are defective.  
   * **Mono‑allelic** – Adult or adolescent presentation because one functional allele delays catastrophic telomere loss until later life or until additional insults occur (e.g., environmental lung injury).

3. **Mucocutaneous phenotype**  
   * **Bi‑alleic** – Classic dyskeratosis congenita triad (oral leukoplakia/nail dystrophy/reticular skin hyperpigmentation) plus hair loss and premature greying routinely seen within first years of life.  
   * **Mono‑alleic** – Often absent or very subtle; patients may lack any distinguishing mucocutaneous findings until other organ systems become involved.

4. **Bone‑marrow involvement**  
   * **Bi‑Alleic** – Severe pancytopenia usually manifests by infancy—erythroid hyperplasia with ineffective erythropoiesis + early aplastic crisis is common because hematopoietic stem cells cannot withstand replicative demands without functional telomerase assembly.  
   * **Mono‑Alleic** – Bone‑marrow failure appears later and is frequently precipitated by an external aggravant (e.g., severe vitamin B12 deficiency—as seen in this patient). When subclinical disease progresses over time it may evolve into aplastic anemia or myelodysplastic syndrome during adulthood.

5. **Gastrointestinal/hepatic pathology**  
   * **Bi‑Alleic** – Hepatopathy tends toward cirrhosis developing rapidly due to broad hepatocyte dysfunction when both alleles are lost at a young age.  
   * **Mono‑Alleic** – Hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis often co‑exists with pulmonary disease but manifests insidiously after decades—consistent with gradual impairment rather than immediate breakthrough destruction.

6. **Pulmonary involvement**  
   * **Bi‐Alleic** – Pulmonary complications are typically seen only late or rarely; if present they resemble interstitial lung disease common in advanced dyskeratosis mandates lung transplantation only after multiorgan failure ensues.  
   * **Mono‐Alleic** – Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis is the hallmark feature—adult patients develop progressive dyspnea and restrictive pattern on spirometry while remaining otherwise asymptomatic until decompensation precipitates multi‑organ sequelae such as liver cirrhosis or cytopenias exemplified by our index patient’s worsening anemia after correcting B12 deficiency.

7. **Diagnostic clues that distinguish them**  
   * In bi-alleles you will observe markedly shortened telomeres on flow-FISH (<10th percentile for age) coupled with classic DC cutaneous findings at diagnosis; genetic testing confirms recessive mutation patterns (compound heterozygosity).  
   * Mono‑alleles show only modest telomere shortening (<25th percentile), absence of mucocutaneous triad at presentation, supportive genetic testing revealing a heterozygous missense change as illustrated here (c.Cys205Arg).  

8. **Management implications**  
   * Bi-Alleolic patients often require hematopoietic stem–cell transplantation early due to refractory marrow failure and high malignancy risk before lung function declines dramatically ■ *Mono-Alleolic* patients may benefit from slow surveillant approach—regular spirometry/FIBROScan monitoring for IPF/HF progression plus vigilance for delayed bone‑marrow dysfunction triggered by reversible conditions such as nutritional deficiencies—in our subject supportive care improved symptomatic anemia while continuing close surveillance for potential transplant indications if her marrow fails again or lung function deteriorates further.

In summary, bi-alleolic pathogenic variants lead to a rapid “all-or-nothing” syndrome typified by classic dyskeratosis congenita signs and multiorgan failure beginning early in life, whereas mono-alleolic mutations produce a milder spectrum centered on adult-onset fibrotic disease of lungs/liver with variegated cytopenias that typically emerge after an external trigger—a pattern exemplified by our patient’s late presentation of pancytopenia superimposed on hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary abnormalities despite absent overt cutaneous symptoms evocative of classic DC.
