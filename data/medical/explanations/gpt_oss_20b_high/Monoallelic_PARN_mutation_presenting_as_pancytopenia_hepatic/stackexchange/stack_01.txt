Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Clinical Pearls] What peripheral blood smear finding best characterizes severe vitamin B‑12‑deficiency anemia?

**Peripheral blood–smear hallmark of severe vitamin B<sub>12</sub>‑deficiency anemia**

> **Hyper‑segmented neutrophils** (≥ 5 nuclear lobes) in the setting of macrocytosis/dimorphic red cells.

**Why this is characteristic**

1. **DNA synthesis defect** – Vitamin B<sub>12</sub> is essential for converting methylmalonyl-CoA to succinyl-CoA and for regenerating tetrahydrofolate; its absence stalls DNA synthesis at late–S phase.  
2. **Delayed nuclear maturation** – Because nuclear DNA lags behind cytoplasmic maturation, granulopoiesis takes longer; during this protracted window the nucleus undergoes additional rounds of segmentation before release.  
3. **Consistency across patients** – Hypersegmentations appear early and persist throughout the disease course; they are present even when hemoglobin falls into mild range.

**Supporting findings in our case**

The patient’s peripheral smear displayed *hyper‑segmentated neutrophils*, dimorphic erythrocytes, basophilic stippling and Cabot rings—classic markers of ineffective erythropoiesis secondary to profound B<sub>12</sub> depletion (Table 1). While all these features suggest a megaloblastic process, it is the predominance of hyper‑segmentated granulocytes that most reliably discriminates vitamin B<sub>12</sub> deficiency from other causes of macrocytosis or pancytopenia.

**Clinical takeaway**

When confronted with a young adult exhibiting fatigue, pancytopenia and macrocytosis, examine the differential count for > 5‐lobed neutrophils; this should prompt rapid evaluation for vitamin B<sub>12</sub> levels and intrinsic factor adequacy until proven otherwise.
