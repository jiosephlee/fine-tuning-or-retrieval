## Clinical Presentation & Initial Evaluation

The patient was a young adult male who first presented during an annual health examination rather than as an emergency referral for overt diabetic complications. Routine laboratory screening revealed a strikingly high glycated haemoglobin (HbA₁c = 10.2 %, equivalent to 88 mmol/mol) and an equally alarming fasting plasma glucose level (12.4 mmol/L). These findings confirm chronic hyperglycaemia that has been present for several months—HbA₁c reflects average glycaemia over three time‑points while fasting glucose captures baseline glycaemic control independent of post‑prandial fluctuations. In obese adolescents or adults with classical type 2 disease one would expect concurrent insulin resistance markers such as acanthosis nigricans; however this patient’s skin exam was unremarkable apart from the absence of acanthosis nigricans—a notable finding because this cutaneous sign often accompanies excess adiposity‑related insulin resistance seen in many adults with type 2 diabetes.

Vital signs were essentially normal – blood pressure was recorded at 112/70 mmHg and resting heart rate was 78 bpm – suggesting a lack of cardiovascular decompensation at presentation. The body mass index calculated from height and weight was 24·6 kg/m², placing him within the upper range of “normal” weight according to WHO criteria yet still considerably lower than most patients delivering typical type 2 presentations who are frequently at least mildly obese or display abdominal adiposity on physical inspection.

Metabolic laboratory studies provided additional nuance beyond classic tri‑adipokine profiles usually expected in type‑2 states. Triglycerides were markedly elevated at 4·31 mmol/L (≈380 mg/dL) while high‑density lipoprotein cholesterol was low at 1·01 mmol/L (<40 mg/dL). Although dyslipidaemia points toward an underlying metabolic disturbance—often seen in pre‑diabetes or established insulin‑resistance syndromes—it does not necessarily distinguish between monogenic forms versus polygenic endocrinopathies when isolated from obesity metrics or additional cardiometabolic comorbidities such as hypertension or impaired fasting glucose. Importantly, subsequent hepatic function tests were within normal limits, a vital clue when considering HNF1A mutations that may also alter hepatic transcriptional programmes affecting glycogen regulation yet rarely produce overt hepatic failure early on.

The most striking biochemical evidence came from a standard oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). Two hours after ingestion of a 75‑gram carbohydrate load the plasma glucose spiked dramatically to 19·8 mmol/L (>350 mg/dL), far exceeding thresholds used by the American Diabetes Association (>11·1 mmol/L) to define impaired glucose tolerance or overt diabetes based on post‑prandial response alone. Such precipitous elevations indicate either deficient first‑phase insulin release or pronounced hepatic gluconeogenic activity overexpressed by β‑cell dysfunction – features common to HNF1A‑associated maturity‑onset diabetes of youth (MODY).

Anaphylactic artifacts such as ketoacidosis were sought progressively; despite marked hyperglycaemia the patient exhibited clinically uncomplicated polyuria–polydipsia without diarrhoea or vomiting indicative of catabolism downregulated by glucagon itself or dehydration states known to precipitate ketogenesis in absolute insulin deficiency typically seen with type I disease. ## Differential Diagnosis & Diagnostic Reasoning

Young‑onset hyperglycaemia obliges clinicians first to delineate whether the underlying mechanism is immune‑mediated loss of β‑cell mass (type I diabetes), insulin resistance (type II diabetes) or a monogenic etiology such as maturity‑onset diabetes of the young (MODY). The constellation observed in this patient – non‑obese BMI (24.6 kg/m²), absence of classic lipid surrogate markers of severe metabolic syndrome except for mildly elevated triglycerides, significant familial clustering across three–four generations and an initial HbA1c exceeding 10 % – argues against an exuberant insulin resistance state while excluding overt autoimmunity once antibody studies are considered.*

### Strong phenotypic suggestion against autoimmune type I disease based on negative GADA/IA‑2 antibodies  

The first decisive exclusion was provided by serologic testing for pancreatic autoantibodies routinely used in diagnosing Type I diabetes or latent autoimmune diabetes in adults (LADA). In this patient both glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA) and islet antigen‑2 antibodies (IA‑2A) were negative at presentation and again after one month’s follow‑up when the diagnosis had yet be confirmed genetically (§“Investigations”). Autoimmune β‑cell destruction typically secretes these autoantibodies well before overt hyperglycaemia manifests; therefore persistent seronegativity is highly reliable evidence against a conventional Type I immunopathogenic process.^[1] The absence of any other systemic signs supporting an autoimmune endocrine disorder further reinforced this conclusion.[^2]

Because LADA epitomises slow‐progressive autoimmune beta‑cell loss even in adults over forty ‑ years old,^([3]) its exclusion is critical when considering monogenic causes such as MODY whose pathophysiology does not involve an immune attack field^[4].

> **Clinical take‑away:** In young adults who present with dysglycaemia yet show scant obesity or dyslipidaemia, a seronegative panel strongly tips the differential away from Type I diabetes and prioritises alternative diagnoses including MODY.

### Glucagon stimulation indicating insufficient endogenous insulin secretion evidenced by stimulated C‑peptide levels < ½ nmol/L  

β‑cell reserve can be quantified through dynamic measures such as the glucagon stimulation test or mixed‑meal trial.^[5] In healthy subjects, intravenous glucagon elicits a prompt surge in serum C‑peptide surpassing 5–10 ng/mL (~ 15–30 nmol/L) within five minutes.^[6] Persuading physiologically does it hold that any peak below approximately half a nanomole per liter represents significant hyposecretion relative to normal physiology—as corroborated by large cohort studies evaluating post‑glucagon recovery.^[7]

The present case showed a basal value of 0·39 nmol/L skyrocketing to only 0·83 nmol/L after six minutes—a response below the conventional “preserved” threshold (> 1·32 nmol/L).^([8]) This blunted stimulus response suggests two overlapping realities:
* The pancreas retains **some** secretory capacity — enough that baseline isn’t nil—yet
* The functional reserve is starkly reduced when juxtaposed against normal individuals who normally achieve ≥ ≥ ≈ 4–8 nmol/L at peak.^[9]

Such an intermediate phenotype fits neatly within what has been described for heterozygous HNF1α mutations—β‑cell dysfunction begins insidiously rather than abruptly initiating abrupt catabolism seen in classic Type I disease.^[10] Importantly, occult dysfunction detected via this kinetic study also distinguishes monogenic MODY from clinically silent prediabetes commonly seen across diverse ethnicities where routine fasting panels alone may exaggerate metabolic risk clues.[^11]

> **Clinical take‑away:** A *low* C‑peptide rise after glucagon remains pivotal not merely proving lack-of-insulin production—typical for late Type I disease—but also alerting clinicians that residual function still exists—a hallmark for many MODY variants including HNF1α mutations.

### Integrating findings into a diagnostic trajectory  

Combining the salient features—the young age (<25), nonsmoker BMI ≈25 kg/m² unaccompanied by clear metabolic syndrome traits beyond raised TGs, abbreviated yet significant fasting-glucose derangement without ketonaemia—and most importantly the *seronegative status* coupled with *blunted endogenous secretory response*, yielded an elevated clinical probability equation per contemporary Exe­ron model assessments (~62 %).[^12] This probability pivoted care toward comprehensive molecular testing using next-generation sequencing panels targeting known MODY genes—a step mandated until definitive genetic confirmation provided concrete evidence beyond analogue biochemical inference.

Once genetic testing revealed a heterozygous pathogenic p.N270S mutation in HNF1α™, all clinical premises aligned into one coherent entity: classically labelled “HNF1α-MODY.” The initial pre-genetic suspicion guided by seronegative immunology and poor yet partially intact insulin kinetics proved sound given eventual therapeutic adjustment—discontinuation of basal insulin followed by successful glycaemic control on GLP-­R agonists—with durable remission well below both her baseline HbA₁c (10·2 %) and contemporaneous thresholds found riskily high (^≥8·5 %).

## Genetic Pathogenesis & Variant Interpretation

Hepatocyte nuclear factor‑1α (HNF‑1α) is a transcription factor encoded by **HNF 1A**, located on chromosome 12 q24. It possesses an N‑terminal dimerization/dna‑binding motif followed by a carboxy‑terminal transactivation domain that harbors one or more activation‐repression motifs modularly engaged through interaction partners such as co‑activators p300/TATA binding protein complexes identified mainly within pancreatic β cells, hepatic hepatocytes, renal tubules and intestinal mucosae.  
The mutated protein in this patient substitutes an alanine residue at nucleotide 809 (c.809 A> > G) generating p.Asn 270→Ser located inside the DNA‑binding helix–turn–helix region—a ∼15 % consensus motif highly conserved across vertebrates (>96 % identity between human Amniota) which directly contacts the consensus “GTTC” sequence upstream of target promoters [e.g., INS, GLUT‑2/SLC2A2 and PKLR]. Loss‐of‐function mutations in this segment impede chromatin opening or primer recruitment both downstream signaling events critical for early life β‑cell differentiation and mature glucose‐stimulated insulin release pathways [18]. Consequently patients develop both basal hyperglycaemia—as insulin transcription reaches residual cellular levels—and marked post‑prandial excursions because NO-mediated GLUT −02 uptake becomes blunted [19]. In renal proximal tubules dysregulated SGLT − 2 expression explains intrarenal hypo‑threshold glucose reabsorption responsible for persistent glycosuria seen before plasma levels exceed the traditional range ≥180 mg/dL [20].

The clinical phenotype observed—in particular pronounced fasting hyperglycaemia at age 24 coupled with normal body mass index (BMI ≈24 kg/m²), lack of autoantibodies against GADA/Ia₂/ZnT8 glands, progressively impaired C‑peptide responses after glucagon despite relatively preserved residual secretion (<0·8 nmol/L)—reads coherently against an autoimmune process and fits well into an autosomal dominant monogenic framework designated maturity‑onset diabetes of youth (MODY). Most notable is the family pedigree reporting maternal grandmother, mother and younger brother diagnosed between ages 30–20 years—three generations harboring identical disease characteristics—this stands against sporadic type Ⅱ diabetes whereas childbearing anonymity suggests dominant inheritance [21].

To formally address whether the variation c.*809 A> > G qualifies as “pathogenic,” American College Of Medical Genetics (ACMG) criteria were applied systematically:

In practice this classification has therapeutic import: Established guidelines advocate first-line sulfonylureas—owing their K_ATP channel agonism—as effective because residual insulin machinery remains intact until later stages [23]. However our patient’s baseline glucagon-stimulated C‑peptide was modest (~0·83 nmol/L); GLP‑‑R agonists were therefore selected after confirmatory genetics given their lesser hypoglycaemic profile while preserving β‑cell integrity—a strategy supported by double-blind crossover data showing comparable glycaemic improvement versus glimepiride yet lower incidence of shunt-related hypoglycaemic events [24].

## Management Strategy & Therapeutic Decisions

The therapeutic journey in this case illustrates how a clinical strategy grounded in initial empirical control can evolve into a precision‑guided regimen once the underlying genetics are clarified. The care pathway progressed through three distinct phases—baseline rapid‑control therapy presumed for type 2 diabetes (T2D), transition to a GLP‑1 receptor agonist–driven approach following molecular confirmation of HNF1A‑MODY, and finally simplification of the regimen while maintaining tight glycaemic control.

### Initial Intensive Regimens Employing Basal Degludec Plus Oral Agents Utilised Prior Diagnostic Clarification

At presentation, the young adult displayed markedly elevated fasting plasma glucose (12.4 mmol/L), an HbA1c of 10.2 % and a 2‑hour post‑OGTT glucose of 19.8 mmol/L—all severe biochemical markers typical of both type I and type II diabetes when viewed alone. Although negative islet autoantibodies suggested a non‑type I phenotype, the rapid hyperglycaemia combined with low stimulated C‑peptide (0.83 nmol/L) warranted aggressive early control before definitive diagnostic conclusions could be reached. Pending confirmatory studies, clinicians adopted a conventional T2D paradigm:

* **Basal Insulin Degludec** – Eight units daily addressed fasting hyperglycaemia and provided broad coverage over a steady state period.
* **Metformin** – At a dose of 1500 mg/day it enhanced hepatic insulin sensitivity and suppressed gluconeogenesis.
* **Saxagliptin** – A DPP‑4 inhibitor that prolongs endogenous GLP‑1 activity offered an additional stimulus for glucose‑dependent insulin secretion without increasing hypoglycaemic risk.

This tripartite approach was intended not only to lower blood glucose rapidly but also to minimise endogenous glucagon elevation—a key driver of hepatic glucose output in poorly controlled diabetes mellitus—while preserving remaining β‑cell function within the first critical weeks following diagnosis.

One month later the patient’s HbA1c had fallen modestly from 10.2 % to 9.1 %, indicating partial efficacy but also raising questions about optimal long‑term strategy given his family history and negative autoimmunity panel.

### Transition Following Genetic Confirmation: From Basal Insulin to GLP‑1 Receptor Agonist Therapy

Genetic evaluation identified a heterozygous p.N270S mutation in HNF1A—a pathogenic variant situated within the DNA‑binding domain responsible for regulating genes essential to β‑cell function (e.g., GLUT2, pyruvate kinase). Understanding that HNF1A mutations compromise insulin gene transcription and compromise β‑cell responsiveness led clinicians toward therapies that augment residual β‑cell capacity rather than impeding it or forcing exogenous insulin administration indefinitely.

The decision thus was dual:

1. **Discontinuation of Basal Insulin** – Given that β‑cell secretory reserve remained evident (stimulated C‑peptide above 0.6 nmol/L), continued exogenous insulinemia was deemed unnecessary once adequate homeostasis could be achieved.
   
2. **Initiation of Dulaglutide** – A long‑acting GLP‑1 receptor agonist administered once weekly offers several mechanistic advantages in HNF1A‑MODY:
   * Stimulates glucose-dependent insulin secretion via cAMP pathways independent of K_ATP channels that are dysfunctional in these patients.
   * Suppresses postprandial glucagon release without provoking hypoglycaemia.
   * Promotes weight neutrality or modest loss—potentially beneficial against polyphagia observed at baseline.
   * May exert direct cytoprotective effects on islet cells by upregulating anti‑apoptotic genes mediated by PDX‐single protein interaction pathways altered by HNF1A loss.

Within three months under dulaglutide alone (plus adjunctive metformin remaining), the patient’s HbA1c plummeted from 9.1 % down to an impressive 6.0 %, illustrating the superior match between drug mechanism and disease biology afforded by precision medicine.

### Long-Term Maintenance and Simplification 

After sustained improvement over six months—confirmed by consistent HbA01 levels below 6½ %—clinicians considered further simplification without compromising metabolic control or patient convenience:

* **Metformin** remained as it continues to address hepatic steatosis risk indicated by elevated triglycerides (4.31 mmol/L) while still supportive against ectopic fat deposition contributing to impaired β-cell function.
  
* **Saxagliptin** was retained at its initial dose; its modest effect on incretin turnover effectively loops into maintaining baseline postprandial regulation now that glucagon secreting goes dampened physiologically under dulaglutide washout influence.
  
GLP‑1 agonists were withdrawn given tolerable adverse effect profile under their controlled dosing schedule such that long-term maintenance could rely on oral twinstones without exposing patients toward injection burden or rare pancreatitis risk associated with chronic parenteral therapy at high pharmacologic concentrations only transiently employed during race around identification of pathogenesis.

The resulting regimen—metformin plus saxagliptin—kept the patient achieving below target HbA01 readings while significantly simplifying life‐style adherence dynamics, illustrating an evolution from empiric intense therapy toward a judicious treatment plan fine-tuned by disease genotype knowledge.

### Concluding Perspective

Initially adopting a broad T2D aggressive control schema ensured prompt reduction of potentially ketosis-prone hyperglycemia until diagnostic certainty awaited genome sequencing results. Subsequent targeted manipulation toward GLP‑1 agonism capitalised upon intact glucagon/insulin dynamics uniquely deranged in HNF1A mutation carriers yet still functional enough for functional pharmacologic upregulation whose mechanisms bypass mutated transcription factors’ downregulation signals within pancreatic endocrine tissue.\n\nThis sequential management highlights how genetic confirmation can decisively pivot treatment direction—from simple diversionary basal insulin followed by strategic incretin modulation—ultimately culminating in simplified nomenclature preserving durable glycaemic health while honouring precision therapeutics essential in MODY care courses.\n

## Outcomes, Follow‑Up & Clinical Significance

At presentation the patient’s glycated haemoglobin (HbA₁c) was markedly elevated at 10.2 % (88 mmol/mol), with fasting plasma glucose of 12.4 mmol/L and an abnormal two‑hour OGTT peak of 19.8 mmol/L. Twelve days after initiating a baseline regimen that combined basal insulin degludec (8 U/24 h), metformin (1500 mg/day) and a DPP‑4 inhibitor (saxagliptin 5 mg/day), his HbA₁c had fallen to 9.1 %. The rapid decline within merely four weeks reflected good adherence but also indicated that residual β‑cell function was still present—a finding reinforced by a stimulated C‑peptide of 0.83 nmol/L during glucagon testing and negative islet autoantibodies. These data prompted cessation of insulin once HNF1A‑MODY was confirmed genetically; insulinemic therapy was replaced by once‑weekly dulaglutide (GLP‑1 receptor agonist) while maintaining metformin and saxagliptin.

Over the next five months on dulaglutide monotherapy plus background oral agents, the patient’s glycaemic control continued improving steadily—HbA₁c reached 6.0 % at month six post‑switch—demonstrating near–normoglycaemia without hypoglycaemic episodes or adverse reactions such as nausea commonly associated with GLP‑1 RA initiation. Given the sustained target attainment and a return interval exceeding three months between doses, therapy was subsequently simplified further by eliminating dulaglutide in favour of a dual oral regimen (metformin 1500 mg/day + saxagliptin 5 mg/day). At present the patient remains well controlled with an average HbA₁c <6.5 %, no microalbuminuria or retinopathy detected on baseline examination, and normal weight status (BMI 24.6 kg/m²).

From a precision‑medicine perspective this clinical course underscores several key lessons:

1. **Genotype-guided drug selection** – In HNF1A‑MODY sulfonylureas are traditionally first line because they directly stimulate residual β‑cell ATP‐sensitive K⁺ channels. However, GLP‑1 receptor agonists offer comparable efficacy with a lower hypoglycaemia risk profile—essential when β‑cell reserve is already diminished—as exemplified by our patient’s uncomplicated transition from basal insulin+oral agents to dulaglutide alone without hypoglycaemic episodes or renal side effects commonly observed in type 2 diabetes cohorts exposed to SGLT₂ inhibitors.

2. **Early genetic confirmation expedites treatment optimisation** – If HNF1A mutation had been delayed until after successful glycaemic response to basal–bolus insulin, unnecessary exposure to exogenous insulin would have continued because clinicians might still be uncertain whether endogenous β‑cell secretion could be preserved long enough for oral therapy alone—a scenario avoided here by early next‑generation sequencing shorted both cost per patient and time away from evidence‐based pharmacotherapy tailored for MODY physiology.

3. **Cascade screening potential** – Identification of the novel p.N270S pathogenic variant provides actionable information for family members who share the same autosomal dominant inheritance pattern; proactive monitoring can prevent late presentation with uncontrolled disease among siblings or unaffected relatives carrying this mutation.

4. **Evidence generation for rare subtypes** – The clinical response documented herein adds real‐world efficacy data supporting GLP‑1 RA usage beyond conventional type 2 diabetes trials—data valuable for refining therapeutic algorithms used in future consensus guidelines for MODY subtypes where evidence is sparse.

In summary, this case demonstrates how timely molecular diagnosis can transform therapeutic strategy—from an initial multi‑agent basal insulin approach toward a targeted pharmacologic regimen that normalises glucose control safely while preserving patient quality of life—and illustrates broader applicability across other monogenic diabetes entities where tailored precision medicine offers tangible benefits over empiric stepwise approaches typical of type 2 diabetes management.[citation needed]
