Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

Chapter 2: ## Differential Diagnosis & Diagnostic Reasoning

Young‑onset hyperglycaemia obliges clinicians first to delineate whether the underlying mechanism is immune‑mediated loss of β‑cell mass (type I diabetes), insulin resistance (type II diabetes) or a monogenic etiology such as maturity‑onset diabetes of the young (MODY). The constellation observed in this patient – non‑obese BMI (24.6 kg/m²), absence of classic lipid surrogate markers of severe metabolic syndrome except for mildly elevated triglycerides, significant familial clustering across three–four generations and an initial HbA1c exceeding 10 % – argues against an exuberant insulin resistance state while excluding overt autoimmunity once antibody studies are considered.*

### Strong phenotypic suggestion against autoimmune type I disease based on negative GADA/IA‑2 antibodies  

The first decisive exclusion was provided by serologic testing for pancreatic autoantibodies routinely used in diagnosing Type I diabetes or latent autoimmune diabetes in adults (LADA). In this patient both glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA) and islet antigen‑2 antibodies (IA‑2A) were negative at presentation and again after one month’s follow‑up when the diagnosis had yet be confirmed genetically (§“Investigations”). Autoimmune β‑cell destruction typically secretes these autoantibodies well before overt hyperglycaemia manifests; therefore persistent seronegativity is highly reliable evidence against a conventional Type I immunopathogenic process.^[1] The absence of any other systemic signs supporting an autoimmune endocrine disorder further reinforced this conclusion.[^2]

Because LADA epitomises slow‐progressive autoimmune beta‑cell loss even in adults over forty ‑ years old,^([3]) its exclusion is critical when considering monogenic causes such as MODY whose pathophysiology does not involve an immune attack field^[4].

> **Clinical take‑away:** In young adults who present with dysglycaemia yet show scant obesity or dyslipidaemia, a seronegative panel strongly tips the differential away from Type I diabetes and prioritises alternative diagnoses including MODY.

### Glucagon stimulation indicating insufficient endogenous insulin secretion evidenced by stimulated C‑peptide levels < ½ nmol/L  

β‑cell reserve can be quantified through dynamic measures such as the glucagon stimulation test or mixed‑meal trial.^[5] In healthy subjects, intravenous glucagon elicits a prompt surge in serum C‑peptide surpassing 5–10 ng/mL (~ 15–30 nmol/L) within five minutes.^[6] Persuading physiologically does it hold that any peak below approximately half a nanomole per liter represents significant hyposecretion relative to normal physiology—as corroborated by large cohort studies evaluating post‑glucagon recovery.^[7]

The present case showed a basal value of 0·39 nmol/L skyrocketing to only 0·83 nmol/L after six minutes—a response below the conventional “preserved” threshold (> 1·32 nmol/L).^([8]) This blunted stimulus response suggests two overlapping realities:
* The pancreas retains **some** secretory capacity — enough that baseline isn’t nil—yet
* The functional reserve is starkly reduced when juxtaposed against normal individuals who normally achieve ≥ ≥ ≈ 4–8 nmol/L at peak.^[9]

Such an intermediate phenotype fits neatly within what has been described for heterozygous HNF1α mutations—β‑cell dysfunction begins insidiously rather than abruptly initiating abrupt catabolism seen in classic Type I disease.^[10] Importantly, occult dysfunction detected via this kinetic study also distinguishes monogenic MODY from clinically silent prediabetes commonly seen across diverse ethnicities where routine fasting panels alone may exaggerate metabolic risk clues.[^11]

> **Clinical take‑away:** A *low* C‑peptide rise after glucagon remains pivotal not merely proving lack-of-insulin production—typical for late Type I disease—but also alerting clinicians that residual function still exists—a hallmark for many MODY variants including HNF1α mutations.

### Integrating findings into a diagnostic trajectory  

Combining the salient features—the young age (<25), nonsmoker BMI ≈25 kg/m² unaccompanied by clear metabolic syndrome traits beyond raised TGs, abbreviated yet significant fasting-glucose derangement without ketonaemia—and most importantly the *seronegative status* coupled with *blunted endogenous secretory response*, yielded an elevated clinical probability equation per contemporary Exe­ron model assessments (~62 %).[^12] This probability pivoted care toward comprehensive molecular testing using next-generation sequencing panels targeting known MODY genes—a step mandated until definitive genetic confirmation provided concrete evidence beyond analogue biochemical inference.

Once genetic testing revealed a heterozygous pathogenic p.N270S mutation in HNF1α™, all clinical premises aligned into one coherent entity: classically labelled “HNF1α-MODY.” The initial pre-genetic suspicion guided by seronegative immunology and poor yet partially intact insulin kinetics proved sound given eventual therapeutic adjustment—discontinuation of basal insulin followed by successful glycaemic control on GLP-­R agonists—with durable remission well below both her baseline HbA₁c (10·2 %) and contemporaneous thresholds found riskily high (^≥8·5 %).

|---|---|---|
| **Autoimmune antibodies** | Positive GAD65 / IA₂ | Negative |
| **BMI** | >30 kg/m² usually | ~25 kg/m² |

| **Early loss vs progressive decline** | Abrupt β-cell collapse | Slow progressive dysfunction |

This diagnostic cascade underscores how judicious combined laboratory interrogation distinguishes monogenic diabetes from classic types I/II—and emphasizes why continued vigilance over “negative” serologies and subtle kinetic deficits can preempt mislabeling young patients as Type II diabetics awaiting inappropriate sulfonylurea therapy or long-term basal-intensified regimens.Institutional practice treating unusual early-onset diabetes should therefore embed suspecting MONOLOGIC etiologies deterministically prioritized through serology coupled iteratively with dynamic secretory indices before embarking upon exhaustive therapeutic trials.
