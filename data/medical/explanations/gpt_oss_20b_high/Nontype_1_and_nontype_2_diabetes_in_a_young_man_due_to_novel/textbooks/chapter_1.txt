Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation & Initial Evaluation

The patient was a young adult male who first presented during an annual health examination rather than as an emergency referral for overt diabetic complications. Routine laboratory screening revealed a strikingly high glycated haemoglobin (HbA₁c = 10.2 %, equivalent to 88 mmol/mol) and an equally alarming fasting plasma glucose level (12.4 mmol/L). These findings confirm chronic hyperglycaemia that has been present for several months—HbA₁c reflects average glycaemia over three time‑points while fasting glucose captures baseline glycaemic control independent of post‑prandial fluctuations. In obese adolescents or adults with classical type 2 disease one would expect concurrent insulin resistance markers such as acanthosis nigricans; however this patient’s skin exam was unremarkable apart from the absence of acanthosis nigricans—a notable finding because this cutaneous sign often accompanies excess adiposity‑related insulin resistance seen in many adults with type 2 diabetes.

Vital signs were essentially normal – blood pressure was recorded at 112/70 mmHg and resting heart rate was 78 bpm – suggesting a lack of cardiovascular decompensation at presentation. The body mass index calculated from height and weight was 24·6 kg/m², placing him within the upper range of “normal” weight according to WHO criteria yet still considerably lower than most patients delivering typical type 2 presentations who are frequently at least mildly obese or display abdominal adiposity on physical inspection.

Metabolic laboratory studies provided additional nuance beyond classic tri‑adipokine profiles usually expected in type‑2 states. Triglycerides were markedly elevated at 4·31 mmol/L (≈380 mg/dL) while high‑density lipoprotein cholesterol was low at 1·01 mmol/L (<40 mg/dL). Although dyslipidaemia points toward an underlying metabolic disturbance—often seen in pre‑diabetes or established insulin‑resistance syndromes—it does not necessarily distinguish between monogenic forms versus polygenic endocrinopathies when isolated from obesity metrics or additional cardiometabolic comorbidities such as hypertension or impaired fasting glucose. Importantly, subsequent hepatic function tests were within normal limits, a vital clue when considering HNF1A mutations that may also alter hepatic transcriptional programmes affecting glycogen regulation yet rarely produce overt hepatic failure early on.

The most striking biochemical evidence came from a standard oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). Two hours after ingestion of a 75‑gram carbohydrate load the plasma glucose spiked dramatically to 19·8 mmol/L (>350 mg/dL), far exceeding thresholds used by the American Diabetes Association (>11·1 mmol/L) to define impaired glucose tolerance or overt diabetes based on post‑prandial response alone. Such precipitous elevations indicate either deficient first‑phase insulin release or pronounced hepatic gluconeogenic activity overexpressed by β‑cell dysfunction – features common to HNF1A‑associated maturity‑onset diabetes of youth (MODY).

Anaphylactic artifacts such as ketoacidosis were sought progressively; despite marked hyperglycaemia the patient exhibited clinically uncomplicated polyuria–polydipsia without diarrhoea or vomiting indicative of catabolism downregulated by glucagon itself or dehydration states known to precipitate ketogenesis in absolute insulin deficiency typically seen with type I disease.
