Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

Chapter 1: ## Initial Clinical Presentation

A woman in her early forties witnessed a sudden trajectory of evolving complaints that began with a motor‑vehicle collision and ended with unaided nocturnal choking episodes that precipitated measurable hypoxia at home. Shortly after a minor car accident involving brief loss of consciousness, she developed a persistent pressure‑like ache localized over the posterior vertex and occipital region—a symptom described as constant, coin‑sized localised pain that would not worsen with coughing or Valsalva manoeuvres. Despite multiple consultations over several years, these headaches were repeatedly attributed to “nummular” or “occipital” headache syndromes and were treated with superficial scalp injections offering only short‑lasting relief. This chronic vertex pain had become so ingrained that the patient eventually found relief only by cutting her hair short—a drastic cultural compromise underlining the severity of her discomfort.

Approximately seven years into this chronic vertex discomfort, she began reporting sudden choking sensations during meals. She described these events as abrupt involuntary coughs that interrupted both solid foods and liquids alike—a hallmark sign suggestive of dysphagia or transient obstruction rather than classic gastro‑esophageal reflux phenomena. Initially infrequent—perhaps one episode every few months—these choking events became progressively aggressive in both number and intensity over subsequent years, at times manifesting even without deliberate provocation such as heavy swallowing effort or sudden positional change.

The turning point came when the patient’s nightly pattern itself changed: over weeks without explanation she awoke from sleep after being abruptly roused by violent coughing and gasping—episodes occurring three to four times each week. Breakfast’s routine consumption loomed less pleasurable as each satisfactory meal risked being accompanied by an unscheduled “choking attack.” During one airport incident when she was waiting for boarding she reported dizziness followed by an objective SpO₂ drop to 78 %, an event captured by auxiliary staff on her phone’s pulse‑oximeter measurement in spite of her own reporting no dyspnea or distress at the time. Subsequent self‑monitoring during COVID‑19 isolation revealed intermittent asymptomatic drops in saturation down to between 70 %–86 %, which were noted later in continuous mode red flags alone when reviewed by herself after minutes help‑tried for those episodes resulted in life-threatening hypoxia eventually resolved only by supplemental oxygen administration.^1 These desaturations appeared disconnected from conscious perception—there was no sense of air hunger or panic accompanying these steep falls—suggestive more of an autonomous dysfunction than extrinsic airway obstruction.

Given these perplexing symptoms a multidisciplinary effort ensued with repeated flexible laryngoscopy three times and repeated upper gastrointestinal endoscopy twice over two separate visits—all showed normal mucosal surfaces, intact vocal cords, pharyngeal muscles without masses or lesions, intact esophageal sphincter tone, and no evidence of Mallampati grade changes or anatomical distortion.^2 Therefore any mechanical microlaryngeal etiologies—including partial obstruction by vegetative debris or structural lesions—could be ruled out confidently. No mechanical airway anomaly could explain both daytime choking during meals (which affected solids and liquids equally) nor nighttime desaturations objectively measured at low saturation ranges when the patient remained ostensibly asleep without experiencing any subjective breathlessness.
