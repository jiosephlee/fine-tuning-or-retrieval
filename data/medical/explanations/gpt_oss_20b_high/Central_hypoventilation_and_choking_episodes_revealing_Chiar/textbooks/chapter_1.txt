Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and Initial Evaluation

The patient’s chief complaint was a longstanding pattern of sudden choking that occurred both during meals and while sleeping, indiscriminately affecting solids and liquids. These episodes were described as abrupt, hard‑to‑control coughs that often woke her from sleep or interrupted her eating. No complete airway obstruction was reported, yet the events were sufficiently frequent (three to four occurrences per week at night) to disrupt daily life significantly. Importantly, she denied any paresthesia of the throat or sensation of breathlessness accompanying the paroxysms—an observation that later suggested a failure of automatic respiratory control rather than a mechanical or irritant stimulus.

Her medical history revealed a temporal link between a minor motor‑vehicle collision (approximately fourteen years prior) and a gradual onset of new symptoms. Immediately after the accident she began experiencing chronic occipital headache—a constant pressure‑like pain localized over a coin‑sized area at the vertex—that failed to respond to standard medical therapy. Although this headache predominated in the early years, it gradually gave way to more catastrophic respiratory symptoms. About six years after symptom onset, nocturnal choking episodes intensified in frequency and severity, evolving from occasional nighttime coughs into repeated overnight desaturations prompting emergency care at an international airport where supplemental oxygen promptly relieved her vertigo and nausea with no recovery of breathlessness.  

Home monitoring over the COVID‑19 pandemic period added a critical dimension to her clinical picture. Self‑bedside pulse‑oximetry revealed asymptomatic drops in pulse oximetry readings down to 70–80 % despite an absence of subjective dyspnea or tachypnea. These findings implied intermittent hypoventilation or transient obstructions not perceived by the patient—features reminiscent of acquired central hypoventilation (the so‑called Ondine’s curse). The lack of complaints about shortness of breath during these events underscored an impairment in automatic respiratory drive rather than overt mechanical airway compromise.  

Baseline clinical examination was remarkably unremarkable apart from these brief desaturation fluctuations: blood pressure 118/76 mm Hg, heart rate 72 bpm, respiratory rate 14 breaths per minute, and resting oxygen saturation 96 % on room air at clinic visit. No dysphagia signs such as tongue deviation or palatal weakness were noted; cranial nerve examination was otherwise intact except for mild allodynia over scalp hair follicles—a distinctive feature later attributed to occipital neuralgia associated with Chiari malformation type I.  

Prior diagnostic workup had focused largely on presumed structural or functional airway disorders. Three flexible laryngoscopies examined mucosal integrity and structural pathology within the vocal cords and upper airway; none demonstrated abnormality. Two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies excluded swallowing disorders originating from esophageal motility or reflux disease—again yielding normal results. The persistence of choking despite exhaustive ENT evaluation highlighted the necessity of expanding differential considerations to include neuronal causes such as central hypoxic drive failure induced by posterior fossa crowding or brainstem compression.  

Socially, these disabling symptoms translated into significant impact on quality of life metrics typical in patient-reported outcomes studies on dysphagia-related anxiety. The patient routinely avoided restaurants, parties, and even casual dining with friends due to fear of public choking episodes—behaviors linked with increased anxiety–depression scores seen in previous population-based studies among patients with respiratory dysphagia symptoms lacking clear structural etiology.^1 Concomitant social withdrawal compounded psychological distress thereby creating a secondary burden that might obscure objective assessment if background stressors are not acknowledged during history taking.^2  

Taken together—the insidious spread from mild headache to frequent nocturnal choking minus conventional ENT pathology, home-derived evidence

of silent hypoxia with minimal dyspnea—all converged into a suspicion that her underlying disorder involved impaired automatic control of respiration consistent with acquired central hypoventilation secondary to structural brainstem compromise—ultimately pointing toward Chiari malformation type I upon imaging review.
