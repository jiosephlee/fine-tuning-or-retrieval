Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Evaluation

The patient’s chief complaint was a longstanding pattern of sudden choking that occurred both during meals and while sleeping, indiscriminately affecting solids and liquids. These episodes were described as abrupt, hard‑to‑control coughs that often woke her from sleep or interrupted her eating. No complete airway obstruction was reported, yet the events were sufficiently frequent (three to four occurrences per week at night) to disrupt daily life significantly. Importantly, she denied any paresthesia of the throat or sensation of breathlessness accompanying the paroxysms—an observation that later suggested a failure of automatic respiratory control rather than a mechanical or irritant stimulus.

Her medical history revealed a temporal link between a minor motor‑vehicle collision (approximately fourteen years prior) and a gradual onset of new symptoms. Immediately after the accident she began experiencing chronic occipital headache—a constant pressure‑like pain localized over a coin‑sized area at the vertex—that failed to respond to standard medical therapy. Although this headache predominated in the early years, it gradually gave way to more catastrophic respiratory symptoms. About six years after symptom onset, nocturnal choking episodes intensified in frequency and severity, evolving from occasional nighttime coughs into repeated overnight desaturations prompting emergency care at an international airport where supplemental oxygen promptly relieved her vertigo and nausea with no recovery of breathlessness.  

Home monitoring over the COVID‑19 pandemic period added a critical dimension to her clinical picture. Self‑bedside pulse‑oximetry revealed asymptomatic drops in pulse oximetry readings down to 70–80 % despite an absence of subjective dyspnea or tachypnea. These findings implied intermittent hypoventilation or transient obstructions not perceived by the patient—features reminiscent of acquired central hypoventilation (the so‑called Ondine’s curse). The lack of complaints about shortness of breath during these events underscored an impairment in automatic respiratory drive rather than overt mechanical airway compromise.  

Baseline clinical examination was remarkably unremarkable apart from these brief desaturation fluctuations: blood pressure 118/76 mm Hg, heart rate 72 bpm, respiratory rate 14 breaths per minute, and resting oxygen saturation 96 % on room air at clinic visit. No dysphagia signs such as tongue deviation or palatal weakness were noted; cranial nerve examination was otherwise intact except for mild allodynia over scalp hair follicles—a distinctive feature later attributed to occipital neuralgia associated with Chiari malformation type I.  

Prior diagnostic workup had focused largely on presumed structural or functional airway disorders. Three flexible laryngoscopies examined mucosal integrity and structural pathology within the vocal cords and upper airway; none demonstrated abnormality. Two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies excluded swallowing disorders originating from esophageal motility or reflux disease—again yielding normal results. The persistence of choking despite exhaustive ENT evaluation highlighted the necessity of expanding differential considerations to include neuronal causes such as central hypoxic drive failure induced by posterior fossa crowding or brainstem compression.  

Socially, these disabling symptoms translated into significant impact on quality of life metrics typical in patient-reported outcomes studies on dysphagia-related anxiety. The patient routinely avoided restaurants, parties, and even casual dining with friends due to fear of public choking episodes—behaviors linked with increased anxiety–depression scores seen in previous population-based studies among patients with respiratory dysphagia symptoms lacking clear structural etiology.^1 Concomitant social withdrawal compounded psychological distress thereby creating a secondary burden that might obscure objective assessment if background stressors are not acknowledged during history taking.^2  

Taken together—the insidious spread from mild headache to frequent nocturnal choking minus conventional ENT pathology, home-derived evidence

of silent hypoxia with minimal dyspnea—all converged into a suspicion that her underlying disorder involved impaired automatic control of respiration consistent with acquired central hypoventilation secondary to structural brainstem compromise—ultimately pointing toward Chiari malformation type I upon imaging review.

## Differential Diagnosis And Systematic Work‑up

The patient’s chief complaints—recurrent choking during meals and sleep combined with asymptomatic oxygen desaturations detected by home pulse oximetry—prompted an expanded differential spanning the upper aerodigestive tract, pulmonary pathology, sleep‑related breathing disorders, neuromuscular disease and anatomic brainstem lesions. An algorithmic “rule‑out” approach was applied: conditions were evaluated in order of predisposing probability against available objective data until only a neuro‐anatomic etiology remained plausible.

Enthusiasm first turned toward common gastro‑esophageal conditions because hypopharyngeal irritation is a frequent precursor of pharyngeal spasm or coughing fits that mimic dysphagia. **Laryngopharyngeal reflux** (LPR) was considered due the patient’s early complaints of nausea. However, a 24‑hour ambulatory laryngopharyngeal pH probe revealed no supraglottic acid exposure over baseline levels (pH ≤ 4), negating reflux as the trigger for her cough‑like choking episodes.^[1] **Gastro‑oesophageal reflux disease** (GERD) was similarly excluded clinically: the patient received maximum tolerable proton pump inhibitor therapy yet had neither symptomatic relief nor evidence of erosive esophagitis on repeated upper gastrointestinal endoscopy.^[2] Hence refractory bile/pus aspiration could be ruled out early.
  
Next attention focused on **myasthenia gravis**, whose bulbar manifestations can range from intermittent aspiration coughs to life‑threatening dysphagia.^[3] Serum testing showed negative acetylcholine receptor–antibody levels (binding < 0·5 U/mL) plus MuSK antibodies (< 0·5 U/mL). Reciprocal clinical evaluation disclosed no diurnal fatigue or decrement after repetitive testing of ocular or bulbar strength.^[4] These findings invalidated an autoimmune cholinergic blockade explanation for her airway compromise.^[5]  

Concurrent **primary pulmonary disease** was assessed through imaging and functional testing because underlying lung parenchymal injury could explain hypoxic spells independent of airway mechanics.^[6] Both standard chest radiograph and high-resolution computed tomography demonstrated clear lung fields devoid of consolidations or interstitial opacities; spirometry reiterated preserved airflow limitation (FEV1 = 98 % predicted), alveolar ventilation remained intact (FVC = 96 % predicted), maximal inspiratory pressure underscored muscular strength (80 % predicted), while maximum expiratory pressure stayed within normal limits (75 % predicted). ^[7] Thus any intrinsic ventilatory illness such as COPD/heath litigation could be effectively dismissed.^[8]

Spontaneous oxygen desaturations raised suspicion of **obstructive sleep apnoea** (OSA); however, overnight surveillance via a type III portable monitor yielded an overall apnoea–hypopnoea index (AHI) of only 6·0/h—a value below most screening thresholds (>5/h) for moderate/severe pathology.^[9] A distinct feature in this patient’s record was that 72 % of recorded respiratory pauses were *central* rather than obstructive—a profile atypical for isolated upper airway collapse yet consistent with altered medullary drive.^[10] Accordingly, classic OSA was deemed unlikely although residual occasional apnoeic events persisted independent of airway impedance measurements.^[11]

Further thought turned toward **neurogenic dysphagia**, given the previous three flexible laryngoscopies favored mechanical patency while simultaneous subjective reports described identical trouble taking both solids and liquids without regime‐specific triggers—a hallmark pattern favoring neuromuscular dysfunction over structural stenosis.^[12] Videofluoroscopic swallowing study confirmed elevated pharyngeal pressure transients and premature bolus spurting into the airway across all consistencies without evidence of cricopharyngeal spasm or anatomical narrowing,^[13] cementing swallowing disorder classification as neurogenic rather than mechanical in origin.^[14]

At this juncture—the array of investigations capable specialties all returned unremarkable results—the clinical picture nonetheless worsened progressively over years in ways incompatible with benign functional disorders alone. Sequential inspiratory worsening noted during outpatient spirometry examinations hinted at rising upper cervical motor input compromise while bulbomotor nuclei may have been under sustained pressure.^[15] Such patterns steered diagnostic attention toward **brainstem compression**, specifically cerebellar tonsillar herniation pressing against medullary centers regulating respiration and swallowing reflexes—an anomaly historically referred to as Chiari malformation type I (CMI).^[] Moreover, subtle occipital neuralgia reported earlier linked directly with posterior fossa crowding complemented this tissue‐based hypothesis

Ultimately MRI revealed 11 mm cerebellar tonsillar descent through the protuberance at the **foramen magnum** coupled with compromised CSF flow dynamics—a finding fully compatible with CMI generating both cranial neuropathies manifesting as neurogenic swallowing difficulties *and* centrally driven hypoventilation evident in her duplex nocturnal pulse oximetry traces and home desaturations.^[16]

In sum, the stepwise exclusion methodically eliminated superficially plausible gastroenterological contributors, autoantibodies against neuromuscular junctions, primary pulmonary disease deficits—as well as idiopathic sleep–breathing disorders—with progressively insightful neuro‑respiratory correlations pointing decisively toward anatomic compressive pathology at the craniocervical junction whose imaging confirmation resolved most previously enigmatic symptoms.’’

## Pathophysiology Of Chiari I Malformation And Respiratory Manifestations  

**Chiari I definition**  
Chiari malformation type I is defined by descent of the cerebellar tonsils at least 5 mm below the level of the foramen magnum on sagittal MRI series. This anatomical criterion distinguishes clinically significant malformation from incidental tonsillar displacement that may be noted on routine scans without symptoms. In the index patient an MRI showed an 11‑mm ectopia, well above the threshold typically associated with neurologic sequelae such as autonomic breathing disturbances and cranial‑nerve deficits.  

**Posterior fossa volume mismatch**  
The posterior fossa is a confined pyramid‑shaped cavity bounded by bone on three sides and arthrogryposis‑based tissue on one side, harboring the vermiform cerebrum along with brainstem structures. In congenital cases a downward‑trending growth defect results in a chronically reduced posterior‑fossa volume relative to neural elements (the “volume mismatch”). This disproportion forces caudal migration of non‑compressible tissues—the cerebellar tonsils—and predisposes them to impinge on adjacent spinal cord and cranial‑nerve pathways when upright or during Valsalva manoeuvres. Though largely developmental, this patient’s prior trauma may have accelerated herniation once an abnormal capacity was revealed.  

**CSF outflow obstruction**  
The upthrust created by herniated tonsils interrupts normal cerebrospinal fluid dynamics at several levels—craniospinal conduit, aqueductus cerebri, and cervical canal distal tracts—producing a pressure gradient across the occlusion site. Over time this hampers pulsatile CSF wave propagation and increases intracranial stiffness, raising energy demands for ventilation and exacerbating brainstem ischemia during periods of low perfusion (e.g., sleep). The resultant metabolic distress may further impair central gas‑sensing mechanisms involved in automatic respirio­n regulation​6^​.  

**Medullary centre compression**  
The key anatomical target for maladaptive breathing is the ventrolateral medulla complex comprising the nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS), demarcating inspiratory/expiratory rhythmic activity through its projections onto pre‑Bötzinger cluster neurons responsible for automatic respiration (“chemoreceptor system”). In patients with ≥5‑mm tonsillar descent there is variable compressive deformation of these nuclei directly adjacent or partially occluded by displaced tissue (evidence even shows degree correlating poorly with symptom severity). A mechanical “spike” disrupts afferent chemosensory signals from peripheral O₂/H₂O₂ receptors travelling through the glossopharyngeal/ vagus afferents converging at the NTS and thereby blunts automatic drive during REM/slow‑wave stages when cortical supervision wanes​8^. In our patient this explains episodes of severe oxygen desaturation without subjective breathlessness typical of centrally caused apnoea (“acquired Ondine’s curse”).  

**Voluntary versus automatic breathing anesthesia**  
Voluntary inspiration is under cortical dominion via corticobulbar tracts descending directly onto hypoglossal and phrenic motor nuclei independent policy templates entrained through high‑level control centers such as premotor area M1^10^. Because these pathways bypass compromised medullary chemistry after cerebellar ectopia remains intact neurologically until direct callosal compromise occurs,^12 patients retain efficient airway patency during deliberate activities (mealtimes) yet lose spontaneous rhythmic drive during sleep or subtle distraction—exactly mirroring clinical presentations seen here where choking triggers occur unabated even when locomotively unaware.^13  

**Lower cranial nerves IX–XII injury**  
Compression at C1/C2 levels perturbs multiple lower cranial nervous output tracts that ascend alongside brainstem corticospinal fibres traversing Virchow–Robin spaces adjacent to herniated masses^15^. Disruption gives rise to “neurogenic” dysphagia manifest when both solids and liquids produce significant laryngeal spasm worsening safety swallowing patterns.^16 The symmetrical presentation aligns closely with involvement in nuclei devoted primarily to pharyngeal contraction rather than oral feeding mechanics alone.^17 Concomitant allodynic vertex pain reflects irritation or mild demyelination around larger peripherally oriented trigeminal roots receiving neuropathic signals via lamina IV/V pathways scattered across the dorsal columns adjacent vertebral column segments now restricted by crowding—representative historically defined as occipital neuralgia among CM patients^19^.  

**Acquired ‘Ondine’s curse’ distinction**  
The classic phenotype known as Ondine’s curse refers originally to congenital central hypoventilation stemming from PHOX2B mutations causing absence-of-black gene encoding transcription factor essential for autonomic network maturation.^22 In contrast acquired forms result when otherwise normal respiratory centres are physically compressed or dynamically displaced after head injury/therapeutic his craniovertebral junction pathology.^24 While genetics predispose only ~70%‐80% individuals from inherited PHOX2B variants presenting early before obesity checkpoints/pain stimuli trigger catastrophic ineffective ventilation^25;, adult-onset situations such as our case reflect mechanical interruption exclusively affecting chemosensitive signals required for unconscious breathing rather than primary transcriptional defects directly compromising central pattern generator maintenance.^26 The distinction matters clinically because surgical decompression often restores function whereas genetic disease requires ventilation support strategies covering inhalation deficits.^27

## Confirmatory Diagnostics And Imaging Findings

The definitive recognition of Chiari I malformation (CMI) in this patient relied on a sequential radiologic evaluation beginning with a re‑analysis of an older non‑contrast head computed tomography (CT) scan, followed by a detailed sagittal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) study and corroborative overnight sleep monitoring. Each modality contributed complementary evidence for the presence of a clinically significant tonsillar herniation while also refining the spectrum of associated complications that could underlie her respiratory symptoms.

### Re‑reviewed non‑contrast head CT  

The initial trauma CT performed 14 years ago had been reported at the time as normal because routine neonatal or adult brain protocols are not designed to detect subtle cranio‑cervical junction anomalies. In our retrospective review we systematically evaluated the posterior fossa architecture, focusing on measurements across the foramen magnum and the sagittal length of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) space at the craniocervical junction. The CT revealed two key abnormalities:

1. **Crowding at the foramen magnum** – The bony margins surrounding the foramen appeared narrow relative to adjacent basilar structures, resulting in reduced transverse diameter and an obliterated CSF cuff.
2. **Loss of CSF space** – A faint linear hyperdense line traced along the ventral aspect of the cerebellar tonsils extended just below the occipital condyles, suggesting displacement or restriction against CSF streams distally into the cervical canal.

These findings were subtle by CT standards but are highly suggestive when combined with clinical suspicion—particularly when observed contemporaneously with a post‑traumatic onset of headaches and choking episodes. The presence of these features on a routine non‑contrast scan underscores why formal radiologic review is warranted whenever unexplained neuro‑oropharyngeal symptoms arise.

### Sagittal MRI sequence – confirmation of tonsillar descent

The confirmatory MRI incorporated high‑resolution T2‑weighted sagittal images acquired through both cisterns and the craniovertebral junction, allowing precise measurement of cerebellar tonsillar position relative to Basion–opisthion line—a classic metric for CMI assessment. In this patient:

- **Tonsillar descent exceeded 11 mm below Basion**—well above the ≥5 mm threshold defining symptomatic Chiari I.
- **Cerebellar tonsils compressed against the posterior surface of the medulla**—producing a mild indentation where tegmentum overlapped with CSF pathways.
- **No secondary syrinx formation** was identified on T2 axial sections through cervicothoracic segments, discounting a common sequelae that may exacerbate spinal cord dysfunction or respiratory compromise.

A palpable reduction in CSF volume at five distinct levels along the cervical cord further illustrated active obstruction at multiple points within the CSF conduits—another hallmark solidifying CMI’s role as an underlying structural lesion rather than a transient post‑traumatic swelling phenomenon.

### Absence of syrinx – narrowing disease spectrum

Syringomyelia, defined by intramedullary fluid cavities arising primarily from hindbrain herniation, can amplify both neurological deficits (e.g., upper‑limb sensory loss) and respiratory dysregulation through midbrain lesions. However, axial MRIs demonstrated an intact spinal cord devoid of any fluid collection or cavitation across all cervical segments examined (C1–C7). This absence rules out concomitant syringomyelia—and therefore rules out roughly one-third to one-half of patients who develop symptomatic disease when cerebellar tonsil descent approaches >15 mm—contributing substantially to our estimation that developmental brainstem compression alone is sufficient to account for respiratory symptoms in this case.

### Polysomnography limitations – type III constraints

Overnight type III polysomnography captured airflow via nasal cannula/thermistor, respiratory effort via thoracic bands, SpO₂ waveforms, and heart rate but lacked capnographic data and scalp electroencephalograms (EEG). Consequently:

- **Apnea–hypopnea index (AHI) was calculated over total recording time**, which incorporates awake periods during night travel or sedation cues inherent in an ambulatory test set‑up.
- Because no EEG was available to delineate true sleep stages, AHI = 6 events/hour may overstate events relative to true‐sleep hours.
- Importantly, since supine postural changes during a home-based test are highly variable, SPT—hours slept versus hours monitored—is indeterminable without EEG confirmation.
  
The result is an underappreciated magnitude in both central apnea frequency and overall breathing irregularity when these data are interpreted outside their original context—but recommended routine care dictates supplemental full-modality studies when clinical suspicion is high as in this patient’s scenario.

### Predominant central apnea component (>72 %) – Evidence against obstructive pathology

Despite an overall mild Arousional Apnea-Hypopnea Index (AHI = 6), event analysis revealed that **more than 70 % were classified as central apneas**—events characterized by absent airflow vibrations without corresponding thoracoabdominal effort spikes noted on phase‐difference recordings from thoracic bands. Central apneas arise from impaired automatic respiratory control directed by medullary centers—or temporally disrupted axonal routes linking upper airway reflex nerves to cortical command networks—which are directly affected by atlantoaxial tonsillar herniation in CMI cases. The high proportion thus strongly supports a structure‑based impairment rather than mere upper airway mechanical collapse seen in classic obstructive sleep apnea models involving patients’ pharyngeal-lower palate tissues or related muscular weakness.

In summary, re-examining older head CT provided initial structural clues prompting dedicated MRI scanning—a decisive diagnostic step establishing ≥11 mm cerebellar tonsillar descent consistent with symptomatic Chiari I malformation while ruling out an often co-occurring syrinx complication. Sleep phenomena documented through type III polysomnography underscored a predominance of centrally driven hypoventilation events aligning with medullary dysfunction known to result from such brainstem compression.
