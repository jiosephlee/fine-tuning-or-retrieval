# Finding Hidden Treasures  
**How Re‑examining Routine Head CTs Can Unmask Symptomatic Chiari Malformations**

---

## Introduction  

Two things share an unsettling similarity: you expect a routine tool to be reliable, yet sometimes it fails you in the most dangerous moments—just like a patient who wakes up gasping in her sleep because her own breathing has shut down without her realizing it. That’s precisely what happened to a woman in her early 40s whose chronic choking episodes and nocturnal desaturations were finally explained when an old head‑CT was re‑read months earlier. Her story reminds us that occasionally the “normal” scan is not normal after all—and that digging deeper into old images can unveil life‑changing diagnoses such as Chiari type I malformation (CMI).

---

## A Case Worth Rerunning  

The patient long ago suffered a minor motor‑vehicle collision that left her briefly unconscious. The emergency dept took an uncomplicated non‑contrast head scan, which was reported “normal” because the radiologist was focused on finding skull fractures or intracranial bleed—classic trauma priorities leave little room for subtle cerebellar nuances. Two decades later she began having **unpredictable choking during meals and sleep**, followed by unexplained bouts of low oxygen saturation (70 %–80 %) while being asymptomatic—a classic “silent” hypoxia pattern akin to Ondine’s curse (acquired central hypoventilation syndrome). Multiple ENT laryngoscopies and GI endoscopies sung her swallow sound entirely normal while she struggled socially and emotionally like a silent wrestler against her own airway.

When she finally had someone pulse‑watch something during COVID lockdown, multiple off‑track oxycations spiraled into severe daytime oxygen dips that did *not* trigger awareness of dyspnea—a hallmark that pointed straight toward automatic respiratory failure rather than mechanical airway obstruction or lung disease. A flying brakeage caused another near‑desaturation event when she was treated at an airport kiosk → supplemental O₂ sedated her within minutes, but without any perception of breathlessness before it happened.

It was **the old trauma CT**, re–reviewed after two new neurologic symptoms surfaced—a decades–old canvas bearing crowded posterior fossa anatomy—and went from “radiologically irrelevant” into a goldmine:

* **Cerebellar tonsils** sunk 11 mm past McRae’s line (the craniocervical junction baseline).
* A marked loss of midline CSF space at the opisthion signaled crowding.
* No acute bleed; no fracture – just mild congenital/traumatic malformation now pressing on the medulla.

MRI confirmed CMI and demonstrated centrally dominant apnea events on polysomnography – evidence we were dealing with *brainstem*, not chest wall pathology. Posterior fossa decompression remains the definitive rescue operation, though our patient opted for observation + smart coping strategies (short haircuts helps relieve vertex pain) while continuing monitoring.

## Why CMI Gets Overlooked on Routine Scans  

### Radiology Focus – Trauma Only  

In most centres, head–CTs performed after acute injury are read through “shell” filters trained specifically toward traumatic lesions: fractures, bleeds, skull base disruption – all flagged within seconds because risk management demands them first priority. Any incidental finding outside these criteria risks falling through the cracks unless:
1. The image includes multi‑phase reconstructions covering the cervical spine,
2. The radiologist chooses extra slices through C2/C3 levels,
3. There is an actually abnormal crossing thrombus;

Otherwise because it sits outside triage scope—and because many head scans are axial only without proper sagittal reconstruction—tonsillar descent inadvertently slips past even seasoned readers who are chasing obvious life‑threatening problems first-line shift/pseudo lines & stripes detection algorithms.


### Small Quantities Matter – Traditional Thresholds vs Clinical Reality  

CMI is officially defined as >5 mm cerebellar tonsillar descent *below* McRae’s line **on sagittal MRI** (or proportional vertical distance). On low‑resolution CT systems lacking thin cuts (<1 mm), tiny excursions often look muted against thick coronal slices typical in routine trauma workstreams → many cases fall between scanners' gutters value <5 mm historically labeled “variant”.

Furthermore, the neck flexion position during imaging amplifies descent but most emergency protocols keep patients supine with minimal cervical extension → underestimates potential crowding seen when awake & maximal neck extension occurs during regurgitation episodes.[^1]

## Why This Matters Clinically  

1. **Stop Chronological Arsenic Chase**  
   Instead of looping patients through ENT, gastroenterology & pneumology workups ("All those negative swallowing studies!"), check old scans first—save environmental time & reduce loan-surgical burden of misdiagnosis (“Wait until discharge…?”).
   
2. **Unmask Life‐Threatening Hypoventilation Early**  
   Oxygen dips at home that cross alarmers automatically generate seen here were episodic central apnea hits where nasal mask would never help because they’re driven out crossing medullary reticular centers—not airways themselves.
   
3.(‍) Attachment Aware Diagnostics™  
   Herniation swelling may produce mechanical compression during coughing exertion + Valsalva → classic headaches poorly responsive to NSAIDs leading some sets-driven headache programs [i.e., airborne treat nummular vs Hook small].    If typical hospital workflow acknowledged choked coughing as a neurological red flag earlier “post trauma”, unnecessary multiple specialists within five breaths would be avoided—CO₂ budgets can then be reallocated toward MR isotonic diving sublesional lumbar spinal MRI earlier when ancillary studies fail.
