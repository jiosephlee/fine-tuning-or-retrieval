# Choking Without a Shadow

## Why her “I’m choking” moments looked less like panic and more like silent brainstem mishaps  

When Dr Ali comes into the clinic with seven years of sudden choking spells — disturbing meals today and waking up every night gasping into her pillow **without any obvious airway collapse** — she turns me inside out more than any thick mucus plug ever did during a scolded endoscopy session a decade ago.  

It wasn’t simply an uncomfortable culinary mishap; it was an awakening cue pointing right up into her brainstem where the “automatic” drive to breathe waits its cue behind a crowding roof‑style skull base…  

---

## The medical detective trail  
The woman’s story you’ll remember most vividly hinges on three “aha!” moments:  

1. **Sneaky hypoxia:** during COVID she started checking her own SpO₂ and found drifting values plummeting 70–80 % on **no distress**, which happened again on an international layover where supplemental oxygen cured instant dizziness without any sighs or chest tightness!  
2. **A carousel of normalities:** over ten flexible laryngoscopies plus two GI endoscopies yielded abandoned normality lights‑out patterns while her history kept growing tauter with every appointment barbed back the same red‑flag “choking.”  
3. **A long‑hidden CT hit:** retrieving her traumatic brain scan from 14 years ago cracked open a crowded foramen magnum — the look‑and‑feel brake pull from behind a tiny posterior fossa pushing her cerebellar tonsils downwards by 11 mm meets—and overruled any earlier “so‐and‐so” head injury thought appropriate!  

Once an MRI confirmed **Chiari I malformation (CMI)** representing obvious cervico‑medullary compression, every otherwise puzzling feature collided into one anatomical picture: **brainstem compression causing loss of autonomous respiratory timing** (akin in effect —but not etiology —to congenital Ondine’s curse).

**Take‑Away Pearls**

- Chronic non–mechanical choking affecting solids AND liquids equally should prompt you to consider neurogenic dysphagia early rather than prolong ENT workups alone.
- Residual hypoxia unaccompanied by dyspnea during sleep or distractive states is classic sign of acquired central hypoventilation linked either directly or indirectly to posterior fossa compression.
- A well-directed Video Fluoroscopy will reveal characteristic neurogenic features such as delayed pharyngeal transition timing (> 4 s), inadequate tongue base retraction, incomplete epiglottic closure, and early supraglottic widening—all hints toward medullary/upper spinal root involvement.
