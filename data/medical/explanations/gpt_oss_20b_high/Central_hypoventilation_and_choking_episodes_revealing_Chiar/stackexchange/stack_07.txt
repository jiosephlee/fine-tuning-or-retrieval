Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

### [Differential Diagnosis] How would you differentiate acquired clinically ‘Ondine’s curse’ secondary to Chiari I from congenital central hypoventilation syndrome due to a PHOX2B mutation?

**Differentiating Acquired (“Ondine’s Curse” from Chiari I) Versus Congenital Central Hypoventilation Syndrome (PHOX2B mutation)**  

1. **Age of Onset & Associated Anomalies**  
   *Congenital* cases arise in infancy or early childhood and frequently coexist with Hirschsprung disease, neural crest–derived defects or autonomic nervous system dysplasia. **Patient age**—the woman in her 40s—does not fit this pattern and argues against a congenital etiology.  
   *Acquired* cases appear after a precipitating event (head trauma, surgery) or progressively during adulthood—as in this patient whose choking and hypoxia evolved over 14 years following a motor‑vehicle collision—consistent with secondary Chiari I.

2. **Family History**  
   A positive family history or consanguinity is classic for congenital central hypoventilation syndrome due to familial PHOX2B repeat expansions. The case reports a negative family history, again favoring an acquired process.

3. **Neuroimaging**  
   *Chiari I*: sagittal MRI demonstrates ≥5 mm cerebellar tonsillar descent below the foramen magnum with evidence of cranio‑vertebral junction crowding or syrinx formation—exactly what the MRI showed (11 mm descent).  
   *Congenital CCHS*: brain MRI is usually normal regarding midline structures; absence of tonsillar herniation differentiates it from Chiari pathology. A normal MRI in an infant/child presenting with hypoventilation would support a phox2B‑related diagnosis over an anatomical obstruction.

4. **Genetic Testing**  
   Screening for peripheral nervous system anomalies is unnecessary if imaging demonstrates an obvious mechanical explanation; however, persistent unexplained hypoventilation mandates a PHOX2B repeat expansion assay (preferred first‑line test). In this patient no such mutation was screened because the confirmatory Chiari finding explained symptoms entirely—an approach endorsed by current guidelines recommending genetic workup only when imaging is negative and clinical suspicion remains high.

5. **Polysomnographic Features**  
   Congenital disease typically produces marked hypercapnia at night from the outset and often demonstrates very low tidal volumes even during wakefulness if ventilatory support is needed early in life—features absent here because the patient maintained normal voluntary breathing during day and only exhibited central events during sleep/when distracted (classic “forgotten‑to‑breathe” pattern seen in Chiari).

6. **Other Clinical Clues**  
   - Presence of chronic occipital headache responsive to hair removal suggests scalp neuralgia linked to posterior fossa crowding—this neurocutaneous correlation has no precedent in PHOX2B disease.
   - Absence of lower cranial nerve deficits or autonomic instability beyond respiration further supports an isolated compressive lesion rather than widespread neural crest dysgenesis seen in CCHS.


**Bottom Line:** In practice begin by obtaining MRI whenever adult patients present with unexplained central apnea/reflex choked episodes—especially if accompanied by headaches or sensory complaints around the vertex/occiput as seen here. A predominant radiographic finding of tonsillar herniation essentially establishes an acquired “Ondine’s curse” etiology and obviates routine PHOX2B testing unless additional syndromic features are present.*
