# Cystic Crossroads  
**Differentiating Endometriomas From Leiomyomas With Ultrasound And Non‑Contrast MRI**

---

## 👀 Why This Case Stumped Us

Picture it: a nulliparous woman at ~21 weeks gestation groans over a massive left upper abdomen—pain sharp enough for her visu analogue score (VAS) 5—and breathlessness so potent it almost feels like a congenital lung disease rather than a uterine oesophageal stretch injury. Two gigantic unilocular cysts sit where your ovaries should—one dwarfs the left upper quadrant all the way up to the fundus, the second blooms right into the cul-de-sac beside what will soon be a full-term baby’s head—yet their true origin is anything but obvious until you go under the knife **and discover one is an atypical pedunculated leiomyoma twisting itself into hemorrhagic necrosis**! That twist not only explains her fever and anemia but also steered definitive management away from a routine “remove ovarian cyst” mentality toward carefully balancing life‑saving surgery against preserving an ongoing pregnancy.

---

## 📖 Clinical Snapshot (From the Report)

*40s, BMI 33, G 1 P 0*  
*21 + 6 w GA → severe upper LUQ and pelvic pain + dyspnea*  

|                          | | Details |
|-------------------------|---|---------|
| **Imaging** | Ultrasound | 2× unilocular cysts |
|                | Size | Upper ≈18×12×7 cm; Lower ≈9½ ×8×8 cm |
|                | Peripherally vascularised |

| **Lab** | CA‑125 ↑ 41 U/mL (trend ↑) |

Intraoperatively:
* **Upper lesion:** Pedunculated uterine leiomyoma—thick vessel-rich stalk twisted → leaking & ischemia → fever & anemia  
* **Lower lesion:** Left‐sided intracavitary‐subserosal myoma—mildly enlarged but left untouched  

Outcome: Vaginal delivery avoided surgically because lower mass would have impeded head passage—elective Caesarean at 37 w on scar—and both mother & neonate remained excellent “wins”.

---

## 🎚️ Imaging Jeopardy ― Lessons From Ultrasound

Top‑down bowel obstetrics UI would usually not gamely delimit *which* side epithelium or muscle architecture constitutes your culprit—especially when maternal size issues mask ovarian geometry—or something like this could easily mislead even a seasoned sonographer into characterized “decidualised endometrioma.” Here’s what you need to factor into your differential when seeing two massive lesions in pregnancy:

| Feature | Classic Endometrioma | Vanishing Leiomyoma ‑ Degeneration |
|---------|---------------------|-----------------------------------|
| **Wall Thickness** | Usually ≤5–10 mm; simple smooth contour | May be ≥10–15 mm if infarction/adpated | 
| **Internal echoes** | “Shading” signal – low-level heterogeneous echoes from old haemorrhage | More superimposed heterogeneity from red‑cell debris / mucinous fluid |
| **Vascularity** | Peripheral color Doppler pooling mainly tiny venules; minimal arterial flow | Often peripherally prominent arterial pulsations intruding through thickness |
| **Calcification** | Very rare unless old rupture ensues | Occurs especially as subserosal borders harden over time |

In our patient’s pitfall case:
* Both lesions had peripheral flow—but overall intense feeders suggested trophogenic activity more typical of *leiomyomata*.  
* Thick borders (~14 mm) were not what we'd expect from an “endometriotic” phenotype dragging around primordial ovaries alone.)

## ✅ Clinical Pearls – Take Home Messages

• Employ low field (< 1.5 T) non‑contrast MRI beyond first trimester safely when US finds large unilocular adnexal lesions whose demarcation is unclear!  

• Thick (>10 mm) pigmented walls plus discrete internal solid foci favor *degenerating subserosal/pedunculated *leio­myo­mas over simple endometrio­lomatous cysts.*

• Preserving less becoming best idea – Leaving pitch catalyzed oophorospinal metastasis often yields better outcomes than conquering all lesions across uterus impetus `intramural` risk profile!

# Interpreting Tumor Markers In Pregnancy  
### *The Role Of Serial CA 125 Levels*

## Why This Case Makes You Pause

Picture a multiply‑travelling patient—an obese nulliparous woman in her forties presenting at **21 weeks + 6 days** gestation with sharp left upper quadrant pain and pelvic discomfort coupled with mild dyspnea. Two huge cystic lesions appear on ultrasound—a persistent **18×12×7 cm** shoulder‐to‑fundus mass on the left upper quadrant and a **9½×8×8 cm** belly‑in‑the‑pouch mass tethered “adjacent” to an intramural leiomyoma deep inside her pelvis. After lab work shows fever‐producing Klebsiella infection and iron deficiency anemia worsening during admission, her **CA‑125 climbs from 36 U/ml two weeks prior up to an alarming 41.8 U/ml** just before surgical decision‐making.

At face value you’d think about ovarian carcinoma—especially when you hear “just *above* 35 U/ml”, a classic red flag exceeding most obstetric labs’ cutoffs for postmenopausal women—even though she’s far younger than that threshold—and feel drawn into an urgent operation stackpile… until you see the operative findings thank God:

* The gigantic upper mass is actually a **pedunculated leiomyoma** twisted on its own blood supply—a true rediagnosis shift!
* Only half surfaced an urgent cystic tumor whose anatomy was ambiguous until laparotomy laid it bare.
* A follow‐up surgical approach consisted only of removing the dangerous pedunculated portion while leaving the cannily tethered pelvic cyst intact after discussing neonatal readiness through multidisciplinary corners.
  
What tipped us out *early* was recognizing how far **serial changes in serum tumor markers differ from static bedside numbers** when you’re treating a pregnant patient flying through fluorescent anatomy’s dynamic loops.

## Normal Physiology of *CA–125* During Gestation

| Timing   | Pharmacodynamics | Typical Reference Median |
|----------|------------------|----------------------------|
| First trimester | Greater production by decidual & trophoblastic cells + proliferation of supportive stromal tissue | ~23 U/ml (significantly higher than null state) |
| Second trimester | Decidua shrinks & metabolically quiesces → ‘nadir’ phase | ~12–14 U/ml |
| Third trimester | Re‐activation of trophoblasts → another uptick just before delivery | ~20–21 U/ml |

> **Bottom line:**  
> *A solitary value at any point can resemble cancer versus physiologic up‑shift.*  

Our patient’s initial value at **36 U/ml**, measured later than most normal mid‑gestational cadavers (~7–10 weeks after enrolment), falls comfortably ahead on the physiological curve—notably above median—but also sits very close to traditional **35 U/ml oncologic cut‐off** used for non-pregnant checkpoints.

## Why Sit Down For “Serial” Readings

In clinical practice we often register one labs snapshot—“the current number”—and lean heavily on textbook thresholds. That works well only when every factor that might raise or lower that number stays constant over time—or at least it’s irrelevant until then—and those factors don’t exist in pregnancy!  

Serial readings (“trend”) provide extra layers:

1. **Slope matters** – an incremental rise >5 U/ml over a fortnight raises alarms almost irrespective of absolute load because it writes X onto rising physiological background.
2. **Rounds down near normal peaks when accompanied by stable imaging findings** hint towards benign fluctuation (e.g., JG low B weights).
3. **Rapid plateau followed by spike correlates strongly** with necrosis or torsion generating acute release into perihematological milieu—precisely what happened for our patient’s twisted peduncle.

# When To Operate On A Singleton Mass In The Second Trimester?

**In a patient who received an emergency admission at 22 weeks for two gigantic cystic masses causing abdominal pain and mild dyspnoea, the surgical team faced an immediate dilemma** – obstructive symptoms had started coming back after several days of observation, a fever appeared (Klebsiella pontiacera isolated from urine), her haemoglobin dropped precipitously and ultrasound showed one mass attached to a pedunculated subserosal myoma about 9 cm long – an anatomical arrangement that screams “treatment‑ready”.  
What followed was a crack‑wide‑accessible open myomectomy followed by an elective caesarean at 37 weeks that turned out fine for both mother and baby – but why intervene **now**, **where** exactly is the tipping point between conservative watchful waiting and surgical relief?

---

## What Makes This Report Stand Out?

1. **Two synchronous cystic ovarian‑like lesions found mimicking massive endometriomas** – yet both turned out to be giant pedunculated/subserosal leiomyomas once we opened them up.
2. **Impressive weight‑lifting** – one lesion measured ~18 × 12 × 7 cm cradling almost five pregnant uterine volumes sideways on the fundus while another touched the pouch of Douglas.
3. **Clinical picture tipping** – persistent moderate pelvic pain (VAS = 5), new-onset mild dyspnoea attributed solely to mechanical compression **and** iron‑deficiency anemia deepening from Hb 9.9 g/dL → 7.5 g/dL within days.
4. **Diagnostic tango** – ultrasound + non‑contrast MRI + serial CA‑125 trends all pointing toward potential complication rather than reassuring benignity.
5. **Interdisciplinary Morse code** – obstetricians, gynaecologists, surgeons and anaesthetists coordinating around fetal viability checks every step of the way.

---

## Key Questions When Confronted With One Giant Abdominal Mass Mid‑Pregnancy

| Symptom / Finding | Why It Matters | Threshold For Surgery |
|-------------------|----------------|-----------------------|
| **Severe abdominal/pelvic pain (VAS > 4)** | Directly impairs quality‑of‑life *and* may prelude rupture or torsion | Immediate work‑up + consider expeditious intervention |
| **Dyspnoea / respiratory compromise** | Usually mechanical from mass effect (>18 cm occupying left subphrenic space commonernally causes diaphragmatic elevation) | Acceptance only if dyspnoea leads to hypoxaemia or pulmonary oedema |
| **Fever / infection signs** | Infection from necrotic/first‐torsioned pedicle raises sepsis risk & mandates emergent removal | Fever + positive cultures ≠ immediate intervention unless high WBC & CRP ↑ |
| **Rapid growth rate (+2–3% per week)** | Predicts imminent torsion/rupture — especially w/ subserosal pedicle | Serial ultrasounds every 2–3 weeks once growth >1 cm/month |
| **Solid components / irregular margins on US/MRI** | Heightened suspicion of malignancy •CA‑125 rising trend adds weight | Full non‑contrast MRI + tumor markers → strong indication if solid pieces present |

---

## How Was Our Patient Evaluated?

1. **Bedside Clinical Exam & Bimanual Vaginal Assessment**  
   • Found mobile hard mass (~9 cm) bulging into posterolateral fornix → probable pedunculated myoma anchoring a sacculated cystic niche.

2. **Ultrasound**

   *Upper* cyst 18×12×7 cm → unilocular with peripheral flow  
   *Lower* cyst 9½×8×8 cm → attached *to* intramural/subserosal posterior myoma (58×71 mm) showing predilection for cystic degeneration

   Remarkable lack of ascites despite huge bulk made massive internal pressure plausible explanation for low‑grade fever rather than peritonitis (“congestive tamponade” theory).

3. **Lab Work**

   *CBC*: Iron deficiency anemia  
   *CRP*: Mild elevation  
   *Urine culture*: Klebsiella pneumoniae  
   *Tumor markers*: CA‑19–9/15–3/CEA/LDH normal; CA‑125 rose from 36→41 U/mL over two weeks

4. **MRI (non‑contrast)**  

   Thick wall (~1⁄2 cm); T1/T2 heterogeneity suggestive of central necrosis plus small solid nodular areas—“red flag” cornerstones pointing towards degenerating tissue more than simple serous fluid collection found in uncomplicated endometriomas.



---

## Why Surgery Was Chosen Over Expectant Care  

The multidisciplinary consensus pulled together several clinical “red flags”:

1. **Painful compromise despite analgesia** – paracetamol IV reduced VAS from 5 to 1 only after two days hinting at reversible mechanical cause rather than inflammatory derangement.
2. **Dyspnoea attributable solely to compression** – physiologic micromechanics inside the thorax would heighten placental perfusion demands manually suppressed by the mass’s presence.
3. **Torsion‐like presentation on imaging/during exploration** – evident twisted pedicle seen intraoperatively releasing serosal fluid => high risk for rupture with fatal bleed or infection spread into peritoneum.
4. **Infection evidence** + anemia spikes = early inflammatory cascade probably circulating through venous plexus from compromised tissue bed.
5▪️ Rapid decreases in hemoglobin plus TXS boosting within critical threshold (“hemoglobin <8 g/dL” crucial if projected operative blood loss >400 mL).
6⚠️ Rising CA‑125 levels—while not diagnostic alone—coupled with imaging suspiciousity pushed us toward definitive diagnosis (and potential malignant features are something we cannot theoretically miss during pregnancy).

Hence opting for an early trimestical intervention aligned literature evidence suggesting that early surgical removal is safer when symptoms stay “nonexemptible” vs leaving large masses prone to episodic tearing midthird trimester into labor, possibly leading you towards emergency c‑section w/ uncontrolled haemorrhage.

## So… When Should You Actually Pull Out Your Knife?

The patient’s journey summarises a pragmatic algorithm:

• Symptom severity incompatible w/out treatment should prompt action (*pain >4/10 within ±24h despite analgesia ±Vasicalm stops paying shear*).  
• Any respiratory compromise induced mechanically warrants early operation (*dyspnea unresponsive tO support oxygen—caution extravascular compression matters).  
• Evident infectious markers (fever >38°C + positive urine/blood culture) rise urgency gradul.; treatment simultaneously combined w/intraoperative antibiotics standardized by culture sensitivity panel (**cefazolin + clarithromycin sequence guidelines eg consensus table**) .  
• Radiographic findings showing broad border irregularities or >10% rapid expansion (>1 cm/month) over previous scan push toward surgical consensus beyond expectant watchfulness (*e.g., every other sonogram doubling distances over baseline·blurred central hyperintensity).* 
• New onset solid septa or thickened wall suspicious on MRI mandates removal unless daughter working antibody negativity lifts suspicion—mostly limited by lack-of contrast safety considerations (*nonenhanced MRI safe >1T after first trimester.*).

# Obstetric Considerations After Mass Excision  
**Monitoring Fetal Well‑being And Timing Of Delivery**

---

## Introduction  

We handed a nulliparous woman in her forties into the OR at nearly 22 weeks’ gestation for an “unusual” abdominal picture: two giant cystic masses—one spanning the left sub‑phrenic space down into the uterine fundus and another filling the pouch of Douglas—causing severe pain and dyspnea.  

The diagnosis was *unlikely* ovarian cancer (normal tumor markers except a modest rise in CA‑125) yet unmistakably dangerous enough that we elected an open laparotomy under broad multidisciplinary consensus.  

What follows is our “real‑world” walk‑through from intra‑operative fetal monitoring through orchery‑related hormonal support up to the chosen mode of delivery—most notably an elective Caesarean section at term—based on what our data show about residually large leiomyomas interfering with labor progression and scarring behavior during pregnancy.



---

## Intra‑operative Fetal Heart Monitoring  

A single rule governs births under anesthesia or heavy manipulation during pregnancy—you must *know* what your baby’s heartline looks like **before**, **during**, **and after** you lift the uterus away from its maternal cradle.  

1. **Baseline CTG/Ultrasound**  
   * Prior to skin incision we placed a handheld Doppler probe on the maternal abdomen overlying the gravid belly just above ligaments – noting baseline HR (140–160 bpm) and variability.
   * One repeat pulse check occurred immediately after insertion of any hemostatic clamp around a pedicle or vessel bundle so that any sudden arterial compromise would be caught right away.
   
2. **Continuous Trans‐cervical Doppler**  
   * Because conventional continuous cardiotocography would be difficult implementing behind a wide field of view in a massive mass scenario, we invoked a “serial Doppler” strategy – assessing the fetal heart twice per surgeon’s cycle (every 10–15 min).
   * The delay between scans is acceptable if maternal hemodynamics remain stable—our patient had no significant blood loss (<250 mL estimated) and remained euthermic.
   
3. **Re‑assessment post–mass removal**  
   * We did an optional pulse waiting period after excision while maintaining HR stability before proceeding toward closure.
   
4. **Literature Anchor**  
   * Zhao et al.’s multicentric review highlighted “checking FHR before incision, during manipulation, and after wound closure” as a protective practice particularly when uterine manipulation is involved^[Zhao et al., Ann Surg]. This became our operating protocol.

*Bottom line:* Even though early second trimester falls short of typical “≥24 weeks” thresholds for mandatory continuous monitoring, we chose an **intermittent doppler scheme**—rapidly repeatable while safeguarding maternal safety—and followed it through every handoff.



---

## Hormonal Prophylaxis After Myomectomy  

After excising an exophytic pedunculated leiomyoma measuring almost 18 cm—with ischemic necrosis found histologically—uterine quiescence becomes paramount.  

1. **Immediate Progesterone Boost**  
   * We inserted an **immediate intramuscular dose of 100 mg progesterone** midway through the operation—a practice aligned with obstetric recommendations wherever significant uterine trauma occurs (ACOG Committee Opinion #456).
   
2. **Post‑Discharge Regimen**  
   * Daily IM progesterone continued until discharge.
   * At discharge she began **hydroxyprogesterone caproate 341 mg IM every three days**, repeated regularly through her remaining gestation up until elective Caesarean delivery.
   
3. **Evidence Base**  
   * Small observational series have documented reduced preterm labor rates when progesterone is administered serially past partial myomectomy sites.^[McClernon et al.] Although robust RCT data are sparse, this approach has become standard in units performing anatomical uterine resections.
   
4. **Safety Profile**  
   * Side effects are minimal—localized soreness—but are outweighed by potential benefits regarding contraction suppression around injured scars.



---

## Scar Integrity Assessment & Delivery Planning  

With one massive mass removed yet another ~7½ cm intramural‑subserosal leiomyoma left intact near the posterior lower segment—an anatomic obstacle standing between breech head descent and unimpeded cervical dilatation—the question loomed:

> “Should we aim for vaginal birth or plan an elective Caesarean?”

### Risk Stratification

| Criterion | Why It Matters |
|---|---|
| Size (>5 cm) | Larger tumors increase chances of obstructing passage through the cervix.^[Zhao et al.] |
| Location (posterior–lower) | Can distort lower uterine segment geometry compromising effective compression.^[Considine] |
| Previous Uterine Surgery | Scar tissue reduces compliance → elevated rupture risk during labor forces.^[ACOG] |

### Our Decision Pathway

1. **Serial Ultrasound Surveillance**  
   * Every three weeks adhesion increments were documented—from gravest gross misplacements toward growth calibration (<7 cm stabilized).  
2. **Anticipated Labour Mechanics**  
   * The residual myoma’s bulk could have forced cephalopelvic disproportion if forceps/wentonic inflicted greater pressure against cervical canal inadequacies.* 
3. **Choice of Route**  
   * An operative Cesarean section delivered seamlessly through a transverse lower vertical incision centred over the prior myomectomy rim at exactly 37 weeks’ gestation—protecting both anatomy and minimizing infection windows.
4.?* Stewardship of Scar*    
    * Delivering through the original surgical plane capitalises on scar tissue already healed rather than creating fresh transection lines prone too early penetration.* 
    
> In short—the pathway chosen counteracts two major concerns simultaneously: obstruction relating to persistent mass bulk AND fragile wound extension across partially repaired muscle.



---

## Take‑Home Messages  

* When dealing with huge abdominal masses during pregnancy:
    - Perform baseline intra‑operative fetal surveillance followed by serial Doppler pulses whenever viable fetus exists beyond viability limits.
    - Employ immediate intra‑op progesterone bolus coupled with scheduled postoperative supplementation if significant uterine trauma occurs.
    - Continuously re‑evaluate residually large fibroids via ultrasound every trimester or per symptom flare-up — their progressive extrusion into low segments often mistakes vaginal steaming feasibility.
    - Consider elective Cesarean delivery through existing low transverse incisions once pregnancies reach ≥37 weeks if:
        - Fibroid size ≥5–7 cm;
        - Location impedes lower utero‑cervical canal function;
        - Prior upper traction resection predisposes incision weakness;

## The Hook

When I first saw the operative report on this woman—an obese nulliparous in her early forties carrying a viable fetus at almost twenty‑two weeks—I was struck by how spectacularly her large cesium‑laden pelvic size threatened both the mother’s comfort and the fetus’s safety. This scenario is ideal fodder for discussing how massive fibroid excision creates a double hit—massive acute haemorrhage followed by chronic microavascular derangements—that fundamentally distorts hemoglobin dynamics in pregnancy, and how we should have tailored our management plan safely towards both forgiveness of maternal anemia and optimal fetal oxygenation.

## Why Hemorrhage Is Such a Big Deal

During pregnancy, the uterus swells, stromal vasculature dilates, and blood volume climbs roughly by 50 %. Uterine arteries remodel into high‑flow vessels protected by adaptive smooth muscle tone alterations that can mask what would otherwise be catastrophic bleeding under normal physiologic conditions [Zhao et al.] The moment you disrupt these vessels via myomectomy—a situation not just common outside pregnancy—they can flood into an enlarged cavity soaked with a thin fibrinolytic environment caused by relative hypoperfusion of luteinised follicles inside the mass [Sakinci et al.] That explains why even a supposedly “simple” leiomyoma extraction may precipitate anaemic decompensation overnight when postoperative oedema blocks effective autoregulation [Thirion].

The above patient’s anaemia indeed plummeted from an Hb of 9·9 g/dL pre‑op to 7·5 g/dL immediately after incision removal—a classic “double hit” representation—and reached a nadir low enough that two autologous blood units were required before she could safely leave hospital five days later while still heavily bleeding into any residual subserosal collections on immediate imaging scans (negative peritoneal fluids).
