Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

## Clinical Presentation & Initial Assessment

At approximately **21 + 6 weeks’ gestation** the patient presented to the emergency department with acute, **severe left‑upper‑quadrant abdominal pain** (Visual Analogue Scale > 5) that had been developing over the previous 24–48 hours. The pain’s intensity prompted self‑reported analgesic requests and quick bedside management. Concomitant with the pain was **mild shortness of breath** that was observed on presentation and relieved modestly by positioning adjustments; this respiratory discomfort was interpreted by the team as **mechanical compression of the diaphragm** by an enlarged uterus and additional intraperitoneal masses rather than pulmonary or cardiac pathology, given the intact oxygen saturation and normal cardiac examination.

Immediate vital‑sign assessment showed a **blood pressure of 100/60 mm Hg**, a heart rate of 74 beats per minute, and normal temperature after initial evaluation (though a low‑grade fever subsequently emerged). These values were reassuring against acute shock or sepsis but necessitated ongoing monitoring due to the potential for rapid decompensation with gravid abdominal pathology.

The **abdominal examination** revealed an overwhelmingly enlarged abdomen that appeared larger than expected for gestational age. A firm, taut‑elastic swelling filled the entire left upper quadrant, tender on palpation but without clinical ascites—suggestive of a sizeable cystic or solid intra‑abdominal lesion exceeding the uterine contour. In light of this finding, an immediate multidisciplinary referral was initiated involving obstetrics, gynecologic surgery, radiology, and anesthesiology—a standard practice when a pregnant patient presents with large intra‑abdominal masses causing pain and dyspnea.

A **bimanual vaginal examination** further delineated pelvic pathology: a fixed, rounded, hard mass approximately **9 cm in diameter** was palpable in the lower pelvis and protruded into the posterior fornix. The firmness intimated a fibrotic component rather than a typical soft cystic structure such as an ovarian cyst; nevertheless, intraperitoneal herniation could not be ruled out without imaging. The finding underscored the urgency for early imaging while maintaining rapid access to obstetric care if fetal compromise were suspected.

Early laboratory workup supported clinicians’ suspicion of an underlying inflammatory process:
- Hemoglobin was severely reduced at **9.9 g/L** (the reference range for pregnant adults is 12–16.6 g/L), indicating **iron‑deficiency anaemia**, common in pregnancy but also potentially exacerbated by chronic blood loss from bulky masses or infection.
- Ferritin was low at **25 µg/L**, confirming depleted iron stores.
- A mild leukocytosis (**WBC 11.3 ×10⁹/L**) with neutrophil predominance suggested an infectious component.
- Urine cultures identified **Klebsiella pneumoniae**, indicating a urinary tract source that could explain both her low‑grade fever and neutrophilia.
These findings are significant because they raise red flags for potential systemic infection or complications such as torsion or rupture of a cystic mass—conditions that warrant urgent evaluation.

Management commenced promptly with intravenous paracetamol (**1 g bolus twice daily**) for analgesia—a first‑line agent in pregnancy known to cross the placenta safely yet effectively reduce visceral pain. Notably, after two doses this therapy lowered her pain severity from a VAS score of **5** down to **1**, demonstrating adequate control while awaiting definitive diagnostic studies. No supplemental oxygen or ventilatory support was required at this stage.

The constellation of findings—large anterior abdominal mass with associated pelvic protrusion, acute severe pain unresponsive to conservative measures, mild dyspnea plausibly caused by mechanical lung restriction, coupled with laboratory evidence of infection—constituted key red flags necessitating fast‑track imaging (ultrasound) and a comprehensive evaluation by obstetrics/gynecology specialists before definitive intervention could be planned.

## Imaging Evaluation & Biomarker Interpretation During Pregnancy  

In pregnant patients who present with an abdominal mass, ultrasound remains the initial investigation because it provides real‐time information without ionising radiation and is well tolerated even in obese subjects. In this case study a high‑resolution transabdominal scan identified two large unilocular cystic lesions measuring approximately 18 × 12 × 7 cm (upper) and 9½ × 8 × 8 cm (lower). Colour Doppler interrogation revealed peripheral vascularisation surrounding both lesions key in differentiating a potentially pedunculated myoma from an ovarian cyst or an endometriotic collection. Because the left-handed uterus occupied most of the farmer’s occupational cavity, acoustic windows were limited yet sufficient for identifying gross anatomic relationships between the uterus, adnexa and abdominal wall—a fact that guided subsequent decision making about surgical versus expectant management.”  

Because sonographic features were equivocal – both entities displayed thick walls (>1 cm), mixed fluid characteristics and a solid component near one mass – a non‑contrast magnetic resonance image was requested once the patient was beyond her first trimester. Current consensus permits **1–1½ T** field strength examinations that use T1‑weighted fat saturation sequences together with T2‑weighted fat saturation images in order to achieve optimal soft tissue contrast while avoiding gadolinium exposure which carries an established risk profile in pregnancy after gestational week 12 unless absolutely imperative for diagnosis.orgally abnormal findings such as hyperintense solid elements within otherwise cystic formations were seen predominantly around the lower lesion which appeared contiguous with a posterior intramural–subserosal myoma previously described by sonography.”  

While tumour markers such as **CA‑125** are universally recognised indicators of ovarian malignancy outside obstetrics, their interpretation requires contextualisation during gestation where physiological hormonal fluctuations provide systematic background noise. In early pregnancy peak mean concentrations exceed **20 U/mL**, recede during midtrimester (median ≈ 14 U/mL), only modestly rise again late into gestation (~21 U/mL). In the presented patient ß‑factor levels were measured daily over several weeks demonstrating an upward trajectory—from **36** up to **41.8 U/mL**—even while other markers such as CA19‑9 or CEA remained within normal limits.“  

**Serial monitoring amplifies diagnostic confidence** by distinguishing between benign physiologic rise‐and‐fall patterns and sustained elevation that may accompany true neoplastic stimulation or irreversible complications such as migration/necrosis seen here with oxidative stress induced cellular apoptosis within a twisted pedicle (“significant rise over time correlated more strongly with erosion/necrosis rather than transient inflammatory loading”). A single elevated value could have triggered premature intervention given proximity thresholds prescribed by many institutions (< 35 U/ml), yet its gradual increase deposited weight upon multi‑modal evidence warranting operative exploration despite significant obstetric risk.“  

The integrative picture assembled by applying ultrasounds tailored against anatomic constraints plus complementary non‑enhanced MRI plus honed biomarker surveillance called into action immediate surgical management at **21 + 6 gestational days** under informed consent—it ensured removal of a hemorrhagic pedunculated leiomyoma while conserving the second cystic mass which could safely regress post‐delivery – preserving maternal health without compromising placental function nor fetal viability.“  

In short, iterative imaging studies coupled with kinetic monitoring of tumoral biomarkers form a triad essential for discriminating benign disease processes from malignant ones—in particular where reassurance cannot solely rely upon single measurements—and lay groundwork upon which multidisciplinary teams can formulate time-sensitive procedural decisions amid delicate maternal‑fetal tradeoffs.”

## Building the Differential Diagnosis – Integrating Imaging & Labs

The patient presented with two massive unilocular abdominal lesions detected by ultrasonography (US) during her 21‑week gestation. The superior lesion measured 18 × 12 × 7 cm while the inferior one was 9.5 × 8 × 8 cm in size. Both masses displayed regular margins and peripheral vascularisation on Doppler US – a typical feature of cystic adnexal lesions that can arise either from ovarian endometrium (endometrioma) or from degenerative uterine myometrium (cystic leiomyoma). On non‑contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), both masses showed thick walls (≈1.4 cm), inhomogeneous fluid content that was dark on T2 fat‑suppressed sequences but had focal hyperintensity consistent with solid debris or haemorrhagic foci. The lower lesion appeared contiguous with a posterior subserosal‑intramural myoma (58 × 71 mm) previously identified at US; this architectural continuity strongly suggested a myomatous origin for the caudal mass even though the post‑natal specimen confirmed it was an intramural leiomyoma with cystic degeneration rather than an ovarian lesion (Fig 1). The upper cyst did not demonstrate obvious attachment to the uterus—the neck was occult—so it was interpreted as an ovarian source until definitive histology was obtained intra‑operatively, when it was found to be a pedunculated uterine leiomyoma arising from the fundus.

Laboratory studies provided further discriminants. Serum CA‑125 was mildly elevated at 41.8 U/mL (normal <35 U/mL) and trending upward from 36 U/mL just two weeks earlier; however, other tumour markers—CA‑19‑9, CA‑15‑3, carcinoembryonic antigen, β2‑microglobulin and lactate dehydrogenase—were all within normal limits. In pregnancy baseline CA‑125 rises are well recognised during early gestation but now characterised by a second‑trimester trough around 14 U/mL; therefore any elevation above this physiologic range warrants suspicion but remains non‑specific because both endometriotic tissue and myomatous degeneration can increase this marker similarly due to inflammatory cytokines released by ectopic endometrium or reactive fibrosis surrounding a degenerated leiomyoma.

The most decisive ancillary finding was the absence of any nodes greater than 10 mm on either US Doppler images or MR axial scans—a reliable indicator that nodal spread is unlikely at this stage. Metastatic disease from ovarian carcinoma would almost invariably present with regional adenopathy before multimodal treatment becomes necessary; thus the normal nodal assessment favored benign processes over malignancy[1]. Notably also none of the masses displayed papillary projections, calcification foci, irregular vascular blushes or rapid growth—all classical red flags for epithelial ovarian cancer—even though their sheer size could have misleadingly suggested malignancy if assessed in isolation.

Integrating these data leads to a focused differential:

| Entity | Imaging signatures | Biochemical correlates | Clinical clues | Exclusion rationale |
|--------|--------------------|-----------------------|----------------|---------------------|
| **Decidualised endometrioma** | Simple unilocular **anechoic** lesion with subtle internal echoes (“chocolate” fluid); thin wall (<3 mm); usually midline/ovarian location | Slight rise in CA‑125 common; other tumour markers normal | Usually asymptomatic unless torsion encourages pain | Absence of nodal uptake limits malignant potential |
| **Cystic degeneration of an intramural/subserosal leiomyoma** | Thick irregular wall (>5 mm); heterogeneous signal – low intensity focal areas on T2 suggestive of fibrosis/haemorrhage ± hyperintense “solid” spots representing degenerated muscle bundles; continuity with uterus on MRI | Mild CA‑125 elevation secondary to inflammatory response around necrotising myometrial tissue | Pedunculated morphology predisposes to torsion → pain & fever | Lack of solid echogenicity inside culm and lack of periuterine infiltration reduce suspicion for sarcoma |

In this patient’s presentation – extreme size exceeding 18 cm for one lesion yet lined uniformly after subjective evaluation – convergent radiologic impressions favored a pedunculated intramyometrial tumour rather than an adnexal origin because it extended continuous into uterine fundus at LR view while remaining clearly separated from ovaries at colour Doppler interrogation[2]. Simultaneously there were no signposts for malignant potential such as calcification nodules or papillary excrescent projections noted during scalpel inspection preoperatively (Fig 3). Importantly sequencing all available diagnostic modalities fortified each other's weaknesses—US lacked depth resolution for uterine attachment which MRI clarified; MRI’s non-cancer typical features were supported by normal nodal imaging and unremarkable tumour marker profile.

Thus, by merging sonographic morphometry (unilocular appearance yet peripheral pulsatility), MR grey-scale fidelity (wall thickness plus signal heterogeneity) and biochemical surveillance (isolated modest rise in CA‑125 against broader stability in other tumour antigens), we distinguished between decidualised endometriomas versus developing degenerated intramural leiomyomas—and excluded more ominous pathologies such as metastatic cancers or malignancies arising within the ovary.[3]

*Absence of adenopathy rules out metastatic disease.*

## Operative Management Principles During Pregnancy

When an abdominal or pelvic mass presents in pregnancy with acute pain, dyspnea, risk of torsion or rupture, or an uncertainty regarding malignancy, the benefits of prompt operative intervention often outweigh the obstetric risks. In such cases, multidisciplinary planning is essential to preserve both maternal and fetal wellbeing. The primary indications for surgery during gestation include (1) life‑threatening complications such as hemorrhage or sepsis; (2) severe maternal symptoms that cannot be managed conservatively; (3) diagnostic uncertainty where imaging is inconclusive and could delay definitive therapy; and (4) a likelihood that the lesion will grow sufficiently large to impede uterine expansion or fetal descent during delivery.

Optimal intra‑operative technique revolves around minimal uterine manipulation and meticulous fetal safeguarding. A low midline or unilateral low transverse incision is favored when feasible because it allows rapid access while limiting uterine incisions that might compromise its future elasticity. In our case, a longitudinal xipho‑pubic incision was chosen at 21 w + 6 d because it provided wide exposure for two large masses without risking uterine trauma. Continuous fetal heart rate monitoring—usually via transabdominal Doppler—is mandatory from pre‑incision through closure to ensure rapid detection of distress and provision of immediate intervention if warranted. Maternal positioning (e.g., semi‑orthostatic) reduces diaphragmatic compression from the gravid uterus and helps mitigate respiratory compromise from massive abdominal lesions. Anesthesia should be lightweight with agents that have minimal placental transfer while preserving uterine tone unless hysteroscopic or laparoscopic approaches require general anesthesia.

Peri‑operative measures focus on infection control, hemostasis, and prevention of preterm labor following uterine manipulation. A single dose of broad‑spectrum antibiotic prophylaxis (e.g., cefazolin) is given at skin incision followed by tailored therapy based on intra‑operative cultures—as was done with post‑op clarithromycin after detecting Klebsiella pneumoniae urinary infection in this patient—to minimize postoperative sepsis risks associated with uterine surgery during pregnancy. For patients who undergo myomectomy or other procedures that disrupt the uterine wall’s integrity—particularly when there is concern about cervical incompetence—prophylactic intramuscular progesterone is often administered immediately post‑operatively and continued until delivery. Several studies have demonstrated that IM progesterone reduces spontaneous preterm birth rates in high‑risk groups by enhancing cervical collagen synthesis and maintaining myometrial quiescence. In this report, 100 mg IM progesterone was started intra‐operatively and then continued daily until discharge before switching to longer‑acting hydroxyprogesterone caproate every three days up until elective cesarean delivery at term. Together with stringent fetal surveillance during surgery, these measures help ensure favorable maternal outcomes while preserving term delivery despite substantial abdominal pathology.
