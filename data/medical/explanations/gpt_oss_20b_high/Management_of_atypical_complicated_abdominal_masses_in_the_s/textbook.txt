Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

## 1. Epidemiology and Pathophysiology of Abdominal Masses in Pregnancy

Pregnancy is a state that predisposes to both physiologic uterine expansion and the emergence or detection of structural lesions such as leiomyomas or endometriotic cysts. In clinical practice, the observed prevalence of uterine fibers during gestation ranges from about 0.3 % to more than 10 % in specialized obstetric series, depending largely on population characteristics and imaging modality used for screening (see Figure 13 in the text). The heterogeneity reflects variations in study design, racial background, maternal age distribution, and the shift from ultrasound alone to serial scans combined with MRI where available.

A common diagnostic challenge is distinguishing normal gestational enlargement from lesion‑associated mass formation. A competent uterus increases roughly three‑fold in size by term owing to placental demand and hormonal progesterone–mediated smooth‑muscle hypertrophy. This segmental growth is uniform, smooth and symmetric on ultrasound or MRI and typically does not involve focal wall thickening or discrete fluid collections. In contrast, fibroids (leiomyomas) are benign smooth‑muscle tumors arising from the myometrium that may persist throughout pregnancy or even expand under the influence of progesterone receptors expressed on smooth‑muscle cells. They frequently present as rounded densities possibly accompanied by cystic degeneration when necrosis occurs centrally or when peripheral hemorrhage fills intralesional cavities. Another entity that can resemble an intra‑abdominal cyst is a decidualised ovarian endometrioma – an ectopic collection of old blood rendered opaque by progesterone‑stimulated stromal changes – which likewise demonstrates homogenous low‑to‑high signal on T2‑weighted imaging after a gestational thickening ring forms externally around the ovary’s capsule (see Figure 1).

Etiologically, both decidualised endometriomas and cystic leiomyomas arise under different mechanisms yet share overlapping morphological clues on routine imaging modalities used early in pregnancy – color Doppler ultrasonography detects sparse peripheral vascularity while non‑contrast MRI delineates fluid content versus solid components without gadolinium exposure because fetal safety dictates a conservative imaging strategy after the first trimester (multi‑vessel blood flow studies are recommended only when clinically indicated). In the present patient’s situation—an overweight nulliparous woman aged nearly 40—a higher load of estrogenic stimuli coupled with chronic inflammatory states predisposes her more strongly toward gaining several sizable lesions rather than merely expanding her uterus physiologically.

Risk stratification highlights significant contributors such as advanced maternal age (>35 y), elevated body mass index (BMI > 30 kg/m²), nulliparity or early pregnancies—with aging related upregulation of transforming growth factor β1 stimulating fibroid proliferation—and reproductive history influencing estrogen availability over many years before conception (although parity has been reported to confer a protective effect against subsequent fibroid development after RCTs have controlled for other variables). Obesity amplifies insulin resistance pathways that concurrently enhance ovarian hormone production; both effects sustain an environment conducive to tumor initiation or expansion inside myometrial tissue or ectopic sites such as peritoneal implants.

Large intra‑abdominal masses impose mechanical compression beyond their own vascular demands—a fact exemplified by dyspnoea stemming from diaphragmatic displacement seen at admission in our patient’s left upper quadrant lesion extending into the fundus region—causing restrictive lung disease independent of pulmonary pathology. These lesions’ size was directly responsible for inhibiting fetal head descent at term; therefore an elective caesarean birth through scopic scar was chosen after deliberate deliberation among obstetricians, surgeons, and anesthetists so that perforative injury was minimized while avoiding intra‑uterine pressure loss which could precipitate preterm labour or spontaneous rupture later on (Suggested delivery approach debated elsewhere).

Recognizing these epidemiological patterns empowers clinicians to tailor evaluation strategies: large unilocular cysts should prompt differential consideration between endometriotic versus myomatous origins guided by lesion contour, wall thickness along with correlating CA‑125 dynamics—but always within clinical context since pregnancy itself alters baseline markers drastically—and practical imaging limits available during gestation.

## 2 Clinical Presentation and Initial Evaluation

The patient’s encounter began in the emergency department at 21 weeks gestation (GA21 w + 6 d), determined by routine ultrasonography dating the fetal CRL and biparietal diameter according to established obstetric guidelines. Dedicated prenatal screening had already revealed a low‑risk karyotype via non‑invasive testing and an unremarkable fetal morphology scan, thereby excluding major chromosomal anomalies or congenital structural defects that could account for her symptoms.  

She complained of sharp abdominal pain rated five points on a visual analogue scale (VAS), localized primarily in the left upper quadrant (LUQ) and secondarily in the pelvis. The polarized distribution suggested an organ‐level source traversing the abdominal cavity rather than diffuse visceral irritation typical of mastoid inflammation or small‑bowel upset. Pain exacerbated by lying flat was initially managed with intravenous paracetamol (1 g q12h), which produced a noticeable drop from VAS 5 to VAS 1—a functional response indicating an underlying mechanical component amenable to pharmacologic interruption of nociceptive pathways as well as an improvement in diaphragmatic excursion indirectly mediated by reduced compressive force once opioid analgesia attenuated sympathetic tone around the thoracoabdominal stretch receptors.



Concomitantly, she reported “dyspnoea” linked temporally with her position change from sitting to supine. Objective assessment revealed preserved oxygen saturation (median 97 %) alongside normotensive heart rate (74 bpm) and blood pressure (100/60 mm Hg). This profile distinguished mechanical dyspnoea caused by diaphragmatic restriction from primary pulmonary insults such as aspiration pneumonia or pulmonary embolism—conditions that would typically elevate heart rate, lower SpO₂, or trigger tachypnoea. The dyspnoea was explained physiologically by a large abdominal effusion imposing downward traction on the diaphragm, thereby reducing thoracic compliance without impacting gas exchange.



A bimanual vaginal examination performed immediately after stabilization yielded further clues: a fixed, hard lesion measuring roughly 9 cm extended into the posterior fornix and restricted range of motion across the cervical canal. Such firmness rules out fluid‑filled cystic lesions that are usually pliable upon palpation; instead it points towards fibrous or muscular tissue consistent with either ovary‑based fibroids or pedunculated leiomyomas attached near the posterior wall of the uterus. The fixed nature indicates an anchoring point inside or outside the myometrium rather than a free-floating adnexal cyst.



The patient’s response to positional change exposed additional insights. Adopting a semi‑orthostatic posture relieved diaphragmatic load—as evidenced by subjective reduction in dyspnoea—and facilitated more effective inspiration—a classic manoeuvre benedictally employed when evaluating large intra‑abdominal masses during pregnancy. Although her oxygenation remained stable throughout these tests, her discomfort eased considerably when abdominal contents were displaced cranially away from prediaphragmatic space.



Taken together—the gestational age providing context for trimester‑specific variations in CA‑125 kinetics; ambiguous yet cramping LUQ pain leaning toward an organ impingement scenario; nascent yet reassuring vitals discounting systemic decompensation; presence of a hard fixed pelvic nodule confirming a tangible intrapelvic abnormality; plus paradoxical improvement with semi‑terrestial positioning—all guided clinicians toward reaching a provisional diagnosis of one or more sizeable intra‑abdominal cystic masses exerting extrinsic pressure on respiratory mechanics while being anchored within either gynecologic or orthotopic structures.



Consequently, imminent imaging was deemed essential before any definitive surgical decision could be made. Ultrasound provided rapid bedside confirmation of two expansive unilocular cysts confirming suspicion raised by physical examination while counselling obstetric gestational safety strategies lay down borderlines where surgery might selectively intervene under multidisciplinary scrutiny—all rooted firmly in this initial clinical premise.[56]
