# Delivery Planning With Huge Abdominal Masses

## Introduction

Picture this: a 21‑week pregnant woman arrives jog‑haunted by pain in both upper left quadrant and pelvis, her breath becoming shallow despite an otherwise normal pulse and blood pressure. Two enormous unilocular cystic structures are stamped onto her abdomen on ultrasound—one gargantuan ventral mass leaping toward the uterus’ fundus, another lurking deep in the pouch of Douglas adjacent to a posterior fibroid. The first thing you might think is “obviously an ovarian problem,” but no – it turns out she’s battling two gigantic fibroids—one pedunculated and laterally twisted, the other a cystic‑degenerating intramural leiomyoma wrapped around the posterior uterine wall—both threatening her health *and* her baby’s future obstetric course.

Why is this all so exciting? Because it forces us into a very narrow window where imaging, biomarkers, multidisciplinary teamwork, timely surgery—and smart delivery planning—converge into one life‑saving tightrope walk that’s rarely glossed over in everyday obstetric practice discussions.

Everything we’ll talk about will come straight from that journey.



## What Made This Case Shocking?

1. **Massive Size + Symptomatology:** An 18 × 12 × 7→cm pedunculated fibroid curling up toward the fundus is huge enough to compress both diaphragmatic movement (thus dyspnoea) *and* compromise normal fetal growth space.
2. **Diagnostic Ambiguity:** Initially everything looked like endometriomas on planar sonography up until MRI disaggregated them into fibroids intermingled with cortical degeneration—a classic imaging nightmare during pregnancy.
3. **Dual Surgical Decision‐Making:** The obvious life‑saving move was removing only the threat harbinger—the twisted hooded peduncle—but deciding *what* remains unnoticed leafed deeper issues on any eventual vaginal route or operative scar integrity through which labor would travel later.



## The Decision Matrix

### Imaging Branch

> “You might think only US is enough… not quite.”  
>   
> Ultrasound remains your first line—but add *colour Doppler* and you can see peripheral flow pattern typical for fibroid rather than hemorrhagic cysts or malignant adnexal lesions.
///   
> Then step in non‑contrast MRI (≤1½ T) after the first trimester – it’s considered safe once early organogenesis ends—and you get wall thickness & “solid component” localization key data.



### Marker Bracket

> CA‑125’ll climb during pregnancy [%] — you cannot rely on an absolute value alone!  
> Serial kinetics are more telling than a single up‐tail spike.



### Multidisciplinary Meeting

> This team was not stalled by “we keep her expectant” homework because fetal distress was absent on intraoperative monitoring but *clinical*:  

* Imaging ➜ indicated torsion/ischemia sign  
* Bacteriologic results suggesting infection  
* Rising CA‑125 trend  
* Dyspnea threatening maternal oxygen saturation  

The conclusion came quickly – intervene surgically *before* labour complications.
