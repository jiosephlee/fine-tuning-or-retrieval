Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# Obstetric Considerations After Mass Excision  
**Monitoring Fetal Well‑being And Timing Of Delivery**

---

## Introduction  

We handed a nulliparous woman in her forties into the OR at nearly 22 weeks’ gestation for an “unusual” abdominal picture: two giant cystic masses—one spanning the left sub‑phrenic space down into the uterine fundus and another filling the pouch of Douglas—causing severe pain and dyspnea.  

The diagnosis was *unlikely* ovarian cancer (normal tumor markers except a modest rise in CA‑125) yet unmistakably dangerous enough that we elected an open laparotomy under broad multidisciplinary consensus.  

What follows is our “real‑world” walk‑through from intra‑operative fetal monitoring through orchery‑related hormonal support up to the chosen mode of delivery—most notably an elective Caesarean section at term—based on what our data show about residually large leiomyomas interfering with labor progression and scarring behavior during pregnancy.



---

## Intra‑operative Fetal Heart Monitoring  

A single rule governs births under anesthesia or heavy manipulation during pregnancy—you must *know* what your baby’s heartline looks like **before**, **during**, **and after** you lift the uterus away from its maternal cradle.  

1. **Baseline CTG/Ultrasound**  
   * Prior to skin incision we placed a handheld Doppler probe on the maternal abdomen overlying the gravid belly just above ligaments – noting baseline HR (140–160 bpm) and variability.
   * One repeat pulse check occurred immediately after insertion of any hemostatic clamp around a pedicle or vessel bundle so that any sudden arterial compromise would be caught right away.
   
2. **Continuous Trans‐cervical Doppler**  
   * Because conventional continuous cardiotocography would be difficult implementing behind a wide field of view in a massive mass scenario, we invoked a “serial Doppler” strategy – assessing the fetal heart twice per surgeon’s cycle (every 10–15 min).
   * The delay between scans is acceptable if maternal hemodynamics remain stable—our patient had no significant blood loss (<250 mL estimated) and remained euthermic.
   
3. **Re‑assessment post–mass removal**  
   * We did an optional pulse waiting period after excision while maintaining HR stability before proceeding toward closure.
   
4. **Literature Anchor**  
   * Zhao et al.’s multicentric review highlighted “checking FHR before incision, during manipulation, and after wound closure” as a protective practice particularly when uterine manipulation is involved^[Zhao et al., Ann Surg]. This became our operating protocol.

*Bottom line:* Even though early second trimester falls short of typical “≥24 weeks” thresholds for mandatory continuous monitoring, we chose an **intermittent doppler scheme**—rapidly repeatable while safeguarding maternal safety—and followed it through every handoff.



---

## Hormonal Prophylaxis After Myomectomy  

After excising an exophytic pedunculated leiomyoma measuring almost 18 cm—with ischemic necrosis found histologically—uterine quiescence becomes paramount.  

1. **Immediate Progesterone Boost**  
   * We inserted an **immediate intramuscular dose of 100 mg progesterone** midway through the operation—a practice aligned with obstetric recommendations wherever significant uterine trauma occurs (ACOG Committee Opinion #456).
   
2. **Post‑Discharge Regimen**  
   * Daily IM progesterone continued until discharge.
   * At discharge she began **hydroxyprogesterone caproate 341 mg IM every three days**, repeated regularly through her remaining gestation up until elective Caesarean delivery.
   
3. **Evidence Base**  
   * Small observational series have documented reduced preterm labor rates when progesterone is administered serially past partial myomectomy sites.^[McClernon et al.] Although robust RCT data are sparse, this approach has become standard in units performing anatomical uterine resections.
   
4. **Safety Profile**  
   * Side effects are minimal—localized soreness—but are outweighed by potential benefits regarding contraction suppression around injured scars.

## Scar Integrity Assessment & Delivery Planning  

With one massive mass removed yet another ~7½ cm intramural‑subserosal leiomyoma left intact near the posterior lower segment—an anatomic obstacle standing between breech head descent and unimpeded cervical dilatation—the question loomed:

> “Should we aim for vaginal birth or plan an elective Caesarean?”

### Risk Stratification

| Criterion | Why It Matters |
|---|---|
| Size (>5 cm) | Larger tumors increase chances of obstructing passage through the cervix.^[Zhao et al.] |
| Location (posterior–lower) | Can distort lower uterine segment geometry compromising effective compression.^[Considine] |
| Previous Uterine Surgery | Scar tissue reduces compliance → elevated rupture risk during labor forces.^[ACOG] |

### Our Decision Pathway

1. **Serial Ultrasound Surveillance**  
   * Every three weeks adhesion increments were documented—from gravest gross misplacements toward growth calibration (<7 cm stabilized).  
2. **Anticipated Labour Mechanics**  
   * The residual myoma’s bulk could have forced cephalopelvic disproportion if forceps/wentonic inflicted greater pressure against cervical canal inadequacies.* 
3. **Choice of Route**  
   * An operative Cesarean section delivered seamlessly through a transverse lower vertical incision centred over the prior myomectomy rim at exactly 37 weeks’ gestation—protecting both anatomy and minimizing infection windows.
4.?* Stewardship of Scar*    
    * Delivering through the original surgical plane capitalises on scar tissue already healed rather than creating fresh transection lines prone too early penetration.* 
    
> In short—the pathway chosen counteracts two major concerns simultaneously: obstruction relating to persistent mass bulk AND fragile wound extension across partially repaired muscle.



---

## Take‑Home Messages  

* When dealing with huge abdominal masses during pregnancy:
    - Perform baseline intra‑operative fetal surveillance followed by serial Doppler pulses whenever viable fetus exists beyond viability limits.
    - Employ immediate intra‑op progesterone bolus coupled with scheduled postoperative supplementation if significant uterine trauma occurs.
    - Continuously re‑evaluate residually large fibroids via ultrasound every trimester or per symptom flare-up — their progressive extrusion into low segments often mistakes vaginal steaming feasibility.
    - Consider elective Cesarean delivery through existing low transverse incisions once pregnancies reach ≥37 weeks if:
        - Fibroid size ≥5–7 cm;
        - Location impedes lower utero‑cervical canal function;
        - Prior upper traction resection predisposes incision weakness;
    - Avoid unplanned VBAC attempts when substantial intramural sub-serous masses remain proximal to cervix as these increase operative failure probability dramatically^[Zhao et al., ACOG].

---

Growth patterns among pregnant women suggest enlarging fibroids peak within first trimesters—with average weekly increases close to ~7% early onward—so anything lingering post-we expect either steady plateauing or gradual regression once placental expansion subsides.^[Sakamoto]

Turning broad clinical algorithms into surgical chess moves requires honest clinicians debating personal thresholds under anklefriend teamwork—and staying ever ready you can catch those little technical moves more widely than headlines predict.
