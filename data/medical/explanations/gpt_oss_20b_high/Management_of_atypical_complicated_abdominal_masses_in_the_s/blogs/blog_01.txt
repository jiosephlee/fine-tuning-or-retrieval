# Ultrasound First — Non‑Contrast MRI Second  

## What’s so striking about this one?  

In early–mid gestation you’re rarely fretting over “one more giggle’s real analysis”—you’re usually counting cytokines from white blood cells or wondering whether your patient’s fetal heart rate looks normal on Doppler magnification—but here we have a 40‑year‑old primigravida with acute left upper quadrant pain felt like a gnawing grip around her uterus that suddenly turns into discoid dyspnea… All under the shadow of two **giant unilocular cystic lesions** discovered only by an early obstetric ultrasound at almost exactly 22 weeks’ gestation!  

The disturbing twist was seeing those lesions look suspiciously like “decidualised endometriomas.” Yet they turned out not to be ovarian at all – they were ***cystic leiomyomas** arising from the uterus** that had inflated so much they obliterated any discernible stalk or feeding vessel.* The prime takeaway—my colleagues—was how easily enormous intra‑uterine masses can masquerade as ovarian pathology on uncomplicated bedside sonography, even in experienced hands.’'  

## Timing is everything: image your way toward certainty  

Bedside transabdominal ultrasound is fast, safe, free and the universal first line in pregnancy—it still outperforms any other modality regarding accessibility and radiation safety profile *in utero*. However, its one Achilles heel is its limited ability to surgically locate an ominous mass when that mass stretches beyond nearly every fascial limit you can easily map on paper.*  

In this patient the upper lesion saturated most of her left subphrenic space and stacked against the uterine fundus like a gutter across her abdomen while the lower lesion sat deep within her pelvis touching her posterior fornix. With **no discernible pedicle** visible and thickening seen nowhere obvious on colour Doppler tracing beyond exhibiting “peripheral vascularisation”, simply trusting those findings kept us on slow ground waiting for confirmatory evidence.*  

When faced with an >10 cm lesion where you cannot confidently assign it as ovarian or uterine by sonography alone–or when morphological clues (thick irregular walls coupled with focal hyperintense areas on T1/T2 scales) raise suspicion for *cystic degeneration* rather than simple “fluid”—the *natural next step* is non‑contrast T2‑fat saturated MR scanning.* This approach balances safety (no ionising radiation/star-tracked contrast agents) with improved diagnostic fidelity -- it pinpoints anatomical relationships (“is this lesion hooking onto extrauterine structures?”) while providing tissue characterisation that cannot be teased apart by grayscale alone.*

## Paired markers as flags rather than guarantees  

Second‑trimester CA‑125 climbing from 36 U/mL two weeks earlier up to 41 U/mL could have spurred red flag anxiety toward malignancy—but physiologic patterns meet elevated baseline levels during late second trimester (~14 U/mL median) while actual diagnostic thresholds begin >35 U/mL especially over age fifty.^[the evidenced repeats]. In our report no tumor marker above norm aside from CA12 sick ulcer came down regularly so serial trends seemed more relevant than isolated spikes.* 

Realising CA‑125 could confuse inflammatory states—a notable contributor during pregnancy—is essential before recurrent reliance triggers unnecessary imaging mania or surgical determination.*
