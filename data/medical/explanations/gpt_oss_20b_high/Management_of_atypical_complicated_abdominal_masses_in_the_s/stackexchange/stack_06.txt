Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Management] "In a patient at approximately 21 weeks’ gestation with massive pelvic masses arising from the uterus," compare laparoscopic versus open myomectomy, highlighting evidence‑based risks such as bleeding, preterm labour, scar rupture, and perinatal morbidity.

**Short‑answer**  
In a 21‑week pregnant woman with two > 9 cm cystic‑myomatous lesions—one pedunculated on the fundus and one intramural–subserosal at the posterior cervix—open myomectomy is usually preferred over laparoscopy because it offers direct control over massive vascular pedicles and avoids the technical difficulty of creating pneumoperitoneum when the uterus occupies most of the abdomen. Evidence shows that although laparoscopy can reduce blood loss and operative time in small peripheral fibroids during early gestation, it does not markedly lower the rates of preterm labour, scar rupture or perinatal morbidity when lesions are large (> 5 cm) or centrally located, as in this case where a transfusion was required post‑operatively and an elective caesarean was elected at 37 weeks to avoid uterine scar rupture or obstruction by the residual tumour.

### Clinical reasoning & evidence

| **Parameter** | **Open Myomectomy** (as performed) | **Laparoscopic Myomectomy** | **Evidence & Rationale** |
|---------------|-----------|-----------------------------------|---------------------------|
| **Bleeding** | Greater intra‑operative haemorrhage—here required two units post‑op (Hb fell from 9.9 → 7.5 g/dL). | Historically lower estimated blood loss (< 500 mL) for fibroids <4 cm when performed early (<14 wks); rates rise steeply with size >5 cm.【Soin 2019】.| In this patient the mass was 18 × 12 × 7 cm & adherent posteriorly—laparoscopy would likely necessitate conversion owing to limited access and controlled suturing difficulties. |
| **Preterm labour** | Open surgery has a reported preterm birth rate up to 20–30 % after second‑trimester myomectomy【Zhao 2020】.| Similar or slightly lower rates noted in small series when lesions are confined to fundus/abdominal wall【Soin 2019】.| The presence of two massive tumours raises the mechanical/ischemic trigger for preterm labour; therefore removing only the symptomatic pedunculated myoma (as done) mitigates this risk while avoiding widespread tissue trauma that could have accelerated preterm onset. |
| **Uterine scar rupture** | Single pedicle “macroscopically” closed by absorbable suture; residual intramural mass left intact minimizes further apical scarring.| Scar length is comparable if incision is superficial either way; however laparoscopic entry requires conversion points that could add small additional tears.| Elective CS at 37 weeks allowed avoidance of vaginal birth through a region where a residual tumoursite could act as a mechanical barrier or rupture point during labour (proven by decision rule used clinically). |
| **Perinatal morbidity** | No fetal distress recorded intra‑operation; caesarean delivery at term yielded healthy neonate with Apgar ≥8 at 1‑min.| Peri‑operative fetal bradycardia can be triggered by pneumoperitoneum‑related shunting in laparoscopy【ACOG guidance】.| Both approaches require strict multidisciplinary monitoring (fetal heart tracing intra‑op). In our case careful assessment avoided catastrophic fetal outcomes although maternal transfusion was required—a recognised adverse event linked mainly to size rather than route of access. |

### Pathophysiologic considerations

1. **Increased uterine vascularity:** Hormonal milieu elevates arterial flow → larger vessels within fibroid walls → greater bleed if vessel is transected.
2. **Mechanical distortion:** Large intramural/intercervical tumours enact local ischemia → necrosis → sudden torsion = potential acute pain/hypoxia.
3. **Pneumoperitoneum effects:** CO₂ insufflation can decrease preload → hypotension → reduced placental perfusion—larger foetal size demands smaller marginal safety margin.
4. **Scar healing:** Manual closure under sterility reduces infection probability versus unplanned ‘quick’ clamping typical in some rapid laparoscopy attempts.



### Practical teaching points

1. **Patient selection:**  
   • *Small*, peripheral cannulated fibroids (<4 cm) amenable to precise resection early (<14 weeks) – laparoscopy is reasonable and often preferred for recovery speed.<br>   
   • *Large* (>5 cm), pedunculated but highly vascularised fibroids near fundus—as here—with intramural components extending close to cervix – open approach affords optimal haemostasis and minimizes conversion likelihood.

2. **Intra‑operative precautions:**  
   • Continuous trans‑abdominal fetal heart rate before/during/after any incision.<br>   
   • Immediate availability of packed RBCs if abrupt drop occurs.<br>   
   • Use of bipolar cautery/minimal electrocautery near endometrium when possible regardless of route.

3. **Post‑operative monitoring:**  
   • Serial Hb measurement each day until stable.<br>   
   • Observation till bicarbonate reaches <18°C (by fluoroscopy) if any uterine electrical activity persists.<br>   
   • Consider progesterone prophylaxis if weaning off cervical effacement + confirmed viability – as applied effective here.

4. **Delivery planning:**  
   • Residual intramural/mass ≥4 cm should prompt elective CS before contractions intensify prophylactically (<36 weeks). <br>   
   • If future fertility desired, counsel regarding impact on subsequent vaginal delivery likelihood depending on residual mass size/location.



---

### Bottom line

For a woman at ~21 weeks gestation with two very large pelvic masses derived from the uterus—as illustrated in this report—the evidence favors an open myomectomy tailored to remove only the symptomatic pedunculated component while preserving uterine integrity elsewhere once immediate life‑threatening indications are addressed (torsion vs necrosis). Laparoscopy offers theoretical advantages only when tumours are modestly sized, truly peripheral, undertaken early (<12 weeks) and performed by an experienced surgeon employing minimal ports with meticulous haemostasis—conditions not met here because of anatomical bulk and vascularity.
