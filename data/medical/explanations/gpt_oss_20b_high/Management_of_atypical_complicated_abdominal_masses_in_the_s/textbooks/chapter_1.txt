Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation & Initial Assessment

At approximately **21 + 6 weeks’ gestation** the patient presented to the emergency department with acute, **severe left‑upper‑quadrant abdominal pain** (Visual Analogue Scale > 5) that had been developing over the previous 24–48 hours. The pain’s intensity prompted self‑reported analgesic requests and quick bedside management. Concomitant with the pain was **mild shortness of breath** that was observed on presentation and relieved modestly by positioning adjustments; this respiratory discomfort was interpreted by the team as **mechanical compression of the diaphragm** by an enlarged uterus and additional intraperitoneal masses rather than pulmonary or cardiac pathology, given the intact oxygen saturation and normal cardiac examination.

Immediate vital‑sign assessment showed a **blood pressure of 100/60 mm Hg**, a heart rate of 74 beats per minute, and normal temperature after initial evaluation (though a low‑grade fever subsequently emerged). These values were reassuring against acute shock or sepsis but necessitated ongoing monitoring due to the potential for rapid decompensation with gravid abdominal pathology.

The **abdominal examination** revealed an overwhelmingly enlarged abdomen that appeared larger than expected for gestational age. A firm, taut‑elastic swelling filled the entire left upper quadrant, tender on palpation but without clinical ascites—suggestive of a sizeable cystic or solid intra‑abdominal lesion exceeding the uterine contour. In light of this finding, an immediate multidisciplinary referral was initiated involving obstetrics, gynecologic surgery, radiology, and anesthesiology—a standard practice when a pregnant patient presents with large intra‑abdominal masses causing pain and dyspnea.

A **bimanual vaginal examination** further delineated pelvic pathology: a fixed, rounded, hard mass approximately **9 cm in diameter** was palpable in the lower pelvis and protruded into the posterior fornix. The firmness intimated a fibrotic component rather than a typical soft cystic structure such as an ovarian cyst; nevertheless, intraperitoneal herniation could not be ruled out without imaging. The finding underscored the urgency for early imaging while maintaining rapid access to obstetric care if fetal compromise were suspected.

Early laboratory workup supported clinicians’ suspicion of an underlying inflammatory process:
- Hemoglobin was severely reduced at **9.9 g/L** (the reference range for pregnant adults is 12–16.6 g/L), indicating **iron‑deficiency anaemia**, common in pregnancy but also potentially exacerbated by chronic blood loss from bulky masses or infection.
- Ferritin was low at **25 µg/L**, confirming depleted iron stores.
- A mild leukocytosis (**WBC 11.3 ×10⁹/L**) with neutrophil predominance suggested an infectious component.
- Urine cultures identified **Klebsiella pneumoniae**, indicating a urinary tract source that could explain both her low‑grade fever and neutrophilia.
These findings are significant because they raise red flags for potential systemic infection or complications such as torsion or rupture of a cystic mass—conditions that warrant urgent evaluation.

Management commenced promptly with intravenous paracetamol (**1 g bolus twice daily**) for analgesia—a first‑line agent in pregnancy known to cross the placenta safely yet effectively reduce visceral pain. Notably, after two doses this therapy lowered her pain severity from a VAS score of **5** down to **1**, demonstrating adequate control while awaiting definitive diagnostic studies. No supplemental oxygen or ventilatory support was required at this stage.

The constellation of findings—large anterior abdominal mass with associated pelvic protrusion, acute severe pain unresponsive to conservative measures, mild dyspnea plausibly caused by mechanical lung restriction, coupled with laboratory evidence of infection—constituted key red flags necessitating fast‑track imaging (ultrasound) and a comprehensive evaluation by obstetrics/gynecology specialists before definitive intervention could be planned.
