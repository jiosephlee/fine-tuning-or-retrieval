Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

Chapter 3: ## Building the Differential Diagnosis – Integrating Imaging & Labs

The patient presented with two massive unilocular abdominal lesions detected by ultrasonography (US) during her 21‑week gestation. The superior lesion measured 18 × 12 × 7 cm while the inferior one was 9.5 × 8 × 8 cm in size. Both masses displayed regular margins and peripheral vascularisation on Doppler US – a typical feature of cystic adnexal lesions that can arise either from ovarian endometrium (endometrioma) or from degenerative uterine myometrium (cystic leiomyoma). On non‑contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), both masses showed thick walls (≈1.4 cm), inhomogeneous fluid content that was dark on T2 fat‑suppressed sequences but had focal hyperintensity consistent with solid debris or haemorrhagic foci. The lower lesion appeared contiguous with a posterior subserosal‑intramural myoma (58 × 71 mm) previously identified at US; this architectural continuity strongly suggested a myomatous origin for the caudal mass even though the post‑natal specimen confirmed it was an intramural leiomyoma with cystic degeneration rather than an ovarian lesion (Fig 1). The upper cyst did not demonstrate obvious attachment to the uterus—the neck was occult—so it was interpreted as an ovarian source until definitive histology was obtained intra‑operatively, when it was found to be a pedunculated uterine leiomyoma arising from the fundus.

Laboratory studies provided further discriminants. Serum CA‑125 was mildly elevated at 41.8 U/mL (normal <35 U/mL) and trending upward from 36 U/mL just two weeks earlier; however, other tumour markers—CA‑19‑9, CA‑15‑3, carcinoembryonic antigen, β2‑microglobulin and lactate dehydrogenase—were all within normal limits. In pregnancy baseline CA‑125 rises are well recognised during early gestation but now characterised by a second‑trimester trough around 14 U/mL; therefore any elevation above this physiologic range warrants suspicion but remains non‑specific because both endometriotic tissue and myomatous degeneration can increase this marker similarly due to inflammatory cytokines released by ectopic endometrium or reactive fibrosis surrounding a degenerated leiomyoma.

The most decisive ancillary finding was the absence of any nodes greater than 10 mm on either US Doppler images or MR axial scans—a reliable indicator that nodal spread is unlikely at this stage. Metastatic disease from ovarian carcinoma would almost invariably present with regional adenopathy before multimodal treatment becomes necessary; thus the normal nodal assessment favored benign processes over malignancy[1]. Notably also none of the masses displayed papillary projections, calcification foci, irregular vascular blushes or rapid growth—all classical red flags for epithelial ovarian cancer—even though their sheer size could have misleadingly suggested malignancy if assessed in isolation.

Integrating these data leads to a focused differential:

| Entity | Imaging signatures | Biochemical correlates | Clinical clues | Exclusion rationale |
|--------|--------------------|-----------------------|----------------|---------------------|
| **Decidualised endometrioma** | Simple unilocular **anechoic** lesion with subtle internal echoes (“chocolate” fluid); thin wall (<3 mm); usually midline/ovarian location | Slight rise in CA‑125 common; other tumour markers normal | Usually asymptomatic unless torsion encourages pain | Absence of nodal uptake limits malignant potential |
| **Cystic degeneration of an intramural/subserosal leiomyoma** | Thick irregular wall (>5 mm); heterogeneous signal – low intensity focal areas on T2 suggestive of fibrosis/haemorrhage ± hyperintense “solid” spots representing degenerated muscle bundles; continuity with uterus on MRI | Mild CA‑125 elevation secondary to inflammatory response around necrotising myometrial tissue | Pedunculated morphology predisposes to torsion → pain & fever | Lack of solid echogenicity inside culm and lack of periuterine infiltration reduce suspicion for sarcoma |
| **Ovarian carcinoma** | Solid‐complex adnexal mass often containing papillary projections/growth thickenings; irregular vascular blush ± nodal enlargement | Marked CA‑125 elevation (>35 U/mL), sometimes accompanied by other serous markers (CA‑15‐3, LDH) | Rapid growth, ascites, systemic symptoms | Normal node status + lack of solid inner components |
| **Serous versus mucinous borderline tumours** | Bilateral cysts usually <5 cm each <10 cm aggregate if multicystic >10 cm eccentric border fluctuation + septa >3 mm thicker than others | Elevated CA‑125 only when cysts >10 cm atypical growth<wid> | Often minimal symptoms until complication such as torsion/regression <3 years| Size enormous >18 cm limits borderline possibilities |

In this patient’s presentation – extreme size exceeding 18 cm for one lesion yet lined uniformly after subjective evaluation – convergent radiologic impressions favored a pedunculated intramyometrial tumour rather than an adnexal origin because it extended continuous into uterine fundus at LR view while remaining clearly separated from ovaries at colour Doppler interrogation[2]. Simultaneously there were no signposts for malignant potential such as calcification nodules or papillary excrescent projections noted during scalpel inspection preoperatively (Fig 3). Importantly sequencing all available diagnostic modalities fortified each other's weaknesses—US lacked depth resolution for uterine attachment which MRI clarified; MRI’s non-cancer typical features were supported by normal nodal imaging and unremarkable tumour marker profile.

Thus, by merging sonographic morphometry (unilocular appearance yet peripheral pulsatility), MR grey-scale fidelity (wall thickness plus signal heterogeneity) and biochemical surveillance (isolated modest rise in CA‑125 against broader stability in other tumour antigens), we distinguished between decidualised endometriomas versus developing degenerated intramural leiomyomas—and excluded more ominous pathologies such as metastatic cancers or malignancies arising within the ovary.[3]

*Absence of adenopathy rules out metastatic disease.*
