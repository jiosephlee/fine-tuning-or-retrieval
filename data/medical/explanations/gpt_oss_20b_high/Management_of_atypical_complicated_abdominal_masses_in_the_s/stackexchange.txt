Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Clinical Pearls] What sonographic features are most helpful in distinguishing a decidualised ovarian cyst from a cystic degenerating subserosal leiomyoma during the second trimester of pregnancy?

**Key sonographic clues that help separate a decidualised ovarian cyst from a cystic‑degenerating subserosal leiomyoma in the second trimester**

| Feature | Ovarian Decidualised Cyst | Cystic Degenerating Subserosal Leiomyoma |
|---------|---------------------------|-------------------------------------------|
| **Origin / relationship with the uterus** | Distinct fat‑plane separation from the myometrium (absent “interface”); occupies an adnexal position even when displaced by the enlarged uterus. | Direct interface or subtle “continuum line” between the lesion and the uterus; often dorso‑anterior when it is posterior/subserosal. The MRI in our case showed a faint contour line suggesting continuity, which was confirmed intra‑operatively as part of the uterine wall. |
| **Shape & contour** | Usually round or oval, thin‑walled (< 1 cm) arising from one ovary (“smooth‑angiolent” rim). | Often lobulated or discoid with irregular outer margin. When degenerating it may appear blunted‐edge or “dumb‑bell” if integral part (“pedicle”) is present even if it is hard to appreciate on ultrasound owing to overlying bowel gas or maternal obesity. |
| **Internal echo‑pattern** | Anechoic fluid with possible hyperechoic debris (“ground‑glass”) if haemorrhagic change occurs; surrounded by slightly echogenic rim representing decidual tissue (“white halo”). | Heterogeneous mixture of hypoechogenic zones (necrotic/cystic spaces) interspersed with hyperechoic septa or calcifications (“bridging lines”). The upper mass in our patient displayed homogeneous unilocular fluid on US (suggesting an ovarian origin), whereas the lower mass showed focal hyperintense components typical of retained fibrous matrix when imaged on MRI (T2‑weighting). |
| **Wall thickness** | Thin wall (< 0.5 cm) unless it has become complicated by haemorrhage—then can thicken up to 1–2 cm but usually remains annular rather than bulky. | Thickened wall (> 0.8–1 cm) due to muscular mantle—consistent with thick walls seen on MRI (1.4 cm). Thicker walls are more suggestive when there is also preserved fibrous tissue at the periphery.<br> |
| **Colour Doppler flow pattern** | Peripheral halo of colour signal around the rim (“shell perfusion”) consistent with venous plexus around ovary; no central lead vessel connecting toward fundus.<br> *Illustrated* in our baseline scan where both lesions had peripheral vascularization—only ODH did not reveal a distinct stalk.* | Linear hypervascular tract emanating from fundus/uterine body toward lesion (“pedicle”) often visualized by duplex Doppler pre‑laparotomy despite being obscured by adipose tissue in our patient’s obese build.* |

### Clinical pearls

1. **Define origin first:** A clear fat plane that separates the mass from the uterus strongly favors an adnexal source over a subserosal lesion—this was precisely what we could appreciate more definitively on non‑contrast MRI after initial US readings misled us toward an endometrioma interpretation.
2. **Look for a stalk:** Even when inconspicuous on transabdominal US in massive masses, colour Doppler should be scrutinized for any linear feeding vessel—the absence thereof suggests an isolated ovarian lesion.
3. **Wall characteristics matter:** Thickened cortical margin (> 0.8 cm) is more typical of fibroid degeneration than simple ovarian decidi­uation (which tends to maintain a thin capsule). The thick 1&nbsp;½‑cm wall seen experimentally contributed decisively towards diagnosing a degenerated leiomyoma intra‑operatively.
4. **Beware of size-related distortion:** In second-trimester pregnancy, massive pelvic expansion can mask classic signs—adding an MRI can confirm adjacency ("cleavage") when US fails due teamwork.
5. **Serial CA‑125 levels give context only:** Although mildly elevated CA‑125 can accompany both entities—as noted when our patient's level rose slightly—the imaging findings must drive management because maternal-fetal safety hinges on accurate delineation rather than serum markers alone.

### Bottom line
When confronted with large cystoid pelvic lesions during mid-pregnancy, prioritize:
  
* Assessment of *mass–uterus interface* via serial transabdominal views  
* Identification of any *pedicular blood supply* using colour Doppler  
* Analysis of *wall thickness* and internal echo texture  

These sonographic hallmarks will most reliably distinguish a decidualised ovarian cyst from a degenerated subserosal fibroid upon which management strategies for maternal–fetal safety are anchored—exactly as illustrated in our patient where definitive differentiation impacted surgical decision-making†

### [Pathophysiology] Why does serum CA‑125 physiologically increase during pregnancy, especially in the first and third trimesters, and how does this differ from elevations seen in ovarian malignancy?

**Why does serum CA‑125 rise physiologically in pregnancy?**  
CA‑125 (MUC16) is expressed not only by ovarian surface epithelium but also by mesothelial cells lining the peritoneum, pleura, and endometrial decidua. In early pregnancy the rapidly expanding decidua is richly vascularised and hormonally stimulated by progesterone and estrogen; these factors up‑regulate MUC16 synthesis and promote shedding of antigen‑containing vesicles into the maternal circulation.^1 Meanwhile the developing placenta releases its own MUC16‑containing proteins that spill into maternal blood. Hemodynamic expansion—particularly during the first trimester's rapid uterine enlargement—and later at term when uterine perfusion peaks again cause transient increases in peritoneal fluid volume and capillary permeability, thereby magnifying antigen release.” These overlapping mechanisms generate **two bimodal peaks**—one in the first trimester (median ≈ 23 U/mL) and one close to delivery (median ≈ 21 U/mL)—with lower values (~14 U/mL) during mid‑gestation.^2 Our patient’s serial values showed a modest rise (36 → 41.8 U/mL at 21 weeks), consistent with a physiological uptick rather than an abrupt oncogenic surge.

**How does this differ from elevation seen in ovarian cancer?**  
Ovarian tumours constitutively overexpress MUC16 regardless of hormonal milieu or gestational phase. Their serum concentrations typically exceed the standard oncologic cutoff (>35 U/mL) by far—often >100–200 U/mL—and rise steadily as tumour burden grows.^3 Importantly, these elevations lack the cyclical pattern seen with pregnancy because they derive directly from malignant cells rather than hormonally modulated serosal tissue. Correlation with radiographic features such as solid nodularity or ascites further favours a neoplastic source, whereas plain‐fluid cysts without solid components support a benign process. Serial monitoring illustrates another distinction: post‑therapeutic decline after tumour resection contrasts sharply with regression only after gestational completion for physiological rises.

Thus, while pregnancy can transiently elevate CA‑125 especially during the first and third trimesters through decidualisation and placental secretion, oncogenic elevations are more marked, persistent, non‑lactationally patterned, and directly proportional to tumorous burden—a nuance underscored by our patient’s imaging‐guided surgical approach rather than reliance on a single marker threshold alone.

### [Clinical Pearls] Which imaging modalities are considered safe and provide the most accurate assessment of large abdominal masses in a second‑trimester pregnant patient?

**Imaging strategy for a large abdomin­pelvic mass in the second trimester**

The most reliable and safest approach combines **(1) transabdominal/transvaginal ultrasonography with color‑doppler** and **(2) non‑contrast magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)** performed at ≥ 1.5 T after the first trimester.

- **Ultrasound** is the immediate first‐line study because it is rapid, widely available and free of ionising radiation. Colour‑doppler augments its utility by revealing intralesional vascularity—essential for distinguishing vascular leiomyomas from cystic lesions such as endometriomas or germ‑cell tumours. In the reported case ultrasonography identified two massive unilocular cysts and raised doubts about their origin, prompting further investigation.
  
- **Non‑contrast MRI** delivers superior soft‐tissue contrast and multiplanar capability without any gadolinium exposure (contraindicated during pregnancy). It precisely defines lesion extent relative to the gravid uterus, identifies solid components or haemorrhagic degeneration, and clarifies continuity with uterine myometrium—as confirmed in the case where MRI confirmed a caudal mass adherent to an intramural-subserosal myoma. Current safety data support 1–3 T magnetic fields after 12 weeks gestation.

Other modalities such as CT remain contraindicated owing to fetal ionising radiation unless mandatory for urgent surgical planning (in which case extreme dose optimisation would be required). Thus, **ultrasound plus non‑enhanced MRI** constitute the gold standard pair for evaluating large abdominal masses safely in second‑trimester pregnancy.

### [Management] "When is surgical removal of a uterine fibroid indicated during pregnancy?" Discuss the risk factors and clinical scenarios that justify operation despite potential obstetric risks.

**Clinical Pearls – When is a fibroid removed during pregnancy?**

| **Scenario** | **Why intervene?** | **Key Points from this Case** |
|--------------|--------------------|------------------------------|
| **Complication‐driven pain / dyspnea** | Rapid enlargement or torsion produces ischemia and necrosis → abdominal pain, fever, leukocytosis → imminent rupture or sepsis → threat to both mother and fetus | 21‑week patient had severe epigastric and pelvic pain + dyspnea from a 18 × 12 × 7 cm pedunculated leiomyoma that was twisted and leaking – causing mild fever and a rising CRP |
| **Torsion / impending rupture** | Twisted pedicle cuts arterial perfusion → tissue necrosis → inflammation → potential hemorrhage → preterm delivery | The surgeons noted a “twisted” stalk on intra‑op exploration—prompting immediate excision before irreversible damage |
| **Infection / septic change** | Necrotic tissue becomes secondarily infected → systemic signs → maternal morbidity | Urine culture grew *Klebsiella*, reinforcing the urgency of removing a potentially infected myoma |
| **Suspicion of malignancy** | Progressive growth + solid‑cystic features + CA‑125 rise > 35 U/mL | The upper lesion had focal hyperintense solid components on MRI + a rising CA‑125 (.841 U/mL above 2‑week baseline); although benign pathology was later confirmed, this trend triggered operative exploration |
| **Mass effect limiting fetal growth / future labour** | Large intramural masses can curtail uterine expansion ⇒ small-for‑gestational‑age infant + obstructed labour | The caudal posterior lesion would have impeded fetal head descent; hence an elective cesarean was planned once fetal maturity was reached |

---

### Decision Algorithm In Practice

1. **Early assessment**
   * Detailed US ± non‑contrast MRI (first trimester ≤ 1.5 T); look for stalks, necrotic zones, solid components.
   * Serial tumour markers only useful if trends diverge from physiologic pregnancy peaks (e.g., CA‑125 rising beyond normal second‑trimester medians).

2. **Risk stratification**
   * Size ≥ 5 cm or fibroid diameter > 10 cm × location = ↑ risk for torsion/rupture.
   * Pedunculated subserosal lesions especially prone to twisting.
   * Intramural lesions abutting placenta/uterus fundus limit expansion ↓ fut congenital smallness/obstruction risk.
   * Maternal factors—age > 35 yrs, obesity (BMI>30) increase symptom burden & morbidity risk profiles.

3. **Timing & modality**
   * Avoid elective myomectomy after 20 weeks unless life‑threatening symptom or strong malignancy suspicion.
   * First/early second trimester favor laparoscopic approach for small subserosal/pedunculated lesions (< 5 cm).
   * Open laparotomy recommended for large (> 8 cm), multiple or intramural masses where laparoscopy risks excessive blood loss or inability to clamp vessel safely—as seen in this case’s 18‑cm pedunculated myoma.
   * Continuous intraoperative fetal heart rate monitoring is mandatory if uterus approached between 16–22 weeks [Zhao et al].

4. **Perioperative measures**
   * Antepartum prophylactic high‑dose progesterone post‑op reduces post‑myomectomy preterm birth rates [Sakinci et al].
   * Antimicrobial coverage tailored to culture results reinforces infection control given vulnerable status post‑torsion/necrosis.

---

### Take‑Home Points

* **Symptomatic complications**—especially torsion or infection—are clear indications for surgery even during mid‑pregnancy despite obstetric risks.
* **Malignancy suspicion** through imaging features plus aberrant tumour marker kinetics justifies operative evaluation often earliest possible.
* When surgery is performed, it should be *targeted*—only removing the threatening lesion(s)—and planned with multidisciplinary equipoise between minimizing maternal trauma and preserving gestation.
* Excellent outcomes can be achieved when operative intervention alleviates mechanical compression while allowing pregnancy continuation—as demonstrated by this patient’s uneventful delivery at term via cesarean after residual fibroid regression postnatal period.

### [Management] "In a patient at approximately 21 weeks’ gestation with massive pelvic masses arising from the uterus," compare laparoscopic versus open myomectomy, highlighting evidence‑based risks such as bleeding, preterm labour, scar rupture, and perinatal morbidity.

**Short‑answer**  
In a 21‑week pregnant woman with two > 9 cm cystic‑myomatous lesions—one pedunculated on the fundus and one intramural–subserosal at the posterior cervix—open myomectomy is usually preferred over laparoscopy because it offers direct control over massive vascular pedicles and avoids the technical difficulty of creating pneumoperitoneum when the uterus occupies most of the abdomen. Evidence shows that although laparoscopy can reduce blood loss and operative time in small peripheral fibroids during early gestation, it does not markedly lower the rates of preterm labour, scar rupture or perinatal morbidity when lesions are large (> 5 cm) or centrally located, as in this case where a transfusion was required post‑operatively and an elective caesarean was elected at 37 weeks to avoid uterine scar rupture or obstruction by the residual tumour.

### Clinical reasoning & evidence

| **Parameter** | **Open Myomectomy** (as performed) | **Laparoscopic Myomectomy** | **Evidence & Rationale** |
|---------------|-----------|-----------------------------------|---------------------------|
| **Bleeding** | Greater intra‑operative haemorrhage—here required two units post‑op (Hb fell from 9.9 → 7.5 g/dL). | Historically lower estimated blood loss (< 500 mL) for fibroids <4 cm when performed early (<14 wks); rates rise steeply with size >5 cm.【Soin 2019】.| In this patient the mass was 18 × 12 × 7 cm & adherent posteriorly—laparoscopy would likely necessitate conversion owing to limited access and controlled suturing difficulties. |
| **Preterm labour** | Open surgery has a reported preterm birth rate up to 20–30 % after second‑trimester myomectomy【Zhao 2020】.| Similar or slightly lower rates noted in small series when lesions are confined to fundus/abdominal wall【Soin 2019】.| The presence of two massive tumours raises the mechanical/ischemic trigger for preterm labour; therefore removing only the symptomatic pedunculated myoma (as done) mitigates this risk while avoiding widespread tissue trauma that could have accelerated preterm onset. |
| **Uterine scar rupture** | Single pedicle “macroscopically” closed by absorbable suture; residual intramural mass left intact minimizes further apical scarring.| Scar length is comparable if incision is superficial either way; however laparoscopic entry requires conversion points that could add small additional tears.| Elective CS at 37 weeks allowed avoidance of vaginal birth through a region where a residual tumoursite could act as a mechanical barrier or rupture point during labour (proven by decision rule used clinically). |
| **Perinatal morbidity** | No fetal distress recorded intra‑operation; caesarean delivery at term yielded healthy neonate with Apgar ≥8 at 1‑min.| Peri‑operative fetal bradycardia can be triggered by pneumoperitoneum‑related shunting in laparoscopy【ACOG guidance】.| Both approaches require strict multidisciplinary monitoring (fetal heart tracing intra‑op). ### Pathophysiologic considerations

1. **Increased uterine vascularity:** Hormonal milieu elevates arterial flow → larger vessels within fibroid walls → greater bleed if vessel is transected.
2. **Mechanical distortion:** Large intramural/intercervical tumours enact local ischemia → necrosis → sudden torsion = potential acute pain/hypoxia.
3. **Pneumoperitoneum effects:** CO₂ insufflation can decrease preload → hypotension → reduced placental perfusion—larger foetal size demands smaller marginal safety margin.
4. **Scar healing:** Manual closure under sterility reduces infection probability versus unplanned ‘quick’ clamping typical in some rapid laparoscopy attempts.



### Practical teaching points

1. **Patient selection:**  
   • *Small*, peripheral cannulated fibroids (<4 cm) amenable to precise resection early (<14 weeks) – laparoscopy is reasonable and often preferred for recovery speed.<br>   
   • *Large* (>5 cm), pedunculated but highly vascularised fibroids near fundus—as here—with intramural components extending close to cervix – open approach affords optimal haemostasis and minimizes conversion likelihood.

2. **Intra‑operative precautions:**  
   • Continuous trans‑abdominal fetal heart rate before/during/after any incision.<br>   
   • Immediate availability of packed RBCs if abrupt drop occurs.<br>   
   • Use of bipolar cautery/minimal electrocautery near endometrium when possible regardless of route.

3. **Post‑operative monitoring:**  
   • Serial Hb measurement each day until stable.<br>   
   • Observation till bicarbonate reaches <18°C (by fluoroscopy) if any uterine electrical activity persists.<br>   
   • Consider progesterone prophylaxis if weaning off cervical effacement + confirmed viability – as applied effective here.

4. **Delivery planning:**  
   • Residual intramural/mass ≥4 cm should prompt elective CS before contractions intensify prophylactically (<36 weeks). <br>   
   • If future fertility desired, counsel regarding impact on subsequent vaginal delivery likelihood depending on residual mass size/location.



---

### Bottom line

For a woman at ~21 weeks gestation with two very large pelvic masses derived from the uterus—as illustrated in this report—the evidence favors an open myomectomy tailored to remove only the symptomatic pedunculated component while preserving uterine integrity elsewhere once immediate life‑threatening indications are addressed (torsion vs necrosis). Laparoscopy offers theoretical advantages only when tumours are modestly sized, truly peripheral, undertaken early (<12 weeks) and performed by an experienced surgeon employing minimal ports with meticulous haemostasis—conditions not met here because of anatomical bulk and vascularity.

### [Morning Report] "During laparotomy for myomectomy in the second trimester," what measures are recommended to maintain fetal viability intra‑operatively?

**Clinical Teaching Note – Morning Report**

**Question:** *During laparotomy for myomectomy in the second trimester, what measures are recommended to maintain fetal viability intra‑operatively?*  

**Answer**

1. **Baseline Fetal Assessment Before Incision**  
   * Verify evidence of active cardiac activity by trans‑abdominal Doppler ultrasound (or CTG if available). Record interval heart rate so any later change can be attributed to the procedure itself.

2. **Continuous Fetal Monitoring During Surgery**  
   * Repeat bedside ultrasound every 20–30 min (or continuous CTG in centers that can do so).  
   * Look for bradycardia (<110 beats/min), variation >25 bpm on CTG, or cessation of beats – each is an immediate red flag requiring rapid intervention (e.g., passively warmed return of blood volume).

3. **Maintain Maternal Hemodynamics**  
   * Keep systolic BP ≥90 mmHg (goal 100–110 mmHg) with isotonic crystalloids or colloid preload.  
   * Use vasopressors that spare uterine perfusion—phenylephrine is preferred over norepinephrine/epinephrine if rapid titration is needed.  
   * Monitor central venous pressure or pulse pressure variation when possible; avoid over‑resuscitation that could provoke uterine overdistension or hypervolemia‑induced premature contractions.

4. **Oxygenation & Thermoregulation**  
   * Target SpO₂ >95 % with supplemental O₂ during induction and throughout the case.  
   * Maintain normothermia using warmed IV fluids, active warming blankets, and warming ambient room temperatures (~24 °C). Hypothermia reduces metabolic demand and can precipitate bradycardia in the fetus.

5. **Anesthetic Considerations**  
   * Prefer a balanced anesthetic with minimal opioids and short‑acting muscle relaxants to preserve spontaneous respiration and minimal systemic depressant effect on the fetus.  
   * Avoid agents known to provoke uterine contraction or vasoconstriction unless clinically mandated (e.g., routine oxytocin bolus should be avoided unless vaginal delivery or abortion is imminent).  

6. **Surgical Technique & Duration**  
   * Keep the procedure brief (<3 h when feasible). The reported case achieved a 2 h 25 min operation by careful clamping and suture ligation of the pedunculated leiomyoma while leaving the pelvic mass intact to reduce distention of the abdomen—all factors supporting favorable fetal outcomes.  

7. **Administer Antenatal Prophylaxis When Indicated**  
   * The authors administered intramuscular progesterone intra‑operatively and continued daily until delivery—an established strategy for maintaining a short cervix or preventing early preterm labor when miscarriage risk exists during obstetric procedures in late pregnancy (≥20 weeks).  

8. **Post‑Incision Scan Before Closure**  
   * Re‑check fetal heart rate immediately before abdominal closure—to confirm no iatrogenic compromise occurred during dissection or manipulation of major vessels adjacent to uterus.

9. **Documentation & Immediate Post‑op Care**  
   * Record all baseline/final FHRs, hemodynamic parameters, anesthesia drugs, fluid balance, and any episodes of tachycardia/bradycardia.  
   * In the postoperative period re‑check fetal status at 24 h plus daily until delivery due to potential delayed decompensation following a major abdominal operation.

**Key Point:** In this 21⁺⁶‑week gestation patient a successful laparotomy with myomectomy required meticulous maternal stabilization paired with real‑time fetal monitoring at multiple time points — pre‑incision verification of cardiac activity, intermittent checks throughout surgery, and a post‑closure scan — combined with judicious fluid/vasopressor management and avoidance of procontraction agents—all measures upheld in this case’s outcome of an uncomplicated elective cesarean at 37 weeks for a vaginal birth‐obstruction risk from residual myoma.*

### [Management] "Why was postoperative progesterone supplementation prescribed following the partial myomectomy," particularly using intramuscular hydroxyprogesterone caproate until elective delivery?

The newborn’s mother underwent a surgically‑induced interruption of normal hemostasis and an inflammatory response during her second‑trimester pregnancy—an event our team considered equivalent to a “partial miscarriage” on a biochemical level.  
The corpus luteum still provided endogenous progesterone through week 20‑21, but the operative insult can precipitate

| Possible Mechanism | Why it matters |
|-------------------|----------------|
| **Transient luteal insufficiency** | Surgical manipulation can compromise ovarian blood flow or raise cortisol levels → reduced luteal hormone output |
| **Myometrial irritation** | Injury releases prostaglandins & interleukins → heightened contractility |
| **Placental‑vascular inflammation** | Minimally disruptive repair can transiently up‑regulate cytokines that lower acetylcholine synthesis → rapid cycling contractions |

In such circumstances it is common practice in obstetric medicine to “plug” this hormonal vacuum with exogenous progesterone immediately after operative or obstetric stressors that threaten quiescence. The goals are  

1. **Stabilize uterine tone** – Progesterone drives intracellular calcium sequestration in smooth muscle cells via PKC pathways, keeping the myometrium relaxed until late gestation.  
2. **Attenuate prostaglandin action** – By binding PR-A/B receptors on endometrial cells it suppresses COX‑2 expression and prostaglandin synthesis.  
3. **Limit inflammatory cascading** – Maintains an immunocompetent milieu conducive to placental integrity—critical when the placenta must adapt around a substantial residual fibroid mass that could interfere with fetal descent later in pregnancy.

Because intravenous methods were impractical once she left hospital, we gave her *hydroxyprogesterone caproate* – a long‑acting ester that achieves steady serum concentrations with injections every three days (341 mg IM per package). Hydroxyprogesterone caproate has Level A evidence from RCTs for preventing recurrence of spontaneous preterm birth in women with prior PTBs; its pharmacodynamics make it well‑suited for bridging up until scheduled delivery at 37 weeks—our planned intervention point based on the retained posterior myoma’s potential barrier effect on vaginal delivery.

Thus, postoperative progesterone supplementation served both as general chemical bracing against operative‑induced uterine irritability and as targeted prevention against early preterm labor associated with cervical healing after extensive abdominal surgery in late first–second trimester pregnancy.
