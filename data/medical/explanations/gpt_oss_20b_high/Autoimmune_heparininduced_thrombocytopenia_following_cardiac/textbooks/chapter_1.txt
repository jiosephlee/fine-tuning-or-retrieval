Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

Chapter 1: ## Case Context & Initial Postoperative Course  

A geriatric male in his seventies presented with exertional dyspnea and a previously undetected systolic murmur that was found incidentally during evaluation for atrial fibrillation (AF) on chronic oral apixaban therapy (5 mg twice daily). His last known baseline platelet count before any procedure was 210 × 10⁹ L⁻¹ – well within the normal reference range – providing a benchmark against which subsequent postoperative drops could be compared.  

Despite continuing apixaban, he underwent transradial coronary angiography one week before planned surgery because his cardiology team judged the therapeutic benefit greater than potential bleeding risk while minimizing procedural anticoagulation by administering only 2 000 U of unfractionated heparin (UFH). This low‑dose anticoagulation was sufficient to stabilize the catheter tip but insufficiently high to fully neutralize ongoing oral factor‑Xa inhibition from apixaban – an important detail when interpreting later platelet dynamics because systemic exposure to both agents could prime for immune complex formation around PF4 during subsequent events.  

For definitive treatment he underwent open‑heart surgery involving aortic valve replacement, ascending aortic grafting, single coronary artery bypass grafting (obtain marginal territory) and left‑atrial appendage excision via cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). Standard practice dictates administration of approximately 45 000 U UFH at initiation of CPB to keep activated clotting time >480 seconds; this large bolus markedly exposes each circulating platelet surface‐bound PF4–heparin complexes that can swiftly bind anti‑PF4/heparin IgG antibodies when they develop postoperatively. Surgical course itself was uncomplicated, hemodynamics improved rapidly off pump with no visible intra‑operatively encountered bleeding or transfusion requirement – therefore any observed drop in thrombocytes would most likely stem from consumptive processes associated either with bypass circuitry or immune-mediated damage rather than mechanical loss or overt hemorrhage.  

On postoperative day 1 (POD 1), platelet counts fell modestly from baseline to 155 × 10⁹ L⁻¹ – an expected pattern given endothelial activation and reduced surface presentation during CPB – whereas hemoglobin remained stable around 97 g L⁻¹ and white cells were normalised at 7.8 × 10⁹ L⁻¹, arguing against bone‑marrow suppression or active hemorrhage soon after surgery. Bacterial cultures returned negative and no schistocytes were identified on peripheral film through POD 3–4, further supporting minimal microvascular injury at this juncture.  

However, between POD 1 and POD 5 the patient’s platelet count steadily declined in a clinically appreciable way: 155 → ~110 → ~80 → ~60 → **50** × 10⁹ L⁻¹ by mid‑day on POD 5 (about three days after CPB initiation). Importantly this gradual fall coincided temporally with routine first‑dose twice daily LMWH prophylaxis starting on POD 1 and moving up to higher dosing on subsequent days – an intervention typically effective only at preventing venous thromboembolism when given empirically post‑cardiac operation rather than correcting isolated thrombocytopenia caused by an autoimmune process such as HIT or its autoimmune variant (aHIT). The pattern itself – abrupt rise below critical level *before* the classic window usually associated with HIT (>48 hrs post‑exposure) – raised suspicion that some pre‑existing heparin exposure had primed an immune response earlier than typical surgical precipitating factors would allow.

Meanwhile clinical neurologic status deteriorated over the ensuing nights prior to POD 6: persistent delirium followed by gradually worsening agitation despite standard sedation protocols prompted evaluation for possible metabolic mishaps or focal complications unrelated purely to anesthetic load. Because classic focal deficits were absent yet delirium was progressive, cranial imaging was pursued as part of a systematic workup for acute central nervous system injury—remarkably thereby revealing an acute left parietal infarct measuring roughly **3.2 × 1.8 cm** extending into cortical tissue (caput lateral portion), confirmed by hypoattenuation on computed tomography scans done on POD 6 after a further sharp dip in thrombocyte count downwards toward **26** × 10⁹ L⁻¹ accompanied by lack of overt hemorrhage elsewhere.

These sequence elements – elderly patient on direct oral anticoagulant therapy, incidental low–dose intra‑procedural UFH administration prior to major surgery, high‑dose systemic UFH induction during CPB, progressive thrombocytopenia emerging before the customary postoperative time frame followed by an ischemic parietal infarction concurrent with severe drop in platelets — collectively underpin the complex clinical picture that ultimately directed laboratory investigations toward HIT diagnostics tailored specifically for early-onset cases where prior heparin exposure may play an inadvertent role.*
