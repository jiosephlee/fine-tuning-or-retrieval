Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

## Case Context & Initial Postoperative Course  

A geriatric male in his seventies presented with exertional dyspnea and a previously undetected systolic murmur that was found incidentally during evaluation for atrial fibrillation (AF) on chronic oral apixaban therapy (5 mg twice daily). His last known baseline platelet count before any procedure was 210 × 10⁹ L⁻¹ – well within the normal reference range – providing a benchmark against which subsequent postoperative drops could be compared.  

Despite continuing apixaban, he underwent transradial coronary angiography one week before planned surgery because his cardiology team judged the therapeutic benefit greater than potential bleeding risk while minimizing procedural anticoagulation by administering only 2 000 U of unfractionated heparin (UFH). This low‑dose anticoagulation was sufficient to stabilize the catheter tip but insufficiently high to fully neutralize ongoing oral factor‑Xa inhibition from apixaban – an important detail when interpreting later platelet dynamics because systemic exposure to both agents could prime for immune complex formation around PF4 during subsequent events.  

For definitive treatment he underwent open‑heart surgery involving aortic valve replacement, ascending aortic grafting, single coronary artery bypass grafting (obtain marginal territory) and left‑atrial appendage excision via cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). Standard practice dictates administration of approximately 45 000 U UFH at initiation of CPB to keep activated clotting time >480 seconds; this large bolus markedly exposes each circulating platelet surface‐bound PF4–heparin complexes that can swiftly bind anti‑PF4/heparin IgG antibodies when they develop postoperatively. Surgical course itself was uncomplicated, hemodynamics improved rapidly off pump with no visible intra‑operatively encountered bleeding or transfusion requirement – therefore any observed drop in thrombocytes would most likely stem from consumptive processes associated either with bypass circuitry or immune-mediated damage rather than mechanical loss or overt hemorrhage.  

On postoperative day 1 (POD 1), platelet counts fell modestly from baseline to 155 × 10⁹ L⁻¹ – an expected pattern given endothelial activation and reduced surface presentation during CPB – whereas hemoglobin remained stable around 97 g L⁻¹ and white cells were normalised at 7.8 × 10⁹ L⁻¹, arguing against bone‑marrow suppression or active hemorrhage soon after surgery. Bacterial cultures returned negative and no schistocytes were identified on peripheral film through POD 3–4, further supporting minimal microvascular injury at this juncture.  

However, between POD 1 and POD 5 the patient’s platelet count steadily declined in a clinically appreciable way: 155 → ~110 → ~80 → ~60 → **50** × 10⁹ L⁻¹ by mid‑day on POD 5 (about three days after CPB initiation). Importantly this gradual fall coincided temporally with routine first‑dose twice daily LMWH prophylaxis starting on POD 1 and moving up to higher dosing on subsequent days – an intervention typically effective only at preventing venous thromboembolism when given empirically post‑cardiac operation rather than correcting isolated thrombocytopenia caused by an autoimmune process such as HIT or its autoimmune variant (aHIT). The pattern itself – abrupt rise below critical level *before* the classic window usually associated with HIT (>48 hrs post‑exposure) – raised suspicion that some pre‑existing heparin exposure had primed an immune response earlier than typical surgical precipitating factors would allow.

Meanwhile clinical neurologic status deteriorated over the ensuing nights prior to POD 6: persistent delirium followed by gradually worsening agitation despite standard sedation protocols prompted evaluation for possible metabolic mishaps or focal complications unrelated purely to anesthetic load. Because classic focal deficits were absent yet delirium was progressive, cranial imaging was pursued as part of a systematic workup for acute central nervous system injury—remarkably thereby revealing an acute left parietal infarct measuring roughly **3.2 × 1.8 cm** extending into cortical tissue (caput lateral portion), confirmed by hypoattenuation on computed tomography scans done on POD 6 after a further sharp dip in thrombocyte count downwards toward **26** × 10⁹ L⁻¹ accompanied by lack of overt hemorrhage elsewhere.

These sequence elements – elderly patient on direct oral anticoagulant therapy, incidental low–dose intra‑procedural UFH administration prior to major surgery, high‑dose systemic UFH induction during CPB, progressive thrombocytopenia emerging before the customary postoperative time frame followed by an ischemic parietal infarction concurrent with severe drop in platelets — collectively underpin the complex clinical picture that ultimately directed laboratory investigations toward HIT diagnostics tailored specifically for early-onset cases where prior heparin exposure may play an inadvertent role.*

## Creating a Differential Diagnosis

Rapid hemorrhagic transformation of the peri‑operative coagulation cascade following cardiac surgery frequently introduces several competing hypotheses for an acute drop in platelet count. In this case the work‑up confidently trapped the culprit within four major categories – “**drug‑induced immune thrombocytopenia associated with antibiotics given during surgery**, “**sepsis‑associated consumptive coagulopathy ruled out by sterile cultures**, “**thrombotic microangiopathy unlikely because absence very early schistocytes**, and “**early‑onset severe fall prior to usual POV ≤ day 5 implicates pre‑operative UFH exposure rather than classic late‑onset post‑operative factors**.” Each is systematically rejected based on timing, laboratory data and clinical stability as described below.


### Drug‑induced immune thrombocytopenia associated with antibiotics given during surgery  
A classic trigger for post‑operative platelet fall is an immunologic reaction against plates coated with antimicrobial agents—in particular vancomycin or cefazolin administered intra­operatively under short courses or high doses during cardiac procedures. However, such drug‐dependent immune thrombocytopenias generally require multiple exposures or manifest after several days once peripheral B cells have generated pathogenic autoantibodies. In this patient only a single dose of each antibiotic was given peri‑camerally during heart catheterisation <12 hours before bypass surgery; no further dosing occurred until well into the postoperative course when platelet counts were already severely depressed. Moreover, there were no accompanying laboratory hallmarks such as eosinophilia, leukocyte–platelet aggregates or microvascular occlusions that would hint at an immediate hypersensitivity reaction driven by these drugs. Consequently the extent and speed of the fall—dropping below 60 × 10⁹/L within three days—did not fit the temporal profile expected for antibiotic‑induced immune thrombocytopenia.


### Sepsis-associated consumptive coagulopathy ruled out by sterile cultures  
Post‑cardiac surgical sepsis can precipitate disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), characterized by protracted PT/PTT times, profound hypofibrinogenemia followed by fibrinogen replacement spikes, markedly elevated D-dimer values exceeding hepatic synthesis rates, plus peripheral schistocyte formation and hypo­albuminemia from capillary leakage. In this case all serum biochemistry remained essentially normal except for an expected acute-phase rise in fibrinogen seen immediately postoperative – indeed fibrinogen levels peaked around POD12 then decreased consistently with therapeutic anticoagulation initiation rather than consumption from DIC. Blood cultures drawn at ages IPO were negative twice over two consecutive days; liver function tests were unremarkable (ALT/AST within normal limits); renal clearance remained intact (creatinine steady). Microangiopathic markers such as LDH did not rise appreciably beyond baseline, nor was hemolysis evidenced by low haptoglobin levels instead being elevated at 3.19 g/L—a strikingly protective signal against ongoing red cell destruction typical of sepsis-driven consumptive processes.


### Thrombotic microangiopathy unlikely because lack very early schistocytes  
A hallmark microscopic signature of thrombotic microangiopathies—including thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) or hemolytic uremic syndrome—is the presence of dysmorphic red cell fragments (schistocytes) on peripheral smear taken shortly after symptom onset. Additionally one expects laboratory evidence of intravascular hemolysis: elevated lactate dehydrogenase (>600 U/L), low haptoglobin (<0.25 g/L), indirect hyperbilirubinemia and sometimes acute kidney injury reflecting microvascular ischemia. The patient’s initial film obtained on POD7 showed resolved platelet clumping without any schistocytes; haptoglobin remained above baseline throughout the immediate recovery trajectory (>3 g/L), arguing strongly against active hemolysis midstream through septic shock or TTP triggers.


### Early‑onset severe fall prior to usual POV ≤ day 5 implicates pre‑operative UFH exposure rather than classic late‑onset post-operative factors  
Classical HIT generally manifests between POD 5–10 after initial heparin contact in patients naïve to it; it represents an adaptive immune response needing several days of antigen stimulation followed by conformational assembly between PF4 and plasma heparin forming an immunogenic complex that stimulates IgG secretion via CD4⁺ helper T cells sequentially primed over periods ranging from inpatient hospitalization through weeks following office visits. In contrast, patients who undergo **preliminary aure 心 catheterisation**—receiving even modest amounts (~2000 U) of unfractionated heparin—may develop sensitizing anti–PF4/heparin antibodies retroactively sufficient to elicit an overnight reaction upon subsequent reexposure during bypass therapy (high dose unfractionated heparin) or prophylactic LMWH administration immediately afterward if circulating short-lived high-affinity clones persist within bone marrow B lymphoid stores(a hybrid phenomenon described in recent literature). In this subject weaning off cardiopulmonary bypass occurred without notable bleeding complications yet marked **platelet decline began already on POD 1**, preceding when large doses of LMWH would normally drive up catabolic hypocoagulative processes seen elsewhere in surgical patients (~day ≥3). Thus **early commencement** coupled with the presence—at screening serology—in strong positive PF4/polyanion ELISA strongly situates this clinical presentation in **autoimmune HIT** where antibody binding does not require exogenous unfractionated heparin in order remain stimulatory—a hallmark it's manifested minutes after any residual heparine half-life expires during cardiothoracic bypass.



**Synthesis:**  
When seen collectively—the absence of extended multi-drug administration history appropriate for DITP partner numbering sequence; normal culture results negating septic DIC drivers; lack early schistocyytic evidence excluding unimproved TTP/HUS paths; and critical timing consistent with exemption reimbursement via pre-op UFH preparation—the remaining explanation converges decisively onto autoimmune HIT triggered *before* operative admission despite being uncovered only histologically afterwards through SRA interrogation revealing **both** ‘classic’ pattern activation at low therapeutic concentrations *and* spontaneous activation under zero-heparin conditions (“hepat-independent” activity) further confirmed by partial FcγR inhibition resistance.
