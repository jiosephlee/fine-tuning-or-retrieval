Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

## Initial Presentation and Early Differential Diagnosis  

An otherwise healthy male around 45 years old arrived at the emergency department with a three‑week history of progressively worsening cervical fullness coupled with tender hip flexor weakness and mild lower‑limb myalgia. On examination he was afebrile (37°C), pulse 88 bpm and saturating 99 % on room air without overt dysphagia or respiratory distress. Distinctive features included an erythematous spread along the nasolabial folds, non‑tender soft tissue bulging through the anterior triangle extending toward the retropharyngeal space, pale‑red constriction along both ears’ helix endings suggestive of periungual erythema, and fingertip capillaroscopy revealing microhaemorrhages—findings that are hallmark clues pointing toward small‑vessel vasculopathy seen in connective‑tissue processes such as dermatomyositis[^1].

Laboratory investigation showed leukocytosis was within normal limits (white cell count = 9.1 × 10⁹ L⁻¹) while C‑reactive protein was markedly elevated at 55 mg L⁻¹ (normal < 5 mg L⁻¹), indicating an inflammatory state without overt bacteremia or leukocyte surge typically seen in severe soft‑tissue infection[^2]. These data suggested either an infectious process progressing slowly without systemic toxicity or an immune‑mediated phenomenon causing active inflammation without organ failure manifestation.

Contrast‑enhanced computed tomography confirmed diffuse edema involving the strap muscles extending into the retropharyngeal space—a classic radiologic appearance can be seen both in Ludwig’s angina when cellulitic exudates permeate extensive fascial planes and in non‑infectious inflammatory states such as membranoproliferative vasculitides where soft tissues become generalized edematous owing to capillary leak.[^3] Importantly there were no discrete fluid collections with rim enhancement that would have favored abscess formation over diffuse oedema associated with immunologic disease activity[^4].

These combined clinical clues—slowly developing cervical swelling without fever or leucocytosis together with markedly increased CRP—steered initial diagnostic thinking towards two primary entities encountered frequently at tertiary networks:

| Entity | Key Typical Features | How They Were Questionable Here |
|--------|----------------------|--------------------------------|
| **Ludwig’s Angina** | Rapidly ascending cellulitis following dental/facial sepsis; often febrile, tachycardic, with firm oozing pus on palpation | Patient was afebrile, compliant asymmetry notable only after extensive imaging |
| **Idiopathic Angioedema** | Episodic cyanotic/shiny edema limited in duration <48 hrs; responds rapidly to antihistamine/epinephrine | Edema persisted for weeks despite stable vitals and lacked pruritic urticaria sign |

Spontaneous bradykinin‐mediated forms shared similar presentation but would usually demonstrate preserved mucosal colouration rather than erythema noted here[^5]. The overarching family history revealed neither hereditary factor nor medication regimen predisposing toward ACE‐inhibitor induced angioedema.[^6] Hence whilst physicians entertained both diagnoses as first screening hypotheses—immediate empiric intravenous antibiotic coverage for potential deep‐neck space infection alongside readiness for epinephrine administration—a circumstantial constellation tipped investigative strategies toward investigating an autoimmune myopathy rather than an acute septic process.^[7] Subsequent laboratory workup eventually yielded classic autoantibody positivity confirming anti‑SAE1 dermatomyositis—a diagnosis nested between these two broad differentials owing primarily to skin‑muscle involvement ambiguous upon initial observation.^[8]
