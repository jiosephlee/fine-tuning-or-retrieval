## Dermatomyositis Overview and the Anti‑SAE1 Subtype  

Dermatomyositis is a prototypical idiopathic inflammatory myopathy that manifests with a constellation of cutaneous rashes, proximal muscle weakness and frequent extramuscular disease involving the lungs, gastrointestinal tract or heart. Its crude incidence is roughly ten cases per million persons per year, displaying a bimodal age distribution centred on adolescence (5–14 y) and mid‑adulthood (45–64 y). The peak age seen in the index case—a previously healthy man in his forties—reflects the adult onset predominance found most often in males who are at relatively higher risk for malignant sequelae than their female counterparts.  

In the workup of suspected dermatomyositis a panel of “myositis‑specific” autoantibodies (MSAs) provides essential diagnostic guidance because these directed humoral responses correlate strongly with clinical phenotypes, prognosis and cancer‐associated risks. Classic MSAs comprise antibodies against aminoacyl‑tRNA synthetases (anti‑Jo‐1, anti‑Ja‐1/Alu‐X/Y), transcriptional intermediary factor‑1γ/MDA5 (TIF‐γ/MDA5) and specific nuclear helicases such as Mi‑2α/β or plakins like p155/140/TIF‐γ from better recognized patients with “classically” dermatomyosis skin findings and lung involvement respectively. In contrast to these immunodominant targets is the small ubiquitin‑like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE‑1) antibody—an MSA recognised only approximately five percent of all dermatomyosis cases worldwide—but one increasingly appreciated for its unique immunopathogenic clues.  

The SAE protein functions as part of the SUMOylation machinery that conjugates Small Ubiquitin‑Related Modifier peptides onto lysine residues of nuclear proteins governing DNA repair, transcription regulation and chromatin structure. In healthy cells this post‑translational modification prevents misfolded proteins from aggregating while modulating transcription factor activity. Autoimmunity targeting SAE disrupts this balance by either directly antagonising enzyme function or driving an antigenic cascade that leads to aberrant recognition of SUMOylated substrates by cytotoxic T cells or innate immune sensors—ultimately precipitating inflammatory cell recruitment within affected tissues.\

This mechanistic disruption intersects vividly with another hallmark pathological feature common to dermatomyositis—microvascular injury driven by complement activation. ## Clinical Presentation Red Flags – Neck Swelling Masquerading Angioedema  

The patient’s index presentation was deceptively simple yet laden with clues that should raise suspicion for an uncommon inflammatory etiology rather than a classic infectious or allergic disease. A systematic analysis of these red flags helps clinicians orient their diagnostic reasoning early on, avoiding futile procedures directed at more common entities such as Ludwig’s angina or bradykinin‑mediated angioedema.

### Three‑week history of progressive cervical soft‑tissue swelling  
An evolving course over three weeks favors a subacute inflammatory process. Acute deep‑neck infections usually present within 48–72 h with rapid onset pain, fever and localized cellulitis or abscess formation detectable on imaging. In contrast, the slow gain in volume seen here suggests continuous leakage into perimuscular spaces—a pattern typical of chronic myositis rather than a bacterial exudate. Moreover, the degree of oedema extended into retropharyngeal space without frank abscess formation—a key feature distinguishing it from Ludwig’s angina where pus pockets are readily apparent on CT.

### Differentiating Ludwig’s angina versus histamine/bradykinin‑mediated edema  
Ludwig’s angina classically displays uvular distortion, firm tenderness over the floor of mouth, pharyngeal redness and an elevated nasopharyngeal temperature (>38 °C). The patient was afebrile and exhibited no significant Pain‑to‑touch tenderness; glottic swellings were uniform rather than focal collections. Bradykinin‑driven angioedema presents with sudden facial/neck fullness accompanied by mucosal sparseness (no ecchymosis) and minimal constitutional symptoms. Our patient demonstrated a markedly raised C‑reactive protein (55 mg/L) yet lacked fever and headache—a discordant laboratory–clinical picture suggestive of a systemic inflammatory condition such as dermatomyositis, where cytokine storm drives widespread vascular leakage even without overt infection.

### “Pocket” weakness at hip flexors preceding overt facial rash  
Subclinical proximal muscle involvement can precede cutaneous manifestations in dermatomyositis by days or weeks. Hip–flexor “pocket” weakness (4/5 strength), isolated at presentation months before rash authorisation (later heliotrope eruption), signals an early autoimmune infiltrate targeting skeletal muscle fibers—often missed if clinicians focus exclusively on facial swelling. Recognising this loss bolsters suspicion for myositis rather than purely vascular disorders which seldom appear with selective limb muscle paresis early on.

### Non‑tender erythema over nasolabial folds raising suspicion against allergic etiology  
Erythematous patches found around nasal folds were diffuse and not associated with any itching or pain—features uncharacteristic for urticaria which is pruritic and fleetingly migratory. ### Subtle nailfold changes revealing periungual erythema  
Periungual capillaroscopic abnormalities—microhaemorrhages and avascular zones—in our case were highly suggestive of a vascular injury secondary to immune complex deposition inherent in dermatomyositis pathogenesis (complement activation leading to C5b-9 deposition). Unlike systemic inflammatory disorders such as systemic lupus where nailfolds show giant capillaries or angiogenetic loop elongation, these small red dots rarely hint at nonspecific vascular leak seen in isolated angioedemas.

### Limited purpura absent rules out urticarial vasculitis  
The absence of palpable purpuric lesions alongside normal coagulation screens effectively excluded leukocytoclastic vasculitides which frequently manifest with linear/patchy purpura along extensor surfaces—criteria starkly missing in this patient’s examination findings despite widespread skin discolouration outside initial lesion sites.

### Normal respiratory rate arguing against sepsis  
A key bedside parameter was maintained baseline respiration (~22 breaths/min), conflicting with early septic physiology wherein tachypnea (>20 breaths/min), metabolic acidosis or altered mental status are inevitable if bacterial cellulite progressed dramatically within neck spaces. Heightened systemic cytokines from severe myositis likewise do not elicit profound respiratory compromise until later stages (here witnessed only after dysphagia progression).

### Absence of medication triggers discount ACEI/-DPP4‑related angioedema  
Bradykinin-mediated reactions correlate strongly with ACE inhibitors or DPP4 inhibitors via reduced kinin degradation pathways leading predominantly laryngeal and tongue edema sporadically accompanied by dyspnea—none observed clinically despite extensive medication review confirming no exposure. This directed attention away from drug-induced risk factor landscapes toward an intrinsic immunologic disorder driving tissue fluid accumulation through autoantibody-mediated muscular injury.

## Diagnostic Work‑up – From Routine Labs to Confirmation Biopsy  

When a patient presents with progressive cervical swelling that does not respond to conventional treatment for infection or allergy, the work‑up must proceed methodically from general screening tests toward disease‑specific investigations that can confirm an autoimmune‐myopathic process versus a purely vascular phenomenon such as idiopathic angioedema.  

**CBC & CRP**  
An initial routine panel revealed a white‑cell count within normal limits (9.1 × 10⁹ L⁻¹) but an elevated acute–phase response (CRP 55.6 mg L⁻¹). The absence of leukocytosis argues against overwhelming bacterial infection, while the mild systemic inflammation supports an alternative inflammatory cause that could explain both the neck edema and proximal myalgias reported by the patient.  

**Serum Muscle Enzymes**  
Creatine kinase (CK) was markedly elevated at 16 184 U L⁻¹—far above the laboratory upper limit (≈200 U L⁻¹)—indicating widespread skeletal‑muscle necrosis reserved for acute inflammatory myopathies or severe exertional injury. This extreme CK elevation is far less compatible with idiopathic angioedema or isolated skin conditions and triggered deeper inquiry into muscular disease within this patient’s history of progressive weakness and dysphagia.  

**ANA Speckled Titers**  
A high‑titer antinuclear antibody (ANA) with a speckled pattern at 1:1280 underscored an underlying systemic autoimmune milieu that is frequently seen in idiopathic inflammatory myopathies but is non‑specific for any single subtype. The pattern suggested nuclear antigen involvement while ruling out synovial pathology alone as differential etiology for his symptoms.  

**Euroimmun Myositis Panel**  
The next step was a comprehensive auto‑antibody screen dedicated to inflammatory myopathies. The Euroimmun Euroline assay yielded a strikingly strong signal against small ubiquitin‑like modifier activating enzyme (SAE1) (signal intensity 173 against a normal range <35). Detection of anti‑SAE1 is highly specific for a subset of dermatomyositis characterized by distinct cutaneous manifestations and increased rates of dysphagia. Other common myositis antibodies—including Mi‑2α, TIF1‑γ, Jo-1 and OJ—were negative, refining the diagnosis towards SAE1–positive dermatomyositis rather than other autoimmune muscular disorders such as immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy or inclusion body myositis which lack these serologic hallmarks.  

**Complement Levels**  
C3/C4 concentrations and functional C1 esterase activity were all within normal limits, effectively excluding hereditary angioedema—a condition driven by bradykinin due to C1 inhibitor deficiency—and solidifying that any VEGF- or histamine-mediated swelling was unlikely responsible for the cervical oedema observed radiologically and clinically in this patient.  

**Capillaroscopy**  
Nailfold video capillaroscopy demonstrated periungual erythema combined with telangiectatic microvasculature surrounding small vessels—a hallmark microangiopathic feature commonly seen in dermatomyositis but absent in isolated angioedema events where capillary integrity remains preserved after swelling resolves.  

**CT Neck**  
Computed tomography outlined diffuse soft‐tissue attenuating edema involving all strap muscles with extension into the retropharyngeal space, without evidence of abscess formation or discrete pus collections that would diagnose deep neck space infection such as Ludwig’s angina. The symmetrical extent of edema across fat planes is more consistent with inflammatory involvement affecting striated muscle tissue than with localized hydraulic distension typical of classic angioedematous processes.  

**Chest CT**  
Incidental findings on thoracic imaging included basal ground–glass opacities attributable mostly to aspiration pneumonitis secondary to dysphagia—a sequela often seen in severe dermatomyositis—but no interstitial fibrosis or honeycombing patterns that might indicate chronic ILD commonly associated with seronegative dermatomyositis subsets harbouring different autoantibodies (e.g., TIF1-γ). The absence of pulmonary infiltrates beyond aspiration minimised the likelihood that respiratory failure was influencing cervical swelling through pulmonary-induced reflex mechanisms rather than direct muscular inflammation.  

**MRI Thighs**  
Sagittal fat‐suppressed T2‐weighted sequences highlighted classic perifascicular areas of edema involving internal obturator/externus muscles as well as more focal shortening within distal iliopsoas fibers—an imaging signature strongly associated with dermatomyositis’ predilection for perifascicular atrophy due both physiologic strain injury and immune-mediated vascular ischemia along vascular end arcs supplied by musculocutaneous perforators. This specificity helped exclude other inflammatory neuromuscular entities like polymyositis which typically shows more diffuse symmetric edema without preferential perifascicular predilection.
