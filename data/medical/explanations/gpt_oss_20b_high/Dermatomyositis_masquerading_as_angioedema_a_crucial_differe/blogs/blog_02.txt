Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# Creatine Kinase Is Your Early Red Flag  

## The 3‑Week Neck Swelling That Wasn’t “Ludwig’s”

Three weeks ago I saw a healthy male in his forties come into the ED with what looked like classic Ludwig’s angina – diffuse swelling over both strap muscles, mild trismus and a “corkscrew” voice on flexible nasendoscopy. No fever, normal vital signs and a CT neck revealed diffuse edema extending into the retropharyngeal space but no abscess or source of infection. The first instinct was to drive an ENT consult for deep‑neck space infection work‑up – potentially a surgical scenario if it worsened.

What didn’t fit was the lack of any infectious source (no neutrophilic leukocytosis beyond 9 × 10^9/L, CRP at 55 mg/L but otherwise unremarkable cultures) and the observation that his face carried subtle erythema over nasolabial folds—an odd peripheral finding on a patient who was sipping water quietly while we consulted with ENT and had no crinoid breathing sounds or stridor.

If we had stopped there… everything would have reasonably stayed in the “unknown cause” bubble: possible allergic reaction (histamine‑mediated), idiopathic angioedema (AE), or maybe even ACE inhibitor–related angioedema (he was medication‑free). But I remembered an old piece of simple medical wisdom: if your work‑up for infection is negative but major soft‑tissue risk factors are present, draw creatine kinase (CK).  

The lab call came back two days later – CK 16 184 U/L (the upper limit of normal being ~200 U/L).  
That single number shifted our trajectory from ENT surgery simulations to an examination field trial for inflammatory myopathy.



## What CK Tells Us – A Red‑Flag Threshold  

* **HyperCKemia ≥10 000 U/L** is virtually unheard of in reactive or metabolic muscle injury in isolation without systemic signs like fever or rash during routine inflammation episodes.  
* Inflammatory myopathies—including dermatomyositis—can precipitate dramatic spikes (rise often >5–10 times baseline), especially when muscle fibers undergo necrosis (as visualised on biopsy).  
* A marked increase amid “mild” systemic inflammation (CRP up by ~50 mg/L but afebrile) is almost as telescopic as hypereosinophilic syndromes where you don't see classic organ infiltrates immediately; you see labs scream first.  
* In this patient’s case, anti‑SME1 autoantibody positivity narrowed diagnosis quickly once the muscle picture unfolded.



## Leeds Drummed Up a Differential – Lance Armstrong Level Dissecting  

1. **Dermatomyositis** – classic skin findings initially absent (Gottron’s papules/hleo­trope visible later), yet peri­ungual capillaroscopy revealed micro‑haemorrhages pointing toward connective tissue disease.
2. **Idiopathic Angus Edema** – ruled out through normal C1‑inhibitor function and complement levels.
3. **Cervical lymphadenitis / deep neck infections** – CT showed no purulence.
4. **Other myopathies** – Myositis panel excluded Ali‑α/mi‑2/TIF1γ/Jo‑1/OJ antibodies.
5. **Malignancy** – PET/CT was unremarkable except metabolically active muscles; multiple malignancy screening studies were negative so far.



## Managed With Heavy Surgeries… Virtually  

The patient received:
 * **High‐dose IV methylprednisolone 1 g/day ×3 days**, transitioned to oral prednisone taper.
 * **IVIG** base dosing + maintenance courses every four weeks.
 * **Mycophenolate mofetil** 1 g twice daily.
 * **Rituximab** ×2 infusions 1000 mg II 2 weeks apart.
 * PCP prophylaxis switched midway from TMP–SMX after hemolytic anemia produced Nebulized pentamidine every month.
 * Persistent dysphagia managed endoscopically via nasogastric tube until oral intake improved ≈3 months post‑initial treatment.


Outcome? Aspiration pneumonia resolved on imaging, CK normalized by week 3 post admission, muscular strength recovered fully by month three—over half a year before steroids could safely be stopped.



## Bottom Line – Keep Your Eyes On That Enzyme  

We often think of serum creatine kinase as “just for athletes” or “creepy crossfit injury,” but when it shoots well into six digits it should raise eyebrows fast enough to tip our differential off its usual track of bacterial infection or allergic reaction entirely.


### Take‑aways

- **≥10 000 U/L CK is enough reason to suspect an inflammatory myopathy**, even when accompanying fevers are absent or minimal.
- When encountering unexplained soft‐tissue swelling—especially over heads/neck/face—in patients devoid of clear infectious sources check serum muscle enzymes promptly.
- Subtle dermatologic clues can conform less obvious cases; don’t dismiss peri­ungual vascular changes or minor facial erythema because they are “not classic.”
- A confirmed elevated CK in such settings should push clinicians toward rheumatologic evaluation rather than prolonged empiric antibiotics or steroids alone until results resolve.



Whether you’re pushing antibiotics down a septic patient’s line or giving steroid boluses for presumed angioedema, let that enormous spike in serum CK remind you that inflammatory myopathies can present subtly—and beating the clock likes early lab work just as much as bedside nuance does.
