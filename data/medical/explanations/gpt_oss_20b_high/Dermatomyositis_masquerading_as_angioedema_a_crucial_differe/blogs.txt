Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# Cervical Oedema That Is Not an Infection  

## A Little‑Known Giant in the Neck

When a young man presents to the ED with a three‑week history of progressive neck swelling, you imagine Ludwig’s angina or a soft‑tissue infection first. In our community hospital we raised antibiotics and CT dials expectedly. The twist wasn't an abscess or malignancy – it was dermatomyositis masquerading as a pseudo‑angioedema. This case shows that sometimes the “simple” neck flare hides a diffuse autoimmune catastrophe that can threaten the airway before you even get an infectious screen back.

---

## The First Look – What Was Unusual?

* **Persistently swollen neck** – striking diffuse edema of strap muscles extending into retropharyngeal space.  
* **No systemic infection** – afebrile, saturations normal, WCC only mildly elevated (9 × 10^9 L).  
* **Proximal weakness** – hip flexor power downgraded to 4/5 within days of presentation.  
* **Subtle cutaneous clues** – periungual erythema on capillaroscopy, mild facial erythema over nasolabial folds, but no classic Gottron’s papules or heliotrope rash.  
* **Laboratory surprise** – CK catapulted to **16 184 U/L**, far exceeding any inflammatory‐mediated angioedema expectation.

The last item made us toss the “angioedema” hypothesis out the window sooner than usual. In practice we tend to wait for labs before contemplating steroids; here we had a biologic alarm and had to act on it fast.

---

## Differential Diagnosis – The Classic Wall‑Off

| Pt’s history | Initial suspects |
|--------------|-------------------|
| Neck swelling + aspiration risk | Ludwig’s angina / deep‐neck space infection |
| No palpable mass or abscess | Microbial cellulitis or sialadenitis |
| Pruritic facial rash | Angiotensin‑converting enzyme inhibitor excess |
| Elevated temperature/CRP | Bacterial meningitis/thyroid storm |

The skin sign that slipped past many ENT surgeons was a nailfold microhaemorrhage pattern typical of connective‐tissue disease—rarely considered when you’re focussing on pharyngeal flora. On top of that we had a **positive ANA** (1:1280 speckled) and a myositis panel murmuring “SAE‑1”—the 'SMARTE' indicator you'd expect in an autoimmune myopathy bull shaking your ENT colleagues into silence.

---

## Work‑Up Snapshot

* **CT Neck (contrast)** – diffuse soft‑tissue edema without discrete abscesses → ruled out bacterial focus on day one.
* **Serum CK** – >10× ULN → strongly argues against purely histamine‐driven angioedema.
* **Myositis antibody panel** – EVP® Euroline test → high intensity anti‑SAE1 titre (**173 > 35**).
* **MRI Thigh + PET** – widespread peripheral muscle uptake plus active fascia infiltration consistent with dermatomyositis rashless subtype.
* **Muscle biopsy** – peri‑fascicular necrosis + C5b–9 deposition → classic dermatomyopathy blue‑print.
* **Complement levels / C1 esterase inhibitor** – normal → excludes acquired HAE.
* **Chest CT + PFTs** – mid‑lower zone nodules yet no fibrosis at baseline; mild restrictive pattern likely nonspecific initially but followed up serially.
 
A single lab value hammered home the diagnosis—high CK in the context of localized soft tissue edema is akin to reading “myositis” instead of “infection” from an ultrasound report gunshot wound.

---

## Management Course – Rapid Steroids First

In this scenario airway compromise risks climb steeply within hours because neck fibrosis can encroach on laryngeal structures and dysphagia will worsen rapidly.

1. **IV methylprednisolone 1000 mg daily × 3 days** for immediate anti‑inflammatory effect & swollen tissue reversibility.  
2. Transition to oral prednisone taper starting at 60 mg daily – reducing by 5 mg every 2–3 weeks based on CK & symptom trends.  
3. Send off IVIG cycle (2 g/kg over five days) followed by maintenance every four weeks as adjunct rescue therapy.  
4. Initiate **mycophenolate mofetil 1 g BID** as steroid‐sparing agent early due to LT infiltration risk with methotrexate (IL–D lung function abnormal).  
5. Administer two rituximab infusions (1000 mg IV) two weeks apart as we tend toward sustained remission—especially useful after steroid taper decreases breakthrough symptoms.  
6. Allergy risk control – stop TMP/SMX prophylaxis due to hemolytic anaemia; switch to nebulised pentamidine quarterly instead of oral PCP prophylaxis because baseline glucocorticoids persist longer than anticipated.

> 💡 A quick high‑dose steroid pulse can buy time for diagnostics because delayed treatment in dermatomyositis—particularly with airway involvement—surpasses what any antibiotic ever could save you from a “Ludwig’s” emergency collapse.

---

## Outcome – Airway First, Then Muscle Recovery Sequentially

By day 21 postpartum:

* Neck edema shrank markedly though still not completely resolved at discharge.
* Hip flexor strength partially improved; dysphagia warranted NG tube feeding for another two months.
* Creatine kinase fell from >16k U/L to almost normal (**37** U/L) after three weeks of therapy irrespective of taper speed.
* Within five months: full resolution of cervical edema, uncomplicated swallow function inferred by nasogastric removal before six months.
* Longitudinal pulmonary CT at month 11 showed near normalization—likely attributable both to therapy and natural course rather than active ILD progression.

While he survived this critical window without mechanical ventilation or lateral pharyngoesophageal collapse documented on laryngoscopy—a heartbreaking tool not used—we relied on serial clinical assessment and repeat imaging taken wisdom by clinicians rather than invasive tests whenever possible.

---

## Clinical Pearls & Take‑Home Messages

- **Dermatomyositis can present as painless neck pseudoburst**, especially when accompanied by proximal weakness—even if skin rash is minimal or absent.
- A markedly elevated CK (>10× ULN) in any patient presenting with non‑exudative edema should redirect your bedside suspicion away from classic infections toward inflammatory myopathy.
- Subtle nailfold changes are often ignored but can provide crucial clues when cutaneous hallmark features are not yet manifest.
- When airway involvement is possible (dysphagia or retropharyngeal extension), initiate a high‑dose IV steroid trial before awaiting full immunologic workup—it’s more life-saving than sitting around for lab results.
- Keep in mind that anti‑SAE1 positivity associates with dysphagia and pulmonary infiltrates; hence simultaneous thoracic imaging is essential even if chest X-ray looks clean at first glance.
- Even when initial ENT evaluation uncovers no obvious pathology on CT or flexible nasendoscopy, dexamethasone may still hold the key if autoimmunity is suspected—it can quell inflammatory edema that otherwise would thicken tissue thickness overnight.
- In patients under twenty–five years old? Not relevant here but pertinent for younger ages as this condition also pops up sporadically—they flourish differently long term.

---

## Bottom Line?

Don’t let the words “swelling” hide fibrosis behind an autoimmune veil! An unexpected triad of neck edema + proximal weakness + huge CK load arouses suspicion for dermatomyositis masquerading as pseudo‑angioedema or Ludwig’s angina—a scenario amenable only to prompt immunomodulation rather than antibiotics alone. Keep your differential broad enough that you’ll start steroids early while still chasing pitfalls like true infection—and watch your airway—all while gearing up for an aggressive but survivable rheumatologic disease course.”



# Creatine Kinase Is Your Early Red Flag  

## The 3‑Week Neck Swelling That Wasn’t “Ludwig’s”

Three weeks ago I saw a healthy male in his forties come into the ED with what looked like classic Ludwig’s angina – diffuse swelling over both strap muscles, mild trismus and a “corkscrew” voice on flexible nasendoscopy. No fever, normal vital signs and a CT neck revealed diffuse edema extending into the retropharyngeal space but no abscess or source of infection. The first instinct was to drive an ENT consult for deep‑neck space infection work‑up – potentially a surgical scenario if it worsened.

What didn’t fit was the lack of any infectious source (no neutrophilic leukocytosis beyond 9 × 10^9/L, CRP at 55 mg/L but otherwise unremarkable cultures) and the observation that his face carried subtle erythema over nasolabial folds—an odd peripheral finding on a patient who was sipping water quietly while we consulted with ENT and had no crinoid breathing sounds or stridor.

If we had stopped there… everything would have reasonably stayed in the “unknown cause” bubble: possible allergic reaction (histamine‑mediated), idiopathic angioedema (AE), or maybe even ACE inhibitor–related angioedema (he was medication‑free). But I remembered an old piece of simple medical wisdom: if your work‑up for infection is negative but major soft‑tissue risk factors are present, draw creatine kinase (CK).  

The lab call came back two days later – CK 16 184 U/L (the upper limit of normal being ~200 U/L).  
That single number shifted our trajectory from ENT surgery simulations to an examination field trial for inflammatory myopathy.



## What CK Tells Us – A Red‑Flag Threshold  

* **HyperCKemia ≥10 000 U/L** is virtually unheard of in reactive or metabolic muscle injury in isolation without systemic signs like fever or rash during routine inflammation episodes.  
* Inflammatory myopathies—including dermatomyositis—can precipitate dramatic spikes (rise often >5–10 times baseline), especially when muscle fibers undergo necrosis (as visualised on biopsy).  
* A marked increase amid “mild” systemic inflammation (CRP up by ~50 mg/L but afebrile) is almost as telescopic as hypereosinophilic syndromes where you don't see classic organ infiltrates immediately; you see labs scream first.  
* In this patient’s case, anti‑SME1 autoantibody positivity narrowed diagnosis quickly once the muscle picture unfolded.



## Leeds Drummed Up a Differential – Lance Armstrong Level Dissecting  

1. **Dermatomyositis** – classic skin findings initially absent (Gottron’s papules/hleo­trope visible later), yet peri­ungual capillaroscopy revealed micro‑haemorrhages pointing toward connective tissue disease.
2. **Idiopathic Angus Edema** – ruled out through normal C1‑inhibitor function and complement levels.
3. **Cervical lymphadenitis / deep neck infections** – CT showed no purulence.
4. **Other myopathies** – Myositis panel excluded Ali‑α/mi‑2/TIF1γ/Jo‑1/OJ antibodies.
5. **Malignancy** – PET/CT was unremarkable except metabolically active muscles; multiple malignancy screening studies were negative so far.



## Managed With Heavy Surgeries… Virtually  

The patient received:
 * **High‐dose IV methylprednisolone 1 g/day ×3 days**, transitioned to oral prednisone taper.
 * **IVIG** base dosing + maintenance courses every four weeks.
 * **Mycophenolate mofetil** 1 g twice daily.
 * **Rituximab** ×2 infusions 1000 mg II 2 weeks apart.
 * PCP prophylaxis switched midway from TMP–SMX after hemolytic anemia produced Nebulized pentamidine every month.
 * Persistent dysphagia managed endoscopically via nasogastric tube until oral intake improved ≈3 months post‑initial treatment.


Outcome? Aspiration pneumonia resolved on imaging, CK normalized by week 3 post admission, muscular strength recovered fully by month three—over half a year before steroids could safely be stopped.



## Bottom Line – Keep Your Eyes On That Enzyme  

We often think of serum creatine kinase as “just for athletes” or “creepy crossfit injury,” but when it shoots well into six digits it should raise eyebrows fast enough to tip our differential off its usual track of bacterial infection or allergic reaction entirely.


### Take‑aways

- **≥10 000 U/L CK is enough reason to suspect an inflammatory myopathy**, even when accompanying fevers are absent or minimal.
- When encountering unexplained soft‐tissue swelling—especially over heads/neck/face—in patients devoid of clear infectious sources check serum muscle enzymes promptly.
- Subtle dermatologic clues can conform less obvious cases; don’t dismiss peri­ungual vascular changes or minor facial erythema because they are “not classic.”
- A confirmed elevated CK in such settings should push clinicians toward rheumatologic evaluation rather than prolonged empiric antibiotics or steroids alone until results resolve.



Whether you’re pushing antibiotics down a septic patient’s line or giving steroid boluses for presumed angioedema, let that enormous spike in serum CK remind you that inflammatory myopathies can present subtly—and beating the clock likes early lab work just as much as bedside nuance does.

# Blending Pulse Steroids With Biologics For Severe Dermatomyositis

This one starts classic‑looking in the emergency department – a man in his forties comes in with painful cervical swelling best described as “Ludwig’s angina”. What follows is a tongue‑in‑cheek reminder that **neck edema can be the herald of dermatomyositis** rather than an infectious deep space abscess or idiopathic angioedema. When the picture is atypical—no fever, no frank purulence, an otherwise well patient—the head‐to‑toe skin check becomes the bedside strate­gy that pivots management entirely toward aggressive medical therapy instead of surgical drainage or antihistamines alone.



## What Makes This Case Interesting

- **Angioedema masquerade** – Subcutaneous edema confined largely to the neck mimicked anaphylaxis or bradykinin‑mediated angioedema despite normal C1 esterase activity and normal complement fractions.
- **Neck swelling caused by muscle inflammation** – Pathological work‑up revealed diffuse myofascial inflammation that produced “pseudo‑angioedema” rather than vascular leakage.
- **Serum CK spiking >16 000 U/L** – The bread‐and butter laboratory value that tipped off clinicians toward an inflammatory myopathy amid an otherwise misleading ENT work‑up.
- **Anti‑SAE1 positivity** – A relatively uncommon autoantibody associated most consistently with dysphagia and interstitial lung disease within dermatomyositis populations.
- **Aggressive combination therapy** – Pulse methylprednisolone followed by a structured prednisone taper plus intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) *and two rituximab infusions* defeated airway‐relevant disease activity within weeks.



## Clinical Vignette Recap

| Day | What Happened | Key Finding |
|-----|---------------|-------------|
| ED arrival | Progressive neck swelling + proximal hip flexor weakness | Neck CT = diffuse strap muscle edema extending into retropharyngeal space |
| Same day | ENT flexible nasendoscopy | No anatomical abnormality; cough/high secretions noted |
| Next day | Serum labs sent | Creatine kinase ↑ → 16 kU/L (normal <200); axillary CRP elevated |
| Initial interpretation | “Ludwig’s angina” → CTA → “Idiopathic angioedema” | Unexplained neutropenia absent |
| Re‑examined† | Nailfold capillaroscopy = periungual erythema + microblan vascular changes | Erythematous face + rash on lateral thigh |

†The subtle periungual findings are one example of “skin-findings-on-the-fly” clinicians should always keep an eye out for when puzzling musculoskeletal presentations appear.



## Diagnostic Toolbox That Separated Angioedema From Dermatomyositis

| Modality | Result | Why It Matters |
|----------|--------|----------------|
| Serum CK | **16 184 U/L** | Acute skeletal muscle injury index far exceeding what you’d expect with isolated angioedema |
| ANA screen | Speckled pattern titer 1:1280 | Encourages broad autoimmune evaluation |
| Myositis panel | **Anti‑SAE1 strongly positive** (signal 173) • Negative Mi‑2α/TIF1γ/Jo‑1/others | Stronger evidence pointing directly toward dermatomyositis spectrum |
| Muscle biopsy | Perifascicular necrosis + C5b‑9 deposition on endomysial capillaries | Classic histologic fingerprint distinguishing classic inflammatory myopathies |
| MRI thighs & PET scan | Active perifascicular inflammation w/ FDG uptake diffusely across head + neck, deltoids, forearms & pelvis | Confirms loooong deep tissue involvement rather than peripheral subcutaneous edema alone |

Sequence matters – getting serum CK promptly reversed an initial misguided surgical extension on the basis of >70% “deep space infection” probability score.



## Why Anti‑SAE1 Is Emblematic For This Disease Course

Patients harboring anti‐SAE1 autoantibodies often present late‐onset proximal weakness *and*, crucially,
**dysphagia complemented by interstitial lung disease** in nearly half they turn out present:
> *In a semi–real world sample of SAE1 + dermatomyositis,* dysphagia = 46 %, ILD = 43 %.  
> *Malignancy,* though less frequent than other DM autoantibody cohorts (~18 %), mandates extensive screening per IMACS guidelines because SAEs contribute both oncologic signalling pathways and taste‑bottleneck immune dysfunction.*

That’s why our patient underwent:
* PET—to rule out occult adenopathy even though no active metabolically bright node was present,
* Multiple tumour marker panels,
* Baseline thorax CT,
* Gastrointestinal endoscopies planned shortly thereafter.



## Treatment Strategy Map

High intensity treatment focuses on rapid aero‑thymic decompression followed by long-term control without cumulative organ toxicity—all packaged under an interprofessional framework touching rheumatology, immunology and ENT realms.


### A. Pulse Steroids + Taper

> **Day 0–3** IV methylprednisolone  =  1000 mg/day  
> Rationale – Clearance of edematous tissue deposits within hours → airway protection milestones  
> **Day 4** Start oral prednisone ####30–60   mg/day (titrated)
> Every **2–3 weeks**, reduce dose by ~5 mg applying how soon signs resolve / CRP/CK trend
>  
> *Avoid abrupt termination.*

### B. Intravenous Immunoglobulin (`IVIG`)

> Initial loading = 2 g/kg ÷5 days → glomerular support + blockade of pathogenic autoantibodies 
> Follow-up weekly/monthly infusions (~2 g/kg) spaced ~4 weeks apart through **6–7 months** depending on clinical remission status.


### C.`Rituximab`

> Two doses = 1000 mg IV , spaced ≈ 14 days   
> Goal – B cell depletion tempts sustained antibody blockade downtrading long term dose exposures compared some standard csDMARDs.



### D.`Steroid–Sparing Agent`

> **Mycophenolate mofetil**(MMF) ‖ *Choice rational*:  
> * Reduced risk vs methotrexate in presence pft ≥10% restrictive component (+aspiration)*
> * Oral twice-daily dosing ‑ patient preference & comorbidity safety profile 
> 
> Alternatives (*not chosen*) -> Azathioprine/tacrolimus faced intolerability / slower onset  
> Methotrexate avoided upfront simply because it carries potential cumulative pulmonary toxicity especially when baseline ILD is detected.


### E.`PCP Prophylaxis Customization`

# Cancer Screening Checklist When Facing Myopathy Impact  

---

## Why think about tumours when you see a rash and muscle weakness?

Dermatomyositis (DM) is classically a “rash‑first” disease—helio­tope rashes, Gottron’s papules and nailfold changes often herald muscle inflammation years before the headache‑like pain beyond your shoulder blades becomes obvious. Yet under that fishy skin lies a very real cancer clock ticking hands down every week it takes days longer than you think. In adult males aged 45–64—the very demographic of our index patient—a prospective registry found roughly **18 %** harbouring an occult malignancy within five years of DM diagnosis when anti‑SAE1 antibodies were present. 

This isn’t just academic nuance—any snapshot from a clinical trial tells us DM is one of the most common connective tissue diseases where “watchful waiting” turns into *watchful fighting* against nascent tumours.  

---

## The IMACS scaffold—how do we move from “maybe” to “definitely urgent”?

IMACS (International Myositis Assessment & Clinical Studies Group) built a pragmatic rubric around three classic variables:

| Category | Examples | Weight |
|---------|----------|--------|
| **Myositis type** | Dermatomyositis = **high** | |
| **Clinical features** | Dysphagia = **high**, symmetric proximal weakness = **moderate** | |
| **Specific auto‑antibodies** | Anti‑SAE1 = **intermediate**, anti‑TIF‑γ/anti‑MDA5 = **intermediate**, others < 5 % | |

**Rule:**  
- Two *high* risk factors → *high‐risk* group → full immediate cancer work‑up within weeks.  
- One *high* + two *intermediate* → still *high‐risk*.  
- Any isolated *intermediate* alone = low–moderate—no immediate imaging unless organ involvement surfaces.

Our patient clearly overlapped into that top bin:
* Dermatomyositis – high  
* Dysphagia – high  
* Male sex – intermediate  
* Anti‑SAE1 positivity – intermediate  

Total score = two highs + two intermediates → unequivocally “high‐risk”.  

---

## From rash to referral desk—case follow‑through

> A man in his forties presents with “neck swelling” billed initially as Ludwig’s angina—afterward recognized as pseudo‑angioedema secondary to inflammatory myofascial edema from dermatomyositis.

Key lab values on day 2 mirrored classic inflammatory myopathies:
- CK ~ 16 000 U/L (normal < 200)  
- ALT ≈ 140 U/L, AST ≈ 400 U/L  
- LDH ≈ 700 U/L  
- ANA titer 1:1280 speckled pattern  

Imaging crescendoed:
- Chest CT showed multiple pulmonary nodules without classic fibrosis—a red flag prompting *PET/CT*, which came back clean for metastases.
- Video fluoroscopy confirmed severe pharyngeal dysphagia—a real early sign of airway compromise.
- MRI/MRS equivocal—multifocal active myositis confined mainly to thigh compartments rather than cardiac muscle yet still indicated systemic inflammatory burden.

## Essentials checklist—for every practitioner fearing hidden tumours

### Baseline immediately upon confirmation of idiopathic inflammatory myopathy

1. **Chest imaging**  
   * Chest CT whether or not ILD present *if organotropism* flag exists (e.g., lymphadenopathy > 4 mm); 
   * PET/CT optional if unremitting symptoms unseen on conventional imaging despite steroid trial.
2. **Endoscopic evaluation**  
   * Gastroscopy & colonoscopy regardless of gastrointestinal symptoms—colorectal cancers lag behind new-onset DM by months!
3. **Tumour marker panel**  
   * CEA, CA19‑9 — especially adult pneumonias > 50 y;
   * CA125 — give cross‑check between pleural effusions and ascites;
   * PSA — men over 50 may stalk silent prostate carcinoma hidden behind CK spikes.
4. **Full history & physical emphasis resolution**  
   * Look for subtle facial telangiectasias, Heliotrope rash—even absent subhepatic jolts!

### Follow-up cadence—how does it shift over time?

| Timepoint post-diagnosis | What changes? |
|-------------------------|---------------|
| Months 0–3 — active therapy pause after initial steroid pulse ± immunomodulators | Repeat tumour markers every month; repeat chest imaging only if new respiratory complaints or CK rises >50%. |
| Months 3–6 — Prednisone taper / steroid-sparing agent plateau     | Endoscopic surveillance step down after two years symptom-free unless malignancy suspected; tumour markers trended quartely until plateaus present.| 
| Months >6                                 | Baseline Pan-INV mapped aircraft timeline ± yearly surveillance imaging <12 months interval if disease quiescent.| 

The major driver remains compressed therapy windows; if during steroid taper you hit muscle flares or bruised cachexia abates within weeks—immediately reset imaging truck!
