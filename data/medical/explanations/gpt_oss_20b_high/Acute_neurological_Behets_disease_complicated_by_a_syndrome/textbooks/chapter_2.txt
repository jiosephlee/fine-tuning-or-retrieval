Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

Chapter 2: ## Case Presentation & Initial Assessment  

A man in his sixties presented to the emergency department after a three‑day history of fever and progressively worsening headache accompanied by persistent hiccups and arthralgia involving his shoulders and ankles. He reported an accompanying loss of appetite since symptom onset. At arrival he was alert but appeared fatigued; no focal neurological deficits were noted during bedside examination.  

Early in the encounter clinicians noted several classic mucocutaneous manifestations characteristic of Behçet’s disease (BD). The patient exhibited multiple painful oral aphthae visible on inspection of the oral cavity; erythematous folliculitis‑like eruptions distributed over his face and trunk were prominent upon careful dermatologic review. A genital ulcer – later visible on examination of the scrotum – further supported a systemic inflammatory process involving mucosal surfaces common to BD.

Vital‑sign monitoring revealed a heart rate of 119 beats per minute against a systolic blood pressure of approximately 135/73 mm Hg, indicating tachycardia without hypotension—a pattern compatible with an euvolemic status rather than hypovolemic shock or hypervolemic states such as heart failure. Physical evaluation showed absent peripheral edema and dry skin patches were absent, reinforcing clinical euvolemia despite mild tachycardia likely driven by febrile stress rather than volume depletion or excess.

Routine laboratory studies immediately highlighted profound hyponatraemia ([Na⁺] = 124 mmol/L) accompanied by a low serum osmolality (267 mOsm/kg), classically denoting a hypo‑osmolar state produced by excess water retention rather than extrarenal fluid loss. Urine–studies were equally telling: an elevated urine osmolality (900 mOsm/kg) combined with a high urinary sodium concentration (88 mmol/L) confirmed inappropriate water reabsorption under euvolemic conditions—a hallmark finding supporting syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). Serum thyroid‑stimulating hormone, free thyroxine and plasma cortisol concentrations were all within normal ranges ([TSH] = 1.11 mIU/L; [free T₄] = 1.25 ng/dL; cortisol = 42 µg/dL), effectively ruling out hypothyroidism or adrenal insufficiency as underlying causes manifesting similarly biochemically.

Further endocrine testing confirmed an elevated plasma antidiuretic hormone level ([21–24] pg/mL) consistent with central overproduction rather than ectopic secretion or assay artifact—a criterion required for finalising an SIADH diagnosis per Consensus Guidelines (avoidances such as hyperglycemia‑induced dilution were accounted for through correction equations).

The convergence of inflammatory mucocutaneous lesions suggestive rapidly evolving BD and objective laboratory evidence confirming central dysregulation of water handling through excessive ADH prompted an expedited diagnostic pathway directed toward acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease complicated by SIADH – conditions whose overlapping clinical profiles demand high index indexing during initial triage.【source】
