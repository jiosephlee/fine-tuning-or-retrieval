Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

Chapter 1: ## Initial Presentation and Sepsis Induced by Capnocytophaga canimorsus

Four days after an upper‑lip dog bite the patient presented with an abruptly worsening picture dominated by fever exceeding 40 °C, mottled cool extremities and severe hypoglycaemia that failed to respond even to glucagon injections. These findings were typical of septic shock precipitated by Gram‑negative bacteremia soon after a foreign animal laceration—a classic setting for *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*. The absence of any other obvious infectious source or pre‑existing immunosuppression further heightened clinical suspicion, even though blood cultures remained negative at the bedside.

Laboratory interrogation immediately underscored the severity of systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS). A complete blood count revealed pancytopenia—white cell count fell below 3 × 10⁹ L⁻¹—and thrombocytopenia consistent with consumptive platelet loss in DIC. Renal function was acutely impaired, with serum creatinine rising toward 170 µmol L⁻¹, while hepatic transaminases were moderately elevated, reflecting multi‑organ dysfunction typical of high‑grade sepsis.  

Coagulation studies were profoundly abnormal: prothrombin time (PT) and activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) exceeded 70 s in most assays; fibrinogen dropped below 80 mg dL⁻¹ while d‑dimer was markedly increased—all hallmarks of sepsis‑induced disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Metabolic profiling showed a pH of 7.27 accompanied by lactate over five millimoles per liter, reflecting anaerobic tissue hypoperfusion commonly seen in septic shock states that fail to achieve adequate microvascular oxygen delivery.

With this constellation—dog bite history plus fever >40 °C, refractory hypoglycaemia, mottled extremities suggestive of peripheral ischemia, pancytopenia with low fibrinogen and high d‑dimer values—the provisional diagnosis was septic shock secondary to a *Capnocytophaga* infection triggered by animal contact. Empiric antimicrobial therapy was therefore initiated with intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam—a broad‑spectrum β‑lactam/β‑lactamase inhibitor combination that covers fastidious Gram‑negative rods including *C.*​canimorsus—while awaiting definitive microbiologic identification through cultures or molecular techniques such as real‐time sequencing. This early empiric coverage aimed both at curbing bacterial proliferation and preventing further endothelial injury that could precipitate irreversible organ failure.

The temporal link between the venomous event (the bite) and rapid systemic deterioration underscores how epidemiologic clues can directly shape immediate clinical judgments—prompting aggressive antimicrobial management even before confirmatory laboratory data emerge in cases presumed attributable to *Capnocytophaga*.
