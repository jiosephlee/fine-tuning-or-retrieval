# From Canine Bite to Complement Crisis  

---

When a woman arrives alive and alert a few hours after being bitten on her upper lip by her own dog you would normally think she needs broad‑spectrum antibiotics and supportive care – that’s pretty standard practice when thinking “dog‑bite sepsis”. Fifteen minutes later she keeps piecing together patchy fevers until her vitals settle enough for admission, her platelets drop into the teens × 10⁹ /L range, she becomes anephric overnight and finally her hemoglobin plunges without any documented bleed while schistocytes appear on a smear taken seven days later. That “late” picture is what turned out not just to be septic shock gone quiet – it was a capnopathologic fulminant thrombotic microangiopathy driven by uncontrolled alternative‑pathway activation downstream of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*. In essence this simple animal bite turned into an orchestrated cascade where immune dysregulation trumped even profound sepsis education most clinicians would anticipate.”

# When Sepsis Feels Like TTP – Distinguishing TMAs in Critical Illness

## Introduction  
A 48‑year‑old woman who had been bitten by her dog four days earlier was brought in as a septic shock story – high fever, hypoglycaemia, mottled extremities and an ominous lab picture of pancytopenia with DIC‐type coagulopathy. “Could this be thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP)?” her ICU team wondered as she developed anaemia and anuria. It turned out to be a *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* septicemia that later unmasked a complement‑mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (CM‑HUS). This case flipped the usual teaching that sepsis *causes* consumptive coagulopathy while TTA “trickles up” from an occult haematologic disorder – illustrating how a bacterial infection can masquerade as a platelet‑microthrombus disease and why we must systematically dissect the laboratory clues instead of jumping straight into plasmapheresis or steroids.

## Sepsis vs. TMA – A Diagnostic Topsy‑Turvy  
Severe infections can produce a host of abnormalities that overlap strikingly with TMAs:

| Feature | Typical Sepsis/DIC | Classic TMA (TTP/CM‑HUS) |
|---------|--------------------|---------------------------|
| Platelets | ↓ → often <50 ×10⁹/L | ↓ — usually dramatically low |
| Coagulation panel | Prolonged PT/aPTT, ↓ fibrinogen, ↑ D‑dimer | Normal PT/aPTT & fibrinogen (unless secondary DIC) |
| LDH/Haptoglobin | Elevated LDH hyperinterpreted as tissue injury | ↑ LDH / ↓ haptoglobin due to intravascular haemolysis |
| Schistocytes | Patchy / low counts unless very severe | Often ≥5–10 % findings |
| Presence of a trigger (infection?) | Common | Common in CM‑HUS but less so in classic TTP |

What you see clinically is often ambiguous – you have a WBC drop, rising lactate, thrombocytopenia, frothy urine all at once. The moment that acts as a litmus test is **ADAMTS13 activity** and **PLASMIC score**.

## ADAMTS13 Activity ‑ The Gold Standard  
In classic acquired TTP an immune complex suppresses von Willebrand factor cleaving protease ADAMTS13 – levels drop below **10 %**. In contrast:

* **CM‑HUS** patients typically show activity **>10 %**, often 40–50 %.  
* However, extreme systemic inflammation can transiently push values toward borderline levels; thus absolute numbers must be interpreted in context.  
* Our patient’s ADAMTS13 came back at **43 %** after being symptomatic for 8 days – charting her safely out of the TTP territory.

In practice this means you should obtain ADAMTS13 early whenever you suspect severe microangiopathy—especially if it will influence whether you start plasma exchange or move straight to complement inhibition.

## PLASMIC Score – A Quick Estimator  
The PLASMIC algorithm assigns points for Platelets ≤30 ×10⁹/L, Hemolysis (elevated LDH/low haptoglobin), Absence of active Cancer or Solid Organ Transplantation (SOT), Negative Autoimmune Lupus Antibodies (SLE/ANCA), MCV <90 fL, INR <1.5 and Creatinine <2 mg/dL.  

Our patient scored **4/7**, indicating low pre‑test probability for severe ADAMTS13 deficiency—helpful evidence when waiting for lab confirmation.

> *Clinical pearl:* Even if you suspect “TMA”, use PLASMIC concurrently with ADAMTS13 because it adds confidence when results lag behind bedside decisions.

## Schistocytes vs Intravascular Coagulation – How to Tell Them Apart  
A smear showing ≥5–10 % schistocytes usually favors microangiopathic RBC fragmentation over platelet consumption seen in DIC‐only injuries—although some patients with large‑scale vascular injury can show fragmented cells too. While schistocyte assessment remains experience‑driven and subject to observer variability:

* Look at morphology & percentage.
* Correlate with coagulation panels—if fibrinogen remains reasonable and PT/aPTT stays within normal limits despite profound cytopenias → think MTA rather than pure sepsis coagulopathy.
* Note that our patient had patchy schistocytosis (15–20 %) upon the acute drop in hemoglobin ― aligning with a superimposed microangiopathy rather than pure consumption coagulopathy.

## Complement Testing – The Missing Piece for CM‑HUS  
Normal C3/C4 levels do **not** exclude complement dysregulation because acute activation can transiently normalize classical pathway components while depleting alternative pathways or fix until sampling happens mid‐therapy or post–plasma exchange. In our case:

* Traditional assays were within reference ranges during initial workup.
* A subsequent AH50-based assay revealed reduced alternative pathway activity (*6 %*) after plasmapheresis but before eculizumab—the likely influence of therapy rather than inherent dysregulation.
* Genetic panel returned negative for pathogenic variants in factor H/I/B/CD46—supporting an *infection‑triggered* event rather than hereditary predisposition.

Hence don’t throw out “normal complements” when suspecting CM‑HUS—especially if patient had undergone multiple plasma exchanges or received complement blockers meanwhile.

## When Is Plasma Exchange Life‑Saving?  
Plasma exchange (**PEX**) removes circulating autoantibodies against ADAMTS13 *and* pathogenic coated complexes driving endothelial injury — making it lifesaving in oncology *rare autoimmune episodes* like classic acquired TTP where the underlying lesion is an auto‐competition targetting the protease itself. In CM‑HUS PEX has historically served more as:

1. **Bridge therapy** pending definitive diagnosis or awaiting initiation of eculizumab (when rapid HMA on admission).  
2. A small transient suppression effect on activated complement fragments — briefly improving platelet counts or renal perfusion without tackling genetic/molecular underpinnings.

In our patient PEX was attempted right after her second day of ICU care because classic signs of sepsis could still coexist with possible covert CM-HUS—but resolution only appeared after the first monoclonal antibody dose, underlining its temporizing role here.

> *Clinical pearl:* Never default to plasmapheresis based solely on provisional “TMA” diagnosis; confirm with ADAMTS13 first unless polygenic emergency demands immediate intervention—as is often done when platelets stabilize quickly following PEX but do not respond definitively thereafter.

## Eculizumab — Treatment With Caution Amidst Infection  
Blocking terminal complement (**C5**) yields dramatic haemolytic control and improved renal outcomes *if started early*. Infectious precaution becomes paramount because Terminal blockade damages defenses against encapsulated organisms (*Neisseria*, *Streptococcus*, *C. canimorsus*):

1. Start after clearance of bloodstream cultures—our patient herself got stable cultures before drug intake.
2. Prior vaccination against meningococcal disease (**Menveo/Bexsero**) signposted into your ED chart.
3. Continuous penicillin prophylaxis during therapy window helps preclude meningococcemia risk.
4. Vigilant monitoring for any febrile episodes thereafter is essential—even mild inflammation may signal active infection unmasked by blockade.

These precautions prevented fatal sequelae while yielding near–renal recovery (~50 %) though she remained dialysis‐dependent at month four—a realistic digitalization moments known from contemporary prospective studies _what if you've waited?_

## Summary & Take‑Home Messages

Before you reach for plasma exchange or steroids during critical septic illness:

✓ **Differentiate quickly:** Check ADAMTS13 early (<24 h) — <10 % → classic TTP → PEX essential; >10 % → consider CM‑HUS/TU involvement → evaluate complements & CT imaging ❌ wait on PEX unless immediate life threat arises  

✓ **Use PLASMIC wisely:** Low score suggests unlikely severe ADAMTS13 deficiency → bolsters diagnostic decision-making  

✓ **Schistocytes vs DIC:** Confirm fragment presence >5–10 % & ensure overall coagulation panel does not suggest full-blown consumptive coagulopathy  

✓ **Complement workup nonzero:** Normal C3/C4 does NOT rule out CM‑HUS especially post–PEX; consider alternate-pathway assays and genetic assays where resources allow  

✓ **Plasma exchange’s role:** Typically a bridge—not curative—for CM‑HUS unless overt fulminant presentation or imminent death warrants it  

✓ **Eculizumab’s timing & safety:** Initiate when cultures negative & provide meningococcal vaccinations + antibiotic prophylaxis; monitor carefully  

✓ **Keep infection triggers on the radar:** Even obscure zoonoses such as *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* can ignite a complement storm ― maintain suspicion in septic patients developing unexplained AKI/Necros!  

By anchoring your approach firmly around these structured laboratory pillars you safeguard against unnecessary PEX while ensuring timely access to life‐saving complement blockade whenever indicated—turning the scary “sepsis = bleeding” narrative into clear evidence‐based pathways for optimal patient outcomes.[END]



# Beyond C₃/C₄ – Interpreting Complement Function During Atypical HUS  

## The picture‑puzzling dog‑bite eruption  

Imagine a healthy woman in her forties who bites her canine companion on the lip, forgets her prescription for penicillin at home, then develops fever higher than 40 °C followed by rapid‑onset hypotension, hypoglycaemia and mottled skin all over both lower limbs—a classic emergency department presentation for septic shock **that suddenly turns into “what’s next?”**  

When she arrived at our ICU she already had pancytopenia, significant liver enzyme elevation, an arterial lactate above five mmol/L and acute kidney injury necessitating continuous renal replacement therapy despite aggressive fluid resuscitation. Blood cultures grew *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* seven days downstream – a notorious Gram‑negative rod living harmlessly in dogs but potentially lethal when it escapes into human bloodstream.  

Her sepsis resolved relatively quickly once intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam covered her zoonotic culprit. But then something else unfurled – her haemoglobin plummeted from 111 g/L to below 70 g/L over six days *without any obvious bleeding*, platelets remained stubbornly low (~30–40 × 10⁹/L), haptoglobin vanished and lactate dehydrogenase climbed beyond seven times baseline. In other words, microangiopathic haemolysis had erupted just as her infection was pulling itself together – a hallmark sign that something more sinister was brewing beneath those seemingly “normal” lab bars.

## Why routine C₃/C₄ panels were deceptive  

You’ve probably come across **sometimes “normal” C₃/C₄ levels** during a hyperactive disease state – especially in atypical haemolytic uraemic syndrome (aHUS), now better called **complement‑mediated HUS** (CM‑HUS). In our patient’s early course these static protein concentrations stayed within reference ranges despite massive endothelial injury visible on renal biopsy (fibrin thrombi lodged inside small vessels). By the time we sent plasma‐derived functional assays two weeks into hybrid therapy (plasma exchanges plus one dose of eculizumab), the alternative‑pathway test (AH₅₀) crashed down to six percent – almost all gone!  

*What explains this paradox?* Two main reasons:

1. **Compartmentalised activation** – In CM‑HUS activation occurs mainly at the vascular endothelium where local amplification stalls before spilling into circulation en masse enough to depress serum C₃ or C₄ concentrations detectable by nephelometry.
2. **Diagnostic timing** – Conventional panels measure protein abundance only—they say nothing about *activity*. Even if local consumption is enormous, circulating supply might remain unchanged long enough for your routine workup at admission to read ‘normal’.

Thus relying solely on “C₃/​C₄ = normal” sends you down the wrong rabbit hole (“probably not aCM–HUS”), especially when other red flags such as schistocytes, thrombocytopenia combined with worsening renal function loom large.

## Functional assays—your new adjunctive toolbox  

Functional evaluation does what static panels cannot:
* **AH₅₀ (Alternative Pathway Hemolytic Activity):** Provides global read‑out of AP initiation/dysregulation by quantifying haemolysis induced by patient plasma on rabbit erythrocytes coated with cobra venom factor analogues. It’s precisely how you monitor therapeutic impact using eculizumab because blocking terminal cascade silent shutdown leaves upstream activity altered yet visible here—and as your case demonstrates typical values fall drastically during PEX + blockade phases unless you anticipate this effect beforehand.
* **Factor Levels:** Quantitative ELISA or nephelometry–measurements of factors **H**, **I**, **B** give clues whether regulatory checkpoints are under-supplied or auto‑antibody–mediatedly abolished—in our patient these were all normal despite extremely low AH₅₀ once therapeutic PEX drained them out.
* **Anti‑Factor H Antibodies:** Optical density cutoffs corresponding with clinically relevant titres rule out immune‑mediated opsonisation failure—our patient tested negative both ways.

Putting them together is how modern guidelines differentiate CM‑HUS from other TMAs such as thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) or Shiga-toxin associated HUS:

| Diagnostic Red Flag | Typical Finding | Our Patient |
|----------------------|-----------------|-------------|
| ADAMTS13 <10 % | Indicates TTP | 43 % → no |
| Persistent schistocytes + ↑LDH + ↓haptoglobin | Suggests TMA | ✔ |
| Stool PCR Shiga toxin | STEC-HU | negative |
| Complement factor B/H/I abnormal OR anti‑factor H antibody positive | Confirms genetic/autoimmune predisposition | All normal |

The bedside takeaway? Whenever you encounter MAHA + thrombocytopenia + AKI *and* suspicion is high through any amalgamative sourceive score such as PLASMIC being low (<5), load up early *functional* APS assessors rather than waiting for classic panel completion alone—which can mislead if taken out of context after any plasma‑based therapy has begun correcting circulation signature parameters abruptly.

# Putting Eculizumab on Hold?  
Navigating Complement Inhibition During Active Bacterial Sepsis  

---

## The Unexpected Twist  

When a previously healthy woman in her late‑40s suffered a dog bite and then collapsed with sepsis, bruised toes, anuric kidneys and a “spontaneous” drop in hemoglobin on day 8, we all thought one thing first: i.e., she had septic shock with DIC.  
What pushed us into the TMA realm was the sudden rise in LDH, disappearance of haptoglobin and a smear full of schistocytes once her inflammatory markers began to abate – classic haemolytic‐microangiopathy when everything else looked better. The diagnostic dilemma was clear: how do you convince yourself that this is not simply “late‐septic” microangiopathy but **complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS)**?  

The answer lay in ADAMTS‑13 activity (>10 %) ruling out TTP, negative Shiga toxin PCR ruling out STEC‑HUS, and biopsy showing classical TMA lesions – all pointing at CM‑HUS triggered by **Capnocytophaga canimorsus** bacteraemia.  

The second twist was therapeutic: starting **eculizumab** loomed large but was also potentially more dangerous than we had feared because **C. canimorsus is an encapsulated organism** that relies on terminal complement for clearance.  

---

## Why Complement Blockade Is a Double‑Edged Sword  

| Issue | What Happens When You Block C5? |
|-------|--------------------------------|
| **Loss of opsonic killing** | C5 cleavage creates C5a (anaphylatoxin) & C5b‑9 (MAC). These structures flag bacteria to phagocytes and directly lyse them. Blocking C5 prevents this – capsules that normally resist phagocytosis are now even more formidable. |
| **Risk of meningococcal/Casper‑like infections** | Classic data show meningococcal outbreaks in patients on anti‑C5 therapy if not vaccinated or prophylaxed. |
| **Uncertain impact on ongoing bacteraemia** | Some studies suggest that while the initial bacteria are already weaken/cleared by antibiotics, any residual organisms *might* proliferate once the complement engine stops firing off its “kill zone”. |
| **Clinical trade‑off** | Untreated CM‑HUS often leads to irreversible AKI or death; delaying eculizumab because you fear an infection could give the kidney more time to die halt |

In short, you want both *efficacy* against complement‐driven endotheliopathy AND *continued bacterial clearance*. That requires precise timing.

---

## Stepwise Decision Tree: When Can We Safe Start Eculizumab?

> **⚠️ This is a practical guide drawn from real patient care (see case report) plus current guidelines/authoritative sources (e.g., KDIGO 2024 HUS updates).**

### Step 1 – Confirm No Active Bacteremia  

1. **Blood culture status**  
   - *Acceptable start*: All cultures cleared **and** antigen/gram‑stain negative for capsulated organisms overnight (usually ≥48 h after first negative).
   - *If still positive*: Continue antibiotics and hold eculizumab until clearance is confirmed.
2. **Alternative diagnostics if cultures lag**  
   - Real‐time PCR panels or metagenomic sequencing for rapidly identifying pathogens (including Capnocytophaga). A positive result demands completion of active therapy before blockade.

### Step 2 – Establish Full Antibiotic Coverage & Follow-Up  

1. Empirica covering plugging capenotophaga initially:  
   - Piperacillin–tazobactam or ceftriaxone + metronidazole (given dog bite risks).
2. De-escalation per culture/sensitivity – 50–72 h after adequate source control usually gives good confidence.
3. Ensure you have *no residual organ impairment* that precludes safe IV therapy (e.g., severe liver failure).

### Step 3 – Vaccinate Against Encapsulated Organisms  

| Vaccine | Target serogroups | Timing |
|---|---|---|
| Menveo® | A, C, Y, W-135 | Ideally given ≥24 h before antimicrobials; if delay unavoidable – give ASAP |
| Bexsero® | B | Same as above; administer both together where feasible |

*Why two?* Cranial meningococci may not be covered by Menveo alone; plus born charges of missing serogroup B when using Menveo+Bexsero give broader safety net when inhibiting C5.

### Step 4 – Provide Prophylactic Penicillin  

- Start oral amoxicillin-clavulanate *or*, better yet,
- Ampicillin 1000–2000 mg IV q6h until cultures negative,
- Then transition to high‑dose oral phenoxymethylpenicillin (“penicillins”) for at least 7–10 days after eculizumab initiation.

This approach mimics meningococcal vaccine strategies historically used before the era of monoclonal antibodies.

### Step 5 – Initiate Eculizumab Once All Prior Conditions Are Met  

1. First dose **900 mg IV weekly ×4 weeks** as induction.
2. Switch to maintenance (“12/14” dosing) based on weight/clinical labs.
3. Monitor complement levels closely — partial inhibitors may bring down AH50 but do not preclude safety.

| Day | Action | Rationale |
|-----|--------|-----------|
| ‑14–0 | Dog bite → tetanus vaccine + withholding antibiotics due to delayed pharmacy reach → abrupt fever & neurologic signs → ICU admission & immediate piperacillin/tazobactam covering Capnocytophaga & general sepsis spectrum.| Broad coverage while awaiting cultures |

This timetable aligns closely with what happened in our patient study—she cleared blood cultures before starting therapy and never developed invasive complications during eculizumab treatment.

---

## Practical Pearls

- **Never stop antibiotics while waiting for culture clearance**, even if CRP has fallen—early histology can resume sepsis in days.
- A single negative culture does *not* guarantee bacterial eradication; repeat and/or add targeted PCR when high suspicion remains.
- Vaccines must be given *before* the first dose of eculizumab whenever possible—neither can you wait two weeks post‑vaccination clinically.
- If you’re outside the standard hospital network and cannot get rapid pathogen sequencing or AH50 testing—think “pacify” : shorten empiric antibiotic course but keep monitoring labs extremely close (daily CBC + LDH/Haptoglobin).
- Consider prophylactic *methylene blue* infusion if you observe breakthrough complement activation despite blockade due to underlying autoimmune triggers—but this is rarely required in bacterial trigger scenarios.
- Do not downplay the importance of supportive renal replacement fluids—Eculizumab does not reverse established ATN instantly; diuretics and dialysis may still prove necessary till endothelial healing occurs.

---

## Take‑Home Messages

✔️ Start eculizumab only after all active bacteremia is eradicated—validated by at least two negative blood culture sets two days apart or confirmed PCR negativity.

✔️ Simultaneously vaccinate against meningococci (Menveo® + Bexsero®) preceding complement inhibition where possible.

✔️ Mandate prophylactic high-dose penicillin during eculizumab initiation phase; keep it >=7 days post-first dose ideally overlapping with complete switch from IV antibiotics to oral regimens.

✔️ Closely track daily labs: CBC/platelets, LDH/haptoglobin trend, renal function tests—detailed trending often tells us how soon we can consider reducing PEX sessions vs committing fully to eculizumab maintenance.

✔️ Ultimately, rapid but judicious patient stratification saves time ― dilemmas between delaying treatment and risking unmasked infection should tip toward ensuring bacterium neutrality first without compromising redirecting endothelial microbial sabotage capitally timely.

In our canine bite protagonist’s story, microangiopathy’s double trouble presented faster than conventional sepsis management alone could salvage kidney function—a timely lesson that adding a guard (eculizumab) only after you’ve removed the invader’s foothold yields best outcomes without pushing patients into new infection nightmares.

# From DIC to MAHA – A Dynamic Clinical Trajectory in Sepsis‑Triggered TMA  

---

## TL;DR  
A woman who slipped into **septic shock** after a dog bite developed **DIC** and AKI that looked like overt sepsis—but when her **anemia dropped sharply** and **platelets never fully recovered**, she hit an unsuspected climax: **CM‑HUS** triggered by *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*.  
Key take‑away:  
* Keep hunting for evolving hemolysis **even when septic markers improve**  
* Daily CBC smear + haptoglobin checks turn out cheaper than many fancy algorithms  
* When doubt arises, prepare plasma exchange quickly while awaiting definitive diagnostics (*ADAMTS13*, kidney biopsy)  
* Initiate eculizumab **after infections are cleared** yet before irreversible renal damage ensues  

---

## What Makes This Case Surprising  
Most people think “septic shock → DIC → death” is a linear decline. In real life, patients often pick up a *second act* while their original illness improves—microangiopathic hemolysis that unfolds almost unnoticed because everyone is busy chasing lactate levels and organ perfusion pressures.  

The headline twist here is that our patient’s earliest video‐talk pitched her into massive multisystem failure driven by *Capnocytophaga*, yet almost three weeks into care she developed **isolated cytopenias** against the backdrop of improving inflammatory markers—but still rampant platelets exhaustion—and it turns out she's battling *complement‑mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome*.  

That adds two hoops reluctantly required by every intensivist & nephrologist practising today:
1️⃣ Recognize late‑onset TMAs after apparent bacterial recovery  
2️⃣ Treat productively without stepping on a lethal rabbit hole (severe infections plus terminal‐complement inhibition)

## Why KM4 Sepsis Masks Complement‑Driven Events  

In any severe infection you see:
* Disrupted endothelium  
* Cytokine surge (“smoky knives”) enhancing clotting cascade  
* Elevated fibrinolytic products  

During *acute* stabilization you’ll notice SHARP declines in PT/PTT/APTT followed by normalization—but microscopic “helmet cells” hidden behind routine red cell counts often remain silent until you look hard enough. Consequently remaining thrombocytopenia feels “expected” while an occult Congo‐red lung membrane emerges later.

### Monitoring Checkpoints That Saved Her Renal Function
In any ICU setting where bacterial cultures are pending longer than expected—or cases fueled by fastidious organisms upper airway flora are suspected—you should consider implementing these cheap "rules":

1️⃣ **Daily CBC + Morphologic Review**  
   * Even one minute an eye scan can detect ≥5 % schistocytes—pocket meta‑analysis suggests >=5% strongly predictive of ongoing MAC ↔ CM‑HUS.*  
2️⃣ **Every Other Day Haptoglobin Measurement** (*if nightly labs allow*)***  
   * Drop ⩽30% IV baseline signals intravascular hemolysis (*fast shift <24h*) which precedes ↓Hb by several days.*   
3️⃣ **Serial LDH/Bilirubin Profiles Around Day 6–8 After Stabilization**  
   * These quantify ongoing endothelial breakdown.*  

When your prohepatitic picture clears while nicking red cells remains continuous—that's your “red flag.” Get PLASMIC score instantly if you have digital algorithm ready—and work towards ADAMTS13 early!

## Differential Diagnosis Roadmap When You See Schistocytes Later On  

| Potential Etiology | First Lines Of Testing / Key Features |
|--------------------|--------------------------------------|
| **Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP)** | Low ADAMTS13 (<10%) + <10 hr rise LDH + high platelet consumption ‑ did quickly yes/no test integrate into ED workflow |
| **Shiga toxin–produced HUS** | Recent diarrheal prodrome + stool PCR negative unequivocal withdrawal footnote |
| **Complement‐Mediated HUS** (CM-HUS)/Atypical HUS | Normal or low-normal C3/C4 ok ➜ re-evaluate functional alternative pathway (AH50); normal factor B/H/I skip BCAP inhibitors ✅|
| **Drug–induced Antiphospholipid syndrome** or other drug TMAs ✔ Test ANA/ENA panels╫ comparably reassess promptly |

The point isn’t you get all tests perfect —the diagnostic journey pragmatically rules them out stepwise hits towards CM-HUS after negative results confirm high reliability defaults.
