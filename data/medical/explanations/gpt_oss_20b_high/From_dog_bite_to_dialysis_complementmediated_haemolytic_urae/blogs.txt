# When Pets Bite Back  
Capnocytophaga canimorsus as an Unusual Trigger for Complement‑Mediated HUS  

---

## A Fast‑Track Look‑back

The first time I saw myself staring down a decent‑looking dog’s molar fangs was during my own training week of emergency medicine when a colleague’s mutt chose me as “target practice.” A few small bruises later I expected mild cellulitis—but my own practice review revealed something scarier hiding behind that superficial injury: an infection capable of forging sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), and ultimately a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).  
The clinical intrigue here is twofold—capnocyte myths are more prosaic than most clinicians imagine—and practice parity around treating “dog‑bite” infections is surprisingly patchy even among seasoned ED physicians.



---

## The Patient’s Odyssey – In One Paragraph  

An otherwise healthy woman in her late forties was hospitalized four days after an upper‑lip bite from her pet dog. She presented with high fever (>40 °C), profound hypoglycaemia refractory to glucose‐glucagon infusions, rapidly dropping hematocrits and oliguria accompanied by proteinuria (2+), liver enzymes rising in tandem with a cotton‑wet metabolic acidosis (pH 7.27) and lactate 5·7 mmol/L." In short—a picture fitting “septic shock with presumptive DIC”. Broad‑spectrum Piperacillin/Tazobactam was initiated promptly while standard workup unfurled—blood cultures needing seven days to grow *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*.  
After ICU transfer her kidneys failed abruptly (“complete anuria”) despite aggressive diuresis attempts and she was dialyzed continuously while awaiting infectious clearance.



---

### Why *C.* canimorsus Carries Magnetism Behind Brand Names

* **Fastidious growth** – Classical media require over a week for colonies → delaying diagnosis by several critical hours;
* **Gram‑negative rod** – Often overlooked when “dog‐bite” infections are catalogued under Pasteurella/Pseudomonas/*Eikenella*;
* **Encapsulated capsule** – Enables evasion of early phagocytic clearance yet renders some isolates partially resistant to complement lysis;
* **Catapult effect** – One organism cohort causing mortality rates nearing 30 %, often misdirected through stubborn neutropenia and coagulopathy resembling pure septic shock.

# Beyond Sepsis‑Induced DIC: Recognizing the Silent Onset of Microangiopathic Haemolysis  

---

## 1 – Why this case still feels like a “learning moment”

A woman in her 40s presented three days after a dog bite with septic shock so fulminant that she hit hypoglycaemia, leukopaenia and classic markers of disseminated intravascular coagulation (low fibrinogen, prolonged PT/INR). The diagnosis was clear enough—Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteraemia—so you’d think you’re ready to “call it quits” on further work‑up once cultures turned back negative and her vital signs stabilized.  

What made this case unforgettable was what happened *after* she finally began to recover from shock: her haemoglobin plunged precipitously without any overt bleeding source, reticulocyte production did not catch up, and blood smears suddenly filled with schistocytes. With platelets still low despite clotting parameters improving, it became evident that a second process—complement‑mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (CM‑HUS)—had sneaked in during her post‑septic phase unsuspected by most clinicians.

## 3 – How to keep an eye on “hidden” microangiopathy while ships are afloat  

### A) Persistent thrombocytopenia is your first red‑flag  
When platelets stay below 50 ×10⁹/L despite resolving coagulopathy or even when fibrinogen levels have normalized, think beyond pure DIC—especially if other organ functions begin to hurt again.  

### B) Watch for serial hemolysis indicators  
A sharp drop in haemoglobin **without** external bleeding is ominous, especially if LDH rises dramatically while haptoglobin falls—in this patient it jumped from ~90 U/L at baseline to >700U/L shortly after improvement started.

### C) Don’t rely on DAT alone  
Penicillins can spike IgG positivity on Coombs testing—initially it looks like warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA), but later tests were negative and DSM was established instead.

### D) Check schistocyte load quickly  
Even small (≈15–20%) elevations on blood film are clinically meaningful—a simple smear review can tip you into suspecting an evolving TMA instead of just ongoing sepsis‑driven anemia.

### E) Quick ADAMTS13 activity & kidney biopsy give road‑maps  
A value >10% leans away from TTP; pathological confirmation via biopsy further clarifies mechanism (renal ischemia & thrombi vs prerenal AKI).

### F) Complement activity doesn’t always light up early  
Normal C3/C4 cannot rule out CM‑HUS—functional assays may need serial testing or may be suppressed by active PEX or eculizumab itself.

> **Key practice:** Daily platelet counts → ocular eye over them plus a daily trending picture helps catch “late halo” microangiopathic changes before organ damage spirals out of control.

---

## 4 – Therapeutic balancing act – what we learned  

1️⃣ Eculizumab is the game‑changer for CM‑HUS but carries its own infection risk because it blocks terminal complement needed to kill encapsulated organisms like C. canimorsus or Neisseria meningitidis.  

2️⃣ Start eculizumab only after blood cultures become negative AND when there’s compelling evidence that another disease process (TMA) is driving ongoing organ failure—not merely surviving sepsis alone.  

3️⃣ Complement inhibition can coax bowels back into motion even when plasma exchange appears transiently effective—your kidneys can recover remarkably once terminal complement is shut down permanently at least temporarily.  

4️⃣ Must vaccinate *and* give prophylactic penicillin before starting complement blockade if you’re still dealing with an active pathogen otherwise you’ll risk fulminant meningococcal disease *in patients who’re already critically ill.*  

5️⃣ If no pathogenic complement mutation shows up—and still you had severe clinical disease during infection—you’re still justified continuing maintenance therapy until renal function holds steady or fully recovers because that’s what improves outcomes across IGD guidelines.



---

## 5 – Clinical pearls & Take‑home messages  

- Persistent thrombocytopenia ≥7 days into septic recovery warrants evaluation for underlying microangiopathy.
- A sudden drop in haemoglobin accompanied by rising LDH and falling haptoglobin should prompt looking at blood smears for schistocytes *before turning to “bleeding” diagnoses.*
- A single positive DAT in patients treated with β‑lactams can mislead – repeat testing often clarifies.
- Normal ADAMTS13 activity (>10%) rules out typical TTP quickly – redirect focus to complement dysregulation.
- Normal C3/C4 does not exclude CM‑HUS; obtain functional assays if possible.
- Early initiation of eculizumab (<24 h after TMA recognition, once infection cleared) has the strongest association with renal recovery.
- Complement blockade must be paired with meningococcal vaccination *and* appropriate antibiotic prophylaxis until you can stop therapy safely.



---

### Bottom line  
Septic shock will always wear its own “coat” of low fibrinogen and thrombocytopenia—but just because those abnormalities persist doesn’t mean they’re all you’ve got going on! Keep those plates high on your radar sheet even past day five of sepsis felt better—to catch our patient’s silent switch into CM‑HUS before kidney function takes a permanent nosedive.

# Fast‑Track TMA Work‑Up – The “ADAMTS13 Sauce Tip”  
*Fastest route from ICU admission to certainty between **TTP** and **CM‑HUS***

## What makes this case remarkable  

A healthy woman in her late forties presents four days post dog bite with septic shock, pancytopenia and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Blood cultures grow *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* after a week’s incubation—a slow‐growing bacterium that rarely makes headlines outside infectious disease circles. Her picture improves once antibiotics are switched to ampicillin/penicillin G against *C.* canimorsus, yet six days later she suddenly loses half her hemoglobin without overt bleeding while her platelets rebound only modestly to ~35 × 10⁹/L. A peripheral smear lately demonstrates schistocytes (>15%), haptoglobin falls below detection limits, LDH rises sharply—all hallmarks of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA).  

The differential narrows toward thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), but two entities sit directly opposite each other on treatment choice spectrum :  
* thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), where life-saving plasma exchange (PEX) is mandatory regardless — wait “not‐tests” lag months —  
* complement‐mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM‑HUS), where prompt termination of complement‐overdrive via eculizumab dramatically improves renal recovery.



When an ICU team faces such a tableau—and it almost occurs every night—the *PLASMIC* score plus an instant *ADAMTS13* assay change everything.”

## Rapid triage logic in practice  

### Step 0 – Gather bedside data NOW  
On arrival me obtain buffy‐coat numbers **platelet count**, **Hb/Hct**, **severely elevated LDH (>2×ULN)**, **haptoglobin** (>10 mg/dL → “rule out” strong hemolysis), **reticulocyte count**, **direct antiglobulin test**, **’causes’​/T‐index** (**INR<1½**), all *within four hours*. ### Step 1 – Compute the **PLAT**Smodified MICRObiopathologic Index (**PLASMICS**)

| Variable | Why it matters |
|----------|----------------|
| Platelets <30 ×10⁹/L | Severe consumption signals “classic” endothelial injury |
| Schistocytes ≥1 % / LDH >2×ULN | Objective proof that red cells are being fragmented |
| No evidence of prior AIDS / malignancy / transplantation | These conditions raise background risk faints |
| Serum creatinine ≤2 mg/dL | Acute kidney injury alone does not mandate PEX |
| Normal INR (<1․½) | Point mutation denoting minimal coagulation cascade hyperactivation |
| No ongoing heparin exposure | Heparin‐induced thrombocytopenia would confound counts |
| Age under /*≤65 yrs*/ | Younger cohort shows higher overall predilection |

Score ranges from **0–7** :

* **6–7** → very high pretest probability → *start PEX immediately* even before lab confirmation  
* **3–5** → medium certainty → *send rapid ADAMTS13 assay AND PEX as bridge service unless stability allows delayed*.  
* **≤2** → very low → *monitor closely*, get confirmatory studies ASAP before committing resources toward PEX.\  

> In our dog‑bite patient the calculated score was precisely **4/7**, putting her right smack ‘midway’. The AMB records spelled modest platelet despondency yet did *not* tick the “platelet<30×10⁹/L” bin outright—our clinical gut prompted us either way while awaiting real numbers.\
