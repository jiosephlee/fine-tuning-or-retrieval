Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# When Sepsis Feels Like TTP – Distinguishing TMAs in Critical Illness

## Introduction  
A 48‑year‑old woman who had been bitten by her dog four days earlier was brought in as a septic shock story – high fever, hypoglycaemia, mottled extremities and an ominous lab picture of pancytopenia with DIC‐type coagulopathy. “Could this be thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP)?” her ICU team wondered as she developed anaemia and anuria. It turned out to be a *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* septicemia that later unmasked a complement‑mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (CM‑HUS). This case flipped the usual teaching that sepsis *causes* consumptive coagulopathy while TTA “trickles up” from an occult haematologic disorder – illustrating how a bacterial infection can masquerade as a platelet‑microthrombus disease and why we must systematically dissect the laboratory clues instead of jumping straight into plasmapheresis or steroids.

## Sepsis vs. TMA – A Diagnostic Topsy‑Turvy  
Severe infections can produce a host of abnormalities that overlap strikingly with TMAs:

| Feature | Typical Sepsis/DIC | Classic TMA (TTP/CM‑HUS) |
|---------|--------------------|---------------------------|
| Platelets | ↓ → often <50 ×10⁹/L | ↓ — usually dramatically low |
| Coagulation panel | Prolonged PT/aPTT, ↓ fibrinogen, ↑ D‑dimer | Normal PT/aPTT & fibrinogen (unless secondary DIC) |
| LDH/Haptoglobin | Elevated LDH hyperinterpreted as tissue injury | ↑ LDH / ↓ haptoglobin due to intravascular haemolysis |
| Schistocytes | Patchy / low counts unless very severe | Often ≥5–10 % findings |
| Presence of a trigger (infection?) | Common | Common in CM‑HUS but less so in classic TTP |

What you see clinically is often ambiguous – you have a WBC drop, rising lactate, thrombocytopenia, frothy urine all at once. The moment that acts as a litmus test is **ADAMTS13 activity** and **PLASMIC score**.

## ADAMTS13 Activity ‑ The Gold Standard  
In classic acquired TTP an immune complex suppresses von Willebrand factor cleaving protease ADAMTS13 – levels drop below **10 %**. In contrast:

* **CM‑HUS** patients typically show activity **>10 %**, often 40–50 %.  
* However, extreme systemic inflammation can transiently push values toward borderline levels; thus absolute numbers must be interpreted in context.  
* Our patient’s ADAMTS13 came back at **43 %** after being symptomatic for 8 days – charting her safely out of the TTP territory.

In practice this means you should obtain ADAMTS13 early whenever you suspect severe microangiopathy—especially if it will influence whether you start plasma exchange or move straight to complement inhibition.

## PLASMIC Score – A Quick Estimator  
The PLASMIC algorithm assigns points for Platelets ≤30 ×10⁹/L, Hemolysis (elevated LDH/low haptoglobin), Absence of active Cancer or Solid Organ Transplantation (SOT), Negative Autoimmune Lupus Antibodies (SLE/ANCA), MCV <90 fL, INR <1.5 and Creatinine <2 mg/dL.  

Our patient scored **4/7**, indicating low pre‑test probability for severe ADAMTS13 deficiency—helpful evidence when waiting for lab confirmation.

> *Clinical pearl:* Even if you suspect “TMA”, use PLASMIC concurrently with ADAMTS13 because it adds confidence when results lag behind bedside decisions.

## Schistocytes vs Intravascular Coagulation – How to Tell Them Apart  
A smear showing ≥5–10 % schistocytes usually favors microangiopathic RBC fragmentation over platelet consumption seen in DIC‐only injuries—although some patients with large‑scale vascular injury can show fragmented cells too. While schistocyte assessment remains experience‑driven and subject to observer variability:

* Look at morphology & percentage.
* Correlate with coagulation panels—if fibrinogen remains reasonable and PT/aPTT stays within normal limits despite profound cytopenias → think MTA rather than pure sepsis coagulopathy.
* Note that our patient had patchy schistocytosis (15–20 %) upon the acute drop in hemoglobin ― aligning with a superimposed microangiopathy rather than pure consumption coagulopathy.

## Complement Testing – The Missing Piece for CM‑HUS  
Normal C3/C4 levels do **not** exclude complement dysregulation because acute activation can transiently normalize classical pathway components while depleting alternative pathways or fix until sampling happens mid‐therapy or post–plasma exchange. In our case:

* Traditional assays were within reference ranges during initial workup.
* A subsequent AH50-based assay revealed reduced alternative pathway activity (*6 %*) after plasmapheresis but before eculizumab—the likely influence of therapy rather than inherent dysregulation.
* Genetic panel returned negative for pathogenic variants in factor H/I/B/CD46—supporting an *infection‑triggered* event rather than hereditary predisposition.

Hence don’t throw out “normal complements” when suspecting CM‑HUS—especially if patient had undergone multiple plasma exchanges or received complement blockers meanwhile.

## When Is Plasma Exchange Life‑Saving?  
Plasma exchange (**PEX**) removes circulating autoantibodies against ADAMTS13 *and* pathogenic coated complexes driving endothelial injury — making it lifesaving in oncology *rare autoimmune episodes* like classic acquired TTP where the underlying lesion is an auto‐competition targetting the protease itself. In CM‑HUS PEX has historically served more as:

1. **Bridge therapy** pending definitive diagnosis or awaiting initiation of eculizumab (when rapid HMA on admission).  
2. A small transient suppression effect on activated complement fragments — briefly improving platelet counts or renal perfusion without tackling genetic/molecular underpinnings.

In our patient PEX was attempted right after her second day of ICU care because classic signs of sepsis could still coexist with possible covert CM-HUS—but resolution only appeared after the first monoclonal antibody dose, underlining its temporizing role here.

> *Clinical pearl:* Never default to plasmapheresis based solely on provisional “TMA” diagnosis; confirm with ADAMTS13 first unless polygenic emergency demands immediate intervention—as is often done when platelets stabilize quickly following PEX but do not respond definitively thereafter.

## Eculizumab — Treatment With Caution Amidst Infection  
Blocking terminal complement (**C5**) yields dramatic haemolytic control and improved renal outcomes *if started early*. Infectious precaution becomes paramount because Terminal blockade damages defenses against encapsulated organisms (*Neisseria*, *Streptococcus*, *C. canimorsus*):

1. Start after clearance of bloodstream cultures—our patient herself got stable cultures before drug intake.
2. Prior vaccination against meningococcal disease (**Menveo/Bexsero**) signposted into your ED chart.
3. Continuous penicillin prophylaxis during therapy window helps preclude meningococcemia risk.
4. Vigilant monitoring for any febrile episodes thereafter is essential—even mild inflammation may signal active infection unmasked by blockade.

These precautions prevented fatal sequelae while yielding near–renal recovery (~50 %) though she remained dialysis‐dependent at month four—a realistic digitalization moments known from contemporary prospective studies _what if you've waited?_

## Summary & Take‑Home Messages

Before you reach for plasma exchange or steroids during critical septic illness:

✓ **Differentiate quickly:** Check ADAMTS13 early (<24 h) — <10 % → classic TTP → PEX essential; >10 % → consider CM‑HUS/TU involvement → evaluate complements & CT imaging ❌ wait on PEX unless immediate life threat arises  

✓ **Use PLASMIC wisely:** Low score suggests unlikely severe ADAMTS13 deficiency → bolsters diagnostic decision-making  

✓ **Schistocytes vs DIC:** Confirm fragment presence >5–10 % & ensure overall coagulation panel does not suggest full-blown consumptive coagulopathy  

✓ **Complement workup nonzero:** Normal C3/C4 does NOT rule out CM‑HUS especially post–PEX; consider alternate-pathway assays and genetic assays where resources allow  

✓ **Plasma exchange’s role:** Typically a bridge—not curative—for CM‑HUS unless overt fulminant presentation or imminent death warrants it  

✓ **Eculizumab’s timing & safety:** Initiate when cultures negative & provide meningococcal vaccinations + antibiotic prophylaxis; monitor carefully  

✓ **Keep infection triggers on the radar:** Even obscure zoonoses such as *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* can ignite a complement storm ― maintain suspicion in septic patients developing unexplained AKI/Necros!  

By anchoring your approach firmly around these structured laboratory pillars you safeguard against unnecessary PEX while ensuring timely access to life‐saving complement blockade whenever indicated—turning the scary “sepsis = bleeding” narrative into clear evidence‐based pathways for optimal patient outcomes.[END]
