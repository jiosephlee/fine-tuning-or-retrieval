Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# Beyond C₃/C₄ – Interpreting Complement Function During Atypical HUS  

## The picture‑puzzling dog‑bite eruption  

Imagine a healthy woman in her forties who bites her canine companion on the lip, forgets her prescription for penicillin at home, then develops fever higher than 40 °C followed by rapid‑onset hypotension, hypoglycaemia and mottled skin all over both lower limbs—a classic emergency department presentation for septic shock **that suddenly turns into “what’s next?”**  

When she arrived at our ICU she already had pancytopenia, significant liver enzyme elevation, an arterial lactate above five mmol/L and acute kidney injury necessitating continuous renal replacement therapy despite aggressive fluid resuscitation. Blood cultures grew *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* seven days downstream – a notorious Gram‑negative rod living harmlessly in dogs but potentially lethal when it escapes into human bloodstream.  

Her sepsis resolved relatively quickly once intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam covered her zoonotic culprit. But then something else unfurled – her haemoglobin plummeted from 111 g/L to below 70 g/L over six days *without any obvious bleeding*, platelets remained stubbornly low (~30–40 × 10⁹/L), haptoglobin vanished and lactate dehydrogenase climbed beyond seven times baseline. In other words, microangiopathic haemolysis had erupted just as her infection was pulling itself together – a hallmark sign that something more sinister was brewing beneath those seemingly “normal” lab bars.

## Why routine C₃/C₄ panels were deceptive  

You’ve probably come across **sometimes “normal” C₃/C₄ levels** during a hyperactive disease state – especially in atypical haemolytic uraemic syndrome (aHUS), now better called **complement‑mediated HUS** (CM‑HUS). In our patient’s early course these static protein concentrations stayed within reference ranges despite massive endothelial injury visible on renal biopsy (fibrin thrombi lodged inside small vessels). By the time we sent plasma‐derived functional assays two weeks into hybrid therapy (plasma exchanges plus one dose of eculizumab), the alternative‑pathway test (AH₅₀) crashed down to six percent – almost all gone!  

*What explains this paradox?* Two main reasons:

1. **Compartmentalised activation** – In CM‑HUS activation occurs mainly at the vascular endothelium where local amplification stalls before spilling into circulation en masse enough to depress serum C₃ or C₄ concentrations detectable by nephelometry.
2. **Diagnostic timing** – Conventional panels measure protein abundance only—they say nothing about *activity*. Even if local consumption is enormous, circulating supply might remain unchanged long enough for your routine workup at admission to read ‘normal’.

Thus relying solely on “C₃/​C₄ = normal” sends you down the wrong rabbit hole (“probably not aCM–HUS”), especially when other red flags such as schistocytes, thrombocytopenia combined with worsening renal function loom large.

## Functional assays—your new adjunctive toolbox  

Functional evaluation does what static panels cannot:
* **AH₅₀ (Alternative Pathway Hemolytic Activity):** Provides global read‑out of AP initiation/dysregulation by quantifying haemolysis induced by patient plasma on rabbit erythrocytes coated with cobra venom factor analogues. It’s precisely how you monitor therapeutic impact using eculizumab because blocking terminal cascade silent shutdown leaves upstream activity altered yet visible here—and as your case demonstrates typical values fall drastically during PEX + blockade phases unless you anticipate this effect beforehand.
* **Factor Levels:** Quantitative ELISA or nephelometry–measurements of factors **H**, **I**, **B** give clues whether regulatory checkpoints are under-supplied or auto‑antibody–mediatedly abolished—in our patient these were all normal despite extremely low AH₅₀ once therapeutic PEX drained them out.
* **Anti‑Factor H Antibodies:** Optical density cutoffs corresponding with clinically relevant titres rule out immune‑mediated opsonisation failure—our patient tested negative both ways.

Putting them together is how modern guidelines differentiate CM‑HUS from other TMAs such as thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) or Shiga-toxin associated HUS:

| Diagnostic Red Flag | Typical Finding | Our Patient |
|----------------------|-----------------|-------------|
| ADAMTS13 <10 % | Indicates TTP | 43 % → no |
| Persistent schistocytes + ↑LDH + ↓haptoglobin | Suggests TMA | ✔ |
| Stool PCR Shiga toxin | STEC-HU | negative |
| Complement factor B/H/I abnormal OR anti‑factor H antibody positive | Confirms genetic/autoimmune predisposition | All normal |

The bedside takeaway? Whenever you encounter MAHA + thrombocytopenia + AKI *and* suspicion is high through any amalgamative sourceive score such as PLASMIC being low (<5), load up early *functional* APS assessors rather than waiting for classic panel completion alone—which can mislead if taken out of context after any plasma‑based therapy has begun correcting circulation signature parameters abruptly.
