Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### ["Clinical Pearls"] "What clinical change observed during her recovery phase suggests progression toward complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy rather than ongoing disseminated intravascular coagulation?

**Key clinical clue:**  
While her sepsis‑related coagulopathy was improving (PT/aPTT, fibrinogen, platelet trend normalizing), she developed a **sudden isolated fall in haemoglobin (111 → 68 g/L over ~6 days) with no evidence of bleeding**. Platelets remained persistently low (30–40 ×10⁹/L), haptoglobin became undetectable, LDH rose sharply and a peripheral smear showed **15–20 % schistocytes**—classic signs of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA).

**Why this favours CM‑HUS over resolving DIC**  
- **DIC** is driven by consumptive clotting factor loss; once the trigger abates the coagulation profile improves and the anaemia stabilizes or improves.  
- **Complement‑mediated HUS** produces endothelial injury that generates platelet‑rich microthrombi without consuming coagulation factors, leaving PT/aPTT largely unchanged once the initial sepsis subsides. The emergent MAHA—new drop in Hb despite stable or improving coagulation parameters and absence of bleeding—points to ongoing endothelial damage driven by uncontrolled alternative‑complement activation rather than residual consumptive coagulopathy.
- Additionally, the presence of schistocytes (>10 %) together with persistent thrombocytopenia is virtually absent in resolving DIC but characteristic of a thrombotic microangiopathy such as CM‑HUS triggered by the recent *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* infection.

### ["Differential Diagnosis"] "How does an isolated drop in hemoglobin coupled with persistent thrombocytopenia help differentiate Capnocytophaga-induced atypical HUS from Shiga-toxin-associated HUS?

**Key teaching point:**  
In a patient who has recently recovered from severe sepsis/ DIC and then develops a *sudden* fall in haemoglobin while both platelet counts remain low (“persistent thrombocytopenia”) this laboratory picture should make you suspect **infection‑triggered complement‑mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (CM‑HUS)** rather than **Shiga‑toxin–associated HUS (STEC‑HUS).**  

**Why this pattern favors CM‑HUS**

| Feature | CAPNOCYTOPHAGA‑Induced CM‑HUS | STEC‑Associated HUS |
|---------|---------------------------------|----------------------|
| **Antecedent illness** | Bacterial sepsis (dog bite → Capnocytophaga canimorsus) | Gastroenteritis → bloody diarrhoea 48–72 h before onset |
| **Timing of anaemia** | Often *delayed*, appears once systemic inflammation subsides (as seen 6 days after improvement) | Usually present at initial TMA presentation |
| **Platelet behaviour** | Persistent consumption → platelets stay markedly depressed despite blood transfusion | Platelet count falls rapidly at onset but may recover sooner once haemostasis improves |
| **Evidence of ongoing haematological injury** | Isolated haemoglobin drop + rising LDH + undetectable haptoglobin + schistocytes only after septic phase clears → supports *new* intravascular haemolysis driven by endothelial damage | Anaemia co‑occurs immediately with diarrhoea/diarrhoeal prodrome |
| **Diagnostic work‑up needed** | Negative stool PCR for Shiga toxin ❌; normal ADAMTS13 activity (>10 %); normal ANA/ANCA → rule out TTP/diseases unrelated to shiga toxin | Positive Shiga toxin PCR or FITC‑ELISA definitive |
| **Clinical implications** | Prompt recognition drives early eculizumab/high‑dose steroids + plasma exchange ± antibiotics → renal recovery | Avoid unnecessary complement blockade; manage supportive care unless secondary infection arises |

**Case evidence**

* The patient improved clinically from septic shock and DIC but then suffered a rapid haemoglobin fall (111 → 68 g/L over 6 days) without evidence of bleeding—a hallmark sign that new intravascular haemolysis had begun.
* Platelet count remained persistently low (30–40 ×10⁹/L), indicating continuous platelet consumption typical of a microangiopathic process driven by endothelial injury.
* Direct antiglobulin test turned negative on repeat testing, arguing against autoimmune haemolytic anaemia.
* Peripheral smear showed 15–20 % schistocytes only after this haemolytic episode began.
* ADAMTS13 activity was 43 % → not consistent with TTP.
* Stool PCR for Shiga toxin was negative → Rules out STEC‐HUS.
* Kidney biopsy revealed ischemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi—all consistent with TMA caused by uncontrolled alternative pathway activation seen in CM‑HUS.

Thus, **the combination of a late-onset isolated anaemia following resolution of septic inflammation plus ongoing thrombocytopenia—in the absence of diarrhoeal prodrome or shiga toxin positivity—points toward Capnocytophaga‐triggered complement‑mediated HUS over Shiga‐toxin–associated HUS.** Recognizing this pattern allows initiation of appropriate therapy (plasma exchange followed by eculizumab) at a critical point where renal recovery is still feasible.

### ["Pathophysiology"] "Describe how loss‑of‑function variants or autoantibodies against factor H amplify the alternative‑pathway cascade during bacterial sepsis so that endothelial cells sustain MAHA.

**Alternative‑pathway dysregulation in septic CM‑HUS**

Capnocytophaga canimorua initiates robust alternative‑pathway (AP) activation through its outer‑membrane phosphatidylinositol‑containing lipopolysaccharide and capsule polysaccharides that bind surface‑coated C3b. Under normal circumstances — in a healthy host with intact factor H (FAH)-regulation — this initiation is quickly halted:

| Step | Normal control | Effect if FAH is lost or neutralised |
|------|---------------|---------------------------------------|
| **C3bBb assembly** | Formed only transiently on surfaces ‑ requires factor D | Persistent conversion enzyme on pathogen surface |
| **Decay acceleration** | FAH recruits Bb dissociation within seconds | Convertase persists >30 min – unlimited cycles |
| **Cofactor for factor I** | FAH coats C3b making it a substrate for cleavage into iC3b | iC3b production stalls – unchecked C3 activation |
| **Resulting AP output** | Low–level opsonisation + few anaphylatoxins | Excessive generation of C5a and necessarily MAC |

In a septic host exposed to a strong activator (e.g., Capnocytophaga), an already high platelet‑activating stimulus is compounded by an unrestrained AP loop when FAH activity is absent or occluded by anti‑FAH autoantibodies:

1. **Amplification** – without FAH decay acceleration each new Bb added turns another C3b into additional proconvertases; the loop multiplies because each cycle leaves an additional copy of the proconvertase.
2. **Terminal cascade** – unchecked accumulation produces copious amounts of C5a (systemic inflammation driver) and strongly promotes Assembly of *C5b–9* on exposed endothelial surfaces—a potent local cytolytic stimulus.
3. **Endothelial injury** – MAC damages peritubular glomerular endothelial cells, exposing subendothelial collagen and von Willebrand factor, triggering platelet adhesion & aggregation · simultaneous release of tissue‑factor-rich microparticles promoting coagulation hyper‑activity.
4. **Microthrombi** – platelets aggregate in capillaries rich in damaged endothelium creating dense fibrinoid thrombi that shear passing erythrocytes → schistocytes ∧ intravascular haemolysis (MAHA).  
   The same process depletes platelets (“consumption”) → thrombocytopenia.
5. **Non‑immune haemolysis** – Lactate dehydrogenase rises; haptoglobin falls; DAT may give transient false positives (e.g., penicillin exposure). These laboratory clues mirror those seen in the reported patient after her clinical recovery from sepsis.

In this case report, despite small late coagulation laboratory values within reference ranges (C3/C4 normal), functional tests revealed markedly reduced AH50 activity after PEX/ecuizumab (suggesting loss‐of‐function phenotype). Transient EGCI therapy targeting terminal complement restored normal ocular functions within days—highlighting that *unopposed* AP activation was indeed the pathologic driver.

**Bottom line:** loss–of–function variants or antibodies against FAH eliminate key checks on the AP’s accelerating loop so that bacterial sepsis provides a “second hit” that diverts previously well‑controlled activation into a self-perpetuating cascade producing unregulated terminal complex deposition on endothelium — thereby sustaining MAHA common in CM‑HUS.*

### ["Management"] "When treating a patient who remains culture positive for an encapsulated organism with terminal-complement inhibition (eculizumab), what factors guide the timing decision and how do you mitigate infection risk?

**Clinical Pearls – Timing Complement Blockade in Persistent Encapsulated Bacteremia**

*Key Issue*: Eculizumab blocks C5‑mediated membrane‑attack complex formation—essential for killing encapsulated organisms such as *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*. When a patient remains culture positive, the decision to initiate terminal‑complement inhibition hinges on two opposing priorities:

1. **Control the Triggering Infection**  
   *If the infectious source is still active, immune escape will be inevitable.*  
   • **Microbiological clearance** – eculizumab should be withheld until two consecutive sterile blood cultures are obtained *or* when clinical evidence demonstrates full resolution of bacteremia (afebrile >72 h with leukocyte count trending toward normal).  
   • **Effective antimicrobial coverage** – initiate or optimize therapy that eliminates the pathogen based on sensitivity data—here IV ampicillin restarted after *C. canimorsus* identification yielded sustained clearance over ≥3 days before any complement blockade was considered.

2. **Prevent Severe Complement‑Related Damage**  
   *CM‑HUS progresses rapidly once endothelial injury is triggered.*  
   • **Biomarker trajectory** – rising lactate dehydrogenase (>2× upper limit), persistent schistocytes (>10 %) and thrombocytopenia (<50 ×10⁹/L) define imminent organ injury. Once these parameters plateau in response to broad‑spectrum antibiotics and supportive care, early initiation offers maximal renoprotective benefit.

#### Factors Guiding Timing

| Factor | Why It Matters | Practical Trigger |
|--------|----------------|-------------------|
| **Microbiology** | Active bacteremia defeats neutralised terminal complement | Start only after ≥2 sterile cultures |
| **Antibiotic Coverage** | Maps residual bacterial load | Adequate duration per IDSA guidance (≥7–10 days depending on organism) |
| **Sepsis Resolution** | Hyperinflammatory state normalizes when organisms are cleared | Afebrile >72 h + stable vital signs |
| **Vaccination Status** | Protects against *Neisseria* escapes once blocking C5 | MenACWY + serogroup B given ≥10 days pre‑therapy or as first dose prior to treatment |
| **Pheasants & Bridging Therapy** | Allows time between infection control & blockade | Plasma exchange sessions bridge while awaiting safety window |

In our patient, plasma exchange was employed until platelet count doubled; then Sterile cultures were documented two days later; vaccinations were administered immediately—Menveo and Bexsero—and eculizumab induction commenced once the first dose had been delivered and two sterile cultures obtained.

#### Mitigating Infection Risk

1. **Meningococcal Immunization**  
   • Give conjugate vaccine series immediately (`MenACWY` + `MenB`). A single dose confers some protection by day 7; a booster can be added once renal function permits.

2. **Prophylactic Antibiotics**  
   • Administer high‑dose penicillin G or ampicillin during the first week following each eculizumab infusion (common practice is 9 kIU IV q6h while waiting for innate immunity recovery).

3. **Close Microbiological Surveillance**  
   • Repeat blood cultures every 24–48 h during the first week after starting complement blockades; pause infusion if persistent fevers develop despite adequate coverage.

4. **Patient Education & Follow‑Up**  
   • Counsel about early signs of infection—fever, malaise—and arrange prompt evaluation should they arise.

5. **Limiting Duration During Urgency**  
   • In life‑threatening TMA where delaying therapy risks irreversible renal failure *and* active bacteremia cannot be cleared quickly enough, a short course of empirical plasmapheresis can stabilize hemodynamics while awaiting confirmation that infections are under control prior to radical blockade.

---

**Bottom line:**  
Start eculizumab only **once microbiology confirms bacterial clearance**, ideally preceded by successful antimicrobial therapy and receipt of meningococcal vaccination. Bridge with plasma exchange if needed, supplement with prophylactic antibiotics exactly during this vulnerable period, monitor serial blood cultures meticulously, and resume full treatment once all three criteria are satisfied—a strategy that balances rapid TMA reversal with safety around encapsulated pathogens.

### ["Management"] "Why was plasma exchange initiated before starting eculizumab when ADAMTS13 activity was found well above 10 %?

**Answer**

Because the initial clinical picture met no lower limit of exclusion for thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), treating as if it were TTP while awaiting laboratory confirmation is standard care.

* **Diagnostic uncertainty** – On day 8 the patient developed sudden haemoglobin fall, persistent thrombocytopenia, schistocytosis and undetectable haptoglobin – classic hallmarks of a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).  Both TTP and complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS) can present identically; only an **ADAMTS13 activity < 10 %** reliably excludes TTP.
* **Guideline‑derived urgency** – Current American Society of Hematology guidelines advise initiating **therapeutic plasma‑exchange (PEX)** immediately in any patient who fulfills criteria for possible TTP (≥1 episode of MAHA + thrombocytopenia), even before laboratory confirmation.  A delay postpones life‑saving therapy if the disease is actually TTP.
* **Bridge while awaiting proof** – The ADAMTS13 result returned 43 %, ruling out severe deficiency but after PEX had already begun (the first session occurred “shortly after” discovery).  These exchanges removed circulating autoantibodies/deranged vWF multimers *and* inflammatory mediators that could transiently stabilize renal function—evidenced by return of diuresis after the third session—without correcting underlying complement dysregulation.
* **Sequential logic** – Once CM‑HUS was established (normal ADAMTS13, negative shiga toxin PCR, biopsy consistent with end‑organ microthrombi), terminal‑complement blockade could be introduced safely only after bacteremia resolved and vaccinations administered to mitigate infection risk from terminal C5 inhibition.

In short, plasma exchange was started promptly as a safety net against possible fatal TTP while maintaining patient stability pending definitive differentiation between TTP and CM‑HUS—only afterwards could targeted eculizumab therapy be justified.

### ["Morning Report"] "In a septic patient presenting AKI, low platelets, schistocytes on smear and falling hemoglobin despite resolving coagulopathy—what immediate laboratory studies would you order upon admission?

**Immediate diagnostic algorithm for a septic patient who develops new‐onset AKI with thrombocytopenia, net falling hemoglobin and schistocytosis**

1. **Full CBC + peripheral blood smear**  
   *Reticulocyte count* – distinguishes recent hemoglobin loss from low production.  
   *Schistocyte quantification* – confirms microangiopathic hemolysis in real time (≥1–2 %).  

2. **Hemolysis panel** (performed serially every 12–24 h)  
   • **LDH** – ↑ >2× upper limit indicates intravascular hemolysis  
   • **Indirect bilirubin** – ↑ bilirubin accompanies cell lysis  
   • **Haptoglobin** – ≥3‑fold decrease is diagnostic of ongoing MAHA  
   These values rapidly gauge severity (as seen when haptoglobin became undetectable in our patient).

3. **Coagulation screen**  
   • PT/INR, aPTT  
   • Fibrinogen & D‑dimer  
   Normalization of these parameters differentiates resolving DIC from persistent consumptive coagulopathy associated with TMA—exactly what our patient experienced when coagulopathy resolved yet hemolysis continued.

4. **ADAMTS13 activity** (turn‑around ≤24 h)  
   *Rationale*: Severe deficiency (<10 %) unequivocally diagnoses thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), which requires urgent plasma exchange regardless of other data—our patient’s 43 % activity ruled out TTP promptly before initiating plasma exchange as bridge therapy.\(^1\)

5. **Complement assessment** – screening for complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS)  
   • Serum C3 & C4 levels – normal values do **not** exclude CM‑HUS but help document baseline complement status.\(^2\)  
   • Functional assay of total complement activity (CH50) *or* alternative pathway activity (AH50). A markedly reduced AH50 during active disease supports alternative‑pathway activation as seen when our patient’s AH50 fell during treatment.\(^3\)

6. **Stool PCR for Shiga toxin** – mandatory exclusion of STEC‐HUS even in adults without prodromal diarrhea.\(^4\)

7. **Direct antiglobulin test (DAT)** – IgG or IgM positive assays can mimic MAHA due to immune‐mediated hemolysis common in transfusion or antibiotic exposure.\(^5\)

8. **Antibiotic–sensitive blood cultures + Gram stain** – identify infective trigger (“Capnocytophaga canimorsus” in our index case) that may precipitate CM‑HUS or trigger other systemic effects.

These investigations provide an evidence‐based roadmap:

* If ADAMTS13 <10 %, start plasma exchange without delay (*e.g.*, our patient received PEX once the low activity was confirmed).  
* Normal ADAMTS13 activity plus persistent schistocytosis and rising LDH directs attention toward alternative complement activation → prompt completion of the panel above → consultation of nephrology/hematology → consider eculizumab if LA remains unchecked.\(^6\)

Early execution of this diagnostic sequence—ideally within the first 24 h in an ICU setting—prevents unnecessary delays between recognition of microangiopathic injury and definitive intervention with complement blockade or plasma exchange when appropriate​.


---

> **Key take‐away:** In sepsis evolving into AKI with unexplained anemia & thrombocytopenia, “triage by lab” begins with an urgent *ADAMTS13* level alongside standard coagulation panels and a basic complement screen; these tests permit life‑saving decisions between plasma exchange for TTP versus rapid initiation of terminal complement inhibition for CM‑HUS triggered by infection—as demonstrated by this Capnocytophaga case.\(^1–^6\)

### ["Pathophysiology"] "Why can serum C3/C4 remain normal during an episode of complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy even when renal injury is profound?

**Answer**

In complement‑mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (CM‑HUS), marked vascular injury can occur while plasma levels of C3 and C4 remain within reference ranges because the diagnostic tests evaluate quantity—not function—of these proteins and because activation is largely *localized* to endothelial surfaces rather than systemic.

1. **Baseline abundance and rapid hepatic synthesis** – C3 exists at ~180 mg/dL in adult plasma; its production exceeds even substantial consumptive losses unless the trigger is overwhelming (as in septic DIC). During early sepsis, hepatic acute‑phase response up‑regulates C3 synthesis, masking modest consumption.

2. **Alternative‑pathway “on‑site” activation** – CM‑HUS is driven by uncontrolled formation of alternative‑pathway convertases on damaged endothelium (C3b‑Bb). This process primarily activates downstream components (C5 release, MAC formation) without large global consumption of soluble C3 or C4. Thus total plasma protein mass can stay “normal”.

3. **Normal C3/C4 do not equate to intact function** – Complement assays such as AH50 (alternative‐pathway activity) or CH50 (classical/lectin pathways) assess functional capacity. In this patient’s extended workup, AH50 was markedly reduced after PEX and eculizumab initiation—an effect of treatment rather than baseline depletion—while plasma C3/C4 were unchanged pre‑therapy, illustrating how functional assays detect disease that basic panels miss.

4. **Clinical context matters** – The case highlights that bedside clues (schistocytes, thrombocytopenia with MAHA, renal biopsy showing TMA) drive diagnosis irrespective of “normal” serum complements. Normal levels simply indicate that systemic depletion is not occurring; they do not rule out profound endothelial injury mediated by locally high terminal‑complement activity.

**Bottom line:** Serum C3 and C4 levels measure protein concentration alone; they can remain normal during CM‑HUS because (a) the liver compensates for modest consumption, and (b) the pathogenic complement activation is predominantly local—not reflected in circulating concentrations). Therefore clinicians should rely on functional assays and clinical findings rather than routine C3/C4 values when evaluating suspected complement‑mediated TMA.

### ["Clinical Pearls"] "Penicillin exposure can yield hostile direct antiglobulin positivity while mechanical hemolysis shows schistocytes—how would peripheral smear morphology combined other labs distinguish warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia from microangiopathic hemolysis?

**How a peripheral‑smear morphology‐plus‑lab panel separates warm autoimmune haemolytic anaemia (WAIHA) from microangiopathic haemolysis**

| Feature | Warm autoimmune haemolytic anaemia | Microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (TMA / CM‑HUS) |
|--------|------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------|
| **Peripheral‑smear** | Predominantly *spherocytes* or mildly altered red cells; **≤ 1 % schistocytes** | **> 5 % (> 15 % abnormal cases)** *schistocytes*—fine angular fragments (“helmet”‐shaped), bite/cerebral crescents |
| **Platelet count** | Usually normal or only mildly reduced unless underlying SLE/Sjögren | Markedly reduced (< 150×10⁹/L); often < 50×10⁹/L |
| **Direct antiglobulin test (DAT)** | Positive for *IgG* (and often anti‑IgM) – true antibody coating | Negative when no autoantibody present – drug‑induced *false‑positive* “hostile” results seen with peniccillins (as in this case where Apocillin triggered an early IgG+ assay that subsequently became negative once the drug was cleared) |
| **Reticulocyte response** | Sharp increase reflecting marrow compensation (“high retic”) | Can be blunted by concurrent marrow suppression early in sepsis or CKD |
| **Complement consumption** | Low C3/C4 only if anti‑C3 cold AGs present | Often normal early in CM‑HUS but functional alternative pathway activity may be reduced (seen here after plasma exchange) |
| **ADAMTS13 activity** | Not required – values usually > 10 % | Low (< 10 %) suggests TTP (excluded here with 43 %) |
| **Clinical context** | Usually painless splenomegaly or underlying autoimmune disease | Thrombosis/parenchymal injury—in our patient AKI + oliguria + skin ischemia |

### Why morphology trumps a single test

1. **Penicillin‑associated “hostile” DATs**  
   Many β‑lactams create transient drug‐dependent antibodies that bind only when the drug is present on the red‑cell surface. A mere pulse or poor compliance can therefore yield a *positive IgG‐DAT* even when no pathological autoantibody exists. In our patient, a repeat Coombs test after clarifying medication exposure was negative—clear evidence that the initial finding was a drug artifact rather than true WAIHA.

2. **Schistocyte burden**  
   A quantitative surrogacy for intravascular crushing is straightforward: ≥15 % schystocytes almost always signifies a microangiopathic process such as DIC—infections like Capnocytophaga can ignite complement activation leading to endothelial injury—and ruled out pure immune haemolysis which never generates substantial fragmentation.

3. **Platelet involvement**  
   The platelet fall seen here (30–40×10⁹/L) points toward consumptive coagulopathy rather than isolated autoimmune destruction of red cells which typically spares platelets unless concomitant disorders exist.

4. **Complement & ADAMTS13 workup**  
   A normal-to-mildly reduced C3/C4 does not exclude complement‐mediated HUS because acute consumption may be transient or masked by ongoing therapy—all this was confirmed by an alternative pathway assay showing very low activity post‑PEX and eculizumab initiation. Conversely, an ADAMTS13 level at 43 %, well above the >10 % threshold needed for non‑TTP diagnosis, argues against thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura while supporting CM‑HUS after ruling out Shiga toxin source.

### Practical pearls

1. **Always combine smear analysis with serial retics and platelet counts before initiating steroids**—an unnecessary pulse dose increases infection risk without benefit when the culprit is not autoantibody driven.
2. **If a patient presents after recent β‑lactam therapy**, repeat the DAT after clearance or use a drug neutralization protocol before trusting any antibody–positive result.
3. **Quantify schists precisely:** Count ≥50 blasts/macroscopic fields using a wet fixed slide under oil immersion; ≥5 % typically mandates evaluation for MAHA/TMI regardless of other findings.
4. **Prioritize early ADAMTS13 and complement testing:** A rapid assay will steer you away from unnecessary plasma exchange if activity is high enough (>10 %) while confirming complement blockade targets when below shutdown point (>90 %).

In summary, this case illustrates how emergent “hostile” DAT positivity triggered by penicillin exposure could easily misdirect management toward corticosteroid therapy if unverified against smears showing overwhelming schistosclae and thrombocytopenia indicative of CM‑HUS driven by Capnocytophaga infection—a diagnosis correctly confirmed only after integrating morphology with comprehensive laboratory profiling.(NEJM Clinical Pearls style)
