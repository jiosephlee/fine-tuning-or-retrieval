Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

Chapter 1: ## Case Overview & Timeline

A woman in her mid‑60s presented with a long‑standing history of obesity (body mass index >35 kg/m²), non‑alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) diagnosed three years earlier on liver biopsy with activity score 4/8 and stage 0, and well‑controlled hypertension. She had been advised lifestyle changes only until her hepatic steatosis remained stable, prompting initiation of weight‑loss pharmacotherapy with the glucagon‑like peptide‑1 (GLP‑1) receptor agonist semaglutide at the standard starting dose of 0.25 mg subcutaneously once weekly. In her case only one injection was administered.

Within a few hours of this single dose she developed intense nausea and vomiting that progressed over the ensuing days to include dyspnoea and marked oral intake reduction. Two days later she was admitted for severe gastrointestinal upset accompanied by acute renal failure and metabolic acidosis secondary to presumed hypovolaemia from persistent vomiting. The admission laboratory data documented a mild elevation of creatine kinase (CK) at 337 U/L (normal range 12–191 U/L). No myalgia or colour change in urine was reported at that time.

Subsequent recovery was slow; during the next few weeks she remained weakly deconditioned but did not report overt muscle complaints. Over the following four months her functional status worsened gradually: she lost the ability to stand independently or sit without assistive support, experienced difficulty swallowing both liquids and solids (dysphagia) plus vocal cord dysfunction leading to dysphonics, and had increasingly pronounced proximal limb weakness consistent with an upper limb girdle pattern when physically assessed by an outside facility. At that visit—four months after starting semaglutide—the laboratory workup revealed dramatically rising muscle injury markers, with CK spiking to approximately 3 992 U/L (nearly twenty‑fold upper limit). This elevation reflected ongoing necrotising myopathy rather than transient drug toxicity alone and prompted urgent immunosuppressive therapy elsewhere in her hospital course._
