Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and Initial Evaluation

A woman in her sixth decade presented with an insidious onset of proximal muscle weakness and dysphagia that evolved over a four‑month period following her first exposure to the glucagon‑like peptide‑1 receptor agonist semaglutide. She had previously been diagnosed with obesity–related non‑alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and had no history of statin therapy, excessive exertion, or other known precipitants of myopathy. At admission she was fully independent with normal activities of daily living until the sudden development of nausea and vomiting a few hours after her single intramuscular dose of semaglutide (0.25 mg).

Upon presentation during the acute gastrointestinal episode she developed hypovolaemic azotemia and metabolic acidosis severe enough to require intravenous hydration and bicarbonate therapy, along with brief non‑invasive ventilation support due to dyspnoea. Her serum creatine kinase (CK) at this time was modestly elevated at 337 U/L (reference: 12–191 U/L), far lower than levels typically associated with classic drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis but still above normal by a significant margin. Her liver enzymes were mildly deranged (AST 191 U/L, ALT 165 U/L), findings consistent with her known hepatosteatosis rather than acute hepatic injury from chemotherapy or ischemia. No myalgia, "cola‑colored" urine, cutaneous rash, or other systemic features typical of toxin‑induced muscle injury were reported during this episode.

Over the next four months while off semaglutide she noted progressive difficulty initiating upright posture and ambulation without assistance. She began reporting dysphagia for both liquids and solids as well as phonatory strain suggestive of bulbar involvement—findings that are characteristic of a proximal myopathy involving both limb and pharyngeal muscles. The progression was linear rather than episodic; by hospitalisation she was unable to sit independently or lift her head without help (\*) Table 1 in the report details her muscular power grading at this stage.

Laboratory surveillance during this course uncovered a steep rise in CK from its earlier plateau (~1 200 U/L) to a peak of 3 992 U/L—a twelve‑fold increase—whose timing correlated closely with worsening symptoms. This pattern generated two competing hypotheses in the differential diagnosis:

| Hypothesis | Typical Clinical Features | Laboratory Signature | Key Ruling In/Out Points |
|------------|----------------------------|----------------------|--------------------------|
| Acute drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis | Sudden outset within hours/days after exposure; often severe pain; dark urine | Extremely high (>5,000–10,000 U/L) | Slightly lower CK on initial exposure; lack of classic “marathon” symptoms |
| Immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy | Progressive proximal weakness over weeks/months; bulbar impairment possible | Moderately elevated CK (hundreds–thousands); often normal ANA panel | Sustained rise over months rather than immediate spike; absence of widespread systemic autoimmune disease |

The lack of classic rhabdomyolysis features—especially pain or renal tubular injury from myoglobinuria—and the slowly evolving proximal deficit favoured an immune‑mediated process despite the initial modest CK rise post‑semaglutide injection. Additionally, serum autoantibody testing including anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP (the two serologic pearls in IMNM) returned negative yet was not unexpected given up to one third of patients may be seronegative—a diagnostic nuance important for trainees considering IMNM in atypical scenarios.

In summary — by four months after semaglutide initiation —the patient demonstrated distinct early features that raised suspicion for an immune‐mediated necrotising myopathy rather than direct drug toxicity: gradual proximal muscle deconditioning alongside significant CK escalation without overt clinical paralyses typical for rhabdomyolysis. The diagnostic trajectory set the stage for subsequent biopsy corroboration and therapeutic immunosuppression described later in this report.[\*]
