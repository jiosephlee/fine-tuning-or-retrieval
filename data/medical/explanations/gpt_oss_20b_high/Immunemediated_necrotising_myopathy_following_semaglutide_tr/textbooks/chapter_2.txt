Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

Chapter 2: ## Pathophysiology of Immune‑mediated Necrotising Myopathy

Immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is an autoimmune muscle disease that is distinct from classic myositis entities by the predominance of myofiber necrosis over inflammatory infiltrates. The disorder is defined by proximal muscle weakness that typically progresses rapidly over weeks to months, with markedly elevated serum creatine kinase (CK) reflecting loss of muscle integrity. Histologically, these patients demonstrate clusters of necrotic and regenerating fibre segments accompanied by macrophage infiltration and deposition of the terminal complement complex C5b‑9 on intact and damaged fibers—features that are not seen in other inflammatory myopathies such as polymyositis or dermatomyositis.

The serologic subset of IMNM is divided most commonly into anti‑hydroxy‑3‑methylglutaryl‑CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti‑signal recognition particle (SRP) autoantibody–positive cases, accounting for roughly two thirds of patients. Each antibody is associated with its own predilection for precipitating factors and clinical nuances: HMGCR antibodies are strongly linked to prior statin exposure, while SRP antibodies appear in patients without identifiable drug exposure yet tend to cause a more severe initial presentation. Approximately one third of IMNM cases are seronegative (“non‑autoantibody”) which poses additional diagnostic challenges but suggests the presence of yet unidentified pathogenic antibodies or T‑cell–mediated mechanisms.

Drug triggers are a recognized cause of IMNM beyond statins. Statin use has contributed to a paradigm shift in how clinicians interpret statin-associated myopathies because it linked a clear external stimulus with both the onset of disease and the presence of HMGCR antibodies. Statins upregulate HMGCR expression in skeletal muscle, creating a target epitope against which the immune system reacts once tolerance breaks down—a classic “immune‑adjuvant” effect that culminates in autoantibody generation. Other medications have been implicated sporadically (e.g., certain antiepileptics), but these clusters remain small compared with the frequency seen with statins. In emerging reports such as this case involving semaglutide—a glucagon‑like peptide‑1 (GLP‑1) receptor agonist—the association remains hypothetical until further pharmacovigilance data accumulate. Nevertheless, it illustrates that any external agent capable of altering muscle antigenicity or immune homeostasis could conceivably precipitate IMNM.

The histologic hallmarks subserve both diagnosis and insight into pathogenesis. On routine staining, there is extensive fibre necrosis accompanied by scattered regenerating fibers labeled by embryonic myosin heavy chain positivity—evidence that injured myocytes attempt repair but cannot persistently escape loss. Immunohistochemical analysis reveals abundant CD68⁺ macrophages actively phagocytosing myofibrillar debris (“myophagocytosis”) alongside acid phosphatase activity reflecting lysosomal involvement. Complement deposition—most consistently seen as membrane attack complex (C5b‑9) staining on both damaged and intact fibers—suggests that antibody–antigen complexes activate the classical pathway locally within the muscle milieu rather than systemically via circulating immune complexes alone.

In contrast to polymyositis or dermatomyositis—where perimysial or perivascular cuffing by CD4⁺ T cells dominates—the leucocytic infiltrate in IMNM is sparse and mostly involves resident monocytes/macrophages rather than T cells or B cells. This immunoprofile hints at an innate mediated injury orchestrated through complement activation after an adaptive humoral event has primed target antigens on myofibers.

The exact mechanisms by which GLP‑1 receptor agonists might trigger this scenario remain speculative but can be framed within known pathways. One hypothesis proposes that semaglutide may act as a hapten-like molecule when it binds intracellular proteins or modifies membrane glycoproteins in skeletal muscle cells exposed to high systemic concentrations following a single dose in susceptible individuals. Formation of neo–epitopes could prompt cross‐reactive B cell activation leading to antibody production against a self‑protein such as HMGCR or another undiscovered antigen unique to glucoid pathways activated during GLP‑1 signaling. Alternatively, GLP‑1 receptors expressed on resident immune cells could shift cytokine profiles toward an inflammatory milieu—elevating IFNγ or TNFα—which primes complement components locally within muscle tissue even without overt infection or systemic inflammation.

While definitive mechanistic data are lacking for GLP‑1 agonist–associated cases, the immunopathologic juxtaposition seen in this patient—necrosis with CD68⁺ macrophages and complement deposition on muscle fibers yet minimal lymphocytic infiltrates—aligns well with what would be expected if an autoantibody generated by drug exposure activated classic complement pathways at sites of altered self-antigen exposure on striated muscle. Thus, though causality remains unproven at this point, these observations support ongoing vigilance for ILM patterns following exposure to novel pharmacologic agents capable of modulating either antigen presentation or innate immunity within skeletal muscle.
