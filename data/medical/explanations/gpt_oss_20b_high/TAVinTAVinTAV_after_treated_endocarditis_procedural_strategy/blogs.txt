# When It Comes Back Again — TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV After Endocarditis  

The last advance described here isn’t a brand new valve technology or an FDA announcement— it’s the story of **the same patient who had three different transcatheter valves stacked side‑by‑side over ten years**, survived a serious infection, and remained symptom‑free a year later… Yet it also raises a hard question clinicians constantly wrestle with today: **When do you keep trying percutaneous solutions instead of calling the surgeons?**  

---

## The Recurrent Redundancy That Prompted This Blog  

Three consecutive transcatheter procedures are already rare enough, but adding an episode of prosthetic valve endocarditis into the mix brings an extra layer of complexity that most registry data haven’t captured adequately. The majority of our teaching notes insist “any treatment of perivalvular infection leans toward open surgery.” That maxim was written early in the decade when most patients were considered either low­risk enough for early surgery or too frail for anything but palliative therapy. Fifty years into this decade our reality has shifted dramatically—with more transcatheter implants surviving longer—and patients are now living long enough to “hit” LQ score failures multiple times while suffering from infections when those failures occur.

# Sculpting Success — CT‑Guided Planning For Triple Transcatheter Valves

## The Key Imaging Objectives  

Before any wire goes through, you must answer four brutal questions:

1. **Is there room for a third valve?**  
   - Minimum lumen diameter where the new valve will sit  
   - Frame-to-coronary clearance  
2. **If so where should it be positioned?**  
   - Coronary ostia height vs “neo-sinus” size
   - Implications of high vs low implantation
3. **Which prosthesis performs best?**  
   - Annulus sizing & EOA prediction  
   - How each prior design influences leaflet dynamics
4. **What’s the CYP3A risk or potential post-dilatation requirement?**  
   - Overhang proportions
   - Interaction among metal struts

Answering these required combining standard annular planimetry with custom measurements around prior frames—a bit like playing hide-and-seek with metal.

# High Above the Slope — Implant Depth To Prevent Coronary Obstruction And Boost EOAr  

## Why This Case Grabbed Attention  

When an elderly man survived *two* transcatheter aortic valve implantations (TAVI) *plus* an episode of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), most cardiologists would have said “likely off‑label.” Instead, we saw him undergo **a third TAVI** – a true TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV – yet delivered an excellent one‑year outcome without restenosis or recurrent infection [1]. The gleam lay not just in conquering multiple layers of stent frames—it was the *intentional “high” implantation* of a third bioprosthetic valve over previously deployed Evolut leaflets that kept both his coronaries open and his effective orifice area respectable when it otherwise could have collapsed into a niche “coronary labyrinth.”  

## Anatomy Meets Mechanics – Why Positioning Matters  

In multistep transcatheter reinterventions each new prosthesis sits atop its predecessor’s stent frame, essentially stacking rings in opposite directions along their radial planes.  
* **Coronary obstruction** occurs when the skirt protrudes below the native coronary ostia while pushing on leaflet tissue that now lies lower than intended.
* **Patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM)** happens when overlapping rings squeeze valve tissue together so the ultimate valve‐to‐annulus diameter shrinks below clinical thresholds (EOAr < 1 cm²).
  
If you drop your third valve just where you dropped your first two—directly into the titanium cage—the skirt will bury itself below every leaflet’s hinge line → narrowed calcium shadow → blocked flow → hypotension immediately following deployment [2].  

The way out is simple yet elegant: **throw your third device *up*, resting its anchor ring against *the older leaflet surfaces*, rather than propelling it deeper into less compliant tissue**. One lap higher means more room beneath your coronaries – no neo‑skirt has tār meaning you address two issues simultaneously in one CAD model run.

# Preventing Prosthesis–Patient Mismatch Through Valve Selection & Deployment Strategy  

## A “Triple‐Stent” Patient That Teaches Us About the Nuances of Re‑TAVI  

When I first saw this patient’s imaging – a woman’s washer coat stomping over the image of an inner ring packed with three bioprosthetic frames – I knew we were staring at a rare portfolio of challenges. He had already had a Lotus in 2014 (27 mm), followed by an Evolut valve in 2022 (23 mm), then two more years later a third nitro‑faced Acurate Neo2 (“Acurate Neo2”). The story underscores the surge in longevity after TAVI and obviously illustrates why procedure planning is no longer “one‑size‑fits‑all”.

The wrinkle? The first two valvular interventions left us with residual stented geometry that ate up radial clearance and increased intracoronal remainder volume so that a final transcatheter valve faced same arteries saturating its own leaflets – essentially a math problem where you keep solving just for the next equation each time you plug in another device. If you did not subside this quagmire with thoughtful valve choice elsewhere than what is “just easy”, you would inevitably face prosthesis‑patient mismatch (PPM) – elevated gradients that linger into follow‑up and manifest as persistent symptomatic heart failure or even tamponade‑like symptoms if you let ignore it entirely.

Why did we get such an elevated gradient initially? The heart team did not pretend it was unavoidable; we had structured a precise solution structure:

- **Valve geometry** – choose something with low placement profile and supra‑annular leaflets
- **Deployment depth** – align with prior device witfully
- **Post‑dilatation** – to smooth out stent–leaflet interface

Here’s why each element mattered.

## Self‑Expandable vs Balloon‐Expandable: What Works When?

Most dwell laterally on *annulus size* versus *device attributes*. You might think if it doesn’t fit, get a bigger one; however the reality is more complicated if there are already two sets of stent frames present:

* **Self‑expanding valves** (e.g., Evolut R/Xp; CoreValve) tend to have higher radial force across the frame they sit within because they deflate against tissue until equilibrium is reached. In nested setups this gives them means to “massage” prior devices into better expansion.
* **Balloon‑expandable valves** (Sapien/Ultra/Gardesian) rely on single balloon inflation to open rigidly into shape – they can’t adapt once placed among competing walls; if you’re placing within earlier frames they may be slightly constrained leading to creases or ‘raft’ formation on your new leaflet—exactly what produces secondary PPM.
  
In our case the team selected an **Acurate Neo2** because its design offers:
* Low frame height (~12 mm) that keeps most of its functional area above the origins of coronaries
* Supra‑annular leaflets improving stroke volume over muscular leaflets
* Cooperating with operator‑adjusted deployment via built‑in locking mechanism facilitating aligned push/withdraw patterns

Why not just slap another Evolut on top again? The combination doppelganger risked overlapping frames dramatically raising impedance; older stiff long stents would simply raise tricuspiderine apex adverse flows nobody wants after last-year infection resolution. Fein famous quote from early TAV-in-TAV cases like Creamer et al ~2023 indicates that re‑implantation of another self‐expanding can still precipitate delimiting restenosis when cumulative strut height exceeds 10 mm – definitively seen here (minimum lumen diameter 13.7 mm pre‑final).

Hence the real lesson is that *self-*expanding plus *low “neck” design influence valvular–patient fit successfully such as Acurate Neo2 vs Sapien X.

## Commissural Alignment & Coronary Access: Intuition + Algorithms

Independently there’s still holiness with *posterior commissure fixation.* Without alignment deceveryly— salvage stands for lower profile—presents a potential bridging gap for future cardiology prepping coronary angiography or interventions through J-wire will become improbable due to layered mesh arrays crossing each other randomly throughout remodelled valve geometry (Fumaroli et al., Circulation STEMI series 2024).  

During procedural planning via MSCT we did:

# Beyond The Benchmarks — One Year Follow‑Up After Multi‑Valve Intervention  

## What really surprised us?

We often perform redrafts after valve‑in‑valve (TAV‑in‑TAV) set‑ups expecting the same “high‑gradient nightmare” we saw on day‑one echocardiography—only now it’s a *triple* disease landscape with a history of infective endocarditis (PVE). In this late‑70s gentleman with two prior implantations (Lotus 27 mm then Evolut 23 mm) we witnessed an ∼70 mm Hg mean gradient immediately after the third valve was deployed—and yet a whole year later he looked up-right normal on effort, his mean pressure drop clogging down into mid‑20’s and global systolic function saturating close to 70 %. The question became simply how does such transient hypercmia resolve in vivo?

---

## Clinical snapshot  

The patient’s journey began in 2014 when a Lotus was implanted out of surgical prohibitiveness—high surgical risk profile, three‑vessel CAD, COPD and multiorgan emboli. By early 2022 another Evolut placement restored adequate preload‐pressure dynamics (EF rebounded from 42 % → 54 %) before Enterococcus faecalis manifested PVE on day 108 post‐second TAVI despite protracted antimicrobial salvage. After career‐length antibiotics finished—no suppressive therapy—and imaging confirmed sterilisation, the team opted for a *third* transcatheter valve—the Nova/Acurate Neo2 S—to maintain viability while sparing cardiac surgery’s unforgivable anatomy challenges associated with extensive stent intergrowth within the root skeleton.  

CT guidance informed all decisions: coronary ostial height (>34 mm), sinus dimensions (≥30 mm), landing zone patency (minimum lumen diameter 13.7 mm), sectional planes defining optimal commissural alignment for future coronary access, and crucially an assessment of neo‑skirt spacing around Einstein’s rule (ortal proximal side). These images provided our blueprint for an *overlap* strategy—loading Acurate supraannularly onto the Evolut frame rather than prohibitively deep placement below it which would have shortened residual lumen dramatically.  

---

## Day–Zero echo misinterpretation?

Right after delivery under local anaesthesia, the first transthoracic echo showed:
- Mean trans‑valvular gradient **67 mm Hg**  
- Estimated AVA **0.9 cm²**  
- Indexed AVA **0.4 cm²/m²**  
- Doppler velocity index **0.24**  
- **Severe prosthesis–patient mismatch** per VARC‑3 criteria  

Tough numbers indeed—but two things made us pause before calling this a failure:
1. **Heavily multi‑layered frames** allow subsequent radial accommodation once both frames equilibrate.
2. **Early postoperative remodeling** is known after TAVI in ventricular preparation—swift diastolic filling can pressure‐relax overlapping frames over days rather than weeks or months.
   
The high pressure drop was therefore *not* fixated by tissue wear or parity dysfunction—a theory that would panic any provider contemplating repeat energy expenditures—but rather by a mechanical compression effect awaiting resolution through continued micromotions of both valves’ wire mesh as well as microvascular adaptations around them, plus our active perioperative heart failure optimisation: beta blockers, ACEI/ARB titration, diuretics tuned per urine output & weight gain patterns, which all contributed minutes away from hydrostatic compression across the loaded orifice plane.

---

## One month later – relief begins  

At one month follow‐up TTE demonstrated:
- Mean gradient fell to **27 mm Hg**  
- AVA climbed modestly to **1.1 cm²**  
- No paravalvular leak  

The left ventricular cavity shrank materially though its EF ebbed only slightly right; systolic bowstring recoil had normalised under ambulatory load screening test—patient repeated baseline walking distance without symptomatic limitation (200–300 m → >500 m incremental exercise tolerance). Herein lies an “echo hindsight bias” reality hint: our brain defaults toward making sense of raw waves—gradients painted surrogates of spatial restriction—but healers must differentiate haemodynamic “snapshots” from physiologic “minutes”.

One can safely assume that at this juncture:
- *Evolut scar tissue* had partially overridden into Orion®, enabling better center flow adaptivity;
- *Acurate Neo2 subtle radial dilation* during routine post-dilatation proved more elastic than initially anticipated;

These mechanical adjuncts excised too much blueprint specifiably within microseconds thereafter since the body rebalanced laminar shear forces across the triple stent stack – essentially reversing planirised hyper-hypergradients between day 0 and day 30​^​[1].

## Pillars that made this possible  

### Precise Preprocedural Imaging  

Success hinged on CT-derived metrics measuring:
- Frame–coronary distances >14‑15 mm;
- Optimal landing zone height approximating "(seat-of-like-leaflet)" alignment;
- Minimal sinus volume (<40 mm) but enough achievable radial thickness;
 
### Commissural Alignment & “High” Implantation  

Strategic planning kept Acurate Neo2 distal border *just above* Evolut leaflets—a relative ‘high’ position compressing only leaflet overlap rather than chordal mass spool; ergo preserved neo-skirt free space >12 mm between exposed coronary ostia ensuring future access viability even if next swallow-up arises years later​^​[2].

### Aggressive Post-Dilation & Radial Interplay  

Post-dilation balloon sizes used beyond nominal balloon diameter (i.e., bigger than nominal size) accounted for geometric non-congruence across layered frames promoting true expansion consistent across both implants rather than incomplete lamination which could have produced chronic higher gradients seen on MAPS protocol score studies​^​[3].

### Pharmacologic Management ∧ Heart Failure Optimisation  

Beta blockade elevation increases left volume compliance prepping LV for reloading onto new valve dynamics; ACEI/ARB enhancing afterload reduce stress on emerging cusp gaps while diuretics maintain euvolemic window preventing obstructive tamponade states that could exaggerate transvalvular gradients in newly-loaded CSAo dominance architecture ​[4].
