Chapter 1: ## Disease Overview and Rationale for Repeat Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has evolved from a rescue option for exclusively high‑risk octogenarians into a mainstream therapy that now encompasses intermediate‑risk and low‑risk patients across a broad age range. Early generations were balloon‑expandable Sapien valves delivered through large femoral cannulation under general anaesthesia; subsequent iterations introduced self‑expanding platforms such as the Lotus disc and Medtronic Evolut systems that offered lower paravalvular leak rates and improved hemodynamics owing to their nitinol stent frames and supra‑annular leaflets. The cumulative effect of these design refinements is an extended durability profile—yet bioprosthetic valves inevitably succumb over time to ongoing wear, calcification, pannus ingrowth, or inflammatory sequelae initiated by host factors such as systemic disease or infection (e.g., infective endocarditis). As survival after initial TAVI extends beyond twenty years in many cohorts, clinicians increasingly confront progressive structural valve deterioration (SVD), which manifests clinically as elevated gradients across an otherwise patent prosthesis or annulus enlargement leading to worsening regurgitation. Consequently, repeat interventions have become an unavoidable consideration in contemporary practice.
In this case report the patient first received a Lotus prosthesis in 2014 after being deemed too frail for surgery. Seven years later the mean transvalvular gradient had climbed above 80 mm Hg—a threshold prompting evaluation for rereplacement—yet his comorbidities deferred operative assessment until he elected another percutaneous route in May 2022 under the auspice of a problem‑solving “TAV‑in‑TAV” strategy utilizing a Medtronic Evolut frame placed within the earlier Lotus structure. Complicating matters further was an episode of Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis three months later which accelerated leaflet destruction and restored moderate stenosis despite appropriate antimicrobial stewardship.
Serial re‑interventions are guided by four fundamental considerations:

1. **Structural integrity**  
   Bioprosthetic SVD typically follows one of three pathways relevant here: thickening via calcium deposition around the tissue cusps culminates in restricted opening (Stenotic SVD); pannus proliferation impedes through-flow by bulk accumulation around stent struts leading both stenosis and regurgitation; finally discrete leaflet tears—either mechanical shear from implant design or microbial lysis during endocarditis—produce uncontrolled volume holosystolic backflow. These changes compromise effective valvular area regardless of whether healing valves remain clinically silent until thresholds are breached.
   
2. **Technical feasibility**  
   When inserting a third valve within two preexisting prostheses (a “TAV‐in‐TAV‐in‐TAV”), each additional metallic layer reduces annular clearance available for sinus perfusion and increases radial resistance against which the newest device must expand against intrinsic commissural geometry already altered by previous postsurgical deployment angles. Exact depth placement therefore demands advanced multimodality imaging—multislice computed tomography offering near‑planimetric mapping between coronary ostia height, sinus dimension, annulus size and projected stent apposition—to prevent obstructive “neocoiling” beneath coronaries either at baseline implantation or by subsequent pollen expansion into these tunnels.
   
3. **Patient presentation**  
   The clinical timeline supplies critical clues: symptom progression correlating tightly with rising Doppler gradients suggested true SVD rather than hereditary loading variability. Repeat transthoracic echo revealed progressively thinner commissural peaks symptomatic upon continual LV rescue pressure elevation culminating eventually in left ventricular Ejection Fraction recovery only upon alleviation via second reimplantation—an encouraging sign that salvage percutaneous therapy remains physiologically potent when timed correctly.
   
4. **Guideline guidance**  
   Current European Society of Cardiology/EUA consensus documents underscore open surgery as preferred therapy when prosthetic valve endocarditis coexists with uncontrollable vegetations, root abscesses or persistent bacteremia—the checklists emphasizing >10 days organism culture positivity before deeming device eligible for revival via catheterization protocols[^1]. In contrast when complete infection resolution is proven through repeat blood cultures, transthoracic/echocardiographic remodeling devoid kelp-like vegetations provisionally permits percutaneous marsupialization approaches assuming acceptable surgical risk thresholds are exceeded—as they were documented here via EuroSCORE II >27 %. Subsequent ESC/EACTS recommendations advocate shared decision making whereby low operative burden patient profiles offset repercussions from continuous structural stress until definitive open repair becomes obligatory.
   
Guidelines also caution against delayed intervention when significant regurgitation coexists; implantation timing primarily shifts towards minimizing procedural morbidity rather than strictly adhering set thresholds proving borderline increases above echocardiographic grading cutpoints alone[^2]. Hence this patient's sequence—infection treated first followed by gradual gradient buildup—fits within accepted practice frameworks endorsing stepwise catheter-based rescue rather than premature open replacement.
