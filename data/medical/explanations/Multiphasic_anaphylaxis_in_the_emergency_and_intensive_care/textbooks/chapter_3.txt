Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic workup and etiologic reasoning, including mast cell disease

The diagnostic goal after a complex anaphylactic presentation is threefold: (1) confirm that the events represented true anaphylaxis and not a mimicker, (2) identify the most likely culprit and exclude explanations for apparent relapse that do not represent new mast cell activation, and (3) assess for an underlying mast cell disorder that would change risk stratification and follow‑up. The investigations and reasoning used in this case illustrate a pragmatic, stepwise approach.

Culprit assessment
Temporal relationship is the strongest clue to causality in drug‑induced anaphylaxis. In this patient the first anaphylactic symptoms began about one hour after taking the first oral dose of clarithromycin, and no further doses were taken — a timing that strongly implicates clarithromycin as the trigger. The rash that appeared five days after starting clindamycin is most consistent with a delayed exanthem (a non–immediate, T‑cell–mediated reaction) rather than an IgE‑mediated immediate allergy; such delayed reactions do not usually predict immediate anaphylaxis on later exposure to unrelated antibiotics. Macrolide antibiotics have low reported cross‑reactivity with other antibiotic classes, so prior clindamycin exposure does not make clarithromycin the inevitable cause, but the clear temporal association with the clarithromycin dose is the dominant piece of evidence supporting it as the culprit. Because skin testing or challenge in this setting would risk provoking life‑threatening recurrence, the team appropriately elected to avoid provocation testing and to document the suspected agent for avoidance.

Excluding pseudo‑biphasic causes
When symptoms recur after apparent resolution, consider whether the picture represents true biphasic/multiphasic anaphylaxis (recurrent mast cell activation) versus a pseudo‑relapse from ongoing exposure or persistent absorption. Mechanisms of pseudo‑relapse include retained allergen (eg, ingested food, intradermal depot), extended absorption from sustained‑release formulations, or a persistent septic or cardiogenic process mimicking shock. In this case an oral single 500 mg dose of clarithromycin with no further exposure makes continued absorption or a depot effect unlikely; no sustained‑release preparation was involved and no documented retained allergen or continued exposure existed. Thus the relapses are most consistent with recurrent mast cell mediator release rather than continued antigen exposure.

Differential diagnoses to consider
A rapid onset of flushing, urticaria, hypotension, hypoxaemia and an excellent response to intramuscular epinephrine point strongly to anaphylaxis. Important alternatives that should be considered in practice include septic shock, cardiogenic shock, and non‑histaminergic angioedema (eg, ACE‑inhibitor mediated), as well as anxiety/panic with vasovagal features. In this patient, the temporal relation to drug ingestion, the dominant cutaneous features (generalised urticaria and angioedema), and reproducible improvement with epinephrine make sepsis and primary psychiatric causes unlikely; the patient was not taking an ACE inhibitor, which argues against ACE‑inhibitor angioedema. Thus the clinical picture supports recurrent anaphylaxis.

Tryptase measurement strategy and interpretation
Serum tryptase is the most useful laboratory marker to corroborate mast cell activation in suspected anaphylaxis, but timing and interpretation are essential. Recommended sampling is: an acute sample as soon as feasible after onset (ideally within 1–2 hours, and certainly within 4 hours when levels commonly peak), and a baseline sample at least 24 hours after symptom resolution to establish the patient’s steady‑state tryptase. Many authorities suggest obtaining two acute samples when possible (early and at peak) plus the baseline.

Results are interpreted using the “20% + 2” rule: an acute tryptase elevation is considered significant if the measured level exceeds baseline by more than (baseline × 0.2) + 2 μg/L, which can be rearranged to acute > 1.2 × baseline + 2 μg/L. This accounts for interindividual baseline differences and improves sensitivity for true mast cell activation.

Case‑specific issues and pitfalls
In the presented case the tryptase sampling was imperfect: levels were not drawn during the initial ED episode(s), and the first tryptase was obtained after the third anaphylactic episode. Nevertheless, an elevated event sample (taken within the recommended window relative to that episode) together with a persistently elevated baseline sample obtained 27 hours after the last recurrence supports two conclusions: there was true mast cell activation during the illness, and the patient has a chronically raised baseline tryptase. The imperfect timing means the test is not as definitive as an optimally timed series, but the available values are still informative and should prompt further evaluation.

Workup for systemic mastocytosis (SM)
Because a persistently elevated baseline tryptase and unusually severe or recurrent anaphylaxis raise concern for a clonal mast cell disorder, a structured workup is indicated. The World Health Organization criteria for systemic mastocytosis require either the major criterion plus one minor, or three minor criteria. The major criterion is multifocal, dense aggregates of mast cells in bone marrow or extracutaneous tissue. Minor criteria are: (1) KIT D816V (or other activating c‑KIT) mutation in blood, bone marrow or extracutaneous organs; (2) aberrant mast cell immunophenotype (typically CD25 and/or CD2 expression on mast cells); (3) atypical mast cell morphology (spindle shape); and (4) baseline serum tryptase >20 μg/L (unless there is an associated clonal myeloid disorder that might otherwise explain tryptase elevation). Workup therefore should include a bone marrow biopsy with histology and mast cell immunophenotyping, sensitive molecular testing for KIT D816V (on marrow and/or peripheral blood), and serial serum tryptase measurements.

In this patient the team pursued those investigations but could not obtain sufficient marrow material to assess the major criterion; the report states that only two minor criteria were fulfilled, so formal diagnostic criteria for SM were not met. Ancillary findings that support a mast cell disorder in this patient include hepatomegaly on sonography and neuropsychiatric symptoms (eg, depression, memory problems), which can be seen in mast cell activation syndromes or systemic disease even when full diagnostic criteria are not satisfied.

Other causes of elevated baseline tryptase
When baseline tryptase is chronically elevated, differential considerations include systemic mastocytosis, monoclonal hematologic disease, and hereditary α‑tryptasemia (a TPSAB1 copy number gain syndrome). Testing for KIT D816V and bone marrow evaluation helps distinguish SM from other causes. If SM is not proven but baseline tryptase remains high and clinical phenotype suggests mast cell activation, management should still be intensified because these patients are at increased risk for severe anaphylaxis.

Implications for monitoring and follow‑up
From a diagnostic and safety perspective this patient’s initial severe hypotension, requirement for multiple epinephrine doses, and persistently elevated tryptase all argue for prolonged and monitored observation (as occurred with ICU admission) and specialist follow‑up. Even in the absence of a confirmed diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis, a working presumption of increased anaphylaxis risk is reasonable: provide epinephrine auto‑injector(s), start regular H1 antihistamine prophylaxis as clinically appropriate, document suspected clarithromycin allergy, and refer to allergy/immunology and hematology for further testing (repeat marrow if initial biopsy was inadequate, sensitive KIT testing, and consideration of TPSAB1 testing if available).

Key practical points for learners
- Use the clinical picture and timing to identify the most likely trigger; clarify immediate versus delayed reactions. In this case, clarithromycin is the most likely cause because symptoms began one hour after a first dose.  
- Exclude pseudo‑relapse from ongoing exposure or depot effects; when none are present, recurrent episodes are most consistent with multiphasic mast cell activation.  
- Obtain tryptase samples when feasible: acute (ideally within 1–2 hours), and a baseline ≥24 hours after resolution; interpret using the 20% + 2 rule.  
- Persistently elevated baseline tryptase or unusually severe/recurrent anaphylaxis warrants investigation for systemic mastocytosis (bone marrow histology, flow cytometry for CD25/CD2, and KIT mutation testing) and consideration of other causes of elevated tryptase.  
- Even without a definitive SM diagnosis, elevated baseline tryptase and multi‑dose epinephrine needs justify prolonged monitored care, specialist referral, education, and prescription of epinephrine auto‑injector(s).
