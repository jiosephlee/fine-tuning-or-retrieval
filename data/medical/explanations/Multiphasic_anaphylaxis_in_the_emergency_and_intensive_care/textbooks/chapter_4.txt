Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Chapter 4: ## Acute management of recurrent/multiphasic anaphylaxis in ED/ICU

Epinephrine remains the single most important intervention in any anaphylactic episode and should be administered promptly for each recurrence. For adults the recommended intramuscular (IM) dose is 0.01 mg/kg up to a maximum of 0.5 mg per injection, given into the anterolateral thigh and repeatable every 5 minutes as needed for persistent or recurrent symptoms. In the patient described (104 kg) this means a 0.5 mg IM dose was appropriate from the outset; the later, individually prepared 0.5 mg IM doses therefore matched guideline dosing and are preferable to underdosing.

Auto‑injectors are invaluable for rapid treatment, but commonly available adult auto‑injectors deliver 0.3 mg. That dose can be subtherapeutic in many adults and has been associated with more frequent need for escalation compared with an initial 0.5 mg dose. In the hospital setting, teams should have a clear plan to assure correct dosing: stock 0.5 mg devices where possible, train staff to draw and give 0.5 mg IM from 1 mg/mL (1:1,000) ampoules when larger auto‑injectors are unavailable, and emphasise injection into the anterolateral thigh. Using an auto‑injector can avoid delays and hesitancy in low‑frequency situations, but staff must recognise its dose limitation for heavier adults.

When repeated IM doses do not restore or maintain adequate perfusion, or when relapses are extremely frequent or the patient develops progressive cardiovascular collapse, an intravenous (IV) epinephrine infusion should be started and the patient managed in an ICU environment. Typical practical approaches are to begin a titratable infusion and to use repeated IM boluses for immediate effect until the infusion is established. Infusion starting rates commonly used in adults are modest and titrated to clinical effect (for example, an initial range equivalent to a few micrograms per minute, titrated upward, often expressed clinically as 0.05–0.2 µg/kg/min or roughly 1–10 µg/min depending on local protocol). Precise regimens vary; the key principles are slow titration to restore perfusion while monitoring for tachyarrhythmia, ischemia and excessive hypertension. Anaphylactic patients on IV epinephrine require continuous ECG and blood pressure monitoring, frequent bedside reassessments, skilled nursing and physician supervision, and ideally arterial pressure monitoring if high infusion rates are needed.

Supportive measures remain essential. Give high‑flow oxygen for hypoxaemia, and aggressive isotonic crystalloid boluses for anaphylactic distributive shock — initial boluses of several hundred millilitres to litres guided by response and repeated as needed. In practice it is common to give 500–1000 mL quickly and reassess, with larger cumulative volumes often required. Treat bronchospasm with inhaled short‑acting beta‑agonists. For patients on chronic beta‑blockade with refractory hypotension or bronchospasm, parenteral glucagon should be considered because it acts independent of beta receptors; this was not applicable in the reported patient. Vasopressors other than epinephrine may be required in the ICU if hypotension persists despite epinephrine and fluid resuscitation.

Antihistamines (H1, and sometimes H2) and corticosteroids have specific, limited roles. H1 and H2 blockers can relieve cutaneous symptoms (urticaria, pruritus) but do not reverse airway obstruction, hypotension or bronchospasm and have slow onset; they are adjuncts only. Corticosteroids were historically given to try to prevent biphasic reactions, but current evidence does not support routine steroid use for that purpose and suggests possible harms (eg, hyperglycaemia, increased admissions). Many hospitals still include steroids in local protocols; when used clinicians should be aware of the weak evidence base. In the presented case corticosteroids were administered after the first recurrence in keeping with older local protocols; that choice reflects common practice but is not supported as an effective strategy to prevent further relapses.

Monitoring and disposition must be individualized for recurrent or multiphasic courses. Any patient with recurrent episodes, need for multiple epinephrine doses, severe initial presentation, or suspected underlying mast cell disorder merits ICU admission for continuous cardiorespiratory monitoring, rapid access to medications and airway support, and consideration of vasoactive infusions. High‑risk patients typically require observation for a prolonged interval after the last recurrence; many centres will observe for at least 12–24 hours, and longer if symptoms recur or if there is an underlying disorder such as elevated baseline tryptase or suspected mastocytosis. The case patient had multiple recurrences in the first 12 hours and was appropriately escalated to ICU; because her symptom intensity diminished and each episode responded to IM epinephrine, withholding an immediate IV infusion and watching closely in ICU was a reasonable, measured approach.

Interdisciplinary coordination improves care in complex courses. Early allergy/immunology consultation helps plan avoidance strategies, diagnostic testing and longer‑term management (and to weigh the risks of provocative testing), while ICU involvement is essential when frequent relapses, refractory hemodynamic instability, or need for vasopressor infusions are present. In this case allergology was involved early and ICU monitoring was instituted after the first relapse — both actions align with recommended practice. The team considered an IV infusion but appropriately balanced the risks (arrhythmia, ischemia, need for intensive monitoring and central access in some cases) against the patient’s improving, progressively milder episodes that were responsive to appropriately dosed IM epinephrine.

In summary: for recurrent/multiphasic anaphylaxis, continue prompt IM epinephrine at 0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg repeated every 5 minutes as needed (use 0.5 mg in most adults over 50 kg), provide aggressive supportive care, admit to ICU if relapses are frequent or hemodynamics unstable, consider IV infusion only for refractory or rapidly recurrent events despite correct IM dosing, use antihistamines for symptom control but do not rely on steroids to prevent recurrence, and coordinate early with allergy and critical care teams. The management choices made in the case — rapid IM epinephrine, escalation to ICU after relapse, and avoidance of an early IV infusion while monitoring response — are consistent with these practical, evidence‑based priorities, though the initial use of a 0.3 mg auto‑injector highlights the important dosing pitfall to address in hospital protocols.
