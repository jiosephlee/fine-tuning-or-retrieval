Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

Chapter 2: ## Pathophysiology and phenotypes of complex anaphylaxis

Anaphylaxis is driven by rapid activation of mast cells and basophils with consequent release of a broad array of pre-formed and newly synthesized mediators. Pre-formed mediators such as histamine, heparin and proteases (for example, tryptase) are released within minutes and account for the earliest manifestations: vasodilation, increased vascular permeability with capillary leak and urticaria/angioedema, bronchial smooth muscle contraction producing bronchospasm, and pruritus/flushing. Newly synthesized lipid mediators (leukotrienes and prostaglandins) and platelet-activating factor are produced within minutes to hours and sustain bronchoconstriction, vascular permeability and inflammatory cell recruitment. Cytokines and chemokines generated later recruit eosinophils, monocytes and additional inflammatory cells, contributing to a slower “late-phase” inflammatory response that can last many hours. Tryptase, released from mast cell granules, typically peaks about 1–2 hours after onset and is a useful marker of mast cell activation when measured in appropriate time windows.

These temporal differences in mediator release and cell recruitment underlie how anaphylaxis can show recurrent or prolonged patterns. An immediate wave of mediator release produces the classic, rapidly reversible features responsive to epinephrine. A later wave — due to de novo synthesis of lipid mediators and cytokine-driven recruitment and activation of additional effector cells — can produce a delayed recurrence of symptoms hours after apparent clinical resolution. Mechanistically, recurrence can therefore reflect (a) a true second inflammatory wave (late-phase cellular activation and renewed mediator production), (b) ongoing or intermittent antigen exposure or depot absorption (for example slow release from ingested or depot-forming drugs), or (c) pharmacodynamic waning of therapeutics (epinephrine’s effect after a single IM dose is rapid but not permanent). Distinguishing these possibilities is important because the implications for monitoring and treatment differ: continued antigen exposure or a persistent circulating trigger may require identification and removal, whereas a late-phase wave reflects intrinsic immune kinetics.

Terminology helps clarify these different clinical patterns. A biphasic reaction is a recurrence of anaphylactic signs after a symptom-free interval and without further exposure to the putative trigger. Biphasic recurrences classically occur within the first 24 hours, though reports extend to 72–78 hours. Multiphasic anaphylaxis refers to two or more distinct recurrences (≥2 relapses) after the initial event. Refractory anaphylaxis denotes an episode that does not adequately respond to appropriately administered epinephrine (persistent or progressive instability despite correct treatment). Protracted anaphylaxis describes a single sustained episode lasting many hours without a symptom-free interval. These distinctions are clinically useful: biphasic/multiphasic courses imply discrete relapses separated by recovery, whereas refractory/protracted disease implies ongoing instability despite therapy.

Epidemiologically, true biphasic reactions are uncommon. Most modern series place the incidence in the low single digits (roughly 4–6% in many reports), with wide variation between studies due to differing definitions and observation practices. The majority of biphasic events occur within 6–24 hours of the index reaction, but delayed recurrences up to several days have been documented, which is why observation strategies and patient counselling are debated.

Several clinical features have been associated with a higher risk of biphasic or more severe courses: a more severe initial presentation (profound hypotension, hypoxaemia or respiratory compromise), delayed administration of the first epinephrine dose, requirement for multiple epinephrine doses during the initial event, and underlying mast cell disorders such as systemic mastocytosis. Systemic mastocytosis increases mast cell numbers and/or reactivity, raises baseline mediator release (often reflected in elevated baseline tryptase), and predisposes to exaggerated, prolonged or relapsing mediator-driven events. In the context of mast cell disorders, even small triggers can produce severe or unpredictable courses.

Drug-induced anaphylaxis varies by drug class. Macrolides (clarithromycin) are an uncommon cause of true IgE-mediated anaphylaxis but have been implicated in immediate hypersensitivity reactions. First-dose anaphylaxis to an apparently novel macrolide can occur either because of prior sensitization through cross-reactive determinants or because of non–IgE-mediated direct mast cell activation. In this report, clarithromycin was the temporal trigger, but the patient’s preceding cutaneous reaction to clindamycin and elevated tryptase raise the possibility that an underlying mast cell disorder amplified the response.

Applying these concepts to the presented patient clarifies why the authors classify her course as multiphasic rather than refractory or protracted. She experienced an initial anaphylactic event followed by several discrete relapses separated by clinical improvement, and each relapse responded promptly and fully to intramuscular epinephrine. That pattern — recovery to near-baseline between episodes and repeated responsiveness to standard therapy — fits multiphasic anaphylaxis. The gradually decreasing intensity of successive episodes is consistent with depletion of readily releasable mediators, diminishing antigen effect, or attenuation of recruited-cell responses over time. Contributing factors likely include intrinsic mast cell hyperreactivity suggested by elevated baseline tryptase and possible systemic mastocytosis; however, absence of ongoing exposure (only a single oral clarithromycin dose) and the timing of relapses point toward intermittent late-phase mediator waves and pharmacodynamic intervals rather than continuous refractory shock.

In summary, multiphasic anaphylaxis is best understood as recurrent waves of mediator-driven pathology arising from the biology of mast cell/basophil activation, late-phase mediator synthesis and cellular recruitment, potential persistent antigen exposure, and the time-limited effects of therapies. Recognition of the phenotype (biphasic versus multiphasic versus refractory) depends on careful observation of symptom-free intervals and treatment responsiveness and has direct relevance for monitoring and further diagnostic evaluation — for example, the search for an underlying mast cell disorder when relapsing or unusually severe courses occur, as illustrated by this case.
