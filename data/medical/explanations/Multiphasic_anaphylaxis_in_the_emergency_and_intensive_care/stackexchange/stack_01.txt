### [Morning Report] In an adult who develops hypotension, hypoxemia, generalized urticaria, and facial swelling within 1–2 hours of taking an antibiotic, what consensus diagnostic criteria confirm anaphylaxis, and which of those criteria are met in this presentation?

The widely used consensus diagnostic criteria for anaphylaxis are those developed by the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases/Food Allergy and Anaphylaxis Network (NIAID/FAAN) and adopted by international bodies (and echoed in World Allergy Organization guidance). They define anaphylaxis as likely when any one of three clinical scenarios is present within minutes to hours of exposure to a likely allergen:

1) Acute onset of illness (minutes to hours) with involvement of skin or mucosal tissue (hives, flushed skin, angioedema) AND at least one of the following: respiratory compromise (dyspnea, wheeze, stridor, hypoxemia) or reduced blood pressure/end-organ hypoperfusion.

2) Two or more of the following that occur rapidly after exposure: skin–mucosal involvement, respiratory compromise, reduced blood pressure or associated symptoms, or persistent severe gastrointestinal symptoms.

3) Reduced blood pressure after exposure to a known allergen (for adults: systolic blood pressure <90 mm Hg or >30% decrease from baseline).

Which criteria this case meets and why:
This patient clearly meets the consensus diagnostic criteria for anaphylaxis. She had an acute onset (within 1 hour of taking clarithromycin) of generalized urticaria and facial swelling (skin/mucosal involvement) together with respiratory compromise (hypoxemia, dyspnea) and profound hypotension (BP 74/50 mm Hg). That presentation satisfies criterion 1 (skin/mucosal signs plus respiratory compromise or hypotension). It also meets criterion 2 because two or more organ systems were involved (skin, respiratory, and circulatory). Finally, it meets criterion 3 because she developed marked hypotension after exposure to a likely allergen.

Clinical point: the diagnosis is clinical and should prompt immediate treatment with intramuscular epinephrine without waiting for laboratory confirmation. The presence of multiple organ-system involvement and hypotension predicts severe anaphylaxis and need for close monitoring (and explains the rationale for ICU observation in this multiphasic case).
