### [Differential Diagnosis] What clinical and historical features help distinguish ACE inhibitor–induced bradykinin-mediated angioedema from histamine-mediated anaphylaxis, and why does this distinction matter for acute therapy?

Key point: ACE inhibitor–related angioedema is a bradykinin‑mediated increase in vascular permeability that looks like facial or oropharyngeal swelling but is not an allergic, mast‑cell driven process. Distinguishing the two is important because the acute treatments and expected responses differ.

Clinical and historical features that favor ACE‑inhibitor (bradykinin) angioedema over histamine‑mediated anaphylaxis
- Medication history: current or recent use of an ACE inhibitor (eg, lisinopril, enalapril). ACE‑I angioedema can occur anytime during therapy—even after months or years—so a remote start date does not exclude it.
- Distribution and symptoms: prominent localized non‑pitting edema of the face, lips, tongue, and/or oropharynx (often asymmetric) without urticaria or generalized flushing. Isolated deep tissue swelling is typical.
- Lack of systemic allergic features: minimal or absent pruritus, no widespread urticaria, and typically no bronchospasm or wheeze. Hypotension and shock are uncommon unless airway obstruction produces severe hypoxia.
- Onset and temporal pattern: often gradual over hours (but can be rapid), and recurrences may occur despite antihistamines/steroids/epinephrine because the mechanism persists while ACE is active.
- Objective testing: normal serum tryptase (no mast cell degranulation) during episodes supports bradykinin mechanism; elevated tryptase points toward mast‑cell mediated anaphylaxis.
- Risk‑factors: female sex, Black race, smoking, and prior angioedema history increase ACE‑I risk.

Clinical and historical features that favor histamine‑mediated anaphylaxis
- Immediate onset (minutes) after exposure to a new/known allergen (food, drug, insect sting), with rapid progression.
- Prominent cutaneous signs: generalized urticaria, intense pruritus, flushing.
- Multi‑system involvement: bronchospasm, respiratory distress, hypotension, syncope, gastrointestinal cramping or vomiting.
- Rapid response to intramuscular epinephrine and to supportive antihistamines/steroids (though steroids do not acutely treat anaphylaxis, many mast‑cell mediated reactions improve after epinephrine).

Why this distinction matters for acute therapy
- Mechanism dictates response: bradykinin‑mediated swelling does not result from histamine/mast‑cell mediator release, so antihistamines and corticosteroids (and frequently epinephrine) are often ineffective. Conversely, anaphylaxis typically responds promptly to intramuscular epinephrine, which is life‑saving.
- Airway strategy differs in emphasis: in suspected ACE‑I angioedema, the priority is early airway assessment and aggressive airway control (prepare for difficult intubation, early ENT/anesthesia involvement, consideration of awake fiberoptic intubation or early surgical airway), because medical reversal is unreliable. In anaphylaxis, epinephrine is the first intervention and commonly reverses airway and circulatory collapse.
- Targeted pharmacologic options: for bradykinin angioedema, specific therapies (when available and in severe cases) include C1 esterase inhibitor concentrate, icatibant (bradykinin B2 receptor antagonist), or kallikrein inhibitors; fresh frozen plasma has been used when specific agents unavailable. These agents are not helpful in histamine‑mediated anaphylaxis. Conversely, delaying epinephrine for presumed ACE‑I angioedema when anaphylaxis is still possible risks fatal outcome.
- Pragmatic approach: when the diagnosis is unclear at presentation, treat for anaphylaxis first (IM epinephrine, airway, oxygen, fluids) because of its immediacy and mortality risk. If the picture clearly fits ACE‑I angioedema (ACE use, isolated oropharyngeal/facial swelling, absence of urticaria/systemic features, normal tryptase), prioritize airway control and pursue bradykinin‑directed therapy and stop the ACE inhibitor.

Clinical pearl: Always ask about ACE inhibitors in any isolated facial/oropharyngeal swelling. If ACE‑I angioedema is likely, remove the drug, monitor airway closely, gather tryptase if there is diagnostic uncertainty, and mobilize airway expertise and bradykinin‑targeted therapies rather than relying on antihistamines/steroids alone. If uncertain, give epinephrine immediately—it's the correct first move when anaphylaxis cannot be excluded.
