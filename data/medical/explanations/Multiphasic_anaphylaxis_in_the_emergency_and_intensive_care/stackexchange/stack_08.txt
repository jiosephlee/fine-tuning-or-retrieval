### [Morning Report] How do you interpret paired acute and baseline tryptase levels using the 20% + 2 ng/mL rule, and how does this help confirm mast cell activation when baseline tryptase is elevated?

Pearl: Tryptase is most useful when you interpret an “acute” sample against a true baseline — not by an absolute cut‑off alone. The validated rule is: an acute rise is meaningful for mast‑cell activation if the acute tryptase > (baseline × 1.2) + 2 ng/mL. This accounts for both proportional and fixed increases and works when baseline tryptase is normal or chronically elevated (eg, mast cell disease).

Why that formula? Tryptase has both a baseline component (constitutive mast‑cell burden) and a release component during activation. A fixed absolute threshold misses clinically important rises when baseline is high, and a pure percentage missessmall absolute rises at low baselines. The 20% + 2 rule captures both scenarios and has been validated against clinical anaphylaxis.

How to use it in practice
- Obtain the acute tryptase as soon as feasible and ideally within 1–4 hours of symptom onset (levels usually peak in that window).  
- Obtain a baseline tryptase at least 24 hours after complete clinical resolution (often at follow‑up).  
- Apply the formula: acute > baseline × 1.2 + 2. If yes, that supports mast‑cell activation from anaphylaxis.

How this helps when baseline is elevated
If a patient has an elevated baseline (for example, from systemic mastocytosis or hereditary alpha‑tryptasemia), an absolute elevated acute tryptase may simply reflect the baseline. The 20% + 2 rule requires a rise above that baseline proportional to the underlying level, so a true degranulation event is still detectable. For example, with a baseline of 30 ng/mL the threshold for activation is 30 × 1.2 + 2 = 38 ng/mL — so an acute 35 ng/mL would not meet criteria, but 40 ng/mL would.

Clinical implications in the presented case
Because the team obtained an acute sample during a relapse and a later baseline that were both elevated, applying the 20% + 2 rule distinguishes whether those acute elevations represented true mast‑cell activation (anaphylaxis) superimposed on chronic elevation versus only chronically high tryptase. A positive result supports that the multiphasic episodes were mediated by mast‑cell degranulation and strengthens the case for an underlying mast‑cell disorder, prompting specialist evaluation (hematology/allergy, targeted bone‑marrow testing, KIT D816V testing, and consideration of hereditary alpha‑tryptasemia testing). Remember limitations: timing matters (missed peak can give false‑negative), other causes can raise tryptase modestly, and a negative result does not exclude clinical anaphylaxis if sampling was delayed.
