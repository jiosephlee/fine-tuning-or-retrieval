Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

### [Differential Diagnosis] Does prior delayed rash to clindamycin predict immediate anaphylaxis to clarithromycin, and what is known about cross-reactivity between lincosamides and macrolides?

Short answer: No — a delayed, likely T‑cell–mediated maculopapular rash to clindamycin does not reliably predict immediate, IgE‑mediated anaphylaxis to clarithromycin. True cross‑reactivity between lincosamides (eg, clindamycin) and macrolides (eg, clarithromycin) is biologically unlikely and is not supported by meaningful clinical evidence.

Why: The clinical pattern matters. A rash that appears after days of clindamycin is most consistent with a delayed-type hypersensitivity (T‑cell mediated) or a non‑specific antibiotic eruption. Those reactions do not imply circulating drug‑specific IgE, which is the usual mechanism for immediate anaphylaxis occurring within minutes to hours of exposure. Clarithromycin produced rapid-onset hypotension, urticaria and respiratory symptoms in this patient — a picture of immediate mast cell mediator release. Two different immunologic mechanisms acting on structurally unrelated drugs explain why the earlier clindamycin rash would not predict the later clarithromycin anaphylaxis.

On cross‑reactivity: Lincosamides and macrolides have distinct chemical structures and different antigenic determinants. Published reports of cross‑reactivity between these classes are rare and largely anecdotal. By contrast, cross‑reactivity is more clinically relevant within antibiotic families that share common beta‑lactam or sulfonamide moieties. Therefore, when a patient has a delayed clindamycin reaction, avoiding all macrolides is not automatically necessary on that basis alone.

Clinical implications: In this case clarithromycin should be considered the presumptive culprit and documented as a severe immediate allergy. Because the patient suffered severe anaphylaxis (and probable mast cell disorder), skin testing or drug challenge is not appropriate acutely and may be contraindicated; referral to an allergist/immunologist for later specialist evaluation (including cautious testing strategies if necessary, baseline and event tryptase measurement review, and counseling) is the right next step. For future antibiotic choices, avoid clarithromycin and possibly other macrolides unless an allergist performs graded challenge or testing; do not assume macrolide tolerance based on a prior delayed reaction to clindamycin. Finally, recognize that mast cell disorders (elevated baseline tryptase in this patient) increase the risk and severity of anaphylaxis irrespective of cross‑reactivity, so emphasis should be on avoidance of the identified culprit, education, and ready access to epinephrine.
