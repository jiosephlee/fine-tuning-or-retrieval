### [Morning Report] When managing multiphasic anaphylaxis in the ICU, what are the benefits and risks of preemptive continuous epinephrine infusion versus expectant management with rapid intramuscular dosing at relapse, and how would you individualize this decision?

Short answer: preemptive continuous IV epinephrine buys control of ongoing mediator-driven cardiovascular collapse and can prevent further relapses, but it substitutes one life‑saving drug for a continuous vasoactive therapy that carries predictable cardiometabolic and monitoring burdens. Expectant management with rapid intramuscular (IM) dosing spares the patient the toxicity and complexity of an infusion, but risks recurrent, rapidly progressive episodes that may be harder to treat once established and that require immediate availability of skilled staff. The correct choice is individualized to the patient’s physiology, the pattern of relapses, comorbidity and the resources available.

Clinical reasoning and how I decide at the bedside: epinephrine is both anti‑mediator (mast cell stabilisation, bronchodilation) and a vasopressor. If relapses are infrequent, milder, and the patient is hemodynamically stable with reliable continuous monitoring and immediate access to IM epinephrine, an expectant approach is reasonable because it avoids the cardiovascular and metabolic side effects of a continuous infusion. If relapses are frequent, progressive, or associated with persistent hypotension or hypoxaemia despite appropriately dosed IM injections or require repeated epinephrine doses in short succession, the underlying process is effectively “uncontrolled.” In that situation a continuous infusion is preferred to maintain stable perfusion, allow titration, and prevent the delay or underdosing that occurs when relying on repeated IM injections.

How this applies to the presented case: this patient had a severe initial shock, multiple recurrences within 12 hours, and required four IM doses (two at only 0.3 mg). She also has elevated baseline tryptase and probable mast cell disease — both features that raise the pretest probability of further relapses. Those factors lower my threshold to move from expectant care to a continuous infusion if she relapses again or if instability recurs shortly after an IM dose. Because in her course the relapses decreased in intensity and resolved with IM epinephrine and she quickly stabilized, prolonged infusion was not necessary — but close ICU observation and readiness to start an infusion promptly were appropriate.

Practical benefits and risks, briefly:
- Benefits of preemptive continuous infusion: immediate and titratable haemodynamic support; suppression of recurrent mediator effects; avoids repeated delays or underdosing with IM auto‑injectors; useful when ongoing vascular leak or persistent mediator release is suspected (eg, mast cell disorders, refractory or recurrent episodes).
- Risks of infusion: tachyarrhythmia, myocardial ischemia (especially in older patients or coronary disease), severe hypertension on overshoot, lactic acidosis, hyperglycaemia, peripheral ischaemia if extravasation occurs, need for arterial pressure monitoring/ICU staffing and careful titration — and the possibility of masking inadequate source control (eg, ongoing allergen exposure).
- Benefits of expectant IM strategy: avoids continuous adrenergic exposure and its metabolic/cardiac toxicity, simple, familiar, and effective in many biphasic cases.
- Risks of expectant IM strategy: delayed recognition or administration during sudden relapse, underdosing (common with 0.3 mg auto‑injectors in adults >50 kg), multiple repeated doses, potential for escalation to refractory shock if relapses are rapid.

How I individualize dosing and monitoring when choosing an infusion: start low and titrate to effect rather than box‑fixed high doses. For adults a common approach is to begin at a low infusion (eg, 0.03–0.1 µg/kg/min or facility standard low‑dose range) and titrate to restore/maintain MAP and oxygenation; increase carefully if needed and wean slowly after a sustained period (several hours) of stability. Ensure continuous ECG, frequent BP and glucose checks, consider arterial line if vasopressor requirement is significant, and watch for arrhythmia or ischemia. Maintain aggressive volume resuscitation and adjunctive therapies (high‑flow O2, H1/H2 blockade when indicated, avoid routine steroids solely to prevent biphasic reactions).

Operational thresholds I use in practice: pursue continuous infusion when the patient requires more than two appropriately dosed epinephrine administrations within a short interval, has persistent hypotension or need for escalating doses, shows progressive relapses despite IM therapy, or cannot be reliably observed and treated immediately (limited staffing). In patients with known or suspected mast cell disease, lower the threshold for ICU admission and consider early infusion if there is any recurrence or if initial presentation was severe.

Takeaway pearl: in multiphasic anaphylaxis the decision is not binary — it’s dynamic. Use the clinical trajectory (frequency and severity of relapses), host risk (cardiac disease, mast cell disorders, obesity — which raises the risk of IM underdosing), and monitoring resources to choose between careful expectant management with immediate IM capability versus early controlled IV infusion with ICU‑level monitoring. In the case you presented, ICU observation with readiness to start a titratable epinephrine infusion if relapses recurred was the prudent middle path; the actual need for infusion is dictated by subsequent clinical response.
