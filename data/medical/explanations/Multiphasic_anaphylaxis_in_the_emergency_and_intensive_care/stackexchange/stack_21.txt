### [Management] What type and volume of intravenous fluids are recommended in anaphylactic shock, and how do epinephrine’s alpha-1 and beta-2 effects interact with the pathophysiology of distributive shock and capillary leak?

In anaphylactic shock the immediate priorities are to restore perfusion and reverse airway/bronchial edema — fluids and epinephrine work together for those goals. Use isotonic crystalloids (0.9% saline or a balanced crystalloid) and give rapid, relatively large boluses because anaphylaxis produces both precapillary vasodilatation and marked capillary leak with loss of intravascular volume.

A practical approach is to give an initial rapid bolus of 500–1000 mL of isotonic crystalloid in adults immediately while giving epinephrine. If hypotension persists give additional boluses up to roughly 20 mL/kg (often 1–2 liters or more in an average adult) and reassess frequently; expect to give multiple liters in severe cases because fluid rapidly shifts into the interstitium. Monitor respiratory status and cardiac function (ischemia/volume overload risk) as you replete; patients with heart failure or advanced age need closer balance between restoring intravascular volume and avoiding pulmonary edema. If hypotension is refractory to repeated boluses and repeated appropriate-dose IM epinephrine, start continuous IV vasopressor therapy (epinephrine infusion preferred in severe refractory anaphylaxis) while obtaining central access as needed.

How epinephrine’s receptor effects address the pathophysiology: anaphylaxis is a distributive shock from widespread vasodilatation plus increased microvascular permeability (capillary leak), and bronchospasm and mucosal edema from mediator release. Epinephrine’s alpha-1 agonism causes arteriolar and venous vasoconstriction that raises systemic vascular resistance and venous return and reduces mucosal and dermal edema, directly opposing the vasodilatory and permeability effects that produce hypotension and airway compromise. Its beta-1 effects increase heart rate and contractility, improving cardiac output against the reduced afterload and hypovolaemia. Its beta-2 effects produce bronchodilation and increase intracellular cAMP in mast cells, which suppresses further mediator release; beta-2–mediated vasodilation can theoretically oppose alpha vasoconstriction, but at therapeutic doses in anaphylaxis the alpha-1 effect usually predominates systemically while the beta-2 bronchodilation and mast-cell stabilisation are clinically important.

Clinically, therefore, fluids replace the intravascular volume lost through capillary leak while epinephrine both restores vascular tone and treats airway/bronchial pathology and ongoing mediator release. Because fluids extravasate in severe reactions, be prepared for repeated boluses and for early escalation to continuous IV epinephrine (or other vasopressors if needed) when IM doses and fluids do not restore perfusion.
