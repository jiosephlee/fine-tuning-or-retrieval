### [Clinical Pearls] Which index-event factors increase the risk of a biphasic anaphylactic reaction (for example, delayed epinephrine, severe presentation, multiple epinephrine doses, prior biphasic reactions), and how do these influence observation time?

Teaching pearl:

A biphasic anaphylactic reaction is most likely when the index event is severe, incompletely treated, prolonged in exposure/absorption, or occurs in a patient with underlying mast cell disease — and those same features should prolong your observation.

Why these factors matter: delayed or inadequate epinephrine allows ongoing mediator-driven pathology to continue and re‑emerge once the short-lived hemodynamic benefits wear off; a severe initial presentation (airway involvement, profound hypotension or hypoxaemia, need for vasopressors, ICU care) reflects a large mediator release and greater physiological stress, which both increase the chance of recurrence; needing multiple epinephrine doses or escalating care is a marker that the reaction is harder to control and therefore more likely to relapse; and disorders that increase mast cell burden or reactivity (elevated baseline tryptase, suspected or confirmed mastocytosis) predispose to repeated mediator release and multiphasic courses. Other contextual risks that increase likelihood of recurrence are exposures with prolonged absorption (oral medications, depot injections, ongoing allergen exposure), comorbid severe asthma or significant cardiovascular disease, and use of beta‑blockers (which can blunt epinephrine response). Prior biphasic reactions, when known, also predict recurrence.

How that changes observation and disposition: use a risk‑stratified, not one‑size‑fits‑all, approach. Patients who had a mild reaction, rapid and complete response to a single timely intramuscular epinephrine dose, no airway or cardiovascular involvement, no comorbidities and clear removal of the trigger can be observed for a short period (commonly 4–6 hours after complete symptom resolution) and discharged with written instructions and an auto‑injector. Patients with any high‑risk feature — severe initial presentation (hypotension, hypoxaemia, airway compromise), delayed administration of epinephrine, need for multiple epinephrine doses, ongoing absorption risk (oral drug, food still in gut), beta‑blocker use, elevated or unknown baseline tryptase, suspected mast cell disorder, or prior biphasic/refractory anaphylaxis — merit prolonged observation and lower threshold for ICU/telemetry monitoring. In these patients aim for at least 24 hours of observation after the last anaphylactic signs, and consider 24–48 hours (or ICU-level continuous monitoring) for refractory or multiphasic courses or when the trigger cannot be confirmed/removed.

Practical points from the case: this patient had hypotension, hypoxaemia, multiple IM epinephrine doses and suspected mast cell disease — all indicators for extended monitoring. Routine corticosteroids are not supported to prevent biphasic reactions and should not replace careful observation and appropriate escalation of care. Always document peak and baseline tryptase with the recommended timing (as soon as feasible, 1–4 hours after onset for peak, and ≥24 hours after resolution for baseline) to help identify underlying mast cell disorders that will influence future management and observation planning.
