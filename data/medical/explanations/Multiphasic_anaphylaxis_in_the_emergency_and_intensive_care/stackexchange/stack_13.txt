### [Management] What is the current evidence regarding corticosteroid use in anaphylaxis, do steroids prevent biphasic reactions, and what patient-specific harms might you anticipate in someone with diabetes?

Corticosteroids have historically been given in anaphylaxis mainly to try to prevent a delayed (biphasic) recurrence, but the evidence does not support that practice. Multiple observational studies and a systematic review (including 22 studies) found no consistent reduction in biphasic reactions with steroid use; only a single small study suggested benefit while the rest did not. More recent observational data have even associated prehospital steroids with increased likelihood of hospital/ICU admission (confounding by indication is possible), and contemporary guideline panels increasingly advise against routine corticosteroids in straightforward anaphylaxis. In short: steroids do not reliably prevent biphasic (or multiphasic) reactions and should not be given reflexively for that purpose.

When might you still consider steroids? In select situations where there is another clear indication (severe protracted urticaria/angioedema refractory to antihistamines, comorbid adrenal insufficiency, or other inflammatory comorbidity), or perhaps to blunt prolonged laryngeal oedema in consultation with specialists. Even then, recognize the evidence for preventing biphasic events is weak.

For a patient with type 2 diabetes, the harms of systemic corticosteroids are immediate and clinically important. Expect pronounced hyperglycaemia within hours of even a single moderate-to-high dose; in an insulin‑naïve patient this commonly requires initiation or escalation of insulin (sliding‑scale or basal/bolus as appropriate) during the inpatient stay. Acute hyperglycaemia increases the risk of osmotic symptoms, infection, and—if severe and prolonged—ketosis or hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state in vulnerable patients. Other short‑term steroid harms to anticipate are fluid retention and worsening hypertension, agitation or delirium in older patients, transient leukocytosis (which can confuse infection assessment), and impaired wound healing or increased infection risk if there are breaks in the skin or invasive procedures planned.

Practically: do not use steroids routinely to prevent biphasic anaphylaxis. If you decide steroids are needed for another reason in a patient with diabetes, use the lowest effective dose for the shortest duration, institute frequent capillary glucose monitoring, plan insulin therapy rather than relying on oral hypoglycaemic agents (hold sulfonylureas if oral intake is uncertain), and monitor for fluid and neuropsychiatric effects. Finally, base observation and disposition decisions on clinical severity, response to epinephrine, and risk factors for relapse (delayed epinephrine, severe initial presentation, multiple epinephrine doses, mast cell disorders), not on whether steroids were given.
