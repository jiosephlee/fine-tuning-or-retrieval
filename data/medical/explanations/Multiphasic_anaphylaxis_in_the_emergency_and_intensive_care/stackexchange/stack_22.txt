Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

### [Pathophysiology] By which receptor-mediated mechanisms does epinephrine reverse the life-threatening features of anaphylaxis, and how does it affect further mediator release from mast cells and basophils?

Epinephrine works in anaphylaxis by engaging distinct adrenergic receptors on the vasculature, heart, airways and immune effector cells to rapidly reverse the physiologic consequences of massive mast cell/basophil activation and to blunt further mediator release.

Hemodynamic and airway effects are mediated primarily through alpha‑1 and beta‑1/beta‑2 receptors. Alpha‑1 stimulation produces powerful peripheral vasoconstriction that increases systemic vascular resistance, raises blood pressure, reduces mucosal and laryngeal edema (improving the airway), and decreases vascular leak that otherwise produces angioedema and urticaria. Beta‑1 stimulation increases cardiac contractility and heart rate, improving cardiac output and counteracting distributive shock. Beta‑2 stimulation causes bronchodilation (reducing bronchospasm), decreases microvascular permeability in the lung, and improves oxygenation.

Beyond these hemodynamic and bronchodilator actions, epinephrine directly suppresses further mediator release from mast cells and basophils via beta‑2 receptor signaling. Beta‑2 receptor activation increases intracellular cyclic AMP in these cells, which interferes with the signaling pathways (calcium mobilization and exocytosis) required for IgE‑mediated degranulation and reduces synthesis/release of proinflammatory mediators (histamine, tryptase, leukotrienes and prostaglandins). This beta‑2–cAMP–mediated “mast cell stabilizing” effect helps limit propagation or recurrence of the reaction, although it cannot undo mediators already released.

Clinically this receptor profile explains why intramuscular epinephrine is first‑line: it rapidly delivers alpha‑ and beta‑agonism to restore blood pressure, open the airway and blunt further mediator release. It also explains limitations: epinephrine attenuates further degranulation but does not instantly neutralize mediators already in the circulation (hence need for supportive measures such as fluids, oxygen and repeated doses if symptoms recur).
