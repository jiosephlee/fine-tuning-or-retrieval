### [Clinical Pearls] How do you differentiate biphasic, multiphasic, and refractory anaphylaxis, and which definition best fits a patient who has four discrete anaphylactic episodes after a single exposure, each resolving fully between episodes?

Think of the three patterns as clinical courses, not different diseases — you distinguish them by timing, response to epinephrine, and by whether symptoms relapse after complete resolution.

Biphasic anaphylaxis is a single initial anaphylactic event that resolves, followed hours later (classically within 24, most often within 6–12 hours) by a recurrence of symptoms without further exposure to the trigger. The key features are an asymptomatic interval and a second wave of symptoms; the second wave may be milder or as severe as the first.

Multiphasic anaphylaxis is the same phenomenon extended to more than two discrete episodes — multiple distinct relapses separated by intervals of full or near‑full clinical recovery, again without re‑exposure. Definitions are not perfectly harmonized in the literature, but “multiphasic” is used when you see three or more distinct episodes.

Refractory anaphylaxis is persistent, ongoing anaphylaxis that does not respond adequately to correctly administered epinephrine (or that requires continuous high‑dose vasopressor support). There is no true asymptomatic interval; symptoms are sustained or progressive despite treatment.

Apply this to the case: this patient had one exposure to clarithromycin, an initial severe anaphylactic episode with complete clinical response after treatment, and then three further separate relapses over the next 12 hours, each resolving fully between episodes with intramuscular epinephrine. That pattern — multiple discrete recurrences after intervals of clinical recovery and without re‑exposure — best fits multiphasic anaphylaxis. It is not refractory because each episode responded to epinephrine.

Clinical implications: multiphasic courses are uncommon but predictable from the same risk factors for biphasic reactions (severe initial presentation, delayed or underdosed epinephrine, need for multiple epinephrine doses, and conditions that increase mast cell burden or mediator release such as mast cell disorders). Because definitions and incidence vary, management is pragmatic: ensure adequate epinephrine dosing up front, monitor patients with severe presentation or multiple doses for an extended period (individualize beyond routine 4–6 hours), consider ICU-level monitoring if relapses are frequent or severe, and reserve continuous epinephrine infusion for true refractory cases. Routine corticosteroids have not been shown to prevent biphasic or multiphasic relapses and should not substitute for careful observation and appropriate use of epinephrine.
