Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

### [Management] What does current evidence suggest about the role of corticosteroids in acute anaphylaxis, particularly in preventing biphasic reactions, and what potential harms or unintended consequences should steer practice away from routine steroid use?

Short answer
Current evidence does not support routine use of corticosteroids in the acute management of anaphylaxis for the purpose of preventing biphasic (or multiphasic) reactions. Randomized and observational data and systematic reviews show no convincing reduction in biphasic events, and potential harms and unintended consequences make routine steroid administration inadvisable. Steroids should be reserved for clear indications (eg, concomitant severe bronchospasm/asthma exacerbation, adrenal insufficiency) and not given reflexively to “prevent” a late phase.

Why steroids were used historically — and why that rationale is weak
The theoretical rationale is that corticosteroids suppress late inflammatory gene transcription and cellular recruitment and therefore might blunt a delayed, late-phase mediator response. That biologic effect requires hours to manifest (gene transcription, protein synthesis) and is not immediate. Most biphasic recurrences occur within the first 24 hours (commonly within 6–12 hours), and mast cell–mediated mediator release occurs early; therefore a single steroid dose given at presentation is biologically unlikely to abolish an early-to-intermediate recurrence.

What the evidence shows
- Systematic reviews and the preponderance of observational studies do not demonstrate that corticosteroids reduce the risk of biphasic anaphylaxis. The case report cites a 2017 systematic review of 22 studies in which only one study suggested benefit and 21 showed no reduction in biphasic risk.  
- Some contemporary analyses even show associations between prehospital steroid administration and higher rates of hospital or ICU admission (a 2019 study cited in the report). While confounding by indication may contribute, these data underscore the absence of clear benefit and raise concerns about unintended consequences.  
- Consequently, contemporary guidelines have moved away from routine steroid use in anaphylaxis and emphasize prompt epinephrine, supportive care and risk‑stratified observation instead.

Potential harms and unintended consequences
- Immediate physiologic harms from a single dose are usually small, but in vulnerable patients they matter: hyperglycaemia (important in diabetics like the patient in this case), mood/psych effects, and—if repeated—immunosuppression and infection risk.  
- Steroid administration can encourage anchoring on an ineffective preventive strategy and distract from evidence‑based measures (timely epinephrine, appropriate observation, measurement of tryptase when indicated).  
- Use of steroids may lengthen hospital stays or increase admissions (reported associations), contribute to polypharmacy (the case patient received both prednisone and dexamethasone because of protocol differences), and complicate glycemic control in diabetics.  
- Finally, giving steroids may induce a false sense of security about preventing recurrence and thereby change observation or disposition decisions inappropriately.

Clinical application to the presented case
In the presented multiphasic anaphylaxis the team administered systemic corticosteroids (IV prednisone daily for 5 days plus one dose of dexamethasone) after the first recurrence because of traditional practice. The patient ultimately had no further relapses, but this single case does not establish causation; it is consistent with broader data showing spontaneous resolution in most cases and no clear steroid effect. Importantly, the patient had diabetes—an individual risk for steroid-related hyperglycaemia—highlighting one of the harms to weigh before routine steroid use.

Practical guidance / teaching pearls
- First-line treatment: give intramuscular epinephrine promptly and repeat as needed; provide airway, breathing, circulation support.  
- Do not give corticosteroids routinely solely to prevent biphasic reactions. Reserve steroids for separate indications (eg, persistent severe bronchospasm, known adrenal insufficiency, or other accepted reasons).  
- Focus instead on adequate observation and disposition: prolong observation for patients with severe initial presentations, delayed epinephrine, multiple epinephrine doses, comorbid respiratory disease or suspected mast cell disorders (as in this patient). Individualize ICU monitoring for refractory or multiphasic courses and consider continuous epinephrine infusion in refractory cases.  
- When steroids are given, document the indication, choose the shortest effective regimen, and monitor (eg, glucose in diabetics). Continue standard secondary measures (antihistamines for cutaneous symptoms, allergist follow-up, tryptase sampling per protocol).

Bottom line
Routine corticosteroids do not prevent biphasic anaphylaxis and carry potential adverse effects and practical downsides. Management should prioritize rapid epinephrine, appropriate monitoring and disposition decisions; steroids should be used selectively for other clear clinical indications rather than as a reflexive strategy to prevent late anaphylactic relapses.
