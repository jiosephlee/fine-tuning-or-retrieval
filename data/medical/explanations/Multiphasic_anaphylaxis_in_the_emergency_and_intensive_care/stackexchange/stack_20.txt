### [Management] What clinical features should prompt ICU admission for anaphylaxis (for example, hypotension, hypoxemia, need for repeated epinephrine, comorbidities, suspected protracted/multiphasic course), and how would you frame goals of care in the ICU?

In practice the decision to admit anaphylaxis to the ICU is driven less by a single number and more by the patient’s physiologic instability, the intensity and trajectory of the reaction, and the resources needed to safely anticipate or treat further deterioration. Admit to ICU when the clinical picture or treatment needs exceed what a monitored ward can safely manage.

The features that should prompt ICU admission are: persistent or recurrent haemodynamic instability (hypotension requiring vasopressor support, MAP <65 mmHg despite fluids, or repeated boluses of epinephrine), significant respiratory compromise (hypoxaemia requiring high‑flow oxygen, escalating oxygen needs, stridor/airway oedema or concern for rapid progression to loss of airway), need for frequent or repeated intramuscular epinephrine doses (more than one dose, or ongoing requirement within hours), refractory anaphylaxis (inadequate response to appropriately dosed epinephrine), requirement or consideration of continuous intravenous vasoactive infusions (epinephrine, norepinephrine) or airway intervention (protection/intubation), and comorbidities that increase risk of fatal outcome (cardiovascular disease, beta‑blocker or ACE‑inhibitor therapy, severe COPD, pregnancy, advanced age, obesity). Also consider ICU for suspected mast cell disease or markedly elevated baseline tryptase, for multiphasic courses (multiple relapses) or when the trigger is a sustained‑release medication or exposure that may produce prolonged absorption. Finally, limited monitoring resources on the ward (no telemetry, inadequate nursing ratio) are a practical reason to escalate.

How to frame ICU goals of care: first priority is the ABCs — secure airway early when there is progressive upper‑airway oedema or increasing work of breathing, reverse shock with intramuscular epinephrine promptly and escalate to IV vasopressor support if hypotension persists despite fluids, and restore oxygenation with appropriate respiratory support. Simultaneously minimize harm from treatment by using guideline‑based epinephrine dosing (0.01 mg/kg IM up to 0.5 mg in adults; consider IV infusion only in refractory cases under close monitoring), avoid delays in repeated dosing when indicated, and limit unnecessary therapies (recognize that routine corticosteroids have not been shown to prevent biphasic reactions). Establish frequent reassessment intervals (vital signs, airway exam, pulse oximetry, telemetry) and a timeframe for observation — for patients with multiphasic or severe presentations plan prolonged ICU observation (commonly 24 hours or more) with low threshold to re‑escalate. Begin diagnostic workup in parallel without delaying stabilization: draw tryptase at recommended intervals to help identify mast cell disorders, document exposures, and consult allergy/immunology early. Before discharge from ICU, ensure the patient has a clear plan: thresholds for step‑down, education and prescription of epinephrine auto‑injector (appropriate dose), written action plan, allergy follow‑up, and contraindications/alternatives to implicated agents.

Finally, set explicit, time‑bound goals with the patient and team: immediate goal — reverse life‑threatening airway or circulatory compromise; short term (ICU) goal — maintain stability without recurrent anaphylaxis for a defined observation period and complete essential diagnostics; disposition goal — safe transfer to ward when stable off vasopressors and oxygen with no recurrence for the agreed monitoring interval, and discharge planning with follow‑up and prevention measures. This approach balances aggressive resuscitation when needed with targeted monitoring to detect and treat multiphasic relapses.
