Clinical Pearls & Morning Report: Multiphasic_anaphylaxis_in_the_emergency_and_intensive_care

### [Morning Report] In an adult who develops hypotension, hypoxemia, generalized urticaria, and facial swelling within 1–2 hours of taking an antibiotic, what consensus diagnostic criteria confirm anaphylaxis, and which of those criteria are met in this presentation?

The widely used consensus diagnostic criteria for anaphylaxis are those developed by the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases/Food Allergy and Anaphylaxis Network (NIAID/FAAN) and adopted by international bodies (and echoed in World Allergy Organization guidance). They define anaphylaxis as likely when any one of three clinical scenarios is present within minutes to hours of exposure to a likely allergen:

1) Acute onset of illness (minutes to hours) with involvement of skin or mucosal tissue (hives, flushed skin, angioedema) AND at least one of the following: respiratory compromise (dyspnea, wheeze, stridor, hypoxemia) or reduced blood pressure/end-organ hypoperfusion.

2) Two or more of the following that occur rapidly after exposure: skin–mucosal involvement, respiratory compromise, reduced blood pressure or associated symptoms, or persistent severe gastrointestinal symptoms.

3) Reduced blood pressure after exposure to a known allergen (for adults: systolic blood pressure <90 mm Hg or >30% decrease from baseline).

Which criteria this case meets and why:
This patient clearly meets the consensus diagnostic criteria for anaphylaxis. She had an acute onset (within 1 hour of taking clarithromycin) of generalized urticaria and facial swelling (skin/mucosal involvement) together with respiratory compromise (hypoxemia, dyspnea) and profound hypotension (BP 74/50 mm Hg). That presentation satisfies criterion 1 (skin/mucosal signs plus respiratory compromise or hypotension). It also meets criterion 2 because two or more organ systems were involved (skin, respiratory, and circulatory). Finally, it meets criterion 3 because she developed marked hypotension after exposure to a likely allergen.

Clinical point: the diagnosis is clinical and should prompt immediate treatment with intramuscular epinephrine without waiting for laboratory confirmation. The presence of multiple organ-system involvement and hypotension predicts severe anaphylaxis and need for close monitoring (and explains the rationale for ICU observation in this multiphasic case).

### [Clinical Pearls] How do you differentiate biphasic, multiphasic, and refractory anaphylaxis, and which definition best fits a patient who has four discrete anaphylactic episodes after a single exposure, each resolving fully between episodes?

Think of the three patterns as clinical courses, not different diseases — you distinguish them by timing, response to epinephrine, and by whether symptoms relapse after complete resolution.

Biphasic anaphylaxis is a single initial anaphylactic event that resolves, followed hours later (classically within 24, most often within 6–12 hours) by a recurrence of symptoms without further exposure to the trigger. The key features are an asymptomatic interval and a second wave of symptoms; the second wave may be milder or as severe as the first.

Multiphasic anaphylaxis is the same phenomenon extended to more than two discrete episodes — multiple distinct relapses separated by intervals of full or near‑full clinical recovery, again without re‑exposure. Definitions are not perfectly harmonized in the literature, but “multiphasic” is used when you see three or more distinct episodes.

Refractory anaphylaxis is persistent, ongoing anaphylaxis that does not respond adequately to correctly administered epinephrine (or that requires continuous high‑dose vasopressor support). There is no true asymptomatic interval; symptoms are sustained or progressive despite treatment.

Apply this to the case: this patient had one exposure to clarithromycin, an initial severe anaphylactic episode with complete clinical response after treatment, and then three further separate relapses over the next 12 hours, each resolving fully between episodes with intramuscular epinephrine. That pattern — multiple discrete recurrences after intervals of clinical recovery and without re‑exposure — best fits multiphasic anaphylaxis. It is not refractory because each episode responded to epinephrine.

Clinical implications: multiphasic courses are uncommon but predictable from the same risk factors for biphasic reactions (severe initial presentation, delayed or underdosed epinephrine, need for multiple epinephrine doses, and conditions that increase mast cell burden or mediator release such as mast cell disorders). Because definitions and incidence vary, management is pragmatic: ensure adequate epinephrine dosing up front, monitor patients with severe presentation or multiple doses for an extended period (individualize beyond routine 4–6 hours), consider ICU-level monitoring if relapses are frequent or severe, and reserve continuous epinephrine infusion for true refractory cases. Routine corticosteroids have not been shown to prevent biphasic or multiphasic relapses and should not substitute for careful observation and appropriate use of epinephrine.

### [Management] What is the recommended initial intramuscular epinephrine dose and site for adults with anaphylaxis, and what specific dose would you give a 104-kg patient?

Recommended initial intramuscular epinephrine for adult anaphylaxis is 0.01 mg/kg (10 µg/kg) up to a practical maximum of 0.5 mg per dose, given into the mid‑anterolateral thigh (vastus lateralis). The thigh provides the most reliable, rapid absorption compared with deltoid or subcutaneous injection and is the recommended site in all major guidelines.

For your 104‑kg patient the weight‑based calculation (0.01 mg/kg) would be 1.04 mg, but you do not give that as a single IM bolus — you cap the initial IM dose at 0.5 mg. Give 0.5 mg IM in the mid‑anterolateral thigh promptly, reassess within minutes, and repeat IM doses (0.3–0.5 mg every 5–15 minutes as needed) or escalate to intravenous therapy/infusion if refractory or hemodynamically unstable despite repeated IM doses.

### [Management] Why can use of a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector lead to underdosing in adults over 50 kg, and what clinical consequences have been associated with initial underdosing compared with 0.5 mg?

The recommended intramuscular epinephrine dose for anaphylaxis is 0.01 mg/kg up to a maximum of 0.5 mg. A 0.3 mg auto‑injector therefore delivers the target dose only up to a body weight of about 30 kg; for adults above ~50 kg it provides substantially less than  guideline dose (for a 70‑kg adult 0.3 mg = 0.0043 mg/kg, well below 0.01 mg/kg). In other words, a 0.3 mg autoinjector can produce a physiologic underdose in the typical adult because it fails to achieve the weight‑based therapeutic concentration needed to promptly reverse mast cell–mediated vasodilation, bronchospasm and edema.

Clinically, initial underdosing has been associated with need for escalation of care. Compared with patients who receive an initial 0.5 mg IM dose, those who initially receive 0.3 mg have required additional epinephrine doses and other interventions more often and have been more likely to require higher‑level care (eg, repeat IM injections, IV epinephrine, ICU admission or prolonged observation). Underdosing can prolong hypotension and hypoxemia, increase the risk of ongoing or recurrent (biphasic/multiphasic) episodes by allowing incomplete early mediator control, and may increase organ hypoperfusion and time to definitive stabilization. The patient in this report illustrates that pattern: initial 0.3 mg produced a transient response but recurrent episodes required further 0.5 mg injections and ICU monitoring.

Practical takeaway: for adults >50 kg aim for 0.5 mg IM epinephrine as first‑line therapy. If only a 0.3 mg auto‑injector is used and clinical response is incomplete or transient, give additional epinephrine promptly (repeat IM every 5–15 minutes as needed) and escalate monitoring/therapy rather than relying on a single 0.3 mg dose. Address system issues (stocking appropriate adult autoinjectors, staff familiarity) to avoid underdosing in time‑sensitive situations.

### [Management] When should you escalate from repeated intramuscular epinephrine to a continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion in anaphylaxis, and what is a reasonable starting dose and monitoring plan for such an infusion?

Escalation to a continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion is not routine for the typical single, rapidly responsive anaphylactic episode. You should move to an infusion when the clinical course is not controlled by appropriately dosed intramuscular epinephrine plus basic supportive care, or when repeated boluses are required to maintain hemodynamic or respiratory stability. Practically, escalate when any of the following apply: persistent or recurrent hypotension (or shock) despite adequate IM epinephrine and isotonic fluid resuscitation; failure to sustain clinical response after two properly administered IM doses (0.01 mg/kg, up to 0.5 mg adult); or repeated relapses over a short interval (for example, needing ≥2 IM doses within hours) that make intermittent boluses impractical and unsafe. In patients with suspected mast cell disorders or multiphasic courses (as in this case), a lower threshold to escalate is reasonable because mediator release may be prolonged and unpredictable.

A reasonable starting infusion and titration plan for an adult is to begin at 0.05–0.1 mcg/kg/min (≈3–7 mcg/min for a 70‑kg patient) and titrate every 5–15 minutes to effect, aiming for restoration of blood pressure, improved perfusion and resolution of airway/respiratory compromise. Typical effective ranges are 0.05–0.5 mcg/kg/min (some patients may require higher doses transiently), but the goal is the minimal dose that achieves hemodynamic and clinical stability. If weight-based dosing is impractical, a pragmatic adult starting infusion is 0.03–0.1 mcg/kg/min (3–7 mcg/min) with stepwise increases; document each change and response.

Monitoring and safety plan: manage the infusion in an ICU setting with continuous ECG, continuous pulse oximetry, and frequent (ideally invasive) blood pressure monitoring—an arterial line is preferred for titration if ongoing vasopressor support is expected. Establish central venous access if feasible to reduce risk of local tissue injury, but peripheral infusion may be used temporarily with a reliable large-bore IV in a proximal vein and frequent site checks. Monitor heart rate, rhythm (watch for tachyarrhythmias and ischemia), urine output, lactate and glucose, and reassess respiratory status and airway edema. Titrate fluids to restore intravascular volume while avoiding fluid overload. Reassess for alternative or adjunctive therapies (additional H1/H2 antihistamines, nebulized bronchodilators for bronchospasm); consider adjunct vasopressors or inotropes (norepinephrine) if response to epinephrine is inadequate. Maintain the infusion for an individualized period — commonly at least several hours after the last recurrence (many clinicians continue 6–24 hours depending on severity and underlying risk such as suspected mastocytosis) and wean gradually while monitoring for relapse.

Finally, document the indications and dosing clearly, obtain serum tryptase per recommended timing, involve allergy/immunology for investigation of underlying mast cell disease, and ensure the patient leaves with clear instructions and an epinephrine auto‑injector.

### [Clinical Pearls] Which patients with anaphylaxis warrant prolonged observation or ICU admission, and what high-risk features predict biphasic or severe relapse that would influence your monitoring duration?

Teaching pearl:

Observation intensity after anaphylaxis should be driven by the severity and behaviour of the initial episode and by patient factors that raise the pre‑test probability of recurrence or of a catastrophic relapse. Patients with mild, single‑episode anaphylaxis who respond rapidly to a single intramuscular epinephrine dose, have no cardiopulmonary instability, few comorbidities and reliable access to care can usually be observed for a short interval on the emergency ward (commonly 4–6 hours after complete resolution) and discharged with an epinephrine auto‑injector and follow‑up. By contrast, patients who are at higher risk for biphasic, multiphasic or life‑threatening relapse deserve prolonged observation or ICU admission.

Which features put a patient into the high‑risk category — and why:
- Severe initial presentation: hypotension or shock, hypoxaemia, respiratory compromise (laryngeal oedema, bronchospasm), or need for intubation. These reflect a large mediator burden and/or cardiovascular collapse and predict unstable physiology that can recur.
- Need for repeated or escalating epinephrine (more than one IM dose, need for continuous IV epinephrine, or vasopressors). Multiple doses imply persistent or recurrent mediator release; such patients have already demonstrated refractory physiology.
- Delayed administration of epinephrine for the index event. Delay is associated with worse outcomes and higher biphasic risk because early control of mediator effects is incomplete.
- Persistent or relapsing symptoms despite standard therapy (ongoing urticaria, angioedema, bronchospasm, hypotension) or episodes occurring after initial apparent resolution. These courses can evolve unpredictably and warrant ICU monitoring.
- Underlying mast cell disorder or high baseline tryptase (systemic mastocytosis, hereditary alpha‑tryptasemia). These conditions increase mast cell burden and propensity for recurrent severe mediator release.
- Significant comorbidities that reduce physiologic reserve — active cardiovascular disease, arrhythmia, poorly controlled asthma, severe obesity, or beta‑blocker use (which can blunt epinephrine response). These increase the risk that a relapse will be catastrophic or harder to treat.
- Unclear or ongoing allergen exposure/absorption (e.g., depot medications, transdermal or oral agents not fully absorbed) — sustained antigen exposure can mimic biphasic anaphylaxis but requires prolonged monitoring.
- Social/health system factors: limited access to emergency care after discharge, inability to self‑administer epinephrine, or poor follow‑up.

How this guides monitoring and disposition:
- ICU admission is indicated for patients with hemodynamic instability requiring vasopressors or fluid resuscitation, those needing airway intervention or continuous IV epinephrine, or those with recurrent significant episodes despite treatment. The ICU allows close titration of vasopressors, airway support and rapid reinstitution of therapy if relapse occurs.
- Prolonged ward observation (24 hours) should be considered for patients with any of the high‑risk features above short of fulminant shock, for those with repeated IM epinephrine doses, or for patients with known mast cell disorders/high baseline tryptase.
- Short observation (4–6 hours after clinical resolution) may be reasonable only for low‑risk patients who received prompt epinephrine, required a single dose, and have no comorbidities or mast cell disorder. Even then, give an auto‑injector and clear discharge instructions.

Ancillary points that affect decisions:
- Corticosteroids should not be relied on to prevent biphasic reactions; routine steroid use does not obviate the need for appropriate observation if risk features are present.
- Obtain tryptase (ideally within 1–4 hours of onset and a baseline ≥24 hours later) when the course is severe, atypical, or when a mast cell disorder is suspected — results inform prognosis and future management.
- Document the number and route of epinephrine doses given; adult dosing should approximate 0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg IM (0.5 mg is preferable in many adults — 0.3 mg auto‑injectors may underdose heavier adults and correlate with more escalation).
- When in doubt, err on the side of longer observation or ICU monitoring; biphasic/multiphasic events are uncommon but can be unpredictable and rapidly life‑threatening.

In the presented case the combination of initial shock with hypoxaemia, need for multiple IM epinephrine doses and persistently elevated tryptase (suggesting a mast cell disorder) justified ICU admission and prolonged monitoring — exactly the constellation that predicts recurrence and warrants intensive observation.

### [Morning Report] What is the optimal timing for serum tryptase sampling in suspected anaphylaxis (number of samples and intervals), and why does timing matter?

Take at least two samples and preferably three. Draw the first tryptase as soon as possible after recognition of anaphylaxis (do not delay life‑saving treatment to obtain it), draw a second (the “peak” sample) at about 1–2 hours after symptom onset (acceptable up to 4 hours), and obtain a third sample to document baseline ≥24 hours after complete clinical resolution (commonly 24–48 hours).

Why this schedule? Tryptase is released from mast cells with rapid kinetics: levels begin to rise within minutes, typically peak around 1–2 hours, and usually fall toward baseline over the next 4–6 hours. Without a timely “acute” sample you can miss the peak and underestimate mast cell activation; without a baseline you cannot tell whether an elevated value reflects acute release or a chronically raised level (as in systemic mastocytosis). Diagnostic interpretation uses the change from baseline (acute tryptase ≥ 1.2 × baseline + 2 ng/mL is considered significant), so both an acute (ideally peak) and a baseline measurement are required. In practice, if only two samples are feasible, obtain one ASAP and a true baseline ≥24 h later; if three are obtainable, add the 1–2 h peak sample to maximize sensitivity. In the case presented, early samples were missed, which limits interpretation and complicates detection of mast cell disease as a predisposing factor for multiphasic anaphylaxis.

### [Morning Report] How do you interpret paired acute and baseline tryptase levels using the 20% + 2 ng/mL rule, and how does this help confirm mast cell activation when baseline tryptase is elevated?

Pearl: Tryptase is most useful when you interpret an “acute” sample against a true baseline — not by an absolute cut‑off alone. The validated rule is: an acute rise is meaningful for mast‑cell activation if the acute tryptase > (baseline × 1.2) + 2 ng/mL. This accounts for both proportional and fixed increases and works when baseline tryptase is normal or chronically elevated (eg, mast cell disease).

Why that formula? Tryptase has both a baseline component (constitutive mast‑cell burden) and a release component during activation. A fixed absolute threshold misses clinically important rises when baseline is high, and a pure percentage missessmall absolute rises at low baselines. The 20% + 2 rule captures both scenarios and has been validated against clinical anaphylaxis.

How to use it in practice
- Obtain the acute tryptase as soon as feasible and ideally within 1–4 hours of symptom onset (levels usually peak in that window).  
- Obtain a baseline tryptase at least 24 hours after complete clinical resolution (often at follow‑up).  
- Apply the formula: acute > baseline × 1.2 + 2. If yes, that supports mast‑cell activation from anaphylaxis.

How this helps when baseline is elevated
If a patient has an elevated baseline (for example, from systemic mastocytosis or hereditary alpha‑tryptasemia), an absolute elevated acute tryptase may simply reflect the baseline. The 20% + 2 rule requires a rise above that baseline proportional to the underlying level, so a true degranulation event is still detectable. For example, with a baseline of 30 ng/mL the threshold for activation is 30 × 1.2 + 2 = 38 ng/mL — so an acute 35 ng/mL would not meet criteria, but 40 ng/mL would.

Clinical implications in the presented case
Because the team obtained an acute sample during a relapse and a later baseline that were both elevated, applying the 20% + 2 rule distinguishes whether those acute elevations represented true mast‑cell activation (anaphylaxis) superimposed on chronic elevation versus only chronically high tryptase. A positive result supports that the multiphasic episodes were mediated by mast‑cell degranulation and strengthens the case for an underlying mast‑cell disorder, prompting specialist evaluation (hematology/allergy, targeted bone‑marrow testing, KIT D816V testing, and consideration of hereditary alpha‑tryptasemia testing). Remember limitations: timing matters (missed peak can give false‑negative), other causes can raise tryptase modestly, and a negative result does not exclude clinical anaphylaxis if sampling was delayed.

### [Differential Diagnosis] After severe or recurrent anaphylaxis with persistently elevated baseline tryptase, who should be evaluated for clonal mast cell disorders, and what initial tests should be ordered?

When an adult has severe, recurrent, unexplained, or atypical anaphylaxis and their baseline tryptase is persistently elevated, you should actively evaluate for a clonal mast cell disorder because mastocytosis (and other clonal mast cell diseases) both raise the risk of severe and recurrent mediator‑driven events and change long‑term management.

Who to evaluate
Evaluate adults who have had severe anaphylaxis (shock, hypoxaemia, need for multiple epinephrine doses, ICU admission), recurrent or biphasic/multiphasic episodes without an obvious ongoing trigger, or anaphylaxis after Hymenoptera stings — if they also have a persistently elevated baseline tryptase. A single elevated tryptase measured after an attack is not enough; persistent elevation is concerning. A baseline tryptase ≥20 ng/mL is one WHO minor criterion for systemic mastocytosis and should prompt formal investigation; values below that (and even modest elevations above the normal reference) also warrant workup when the clinical picture (recurrent/severe/idiopathic anaphylaxis, hepatosplenomegaly, abnormal blood counts, skin lesions, or neuropsychiatric symptoms) raises suspicion.

Initial tests to order (practical, risk‑stratified approach)
1) Confirm tryptase kinetics: obtain an acute sample as soon as feasible during/after an episode (ideally within 1–4 hours of symptom onset) and a baseline sample at least 24–48 hours after full clinical resolution. Use the rise-and-fall pattern: a peak that exceeds baseline by 20% + 2 ng/mL supports mast cell activation; a persistently elevated baseline supports underlying mast cell disease.

2) Baseline investigations: repeat serum total tryptase (when patient is well), CBC with differential, liver function tests and creatinine (to look for organ involvement), and abdominal imaging (ultrasound) if hepatosplenomegaly is suspected on exam.

3) Molecular and hematologic screening from peripheral blood: sensitive testing for KIT D816V mutation by allele‑specific PCR or next‑generation sequencing; if KIT D816V is positive in blood this supports a clonal process and can obviate immediate bone‑marrow sampling in some patients.

4) Allergy and genetic considerations: refer to allergy/immunology for acute/remote testing decisions and to evaluate for triggers. If baseline tryptase is chronically raised and family history or persistently high tryptase is present, order TPSAB1 copy‑number testing (hereditary alpha‑tryptasemia), because that can explain baseline elevation and also increases anaphylaxis risk.

5) Specialist referral and bone‑marrow evaluation: if peripheral blood KIT testing is positive, baseline tryptase ≥20 ng/mL, or clinical/organ findings suggest systemic disease, refer to a hematologist/mast cell disorder center for bone‑marrow biopsy with morphology, flow cytometry for aberrant mast cell markers (CD25, CD2), and KIT testing on marrow. Bone‑marrow histology and immunophenotyping remain essential to confirm systemic mastocytosis by WHO criteria.

Clinical reasoning summary
Persistent tryptase elevation identifies patients in whom released mediators may reflect an underlying excess of clonal mast cells. Finding a clonal disorder matters because it explains unusually severe/recurrent anaphylaxis, guides avoidance and emergency planning, affects use of therapies (eg, aspirin, venom immunotherapy decisions, antipruritic and anti‑mediator strategies), and raises consideration of disease‑directed therapy. Start with confirmatory tryptase kinetics, screen with CBC/LFTs and sensitive peripheral blood KIT testing, consider hereditary alpha‑tryptasemia testing, and proceed to bone‑marrow evaluation when molecular or clinical features point to a clonal mast cell disorder.

### [Pathophysiology] What are the 2022 WHO major and minor diagnostic criteria for systemic mastocytosis, and how can limited bone marrow tissue availability affect your ability to meet the major criterion?

The 2022 WHO diagnostic framework for systemic mastocytosis (SM) is unchanged in its two-step structure: one major criterion plus one minor criterion, or three minor criteria, are required to make the diagnosis.

The major criterion is demonstration, by histology, of multifocal dense aggregates of mast cells (classically defined as aggregates of ≥15 mast cells) in the bone marrow and/or in extracutaneous organs.

The four minor criteria are: (1) >25% of mast cells in the bone marrow or other extracutaneous organ showing atypical (often spindle-shaped) morphology or mast cell atypia on cytology; (2) detection of a pathogenic KIT mutation (most commonly D816V) in bone marrow, peripheral blood, or another extracutaneous organ; (3) aberrant immunophenotype of mast cells with expression of CD25 and/or CD2 (by flow cytometry or immunohistochemistry); and (4) a persistently elevated baseline serum tryptase level >20 ng/mL (provided there is no concomitant clonal myeloid neoplasm that would itself raise tryptase).

How limited bone‑marrow tissue affects the major criterion
The major criterion is a histopathologic one and therefore entirely dependent on obtaining an adequate marrow trephine (core) biopsy that samples enough marrow to detect focal, multifocal aggregates. A small or crushed core, a biopsy that misses involved areas, or reliance only on an aspirate smear can easily miss the characteristic dense mast cell clusters and therefore produce a false‑negative major criterion. In practical terms, an insufficient or non‑representative marrow specimen means you cannot confidently apply the major criterion and must rely on the minor criteria pathway.

Clinical consequences and approach
When tissue is limited, do not overcall absence of the major criterion; pursue the minor-criteria route. Obtain sensitive molecular testing for KIT D816V in peripheral blood (highly specific and may be detectable even when marrow is non‑diagnostic), perform flow cytometry/IHC for CD25/CD2 on any available marrow material, and document baseline tryptase (and repeat after recovery if levels were taken during/after anaphylaxis). If initial marrow is non‑diagnostic and clinical suspicion remains high, repeat a properly sized marrow trephine or sample an extracutaneous site (e.g., liver, lymph node) if involved. In short, inadequate marrow sampling can prevent fulfillment of the major criterion and should prompt additional molecular, immunophenotypic testing and repeat or alternative-site biopsy rather than concluding SM is absent.

### [Differential Diagnosis] How do you distinguish hereditary alpha-tryptasemia from systemic mastocytosis in a patient with elevated baseline tryptase, and what specific testing confirms hereditary alpha-tryptasemia?

Elevated baseline tryptase narrows the differential but does not tell you the mechanism. The two common explanations you’re asking about — hereditary alpha‑tryptasemia (HαT) and systemic mastocytosis (SM) — have very different pathophysiology and therefore different downstream testing.

Think first in terms of mechanism and associated findings. HαT is a germline copy‑number gain of the alpha‑tryptase–encoding TPSAB1 allele that produces chronically higher total serum tryptase without clonal mast cell expansion. Patients often have lifelong mildly-to-moderately elevated baseline tryptase, a family history of elevated tryptase or allergic/functional symptoms (urticaria, flushing, dysautonomia, GI symptoms, connective‑tissue complaints), but they lack the organ infiltration, cytopenias, bone lesions or skin lesions typical of SM. SM is a clonal mast cell disorder (usually KIT D816V) with multifocal dense mast cell aggregates in bone marrow or extracutaneous tissue and systemic features from mast cell infiltration and mediator release; baseline tryptase is frequently >20 ng/mL and other WHO criteria or end‑organ signs are often present.

Clinically, distinguish them by the overall syndrome and by directed testing rather than by baseline tryptase alone. If the history shows lifelong elevated tryptase or a family pattern and no evidence of mast cell organ infiltration, HαT becomes likely. If there are skin lesions (urticaria pigmentosa), hepatosplenomegaly, unexplained cytopenias, osteoporosis/lytic lesions, or very high tryptase with clonal features, pursue SM.

Specific testing to confirm HαT: germline TPSAB1 copy‑number analysis demonstrating extra alpha‑tryptase copies. This is done by specialized molecular assays — digital droplet PCR (ddPCR) or other quantitative copy‑number assays (some labs use MLPA or targeted NGS with copy‑number calling). A positive result is increased TPSAB1 alpha‑tryptase copy number consistent with HαT. Important point: this is germline testing (usually from blood); family testing can corroborate inheritance.

Workup algorithm in practice: measure a true baseline tryptase (≥24 hours after symptoms have resolved). If elevated, obtain KIT D816V testing (sensitive PCR/ddPCR) on peripheral blood and, if suspicion persists or KIT is positive, refer for bone marrow biopsy with histology, mast cell immunophenotyping (CD25/CD2), and KIT testing to apply WHO criteria for SM. If bone marrow and KIT do not support SM and family history or phenotype suggests HαT, order TPSAB1 copy‑number testing (ddPCR) to confirm hereditary alpha‑tryptasemia.

In short: elevated baseline tryptase is a clue only. Confirm HαT by TPSAB1 copy‑number testing (ddPCR/quantitative assay). Exclude or diagnose SM by combining KIT D816V testing and bone marrow evaluation against WHO criteria — because SM (clonal expansion) and HαT (germline copy gain) can coexist, both sets of tests are often necessary.

### [Pathophysiology] What immunologic mechanisms underlie early-phase and late-phase reactions in anaphylaxis, and what pathophysiologic processes are hypothesized to drive biphasic recurrence hours after apparent resolution?

Early-phase anaphylaxis is a classic immediate hypersensitivity reaction: antigen cross-links IgE bound to FcεRI on mast cells and basophils, triggering rapid degranulation and release of preformed mediators (histamine, tryptase, heparin) together with newly generated lipid mediators (prostaglandin D2, leukotrienes C4/D4/E4) and vasodilatory peptides. Those mediators produce the cardinal early physiologic effects — systemic vasodilation and increased vascular permeability (hypotension, angioedema), bronchial smooth‑muscle constriction (wheeze, hypoxaemia), mucous secretion, and cutaneous urticaria — within minutes.

The late‑phase reaction, starting hours after the index event, is not simply “more of the same” degranulation. It reflects a secondary inflammatory program driven by cytokine and chemokine synthesis (eg, TNF‑α, IL‑4, IL‑5, IL‑13), continued production of lipid mediators, and recruitment and activation of inflammatory effector cells — eosinophils, neutrophils, and additional mast cells — into affected tissues. These recruited cells produce proteases, cationic proteins, leukotrienes and other mediators that sustain or re‑amplify edema, bronchospasm and tissue inflammation even after the initial mediator surge has subsided. Tryptase may remain elevated for hours and is a marker of mast cell activation during both phases.

Biphasic (and multiphasic) recurrence is mechanistically heterogeneous and best thought of as several overlapping possibilities rather than a single cause. First, ongoing antigen exposure or delayed/continued absorption (for example residual food in the gut, slow‑release or depot drugs, or mucosal uptake of an ingested allergen) can re‑stimulate the immune response after an apparent clinical recovery. Second, the recruited inflammatory cells and locally produced cytokines can constitute a second, self‑sustaining wave of mediator release hours later; essentially a delayed tissue‑level amplification independent of further antigen. Third, non‑IgE pathways and accessory amplifiers — complement activation, platelet‑activating factor (PAF), and neuroimmune receptors such as MRGPRX2 on mast cells — can produce delayed or repeated activation. Fourth, host factors that increase mast cell burden or reactivity (for example systemic mastocytosis or elevated baseline tryptase) lower the threshold for recurrent mediator release and can prolong the time course. Finally, inadequate or delayed initial therapy (especially delayed epinephrine) may allow more intense inflammation to develop and therefore increase the risk of recurrence.

Clinically this explains several observations from the case: an orally ingested antibiotic (possible ongoing absorption), elevated baseline and peri‑event tryptase suggesting increased mast cell burden, and progressively attenuated relapses consistent with diminishing mediator stores and recruitment dynamics. It also explains why corticosteroids, which target later transcriptional inflammatory pathways, have been appealing to try to prevent biphasic reactions but have not shown convincing prevention in trials — they do not reverse the immediate mediator surge and their timing and magnitude of effect may be inadequate to stop the recruited‑cell secondary wave in many patients.

In practice, therefore, think first about halting the immediate mediator effects with prompt intramuscular epinephrine, addressing possible ongoing exposure, and recognizing host factors (high baseline tryptase, mast cell disorders) that raise the pretest probability of recurrence and may justify longer observation. Understanding the biology — immediate IgE‑mediated degranulation followed by a recruited, cytokine‑driven inflammatory wave, potentially amplified by persistent antigen, non‑IgE pathways, or increased mast cell load — helps frame monitoring and therapeutic choices in biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis.

### [Management] What is the current evidence regarding corticosteroid use in anaphylaxis, do steroids prevent biphasic reactions, and what patient-specific harms might you anticipate in someone with diabetes?

Corticosteroids have historically been given in anaphylaxis mainly to try to prevent a delayed (biphasic) recurrence, but the evidence does not support that practice. Multiple observational studies and a systematic review (including 22 studies) found no consistent reduction in biphasic reactions with steroid use; only a single small study suggested benefit while the rest did not. More recent observational data have even associated prehospital steroids with increased likelihood of hospital/ICU admission (confounding by indication is possible), and contemporary guideline panels increasingly advise against routine corticosteroids in straightforward anaphylaxis. In short: steroids do not reliably prevent biphasic (or multiphasic) reactions and should not be given reflexively for that purpose.

When might you still consider steroids? In select situations where there is another clear indication (severe protracted urticaria/angioedema refractory to antihistamines, comorbid adrenal insufficiency, or other inflammatory comorbidity), or perhaps to blunt prolonged laryngeal oedema in consultation with specialists. Even then, recognize the evidence for preventing biphasic events is weak.

For a patient with type 2 diabetes, the harms of systemic corticosteroids are immediate and clinically important. Expect pronounced hyperglycaemia within hours of even a single moderate-to-high dose; in an insulin‑naïve patient this commonly requires initiation or escalation of insulin (sliding‑scale or basal/bolus as appropriate) during the inpatient stay. Acute hyperglycaemia increases the risk of osmotic symptoms, infection, and—if severe and prolonged—ketosis or hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state in vulnerable patients. Other short‑term steroid harms to anticipate are fluid retention and worsening hypertension, agitation or delirium in older patients, transient leukocytosis (which can confuse infection assessment), and impaired wound healing or increased infection risk if there are breaks in the skin or invasive procedures planned.

Practically: do not use steroids routinely to prevent biphasic anaphylaxis. If you decide steroids are needed for another reason in a patient with diabetes, use the lowest effective dose for the shortest duration, institute frequent capillary glucose monitoring, plan insulin therapy rather than relying on oral hypoglycaemic agents (hold sulfonylureas if oral intake is uncertain), and monitor for fluid and neuropsychiatric effects. Finally, base observation and disposition decisions on clinical severity, response to epinephrine, and risk factors for relapse (delayed epinephrine, severe initial presentation, multiple epinephrine doses, mast cell disorders), not on whether steroids were given.

### [Management] What is the role of H1 and H2 antihistamines in anaphylaxis management, and which symptoms do they address versus which life-threatening features they do not treat?

Teaching pearl:

H1 and H2 antihistamines are adjunctive, symptom-directed therapies in anaphylaxis — they relieve histamine-mediated cutaneous and some gastrointestinal symptoms but do not treat the life‑threatening cardiopulmonary manifestations that define anaphylactic shock.

Clinically, an H1 antagonist (eg, diphenhydramine, clemastine, cetirizine) will reduce urticaria, pruritus, flushing and some upper‑airway mucosal itching and rhinorrhea. An H2 blocker (eg, famotidine; historically ranitidine) can provide additional suppression of histamine‑mediated gastric acid secretion and may enhance control of cutaneous symptoms when combined with an H1 blocker. Both classes act relatively slowly (minutes for IV agents, longer for oral), and their effects are primarily symptomatic.

What they do not do is reverse the life‑threatening pathophysiology of anaphylaxis: they will not reliably relieve upper‑airway edema causing impending airway obstruction, will not reverse bronchospasm causing hypoxemia, nor will they correct the vasodilation and capillary leak that produce hypotension and shock. Epinephrine is the only immediate, life‑saving medication that addresses these critical features (alpha‑adrenergic vasoconstriction, beta‑adrenergic bronchodilation and inotropy). Antihistamines should therefore never be used as first‑line or sole therapy for respiratory or circulatory compromise and should be given only after or in parallel with epinephrine and supportive measures.

Practical points illustrated by the case: the team gave clemastine and saw improvement in cutaneous signs but required repeated epinephrine for recurrent hemodynamic and respiratory compromise — exactly why antihistamines cannot substitute for epinephrine. Also remember IV H1 agents can cause sedation and hypotension; use them judiciously in unstable patients. Finally, neither H1/H2 antihistamines nor corticosteroids have robust evidence to prevent biphasic or multiphasic relapses — observation and access to repeated epinephrine remain the key safety measures.

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a patient with recurrent symptoms after initial improvement, what differential diagnoses should you consider besides true biphasic anaphylaxis (for example, ongoing allergen absorption, sustained anaphylaxis, or alternative diagnoses), and how would you distinguish among them clinically?

When a patient who initially improves after treatment for anaphylaxis then develops recurrent symptoms, do not assume “biphasic anaphylaxis” is the only explanation. The main alternatives are ongoing allergen exposure/absorption, sustained or refractory anaphylaxis (including inadequate initial dosing), an underlying mast cell disorder that lowers the threshold for relapse, and non‑anaphylactic mimics (cardiovascular, pulmonary, neurologic, or psychogenic). Distinguishing among these possibilities is pragmatic and centers on chronology, objective findings, treatment response, targeted testing, and a careful review of exposures and medications.

First consider ongoing exposure or slow absorption. Oral, transdermal, depot, or skin‑embedded sources (food residues, long‑acting preparations, transdermal patches, intramuscular/depot drugs) can continually release antigen and produce recurrent episodes. Clues are a clear temporal link to ingestion or continuing contact, recurrence shortly after the known exposure window, and lack of sustained response to epinephrine until the source is removed or absorbed. Management focuses on stopping exposure and, if appropriate, decontamination or gastric emptying considerations.

Second, distinguish true sustained or refractory anaphylaxis from a biphasic relapse. Sustained anaphylaxis shows incomplete or transient response to epinephrine with persistent mediator release; refractory anaphylaxis fails to respond adequately to otherwise appropriate epinephrine dosing. Clinically, sustained/refractory disease presents with persistent hypotension, bronchospasm, or hypoxaemia despite correct intramuscular dosing (0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg) and often requires repeat doses or an intravenous epinephrine infusion and adjunctive therapies (fluid resuscitation, vasopressors, glucagon if beta‑blocked). Inadequate initial dosing (for example, adult >50 kg only receiving a 0.3 mg auto‑injector) will masquerade as relapse when in fact the initial insult was undertreated; review the doses and timing immediately.

Third, consider an underlying mast cell disorder (eg, systemic mastocytosis or monoclonal mast cell activation syndrome). These increase severity and likelihood of recurrent or multiphasic courses. Clues are unusually severe reactions to small exposures, multiple recurrences, persistently or basally elevated tryptase, hepatosplenomegaly, or historical mediator symptoms. Diagnostic confirmation rests on serial tryptase measurements (acute sample ideally within 1–2 hours of symptom onset, then baseline >24 hours after resolution) and hematologic evaluation.

Fourth, differentiate non‑anaphylactic mimics. Vasovagal syncope produces bradycardia, pallor, and a prodrome of lightheadedness rather than urticaria, bronchospasm, or angioedema. Isolated panic or hyperventilation causes dyspnoea and anxiety without hypotension, cutaneous flushing, wheeze, or laryngeal oedema. Pulmonary embolism or cardiogenic shock will have different risk factors and objective data (ECG changes, right‑heart strain, elevated lactate, imaging). Bradykinin angioedema (eg, ACE inhibitor) causes isolated angioedema without urticaria and will not respond to antihistamines/epinephrine. Distinguish these with focused exam, response to epinephrine, and targeted testing (ECG, troponin, D‑dimer, chest imaging, ABG).

Use objective tests early to help separate causes. Serial tryptase (acute and baseline) supports mast cell–mediated reactions and can corroborate anaphylaxis if measured within the proper window. A clear and rapid clinical response to correct intramuscular epinephrine and antihistamines supports histamine‑mediated anaphylaxis; persistent shock despite appropriate dosing suggests refractory anaphylaxis. The pattern of skin findings (urticaria/angioedema) and bronchospasm favors anaphylaxis over vasovagal or panic. Review medication lists for beta‑blockers (may blunt epinephrine effect) or recent long‑acting preparations that could sustain antigen exposure.

Applied to this case: the single oral 500 mg clarithromycin dose makes prolonged ongoing antigen exposure less likely than with slow‑release oral drugs or dermal sources, but would not be impossible if there were retained formulation or unusual absorption. Recurrent episodes despite initial improvement raise concern for underdosing (the patient initially received 0.3 mg rather than the preferred 0.5 mg for her weight), for an underlying mast cell disorder (her elevated serial tryptase and hepatomegaly are consistent with that), or for transiently sustained mediator release that eventually waned. The fact that each recurrence responded to intramuscular epinephrine argues for continued mast cell–mediated events rather than a non‑anaphylactic mimic.

Practical approach at the bedside: reassess exposures and formulation, confirm epinephrine dosing and give repeat appropriate intramuscular doses if needed, monitor closely (consider telemetry/ICU for severe or recurrent cases), obtain an acute tryptase as soon as feasible and a baseline >24 hours later, evaluate for mast cell disorder if recurrent or unusually severe, and broaden testing only when the clinical picture suggests an alternative diagnosis. Remember that corticosteroids have not been shown to prevent biphasic reactions and should not substitute for correct epinephrine dosing and vigilant monitoring.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Does prior delayed rash to clindamycin predict immediate anaphylaxis to clarithromycin, and what is known about cross-reactivity between lincosamides and macrolides?

Short answer: No — a delayed, likely T‑cell–mediated maculopapular rash to clindamycin does not reliably predict immediate, IgE‑mediated anaphylaxis to clarithromycin. True cross‑reactivity between lincosamides (eg, clindamycin) and macrolides (eg, clarithromycin) is biologically unlikely and is not supported by meaningful clinical evidence.

Why: The clinical pattern matters. A rash that appears after days of clindamycin is most consistent with a delayed-type hypersensitivity (T‑cell mediated) or a non‑specific antibiotic eruption. Those reactions do not imply circulating drug‑specific IgE, which is the usual mechanism for immediate anaphylaxis occurring within minutes to hours of exposure. Clarithromycin produced rapid-onset hypotension, urticaria and respiratory symptoms in this patient — a picture of immediate mast cell mediator release. Two different immunologic mechanisms acting on structurally unrelated drugs explain why the earlier clindamycin rash would not predict the later clarithromycin anaphylaxis.

On cross‑reactivity: Lincosamides and macrolides have distinct chemical structures and different antigenic determinants. Published reports of cross‑reactivity between these classes are rare and largely anecdotal. By contrast, cross‑reactivity is more clinically relevant within antibiotic families that share common beta‑lactam or sulfonamide moieties. Therefore, when a patient has a delayed clindamycin reaction, avoiding all macrolides is not automatically necessary on that basis alone.

Clinical implications: In this case clarithromycin should be considered the presumptive culprit and documented as a severe immediate allergy. Because the patient suffered severe anaphylaxis (and probable mast cell disorder), skin testing or drug challenge is not appropriate acutely and may be contraindicated; referral to an allergist/immunologist for later specialist evaluation (including cautious testing strategies if necessary, baseline and event tryptase measurement review, and counseling) is the right next step. For future antibiotic choices, avoid clarithromycin and possibly other macrolides unless an allergist performs graded challenge or testing; do not assume macrolide tolerance based on a prior delayed reaction to clindamycin. Finally, recognize that mast cell disorders (elevated baseline tryptase in this patient) increase the risk and severity of anaphylaxis irrespective of cross‑reactivity, so emphasis should be on avoidance of the identified culprit, education, and ready access to epinephrine.

### [Management] What discharge steps should follow an ED/ICU-treated anaphylaxis episode (medications, number and dose of auto-injectors, education, action plan, referrals), and how would you tailor these for an adult over 50 kg?

Brief clinical pearl: discharge planning after an ED/ICU-treated anaphylactic episode is as important as the acute resuscitation. Your goals on discharge are immediate safety (access to epinephrine and clear instructions), secondary prevention (avoidance and identification of the trigger), risk stratification and targeted follow‑up (allergy/immunology ± hematology). Tailor intensity of observation and follow‑up to severity, need for multiple epinephrine doses, incomplete recovery, or suspicion of an underlying mast‑cell disorder (as in this case).

Medications and doses
Prescribe epinephrine auto‑injectors as first priority. For adults >50 kg the recommended IM dose is 0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg; therefore adult patients should receive 0.5 mg per dose. If 0.5‑mg auto‑injectors are available, prescribe two devices and teach IM administration into the mid‑outer thigh. If only 0.3‑mg devices are the only immediately available option, advise patients to use them (better than no epinephrine) but replace with 0.5‑mg devices as soon as possible; still supply two devices. Instruct to use an auto‑injector at the first sign of anaphylaxis, call emergency services, and repeat every 5 minutes if there is no improvement, up to the number of devices available while awaiting EMS (practical target: at least two to three doses accessible).

Antihistamines and corticosteroids
Offer an oral second‑generation H1 antihistamine for symptomatic relief (eg, cetirizine/levocetirizine) and consider adding an H2 blocker for cutaneous/GI symptoms if helpful — these are adjuncts only and do not treat airway or circulatory compromise. Do not consider antihistamines a substitute for epinephrine. Routine systemic corticosteroids to prevent biphasic reactions are no longer supported by evidence; do not routinely prescribe systemic steroids at discharge for the purpose of preventing relapse. Consider a short steroid course only if there is a separate indication (eg, severe refractory cutaneous disease) and document rationale.

Observation and criteria for inpatient admission/longer observation
Observation duration should be individualized. Patients with a single, rapidly and completely resolved, mild episode who received prompt epinephrine, with reliable social support and access to autoinjectors may be observed 4–6 hours and discharged with clear guidance. Those with severe initial hypotension, respiratory failure, need for multiple epinephrine doses, recurrent/biphasic or multiphasic course, or suspected mast‑cell disease (as in this patient) warrant extended observation (often 24 hours or longer) or inpatient care. In this case (multiphasic reaction and high baseline tryptase), a lower threshold for prolonged observation or inpatient admission is appropriate.

Education and action plan
Before discharge, provide a written, easy‑to‑follow anaphylaxis action plan and demonstrate auto‑injector technique with return demonstration. Teach recognition of early symptoms, immediate epinephrine use, calling EMS, positioning (lie supine with legs elevated if hypotensive unless vomiting/respiratory distress), and when to repeat epinephrine. Advise removal/avoidance of the suspected trigger (stop and label offending medication), and give a medical alert card/bracelet. Explain that antihistamines and inhalers may relieve symptoms but are not definitive therapy.

Number and accessibility of auto‑injectors
Provide two auto‑injectors at minimum; explain that two are carried so a second dose is available if symptoms persist or recur while awaiting EMS. For patients with severe disease or recurrent episodes, discuss carrying three devices (some guidelines recommend this in high‑risk individuals). Ensure the patient understands storage, expiration monitoring, and replacement plan.

Referrals and investigations
Arrange urgent outpatient follow‑up with allergy/immunology within 1–4 weeks for trigger testing and risk stratification. Obtain baseline tryptase (≥24 hours after symptoms resolve) and review peri‑episode tryptase levels if available; if baseline tryptase is persistently elevated or clinical features suggest mast‑cell disease, refer to hematology/mast‑cell disorder clinic for further workup. For older adults or those with cardiac disease who had syncope or arrhythmia, consider cardiology input if any concerns exist.

Special considerations for suspected mast‑cell disorders (this case)
Counsel that patients with elevated tryptase or suspected systemic mastocytosis have higher risk of severe and recurrent anaphylaxis and should carry epinephrine indefinitely, have low threshold for prolonged monitoring after an episode, and be expedited to specialist care for definitive diagnosis and individualized risk mitigation (eg, perioperative prophylaxis, trigger avoidance, consideration of continuous H1/H2 blockade if symptomatic). Provide written documentation for other providers about this increased risk.

Practical checklist to give the patient at discharge
Confirm two (preferably 0.5‑mg) auto‑injectors prescribed and demonstrated; give written anaphylaxis action plan; start regular second‑generation antihistamine for symptomatic control if needed; document offending agent(s) in discharge summary and allergy bracelet; arrange allergy/immunology follow‑up within weeks, and hematology referral if tryptase elevated or mast‑cell disease suspected; instruct to seek immediate care for any recurrent symptoms and to use epinephrine without delay.

Rationale in one line
Epinephrine is the only treatment that reverses airway and circulatory collapse; adults >50 kg should receive up to 0.5 mg IM, carry at least two doses, and receive targeted follow‑up because severity, multiple epinephrine doses, and elevated tryptase predict higher relapse risk and possible underlying mast‑cell disease.

### [Clinical Pearls] How does body habitus affect intramuscular delivery of epinephrine in obese adults, what needle length considerations apply, and how might choice of device (auto-injector vs drawn-up syringe) influence effective dosing?

Clinical pearl: effective intramuscular epinephrine requires that the drug actually reach muscle — not subcutaneous fat — at a therapeutic dose and without delay. In obese adults the main problem is physics, not pharmacology.

Subcutaneous fat is thicker in many adults with obesity, particularly over the deltoid and lateral thigh. If the needle is too short the epinephrine deposits into subcutaneous tissue where absorption is slower, peak plasma concentrations are lower and onset is delayed — exactly the opposite of what you need in anaphylaxis. Studies measuring skin‑to‑muscle distance (STMD) show a sizable proportion of women and men with BMI ≥30 have STMD greater than the typical auto‑injector needle (≈15–16 mm). At a weight of 104 kg (as in the case) the risk of subcutaneous rather than intramuscular injection is substantial.

Needle‑length considerations and practical rules
Use the lateral thigh (vastus lateralis) because it tends to have less subcutaneous fat and larger muscle mass than the deltoid. For routine adults without obesity a 16 mm (5/8–3/4 in) auto‑injector commonly reaches muscle. For adults with increased adiposity consider a longer needle: a 25 mm (1 in) needle will reach muscle in many but not all; a 38 mm (1.5 in) needle is more reliable in patients with large thigh pannus. The pragmatic target is a needle long enough to traverse skin + subcutaneous tissue to reach muscle while avoiding bone; in practice that means selecting a longer needle when STMD is likely >16 mm (eg, BMI >30, thigh circumference large, visible pannus).

Device choice and dosing implications
Auto‑injectors are lifesaving because they eliminate delay and dosing hesitancy; however, most adult auto‑injectors deliver 0.3 mg with a relatively short needle (~15–16 mm). Two hazards in an obese adult are (1) underdosing relative to the guideline dose of 0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg (so a 104 kg patient ideally would receive ~1.0 mg by weight but limited by the 0.5 mg maximum), and (2) failure to reach muscle because of short needle length. In emergency settings the benefit of immediate auto‑injector use usually outweighs these risks, but expect to repeat dosing and escalate if response is incomplete.

In a hospital where you can draw up medication and choose needle length, a clinician can and should give 0.01 mg/kg (up to 0.5 mg) IM with a needle long enough for IM deposition (typically 25–38 mm into the lateral thigh in obese patients). That approach achieves a larger, appropriately delivered dose and more reliable pharmacokinetics than a short‑needle 0.3 mg auto‑injector. Conversely, short‑needle auto‑injectors may be preferable for first response by laypersons or in chaotic settings because they minimize delay; but if you have a patient who is not responding, switch to drawn‑up epinephrine with an appropriate needle length (or consider IV infusion/bolus per ACLS/anaphylaxis refractory protocols) rather than persisting with repeated short‑needle injections alone.

Clinical application to the case
The patient weighed 104 kg and received two 0.3 mg auto‑injector doses initially and later two 0.5 mg drawn‑up IM doses. Two problems likely contributed to the need for repeated dosing: the 0.3 mg dose is below the recommended 0.01 mg/kg (and below the commonly used adult 0.5 mg ceiling), and the auto‑injector needle length may have deposited some doses subcutaneously. When immediate stabilization is needed, give an auto‑injector without delay; once in the ED or ICU, switch to drawn‑up epinephrine IM at 0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg using a longer (1–1.5 in) needle aimed at the mid‑anterior lateral thigh, and escalate to IV epinephrine infusion if recurrent or refractory anaphylaxis persists.

Takeaway: give epinephrine immediately; in obese adults anticipate short‑needle auto‑injector failure and be ready to use drawn‑up 0.01 mg/kg IM with a 25–38 mm needle into the lateral thigh (or IV therapy if refractory).

### [Clinical Pearls] Which index-event factors increase the risk of a biphasic anaphylactic reaction (for example, delayed epinephrine, severe presentation, multiple epinephrine doses, prior biphasic reactions), and how do these influence observation time?

Teaching pearl:

A biphasic anaphylactic reaction is most likely when the index event is severe, incompletely treated, prolonged in exposure/absorption, or occurs in a patient with underlying mast cell disease — and those same features should prolong your observation.

Why these factors matter: delayed or inadequate epinephrine allows ongoing mediator-driven pathology to continue and re‑emerge once the short-lived hemodynamic benefits wear off; a severe initial presentation (airway involvement, profound hypotension or hypoxaemia, need for vasopressors, ICU care) reflects a large mediator release and greater physiological stress, which both increase the chance of recurrence; needing multiple epinephrine doses or escalating care is a marker that the reaction is harder to control and therefore more likely to relapse; and disorders that increase mast cell burden or reactivity (elevated baseline tryptase, suspected or confirmed mastocytosis) predispose to repeated mediator release and multiphasic courses. Other contextual risks that increase likelihood of recurrence are exposures with prolonged absorption (oral medications, depot injections, ongoing allergen exposure), comorbid severe asthma or significant cardiovascular disease, and use of beta‑blockers (which can blunt epinephrine response). Prior biphasic reactions, when known, also predict recurrence.

How that changes observation and disposition: use a risk‑stratified, not one‑size‑fits‑all, approach. Patients who had a mild reaction, rapid and complete response to a single timely intramuscular epinephrine dose, no airway or cardiovascular involvement, no comorbidities and clear removal of the trigger can be observed for a short period (commonly 4–6 hours after complete symptom resolution) and discharged with written instructions and an auto‑injector. Patients with any high‑risk feature — severe initial presentation (hypotension, hypoxaemia, airway compromise), delayed administration of epinephrine, need for multiple epinephrine doses, ongoing absorption risk (oral drug, food still in gut), beta‑blocker use, elevated or unknown baseline tryptase, suspected mast cell disorder, or prior biphasic/refractory anaphylaxis — merit prolonged observation and lower threshold for ICU/telemetry monitoring. In these patients aim for at least 24 hours of observation after the last anaphylactic signs, and consider 24–48 hours (or ICU-level continuous monitoring) for refractory or multiphasic courses or when the trigger cannot be confirmed/removed.

Practical points from the case: this patient had hypotension, hypoxaemia, multiple IM epinephrine doses and suspected mast cell disease — all indicators for extended monitoring. Routine corticosteroids are not supported to prevent biphasic reactions and should not replace careful observation and appropriate escalation of care. Always document peak and baseline tryptase with the recommended timing (as soon as feasible, 1–4 hours after onset for peak, and ≥24 hours after resolution for baseline) to help identify underlying mast cell disorders that will influence future management and observation planning.

### [Management] What clinical features should prompt ICU admission for anaphylaxis (for example, hypotension, hypoxemia, need for repeated epinephrine, comorbidities, suspected protracted/multiphasic course), and how would you frame goals of care in the ICU?

In practice the decision to admit anaphylaxis to the ICU is driven less by a single number and more by the patient’s physiologic instability, the intensity and trajectory of the reaction, and the resources needed to safely anticipate or treat further deterioration. Admit to ICU when the clinical picture or treatment needs exceed what a monitored ward can safely manage.

The features that should prompt ICU admission are: persistent or recurrent haemodynamic instability (hypotension requiring vasopressor support, MAP <65 mmHg despite fluids, or repeated boluses of epinephrine), significant respiratory compromise (hypoxaemia requiring high‑flow oxygen, escalating oxygen needs, stridor/airway oedema or concern for rapid progression to loss of airway), need for frequent or repeated intramuscular epinephrine doses (more than one dose, or ongoing requirement within hours), refractory anaphylaxis (inadequate response to appropriately dosed epinephrine), requirement or consideration of continuous intravenous vasoactive infusions (epinephrine, norepinephrine) or airway intervention (protection/intubation), and comorbidities that increase risk of fatal outcome (cardiovascular disease, beta‑blocker or ACE‑inhibitor therapy, severe COPD, pregnancy, advanced age, obesity). Also consider ICU for suspected mast cell disease or markedly elevated baseline tryptase, for multiphasic courses (multiple relapses) or when the trigger is a sustained‑release medication or exposure that may produce prolonged absorption. Finally, limited monitoring resources on the ward (no telemetry, inadequate nursing ratio) are a practical reason to escalate.

How to frame ICU goals of care: first priority is the ABCs — secure airway early when there is progressive upper‑airway oedema or increasing work of breathing, reverse shock with intramuscular epinephrine promptly and escalate to IV vasopressor support if hypotension persists despite fluids, and restore oxygenation with appropriate respiratory support. Simultaneously minimize harm from treatment by using guideline‑based epinephrine dosing (0.01 mg/kg IM up to 0.5 mg in adults; consider IV infusion only in refractory cases under close monitoring), avoid delays in repeated dosing when indicated, and limit unnecessary therapies (recognize that routine corticosteroids have not been shown to prevent biphasic reactions). Establish frequent reassessment intervals (vital signs, airway exam, pulse oximetry, telemetry) and a timeframe for observation — for patients with multiphasic or severe presentations plan prolonged ICU observation (commonly 24 hours or more) with low threshold to re‑escalate. Begin diagnostic workup in parallel without delaying stabilization: draw tryptase at recommended intervals to help identify mast cell disorders, document exposures, and consult allergy/immunology early. Before discharge from ICU, ensure the patient has a clear plan: thresholds for step‑down, education and prescription of epinephrine auto‑injector (appropriate dose), written action plan, allergy follow‑up, and contraindications/alternatives to implicated agents.

Finally, set explicit, time‑bound goals with the patient and team: immediate goal — reverse life‑threatening airway or circulatory compromise; short term (ICU) goal — maintain stability without recurrent anaphylaxis for a defined observation period and complete essential diagnostics; disposition goal — safe transfer to ward when stable off vasopressors and oxygen with no recurrence for the agreed monitoring interval, and discharge planning with follow‑up and prevention measures. This approach balances aggressive resuscitation when needed with targeted monitoring to detect and treat multiphasic relapses.

### [Management] What type and volume of intravenous fluids are recommended in anaphylactic shock, and how do epinephrine’s alpha-1 and beta-2 effects interact with the pathophysiology of distributive shock and capillary leak?

In anaphylactic shock the immediate priorities are to restore perfusion and reverse airway/bronchial edema — fluids and epinephrine work together for those goals. Use isotonic crystalloids (0.9% saline or a balanced crystalloid) and give rapid, relatively large boluses because anaphylaxis produces both precapillary vasodilatation and marked capillary leak with loss of intravascular volume.

A practical approach is to give an initial rapid bolus of 500–1000 mL of isotonic crystalloid in adults immediately while giving epinephrine. If hypotension persists give additional boluses up to roughly 20 mL/kg (often 1–2 liters or more in an average adult) and reassess frequently; expect to give multiple liters in severe cases because fluid rapidly shifts into the interstitium. Monitor respiratory status and cardiac function (ischemia/volume overload risk) as you replete; patients with heart failure or advanced age need closer balance between restoring intravascular volume and avoiding pulmonary edema. If hypotension is refractory to repeated boluses and repeated appropriate-dose IM epinephrine, start continuous IV vasopressor therapy (epinephrine infusion preferred in severe refractory anaphylaxis) while obtaining central access as needed.

How epinephrine’s receptor effects address the pathophysiology: anaphylaxis is a distributive shock from widespread vasodilatation plus increased microvascular permeability (capillary leak), and bronchospasm and mucosal edema from mediator release. Epinephrine’s alpha-1 agonism causes arteriolar and venous vasoconstriction that raises systemic vascular resistance and venous return and reduces mucosal and dermal edema, directly opposing the vasodilatory and permeability effects that produce hypotension and airway compromise. Its beta-1 effects increase heart rate and contractility, improving cardiac output against the reduced afterload and hypovolaemia. Its beta-2 effects produce bronchodilation and increase intracellular cAMP in mast cells, which suppresses further mediator release; beta-2–mediated vasodilation can theoretically oppose alpha vasoconstriction, but at therapeutic doses in anaphylaxis the alpha-1 effect usually predominates systemically while the beta-2 bronchodilation and mast-cell stabilisation are clinically important.

Clinically, therefore, fluids replace the intravascular volume lost through capillary leak while epinephrine both restores vascular tone and treats airway/bronchial pathology and ongoing mediator release. Because fluids extravasate in severe reactions, be prepared for repeated boluses and for early escalation to continuous IV epinephrine (or other vasopressors if needed) when IM doses and fluids do not restore perfusion.

### [Pathophysiology] By which receptor-mediated mechanisms does epinephrine reverse the life-threatening features of anaphylaxis, and how does it affect further mediator release from mast cells and basophils?

Epinephrine works in anaphylaxis by engaging distinct adrenergic receptors on the vasculature, heart, airways and immune effector cells to rapidly reverse the physiologic consequences of massive mast cell/basophil activation and to blunt further mediator release.

Hemodynamic and airway effects are mediated primarily through alpha‑1 and beta‑1/beta‑2 receptors. Alpha‑1 stimulation produces powerful peripheral vasoconstriction that increases systemic vascular resistance, raises blood pressure, reduces mucosal and laryngeal edema (improving the airway), and decreases vascular leak that otherwise produces angioedema and urticaria. Beta‑1 stimulation increases cardiac contractility and heart rate, improving cardiac output and counteracting distributive shock. Beta‑2 stimulation causes bronchodilation (reducing bronchospasm), decreases microvascular permeability in the lung, and improves oxygenation.

Beyond these hemodynamic and bronchodilator actions, epinephrine directly suppresses further mediator release from mast cells and basophils via beta‑2 receptor signaling. Beta‑2 receptor activation increases intracellular cyclic AMP in these cells, which interferes with the signaling pathways (calcium mobilization and exocytosis) required for IgE‑mediated degranulation and reduces synthesis/release of proinflammatory mediators (histamine, tryptase, leukotrienes and prostaglandins). This beta‑2–cAMP–mediated “mast cell stabilizing” effect helps limit propagation or recurrence of the reaction, although it cannot undo mediators already released.

Clinically this receptor profile explains why intramuscular epinephrine is first‑line: it rapidly delivers alpha‑ and beta‑agonism to restore blood pressure, open the airway and blunt further mediator release. It also explains limitations: epinephrine attenuates further degranulation but does not instantly neutralize mediators already in the circulation (hence need for supportive measures such as fluids, oxygen and repeated doses if symptoms recur).

### [Management] In a patient on beta-blockers who develops anaphylaxis with poor response to epinephrine, when should you administer glucagon, and what dosing and monitoring considerations apply?

If a patient on beta‑blockers is in anaphylactic shock and fails to improve adequately with correctly administered intramuscular epinephrine (and with reasonable additional measures — high‑flow oxygen, aggressive IV fluids, repeated epinephrine doses or an IV epinephrine bolus/infusion as indicated), suspect beta‑blocker–mediated resistance and give glucagon promptly. The clinical reasoning is that glucagon activates myocardial cAMP via a non‑adrenergic receptor, restoring heart rate, contractility and vasomotor tone when beta receptors are blocked.

Practical dosing and administration: give an initial IV bolus of glucagon 1–5 mg (commonly 1–2 mg over 1–2 minutes in adults). If there is a hemodynamic response, start a continuous infusion — typically 1–5 mg per hour (many centers use 1–2 mg/hr initially and titrate; some protocols use up to 5–10 mg/hr if needed). If the bolus produces little effect, repeat boluses every 3–5 minutes while arranging an infusion and continuing vasopressors. Use the higher end of dosing for severe refractory shock, but escalate cautiously.

Monitoring and safety: give antiemetic coverage when feasible (glucagon commonly causes nausea and vomiting); monitor blood glucose (glucagon causes hyperglycaemia) and serum potassium (may cause hypokalaemia). Continuous cardiac and blood‑pressure monitoring is required; obtain IV access suitable for infusion (prefer central access if available). Once hemodynamics are stable, taper the glucagon infusion slowly while ensuring ongoing epinephrine/vasopressor support as needed. Remember that availability of parenteral glucagon and staff familiarity are common barriers — prepare the drug early in any beta‑blocked patient with refractory anaphylaxis.

In short: in beta‑blocked patients with inadequate response to epinephrine, give glucagon without undue delay (1–2 mg IV bolus, repeat if needed, then 1–5 mg/hr infusion), watch for nausea, hyperglycaemia and hypokalaemia, monitor continuously, and taper only after sustained stability.

### [Management] For a patient with erysipelas and a history of beta-lactam allergy who later experiences antibiotic-associated anaphylaxis, how would you approach future antibiotic selection, including the role of cephalosporins, graded challenge, desensitization, and allergist referral?

Key principle: future antibiotic choices must be individualized to the documented reactions, the likely immunologic mechanism, and the patient’s underlying predisposition to severe reactions (here, elevated baseline tryptase and probable mast cell disorder). When an antibiotic has already caused anaphylaxis, it should be treated as a confirmed immediate-type allergy until proven otherwise; alternatives or formal allergy evaluation are required before re‑exposure.

For this patient specifically I would do the following.

1) Immediate practical rules
Do not re‑expose her to clarithromycin (and avoid macrolides as a class pending evaluation). Treat the remote childhood amoxicillin episode (facial swelling only) as an uncertain but potentially IgE‑mediated penicillin reaction; it is insufficient to declare lifetime penicillin allergy without testing. Ensure she keeps and knows how to use an epinephrine auto‑injector and that all future antibiotic plans are made with allergy input and anaphylaxis precautions in place.

2) Refer to an allergist/immunologist
Formal referral is essential. An allergist will review the history, confirm baseline tryptase (if not already established), and plan risk‑stratified testing. Given her anaphylaxis and probable mast cell disorder, testing should be done in a monitored setting (clinic with resuscitation capability or hospital).

3) Role of penicillin skin testing and cephalosporin use
Penicillin (and amoxicillin) skin testing is the standard way to assess IgE‑mediated penicillin allergy. A negative penicillin skin test (with negative amoxicillin/penicillin reagents and/or negative direct amoxicillin testing if available) reliably predicts tolerance to penicillins and many cephalosporins. If testing is negative, a supervised graded oral challenge to the intended beta‑lactam (or a single‑dose challenge) is reasonable to confirm tolerance and then the beta‑lactam may be used. If testing is positive, cefalosporins are not uniformly contraindicated — the risk of cross‑reactivity is low overall and depends chiefly on shared R‑side chains. Choosing a cephalosporin with a dissimilar side chain may be safe, but in a patient with prior anaphylaxis this decision should follow expert allergy testing and, if necessary, a graded challenge or controlled desensitization.

4) Graded challenge versus desensitization — how to choose
Use a graded challenge (incremental supervised dosing) when you believe the patient is low risk (negative testing or non‑immediate/uncertain history) and the goal is to document tolerance. Use desensitization only when the antibiotic is essential, there is evidence of IgE‑mediated allergy (or prior anaphylaxis), and no safe alternative exists. Desensitization induces temporary tolerance only for the course of that exposure and must be done in a monitored setting with readiness for full resuscitation. In this patient, if a beta‑lactam is the best therapy for future erysipelas but penicillin testing is positive or clinical risk remains high, desensitization in the hospital (preferably ICU or step‑down with experienced staff) is the appropriate pathway.

5) Special considerations with elevated baseline tryptase / suspected mastocytosis
Patients with mast cell disorders are at higher risk of severe and multiphasic anaphylaxis and may have more labile responses to provocations. This raises the threshold for any testing that risks provoking an IgE reaction (skin testing, challenges) and for desensitization procedures. All testing and any desensitization should occur where intensive monitoring and immediate therapy (IV epinephrine, airway support) are available. Pre‑medication (H1/H2 antihistamines) may be used per allergist protocol but does not prevent anaphylaxis and should not give false reassurance. Coordinate antibiotic plans with the mast cell specialists/allergist.

6) Documentation and stewardship
Document the specific implicated agents (clarithromycin) and the nature of the reactions in the chart and on the allergy list. If penicillin testing is negative, remove “penicillin allergy” from active problem list or clarify it as “historical, negative testing” to avoid unnecessary broad‑spectrum substitutions. When beta‑lactams are required, prefer tested tolerated agents or perform supervised graded challenge; reserve desensitization for indispensable cases.

Bottom line: do not re‑use clarithromycin; refer to allergy for penicillin skin testing and risk‑stratified planning. If testing is negative, a cephalosporin (with a dissimilar side chain) can be given with a supervised challenge; if testing confirms IgE‑mediated penicillin allergy and a beta‑lactam is required, perform hospital‑based desensitization. Given her probable mast cell disorder, all testing and any desensitization must be done under specialist supervision in a monitored setting with immediate resuscitation capacity.

### [Differential Diagnosis] What clinical and historical features help distinguish ACE inhibitor–induced bradykinin-mediated angioedema from histamine-mediated anaphylaxis, and why does this distinction matter for acute therapy?

Key point: ACE inhibitor–related angioedema is a bradykinin‑mediated increase in vascular permeability that looks like facial or oropharyngeal swelling but is not an allergic, mast‑cell driven process. Distinguishing the two is important because the acute treatments and expected responses differ.

Clinical and historical features that favor ACE‑inhibitor (bradykinin) angioedema over histamine‑mediated anaphylaxis
- Medication history: current or recent use of an ACE inhibitor (eg, lisinopril, enalapril). ACE‑I angioedema can occur anytime during therapy—even after months or years—so a remote start date does not exclude it.
- Distribution and symptoms: prominent localized non‑pitting edema of the face, lips, tongue, and/or oropharynx (often asymmetric) without urticaria or generalized flushing. Isolated deep tissue swelling is typical.
- Lack of systemic allergic features: minimal or absent pruritus, no widespread urticaria, and typically no bronchospasm or wheeze. Hypotension and shock are uncommon unless airway obstruction produces severe hypoxia.
- Onset and temporal pattern: often gradual over hours (but can be rapid), and recurrences may occur despite antihistamines/steroids/epinephrine because the mechanism persists while ACE is active.
- Objective testing: normal serum tryptase (no mast cell degranulation) during episodes supports bradykinin mechanism; elevated tryptase points toward mast‑cell mediated anaphylaxis.
- Risk‑factors: female sex, Black race, smoking, and prior angioedema history increase ACE‑I risk.

Clinical and historical features that favor histamine‑mediated anaphylaxis
- Immediate onset (minutes) after exposure to a new/known allergen (food, drug, insect sting), with rapid progression.
- Prominent cutaneous signs: generalized urticaria, intense pruritus, flushing.
- Multi‑system involvement: bronchospasm, respiratory distress, hypotension, syncope, gastrointestinal cramping or vomiting.
- Rapid response to intramuscular epinephrine and to supportive antihistamines/steroids (though steroids do not acutely treat anaphylaxis, many mast‑cell mediated reactions improve after epinephrine).

Why this distinction matters for acute therapy
- Mechanism dictates response: bradykinin‑mediated swelling does not result from histamine/mast‑cell mediator release, so antihistamines and corticosteroids (and frequently epinephrine) are often ineffective. Conversely, anaphylaxis typically responds promptly to intramuscular epinephrine, which is life‑saving.
- Airway strategy differs in emphasis: in suspected ACE‑I angioedema, the priority is early airway assessment and aggressive airway control (prepare for difficult intubation, early ENT/anesthesia involvement, consideration of awake fiberoptic intubation or early surgical airway), because medical reversal is unreliable. In anaphylaxis, epinephrine is the first intervention and commonly reverses airway and circulatory collapse.
- Targeted pharmacologic options: for bradykinin angioedema, specific therapies (when available and in severe cases) include C1 esterase inhibitor concentrate, icatibant (bradykinin B2 receptor antagonist), or kallikrein inhibitors; fresh frozen plasma has been used when specific agents unavailable. These agents are not helpful in histamine‑mediated anaphylaxis. Conversely, delaying epinephrine for presumed ACE‑I angioedema when anaphylaxis is still possible risks fatal outcome.
- Pragmatic approach: when the diagnosis is unclear at presentation, treat for anaphylaxis first (IM epinephrine, airway, oxygen, fluids) because of its immediacy and mortality risk. If the picture clearly fits ACE‑I angioedema (ACE use, isolated oropharyngeal/facial swelling, absence of urticaria/systemic features, normal tryptase), prioritize airway control and pursue bradykinin‑directed therapy and stop the ACE inhibitor.

Clinical pearl: Always ask about ACE inhibitors in any isolated facial/oropharyngeal swelling. If ACE‑I angioedema is likely, remove the drug, monitor airway closely, gather tryptase if there is diagnostic uncertainty, and mobilize airway expertise and bradykinin‑targeted therapies rather than relying on antihistamines/steroids alone. If uncertain, give epinephrine immediately—it's the correct first move when anaphylaxis cannot be excluded.

### [Management] In patients with suspected or confirmed systemic mastocytosis and recurrent anaphylaxis, what long-term strategies reduce event frequency and severity (for example, H1 antihistamines, leukotriene antagonists, omalizumab, trigger avoidance), and when are KIT inhibitors (such as avapritinib or midostaurin) considered?

When you see recurrent anaphylaxis in a patient with suspected or proven systemic mastocytosis the first principle is to treat the disorder of excessive mast cell mediator release with rigorous, tiered symptomatic prevention and to reserve disease‑directed cytoreduction for patients with high mast‑cell burden or organ‑threatening disease.

Begin with baseline preventive measures that are low‑risk and evidence‑supported: lifelong access to and training with epinephrine auto‑injectors and an individualized action plan; daily non‑sedating H1‑antihistamine (often at standard or sometimes up‑titrated doses) to blunt histamine‑mediated symptoms; add an H2 blocker for upper GI/vascular mediator effects if symptoms suggest benefit; and consider a leukotriene receptor antagonist for persistent respiratory or cutaneous symptoms. These measures reduce frequency and severity of mediator‑driven flares in many patients and are the first-line long‑term strategy.

Omalizumab (anti‑IgE) has become an important next step for patients with recurrent severe anaphylaxis despite optimal antihistamine/H2/ leukotriene therapy. Although evidence is largely from case series and cohort data rather than randomized trials, omalizumab reproducibly reduces anaphylactic episodes and mediator symptoms in many patients with mast cell disorders and is used off‑label for this indication. Trial treatment for several months is reasonable in referral centers when allergy/immunology and hematology agree.

Trigger avoidance is essential and must be individualized. Avoidance includes obvious precipitants (known drugs, foods, stings), peri‑procedural precautions (premedication and controlled environment), and careful review of medicines that may potentiate mediator release. For patients with a clear Hymenoptera venom allergy, venom immunotherapy is generally recommended even in mastocytosis patients because the baseline risk of fatal sting reactions is high; such therapy should be delivered in specialty centers.

Long‑term systemic corticosteroids are not a routine strategy for prevention and are avoided unless there is a separate indication; they do not reliably prevent biphasic relapses and carry substantial harm.

When to consider KIT inhibitors (midostaurin, avapritinib): these are disease‑directed therapies best managed by a hematologist with expertise in mast cell neoplasms and are indicated primarily for advanced systemic mastocytosis with high mast‑cell burden or organ dysfunction (so‑called C‑findings) or for severe, refractory mediator symptoms when optimal symptomatic therapy (maximized antihistamines/H2 blockers/leukotriene antagonists, omalizumab) has failed. Before embarking on kinase inhibitor therapy you should document systemic disease (bone marrow evaluation, KIT D816V testing, baseline tryptase and organ assessment) and weigh benefits against toxicity. Midostaurin and more recently avapritinib have demonstrated activity against KIT D816V‑positive disease and can reduce mast‑cell burden and mediator release; they are not first‑line for routine prevention of anaphylaxis in patients with indolent disease without organ damage.

In short: maximize proven, low‑risk symptomatic prevention (H1 ± H2 antihistamines, leukotriene antagonist, epinephrine availability, trigger avoidance, consider venom immunotherapy when appropriate), escalate to omalizumab for refractory recurrent anaphylaxis, and reserve KIT inhibitor therapy for patients with documented systemic disease with organ‑threatening involvement or refractory mediator symptoms after optimal symptomatic therapies and specialist review. Always manage these patients in concert with an allergy/immunology and hematology mast‑cell disease center.

### [Morning Report] When managing multiphasic anaphylaxis in the ICU, what are the benefits and risks of preemptive continuous epinephrine infusion versus expectant management with rapid intramuscular dosing at relapse, and how would you individualize this decision?

Short answer: preemptive continuous IV epinephrine buys control of ongoing mediator-driven cardiovascular collapse and can prevent further relapses, but it substitutes one life‑saving drug for a continuous vasoactive therapy that carries predictable cardiometabolic and monitoring burdens. Expectant management with rapid intramuscular (IM) dosing spares the patient the toxicity and complexity of an infusion, but risks recurrent, rapidly progressive episodes that may be harder to treat once established and that require immediate availability of skilled staff. The correct choice is individualized to the patient’s physiology, the pattern of relapses, comorbidity and the resources available.

Clinical reasoning and how I decide at the bedside: epinephrine is both anti‑mediator (mast cell stabilisation, bronchodilation) and a vasopressor. If relapses are infrequent, milder, and the patient is hemodynamically stable with reliable continuous monitoring and immediate access to IM epinephrine, an expectant approach is reasonable because it avoids the cardiovascular and metabolic side effects of a continuous infusion. If relapses are frequent, progressive, or associated with persistent hypotension or hypoxaemia despite appropriately dosed IM injections or require repeated epinephrine doses in short succession, the underlying process is effectively “uncontrolled.” In that situation a continuous infusion is preferred to maintain stable perfusion, allow titration, and prevent the delay or underdosing that occurs when relying on repeated IM injections.

How this applies to the presented case: this patient had a severe initial shock, multiple recurrences within 12 hours, and required four IM doses (two at only 0.3 mg). She also has elevated baseline tryptase and probable mast cell disease — both features that raise the pretest probability of further relapses. Those factors lower my threshold to move from expectant care to a continuous infusion if she relapses again or if instability recurs shortly after an IM dose. Because in her course the relapses decreased in intensity and resolved with IM epinephrine and she quickly stabilized, prolonged infusion was not necessary — but close ICU observation and readiness to start an infusion promptly were appropriate.

Practical benefits and risks, briefly:
- Benefits of preemptive continuous infusion: immediate and titratable haemodynamic support; suppression of recurrent mediator effects; avoids repeated delays or underdosing with IM auto‑injectors; useful when ongoing vascular leak or persistent mediator release is suspected (eg, mast cell disorders, refractory or recurrent episodes).
- Risks of infusion: tachyarrhythmia, myocardial ischemia (especially in older patients or coronary disease), severe hypertension on overshoot, lactic acidosis, hyperglycaemia, peripheral ischaemia if extravasation occurs, need for arterial pressure monitoring/ICU staffing and careful titration — and the possibility of masking inadequate source control (eg, ongoing allergen exposure).
- Benefits of expectant IM strategy: avoids continuous adrenergic exposure and its metabolic/cardiac toxicity, simple, familiar, and effective in many biphasic cases.
- Risks of expectant IM strategy: delayed recognition or administration during sudden relapse, underdosing (common with 0.3 mg auto‑injectors in adults >50 kg), multiple repeated doses, potential for escalation to refractory shock if relapses are rapid.

How I individualize dosing and monitoring when choosing an infusion: start low and titrate to effect rather than box‑fixed high doses. For adults a common approach is to begin at a low infusion (eg, 0.03–0.1 µg/kg/min or facility standard low‑dose range) and titrate to restore/maintain MAP and oxygenation; increase carefully if needed and wean slowly after a sustained period (several hours) of stability. Ensure continuous ECG, frequent BP and glucose checks, consider arterial line if vasopressor requirement is significant, and watch for arrhythmia or ischemia. Maintain aggressive volume resuscitation and adjunctive therapies (high‑flow O2, H1/H2 blockade when indicated, avoid routine steroids solely to prevent biphasic reactions).

Operational thresholds I use in practice: pursue continuous infusion when the patient requires more than two appropriately dosed epinephrine administrations within a short interval, has persistent hypotension or need for escalating doses, shows progressive relapses despite IM therapy, or cannot be reliably observed and treated immediately (limited staffing). In patients with known or suspected mast cell disease, lower the threshold for ICU admission and consider early infusion if there is any recurrence or if initial presentation was severe.

Takeaway pearl: in multiphasic anaphylaxis the decision is not binary — it’s dynamic. Use the clinical trajectory (frequency and severity of relapses), host risk (cardiac disease, mast cell disorders, obesity — which raises the risk of IM underdosing), and monitoring resources to choose between careful expectant management with immediate IM capability versus early controlled IV infusion with ICU‑level monitoring. In the case you presented, ICU observation with readiness to start a titratable epinephrine infusion if relapses recurred was the prudent middle path; the actual need for infusion is dictated by subsequent clinical response.

### [Morning Report] Which laboratory tests are useful during and after anaphylaxis (for example, serial tryptase, ECG monitoring, point-of-care glucose, electrolytes if multiple epinephrine doses), which are not immediately actionable (for example, specific IgE), and how should documentation support later allergy evaluation?

Clinical pearl — What to measure, what not to chase acutely, and how to document so the allergy team can make sense of a complex course.

Useful tests during and shortly after anaphylaxis are those that change immediate management or establish a biochemical record of mast cell activation and physiologic effects of treatment. Draw an initial set as soon as feasible without delaying life‑saving therapy, then time further samples deliberately.

- Serum tryptase (serial): collect an early sample as soon as possible after presentation, a second sample 1–2 hours after symptom onset (acceptable up to 4 hours) when tryptase usually peaks, and a third at least 24 hours after full clinical recovery to establish baseline. The delta between peak and baseline (and an elevated baseline) helps confirm mast cell activation and may point to underlying mast cell disorder (eg, systemic mastocytosis). Missing early samples — as in this case — undermines later interpretation.

- Point‑of‑care glucose on presentation and during observation: hyper‑ or hypoglycaemia may mimic or complicate symptoms (and this patient is diabetic on sulfonylurea). Epinephrine and shock raise glucose; sulfonylureas can cause delayed hypoglycaemia.

- ECG monitoring and serial ECGs while on observation and during/after epinephrine doses: epinephrine can precipitate tachyarrhythmias, ischemia, or QT changes; hypotension/shock also affects myocardial perfusion. Continuous telemetry in patients with recurrent episodes, cardiac symptoms, older age, or cardiovascular disease is warranted.

- Basic electrolytes and renal function if the patient received multiple epinephrine doses, large-volume resuscitation, or has renal disease: epinephrine can cause hypokalemia and lactic acidosis; repeated doses and IV infusions increase risk of arrhythmia and metabolic disturbance. Check lactate and blood gas if there was significant hypoperfusion.

- Other samples selectively: try to obtain blood for pregnancy test in women of childbearing potential, and consider arterial blood gas if severe respiratory failure or shock persists.

Tests that are not helpful acutely or are not actionable in the ED/ICU

- Specific IgE, skin testing, and many other allergy tests should not be done during the acute phase. Skin testing/specific IgE can be false negative or misleading in the weeks following anaphylaxis and can provoke reactions. These are planned later by allergists, typically after weeks to months, when the patient is stable and medications (antihistamines, steroids) are managed for testing.

- Plasma histamine is labile and not practical for routine clinical use; tryptase is the preferred marker.

How to document to support later allergy evaluation and patient safety

Documentation is part of the diagnostic workup. Record a clear, chronological, time‑stamped narrative of events: exact time of exposure(s) (drug ingestion, food, stings), time of symptom onset, nature and sequence of symptoms (airway, breathing, circulation, skin, GI), vital signs at each assessment, and the timing and route of all interventions (each epinephrine dose: dose, route, time; oxygen; IV fluids and volumes; antihistamines; corticosteroids) and response to each intervention. Photographs of rashes/angioedema are valuable. Note other concurrent medications, comorbid conditions (eg, beta‑blockers, mast cell disease, cardiovascular disease, diabetes), and any preexisting allergy histories. Record blood tests and exact times specimens were drawn (especially tryptase) and the values. Document informed decisions to defer testing (eg, decision not to skin test acutely) and plans for outpatient allergy follow‑up, plus that an epinephrine auto‑injector was prescribed and taught.

Finally, synthesize the acute data for the allergist: attach a succinct timeline table (exposure → onset → interventions and responses → tryptase times/results → ECG findings), state suspected culprit(s), and highlight any persistent abnormal baseline testing (eg, elevated baseline tryptase prompting mast cell disorder workup). That targeted documentation preserves the diagnostic value of acute samples and guides safe, definitive allergy evaluation later.
