Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

### [Differential Diagnosis] How would you distinguish a true biphasic anaphylactic reaction from refractory anaphylaxis or ongoing allergen absorption, and what clinical clues (e.g., a symptom-free interval, complete initial resolution, type of exposure) would you seek to guide management?

Direct answer
A true biphasic anaphylactic reaction is separated from refractory anaphylaxis or ongoing allergen absorption by the pattern of clinical response to appropriate treatment and the relationship to continued exposure. Key distinguishing features are (1) a complete or near‑complete resolution of signs and symptoms after correct therapy followed by a symptom‑free interval and then recurrence (biphasic); (2) persistent or progressive symptoms without a sustained symptom‑free interval despite correctly dosed epinephrine (refractory); and (3) ongoing or repeated exposure or slow absorption of the trigger producing protracted symptoms or waxing and waning without a true intervening recovery (ongoing absorption).

Clinical reasoning and discriminating clues
1) Symptom‑free interval and completeness of recovery
- Biphasic: look for a clear asymptomatic period after appropriate resuscitation (hours). Most biphasic recurrences occur within the first 4–12 hours (reports up to 78 hours exist), so a true asymptomatic interval is key.  
- Refractory: no sustained interval—patient never stabilizes or deteriorates again shortly after treatment.  
In the case, the patient had an initial good response (BP and swelling improved) and then recurrent episodes over the next 12 hours with intervening improvement — this pattern supports a biphasic/multiphasic course rather than refractory anaphylaxis.

2) Response to correctly dosed epinephrine
- Biphasic: recurrent episodes usually respond to repeat IM epinephrine.  
- Refractory: inadequate response to properly dosed epinephrine; may require continuous IV infusion, higher doses, or additional vasopressors.  
In the case each relapse improved with IM epinephrine (0.3 mg autoinjector for the first two, 0.5 mg later), consistent with relapsing (biphasic) rather than refractory physiology. Note, however, that underdosing (0.3 mg in an adult >50 kg) can masquerade as inadequate treatment—always verify dose and route.

3) Timing and type of exposure
- Ongoing absorption is more likely with continued exposure (repeat dosing, patches, IV infusions) or with agents that have prolonged absorption/slow release or are still in the GI tract after ingestion (large meal, extended‑release formulations). Removing the source usually leads to improvement.  
- Single, limited exposures that are stopped (single oral dose switched off, as in this case) make ongoing exposure less likely. The patient received a single clarithromycin dose and no further doses were taken; therefore ongoing absorption was an unlikely primary cause.

4) Severity, number of epinephrine doses and risk factors
- Predictors for biphasic recurrence include severe initial reaction, delayed initial epinephrine, and multiple epinephrine doses. Mast cell disorders (elevated baseline tryptase or systemic mastocytosis) increase the risk of severe or recurrent reactions.  
- In this report the patient had multiple relapses and an elevated tryptase with clinical features suspicious for systemic mastocytosis, a plausible explanation for a multiphasic course.

5) Laboratory support
- Serum tryptase sampling: obtain as soon as feasible, another sample 1–2 (up to 4) hours after onset to capture peak, and a baseline ≥24 hours after resolution. A rise and fall pattern supports true mast cell activation; a persistently high baseline suggests an underlying mast cell disorder that predisposes to recurrent events. In the case, tryptase was elevated during a later episode and baseline remained high on follow‑up, supporting mast cell disease as a predisposing factor.

How this guides management
- If refractory physiology is suspected (no response to correct epinephrine dosing): escalate promptly — secure airway, IV fluids, consider continuous IV epinephrine infusion and ICU level care, and search for alternate diagnoses (cardiogenic shock, pulmonary embolism, medication interactions).  
- If ongoing exposure is possible: immediately remove or stop exposure (stop medication/infusion), consider decontamination if appropriate, and anticipate ongoing absorption (longer observation).  
- If biphasic risk is suspected (initial resolution, then recurrence): observe and monitor. Observation duration should be risk‑stratified — low‑risk patients may be observed 4–6 hours, higher‑risk patients (severe initial reaction, multiple epinephrine doses, mast cell disorder, older age, hypotension, delayed epinephrine) warrant prolonged observation (often 24 hours) and lower threshold for ICU monitoring. Routine corticosteroids are not proven to prevent biphasic reactions and are no longer recommended solely for that purpose; antihistamines treat cutaneous symptoms. Ensure correct epinephrine dosing (0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg IM in adults) and teach avoidance and auto‑injector use at discharge.

Practical, stepwise bedside approach
1. Confirm correct initial treatment and dose of epinephrine. If underdosed, treat as adequate therapy was not given.  
2. Did the patient have a true symptom‑free interval? Yes → consider biphasic; No → consider refractory or ongoing exposure.  
3. Correlate recurrence timing with type of exposure (continued exposure or slow‑release agents → ongoing absorption).  
4. Assess response to repeat epinephrine: good response → supports biphasic; poor response → refractory (escalate).  
5. Obtain tryptase series (as soon as possible, 1–2 h after onset, and baseline ≥24 h post‑recovery) and consider allergy/mast cell workup if indicated.  
6. Determine monitoring location and duration by risk factors (severe initial shock, multiple doses of epinephrine, mast cell disorder → prolonged/ICU observation).

Takeaway anchored to the case
This patient’s course—initial complete response to epinephrine, intervening symptom‑free periods, recurrent episodes that each responded to IM epinephrine, no further clarithromycin doses, and elevated tryptase with features suggesting systemic mastocytosis—is most consistent with a multiphasic (recurrent biphasic) anaphylactic course rather than refractory anaphylaxis or ongoing exposure. That combination of clinical pattern and laboratory clue (tryptase) should prompt prolonged monitoring, involvement of allergy/mast cell specialists, correct epinephrine dosing for future episodes, and counseling with an auto‑injector and avoidance strategies.

### [Management] For a 104-kg adult presenting with anaphylaxis, what is the recommended intramuscular epinephrine dose, and how does using a 0.3 mg auto-injector versus 0.5 mg impact the likelihood of requiring additional epinephrine or escalation of care? What practical steps can an ED take to minimize underdosing in adults?

Clinical answer (clinical pearl)

Recommended IM dose
- Use weight‑based dosing: 0.01 mg/kg IM with an upper single‑dose limit of 0.5 mg. For a 104‑kg adult the recommended intramuscular dose is therefore 0.5 mg (the 0.01 mg/kg calculation would exceed the usual maximum; the guideline cap is 0.5 mg).

Why the dose matters (clinical reasoning / physiology)
- Epinephrine’s life‑saving effects in anaphylaxis depend on adequate alpha‑1 vasoconstriction (restore BP, reduce mucosal oedema) plus beta‑1/2 effects (increase cardiac output, bronchodilation, reduce mediator release). Underdosing can produce incomplete reversal of hypotension/airway obstruction and may leave patients reliant on repeated doses or escalation to IV therapy or ICU care.
- In the case presented, the 104‑kg patient initially received 0.3 mg IM auto‑injector doses and had recurrent anaphylactic episodes; later 0.5 mg IM doses were used. The authors note (and cite recent data) that initial treatment with 0.3 mg in adults >50 kg is associated with significantly greater need for additional epinephrine or escalation of care than initial 0.5 mg.

Practical ED steps to minimize underdosing
1. Make the correct adult dose immediately available
   - Stock 0.5 mg IM epinephrine in the ED (either 0.5 mg auto‑injectors or pre‑prepared labelled 0.5 mg/0.5 mL syringes drawn from 1 mg/mL ampoules).
   - If only 0.3 mg auto‑injectors are on the shelf, ensure staff know how to draw 0.5 mg from ampoule (0.5 mL of 1 mg/mL solution) without delay.
2. Standardize anaphylaxis order sets and standing orders
   - Create a clearly visible protocol: adults >50 kg → 0.5 mg IM into the mid‑anterior lateral thigh; repeat every 5–15 minutes as needed until response.
   - Embed weight prompts or a simple nomogram in resuscitation areas/EMR.
3. Reduce hesitation and delay
   - Place anaphylaxis kits in the triage/ED bay that include 1 mg/mL epinephrine ampoule, 1 mL syringes, labelled 0.5 mg syringe, checklist for IM site and repeat intervals.
   - Train nursing and ED staff in IM technique (vastus lateralis) and in rapid preparation of 0.5 mg IM when auto‑injectors are absent.
   - Continue to prefer auto‑injector use when they reduce delay—but ensure the dose matches the patient’s weight.
4. Escalation plan for refractory cases
   - If multiple IM doses fail, proceed early to advanced measures: IV bolus/continuous infusion of epinephrine with critical‑care monitoring, aggressive fluid resuscitation, airway management and ICU transfer.
5. Education and quality improvement
   - Audit anaphylaxis cases for initial dose given and outcomes; reinforce the 0.5 mg adult dose in teaching sessions.
   - Update local protocols to reflect current guidance (0.01 mg/kg, max 0.5 mg) and the evidence that 0.3 mg can be insufficient for many adults.

A practical note on safety
- Cardiovascular adverse effects are possible, but in anaphylaxis the priority is adequate epinephrine dosing. Do not withhold appropriate IM epinephrine in older adults or those with cardiac disease when anaphylaxis is present.

Bottom line
- For a 104‑kg adult give 0.5 mg IM epinephrine (0.01 mg/kg capped at 0.5 mg). Using a 0.3 mg auto‑injector for adults >50 kg risks underdosing and, as seen in the presented case and recent data, is associated with higher rates of additional epinephrine requirements and escalation of care. Simple ED changes—stocking 0.5 mg options, prefilled syringes or clear draw instructions, standardized order sets and staff training—will markedly reduce underdosing and delays.

### [Management] In a patient with multiphasic anaphylaxis who has recurrent episodes despite appropriately dosed intramuscular epinephrine, when would you transition to a titratable intravenous epinephrine infusion, and what safety measures (monitoring, line selection, titration strategy) are essential when initiating IV epinephrine?

Bottom line
- Transition to a titratable IV epinephrine infusion when the anaphylaxis is “refractory” to appropriately dosed IM epinephrine or when repeated IM boluses are required to maintain airway/ventilation or blood pressure. In practice this means persistent or recurrent life‑threatening symptoms (hypotension, severe bronchospasm, or progressive airway compromise) despite 2–3 correctly dosed IM injections (0.01 mg/kg, max 0.5 mg), or when the patient needs frequent IM dosing within a short time window such that intermittent boluses are unsafe or impractical. 
- When you start an infusion, manage it in an ICU setting with continuous monitoring, use appropriate vascular access, prepare a standard dilute concentration and a clear titration plan, and have extravasation and arrhythmia management ready.

When to switch (clinical reasoning, tied to the case)
- Aim first-line therapy at rapid IM epinephrine (0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg for adults). Most patients respond. “Refractory anaphylaxis” is failure of haemodynamic/respiratory stabilization after appropriate IM dosing. Recurrent/multiphasic anaphylaxis that requires multiple IM doses (commonly >2 doses, or repeated doses within 1–2 hours) to treat recurrent hypotension or hypoxaemia is a reasonable trigger to escalate to a continuous infusion.  
- The presented patient required four IM epinephrine administrations (two at 0.3 mg auto‑injector then two 0.5 mg IM) and had recurrent shock and hypoxaemia despite initial response—precisely the situation in which many clinicians would consider an IV infusion rather than repeated boluses. An infusion smooths out peaks and troughs, provides steady vasopressor/bronchodilator effect, and avoids delays or dosing errors with repeated IM injections.  

Safety measures and practical steps when initiating IV epinephrine

1) Location and monitoring
- Admit to ICU or an area with continuous cardiac monitoring (telemetry), continuous pulse oximetry, frequent noninvasive BP or invasive arterial BP monitoring if instability is severe or frequent titration is expected.  
- Continuous ECG monitoring for arrhythmia; frequent assessment of mental status, skin perfusion, urine output, and serial lactate/chemistry as indicated.  
- If airway compromise is evolving, be prepared for early definitive airway and capnography.

2) Vascular access (line selection)
- Central venous access is preferred for prolonged infusions or when high concentrations/ high doses are anticipated (reduces risk of tissue injury with extravasation and allows higher flows).  
- A large-bore peripheral IV in a proximal, well‑functioning vein (antecubital or external jugular) is acceptable for short-term lower‑concentration infusions if central access is not immediately available — but inspect the site vigilantly for infiltration.  
- Have phentolamine and protocols available for extravasation management.

3) Preparation, concentration and labeling
- Use a standardized dilute concentration made by pharmacy or trained staff and label clearly. Two commonly used preparations:  
  - 1 mg (1000 mcg) epinephrine in 100 mL NS → 10 mcg/mL; or  
  - 1 mg in 250 mL NS → 4 mcg/mL (safer for peripheral infusion because lower concentration).  
- Use an infusion pump; double‑check calculations and have another clinician confirm.

4) Starting dose and titration strategy (practical)
- Start low and titrate to effect. Reasonable adult starting infusion: 0.05–0.1 mcg/kg/min (≈ 5–10 mcg/min for a 70‑kg adult). An alternative simple approach: start 5–10 mcg/min and increase by 2.5–5 mcg/min every 2–5 minutes until target achieved.  
- Titrate to clinical goals (restoration of systolic BP ≥90 mmHg or MAP ≥65 mmHg, resolution of severe bronchospasm or stridor, improvement of oxygenation) using the minimum effective dose.  
- If immediate correction of severe hypotension is needed, small IV boluses of epinephrine (e.g., 50–100 mcg IV) can be used under monitoring, then convert to infusion. Avoid large IV boluses without monitoring because of arrhythmia/hypertension risk.  
- Upper limits: doses up to 0.5 mcg/kg/min (or higher in extreme cases) are described, but risks (tachyarrhythmia, severe hypertension, myocardial ischemia) increase—escalate with intensivist/venous access and invasive monitoring.

5) Safety precautions and adjuncts
- Be vigilant for tachycardia, arrhythmias, myocardial ischemia, and severe hypertension—continuous ECG and, when indicated, arterial line.  
- Prepare for extravasation: stop infusion, aspirate, give local phentolamine infiltration (5–10 mg diluted) if extravasation suspected.  
- Review medications and comorbidities: beta‑blocker exposure can blunt epinephrine response—consider glucagon if refractory. MAOI or tricyclics can potentiate epinephrine effects.  
- Continue standard adjunctive therapy: high‑flow oxygen, aggressive IV fluids for vasodilation, inhaled bronchodilators for bronchospasm, H1/H2 blockers for cutaneous symptoms as needed. Routine steroids are no longer recommended solely to prevent biphasic events, but may be used case‑by‑case.

6) When to wean and stop
- Once haemodynamics and respiratory status are stable off boluses for several hours, wean slowly: decrease infusion in small steps and watch for recurrence of symptoms for several hours before stopping. Consider ICU observation for ≥6–24 hours depending on severity and frequency of relapses.

How this applies to the case
- The team appropriately escalated monitoring (ICU considered) after recurrent episodes. Given that she required multiple IM injections including two 0.5‑mg IM doses and had repeat hypotension/hypoxaemia, a continuous infusion would have been a reasonable choice at that point to prevent further relapses and avoid repetitive IM dosing — provided ICU monitoring, appropriate IV access and a formal titration plan were in place. In this patient the relapses abated and an infusion was not ultimately needed; but the decision to prepare for infusion was appropriate and consistent with best practice for refractory or frequently recurrent anaphylaxis.

Summary
- Consider IV epinephrine infusion when appropriately dosed IM epinephrine fails to produce sustained control (persistent hypotension, severe bronchospasm, or repeated need for IM boluses). Start the infusion in ICU, use appropriate access (central preferred for prolonged/high‑dose infusions), standardize concentration, start at 0.05–0.1 mcg/kg/min (≈5–10 mcg/min) and titrate to effect with continuous monitoring, and be prepared to treat extravasation and arrhythmias.

### [Pathophysiology] Explain the pathophysiologic mechanisms driving hypotension and hypoxemia in anaphylactic shock (e.g., vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, bronchospasm) and why both intramuscular epinephrine and early crystalloid boluses are indicated.

Direct answer
Anaphylactic shock causes hypotension and hypoxemia by a combination of profound systemic vasodilation, rapid loss of intravascular volume from increased capillary permeability, direct cardiac effects of mediators, and airway/bronchial obstruction from mucosal edema and bronchospasm. Intramuscular epinephrine treats all of these processes rapidly by alpha- and beta-adrenergic effects, and early liberal crystalloid boluses are needed to restore circulating volume that has redistributed into the interstitium. This is why guidelines emphasize immediate IM epinephrine (repeat as needed) plus early aggressive IV fluids rather than relying on antihistamines or steroids alone.

Pathophysiology—how hypotension develops
- Vasodilation: IgE‑mediated mast cell and basophil degranulation releases histamine, prostaglandins and nitric oxide–mediating factors that cause widespread arteriolar and venous dilation. The fall in systemic vascular resistance produces distributive shock and pooling of blood in the capacitance vessels.
- Increased vascular permeability: Mediators (histamine, platelet‑activating factor, leukotrienes) increase endothelial gap formation and transudation of plasma into the interstitium. Large intravascular volume loss may occur within minutes (so-called “relative hypovolemia”), reducing venous return and cardiac preload.
- Myocardial effects: Mediators can depress myocardial contractility directly and, in severe cases, ischemia from hypotension worsens cardiac output.
Together these mechanisms produce rapid hypotension that may be profound (as in the case: BP 74/50 mm Hg).

Pathophysiology—how hypoxemia develops
- Upper‑airway edema: Laryngeal and pharyngeal mucosal swelling causes stridor/airway obstruction and impaired oxygenation.
- Bronchospasm and lower‑airway narrowing: Beta‑mediated bronchoconstrictor substances and smooth‑muscle contraction cause wheeze, increased work of breathing and V/Q mismatch.
- Noncardiogenic pulmonary edema and increased secretions can further impair gas exchange.
In the case, the patient had hypoxemia (SpO2 88% on room air) and dyspnea even when initial chest auscultation was normal—consistent with early bronchospasm, mucosal edema, and V/Q mismatch.

Why intramuscular epinephrine is central
- Alpha‑1 agonism: rapid vasoconstriction increases systemic vascular resistance, raises blood pressure, and reduces mucosal edema by constricting leaky microvasculature in the airway.
- Beta‑1 agonism: increases heart rate and contractility to restore cardiac output.
- Beta‑2 agonism: bronchodilation improves airflow and oxygenation; beta‑2 signals also suppress further mediator release from mast cells (reducing ongoing degranulation).
- Practical pharmacology: IM injection into the anterolateral thigh gives rapid and reliable absorption without the arrhythmogenic risk of IV bolus epinephrine. Recommended adult dose is 0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg (many auto‑injectors supply 0.3 mg, which can underdose adults >50 kg). Repeat every 5–15 minutes as needed. In the reported patient, an initial 0.3‑mg IM auto‑injector produced a transient response; subsequent 0.5‑mg IM doses were required for later episodes — illustrating both the need to reach an adequate dose and to repeat dosing.

Why early crystalloid boluses are indicated
- Because capillary leak creates substantial intravascular volume loss, vasoconstriction alone (from epinephrine) may be insufficient if preload is low. Early rapid IV crystalloid boluses restore circulating volume, improve venous return, and synergize with vasoconstriction to raise blood pressure.
- Practical guidance: give an initial rapid bolus (eg, 20 mL/kg in adults; commonly 1–2 L up front in a typical adult) and reassess; repeat boluses as necessary. Smaller volumes (eg, a single 500 mL) are often inadequate when capillary leak is large, which may contribute to recurrent hypotension — a pattern seen in this case where only 500 mL was given initially and additional boluses were needed with relapse.
- If hypotension is refractory despite adequate IM epinephrine and aggressive fluids, consider IV epinephrine infusion under monitoring (or an IV bolus in extreme situations) and intensive care transfer.

Clinical implications from the case
- The patient presented with hypotension (74/50 mm Hg) and hypoxemia (SpO2 88%) and had prompt IM epinephrine, oxygen, and a single 500‑mL bolus of NS; she initially improved but had recurrent episodes requiring further IM epinephrine and another 500‑mL bolus. This pattern highlights two teaching points:
  1) IM epinephrine given early is lifesaving because it reverses vasodilation, reduces airway edema and bronchodilates.
  2) Early, larger-volume fluid resuscitation is often required to correct the profound intravascular hypovolemia of anaphylaxis; inadequately small initial boluses can contribute to relapse.
- Underlying mast cell disorders (elevated baseline tryptase in this patient) increase severity and risk of biphasic/multiphasic courses because of higher mast cell burden and propensity for prolonged mediator release — increasing the importance of early epinephrine, adequate fluids, close observation and readiness to repeat therapy or escalate to continuous IV epinephrine.

Bottom line (clinical pearl)
Treat anaphylactic shock as a combined distributive plus hypovolemic process with possible airway compromise. Give immediate intramuscular epinephrine (0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg into the lateral thigh; repeat as needed), high‑flow oxygen, and early liberal IV crystalloids (initial bolus ~20 mL/kg or 1–2 L in adults) while preparing for escalation (IV epinephrine infusion, airway control, ICU) if hypotension or hypoxemia persist. Antihistamines and steroids are adjunctive and do not replace epi or fluids.

### [Morning Report] After initial stabilization of severe anaphylaxis with hypotension, hypoxemia, and the need for multiple epinephrine doses, how would you determine the duration and level of observation, and which features specifically mandate ICU-level monitoring in this patient?

Direct answer
- Use the clinical severity of the index reaction and presence of risk factors to choose level and duration of observation. A single, promptly treated mild reaction can often be observed 4–6 hours; severe reactions with hypotension, hypoxaemia, need for multiple epinephrine doses, refractory or relapsing disease, or underlying mast‑cell disorder warrant ICU‑level monitoring for at least 24 hours (often 24–48 hours) because of the high risk of biphasic or multiphasic relapse.

Why — clinical reasoning and pathophysiology
- Biphasic/multiphasic anaphylaxis reflects ongoing mediator release or delayed secondary waves of mast‑cell/basophil activation; predictors are imperfect but cluster around features that signify a large mediator burden or unstable physiology. In practice the strongest practical predictors are: (1) severe initial cardiorespiratory compromise (profound hypotension, hypoxaemia, airway oedema), (2) need for more than one dose of intramuscular epinephrine or ongoing vasopressor support, (3) delayed initial epinephrine, (4) suspected mast‑cell disorder or markedly raised baseline/acute tryptase, and (5) refractory or protracted symptoms. These features increase the likelihood of recurrence and of rapid decompensation if relapse occurs — hence the need for more intensive monitoring and immediate access to advanced airway and vasoactive support.

Which features mandate ICU‑level monitoring (apply to this patient)
- Any of the following should push you to ICU monitoring:
  1. Persistent or recurrent hypotension requiring repeated epinephrine doses, large fluid boluses, or vasopressors.
  2. Respiratory compromise: hypoxaemia, stridor, progressive airway oedema, need for high‑flow oxygen or concern for impending intubation.
  3. Requirement for >1 IM epinephrine dose, or anticipated need for continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion.
  4. Refractory or multiphasic course (recurrent anaphylaxis after initial clinical response).
  5. Known or suspected mast‑cell disorder (elevated tryptase or features suggesting systemic mastocytosis).
  6. Significant comorbidity (cardiovascular disease, beta‑blocker use) or arrhythmia/ischemia during the event.
- In the presented case the patient had profound hypotension (74/50 mm Hg), hypoxaemia (SpO2 88%), required multiple epinephrine injections (total of 4 episodes), and later was found to have elevated tryptase and probable systemic mastocytosis — all of which justified ICU admission.

Practical monitoring and management principles in ICU
- Continuous cardiac telemetry and pulse‑oximetry; frequent clinical airway checks.
- Maintain IV access, document frequent vitals (initially q15–30 min), and have epinephrine immediately available (IM for recurrences, IV infusion if recurrent despite IM or if circulation unstable).
- Consider early escalation to continuous IV epinephrine if repeated IM doses fail, or if hemodynamic instability persists.
- Serial tryptase sampling per guidelines (as soon as feasible; 1–2 h after onset — within 4 h for the peak; and a third sample ≥24 h after resolution to establish baseline) — the case illustrates missed early sampling but later elevated tryptase supported mast‑cell disease.
- Routine corticosteroids are no longer recommended solely to prevent biphasic reactions; their use is individualized. Antihistamines treat cutaneous symptoms but do not prevent airway or cardiovascular collapse.
- Duration: for high‑risk patients (features above) plan for at least 24 hours of ICU observation; consider 24–48 hours in protracted or multiphasic courses or with mast‑cell disorders. For lower‑risk patients who are asymptomatic off oxygen and vasopressors after observation, shorter (4–6 h) monitoring may be acceptable.

Tie‑back to the case
- The team’s decision to transfer the patient to ICU after the first relapse was appropriate given her initial severe hypotension and hypoxaemia, the need for multiple epinephrine doses, and the later finding of raised tryptase and probable systemic mastocytosis. She experienced further relapses within 12 hours (typical timing for many biphasic events) and stabilized without need for continuous infusion; after ~24 hours without recurrence she was stepped down — an approach consistent with risk‑based practice.

### [Management] What does current evidence suggest about the role of corticosteroids in acute anaphylaxis, particularly in preventing biphasic reactions, and what potential harms or unintended consequences should steer practice away from routine steroid use?

Short answer
Current evidence does not support routine use of corticosteroids in the acute management of anaphylaxis for the purpose of preventing biphasic (or multiphasic) reactions. Randomized and observational data and systematic reviews show no convincing reduction in biphasic events, and potential harms and unintended consequences make routine steroid administration inadvisable. Steroids should be reserved for clear indications (eg, concomitant severe bronchospasm/asthma exacerbation, adrenal insufficiency) and not given reflexively to “prevent” a late phase.

Why steroids were used historically — and why that rationale is weak
The theoretical rationale is that corticosteroids suppress late inflammatory gene transcription and cellular recruitment and therefore might blunt a delayed, late-phase mediator response. That biologic effect requires hours to manifest (gene transcription, protein synthesis) and is not immediate. Most biphasic recurrences occur within the first 24 hours (commonly within 6–12 hours), and mast cell–mediated mediator release occurs early; therefore a single steroid dose given at presentation is biologically unlikely to abolish an early-to-intermediate recurrence.

What the evidence shows
- Systematic reviews and the preponderance of observational studies do not demonstrate that corticosteroids reduce the risk of biphasic anaphylaxis. The case report cites a 2017 systematic review of 22 studies in which only one study suggested benefit and 21 showed no reduction in biphasic risk.  
- Some contemporary analyses even show associations between prehospital steroid administration and higher rates of hospital or ICU admission (a 2019 study cited in the report). While confounding by indication may contribute, these data underscore the absence of clear benefit and raise concerns about unintended consequences.  
- Consequently, contemporary guidelines have moved away from routine steroid use in anaphylaxis and emphasize prompt epinephrine, supportive care and risk‑stratified observation instead.

Potential harms and unintended consequences
- Immediate physiologic harms from a single dose are usually small, but in vulnerable patients they matter: hyperglycaemia (important in diabetics like the patient in this case), mood/psych effects, and—if repeated—immunosuppression and infection risk.  
- Steroid administration can encourage anchoring on an ineffective preventive strategy and distract from evidence‑based measures (timely epinephrine, appropriate observation, measurement of tryptase when indicated).  
- Use of steroids may lengthen hospital stays or increase admissions (reported associations), contribute to polypharmacy (the case patient received both prednisone and dexamethasone because of protocol differences), and complicate glycemic control in diabetics.  
- Finally, giving steroids may induce a false sense of security about preventing recurrence and thereby change observation or disposition decisions inappropriately.

Clinical application to the presented case
In the presented multiphasic anaphylaxis the team administered systemic corticosteroids (IV prednisone daily for 5 days plus one dose of dexamethasone) after the first recurrence because of traditional practice. The patient ultimately had no further relapses, but this single case does not establish causation; it is consistent with broader data showing spontaneous resolution in most cases and no clear steroid effect. Importantly, the patient had diabetes—an individual risk for steroid-related hyperglycaemia—highlighting one of the harms to weigh before routine steroid use.

Practical guidance / teaching pearls
- First-line treatment: give intramuscular epinephrine promptly and repeat as needed; provide airway, breathing, circulation support.  
- Do not give corticosteroids routinely solely to prevent biphasic reactions. Reserve steroids for separate indications (eg, persistent severe bronchospasm, known adrenal insufficiency, or other accepted reasons).  
- Focus instead on adequate observation and disposition: prolong observation for patients with severe initial presentations, delayed epinephrine, multiple epinephrine doses, comorbid respiratory disease or suspected mast cell disorders (as in this patient). Individualize ICU monitoring for refractory or multiphasic courses and consider continuous epinephrine infusion in refractory cases.  
- When steroids are given, document the indication, choose the shortest effective regimen, and monitor (eg, glucose in diabetics). Continue standard secondary measures (antihistamines for cutaneous symptoms, allergist follow-up, tryptase sampling per protocol).

Bottom line
Routine corticosteroids do not prevent biphasic anaphylaxis and carry potential adverse effects and practical downsides. Management should prioritize rapid epinephrine, appropriate monitoring and disposition decisions; steroids should be used selectively for other clear clinical indications rather than as a reflexive strategy to prevent late anaphylactic relapses.

### [Clinical Pearls] Outline the recommended timing and interpretation of serum tryptase measurements in suspected anaphylaxis, including when to obtain acute and baseline samples and how to apply the “20% + 2 ng/mL” rule; how would persistent baseline elevation influence evaluation for a mast cell disorder?

Key point: serum tryptase is a marker of mast‑cell degranulation and can support (but not replace) the clinical diagnosis of anaphylaxis. Timing of sampling and comparison with a patient’s baseline are essential for correct interpretation.

Recommended timing
- Obtain an acute sample as soon as possible after recognition of anaphylaxis — do not delay life‑saving treatment to draw blood.  
- Obtain a second “peak” sample at about 1–2 hours after symptom onset (acceptable up to 4 hours) because tryptase generally peaks about 1–2 hours after mast‑cell activation.  
- Obtain a baseline sample at least 24 hours after complete clinical resolution (commonly 24–48 hours) to establish the patient’s steady‑state tryptase. Ideally you will have: (1) immediate, (2) 1–2 h (peak), and (3) ≥24 h (baseline) samples; many centres obtain at least an acute and a baseline sample.

Interpretation: the “20% + 2 ng/mL” rule
- Absolute cutoffs (eg, >11.4 ng/mL) are insensitive. The accepted diagnostic approach is to compare the acute (peak) level to the patient’s baseline using the “20% + 2 ng/mL” rule.  
- Formula: a rise is significant if acute tryptase ≥ (baseline × 1.2) + 2 ng/mL.  
- Examples:
  - baseline 5 ng/mL → threshold = 5×1.2 + 2 = 8 ng/mL (acute ≥8 is significant).  
  - baseline 11 ng/mL → threshold = 11×1.2 + 2 = 15.2 ng/mL (acute ≥15.2 is significant).  
This rule increases sensitivity for true mast‑cell activation across a range of baselines.

Clinical caveats
- A normal tryptase does not exclude anaphylaxis (especially food‑triggered anaphylaxis or if sampling is late). Clinical diagnosis and prompt treatment remain paramount.  
- Missing the peak sample (eg, only a late acute sample or only a single baseline later) limits interpretability — as in the presented case, where recommended intervals were not fully adhered to and peak measurements were therefore suboptimal.

Persistent baseline elevation — next steps and implications
- A persistently elevated baseline tryptase (measured after full recovery, and confirmed on repeat testing) raises concern for an underlying mast‑cell disorder.  
- WHO minor criterion for systemic mastocytosis: baseline serum tryptase persistently >20 ng/mL (in the absence of an associated clonal myeloid neoplasm). Even lower persistent elevations merit further evaluation when clinical features (recurrent severe anaphylaxis, hepatosplenomegaly, skin lesions, bone disease) are present.  
- Recommended evaluation if baseline is persistently elevated or clinical suspicion is high:
  - Repeat baseline tryptase to confirm persistence.  
  - Refer to allergy/immunology and hematology for: bone marrow examination with morphology and immunophenotyping (CD25/CD2 on mast cells), testing for KIT D816V mutation (PCR), and consideration of other investigations (abdominal imaging for hepatosplenomegaly, bone density).  
  - Test for hereditary alpha‑tryptasemia (TPSAB1 copy number) because increased TPSAB1 copies can raise baseline tryptase and increase anaphylaxis risk; this has different implications from clonal mast‑cell disease.  
  - Exclude other causes of elevated tryptase (eg, certain myeloid neoplasms, severe renal failure) as clinically indicated.

Apply this to the case
- The case illustrates the pitfalls of incomplete sampling: the team obtained a tryptase 1 hour after the third episode and a “baseline” 27 hours after the last recurrence, but missed earlier recommended timepoints, limiting some interpretability. Nevertheless, the report documents persistently elevated baseline tryptase on follow‑up and other suggestive findings (hepatomegaly, recurrent severe/multiphasic anaphylaxis), prompting further evaluation for systemic mastocytosis. Bone‑marrow sampling was inconclusive, so formal diagnostic criteria were not fully met — a situation where KIT testing, flow cytometry for aberrant mast‑cell markers and TPSAB1 genotyping are particularly useful next steps.

Bottom line (clinical pearl)
- In any suspected anaphylaxis: draw tryptase early and again at ~1–2 hours (or within 4 hours), and obtain a baseline ≥24 h after complete recovery. Use the 20% + 2 ng/mL rule to identify significant mast‑cell activation. If baseline tryptase is persistently elevated, refer for evaluation for systemic mast‑cell disease (including KIT mutation testing, bone‑marrow assessment) and consider hereditary alpha‑tryptasemia testing — because identifying a mast‑cell disorder alters risk stratification and long‑term management.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Summarize the WHO diagnostic criteria for systemic mastocytosis (major and minor) and indicate which clinical and laboratory findings in this patient raise suspicion for systemic mastocytosis despite not meeting full diagnostic criteria.

Short answer
- WHO diagnostic algorithm: systemic mastocytosis (SM) is established by either (1) the major criterion plus ≥1 minor criterion, or (2) ≥3 minor criteria. In this patient the team found two minor criteria but the major criterion could not be assessed because the bone‑marrow specimen was inadequate — hence SM could be suspected but not formally diagnosed.

WHO criteria (concise)
- Major criterion
  - Multifocal, dense infiltrates of mast cells (clusters of ≥15 mast cells) in bone marrow and/or extracutaneous organ(s) on histology.
- Minor criteria (any of the following)
  1. >25% of mast cells in the infiltrate are spindle‑shaped or atypical on morphology, or >25% of mast cells in bone‑marrow smears are immature.
  2. Presence of an activating KIT mutation at codon 816 (eg, KIT D816V) in blood, bone marrow or other extracutaneous organ.
  3. Mast cells in marrow or extracutaneous tissue express aberrant surface markers (CD2 and/or CD25).
  4. Persistently elevated baseline serum tryptase concentration >20 ng/mL (unless there is an associated clonal myeloid neoplasm that explains the elevation).

How the disease is confirmed
- Diagnosis: major + ≥1 minor, or ≥3 minor criteria.

Which features in this patient raise strong suspicion despite not meeting full criteria
- Persistently elevated serum tryptase: the report documents increased tryptase measured during an anaphylactic episode and a persistently elevated baseline level on follow‑up. A baseline tryptase >20 ng/mL is one of the WHO minor criteria and is an important laboratory clue to an underlying clonal mast cell disorder.
- Clinical phenotype highly suggestive of a mast cell disorder:
  - Severe, recurrent (multiphasic) anaphylaxis requiring multiple epinephrine doses — SM greatly increases risk of severe and recurrent mediator‑driven reactions.
  - Marked cutaneous reactivity history (strong local reaction to a wasp sting) and a first severe systemic reaction in later adulthood — both raise suspicion for an underlying mast cell proliferative disorder.
- Imaging evidence of organ involvement: hepatomegaly on abdominal sonography supports extracutaneous involvement (compatible with systemic disease when caused by mast cell infiltration), even though it is not by itself sufficient for diagnosis.
- Procedural limitation that delayed formal confirmation: the major histologic criterion could not be assessed because the bone‑marrow biopsy material was limited. The authors state only two WHO minor criteria were met, so the formal diagnostic threshold (major+1 or three minors) was not reached.

Clinical teaching point
- In any adult with severe, recurrent or atypical anaphylaxis, check serum tryptase (acute and baseline samples) and consider evaluation for systemic mastocytosis. A persistently elevated baseline tryptase and clinical features of severe/recurrent mediator release should prompt hematology/allergy referral and a carefully performed bone‑marrow evaluation (with adequate material for morphology, immunophenotyping for CD25/CD2 and molecular testing for KIT D816V) because incomplete sampling can leave the diagnosis unresolved.

### [Management] What are the key elements of discharge planning after multiphasic anaphylaxis to a new antibiotic, including selection and dosing of epinephrine auto-injectors for an adult over 50 kg, patient education and action plan, medication avoidance and documentation, and timing of allergy/immunology follow-up?

Bottom line
- Discharge after multiphasic anaphylaxis must treat the event as high-risk: give the patient the correct epinephrine auto‑injector(s), a written action plan, clear avoidance instructions and documentation, and timely allergy/immunology follow‑up to define the trigger and investigate mast‑cell disease. In this case (woman in her  60s, 104 kg, multiphasic reaction to clarithromycin with elevated tryptase), that means prescribing and teaching use of 0.5 mg intramuscular epinephrine devices (and providing two devices), explicit macrolide avoidance until specialist review, and rapid allergist referral.

Key elements — practical checklist with clinical reasoning

1) Epinephrine auto‑injector selection and dosing
- Target dose: epinephrine IM 0.01 mg/kg, maximum single IM dose 0.5 mg. For adults >50 kg this is effectively 0.5 mg. (Case note: the team initially used 0.3 mg auto‑injectors; authors emphasise this can underdose heavier adults and prompt additional doses or escalation.)
- Prescribe a 0.5 mg auto‑injector for this patient (if available in your country). Because she had severe, multiphasic anaphylaxis and elevated tryptase suggesting mast‑cell disease, prescribe two auto‑injectors (carry two at all times). Rationale: severe prior reaction and suspected mastocytosis increase risk of recurrence and need for repeated dosing before EMS arrival.
- If a 0.5 mg auto‑injector is not available locally, give a 0.3 mg device but reinforce that repeat dosing is appropriate: if symptoms persist/worsen after 5 minutes, give a second injection (repeat every 5–15 minutes as needed) and call emergency services. Preferably replace 0.3 mg devices with a 0.5 mg device as soon as available.

2) Patient education and written action plan
- Teach and practice injection technique (anterolateral thigh, through clothing if necessary). Emphasize to hold device in place per manufacturer instructions (follow device label for seconds to hold). Demonstrate removal and disposal.
- Explicit action steps to give to the patient in writing and review verbally:
  1. At first signs of anaphylaxis (respiratory difficulty, throat tightness, hives with hypotension, collapse, severe lip/tongue swelling, persistent lightheadedness) administer epinephrine immediately.
  2. Call emergency services immediately after injection — go to the ED even if symptoms improve.
  3. If no improvement or symptoms recur, administer a second epinephrine dose after 5 minutes (repeat every 5–15 minutes as needed) and continue to seek emergency care.
  4. Lie supine with legs elevated if tolerable; if vomiting or respiratory distress, sit up to protect airway.
  5. Inform caregivers and wear medical‑alert identification.
- Reinforce that antihistamines and corticosteroids are NOT substitutes for epinephrine; they may be used for symptom control but do not prevent respiratory/circulatory collapse.
- Demonstrate storage (room temperature, check expiry), replacement after use, and advising family members/caregivers in device use.
- Provide printed emergency card summarizing the allergic trigger, reaction severity, epinephrine dosing, and emergency contacts.

3) Short‑term discharge medications and counseling
- Provide two epinephrine auto‑injectors with clear dosing labeled (0.5 mg IM each) and prescriptions for replacements.
- Non‑sedating H1 antihistamine (eg, cetirizine/levocetirizine) may be prescribed for urticaria/pruritus; explain it is adjunctive only.
- Routine systemic corticosteroids are not recommended solely to prevent biphasic reactions; evidence does not support routine use and harms exist. Use steroids only if there is a separate clinical indication or by specialist advice. (In the case report steroids were used empirically, reflecting variability in practice.)
- Consider advising on avoidance of beta‑blockers if possible (they blunt epinephrine response) — discuss with primary clinician if patient needs beta‑blocker therapy.

4) Medication avoidance, documentation and communication
- Explicitly document “Anaphylaxis to clarithromycin (macrolide?) — avoid clarithromycin and consider avoiding all macrolides until evaluated by an allergist” in the hospital chart, discharge summary, and primary care record.
- Provide the patient with a written allergy card/letter that includes: agent (clarithromycin), date, nature and severity (anaphylaxis with hypotension and hypoxaemia), treatments given (epinephrine x number of doses, ICU admission), and recommended avoidance.
- Update electronic medical record allergy list and communicate directly (phone or secure message) with the primary care physician and any treating specialists about the reaction and planned follow‑up.

5) Allergy/immunology follow‑up: timing and testing principles
- Arrange allergy/immunology follow‑up promptly (preferably within 1–4 weeks) to:
  - Review the event and determine plan for further testing.
  - Arrange formal baseline tryptase measurement (≥24 hours after full resolution) and repeat testing to establish baseline if elevated — important because persistent elevation suggests mast‑cell disorder and alters risk counseling.
  - Plan definitive allergy testing only when safe: skin testing and drug provocation (if indicated) are usually deferred for at least 4–6 weeks after anaphylaxis, and require stopping antihistamines for an appropriate washout before skin testing.
- In patients with elevated baseline tryptase or clinical suspicion of systemic mastocytosis (as in this case), expedite specialist assessment (allergy/immunology ± hematology) because mast‑cell disease changes risk stratification and peri‑procedural/premedication recommendations.
- Do not attempt provocative testing with the implicated antibiotic in the acute post‑event period; consider in vitro testing only if available and useful; in many instances avoidance without provocation is reasonable if safe alternative antibiotics exist.

6) Special considerations for this case (illustrated by the report)
- The patient weighed 104 kg and had recurrent episodes requiring multiple epinephrine doses; this history supports prescribing 0.5 mg auto‑injector(s) and carrying two devices.
- Her elevated tryptase and suspected systemic mastocytosis raise her baseline risk for severe and recurrent episodes — strengthen the case for two devices, close follow‑up, and expedited specialist assessment.
- Document the multiphasic course and ICU admission in discharge paperwork so that outpatient providers understand the severity and rationale for aggressive follow‑up and device prescriptions.

Summary statement for discharge note
- “Severe, multiphasic anaphylaxis to clarithromycin with ICU admission and elevated tryptase. Discharged with two epinephrine auto‑injectors (0.5 mg IM each), instructed in immediate use and to call EMS after use; carrying medical‑alert ID; avoid clarithromycin (and consider avoiding macrolides until allergist review); started on oral nonsedating antihistamine for symptom control. Allergy/immunology follow‑up arranged within 1–4 weeks for tryptase baseline and to plan safe testing/definitive management; consider hematology input for suspected mastocytosis.”

### [Clinical Pearls] List established and proposed risk factors for biphasic anaphylaxis (e.g., delayed epinephrine, severe initial presentation, need for multiple epinephrine doses, prior biphasic reactions), and identify which of these were present in this case.

Clinical answer (Clinical Pearl)

What predicts biphasic (or multiphasic) anaphylaxis?
- Established and repeatedly proposed risk factors include:
  - Severe initial presentation (circulatory collapse, hypoxaemia, airway compromise). Severe initial mediator release and end‑organ involvement are consistently associated with higher risk of recurrence.
  - Delayed administration of epinephrine. Data show that delayed first epinephrine is linked to more complications and higher biphasic risk—presumably because ongoing mediator release is less well controlled.
  - Need for multiple doses of epinephrine or escalation of care during the index event. Requirement for repeated epinephrine is a marker of refractory or more intense mediator activation and correlates with recurrence.
  - High or rising serum tryptase during the event and elevated baseline tryptase. These reflect a greater mast cell burden/activation and are increasingly recognized as markers of severe and potentially recurrent reactions.
  - Prior biphasic or severe anaphylactic reactions (history of recurrent severe reactions) — a history of complicated prior events suggests predisposition.
- Other proposed/less well‑validated risk factors (reported variably):
  - Large or ongoing antigen exposure (oral ingestion with ongoing absorption, depot formulations).
  - Older age, obesity, or significant cardiopulmonary comorbidity.
  - Concomitant medications that interfere with response to epinephrine (eg, beta‑blockers) or enhance severity (ACE inhibitors) — data are mixed.
  - Absence of early cutaneous signs or predominant cardiovascular/respiratory features (harder to recognize early).
- Important caveat: predictors are imperfect; overall incidence of biphasic anaphylaxis is low and the literature shows wide variability. Routine corticosteroids do not reliably prevent biphasic reactions.

Applying these to the presented case
- Present and likely contributed:
  - Severe initial presentation: yes — profound hypotension (74/50 mm Hg), hypoxaemia (SpO2 88%), widespread urticaria and facial swelling with rapid progression. This meets the classic “severe” phenotype linked to recurrence.
  - Requirement for multiple epinephrine doses: yes — the patient had four anaphylactic episodes and required repeated intramuscular epinephrine to treat each relapse, which is a marker of more refractory mediator activity and higher relapse risk.
  - Elevated/baseline tryptase and probable systemic mastocytosis: yes — tryptase was elevated during the course and remained high on follow‑up, and further workup suggested a high probability of systemic mastocytosis. A high mast cell burden is a biologically plausible and increasingly recognized risk factor for severe and recurrent (including biphasic or multiphasic) anaphylaxis.
  - Ongoing antigen exposure: unlikely — only a single 500 mg oral clarithromycin dose was taken and the drug was discontinued; ongoing absorption was not suspected.
  - Prior biphasic reactions: no — this was her first anaphylactic episode historically.
- Potentially important caveat in this case:
  - Timing of first epinephrine was prompt after ED arrival (not classically “delayed”), but the initial dose used was 0.3 mg IM (auto‑injector). For a 60s woman weighing 104 kg this is below the commonly cited adult target (0.01 mg/kg, often rounded to 0.5 mg for adults), and underdosing has been associated in some series with need for additional doses/escalation of care — effectively behaving like inadequate initial therapy. Thus, although treatment was timely, the initial underdosing and the patient’s obesity likely reduced initial potency of intervention and may have contributed to relapses.
- Other factors in the case that do not explain biphasic risk:
  - No beta‑blocker use or other medications clearly known to blunt epinephrine effect; no clear ongoing allergen source; age and comorbidities (diabetes, hypertension, obesity) may have modest influence but are not primary drivers in the literature.

Bottom line (teaching point)
- Biphasic/multiphasic anaphylaxis is uncommon but more likely after a severe initial event, when multiple epinephrine doses are required, and when mast cell burden is high (eg, elevated tryptase/systemic mastocytosis). In this patient the key risk factors present were severe initial shock, need for multiple epinephrine doses, and raised tryptase with probable systemic mastocytosis; initial epinephrine dosing was timely but suboptimal for body weight, which may have functionally contributed to relapse risk. These findings support prolonged observation and specialist follow‑up (allergology/immunology, consideration of mast cell disorder workup) after the index event.
