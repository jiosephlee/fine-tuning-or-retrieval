# Multiphasic Anaphylaxis: Clinical Approach, Mechanisms, and Management Through a Clarithromycin-Triggered Case

## The Case Vignette

A woman in her 60s with type 2 diabetes, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression presented one hour after her first-ever dose of oral clarithromycin (500 mg) with pruritus, flushing, agitation, throat tightness, hypotension (74/50 mm Hg), hypoxemia (SpO2 88% on room air), diffuse urticaria, and facial edema. She had a remote history of nonanaphylactic facial swelling with amoxicillin in adolescence and a large local reaction to a wasp sting. Initial treatment with intramuscular epinephrine (0.3 mg), oxygen, intravenous antihistamine, and fluids produced rapid improvement. Clarithromycin was discontinued.

Over the ensuing 12 hours, she experienced three further anaphylactic relapses without re-exposure, each requiring intramuscular epinephrine (two 0.3 mg auto-injector doses followed by two 0.5 mg doses). She was admitted to the ICU after the first relapse for monitoring. Corticosteroids (prednisone 30 mg IV daily for five days and a single 8 mg dose of dexamethasone IV) and additional antihistamine were administered. Each episode was less severe than the prior one, and no intravenous epinephrine infusion was ultimately required. After 24 hours without recurrence she returned to the ward and was discharged three days after admission.

Given the atypical, multiphasic course, further evaluation demonstrated an elevated acute tryptase during the third episode and persistently elevated baseline tryptase on follow-up, hepatomegaly on ultrasound, and features suggestive of an underlying mast cell disorder. Bone marrow material was limited; two minor World Health Organization (WHO) criteria for systemic mastocytosis were met, but the major criterion could not be assessed, precluding a formal diagnosis. She was discharged with education, an epinephrine auto-injector, and a non-sedating H1 antihistamine, and has had no further episodes.

This case illustrates the diagnostic and management challenges of biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis and highlights the importance of recognizing underlying mast cell disorders that increase risk and severity.

## Initial Clinical Approach: Recognize, Treat, and Anticipate Recurrence

Anaphylaxis is a clinical diagnosis. In adults, it is suspected when there is an acute illness (minutes to hours) with skin/mucosal involvement plus respiratory compromise or hypotension; or hypotension after exposure to a known or likely allergen; or two or more organ systems involved after exposure to a likely allergen. The patient’s hypotension, hypoxemia, diffuse urticaria, and facial edema one hour after a new oral antibiotic meets diagnostic criteria.

Immediate priorities are to remove the trigger if possible, position the patient supine with legs elevated (or left lateral if pregnant), administer high-flow oxygen, secure IV access, and give intramuscular epinephrine in the mid-anterolateral thigh without delay. Adults should receive 0.5 mg of 1 mg/mL (1:1000) solution IM, repeated every 5–15 minutes as needed based on clinical response. Early and generous crystalloid resuscitation (1–2 L) supports intravascular volume depleted by vasodilation and capillary leak. Inhaled beta-2 agonists are helpful for lower airway bronchospasm. H1 and H2 antihistamines may improve cutaneous symptoms but do not treat airway edema or shock and must never delay epinephrine. Glucocorticoids have no role in the acute reversal of airway edema or hypotension.

From the initial response onward, clinicians must anticipate clinical trajectories: (1) prompt resolution without recurrence, (2) protracted or refractory symptoms requiring escalation (eg, epinephrine infusion, vasopressors), or (3) resolution followed by relapse—biphasic or, rarely, multiphasic anaphylaxis. The last was exemplified here.

## Building the Differential: What This Presentation Suggests—and What to Exclude

The immediate differential for acute hypotension with flushing, urticaria, and dyspnea strongly favors anaphylaxis. Alternative diagnoses may mimic components of the syndrome but typically lack its full constellation:

- Bradykinin-mediated angioedema (ACE inhibitor-induced, hereditary/acquired C1 inhibitor deficiency) causes angioedema without urticaria and usually lacks bronchospasm or hypotension.
- Sepsis, pulmonary embolism, and cardiogenic shock cause hypotension and hypoxemia but are not associated with abrupt pruritus, urticaria, and flushing.
- Carcinoid crisis, pheochromocytoma, scombroid poisoning, and “red man syndrome” (vancomycin) have overlapping features but specific contexts and triggers.
- Panic attacks and vasovagal syncope may cause dyspnea or hypotension, but urticaria and angioedema are absent.

Within anaphylaxis, the mechanism can be IgE-mediated (classical), non–IgE immunologic (eg, complement activation), or nonimmunologic direct mast cell activation (eg, via MRGPRX2 with some medications). Macrolide antibiotics are uncommon but documented triggers of IgE-mediated anaphylaxis. The timing (one hour after first dose), mixed organ involvement, and hypotension point to a systemic mast cell–mediated reaction.

Recurrence without re-exposure compels differentiation among phenotypes:
- Biphasic anaphylaxis: recurrence of symptoms after complete or near-complete resolution, typically within 1–24 hours (reported up to 72 hours), without re-exposure.
- Multiphasic anaphylaxis: more than one recurrence.
- Protracted anaphylaxis: persistent symptoms and signs for hours without full resolution.
- Refractory anaphylaxis: inadequate response to appropriate intramuscular epinephrine and supportive care.

In practice, distinguishing true biphasic relapse from ongoing allergen absorption (eg, retained oral medication, slow-release preparation) or transient epinephrine “wearing off” requires careful attention to timing, treatment, and clinical resolution. Oral allergens, including some drugs, are absorbed over hours and may contribute to late symptom waves.

## Pathophysiology: From Immediate to Late-Phase Responses

Classical anaphylaxis is driven by allergen-specific IgE bound to the high-affinity FcεRI receptor on mast cells and basophils. Allergen cross-linking causes rapid degranulation and synthesis of mediators:

- Preformed mediators (seconds to minutes): histamine, tryptase, chymase, heparin activate endothelium and sensory nerves, increase vascular permeability, and promote smooth muscle constriction.
- Newly synthesized mediators (minutes): leukotrienes (LTC4/D4/E4) and prostaglandins (PGD2) intensify bronchoconstriction, vasodilation, and mucous secretion.
- Cytokines and chemokines (hours): TNF, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-13 recruit inflammatory cells and sustain late-phase inflammation.

Cardiovascular collapse in anaphylaxis reflects a combination of distributive shock (vasodilation and increased permeability), relative hypovolemia, and in some cases myocardial depression and coronary vasospasm. Platelet-activating factor (PAF) correlates with severity; low PAF acetylhydrolase activity has been associated with severe reactions.

Biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis likely reflect converging mechanisms:
- Ongoing or delayed allergen absorption (notably with oral or depot exposures), enterohepatic recirculation, or slow tissue release.
- Late-phase inflammatory cascades with sustained mediator generation independent of further allergen exposure.
- Insufficient initial adrenergic counter-regulation (eg, delayed or inadequate epinephrine dosing, subcutaneous rather than intramuscular deposition in obesity).
- Enhanced mast cell burden or reactivity in clonal disorders (eg, systemic mastocytosis) or hereditary alpha-tryptasemia.

In this case, a single oral 500 mg dose of clarithromycin offers a plausible reservoir for continued absorption. Clarithromycin’s half-life and active metabolite could contribute to prolonged exposure. The patient’s elevated baseline tryptase and additional features suggest a mast cell disorder that can amplify severity and recurrence.

## Mast Cell Disorders and Anaphylaxis Severity

Systemic mastocytosis (SM) is a clonal mast cell neoplasm most commonly driven by the KIT D816V mutation. Mast cells infiltrate bone marrow and extracutaneous tissues, and aberrant surface markers (CD25, CD2, CD30) are expressed. Mast cell burden and hyperreactivity predispose to severe hypotensive anaphylaxis, often with minimal cutaneous signs during episodes. Hymenoptera venom is a classic trigger, but drugs and idiopathic events are also common.

WHO diagnostic criteria (2022) require either one major plus one minor, or three minor criteria:
- Major: multifocal, dense infiltrates of mast cells (≥15 mast cells per aggregate) in bone marrow or another extracutaneous organ.
- Minor: (1) KIT D816V mutation, (2) aberrant expression of CD25 and/or CD2 and/or CD30 on mast cells, (3) elevated baseline serum tryptase >20 ng/mL not explained by another myeloid neoplasm, (4) mast cells with atypical morphology (spindle-shaped).

This patient satisfied two minor criteria and had clinical features (hepatomegaly, neuropsychiatric symptoms) supportive of SM but lacked assessable tissue for the major criterion.

Hereditary alpha-tryptasemia (HαT), due to extra copies of the TPSAB1 alpha-tryptase gene, is relatively common and produces modestly elevated baseline tryptase (often 8–20 ng/mL), multisystem symptoms, and increased anaphylaxis risk. It may coexist with SM and modulate phenotype. In individuals with unexplained baseline tryptase elevation without other SM features, genetic testing for HαT can be considered.

## Diagnostic Evaluation: What to Send and When

Treatment must not be delayed for testing. Nonetheless, targeted diagnostics clarify mechanism and future risk:

- Serum tryptase: draw at least two, ideally three timed samples. Obtain an acute sample as soon as feasible (ideally within 1–2 hours of symptom onset and no later than 4 hours), and a convalescent “baseline” sample at least 24 hours after complete resolution (and when well). An event-related elevation is defined by the “20% + 2” rule: acute tryptase must be ≥ [1.2 × baseline] + 2 ng/mL to confirm mast cell activation. In this case, the first acute sample was delayed until after the third relapse; nonetheless, both acute and baseline levels were elevated, suggesting true mast cell activation and a persistently high baseline.
- Identification of the trigger: in drug-induced anaphylaxis, skin testing and in vitro specific IgE testing can help—but macrolide skin tests are not standardized, and provocation testing carries risk. When the culprit is avoidable and the reaction was severe, confirmatory testing may reasonably be deferred.
- Evaluation for mast cell disorders: in adults with baseline tryptase >20 ng/mL, recurrent hypotensive anaphylaxis, or suggestive systemic symptoms, evaluate for KIT D816V mutation (high-sensitivity PCR from peripheral blood can be informative) and refer for bone marrow biopsy with mast cell immunophenotyping (CD117, tryptase, CD25/CD2/CD30), morphology, and myeloid panel. Consider TPSAB1 genotyping if baseline tryptase is persistently elevated without clear clonal features.
- Consider mimics selectively: C4 and C1 inhibitor testing in urticaria-free angioedema; catecholamines/metanephrines if features suggest pheochromocytoma; 5-HIAA if carcinoid is suspected.

## Risk Factors for Biphasic and Multiphasic Courses

Most series estimate biphasic reactions in 4–6% of anaphylaxis cases, with reported ranges wider due to inconsistent definitions. Reliable bedside prediction is imperfect, but commonly associated features include:
- Delayed administration of epinephrine
- Severe initial presentation with hypotension or hypoxemia
- Need for multiple epinephrine doses
- Oral or depot allergen exposure
- Ongoing absorption (retained food or medication)
- Comorbid asthma or mast cell disorders
- Under-dosing or subcutaneous delivery of epinephrine in obesity
- Possibly, older age and beta-blocker use (blunts adrenergic response)

This patient exhibited several: severe initial hypotension and hypoxemia, oral drug exposure, repeated epinephrine needs, and likely underlying mast cell disease. At 104 kg, the initial 0.3 mg auto-injector dose was below the recommended 0.5 mg adult dose, and auto-injector needle length in obesity can result in subcutaneous rather than intramuscular deposition, further reducing efficacy.

## Observation and Disposition: Matching Risk to Monitoring

Because relapse is unpredictable, observation after symptom resolution is standard. A risk-stratified approach is pragmatic:

- Low risk (prompt response to a single epinephrine dose, mild reaction, reliable follow-up): 2–4 hours of observation after resolution may suffice.
- Intermediate risk (moderate symptoms, need for >1 epinephrine dose, oral exposure, comorbid asthma): at least 6–12 hours of observation.
- High risk (severe hypotension/respiratory compromise, multiple epinephrine doses, suspected mast cell disorder, delayed presentation or treatment, uncertain trigger, poor access to care): at least 12–24 hours; admit to a monitored setting. ICU-level monitoring is appropriate if there is shock, hypoxemia, or recurrent episodes.

Observation time resets after each recurrence. In this case, ICU transfer after the first relapse was appropriate, and a 24-hour recurrence-free interval before step-down matched the high-risk course.

## Management Principles: What Works, What Does Not, and When to Escalate

Epinephrine is the definitive therapy. Its alpha-1 effects counteract vasodilation and edema; beta-1 improves cardiac output; beta-2 produces bronchodilation and may stabilize mast cells.

- Dose and route: 0.5 mg IM in the anterolateral thigh for adults; repeat every 5–15 minutes as needed. Use a long needle when drawing up from ampoules in obesity to ensure intramuscular delivery. Auto-injectors are valuable to minimize delay, but many adult devices deliver only 0.3 mg; in healthcare settings, prepare 0.5 mg IM for adults. Educate patients to carry two devices to permit a second dose if needed.
- Fluids and oxygen: Give isotonic crystalloids rapidly (1–2 L bolus), titrating to perfusion, and high-flow oxygen as needed.
- Airway: Prepare early for airway edema; intubate if progressive stridor, voice change, or swelling develops. Consider awake fiberoptic techniques if feasible.
- Adjuncts: Inhaled albuterol for bronchospasm; H1 antihistamines (eg, cetirizine PO, diphenhydramine IV) for pruritus/urticaria; H2 antagonists may add incremental cutaneous relief. These do not prevent relapse or treat shock/airway edema.
- Glucocorticoids: Contemporary evidence does not support routine corticosteroids to prevent biphasic reactions and suggests potential harms (hyperglycemia, mood changes, infection, longer length of stay). Reserve for refractory asthma, protracted cutaneous symptoms causing significant distress, or specific indications. Do not delay or substitute for epinephrine.
- Special situations: If on beta-blockers with refractory hypotension or bronchospasm, give glucagon (1–5 mg IV over 5 minutes, then infusion as needed) to bypass beta-receptor antagonism. For persistent vasodilatory shock after adequate epinephrine and fluids, add vasopressors (eg, norepinephrine) while continuing epinephrine.
- Refractory anaphylaxis or frequent relapses: Initiate a carefully titrated intravenous epinephrine infusion in an ICU setting with continuous cardiac and blood pressure monitoring. Protocols vary; a common approach is to prepare 1 mg epinephrine in 100 mL (10 mcg/mL) and start at 1–2 mcg/min, titrating to perfusion and symptom control while spacing or stopping IM doses. Avoid IV boluses outside of arrest scenarios due to arrhythmia risk. Persistently hypotensive patients require parallel large-volume fluid resuscitation and consideration of additional vasopressors.

In multiphasic courses, two practical strategies are considered: (1) preemptive low-dose epinephrine infusion to blunt recurrent waves or (2) vigilant observation with prompt IM epinephrine at symptom onset if the intensity appears to be diminishing and resources for immediate treatment are assured. Individualize based on relapse severity and frequency, comorbidities, and monitoring capacity. In this case, because successive episodes were less severe and promptly responsive, watchful ICU observation with immediate IM epinephrine proved adequate.

## Drug-Induced Anaphylaxis: The Macrolide Context

Antibiotics are leading causes of drug-induced anaphylaxis, with beta-lactams most common. Macrolide-induced anaphylaxis is rare but documented. Cross-reactivity within macrolides can occur but is inconsistent; avoidance of the culprit drug is prudent. Clindamycin is more often associated with delayed T cell–mediated exanthems than immediate anaphylaxis. The patient’s macrolide-naïve status and timing fit a de novo IgE-mediated reaction or, less likely, non–IgE mast cell activation.

Practical implications:
- Clearly document the reaction and the suspected culprit (clarithromycin).
- Provide an antibiotic “safe list” when possible, though the absence of standardized macrolide skin testing limits specificity.
- Allergy referral is essential, but in severe reactions to avoidable drugs, confirmatory skin or challenge testing may be deferred.

## Long-Term Prevention and Follow-Up

Every patient with anaphylaxis needs a prevention and action plan:

- Allergen avoidance: Ensure the culprit is documented in the medical record and communicated to the patient.
- Epinephrine auto-injectors: Prescribe and teach use. For adults, consider devices with higher-dose options where available and counsel to carry two devices at all times.
- Education: Provide a written action plan, teach early recognition, immediate epinephrine use, supine positioning, and calling emergency services. Address device expiration and storage.
- Daily symptom control in mast cell disorders: Non-sedating H1 blockers (up-titrated as needed), H2 blockers, leukotriene receptor antagonists, and cromolyn or ketotifen can reduce mediator-related symptoms. Venom immunotherapy is lifesaving when hymenoptera allergy is present. Omalizumab can reduce frequency and severity of idiopathic anaphylaxis and may benefit some mast cell disorders; consider in recurrent, otherwise unpreventable episodes under specialist guidance.
- Further evaluation for clonal mast cell disease: Repeat baseline tryptase measurements when well, peripheral blood KIT D816V testing, and hematology/allergy referral for bone marrow studies if indicated. Consider TPSAB1 genotyping in persistently elevated baseline tryptase without clonal markers.

## Practical Lessons from the Case

- Dose matters. Adults should receive 0.5 mg IM epinephrine. Auto-injectors that deliver 0.3 mg may underdose patients >50 kg; in the hospital, prepare weight-appropriate 0.5 mg IM doses. In obesity, ensure intramuscular delivery with adequate needle length.
- Time matters. Early epinephrine reduces progression, decreases need for additional doses, and may lower biphasic risk.
- Observe wisely. Patients with severe presentations, oral exposures, multiple epinephrine doses, or suspected mast cell disorders warrant prolonged, often ICU-level, monitoring. The clock resets after each relapse; consider at least 24 hours without recurrence before discharge in high-risk cases.
- Steroids are not preventive. Routine corticosteroids do not prevent biphasic reactions and may cause harm; reserve for specific indications and never delay epinephrine.
- Measure tryptase correctly. Use two or three samples: an acute sample within 1–2 hours of onset and a baseline ≥24 hours after resolution. Apply the 20% + 2 rule to confirm mast cell activation, and investigate persistently elevated baseline levels.
- Think mast cells. Recurrent, severe, or hypotensive anaphylaxis—especially with elevated baseline tryptase—warrants evaluation for systemic mastocytosis and hereditary alpha-tryptasemia.

## Clinical Pearls and Pitfalls

- Anaphylaxis is a clinical diagnosis; do not delay epinephrine for tests or IV access.
- Intramuscular thigh injection achieves faster, higher epinephrine levels than subcutaneous routes; obesity increases the risk of subcutaneous deposition—use appropriate needle length.
- Biphasic reactions often occur within 8–12 hours but can appear later; observation should be individualized and risk-stratified.
- In recurrent episodes within a single admission, consider an epinephrine infusion in a monitored setting if relapses are severe or frequent.
- Glucagon is an essential adjunct in patients on beta-blockers with refractory hypotension or bronchospasm.
- Elevated baseline tryptase is not specific to mastocytosis; consider myeloid neoplasms, chronic kidney disease, and HαT in the differential.
- For drug-induced anaphylaxis to an avoidable agent, the risk–benefit of confirmatory testing may favor strict avoidance and education over provocation or nonstandardized skin testing.

This patient’s multiphasic clarithromycin-induced anaphylaxis, coupled with persistently elevated tryptase and suggestive systemic features, underscores how careful acute management, thoughtful observation, and targeted evaluation for mast cell disorders come together to reduce immediate risk and inform long-term care.
