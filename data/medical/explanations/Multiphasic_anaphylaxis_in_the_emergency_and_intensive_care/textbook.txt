Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

## Case overview and immediate stabilization

The patient was a woman in her 60s with multiple medical comorbidities (type 2 diabetes, hypertension, psoriasis, depression), taking a number of chronic medications, and with a body weight of 104 kg. Her allergic history included a mild childhood reaction to amoxicillin (facial swelling only) and a pronounced local reaction to a wasp sting but no prior systemic anaphylaxis. Five days after a course of clindamycin (followed by a presumed delayed rash), she took a first dose of clarithromycin and developed progressive pruritus, flushing, a sensation of throat swelling and diaphoresis beginning approximately one hour after ingestion. By the time she arrived in the emergency department she was hypoxaemic (SpO2 88% on room air) and profoundly hypotensive (74/50 mmHg) with generalized urticaria and facial swelling.

Recognition of anaphylaxis in this case follows standard clinical criteria: an acute onset of illness with skin/mucosal involvement (urticaria/flushing) in combination with either respiratory compromise or circulatory collapse after a likely allergen exposure. The combination of hypotension (systolic 74 mmHg) and diffuse urticaria shortly after a new oral antibiotic makes the diagnosis straightforward. When considering mimics at the bedside, key distinguishing features are helpful: vasovagal syncope usually has bradycardia and pallor rather than flushing and urticaria; panic/hyperventilation produces breathlessness without hives or hypotension; sepsis is typically subacute with fever and a clear infectious source rather than minutes–hours after a drug dose; isolated angioedema presents with localized swelling but often lacks generalized urticaria and profound hypotension. In this patient the temporal relationship to clarithromycin, the combination of cutaneous signs with hypotension and hypoxaemia, and the rapid progression of symptoms support anaphylaxis rather than an alternative diagnosis.

Initial management in the ED adhered to first‑line principles. Intramuscular epinephrine was given immediately (0.3 mg via auto‑injector), high‑flow supplemental oxygen was administered (non‑rebreather 15 L/min), intravenous access was established and 500 mL of normal saline was infused, and an H1 antihistamine (clemastine) was administered intravenously. The suspected offending agent was stopped and no further clarithromycin doses were given. These interventions reflect priorities in anaphylaxis: secure the airway and oxygenation, reverse life‑threatening bronchospasm and vasodilation with epinephrine, support circulation with fluids, and use adjunctive therapies for cutaneous symptoms. It is important to emphasize that antihistamines and corticosteroids are adjuncts and do not replace epinephrine for airway or circulatory compromise.

Two pragmatic points from this case have direct clinical relevance. First, epinephrine dosing: guideline dosing for adults is typically 0.01 mg/kg IM up to 0.5 mg (commonly rounded to 0.5 mg for most adults). The clinic‑available auto‑injector delivered 0.3 mg, which is a commonly used adult dose but is submaximal for adults over ~50 kg and can lead to the need for additional doses. Second, fluid resuscitation for anaphylactic shock often requires larger volumes than the single 500 mL bolus used initially; aggressive crystalloid boluses (eg, 20 mL/kg) are frequently necessary to restore preload in distributive shock from histamine‑mediated vasodilation.

After the initial treatment she showed a prompt clinical response: oxygenation improved and blood pressure normalized. Because of the severity of the initial presentation, the team admitted her to a closely monitored ward for observation. This decision recognized two important principles: (1) anaphylaxis can recur (biphasic or multiphasic courses), especially after a severe initial episode, and (2) early response to therapy does not guarantee sustained stability. In this case the patient’s initial stabilization set the stage for vigilant monitoring and rapid escalation of care when she relapsed: repeated IM epinephrine was required for subsequent episodes and the patient ultimately needed transfer to the ICU. The early choices—timely epinephrine, oxygen, IV fluids, stopping the trigger and admission to a monitored setting—were the critical interventions that both reversed immediate life‑threat and created the opportunity to detect and treat relapses promptly.

## Pathophysiology and phenotypes of complex anaphylaxis

Anaphylaxis is driven by rapid activation of mast cells and basophils with consequent release of a broad array of pre-formed and newly synthesized mediators. Pre-formed mediators such as histamine, heparin and proteases (for example, tryptase) are released within minutes and account for the earliest manifestations: vasodilation, increased vascular permeability with capillary leak and urticaria/angioedema, bronchial smooth muscle contraction producing bronchospasm, and pruritus/flushing. Newly synthesized lipid mediators (leukotrienes and prostaglandins) and platelet-activating factor are produced within minutes to hours and sustain bronchoconstriction, vascular permeability and inflammatory cell recruitment. Cytokines and chemokines generated later recruit eosinophils, monocytes and additional inflammatory cells, contributing to a slower “late-phase” inflammatory response that can last many hours. Tryptase, released from mast cell granules, typically peaks about 1–2 hours after onset and is a useful marker of mast cell activation when measured in appropriate time windows.

These temporal differences in mediator release and cell recruitment underlie how anaphylaxis can show recurrent or prolonged patterns. An immediate wave of mediator release produces the classic, rapidly reversible features responsive to epinephrine. A later wave — due to de novo synthesis of lipid mediators and cytokine-driven recruitment and activation of additional effector cells — can produce a delayed recurrence of symptoms hours after apparent clinical resolution. Mechanistically, recurrence can therefore reflect (a) a true second inflammatory wave (late-phase cellular activation and renewed mediator production), (b) ongoing or intermittent antigen exposure or depot absorption (for example slow release from ingested or depot-forming drugs), or (c) pharmacodynamic waning of therapeutics (epinephrine’s effect after a single IM dose is rapid but not permanent). Distinguishing these possibilities is important because the implications for monitoring and treatment differ: continued antigen exposure or a persistent circulating trigger may require identification and removal, whereas a late-phase wave reflects intrinsic immune kinetics.

Terminology helps clarify these different clinical patterns. A biphasic reaction is a recurrence of anaphylactic signs after a symptom-free interval and without further exposure to the putative trigger. Biphasic recurrences classically occur within the first 24 hours, though reports extend to 72–78 hours. Multiphasic anaphylaxis refers to two or more distinct recurrences (≥2 relapses) after the initial event. Refractory anaphylaxis denotes an episode that does not adequately respond to appropriately administered epinephrine (persistent or progressive instability despite correct treatment). Protracted anaphylaxis describes a single sustained episode lasting many hours without a symptom-free interval. These distinctions are clinically useful: biphasic/multiphasic courses imply discrete relapses separated by recovery, whereas refractory/protracted disease implies ongoing instability despite therapy.

Epidemiologically, true biphasic reactions are uncommon. Most modern series place the incidence in the low single digits (roughly 4–6% in many reports), with wide variation between studies due to differing definitions and observation practices. The majority of biphasic events occur within 6–24 hours of the index reaction, but delayed recurrences up to several days have been documented, which is why observation strategies and patient counselling are debated.

Several clinical features have been associated with a higher risk of biphasic or more severe courses: a more severe initial presentation (profound hypotension, hypoxaemia or respiratory compromise), delayed administration of the first epinephrine dose, requirement for multiple epinephrine doses during the initial event, and underlying mast cell disorders such as systemic mastocytosis. Systemic mastocytosis increases mast cell numbers and/or reactivity, raises baseline mediator release (often reflected in elevated baseline tryptase), and predisposes to exaggerated, prolonged or relapsing mediator-driven events. In the context of mast cell disorders, even small triggers can produce severe or unpredictable courses.

Drug-induced anaphylaxis varies by drug class. Macrolides (clarithromycin) are an uncommon cause of true IgE-mediated anaphylaxis but have been implicated in immediate hypersensitivity reactions. First-dose anaphylaxis to an apparently novel macrolide can occur either because of prior sensitization through cross-reactive determinants or because of non–IgE-mediated direct mast cell activation. In this report, clarithromycin was the temporal trigger, but the patient’s preceding cutaneous reaction to clindamycin and elevated tryptase raise the possibility that an underlying mast cell disorder amplified the response.

Applying these concepts to the presented patient clarifies why the authors classify her course as multiphasic rather than refractory or protracted. She experienced an initial anaphylactic event followed by several discrete relapses separated by clinical improvement, and each relapse responded promptly and fully to intramuscular epinephrine. That pattern — recovery to near-baseline between episodes and repeated responsiveness to standard therapy — fits multiphasic anaphylaxis. The gradually decreasing intensity of successive episodes is consistent with depletion of readily releasable mediators, diminishing antigen effect, or attenuation of recruited-cell responses over time. Contributing factors likely include intrinsic mast cell hyperreactivity suggested by elevated baseline tryptase and possible systemic mastocytosis; however, absence of ongoing exposure (only a single oral clarithromycin dose) and the timing of relapses point toward intermittent late-phase mediator waves and pharmacodynamic intervals rather than continuous refractory shock.

In summary, multiphasic anaphylaxis is best understood as recurrent waves of mediator-driven pathology arising from the biology of mast cell/basophil activation, late-phase mediator synthesis and cellular recruitment, potential persistent antigen exposure, and the time-limited effects of therapies. Recognition of the phenotype (biphasic versus multiphasic versus refractory) depends on careful observation of symptom-free intervals and treatment responsiveness and has direct relevance for monitoring and further diagnostic evaluation — for example, the search for an underlying mast cell disorder when relapsing or unusually severe courses occur, as illustrated by this case.

## Diagnostic workup and etiologic reasoning, including mast cell disease

The diagnostic goal after a complex anaphylactic presentation is threefold: (1) confirm that the events represented true anaphylaxis and not a mimicker, (2) identify the most likely culprit and exclude explanations for apparent relapse that do not represent new mast cell activation, and (3) assess for an underlying mast cell disorder that would change risk stratification and follow‑up. The investigations and reasoning used in this case illustrate a pragmatic, stepwise approach.

Culprit assessment
Temporal relationship is the strongest clue to causality in drug‑induced anaphylaxis. In this patient the first anaphylactic symptoms began about one hour after taking the first oral dose of clarithromycin, and no further doses were taken — a timing that strongly implicates clarithromycin as the trigger. The rash that appeared five days after starting clindamycin is most consistent with a delayed exanthem (a non–immediate, T‑cell–mediated reaction) rather than an IgE‑mediated immediate allergy; such delayed reactions do not usually predict immediate anaphylaxis on later exposure to unrelated antibiotics. Macrolide antibiotics have low reported cross‑reactivity with other antibiotic classes, so prior clindamycin exposure does not make clarithromycin the inevitable cause, but the clear temporal association with the clarithromycin dose is the dominant piece of evidence supporting it as the culprit. Because skin testing or challenge in this setting would risk provoking life‑threatening recurrence, the team appropriately elected to avoid provocation testing and to document the suspected agent for avoidance.

Excluding pseudo‑biphasic causes
When symptoms recur after apparent resolution, consider whether the picture represents true biphasic/multiphasic anaphylaxis (recurrent mast cell activation) versus a pseudo‑relapse from ongoing exposure or persistent absorption. Mechanisms of pseudo‑relapse include retained allergen (eg, ingested food, intradermal depot), extended absorption from sustained‑release formulations, or a persistent septic or cardiogenic process mimicking shock. In this case an oral single 500 mg dose of clarithromycin with no further exposure makes continued absorption or a depot effect unlikely; no sustained‑release preparation was involved and no documented retained allergen or continued exposure existed. Thus the relapses are most consistent with recurrent mast cell mediator release rather than continued antigen exposure.

Differential diagnoses to consider
A rapid onset of flushing, urticaria, hypotension, hypoxaemia and an excellent response to intramuscular epinephrine point strongly to anaphylaxis. Important alternatives that should be considered in practice include septic shock, cardiogenic shock, and non‑histaminergic angioedema (eg, ACE‑inhibitor mediated), as well as anxiety/panic with vasovagal features. In this patient, the temporal relation to drug ingestion, the dominant cutaneous features (generalised urticaria and angioedema), and reproducible improvement with epinephrine make sepsis and primary psychiatric causes unlikely; the patient was not taking an ACE inhibitor, which argues against ACE‑inhibitor angioedema. Thus the clinical picture supports recurrent anaphylaxis.

Tryptase measurement strategy and interpretation
Serum tryptase is the most useful laboratory marker to corroborate mast cell activation in suspected anaphylaxis, but timing and interpretation are essential. Recommended sampling is: an acute sample as soon as feasible after onset (ideally within 1–2 hours, and certainly within 4 hours when levels commonly peak), and a baseline sample at least 24 hours after symptom resolution to establish the patient’s steady‑state tryptase. Many authorities suggest obtaining two acute samples when possible (early and at peak) plus the baseline.

Results are interpreted using the “20% + 2” rule: an acute tryptase elevation is considered significant if the measured level exceeds baseline by more than (baseline × 0.2) + 2 μg/L, which can be rearranged to acute > 1.2 × baseline + 2 μg/L. This accounts for interindividual baseline differences and improves sensitivity for true mast cell activation.

Case‑specific issues and pitfalls
In the presented case the tryptase sampling was imperfect: levels were not drawn during the initial ED episode(s), and the first tryptase was obtained after the third anaphylactic episode. Nevertheless, an elevated event sample (taken within the recommended window relative to that episode) together with a persistently elevated baseline sample obtained 27 hours after the last recurrence supports two conclusions: there was true mast cell activation during the illness, and the patient has a chronically raised baseline tryptase. The imperfect timing means the test is not as definitive as an optimally timed series, but the available values are still informative and should prompt further evaluation.

Workup for systemic mastocytosis (SM)
Because a persistently elevated baseline tryptase and unusually severe or recurrent anaphylaxis raise concern for a clonal mast cell disorder, a structured workup is indicated. The World Health Organization criteria for systemic mastocytosis require either the major criterion plus one minor, or three minor criteria. The major criterion is multifocal, dense aggregates of mast cells in bone marrow or extracutaneous tissue. Minor criteria are: (1) KIT D816V (or other activating c‑KIT) mutation in blood, bone marrow or extracutaneous organs; (2) aberrant mast cell immunophenotype (typically CD25 and/or CD2 expression on mast cells); (3) atypical mast cell morphology (spindle shape); and (4) baseline serum tryptase >20 μg/L (unless there is an associated clonal myeloid disorder that might otherwise explain tryptase elevation). Workup therefore should include a bone marrow biopsy with histology and mast cell immunophenotyping, sensitive molecular testing for KIT D816V (on marrow and/or peripheral blood), and serial serum tryptase measurements.

In this patient the team pursued those investigations but could not obtain sufficient marrow material to assess the major criterion; the report states that only two minor criteria were fulfilled, so formal diagnostic criteria for SM were not met. Ancillary findings that support a mast cell disorder in this patient include hepatomegaly on sonography and neuropsychiatric symptoms (eg, depression, memory problems), which can be seen in mast cell activation syndromes or systemic disease even when full diagnostic criteria are not satisfied.

Other causes of elevated baseline tryptase
When baseline tryptase is chronically elevated, differential considerations include systemic mastocytosis, monoclonal hematologic disease, and hereditary α‑tryptasemia (a TPSAB1 copy number gain syndrome). Testing for KIT D816V and bone marrow evaluation helps distinguish SM from other causes. If SM is not proven but baseline tryptase remains high and clinical phenotype suggests mast cell activation, management should still be intensified because these patients are at increased risk for severe anaphylaxis.

Implications for monitoring and follow‑up
From a diagnostic and safety perspective this patient’s initial severe hypotension, requirement for multiple epinephrine doses, and persistently elevated tryptase all argue for prolonged and monitored observation (as occurred with ICU admission) and specialist follow‑up. Even in the absence of a confirmed diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis, a working presumption of increased anaphylaxis risk is reasonable: provide epinephrine auto‑injector(s), start regular H1 antihistamine prophylaxis as clinically appropriate, document suspected clarithromycin allergy, and refer to allergy/immunology and hematology for further testing (repeat marrow if initial biopsy was inadequate, sensitive KIT testing, and consideration of TPSAB1 testing if available).

Key practical points for learners
- Use the clinical picture and timing to identify the most likely trigger; clarify immediate versus delayed reactions. In this case, clarithromycin is the most likely cause because symptoms began one hour after a first dose.  
- Exclude pseudo‑relapse from ongoing exposure or depot effects; when none are present, recurrent episodes are most consistent with multiphasic mast cell activation.  
- Obtain tryptase samples when feasible: acute (ideally within 1–2 hours), and a baseline ≥24 hours after resolution; interpret using the 20% + 2 rule.  
- Persistently elevated baseline tryptase or unusually severe/recurrent anaphylaxis warrants investigation for systemic mastocytosis (bone marrow histology, flow cytometry for CD25/CD2, and KIT mutation testing) and consideration of other causes of elevated tryptase.  
- Even without a definitive SM diagnosis, elevated baseline tryptase and multi‑dose epinephrine needs justify prolonged monitored care, specialist referral, education, and prescription of epinephrine auto‑injector(s).

## Acute management of recurrent/multiphasic anaphylaxis in ED/ICU

Epinephrine remains the single most important intervention in any anaphylactic episode and should be administered promptly for each recurrence. For adults the recommended intramuscular (IM) dose is 0.01 mg/kg up to a maximum of 0.5 mg per injection, given into the anterolateral thigh and repeatable every 5 minutes as needed for persistent or recurrent symptoms. In the patient described (104 kg) this means a 0.5 mg IM dose was appropriate from the outset; the later, individually prepared 0.5 mg IM doses therefore matched guideline dosing and are preferable to underdosing.

Auto‑injectors are invaluable for rapid treatment, but commonly available adult auto‑injectors deliver 0.3 mg. That dose can be subtherapeutic in many adults and has been associated with more frequent need for escalation compared with an initial 0.5 mg dose. In the hospital setting, teams should have a clear plan to assure correct dosing: stock 0.5 mg devices where possible, train staff to draw and give 0.5 mg IM from 1 mg/mL (1:1,000) ampoules when larger auto‑injectors are unavailable, and emphasise injection into the anterolateral thigh. Using an auto‑injector can avoid delays and hesitancy in low‑frequency situations, but staff must recognise its dose limitation for heavier adults.

When repeated IM doses do not restore or maintain adequate perfusion, or when relapses are extremely frequent or the patient develops progressive cardiovascular collapse, an intravenous (IV) epinephrine infusion should be started and the patient managed in an ICU environment. Typical practical approaches are to begin a titratable infusion and to use repeated IM boluses for immediate effect until the infusion is established. Infusion starting rates commonly used in adults are modest and titrated to clinical effect (for example, an initial range equivalent to a few micrograms per minute, titrated upward, often expressed clinically as 0.05–0.2 µg/kg/min or roughly 1–10 µg/min depending on local protocol). Precise regimens vary; the key principles are slow titration to restore perfusion while monitoring for tachyarrhythmia, ischemia and excessive hypertension. Anaphylactic patients on IV epinephrine require continuous ECG and blood pressure monitoring, frequent bedside reassessments, skilled nursing and physician supervision, and ideally arterial pressure monitoring if high infusion rates are needed.

Supportive measures remain essential. Give high‑flow oxygen for hypoxaemia, and aggressive isotonic crystalloid boluses for anaphylactic distributive shock — initial boluses of several hundred millilitres to litres guided by response and repeated as needed. In practice it is common to give 500–1000 mL quickly and reassess, with larger cumulative volumes often required. Treat bronchospasm with inhaled short‑acting beta‑agonists. For patients on chronic beta‑blockade with refractory hypotension or bronchospasm, parenteral glucagon should be considered because it acts independent of beta receptors; this was not applicable in the reported patient. Vasopressors other than epinephrine may be required in the ICU if hypotension persists despite epinephrine and fluid resuscitation.

Antihistamines (H1, and sometimes H2) and corticosteroids have specific, limited roles. H1 and H2 blockers can relieve cutaneous symptoms (urticaria, pruritus) but do not reverse airway obstruction, hypotension or bronchospasm and have slow onset; they are adjuncts only. Corticosteroids were historically given to try to prevent biphasic reactions, but current evidence does not support routine steroid use for that purpose and suggests possible harms (eg, hyperglycaemia, increased admissions). Many hospitals still include steroids in local protocols; when used clinicians should be aware of the weak evidence base. In the presented case corticosteroids were administered after the first recurrence in keeping with older local protocols; that choice reflects common practice but is not supported as an effective strategy to prevent further relapses.

Monitoring and disposition must be individualized for recurrent or multiphasic courses. Any patient with recurrent episodes, need for multiple epinephrine doses, severe initial presentation, or suspected underlying mast cell disorder merits ICU admission for continuous cardiorespiratory monitoring, rapid access to medications and airway support, and consideration of vasoactive infusions. High‑risk patients typically require observation for a prolonged interval after the last recurrence; many centres will observe for at least 12–24 hours, and longer if symptoms recur or if there is an underlying disorder such as elevated baseline tryptase or suspected mastocytosis. The case patient had multiple recurrences in the first 12 hours and was appropriately escalated to ICU; because her symptom intensity diminished and each episode responded to IM epinephrine, withholding an immediate IV infusion and watching closely in ICU was a reasonable, measured approach.

Interdisciplinary coordination improves care in complex courses. Early allergy/immunology consultation helps plan avoidance strategies, diagnostic testing and longer‑term management (and to weigh the risks of provocative testing), while ICU involvement is essential when frequent relapses, refractory hemodynamic instability, or need for vasopressor infusions are present. In this case allergology was involved early and ICU monitoring was instituted after the first relapse — both actions align with recommended practice. The team considered an IV infusion but appropriately balanced the risks (arrhythmia, ischemia, need for intensive monitoring and central access in some cases) against the patient’s improving, progressively milder episodes that were responsive to appropriately dosed IM epinephrine.

In summary: for recurrent/multiphasic anaphylaxis, continue prompt IM epinephrine at 0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg repeated every 5 minutes as needed (use 0.5 mg in most adults over 50 kg), provide aggressive supportive care, admit to ICU if relapses are frequent or hemodynamics unstable, consider IV infusion only for refractory or rapidly recurrent events despite correct IM dosing, use antihistamines for symptom control but do not rely on steroids to prevent recurrence, and coordinate early with allergy and critical care teams. The management choices made in the case — rapid IM epinephrine, escalation to ICU after relapse, and avoidance of an early IV infusion while monitoring response — are consistent with these practical, evidence‑based priorities, though the initial use of a 0.3 mg auto‑injector highlights the important dosing pitfall to address in hospital protocols.

## Discharge planning, long-term management, and clinical lessons

Safe discharge and durable risk reduction after a complex anaphylaxis episode require clear documentation, patient education, targeted secondary prevention, and specialist follow‑up. In this case the patient left hospital with a diagnosis of clarithromycin‑triggered multiphasic anaphylaxis and a working diagnosis of probable systemic mastocytosis. Her care plan should therefore be explicit about the immediate actions she must take if symptoms recur, how to avoid future exposures and co‑factors, and what additional investigations and specialist input are required.

Before discharge give the patient an adrenaline (epinephrine) auto‑injector and hands‑on training in its use. Adults who weigh more than about 50 kg should be considered for a 0.5 mg device where available; the 0.3 mg devices commonly supplied can underdose large adults, as occurred in the presented patient (104 kg) and likely contributed to the need for repeated doses. Patients should be advised to carry two auto‑injectors at all times (one may fail, and some reactions require repeat dosing) and instructed in when to inject (immediately for airway compromise, breathing difficulties, or circulatory compromise), how to position themselves after injection (lie flat with legs raised unless breathing is difficult), and to call emergency services after use. Teach safe storage, checking expiry dates, and show how to dispose and replace used devices. Provide a written, easy‑to‑follow anaphylaxis action plan and consider an allergy card or medical alert bracelet documenting “anaphylaxis to clarithromycin (macrolide class).”

Medication reconciliation and avoidance counseling are critical. Ensure the allergy (clarithromycin/macrolide) is clearly documented in the medical record and communicated to the patient’s primary care provider and pharmacy to prevent inadvertent re‑exposure. Because the patient also has a history of a non‑severe amoxicillin reaction and recent clindamycin dermatitis, future antibiotic choices should be made with specialist input; do not assume cross‑reactivity patterns without allergist assessment. Advise avoidance of common co‑factors that can amplify allergic reactions (for example non‑steroidal anti‑inflammatory drugs, alcohol, vigorous exercise, hot showers) in the hours before and after exposure to known triggers, and review other medications (such as beta‑blockers) that can complicate anaphylaxis management.

Given the elevated baseline tryptase and the high probability of an underlying mast cell disorder in this patient, institute chronic symptom control measures and arrange specialist follow‑up. Routine daily non‑sedating H1 antihistamines (for example cetirizine or levocetirizine, as started in this patient) are reasonable; adding an H2 blocker (ranitidine is no longer widely available in some regions; use alternative H2 agents) can be considered when cutaneous or gastrointestinal mediator symptoms persist. Leukotriene receptor antagonists may help selected patients with persistent symptoms. For patients with recurrent idiopathic or mast cell–mediated anaphylaxis despite optimized antihistamine therapy, referral to an allergist/immunologist for consideration of omalizumab or other specialist therapies is appropriate. All chronic pharmacologic strategies for suspected mast cell disease should be coordinated with the specialist team.

Follow‑up evaluation must include early allergology and hematology input. Repeat baseline tryptase (at least once, ideally confirmed on more than one occasion >24 hours after complete recovery) is necessary to establish a true baseline and to interpret previous acute samples; guidelines recommend an acute sample as soon as possible after reaction onset, a second sample 1–2 hours (but within 4 hours) after onset when levels peak, and a third sample at least 24 hours after resolution to define baseline. Because the initial tryptase sampling in this case was incomplete, repeat testing and careful documentation are required. If baseline tryptase remains elevated or clinical suspicion persists, a more complete evaluation for systemic mastocytosis is indicated—this may include peripheral immunophenotyping, KIT D816V mutation testing (blood and/or bone marrow), and a focused bone marrow biopsy with mast cell markers (CD25, CD2) when feasible. Specialist follow‑up should also screen for organ involvement suggested by hepatomegaly, bone pain, or gastrointestinal symptoms and plan appropriate monitoring (liver tests, bone density/imaging, GI evaluation) as indicated.

Provide explicit guidance to the patient about observation and management of future events. While there is no universally accepted observation period, decisions should be risk‑stratified: patients with mild, single‑dose, fully resolved reactions and no risk factors may be observed 4–6 hours, whereas those with severe initial presentation, need for multiple epinephrine doses, or underlying mast cell disorder (as in this patient) warrant prolonged observation or admission and close monitoring. Emphasize prevention: early recognition and prompt intramuscular epinephrine at the recommended dose (0.01 mg/kg, up to 0.5 mg in adults) reduce severity and may lower the risk of biphasic relapse. For future severe, refractory, or rapidly recurrent episodes, escalation to intravenous epinephrine infusion (managed in an ICU setting) should be considered rather than repeated sole reliance on serial intramuscular injections.

Key practical takeaways from this case for clinical practice are: do not underdose epinephrine—use the correct IM dose for weight and consider 0.5 mg devices for most adults; obtain properly timed serial tryptase samples when possible to help identify mast cell activation and baseline elevation; de‑emphasize routine corticosteroids as a strategy to prevent biphasic reactions—steroid use should be individualized and is not a substitute for epinephrine; and recognize mast cell disorders as important modifiers of risk that mandate specialist follow‑up and tailored chronic therapy. For multiphasic or refractory anaphylaxis, institution of continuous IV epinephrine under monitored settings is an important option and local protocols should spell out thresholds for escalation.

Finally, from a systems perspective, this case highlights important research and policy gaps: the need for standardized definitions of biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis, prospective registries to capture rare recurrent courses, and expert consensus guidance on observation duration and infusion strategies. Clinicians should document cases of unusual anaphylaxis courses in institutional reporting systems and collaborate with allergy services to improve care pathways and inform future recommendations.
