Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

## Clinical Approach to Multiphasic Anaphylaxis: Case-Based Introduction

The patient described — a woman in her 60s who developed a life‑threatening systemic reaction shortly after taking a first dose of clarithromycin and then experienced multiple relapses over the next 12 hours — illustrates the real‑world challenges of recognising, treating and dispositioning patients with biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis. The clinical approach in such cases follows the same principles as initial anaphylaxis care, but with a consistently heightened index of suspicion for recurrence, a lower threshold for escalation of monitoring and a readiness to move from episodic intramuscular therapy to continuous vasopressor support when relapses are frequent or prolonged.

Recognition must be rapid and clinical. Anaphylaxis is a clinical diagnosis: the typical presentation is an acute onset illness with skin or mucosal involvement (urticaria, flushing, angioedema) plus evidence of respiratory compromise (wheeze, stridor, hypoxaemia) and/or circulatory compromise (tachycardia, syncope, hypotension), or isolated hypotension or respiratory distress after a likely exposure. In this case the temporal relationship — symptoms beginning roughly one hour after clarithromycin ingestion — together with generalized urticaria, facial swelling and profound hypotension and hypoxaemia, made anaphylaxis the correct working diagnosis before any laboratory testing. Do not wait for confirmatory tests to treat: epinephrine is a time‑critical intervention.

Immediate stabilisation priorities are airway, breathing and circulation. Intramuscular epinephrine into the anterolateral thigh is the first and most important treatment and should not be delayed for IV access, imaging or bloodwork. The recommended dose is 0.01 mg/kg (maximum 0.5 mg) for adults; practically this is typically 0.5 mg IM for most adults. Repeat dosing every 5–15 minutes as needed for persistent or recurrent signs of anaphylaxis. In many systems auto‑injectors of 0.3 mg are commonly used in prehospital or emergency situations; they facilitate rapid delivery and reduce hesitation by staff unfamiliar with drawing up epinephrine, but 0.3 mg may underdose heavier adults (as in this patient, who later required 0.5 mg IM doses). Supplemental oxygen (up to 100%), positioning the patient supine with legs elevated if tolerated, and rapid administration of isotonic crystalloid boluses are the immediate adjunctive measures for hypotension and hypoperfusion — an initial 20 mL/kg bolus (roughly 1–2 L in many adults) is reasonable with reassessment and repeat boluses as needed, modified by cardiac comorbidity and response. Early airway assessment is essential; progressive upper‑airway oedema, stridor, or worsening hypoxaemia require preparation for definitive airway control and specialty assistance.

Antihistamines (H1 ± H2 blockers) and inhaled bronchodilators address cutaneous and bronchospasm symptoms but are adjunctive and not substitutes for epinephrine. Corticosteroids have traditionally been given with the intention of reducing the risk of biphasic reactions, but contemporary evidence does not support routine steroid administration to prevent relapse; steroids may be considered in selected situations but should not delay or replace guideline treatments. In the presented case, steroids were started after the first relapse — a common practice — but clinicians should be aware of the limited evidence for this strategy.

Multiphasic courses — repeated recurrences after apparent resolution — require early critical‑care involvement. Indications for ICU consultation and consideration of invasive monitoring include persistent or refractory hypotension, hypoxaemia requiring high‑flow oxygen or ventilatory support, airway compromise, need for multiple epinephrine doses, and documented relapses. In the emergency room this patient’s hypotension (74/50 mm Hg), hypoxaemia, and then repeated episodes of collapse warranted transfer to the ICU after the first relapse. While some relapses may decrease in intensity, as in this case, the unpredictability of timing and severity argues for a cautious disposition plan rather than early discharge.

When repeated intramuscular epinephrine is required for recurrent episodes, transitioning to a continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion is often appropriate. This decision is made in the ICU by clinicians experienced with vasopressor therapy because IV epinephrine carries a higher risk of arrhythmia and hypertension if not titrated carefully. Typical practice is to give additional IM or small IV bolus doses to stabilise a patient and then start an infusion at a low rate (for adults, an infusion may be started in the ballpark of 0.05–0.1 μg/kg/min and titrated to effect; some centres use μg/min algorithms) with continuous cardiac monitoring and frequent blood pressure reassessment. Alternative vasopressors such as norepinephrine may be considered for persistent distributive shock after alpha‑ and beta‑agonist effects of epinephrine have been optimised. The option of a pre‑emptive infusion (i.e., starting infusion before recurrence) versus an expectant approach needs to be individualized; in practice, if relapses are frequent or escalating, proactive infusion is safer than repeatedly relying on IM boluses.

Systematic documentation and diagnostic follow‑up are integral parts of care. Record the suspected trigger (clarithromycin in this case), timing of exposure and symptom onset, precise medication doses and routes (including auto‑injector use and any IV boluses/infusions), the patient’s response to each intervention and any adverse effects. Obtain tryptase measurements when feasible: an early sample as soon as possible after the onset of anaphylaxis (without delaying life‑saving care), a second sample ideally 1–2 hours after symptom onset (within 4 hours) when levels typically peak, and a baseline sample at least 24 hours after complete clinical resolution to establish pre‑event mast cell burden. In this patient tryptase sampling was imperfectly timed but subsequent elevated baseline and perireaction values raised the suspicion of an underlying mast cell disorder — knowledge that influences long‑term risk stratification and follow‑up.

Disposition decisions should reflect risk. For routine single‑episode, rapidly resolving anaphylaxis in low‑risk patients, many clinicians observe for 4–6 hours after symptom resolution. For patients who had severe presentations, required multiple epinephrine doses, have airway involvement, have young or elderly age, poor access to emergency care, or evidence of mast cell disease, prolonged observation or ICU admission is prudent. Biphasic reactions most commonly occur within the first 6–12 hours, but relapses have been reported much later; multiphasic courses like the one described justify an extended ICU observation frame — commonly 24 hours or more — until the treating team is confident the risk of relapse has passed. Before discharge, provide an anaphylaxis action plan and at least one epinephrine auto‑injector appropriate for the patient’s weight (in many countries standard adult auto‑injectors are 0.3 mg, though a 0.5 mg dose is often cited for adults >50 kg if available), coach the patient in its use, and arrange prompt specialist follow‑up with allergy/immunology for evaluation and consideration of further testing. When an underlying mast cell disorder is suspected, refer for haemato‑/immunologic evaluation rather than routine skin or drug‑provocation testing in the immediate post‑anaphylaxis period, as those tests may provoke recurrence.

Finally, counselling and risk reduction are practical components of care. Patients should be instructed to avoid the suspected trigger (clarithromycin) and to wear medical alert identification if appropriate. Document the drug allergy clearly in the medical record and communicate it to primary care. If testing for drug allergy will be pursued, coordinate this with allergy specialists at a controlled setting. Education about early recognition of anaphylaxis and prompt self‑administration of epinephrine can prevent delay in future episodes and is a key part of discharge planning.

The clarithromycin case underscores several teaching points: treat anaphylaxis clinically and immediately with IM epinephrine; expect and plan for possible recurrence; escalate monitoring early when there are repeated doses or severe initial physiology; consider IV epinephrine infusion under ICU care for frequent relapses; and complete detailed documentation, tryptase sampling, and specialist follow‑up. Applying these principles helps clinicians manage the rare but high‑risk problem of multiphasic anaphylaxis in both the emergency department and intensive care settings.

## Pathophysiology and Definitions: From Single-Episode to Multiphasic Anaphylaxis

Anaphylaxis is the clinical consequence of widespread, rapid activation of mast cells and basophils with consequent release of preformed and newly synthesized mediators. In the prototypical IgE‑mediated reaction an allergen cross‑links antigen‑specific IgE bound to the high‑affinity FcεRI receptor on tissue mast cells and circulating basophils. Intracellular signaling (eg, via Syk kinase) leads within seconds to a rise in intracellular calcium, exocytosis of granule contents and release of preformed mediators such as histamine, tryptase, heparin and proteases. Within minutes the cell also initiates de novo synthesis of lipid mediators—prostaglandin D2 and cysteinyl leukotrienes (LTC4/D4/E4)—and secretes cytokines and chemokines (eg, TNFα, interleukins) that perpetuate and modulate the response. Other pathways produce clinically similar syndromes: direct, non‑IgE mast cell activation (by drugs such as opiates, vancomycin or radiocontrast in some patients), IgG‑mediated mechanisms, and complement‑ or contact pathway–mediated pseudoallergic reactions. Platelet‑activating factor (PAF), generated during severe reactions, has emerged as a marker and mediator linked to cardiovascular collapse.

The clinical signs and timing of anaphylaxis reflect this mediator biology. Histamine is responsible for early symptoms—vasodilation, increased vascular permeability (urticaria, angioedema, hypotension) and bronchospasm—and acts within minutes. Tryptase, a protease released from mast cell granules, typically peaks in blood about 1–2 hours after onset and returns toward baseline over the next day; serial tryptase sampling is therefore a useful tool to document mast cell activation. Lipid mediators and cytokines are produced on a delayed timescale (hours) and contribute to sustained or delayed symptoms such as bronchospasm, wheeze and late‑phase cutaneous reactions. These differences in timing create the biological substrate for single‑episode (monophasic), protracted/persistent, biphasic and rarely multiphasic clinical courses.

Because the clinical patterns differ and because terminology has been inconsistent in the literature, it is useful for clinicians to adopt operational definitions. A single‑episode (monophasic) anaphylactic reaction resolves following acute therapy without later recurrence. A protracted or persistent anaphylactic reaction describes ongoing signs and symptoms that fail to remit between treatment doses and may require continuous or repeated interventions. Biphasic anaphylaxis denotes an initial anaphylactic episode with apparent clinical resolution followed—after a symptom‑free interval—by a spontaneous recurrence of anaphylactic signs without further exposure to the trigger; most recurrences occur within the first 24 hours but documented relapses have been reported at 48–72 hours (and rarely later). Multiphasic anaphylaxis refers to more than one relapse after the initial event—two or more distinct, temporally separated recurrences—each meeting criteria for anaphylaxis. Refractory anaphylaxis is a separate concept and refers to persistent or progressive cardiovascular or respiratory compromise despite appropriately dosed and correctly administered intramuscular epinephrine and optimal supportive care.

Several mechanistic hypotheses explain why some patients develop biphasic or multiphasic courses. First, secondary mediator synthesis by activated mast cells and recruited leukocytes (lipid mediators, cytokines) unfolds over hours and can produce a delayed wave of physiologic effects even after granule‑derived mediators have dissipated. This “late‑phase” biology mirrors the cutaneous late allergic reaction and is a plausible intrinsic driver of biphasic events. Second, ongoing or delayed antigen exposure/absorption can sustain or rekindle mast cell activation—examples include continued absorption from gastrointestinal tract contents, slow‑release drug formulations, depot injections, or persistent venom antigen after a sting. Distinguishing true biphasic reactions from ongoing antigen exposure can be clinically challenging but is important because the latter represents ongoing trigger presence rather than a delayed mediator wave. Third, host factors modify the severity and course: inadequate or delayed administration of epinephrine during the initial episode, large antigen load, older age, comorbid cardiopulmonary disease, and concomitant medications (eg, beta‑blockers, ACE inhibitors) have all been associated with more severe or prolonged courses. Finally, clonal mast cell disorders—most notably systemic mastocytosis—create a fundamentally different substrate. Increased mast cell burden, aberrant mast cell activation thresholds (eg, due to KIT D816V gain‑of‑function mutations), and a propensity for spontaneous mediator release mean that a small trigger can produce a disproportionately large and sometimes waxing‑and‑waning clinical response, increasing the risk of severe, protracted, or multiphasic anaphylaxis.

Systemic mastocytosis deserves special attention because it is the archetype of a condition that predisposes to severe and unpredictable anaphylaxis. Patients commonly have elevated baseline serum tryptase, hepatosplenomegaly or bone marrow involvement and may present with hypotension or syncope in the absence of prominent cutaneous signs. In the context of anaphylaxis, the combination of a high mast cell burden and dysregulated activation can produce repeated waves of mediator release and clinical relapses. Establishing the diagnosis requires specific laboratory and histopathologic criteria; in practice, a markedly increased baseline tryptase or recurrent unexplained anaphylaxis should prompt referral to allergy/immunology and hematology for appropriate workup rather than repeated provocative testing in the acute setting.

Refractory anaphylaxis has overlapping but distinct mechanisms: in addition to massive mediator release, resistance to catecholamines (eg, in patients on chronic beta‑blockers), decreased intravascular volume, and distributive shock physiology can blunt the expected response to intramuscular epinephrine and necessitate intravenous boluses or continuous infusions. Recognizing the difference between a biphasic relapse that typically responds to repeated intramuscular epinephrine and a refractory collapse requiring advanced circulatory support is essential for timely escalation of care.

Understanding these pathophysiologic variants has practical implications. It explains why early and adequate intramuscular epinephrine is the cornerstone of therapy, why certain patients require prolonged observation (because delayed mediator synthesis can cause later relapses), and why routine corticosteroids have not been shown to reliably prevent biphasic events—their mechanisms and timing do not necessarily interrupt the cellular processes that generate late‑phase mediators. It also underpins targeted evaluation after an atypical course: serial tryptase measurements (ideally a sample as soon as possible after presentation, a peak sample 1–4 hours after onset, and a baseline >24 hours after complete resolution) can document mast cell activation and identify an elevated baseline that warrants further workup for mast cell disease.

In summary, anaphylaxis is a spectrum disorder produced by mast cell and basophil activation with immediate and delayed mediator effects. Biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis are biologically plausible outcomes of the temporal dynamics of mediator release, ongoing antigen exposure, or underlying host factors such as clonal mast cell disease. Clear operational definitions—single‑episode (monophasic), protracted/persistent, biphasic (one relapse), multiphasic (≥2 relapses), and refractory (nonresponsive to appropriate epinephrine)—help clinicians recognize patterns, tailor monitoring and escalation strategies, and select patients for further diagnostic evaluation such as tryptase measurement and referral for mast cell disorder assessment.

## Differential Diagnosis and Key Distinctions

When a patient presents with a waxing and waning systemic reaction, the clinician’s first task is to separate true biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis from other patterns that mimic it — sustained anaphylaxis due to ongoing antigen exposure, refractory anaphylaxis that never adequately responds to therapy, and a broad array of non‑allergic mimics. Accurate classification matters: it directs immediate management (for example, whether to escalate to continuous vasopressor support or to seek and remove an ongoing exposure) and it determines observation strategy and further diagnostic work‑up such as tryptase measurement or assessment for mast cell disease.

True biphasic anaphylaxis is defined by an initial anaphylactic event, a symptom‑free interval, and then a recurrence of allergic symptoms without further exposure to the inciting antigen. Multiphasic anaphylaxis is a less common variant in which there are two or more recurrences after the initial event. In contrast, sustained anaphylaxis represents continuous pathophysiologic activation — the patient never achieves a clear asymptomatic interval and symptoms improve only transiently after epinephrine because the antigen continues to be absorbed (examples include retained hymenoptera stingers, ongoing absorption from depot or slow‑release drug formulations, or continued ingestion/absorption of an offending food). Refractory anaphylaxis is a separate problem: it denotes persistent or progressive shock and respiratory compromise despite correctly dosed and repeated administration of epinephrine and appropriate supportive measures. The distinction is not merely semantic; refractory cases prompt escalation to intravenous epinephrine infusions, consideration of adjuncts (eg, glucagon in beta‑blocked patients), and invasive monitoring, whereas biphasic disease may be managed with vigilant observation and episodic rescue dosing if recurrences are limited.

Several practical clues help to differentiate these entities at the bedside. The temporal pattern is key: a true biphasic course will include a clear interval of clinical quiescence. If symptoms never fully resolve or wax and wane without a symptom‑free period, suspect sustained exposure or treatment failure rather than a biphasic reaction. Consider ongoing antigen sources: retained insect parts, slow‑release or depot injections, topical or transdermal agents, or medications in the gastrointestinal tract — in such settings absorption may continue for many hours and produce recurrent or persistent symptoms. In the presented case, only a single oral 500 mg dose of clarithromycin was taken and not repeated; although oral absorption can be prolonged, no ongoing external source was identified, making multiphasic anaphylaxis more likely than sustained exposure.

Evaluating therapeutic adequacy is equally important. Inadequate or delayed epinephrine — either from an underdose (for example routine use of a 0.3 mg auto‑injector in a large adult when a 0.5 mg dose is appropriate) or from delayed administration — increases the likelihood of relapse and of biphasic courses. Recurrent hypotension or respiratory compromise that promptly and fully reverses with a correctly administered epinephrine dose favors anaphylaxis; lack of response despite correct dosing should raise concern for refractory anaphylaxis or for alternative diagnoses.

Laboratory testing provides supportive, though not definitive, discrimination. Serum tryptase rises within 1–2 hours of mast cell degranulation, peaks around that time, and typically returns toward baseline over 12–24 hours; obtaining an early sample (as soon as feasible), a peak sample 1–2 (up to 4) hours after onset, and a baseline sample at least 24 hours after full recovery helps confirm mast cell activation and supports anaphylaxis over mimics. A substantially elevated baseline tryptase or persistently raised levels should prompt consideration of an underlying mast cell disorder (systemic mastocytosis or monoclonal mast cell activation syndrome), which predisposes to severe and sometimes recurrent reactions — as was suspected in the patient described. Histamine measurements are less useful clinically because they fall rapidly.

Distinguishing anaphylaxis from its mimics requires systematic assessment of symptoms and signs. Cutaneous findings such as urticaria, angioedema, flushing and pruritus are common in anaphylaxis and are strong discriminators when present; bradykinin‑mediated angioedema (eg, hereditary angioedema or ACE‑inhibitor induced angioedema) typically causes prominent swelling without urticaria or pruritus and does not respond to epinephrine or antihistamines. Respiratory symptoms due to bronchospasm in anaphylaxis produce wheeze and hypoxaemia and often improve measurably with epinephrine; isolated upper‑airway dysfunction such as vocal cord dysfunction or panic‑related hyperventilation may cause stridor or dyspnoea but generally lacks hypotension, cutaneous features, and marked oxygen desaturation. Vasovagal syncope causes transient hypotension and bradycardia with pallor rather than flushing and is relieved rapidly by recumbency and fluids. Cardiogenic shock and severe sepsis can mimic distributive shock seen in anaphylaxis but will usually reveal alternate organ dysfunction (pulmonary oedema, ischemic ECG changes, elevated lactate, fever, and an identified infectious source) and do not show mast cell mediator‑related cutaneous signs. Toxins and food‑related histamine poisoning (scombroid) or carcinoid syndrome may present with flushing and hypotension but have distinguishing exposures or chronic features. A careful history — including temporal relationship to drug or insect exposure, any recent ingestion of fish, use of ACE inhibitors, family history of angioedema, or features of infection — is indispensable.

Response to therapy is also diagnostic: anaphylaxis typically shows a rapid and durable response to intramuscular epinephrine (improvement in blood pressure, bronchospasm, and cutaneous symptoms). Failure to respond should prompt an immediate reassessment for airway obstruction, ongoing exposure, inadequate dosing, and contraindicating co‑medications (notably beta‑blockers) and consideration of refractory anaphylaxis management pathways (intravenous epinephrine infusion, vasopressor support, glucagon). Conversely, if a patient has a dramatic recovery after epinephrine and then deteriorates again after a symptom‑free period, biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis is likely.

Medication‑specific nuances deserve attention. Macrolide hypersensitivity is less common than beta‑lactam allergy but can occur as immediate IgE‑mediated reactions or as non‑IgE mechanisms; cross‑reactivity among macrolides is unpredictable. In practice, once a macrolide has been implicated in a severe immediate reaction, avoidance of the specific drug is prudent and alternative antibiotic classes should be used when possible; if macrolide therapy is essential, allergy consultation for supervised testing or desensitisation may be required, but such steps are high risk in someone with a history of severe anaphylaxis. Documentation and patient education (epinephrine auto‑injector, avoidance counselling) are essential.

Finally, apply a pragmatic diagnostic approach when recurrences occur. First, re‑examine the airway and cardiorespiratory status and repeat epinephrine if indicated. Actively search for ongoing antigen (stingers, medication depots, continued ingestion). Draw tryptase early and plan a baseline sample after recovery. If symptoms recur repeatedly or do not respond to repeated intramuscular doses, escalate to continuous intravenous epinephrine and involve critical care and allergy/immunology specialists; consider testing for beta‑blockade and treat accordingly (glucagon). If the clinical picture is atypical (absence of skin findings, predominance of isolated swelling without urticaria, or features suggesting sepsis or cardiogenic disease), broaden the diagnostic evaluation promptly with cardiac biomarkers, cultures, imaging and consultations as indicated.

In the case reported, the patient’s multiple discrete relapses after a symptom‑free interval, prompt improvement with repeated intramuscular epinephrine, and elevated tryptase values — including a high baseline — argue for multiphasic anaphylaxis superimposed on a probable mast cell disorder rather than sustained antigen exposure or a non‑allergic mimic. That distinction guided both the clinical monitoring strategy and the later diagnostic focus on mast cell disease.

## Diagnostic Workup and Risk Stratification

When a patient presents with anaphylaxis, the immediate priorities are airway, breathing, circulation and prompt administration of intramuscular epinephrine. Parallel to resuscitative measures, however, clinicians should initiate a targeted diagnostic workup and begin thinking about risk stratification for observation and disposition. The diagnostic workup has two overlapping goals: (1) confirm recent mast cell activation when possible, and (2) identify factors that alter the likelihood of recurrent or severe episodes and that would prompt more prolonged monitoring or specialist evaluation for underlying mast cell disease.

Serum tryptase is the single most useful laboratory test to document mast cell activation in the acute setting, but it has important limitations. Tryptase is released from mast cells and typically rises within the first hour after the onset of anaphylaxis, peaks by about 1–2 hours (and generally within 4 hours), and returns toward baseline over the next day. Therefore, obtaining timed samples is critical: obtain an acute sample as soon as feasible without delaying treatment, another sample at about 1–2 hours after symptom onset (within 4 hours) when the peak is most likely, and a baseline sample at least 24 hours after full clinical resolution (many groups recommend ≥24–48 hours) to establish the patient’s interictal level. In practice this means: a “first” tryptase drawn in the ED when the diagnosis is suspected, a “peak” sample drawn 1–2 hours after symptom onset, and a “baseline” drawn days later in the outpatient setting or before discharge if >24 hours from the event.

Interpreting tryptase requires comparing the acute and baseline values. A commonly used criterion to support mast cell activation is the “20% + 2” rule: an acute tryptase that exceeds the baseline by at least 20% of the baseline value plus 2 ng/mL (acute ≥ baseline × 1.2 + 2 ng/mL) is regarded as evidence of significant mast cell degranulation. Because tryptase release varies by trigger, a normal or non‑elevated acute tryptase does not exclude anaphylaxis—food-triggered reactions, for example, are relatively more likely to have normal tryptase measurements. Proper specimen handling also matters: send serum (not plasma), allow the blood to clot, centrifuge promptly, and refrigerate or freeze if there will be any delay in analysis.

Baseline elevation of tryptase on the post‑resolution sample should prompt consideration of an underlying mast cell disorder, but the differential is broader than systemic mastocytosis alone. Causes of chronically elevated baseline tryptase include systemic mastocytosis, hereditary alpha‑tryptasemia (HαT, associated with increased TPSAB1 copy number), myeloid neoplasms, chronic kidney disease (reduced clearance), and less commonly other inflammatory or hematologic conditions. An isolated mild elevation of baseline tryptase without clinical features may not mandate invasive testing, but in the context of unusually severe, recurrent, or idiopathic anaphylaxis, further evaluation is indicated.

When should clinicians pursue an investigation for mast cell clonal disease? Indications include anaphylaxis that is unusually severe (profound hypotension, hypoxaemia), recurrent or unexplained episodes, anaphylaxis triggered by hymenoptera sting or idiopathic anaphylaxis, persistently elevated baseline tryptase (particularly >20 ng/mL), or extracutaneous features suggestive of systemic involvement (hepatosplenomegaly, cytopenias, bone pain, or malabsorption). The initial work-up typically includes repeat baseline serum tryptase, a careful history and physical exam for signs of systemic disease, and targeted laboratory tests (CBC with differential, liver function tests). Specialist testing may include peripheral blood testing for the KIT D816V mutation using sensitive PCR methods (allele-specific PCR, digital PCR), flow cytometry on blood or marrow looking for mast cells expressing aberrant markers (CD25 and/or CD2), and ultimately a bone marrow biopsy when peripheral testing suggests clonal mast cell disease or when baseline tryptase is substantially elevated (>20 ng/mL) or there are other WHO-based reasons to examine marrow. The WHO diagnostic criteria for systemic mastocytosis require either a major criterion (multifocal dense infiltrates of mast cells in bone marrow or other extracutaneous organs) plus one minor criterion, or three minor criteria: (1) >25% of mast cells are spindle-shaped or atypical; (2) KIT D816V mutation in blood/marrow; (3) aberrant mast cell surface expression of CD25 (with or without CD2) by flow cytometry or IHC; and (4) baseline serum tryptase persistently >20 ng/mL (unless there is an associated hematologic neoplasm that can elevate tryptase). Because bone marrow sampling is invasive and can yield nondiagnostic material, decisions to biopsy should be made in conjunction with allergy/immunology and hematology.

Drug allergy testing deserves special mention in the diagnostic algorithm. For many drugs there are validated skin testing or in vitro IgE assays, but for several drug classes, including macrolides such as clarithromycin, skin testing is unreliable and non‑validated. When the initial reaction is severe, testing by skin prick or intradermal challenge may be unsafe and is often deferred; drug provocation testing (graded challenge) is considered the gold standard for defining drug tolerance but should only be performed in a controlled setting by specialists and usually only when the suspected culprit needs to be used or when avoidance is problematic. In the presented case the team reasonably deferred provocative testing given the severity and multiphasic nature of the reaction and the ability to avoid the suspected drug.

Risk stratification for observation and disposition after an initial anaphylactic event is imperfect because no validated predictive model robustly identifies which patients will have biphasic or multiphasic courses. Nevertheless, clinical factors associated with higher likelihood of relapse or severe biphasic reactions are helpful for bedside decisions. Suggested predictors include delayed administration of the first epinephrine dose, a severe initial presentation (prolonged hypotension, hypoxia, loss of consciousness), the need for multiple epinephrine doses during the initial event, and a history of prior biphasic reactions. Comorbidities that increase risk include poorly controlled asthma, significant cardiovascular disease, pregnancy, and concurrent use of beta‑blockers or other medications that blunt sympathetic response. Social factors—unreliable access to emergency care, lack of social support, or inability to carry/use an epinephrine auto‑injector—also influence disposition.

Because universal prolonged admission after every anaphylactic episode is impractical, clinicians should apply a pragmatic risk-stratified surveillance strategy. A widely used approach is:

- Low risk: single, brief anaphylactic episode that completely responds to a single intramuscular epinephrine dose, no persistent cardiopulmonary compromise, identifiable and avoidable trigger, reliable patient support and access to care. These patients may be observed for a shorter interval (often at least 4–6 hours after symptom resolution) and discharged with education, an epinephrine auto‑injector, and outpatient allergy follow-up.

- Intermediate risk: more severe initial presentation (prolonged hypotension or respiratory symptoms), need for more than one dose of epinephrine, delayed response to therapy, uncertain access to prompt care after discharge, or significant comorbidity. Many clinicians observe these patients for 6–12 hours after symptom resolution and consider overnight admission if there is any clinical concern.

- High risk: refractory or multiphasic anaphylaxis (recurrent episodes despite appropriate therapy), persistent hemodynamic instability or hypoxaemia, need for continuous vasopressor support, ongoing or uncertain exposure to trigger (eg, sustained absorption of an allergen), or laboratory/clinical concern for underlying mast cell disease. These patients warrant admission and often ICU-level monitoring with continuous cardiorespiratory observation, and consideration of advanced strategies (eg, continuous epinephrine infusion if epinephrine-requiring recurrences are frequent and predictable).

These time frames are not universally standardized; guidelines differ, and local resources, expertise, and patient preferences will affect practice. In all cases, discharge planning should include avoidance counseling for the suspected trigger, prescription and teaching of an epinephrine auto‑injector (dosed appropriately for body weight), a written anaphylaxis action plan, and expedited referral to allergy/immunology for outpatient evaluation.

Finally, remember that the diagnostic workup is iterative. In the case described, early tryptase sampling was incomplete and the first event’s tryptase was not obtained, limiting diagnostic certainty. The patient’s presentation (severe hypotension and hypoxaemia requiring multiple epinephrine doses and ICU transfer) and persistently elevated baseline tryptase appropriately prompted further evaluation for a mast cell disorder. When planning follow‑up, clinicians should ensure a baseline tryptase is checked at least 24 hours after recovery, consider KIT D816V testing on peripheral blood, and involve allergy/immunology and hematology for guidance on bone marrow biopsy and testing for hereditary alpha‑tryptasemia if baseline tryptase remains elevated.

In summary, apply prompt, timed tryptase sampling during anaphylaxis, interpret acute and baseline values using the 20% + 2 rule, consider a mast cell workup when episodes are severe, recurrent or baseline tryptase is raised, and use clinical risk stratification (severity of initial event, number of epinephrine doses, comorbidities, social support) to guide observation intervals and need for ICU admission. This structured approach maximizes diagnostic yield, identifies patients at risk of recurrence, and helps balance safe care with resource stewardship.

## Management: Acute Treatment, Recurrent Episodes, and Disposition

The single most important principle in the management of anaphylaxis is that epinephrine is life‑saving and must be given promptly. In practice this means treating immediately when the clinical diagnosis of anaphylaxis is made rather than waiting for laboratory confirmation or partial responses to other drugs. The recommended intramuscular dose for adults and children is 0.01 mg/kg (maximum 0.5 mg) into the mid‑outer thigh, and this dose may be repeated every 5–15 minutes as required by persistent or recurrent symptoms. In many emergency settings an epinephrine auto‑injector provides the fastest, most reliable way to give this dose without delay; however, many commercially available adult auto‑injectors contain only 0.3 mg. For an adult patient who weighs more than ~50 kg — as in the case patient described (104 kg) — a 0.3 mg auto‑injector may underdose the patient. In hospital settings where staff are trained and epinephrine ampoules and syringes are available, drawing up 0.5 mg IM (from 1 mg/mL [1:1000] solution) to deliver the recommended adult dose is appropriate and preferable to underdosing. Repeated IM dosing is safe and effective and should be used in preference to waiting or relying on antihistamines or steroids to control airway or circulatory compromise.

Alongside epinephrine, early supportive measures are essential. Establish intravenous access early — preferably two large bore lines — and give rapid isotonic crystalloid boluses for hypotension (for adults this commonly means 20 mL/kg or roughly 1–2 L initially, repeated as needed). High‑flow supplemental oxygen should be administered for hypoxaemia or respiratory distress. Cardiac monitoring, continuous pulse oximetry and frequent blood pressure checks (initially every few minutes until stable) are required in patients with respiratory or circulatory involvement, and arterial monitoring should be considered in those with ongoing shock. In the case presented, early IV access, oxygen and fluid were appropriate adjuncts to the immediate IM epinephrine she received.

Bronchospasm should be treated with inhaled short‑acting β2‑agonists (nebulised salbutamol) in addition to epinephrine; they relieve bronchospasm but do not replace epinephrine. Upper‑airway oedema with stridor may benefit from inhaled/nebulised epinephrine (or racemic epinephrine), while remembering that this is an adjunct to systemic IM epinephrine rather than a substitute. If airway compromise is progressing, early involvement of anaesthesia/ENT and preparation for advanced airway management are warranted.

When should therapy be escalated beyond repeated IM injections? The need for more than one or two IM epinephrine doses, persistent hypotension despite fluids and IM epinephrine, progressive respiratory failure, or evidence of refractory anaphylaxis are indications to escalate care. In patients who require multiple IM doses or who have ongoing shock, a titrated intravenous epinephrine infusion delivered in an intensive care setting is appropriate. IV infusions should be prepared and titrated according to local protocols and administered with continuous monitoring and personnel experienced with vasoactive drug titration. Pragmatic regimens are commonly used: for example, preparing epinephrine so the concentration is known (many hospitals dilute 1 mg in 100 mL to yield 10 µg/mL) and then starting an infusion at a low microgram‑per‑minute rate (for example 2–5 µg/min) and titrating to effect. Intermittent IV boluses of epinephrine (for example 50–100 µg) can restore perfusion in monitored patients but carry a higher risk of arrhythmia and myocardial ischaemia and therefore should be used cautiously and usually only in monitored/critical care settings. If distributive shock persists despite adequate fluid resuscitation and epinephrine, adding a vasopressor with predominantly α‑adrenergic activity (such as norepinephrine) may be required to maintain perfusion. In the presented case, the team considered an IV epinephrine infusion when she required repeated IM doses; the decision to proceed to infusion should balance the frequency and severity of relapses against the risks of IV vasoactive therapy and the availability of ICU monitoring.

Special situations require tailored therapy. Patients taking β‑blockers can be relatively resistant to epinephrine and may present with refractory hypotension or bronchospasm. In such cases intravenous glucagon should be administered promptly while continuing epinephrine and fluids; glucagon has inotropic and chronotropic effects independent of β‑adrenergic receptors. A common practical regimen is an IV bolus of 1–2 mg glucagon (may repeat) followed by a continuous infusion titrated to response, with attention to adverse effects such as vomiting and hyperglycaemia and with availability of antiemetic therapy. Pregnant patients, children, and the elderly require additional attention to airway and perfusion, but the priority remains early IM epinephrine and appropriate supportive care.

Antihistamines (H1 blockers ± H2 blockers) and corticosteroids are adjunctive. H1 antihistamines (eg, IV diphenhydramine or clemastine) and H2 blockers can reduce cutaneous symptoms (pruritus, urticaria) and may be given once immediate life‑threatening features are addressed, but they do not treat bronchospasm or hypotension and must never delay epinephrine administration. Corticosteroids have been used traditionally in the hope of preventing biphasic reactions; however, contemporary evidence does not support routine steroid use for this purpose, and steroids are not effective for acute reversal of airway or circulatory collapse. Steroids increase the risk of hyperglycaemia, mood disturbance and other systemic effects and have been associated in some series with increased resource use. Reserve systemic corticosteroids for situations where they are otherwise indicated (for example a concomitant severe asthmatic component, adrenal insufficiency, or as advised by specialist input), and if given, use standard dosing (eg, IV methylprednisolone 125 mg or equivalent) guided by local protocols.

Because biphasic and multiphasic courses are driven by ongoing mediator release or continued allergen exposure, attention to source control is important. Early identification and removal of a stinger, cessation of a culprit medication, and avoidance of further ingestion are basic and high‑value steps. Decontamination (for example activated charcoal for oral exposures) is rarely useful in anaphylaxis and is only considered in selected cases where ingestion was recent, the airway is protected, and decontamination is otherwise appropriate. In many severe drug‑induced reactions, the safest approach is to stop the suspected agent and refer for allergology assessment rather than attempt provocative testing during the acute phase.

Disposition and observation planning must be individualized. For patients with mild anaphylaxis who respond promptly to a single epinephrine dose, have no respiratory or cardiovascular involvement and have no substantial comorbidity, a period of observation of at least 4–6 hours after symptom resolution is commonly considered reasonable. Patients with severe presentations — hypotension, hypoxaemia, the need for multiple epinephrine doses, ongoing medications that interfere with response (β‑blockers), pregnancy, significant comorbid cardiopulmonary disease, or suspected mast cell disorder — merit longer observation (12–24 hours) and a lower threshold for admission. Admission to a monitored setting or ICU is indicated for those with persistent or recurrent anaphylaxis, need for a continuous epinephrine infusion, airway compromise requiring close monitoring or mechanical ventilation, or haemodynamic instability requiring vasopressors. Multiphasic anaphylaxis with repeated relapses, as in our case, is an example where ICU admission for monitoring and consideration of infusion therapy is prudent. Because biphasic reactions can occur beyond the early observation window, clinicians should apply a risk‑stratified approach: more severe initial reactions and the need for multiple doses predict higher risk for relapse and justify longer observation.

Before discharge, ensure the patient is equipped to manage potential recurrent reactions at home. This includes prescribing and teaching the use of two epinephrine auto‑injectors (dose matched to weight — typically 0.15 mg for children 15–30 kg; 0.3 mg is common for adults and children >30 kg; where available and for adults substantially >50 kg consider a 0.5 mg device or training to use an emergency syringe if local guidance supports this), education in when and how to use them, and a written emergency action plan. Advise the patient to carry the auto‑injectors at all times, to seek emergency care after using an injector, and to wear medical allergy identification. Arrange urgent specialist follow‑up with an allergist/immunologist to confirm the trigger (via history, serum tests and, where safe, skin testing or graded challenge) and to consider investigation for underlying conditions such as mast cell disorders; measure tryptase levels in the acute phase (within 1–2 hours of symptom onset when possible) and again after resolution (>24 hours) to establish a baseline. Document the suspected trigger, the reaction, treatments given, and discharge plan clearly in the medical record and communicate this to the patient’s primary care provider.

Finally, clinical judgment and local resources will influence the precise thresholds for escalation and observation. Because multiphasic anaphylaxis is uncommon and evidence about optimal observation periods and preventive measures is limited, a low threshold for prolonged monitoring and specialist consultation is reasonable when the initial reaction is severe, when multiple epinephrine doses are required, or when an underlying mast cell activation disorder is suspected, as in the patient described. Clear instructions on avoidance, prompt access to epinephrine, and specialist follow‑up are the cornerstones of safe disposition.

## Clinical Pearls, Pitfalls, and Systems Considerations

This case of multiphasic anaphylaxis highlights practical lessons that should change how teams recognize, treat, monitor, and organize care for anaphylaxis. Below are the high‑yield clinical pearls, common pitfalls and the systems-level safeguards that reduce morbidity in these patients.

Recognize and treat the syndrome immediately
Anaphylaxis is a clinical diagnosis. When the clinical constellation of acute onset mucocutaneous signs, respiratory compromise, circulatory collapse or gastrointestinal symptoms suggests anaphylaxis, treatment must not be delayed while waiting for a rash, laboratory confirmation, or progression to hypotension. The priority is rapid intramuscular (IM) epinephrine, oxygen, airway protection and intravascular volume resuscitation. In the presented patient, prompt IM epinephrine reversed initial collapse—demonstrating that early treatment is lifesaving even before confirmatory testing.

Dose accuracy matters—avoid underdosing epinephrine
Under‑dosing epinephrine is a common, remediable cause of incomplete response and recurrence. The recommended dose for IM epinephrine in adults is 0.01 mg/kg (maximum commonly cited 0.5 mg). In practice, clinicians should treat most adults ≥50 kg with 0.5 mg IM in the mid‑outer thigh. Many auto‑injectors supplied in some settings contain only 0.3 mg; this can be an underdose for larger adults and was a practical issue in this case where initial doses were 0.3 mg and additional injections were required. Systems should ensure reliable pathways to deliver correct adult doses—this can include stocking 0.5 mg auto‑injectors where available, having pre‑prepared syringes or rapid pharmacy protocols to draw 0.5 mg from ampoules, and training staff to prepare/administer that dose without dangerous delay.

When to escalate to an infusion
Recurrent, refractory, or severe hemodynamic anaphylaxis should trigger early consideration of continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion rather than repeated, widely spaced IM doses. Repeated delays between IM injections while awaiting relapse risk deterioration. Infusions allow steady, titratable vasopressor support and are generally started in an ICU setting under a standardized protocol with pharmacy and critical care involvement. Exact starting infusion rates vary by institution; therefore, discuss local dosing protocols (and have them readily available). The clinical point is to avoid waiting until multiple relapses occur before escalating care—if multiple IM doses are required or the patient remains hemodynamically fragile, arrange infusion and ICU monitoring early.

Practical administration details that affect efficacy
Technique matters. IM injections must be given into the mid‑outer thigh, not deltoid or subcutaneous tissue. Needle length and body habitus are important—obesity increases the risk of a subcutaneous instead of an IM injection, which slows absorption and reduces efficacy. Auto‑injectors are designed to deliver IM injections but their needle length may be inadequate in very obese patients. Teams should be aware of these limitations and trained in both correct use of auto‑injectors and alternatives when they are likely to fail (e.g., prompt use of manual IM injection with an appropriately long needle, or earlier IV access and infusion if warranted). Rapid access to oxygen, large‑bore IVs, and resuscitation equipment must be ensured.

Laboratory testing—getting tryptase right
Tryptase can be a helpful adjunct to document mast cell activation and to identify patients who may have an underlying mast cell disorder. However, timing and interpretation matter:

- Obtain an acute sample as soon as feasible—ideally within 1–2 hours of symptom onset (peaks by ~1–4 hours). If possible, obtain a second acute sample within 1–4 hours.  
- Obtain a baseline (steady‑state) sample at least 24 hours after full clinical recovery to determine the patient’s baseline tryptase.  
- Use the 20% + 2 rule to determine a significant rise: an acute tryptase greater than baseline × 1.2 + 2 ng/mL supports mast cell activation.  
- A normal tryptase does not exclude anaphylaxis; conversely, an elevated baseline tryptase should prompt evaluation for mast cell disease (systemic mastocytosis or hereditary alpha‑tryptasemia). WHO minor criteria include a persistently elevated baseline tryptase >20 ng/mL.  

In the case, acute sampling was imperfectly timed and baseline sampling occurred, but earlier adherence to the recommended sampling windows would have improved diagnostic certainty.

Suspect underlying mast cell disorders when appropriate
Severe, recurrent, or unexpectedly prolonged anaphylaxis—especially when tryptase is persistently elevated—should raise suspicion for an underlying mast cell disorder. Coordination with allergy/immunology and hematology for further evaluation (repeat tryptase, bone marrow assessment when indicated, KIT mutation testing, and thorough clinical review) is appropriate. Because bone marrow sampling and some provocative tests carry risk, specialist input is essential to balance diagnostic yield and safety. The case patient had features raising the probability of systemic mastocytosis (elevated tryptase, hepatomegaly) despite not meeting full diagnostic criteria on the initial biopsy.

Observation and disposition should be individualized
There is no universal “one‑size‑fits‑all” observation interval. Mild presentations that respond to a single epinephrine dose and have no risk factors may be observed for a shorter period (commonly 4–6 hours) with clear discharge instructions. However, the presence of any of the following should prompt prolonged observation or hospital admission: severe initial presentation (hypotension, hypoxaemia, airway compromise), need for multiple epinephrine doses, persistent or biphasic symptoms, suspected slow‑release trigger (oral medication or depot), or a history or suspicion of mast cell disorder. For these higher‑risk patients, prolonged monitoring and a lower threshold to admit to an intermediate or intensive care setting is prudent. Use institution‑specific, risk‑stratified protocols to guide disposition.

Do not rely on corticosteroids to prevent biphasic reactions
Historically, corticosteroids were given to prevent biphasic anaphylaxis, but contemporary evidence does not support routine steroid use for this purpose and suggests no clear reduction in biphasic events. Steroids have potential harms (hyperglycemia, infection risk, and possible prolongation of hospitalization), and their routine use for preventing biphasic reactions should be abandoned. Steroids remain appropriate when indicated for other reasons (e.g., an asthmatic component) or at the treating team’s discretion, but they should not replace epinephrine, fluids, oxygen, or escalation of vasopressor support when required.

Discharge planning and patient education—safety after the ED
Before discharge, patients should receive:

- Clear oral and written anaphylaxis action plan with recognition of early signs and instructions to administer epinephrine promptly.  
- Prescription for at least two epinephrine auto‑injectors and hands‑on teaching for self‑administration and storage; emphasize calling emergency services immediately after self‑injection.  
- Advice on avoidance of the likely trigger and on when to seek specialist allergy follow‑up for diagnostic testing and desensitization options if relevant.  
- Information about medical alert identification and a plan for notifying primary care and pharmacists about the allergy.  

In this case, the patient was taught autoinjector use and discharged with antihistamine and follow‑up; given her severe multiphasic course and suspected mast cell disease she appropriately required specialist follow‑up.

Systems considerations to reduce preventable harm
Individual clinician skill is necessary but not sufficient—systems must be designed to support reliable, evidence‑based care:

- Ensure emergency areas stock appropriate epinephrine formulations (including convenient access to adult 0.5 mg delivery or standardized means to rapidly prepare correct doses) and that resuscitation trolleys are checked regularly.  
- Develop and disseminate clear anaphylaxis order sets and local protocols for refractory anaphylaxis (including criteria for escalation to infusion and ICU transfer).  
- Make tryptase testing available 24/7 and include standardized instructions for timing/sample labeling to improve diagnostic yield.  
- Use checklists, simulation training and interdisciplinary education to maintain staff competence in recognition and timely administration (technique and dosing) of epinephrine and other supportive measures.  
- Create referral pathways for allergy and hematology evaluation and consider local tracking or registry of complex biphasic/multiphasic cases to learn from outcomes and inform local protocols.  

Common pitfalls summarized
Do not wait for rash or hypotension to treat. Do not accept repeated under‑dosing (0.3 mg in many adults) as adequate. Do not assume corticosteroids will prevent relapse. Do not omit timely tryptase sampling or fail to arrange specialist follow‑up when baseline tryptase is elevated or reactions are unusually severe. Finally, do not let logistics (unfamiliarity with dosing, lack of equipment, or uncertainty about infusion protocols) delay escalation when a patient is not stabilizing.

Takeaway
Effective anaphylaxis care requires rapid recognition, correct epinephrine dosing and route, early escalation for recurrent or refractory cases, appropriate laboratory testing with correct timing, and systems that make the right care the easy care. The presented multiphasic course illustrates how under‑dosing, imperfect sampling and delayed escalation can complicate management—and how coordinated, protocolized responses and patient education can mitigate risk and improve outcomes.
