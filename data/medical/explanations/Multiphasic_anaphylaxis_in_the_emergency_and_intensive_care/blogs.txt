# Multiphasic Mayhem: Managing Anaphylaxis That Keeps Coming Back

This case is the kind of thing that wakes you up at 0200: a woman in her 60s developed anaphylaxis about 1 hour after a first dose of clarithromycin. She improved with initial care (O2, fluids, IM epinephrine via a 0.3 mg autoinjector, antihistamine) but then had three clear relapses over the next 12 hours — four anaphylactic episodes total. Each relapse required another IM epinephrine dose; later doses were given as 0.5 mg IM. Steroids were started after the first relapse. Workup showed persistently raised tryptase and features suspicious for systemic mastocytosis (but not meeting full WHO criteria).

Why this case is interesting
- Multiphasic anaphylaxis (multiple relapses) is rare and poorly guided by existing protocols. Most guidance addresses single biphasic events or refractory cases; the multiphasic pattern forces repeated decisions about escalation, monitoring, and the role of continuous epinephrine.
- Practical dilemmas: keep giving IM epinephrine on each relapse? Start an IV epinephrine infusion pre-emptively? Admit to ICU even if the patient initially stabilised? What about steroids and testing for mast cell disease?

## Terminology — get the categories straight
- Anaphylaxis: clinically diagnosed systemic allergic reaction with airway/breathing/circulation/GI involvement plus usually cutaneous features.
- Biphasic anaphylaxis: recurrence of symptoms after an asymptomatic interval without re-exposure (usually within 24 hours; many within 6–12 hours).
- Multiphasic anaphylaxis: more than one recurrence (multiple relapses over time). Rare and less well-defined in guidelines.
- Refractory anaphylaxis: failure to respond adequately to appropriate epinephrine (persistent shock despite treatment).

Differentiation matters because it drives monitoring and escalation. In practice, it can be hard to distinguish a true biphasic/multiphasic course from ongoing exposure (eg, retained allergen, ongoing absorption) or from a transient response to epinephrine with return of symptoms while the trigger is still acting.

## Epinephrine strategies: repeat IM doses vs IV infusion
What happened in the case: initial 0.3 mg IM autoinjector (common in many EDs), subsequent relapses treated with two 0.5 mg IM injections. All resolved with IM doses; intensity waned with each episode.

Thinking through options:
- Repeat IM epinephrine (0.01 mg/kg, up to 0.5 mg adult): the standard first-line approach for recurrent episodes. Rapid, safe, effective. IM route avoids many of the risks of IV epinephrine (arrhythmia, hypertension). If each relapse responds promptly to IM drug and the patient’s hemodynamics stabilise between episodes, continuing repeat IM doses and close monitoring is reasonable.
- IV epinephrine infusion: consider when:
  - Relapses are frequent or escalating despite correct IM dosing.
  - Patient needs repeated large cumulative doses, or episodes occur rapidly such that IM administration is impractical/delayed.
  - Patient is refractory to IM epinephrine (ie, ongoing shock despite appropriate IM doses).
  - Need for precise titration (eg, persistent hypotension or severe shock).
  Risks: arrhythmias, hypertensive crises, dosing errors. Requires ICU-level monitoring and staff experienced with vasoactive infusions.

Practical bedside rule-of-thumb from this case and common sense:
- If relapses are infrequent, respond rapidly to a single IM dose, and patient has improving trend → continue repeat IM dosing + escalation of monitoring (telemetry/ICU if needed).
- If relapses are frequent, require multiple IM doses in short succession, or don’t reliably reverse with IM epinephrine → escalate to IV infusion in ICU.
- Individual patient factors matter: pregnancy, cardiovascular disease, age, body weight, and suspected mast cell disorder will influence thresholds.

## Dosing and delivery pitfalls
- Recommended adult IM dose: 0.01 mg/kg (max 0.5 mg). Many autoinjectors are 0.3 mg — underdosing for adults >50 kg is common and may increase need for escalation.
- Use autoinjectors for rapid early treatment (they reduce delay/hesitancy), but be prepared to escalate to manual 0.5 mg IM or IV infusion when clinically indicated.
- When starting an IV infusion, use standard concentration protocols, pump, and ICU monitoring — and involve someone experienced promptly.

## Monitoring, disposition, and observation time
- No one-size-fits-all observation time. Traditional 4–6 hours is common but may miss later biphasic events. Many biphasic relapses occur within 6–12 hours; reports extend to 78 hours.
- In multiphasic/recurrent cases, low threshold for ICU or step-up monitoring: continuous observation, telemetry, frequent vitals, and staff able to give IM epinephrine or start an infusion quickly.
- Discharge only after an adequate asymptomatic period (the case patient had ~24 hours without relapse before transfer out of ICU and was discharged after 3 days). Tailor to severity and number of relapses.

## Pharmacologic adjuncts: steroids and antihistamines
- Antihistamines (H1/H2) relieve cutaneous symptoms — supportive, not life-saving.
- Corticosteroids: historically used to prevent biphasic reactions, but evidence does not show benefit; may increase admissions. Routine steroids are no longer strongly supported. In this case steroids were given after the first relapse (reflecting local practice variability).
- Focus efforts on timely epinephrine and appropriate monitoring rather than relying on steroids to prevent relapse.

## When to suspect an underlying mast cell disorder
- Elevated baseline tryptase, unusually severe, recurrent, or idiopathic anaphylaxis should prompt consideration of mast cell disease.
- In this case: raised tryptase samples, hepatomegaly, neuropsychiatric symptoms — suspicious for systemic mastocytosis though diagnostic criteria were not fully met.
- Practical points: tryptase sampling should be timed — initial sample as soon as feasible, second at 1–2 hours (within 4 hours) when it peaks, and a baseline >24 hours after resolution. In this case the timing wasn’t ideal, but persistent elevation on follow-up raised concern.
- If suspected, involve allergy/immunology for directed testing and counselling; patients may need lifelong precautions and specialist follow-up.

## Bedside decision points — a practical roadmap
- Immediate: give IM epinephrine (0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg) without delay. Oxygen, IV fluids, airway support as needed.
- After initial response:
  - Admit for observation. If severe initial shock, use high-acuity monitoring.
  - If relapse occurs:
    - If single, responds quickly to IM epinephrine → continue IM dosing and escalate monitoring (consider telemetry/ICU depending on frequency and comorbidity).
    - If multiple relapses, shorter intervals between relapses, or poor response → strongly consider ICU admission and an IV epinephrine infusion.
- Re-evaluate need for steroid use (evidence weak); continue antihistamines for symptom control.
- Obtain tryptase samples when feasible (timing matters) and involve allergy for follow-up testing if suspicious features.
- Provide discharge plan: epinephrine autoinjector (teach use), antihistamine prescription, clear avoidance advice, and expedited allergy follow-up.

## Clinical pearls
- Autoinjectors save time but many are 0.3 mg — consider immediate manual 0.5 mg IM for adults >50 kg if in-hospital and personnel comfortable.
- Repeat IM epinephrine is appropriate if each relapse responds promptly; escalate to IV infusion when relapses are frequent or refractory.
- Steroids do not reliably prevent biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis — they’re not a substitute for careful observation and epinephrine readiness.
- Think mast cell disease when anaphylaxis is unusually severe, recurrent, or idiopathic; do tryptase testing with appropriate timing.

Key take-home messages
- Prompt IM epinephrine is first-line; repeat doses are acceptable and often sufficient for relapses.
- Move to IV epinephrine infusion in ICU for frequent, escalating, or refractory relapses.
- Monitor longer and more closely after multiphasic or severe anaphylaxis; a patient-free window of 24 hours without recurrence was used successfully in the case presented.
- Routine corticosteroids are not supported by strong evidence for preventing biphasic/multiphasic anaphylaxis.
- Consider mast cell disorders when tryptase is persistently elevated or clinical course is atypical — involve allergy/immunology early.

Multiphasic anaphylaxis is messy, rare, and stressful — treat like an unstable process that can tip at any time. Use epinephrine decisively, watch closely, escalate to ICU and infusion when control slips, and remember to pursue follow-up for possible underlying mast cell disease.



# The Adult Dose We Keep Missing: Why 0.5 mg IM Epinephrine Matters

What’s striking about the multiphasic anaphylaxis case we just read isn’t only that the patient relapsed multiple times — it’s that the first two life‑saving injections were 0.3 mg epinephrine auto‑injectors. She later needed additional IM epinephrine to treat recurrent hypotension and hypoxaemia. This is a concrete example of a broader, underappreciated problem: many adults routinely receive 0.3 mg IM epinephrine (the dose in common auto‑injectors) when guideline dosing for adults commonly aims for 0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg. For many adults — especially >50 kg — 0.3 mg is simply an underdose.

Why this matters
- Epinephrine is the only immediate, life‑saving pharmacologic therapy for anaphylaxis. Dose and timely administration are the two modifiable things that most influence response.
- Guidelines (and pharmacologic rationale) support 0.01 mg/kg IM epinephrine, with a practical adult ceiling of 0.5 mg. That ceiling exists because most adults weigh >50 kg.
- Many prehospital and ED settings rely on 0.3 mg auto‑injectors because they’re familiar, easy to use, and widely available — but that convenience can translate into systematic underdosing.
- The case: initial response to 0.3 mg IM occurred, but relapse required further IM epinephrine (0.5 mg doses used later). Emerging retrospective data (noted in the case report) associate initial 0.3 mg dosing with higher rates of escalation to additional doses or higher levels of care compared with starting at 0.5 mg.

Practical physiology reminder
- IM epinephrine works via alpha and beta receptors: alpha‑mediated vasoconstriction restores perfusion in anaphylactic shock; beta‑1/2 effects support cardiac output and bronchodilation.
- Subtherapeutic dosing risks only partial receptor engagement and transient improvement, which can masquerade as adequate treatment and delay definitive care, or lead to relapses that need repeated dosing.

Barriers that keep 0.3 mg in circulation
- Habit and training: many clinicians and EMS crews have long used 0.3 mg auto‑injectors and are reluctant to change.
- Stock and formulary: some institutions don’t stock 0.5 mg auto‑injectors or have limited access to alternative preparations.
- Fear of harm: concern about overdosing or cardiac effects in older adults — but the balance of risks favors adequate dosing in true anaphylaxis.
- Device availability: the simplest, fastest option (auto‑injector) may not match ideal dosing for heavier adults.

What to do in your ED, ward, ambulance or clinic — practical fixes
- Stock 0.5 mg IM options where adult anaphylaxis is treated. If auto‑injectors aren’t available, ensure ready access to ampoules for drawing 0.5 mg IM doses.
- Use prefilled syringes for immediate use (pharmacy can prepare 0.5 mg IM syringes) to combine speed and correct dosing.
- Build decision supports into order sets and treatment algorithms: weight prompt, “give 0.5 mg IM if ≥50 kg (0.01 mg/kg, max 0.5 mg).”
- Educate and train: simulation scenarios that use 0.5 mg dosing will reduce hesitancy. Emphasize that epinephrine is time‑critical and that cardiac complications are rare at therapeutic doses in true anaphylaxis.
- Clarify auto‑injector messaging: for staff, label that many commercially common adult auto‑injectors are 0.3 mg and may underdose adults >50 kg. For patients, prescribe an appropriate auto‑injector (where 0.5 mg devices are available) and teach weight‑based use.
- Protocolize escalation: when a 0.3 mg dose was used and hypotension/airway compromise persists or recurs, administer additional IM epinephrine (up to recommended dosing), and escalate monitoring/ICU admission as needed.

Tactical reminders when treating anaphylaxis (grounded in the case)
- Give epinephrine early and repeat every 5–15 minutes as needed. Don’t substitute IV epinephrine unless refractory shock or ICU setting with monitoring.
- Aim for 0.01 mg/kg IM, max 0.5 mg — practically, give 0.5 mg IM for most adults.
- Auto‑injectors (0.3 mg) are better than delay, but recognize when they may be inadequate and be ready to give an additional IM dose.
- Consider patient factors that raise the stakes: obesity, delayed presentation, severe initial shock, or known/suspected mast cell disorders (as in the case) — these may increase risk of relapse or refractory courses.
- Don’t rely on steroids to prevent biphasic reactions; focus on correct epinephrine dosing and observation/monitoring.

Clinical pearls
- A 0.3 mg auto‑injector is likely an underdose for most adult patients. If you use one, be prepared to give additional IM epinephrine without delay.
- Institutional change is doable: pharmacy, procurement, education and order‑set updates dramatically lower the risk that adults will be underdosed.
- In multiphasic or relapsing anaphylaxis, repeated IM dosing of epinephrine (and ICU monitoring when necessary) is often preferable to delayed escalation or unproven adjuncts.

Take‑home messages
- For adult anaphylaxis aim for 0.01 mg/kg IM epinephrine, max 0.5 mg — in practice give 0.5 mg IM for most adults.
- 0.3 mg auto‑injectors are convenient but often underdose adults; have systems in place to deliver 0.5 mg quickly.
- Stock 0.5 mg options, use prefilled syringes, update order sets, and train teams to overcome dosing hesitancy.
- Timely, adequate epinephrine saves lives — getting the dose right is low‑hanging fruit to improve outcomes.



# Steroids in Anaphylaxis: Habit, Hype, or Harm?

What makes this case interesting: a woman in her 60s developed multiphasic anaphylaxis after a single 500 mg dose of oral clarithromycin. She needed repeated intramuscular epinephrine across four distinct episodes in the first 12 hours. After the first relapse she was given corticosteroids (IV prednisone + dexamethasone) in the hope of preventing further biphasic/multiphasic events — a practice that is common worldwide. She eventually recovered without further relapses, but the episode raises the question we hear on every shift: do steroids prevent biphasic anaphylaxis, or are we treating habit more than evidence?

This case also highlights two practical issues that matter more than debates about steroids: (1) epinephrine dosing and access (initial auto-injectors used were 0.3 mg — probably underdosing for >50 kg adults), and (2) the need to consider underlying mast cell disease when anaphylaxis is unusually severe, recurrent, or multiphasic (this patient had elevated baseline tryptase and features suspicious for systemic mastocytosis).

What the literature actually says about steroids and biphasic anaphylaxis
- The historical rationale: corticosteroids were thought to blunt late-phase inflammatory responses and therefore reduce delayed or biphasic reactions.
- The evidence: multiple observational studies and the best available systematic reviews do not support a protective effect of corticosteroids against biphasic anaphylaxis. A 2017 systematic review of 22 studies found no clear reduction in biphasic reactions with steroids; most individual studies showed no benefit. More recent observational data have even suggested potential harms — longer ED stays or higher admission rates associated with prehospital steroid use.
- Guidelines are shifting. Many contemporary anaphylaxis guidelines no longer recommend routine corticosteroids to prevent biphasic reactions; steroids are not first-line therapy for acute anaphylaxis.

Why steroids persist in practice
- Momentum: long-standing teaching in textbooks and local protocols.
- Theoretical biologic plausibility and desire to do something after an initial relapse.
- Difficulty separating association from causation in single cases (if a patient doesn’t relapse after steroids, it’s tempting to credit the drug).

Clinical harms and downsides of routine steroid use in anaphylaxis
- Delay or distraction from primary therapy if providers feel steroids are “enough.”
- Potential side effects (hyperglycaemia — particularly relevant in this patient with diabetes; immunosuppression with repeated doses; psychiatric effects).
- Possible increase in resource use (longer ED/ward stays and admissions in observational data).
- No proven reduction in clinically meaningful outcomes like recurrent anaphylaxis or mortality.

Practical, evidence-aligned approach (grounded in this case)
1. Epinephrine-first, epinephrine-often
   - IM epinephrine 0.01 mg/kg (max 0.5 mg) remains the only medication proven to be life-saving in anaphylaxis. For most adults this means 0.5 mg IM. A 0.3 mg auto-injector underdoses many adults and may be associated with need for additional dosing or escalation.
   - Don’t delay epinephrine while obtaining IV access or other interventions.
2. Supportive care
   - Airway, oxygen for hypoxaemia, aggressive IV fluids for hypotension, H1 (± H2) antihistamines for cutaneous symptoms.
   - Consider bronchodilators for bronchospasm.
3. Observation and monitoring trump routine steroids
   - The primary reason to observe is to detect true biphasic recurrence and treat promptly with epinephrine, not to rely on steroids as prophylaxis.
   - Observation duration should be individualized: more prolonged monitoring for patients with severe initial presentations, delayed epinephrine, multiple epinephrine doses, or signs suggesting protracted absorption of allergen (e.g., oral exposures, depot formulations) — but there is no universally agreed fixed time.
4. When might steroids still be reasonable?
   - Not for routine prevention of biphasic anaphylaxis.
   - Reasonable to give if there is another clear indication: concurrent severe asthma exacerbation, known adrenal insufficiency, or another inflammatory condition that would normally get steroids.
   - If you do give steroids, document the indication and recognize they are not a substitute for monitoring or repeat epinephrine.
5. Escalation strategies for refractory or multiphasic cases
   - Recurrent episodes requiring multiple boluses: escalate monitoring (ICU), continuous infusion epinephrine can be considered in refractory cases under critical care supervision.
   - Early involvement of allergy/immunology and consideration of tryptase sampling and mast cell disorder workup if presentation is unusually severe, recurrent, or associated with high baseline tryptase — as in this patient where systemic mastocytosis was suspected.
6. Discharge planning and risk reduction
   - Prescribe and teach proper use of an epinephrine auto-injector (ensure appropriate dose for patient weight).
   - Arrange allergy follow-up; avoid re-exposure to the suspected trigger if reasonably certain.
   - Educate patient on signs of recurrence and when to return.

Clinical pearls from this case
- Multiphasic anaphylaxis happens, but is rare. The key prevention of bad outcomes is prompt recognition, immediate IM epinephrine, and appropriate monitoring — not reflexive corticosteroid administration.
- Many adults will be underdosed by a 0.3 mg auto-injector. If the ED has 0.5 mg IM formulation and staff trained to use it, aim for guideline dose (0.5 mg in most adults).
- Consider mast cell disease when anaphylaxis is unusually severe, recurrent, or multiphasic; get tryptase sampling (acute and baseline) and arrange allergy/immunology follow-up.
- If you give steroids, do it for a clear indication other than “prevent biphasic reaction.” Don’t let steroid administration substitute for observation and readiness to re-treat with epinephrine.

Take-home messages
- Epinephrine is the only proven life-saving therapy in anaphylaxis — give early and in adequate dose (most adults 0.5 mg IM).
- Routine corticosteroids do not prevent biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis and should not replace observation and appropriate escalation.
- Observe patients with severe initial anaphylaxis, delayed epinephrine, or multiple epinephrine doses more closely; criteria for observation should be individualized.
- Think mast cell disorder when anaphylaxis is unusually severe or recurrent — check tryptase and refer to allergy/immunology.
- Document indications if you choose to give steroids (eg, severe asthma, adrenal insufficiency), and prioritize epinephrine-first management and clear follow-up plans.



# How Long Is Long Enough? Risk-Stratified Observation After Anaphylaxis

What makes this case interesting: a woman in her 60s who developed multiphasic anaphylaxis after a single dose of clarithromycin, needed repeated intramuscular epinephrine, was transiently hypotensive and hypoxaemic, and ultimately had a high baseline tryptase suggestive of a mast cell disorder. She had four anaphylactic episodes over about 12 hours with progressively milder symptoms — a tidy example of why “one-and-done” thinking after apparent clinical resolution can be dangerous and why observation strategies must be practical, not dogmatic.

The problem: guideline guidance on post-anaphylaxis observation is inconsistent. Reported biphasic incidence varies widely, but many series put it around 4–6% and show roughly half of biphasic events occur within 6–12 hours. Rarely, biphasic events occur much later (up to days), and multiphasic courses (multiple relapses) pose extra uncertainty for bedside teams. We need a pragmatic, risk-stratified pathway that balances patient safety and resource use.

Quick reality checks from the literature and this case
- Biphasic/multiphasic anaphylaxis exists and can recur despite initial full response to epinephrine.
- Risk signals (not guarantees): severe initial reaction (hypotension, hypoxaemia, airway compromise), delayed initial epinephrine, need for multiple epinephrine doses, underlying mast cell disorder or elevated baseline tryptase, prior biphasic reaction.
- Routine corticosteroids do not reliably prevent biphasic reactions and are falling out of favor for routine use.
- Many adults are underdosed if given 0.3 mg IM epinephrine (auto-injector) — 0.5 mg IM is the usual adult target (0.01 mg/kg, max ≈0.5 mg).

A pragmatic, risk-stratified observation pathway (ED → ward → ICU decisions)

Principles
- Observation duration should be driven by clinical risk features rather than a single fixed rule for everyone.
- Monitoring intensity should match risk: simple vital checks vs. telemetry/ICU with airway/pressors ready.
- Recurrent need for epinephrine or persistent/worsening features escalate disposition.
- Document clear return precautions and ensure epinephrine auto-injector prescription and education on discharge.

Risk tiers and suggested monitoring

1) Low risk — short observation (4–6 hours)
- Typical patient features:
  - Isolated cutaneous involvement or mild respiratory symptoms that fully resolve
  - Normal vitals after initial treatment
  - Single epinephrine dose given promptly (within minutes of symptom onset)
  - No comorbidities that would complicate care (significant cardiopulmonary disease), no suspicion for mast cell disorder
- Monitoring:
  - ED observation 4–6 hours after complete resolution of symptoms
  - Periodic vitals, reassess for recurrent urticaria, wheeze, GI symptoms
  - Discharge with antihistamine, clear return precautions, and epinephrine auto-injector if indicated
- Rationale: half of biphasic events happen within 6–12 hours; low-risk patients have low event rate and benefit from shorter ED stays.

2) Intermediate risk — extended observation (12 hours)
- Typical features:
  - Severe initial reaction but responded to therapy (hypotension or hypoxaemia initially)
  - Delayed initial epinephrine (>30–60 minutes from symptom onset) or need for more than one epinephrine dose
  - Extensive cutaneous symptoms plus respiratory/GI involvement
  - Obesity, pregnancy, or medication factors that might alter absorption (eg, oral meds)
- Monitoring:
  - 12 hours observation in ED observation unit or ward with frequent vitals and nursing reassessment
  - Consider telemetry if hypotension or cardiac risk
  - Consider obtaining tryptase (see below) and allergy follow-up
- Rationale: many biphasic reactions present in the 6–12 hour window; a longer watch is reasonable for patients with higher initial severity or multiple epinephrine doses.

3) High risk — prolonged observation/ICU (24+ hours)
- Any of the following should prompt ICU-level monitoring or prolonged observation:
  - Recurrent or multiphasic anaphylaxis (≥2 recurrences) or persistent symptoms requiring repeated epinephrine
  - Refractory anaphylaxis (inadequate response to appropriate IM epinephrine)
  - Initial cardiovascular collapse requiring vasopressors or significant hypoxaemia/airway compromise
  - Known or suspected mast cell disorder or markedly elevated baseline tryptase
  - Need for continuous IV epinephrine, escalating vasopressor support, or concern for airway edema that might progress
- Monitoring:
  - ICU telemetry, nursing ratio for frequent reassessment, airway equipment and epinephrine at bedside
  - Consider continuous infusions only when necessary; weigh risks vs. benefits
  - Serial reassessment for waning vs. relapsing symptoms; multidisciplinary consult (allergy/immunology)
- Rationale: these patients have the highest likelihood of recurrence or deterioration; early access to vasopressors and airway intervention is key.

Clinical pearls and practical points
- Epinephrine dosing: targeted adult dose ≈0.01 mg/kg IM up to 0.5 mg. Many adult auto-injectors are 0.3 mg — helpful for prompt dosing but may underdose heavier adults. If in-hospital and personnel comfortable, give 0.5 mg IM for adults >50 kg.
- If patient requires >1 IM epinephrine dose or symptoms recur, that pushes you into intermediate-to-high risk territory.
- Steroids: do not give steroids routinely to “prevent” biphasic anaphylaxis. Use steroids selectively for other indications (eg, asthma exacerbation). The evidence for preventing biphasic reactions is lacking.
- Tryptase timing: if available, get tryptase early (as soon as feasible), ideally a sample 1–2 hours after symptom onset (peak within ~1–4 hours) and another baseline sample ≥24 hours after full recovery. Elevated baseline tryptase should prompt allergy/immunology workup for mast cell disorders.
- Airway and oxygen: maintain a low threshold to secure airway if throat/tongue swelling, progressive stridor, or worsening hypoxaemia despite oxygen.
- Discharge planning: ensure patients who go home have a prescription and training for an epinephrine auto-injector, clear return precautions, and timely follow-up with allergy/immunology.

When to consider continuous IV epinephrine or ICU-level infusion
- Consider continuous IV epinephrine only if: refractory hypotension despite repeated IM epinephrine and fluids, or if frequent recurrent episodes occur such that repeated IM dosing is impractical or unsafe.
- Continuous infusions carry risks (arrhythmia, ischemia) and require ICU-level monitoring.

Applying the pathway to the case
- The patient had hypotension and hypoxaemia initially, needed multiple epinephrine doses over 12 hours, and ultimately had elevated tryptase suggesting a mast cell disorder — all features that would place her in the high-risk category and justify ICU monitoring until an extended symptom-free interval (at least 24 hours) and stable baseline tryptase sampling could be obtained and arranged.

Bottom line — takeaways for busy clinicians
- Observation after anaphylaxis should be risk-stratified, not one-size-fits-all.
- Low-risk patients: 4–6 hours observation after symptom resolution may be reasonable.
- Intermediate risk: 12-hour observation with appropriate monitoring.
- High risk or recurrent/refractory cases: ICU-level care and 24+ hours observation; consider tryptase and allergy/immunology involvement.
- Don’t rely on steroids to prevent biphasic reactions; focus on correct epinephrine dosing (consider 0.5 mg IM in adults >50 kg) and appropriate monitoring intensity.
- When in doubt, err on the side of longer observation and involve ICU/allergy early.

If you’re building local protocols, make them explicit about: what defines low/intermediate/high risk, who needs telemetry or ICU, epinephrine dosing (auto-injector vs in-hospital dose), and tryptase timing. Multiphasic anaphylaxis is uncommon — but when it happens, it’s one of those moments where preparation, clear thresholds, and early multidisciplinary input matter.



## Tryptase Timing Pearls: Don’t Miss the 20% + 2

What grabbed me in this case was not just the multiphasic anaphylactic course — which is itself a clinical headache — but that suboptimal timing of tryptase sampling limited how much we could learn. The team suspected systemic mastocytosis later on, but because the samples weren’t taken at the ideal moments, the data were less definitive. That’s a common, fixable problem. If you manage anaphylaxis in the ED or ICU, getting the timing of tryptase right is one of the highest-yield things you can do for downstream diagnosis and safety.

Why tryptase matters (brief)
- Tryptase is the best single lab marker we have for confirming mast cell activation during anaphylaxis.
- A properly timed acute vs baseline comparison can confirm true mast cell degranulation and identify patients who need workup for underlying mast cell disorders (eg, systemic mastocytosis).
- Miss the timing and you lose the opportunity to objectively document the event and to trigger further evaluation.

When to draw tryptase — the practical timeline
- Acute sample: as soon as feasible after the patient is recognized to be having anaphylaxis — ideally within the first hour if possible.
- Peak timing: tryptase typically peaks around 1–2 hours after symptom onset and usually falls toward baseline by 4–6 hours. Aim to capture that peak window.
- Second acute sample: if possible, get a sample at 1–2 hours after onset (or within 4 hours at latest). This is the sample most likely to show the peak.
- Baseline sample: collect at least 24 hours after complete clinical resolution (many recommend 24–72 hours). This establishes the patient’s baseline mast cell burden.
- Best practice: three samples when feasible — immediate/ED (as soon as possible), 1–2 hours post-onset (peak), and ≥24 hours post-resolution (baseline).

How to interpret — the 20% + 2 rule
- Use the change formula: a significant rise indicating mast cell activation is met when the peak tryptase ≥ (1.2 × baseline) + 2 μg/L (the “20% + 2” rule).
- Example: baseline 5 μg/L → significant peak ≥ (1.2 × 5) + 2 = 8 μg/L.
- This relative + absolute change performs better than using a fixed cutoff alone, because baseline tryptase varies between people.

Persistent elevation and when to think mastocytosis
- A persistently raised baseline (≥20 μg/L in many guidelines) should prompt referral to allergy/immunology or hematology for a mast cell disorder workup (bone marrow biopsy with tryptase genotyping, KIT D816V testing, etc).
- Even lower persistent elevations plus clinical features (recurrent anaphylaxis, atypical triggers like hymenoptera stings, hepatosplenomegaly, unexplained GI symptoms, osteoporosis) increase the index of suspicion.
- Don’t chase mastocytosis during the acute event — get baseline tryptase later and refer.

Common pitfalls and gotchas
- No acute sample = missed chance. If you don’t draw during the acute phase, you may never objectively confirm mast cell activation from that episode.
- Single elevated value without baseline: could be acute rise or pre-existing high baseline. You need the baseline to interpret it.
- Waiting too long for the “acute” sample: a sample taken >6 hours after onset may already be falling and miss the peak.
- Corticosteroids, antihistamines, or epinephrine administration do not reliably alter tryptase enough to invalidate the test — treat the patient first, draw blood when feasible.
- Lab unit confusion: tryptase commonly reported in μg/L or ng/mL — they are equivalent numerically; ensure consistent units.

How this applies to the presented case
- The team drew the first tryptase sample 1 hour after the third episode (not ideal as initial acute samples were missed) and the “baseline” was sampled 27 hours after the last recurrence. That limited conclusions: they saw elevated values and suspected mastocytosis, but the lack of an early acute sample after the first/most severe episode and missing the recommended 1–2 hour peak sample weakened the case.
- Lesson: in multiphasic or prolonged anaphylaxis, keep drawing — get at least one acute sample early, a 1–2 hour sample if possible, then a true baseline ≥24 hours after all symptoms have resolved.

Practical workflow to implement in your ED/ICU
- When you diagnose anaphylaxis, order tryptase STAT and label “acute anaphylaxis — draw as soon as possible.”
- If logistics allow, schedule/flag a repeat draw at ~1–2 hours post-onset, and arrange a baseline at ≥24 hours after resolution (or instruct PCP/allergy clinic to do it).
- Document symptom onset time clearly in the chart — interpretation depends on onset-to-draw timing.
- Send a reminder to the team: “If baseline tryptase >20 μg/L or 20%+2 positive, refer for specialist evaluation.”

Clinical pearls
- The 20% + 2 rule is simple, robust, and should be your go/no-go for confirming mast cell activation.
- Don’t let fear of delaying treatment prevent you from drawing tryptase — treat first, then draw as soon as practical.
- In adults >50 kg, aim for 0.5 mg IM epinephrine — many auto-injectors are only 0.3 mg and can lead to underdosing that may increase relapse risk.
- Persistent or unexpectedly high baseline tryptase should prompt specialist referral; skin testing or provocative testing should be deferred until evaluation is complete.

Take-home messages
- Aim to collect tryptase as early as possible in anaphylaxis, get a sample at ~1–2 hours (peak), and obtain a baseline ≥24 hours after full recovery.
- Use the 20% + 2 rule: peak ≥ (1.2 × baseline) + 2 μg/L confirms mast cell activation.
- A persistently elevated baseline (commonly ≥20 μg/L) or recurrent severe anaphylaxis should trigger evaluation for mast cell disorders.
- Build local protocols so tryptase timing becomes automatic — it’s a small effort that yields big diagnostic and safety dividends.



# Unmasking Mast Cell Disease After Severe Anaphylaxis

What grabbed my attention in the case report was not just a classic anaphylaxis — it was a multiphasic course with multiple relapses requiring repeated intramuscular epinephrine, and persistently raised tryptase afterward. That combination should make you pause and ask: is there an underlying mast cell disorder driving this storm?

This woman in her 60s developed severe anaphylaxis after a single oral dose of clarithromycin, responded to epinephrine, then relapsed multiple times in the first 12 hours. Tryptase was elevated during a later episode and remained high on follow-up. Bone marrow sampling was inconclusive but showed two WHO minor criteria — a picture suspicious for systemic mastocytosis (or another clonal mast cell disease) despite not meeting formal criteria. This is an important clinical scenario: even if patients don’t meet every diagnostic box, persistent baseline tryptase elevation + severe or recurrent anaphylaxis warrants further workup and specialist input.

Why this matters
- Clonal mast cell disorders (systemic mastocytosis, monoclonal mast cell activation syndrome) lower the threshold for mediator release and can produce unusually severe, refractory, or recurrent (bi- or multiphasic) anaphylaxis.
- These patients can suffer life-threatening reactions from triggers that normally cause milder reactions.
- Early recognition leads to targeted counseling, appropriate monitoring, prophylaxis and referral to allergy/mast cell specialists.

When to suspect an underlying mast cell disorder
- Severe anaphylaxis (shock, hypoxaemia) disproportionate to the exposure.
- Recurrent or biphasic/multiphasic anaphylaxis without obvious re-exposure.
- Persistently elevated baseline tryptase (classically >20 ng/mL raises concern; values 11–20 are a gray zone — interpret clinically).
- Typical “red flags”: unexplained flushing, hepatosplenomegaly, osteoporosis, unexplained GI symptoms, neurocognitive complaints; also urticaria pigmentosa or skin lesions.
- Anaphylaxis triggered by hymenoptera stings, NSAIDs, opioids, or idiopathic events — particularly if severe.

Immediate ED/ICU management reminders (grounded in the case)
- Epinephrine is first-line. Adult dose: 0.01 mg/kg IM (max 0.5 mg). Many adult patients >50 kg need 0.5 mg — note that common auto‑injectors are 0.3 mg and may underdose adults.
- Recurrent episodes may need repeated IM doses. Consider ICU care for close monitoring if relapsing, severe, or requiring multiple doses.
- Continuous IV epinephrine infusion can be considered when anaphylaxis is refractory or relapsing frequently, but requires ICU-level monitoring and expertise.
- Routine use of systemic corticosteroids to prevent biphasic reactions is not supported by good evidence — they’re commonly used but benefits are unproven.
- Antihistamines and H2-blockers help cutaneous and GI signs but do not replace epinephrine.

Essential follow-up tests and referrals (what to do after stabilization)
- Measure tryptase properly: ideally one sample as soon as feasible, one 1–2 hours after symptom onset (within 4 hours) to capture the peak, and one baseline at least 24–48 hours after full resolution. Compare peak to baseline: an acute rise of 20% + 2 ng/mL above baseline supports mast cell activation.
- If baseline tryptase is persistently elevated (especially >20 ng/mL) or clinical suspicion high, refer to allergy/immunology and hematology for:
  - KIT D816V mutation testing in peripheral blood (sensitive PCR assays available).
  - Bone marrow biopsy with mast cell staining and flow cytometry when indicated.
  - Workup for systemic mastocytosis using WHO criteria (major/minor).
- Consider screening labs and imaging guided by specialist: CBC, LFTs, abdominal ultrasound for organomegaly, bone density if osteopenia/fragility suspected.

Counseling and risk-reduction for patients with suspected or confirmed mast cell disease
- Prescribe and educate on intramuscular epinephrine autoinjector(s). For adults >50 kg ensure discussion about whether a 0.5 mg device is available and appropriate.
- Long-term prophylaxis: regular H1 + H2 antihistamines are commonly used to blunt daily mediator effects and reduce severity of attacks (e.g., cetirizine/levocetirizine). Leukotriene modifiers sometimes used.
- Identify and avoid triggers where possible (insect stings, certain drugs — including NSAIDs, opioids in some patients — alcohol, temperature extremes, vigorous exercise). Individual triggers vary.
- Patients should carry an allergy action plan and wear medical ID; consider venom immunotherapy if sting anaphylaxis and mast cell disease coexist (specialist-managed).
- Discuss peri-procedural precautions: premedication and careful monitoring during anesthesia or surgery.
- Psychosocial support — anxiety and depression can complicate recovery; some patients have cognitive complaints that may relate to mediator effects.

Key diagnostic nuance: you don’t need to meet full WHO criteria to be high-risk
- Persistent baseline tryptase elevation and a history of severe or recurrent anaphylaxis are clinically actionable even if formal criteria for systemic mastocytosis are not fulfilled.
- Workup and precautions should be pursued based on clinical risk, not just diagnostic labels.

Practical pearls from the case
- Always think mast cell disease when anaphylaxis is unusually severe, recurrent, or multiphasic.
- Get the tryptase sampling right — timing matters. Without proper timing you miss diagnostic clues.
- Beware of underdosing with 0.3 mg auto-injectors in larger adults; early adequate epinephrine may reduce relapses and escalation.
- Corticosteroids won’t reliably prevent biphasic relapses — they’re not a substitute for careful observation and readiness to give more epinephrine.
- If a bone marrow biopsy is non-diagnostic but clinical suspicion and labs point toward mast cell disease, involve specialists — diagnosis can be nuanced and may require repeat/advanced testing.

Take-home messages
- Severe, relapsing, or multiphasic anaphylaxis + persistent baseline tryptase elevation = trigger a mast cell disorder workup and specialist referral.
- Time your tryptase samples: acute peak (within 1–4 hrs), and baseline ≥24 hrs after symptoms fully resolve.
- Epinephrine IM (0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg) is the cornerstone; ensure adults aren’t undertreated by 0.3 mg devices when 0.5 mg is indicated.
- Don’t rely on steroids to prevent recurrence — monitor, educate, and plan for repeated epinephrine if needed.
- Even without full WHO criteria, patients with these features can be high risk — take precautions, prescribe epinephrine, and arrange specialty follow-up.

This case is a useful reminder: sometimes the anaphylaxis is the red flag that reveals an underlying mast cell disorder. Be systematic — stabilize first, then investigate thoughtfully.



# Auto-injectors Inside the Hospital: Speed vs Dose Accuracy

What makes this case interesting
A woman in her 60s had a multiphasic anaphylactic course after a single oral dose of clarithromycin. Initial treatment in the ED used a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto‑injector with rapid clinical response, but she needed repeated intramuscular epinephrine doses (two later given as 0.5 mg IM) during subsequent relapses. The team faced a familiar tension: the auto‑injector reduced treatment delay and hesitation, but it probably underdosed an adult who — by weight — would be expected to need 0.5 mg IM. This case highlights the tradeoff between speed/cognitive simplicity and dose accuracy in the hospital setting.

Why this matters
- Timely intramuscular epinephrine is the single most important intervention in anaphylaxis. Delays increase risk of progression and biphasic or multiphasic courses.
- Many auto‑injectors commonly available (and still stocked in many hospitals) deliver 0.3 mg — fine for many adults, but under the guideline dose of 0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg for adults >50 kg. Under‑dosing can lead to need for more frequent redosing and escalation of care.
- In rushed or low‑volume hospital environments (ED triage, wards without regular vasoactive medication use), auto‑injectors reduce hesitation and administration errors. But once the patient is in a monitored setting, you should transition to correctly dosed therapy.

Practical approach — when to use an auto‑injector in hospital
- Use the auto‑injector immediately when it prevents a delay. If the clinician/nurse would otherwise pause to find vial/syringe or is unfamiliar/uncomfortable drawing up epinephrine, the auto‑injector is a reasonable first step.
- In the ED triage or prehospital setting, auto‑injectors are often the fastest and safest choice.
- As soon as the patient is stabilized and moved to a monitored area (resuscitation bay, ED trolley, ICU), transition to protocolized dosing: draw up 0.5 mg IM (or 0.01 mg/kg, max 0.5 mg) and plan repeat dosing q5 min if needed.

Needle length and IM delivery — don’t assume IM just happened
- Effective IM delivery requires reaching muscle (vastus lateralis or deltoid). In patients with high BMI or large subcutaneous fat over injection site, short auto‑injector needles may deposit epinephrine subcutaneously — slower absorption and less peak effect.
- Consider patient habitus: in very obese patients, an auto‑injector with a short needle may underdeliver even if nominal dose is 0.5 mg. When feasible, draw up and use a syringe with an appropriate needle (longer IM needle) for assured IM placement in obese patients.

Transitioning to correct dosing and escalation
- IM epinephrine dosing: 0.01 mg/kg (10 µg/kg) IM, max 0.5 mg in adults. Repeat every 5 minutes as needed.
- If multiple IM doses fail or the patient is in shock/refractory anaphylaxis, escalate to monitored IV epinephrine: small boluses (eg, 50 µg = 0.05 mg) titrated to effect or a low‑dose infusion (eg, 1–4 µg/min adjusted). Make sure experienced staff and cardiac monitoring are present — IV boluses carry risk of arrhythmia, hypertension and myocardial ischemia.
- Consider continuous IV infusion earlier in multiphasic or refractory cases where repeated IM dosing is insufficient or not practical — but only in an ICU/monitored area with clear orders and infusion pump.

Protocol recommendations for hospitals (practical, low friction)
- Stock both 0.3 mg and 0.5 mg auto‑injectors as short‑term bridging tools where permitted. If 0.5 mg auto‑injectors are available, prioritize them for adults >50 kg.
- Create a clear chain‑of‑care in your anaphylaxis protocol:
  1) Immediate IM epinephrine — use auto‑injector if it avoids delay. Document dose and time.
  2) If patient is admitted/transferred to monitored area, immediately draw up weight‑appropriate IM dose (0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg) and plan repeat dosing q5 min as needed.
  3) If two or more adequately delivered IM doses fail or shock persists, escalate to IV boluses/infusion per local protocol with monitoring.
- Train staff with short simulations that include: recognizing anaphylaxis, using auto‑injectors, drawing up 0.5 mg IM, and initiating IV epinephrine infusion. Emphasize the switch from “bridge” to “definitive” therapy.
- Include needle‑length guidance in your anaphylaxis checklist (eg, recommend 25 mm or longer for IM in patients with large BMI and use vastus lateralis for delivery).

Corticosteroids and antihistamines — not substitutes
- Steroids do not reliably prevent biphasic anaphylaxis and should not delay epinephrine. Treat cutaneous symptoms with H1/H2 blockers if helpful for pruritus, but continue epinephrine-based resuscitation as needed.

Special populations and modifiers
- Mast cell disorders (suspected in this case) may lower the threshold for severe or recurrent reactions. Such patients warrant a lower threshold for prolonged monitoring and early escalation.
- Older adults and those with cardiovascular disease are at increased risk of epinephrine side effects when given IV boluses — titrate carefully.

Clinical pearls
- Speed wins early: give epinephrine immediately — auto‑injector is better than delay.
- Dose matters soon after: transition quickly to a properly dosed IM injection (0.01 mg/kg, max 0.5 mg) once in a monitored environment.
- Watch the needle: in obese patients, confirm IM delivery — otherwise absorption is unreliable.
- Have a clear escalation plan: repeated IM dosing → IV boluses → infusion in monitored setting, with explicit dosing and monitoring steps in the protocol.
- Train and stock intentionally: reduce cognitive load with clear kits: prefilled 0.5 mg syringes (or easy‑access 0.5 mg vials), appropriate needles, and IV infusion orders ready.

Take‑home messages
- Auto‑injectors inside the hospital are a powerful tool to cut delays and reduce hesitation — use them as a bridge, not the endgame for adult anaphylaxis care.
- Transition to correctly dosed IM (0.01 mg/kg, max 0.5 mg) or to monitored IV therapy promptly when the patient is in a monitored setting.
- Consider needle length and patient habitus to ensure true IM delivery.
- Embed these steps into short, actionable anaphylaxis protocols and hands‑on training so teams convert speed into effective, guideline‑consistent therapy.

This case reminds us: rapid administration saves lives, but early, clear transitions from “fast” to “accurate” therapy are what prevent repeated dosing, escalation, and prolonged ICU stays.



# Biphasic, Refractory, or Ongoing Absorption? Getting the Labels Right

What makes this case worth a read? A woman in her 60s develops classic anaphylaxis after a single oral dose of clarithromycin. She rapidly responds to epinephrine, then relapses multiple times over the next 12 hours, each episode needing further intramuscular epinephrine. Tryptase is persistently elevated and systemic mast cell disease is suspected. Is this biphasic anaphylaxis, refractory shock, repeated absorption of the offending agent, or true multiphasic disease driven by a mast cell disorder? The distinction matters — it changes how long you monitor, whether you escalate to continuous epinephrine, and whether you intervene to stop ongoing exposure or absorption.

## Why words matter: the clinical implications of labels
We often slap the “biphasic” label on any relapse after initial resolution. That’s convenient but unhelpful if we ignore mechanisms. Three different processes can look the same at the bedside:

- True biphasic anaphylaxis: an initial wave of mediator release, clinical recovery, and then a secondary spontaneous recurrence (typically within 24 hours, commonly 4–12 hours).
- Ongoing absorption / persistent exposure: continued uptake of allergen (e.g., residual food in GI tract, extended-release oral medications, transdermal exposures, ongoing IV infusion) produces recurrent or waxing symptoms until the source is removed or fully absorbed.
- Refractory or persistent anaphylaxis: inadequate or transient response to epinephrine; symptoms never fully controlled despite appropriate therapy.

Why it matters: “biphasic” implies you’re dealing with a delayed host response and mainly monitoring; ongoing absorption or persistent exposure implies an active source that you may be able to remove or neutralize (decontaminate, stop infusion). Refractory physiology may require escalation of vasoactive support (continuous IV epinephrine, ICU-level monitoring).

## Bedside clues to help you sort the three
No single sign rules them in or out. Use the history, timeline, pattern of relapses, and response to therapy.

- Timing
  - True biphasic: classically within hours of apparent resolution (most within 4–12 h; can be up to 24–72 h in some reports).
  - Ongoing absorption: relapses soon after dosing and continue until the allergen is cleared; relapses may be frequent and temporally related to oral dosing or continued exposure.
  - Refractory: failure to sustain response despite correct dosing and routes of epinephrine and supportive measures.

- Pattern and severity
  - Multiphasic/biphasic often shows decreasing intensity with each wave — but not always.
  - Ongoing absorption may have repeated similar-intensity episodes until cleared.
  - Refractory may show persistent hypotension or hypoxaemia despite therapy rather than clear waxing/waning.

- Response to interventions
  - If stopping exposure or gastrointestinal decontamination reduces recurrences, ongoing absorption was the likely driver.
  - If repeated appropriate IM epinephrine consistently terminates each episode and the interval between episodes increases, that favors biphasic or multiphasic host response.
  - If repeated epinephrine is ineffective and haemodynamics remain unstable, think refractory — consider continuous IV infusion and advanced haemodynamic support.

- Clues from history
  - Recent ingestion of long-acting or depot formulations, transdermal patches, or parenteral/infusion exposures point toward ongoing absorption.
  - Known mast cell disorder, elevated baseline tryptase, prior severe reactions — raise the pre-test probability for severe, multiphasic courses.
  - Delayed first epinephrine administration and initial severe presentation increase the risk of biphasic reactions.

In the presented case: a single oral clarithromycin dose preceded repeated episodes over 12 hours, with symptom intensity diminishing across episodes, and persistently elevated tryptase suggesting an underlying mast cell disorder — a picture compatible with multiphasic anaphylaxis in a mast cell–prone host, though ongoing absorption could not be fully excluded.

## Practical therapeutic implications and bedside actions
How you label the relapse drives immediate management choices.

- Initial response (always)
  - IM epinephrine promptly (0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg in adults; many adult patients >50 kg require 0.5 mg). Don’t let availability of 0.3 mg autoinjectors lull you into underdosing adults.
  - Airway, oxygen, IV access, fluid resuscitation, and adjunctive H1/H2 antihistamines for cutaneous symptoms.
  - Document time of symptom onset and each epinephrine dose.

- If you suspect ongoing absorption/exposure
  - Stop further exposure (hold drugs, stop infusions, remove patches).
  - Consider activated charcoal if the offending oral agent was taken recently and benefit would outweigh risk — generally within 1–2 hours for immediate-release drugs (institutional toxicology advice helpful).
  - Consult poison control / toxicology as needed for specific agents (some oral antibiotics have long half-lives but decontamination utility varies).
  - Expect monitoring until the agent is cleared; relapses may continue until then.

- If you suspect true biphasic/multiphasic response
  - Continue observation; consider ICU-level monitoring if multiple recurrences, severe initial presentation, or need for multiple epinephrine doses.
  - Routine corticosteroids are not supported by good evidence for preventing biphasic reactions — guidelines increasingly advise against routine steroid use for this purpose.
  - Arrange allergy/mast cell work-up after recovery (tryptase sampling ideally acutely and at baseline, and specialist referral if baseline elevation or recurrent severe events).

- If you suspect refractory anaphylaxis
  - Escalate: continuous IV epinephrine infusion (with ICU monitoring), vasopressors, airway protection, and advanced haemodynamic support as indicated.
  - Consider other adjuncts (eg, bronchodilators for bronchospasm, IV fluids aggressively) and specialist help early.

## Monitoring: how long is “enough”?
There’s no universal answer. Standard practice often uses 4–6 hours after symptom resolution for low-risk patients. Given variable timing of biphasic reactions, consider prolonged observation in those with:

- Severe initial presentation (hypotension, hypoxia)
- Need for multiple epinephrine doses
- Delayed initial epinephrine administration
- Suspected underlying mast cell disease or markedly elevated tryptase
- Ingested long-acting or depot formulations, or ongoing exposure

Multi-disciplinary discussion (ED, ICU, allergology/toxicology) is reasonable when courses are atypical.

## The role of tryptase and mast cell disorders
Obtain tryptase appropriately: an acute sample as soon as feasible (without delaying treatment), a second sample 1–2 hours after onset (within 4 hours), and a baseline sample >24 hours after resolution. Elevated baseline tryptase or repeated severe events should prompt specialist referral for mast cell disorders — these patients are at higher risk for severe and potentially multiphasic reactions, and they may warrant longer monitoring and lower threshold for ICU admission.

In this case, delayed tryptase sampling hampered interpretation, but persistent elevation and clinical features raised concern for systemic mast cell disease — an important reminder to get timely samples when safe.

## Bottom line — practical takeaways
- Don’t reflexively call every relapse “biphasic.” Distinguish between biphasic, ongoing absorption/exposure, and refractory physiology — because each has different management steps.
- History and timing matter: recent oral dosing of long-acting agents, transdermal patches, or ongoing infusions should trigger active search for ongoing exposure and consideration of decontamination or stopping the source.
- Give the right dose of epinephrine up front. Many adults require 0.5 mg IM; 0.3 mg autoinjectors can underdose larger adults.
- Routine corticosteroids to prevent biphasic reactions are not supported by high-quality evidence.
- Get tryptase testing appropriately and refer for mast cell evaluation when baseline tryptase is raised or the course is atypical.
- When in doubt about severity or mechanism, err on the side of longer observation or ICU monitoring and involve toxicology/allergy/critical care early.

Labels can help communication — but they should also help guide action. Think mechanism first, then name it.



# When “Antibiotic Allergy” Steers You Wrong: Avoiding Anchoring After Drug Rashes

This case is a useful reminder of how a small diagnostic shortcut—a presumed “antibiotic allergy” based on a late rash—can cascade into harm. A woman in her 60s developed a delayed rash while on clindamycin for erysipelas. Her GP switched antibiotics to clarithromycin; one hour after the first clarithromycin dose she developed full‑blown anaphylaxis with hypotension, hypoxia, generalized urticaria and facial swelling. Despite appropriate initial treatment she had multiple relapses (multiphasic anaphylaxis) in the first 12 hours, required repeated intramuscular epinephrine, an ICU stay, and later workup showed persistently raised tryptase with features suspicious for underlying mast cell disease.

Why this matters
- The index event began with a non‑specific delayed rash on clindamycin that was assumed to be an antibiotic allergy and triggered a switch to a macrolide. That assumption set the patient on a path to life‑threatening anaphylaxis to clarithromycin.
- It illustrates two frequent mistakes in real world practice: (1) anchoring on an antecedent rash as a true IgE‑mediated allergy without verification and (2) assuming “cross‑reactivity” or safety between antibiotics when the pattern of reaction and timing aren’t considered.
- The case also highlights system-level issues: variable epinephrine dosing (0.3 mg auto‑injector vs guideline‑preferred 0.5 mg for many adults), uncertainty about observation duration after initial resolution, and limited guidance for multiphasic anaphylaxis.

Practical points for frontline clinicians

1) Don’t reflexively label every rash “allergy”
- Timing and morphology matter. Immediate urticaria/angioedema/anaphylaxis → treat and label. Delayed, maculopapular rashes that appear days into therapy are most often non‑IgE mediated exanthems (T‑cell mediated) and are not absolute contraindications to related antibiotics.
- Before documenting “allergy,” ask: When did the rash start relative to the drug? Was it pruritic/urticarial vs morbilliform? Any systemic signs? Any prior exposure or reactions?
- If the antibiotic is nonurgent, defer substitution until allergy review or consider safer alternatives after risk–benefit discussion.

2) Don’t assume cross‑reactivity patterns blindly
- Clindamycin (lincosamide) and clarithromycin (macrolide) are distinct classes. A delayed rash to one does not predict IgE‑mediated anaphylaxis to the other, but unpredictable hypersensitivity can occur to any antibiotic class.
- Avoid “switching” to another antibiotic as a knee‑jerk unless guided by the likely mechanism of the index reaction and indication urgency.

3) When to involve allergy or delay treatment
- Nonurgent infections (many soft tissue infections) often tolerate a short delay for allergy consultation, patch/skin testing, or oral provocation when appropriate.
- If therapy cannot wait, choose an agent with a nonoverlapping structure and low cross‑reactivity risk, and monitor closely.
- Consider allergy referral after the event for structured testing and to remove incorrect labels from the chart.

4) Documentation: the patient safety currency
- Document the reaction with specifics: timing, description (urticaria vs maculopapular), treatments given, vitals, and whether it was life‑threatening.
- Use allergy lists, not problem lists, and include “reaction type” and “severity.” This improves future prescribing and avoids unnecessary avoidance of first‑line agents.

5) Acute management lessons highlighted by this case
- IM epinephrine remains first‑line. Dose 0.01 mg/kg (max 0.5 mg) — many adults benefit from 0.5 mg. Be aware many auto‑injectors are 0.3 mg and may underdose heavier adults.
- Recurrent (biphasic/multiphasic) anaphylaxis is uncommon but real. Observation after resolution is advised; duration should be individualized based on severity, number of epinephrine doses, comorbidities and possible mast cell disorders.
- Routine corticosteroids do not prevent biphasic reactions and are increasingly discouraged as routine therapy.
- Consider tryptase sampling when the presentation is severe, recurrent, or atypical: obtain an early sample (ideally within 1–4 hours of symptom onset when possible) and a baseline >24 hours after recovery. Elevated baseline or post‑event tryptase should prompt consideration of mast cell disorders.

6) Think about underlying predisposition
- Persistent elevated baseline tryptase and severe or recurrent reactions should raise suspicion for systemic mastocytosis or mast cell activation syndrome. These patients have higher anaphylaxis risk and require tailored secondary prevention (education, epinephrine auto‑injectors, avoidance counseling and specialist follow‑up).

Clinical pearls
- A delayed exanthem days into therapy is rarely IgE‑mediated and does not automatically justify labeling a patient “allergic” to a drug class.
- When in doubt and infection is nonurgent, pause and get help — an allergy consult or a short delay avoids dangerous substitutions.
- Document exact reaction details. “Allergic to X” without context is hazardous.
- Use the correct epinephrine dose for adults; check whether your facility’s auto‑injectors meet guideline dosing for the patient population.
- Tryptase is underused — when anaphylaxis is severe, atypical, or recurrent, send early and follow‑up levels to unmask mast cell disease.

Take‑home messages
- Avoid anchoring: don’t convert a nonspecific delayed rash into a permanent “antibiotic allergy” without proper assessment.
- If therapy can wait, get allergy input; if not, choose antibiotics thoughtfully and monitor.
- Document reaction details precisely to protect future prescribing.
- For severe or recurrent anaphylaxis: give IM epinephrine at the appropriate dose, observe for recurrence, consider tryptase testing, and refer for specialist evaluation for possible mast cell disease.
