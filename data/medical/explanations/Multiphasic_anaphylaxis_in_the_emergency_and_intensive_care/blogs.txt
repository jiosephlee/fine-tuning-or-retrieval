Title: Blog about the case report: Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting

# Stop underdosing anaphylaxis: make 0.5 mg IM epinephrine the adult default

What jumped out from this case wasn’t just that a patient relapsed into anaphylaxis multiple times — it was that the very first, time‑critical treatment given in the ED was a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto‑injector for a 60s woman weighing 104 kg. She initially improved, then had three more IM epinephrine‑responsive relapses (total four episodes), and only the later injections were 0.5 mg IM. She also had persistently raised tryptase and a high probability of systemic mastocytosis — a phenotype that predisposes to severe and recurrent mediator release. Taken together this case highlights a glaring systems problem: the gap between guideline dosing (0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg IM for adults) and the real‑world ubiquity of 0.3 mg auto‑injectors and shorter needles that may not reliably deliver true intramuscular injections in heavier adults.

A quick recap of the relevant facts from the case
- Woman in her 60s, weight 104 kg, presented with clarithromycin‑triggered anaphylaxis: hypotension (74/50), hypoxaemia, widespread urticaria and facial swelling.
- Initial treatment: 0.3 mg IM epinephrine via auto‑injector, oxygen, IV fluids, IM/IV antihistamine. Good immediate response but then relapsed multiple times in the first 12 hours.
- Later IM epinephrine doses were 0.5 mg and were required for each relapse. Steroids were added after first relapse (local practice), though evidence for steroids preventing biphasic reactions is poor.
- Baseline and episode tryptase were elevated; systemic mastocytosis was suspected but not fully confirmed.

Why dose and delivery matter
- The guideline math is simple: 0.01 mg/kg → most adults >50 kg should receive ~0.5 mg IM. Many adult patients therefore receive a suboptimal dose when only 0.3 mg devices are used.
- Underdosing can produce a transient response that appears adequate, but is more likely to require repeat dosing, escalation of care, or prolong observation. In this case the patient needed multiple subsequent IM doses before stable resolution.
- Needle length is not trivial: in patients with higher BMI the short needle on some auto‑injectors may deposit epinephrine subcutaneously rather than intramuscularly. SC injection delays absorption and reduces peak plasma levels, functionally mimicking underdosing.
- Recurrent or “multiphasic” courses are multifactorial (trigger characteristics, ongoing allergen absorption, mast cell disease, timing and adequacy of epinephrine), but inadequate initial intramuscular dosing and delivery plausibly contribute to early relapse and need for escalation.

Practical fixes you can implement today (ED / ICU / EMS / hospital supply)
- Make 0.5 mg IM the default adult dose in order sets and protocols. Use weight thresholds (eg, ≥50 kg → 0.5 mg IM).
- Stock 0.5 mg auto‑injectors in the ED, high‑risk wards, and crash carts where possible. If procurement is constrained, ensure staff know how to draw up 0.5 mg IM from ampoules and that prefilled syringes are available.
- Standardise practice: create quick reference stickers or checklists on resuscitation trolleys: “Adult anaphylaxis: 0.5 mg IM (0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg). Repeat q5–10 min PRN.”
- Train clinicians and nurses to reduce epinephrine hesitancy: short simulation drills where the default is pulling 0.5 mg IM (auto‑injector or drawn up) will increase speed and confidence.
- If only 0.3 mg auto‑injectors are available for discharge: give explicit plans. Teach patients to use two sequential auto‑injectors (use first immediately, repeat after 5–15 minutes if no or inadequate response) and to call emergency services. Consider prescribing two devices at discharge and documenting why (local policy permitting).
- Consider needle length when selecting devices for your population. For patients with high BMI, drawn‑up 0.5 mg IM with a suitably long needle may be more reliable than some auto‑injectors.
- For patients with recurrent or severe anaphylaxis (or high baseline tryptase), involve allergy/mast cell specialists early and ensure epinephrine dosing strategies are clearly documented and available.

A few clinical realities and boundaries
- We don’t claim every multiphasic reaction is caused by a 0.3 mg dose — multiphasic anaphylaxis is uncommon and multi‑factorial. But underdosing and poor IM delivery are fixable system issues that plausibly increase the chance of early relapse and escalation.
- Epinephrine timing trumps dose: give IM epinephrine quickly. But when you give it, give the appropriate adult dose.
- Corticosteroids remain commonly used in practice (this case received steroids after relapse), but robust evidence they prevent biphasic/anaphylactic relapse is lacking — don’t let steroids substitute for correct epinephrine dosing and monitoring.

Clinical pearls
- Adult guideline dose = 0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg IM. Most adults >50 kg need 0.5 mg.
- If you encounter a 0.3 mg auto‑injector in an adult >50 kg, be prepared to give an additional dose promptly if response is incomplete — and consider drawing up 0.5 mg IM if available.
- Intramuscular route matters: IM into the lateral thigh achieves faster and higher epinephrine levels than subcutaneous.
- Patients with elevated baseline tryptase or suspected mast cell disease may require higher vigilance and early specialist follow‑up.

Take‑home messages
- Make 0.5 mg IM the adult default for anaphylaxis in your institution; use weight‑based order sets to avoid underdosing.
- Stock 0.5 mg auto‑injectors or prefilled/drawn‑up 0.5 mg IM syringes on resuscitation trolleys and in ED/ward crash carts.
- Train teams to draw and give 0.5 mg IM quickly and confidently; don’t let fear of “overdosing” keep you at 0.3 mg for adults who need more.
- If you must discharge a patient with only 0.3 mg auto‑injectors available, give two, teach prompt dual use if needed, and emphasise early EMS activation.
- Remember high‑risk groups (obesity, mast cell disorders) and tailor delivery (needle length, readiness to repeat dose) accordingly.

This case should make every ED director and allergy lead ask: what do our trolleys and discharge packs actually contain? Small supply choices — 0.3 mg vs 0.5 mg, short vs adequate needle — translate into real differences in care. If we want fewer repeats, fewer escalations, and fewer patients drifting into multiphasic courses, start by making the right adult epinephrine dose the simple, default option.



# The second wave problem: observation windows and ICU triggers in biphasic/multiphasic anaphylaxis

What makes this case interesting
A woman in her 60s developed a true multiphasic anaphylactic course after a single oral dose of clarithromycin. She had an initial profound hypotensive, hypoxaemic presentation that responded to IM epinephrine, then had three further relapses within 12 hours—each requiring IM epinephrine—before finally settling. Workup afterwards showed persistently elevated tryptase and features suspicious for systemic mast cell disease, although diagnostic criteria were not fully met. This is the sort of case that keeps ED and ICU teams awake: you treated appropriately, the patient improves, and then—without re‑exposure—symptoms return. How long do you watch? When do you upgrade monitoring to telemetry/ICU? Do you give steroids, start continuous epinephrine, or just wait and watch?

Why this matters
Biphasic (one relapse after initial resolution) and multiphasic (multiple relapses) anaphylaxis are uncommon but high‑stakes. Incidence estimates vary widely, but most series cluster around 4–6% for biphasic reactions. Most relapses occur in the first 6–12 hours, but they can happen later. There’s no clean evidence to tell us who will relapse, and guideline recommendations about observation time and advanced management are inconsistent. That uncertainty translates into very real resource pressures on EDs and ICUs—and real risk to patients if we under‑observe a high‑risk case.

Clarifying the terminology
- Biphasic anaphylaxis: recurrence of anaphylactic symptoms after an initial apparent resolution, without further allergen exposure. Usually described as a single relapse but timing definitions vary.
- Multiphasic anaphylaxis: more than one relapse (multiple waves) after the initial event.
- Refractory anaphylaxis: ongoing anaphylaxis that fails to respond adequately to appropriate epinephrine dosing (not a transient response followed by recurrence).

Key timing and lab pearls
- Most recurrences appear in the first 6–12 hours; some are reported up to 24–78 hours later.
- Tryptase: if you suspect anaphylaxis, draw tryptase as soon as feasible (don’t delay treatment), then again at 1–2 hours (within 4 hours) to catch the peak, and finally a baseline sample ≥24 hours after full clinical resolution. Missing the early samples reduces diagnostic utility. The case highlights this: the team missed early sampling during the first episodes, reducing the completeness of the data.
- Epinephrine dosing: recommended IM dose is 0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg (many adults end up getting 0.5 mg). Auto‑injectors commonly available are 0.3 mg and can underdose adults >50 kg; underdosing is associated with need for additional doses and escalation.

Comparing guideline approaches (high level)
Guidelines converge on immediate management (IM epinephrine, airway/oxygen, IV fluids, antihistamines/supportive care). Where they diverge is observation duration and the role of steroids.
- Most authorities recommend some period of observation after symptom resolution—commonly 4–6 hours historically—but many biphasic reactions occur beyond that.
- The UK (Resuscitation Council/Allergy) has taken a risk‑stratified approach in recent guidance: not everyone needs prolonged observation; higher‑risk patients need longer monitoring.
- Routine corticosteroids to prevent biphasic reactions have not shown clear benefit in trials and are being de‑emphasized in contemporary guidance—yet steroids are still often used in practice (as in this case).

Pragmatic criteria—when to extend observation or consider ICU/telemetry
Using this case as a springboard and synthesizing available guidance and practical experience, consider extended observation (12–24+ hours) or ICU/telemetry admission when one or more of the following are present:

High‑risk features that favor ICU/continuous monitoring
- Severe initial anaphylaxis: hypotension requiring vasopressors or marked hypoxaemia, shock physiology, respiratory compromise requiring high‑flow oxygen or intubation.
- Need for multiple epinephrine doses during the initial period (≥2 doses given IM or need for IV epinephrine).
- Poor or transient response to epinephrine (relapses within hours).
- Known mast cell disorder or persistently elevated baseline tryptase, or clinical features suspicious for systemic mastocytosis (hepatosplenomegaly, suspicious history). These patients are prone to severe and recurrent mediator release.
- Suspected exposure to a long‑acting or depot allergen (oral antibiotics, depot injections, venom, ongoing absorption from ingested food in gut) where ongoing allergen absorption is plausible.
- Concomitant medications or comorbidities that may blunt response or worsen hemodynamics (eg, beta‑blocker use, unstable cardiac disease).
- Inadequate social circumstances or inability to access prompt medical care after discharge (unreliable follow‑up, remote location).

Moderate risk—consider extended ED observation or short inpatient monitoring
- Large‑volume angioedema without airway obstruction but with risk of progression.
- Obesity or extremes of age, significant comorbidities (cardio‑pulmonary), or delayed presentation to care (>1 hour from symptom onset).
- Initial severe cutaneous or GI symptoms with significant system involvement even if hemodynamically stable.

Low risk—may be appropriate for shorter observation (eg, 6–8 hours) then discharge with instructions
- Complete resolution after a single IM epinephrine dose, no hypotension, no airway involvement, reliable social circumstances, no risk factors above, and patient understands when/how to use an auto‑injector and to return if symptoms recur.

A practical, adaptable observation algorithm
1. Immediate care (ED/prehospital)
   - IM epinephrine 0.01 mg/kg (max 0.5 mg) into lateral thigh, repeat q5–15 min as required.
   - Airway/oxygen/IV fluids as needed. Early anesthesia/ENT input for evolving airway angioedema.
   - Draw tryptase as soon as possible (if feasible): initial sample (as soon as possible), sample at 1–2 hours (within 4 hours), and baseline ≥24 hours after symptom resolution.
   - Document timing and response to epinephrine doses.

2. Early stratification after initial response (within first 1–2 hours)
   - If patient remains unstable, poorly responsive, needs repeated epinephrine, or has severe features → ICU/telemetry admission and consider IV infusion of vasopressors/epinephrine per ICU protocols.
   - If patient stabilizes and meets no high‑risk criteria → proceed to observation pathway.

3. Observation pathway (tailor to risk)
   - Low risk: observe for minimum 6–8 hours from last symptom resolution in ED with nursing checks and ready access to epinephrine. If no recurrence, clear for discharge with antihistamine, education, and prescription/teaching for epinephrine auto‑injector; arrange allergy follow‑up.
   - Moderate risk: observe 12–24 hours on monitored ward (or ED short stay) with nursing assessment, repeat vitals, and low threshold to escalate if symptoms recur.
   - High risk (criteria above): admit to ICU/telemetry for 24–48 hours (or longer if relapses continue). In ICU, consider continuous hemodynamic monitoring, consider early involvement of allergy/immunology/hematology if mast cell disease suspected. Discuss risks/benefits of continuous IV epinephrine in refractory cases—this is a last resort and requires ICU expertise.

4. Medication considerations
   - Antihistamines and H2 blockers: symptomatic relief for cutaneous/GI symptoms; do not replace epinephrine.
   - Corticosteroids: no strong evidence to prevent biphasic reactions; consider on a case‑by‑case basis (some clinicians still give them, particularly in protracted or severe courses or if underlying asthma/COPD).
   - IV epinephrine infusion: for refractory cases or ongoing hemodynamic instability—managed in ICU with titration and invasive monitoring as indicated.

5. Discharge and follow‑up
   - Provide anaphylaxis action plan, prescribe and teach epinephrine auto‑injector use, arrange specialty follow‑up (allergy/immunology). If tryptase was elevated or features suggest mast cell disease, arrange hematology/allergy workup and avoid triggering drugs in future.

Clinical pearls from this case and practice
- Don’t be falsely reassured by a single good response. Recurrent waves may follow, sometimes with progressively milder symptoms (as in this patient) or sometimes with equal or worse severity.
- Draw tryptase early and on the recommended timeline—missing the early peak reduces ability to interpret results.
- Recognize the limitation of 0.3 mg auto‑injectors in larger adults. Where possible, follow weight‑based dosing up to 0.5 mg in adults (and know your local auto‑injector strengths).
- Consider mast cell disease when anaphylaxis is unusually severe, recurrent, or multiphasic—even with a single exposure. An elevated baseline tryptase should prompt specialist follow‑up.
- There is no one‑size‑fits‑all observation time; use risk stratification. Resource constraints matter, but patient safety comes first—err on the side of extended monitoring for high‑risk features.

Take‑home messages
- Biphasic/multiphasic anaphylaxis is uncommon but real; most relapses occur in the first 6–12 hours but can be later.
- Immediate management is IM epinephrine, airway/oxygen, fluids; draw tryptase early and on the recommended schedule.
- Prolonged observation or ICU admission should be considered for severe initial shock/hypoxaemia, multiple epinephrine doses, suspected/known mast cell disease or elevated baseline tryptase, long‑acting/ongoing allergen exposure, or unreliable follow‑up.
- Steroids do not reliably prevent biphasic reactions and should not replace close observation and appropriate escalation.
- Have a local, pragmatic algorithm that stratifies observation time and ICU triggers—adapt it to local resources and make sure staff know epinephrine dosing and where to find it.

This isn’t an evidence crystal ball—there are gaps in the literature. But practical, criterion‑based approaches like the one above let teams trade guesswork for consistent, defensible care when the “second wave” arrives.



# Steroids in anaphylaxis: time to break the habit

Why this case matters
A woman in her 60s with a clarithromycin-triggered multiphasic anaphylaxis received steroids after her first relapse but went on to have further relapses that required repeated intramuscular epinephrine. She had risk factors for severe anaphylaxis (obesity, probable systemic mastocytosis with elevated baseline tryptase) and yet the steroid doses given did not prevent further episodes. This is a useful, frustratingly common scenario: clinicians reach instinctively for corticosteroids to “prevent biphasic reactions,” but contemporary evidence and guideline thinking no longer support routine steroid use for that purpose.

What the literature and guidelines actually say
- The historical rationale: steroids were thought to blunt the late-phase inflammatory response and therefore reduce the risk of a biphasic/reactivating reaction.
- The reality from systematic reviews and cohort data: multiple studies and at least one systematic review have found no convincing benefit of corticosteroids in preventing biphasic anaphylaxis. Most modern guidelines have moved away from routine steroid use for this indication.
- Potential harms are not trivial: observational data suggest prehospital steroid use may be associated with higher rates of hospitalization/ICU admission; steroids also cause short-term harms (hyperglycemia, delirium, immune suppression) and may complicate management in patients with diabetes (like the patient in this case).
- Bottom line: routine corticosteroids should not be given solely to prevent biphasic anaphylaxis.

Why the habit persists
- Tradition and training: “that’s how we’ve always done it” is powerful in emergencies.
- The fear reflex: biphasic anaphylaxis feels scary and steroids are perceived as a cheap, low-risk “insurance” policy.
- Local protocols and checklists that have not been updated.
- Medico-legal anxiety: clinicians give anything that might help to avoid being blamed for a missed relapse.
- Practical inertia: steroids are easy to order and administer when the team is already stressed.

When steroids may still be reasonable (selective, not routine)
The evidence does not support blanket steroid use, but there are clinical situations where systemic corticosteroids are appropriate:
- Concomitant moderate–severe asthma exacerbation. Use standard asthma steroid dosing (eg, prednisone 40–60 mg PO daily or equivalent) to treat the asthma, not the anaphylaxis per se.
- Refractory shock after adequate epinephrine and fluid resuscitation, where relative adrenal insufficiency is suspected. A pragmatic approach is to give hydrocortisone IV (eg, 100–200 mg total) while you investigate/treat causes of refractory hypotension.
- Patients with known steroid-dependent inflammatory disease or adrenal insufficiency — treat their underlying condition.
- Prolonged severe urticaria or angioedema refractory to antihistamines (weigh risks/benefits).

If you do use steroids, use them for the right indication and dose them appropriately (examples commonly used in acute care):
- Asthma: prednisone 40–60 mg PO (single daily dose) or equivalent.
- Suspected adrenal insufficiency/refractory shock: hydrocortisone 100–200 mg IV (often given as 50–100 mg boluses or infusion depending on context).
- Avoid low, inconsistent doses that create a false sense of security; tailor dosing to the indication.

Practical priorities in anaphylaxis (what to actually do)
- First and always: early, adequate epinephrine. IM epinephrine 0.01 mg/kg (max 0.5 mg in adults) into the mid-anterolateral thigh. For many adults >50 kg, 0.5 mg IM is the appropriate target dose. Don’t let the availability of 0.3 mg auto-injectors make you accept underdosing in larger adults.
- Repeat doses every 5–15 minutes as needed for ongoing symptoms. Escalate to IV epinephrine infusion for refractory anaphylaxis under monitoring/ICU if needed.
- Supportive care: airway positioning, high-flow oxygen, aggressive IV fluids for hypotension, antihistamines for cutaneous symptoms, bronchodilators for wheeze.
- Monitor: observation duration should be individualized. Many biphasic reactions occur within 6–12 hours, but guidelines vary and risk-stratified approaches exist. Consider prolonged observation or ICU monitoring for severe initial presentation, multiple epinephrine doses, or suspected mast cell disorder.
- Investigate: measure tryptase appropriately if you suspect severe anaphylaxis or mast cell disease (first sample as soon as feasible, repeat at 1–2 hours after onset, and again ≥24 hours later for baseline).
- Discharge planning: prescribe and teach autoinjector use, antihistamines, removal/avoidance of trigger, and timely allergy/immunology follow-up.

Clinical pearls from the case
- Steroids were given after the first relapse but did not prevent further relapses — reinforces that steroids are not a reliable prevention strategy for biphasic/multiphasic courses.
- Auto-injectors of 0.3 mg are common and useful, but they can underdose many adults; facilities should have plans to deliver 0.5 mg IM when indicated.
- Multiphasic or refractory anaphylaxis should prompt consideration of mast cell disorders; check tryptase per recommended timing and arrange specialist follow-up rather than reflexively escalating steroid use.
- In patients with diabetes (like this patient), routine steroids risk hyperglycemia and other harms and should be avoided unless specifically indicated.

Short take-home messages
- Don’t give steroids routinely to “prevent” biphasic anaphylaxis — the evidence doesn’t support it.
- Prioritize early, adequate IM epinephrine (0.01 mg/kg, up to 0.5 mg in adults), supportive care, and appropriate monitoring.
- Reserve systemic corticosteroids for clear indications: concomitant asthma exacerbation, suspected adrenal insufficiency/refractory shock, or other steroid-dependent conditions.
- In severe, recurrent, or atypical anaphylaxis think mast cell disease and get tryptase + allergy/immunology follow-up.
- Update local protocols and training to reflect current evidence so reflex steroid use becomes the exception, not the rule.

This case is a timely reminder: habit and fear should not replace evidence. In anaphylaxis the lifesaving interventions are epinephrine, airway/oxygen, fluids and careful monitoring — not routine steroids.



## Tryptase matters: getting the right samples and spotting mast cell disease after anaphylaxis

What makes this case interesting
A woman in her 60s presented in full anaphylactic shock after a first-ever dose of clarithromycin, responded to epinephrine, then had multiple relapses in the first 12 hours—each requiring further intramuscular epinephrine. Tryptase measured during a later episode and a follow-up baseline were both raised, and further workup suggested—but did not confirm—systemic mastocytosis. This report highlights a very common and avoidable pitfall: missing or mistimed tryptase samples that make it hard to prove mast cell activation, identify an underlying mast cell disorder, and change long-term management.

Why tryptase sampling matters
- Tryptase is the best readily available lab marker of systemic mast cell activation. Proper sampling lets you:
  - Confirm that a severe reaction was truly mast-cell-mediated,
  - Apply the accepted change-from-baseline rule to diagnose clinically significant mast cell activation, and
  - Identify persistently raised baseline tryptase that should trigger a mast cell disease workup (eg, systemic mastocytosis), which has major implications for counseling, prophylaxis, and specialty referral.

Timing and sampling — the practical checklist you should use every time
Do not delay life-saving treatment to obtain blood. But once it’s safe, get the samples.

1) Acute/early sample: as soon as feasible after arrival (or at first medical contact). If you can’t get this during the initial collapse, get the sample at the earliest subsequent opportunity. Do not skip this because you think a later one will suffice.

2) Peak sample: one sample taken at 1–2 hours after symptom onset (but definitely within 4 hours). This is when tryptase usually peaks after an anaphylactic event.

3) Baseline sample: at least 24 hours after full clinical resolution (many centers take this >24–48 hours after symptoms have completely resolved, or on a follow-up clinic visit days–weeks later). This establishes the patient’s baseline tryptase and lets you apply the change-from-baseline rule.

How to collect and handle the blood
- Use a plain serum tube (red top) or serum separator tube per your lab’s protocol. Label with exact time of symptom onset and time of blood draw. Note the time relation to the anaphylactic event.
- Allow clotting, centrifuge to obtain serum, and send to the lab promptly. If local logistics are unusual, check sample stability/transport requirements with your lab.

How to interpret results — the clinically useful rule
- Don’t rely on a single number. The accepted criterion for mast cell activation is a rise in acute tryptase above baseline using the formula:
  Acute tryptase > (baseline tryptase × 1.2) + 2 ng/mL
  (often phrased as “20% + 2 ng/mL above baseline”).
- Example: baseline 10 ng/mL → threshold = 1.2×10 + 2 = 14 ng/mL. An acute sample >14 ng/mL supports mast cell activation.
- A normal acute tryptase does NOT exclude anaphylaxis. Food-triggered allergic reactions and some anaphylaxis phenotypes may have only small or no tryptase rises.
- Persistently elevated baseline tryptase (commonly ≥20 ng/mL) is a WHO minor criterion for systemic mastocytosis and should prompt referral, but modest elevations require interpretation in clinical context (renal disease, hematologic disease, hereditary alpha-tryptasemia can also raise baseline tryptase).

When to suspect and refer for mast cell disease
Consider specialty referral (allergy/immunology and hematology/mast cell center) when any of the following are present:
- Baseline tryptase persistently >20 ng/mL.
- Recurrent, unexplained, severe or refractory anaphylaxis (especially multiphasic or life-threatening hypotension).
- Anaphylaxis to Hymenoptera with severe systemic features.
- Clinical features suggestive of systemic mastocytosis: cutaneous mastocytosis, hepatosplenomegaly, unexplained cytopenias, osteoporosis/fragility fractures, or unexplained GI symptoms.
- Family history or symptoms suggestive of hereditary alpha-tryptasemia.

What the referral workup may include
- Repeat baseline tryptase (to confirm persistence).
- Peripheral blood testing for KIT D816V mutation by sensitive PCR (can be diagnostic in some cases).
- Bone marrow biopsy with morphology, immunohistochemistry and KIT mutation testing when indicated (this is what establishes WHO diagnosis).
- Imaging (eg, abdominal ultrasound or CT) if organomegaly is suspected.
- Specialist discussion about prophylactic measures (epinephrine autoinjector prescription and training, peri-procedure prophylaxis, venom immunotherapy planning, consideration of omalizumab in refractory cases, etc).

Why getting tryptase right changes management
- A confirmed mast cell disorder changes long-term recommendations: stricter precautions, lower threshold for prescribing and teaching epinephrine auto-injectors, peri-procedural and peri-vaccination planning, discussion about occupational risks, and specialist therapies for recurrent anaphylaxis (including consideration of anti-IgE or other targeted approaches).
- It affects counselling: patients with mastocytosis have higher risk for severe anaphylaxis and may need tailored follow-up and emergency plans.
- Conversely, a normal or single elevated number without baseline confirmation can lead to missed diagnoses or unnecessary anxiety and invasive testing. The right sequence of samples prevents both mistakes.

The pitfalls demonstrated in the case
- The team did obtain tryptase, but only after the third episode and failed to collect an initial acute sample during the first presentation; that complicates interpretation of which peak corresponds to the primary reaction. They did obtain a baseline sample ~27 hours after the last recurrence (acceptable), and it remained elevated—raising concern for an underlying mast cell disorder. Because full bone marrow assessment could not be completed, the patient ended up with probable but not confirmed systemic mastocytosis.
- Takeaway: early and peak samples matter. Don’t assume a later elevated tryptase alone proves mast cell activation or mastocytosis without baseline confirmation and specialist assessment.

Clinical pearls
- Do not delay epinephrine or life-saving care to draw tryptase—treat first, sample as soon as safely possible.
- Get at least two samples tied to timing: an early/acute sample and a peak at ~1–2 hours (within 4 hours), plus a baseline ≥24 hours after symptoms resolve.
- Use the “20% + 2 ng/mL” rule to detect mast cell activation.
- Persistently raised baseline tryptase (≥20 ng/mL) or recurrent severe anaphylaxis should trigger specialist referral for mast cell disease workup.
- Normal tryptase does not exclude anaphylaxis—interpret numbers in clinical context.

Key take-home messages
- Always attempt an early/acute tryptase and a peak sample (1–2 hours, within 4 hours) after a severe allergic reaction; obtain a baseline sample ≥24 hours after full recovery.
- Use the change-from-baseline formula: acute > (baseline × 1.2) + 2 ng/mL to support mast cell activation.
- Persistently elevated baseline tryptase (commonly ≥20 ng/mL) or recurrent/severe/multiphasic anaphylaxis warrants referral for mast cell disease workup (KIT testing, bone marrow evaluation).
- Don’t let missed or mistimed tryptase be the reason an underlying mast cell disorder is overlooked—getting the samples right at the bedside changes long-term care.

If you look at your next anaphylaxis patient and think “did we get tryptase?” — get the first blood after stabilisation, mark the clock, and consider a second draw at about 1–2 hours and a baseline later. It’s a small step that can change diagnosis, follow-up, and potentially lives.



# When anaphylaxis won’t quit: IM repeat vs IV epinephrine infusion

What makes this case worth your attention? A woman in her 60s (104 kg) developed multiphasic anaphylaxis after a single oral dose of clarithromycin. She needed repeated intramuscular (IM) epinephrine for four distinct anaphylactic episodes in the first 12 hours, with progressively milder symptoms — and the team faced the familiar clinical crossroads: keep giving IM epinephrine as each relapse appears, or move to a continuous IV epinephrine infusion to prevent further relapses and better control hemodynamics. They consulted allergy/critical care, considered a pre-emptive infusion, but ultimately watched, and she did not relapse further. The case is a perfect springboard to walk through practical decision-making — indications, setup, risks, and teamwork — for when repeated IMs aren’t cutting it.

Why this matters
- Multiphasic/biphasic anaphylaxis is uncommon but real; relapse can occur hours to days later. This patient’s course demonstrates how relapses can be clustered and how severity can change with each wave.
- Many adult patients receive 0.3 mg IM auto‑injectors, which may underdose adults >50 kg (the guideline dose is 0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg). Under-dosing or delayed epinephrine are associated with worse outcomes and more frequent need for additional doses.
- Guidelines are thin on what to do when patients have multiple relapses despite adequate IM dosing. That’s where critical-care thinking and local protocols must guide practice.

When to strongly consider transitioning from repeated IM epinephrine to a controlled IV epinephrine infusion
Consider an infusion (in a monitored ICU/ED setting) when one or more of the following apply:
- Recurrent anaphylaxis despite timely, appropriately dosed IM epinephrine (e.g., >2 IM doses or ongoing requirement to restore hemodynamics).
- Hemodynamic instability refractory to IM doses (persistent hypotension, ongoing shock) or frequent rapid relapses that jeopardize perfusion or airway.
- Rapidly escalating frequency or severity of relapses (less time between episodes or worsening hypoxia/hypotension).
- Known or suspected mast cell disorder (e.g., elevated baseline tryptase, clinical features suggesting systemic mastocytosis), or history of severe or refractory anaphylaxis.
- Beta‑blocker use causing poor response to epinephrine (anticipate need for glucagon).
- Logistical limitations for safely delivering repeated IMs (e.g., repeated doses are delaying escalation, staffing constraints, transport to ICU imminent).

When an expectant approach (watchful waiting with repeated IM as needed) may be reasonable
- Patient stabilizes after IM epinephrine, remains hemodynamically stable and oxygenating well, and relapses are rare, mild, and decreasing in intensity (as in this case).
- Only one or two small recurrences that respond promptly to IM epinephrine.
- Reliable monitoring and rapid access to resuscitation and ICU-level care are available, and the team is comfortable with frequent reassessment.
- The patient is not on beta‑blockers and has no strong indicators of underlying mast cell disorder.

Practical checklist — what you need before starting an IV epinephrine infusion
Resuscitation readiness
- Place patient in monitored setting (ICU or high-dependency area). Continuous ECG, continuous non-invasive or invasive BP monitoring (arterial line if unstable).
- Airway equipment and experienced personnel immediately available — these patients can deteriorate quickly.
- Defibrillator and sedation/intubation drugs at bedside.
- Two large-bore IVs (or central access if available and expected duration will be long), fluids ready (crystalloid boluses), and clear escalation plan.

Infusion preparation (use local pharmacy/ICU protocol)
- Use your local protocol — concentrations and starting rates vary by institution. A commonly used prep is 1 mg epinephrine in 100 mL normal saline (10 mcg/mL), but some centers prefer different dilutions (1 mg/250 mL etc.). Confirm the standard at your center.
- Typical starting strategy (examples used in practice; always titrate to effect and follow local guidance):
  - For ongoing hypotension/refractory anaphylaxis, a small IV bolus (e.g., 50–100 mcg = 0.05–0.1 mg) can be given slowly with careful monitoring (not a rapid push), then start infusion.
  - Infusion starting rates commonly between 5–10 mcg/min and titrated up until perfusion and symptoms respond. Some centers titrate to effect in mcg/min; others use mcg/kg/min — know which your unit uses.
- Document concentration, pump settings, and titration plan clearly on the chart. Set alarm limits and ensure frequent bedside nursing observation.

Monitoring and titration
- Titrate to effect: target systolic BP, mental status, resolution of airway compromise and hypoxia. Reduce infusion as symptoms resolve.
- Watch for tachyarrhythmias, myocardial ischemia, hypertension, and metabolic disturbances. Continuous ECG and frequent neurovascular checks.
- If hypotension persists despite escalating infusion, consider alternate/additional vasopressors per ICU protocol (norepinephrine), but remember epinephrine also addresses bronchospasm and mast cell mediator effects.

Adjuncts — fluids, oxygen, antihistamines, steroids, and special scenarios
- Fluids: Give generous crystalloid boluses for distributive component of anaphylaxis; many patients are preload responsive.
- Oxygen and bronchodilators as indicated.
- H1/H2 antihistamines relieve cutaneous symptoms but don’t treat airway or shock — useful adjuncts once the patient is stabilized.
- Corticosteroids: traditionally used to try to prevent biphasic reactions, but current evidence does not support routine steroid use to prevent biphasic anaphylaxis; avoid assuming steroids will prevent future relapses.
- Beta‑blocker–treated patients: anticipate reduced response to epinephrine. Give IV glucagon (usual emergency recommendation: 1–5 mg IV bolus, repeat as needed; consider infusion thereafter — check local protocol) if poor response to epinephrine. Consult critical care/pharmacy for dosing.
- Recurrent relapses despite infusion: discuss additional immunologic/hematologic evaluation (mast cell disease) and contact allergy/immunology.

Risks of IV infusion — why not just start it for everyone?
- IV epinephrine infusion offers precise titration but carries risks: arrhythmias, excessive hypertension, myocardial ischemia, ischemic complications in elderly or those with coronary disease, and iatrogenic over-treatment. It mandates ICU-level monitoring and experienced staff.
- For patients who are stabilizing with IM epinephrine and whose relapses are decreasing and manageable, the risks and resource use of an infusion may outweigh benefits — shared decision-making and team input are key.

Using the case to illustrate the decision pathway
- In this patient: four episodes within 12 hours; first two treated with 0.3 mg auto‑injector (likely underdosed for 104 kg), followed by two 0.5 mg IM doses. Symptoms decreased in intensity with each episode. Allergy/critical care were consulted and considered an infusion vs expectant approach. Given decreasing severity and eventual stability, the team observed and the patient required no infusion.
- Factors that argued for infusion: multiple relapses, initial severe hypoxemia/hypotension, and later discovery of elevated tryptase and likely mast cell disorder — all increase the threshold to consider infusion sooner.
- Factors that argued for expectant management: clear response to each IM epinephrine dose, decreasing severity, and availability of monitoring and ICU back-up.

Teamwork and shared decision-making
- Bring allergy/immunology and critical care into the discussion early when faced with recurrent or refractory anaphylaxis. They add context (e.g., mast cell disorders), support diagnostic work-up (tryptase timing—remember samples during the first 1–4 hours and baseline >24 hours after resolution), and help plan post-discharge strategies (auto‑injector, avoidance plans).
- Discuss risks and benefits with the patient/family where feasible: need for ICU monitoring, potential side effects, and rationale for infusion vs observation.

Clinical pearls
- Dose matters: recommended IM epinephrine is 0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg (many adult autoinjectors are only 0.3 mg — underdosing is common).
- Consider IV infusion when relapses are frequent, hemodynamics are unstable despite IM epinephrine, or when underlying mast cell disease or beta‑blocker therapy is present.
- IV epinephrine infusions must be run in monitored settings with resuscitation readiness — don’t start one in a general ward without appropriate staffing and monitoring.
- Steroids are NOT proven to prevent biphasic anaphylaxis; don’t rely on them to prevent relapses.
- Tryptase timing: try to obtain an acute sample within 1–4 hours of symptom onset and a baseline sample ≥24 hours after full resolution.

Take-home bullet points
- Recurrent anaphylaxis despite appropriate IM epinephrine is an indication to escalate: consider IV infusion in an ICU-level monitored environment.
- Before starting an infusion: ensure continuous cardiac/BP monitoring, airway/resuscitation readiness, IV access, clear titration plan, and pharmacy/ICU protocol for concentration and starting rate.
- Titrate the infusion to clinical effect and watch for arrhythmias and hypertensive complications; always consult critical care and allergy.
- Weigh risks vs benefits: if relapses are mild and decreasing and the patient is stable with rapid response to IM epinephrine, careful observation may be reasonable.
- Document and follow up: teach epinephrine autoinjector use on discharge, arrange allergy/immunology follow-up, and obtain appropriately timed tryptase samples if anaphylaxis is severe or atypical.

Bottom line: patients like the one in this report sit in the gray zone between routine anaphylaxis care and critical‑care escalation. Repeated IM epinephrine is life‑saving and appropriate — but repeated, refractory, or rapidly recurrent episodes are the signals to move to a controlled IV infusion with ICU monitoring. Know your local infusion protocols, have your resuscitation kit and team ready, involve allergy/critical care early, and treat the patient in front of you — not the algorithm alone.



# Clarithromycin anaphylaxis in a beta‑lactam–allergic patient: navigating the antibiotic allergy maze

This case is a wake‑up call for anyone who prescribes antibiotics: a woman in her 60s with a childhood “amoxicillin allergy” and a recent non‑specific rash on clindamycin received a single dose of clarithromycin and developed true, severe, multiphasic anaphylaxis requiring repeated intramuscular epinephrine, ICU monitoring and later allergy work‑up showing persistently raised tryptase (probable, but not diagnostic, systemic mastocytosis). It highlights three common and dangerous problems we face every day in emergency and outpatient care: misattributed antibiotic rashes, shaky assumptions about cross‑reactivity, and the downstream harms of an unchallenged “penicillin allergy” label.

## Why this case matters
- Macrolide anaphylaxis is uncommon, but real — and when it happens it can be severe and multiphasic.
- Many patients labelled “penicillin allergic” are not truly allergic; that label drives alternate prescribing (clindamycin, macrolides, doxycycline, TMP‑SMX, vancomycin) that may be less effective, more toxic, more resistance‑prone, or — as here — itself risky.
- This patient’s immediate switch from clindamycin (delayed rash) to clarithromycin led to a life‑threatening reaction after a single dose. The prior “amoxicillin” reaction (teenage facial swelling without airway compromise) might have been low‑risk or misdocumented — but it changed prescribing choices.

If you see patients with antibiotic allergies, this case should make you stop and think before reflexively avoiding beta‑lactams.

## How to think about antibiotic allergy when treating SSTI (or similar infections)
1. Start with the allergy history, not the label. A focused allergy history will usually tell you whether a reaction was:
   - Immediate IgE‑mediated (urticaria, angioedema, bronchospasm, hypotension within minutes–hours) — higher risk for true type I allergy.
   - Delayed cutaneous/nonimmune (mild maculopapular rash days later) — often not IgE mediated and much lower risk for severe immediate reactions.
   - Unknown/uncertain (“my mom says I’m allergic,” vague childhood story) — very common and calls for de‑labelling pathways when possible.

2. Risk‑stratify for penicillin use. Many low‑risk histories can be evaluated with direct oral amoxicillin challenge or skin testing followed by challenge in allergy clinics. Removing an incorrect penicillin label restores access to preferred beta‑lactam therapy for many infections.

3. Don’t assume cross‑reactivity across classes. Beta‑lactam allergy does not mean macrolide allergy. Conversely, macrolide allergy does not imply beta‑lactam allergy. Cross‑reactivity myths persist (eg, penicillin ↔ macrolide) but are unsupported: macrolides (erythromycin family, clarithromycin, azithromycin) have different structures and mechanisms. Treat each reaction as class‑specific unless there is clear evidence of multisystem reactions to multiple classes.

4. Choose alternatives carefully and be mindful of prior reactions:
   - Clindamycin commonly used for skin/soft tissue infections; it can cause delayed rashes and C. difficile risk and may not be optimal for all patients.
   - Doxycycline, TMP‑SMX, or doxycycline + TMP‑SMX are alternatives in certain regions depending on MRSA prevalence and susceptibility; linezolid or vancomycin for severe MRSA. Local antibiograms and infection severity guide choice.
   - If the patient has a clear immediate beta‑lactam allergy, use non‑beta‑lactam alternatives — but if the beta‑lactam label is unverified or low‑risk, refer for de‑labelling so that beta‑lactams can be used safely.

In short: ask the right questions, avoid knee‑jerk substitutions, and use allergy testing/de‑labelling whenever feasible.

## Practical counseling after a severe macrolide anaphylaxis (what to do for this patient)
This patient had true, severe anaphylaxis to clarithromycin and should receive clear, practical instructions:

- Strict avoidance of the implicated macrolide class (clarithromycin and likely other macrolides until directed otherwise by an allergist). Document the reaction on the chart with date, agent, and clinical features (hypotension, hypoxaemia, airway symptoms, need for epinephrine).
- Provide an anaphylaxis action plan and prescribe an epinephrine auto‑injector. Teach how and when to inject. Note: adult epinephrine dosing is 0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg IM; many auto‑injectors are 0.3 mg and are commonly used, but for larger adults 0.5 mg is the guideline ceiling dose. In this case the team used 0.3 mg auto‑injectors initially then 0.5 mg IM for later doses. Ensure patients and primary care know the device dose and when to seek emergency care after use.
- Medical alert identification and clear discharge instructions (written and verbal). Ensure allergy information is entered into the primary care record, pharmacy records, and any national/electronic allergy registries where available.
- Arrange urgent outpatient allergy/immunology follow‑up. In this patient, specialty work‑up revealed elevated tryptase and suspicion of systemic mastocytosis — an important modifier because mast cell disorders increase risk of severe anaphylaxis and influence vaccine/medication counseling and perioperative precautions.
- Avoid routine provocation testing to confirm macrolide allergy in the early post‑anaphylaxis period — the risk of causing another severe reaction is high. Let the allergist decide on timing and testing strategy.

## Practical alternatives for future infections in this patient
We must individualize choices, but high‑level points:

- Until a specialist revises the allergy list, avoid macrolides.
- Because the patient’s prior “amoxicillin” story was childhood facial swelling (not airway compromise) and penicillin label may be incorrect, arrange de‑labelling pathways. If validated negative by skin testing or oral challenge, beta‑lactams (eg, cephalexin, amoxicillin) are often the preferred agents for many SSTIs.
- If penicillin allergy persists (confirmed immediate IgE‑mediated), select non‑beta‑lactam agents based on infection severity and local susceptibility: doxycycline or TMP‑SMX (for many purulent SSTIs/MRSA coverage), clindamycin (if not previously causing reaction), or IV vancomycin/linezolid for severe infections. In this patient clindamycin had caused a delayed rash 5 days earlier, so avoid clindamycin unless allergist clears it.
- Always weigh antibiotic spectrum, local resistance, patient comorbidities, and risk of adverse effects (eg, C. difficile with clindamycin, photosensitivity with doxycycline).

## Special considerations: mast cell disorders and anaphylaxis risk
This patient had elevated baseline tryptase and episodes consistent with severe anaphylaxis. Systemic mastocytosis (or other mast cell activation disorders) can predispose to severe, recurrent, and multiphasic anaphylaxis. Practical implications:
- Refer to hematology/allergy; they may pursue bone marrow biopsy and further testing.
- Patients with suspected mast cell disease need careful counseling about high‑risk exposures (venom, certain drugs) and may need tighter perioperative and anaphylaxis precautions.
- Higher baseline tryptase supports the need for epinephrine availability and longer observation after anaphylaxis.

## Management and observation after severe anaphylaxis — the real world
- This patient developed multiple relapses within 12 hours and needed ICU monitoring and repeated epinephrine. Biphasic and multiphasic reactions are uncommon but real; observation duration should be individualized based on initial severity, need for multiple epinephrine doses, comorbidities (eg, mast cell disorder), and the ability to access emergency care.
- Routine steroids are commonly given but evidence they prevent biphasic reactions is poor; current guidance discourages routine steroid use solely to prevent biphasic anaphylaxis. Still, practice varies and clinicians may use steroids case‑by‑case.
- Document the events clearly and ensure discharge planning addresses education, epinephrine availability, and allergy follow‑up.

## FOAMy clinical pearls
- “Penicillin allergy” is a label, not destiny. Most people labelled allergic can be safely de‑labelled after targeted evaluation.
- Don’t assume cross‑reactivity between antibiotic classes; clarithromycin reactions do not imply penicillin allergy and vice versa.
- Severe reactions (hypotension, hypoxaemia, airway compromise, need for epinephrine) require documentation, avoidance of the offending agent class, epinephrine auto‑injector, and specialty follow‑up — and often longer observation.
- For adults, epinephrine dosing is 0.01 mg/kg IM up to 0.5 mg. Many auto‑injectors are 0.3 mg; know your patient’s size and whether 0.5 mg is indicated.
- Consider mast cell disorders in patients with severe or recurrent anaphylaxis and raised baseline tryptase.

## Key take‑home messages
- Always take a focused allergy history before changing antibiotics; many “allergies” are low‑risk and removable.
- Macrolide anaphylaxis can be life‑threatening; after a severe macrolide reaction: document clearly, prescribe and teach epinephrine auto‑injector use, and refer urgently to allergy/immunology.
- De‑labelling penicillin allergy when safe restores access to preferred beta‑lactam therapy and reduces unnecessary use of alternative antibiotics that may be less effective or more harmful.
- Individualize observation and management after anaphylaxis; consider longer observation and specialist input for severe, multiphasic, or recurrent reactions and for patients with elevated tryptase or suspected mast cell disease.

If you’re a prescriber: before you reflexively reach for a macrolide or clindamycin because of a “penicillin allergy,” pause, ask the right questions, and consider whether an urgent pathway to de‑label that allergy would yield safer, more effective care.
