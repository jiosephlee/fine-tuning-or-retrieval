Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

## Clinical Presentation and Chronology

The patient, a female in her 60s, presented with a medical history characteristic of metabolic syndrome, including obesity, hypertension, and biopsy-proven non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). These comorbidities provide the clinical context for her treatment; due to persistent obesity and fatty liver, she was prescribed semaglutide, a glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist. Understanding these baseline conditions is essential, as metabolic disorders often necessitate medications that can potentially interact with or trigger systemic inflammatory responses.

The clinical course was marked by two distinct phases: an immediate acute reaction and a subsequent subacute progression of muscle weakness. The acute phase began within hours of the patient receiving her first and only dose of semaglutide (0.25 mg). She experienced severe gastrointestinal distress, including nausea and vomiting, which progressed to a systemic crisis requiring hospitalization. During this initial five-day admission, she developed acute renal failure, severe metabolic acidosis, and dyspnea, necessitating non-invasive ventilation in the intensive care unit. While her creatine kinase (CK) was mildly elevated at this time (337 U/L), she lacked the hallmark symptoms of acute rhabdomyolysis, such as severe muscle pain or myoglobinuria (cola-colored urine).

Following discharge from the acute episode, the patient entered a subacute phase characterized by a progressive decline in functional status over the next four months. This phase was defined by the insidious onset of symmetric proximal muscle weakness, meaning she struggled with movements requiring the large muscles of the hips and shoulders. As the condition advanced, she developed bulbar symptoms, specifically dysphagia (difficulty swallowing) and dysphonia (difficulty speaking), indicating that the myopathy was affecting the muscles of the upper aerodigestive tract. By the end of this four-month window, her weakness had progressed to the point where she could no longer sit or stand upright without assistance.

The temporal relationship between the administration of the GLP-1 receptor agonist and the onset of myopathy is a critical diagnostic detail. Unlike typical drug-induced toxic myopathies or rhabdomyolysis, where CK levels peak rapidly and improve upon drug cessation, this patient experienced a delayed and escalating course. Her CK levels rose from a mild elevation during the acute reaction to a peak of 3,992 U/L four months later—a 12-fold increase. This chronology—initial drug exposure followed by a progressive, inflammatory-style muscle breakdown—suggests that the semaglutide may have acted as a trigger for an immune-mediated process rather than causing direct muscle toxicity.

## Pathophysiology of Immune-Mediated Necrotising Myopathy (IMNM)

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is a distinct subtype of idiopathic inflammatory myopathy characterized by aggressive, symmetric proximal muscle weakness and a specific histological pattern. Unlike other inflammatory myopathies, such as dermatomyositis or polymyositis, which feature prominent lymphocytic infiltration of the endomysium or perimysium, IMNM is defined by the presence of widespread necrotising myofibres with a notable paucity of inflammatory cell infiltrates. On biopsy, this manifests as dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibres, often accompanied by myophagocytosis—where macrophages, identified by markers such as CD68, clear the debris of dead muscle cells. The presence of a membrane attack complex (MAC) on the myofibres further indicates an immune-mediated process, suggesting that the muscle damage is driven by the complement system rather than a direct toxic insult.

The diagnosis of IMNM is often guided by the identification of specific autoantibodies, most notably anti-3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP) antibodies. Anti-HMGCR antibodies are strongly associated with statin use, as these medications inhibit the HMGCR enzyme, potentially triggering an autoimmune response in susceptible individuals. However, a significant challenge in clinical practice is that approximately one-third of patients with IMNM are seronegative, meaning they lack these hallmark antibodies. In such cases, the diagnosis relies heavily on the clinical presentation of progressive weakness, markedly elevated creatine kinase (CK) levels, and characteristic biopsy findings. The existence of seronegative IMNM suggests that other, as-yet unidentified autoantibodies or triggers may be driving the disease, as seen in this case where semaglutide is proposed as a potential contributing factor.

It is critical to distinguish IMNM from toxic rhabdomyolysis, as the two conditions require fundamentally different management strategies. Toxic rhabdomyolysis is a direct result of muscle injury (e.g., from a drug's direct toxic effect or severe trauma) and typically follows a rapid kinetic pattern: CK levels spike acutely and then normalize within days or weeks after the offending agent is removed and the patient is hydrated. In contrast, IMNM is an autoimmune process; the trigger (such as a medication) may initiate the immune response, but the subsequent muscle destruction persists or even progresses after the trigger is gone. 

In this patient, the diagnostic reasoning centered on this distinction. While the patient experienced an acute reaction to semaglutide, her CK levels did not normalize upon drug cessation. Instead, she suffered a 12-fold increase in CK over four months and progressive functional decline. This "smouldering" and progressive course is the hallmark of an autoimmune process rather than direct toxicity. Consequently, while toxic rhabdomyolysis is managed with supportive care, IMNM requires immunosuppression—such as high-dose corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG)—to arrest the immune-mediated attack on the myofibres and allow for muscle regeneration.

## Diagnostic Work-up and Evidence Synthesis

The diagnostic approach to this patient required a synthesis of biochemical markers, advanced imaging, and histological analysis to differentiate between toxic myopathy, rhabdomyolysis, and immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). The primary biochemical indicator was the marked elevation of creatine kinase (CK), which peaked at 3,992 U/L—a 12-fold increase from the initial measurement taken shortly after the semaglutide dose. While an acute, massive spike in CK often suggests rhabdomyolysis (direct muscle toxicity), the progressive nature of this patient's weakness over four months, despite the drug being discontinued, pointed toward a sustained autoimmune process. Furthermore, while aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were elevated, these markers are present in both hepatic and muscle tissue. In this case, the patient's history of biopsy-proven non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and the persistence of transaminase elevation after CK normalization confirmed that the liver, rather than muscle breakdown, was the primary source of these enzymes.

Imaging played a critical role in localizing the pathology and screening for systemic involvement. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the bilateral femurs revealed symmetric muscular edema, particularly in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps. In the context of proximal weakness, these findings are highly suggestive of an inflammatory myopathy and served as a guide for the biopsy site. Because autoimmune myopathies can affect the myocardium and lungs, the team performed a cardiac MRI and high-resolution CT (HRCT) of the chest. The HRCT was negative for interstitial lung disease, helping to rule out antisynthetase syndrome. The cardiac MRI, however, showed mild myocardial edema and hypokinesis, suggesting that the immune-mediated process may have extended to the heart, manifesting as mild myocarditis or a stress-induced cardiomyopathy.

The definitive diagnosis was established through a muscle biopsy of the right vastus lateralis, which is the gold standard for identifying necrotising myopathies. The histological hallmark was the presence of dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers. Unlike other inflammatory myopathies (such as polymyositis), IMNM is characterized by a relative paucity of endomysial lymphocytic infiltrates. Instead, the biopsy showed prominent myophagocytosis, evidenced by immunohistochemistry staining for CD68, a macrophage marker. The discovery of the membrane attack complex (MAC) on both necrotising and non-necrotising myofibres provided critical evidence of a complement-mediated inflammatory response, confirming that the muscle destruction was immune-mediated rather than purely toxic.

Finally, the diagnostic synthesis was completed by ruling out known autoantibodies. The patient was seronegative for the classic IMNM markers, anti-signal recognition particle (SRP) and anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR), as well as antibodies associated with other connective tissue diseases. The absence of these markers classifies this as seronegative IMNM. The temporal relationship between the single dose of semaglutide and the onset of symptoms, combined with the rapid response to corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), allowed the clinicians to conclude that the semaglutide likely acted as a trigger for this immune-mediated reaction.

## Differential Diagnosis and Causality Reasoning

When evaluating a patient with progressive muscle weakness and elevated creatine kinase (CK) following medication initiation, the clinician must distinguish between direct drug toxicity (rhabdomyolysis), an idiopathic autoimmune process, and a drug-induced immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). In this case, the diagnostic reasoning centered on the temporal evolution of symptoms and the biological source of laboratory abnormalities.

A key challenge in this patient's work-up was interpreting the elevated aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT). Because these enzymes are present in both skeletal muscle and hepatic tissue, they often rise in tandem with CK during myopathic episodes. However, this patient had a documented history of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), confirmed by biopsy three years prior to the onset of myopathy. The fact that her transaminases remained borderline elevated even after her CK levels normalized suggests that the hepatic steatosis, rather than muscle breakdown, was the primary driver of her liver enzyme elevations. This distinction is critical to avoid misattributing hepatic dysfunction to muscle necrosis or vice versa.

The patient's serological profile presented further diagnostic nuances. She tested positive for the SS-B autoantibody but was negative for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA) and SS-A. While SS-A and SS-B are hallmarks of Sjögren’s syndrome, isolated SS-B positivity in the absence of ANA and SS-A is uncommon and not a recognized trigger for IMNM. Given the lack of other connective tissue disease features, this finding was likely an incidental or non-specific marker rather than the cause of the myopathy. Furthermore, the patient was seronegative for the classic IMNM-associated antibodies, anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP. This underscores a significant diagnostic challenge in IMNM: approximately one-third of patients are seronegative, requiring clinicians to rely on muscle biopsy and clinical response to treatment for a definitive diagnosis.

To establish a link between semaglutide and the myopathy, the clinical team compared this presentation with other GLP-1 receptor agonist cases, such as reports of tirzepatide-induced rhabdomyolysis. In typical drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, there is an acute, massive spike in CK that resolves rapidly upon drug discontinuation and aggressive hydration. In contrast, this patient experienced a more protracted course: a progressive 12-fold increase in CK over four months and a failure to improve without the introduction of corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). This pattern—sustained muscle necrosis and the requirement for immunosuppression—is characteristic of an immune-mediated process rather than direct toxic necrosis.

To formalize the relationship between the drug and the adverse event, the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories were applied. The causality was determined to be "probable" based on three main criteria: a reasonable temporal relationship between the single dose of semaglutide and the onset of decline, the absence of other plausible triggers (such as statins or other supplements), and a clinically reasonable response to the withdrawal of the agent and subsequent treatment. While a "certain" causality would typically require a positive rechallenge, such a step was deemed unethical given the severity of the patient's reaction. This systematic approach allows clinicians to move from a suspected association to a probable causal link, highlighting the importance of pharmacovigilance in the use of increasingly common metabolic medications.

## Management, Outcome, and Clinical Significance

The management of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) requires a combination of rapidly reducing inflammation and suppressing the aberrant immune response. In this case, the patient presented with severe features, including dysphagia, dysphonia, and profound proximal muscle weakness, necessitating an aggressive induction therapy. She was treated with "pulse" therapy consisting of 1,000 mg of intravenous methylprednisolone daily for five days. This was followed by a maintenance dose of oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg daily. To address the severity of her muscle weakness and the aggressive nature of the necrotising process, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at a total dose of 2 g/kg, split over four days.

Treatment response was monitored through both biochemical markers and clinical functional assessments. A marked improvement in serum creatine kinase (CK) levels was observed within two weeks of initiating therapy, signaling a reduction in active myofiber necrosis. Clinically, the patient demonstrated a steady recovery of muscle strength and a resolution of her swallowing and speaking difficulties. Because the patient responded robustly to the initial course of immunosuppression and the offending agent was removed, she was able to be weaned off prednisone rapidly over a two-month period. By the ten-month follow-up, she had regained her baseline functional status and had remained off all immunosuppressive medications for seven to eight months, with persistently normal CK levels.

A critical component of the patient's recovery was the total avoidance of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. The clinical course highlights a key diagnostic and management distinction between direct drug-induced toxicity (rhabdomyolysis) and drug-induced autoimmunity (IMNM). In cases of toxic rhabdomyolysis, such as those previously reported with tirzepatide, CK levels typically normalize rapidly upon drug withdrawal and hydration. In contrast, this patient’s CK levels rose exponentially over four months *after* the drug was stopped, and her recovery required targeted immunosuppression. This suggests that semaglutide may have acted as a trigger for an autoimmune process rather than causing direct muscle toxicity.

This case provides an important lesson in pharmacovigilance, particularly as GLP-1 receptor agonists become ubiquitous in the treatment of obesity and type 2 diabetes. While gastrointestinal side effects are common, clinicians must remain alert to rare but severe systemic immune reactions. The identification of a "probable" causal link—based on the temporal relationship and the response to immunosuppression—underscores the necessity of reporting such adverse events. For the medical provider, this case emphasizes that when a patient presents with progressive proximal weakness and elevated CK following the initiation of a new medication, the differential must expand beyond simple toxicity to include immune-mediated processes that may require systemic steroid and immunoglobulin therapy.
