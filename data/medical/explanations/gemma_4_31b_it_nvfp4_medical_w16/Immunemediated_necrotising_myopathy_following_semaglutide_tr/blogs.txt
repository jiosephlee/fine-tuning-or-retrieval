Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# Beyond the Nausea: GLP-1 Agonists and a Rare Muscle Mystery

We all know the "GLP-1 glow"—or, more accurately, the GLP-1 nausea. With the meteoric rise of semaglutide and tirzepatide for obesity and diabetes, we've become well-acquainted with the gastrointestinal fallout: vomiting, delayed gastric emptying, and the occasional bout of pancreatitis. But what happens when a "weight-loss wonder drug" triggers a systemic immune attack on the skeletal muscles?

Enter a case that should make every clinician pause: a woman in her 60s who developed profound, progressive muscle weakness and dysphagia after receiving just *one* single dose of semaglutide.

## The Case: A Slow-Burn Decline

The story starts with a common clinical scenario: a patient with obesity and NAFLD is started on semaglutide (0.25 mg). The immediate reaction was classic—severe nausea and vomiting leading to hypovolemic acute renal failure and metabolic acidosis. She spent a few days in the ICU on non-invasive ventilation, recovered from the acute crisis, and went home. At discharge, her CK was only mildly elevated at 337 U/L.

However, the real mystery began in the following months. Instead of returning to baseline, the patient began a steady decline. Over four months, she developed progressive proximal muscle weakness, dysphonia, and dysphagia. By the time she returned to the hospital, she couldn't sit or stand without help and was struggling to swallow. Her CK had skyrocketed to 3,992 U/L—a twelve-fold increase from her initial admission.

## Toxic vs. Immune-Mediated: The Crucial Distinction

When we see high CK levels and muscle weakness after a drug, our brains often jump to "toxic myopathy" or rhabdomyolysis. But there is a critical difference in the clinical trajectory.

In drug-induced rhabdomyolysis (as seen in some reports with tirzepatide), we typically see a massive, acute spike in CK that resolves rapidly with drug cessation and aggressive hydration. In this case, the patient's symptoms *progressed* while the drug was already out of her system. This "slow burn" suggests the drug didn't just damage the muscle directly; it may have triggered an immune response against it.

The diagnosis was Immune-Mediated Necrotising Myopathy (IMNM). A muscle biopsy confirmed the suspicion: dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers with a heavy presence of macrophages (CD68+ staining) and the membrane attack complex, signaling an active inflammatory attack.

## The Diagnostic Challenge

What makes this case even more interesting is that the patient was seronegative. She lacked the hallmark autoantibodies usually associated with IMNM, such as anti-HMGCR (often linked to statins) and anti-SRP. This reminds us that a significant portion of IMNM patients are seronegative, and the absence of a known antibody does not rule out a necrotising myopathy.

While she had a mild elevation in transaminases, the team correctly attributed this to her known NAFLD rather than muscle breakdown, as the liver enzymes remained stable even after the CK normalized.

## The Road to Recovery

The treatment was aggressive and targeted: pulse-dose methylprednisolone followed by oral prednisone and a course of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). The response was striking. Her CK levels normalized, and her functional status returned to baseline. Notably, because this was likely a drug-triggered immune event, she was able to be weaned off immunosuppression rapidly once the offending agent (semaglutide) was permanently removed.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **The Temporal Trap:** Not all drug-induced muscle injuries happen instantly. An immune-mediated response can develop or peak weeks or months after the initial exposure.
*   **Rhabdo vs. IMNM:** If the CK continues to rise or the weakness persists/progresses *after* the drug is stopped and the patient is hydrated, stop thinking "toxic" and start thinking "immune-mediated."
*   **Seronegative doesn't mean "Negative":** A negative myositis panel (SRP, HMGCR, etc.) does not exclude IMNM. When the clinical suspicion is high, biopsy remains the gold standard.
*   **Dysphagia as a Red Flag:** In the context of proximal muscle weakness, the development of swallowing difficulties (dysphagia/dysphonia) suggests a severe myopathy that requires urgent intervention to protect the airway and nutrition.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Heighten Pharmacovigilance:** While rare, GLP-1 receptor agonists have been linked to skeletal muscle loss and, in this case, a probable trigger for IMNM.
*   **Recognize the Pattern:** IMNM presents with aggressive proximal weakness and high CK that does not resolve simply by removing the drug.
*   **Treatment:** Steroids and IVIG are the mainstays for IMNM and can lead to full functional recovery if administered promptly.
*   **The Single-Dose Signal:** A single dose of a medication can be enough to trigger a systemic immune-mediated adverse reaction.



# Rhabdo or IMNM? When Muscle Injury Doesn't Just 'Go Away'

We all know the "GLP-1 glow" (or perhaps the "GLP-1 nausea"). With the explosion in popularity of semaglutide and tirzepatide, we’ve become very comfortable with the gastrointestinal side effects and the rapid weight loss. But there is a much rarer, much more sinister side of muscle loss associated with these drugs. Usually, if a drug causes muscle breakdown, we call it rhabdomyolysis, stop the drug, push fluids, and watch the CK drop. 

But what happens when the drug is gone, the fluids are in, and the patient keeps getting weaker?

## The Case: A Single Dose and a Slow Decline

A woman in her 60s with obesity and NAFLD was started on semaglutide (0.25 mg). The initial reaction was immediate and severe: nausea, vomiting, acute renal failure, and metabolic acidosis. She was hospitalized, stabilized, and discharged. At that time, her CK was only mildly elevated (337 U/L). Semaglutide was obviously off the table.

The surprise came over the next four months. Instead of recovering, she began a steady decline. She developed progressive proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia (difficulty swallowing), and dysphonia (difficulty speaking). By the time she returned to the hospital, she couldn't sit or stand without help, and her CK had climbed 12-fold to nearly 4,000 U/L.

This wasn't the classic "crash and burn" of acute rhabdomyolysis. This was a progressive, immune-mediated inflammatory process.

## The Crucial Distinction: Toxic vs. Immune-Mediated

In the world of drug-induced myopathy, we have to distinguish between a direct toxic effect and an immune-mediated trigger.

**Toxic Rhabdomyolysis** is a direct insult to the muscle fiber. If the offending agent is removed, the source of the injury vanishes. You see a massive spike in CK, but once the drug is cleared and the kidneys are supported, the CK trends down rapidly.

**Immune-Mediated Necrotising Myopathy (IMNM)** is different. Here, the drug (or another trigger) essentially "flips a switch" in the immune system. The body begins attacking its own muscle fibers. Because the immune system is now the driver, stopping the drug isn't enough. The process continues—and often worsens—until you hit the immune system with heavy weaponry.

In this case, the patient didn't just need hydration; she needed 1,000 mg of IV methylprednisolone and IVIG. Once the immune response was suppressed, she made a full recovery and returned to her baseline.

## The Diagnostic Deep Dive

The "gold standard" for diagnosing IMNM is the muscle biopsy. In this patient, the biopsy showed the classic hallmarks: dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers, macrophage infiltration (CD68 positive), and the presence of the membrane attack complex (MAC). 

Interestingly, this patient was seronegative. We often associate IMNM with anti-HMGCR (common with statins) or anti-SRP antibodies, but about a third of these patients don't have a detectable antibody. When the serology is blank but the biopsy shows necrotizing fibers and the patient is failing to improve after drug cessation, you have to think IMNM.

## Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Points

*   **Temporal Patterns Matter:** Acute rhabdo typically resolves quickly after the offending agent is removed. A progressive or "smoldering" course of weakness and rising CK *after* drug cessation is a massive red flag for an immune-mediated process.
*   **Beyond the CK:** Pay attention to "bulbar" symptoms. Dysphagia and dysphonia in the setting of proximal weakness suggest a severe myopathy that requires urgent intervention to protect the airway and nutrition.
*   **Seronegative $\neq$ Negative:** A negative myositis panel (SRP, HMGCR, etc.) does not rule out IMNM. If clinical suspicion is high, biopsy is the definitive path.
*   **GLP-1 Awareness:** While extremely rare, be aware that GLP-1 receptor agonists may be associated with muscle injury. If a patient on these meds presents with unexplained weakness or CK elevation, consider whether this is a simple toxic event or the start of an autoimmune cascade.

**The Bottom Line:** If the muscle injury doesn't "go away" after stopping the drug, stop thinking about toxicity and start thinking about the immune system.



# The Seronegative Trap: Diagnosing IMNM Without the Antibodies

We live in the era of the "panel." Whether it's a comprehensive metabolic panel or a specialized myositis antibody screen, we’ve become accustomed to letting a set of lab values dictate our diagnostic trajectory. But what happens when the panel comes back clean, but the patient is still crashing?

That is the "Seronegative Trap." In the world of Immune-Mediated Necrotising Myopathy (IMNM), the absence of the classic antibodies—specifically anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP—can lead to a dangerous diagnostic plateau. If you stop looking because the antibodies aren't there, you miss the window for immunosuppression.

## The Case: A Slow Burn After a Sharp Start

Consider a patient in her 60s with a history of obesity and NAFLD. She receives a single dose of semaglutide. The immediate reaction is classic GLP-1 intolerance: nausea, vomiting, and a hypovolemic hit leading to acute renal failure. She is treated and discharged, but then something strange happens.

Over the next four months, she doesn't just stay "deconditioned"—she declines. She develops progressive proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia. By the time she presents again, she can't sit upright without assistance. Her CK has climbed 12-fold to nearly 4,000 U/L.

On paper, this looks like a classic myopathy. The team orders a full myositis panel. The result? **Negative.** No SRP, no HMGCR, no Jo-1. Just a low-titer SS-B antibody that doesn't explain the profound muscle necrosis.

## Why the Panel Can Lie to You

In IMNM, we often lean on anti-HMGCR (often associated with statins) and anti-SRP antibodies. When these are positive, the diagnosis is streamlined. However, an estimated one-third of patients with IMNM are seronegative. 

The danger here is conflating "seronegative" with "non-immune." If a clinician sees a negative antibody panel and a history of a drug like semaglutide, they might misdiagnose this as a simple toxic rhabdomyolysis. But rhabdomyolysis typically behaves like a sprint: an acute spike in CK that resolves quickly upon drug cessation and hydration. 

This patient's course was a marathon of decline. The weakness progressed *after* the drug was gone, and the CK continued to rise. That is the hallmark of an immune-mediated process—the drug didn't just poison the muscle; it flipped a switch in the immune system that kept the attack going.

## The Gold Standard: Getting to the Tissue

When the serology fails, the biopsy is the only way out of the trap. In this case, a biopsy of the vastus lateralis revealed the truth: dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers with a macrophage infiltrate (CD68+). Crucially, the membrane attack complex (MAC) was positive on both necrotising and non-necrotising myofibres.

This histological pattern—necrosis with a paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates—is the definitive signature of IMNM. It doesn't matter if the antibodies are missing; the tissue tells the story.

The patient was treated with pulse methylprednisolone, oral prednisone, and IVIG. She made a full recovery and returned to her baseline functional status, emphasizing that the "cure" required targeting the immune system, not just hydrating the kidneys.

## Clinical Pearls and Take-Home Points

*   **Antibodies are suggestive, not definitive:** A negative myositis panel (SRP/HMGCR) does not rule out IMNM. Roughly 30% of cases are seronegative.
*   **Rhabdo vs. IMNM:** Toxic rhabdomyolysis usually improves rapidly after removing the offending agent. Progressive weakness or persistent CK elevation after drug cessation suggests an immune-mediated process.
*   **The Biopsy is King:** When clinical suspicion for myositis is high but serology is negative, a muscle biopsy is the gold standard to identify necrotic fibers and inflammatory infiltrates.
*   **GLP-1 Awareness:** While GI side effects are common with semaglutide and other GLP-1 receptor agonists, be vigilant for rare but severe immune-mediated muscle complications.
*   **Treatment Response:** Seronegative IMNM typically responds well to corticosteroids and IVIG, though the course may be shorter if the trigger (the drug) is removed.



# More Than Just Weak Legs: The Danger of Bulbar Involvement in Myopathy

When we think of myopathy, we usually picture the "classic" presentation: a patient who can’t get out of a low chair or struggles to brush their hair. We look for proximal muscle weakness and we check a CK level. But there is a far more dangerous side to muscle failure that often gets overlooked until the patient is in the ICU with a crash-intubation or aspiration pneumonia. 

I’m talking about bulbar involvement.

Consider this case: a woman in her 60s is started on semaglutide for obesity and NAFLD. After a single dose, she has a rough few days with nausea and renal failure, but she’s discharged. Over the next four months, she doesn’t just get "deconditioned." She slides into profound proximal weakness, but more alarmingly, she develops progressive dysphagia and dysphonia. By the time she hits the hospital, she can't sit upright and can't swallow her own saliva. 

Her CK is nearly 4,000 U/L. Her muscle biopsy reveals the hallmark of Immune-Mediated Necrotising Myopathy (IMNM): necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers with a membrane attack complex positive stain.

## The "Siren Song" of Proximal Weakness

In this case, the proximal weakness was the obvious signal, but the bulbar symptoms were the critical warning. In IMNM, the muscle destruction is aggressive. When this process hits the pharyngeal and laryngeal muscles, the clinical stakes change instantly. 

We often focus on the legs because that’s where the dysfunction is most visible, but the throat is where the mortality lives. Dysphagia in the setting of progressive myopathy isn't just "difficulty eating"; it is a failure of the swallow reflex that leads directly to aspiration. Dysphonia isn't just a "hoarse voice"; it's a sign that the muscles maintaining airway patency and glottic closure are failing.

## Rhabdo vs. IMNM: A Critical Distinction

This case also gives us a great lesson in pharmacological triggers. We’ve seen reports of GLP-1 receptor agonists causing rhabdomyolysis. However, there is a massive difference between a *toxic* muscle injury and an *immune-mediated* one.

In typical drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, you stop the offending agent, provide aggressive hydration, and the CK levels plummet within days. In this patient, the drug was stopped immediately after the first dose, yet she continued to decline for four months, and her CK rose 12-fold *after* the drug was gone. 

That is the hallmark of an autoimmune process. The drug didn't just poison the muscle; it seemingly flipped a switch in her immune system to start attacking the muscle. This is why she didn't get better with fluids—she needed high-dose methylprednisolone and IVIG to shut down the inflammatory cascade.

## The Diagnostic Challenge of the Seronegative Patient

One of the trickiest parts of this case was the antibody screen. The patient was negative for the "big two" markers of IMNM: anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP. 

It is a common clinical pitfall to assume that a negative myositis panel rules out IMNM. In reality, about a third of these patients are seronegative. When the clinical suspicion is high—progressive weakness, skyrocketing CK, and bulbar symptoms—you cannot rely on the bloodwork alone. The biopsy remains the gold standard, showing those dispersed necrotic fibers and macrophage infiltration that confirm the diagnosis.

## Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Points

*   **Look Beyond the Legs:** Proximal weakness is the textbook sign, but dysphagia and dysphonia are the red flags. Any patient with progressive myopathy needs an immediate swallow assessment to prevent aspiration.
*   **Timing is Everything:** If a patient develops myopathy after a drug, but continues to worsen *after* the drug is discontinued, stop thinking "toxicity" and start thinking "immune-mediated."
*   **Seronegative $\neq$ Healthy:** A negative SRP or HMGCR antibody screen does not rule out IMNM. If the clinical picture fits, move toward imaging (MRI) and biopsy.
*   **GLP-1 Vigilance:** While rare, be aware that GLP-1 receptor agonists may be associated with muscle loss or, in extreme cases, trigger an immune-mediated response.
*   **Aggressive Treatment:** IMNM is an aggressive disease. This patient required both pulse steroids and IVIG to regain her functional status; waiting for "watchful waiting" in the face of bulbar symptoms is a recipe for disaster.



# The Delayed Fuse: Rethinking Temporal Relationships in Drug Reactions

We are taught in medical school and residency that drug reactions follow a predictable timeline. If it’s an allergic reaction, it happens fast. If it’s a toxic effect, it usually manifests while the patient is on the drug and resolves when the drug is gone. But what happens when a single dose of a medication acts as a "delayed fuse," triggering a devastating autoimmune cascade that doesn't peak until months later?

This is exactly what happened in a striking case of a woman in her 60s who developed Immune-Mediated Necrotising Myopathy (IMNM) following a single dose of semaglutide.

## The Case: A Slow Slide into Weakness

The patient's journey started with a single 0.25 mg subcutaneous dose of semaglutide for obesity and NAFLD. The immediate reaction was visceral and acute: severe nausea, vomiting, acute renal failure, and metabolic acidosis requiring ICU admission. While her creatine kinase (CK) was mildly elevated at the time (337 U/L), there was no evidence of rhabdomyolysis. She was discharged, and for a while, it seemed like a case of simple medication intolerance.

However, over the next four months, the "fuse" continued to burn. She experienced a progressive, relentless decline in strength. By the time she returned to the hospital, she could no longer sit or stand without assistance and was struggling to swallow. Her CK had climbed twelve-fold to 3,992 U/L.

## Toxicity vs. Autoimmunity: The Critical Distinction

When we see high CK and muscle weakness after a drug, our instinct is to call it "drug-induced rhabdomyolysis." But there is a fundamental difference between a *toxic* myopathy and an *immune-mediated* myopathy.

In a classic toxic reaction—like the rhabdomyolysis sometimes seen with tirzepatide—the CK peaks while the drug is active and plummets shortly after the drug is discontinued. In this case, the opposite happened. The patient was off the semaglutide, yet her muscle destruction accelerated over several months. This suggested that the drug wasn't directly killing the muscle cells; instead, it had shifted the immune system into a state of aggression.

The diagnosis was confirmed via muscle biopsy, which showed necrotic and regenerating fibers with a macrophage infiltrate and a positive membrane attack complex (MAC) stain. Despite a negative myositis panel (negative for the common SRP and HMGCR antibodies), the histology was definitive for IMNM.

## The Lesson in Causality

This case challenges how we use the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) causality criteria. When the trigger is a single dose and the peak symptom occurs 120 days later, clinicians are often tempted to dismiss the temporal relationship as "coincidental."

However, the "probable" causality here was supported by three factors: the reasonable (albeit delayed) time relationship, the absence of other triggers (no statins, no supplements), and the dramatic response to immunosuppression (methylprednisolone and IVIG). Unlike idiopathic IMNM, which often requires years of aggressive therapy, this patient regained her baseline functional status and was successfully weaned off prednisone within two months.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **The Timeline Lie:** Not all drug-induced autoimmune reactions happen immediately. A "trigger" can initiate a process that takes weeks or months to manifest clinically.
*   **CK Trends Matter:** If CK levels continue to rise or stay elevated *after* a drug is stopped, stop thinking about direct toxicity and start thinking about immune-mediated processes.
*   **Seronegative $\neq$ Negative:** A third of IMNM patients are seronegative. A negative HMGCR or SRP panel does not rule out necrotising myopathy; if the clinical suspicion is high, the biopsy remains the gold standard.
*   **GLP-1 Vigilance:** While GI side effects are common with semaglutide and other GLP-1 agonists, be aware of rare but severe reports of muscle loss and potential immune-mediated myopathies.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Rhabdomyolysis vs. IMNM:** Toxic rhabdomyolysis typically resolves quickly after drug cessation; IMNM is progressive and requires immunosuppression.
*   **Delayed Manifestation:** Consider distant triggers when evaluating new-onset autoimmune presentations.
*   **Diagnosis:** Muscle biopsy is essential for diagnosing seronegative IMNM when antibody panels are negative.
*   **Treatment:** Early identification and treatment with corticosteroids and IVIG can lead to full functional recovery in drug-induced IMNM.
