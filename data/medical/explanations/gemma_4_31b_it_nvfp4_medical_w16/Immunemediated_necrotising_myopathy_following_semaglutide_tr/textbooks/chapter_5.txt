Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

Chapter 5: ## Management, Outcome, and Clinical Significance

The management of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) requires a combination of rapidly reducing inflammation and suppressing the aberrant immune response. In this case, the patient presented with severe features, including dysphagia, dysphonia, and profound proximal muscle weakness, necessitating an aggressive induction therapy. She was treated with "pulse" therapy consisting of 1,000 mg of intravenous methylprednisolone daily for five days. This was followed by a maintenance dose of oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg daily. To address the severity of her muscle weakness and the aggressive nature of the necrotising process, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at a total dose of 2 g/kg, split over four days.

Treatment response was monitored through both biochemical markers and clinical functional assessments. A marked improvement in serum creatine kinase (CK) levels was observed within two weeks of initiating therapy, signaling a reduction in active myofiber necrosis. Clinically, the patient demonstrated a steady recovery of muscle strength and a resolution of her swallowing and speaking difficulties. Because the patient responded robustly to the initial course of immunosuppression and the offending agent was removed, she was able to be weaned off prednisone rapidly over a two-month period. By the ten-month follow-up, she had regained her baseline functional status and had remained off all immunosuppressive medications for seven to eight months, with persistently normal CK levels.

A critical component of the patient's recovery was the total avoidance of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. The clinical course highlights a key diagnostic and management distinction between direct drug-induced toxicity (rhabdomyolysis) and drug-induced autoimmunity (IMNM). In cases of toxic rhabdomyolysis, such as those previously reported with tirzepatide, CK levels typically normalize rapidly upon drug withdrawal and hydration. In contrast, this patient’s CK levels rose exponentially over four months *after* the drug was stopped, and her recovery required targeted immunosuppression. This suggests that semaglutide may have acted as a trigger for an autoimmune process rather than causing direct muscle toxicity.

This case provides an important lesson in pharmacovigilance, particularly as GLP-1 receptor agonists become ubiquitous in the treatment of obesity and type 2 diabetes. While gastrointestinal side effects are common, clinicians must remain alert to rare but severe systemic immune reactions. The identification of a "probable" causal link—based on the temporal relationship and the response to immunosuppression—underscores the necessity of reporting such adverse events. For the medical provider, this case emphasizes that when a patient presents with progressive proximal weakness and elevated CK following the initiation of a new medication, the differential must expand beyond simple toxicity to include immune-mediated processes that may require systemic steroid and immunoglobulin therapy.
