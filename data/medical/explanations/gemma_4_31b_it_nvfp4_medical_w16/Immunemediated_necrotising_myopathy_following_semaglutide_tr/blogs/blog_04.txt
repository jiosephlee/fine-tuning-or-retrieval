Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# More Than Just Weak Legs: The Danger of Bulbar Involvement in Myopathy

When we think of myopathy, we usually picture the "classic" presentation: a patient who can’t get out of a low chair or struggles to brush their hair. We look for proximal muscle weakness and we check a CK level. But there is a far more dangerous side to muscle failure that often gets overlooked until the patient is in the ICU with a crash-intubation or aspiration pneumonia. 

I’m talking about bulbar involvement.

Consider this case: a woman in her 60s is started on semaglutide for obesity and NAFLD. After a single dose, she has a rough few days with nausea and renal failure, but she’s discharged. Over the next four months, she doesn’t just get "deconditioned." She slides into profound proximal weakness, but more alarmingly, she develops progressive dysphagia and dysphonia. By the time she hits the hospital, she can't sit upright and can't swallow her own saliva. 

Her CK is nearly 4,000 U/L. Her muscle biopsy reveals the hallmark of Immune-Mediated Necrotising Myopathy (IMNM): necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers with a membrane attack complex positive stain.

## The "Siren Song" of Proximal Weakness

In this case, the proximal weakness was the obvious signal, but the bulbar symptoms were the critical warning. In IMNM, the muscle destruction is aggressive. When this process hits the pharyngeal and laryngeal muscles, the clinical stakes change instantly. 

We often focus on the legs because that’s where the dysfunction is most visible, but the throat is where the mortality lives. Dysphagia in the setting of progressive myopathy isn't just "difficulty eating"; it is a failure of the swallow reflex that leads directly to aspiration. Dysphonia isn't just a "hoarse voice"; it's a sign that the muscles maintaining airway patency and glottic closure are failing.

## Rhabdo vs. IMNM: A Critical Distinction

This case also gives us a great lesson in pharmacological triggers. We’ve seen reports of GLP-1 receptor agonists causing rhabdomyolysis. However, there is a massive difference between a *toxic* muscle injury and an *immune-mediated* one.

In typical drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, you stop the offending agent, provide aggressive hydration, and the CK levels plummet within days. In this patient, the drug was stopped immediately after the first dose, yet she continued to decline for four months, and her CK rose 12-fold *after* the drug was gone. 

That is the hallmark of an autoimmune process. The drug didn't just poison the muscle; it seemingly flipped a switch in her immune system to start attacking the muscle. This is why she didn't get better with fluids—she needed high-dose methylprednisolone and IVIG to shut down the inflammatory cascade.

## The Diagnostic Challenge of the Seronegative Patient

One of the trickiest parts of this case was the antibody screen. The patient was negative for the "big two" markers of IMNM: anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP. 

It is a common clinical pitfall to assume that a negative myositis panel rules out IMNM. In reality, about a third of these patients are seronegative. When the clinical suspicion is high—progressive weakness, skyrocketing CK, and bulbar symptoms—you cannot rely on the bloodwork alone. The biopsy remains the gold standard, showing those dispersed necrotic fibers and macrophage infiltration that confirm the diagnosis.

## Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Points

*   **Look Beyond the Legs:** Proximal weakness is the textbook sign, but dysphagia and dysphonia are the red flags. Any patient with progressive myopathy needs an immediate swallow assessment to prevent aspiration.
*   **Timing is Everything:** If a patient develops myopathy after a drug, but continues to worsen *after* the drug is discontinued, stop thinking "toxicity" and start thinking "immune-mediated."
*   **Seronegative $\neq$ Healthy:** A negative SRP or HMGCR antibody screen does not rule out IMNM. If the clinical picture fits, move toward imaging (MRI) and biopsy.
*   **GLP-1 Vigilance:** While rare, be aware that GLP-1 receptor agonists may be associated with muscle loss or, in extreme cases, trigger an immune-mediated response.
*   **Aggressive Treatment:** IMNM is an aggressive disease. This patient required both pulse steroids and IVIG to regain her functional status; waiting for "watchful waiting" in the face of bulbar symptoms is a recipe for disaster.
