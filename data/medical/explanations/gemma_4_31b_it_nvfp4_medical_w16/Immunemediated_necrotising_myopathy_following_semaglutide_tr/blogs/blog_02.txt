Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# Rhabdo or IMNM? When Muscle Injury Doesn't Just 'Go Away'

We all know the "GLP-1 glow" (or perhaps the "GLP-1 nausea"). With the explosion in popularity of semaglutide and tirzepatide, we’ve become very comfortable with the gastrointestinal side effects and the rapid weight loss. But there is a much rarer, much more sinister side of muscle loss associated with these drugs. Usually, if a drug causes muscle breakdown, we call it rhabdomyolysis, stop the drug, push fluids, and watch the CK drop. 

But what happens when the drug is gone, the fluids are in, and the patient keeps getting weaker?

## The Case: A Single Dose and a Slow Decline

A woman in her 60s with obesity and NAFLD was started on semaglutide (0.25 mg). The initial reaction was immediate and severe: nausea, vomiting, acute renal failure, and metabolic acidosis. She was hospitalized, stabilized, and discharged. At that time, her CK was only mildly elevated (337 U/L). Semaglutide was obviously off the table.

The surprise came over the next four months. Instead of recovering, she began a steady decline. She developed progressive proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia (difficulty swallowing), and dysphonia (difficulty speaking). By the time she returned to the hospital, she couldn't sit or stand without help, and her CK had climbed 12-fold to nearly 4,000 U/L.

This wasn't the classic "crash and burn" of acute rhabdomyolysis. This was a progressive, immune-mediated inflammatory process.

## The Crucial Distinction: Toxic vs. Immune-Mediated

In the world of drug-induced myopathy, we have to distinguish between a direct toxic effect and an immune-mediated trigger.

**Toxic Rhabdomyolysis** is a direct insult to the muscle fiber. If the offending agent is removed, the source of the injury vanishes. You see a massive spike in CK, but once the drug is cleared and the kidneys are supported, the CK trends down rapidly.

**Immune-Mediated Necrotising Myopathy (IMNM)** is different. Here, the drug (or another trigger) essentially "flips a switch" in the immune system. The body begins attacking its own muscle fibers. Because the immune system is now the driver, stopping the drug isn't enough. The process continues—and often worsens—until you hit the immune system with heavy weaponry.

In this case, the patient didn't just need hydration; she needed 1,000 mg of IV methylprednisolone and IVIG. Once the immune response was suppressed, she made a full recovery and returned to her baseline.

## The Diagnostic Deep Dive

The "gold standard" for diagnosing IMNM is the muscle biopsy. In this patient, the biopsy showed the classic hallmarks: dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers, macrophage infiltration (CD68 positive), and the presence of the membrane attack complex (MAC). 

Interestingly, this patient was seronegative. We often associate IMNM with anti-HMGCR (common with statins) or anti-SRP antibodies, but about a third of these patients don't have a detectable antibody. When the serology is blank but the biopsy shows necrotizing fibers and the patient is failing to improve after drug cessation, you have to think IMNM.

## Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Points

*   **Temporal Patterns Matter:** Acute rhabdo typically resolves quickly after the offending agent is removed. A progressive or "smoldering" course of weakness and rising CK *after* drug cessation is a massive red flag for an immune-mediated process.
*   **Beyond the CK:** Pay attention to "bulbar" symptoms. Dysphagia and dysphonia in the setting of proximal weakness suggest a severe myopathy that requires urgent intervention to protect the airway and nutrition.
*   **Seronegative $\neq$ Negative:** A negative myositis panel (SRP, HMGCR, etc.) does not rule out IMNM. If clinical suspicion is high, biopsy is the definitive path.
*   **GLP-1 Awareness:** While extremely rare, be aware that GLP-1 receptor agonists may be associated with muscle injury. If a patient on these meds presents with unexplained weakness or CK elevation, consider whether this is a simple toxic event or the start of an autoimmune cascade.

**The Bottom Line:** If the muscle injury doesn't "go away" after stopping the drug, stop thinking about toxicity and start thinking about the immune system.
