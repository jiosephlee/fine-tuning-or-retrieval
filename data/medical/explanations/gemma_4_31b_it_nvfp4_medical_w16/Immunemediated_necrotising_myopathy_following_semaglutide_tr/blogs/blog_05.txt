Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# The Delayed Fuse: Rethinking Temporal Relationships in Drug Reactions

We are taught in medical school and residency that drug reactions follow a predictable timeline. If it’s an allergic reaction, it happens fast. If it’s a toxic effect, it usually manifests while the patient is on the drug and resolves when the drug is gone. But what happens when a single dose of a medication acts as a "delayed fuse," triggering a devastating autoimmune cascade that doesn't peak until months later?

This is exactly what happened in a striking case of a woman in her 60s who developed Immune-Mediated Necrotising Myopathy (IMNM) following a single dose of semaglutide.

## The Case: A Slow Slide into Weakness

The patient's journey started with a single 0.25 mg subcutaneous dose of semaglutide for obesity and NAFLD. The immediate reaction was visceral and acute: severe nausea, vomiting, acute renal failure, and metabolic acidosis requiring ICU admission. While her creatine kinase (CK) was mildly elevated at the time (337 U/L), there was no evidence of rhabdomyolysis. She was discharged, and for a while, it seemed like a case of simple medication intolerance.

However, over the next four months, the "fuse" continued to burn. She experienced a progressive, relentless decline in strength. By the time she returned to the hospital, she could no longer sit or stand without assistance and was struggling to swallow. Her CK had climbed twelve-fold to 3,992 U/L.

## Toxicity vs. Autoimmunity: The Critical Distinction

When we see high CK and muscle weakness after a drug, our instinct is to call it "drug-induced rhabdomyolysis." But there is a fundamental difference between a *toxic* myopathy and an *immune-mediated* myopathy.

In a classic toxic reaction—like the rhabdomyolysis sometimes seen with tirzepatide—the CK peaks while the drug is active and plummets shortly after the drug is discontinued. In this case, the opposite happened. The patient was off the semaglutide, yet her muscle destruction accelerated over several months. This suggested that the drug wasn't directly killing the muscle cells; instead, it had shifted the immune system into a state of aggression.

The diagnosis was confirmed via muscle biopsy, which showed necrotic and regenerating fibers with a macrophage infiltrate and a positive membrane attack complex (MAC) stain. Despite a negative myositis panel (negative for the common SRP and HMGCR antibodies), the histology was definitive for IMNM.

## The Lesson in Causality

This case challenges how we use the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) causality criteria. When the trigger is a single dose and the peak symptom occurs 120 days later, clinicians are often tempted to dismiss the temporal relationship as "coincidental."

However, the "probable" causality here was supported by three factors: the reasonable (albeit delayed) time relationship, the absence of other triggers (no statins, no supplements), and the dramatic response to immunosuppression (methylprednisolone and IVIG). Unlike idiopathic IMNM, which often requires years of aggressive therapy, this patient regained her baseline functional status and was successfully weaned off prednisone within two months.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **The Timeline Lie:** Not all drug-induced autoimmune reactions happen immediately. A "trigger" can initiate a process that takes weeks or months to manifest clinically.
*   **CK Trends Matter:** If CK levels continue to rise or stay elevated *after* a drug is stopped, stop thinking about direct toxicity and start thinking about immune-mediated processes.
*   **Seronegative $\neq$ Negative:** A third of IMNM patients are seronegative. A negative HMGCR or SRP panel does not rule out necrotising myopathy; if the clinical suspicion is high, the biopsy remains the gold standard.
*   **GLP-1 Vigilance:** While GI side effects are common with semaglutide and other GLP-1 agonists, be aware of rare but severe reports of muscle loss and potential immune-mediated myopathies.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Rhabdomyolysis vs. IMNM:** Toxic rhabdomyolysis typically resolves quickly after drug cessation; IMNM is progressive and requires immunosuppression.
*   **Delayed Manifestation:** Consider distant triggers when evaluating new-onset autoimmune presentations.
*   **Diagnosis:** Muscle biopsy is essential for diagnosing seronegative IMNM when antibody panels are negative.
*   **Treatment:** Early identification and treatment with corticosteroids and IVIG can lead to full functional recovery in drug-induced IMNM.
