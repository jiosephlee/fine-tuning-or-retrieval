Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Evaluation

The initial presentation of this patient underscores the challenge of diagnosing multisystem inflammatory disorders, where non-specific symptoms often mask a complex underlying pathology. The patient, an Asian man in his 60s, presented with a three-day history of fever, headache, general malaise, and arthralgia affecting the shoulders and ankles. While these symptoms are common to many infectious and inflammatory processes, the combination of systemic signs and neurological complaints (headache and vertigo) should prompt a clinician to consider both primary central nervous system (CNS) pathology and systemic diseases with CNS manifestations.

Key to the diagnostic process in this case was the recognition of mucocutaneous hallmarks. Physical examination revealed oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rashes on the face and trunk. In the context of recurrent oral ulcers, these skin manifestations are highly suggestive of Behçet’s disease (BD). The subsequent appearance of a painful scrotal ulcer further solidified the diagnosis, as genital aphthosis is a highly specific marker for BD. Under the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, these findings provided the necessary scoring to categorize the patient as having "definite BD," guiding the clinicians to look for associated organ involvement, such as the CNS.

A critical learning point in this case is the identification of "hidden" or atypical signs of severe hyponatremia. The patient reported persistent hiccups, a symptom that is often overlooked but can serve as a clinical clue to severe hyponatremia resulting from CNS inflammation. Furthermore, initial laboratory tests revealed a markedly elevated creatine kinase (CK) level of 1349 U/L. While elevated CK is often attributed to primary muscle disease or trauma, in this context, it represented rhabdomyolysis—a recognized but frequently underappreciated complication of profound hyponatremia. Recognizing these clues is essential for timely metabolic stabilization and suggests that the neurological symptoms may be linked to an endocrine disturbance.

The initial laboratory profile provided objective evidence of both a systemic inflammatory response and significant metabolic derangements. Marked elevations in C-reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) confirmed a state of high systemic inflammation. Simultaneously, the patient exhibited severe hyponatremia (124 mmol/L) and poor glycemic control, with hyperglycemia (14.2 mmol/L) and an elevated HbA1c (8.2%). These findings necessitated a broad differential diagnosis, requiring the team to distinguish between infectious causes of meningitis and the inflammatory manifestations of Behçet’s disease, while concurrently managing the acute electrolyte imbalance.

## Diagnostic Framework for Behçet's Disease (BD)

Behçet's disease is a chronic, multisystem inflammatory disorder characterized by systemic vasculitis that can affect vessels of all sizes. While the exact etiology remains elusive, the disease manifests as a variety of inflammatory lesions across different organ systems. The most hallmark features are recurrent oral and genital ulcers, skin lesions, and uveitis. However, the disease can also involve the gastrointestinal tract, joints, and the central nervous system (CNS). In this case, the patient demonstrated a systemic presentation involving the skin and mucous membranes, as well as the CNS, highlighting the unpredictable and widespread nature of the inflammatory process.

The diagnosis of Behçet's disease is primarily clinical, as there is no single gold-standard laboratory test. Clinicians often utilize the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease (ICBD) scoring system to standardize the diagnosis. Under this framework, points are assigned based on the presence of specific clinical manifestations. In the reported case, the patient accrued a total of five points: two points for oral aphthae, two points for genital aphthosis, and one point for the folliculitis-like rash. Because a total score of four or more is indicative of "definite BD," this patient met the diagnostic threshold for the systemic disease.

Among the mucocutaneous findings, genital aphthosis is of particular clinical significance. While oral ulcers are a common feature of many inflammatory or infectious conditions, the presence of painful genital ulcers is highly specific for Behçet's disease. In this patient, the appearance of a painful scrotum ulcer on day five was a critical diagnostic pivot. The emergence of this specific lesion, in conjunction with the pre-existing oral and cutaneous findings, allowed the clinical team to move toward a definitive diagnosis of BD after excluding other causes of fever and CNS inflammation.

Genetic testing for HLA-B51 is often performed in suspected cases of BD, as this allele is strongly associated with the disease, particularly in populations along the "Silk Road." In this case, the patient tested positive for HLA-B51. However, it is important to understand the limitations of this marker: HLA-B51 is neither necessary nor sufficient for a diagnosis. Many patients with definite BD are HLA-B51 negative, and many healthy individuals carry the allele without developing the disease. Consequently, the ICBD does not require a positive HLA-B51 result for diagnosis, and the result should be viewed as supportive rather than diagnostic.

## Acute Neuro-Behçet's Disease (Neuro-BD)

Neurological involvement in Behçet’s disease (BD), termed neuro-BD, occurs in approximately 10% to 20% of patients and manifests through two primary clinical phenotypes: parenchymal involvement and non-parenchymal involvement. Parenchymal lesions typically present as meningoencephalitis, frequently involving the brainstem, and often result in focal neurological deficits or behavioral changes. In contrast, non-parenchymal neuro-BD often manifests as aseptic meningitis, characterized by non-specific symptoms such as fever, headache, malaise, and vertigo. In this case, the patient presented with the latter phenotype, exhibiting systemic inflammatory symptoms and headache without focal deficits or cranial nerve involvement.

A critical diagnostic challenge in early neuro-BD is the interpretation of neuroimaging. While parenchymal neuro-BD often shows characteristic lesions on brain Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI), patients with the aseptic meningitis phenotype may have entirely unremarkable imaging. As demonstrated in this case, a normal brain MRI—even in the absence of abnormalities in the hypothalamus or brainstem—does not exclude a diagnosis of neuro-BD. This highlights the necessity of maintaining a high clinical suspicion when a patient with systemic BD presents with central nervous system (CNS) symptoms, as radiological findings may lag behind the clinical presentation during the early phase of the disease.

When imaging is inconclusive, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis becomes the primary tool for identifying CNS inflammation. The hallmark of neuro-BD in the CSF is a pattern of aseptic meningitis: the presence of inflammatory markers without an identifiable infectious agent. In this patient, the CSF analysis revealed a mildly elevated opening pressure, increased protein levels (0.97 g/L), and a mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). To establish the diagnosis of "aseptic" meningitis, clinicians must rigorously rule out other causes through negative cultures and PCR tests for bacterial, mycobacterial, and viral pathogens (such as HSV and VZV), as well as the absence of malignant cells.

The temporal relationship between systemic BD and its neurological manifestations is also a key diagnostic consideration. Typically, neuro-BD is a delayed manifestation, appearing several years after the initial diagnosis of mucocutaneous lesions. Concurrent onset—where systemic BD and neuro-BD are diagnosed simultaneously—is rare, occurring in fewer than 5% of patients. This case is particularly notable because the patient presented with both the diagnostic criteria for systemic BD (oral aphthae, genital ulcers, and folliculitis-like rashes) and acute neurological symptoms at the same time. This synchronicity underscores the importance of evaluating for CNS involvement at the time of initial BD diagnosis, regardless of the patient's disease duration.

## SIADH and the Neuro-Endocrine Link

The Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone secretion (SIADH) is a clinical condition characterized by the excessive release of antidiuretic hormone (ADH), also known as vasopressin, from the posterior pituitary gland despite low plasma osmolality and normal blood volume. In this case, the patient presented with marked hyponatraemia (124 mmol/L) and a clinical state of euvolemia. The pathophysiology of euvolemic hyponatraemia in SIADH involves the continuous action of ADH on the V2 receptors in the renal collecting ducts, leading to increased water reabsorption. This results in an expansion of the total body water and a subsequent dilutional decrease in serum sodium. To compensate for this slight volume expansion, the body increases the excretion of sodium in the urine (natriuresis), which prevents the development of clinical edema but further lowers the serum sodium concentration.

The laboratory diagnosis of SIADH is established by demonstrating a state of hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia coupled with inappropriately concentrated urine. In this patient, the serum osmolality was low (267 mOsm/kg), yet the urine osmolality remained significantly elevated (900 mOsm/kg), indicating that the kidneys were unable to dilute the urine. Furthermore, the urinary sodium concentration was high (88 mmol/L), confirming that the hyponatraemia was not due to volume depletion (where urine sodium would be low). The diagnostic hallmark was the inappropriately elevated ADH level (22.4 pg/mL), which should have been suppressed in the presence of low serum osmolality.

The link between acute neurological Behçet’s disease (neuro-BD) and SIADH lies in the inflammatory processes occurring within the central nervous system (CNS). Inflammation of the brain parenchyma or meninges can disrupt the regulatory mechanisms of the hypothalamus or directly stimulate the release of ADH. Specifically, pro-inflammatory cytokines, most notably interleukin-6 (IL-6), play a critical role in this process. IL-6 is frequently elevated in the cerebrospinal fluid of patients with neuro-BD and is known to stimulate the secretion of ADH. While IL-6 was not measured directly in this patient, the markedly elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) suggests a potent systemic inflammatory response that likely included IL-6, thereby triggering the inappropriate release of vasopressin.

Distinguishing the cause of SIADH is crucial in patients with Behçet’s disease. In many cases, SIADH is drug-induced, resulting from the use of potent immunosuppressants such as cyclophosphamide. However, in this patient, the SIADH occurred concurrently with the initial presentation of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD, prior to the administration of any such medications. Because the hyponatraemia coincided with the onset of fever and aseptic meningitis, the diagnosis was attributed to the neuro-inflammatory process rather than pharmacological triggers. The resolution of the hyponatraemia following the treatment of the underlying neuro-inflammation with colchicine further supports the direct link between the acute neuro-BD and the endocrine dysfunction.

## Differential Diagnosis and Clinical Reasoning

The initial presentation of this patient—characterized by fever, headache, general malaise, and marked hyponatraemia—presented a diagnostic challenge, as these findings are non-specific and can be seen in a wide array of infectious, neoplastic, and inflammatory conditions. Given the neurological symptoms and the presence of hyponatraemia (which can be a manifestation of central nervous system inflammation), the clinicians prioritized a broad differential diagnosis focused on the central nervous system (CNS).

Infectious causes of meningitis and encephalitis were the first priority for exclusion. Viral meningoencephalitis, specifically those caused by herpes simplex virus (HSV) and varicella zoster virus (VZV), was strongly suspected given the acute onset of fever and headache. While acyclovir was administered empirically, the diagnosis was effectively ruled out through negative CSF viral antigen and antibody testing. Bacterial meningitis was considered but deemed unlikely due to the CSF profile; the patient exhibited mild lymphocytic pleocytosis rather than the neutrophilic predominance typically associated with bacterial infections, and CSF cultures remained negative. Similarly, tuberculous meningitis was excluded based on negative results for acid-fast staining and tuberculosis DNA testing.

Neoplastic processes were also evaluated, as primary CNS malignancies or lymphoma can present with non-specific neurological deficits and systemic symptoms. The diagnostic utility of imaging and cytology was central to this exclusion. A brain MRI showed no abnormalities, and CSF cytology revealed no malignant cells, which provided strong evidence against a primary CNS malignancy. While the soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) level was mildly elevated (704 U/mL), the clinicians correctly interpreted this finding as non-specific. In the absence of supportive imaging or cytology, a mild elevation of sIL-2R often reflects general inflammatory activity rather than a definitive diagnosis of CNS lymphoma.

The clinicians further screened for autoimmune and inflammatory mimics, such as neurosarcoidosis and other connective tissue diseases. Neurosarcoidosis was considered because MRI findings can occasionally be normal in the early stages of the disease. However, this was ruled out through the measurement of angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) and lysozyme levels, both of which were within normal limits. Other autoimmune diseases were excluded due to the lack of characteristic systemic symptoms and negative results from screening autoantibody tests.

Ultimately, the diagnostic reasoning shifted toward systemic Behçet’s disease (BD) as the patient developed the highly specific finding of a painful scrotum ulcer on day five. Using the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease, the patient was assigned a total of five points (oral aphthae, genital aphthosis, and a folliculitis-like rash), meeting the threshold for "definite BD." With infectious, neoplastic, and other autoimmune causes systematically excluded, the patient's fever and headache were attributed to acute neuro-BD, presenting as aseptic meningitis.

## Management and Therapeutic Outcomes

The management of this patient required a multi-pronged approach to address the systemic inflammatory process of Behçet’s disease (BD), the acute neurological involvement (neuro-BD), and the resulting electrolyte imbalance caused by the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). Because the patient presented with severe hyponatremia (124 mmol/L) and non-specific CNS symptoms, including persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis, stabilizing the serum sodium was a critical early priority.

The SIADH was managed conservatively using fluid restriction of less than 800 mL per day. In the context of SIADH, where the body retains excess water despite low serum osmolality, restricting intake is the primary method to prevent further dilution of serum sodium. The clinical team emphasized the avoidance of rapid sodium correction, as overly aggressive replacement can lead to osmotic demyelination syndrome. In this case, the hyponatremia responded well to conservative measures; the patient did not require hypertonic saline or vasopressin receptor antagonists, and his serum sodium eventually normalized to 136 mmol/L.

The cornerstone of the patient's inflammatory treatment was colchicine monotherapy, initiated on day eight. Colchicine exerts its anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting neutrophil migration and activation and suppressing the production of proinflammatory cytokines, specifically IL-1β and IL-6. This mechanism is particularly relevant to this case because IL-6 is frequently elevated in the cerebrospinal fluid of patients with neuro-BD and is believed to stimulate the release of antidiuretic hormone (ADH). By suppressing this cytokine-mediated response, colchicine likely contributed to the resolution of both the aseptic meningitis and the SIADH, as evidenced by the subsequent drop in ADH levels from 22.4 pg/mL to 1.8 pg/mL.

This treatment strategy is notable because corticosteroids are typically the mainstay of therapy for acute neuro-BD. While some medical literature suggests that colchicine is ineffective as a standalone treatment for neurological involvement and should only be used as an adjunct to immunosuppressants, this patient achieved complete remission without the need for steroids. This suggests that in cases of neuro-BD presenting as self-limiting meningoencephalitis, colchicine monotherapy may be a viable and practical option, especially when corticosteroids are undesirable or contraindicated.

Following the acute phase, the patient remained on colchicine therapy for approximately four years, during which no recurrences of BD or neurological symptoms occurred. The long-term management also integrated the treatment of the patient's comorbid type 2 diabetes mellitus. Once the systemic inflammatory state of the BD had stabilized, the clinical team initiated biguanide therapy, which successfully achieved glycemic control. This sequence of treatment highlights the importance of prioritizing the resolution of acute, life-threatening inflammatory and endocrine crises before optimizing the management of chronic metabolic comorbidities.
