Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

Chapter 5: ## Outcome Analysis and Lifetime Management Strategies

The evaluation of success in transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is standardized using the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 (VARC-3) criteria. These criteria provide a rigorous framework for assessing procedural success, valve performance, and complications. In this case, procedural success was defined by the successful deployment of the Acurate neo2 prosthesis without immediate major complications, such as coronary obstruction or severe paravalvular leak. However, the immediate hemodynamic data highlighted a common challenge in repeat TAVI procedures: prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM).

PPM occurs when the effective orifice area (EOA) of the implanted prosthesis is too small to adequately support the patient's cardiac output, leading to persistent high gradients. Immediately following the third TAVI, the patient exhibited a mean gradient of 67 mmHg and an aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.9 $\text{cm}^2$. To determine the severity of PPM, clinicians use the indexed AVA (AVA divided by the patient's body surface area) and the Doppler Velocity Index (DVI). An indexed AVA of 0.4 $\text{cm}^2/\text{m}^2$ and a DVI of 0.24 in this patient were indicative of severe PPM. This phenomenon is expected in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV scenarios, as each subsequent valve is constrained by the frames of the previous prostheses, progressively reducing the available lumen for blood flow.

Despite the initial severe PPM, the patient showed significant hemodynamic improvement by the one-month follow-up. The mean gradient dropped to 27 mmHg and the AVA increased to 1.1 $\text{cm}^2$. This improvement is often attributed to a combination of factors: the partial mechanical expansion of the older frames (the Evolut prosthesis) due to post-dilation, the resolution of initial procedural inflammation, and left ventricular (LV) remodeling. The reduction in LV chamber volumes and the stabilization of the ejection fraction (EF 65%) at one year confirm that the third valve provided sufficient hemodynamic relief to allow the heart to recover from chronic pressure overload.

This case underscores the concept of "lifetime management" in structural heart disease. Every TAVI procedure alters the anatomy of the aortic root; by the third implantation, the "cumulative mesh burden"—the layering of multiple metal frames—creates significant challenges for future interventions. Specifically, the overlapping frames can obstruct access to the coronary ostia, making future percutaneous coronary interventions (PCI) technically difficult. The procedural strategy in this case—using CT-guided high implantation and deliberate commissural alignment—was specifically designed to mitigate these risks by preserving coronary flow and maintaining a potential pathway for future access.

Looking forward, if a triple-stented prosthesis fails, surgical explantation is often prohibitively high-risk due to the deep integration of the frames into the aortic wall. In such cases, emerging catheter-based "salvage" therapies are considered. These include bioprosthetic valve fracture (using high-pressure balloons to intentionally crack the frame to increase the orifice area) and the BASILICA technique (bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration), which involves using a catheter to lacerate the leaflets of a failed valve to create a passage for a new valve without obstructing coronary flow. While these techniques are complex, they represent the next frontier in managing patients who have reached the limit of traditional TAV-in-TAV strategies.
