Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

Chapter 2: ## Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis (PVE) and Post-Infectious Degeneration

In this case, the patient’s clinical course was complicated by prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) occurring after his second transcatheter intervention. The diagnosis of PVE was established through a combination of clinical presentation, microbiology, and imaging. The patient presented with persistent fever and worsening heart failure, which, alongside positive blood cultures for *Enterococcus faecalis* and echocardiographic evidence of severe restenosis, confirmed the diagnosis. *E. faecalis* is a common pathogen in PVE, often requiring targeted, prolonged antimicrobial regimens due to its resilience and propensity for biofilm formation on prosthetic material.

The management of PVE requires a rigorous antimicrobial strategy to ensure the complete eradication of the pathogen before any further invasive procedures are considered. In this patient, a targeted 6-week course of intravenous ampicillin-based therapy was administered, followed by a transition to oral antibiotics. This duration is consistent with standard guidelines for treating endocarditis to prevent relapse, particularly when the infection involves prosthetic material where drug penetration can be limited.

A critical challenge in managing PVE is distinguishing between active infection and the structural damage left in the wake of a resolved infection. Active endocarditis is characterized by the presence of vegetations, perivalvular abscesses, and positive blood cultures. In contrast, post-infectious degeneration manifests as permanent structural changes to the valve. To ensure the patient was a candidate for a third TAVI, the clinical team utilized transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and multislice computed tomography (MSCT) to confirm the absence of active vegetations or paravalvular complications. These imaging modalities allowed the team to conclude that the infection had resolved and that the remaining valve dysfunction was due to structural failure rather than ongoing sepsis.

The pathophysiology of this valve failure was likely multifactorial. PVE often leads to accelerated prosthetic valve deterioration through two primary mechanisms: the direct destruction of leaflet tissue by bacteria and the subsequent inflammatory response that triggers fibrosis. In this patient, the infection likely caused leaflet injury and subsequent fibrotic scarring, leading to mechanical restriction and severe restenosis. This is evidenced by the markedly reduced aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.5 cm² and an elevated mean gradient (49 mmHg) observed after the infection had been treated.

The timing of re-intervention after PVE is a high-stakes decision. Intervening too early, while the infection is active, carries an extremely high risk of seeding the new prosthesis. However, waiting until the patient is hemodynamically unstable increases surgical and procedural risk. In this case, the decision to proceed with a third TAVI in 2024 was based on the complete resolution of the infection, as confirmed by negative cultures and imaging, balanced against the patient's prohibitive surgical risk (EuroSCORE II of 27.36%). This highlights the necessity of a multidisciplinary "heart team" approach to determine when the benefit of restoring valve function outweighs the risks associated with a triple-stented anatomy and a history of PVE.
