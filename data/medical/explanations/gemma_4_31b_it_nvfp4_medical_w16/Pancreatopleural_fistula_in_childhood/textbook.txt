Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

## Clinical Presentation and the 'Pulmonary Mask'

The initial presentation of a pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) often mimics primary thoracic pathology, a phenomenon described as a "pulmonary mask." In this case, the patient presented with a one-week history of progressive respiratory distress and right-sided chest pain. On physical examination, the findings were classic for a massive pleural effusion: tachypnea, oxygen saturation of 88–89% on room air, and significantly reduced chest movements on the right. Specifically, the presence of stony dullness on percussion across the subscapular and axillary regions, combined with weakened vocal fremitus and absent breath sounds, pointed toward a significant accumulation of fluid in the pleural space.

A critical challenge in this case was the risk of premature closure—a cognitive bias where a clinician ceases the diagnostic search once a plausible explanation is found. Because the patient had a known contact with a grandfather suffering from pulmonary tuberculosis and displayed a slightly elevated Adenosine Deaminase (ADA) level in the pleural fluid, the clinical team focused on a pulmonary diagnosis. This led to the empirical initiation of antitubercular therapy. This trajectory highlights how a compelling family history can inadvertently mask an unrelated systemic pathology, leading the clinician to overlook non-pulmonary sources of pleural fluid.

The clinical significance of the patient's course lay in the recurrence of symptoms. While initial thoracocentesis provided immediate symptomatic relief and improved oxygen saturation, the pleural effusion returned twice within two weeks. In a clinical setting, the failure of a procedure to provide a permanent cure—despite successful drainage—should serve as a red flag that the fluid is not a primary pleural process (like an empyema or isolated pneumonia) but is instead being replenished by an external source. 

Ultimately, the diagnostic breakthrough occurred not through further imaging or laboratory tests, but through meticulous and repeated history taking. It was only upon further questioning that the child revealed a history of blunt abdominal trauma caused by a bicycle handle one month prior. This detail shifted the diagnostic lens from the chest to the abdomen, allowing the team to consider the possibility of a pancreatic leak. This underscores a vital lesson for residents: when thoracic symptoms are recurrent and refractory, a comprehensive review of the patient's recent trauma and abdominal history is essential to uncover occult pathologies that may be masquerading as pulmonary disease.

## Pathophysiology of Pancreatopleural Fistulas (PPF)

A pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is an abnormal communication that allows pancreatic secretions to leak from the pancreas into the pleural space. The development of this fistula depends on the site of the initial pancreatic injury and the anatomical path of least resistance. When a pancreatic duct or a pancreatic pseudocyst ruptures in the anterior portion of the pancreas, the fluid typically leaks into the peritoneal cavity, resulting in pancreatic ascites. However, if the rupture occurs in the posterior aspect of the pancreas, the enzyme-rich fluid leaks into the retroperitoneal space.

Once in the retroperitoneum, the fluid tracks superiorly toward the mediastinum. The most common conduits for this migration are the natural diaphragmatic hiatuses—specifically the esophageal hiatus or the aortic hiatus. In this case, MRCP confirmed that the pancreatic fluid tracked from the tail of the pancreas, through the esophageal hiatus, and subsequently entered the right pleural cavity. This "internal fistula" acts as a leaky pipe, continuously diverting pancreatic secretions into the chest, which manifests clinically as recurrent, often blood-stained, pleural effusions.

The etiology of PPF differs significantly between pediatric and adult populations. In adults, the overwhelming majority of cases are secondary to chronic alcoholic pancreatitis. In children, however, the triggers are typically non-alcoholic. As demonstrated in this case, blunt abdominal trauma—such as the injury caused by a bicycle handle—can cause a mechanical disruption of the pancreatic duct, leading to the formation of a fistula. In other pediatric cases, PPF may arise from hereditary chronic pancreatitis. Genetic mutations, specifically in the *PRSS1* (cationic trypsinogen) and *SPINK1* (serine protease inhibitor Kazal-type 1) genes, can predispose children to recurrent pancreatitis and subsequent ductal rupture.

The progression from pancreatic inflammation to a pleural fistula often involves the formation of a pancreatic pseudocyst. A pseudocyst is a collection of pancreatic juice enclosed by a wall of fibrous or granulation tissue. When such a collection ruptures or erodes through the retroperitoneal tissues, it creates the initial pathway that allows fluid to migrate toward the thorax. Because the pleural fluid in these patients contains high concentrations of amylase and lipase from the pancreas, the pleural space becomes a reservoir for these caustic enzymes, leading to the respiratory distress and pleural inflammation seen in this patient.

## Diagnostic Workup and Clinical Reasoning

The diagnostic trajectory of this case illustrates the "pulmonary mask" often associated with pancreatopleural fistulas (PPF), where the primary pathology resides in the abdomen but manifests exclusively as thoracic distress. Initial clinical reasoning focused on pulmonary etiologies, such as pulmonary tuberculosis (Koch's), driven by the patient's history of family contact and a mildly elevated adenosine deaminase (ADA) level in the pleural fluid. However, the recurrence of massive pleural effusions despite empirical treatment necessitated a broader diagnostic search. The pivotal moment in the clinical reasoning occurred when a detailed history revealed a forgotten episode of blunt abdominal trauma caused by a bicycle handle, shifting the suspicion toward a pancreatic origin.

The initial imaging hierarchy followed a standard approach for respiratory distress. Chest X-rays and bedside lung ultrasound were used to identify and quantify the pleural effusion, confirming approximately one liter of fluid. While these tools were essential for immediate symptom relief via thoracocentesis, they were non-specific. The transition to a contrast-enhanced CT (CECT) was critical; it moved the diagnosis beyond the chest by identifying a multiloculated hypodense collection tracking from the right paraesophageal region to the tail of the pancreas. This finding provided the first anatomical evidence of a communication between the retroperitoneum and the pleural space, suggesting that pancreatic secretions were following the path of least resistance through the esophageal hiatus.

Biochemical analysis of the pleural fluid provided the definitive diagnostic markers. The aspirate was notably dark red, suggesting a complex or hemorrhagic effusion, which can be seen in PPF due to the enzymatic degradation of tissues and associated inflammation. The pathognomonic finding was the significantly elevated concentration of amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L) within the pleural fluid. In clinical practice, a pleural fluid amylase level $\ge 1000$ IU/L is strongly indicative of PPF. While other conditions—such as esophageal perforation (Boerhaave’s syndrome) or certain malignancies—can cause amylase-rich effusions, these typically involve salivary-type amylase, whereas PPF is characterized by pancreatic-type amylase.

To refine the anatomical diagnosis and plan management, Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) was employed. MRCP allowed for the visualization of the distal pancreatic duct, confirming its communication with a cystic collection that tracked superiorly into the right pleural cavity. While MRCP is highly effective for visualizing the fistula and ductal anatomy, Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) remains the gold standard for both definitive fistula visualization and therapeutic planning, particularly when surgical or endoscopic stenting is required to reduce intraductal pressure. In this case, the combination of the trauma history, the characteristic "tracking" on CT, the MRCP findings, and the extreme elevation of pleural amylase and lipase allowed for a definitive diagnosis of PPF.

## Differential Diagnosis of Amylase-Rich Pleural Effusions

When a clinician encounters a pleural effusion with significantly elevated amylase levels—particularly levels $\ge 1000$ IU/L—pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is a primary consideration. However, amylase-rich fluid is not pathognomonic for PPF, and several thoracic and abdominal pathologies can mimic this presentation. Distinguishing between these entities requires a combination of biochemical analysis, detailed patient history, and targeted imaging.

A critical diagnostic tool in narrowing the differential is amylase typing. Amylase exists in two primary forms: pancreatic-type and salivary-type. In PPF, the pleural fluid contains pancreatic amylase, which leaks from a disrupted pancreatic duct or pseudocyst into the pleural space. In contrast, other conditions, such as certain malignancies or esophageal ruptures, may result in the accumulation of salivary-type amylase. While the case report focuses on the absolute elevation of amylase and lipase, performing an amylase type can definitively distinguish a pancreatic source from non-pancreatic sources of amylase.

In this case, the patient’s history of contact with a relative with pulmonary tuberculosis (Koch’s disease) and a slightly elevated adenosine deaminase (ADA) level in the pleural fluid initially suggested a tubercular effusion. ADA is a sensitive marker for TB but lacks specificity, as it can be elevated in various inflammatory or infectious pleural processes. The diagnosis of pulmonary tuberculosis was ruled out in this patient through the use of GeneXpert MTB/RI, which is a highly specific molecular test for *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* DNA. This highlights the importance of utilizing specific molecular diagnostics over nonspecific markers like ADA when a patient presents with a complex history.

Esophageal perforation, specifically Boerhaave’s syndrome, is another significant mimic that can produce amylase-rich pleural fluid due to the leakage of saliva and esophageal secretions. Clinically, Boerhaave’s syndrome is typically characterized by a sudden onset of severe retrosternal chest pain, often following forceful vomiting or retching. In this case, the absence of such a history and the fact that the child was passing stools normally and lacked acute vomiting symptoms allowed the clinicians to rule out esophageal rupture.

Malignancies, including primary lung cancer, leukemia, lymphoma, or metastatic tumors, can also present with pleural effusions and elevated amylase. To differentiate these from PPF, clinicians must evaluate for systemic "red flags" such as unexplained weight loss, chronic fever, or lymphadenopathy. In this patient, the absence of constitutional symptoms, a normal peripheral blood smear, and negative pleural fluid cytology effectively excluded malignancy.

Finally, other thoracic-abdominal communications must be considered. Hepatic hydrothorax, caused by the movement of peritoneal fluid through diaphragmatic defects in patients with cirrhosis, can cause effusions, but this was ruled out by normal liver function tests and imaging. Similarly, an acquired diaphragmatic hernia could allow abdominal contents to enter the chest; however, the CT and MRCP in this case showed no displaced abdominal organs, but rather a specific fistulous tract tracking from the pancreatic tail through the esophageal hiatus. By systematically ruling out these mimics through imaging and biochemical markers, the diagnosis of PPF was confirmed.

## Management Strategies and Clinical Outcomes

The management of pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) typically follows a tiered approach, beginning with conservative medical therapy and escalating to endoscopic or surgical interventions if the fistula fails to close. Because PPF is exceptionally rare in children, there are no standardized guidelines, and treatment is often guided by individual case reports. In this case, the patient was managed successfully using a conservative regimen.

Conservative management focuses on reducing the secretory burden of the pancreas to allow the fistulous tract to close. The primary components of this approach include bowel rest through "nil per oral" (NPO) status and the provision of Total Parenteral Nutrition (TPN) to maintain nutritional status. To actively suppress the exocrine function of the pancreas, somatostatin analogues are utilized; this patient received an Octreotide infusion at a dose of 30 $\mu$g/kg/day. To compensate for the lack of endogenous pancreatic enzymes during bowel rest and to support overall recovery, Pancreatic Enzyme Replacement Therapy (PERT) was also initiated. Concurrent with these medical therapies, thoracic drainage via an intercostal drain (ICD) was necessary to evacuate the blood-stained pleural fluid and resolve the patient's respiratory distress.

When conservative measures fail—which occurs in a significant portion of pediatric cases—endoscopic intervention is the next step. Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) can be used for trans-papillary stenting or balloon dilatation. These procedures aim to reduce intraductal pressure, thereby decreasing the volume of pancreatic secretions leaking into the pleural space. If the fistula is refractory to both medical and endoscopic therapy, or if there is severe ductal obstruction, surgical options are considered. These include a distal pancreatectomy, a Puestow procedure (pancreatojejunostomy), or a cystojejunostomy to divert the pancreatic secretions.

The clinical outcome for this patient was positive, demonstrating the efficacy of the conservative approach. Over a 10-day period, the volume of fluid draining through the ICD gradually decreased until it stopped completely, allowing for the removal of the chest tube and the patient's discharge. During follow-up, the child remained symptom-free and returned to all normal school and physical activities. Long-term management for this patient emphasized lifestyle modifications, specifically the adoption of a balanced diet and regular exercise, to maintain general health and monitor for any signs of recurrence.
