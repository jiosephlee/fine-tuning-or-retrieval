Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

### [Pathophysiology] The SRA in this case showed only partial inhibition of serotonin release when a platelet Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3) was added. What is the significance of Fc receptor blockade in diagnosing HIT, and what does this partial resistance imply about the patient's antibodies?

In the diagnosis of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), the serotonin-release assay (SRA) remains the gold standard for assessing platelet activation. The use of the monoclonal antibody IV.3—which specifically blocks the platelet Fc$\gamma$RIIa receptor—is a critical diagnostic step to confirm that the observed platelet activation is mediated by the immune-complex-Fc receptor pathway. In classic HIT, IgG antibodies form complexes with platelet factor 4 (PF4) and heparin; these complexes then bind to and cluster Fc$\gamma$RIIa receptors on the platelet surface, triggering the release of serotonin. Consequently, the addition of IV.3 should nearly abolish serotonin release (typically reducing it to <20%), proving that the activation is indeed Fc receptor-dependent.

In this case, the patient exhibited a highly atypical SRA profile. While the serum caused strong serotonin release both in the absence of heparin (a hallmark of autoimmune HIT, or aHIT) and in the presence of low-dose heparin, the addition of IV.3 only partially inhibited this response, leaving 76% of serotonin release intact. 

This partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade implies that the patient’s antibodies possess a uniquely potent or "unusually reactive" serological phenotype. While aHIT antibodies typically still rely on the Fc receptor for activation—even if they no longer require heparin to bind PF4—the lack of complete inhibition by IV.3 suggests a degree of activation that exceeds the blocking capacity of the monoclonal antibody. Clinically, this mirrored the patient's severe presentation, characterized by a profound platelet nadir (13×10⁹/L) and a poor response to high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin, highlighting a hyper-aggressive form of aHIT that transcends the typical laboratory and clinical patterns of the disease.
