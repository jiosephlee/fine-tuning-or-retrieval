Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# The CA-125 Trap: Interpreting Biomarkers in Pregnancy

Imagine you’re in the ER or clinic and you encounter a patient in her second trimester of pregnancy presenting with severe abdominal pain and dyspnea. On exam, her abdomen is massively distended—far beyond what you’d expect for 21 weeks. Imaging reveals two huge cystic masses: one filling the left subphrenic space and another occupying the pouch of Douglas. Then comes the lab result: the CA-125 is elevated and trending upward.

In almost any other clinical context, this constellation of findings—massive adnexal-appearing masses and a rising CA-125—would send an oncological alarm bell ringing through the entire multidisciplinary team. But in the context of pregnancy, this is exactly where the "CA-125 trap" lies.

## The Case: Massive Masses and Mimics

This case involves a nulliparous woman in her 40s at 21 weeks gestation. She presented with acute abdominal pain and a restrictive-pattern dyspnea caused by the sheer "steric bulk" of her abdominal contents. Physical exam revealed a taut, elastic 18 cm mass in the left upper quadrant and a 9 cm fixed mass in the pelvis.

Imaging was complex. Ultrasound and non-contrast MRI showed two unilocular cystic masses. The lower mass seemed adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma. The upper mass was more ambiguous, mimicking a decidualized endometrioma. When the CA-125 came back at 41.8 U/mL (normal <35) and was noted to be increasing from a previous value of 36 U/mL, the suspicion for an ovarian malignancy increased.

However, because of the worsening pain, the risk of torsion or rupture, and the mechanical restriction of her breathing, the team opted for a laparotomy. The surprise? Both masses were uterine leiomyomas. The cranial mass was a huge, pedunculated leiomyoma that had actually twisted and begun leaking—which explained the low-grade fever she developed during her admission.

## The Physiology: Why CA-125 Lies

CA-125 is a mainstay for monitoring ovarian cancer, but its specificity plummets in the presence of peritoneal irritation or physiological changes in the uterus. In pregnancy, CA-125 follows a non-linear path. It typically peaks in the first trimester, dips in the second, and rises again in the third. 

In this case, the patient's value of 41.8 U/mL was higher than the median for the second trimester, but it was far from diagnostic of malignancy. CA-125 can be elevated by a laundry list of benign conditions: endometriosis, adenomyosis, pelvic inflammatory disease, and—crucially—leiomyomas.

When we see a rising CA-125 in a pregnant patient, we have to ask: is this a marker of cancer, or is it a marker of "abdominal chaos"? In this patient, the marker was likely reflecting the presence of massive myomas and the inflammatory response to a twisting pedicle, rather than an epithelial ovarian tumor.

## Management Nuances

The surgical management of myomas during pregnancy is a high-stakes balancing act. Generally, we avoid surgery to prevent preterm labor or uterine rupture. But when you have a pedunculated mass that is torsioning, the risk of necrosis and sepsis outweighs the risk of the surgery.

The surgeons in this case performed a targeted myomectomy of the upper mass but left the caudal mass in situ. This is a critical clinical pearl: removing every mass isn't always the goal. By leaving the pelvic mass alone, the team minimized the risk to the pregnancy. Because that residual mass would have blocked the birth canal, an elective C-section was performed at 37 weeks, resulting in an excellent outcome for both mother and baby.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **CA-125 is not a binary switch:** In pregnancy, isolated elevations of CA-125 are common and non-specific. Trend data is more useful than a single value, but neither replaces histology.
*   **The "Steric Bulk" Effect:** Large abdominal masses in pregnancy can cause restrictive lung disease and dyspnea. If a pregnant patient is short of breath but has normal O2 sats and a clear chest X-ray, look at the abdomen.
*   **Myomas are Chameleons:** Uterine leiomyomas can undergo cystic degeneration, making them look exactly like ovarian cysts or endometriomas on ultrasound and MRI.
*   **Surgical Restraint:** In the second trimester, the goal is pregnancy preservation. If a mass is causing acute complications (torsion/necrosis), intervene; otherwise, consider leaving non-symptomatic masses until the postpartum period.
*   **MRI Safety:** Non-contrast MRI is a safe and powerful tool for differentiating intramural from adnexal masses when ultrasound is inconclusive.

**Bottom Line:** Don't let a slightly elevated CA-125 drive you straight to an oncological diagnosis in pregnancy. Consider the physiological shifts of gestation and the propensity of leiomyomas to mimic malignancy.



# Dyspnea in the Second Trimester: When it's Not a Pulmonary Embolism

We’ve all seen the "pregnant patient with shortness of breath" in the ED. The clinical algorithm usually kicks into high gear: we worry about pulmonary embolism (the classic hypercoagulable state), we check for peripartum cardiomyopathy, or we attribute it to the normal physiological shift of the diaphragm as the uterus grows. 

But every now and then, you encounter a case that reminds you that the simplest explanation—mechanical obstruction—can be the most extreme. Imagine a patient where the "abdominal pressure" isn't just from a growing fetus, but from a massive amount of "steric bulk" that effectively puts the lungs in a vice.

## The Presentation: More Than Just "Pregnant"

A woman in her 40s, G1P0A1, presented at 21 weeks gestation with severe abdominal pain in the upper left quadrant and pelvis, accompanied by mild dyspnea. She was obese (BMI 33), which often complicates the physical exam, but the objective finding was striking: her abdomen was significantly larger than expected for 21 weeks. 

On exam, she had a taut-elastic mass measuring 18 cm occupying the entire upper left quadrant and a separate, hard 9 cm fixed mass in the pelvis. While her oxygen saturation was stable at 97%, the dyspnea was real. Interestingly, simply placing her in a semi-orthostatic position—lifting the head and torso—reduced her symptoms and pain. This is a huge clinical clue: when a patient’s respiratory distress is relieved by gravity shifting the abdominal contents downward, you are dealing with a restrictive mechanical process, not a primary pulmonary or cardiac failure.

## The Diagnostic Dilemma: The CA-125 Red Herring

The imaging was a challenge. Ultrasound showed two large unilocular cystic masses. One extended from the subphrenic region down to the uterine fundus; the other sat in the pouch of Douglas. The team also saw an intramural-subserosal myoma with cystic degeneration. 

Then came the lab work. The CA-125 was elevated at 41.8 U/mL. In a non-pregnant patient, we might jump to ovarian malignancy or endometriosis. However, in the FOAM world, we need to remember the "pregnancy baseline." CA-125 physiologically rises during the first and third trimesters. An isolated, slightly elevated value in the second trimester is often a distractor. 

To get a better look without risking the fetus, the team used a non-contrast MRI. This showed thick walls and inhomogeneous fluid, but the "cleavage" of the masses wasn't clear, making it difficult to tell if these were adnexal cysts (like decidualized endometriomas) or something arising from the uterus itself.

## The "Steric Bulk" and the Surgical Decision

The patient eventually developed a low-grade fever, and the clinical picture was worsening. The "steric bulk" was no longer just a diagnostic curiosity; it was causing restrictive lung disease and threatening the pregnancy. 

The team faced a classic surgical crossroads: do you wait until after delivery to avoid the risks of mid-pregnancy surgery (like preterm labor), or do you operate now to prevent a catastrophe? Given the size of the masses and the risk of torsion or necrosis, they opted for a laparotomy at 21 weeks and 6 days.

The intraoperative finding was the "zebra": the cranial mass was actually a huge, pedunculated, atypical cystic leiomyoma. It had twisted on its stalk and was leaking, which explained the fever. The surgical team excised the upper mass but wisely left the caudal mass (another leiomyoma) in situ to minimize the risk of triggering a miscarriage or preterm birth.

## The Outcome and the Lesson

The patient was managed with progesterone prophylaxis to support the pregnancy and intravenous iron for anemia. She eventually had an elective C-section at 37 weeks because the remaining pelvic myoma was so large it would have blocked the fetal head from descending. Both mother and baby did excellently.

This case highlights a critical point in the differential of dyspnea in pregnancy. While we must rule out the "killers" (PE, heart failure), we cannot ignore the physical space. Massive abdominal masses can create a restrictive ventilatory defect that mimics systemic illness.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **The "Gravity Test":** If a pregnant patient's dyspnea improves significantly with semi-orthostatic positioning, think about mechanical diaphragmatic compression (steric bulk) rather than just pulmonary pathology.
*   **CA-125 in Pregnancy:** Treat isolated CA-125 elevations with caution. Because it fluctuates physiologically during pregnancy, trends are more important than a single snapshot.
*   **MRI Safety:** Non-contrast MRI is a safe and superior tool for characterizing complex pelvic masses in the second and third trimesters when ultrasound is insufficient.
*   **Surgical Timing:** While the gold standard is to manage myomas post-partum, surgical intervention is indicated during pregnancy if there is evidence of torsion, necrosis, or severe symptomatic compression of vital organs.
*   **Delivery Planning:** The location and size of uterine masses dictate the mode of delivery; a large posterior pelvic mass can act as a physical barrier to vaginal delivery, making elective C-section the safer choice.



# To Cut or Not to Cut: The Timing of Myomectomy During Pregnancy

Imagine you’re in the ER and a woman in her 40s, 21 weeks pregnant, presents with severe left upper quadrant pain and dyspnoea. On exam, her abdomen is significantly larger than expected for her gestational age, and you feel a massive, taut-elastic mass occupying the entire left subphrenic space. Now you're facing a classic clinical tension: do you leave it alone to protect the pregnancy, or do you intervene to save the mother from a surgical catastrophe?

The "gold standard" teaching for uterine leiomyomas (fibroids) during pregnancy is generally conservative. We are taught to wait until the postpartum period because the risks of intraoperative hemorrhage, preterm labor, and uterine rupture are daunting. However, this case reminds us that "conservative" isn't always "safe."

In this patient, the diagnostic workup was a bit of a rollercoaster. Ultrasound and non-contrast MRI showed two large, unilocular cystic masses. One was 18 cm, stretching from the diaphragm to the uterine fundus; the other was about 9 cm in the pelvis. The differential was wide: were these decidualized endometriomas or degenerating leiomyomas? A slightly elevated CA-125 (41.8 U/mL) teased a diagnosis of endometrioma or malignancy, but as many of us know, CA-125 is a fickle marker in pregnancy, often peaking in the first and third trimesters.

The team eventually hit a tipping point. The patient wasn't just in pain; she was experiencing restrictive lung disease symptoms because the mass was essentially shoving her diaphragm into her chest. When you add a low-grade fever and the inherent risk of torsion or rupture of a mass that size, the risk of *not* operating began to outweigh the risks of surgery.

The intraoperative findings provided the definitive answer. The cranial mass was a huge, pedunculated leiomyoma that had actually twisted on its stalk and was leaking—explaining that low-grade fever. In a strategic move to balance maternal safety with fetal viability, the surgeons performed a selective myomectomy. They excised the twisted upper mass (the immediate threat) but left the pelvic mass in situ to minimize the risk of triggering preterm labor. To further protect the pregnancy, they used intraoperative and postoperative progesterone prophylaxis and maintained constant monitoring of the fetal heart rate.

The outcome was a success: a healthy baby delivered via elective C-section at 37 weeks. The C-section was necessary not just because of the previous surgical scar, but because the residual pelvic myoma would have acted as a physical blockade to the fetal head, making a vaginal delivery nearly impossible.

This case highlights that while we generally avoid the knife during pregnancy, there are "hard stops" where surgery becomes mandatory. Respiratory compromise, signs of torsion, and acute necrosis are not conditions you can "wait and see" through.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **The CA-125 Trap:** Be cautious with CA-125 in pregnant patients. It can be physiologically elevated; serial trends are more useful than a single isolated value.
*   **Imaging Safety:** Non-contrast MRI is a powerful and safe tool for characterizing complex pelvic masses in the second and third trimesters when ultrasound is insufficient.
*   **Indications for Urgent Myomectomy:** While conservative management is the norm, surgery is indicated for pedunculated myoma torsion, rupture, or severe symptomatic compression (e.g., respiratory distress).
*   **Strategic Surgery:** If multiple masses are present, consider a "risk-stratified" approach—remove the mass at risk for torsion or necrosis, but leave stable intramural masses to reduce the risk of preterm labor.
*   **Delivery Planning:** Large lower-segment or pelvic myomas are significant obstacles to fetal descent; these patients should be flagged early for a planned cesarean delivery.



# Seeing Through the Gravid Uterus: US vs. MRI for Pelvic Masses

Imagine a patient in her 40s, 21 weeks pregnant, presenting to your ED with severe abdominal pain and dyspnea. On exam, she looks significantly more advanced in her pregnancy than the GA suggests. You feel a massive, taut, 18cm mass occupying the entire left upper quadrant and another 9cm hard mass in the pelvis. Now you have a dilemma: how do you figure out what these are and where they are coming from without compromising the fetus?

This is the "steric bulk" problem. When the uterus is gravid, it doesn't just take up space; it obscures anatomy, displaces organs, and makes the "stalk" of a pedunculated mass nearly impossible to find on standard imaging.

## The Imaging Struggle: US vs. MRI

In this case, the initial ultrasound (US) showed two large, unilocular cystic masses. The differential was broad: were these huge decidualized endometriomas or cystic degenerating leiomyomas (fibroids)? While US is our first-line tool because it's safe and fast, it has a major blind spot here. The sheer volume of the pregnant uterus can hide the connective stalk or the feeding vessels of a mass, making it difficult to tell if a lesion is ovarian or uterine.

This is where non-contrast MRI becomes the heavy lifter. By utilizing T1 and T2 fat-sat weighted imaging, the team was able to see thick walls and inhomogeneous fluid content. More importantly, MRI provides the spatial resolution needed to determine the distance between a mass and the uterine cavity. In this patient, the MRI suggested the lower mass was in continuity with a posterior leiomyoma, while the upper mass remained an enigma—appearing as an adnexal cyst.

## The CA-125 Trap

One of the most interesting parts of this case is the use of tumor markers. The patient's CA-125 was 41.8 U/mL (normal <35). In a non-pregnant patient, this might raise a red flag for ovarian malignancy. However, in the FOAM world, we need to remember that pregnancy is a physiological whirlwind. 

CA-125 levels naturally rise during the first and third trimesters. A single "slightly high" number in the second trimester is often a clinical red herring. While serial trends are more useful than isolated values, the physiological fluctuations of pregnancy mean that CA-125 has significantly lower specificity during this period.

## When to Cut: The Surgical Dilemma

The general rule for uterine myomas during pregnancy is "hands off" until after delivery to avoid hemorrhage, preterm labor, or uterine rupture. But this case highlights the exceptions. When you have a patient with restrictive lung disease (dyspnea) due to mass effect, acute pain, and a high risk of torsion or necrosis, conservative management is no longer the safest bet.

The surgical team performed a laparotomy and found that the "upper cyst" was actually a massive, pedunculated, atypical cystic leiomyoma that had already begun to twist and leak. This explains the patient's low-grade fever and pain. 

Crucially, the surgeons practiced "surgical restraint." They excised the twisted upper mass but left the caudal mass in situ. Why? Because the risk of manipulating a fixed mass in the pelvis during the second trimester often outweighs the benefit. By leaving the pelvic mass alone, they minimized the risk of triggering preterm labor, though they knew it would necessitate an elective C-section later because the mass would block the fetal head from descending.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **The "Steric Bulk" Effect:** In pregnancy, US may fail to identify the pedicle of a mass because the gravid uterus displaces and obscures the anatomy. If the origin is unclear and symptoms are severe, non-contrast MRI is the gold standard for mapping the mass's relationship to the uterine cavity.
*   **CA-125 is a Liar in Pregnancy:** Do not rely on a single CA-125 value to diagnose malignancy in a pregnant patient; physiological peaks occur in the first and third trimesters.
*   **Surgical Strategy:** Myomectomy during pregnancy is reserved for complications (torsion, necrosis, or severe symptomatic compression). If multiple masses are present, prioritize removing the "dangerous" one (e.g., the twisted pedunculated mass) and leave stable intramural masses for the postpartum period or the time of C-section.
*   **Delivery Planning:** Large posterior uterine masses are mechanical obstacles. If a mass is located in the isthmus or posterior wall, plan for an elective C-section to avoid failure to progress or uterine rupture during labor.



# Blocking the Exit: How Myoma Location Dictates Delivery Mode

We often think of uterine leiomyomas (myomas) as "incidentalomas" of pregnancy—things we find on ultrasound and generally ignore unless they cause excruciating degeneration. But every so often, we encounter a case where the anatomy of the mass transforms it from a benign bystander into a mechanical obstacle. 

The "aha!" moment in this case isn't just that the patient had massive tumors; it's the strategic decision of what to remove during the second trimester and what to leave behind, and how that decision fundamentally changed the delivery plan.

## The Presentation: More Than Just a "Big Belly"

A nulliparous woman in her 40s presented at 21 weeks gestation with severe abdominal pain and dyspnea. On exam, her abdomen was significantly more enlarged than expected for her gestational age. She had a palpable, tender 18 cm mass in the left upper quadrant and a fixed, hard 9 cm mass in the pelvis. 

When a pregnant patient presents with dyspnea and abdominal masses, your differential is broad, but the immediate concern is mechanical: diaphragmatic compression and restrictive lung disease caused by "steric bulk."

## The Diagnostic Dilemma: CA-125 and Imaging

The imaging was complex. Ultrasound showed two large cystic masses. The upper one was huge (18 cm), and the lower one appeared adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma. 

Here is a clinical pearl: **Be careful with CA-125 in pregnancy.** In this patient, the CA-125 was slightly elevated at 41.8 U/mL. While this often flags ovarian malignancy or endometriosis, CA-125 physiologically rises during the first and third trimesters. A single "positive" value in a pregnant patient is rarely diagnostic on its own.

To refine the diagnosis without risking the fetus with gadolinium, the team used non-contrast MRI. The MRI suggested the masses were likely adnexal cysts or decidualized endometriomas, but the caudal mass remained suspiciously linked to the uterine wall.

## The Surgical Pivot: What to Cut and What to Leave

At 21 weeks, the patient's condition worsened. Due to the risk of torsion, rupture, or infection—and the fact that the masses were already compromising her breathing—the team opted for a laparotomy.

Intraoperatively, the truth came out: the upper mass was actually a giant, pedunculated, atypical cystic leiomyoma that had already begun to twist and leak. The surgical team excised this mass to save the pregnancy and relieve the patient's symptoms. 

However, they encountered a second mass in the pelvis (the caudal mass). Despite its size, the team made a critical decision: **they left the caudal myoma in situ.** Why? Because operating on a fixed, intramural mass during the second trimester carries a high risk of uterine hemorrhage and pregnancy loss. If the mass isn't acutely threatening the life of the mother or fetus, the safest move is often to leave it alone.

## Blocking the Exit: The Logic of the C-Section

This leads us to the core of the delivery strategy. While the upper mass was gone, the caudal mass remained, rooted in the posterior uterine wall near the isthmic site. 

In the expulsive phase of labor, the fetal head must navigate the pelvic inlet and outlet. A large, hard, posterior intramural myoma acts as a physical blockade. No matter how well the labor progresses or how strong the contractions are, the "exit" is physically obstructed. 

Because this mass was positioned exactly where the fetus needs to pass, the team scheduled an elective caesarean section at 37 weeks. By using the same surgical scar from the previous laparotomy, they minimized further trauma and avoided the high probability of obstructed labor and fetal distress.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **Anatomy > Size:** The size of a myoma is important, but its *location* dictates the delivery mode. A large myoma in the fundus may not hinder delivery, but a medium-sized myoma in the lower uterine segment or posterior wall often mandates a C-section.
*   **CA-125 Pitfalls:** Remember that CA-125 is non-specific in pregnancy. Serial trends are more useful than a single value.
*   **Conservative Surgery:** In the second trimester, the goal of myomectomy is to resolve acute complications (like torsion) while avoiding unnecessary manipulation of the uterine wall to prevent preterm labor.
*   **MRI Safety:** Non-contrast MRI is a safe and superior tool for characterizing complex pelvic masses when ultrasound is limited by the "steric bulk" of the gravid uterus.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Identify Mechanical Risks:** Large abdominal masses in pregnancy can cause restrictive lung disease and dyspnea.
*   **Strategic Myomectomy:** Only excise pedunculated or complicated masses during pregnancy; leave intramural masses alone unless absolutely necessary.
*   **Predict Obstructed Labor:** Lower segment or posterior wall myomas "block the exit," making elective C-section the safest route for delivery.
