Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# To Cut or Not to Cut: The Timing of Myomectomy During Pregnancy

Imagine you’re in the ER and a woman in her 40s, 21 weeks pregnant, presents with severe left upper quadrant pain and dyspnoea. On exam, her abdomen is significantly larger than expected for her gestational age, and you feel a massive, taut-elastic mass occupying the entire left subphrenic space. Now you're facing a classic clinical tension: do you leave it alone to protect the pregnancy, or do you intervene to save the mother from a surgical catastrophe?

The "gold standard" teaching for uterine leiomyomas (fibroids) during pregnancy is generally conservative. We are taught to wait until the postpartum period because the risks of intraoperative hemorrhage, preterm labor, and uterine rupture are daunting. However, this case reminds us that "conservative" isn't always "safe."

In this patient, the diagnostic workup was a bit of a rollercoaster. Ultrasound and non-contrast MRI showed two large, unilocular cystic masses. One was 18 cm, stretching from the diaphragm to the uterine fundus; the other was about 9 cm in the pelvis. The differential was wide: were these decidualized endometriomas or degenerating leiomyomas? A slightly elevated CA-125 (41.8 U/mL) teased a diagnosis of endometrioma or malignancy, but as many of us know, CA-125 is a fickle marker in pregnancy, often peaking in the first and third trimesters.

The team eventually hit a tipping point. The patient wasn't just in pain; she was experiencing restrictive lung disease symptoms because the mass was essentially shoving her diaphragm into her chest. When you add a low-grade fever and the inherent risk of torsion or rupture of a mass that size, the risk of *not* operating began to outweigh the risks of surgery.

The intraoperative findings provided the definitive answer. The cranial mass was a huge, pedunculated leiomyoma that had actually twisted on its stalk and was leaking—explaining that low-grade fever. In a strategic move to balance maternal safety with fetal viability, the surgeons performed a selective myomectomy. They excised the twisted upper mass (the immediate threat) but left the pelvic mass in situ to minimize the risk of triggering preterm labor. To further protect the pregnancy, they used intraoperative and postoperative progesterone prophylaxis and maintained constant monitoring of the fetal heart rate.

The outcome was a success: a healthy baby delivered via elective C-section at 37 weeks. The C-section was necessary not just because of the previous surgical scar, but because the residual pelvic myoma would have acted as a physical blockade to the fetal head, making a vaginal delivery nearly impossible.

This case highlights that while we generally avoid the knife during pregnancy, there are "hard stops" where surgery becomes mandatory. Respiratory compromise, signs of torsion, and acute necrosis are not conditions you can "wait and see" through.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **The CA-125 Trap:** Be cautious with CA-125 in pregnant patients. It can be physiologically elevated; serial trends are more useful than a single isolated value.
*   **Imaging Safety:** Non-contrast MRI is a powerful and safe tool for characterizing complex pelvic masses in the second and third trimesters when ultrasound is insufficient.
*   **Indications for Urgent Myomectomy:** While conservative management is the norm, surgery is indicated for pedunculated myoma torsion, rupture, or severe symptomatic compression (e.g., respiratory distress).
*   **Strategic Surgery:** If multiple masses are present, consider a "risk-stratified" approach—remove the mass at risk for torsion or necrosis, but leave stable intramural masses to reduce the risk of preterm labor.
*   **Delivery Planning:** Large lower-segment or pelvic myomas are significant obstacles to fetal descent; these patients should be flagged early for a planned cesarean delivery.
