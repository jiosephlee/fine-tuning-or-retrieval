Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Morning Report] A patient presents with high fever, septic shock, and pancytopenia four days after a dog bite. Which fastidious, gram-negative organism should be suspected, and what are its characteristic clinical presentations?

In a patient presenting with high fever, septic shock, and pancytopenia following a dog bite, the clinician should strongly suspect ***Capnocytophaga canimorsus***. 

*Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is a fastidious, slow-growing, encapsulated, gram-negative rod that resides as normal oropharyngeal flora in dogs and cats. Transmission to humans typically occurs via bites, scratches, or close contact. Due to its slow growth in culture, diagnosis can be delayed, often requiring a high index of suspicion or the use of molecular methods like real-time nanopore sequencing.

The clinical presentation of *C. canimorsus* is highly variable, ranging from isolated meningitis to fulminant sepsis. In severe cases, as seen in this patient, the organism can trigger a catastrophic clinical course characterized by:
*   **Septic Shock and Multiorgan Failure:** Rapidly progressive sepsis often accompanied by metabolic acidosis and lactic acidosis.
*   **Hematologic Derangements:** Severe pancytopenia (particularly leukopenia) and coagulopathy consistent with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC).
*   **Thrombotic Microangiopathy (TMA):** While often obscured by the initial sepsis and DIC phase, *C. canimorsus* can act as a rare trigger for complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). This manifests as microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), persistent thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury, often progressing to anuria and dialysis dependence.

Despite generally broad antibiotic susceptibility, the mortality rate for invasive *C. canimorsus* infections can approach 30%, necessitating aggressive supportive care and timely antimicrobial therapy.
