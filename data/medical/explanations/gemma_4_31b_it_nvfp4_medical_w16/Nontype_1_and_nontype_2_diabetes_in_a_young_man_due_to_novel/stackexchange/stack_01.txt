Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Differential Diagnosis] A young patient presents with hyperglycemia, a strong autosomal dominant family history of diabetes, and no signs of insulin resistance. Which clinical and laboratory findings would most strongly suggest Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY) over Type 1 or Type 2 Diabetes?

In cases of early-onset diabetes, distinguishing Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY) from Type 1 (T1D) and Type 2 Diabetes (T2D) is a frequent diagnostic challenge due to overlapping clinical presentations. When faced with a young patient who has hyperglycemia and a strong autosomal dominant family history but lacks signs of insulin resistance, the following clinical and laboratory findings most strongly suggest a diagnosis of MODY:

**1. Absence of Autoimmunity and Preserved $\beta$-cell Function**
The primary laboratory distinction between MODY and T1D is the absence of pancreatic autoantibodies. In this case, the patient tested negative for glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA) and insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibodies (IA-2A). Furthermore, while T1D is characterized by an absolute insulin deficiency, MODY typically exhibits preserved endogenous insulin secretion. A stimulated C-peptide level (e.g., via glucagon stimulation test) greater than 0.60 nmol/L, as seen in this patient (0.83 nmol/L), points away from T1D and toward a monogenic or Type 2 etiology.

**2. Lack of Insulin Resistance Markers**
Unlike typical T2D, which is strongly associated with obesity and metabolic syndrome, MODY patients often lack these features. The absence of acanthosis nigricans and a relatively normal BMI (24.6 $\text{kg/m}^2$ in this patient) decrease the likelihood of T2D, although it is important to note that rising obesity rates in the general population can occasionally blur this distinction.

**3. Specific Glycemic and Renal Patterns**
Certain MODY subtypes, particularly HNF1A-MODY, present with specific biochemical hallmarks:
*   **Exaggerated Postprandial Glucose:** A significant increase in glucose during a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT)—such as the 19.8 mmol/L seen here—is characteristic of the progressive $\beta$-cell dysfunction in HNF1A-MODY.
*   **Low Renal Glucose Threshold:** The presence of glycosuria even in the absence of severe hyperglycemia is a key clue. In HNF1A-MODY, mutations reduce the expression of SGLT2 in the renal tubules, lowering the threshold for glucose reabsorption.

**4. Strong Vertical Family History**
While T2D often has a family component, MODY is characterized by a clear autosomal dominant transmission pattern. The pedigree in this case—with diabetes appearing in the maternal grandmother, mother, and brother—is highly suggestive of a single-gene mutation rather than the polygenic inheritance of T2D.

**Conclusion and Gold Standard**
When these clinical "red flags" are present, the gold standard for diagnosis is **molecular genetic testing** via next-generation sequencing (NGS). In this patient, genetic testing confirmed a novel pathogenic variant in the *HNF1A* gene. Precision diagnosis is clinically imperative; as demonstrated here, identifying HNF1A-MODY allowed the transition from insulin therapy to a GLP-1 receptor agonist, resulting in superior glycemic control (HbA1c reduction from 10.2% to 6.0%) and a reduced risk of hypoglycemic events.
