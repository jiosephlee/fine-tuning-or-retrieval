Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# Epi-Drip or Expectant? Managing the Multiphasic Nightmare

We’ve all been there: the anaphylaxis patient who responds beautifully to a dose of epinephrine, the swelling goes down, the blood pressure stabilizes, and you start thinking about discharge or a low-acuity observation bed. We’re taught to watch for the "biphasic" reaction—that second wave of symptoms that hits a small percentage of patients hours later. But what happens when the wave doesn't just return once, but keeps crashing back over and over again?

Enter a case that turns a standard emergency into a clinical nightmare: multiphasic anaphylaxis.

A woman in her 60s presented to the ED after taking a single 500mg dose of oral clarithromycin. This wasn't a mild rash; she was in frank anaphylactic shock. We're talking a blood pressure of 74/50 mmHg, oxygen saturation of 88%, facial swelling, and generalized urticaria. She received 0.3mg of epinephrine via auto-injector, fluids, and oxygen, and she responded well. The crisis seemed averted.

But then the pattern emerged. Despite the initial success, she suffered recurrent episodes of anaphylactic shock within the first 12 hours of admission. Not once, not twice, but a total of four distinct episodes. Each time, she crashed, and each time, she required further intramuscular epinephrine to recover.

This is where the clinician's dilemma kicks in. When you are staring at a patient who keeps relapsing despite adequate treatment, you are faced with two precarious choices: the "Epi-drip" or the "Expectant" approach.

On one hand, you can start a continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion. The logic is pre-emptive: keep the receptors occupied and the vasculature tight to stop the next crash before it starts. But this comes with its own set of risks—tachycardia, potential ischemia, and the general instability of a potent vasopressor. On the other hand, you can remain expectant, treating reactively as symptoms appear. This is "safer" in terms of medication side effects, but it means watching your patient repeatedly descend into shock, which is a terrifying prospect for both the provider and the patient.

In this case, the team leaned toward the expectant approach, noting that the intensity of the episodes was gradually decreasing. Eventually, the cycle broke, and the patient stabilized after 24 hours without further relapse.

While the management of the "crash" is intuitive, this case highlights a few critical gaps in how we handle anaphylaxis in the real world. First, there is the "auto-injector trap." In this case, the first two doses were 0.3mg via auto-injector. While auto-injectors are fantastic for reducing treatment delay, 0.3mg is an underdose for most adults over 50kg. The guidelines suggest 0.5mg. When a patient is in refractory or multiphasic shock, every milligram counts.

Then there is the steroid myth. In this patient, prednisone and dexamethasone were administered to prevent further biphasic reactions. However, the modern literature is increasingly clear: corticosteroids do not reliably prevent biphasic anaphylaxis. We are likely giving a drug with a delayed onset and a long list of side effects for a benefit that may not actually exist.

So, why did this happen? The "why" is perhaps the most interesting part of the case. Further workup revealed a high probability of systemic mastocytosis. While she didn't meet every formal diagnostic criterion, she had elevated baseline and peak tryptase levels, hepatomegaly, and a history of neuropsychiatric symptoms like depression. Systemic mastocytosis creates a biological "tinderbox" where mast cells are more numerous and more prone to massive mediator release, potentially explaining why this patient didn't just have one reaction, but a sustained, multiphasic series of them.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **The Dose Matters:** Be wary of the 0.3mg auto-injector in adults. If the patient is >50kg and crashing, they need 0.5mg. Don't let the convenience of the device lead to under-dosing.
*   **Steroids Aren't Magic:** Evidence suggests corticosteroids do not prevent biphasic reactions. Their role in the acute management of anaphylaxis is increasingly questioned.
*   **Tryptase Timing:** If you suspect a mast cell disorder or need to confirm anaphylaxis, timing is everything. Grab a sample as soon as feasible, another 1–2 hours after symptom onset (the peak), and a baseline sample at least 24 hours after resolution.
*   **Think Beyond Biphasic:** While biphasic reactions are rare (4-6%), "multiphasic" courses are even rarer and more challenging. If a patient relapses multiple times, consider underlying mast cell disorders like systemic mastocytosis.
*   **Observation is Key:** Because we cannot reliably predict who will have a biphasic or multiphasic course, a period of observation remains the safest strategy for severe presentations.
