Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

## Initial Presentation and the Clinical Recognition of Anaphylaxis

Anaphylaxis is a severe, systemic Type I hypersensitivity reaction characterized by a rapid onset of symptoms that can lead to respiratory failure and cardiovascular collapse. Diagnosis is primarily clinical, based on the sudden onset of symptoms involving the airway, breathing, or circulation, typically accompanied by cutaneous or mucosal manifestations such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. In the case presented, the patient’s clinical picture—combining respiratory distress, hemodynamic instability, and widespread skin involvement—met these criteria, necessitating immediate emergency intervention to prevent fatality.

The pharmacological trigger in this case was clarithromycin, a macrolide antibiotic. While the patient had a documented history of mild allergy to beta-lactams and a recent rash associated with clindamycin, this episode represented her first experience of true anaphylaxis. It is important to recognize that patients with a history of multiple drug allergies may be more predisposed to severe reactions, though the triggers may be chemically unrelated. Clarithromycin-induced anaphylaxis is relatively rare, but the rapid onset of symptoms (one hour after the first dose) is classic for an IgE-mediated reaction in a sensitized individual.

Analyzing the patient's initial presentation reveals a multi-system failure characteristic of anaphylactic shock. From a circulatory perspective, the patient exhibited profound hypotension (74/50 mmHg), indicating a systemic distributive shock caused by massive histamine release and subsequent vasodilation. Respiratory compromise was evident through hypoxemia (oxygen saturation of 88% on room air) and tachypnea (22 breaths per minute), alongside the subjective feeling of throat swelling. These findings suggest a combination of upper airway edema and potential lower airway bronchoconstriction. The cutaneous manifestations—generalized erythema, urticaria, facial swelling, and pruritus—served as the most visible diagnostic clues, though it is critical to remember that skin signs are absent in a significant minority of anaphylactic cases.

Immediate stabilization in the emergency department (ED) followed the "ABC" (Airway, Breathing, and Circulation) priorities. For the airway and breathing, the patient was promptly started on high-flow supplemental oxygen (15 L via a non-rebreather mask) to treat hypoxemia. The circulation was addressed using the gold-standard treatment: intramuscular epinephrine. Epinephrine acts as an $\alpha_1$-adrenergic agonist to increase systemic vascular resistance (treating hypotension) and a $\beta_2$-adrenergic agonist to induce bronchodilation. To support the volume deficit caused by capillary leak and vasodilation, the patient received an initial bolus of 500 mL of normal saline. Finally, an antihistamine (clemastine) was administered to address the cutaneous symptoms, though it is important to note that antihistamines and corticosteroids are adjunctive therapies and do not treat the life-threatening components of anaphylaxis.

## The Spectrum of Anaphylactic Courses: Biphasic and Multiphasic Reactions

Anaphylaxis typically presents as a single, acute systemic reaction that resolves following prompt treatment with epinephrine. However, a subset of patients experiences a biphasic course, defined as the recurrence of anaphylactic symptoms after the initial resolution of the first episode, occurring without re-exposure to the triggering allergen. While biphasic reactions usually manifest within 24 hours, they can occur up to 78 hours later. In rare and more complex cases, such as the patient in this report, a multiphasic course occurs, characterized by multiple relapses into anaphylactic shock despite repeated successful responses to treatment.

Distinguishing a true biphasic reaction from other patterns of instability is a critical diagnostic challenge. Clinicians must differentiate biphasic anaphylaxis from refractory anaphylaxis, where symptoms persist or recur because the patient is non-responsive to correctly administered epinephrine. It must also be distinguished from "sustained" anaphylaxis, where symptoms recur because the allergen is still being absorbed into the system—such as with slow-release medications or residual food in the gastrointestinal tract. In this case, the patient had received a single oral dose of clarithromycin; the clear windows of clinical resolution between her four distinct episodes of shock support a diagnosis of multiphasic anaphylaxis rather than a sustained reaction to a single dose.

The clinical progression of this patient underscores the unpredictability of multiphasic courses. Following the initial ED presentation—marked by hypotension, hypoxaemia, and generalized urticaria—the patient showed a good initial response to epinephrine. However, she suffered three subsequent relapses within the first 12 hours of admission. A notable feature of this case was the gradual decrease in symptom intensity: the first episode involved deep hypoxaemia and cyanosis, while the final episode was characterized primarily by dyspnoea and pruritus. Despite this decreasing severity, each episode required repeated intramuscular epinephrine to resolve, demonstrating that the patient remained hemodynamically unstable.

The potential for relapse creates a significant dilemma regarding monitoring protocols and the duration of observation. While traditional guidelines suggest observing patients for 4 to 6 hours, evidence suggests that approximately half of biphasic reactions occur between 6 and 12 hours after the initial event. Because reliable predictors for relapse—such as the severity of the initial presentation or the number of epinephrine doses required—are not yet firmly established, risk stratification remains difficult. In this case, the patient’s repeated relapses necessitated a transition from a standard acute internal ward to the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) to ensure continuous monitoring and immediate access to advanced life support.

In the ICU setting, the management of multiphasic anaphylaxis is not well-defined in current literature, forcing clinicians to weigh the risks of different strategies. The medical team considered two divergent paths: a preemptive approach using a continuous intravenous epinephrine pump to prevent further episodes, or an expectant approach, allowing symptoms to resolve spontaneously given the decreasing intensity of the attacks. Ultimately, the patient stabilized without further recurrence after 24 hours of observation, highlighting the necessity of ICU-level monitoring for patients who exhibit a multiphasic pattern until a clear period of stability is achieved.

## Pharmacological Management and Evidence-Based Controversies

The cornerstone of anaphylaxis management is the prompt administration of epinephrine. The standard recommended dose is 0.01 mg/kg of body weight, typically capped at a maximum dose of 0.5 mg for adults. In this case, the patient's weight (104 kg) placed her well above the threshold where the maximum adult dose is indicated. However, her initial treatment involved a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector. This highlights a critical clinical nuance: while many adult auto-injectors are standardized at 0.3 mg, this may result in significant underdosing for adults over 50 kg. Evidence suggests that patients receiving 0.3 mg may require more frequent escalation of care or additional injections compared to those receiving the full 0.5 mg dose.

Despite the risk of underdosing in larger adults, epinephrine auto-injectors remain valuable in both pre-clinical and clinical settings. In the emergency department, they can minimize treatment delays and reduce practitioner hesitancy, particularly for staff who do not routinely administer vasoactive agents. Once the patient in this case was stabilized and moved to the intensive care unit (ICU), the medical team transitioned from auto-injectors to individually prepared 0.5 mg intramuscular doses to ensure the patient received the full recommended therapeutic amount.

Adjunctive therapies are used to manage specific symptoms but do not replace epinephrine. In this case, clemastine (an H1-antihistamine) was administered intravenously to treat cutaneous manifestations, such as pruritus, flushing, and urticaria. To address the circulatory collapse and hypotension associated with anaphylactic shock, the patient received fluid resuscitation with 500 mL boluses of normal saline. These interventions support hemodynamic stability and patient comfort but do not treat the underlying systemic reaction.

A significant point of contention in contemporary anaphylaxis management is the role of corticosteroids, such as the prednisone and dexamethasone administered to this patient. Traditionally, steroids were used to prevent biphasic reactions—the recurrence of symptoms after an initial resolution. However, current evidence-based guidelines have shifted away from this practice. Systematic reviews indicate that corticosteroids provide no significant benefit in reducing the incidence of biphasic anaphylaxis, and some data suggests they may even increase the likelihood of ICU admission. Despite these findings, their use persists in many hospital protocols, as seen in this case.

In rare instances of multiphasic anaphylaxis, where symptoms recur across multiple episodes, standard intermittent dosing may be insufficient. The clinicians in this case considered two advanced strategies: the proactive use of a continuous intravenous epinephrine pump to prevent further relapses, or an expectant management approach, waiting for symptoms to resolve spontaneously as the intensity of the episodes appeared to be decreasing. Neither strategy was ultimately implemented as the patient remained stable for 24 hours, illustrating that for most patients, prompt intramuscular epinephrine remains the most effective intervention even in complex, recurring courses.

## Diagnostic Reasoning: Biomarkers and Tryptase Kinetics

The transition from a clinical diagnosis of anaphylaxis to a biochemical confirmation relies on understanding the pathophysiology of mast cell activation. Anaphylaxis is driven by the systemic degranulation of mast cells and basophils, which release a potent cocktail of inflammatory mediators, including histamine, leukotrienes, and proteases, into the circulation. These mediators act on vascular smooth muscle and mucosal tissues, leading to the hypotension, bronchospasm, and cutaneous manifestations observed in this patient. To objectively confirm that these cellular events occurred, clinicians measure serum tryptase, a protease produced almost exclusively by mast cells.

Serum tryptase is considered the "gold standard" biomarker for confirming anaphylaxis because its elevation in the blood directly reflects the degree of mast cell activation. However, because tryptase has a relatively short half-life and levels fluctuate rapidly during an acute event, the timing of the blood draw is critical for accurate interpretation. In an ideal diagnostic workup, three samples are collected: an initial sample as soon as feasible to capture the rise, a second sample 1–4 hours after symptom onset to capture the peak concentration, and a third sample at least 24 hours after full clinical resolution to establish the patient's baseline level.

In this case, the sampling protocol was not strictly adhered to, as tryptase levels were not measured during the first two anaphylactic episodes. The first sample was only obtained one hour after the third recurrence, and the second was taken 27 hours after the final episode. Despite these delays, the results provided significant diagnostic clues: the tryptase levels were elevated during the acute episode and remained increased at the baseline follow-up. 

The persistence of an elevated baseline tryptase is a key clinical finding. While a transient spike in tryptase confirms a recent anaphylactic event, a chronically high baseline suggests an increased total mass of mast cells in the body, which is characteristic of systemic mastocytosis. This underlying condition explains why the patient experienced an atypical, multiphasic course; an increased burden of mast cells can predispose individuals to more severe and prolonged reactions. In this patient, the biochemical evidence of mast cell proliferation, combined with findings of hepatomegaly and neuropsychiatric symptoms, pointed toward systemic mastocytosis, even though the bone marrow biopsy provided insufficient material to meet all formal WHO diagnostic criteria.

## Underlying Pathology: Systemic Mastocytosis and Long-Term Care

The atypical and prolonged clinical course of this patient—specifically the occurrence of multiphasic anaphylaxis—prompted an investigation into underlying mast cell disorders. In most cases of anaphylaxis, the reaction is a transient event triggered by an allergen in a patient with otherwise normal mast cell function. However, systemic mastocytosis (SM) is a hematologic disorder characterized by the abnormal proliferation and accumulation of mast cells in various organs, most notably the bone marrow, skin, and gastrointestinal tract. This increased mast cell burden leads to excessive mediator release and multiorgan inflammation, significantly predisposing patients to severe, refractory, or biphasic anaphylactic reactions.

The diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis is guided by the World Health Organization (WHO) criteria, which are divided into major and minor criteria. A formal diagnosis typically requires the fulfillment of a major criterion (such as a dense infiltration of mast cells in the bone marrow or another extracutaneous organ) along with at least one minor criterion, or the fulfillment of three minor criteria. In this case, the patient did not meet the formal diagnostic threshold; while the analysis suggested a high probability of SM, the bone marrow biopsy provided limited material, making the major criteria unassessable. However, she met two minor criteria, including persistently elevated serum tryptase levels. Tryptase is a protease found almost exclusively in mast cells and serves as a critical marker for mast cell burden. Ideally, tryptase should be measured at three intervals—immediately upon presentation, 1–2 hours after symptom onset (the peak), and again 24 hours after resolution to establish a baseline—though in this case, the first two windows were missed, and only the peak and baseline samples were obtained.

Beyond the risk of anaphylaxis, systemic mastocytosis can present with various extramastocytic manifestations. This patient exhibited hepatomegaly on abdominal sonography, which can occur when mast cells infiltrate the liver. Furthermore, mast cell disorders are often associated with neuropsychiatric symptoms; in this patient, her history of depression and reported memory problems are consistent with the systemic nature of the disease. These findings, combined with the multiphasic nature of her reaction to clarithromycin, support the clinical suspicion of an underlying mast cell disorder even in the absence of a formal WHO-confirmed diagnosis.

Long-term management for a patient with this profile focuses on risk mitigation and the prevention of future episodes. The primary strategy is strict allergen avoidance; since the trigger in this case was identified as clarithromycin, the patient must avoid this drug and potentially other related macrolides. To manage baseline cutaneous symptoms and reduce the severity of potential future reactions, the patient was started on a maintenance antihistamine, levocetirizine. Finally, because of the high risk of recurrence and the severity of her previous episodes, the patient was provided with an epinephrine auto-injector (0.3 mg) and educated on its use. This ensures that the patient can deliver life-saving treatment immediately upon the onset of symptoms, bridging the gap between the first sign of a reaction and the arrival of emergency medical services.
