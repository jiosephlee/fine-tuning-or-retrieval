Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

## Clinical Presentation and the Challenge of Atypical Symptoms

The initial presentation of this patient highlights the diagnostic complexity that often accompanies Chiari malformation type I (CMI). Rather than presenting with the classic "Chiari headache"—typically a paroxysmal occipital pain triggered by Valsalva maneuvers—this patient exhibited a constellation of atypical symptoms that spanned 14 years, leading to multiple misdiagnoses and a significant delay in identifying the structural brainstem compression.

The patient's neurological symptoms began following a motor vehicle accident, manifesting as chronic occipital and vertex headaches. These were described as constant, pressure-like, and localized to a coin-sized area at the vertex. A particularly striking feature was the presence of marked scalp allodynia, where innocuous stimuli, such as the weight of her hair, triggered significant pain. This phenomenon is rarely documented in CMI but may overlap with occipital neuralgia or a condition known as "burning vertex syndrome," characterized by cutaneous allodynia and scalp pain that does not fit traditional headache classifications. In this case, the patient’s observation that cutting her hair short reduced her pain intensity from 6/10 to 3/10 serves as a clinical clue to the sensory hypersensitivity involving the scalp.

Concurrent with these headaches, the patient developed a seven-year history of recurrent choking episodes affecting both solids and liquids equally. From a clinical reasoning perspective, the equal difficulty with all food consistencies is a critical differentiator; while mechanical obstructions (such as esophageal webs or strictures) typically cause dysphagia primarily for solids, neurogenic dysphagia—resulting from lower cranial nerve or brainstem dysfunction—often affects both solids and liquids. Despite multiple normal laryngoscopies and endoscopies, which ruled out local structural or inflammatory causes in the upper gastrointestinal tract, the persistent nature of these episodes suggested a failure of the neuromuscular coordination required for swallowing.

The most alarming feature of the presentation was the discovery of severe, asymptomatic hypoxia. Through home pulse oximetry, the patient documented oxygen saturation levels dropping to 70%–80% without accompanying respiratory distress or "air hunger." This lack of dyspnea is highly abnormal, as severe hypoxemia typically triggers a powerful drive to breathe. This presentation suggests a failure of the automatic respiratory control centers in the brainstem (reminiscent of "Ondine’s curse") and a disruption of the afferent pathways that transmit the sensation of breathlessness to the cortex. The airport incident, where the patient experienced dizziness and nausea at 78% saturation, further illustrated the danger of this "silent" hypoventilation, where the patient is physiologically distressed but neurologically unaware of the need to increase ventilation.

These prolonged, unexplained symptoms exerted a profound psychological and social toll on the patient. The unpredictability of the choking episodes led to social withdrawal and an avoidant lifestyle. Furthermore, the emotional distress was compounded by the conflict between her need to cut her hair for pain relief and the cultural expectations associated with her appearance. The case underscores a common diagnostic pitfall: when patients present with severe physiological dysfunction (such as desaturation) without the expected subjective symptoms (such as dyspnea), clinicians may prematurely attribute the symptoms to anxiety or psychosomatic causes, potentially overlooking a life-threatening structural lesion.

## Pathophysiology of Chiari Malformation Type I (CMI)

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is a structural abnormality of the posterior fossa defined by the caudal displacement of the cerebellar tonsils through the foramen magnum—the large opening at the base of the skull that connects the cranial cavity to the spinal canal. For a diagnosis of CMI, the tonsils must extend at least 5 mm below the level of the foramen magnum on a sagittal MRI. In the presented case, the patient exhibited a significant 11 mm tonsillar descent, which contributed to the "crowded" appearance of the foramen magnum seen on her historical CT scan and current MRI.

The underlying mechanism of CMI is generally attributed to a volumetric mismatch: the posterior fossa is developmentally too small to accommodate the cerebellum and brainstem. This creates a pressure gradient that forces the cerebellar tonsils downward. While some individuals may have a small posterior fossa as an anatomical variant without experiencing symptoms, a pathological state occurs when this crowding obstructs the normal flow of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) at the craniocervical junction. This obstruction can alter intracranial pressure and lead to the development of syringomyelia or the compression of adjacent neural structures.

The most critical physiological consequence in this case is the compression of the brainstem, specifically the medulla oblongata. The medulla houses the autonomous respiratory control centers responsible for the automatic drive to breathe. When these centers are compressed, the patient may develop a form of acquired central hypoventilation syndrome, often referred to as "Ondine’s curse." A key clinical distinction in this pathophysiology is the separation of voluntary and automatic breathing. Voluntary breathing is mediated by cortical pathways that typically bypass the compressed medullary centers, allowing the patient to breathe normally while awake and attentive. However, automatic breathing—which is essential during sleep or periods of distraction—is compromised. This explains why the patient experienced profound oxygen desaturation at the airport and during sleep, yet remained consciously unaware of the distress (absence of dyspnea), as the afferent signals required to trigger the sensation of "air hunger" were interrupted by the brainstem compression.

Furthermore, the compression of the lower cranial nerve nuclei in the medulla can lead to oropharyngeal dysfunction. This manifests as neurogenic dysphagia, characterized by difficulty swallowing both solids and liquids equally. This is distinct from mechanical obstructions (like a stricture), where difficulty usually varies by food consistency. In this patient, the chronic compression of these nuclei resulted in the recurrent choking episodes and coughing that had been misattributed to gastrointestinal or laryngeal issues for several years.

Finally, CMI can be categorized by its etiology as either primary or secondary. Primary CMI is congenital, resulting from the aforementioned developmental mismatch. Secondary CMI is acquired, often as a result of head trauma, brain surgery, or increased intracranial pressure. In this case, the patient's symptoms and imaging findings emerged following a motor vehicle accident. This suggests that the trauma may have either directly caused the tonsillar herniation or exacerbated a previously asymptomatic congenital variant, transforming a benign anatomical feature into a symptomatic clinical syndrome.

## Diagnostic Reasoning and Multidisciplinary Workup

The diagnostic journey in this case illustrates the challenge of synthesizing disparate symptoms—chronic headaches, oropharyngeal choking, and unexplained hypoxia—into a single unifying diagnosis. For several years, the patient's symptoms were treated as isolated issues, leading to a series of negative investigations that, while unsuccessful in providing a diagnosis, were essential in narrowing the differential.

The patient's recurrent choking on both solids and liquids initially pointed toward a localized oropharyngeal or gastrointestinal pathology. Clinicians first considered gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GERD) and laryngopharyngeal reflux, as these can cause coughing and choking sensations. However, normal pH studies, unremarkable endoscopies, and a lack of response to maximal acid suppression ruled this out. Because the choking affected both solids and liquids equally, the suspicion shifted from a mechanical obstruction (which typically affects solids first) to a neurogenic or functional cause. Myasthenia gravis was considered due to the potential for bulbar weakness; however, the absence of muscle fatigability, the lack of diurnal variation in symptoms, and negative acetylcholine receptor and anti-MuSK antibody tests made this diagnosis unlikely.

A critical pedagogical point in this case is the interpretation of "normal" laryngoscopies and endoscopies. While these procedures are excellent for identifying structural abnormalities, such as tumors, webs, or inflammatory changes in the airway and esophagus, they cannot assess the neurological coordination of the swallow or the automatic drive of the respiratory system. The failure of these tests to find a cause for the choking episodes should have served as a clinical signal that the pathology was not in the throat itself, but in the neurological control centers governing those functions.

The turning point in the diagnostic process was the re-evaluation of historical data. A non-contrast CT scan performed 14 years prior following a motor vehicle accident had been interpreted as normal. However, a detailed retrospective review revealed a "crowded foramen magnum" and a loss of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) space at the craniocervical junction. This finding suggested that the posterior fossa was underdeveloped or compressed, providing the first structural clue to the patient's systemic symptoms.

To confirm this suspicion, an MRI of the brain and cervical spine was performed. The MRI provided definitive evidence of Chiari malformation type I (CMI), demonstrating an 11 mm descent of the cerebellar tonsils below the foramen magnum. This degree of descent, combined with the crowding of the craniocervical junction, explained the mechanical compression of the brainstem.

By integrating these imaging findings with the clinical presentation, the medical team arrived at a unifying neurological diagnosis. The compression of the lower cranial nerve nuclei in the medulla explained the neurogenic dysphagia and choking. The compression of the medullary respiratory centers explained the central hypoventilation and the patient's "Ondine’s curse" phenotype—where voluntary breathing remained intact, but automatic breathing failed during sleep or distraction, leading to severe, asymptomatic oxygen desaturations. Finally, the structural crowding of the posterior fossa and disruption of CSF flow accounted for the chronic, pressure-like occipital and vertex headaches.

## Neurological Basis of Respiratory and Bulbar Dysfunction

The patient’s presentation underscores a critical distinction in the neurology of respiration: the divide between voluntary and automatic breathing. Voluntary breathing is mediated by cortical pathways that allow a person to consciously control their breath. In contrast, automatic breathing is controlled by the respiratory centers in the medulla oblongata of the brainstem, which maintain ventilation during sleep or when the patient is distracted. In this case, the 11 mm descent of the cerebellar tonsils caused significant compression of the medullary structures. While the patient's cortical pathways remained intact—allowing her to breathe normally while conscious and attentive—her automatic respiratory drive was compromised. This selective failure is what led to her "forgetting to breathe" during sleep or during the acute episode of distraction at the airport.

This clinical phenotype represents an acquired form of central hypoventilation syndrome, often referred to as "Ondine’s curse." While the congenital form of this syndrome is typically associated with *PHOX2B* mutations, this patient's condition was structural. The brainstem compression induced a state where the automatic trigger for inspiration was diminished or lost, leading to profound oxygen desaturations (70%–80%) without the patient ever experiencing the sensation of shortness of breath.

The absence of dyspnea (air hunger) despite severe hypoxia is a failure of interoception. Normally, the sensation of breathlessness is generated when afferent signals from chemoreceptors and stretch receptors ascend through the brainstem to the cortex, particularly the anterior insular cortex. In this patient, the compression of the brainstem likely disrupted these ascending neural pathways. Because the cortical centers never received the signal that oxygen levels were dropping, the patient remained unaware of her physiological distress, creating a dangerous situation where the usual "alarm system" of the lungs was silenced.

The patient's bulbar symptoms, specifically her recurrent choking episodes, further illustrate the impact of medullary compression. In clinical practice, it is essential to distinguish between mechanical and neurogenic dysphagia. Mechanical dysphagia usually results from a physical obstruction and typically presents as a difficulty with solids that progresses to liquids. Neurogenic dysphagia, however, results from the dysfunction of cranial nerve nuclei or their pathways and typically affects solids and liquids equally. The patient’s history of choking on both solids and liquids, combined with normal endoscopic and laryngoscopic findings, pointed toward a neurogenic origin. In the context of CMI, this is explained by the compression of the lower cranial nerve nuclei in the medulla, which coordinate the complex muscular movements of swallowing.

Finally, interpreting the sleep study in this case requires an understanding of the limitations of Type III polysomnography. Unlike Type I studies, Type III lacks EEG monitoring, meaning the clinician cannot definitively distinguish between periods of wakefulness and true sleep. Because the Apnea-Hypopnea Index (AHI) is calculated by dividing the number of events by the total recording time rather than true sleep time, the reported AHI of 6.0 likely underestimated the severity of the patient's condition. Despite this low number, the fact that 72% of the events were central (rather than obstructive) provided strong evidence for central hypoventilation and supported the unifying diagnosis of Chiari malformation type I.

## Management Decisions, Monitoring, and Clinical Lessons

Following a multidisciplinary consultation, the management of this patient focused on the tension between surgical intervention and conservative symptom control. The primary surgical recommendation was posterior fossa decompression (PFD). The goal of PFD is to expand the available space at the craniocervical junction, thereby restoring normal cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow dynamics and relieving the compression of the brainstem and lower cranial nerve nuclei. In this patient, PFD was indicated by the presence of significant neurological morbidity: severe nocturnal oxygen desaturations (mimicking acquired central hypoventilation syndrome), persistent oropharyngeal dysphagia, and chronic headaches associated with 11 mm of cerebellar tonsillar descent.

Despite the surgical recommendation, the patient opted for conservative management. This decision highlights the importance of patient autonomy and the role of coping strategies in managing chronic, non-acute manifestations of Chiari malformation type I (CMI). To manage her symptoms, the patient implemented dietary modifications to mitigate choking episodes and continued cutting her hair short to alleviate the cutaneous allodynia at the vertex. Because the patient declined immediate surgery, a structured long-term surveillance plan was established to monitor for clinical or radiological deterioration. This plan includes monthly clinical assessments, quarterly overnight oximetry to track respiratory stability, and annual MRI surveillance to monitor the position of the cerebellar tonsils and screen for the development of syringomyelia.

This case provides two critical clinical lessons for the practicing physician. First, it emphasizes the necessity of neurological screening in patients with persistent, unexplained dysphagia. When a patient presents with choking episodes affecting both solids and liquids equally, and initial gastroenterological or otolaryngological evaluations (such as endoscopy and laryngoscopy) are normal, the clinician should suspect a neurogenic origin rather than a mechanical one. As seen in this case, structural abnormalities like CMI can compress the brainstem, leading to bulbar dysfunction that mimics primary esophageal or pharyngeal pathology.

Second, this case underscores the risk of missed findings on initial imaging, particularly in the context of trauma-related head CTs. The patient's diagnosis was delayed by 14 years because a post-accident CT scan was interpreted as normal. However, a retrospective review revealed a crowded foramen magnum and a loss of CSF space at the craniocervical junction. For the resident or medical student, this serves as a reminder that subtle signs of posterior fossa crowding can be overlooked in the acute setting of trauma. A high index of suspicion is required when a patient develops a constellation of seemingly unrelated symptoms—such as occipital headaches and respiratory dysfunction—following a head injury.
