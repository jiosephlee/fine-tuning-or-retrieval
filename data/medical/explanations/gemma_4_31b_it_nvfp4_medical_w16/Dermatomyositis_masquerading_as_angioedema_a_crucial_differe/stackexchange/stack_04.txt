Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Differential Diagnosis] This patient presented with significantly elevated aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels alongside a very high creatine kinase (CK). In a patient with suspected dermatomyositis, how should an attending interpret an isolated elevation of transaminases in the absence of jaundice or liver failure?

In a patient with suspected dermatomyositis or other inflammatory myopathies, an isolated elevation of aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) in the absence of jaundice, conjugated hyperbilirubinemia, or coagulopathy should be interpreted as a reflection of **skeletal muscle injury** rather than primary hepatic dysfunction.

This phenomenon occurs because AST and ALT are not liver-specific enzymes. While ALT is more specific to the liver, both are present in striated muscle. AST, in particular, is found in high concentrations within skeletal muscle cells. When extensive myonecrosis or inflammation occurs—as evidenced by the profoundly elevated creatine kinase (CK) level of 16,184 U/L in this patient—these enzymes leak from the damaged myocytes into the systemic circulation.

In this case, the patient's AST (396 U/L) and ALT (143 U/L) elevations are "pseudo-transaminitis." The clinical reasoning is supported by the discordance between the laboratory values and the clinical state; the patient lacked the hallmarks of liver failure or cholestasis, yet demonstrated severe muscle involvement confirmed by MRI and biopsy.

The key clinical pearl for residents is that in the setting of severe myositis or rhabdomyolysis, the transaminases serve as additional markers of muscle breakdown. Therefore, an attending should avoid unnecessary hepatic workups or diagnostic delays when these elevations are seen in tandem with a markedly elevated CK and a clinical picture consistent with myopathy.
