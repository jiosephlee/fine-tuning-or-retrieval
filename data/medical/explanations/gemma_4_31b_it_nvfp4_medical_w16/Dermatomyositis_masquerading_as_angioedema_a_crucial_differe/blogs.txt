Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# When 'Angioedema' Isn't Angioedema: The Great Mimics of Neck Swelling

We’ve all been there: a patient rolls into the ED with a neck that looks like it’s about to burst. The immediate mental algorithm is a race to rule out the "killers"—Ludwig’s angina, a ruptured carotid, or anaphylaxis. Once the airway is secured and the CT scan rules out a focal abscess or a hematoma, we often settle into the "angioedema" bucket. We look for ACE inhibitors, check C4 levels, and wonder if it's a weird idiopathic hereditary fluke. 

But every now and then, a case comes along that reminds us that "swelling" is a nonspecific sign and that our diagnostic buckets have holes in them.

## The Case: A Diagnostic Pivot

A male in his 40s presented with a three-week history of progressive neck swelling. He wasn't febrile, and he wasn't tachycardic, but his neck was erythematous and non-tender. The initial CT was concerning for diffuse neck edema involving the strap muscles and extending into the retropharyngeal space. Because it looked like a deep neck space infection, ENT was called. No abscess. No structural abnormality.

The focus then shifted to angioedema. He had no history of hereditary angioedema, no new medications, and no triggers. However, the "angioedema" didn't quite fit the pattern. While the neck was swollen, the patient also had mild myalgias and a subtle weakness in his hip flexors. When you look closer—really look—you find the clues: periungual erythema and a faint rash on the lateral thigh.

The diagnostic pivot happened with a single lab value: a creatine kinase (CK) of 16,184 U/L.

This wasn't a histamine or bradykinin problem; it was an inflammatory myopathy. The patient was eventually diagnosed with anti-SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis. The "angioedema" was actually pseudo-angioedema—massive inflammatory edema arising from muscle inflammation and vascular leakage, rather than the classic pathways of allergic or hereditary swelling.

## Why This Matters: The Mimicry

Dermatomyositis is a classic "great mimic." We usually look for the "textbook" signs: Gottron’s papules on the knuckles or a heliotrope rash on the eyelids. But this case highlights a more sinister and rare presentation. In some patients, particularly those with specific autoantibodies like SAE1 or MDA-5, the cutaneous manifestation isn't just a rash—it's profound edema.

When this happens in the neck, it can be catastrophic. This patient didn't just have a "fat neck"; he had severe pharyngeal dysphagia and micro-aspiration leading to peribronchial consolidation. The swelling wasn't just cosmetic; it was a functional failure of the muscles of deglutition and airway protection.

## The Clinical Rigor: Beyond the Swelling

If you find yourself staring at a patient with "idiopathic" angioedema that isn't responding to standard therapies, you need to broaden your lens. 

First, look at the periphery. Check the nail folds for microhemorrhages or telangiectasias. Look for the "Holster sign" (erythema on the lateral thighs) or the "Shawl sign" (rash across the shoulders). Second, check the strength. A patient who tells you they are "just feeling a bit tired" might actually have 4/5 power in their hip flexors.

Finally, don't be afraid of the CK. If the swelling is atypical or accompanied by any systemic or cutaneous signs, a CK level is a low-cost, high-yield screen that can shift the diagnosis from "mystery edema" to a treatable, systemic autoimmune disease.

## The Long Game: Malignancy and Management

Once the diagnosis of dermatomyositis is made, the job isn't over. These patients are at significant risk for occult malignancy. Based on the IMACS guidelines, this patient was high-risk due to his sex, the presence of dysphagia, and the SAE1 antibody. This requires an aggressive screening workup: CTs, PET scans, and endoscopies.

Treatment is intense because the stakes are high. This patient required "pulse" methylprednisolone (1g daily for 3 days), IVIG, mycophenolate, and rituximab to bring the inflammation under control and restore his ability to swallow.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Pseudo-angioedema is a thing:** Dermatomyositis can present as severe facial and neck swelling that mimics histamine or bradykinin-mediated angioedema.
*   **Look beyond the neck:** When faced with unexplained swelling, perform a full skin check (nails, thighs, chest) and a proximal muscle strength exam.
*   **The CK is your friend:** A massively elevated CK in the setting of "angioedema" should immediately raise suspicion for an inflammatory myopathy.
*   **Screen for cancer:** Dermatomyositis—especially with SAE1 antibodies and dysphagia—is a red flag for underlying malignancy.
*   **Airway and Swallowing:** Be mindful that neck swelling in myositis isn't just skin-deep; it often involves the pharyngeal muscles, leading to aspiration and dysphagia.



# More Than a Rash: Recognizing Atypical Dermatomyositis

We’ve all seen the textbook images of dermatomyositis (DM): the classic heliotrope rash on the eyelids and Gottron’s papules over the knuckles. When a patient doesn't have those, we often subconsciously move DM down the differential list. But what happens when the "rash" isn't a rash at all, but rather massive swelling that looks exactly like an allergic reaction or a deep neck infection?

That is the trap in this case. A man in his 40s presented to the ED with progressive, severe neck swelling. The initial suspicion was Ludwig’s angina—a surgical emergency. When the CT scan showed diffuse edema but no abscess and the ENT surgeons cleared him of a deep neck space infection, the clinical pendulum swung toward idiopathic angioedema. 

## The "Angioedema" Trap

The patient had no history of anaphylaxis, no ACE-inhibitor use, and no family history of hereditary angioedema. He was a "clinical enigma": a swollen, non-tender neck with a slightly elevated CRP, but no clear trigger. This is where the diagnostic pivot occurred. While the neck swelling was the most dramatic feature, a more granular physical exam revealed the "hidden" clues. 

The patient didn't have the heliotrope rash, but he did have generalized facial erythema that crossed the nasolabial folds. He didn't have Gottron’s papules, but he had a rash on his lateral thigh—the "holster sign"—and subtle periungual erythema. A nailfold capillaroscopy revealed microhemorrhages and avascular areas. These are the subtle, high-yield markers of connective tissue disease that often get missed when we are focused on the "chief complaint" of the swelling.

## The Power of the CK

In the face of atypical angioedema, the team requested a creatine kinase (CK) level. This was the turning point. The CK wasn't just high; it was massive—16,184 U/L. 

When you see profound muscle enzyme elevation in a patient with systemic inflammation or unexplained edema, you must broaden your lens to include inflammatory myopathies. In this case, the "angioedema" wasn't caused by histamine or bradykinin; it was actually subcutaneous edema secondary to severe, systemic myositis. The patient was later found to be positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies, a marker associated with a higher prevalence of dysphagia and interstitial lung disease.

## More Than Just Muscle Weakness

The danger in this case wasn't just the swelling; it was what was happening internally. The myositis involved the pharyngeal muscles, leading to severe dysphagia. This created a vicious cycle: the patient was aspirating, leading to peribronchial consolidation and pulmonary nodules, which further complicated his respiratory status. 

This highlights a critical point for the ED and ICU clinician: if a patient with suspected dermatomyositis has neck swelling or facial edema, their airway and aspiration risk are significantly increased. This patient required a nasogastric tube for two months and aggressive immunosuppression (high-dose methylprednisolone, IVIG, and rituximab) to regain his ability to swallow.

## Clinical Pearls & Take-Home Points

*   **Pseudo-angioedema is a real thing:** Dermatomyositis can present as isolated facial or neck swelling. If "idiopathic angioedema" doesn't fit the clinical picture (no triggers, normal complement levels), think about inflammatory myopathy.
*   **Look beyond the "textbook" rash:** If the heliotrope rash and Gottron's papules are absent, look for the **Holster sign** (lateral thighs), **V-sign** (chest), or **Shawl sign** (upper back/shoulders).
*   **Check the nails:** Periungual telangiectasia and nailfold capillary changes are sensitive markers for systemic autoimmune diseases.
*   **The CK is your friend:** In cases of unexplained systemic inflammation and edema, a CK level is a low-cost, high-yield screen that can steer you toward a diagnosis of myositis.
*   **Assess the swallow:** Any patient with DM and neck/facial involvement is at high risk for pharyngeal muscle weakness. Be aggressive with aspiration precautions and consider early swallow evaluations.
*   **Cancer screening:** Anti-SAE1 and other myositis antibodies are associated with an increased risk of malignancy. These patients require a comprehensive malignancy workup (CT, PET, and endoscopies).



# The Silent Airway Threat: Dysphagia and Aspiration in Myositis

When a patient walks into the ED with a swollen neck and difficulty swallowing, our brains immediately go to the "can't breathe" folder. We think Ludwig’s angina, epiglottitis, or a massive angioedema reaction. We call ENT, we grab the glide-scope, and we start thinking about airway management. But what happens when the swelling isn't an infection or an allergic reaction, but rather the manifestation of a systemic autoimmune attack on the muscles themselves?

Enter a case that serves as a stark reminder that dermatomyositis is not just a "skin and proximal limb" disease. A male in his 40s presented with a three-week history of progressive neck swelling and mild hip weakness. Early imaging (CT neck) showed diffuse edema involving the strap muscles and the retropharyngeal space. Because he was afebrile and the swelling was non-tender, the suspicion shifted from Ludwig's angina to idiopathic angioedema. On the surface, it looked like a classic "pseudo-angioedema" presentation—non-pitting, non-pruritic swelling of the neck and face.

The "aha!" moment came from a combination of a thorough physical exam and a high index of suspicion. Subtle clues were hiding in plain sight: periungual erythema, nailfold microhemorrhages, and a mild weakness in the hip flexors (4/5 power). When the team ordered a creatine kinase (CK) level, the result was staggering: 16,184 U/L. This shifted the differential entirely from "allergic/vascular" to "myopathic."

While the neck swelling is a dramatic external sign, the real danger in this case was internal. The patient was exhibiting "silent" aspiration. During an ENT nasendoscopy, the only clues were increased secretions and some coughing. It wasn't until a video fluoroscopic swallow study was performed that the severity became clear: the patient had severe pharyngeal dysphagia, with thin and thick fluids penetrating the vocal cords and residue clogging the pyriform fossae. This wasn't just a "difficulty swallowing" issue; it was an airway protection failure. This had already led to aspiration pneumonitis, evidenced by patchy peribronchial consolidation on CT chest.

This case highlights a critical a subtype of dermatomyositis associated with anti-SAE1 (small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme) antibodies. This specific antibody profile is often linked to a high incidence of dysphagia and interstitial lung disease. In this patient, the "angioedema" was actually muscle inflammation so severe it caused massive soft-tissue edema in the neck, which, coupled with pharyngeal muscle weakness, created a perfect storm for respiratory failure via aspiration.

The management of these patients requires aggressive, multi-modal immunosuppression. This patient required pulse-dose methylprednisolone (1g daily for 3 days), followed by a combination of IVIG, mycophenolate mofetil, and rituximab. Because the pharyngeal muscles were compromised, he required a nasogastric tube for two months to prevent further aspiration while the inflammation subsided.

The takeaway here is that we cannot view myositis as purely a "limb weakness" condition. When the pharyngeal muscles are hit, the patient is at risk of aspiration that can be subtle and silent until they develop pneumonia or respiratory failure. If you see "angioedema" that doesn't respond to standard therapy, or an unusual skin rash paired with any systemic weakness, check a CK and look closely at the nailfolds.

## Clinical Pearls

*   **Beyond the Limbs:** Dermatomyositis can present as "pseudo-angioedema," specifically involving the neck and facial soft tissues, mimicking bradykinin or histamine-mediated swelling.
*   **The Silent Threat:** Pharyngeal involvement in myositis can lead to aspiration pneumonitis. A "mild cough" during an exam may actually be a sign of severe dysphagia.
*   **Diagnostic Utility:** In suspected myositis with swallowing complaints, a video fluoroscopic swallow study is essential to quantify aspiration risk and guide nutritional support (e.g., NGT).
*   **The "Quick" Screen:** If a patient presents with unexplained edema and skin changes, a CK level is a rapid, high-yield screen for inflammatory myopathies.
*   **High-Risk Markers:** Anti-SAE1 antibodies are specifically associated with a higher prevalence of dysphagia and interstitial lung disease, and they carry a significant association with underlying malignancy.



# The Paraneoplastic Puzzle: Cancer Screening in Inflammatory Myopathies

Imagine a patient walks into your ED with progressive, severe neck swelling. Your mind immediately goes to the "can't miss" diagnoses: Ludwig’s angina, a deep neck space infection, or a severe allergic reaction causing angioedema. You’ve cleared the airway, the CT doesn't show a classic abscess, and the patient isn't on an ACE inhibitor. You're staring at "idiopathic" angioedema. 

But then you look closer. There's a subtle rash on the thigh, some weirdness around the nail folds, and the patient mentions they're having a hard time swallowing. You order a Creatine Kinase (CK) on a whim, and it comes back at 16,184 U/L. 

Welcome to the diagnostic maze of dermatomyositis (DM). While we often think of DM as a rash and proximal weakness, this case reminds us that it can masquerade as an acute airway or allergic emergency through "pseudo-angioedema"—and more importantly, it serves as a massive red flag for an occult malignancy.

## The Case: More Than Just a Rash

The patient in this case—a male in his 40s—initially presented with neck swelling and dysphagia. After ENT cleared him of an infection and immunology ruled out histamine or bradykinin-mediated angioedema, the clinical picture shifted. A thorough exam revealed periungual erythema and a "Holster sign" (erythema over the hips/thighs), alongside 4/5 power in the hip flexors.

The workup was classic for severe DM: a skyrocketing CK, positive ANA, and specifically, positive **anti-SAE1 antibodies**. The "swelling" in the neck wasn't traditional angioedema; it was actually profound muscle inflammation and edema of the strap muscles and pharyngeal area, leading to severe dysphagia and aspiration. 

Once the diagnosis of DM is locked in, the clinical question immediately shifts from "How do we treat the inflammation?" to "Where is the cancer?"

## The Paraneoplastic Connection

Dermatomyositis is one of the great "herald" syndromes of internal medicine. In a significant percentage of adult cases, the myositis is a paraneoplastic manifestation—the immune system, while trying to fight a hidden tumor, cross-reacts with the skin and muscles.

The challenge is that the cancer may not have caused symptoms yet, or it may be so small that a standard exam misses it. This is where the tension lies: do we put every patient through a battery of invasive tests, or do we risk missing a treatable malignancy?

## Risk Stratification: The IMACS Framework

We don't just guess who gets a PET scan. The International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) provides a framework to risk-stratify these patients. In this case, the patient was categorized as **high risk** for malignancy based on a combination of factors:

*   **High-Risk Factors:** The diagnosis of dermatomyositis itself and the presence of dysphagia (which can correlate with higher malignancy rates).
*   **Intermediate-Risk Factors:** Being male and the presence of anti-SAE1 antibodies.

When you see these markers, the screening needs to be aggressive. In this patient, that meant a CT of the chest and abdomen, a PET scan, and tumor markers. Despite the high-risk profile, his initial screens were negative, but the "puzzle" isn't solved—he requires ongoing surveillance, including planned gastroscopy and colonoscopy, because the myositis can precede the cancer diagnosis by months or even years.

## Clinical Pearls: The "Aha!" Moments

The beauty of this case is in the subtle clues. If the team had only looked at the neck, they would have spent weeks chasing a rare form of angioedema. 

The "win" here was the combination of a high index of suspicion and a broad physical exam. The nailfold capillaroscopy showing microhemorrhages and the detection of the Holster sign were the keys that unlocked the diagnosis. Furthermore, the use of a PET scan was invaluable—not just for cancer screening, but for confirming the diffuse nature of the myositis across the deltoids, chest wall, and pelvis.

## Take-Home Points

*   **Neck swelling isn't always angioedema:** If "idiopathic" neck swelling is accompanied by systemic symptoms or atypical skin changes, consider inflammatory myopathies.
*   **The CK is your friend:** When the presentation is "weird" and involves muscles or skin, a CK level is a low-cost, high-yield screen for myositis.
*   **DM = Cancer Screen:** Every adult diagnosed with dermatomyositis requires a malignancy workup. Use the IMACS guidelines to determine the intensity (e.g., Male + DM + Dysphagia = High Risk).
*   **Look for the "Hidden" Signs:** Don't just check the chest and arms. Check the nail folds (periungual telangiectasia) and the thighs (Holster sign) to find the clues that lead to the diagnosis.
