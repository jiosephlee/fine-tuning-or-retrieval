Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Chapter 4: ## Diagnostic Workup and Interpretation of HIT/aHIT Testing

Making the diagnosis of heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) is an exercise in integrating clinical probability with laboratory data. In the postoperative cardiac surgical setting this is especially important because cardiopulmonary bypass, surgical blood loss and routine postoperative anticoagulants all commonly change platelet counts and generate PF4‑reactive antibodies that are frequently nonpathogenic. The first step in any patient with a falling platelet count after heparin exposure is therefore to estimate the pretest probability of HIT using a structured clinical score (for example, the 4Ts: Thrombocytopenia magnitude, Timing of onset, Thrombosis or other sequelae, and oTher causes). Use of the 4Ts guides immediate management: patients with a low pretest probability rarely have HIT and generally do not require empiric non‑heparin anticoagulation, whereas those with an intermediate or high probability should have heparin discontinued and a non‑heparin anticoagulant started while laboratory testing proceeds. Importantly, in post‑CPB patients the timing element must be applied with caution—an early platelet fall in the first 1–3 postoperative days is common after surgery, but a platelet decline consistent with HIT that begins prior to postoperative day 5 can occur if there was a recent heparin exposure (for example, catheterization or other heparin use within the preceding days to weeks). The case above illustrates this point: a modest intra‑procedural heparin dose a week before surgery explained the unusually early onset of true HIT.

Laboratory testing falls into two complementary categories: immunoassays that detect anti‑PF4/polyanion antibodies and functional platelet activation assays that confirm pathogenicity. Immunoassays are fast and sensitive; functional assays are more specific and establish that antibodies in the patient’s serum actually activate platelets. The most widely used immunoassay is the PF4/polyanion ELISA. IgG‑specific assays are preferred because pathogenic HIT antibodies are almost always IgG — assays that also detect IgM or IgA will increase false‑positive rates, particularly after surgery. Optical density (OD) on ELISA correlates with clinical significance: low OD values (below the laboratory cutoff, often ~0.4) are unlikely to represent pathogenic antibodies; intermediate values (≈0.4–1.0) are equivocal; and higher ODs (≥1.0, and especially >2.0) are strongly associated with platelet‑activating antibodies and with thrombotic risk. In cardiac surgical populations many patients will be ELISA‑positive with low ODs but most of these antibodies are nonpathogenic — thus an isolated positive ELISA after cardiopulmonary bypass should not by itself be taken as diagnostic of HIT without consideration of clinical context and functional testing.

Functional assays demonstrate whether the patient’s antibodies can cross‑link platelet FcγRIIa and trigger activation. The serotonin‑release assay (SRA) is the historical gold standard: washed donor platelets loaded with radiolabeled serotonin are incubated with patient serum and varying concentrations of heparin; serotonin release indicates platelet activation. A classical HIT SRA pattern is strong activation at therapeutic heparin concentrations (commonly at 0.1–0.3 U/mL), with marked inhibition at very high heparin concentrations (for example 100 U/mL) — the high‑heparin inhibition occurs because excess heparin disrupts the PF4‑heparin antigenic complexes. The heparin‑induced platelet aggregation (HIPA) assay or flow cytometric assays of P‑selectin or platelet microaggregation are alternative functional tests available in specialized centers. Because functional assays rely on fresh washed platelets from susceptible donors and specialized lab expertise, turnaround is often delayed and testing must be sent to reference laboratories; therefore empiric treatment decisions are frequently made before a functional result is available.

Autoimmune HIT (aHIT) represents a subgroup of patients whose antibodies acquire the ability to activate platelets independent of added heparin; recognizing the laboratory signature of aHIT is crucial because these patients often have more severe thrombocytopenia and more fulminant thrombotic or consumptive coagulopathy. The hallmark SRA pattern of aHIT is substantial platelet activation even in the absence of heparin (0 U/mL), often accompanied by the typical pattern of activation at therapeutic heparin concentrations and inhibition at supratherapeutic heparin. Thus, a positive SRA at 0 U/mL (strong serotonin release without added heparin) is highly suggestive of autoimmune HIT. In many aHIT sera, platelet activation is still suppressed by high heparin concentrations, reflecting disruption of PF4‑containing immune complexes; however, atypical serologic behaviors can occur. One such atypical feature — illustrated by the present case — is partial resistance to blockade by an anti‑FcγRIIa monoclonal antibody (IV.3). Under usual circumstances the addition of IV.3 to functional assays markedly attenuates or abolishes serotonin release from HIT antibodies because platelet activation is FcγRIIa‑dependent. If inhibition is incomplete, this suggests unusually strong immune complexes or, rarely, alternative platelet‑activating mechanisms; partial IV.3 resistance does not exclude HIT/aHIT but marks an aggressive serologic phenotype and should prompt careful clinical correlation and consideration of adjunct testing.

When the SRA is negative or indeterminate despite a high clinical probability or a strongly positive ELISA, several strategies can increase diagnostic sensitivity for aHIT. Laboratories experienced in HIT testing can perform PF4‑enhanced functional assays (for example, adding excess PF4 to the SRA or using a PF4‑dependent P‑selectin expression assay), which unmask antibodies that preferentially bind PF4 rather than PF4/heparin complexes. PF4‑modified assays and platelet‑activation tests measured by flow cytometry tend to be more sensitive for detecting heparin‑independent activity. Repeat testing on a separate specimen or testing sera obtained serially over days may also reveal evolving patterns — aHIT serology can change with time, sometimes shifting to a more typical heparin‑dependent pattern as antibodies mature or wane, as occurred in the case described. If a local laboratory reports an “indeterminate” SRA, request a detailed report and consider referral of the specimen to a reference laboratory where these enhanced or alternative functional assays can be performed, and where IV.3 blockade experiments can be explicitly reported.

Practical laboratory and clinical considerations should be emphasized. Communicate to the testing laboratory the clinical context (recent CPB, recent heparin exposures including catheterization heparin, timing of platelet fall, current anticoagulants), because these details inform assay selection and interpretation. Be aware that rapid immunoassays (lateral flow or particle‑gel) and polyspecific ELISAs may give frequent false‑positive results after cardiac surgery; when possible, use an IgG‑specific ELISA and interpret OD magnitude rather than a binary positive/negative result. Because functional assays can have limited availability and require technical expertise, management often proceeds empirically — for patients with intermediate or high pretest probability, heparin should be stopped and an appropriate non‑heparin anticoagulant begun while testing occurs.

Finally, use ancillary coagulation and hematologic tests both as severity markers and as gauges of therapeutic response. In severe HIT or aHIT, consumptive coagulopathy may develop: falling fibrinogen, rising D‑dimer, development of schistocytes, and progressive anemia are warning signals of ongoing platelet and fibrin consumption and increased thrombotic burden. These trends, together with serial platelet counts, help assess whether anticoagulation and interventions such as high‑dose intravenous immunoglobulin are controlling the immune thrombotic process. In summary, a thoughtful diagnostic approach to suspected HIT/aHIT combines careful clinical assessment (4Ts and documentation of recent heparin exposures), judicious use of IgG‑specific immunoassays with attention to OD levels, and functional confirmation with SRA or alternative platelet activation assays — and, in cases of discordant or indeterminate results, referral for PF4‑enhanced testing and serial specimen analysis is often necessary to resolve diagnosis and guide therapy.
