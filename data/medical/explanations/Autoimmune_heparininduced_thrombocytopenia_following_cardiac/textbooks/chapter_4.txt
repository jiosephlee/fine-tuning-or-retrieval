Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Chapter 4: ## Management of Severe aHIT with Arterial Thrombosis

Immediate management priorities in this case were to stop all heparin exposures, assess bleeding risk, and institute effective non‑heparin anticoagulation while limiting interventions that could worsen antibody‑driven platelet activation. As soon as HIT was suspected because of the postoperative platelet fall, strong PF4/polyanion ELISA and the new cerebral infarct, all heparin products and LMWH were discontinued. Platelet transfusions were avoided because transfused platelets can provide additional substrate for HIT immune complexes and worsen thrombosis; they are reserved only for life‑threatening hemorrhage or urgent procedures that cannot be deferred.

Choice of non‑heparin anticoagulant was guided by two competing needs: rapid control of hypercoagulability and the ability to rapidly reduce anticoagulant effect if hemorrhagic transformation of the stroke or major bleeding occurred. The treating team selected argatroban, a parenteral direct thrombin inhibitor with a short half‑life and easy titration by aPTT, allowing prompt adjustment. In this patient argatroban was started at 0.75 mcg/kg/min with a target aPTT range of 55–85 s, reflecting therapeutic anticoagulation chosen to treat arterial thrombosis while permitting prompt discontinuation if bleeding developed. When using argatroban in HIT, clinicians must monitor aPTT and liver function (hepatic clearance), and be aware there is no specific reversal agent; its short half‑life is the main safety feature.

Adjunctive high‑dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was used because autoimmune HIT (aHIT) antibodies can activate platelets independently of heparin and often respond to immune modulation. Mechanistically, high‑dose IVIG competitively saturates FcγRIIa on platelets and other effector cells and can interrupt Fc‑mediated platelet activation by pathogenic IgG. In this case the team administered large doses (100 g on two consecutive days, followed by a third 100 g dose) because of severe thrombocytopenia and ongoing thrombosis. Clinically, a brisk platelet rise is commonly seen after IVIG in aHIT, but the response can be attenuated in very severe antibody phenotypes; this patient had only a modest platelet increase (from 13×10^9/L to an interim 54×10^9/L), indicating unusually refractory disease.

Because aHIT commonly produces a consumptive, hypercoagulable state, serial laboratory monitoring was used to assess ongoing coagulation activation and treatment adequacy rather than relying solely on platelet counts. In this patient the treating team followed fibrinogen (which fell from an elevated postoperative level toward nadir 2.49 g/L by POD17), d‑dimer (peaked ~POD12), and peripheral blood films that began showing schistocytes from POD9–16. A falling fibrinogen, rising d‑dimer and new schistocytosis despite anticoagulation suggested ongoing thrombin generation and microangiopathic red‑cell injury from uncontrolled HIT‑associated coagulation, and therefore argued for reassessment of anticoagulant strategy.

Because the above markers indicated persistent hypercoagulability on argatroban, the team pivoted to fondaparinux on POD14 (therapeutic dose 10 mg once daily). The reasons were twofold: concern that argatroban was not adequately suppressing thrombin generation in this case, and practical advantages (once‑daily subcutaneous dosing that facilitates outpatient management). Fondaparinux is an indirect, AT‑dependent factor Xa inhibitor that has been used off‑label in HIT with good efficacy in many reports. Its disadvantages include renal clearance and a long half‑life without an approved reversal agent, so renal function must be considered when choosing dosing. In this patient fondaparinux was preferred despite those drawbacks because it offered a different mechanism of anticoagulation and easier long‑term administration while the patient’s clinical picture stabilized.

Because fondaparinux requires antithrombin (AT) for activity, the team measured AT and found it mildly reduced (0.70 U/mL; RR 0.80–1.40). To optimize fondaparinux efficacy, they administered 1000 U of AT concentrate prior to the first therapeutic fondaparinux dose. This step illustrates an important principle when using AT‑dependent agents in patients with consumptive coagulopathy or low AT levels: correcting AT can be necessary to achieve the expected anticoagulant effect.

Stroke management in the setting of HIT requires careful balance between preventing further arterial thrombosis and avoiding hemorrhagic transformation. The clinicians favored an anticoagulant strategy that could be rapidly stopped or adjusted if bleeding developed (hence argatroban initially). When the clinical picture later suggested inadequate control of coagulation, they accepted the trade‑off of switching to fondaparinux for better long‑term suppression of thrombin generation, monitoring closely for bleeding. Decisions about timing and intensity of anticoagulation in stroke with HIT must be individualized, taking infarct size, evolving neurological status, and imaging evidence of hemorrhagic conversion into account; in this patient a 3.2 cm parietal infarct was managed with therapeutic anticoagulation without hemorrhagic complication.

Duration and transitions of therapy were determined by platelet recovery and clinical stabilization. In this case fondaparinux was continued until the platelet count had recovered (platelets normalized by POD62) and the patient had stabilized; fondaparinux was stopped on POD65 and long‑term oral anticoagulation for atrial fibrillation (apixaban 5 mg twice daily) was restarted. Serial functional assays (SRA) remained positive for several months despite clinical recovery, underscoring that serological tests may lag behind clinical improvement and that management decisions are guided primarily by clinical status and platelet recovery.

Key practical takeaways from this case: stop all heparin immediately; avoid platelet transfusion unless absolutely necessary; choose a non‑heparin agent that matches the clinical needs (short half‑life and titratability for high bleeding‑risk situations such as recent stroke, or longer‑acting agents for convenience and sustained anticoagulation); use IVIG for severe aHIT to interrupt FcγRIIa‑mediated activation; monitor markers of consumptive coagulopathy (fibrinogen, d‑dimer, peripheral smear) as adjuncts to platelet counts; and be prepared to change anticoagulant strategy if laboratory or clinical evidence suggests ongoing hypercoagulability. In this patient, that staged approach—argatroban plus IVIG, followed by fondaparinux with AT supplementation and continued monitoring—was associated with eventual platelet recovery and full neurological recovery.
