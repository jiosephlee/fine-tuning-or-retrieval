Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Approach to Post–Cardiac Surgery Thrombocytopenia and Thrombosis

Cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) produces a predictable, transient fall in platelet count in most patients; separating that expected post‑CPB thrombocytopenia from pathologic causes—most importantly heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) and the more aggressive autoimmune form (aHIT)—is a common and time‑sensitive diagnostic challenge. The case just presented illustrates many teaching points: an initially modest post‑operative fall in platelets followed by progressive decline, new ischemic stroke, strong PF4/polyanion serology and an atypical functional assay profile that signalled a heparin‑independent, FcγRIIa‑mediated platelet‑activating antibody. Below is a practical clinical framework to evaluate and manage thrombocytopenia and thrombosis after cardiac surgery, with emphasis on recognizing aHIT and initiating urgent therapy.

Distinguish expected CPB‑related thrombocytopenia from HIT/aHIT
- Typical post‑CPB thrombocytopenia is early (largest decline by POD1–2), modest (often 20–50% fall but usually remaining >100×10^9/L), and recovers over the first week. Mechanisms include hemodilution, platelet activation and sequestration during bypass, and contact activation on artificial surfaces.
- HIT classically shows a biphasic or delayed pattern: relative preservation early after surgery, with a new or progressive platelet fall beginning typically between POD5 and POD10 (though this window shifts earlier with recent heparin exposure). Thrombosis (venous or arterial) is common and often paradoxical in the setting of thrombocytopenia.
- aHIT is an atypical, often more severe variant in which antibodies can activate platelets independent of heparin; clinical hallmarks include profound thrombocytopenia, progressive consumptive coagulopathy, marked hypercoagulability with thrombosis despite cessation of heparin, and sometimes an earlier onset if there was recent heparin exposure (as in preoperative catheterization). In practice, any progressive platelet drop after POD1, particularly when accompanied by new thrombosis, should raise immediate concern for HIT/aHIT.

Early clinical evaluation—history, pattern and differential
- Time course is critical. Ask about all recent heparin exposures (intraoperative UFH, catheterization heparin boluses, heparin flushes, prophylactic LMWH). Early onset (before POD5) should prompt review for a prior recent heparin exposure in the preceding 5–10 days that can “prime” the immune response.
- Characterize the platelet trajectory: percent fall from baseline, nadir, and speed of decline. Profound nadirs (<30×10^9/L) and continued decline despite stopping heparin are worrisome for aHIT.
- Review other causes of post‑operative thrombocytopenia: dilutional effects, sepsis/DIC, mechanical destruction (prosthetic valves, ECMO), drug‑induced immune thrombocytopenia, thrombotic microangiopathies (TTP/HUS), post‑transfusion purpura, platelet clumping/artifact. The peripheral blood film (look for schistocytes, platelet clumps) and basic labs (CBC trend, coagulation panel, hemolysis markers, cultures) help exclude many alternatives. In the case, absence of schistocytes initially, stable hemoglobin and WBCs, and a strong PF4 ELISA pointed to HIT.

Use pretest probability (4Ts) but appreciate limitations after CPB
- Apply a structured score (4Ts: Thrombocytopenia degree, Timing of fall, Thrombosis or other sequelae, oTher causes) to stratify pretest probability. A moderate or high 4Ts score justifies immediate action.
- Caveat: the 4Ts has reduced specificity after cardiac surgery because many non‑HIT mechanisms produce early platelet falls; nevertheless, a rising score driven by progressive fall and new thrombosis should not be ignored. In practice, treat clinical probability and trajectory rather than absolute POD cutoffs.

Laboratory testing strategy—what to order and how to interpret results
- Order a high‑sensitivity PF4/polyanion immunoassay (ELISA) and, in parallel, send or arrange a functional platelet activation assay (serotonin‑release assay, SRA, or heparin‑induced platelet activation assay) to a reference laboratory. Do not wait for confirmatory testing before initiating therapy if clinical probability is moderate/high.
- Interpret results in clinical context:
  - PF4 ELISA is very sensitive but less specific; high optical density (OD) values (for example, >1.0–2.0) increase the likelihood of pathogenic antibodies.
  - Functional assays (SRA) are highly specific: classic HIT shows heparin‑dependent platelet activation (strong release at low heparin [0.1–0.3 U/mL], inhibition at very high heparin). aHIT often demonstrates heparin‑independent platelet activation (serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin) or strong activation across a range of heparin concentrations.
  - Specialized testing (e.g., inhibition with FcγRIIa‑blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3) can confirm Fc receptor dependence. However, as this case demonstrates, atypical serologic patterns can occur—partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade or unusual SRA inhibition profiles—so close liaison with an experienced platelet immunology laboratory is essential.
- Repeat or serial testing may be informative: antibody reactivity can change over weeks (aHIT features may wane to more heparin‑dependent profiles as antibodies decline).

Immediate management steps when HIT/aHIT is suspected
- Stop all heparin exposures immediately: discontinue UFH and LMWH, remove heparin‑containing catheters or locks if feasible, and avoid heparinized flushes. This is necessary but by itself insufficient for HIT/aHIT because thrombosis risk is high.
- Start a non‑heparin parenteral anticoagulant without waiting for functional confirmation when clinical probability is intermediate or high. Choice depends on clinical context:
  - Argatroban (direct thrombin inhibitor) is commonly used in the postoperative setting, has a short half‑life and is hepatically metabolized (advantageous in renal dysfunction; needs PTT monitoring and dose adjustment in hepatic impairment).
  - Bivalirudin (direct thrombin inhibitor) is often used in cardiac procedural settings and is an option for perioperative anticoagulation; it is partially renally cleared.
  - Fondaparinux (indirect anti‑Xa via antithrombin) has been used off‑label for HIT and is given subcutaneously once daily; it is renally cleared and requires sufficient antithrombin levels and renal function. In the case, fondaparinux was used after initial argatroban when practical once bleeding risk and AT level were assessed.
  - Note: direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) are increasingly used for outpatient management of HIT once platelet counts have recovered, but they are not the first choice for immediate inpatient therapy when bleeding risk or renal function are concerns.
- Avoid platelet transfusion unless there is life‑threatening bleeding or an urgent invasive procedure; transfusion can exacerbate thrombosis in HIT.

Adjunctive and rescue therapies for aHIT
- High‑dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) can be effective in aHIT by blocking Fcγ receptor‑mediated platelet activation; typical regimens used in reports include 1 g/kg daily for 2 days or single doses of 100 g in severe cases as adjunctive therapy. IVIG can raise thrombotic risk transiently and must be balanced against the severity of aHIT; involve hematology early.
- Plasma exchange (therapeutic plasmapheresis) has been used in extreme or refractory cases to remove pathogenic antibodies, especially when urgent cardiac surgery is required.
- Monitor coagulation biomarkers (d‑dimer, fibrinogen) and peripheral smear serially; worsening consumptive coagulopathy despite anticoagulation suggests uncontrolled HIT‑associated thrombosis and should prompt reassessment of anticoagulant choice/intensity and consideration of adjunctive measures.

Diagnosis and treatment of thrombotic complications
- Maintain a low threshold for imaging when neurologic changes, new limb symptoms, or unexplained organ ischemia occur; delirium or altered mental status in postoperative patients can herald an ischemic stroke and should prompt CT/MRI as clinically appropriate. Detection of arterial thrombosis (as in the parietal infarct in the case) confirms HIT‑associated thrombosis and necessitates therapeutic anticoagulation.
- Treat proven HIT‑associated thrombosis as provoked: anticoagulate with a non‑heparin agent for at least 3 months. If HIT occurs without thrombosis, a common recommendation is approximately 4 weeks of non‑heparin anticoagulation.
- Transition to long‑term oral anticoagulation: warfarin should not be started until platelet count has recovered to a safe threshold (commonly >150×10^9/L) and the patient has been adequately anticoagulated with a non‑heparin agent, because initiating warfarin during active HIT increases risk of venous limb gangrene due to rapid fall in protein C. DOACs (e.g., apixaban, rivaroxaban) are increasingly used as outpatient alternatives once platelets have recovered; timing and choice should be individualized.

Practical algorithm (summary)
1. Recognize pattern: obtain baseline platelet count and follow daily. Any progressive drop after POD1 or new thrombosis should prompt HIT evaluation.
2. Review recent heparin exposures (including preoperative cath) and calculate 4Ts to assess pretest probability, knowing the 4Ts has limits after CPB.
3. If 4Ts is intermediate/high or clinical suspicion is strong:
   - Stop all heparin immediately.
   - Start a non‑heparin anticoagulant (argatroban, bivalirudin or fondaparinux as appropriate to renal/hepatic function and bleeding risk).
   - Order PF4/polyanion ELISA and functional platelet activation testing in parallel; contact a reference platelet immunology laboratory early if results are atypical.
   - Obtain imaging as indicated for suspected thrombosis (brain CT/MRI for neurologic changes).
   - In cases suggestive of aHIT (heparin‑independent activation, profound thrombocytopenia, consumptive coagulopathy), add hematology consultation, consider IVIG and, rarely, plasmapheresis.
4. Monitor platelets, coagulation parameters, hemolysis markers and clinical signs of thrombosis; avoid platelet transfusion unless bleeding.
5. Once platelet counts recover, plan long‑term anticoagulation (typically 3 months for HIT with thrombosis, 4 weeks for isolated HIT) and arrange follow‑up testing and documentation in the medical record to avoid future heparin exposure.

Closing practical notes from the case
- The presented patient’s early onset of HIT before POD5 emphasizes the importance of documenting and asking about any preoperative heparin (he received UFH during cardiac catheterization). Small doses given days earlier can prime an immune response and produce an earlier clinical HIT after surgery.
- Strong ELISA reactivity (high OD) together with an SRA demonstrating heparin‑independent platelet activation should alert clinicians to aHIT. Do not dismiss “indeterminate” SRA patterns without consulting the reference laboratory; atypical profiles—partial resistance to Fc blockade, strong activation at 0 U/mL heparin—are consistent with severe aHIT and mandate aggressive therapy.
- Timely cessation of heparin, immediate non‑heparin anticoagulation, early hematology involvement, and consideration of IVIG or plasmapheresis when aHIT is suspected can be life‑ and limb‑saving.
