Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Chapter 2: ## Mechanisms and Phenotypes: Classic HIT versus Autoimmune HIT (aHIT)

Heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) is an immune‑mediated complication in which antibodies target complexes of platelet factor 4 (PF4) bound to polyanions such as heparin. In the canonical or “classic” model, PF4 is released from platelet alpha granules when platelets are activated. PF4 is highly cationic and, when it binds to an anionic glycosaminoglycan such as heparin, forms multimolecular complexes that expose neoepitopes. IgG antibodies directed against these PF4/heparin complexes form immune complexes that crosslink FcγRIIa receptors on platelets. FcγRIIa clustering triggers potent platelet activation, aggregation and procoagulant microparticle release, with downstream thrombin generation. Activated platelets and thrombin convert hemostasis into a marked prothrombotic state — explaining why HIT is paradoxically associated more with thrombosis than bleeding despite thrombocytopenia. In addition to platelets, PF4/antibody complexes can activate monocytes (upregulating tissue factor) and injure endothelium, further amplifying thrombosis.

Cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) is a particularly high‑risk setting for HIT for several mechanistic reasons that are directly relevant to this case. CPB and the surgical procedure cause substantial platelet activation and degranulation (thus increased PF4 release), a systemic inflammatory response, and exposure of blood to artificial surfaces that promote PF4 binding and complex formation. In addition, cardiac surgery patients routinely receive large intraoperative doses of unfractionated heparin (UFH) and postoperative heparin or low‑molecular‑weight heparin, providing the polyanion partner for PF4 to form the antigenic complexes. These features increase both the likelihood of generating pathogenic anti‑PF4 antibodies and the clinical consequences when such antibodies arise.

Autoimmune HIT (aHIT) refers to a subset of HIT in which the patient’s antibodies can activate platelets independently of exogenous heparin. In aHIT, antibodies recognize PF4 alone or PF4 complexes that are stable without added heparin, so patient serum can cause platelet activation even when no heparin is present. Clinically, aHIT tends to be more severe: platelet counts are often lower, thrombosis risk and consumptive coagulopathy (falling fibrinogen, rising D‑dimer, schistocytes) are greater, and antibody effects are more persistent. The heparin‑independent activity also explains why stopping heparin alone may be insufficient to immediately reverse the process.

Early‑onset HIT (platelet fall before post‑operative day 5 in cardiac surgery patients) is best explained by an anamnestic immune response after recent prior heparin exposure. Memory B cells primed by a recent heparin exposure (for example, UFH given during a preoperative catheterization) can rapidly produce IgG on re‑exposure, so immune complex–mediated platelet activation and thrombocytopenia occur earlier than the usual 5–10 day interval needed for a primary response. The present case illustrates this mechanism: the patient’s intraprocedural UFH during the heart catheterization a week before surgery provided the priming exposure that allowed the postoperative antibody–mediated platelet fall to begin before POD5.

Understanding the laboratory assays helps link serology to these pathophysiologic phenotypes. The PF4/polyanion ELISA is a screening immunoassay that detects antibodies binding PF4/polyanion complexes regardless of whether they are functionally platelet‑activating. A high optical density (OD) in the ELISA correlates with a larger antibody burden and increases the pretest probability that antibodies are clinically significant. The functional serotonin‑release assay (SRA) tests whether patient antibodies actually activate platelets: donor platelets labeled with radiolabeled serotonin are exposed to patient serum with varying heparin concentrations, and serotonin release indicates platelet activation. In classic HIT, the SRA classically shows little activation at 0 U/mL heparin, strong activation at low (therapeutic) heparin concentrations (eg, 0.1–0.3 U/mL), and inhibition of activation at very high heparin concentrations (eg, 100 U/mL) because excess heparin disrupts the multimeric PF4 complexes needed for antibody binding. In aHIT, by contrast, strong serotonin release at 0 U/mL (heparin‑independent activation) is a defining feature.

This case’s serology illustrates an extreme aHIT phenotype. The PF4/polyanion ELISA was strongly positive (OD 2.2), consistent with a high burden of anti‑PF4 antibodies. The SRA showed robust platelet activation at 0, 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL heparin and the expected inhibition at 100 U/mL; the activation at 0 U/mL identifies heparin‑independent (autoimmune) activity. A further unusual laboratory finding was only partial inhibition of SRA activity by the FcγRIIa‑blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3. In typical HIT, blockade of FcγRIIa abolishes antibody‑mediated platelet activation in the SRA, confirming that Fc receptor crosslinking is the dominant mechanism. Partial resistance to IV.3, as seen here, implies an unusually strong or multivalent interaction between antibodies and platelets: possible explanations include very high‑avidity IgG, large immune complex formation that overwhelms the blocking antibody, or engagement of additional platelet‑activating pathways. The clinical correlate was an unusually severe, persistent thrombocytopenia, partial biochemical signs of ongoing consumptive coagulopathy despite anticoagulation, and a blunted platelet recovery after high‑dose IVIG — all features compatible with refractory aHIT.

Finally, the evolution of antibody phenotype in this patient illustrates the dynamic nature of the response. Over weeks to months, the heparin‑independent activating activity waned: later SRA profiles showed the classic pattern of heparin dependence (loss of activation at 0 U/mL with activation at 0.3 U/mL), reflecting decline of the aHIT‑type antibodies and emergence or persistence of more conventional heparin‑dependent antibodies. This serologic evolution paralleled clinical recovery of platelet counts and resolution of neurologic injury, underscoring how shifting immunologic phenotypes map to the clinical course in HIT and aHIT.

In summary, classic HIT depends on PF4/heparin complexes and FcγRIIa‑mediated platelet activation, producing a prothrombotic state; aHIT represents antibodies that can activate platelets without heparin and therefore tend to cause earlier, more severe, and more persistent disease. The patient’s preoperative heparin exposure explains the early onset, and the combination of high ELISA OD, heparin‑independent SRA activation, and partial resistance to FcγRIIa blockade documents an unusually intense aHIT serologic phenotype that accounts for the severe clinical course and modest initial response to therapies such as IVIG.
