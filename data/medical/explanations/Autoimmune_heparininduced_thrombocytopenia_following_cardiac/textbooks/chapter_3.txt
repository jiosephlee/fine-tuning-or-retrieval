Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Reasoning in Early Post-Cardiac Surgery Thrombocytopenia

Evaluating thrombocytopenia after cardiopulmonary bypass requires a structured approach that separates the expected, transient platelet changes of surgery from immune-mediated processes such as heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). Cardiopulmonary bypass typically causes an immediate postoperative platelet decline because of hemodilution, platelet activation and sequestration on extracorporeal surfaces; platelet counts commonly fall to a nadir on POD1–2 and then recover over the first week. By contrast, HIT classically produces a second, delayed decline in platelets beginning about POD5–10 (the “biphasic” pattern): an initial post‑CPB drop followed by recovery and then a new fall driven by anti‑PF4/heparin antibodies. Recognizing this timing pattern is central to distinguishing routine post‑operative thrombocytopenia from HIT, but the pattern is not absolute—recent heparin exposure can cause earlier HIT onset, and many non‑immune causes of platelet fall complicate interpretation after heart surgery.

Common diagnostic algorithms such as the 4Ts (Thrombocytopenia degree, Timing, Thrombosis, oTher causes) remain useful but have important limitations after cardiac surgery. The specificity of the 4Ts score is reduced because most cardiac surgery patients have an early platelet fall (lowering the “Timing” and “Other causes” discrimination), and postoperative conditions that mimic thrombosis (for example, delirium or stroke masked by sedation) may be difficult to interpret. Thus, the 4Ts should be applied cautiously, with a low threshold to pursue laboratory testing when clinical features raise concern (notably a >50% platelet fall, new thrombosis, or very low nadir counts).

The differential diagnosis in the postoperative cardiac patient is broad. Sepsis or severe infection causes thrombocytopenia through consumption and bone marrow suppression; disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) produces falling fibrinogen, elevated D‑dimer and schistocytes; cardiogenic shock and severe illness cause platelet consumption and hemodilution; drug‑induced immune thrombocytopenia (DITP) may occur from perioperative antibiotics or other agents and typically has an abrupt fall soon after drug exposure; post‑transfusion purpura (PTP) and thrombotic microangiopathies such as TTP are other rare causes—PTP requires recent transfusion and TTP is suggested by microangiopathic hemolysis, schistocytes, and severe organ dysfunction. Ongoing bleeding or surgical consumption typically produces falling hemoglobin and hemodynamic instability in addition to thrombocytopenia. Each competing diagnosis has specific clinical or laboratory signatures that guide exclusion: hemodynamic stability and negative cultures argue against shock or sepsis; normal haptoglobin and absence of schistocytes argue against hemolysis or TTP early on; stable hemoglobin and no transfusions argue against bleeding or PTP; and an elevated fibrinogen (an acute‑phase response) argues against fulminant consumption as seen in overt DIC at that time point.

Applying these principles to the presented patient clarifies why HIT rose to the top of the differential despite some atypical features. Clinically he was hemodynamically stable with normal perfusion, blood and urine cultures were negative, and no perioperative transfusions were given—findings that make sepsis, cardiogenic shock and PTP unlikely. The early laboratory profile showed a normal reticulocyte count, elevated (not low) haptoglobin and, initially, a peripheral smear without schistocytes; hemoglobin remained at expected postoperative levels, arguing against ongoing bleeding, hemolysis or TTP. The elevated fibrinogen was consistent with a postoperative acute‑phase response rather than consumptive coagulopathy at that time. Together, these data reduced the likelihood of most alternative explanations and focused attention on an immune cause.

Timing of heparin exposure was the key to resolving the apparent temporal mismatch. Although the intraoperative UFH exposure during cardiopulmonary bypass is usually too near the surgery to produce HIT within the first few postoperative days, the patient had been exposed to UFH (2,000 U) during a transradial heart catheterization one week before surgery. Recent heparin exposure is a well‑recognized precipitant of earlier or “rapid‑onset” HIT: patients who are sensitized by prior heparin within the preceding 30 days can develop antibody‑mediated platelet activation at a time that, superficially, appears earlier than the usual POD5–10 window. Recognition of that preoperative exposure changed the timing interpretation and increased pretest probability for HIT.

Laboratory testing then provided two complementary pieces of evidence. The PF4/polyanion ELISA was strongly positive (OD 2.2), and high OD values strongly suggest the presence of pathogenic anti‑PF4 antibodies rather than low‑titer, clinically irrelevant antibodies frequently seen after surgery. However, immunoassays are sensitive but not entirely specific, particularly after CPB when many patients form anti‑PF4 antibodies without clinical HIT; therefore a functional platelet activation assay is required to confirm that antibodies are platelet‑activating.

The serotonin‑release assay (SRA) reported an “indeterminate” result, but careful interpretation showed that the pattern was actually diagnostic of autoimmune HIT (aHIT). In classic heparin‑dependent HIT, the SRA shows platelet activation only in the presence of low therapeutic heparin concentrations (eg, 0.1–0.3 U/mL) and is inhibited at very high heparin concentrations because excess heparin prevents pathogenic PF4/heparin complex formation. Importantly, activation is absent when no heparin is added. In this patient the SRA demonstrated very strong serotonin release at 0.1–0.3 U/mL (consistent with HIT) but also strong release at 0 U/mL (heparin‑independent activation), a hallmark of aHIT. In addition, addition of the FcγRIIa‑blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3 incompletely suppressed activation; most HIT antibodies are fully blocked by IV.3 in vitro, so incomplete inhibition suggested that the patient’s antibodies were unusually potent or used partially Fc‑independent mechanisms of platelet activation. Rather than excluding HIT, this “atypical” SRA pattern, together with the high ELISA OD and compatible clinical picture, strongly supported a diagnosis of aHIT.

Because functional serology can evolve, repeat and serial testing are important. In this case subsequent samples over weeks showed changing SRA profiles—initially dominated by heparin‑independent activation and later reverting toward a heparin‑dependent pattern as aHIT antibodies waned—correlating with the clinical course and platelet recovery. This temporal evolution both confirmed the diagnosis and documented the expected serologic decline.

Finally, the occurrence of an ischemic stroke on POD6 is a critical piece of the diagnostic puzzle. HIT is prothrombotic and paradoxically more often causes thrombosis than bleeding; new arterial or venous thrombosis substantially increases the post‑test probability that anti‑PF4 antibodies are clinically significant. The brain infarct therefore strengthened the clinical diagnosis and justified urgent management decisions even in the setting of an unconventional SRA result.

In sum, diagnostic reasoning in this patient required integrating the expected postoperative platelet dynamics, careful exclusion of competing causes by clinical and laboratory data, recognition of an antecedent heparin exposure that explained early timing, and nuanced interpretation of immunologic and functional assays. The constellation of a very high‑titer PF4 ELISA, heparin‑independent platelet activation with partial resistance to Fc blockade on SRA, the temporal evolution of serology, and a new thrombotic event together established autoimmune HIT as the leading and ultimately confirmed diagnosis.
