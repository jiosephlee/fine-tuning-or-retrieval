Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Chapter 3: ## Differential Diagnosis of Early Postoperative Thrombocytopenia After CPB

A falling platelet count after cardiac surgery is common and has a wide differential. The case above illustrates how a relatively dramatic, early postoperative thrombocytopenia can be misattributed to routine post-cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) effects unless a systematic approach is taken. The aim of this section is to provide a pragmatic, pathophysiology‑based differential diagnosis for significant platelet declines after CPB and to highlight the clinical and laboratory features that point toward heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) and its autoimmune variant (aHIT).

Common non‑immune causes after CPB
Cardiopulmonary bypass itself is the most frequent cause of an early postoperative platelet fall. Mechanical shear, contact activation on foreign surfaces, haemodilution from priming solutions and intraoperative blood loss and transfusion combine to lower platelet counts in most patients. Typically these declines occur immediately or within the first 24 hours, are modest (often 30%–50% from baseline), and stabilize or begin to recover by postoperative day (POD) 3–4 as platelet production and redistribution normalize. Platelet sequestration in a hypothermic spleen, re‑equilibration of volume status, and consumption related to surgical bleeding also produce predictable, early declines. In most patients there is an uncomplicated postoperative course with stable haemodynamics, no new thrombosis, and recovery of platelet count without additional intervention—this pattern favors CPB‑related, nonimmune etiologies.

Critical illness, sepsis and DIC
When thrombocytopenia occurs in the setting of hemodynamic instability, persistent fever or organ dysfunction, sepsis and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) must be considered. Laboratory hallmarks that distinguish these processes from uncomplicated CPB‑related platelet loss include prolonged prothrombin time (PT)/INR and/or aPTT, marked elevation of D‑dimer with falling fibrinogen (hypofibrinogenemia in consumptive DIC), and biochemical evidence of organ dysfunction. Peripheral smear often shows schistocytes when microangiopathic destruction is present. Sepsis‑associated thrombocytopenia tends to evolve with systemic inflammatory signs and is accompanied by progressive coagulopathy rather than the isolated, profound platelet drop seen with immune causes.

Immune‑mediated thrombocytopenias
Immune mechanisms produce some of the most clinically important postoperative thrombocytopenias because they carry a risk of thrombosis and often require urgent change in management.

- Heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) and autoimmune HIT (aHIT). Classical HIT is antibody mediated (IgG antibodies against PF4/heparin complexes) and typically manifests between POD5 and POD10 after an initiating heparin exposure. After cardiac surgery the incidence is higher than in many settings because of extensive intraoperative UFH use. Typical HIT causes an intermediate platelet fall (often 30%–70% from baseline) with a strong predilection for thrombosis rather than bleeding. aHIT denotes a subset of antibodies that can activate platelets independently of added heparin; clinically these patients may have more severe thrombocytopenia, more aggressive and persistent hypercoagulability, and unusual serologic test patterns (for example, platelet activation in functional assays even without heparin). Important clinical clues favoring HIT/aHIT in the postoperative setting are a new thrombotic event (arterial or venous), a strong‑positive PF4/polyanion ELISA (high optical density, e.g. >1.0–2.0), and functional assay evidence of heparin‑independent platelet activation. Rapid‑onset HIT (within 24 hours of re‑exposure to heparin) occurs in patients with recent prior heparin exposure (for example, the low‑dose UFH given at catheterization in the case) and can produce an apparently “early” postoperative HIT picture—even before POD5.

- Drug‑induced immune thrombocytopenia (DITP). Many drugs can cause an immune thrombocytopenia, producing severe platelet reductions. The characteristic temporal pattern is 5–10 days after first exposure, but earlier onset can occur with prior sensitization. Postoperative DITP should be considered when a new drug with known association (e.g., certain antibiotics, glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors, quinine derivatives) has been started and the timing fits. In the cardiac surgical setting, many of the commonly used perioperative drugs are not classic causes, and DITP is less likely if the timing is very early and no new culprit drug was introduced.

- Post‑transfusion purpura (PTP). PTP requires prior blood product exposure and is characterized by severe thrombocytopenia occurring about 5–10 days after transfusion; it is therefore unlikely in patients who received no transfusion or when the onset is very early.

Thrombotic microangiopathies (TTP/HUS)
Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) and hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS) cause thrombocytopenia from platelet microthrombi and are classically accompanied by microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA): schistocytes on smear, low haptoglobin, elevated LDH, and indirect hyperbilirubinemia. Neurologic symptoms and renal dysfunction are common. If the clinical picture and laboratory evidence support MAHA, ADAMTS13 activity testing and urgent hematology consultation are indicated because TTP requires plasma‑directed therapy (PEX) rather than the anticoagulant strategies used for HIT.

Device‑related and mechanical causes
Mechanical devices that remain in place after surgery—such as intra‑aortic balloon pumps, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) circuits or ventricular assist devices—can cause continuous platelet activation and consumption. These sources produce a pattern of ongoing platelet loss often accompanied by evidence of hemolysis and elevated LDH. Device malfunction or high shear states from valvular prostheses can produce similar features. A focused device inspection and consideration of flow dynamics are necessary when these devices are present.

Pseudo‑thrombocytopenia and laboratory artifacts
Before escalating therapy, confirm true thrombocytopenia. EDTA‑induced platelet clumping can spuriously lower automated platelet counts; a peripheral smear will show clumps and repeating the count in a citrate or heparin tube usually resolves the discrepancy. Other preanalytical errors (sample clotting, platelet satellitism) should be excluded early.

Pattern recognition and laboratory clues that favor HIT/aHIT
Timing. Classical HIT post‑CPB tends to appear in the POD5–10 window. Two exceptions are important: (1) rapid‑onset HIT occurs within hours to 1 day after heparin re‑exposure in patients with recent heparin exposure (within the past 100 days or so), and (2) aHIT can produce earlier and more severe platelet falls because of preformed or heparin‑independent antibodies. Thus an “early” platelet drop should prompt a careful review of heparin exposures within the preceding weeks.

Degree of thrombocytopenia. Typical HIT produces moderate thrombocytopenia (nadir often >20–30×10^9/L). Very profound nadirs (e.g., <20×10^9/L), especially when accompanied by laboratory evidence of consumptive coagulopathy (falling fibrinogen, rising D‑dimer, schistocytes), should raise concern for aHIT or another fulminant consumptive process.

Thrombosis and clinical course. New arterial or venous thrombosis—particularly unusual sites or arterial events such as ischemic stroke soon after surgery—strongly supports HIT/aHIT. Progressive thrombocytopenia despite stopping heparin, or poor response to usual measures, suggests an autoimmune phenotype with sustained platelet‑activating antibodies.

Laboratory testing strategy
- Confirm true thrombocytopenia with a smear and repeat counts in another anticoagulant if clumping is suspected.  
- Obtain coagulation studies (PT/INR, aPTT), fibrinogen, D‑dimer, LDH, haptoglobin and bilirubin to screen for DIC or MAHA.  
- If HIT is suspected, order a PF4/polyanion immunoassay (ELISA) and request that the lab report the optical density; high OD values correlate with clinically significant (platelet‑activating) antibodies. Immunoassays are sensitive but not specific—false positives are common after surgery—so functional testing (serotonin‑release assay, SRA, or other platelet activation assays) is used for confirmation.  
- Interpret functional assays in context: classic HIT shows platelet activation at low heparin concentrations (0.1–0.3 U/mL) with inhibition at very high heparin (100 U/mL). aHIT can produce activation without added heparin (0 U/mL), consistent with heparin‑independent platelet activation; such activation is also typically inhibited at supratherapeutic heparin concentrations. Laboratories sometimes perform FcγRIIa blockade (e.g., with monoclonal antibody IV.3) to show Fc receptor–dependent activation: complete inhibition supports a classic FcγRIIa‑mediated mechanism. Partial or absent inhibition—as in the presented case—is unusual and suggests an unusually potent or atypical mechanism of platelet activation that still may represent aHIT. Because serologic patterns can evolve, repeat testing on later sera can be informative.

Putting it together: a practical approach
1. Immediately confirm platelet count and review the peripheral smear. Exclude clumping and gross hemolysis.  
2. Rapidly assess timing and heparin exposures in the prior days to weeks (operative UFH, catheterization heparin doses, prophylactic LMWH). Early platelet fall does not exclude HIT if there was recent preoperative exposure.  
3. Look for red flags that increase the pretest probability of HIT/aHIT: new thrombosis, severe nadir, consumptive coagulopathy, high OD PF4 ELISA, and functional assay activation at 0 U/mL.  
4. If HIT is reasonably suspected, stop all heparin (including heparin flushes) and begin an alternative non‑heparin anticoagulant while testing is pending; consult hematology early. For suspected aHIT with severe thrombocytopenia or ongoing thrombosis, consider adjunctive therapies such as high‑dose IVIG under specialist guidance.  
5. If features favor sepsis, DIC or TTP, the management diverges (antibiotics and supportive care for sepsis/DIC; urgent plasma exchange for TTP) and these diagnoses must be rapidly excluded or confirmed.

Conclusion
Early postoperative thrombocytopenia after CPB is most often benign and related to CPB, haemodilution and blood loss, but a systematic assessment will identify the minority of patients with immune‑mediated and consumptive causes that mandate very different therapies. HIT and especially aHIT should be suspected when platelet counts fall substantially and/or when thrombosis develops, particularly if there is recent heparin exposure before surgery. A careful combination of timing, clinical features, smear review and targeted laboratory testing (PF4 ELISA with OD and functional assay interpretation) allows discrimination among causes and guides urgent, sometimes lifesaving, therapeutic decisions. The case reported above underscores the importance of this structured approach: an apparently “early” thrombocytopenia was in fact HIT/aHIT triggered by preoperative heparin exposure, and recognition of the atypical serologic profile guided appropriate therapy.
