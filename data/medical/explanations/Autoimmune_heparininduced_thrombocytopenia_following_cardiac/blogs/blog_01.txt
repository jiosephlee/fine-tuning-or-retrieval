Title: Blog about the case report: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

# The POD5 Trap: Recognizing Early-Onset HIT After Cardiac Surgery

This case catches you because it breaks the rules you teach trainees: the platelet count begins falling from POD1 and keeps dropping through POD5, the PF4/polyanion ELISA is strongly positive, and the patient develops an ischemic stroke. “Too early for HIT” is a dangerous thought here — and the reason this patient had early-onset, severe disease was simple and easily overlooked: a tiny preoperative heparin exposure during a radial cath one week earlier primed a brisk anamnestic autoimmune response. Add in LMWH given after surgery (antigenic fuel), and you have the perfect storm for autoimmune HIT (aHIT).

Below I walk through how to separate expected CPB-related thrombocytopenia from true HIT in the first postoperative week, why even small pre-op heparin doses matter, what to do about post-op LMWH, and how to interpret the laboratory messiness that aHIT can produce.

## Why this is surprising (and important)
Cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) routinely causes a transient platelet fall (nadir around POD2–3) with recovery by POD4–5. In contrast, classic HIT almost always starts on POD5–10. This patient’s progressive fall from 210 → 155 (POD1) → 50 (POD5) → 24 (POD6) plus a strong PF4 ELISA and new left parietal infarct signals that the usual timing rules are exceptions, not absolutes.

Key unusual features in this case:
- Onset before POD5 because of preoperative UFH during the cath (2000 U) one week earlier — enough to prime an anamnestic response.
- Very severe thrombocytopenia (nadir 13×10^9/L) with consumptive coagulopathy (falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimer, schistocytes) and stroke — classic for autoimmune HIT.
- Atypical SRA profile: strong platelet activation without heparin (heparin-independent) and only partial inhibition by an FcγRIIa-blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3) — a novel serologic pattern that could produce an “indeterminate” lab result but still represents pathologic aHIT.

If you reflexively rule out HIT because “it’s too early,” you risk missing a dangerous diagnosis.

## How to tell CPB-related thrombocytopenia from HIT in the first week
Think pattern, not a clock. CPB-related platelet decline:
- Nadir typically POD1–3, then recovery by POD4–5.
- Magnitude usually modest (platelets rarely drop into very severe ranges solely from CPB).
- No new thrombosis.

HIT (including aHIT) clues:
- Progressive platelet fall after initial post-op recovery or a continued drop from POD1 through POD5 (especially >30–50% fall).
- Very low nadirs (this case 13×10^9/L) and/or consumptive labs (falling fibrinogen, rising d-dimer, schistocytes).
- New thrombosis (stroke here) or skin necrosis.
- Strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA (OD 2.2 in this case — hard to ignore).
- Recent heparin exposure within the preceding 100 days — even a small intraprocedural dose can prime an anamnestic response.

Practical clinical approach:
- Don’t be reassured by “too early” timing if the platelet trend is progressive, the decline is large, thrombosis occurs, or PF4 ELISA is strongly positive.
- Ask about any heparin exposure in the prior 3 months — caths, central lines flushed with heparin, heparin-coated devices, etc.

## Why small pre-op UFH matters
Immune HIT can be primed by prior heparin exposures. Even a small procedural bolus (2,000 U during a radial cath) can generate an immune response that becomes clinically apparent when the patient receives a larger perioperative heparin exposure or post-op heparinoids. The heart surgery provided a high intraoperative heparin load (45,000 U), and then therapeutic or prophylactic LMWH post-op can sustain antigenic drive because LMWH cross‑reacts with PF4 antibodies. In other words, tiny exposures can seed memory B cells that permit a rapid anamnestic antibody surge after subsequent heparin antigen exposure.

Bottom line: always screen for any heparin exposure in the prior 100 days when you see unexpected postoperative thrombocytopenia.

## Laboratory testing: don’t get fooled by “indeterminate”
- PF4/polyanion ELISA: a high optical density (OD) is strongly predictive of clinically significant HIT. This patient’s OD of 2.2 is a loud red flag.
- Functional assays (SRA): classic HIT shows heparin-dependent platelet activation (low at 0 U, high at therapeutic low heparin concentrations, inhibited at very high heparin). Autoimmune HIT often shows heparin-independent activation (high release at 0 U heparin).
- Some aHIT antibodies are unusually aggressive and may not be fully blocked by FcγRIIa blockade (IV.3), producing “indeterminate” SRA reports. Don’t dismiss an indeterminate SRA when the clinical picture and ELISA are compelling — discuss the profile with the lab and consider the possibility of aHIT.

In short: integrate serology with the clinical picture. A strong ELISA plus clinical thrombotic events should trump a confusing SRA readout.

## Management pearls from the case
- Stop all heparinoids immediately once HIT is suspected (this includes UFH and LMWH). Continuing LMWH prophylaxis after surgery can perpetuate antigenic drive and worsen HIT.
- Start a non-heparin anticoagulant empirically while testing is pending. Agent choice depends on bleeding risk and logistics:
  - Argatroban: intravenous direct thrombin inhibitor with short half-life — sensible when there’s bleeding concern (used in this case).
  - Bivalirudin: alternative in some centers.
  - Fondaparinux: an indirect anti-Xa (off-label for HIT) often used subcutaneously; effective in many cases but dependent on antithrombin and renal function (this case used fondaparinux later).
- Autoimmune HIT may be refractory to usual measures. High‑dose IVIG can neutralize antibody pathogenicity and is recommended for severe aHIT (very low platelets, ongoing thrombosis, heparin-independent activity). This patient received 3×100 g IVIG doses with a modest platelet response — indicating unusually severe disease.
- If switching to fondaparinux and AT is low, consider AT replacement (this case received AT concentrate prior to fondaparinux) — be guided by local practice and hematology input.
- Platelet transfusion: avoid unless bleeding or an invasive procedure is required; transfusion can fuel thrombosis in HIT.
- Duration of anticoagulation: after platelet recovery and stabilization, transition to an oral non-heparin anticoagulant (this patient resumed apixaban on POD65).

## Interpreting this case to guide practice
- Ask explicitly about any heparin exposure in the prior 3 months — small procedural doses are meaningful.
- If platelet count is falling progressively through POD3–5, especially with thrombosis, act as if it’s HIT until proven otherwise.
- A strong PF4 ELISA should prompt cessation of all heparins and initiation of non-heparin anticoagulation even if SRA is indeterminate or shows atypical features.
- Engage hematology early for aHIT management and to interpret unusual functional assay results (heparin-independent activation, partial IV.3 resistance).
- Expect a longer recovery in aHIT; platelet normalization may take many weeks and serologic positivity can persist for months.

## Key take-home messages
- Don’t use “POD ≤5” as an absolute rule to exclude HIT after cardiac surgery — preoperative heparin exposures can produce early-onset HIT.
- Progressive platelet falls, very low platelet nadirs, new thrombosis, and a high OD PF4 ELISA are red flags for HIT/aHIT even in the first postoperative week.
- Stop all heparins (UFH and LMWH) immediately if HIT is suspected; start a non-heparin anticoagulant and involve hematology.
- Autoimmune HIT can show heparin-independent platelet activation and atypical SRA patterns (including partial resistance to FcγRIIa blockade); don’t be reassured by an “indeterminate” lab when the clinical picture fits.
- IVIG is indicated for severe aHIT; be prepared for a prolonged recovery and prolonged serologic positivity.

This case is a great reminder: the clock is a guide, not an inviolable law. When the clinical story, platelet trend, and serology point to HIT — act fast, stop the heparin, and treat aggressively.
