Title: Blog about the case report: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

# Autoimmune HIT in the Lab: When the SRA Lights Up at Zero Heparin

What makes this case interesting?
A man in his 70s developed severe thrombocytopenia and a thrombotic stroke after cardiac surgery. The PF4/polyanion ELISA was strongly positive (OD 2.2), but the serotonin‑release assay (SRA) report was labelled “indeterminate.” Why? Because the patient’s serum caused very strong platelet activation even in the absence of heparin (96% release at 0 U/mL) — a hallmark of autoimmune HIT (aHIT) — yet addition of the FcγRIIa‑blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3 only partially suppressed activation (76% serotonin release remained). That atypical SRA profile can mislead clinicians and laboratories into undercalling a true, highly pathogenic aHIT.

If you only remember one thing from this post: a strong PF4 ELISA + thrombotic event + robust 0‑heparin SRA activation should make you treat for aHIT, even if the functional assay report uses words like “indeterminate” because IV.3 didn’t fully block activation.

Quick primer: classic HIT vs autoimmune HIT (aHIT) on testing
- Classic (heparin‑dependent) HIT: antibodies require heparin/PF4 complexes to activate platelets. On SRA you typically see low release at 0 U/mL, high release at low therapeutic heparin concentrations (eg 0.1–0.3 U/mL), and inhibition at very high heparin (eg 100 U/mL). IV.3 (FcγRIIa blocker) normally abolishes serotonin release.
- Autoimmune HIT (aHIT): antibodies can activate platelets without added heparin — robust release at 0 U/mL — and often still show the classic inhibition at very high heparin. aHIT is associated with more severe thrombocytopenia, consumptive coagulopathy and thrombosis, and frequently requires IVIG on top of non‑heparin anticoagulation.

Why the IV.3 partial inhibition matters (and why it can happen)
IV.3 blocks platelet activation mediated via FcγRIIa. In typical HIT, that blockade almost completely prevents serotonin release. Partial inhibition can create an “indeterminate” functional result and lead to false reassurance. Possible (non‑exhaustive) reasons for partial IV.3 inhibition include:
- Extremely strong, high‑avidity immune complexes that overwhelm blockade.
- Epitope/antibody characteristics (different IgG subclass or exceptionally multivalent complexes) that produce unusually robust activation.
- Technical factors: platelet donor variability, IV.3 lot or concentration, or lab-specific assay conditions.
This case demonstrates that partial IV.3 inhibition does not exclude clinically meaningful aHIT.

How to interpret the lab in the clinical context
Always integrate the whole picture. In this patient:
- Preoperative heparin exposure (transradial cardiac cath) explained the unusually early platelet fall (before POD5).
- Strong ELISA (OD 2.2) + progressive platelet fall to a nadir of 13×10^9/L + stroke = high pretest probability for HIT/aHIT.
- SRA: 96% at 0 U, 97%–99% at 0.1–0.3 U, 0% at 100 U — classic aHIT heparin‑independence profile.
- IV.3 partially inhibited but did not reduce release below the usual negative threshold — an atypical but real serological phenotype of severe aHIT.

What to ask the lab to do (practical requests when SRA is “indeterminate”)
- Request the full SRA data, not just a summary or “indeterminate” label — look for release at 0 U, 0.1/0.3 U and 100 U heparin.
- Ask whether IV.3 was used (concentration), and whether IV.3 suppression brought release below the lab’s negative cutoff. If not, ask for repeated testing with:
  - Multiple platelet donors (donor variability matters).
  - PF4‑enhanced functional assays (PF4‑SRA or PF4‑dependent P‑selectin expression assay) if available.
  - Repeat testing on later samples — antibody profiles can evolve (this case’s SRA profiles changed over months).
- Discuss assay limitations and the clinical implications with the reference lab; a collaborative readout helps avoid false reassurance.

Clinical actions when you suspect aHIT despite an “indeterminate” SRA
- Treat the patient, not the report. If clinical probability is high (falling platelets temporally related to heparin exposure, strong ELISA, thrombosis or consumptive coagulopathy), stop all heparin and start a non‑heparin anticoagulant immediately.
- Consider IVIG for suspected aHIT (this patient received high‑dose IVIG). Expect that most patients improve quickly, but be prepared for slower responses in very severe disease.
- Monitor: platelet trend, fibrinogen, d‑dimer, peripheral smear (schistocytes) — falling fibrinogen, rising d‑dimer and schistocytes suggest ongoing consumptive coagulopathy and incomplete control of hypercoagulability.
- Reassess anticoagulation if markers of hypercoagulability worsen despite therapy — in this case the team switched from argatroban to fondaparinux after evidence of ongoing coagulopathy.
- Plan for prolonged follow-up: functional antibodies can persist for weeks–months; timing of safe switch back to vitamin K antagonists or DOACs should be individualized.

Lessons from this case (clinical pearls)
- aHIT can present early after cardiac surgery if there was recent preoperative heparin exposure (eg catheterization), so don’t be reassured by an “early” timing alone.
- A strongly positive PF4 ELISA (especially very high OD) plus thrombosis should trump a single indeterminate functional assay when making urgent management decisions.
- The SRA pattern to look for in aHIT: strong serotonin release at 0 U/mL and at low heparin concentrations, with inhibition at very high heparin. That pattern is diagnostic even if IV.3 blockade is incomplete.
- Partial resistance to FcγRIIa blockade (IV.3) can occur with very pathogenic aHIT antibodies — interpret such results in the full clinical and serological context.
- Communicate early and directly with the reference lab and request expanded testing (0‑heparin SRA, PF4‑enhanced assays, multiple donors, repeat samples) when results are unusual.

Take‑home messages
- Strong PF4 ELISA + thrombosis + robust SRA activation at 0 U/mL = autoimmune HIT — treat.
- Don’t let the word “indeterminate” on an SRA report override the clinical context; ask for the actual release numbers at 0, low and high heparin and for IV.3 inhibition data.
- If the SRA shows heparin‑independent activation but IV.3 only partially inhibits, consider this an atypically severe aHIT phenotype. Manage aggressively (non‑heparin anticoagulation, consider IVIG) and work with the lab to repeat/expand testing.
- Repeat assays over time and use multiple functional approaches if available; serologic profiles can evolve as antibodies wane.

This case is a good reminder: labs are powerful tools, but they don’t replace clinical judgment. When the SRA lights up at zero heparin, act like it’s real — even when the IV.3 lamp only dims the fire.
