Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

### [Morning Report] How should clinicians interpret a strong-positive PF4/polyanion ELISA with an 'indeterminate' SRA in a high-suspicion setting, and what follow-up testing or interpretive nuances (for example, heparin-independent activation, FcγRIIa blockade) can help confirm autoimmune HIT?

Direct answer
- A very strong-positive PF4/polyanion ELISA (OD 2.2) in a patient with a high pretest probability of HIT should be taken seriously even if the initial SRA is reported as “indeterminate.” In that situation the combination of (1) strong ELISA, (2) clinical picture compatible with HIT (timing, thrombocytopenia, new thrombosis), and (3) SRA features showing robust platelet activation at low/zero heparin that is suppressed at very high heparin is most consistent with autoimmune HIT (aHIT). Treatment for HIT/aHIT should not be deferred pending clarification of an indeterminate SRA; further laboratory workup and repeat/modified functional testing should be done to confirm the diagnosis.

Why the SRA can be “indeterminate” and how to interpret it (clinical reasoning)
- The standard SRA looks for heparin‑dependent, platelet‑activating IgG that causes serotonin release from donor platelets at therapeutic heparin concentrations and is inhibited at very high heparin. In classical HIT you see low release at 0 U/mL, high release at 0.1–0.3 U/mL, and inhibition at 100 U/mL.
- Autoimmune HIT differs because the antibodies can activate platelets in a heparin‑independent manner (strong release at 0 U/mL) or be enhanced by added PF4. That pattern (strong 0 U/mL release with preservation of the “inhibited at 100 U/mL” feature) is diagnostic of aHIT rather than a laboratory artifact.
- The SRA may be labeled “indeterminate” when a functional modifier (for example, blockade with the FcγRIIa monoclonal IV.3) does not fully suppress platelet activation as expected. That can reflect unusually potent antibodies (high titer, high affinity) or atypical mechanisms of Fc receptor engagement — not absence of disease. In the case presented the patient’s serum: 96% release at 0 U/mL, 97–99% at 0.1–0.3 U/mL, 0% at 100 U/mL, and only partial inhibition by IV.3 (76% residual release) — a serological phenotype best explained by aHIT with unusually strong platelet activation and partial resistance to FcγRIIa blockade. That is not a reason to dismiss the diagnosis.

Practical follow‑up testing and interpretive nuances
- Request the laboratory to provide the full SRA profile (0, 0.1, 0.3 and 100 U/mL heparin) rather than a binary positive/negative call. A pattern of strong 0 U/mL release with inhibition at 100 U/mL supports aHIT.
- Ask for repeat/modified functional testing:
  - Heparin‑independent SRA (explicitly report 0 U/mL result) to document heparin‑independent activation.
  - PF4‑enhanced functional assays (PF4‑supplemented SRA or PF4‑dependent platelet activation assays) — these can reveal antibodies that require PF4 but not heparin.
  - Tests of FcγRIIa dependence (IV.3 blockade) should be reported quantitatively. Partial inhibition does not exclude pathogenicity; it can indicate unusually strong antibodies or alternative cross‑linking.
  - Alternative functional assays such as washed platelet aggregation or flow cytometric activation assays (P‑selectin expression) can corroborate SRA findings if available.
- Repeat testing on serial sera if initial results are ambiguous: aHIT antibody profiles can evolve (as in this case, later sera showed changing SRA patterns).
- Communicate with an experienced platelet immunology reference laboratory — interpretation hinges on pattern recognition, not just a single binary test.

Clinical management implications illustrated by the case
- In a high‑suspicion patient (high OD ELISA, falling platelets, new thrombosis), the appropriate clinical action is to stop heparin and start a non‑heparin anticoagulant (the case used argatroban, later fondaparinux), and to consider adjunctive therapy for aHIT such as high‑dose IVIG.
- Do not await a perfectly “positive” SRA to treat when the ELISA is strong and the clinical picture fits. In the reported patient, early recognition of aHIT‑compatible SRA features (heparin‑independent activation and typical inhibition at 100 U/mL) plus the very high ELISA OD justified treatment as aHIT despite the “indeterminate” label.
- Use serial clinical and laboratory monitoring (platelet count trajectory, fibrinogen, d‑dimer) and repeat functional assays to confirm diagnosis and gauge response; conversion of SRA profiles over time (from heparin‑independent to heparin‑dependent) as antibodies wane is a supportive finding, as seen in the case.

Take‑home points
- A very strong PF4/polyanion ELISA in a high‑probability clinical scenario should trump an isolated “indeterminate” SRA; interpret the SRA pattern (0 U/mL, low‑dose, high‑dose heparin, IV.3 inhibition) rather than relying on a single categorical result.
- Heparin‑independent platelet activation and inhibition at very high heparin are hallmark serologic features of aHIT.
- Partial resistance to FcγRIIa blockade (IV.3) may be seen with very potent aHIT antibodies and does not rule out pathogenic HIT — it may be a novel serologic phenotype of aHIT, as in this patient.
- When in doubt, treat as HIT/aHIT and obtain expert laboratory consultation and repeat/modified functional testing to confirm.
