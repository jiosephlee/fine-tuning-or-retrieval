### [Pathophysiology] How does autoimmune HIT (aHIT) differ mechanistically from classic, heparin-dependent HIT in terms of platelet activation requirements?

Classic (heparin‑dependent) HIT and autoimmune HIT (aHIT) share a final common pathway — IgG immune complexes that cross‑link platelet FcγRIIa and trigger platelet activation — but they differ fundamentally in what is required to form those pathogenic immune complexes.

In classic HIT the antibodies recognise complexes of platelet factor 4 (PF4) bound to heparin (or other polyanions). Formation of the PF4–heparin antigen is required for high‑affinity antibody binding and for immune‑complex formation; therefore platelet activation in functional assays (and clinically) is seen only at low therapeutic concentrations of heparin that favour PF4–heparin complex formation and is abolished at very high heparin concentrations that disrupt those complexes. In short: heparin is necessary to create the antigenic PF4–polyanion complex that permits Fc receptor clustering.

In aHIT, by contrast, the pathogenic antibodies can bind to PF4 (or PF4 complexes formed with endogenous polyanions) in the absence of exogenous heparin. These antibodies either recognise PF4 epitopes that are exposed without heparin, have higher affinity/avidity, or stabilise PF4 multimers, allowing the antibodies themselves to form large immune complexes that cross‑link FcγRIIa and activate platelets without added heparin. Laboratory correlates are strong platelet activation at 0 U/mL heparin in the serotonin‑release assay and often persistent activation despite manipulations that would block classic HIT. Rarely, as in this case, the antibodies are so reactive that platelet activation is only partially inhibited by FcγRIIa blockade (reflecting very high antibody avidity, different epitope engagement, or formation of unusually stable immune complexes).

The clinical consequences follow directly from the mechanism: aHIT tends to present earlier (when prior heparin exposure has primed PF4 antibodies), is often more severe and refractory, and requires prompt nonheparin anticoagulation and immune‑modulating therapies (IVIG, plasmapheresis) because stopping heparin alone does not eliminate heparin‑independent platelet activation.
