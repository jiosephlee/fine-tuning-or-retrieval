Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

### [Differential Diagnosis] In the early postoperative period after cardiac surgery, what are three non-HIT causes of severe thrombocytopenia you should consider, and what key clinical or laboratory findings in this case argue against each?

Direct answer: three important non‑HIT causes to consider early after cardiac surgery are (1) cardiopulmonary‑bypass (CPB)–related platelet loss/hemodilution, (2) sepsis with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), and (3) drug‑ or transfusion‑associated immune thrombocytopenia (including post‑transfusion purpura or classic drug‑induced immune thrombocytopenia). Below I state briefly why each is considered and the key features in this case that argue against it.

1) CPB‑related platelet consumption/hemodilution
- Why consider: CPB causes immediate platelet activation, consumption and hemodilution with a predictable early postoperative decline (often greatest on POD0–1).
- Why this case argues against CPB alone: the expected pattern is an early nadir with recovery over the first week. This patient had only a modest fall to 155×10^9/L on POD1 (consistent with CPB) but then a progressive, large fall to 50×10^9/L by POD5 and a nadir of 13×10^9/L — far deeper and later than typical CPB effect. In addition the development of an arterial thrombotic stroke and strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA with platelet‑activating antibodies point to an immune process (HIT/aHIT) rather than isolated mechanical/consumptive loss from bypass.

2) Sepsis‑associated thrombocytopenia / DIC
- Why consider: postoperative infections or sepsis can cause rapid platelet consumption and DIC, particularly in the critically ill.
- Why this case argues against it: the patient was haemodynamically stable, afebrile, with normal white count (7.8×10^9/L) and negative blood and urine cultures. Initial coagulation tests were not consistent with DIC: normal PT/INR (1.1), normal PTT (34 s) and an elevated fibrinogen (6.59 g/L) — fibrinogen is typically low in consumptive DIC. Early peripheral smear showed no schistocytes. These findings make sepsis/DIC unlikely as the primary cause of the progressive thrombocytopenia.

3) Drug‑ or transfusion‑related immune thrombocytopenia (including post‑transfusion purpura)
- Why consider: many perioperative drugs (and transfused platelet/packed RBC products) can trigger immune thrombocytopenia, which can be severe.
- Why this case argues against it: there were no blood transfusions (so post‑transfusion purpura is excluded). The timing and drug history are inconsistent with classic drug‑induced immune thrombocytopenia: the platelet fall began too early to be explained by newly started postoperative medications (and agents given only on the day of surgery or commonly used postoperatively were either not new exposures or were unlikely culprits). Furthermore, DITP usually shows a brisk platelet recovery after withdrawal of the offending agent, whereas this patient had prolonged severe thrombocytopenia with a serologic picture (strong PF4 ELISA and platelet‑activating antibodies on SRA, including heparin‑independent activity) diagnostic of HIT/aHIT.

Takeaway: in a post‑cardiac surgery patient an early modest platelet drop from CPB is common, but a progressive fall after POD1 with markedly low nadir, new thrombosis, lack of infection/DIC features, absence of transfusion exposure and strongly positive PF4 antibody testing should shift the differential toward HIT—here, autoimmune HIT—rather than the three non‑HIT causes above.
