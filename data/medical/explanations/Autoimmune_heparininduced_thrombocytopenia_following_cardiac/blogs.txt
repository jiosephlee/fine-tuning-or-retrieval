# When HIT Hits Early: Pre‑op Cath Heparin and the POD1–4 Platelet Crash

What makes this case jump off the page is the timing. A man in his 70s has uncomplicated cardiac surgery with routine cardiopulmonary bypass heparin, then an unexpected, progressive platelet fall beginning immediately post‑op and reaching severe thrombocytopenia by POD5 — accompanied by an ischemic stroke. The usual mental model for post‑cardiac surgery platelet drops is “it’s the bypass” or consumptive effects of surgery; we’re trained to expect heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) in the POD5–10 window. This patient’s early fall was explained only after someone asked the right question: did he get heparin before surgery? Yes — low‑dose UFH during a diagnostic coronary angiogram one week earlier. That little pre‑op heparin exposure primed a heparin‑dependent immune response that presented early and behaved like autoimmune HIT (aHIT) — a heparin‑independent, highly activating antibody phenotype that can be severe and refractory.

Why this matters
- Early post‑operative platelet declines are common after CPB, so instinct is to attribute them to dilution, platelet activation during bypass, or routine post‑op care. That heuristic usually works — but it will miss HIT when prior heparin exposure has already primed antibody production.
- Missing HIT (or aHIT) can be catastrophic: this patient developed a parietal infarct and had profound thrombocytopenia (nadir 13×10^9/L), consumptive coagulopathy, and lab evidence of persistent platelet activation despite treatment.
- aHIT behaves differently in vitro and clinically: platelet activation can occur without added heparin, and antibodies may be unusually resistant to Fc receptor blockade. This produces atypical platelet activation assay results that can be misread as “indeterminate.”

Clinical pattern to watch for
- Any sustained or progressive platelet fall beginning immediately after surgery (POD0–4) in a patient who had heparin exposures within the prior 10–14 days should raise HIT suspicion. Don’t rely only on the classic POD5–10 window.
- Look for an association with thrombosis — arterial or venous — even when the platelet count is very low.
- If post‑op LMWH or heparin prophylaxis is started, it can amplify the antibody response and accelerate the platelet drop.

Key historical questions that change management
Before you dismiss early thrombocytopenia as “post‑bypass,” ask:
- Did the patient have any heparin in the prior 2 weeks — cath, interventional radiology, dialysis, vascular access, or heparin flushes?
- Was there low‑dose UFH or LMWH between the pre‑op heparin and the operation (eg, post‑op prophylaxis)?
- Any recent transfusions (to consider post‑transfusion purpura), new drugs, or signs of sepsis or DIC?

Adapting the 4Ts in cardiac surgery
The 4Ts (Thrombocytopenia, Timing, Thrombosis, oTher causes) remain useful but need adjustment post‑cardiac surgery:
- Thrombocytopenia: focus on the pattern — progressive fall after the immediate expected decline from CPB. Expect early mild drop from CPB; HIT is a continuing, progressive fall rather than a steady recovery.
- Timing: extend “possible” timing to include platelet falls that begin before POD5 if there was pre‑operative heparin within the prior 5–14 days. In other words, a patient who got cath heparin one week before surgery can present with HIT on POD1–4.
- Thrombosis: new or unexplained thrombotic events (stroke, limb ischemia, catheter thrombosis) during the platelet fall strongly favor HIT.
- oTher causes: be thoughtful — cardiac surgery confounders (CPB-related fall, transfusion, sepsis) are common but don’t exclude HIT. Ask the pre‑op heparin question.

Lab testing pitfalls and interpretation
- PF4 ELISA: sensitive — a strongly positive OD (eg, >2.0) is concerning. In the case, OD was 2.2.
- Functional assays (SRA): a classic HIT pattern shows heparin‑dependent platelet activation (low at 0 U/mL, high at therapeutic heparin concentrations, low at very high heparin). aHIT may show strong activation even without added heparin. Some aHIT antibodies may be partially resistant to FcγRIIa blockade (IV.3), yielding “indeterminate” SRA unless you interpret the pattern in context.
- Don’t be falsely reassured by a non‑classic SRA: if the ELISA is strongly positive and the clinical picture fits, consult the lab and hematology, and treat as HIT until proven otherwise.

Practical management pearls from this case
- Stop all heparins (UFH and LMWH) immediately if HIT is suspected, even for early postoperative presentations.
- Start non‑heparin anticoagulation promptly unless bleeding risk is prohibitive. Argatroban (short half‑life, IV) is a reasonable initial choice in recent stroke or high bleeding risk; fondaparinux is an effective off‑label option for many and was used successfully here once the team felt the bleeding risk was manageable.
- Consider high‑dose IVIG for autoimmune HIT when there is severe thrombocytopenia or ongoing platelet activation; some aHIT patients respond briskly, but severe cases may respond poorly or slowly — as happened here.
- Monitor indirect markers of ongoing hypercoagulability (fibrinogen, d‑dimer, peripheral smear for schistocytes) when clinical and platelet responses are slow to recover.
- Reassess anticoagulant choice if consumptive signs or rising d‑dimers persist; in this case argatroban was switched to fondaparinux with supplemental antithrombin due to concern for persistent hypercoagulability.

Bottom line takeaways
- Early post‑op platelet falls (POD0–4) after cardiac surgery can be HIT if there was recent pre‑operative heparin exposure; a small cath lab UFH dose is enough to prime the immune response.
- aHIT can present earlier, be more severe, and show heparin‑independent platelet activation; functional assays may be atypical — interpret them in context.
- Always ask about recent heparin exposures during the pre‑op and peri‑procedural period; a single targeted history question can change the diagnosis and treatment.
- If clinical suspicion is moderate–high: stop heparin, start a non‑heparin anticoagulant, involve hematology, and pursue PF4 ELISA plus confirmatory functional testing while treating.

Key clinical pearls
- Pre‑op cath heparin can “prime” HIT that declares itself immediately after surgery — always ask about any heparin in the prior 2 weeks.
- Progressive platelet falls (not the initial CPB dip) and any new thrombosis should trigger HIT workup, even before POD5.
- Strongly positive PF4 ELISA + atypical SRA (eg, activation at 0 U/mL heparin or partial resistance to Fc blockade) = suspicious for autoimmune HIT; treat as HIT until proven otherwise.
- Use short‑acting IV non‑heparin anticoagulants when bleeding risk is present; consider IVIG for aHIT but expect variable responses.

If you take one action after reading this: add “Recent heparin exposure?” to your immediate checklist for any post‑cardiac surgery platelet fall. That single question often separates routine post‑bypass thrombocytopenia from something you can’t afford to miss.



# Autoimmune HIT 101: Heparin‑Independent Platelet Activation After Cardiac Surgery

This case will grab your attention because it looks like every cardiothoracic postop thrombocytopenia at first glance — then behaves very differently. An elderly man after cardiopulmonary bypass developed a brisk, early platelet fall with an arterial stroke, incredibly low nadir platelets (13×10^9/L), and a serotonin‑release assay (SRA) that activated platelets even without added heparin. The final diagnosis was autoimmune HIT (aHIT) — a dramatic, heparin‑independent subtype of HIT with important diagnostic and therapeutic implications.

## Why this is surprising and important
Most of us think of HIT as a 5–10 day, heparin‑dependent, platelet‑activating antibody syndrome that causes thrombosis while platelets are only modestly low. Autoimmune HIT breaks these rules:

- onset can be earlier than POD5 if there was recent heparin exposure (this patient had UFH at preop cath)
- antibodies can activate platelets in the absence of heparin (SRA positive at 0 U/mL)
- thrombosis can occur despite severe thrombocytopenia
- antibodies can be unusually potent and partially resistant to FcγRIIa blockade (the IV.3 monoclonal antibody), giving an “indeterminate” SRA if you don’t dig deeper

Missing aHIT costs patients — it’s highly prothrombotic and can cause consumptive coagulopathy, stroke, limb ischemia and death. Conversely, early recognition lets you stop all heparins and switch to non‑heparin anticoagulation and immune modulation.

## Key clues from the case
This report is useful because it documents several real‑world flags:

- Early progressive platelet fall from POD1→POD5 (210→50×10^9/L), then to 13×10^9/L — earlier than classic HIT window. Look for recent heparin exposures (even small cath doses).
- New arterial thrombosis (left parietal stroke) concurrent with falling platelets — remember HIT causes thrombosis despite low platelets.
- Very strong PF4/polyanion ELISA (OD 2.2) — a strong immunoassay increases pretest probability.
- SRA pattern: strong serotonin release at 0, 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL heparin with inhibition at very high heparin (100 U/mL) — hallmark of aHIT (heparin‑independent activation).
- Partial inhibition with IV.3 (platelet FcγRIIa blocker) — indicates unusually potent antibodies; explains an “indeterminate” SRA if the lab reports only binary results.

Laboratory context: in classic HIT you expect low/no SRA activation at 0 U/mL heparin and strong activation at low therapeutic heparin concentrations. Activation at 0 U/mL points to aHIT. Lack of complete IV.3 inhibition suggests an antibody population that may engage platelets by mechanisms that are harder to interrupt.

## Management pearls illustrated by the case
This patient’s treatment follows logical principles for severe aHIT and exposes pragmatic choices you’ll face in real life.

- Stop all heparin immediately (including LMWH, heparin flushes).
- Start a non‑heparin parenteral anticoagulant: the team used argatroban initially because of the recent intracranial infarct and the ability to quickly stop / adjust infusion. Despite argatroban, lab evidence suggested ongoing hypercoagulability.
- Use high‑dose IV immunoglobulin (IVIG) for immune‑mediated platelet activation. Many patients with aHIT respond briskly; this patient’s response was muted, consistent with severe antibody activity.
- Consider fondaparinux as a practical outpatient bridge or when other agents fail; note fondaparinux is AT‑dependent — this group gave antithrombin concentrate when AT was low before starting fondaparinux.
- Monitor more than the platelet count: fibrinogen, D‑dimer and peripheral blood film (schistocytes) provided complementary evidence of ongoing consumptive coagulopathy and helped guide the switch from argatroban to fondaparinux in this case.
- Long term: when platelets recovered and antibodies waned, patient was transitioned back to oral apixaban.

Practical trade‑offs: argatroban — short half‑life and titratable (good for intracranial events) but IV and resource‑intensive. Fondaparinux — once daily, useful off‑label option, but check AT level and renal function.

## Diagnostic takeaways — don’t be fooled
- An “indeterminate” SRA does not exclude aHIT. If ELISA is strongly positive and clinical suspicion is high (early postop fall, recent heparin, thrombosis), treat as HIT while pursuing specialty testing and interpretation.
- SRA activation at 0 U/mL heparin is the key serologic hint of aHIT. Look for:
  - strong release at 0 U/mL
  - strong release at low heparin concentrations (0.1–0.3 U/mL)
  - inhibition at very high heparin (100 U/mL) — still consistent with PF4 interactions
  - incomplete inhibition with FcγRIIa blockers — rare, but indicates particularly aggressive antibodies
- Always review exposure history for any heparin in the preceding 100 days — small cath doses can prime early HIT.

## How aggressive is autoimmune HIT?
aHIT tends to be more prothrombotic than classic HIT and can cause severe thrombocytopenia and consumptive coagulopathy. This patient showed falling fibrinogen, rising D‑dimer and schistocytes despite anticoagulation — signs of ongoing thrombin generation and microangiopathic injury.

IVIG often helps by blocking Fc receptors and neutralizing pathogenic antibodies, but some aHIT cases (like this one) respond only modestly and require prolonged non‑heparin anticoagulation until antibodies wane.

## Clinical pearls
- Recent low‑dose heparin (eg, cath heparin) can shift HIT onset earlier than POD5; always ask about periprocedural heparin.
- A strong positive PF4 ELISA with thrombosis and falling platelets → treat immediately for HIT even if initial SRA is “indeterminate.”
- SRA positive at 0 U/mL heparin = aHIT (heparin‑independent platelet activation).
- In aHIT, consider early IVIG plus therapeutic non‑heparin anticoagulation; tailor agent choice to bleeding risk/need for rapid reversibility.
- Monitor fibrinogen, D‑dimer and peripheral film — they help detect ongoing consumptive coagulopathy when platelets are unreliable or recovering slowly.

## Take‑home messages
- Autoimmune HIT can present early after cardiac surgery if there was prior heparin exposure and may activate platelets without added heparin.
- Strong PF4 ELISA + thrombotic event + platelet fall = treat for HIT now; don’t wait for a “perfect” SRA.
- SRA activation at 0 U/mL heparin is diagnostic of aHIT; partial resistance to FcγRIIa blockade indicates particularly aggressive antibodies and can make assays look “indeterminate.”
- Stop all heparins, start non‑heparin anticoagulation, add IVIG for immune blockade, and monitor fibrinogen/D‑dimer and blood film to guide therapy and detect ongoing coagulopathy.
- Expect a potentially prolonged course; antibody activity and platelet recovery can take weeks to months, and transitions back to oral anticoagulants should follow platelet recovery and clinical stability.

This case is a great reminder: HIT isn’t always classic. When the clinical picture fits — early post‑op thrombocytopenia, recent heparin exposure, thrombosis and a high‑titer ELISA — assume HIT (or aHIT) and act fast.



# The SRA Isn’t Binary: Making Sense of Atypical Results and FcγR Blockade Resistance

This patient taught a blunt lesson: an “indeterminate” serotonin‑release assay (SRA) result can still be heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia — specifically autoimmune HIT (aHIT) — and waiting for a neat positive/negative label can cost a patient thrombotic complications. He was an elderly post‑cardiac surgery patient who developed a profound platelet fall starting before POD5 (because he had preoperative UFH), had a large parietal stroke, a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA (OD 2.2), and an SRA that looked weird: very high release at low heparin (0.1–0.3 U/mL), high release even with 0 U/mL heparin, complete suppression at very high heparin (100 U/mL), and only partial inhibition when IV.3 (an FcγRIIa‑blocking monoclonal antibody) was added. The lab called it “indeterminate.” The clinicians treated aggressively for aHIT — non‑heparin anticoagulation, IVIG, then fondaparinux — and the patient recovered.

Why this is surprising and important
- SRA is often taught as binary: positive = heparin‑dependent platelet activation; negative = no platelet‑activating antibodies. But atypical serologies exist. aHIT antibodies can activate platelets without added heparin (0 U/mL) and may show unusual responses to FcγR blockade.
- Partial resistance to IV.3 is rare but meaningful — it probably reflects unusually potent/abundant immune complexes that are difficult to fully block in vitro. That does not rule out FcγRIIa engagement in vivo, nor does it make the diagnosis less urgent.
- Laboratory nuance matters for bedside decisions: a high‑OD ELISA + clinical thrombosis + an SRA with heparin‑independent activation = treat as aHIT even if IV.3 doesn’t fully suppress release.

Quick SRA primer (practical, not textbook)
- Classic HIT SRA pattern:
  - Low heparin (0.1–0.3 U/mL): strong serotonin release.
  - High heparin (100 U/mL): suppression of release.
  - No release at 0 U/mL.
- aHIT pattern:
  - Significant release at 0 U/mL (heparin‑independent platelet activation).
  - Often still shows the high‑heparin suppression feature.
- IV.3 (or FcγR blockade) is used to show FcγRIIa‑dependent activation: in typical HIT, IV.3 abolishes release. Partial inhibition is atypical and should prompt closer clinical correlation and additional testing, not complacency.

How to interpret an “indeterminate” SRA like this one
- Don’t get hung up on the label. Integrate:
  - Clinical pretest probability (4Ts, but remember early onset if recent heparin exposure).
  - PF4/polyanion ELISA OD magnitude (very high ODs >> more likely clinically relevant).
  - Raw SRA data: look at percent release at 0, 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL, and at 100 U/mL; check the IV.3 column.
  - Presence of thrombosis or consumptive coagulopathy (falling fibrinogen, rising D‑dimer, schistocytes) — these tip the scale toward action.
- If the SRA shows:
  - High release at 0 U/mL → strongly suspicious for aHIT.
  - High release at 0.1–0.3 U/mL that is only partially inhibited by IV.3 → still consistent with FcγR‑mediated platelet activation; could reflect higher antibody avidity or immune‑complex characteristics that are harder to block in vitro.

When to request more lab work (and what to ask for)
- Ask for the raw SRA numbers and the IV.3 inhibition data (not just “indeterminate”).
- Request PF4‑enhanced functional assays when available:
  - PF4‑SRA or PF4‑enhanced SRA can increase sensitivity for aHIT antibodies.
  - PF4‑dependent P‑selectin expression assay (PEA) or flow cytometry platelet activation assays can help corroborate platelet activation and are often more sensitive for aHIT.
- Serial testing helps — aHIT antibody profiles can evolve: heparin‑independent activity may wane over weeks.
- If ELISA is very high OD, that itself is strong evidence; consider repeating ELISA and functional assays over time.

Bedside decision making: what to do now, not later
- If clinical scenario fits HIT/aHIT (falling platelets, recent heparin exposure, strong ELISA, thrombosis or consumptive coagulopathy), treat empirically — do not wait for a perfect SRA.
- Immediate steps:
  - Stop all heparin (including LMWH, flushes).
  - Start a non‑heparin anticoagulant: argatroban, bivalirudin, fondaparinux (off‑label), or a DOAC later. Choice depends on bleeding risk, renal/hepatic function, and need for rapid reversal/monitoring.
  - Consider high‑dose IVIG for confirmed or strongly suspected aHIT with severe thrombocytopenia or ongoing thrombosis/consumptive disease; IVIG can block FcγR interactions in vivo.
- Monitor response: platelet count recovery, fibrinogen, D‑dimer, schistocytes, and clinical thrombosis progression. Lack of response should prompt reassessment of anticoagulant adequacy and consideration of alternative strategies (e.g., change anticoagulant, adjunctive therapies).

Practical lab‑clinician communication tips
- Ask for raw SRA percent releases at 0, 0.1, 0.3, and 100 U/mL, and the IV.3 inhibition percentage.
- Ask whether the lab can run PF4‑enhanced assays or PEAs; not all centers can, but reference labs may.
- Tell the lab the clinical context (recent heparin, timing of platelet fall, thrombosis) — that helps interpretation.
- Don’t equate “indeterminate” with “not HIT.” Request clarification and further testing if the clinical picture is strong.

Clinical pearls
- Early postoperative platelet falls (before POD5) can be HIT if there was recent preoperative heparin exposure (e.g., cath lab heparin).
- A very high PF4/polyanion ELISA OD (≥2.0 in many labs) is strongly predictive of pathogenic antibodies — treat the patient, not just the number.
- Heparin‑independent activation (release at 0 U/mL) is a hallmark of autoimmune HIT and usually means more aggressive disease.
- Partial inhibition by IV.3 does not rule out FcγRIIa‑mediated platelet activation — it may reflect unusually potent antibodies; treat as aHIT if the clinical and serologic context fits.
- Monitor fibrinogen, D‑dimer and peripheral smear for indirect evidence of ongoing consumptive coagulopathy when assessing anticoagulant efficacy.

Take‑home messages
- The SRA is not strictly binary. Read the raw data and integrate with the clinical picture.
- High ELISA OD + heparin‑independent SRA activity = aHIT until proven otherwise.
- Partial IV.3 inhibition is a red flag, not reassurance. It can reflect especially pathogenic antibodies.
- If aHIT is suspected clinically, stop heparin and start appropriate non‑heparin anticoagulation and consider IVIG — do not wait for a perfect laboratory confirmation.
- Communicate directly with the platelet immunology lab and request PF4‑enhanced or P‑selectin assays if available; serial testing clarifies evolving serology.

This case is a reminder: lab nuance matters, but it’s the patient’s clotting and bleeding, not the assay label, that should drive care.



# Argatroban vs Fondaparinux in HIT: Choosing, Switching, and Why Antithrombin May Matter

What makes this case interesting? An elderly post‑cardiac surgery patient developed autoimmune HIT (aHIT) with an early postoperative onset (triggered by preop catheterization heparin), profound thrombocytopenia (nadir 13 × 10^9/L), and an ischemic stroke. Clinically he was challenging: ongoing hypercoagulability (falling fibrinogen, rising D‑dimer, new schistocytes) despite initial anticoagulation, an “indeterminate” SRA because the patient’s aHIT antibodies activated platelets even without heparin and were partially resistant to Fc‑receptor blockade. The treating team used argatroban first (short half‑life desirable after stroke), gave high‑dose IVIG, then switched to fondaparinux with supplemental antithrombin (AT) when hypercoagulability persisted — and the patient ultimately recovered.

What follows is a practical FOAM‑style primer on when to pick argatroban vs fondaparinux in HIT/aHIT, when to consider switching, monitoring signals that anticoagulation may be insufficient, and why checking/fixing AT can matter when using fondaparinux.

## Picking an initial agent: why argatroban made sense here
Argatroban is a direct thrombin inhibitor with several features that favor its use in acutely unstable or bleeding‑risk patients:
- Very short half‑life (helps if you need rapid reversal or neurosurgical intervention) — appealing after an ischemic stroke where hemorrhagic conversion is a concern.
- IV infusion with PTT titration (treating team targeted PTT 55–85 s in this case).
- Hepatic clearance (so adjust/avoid in severe liver failure; preferred over bivalirudin if renal failure is present).
- No dependence on antithrombin levels.

Downsides: continuous IV infusion, need for frequent PTT monitoring, dose adjustments for hepatic impairment, and interference with INR making transition to warfarin tricky.

In this patient, recent stroke + profound thrombocytopenia = reasonable rationale to start argatroban for rapid control and easier holding if bleeding developed.

## Why fondaparinux was used later (and why supplemental AT)
Fondaparinux is a synthetic pentasaccharide that indirectly inhibits factor Xa via antithrombin:
- Once‑daily subcutaneous dosing (convenience, outpatient transition).
- Good efficacy in many HIT series as an “off‑label” alternative.
- No routine lab monitoring required in most patients; anti‑Xa (fondaparinux‑calibrated) can be used if needed.
- Renal clearance — contraindicated or used cautiously with CrCl <30 mL/min.
- Crucially, its action requires adequate antithrombin levels.

In this case the team switched to fondaparinux because: persistent hypercoagulability despite argatroban, logistical advantages (no infusion), and planned longer course. They measured AT (0.70 U/mL, mildly low) and gave 1000 U AT concentrate before starting therapeutic fondaparinux 10 mg daily — a pragmatic step to ensure fondaparinux could work effectively when AT was suboptimal.

Clinical rationale for AT replacement:
- If AT is low, fondaparinux (and heparins) have reduced efficacy because they require AT to inhibit factor Xa.
- Severe consumptive coagulopathy (falling fibrinogen, rising D‑dimer, schistocytes) can be associated with low AT.
- Small supplemental AT doses can restore activity and optimize fondaparinux function, especially when switching due to inadequate anticoagulant response.

## When to suspect your anticoagulant isn’t controlling HIT/aHIT
Bedside/lab signals that should prompt re‑evaluation and consideration of a switch or adjunctive therapies:
- New or progressive thrombosis while therapeutic anticoagulation is in place.
- Worsening consumptive coagulopathy: falling fibrinogen, rising D‑dimer.
- New schistocytes or hemolysis markers suggesting microangiopathic process.
- Persistent or worsening thrombocytopenia despite appropriate alternative anticoagulation and adjunctive IVIG in aHIT.
- Clinical deterioration (organ ischemia, stroke) despite therapy.

In the reported patient, falling fibrinogen, rising D‑dimer and appearance of schistocytes while on argatroban signaled ongoing uncontrolled hypercoagulability and prompted the switch.

## Monitoring strategies and practical tips
- Platelet count daily until recovery, then less frequently as improving.
- Fibrinogen and D‑dimer trends can be helpful surrogates of disease activity and anticoagulant effect in severe HIT/aHIT.
- Peripheral smear to look for schistocytes when consumptive coagulopathy is suspected.
- Argatroban: PTT monitoring with institutional targets; remember argatroban raises INR and complicates warfarin overlap.
- Fondaparinux: no routine monitoring; if needed, anti‑Xa level calibrated to fondaparinux can be used. Check renal function before and during therapy.
- Check antithrombin if ongoing thrombosis or coagulation markers suggest consumption; consider AT concentrate if AT is low and you’re using AT‑dependent agents (fondaparinux/heparins).

## Transitioning off parenteral therapy
- Once platelet counts recover (commonly >150 × 10^9/L, though some centers use >100 × 10^9/L plus clinical stability), consider switch to oral anticoagulant for long‑term management.
- DOACs (apixaban, rivaroxaban) are increasingly used after HIT once platelet recovery is documented; in the case report, apixaban 5 mg BID was resumed on POD65 with good outcome.
- Ensure no ongoing hypercoagulability and that SRA/antibody activity is waning if possible.

## Special considerations in aHIT
Autoimmune HIT antibodies can activate platelets independent of heparin and occasionally show atypical lab behavior (eg, partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade as in this case). These patients can be more severe and may respond less predictably to standard interventions (IVIG, single anticoagulant). Expect prolonged antibody activity and a longer, more cautious course.

## Key clinical takeaways
- Argatroban is a good initial choice when you need short‑acting, titratable anticoagulation (recent stroke, bleeding risk, renal failure); monitor with PTT and watch for hepatic issues and INR effects.
- Fondaparinux is a convenient, effective off‑label option for HIT when renal function is adequate, but its efficacy depends on sufficient antithrombin levels.
- If hypercoagulability persists (rising D‑dimer, falling fibrinogen, schistocytes, new thrombosis) despite anticoagulation, reassess: consider switching agents, measuring AT, and giving AT concentrate before starting an AT‑dependent agent.
- In aHIT expect atypical serology and often more severe, prolonged disease; combine anticoagulation with supportive therapies (IVIG) and close lab surveillance.
- Transition to a DOAC (eg, apixaban) is reasonable after platelet recovery and clinical stability.

This case neatly illustrates the real‑world tradeoffs: choose argatroban for immediate control and safety after stroke, but be ready to switch to fondaparinux for practicality — and check antithrombin when the coagulation picture suggests consumption.



# IVIG for Autoimmune HIT: Expectations, Dosing, and What to Do When It Underwhelms

What makes this case worth your attention? A man in his 70s developed autoimmune heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT) after cardiac surgery with an unusually severe laboratory phenotype: very low platelet nadir (13×10^9/L), ongoing consumptive coagulopathy despite anticoagulation, and a platelet‑activating antibody that caused heparin‑independent activation and was only partially inhibited by an FcγR‑blocking monoclonal antibody in vitro. High‑dose IVIG was used but produced only a modest platelet rise — a perfect springboard to discuss realistic expectations for IVIG in aHIT, dosing, monitoring, safety, predictors of response, and contingency plans when it doesn’t produce the rapid turnaround we hope for.

## Why IVIG in aHIT?
Autoimmune HIT is driven by strongly platelet‑activating anti‑PF4 antibodies that can activate platelets without heparin. High‑dose IVIG (IVIGHD) works largely by saturating Fcγ receptors on platelets and other immune cells and/or providing anti‑idiotypic antibodies — thereby blocking Fc‑mediated platelet activation and the downstream consumptive coagulopathy. In many aHIT cases IVIG produces a rapid and marked platelet count increase and can blunt ongoing thrombosis.

But IVIG is not a guaranteed home run. The case above shows several reasons why response can be muted: extremely high‑titer, unusually reactive antibodies, ongoing strong prothrombotic stimulus, or antibody characteristics that are partially resistant to Fc blockade. Expect variability.

## Dosing and timing — what the literature and practice support
- Typical regimens used for aHIT:
  - 1 g/kg IVIG once daily for 2 consecutive days (most common), OR
  - 0.4 g/kg/day for 5 days (alternate regimen used less commonly).
- Some clinicians will give additional 1 g/kg doses if the response is inadequate and bleeding/thrombosis risk justifies it; the published case received 100 g on two consecutive days and then a third 100 g dose because the initial response was suboptimal.
- Give IVIG early once aHIT is suspected/confirmed — while continuing appropriate non‑heparin anticoagulation — because earlier Fc blockade can limit thrombosis and consumptive coagulopathy.
- Dosing is weight‑based (use ideal body weight for obese patients per institutional policy) and check institutional protocols for maximum infusion rates.

## What to expect after IVIG
- Best‑case: platelet count begins to rise within 24–72 hours and often reaches a protective level quickly.
- Typical: substantial platelet rise over several days.
- Not all patients respond briskly; some have only modest or delayed rises (as in this case), particularly those with very high‑titer or unusually avid antibodies, persistent antigen stimulus, or advanced consumptive coagulopathy.
- While platelets recover, serologic tests (ELISA, SRA) can remain positive for weeks to months — clinical recovery, not laboratory negativity, guides management.

## Predictors of a strong vs weak IVIG response
Features associated with poorer immediate IVIG response:
- Very low platelet nadir (eg, <20×10^9/L).
- Evidence of ongoing consumptive coagulopathy: falling fibrinogen, rising D‑dimer, schistocytes.
- Antibodies with strong heparin‑independent platelet activation in vitro (aHIT).
- Serologic evidence of antibodies that are only partially inhibited by FcγR blockade (as in the case — the IV.3 monoclonal antibody gave only partial inhibition).
- Delayed administration of IVIG after progression of consumptive coagulopathy.
If you see these, plan for a more guarded expectation and earlier escalation.

## Safety and monitoring when you give IVIG
- Pre‑infusion: screen renal function, record baseline platelets, coagulation panel, and consider blood glucose in diabetics (IVIG can contain sucrose).
- Adverse effects to watch for:
  - Infusion reactions (fever, chills) — slow infusion rate and premedicate if needed.
  - Thromboembolic events — paradoxically possible with IVIG; patients with aHIT are already high risk, so weigh risks/benefits.
  - Renal dysfunction (especially with sucrose‑containing products) — monitor creatinine.
  - Volume overload — particularly in elderly or cardiac patients.
  - Hemolysis — check hemoglobin.
- Continue therapeutic, non‑heparin anticoagulation during IVIG unless bleeding risk mandates otherwise.

## What to do when IVIG underwhelms — practical next steps
When platelets lag, consume coagulopathy persists, or thrombosis progresses despite IVIG plus a non‑heparin anticoagulant, consider escalation in this order (tailor to patient context and center resources):

1. Reassess the diagnosis and timing
   - Confirm aHIT serology (ELISA OD, SRA) and review recent heparin exposures (even small procedural doses).
   - Exclude other causes of thrombocytopenia or ongoing bleeding/consumption.

2. Repeat IVIG?
   - If the initial regimen was 1 g/kg ×2 and the patient has no contraindication, a repeat 1 g/kg dose can be considered — many case series include multiple doses.
   - Weigh cumulative IVIG risks (volume, renal, thrombosis).

3. Change or escalate anticoagulation
   - If hypercoagulability markers (D‑dimer, fibrinogen, schistocytes) suggest ongoing thrombosis despite argatroban, consider an alternative non‑heparin agent:
     - Switch between argatroban and bivalirudin or to fondaparinux (off‑label) if appropriate.
     - If using fondaparinux in a low antithrombin state, consider supplementing AT concentrate (as done in the case).
     - Longer‑term transition to a DOAC (eg, apixaban) is typical once platelets recover.
   - Monitor clinical/hemostatic markers (platelets, fibrinogen, D‑dimer) to judge response.

4. Therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE)
   - TPE can rapidly remove pathogenic IgG and is a reasonable escalation for refractory, life‑ or limb‑threatening aHIT or when IVIG fails.
   - Often used as a bridge to control antibody burden while other measures are implemented.
   - Consider TPE early when there is progressive thrombosis, consumptive coagulopathy, or very high antibody activity resistant to IVIG.

5. Other immunomodulation
   - Rituximab has been used in case reports for refractory HIT, but evidence is limited and onset is slower — more a consideration for prolonged antibody persistence.
   - Complement inhibitors are theoretical and investigational in this context.

6. Supportive measures
   - Platelet transfusion: generally avoid unless there is life‑threatening bleeding or the procedure absolutely requires it — transfused platelets may fuel thrombosis in HIT but are sometimes necessary.
   - Correct low antithrombin if using AT‑dependent agents (eg, fondaparinux).
   - Careful blood product decisions and multidisciplinary discussion (hematology, cardiac surgery, neurology/stroke).

## Monitoring strategy after IVIG or escalation
- Daily platelets until trending up and stable.
- Serial fibrinogen and D‑dimer to gauge consumptive activity.
- Peripheral blood film for schistocytes.
- Clinical surveillance for new thrombosis or bleeding.
- Repeat functional assays (SRA) are informative but don’t dictate immediate bedside decisions — clinical course and trend matter more.

## Quick clinical pearls
- Expect a brisk platelet rise in many aHIT cases after IVIG, but not always — severe aHIT with heparin‑independent antibodies can show only modest improvement.
- Standard IVIG dosing for aHIT is usually 1 g/kg once daily for 2 days; weight‑based dosing and infusion precautions matter.
- Continue a non‑heparin anticoagulant while giving IVIG; if hypercoagulability persists, consider switching agents (argatroban ↔ bivalirudin ↔ fondaparinux) or supplementing antithrombin when using fondaparinux in low‑AT states.
- If IVIG fails to control thrombocytopenia or consumptive coagulopathy, escalate rapidly: consider repeat IVIG, therapeutic plasma exchange, and alternative immunomodulation in consultation with hematology.
- Don’t rely on SRA/ELISA alone to guide bedside therapy — integrate serology with clinical trajectory (platelets, fibrinogen, D‑dimer, schistocytes).

Takeaway: IVIG is a powerful tool for aHIT and often turns a dangerous course around quickly, but clinicians must recognize when an unusually aggressive phenotype (very low platelets, consumptive coagulopathy, Fc‑resistant antibodies) predicts a blunted response. In those situations, don’t wait — combine or escalate therapy (anticoagulant change, repeat IVIG, plasma exchange) guided by serial clinical and laboratory markers and multidisciplinary input.



## Reading the Tea Leaves: Using Fibrinogen, D‑dimer, and Schistocytes to Track HIT Control

What makes this case worth your bookmark? A septuagenarian after cardiac surgery developed autoimmune HIT (aHIT) with a dramatic platelet nadir, a thrombotic stroke, and — critically — persistent laboratory evidence of consumptive coagulopathy despite being on what his team thought was “the right” anticoagulant. Fibrinogen fell, D‑dimer climbed, and schistocytes appeared while on argatroban. Those trends were the clue that hypercoagulability was ongoing and the anticoagulant strategy needed reassessment.

If you treat HIT in the real world, you’ll see patients on argatroban, bivalirudin, fondaparinux, or DOACs and assume anticoagulation equals control. But HIT — especially autoimmune variants — can drive a consumptive process that laboratory trends pick up earlier than platelets recover. Knowing how to read fibrinogen, D‑dimer, and peripheral smear changes gives you an early bedside barometer of whether your treatment is actually winning.

Why these markers matter
- Fibrinogen: an acute‑phase protein that rises after surgery, but in uncontrolled consumptive coagulopathy it will begin to fall — a net consumption signal. In this case the fibrinogen climbed early post‑op (expected), then fell progressively during treatment, signaling ongoing thrombin generation and fibrin formation/consumption.
- D‑dimer: a direct marker of fibrin breakdown. Rising D‑dimer despite anticoagulation suggests continued thrombus formation and lysis.
- Schistocytes (fragmented RBCs): their new appearance reflects mechanical/shear damage from microthrombi (or DIC/MAHA). In HIT, they indicate microvascular thrombosis and consumption severe enough to shear RBCs.

How these labs played out in the case
- Baseline postoperative hyperfibrinogenemia was expected. During argatroban therapy fibrinogen fell to near‑normal and then lower, D‑dimer peaked, and schistocytes appeared — a pattern consistent with progressive consumptive coagulopathy driven by HIT antibodies. Those signals prompted a change to fondaparinux (with AT repletion) and continued immune‑modulation with IVIG; platelets then recovered slowly.

A practical bedside algorithm
1. Baseline and early serials
   - At HIT suspicion or diagnosis, send: CBC with smear, fibrinogen, D‑dimer, and standard coagulation panel. Get a baseline before you change anticoagulants if possible.
   - Recheck every 24–48 hours early in the course (more often if unstable).
2. What to watch for
   - Fibrinogen: postoperative patients usually have high fibrinogen early. A falling trend (after the expected early postoperative peak) is concerning for consumption.
   - D‑dimer: rising or persistently very high levels despite therapeutic anticoagulation point to ongoing thrombus turnover.
   - Schistocytes: new or increasing schistocytosis on peripheral smear is a red flag for microangiopathic thrombosis.
   - Platelet count is important but slow to recover in severe aHIT — don’t wait for platelets alone.
3. Interpretation — patterns that should prompt escalation
   - Rising D‑dimer + falling fibrinogen ± new schistocytes despite therapeutic anticoagulation = active consumptive coagulopathy from ongoing HIT activity.
   - Rising fibrinogen with falling D‑dimer and stable/normal smear suggests improving control.
   - Isolated lab noise? Correlate with clinical picture (new thrombosis, ischemia, limb changes, hemolysis).
4. Actions to consider when labs suggest ongoing HIT activity
   - Reassess anticoagulant choice and delivery:
     - Confirm therapeutic dosing/levels (PTT for argatroban; ensure not subtherapeutic).
     - Consider switching agents if concern for inadequate control (e.g., argatroban → fondaparinux or fondaparinux → DOAC after expert input). In the case, argatroban appeared insufficient and the team switched to fondaparinux with IV AT boost.
   - Consider immune‑modulation:
     - High‑dose IVIG can rapidly inhibit FcγR‑mediated platelet activation in many aHIT cases — but some aHIT antibodies are unusually resistant and response may be blunted.
   - Consult hematology early for refractory cases; discuss plasmapheresis for extreme, life‑threatening antibody loads.
   - Imaging to look for occult thrombosis if labs suggest ongoing activity (brain, limb, pulmonary, portal venous systems as indicated).
   - Avoid platelet transfusion unless life‑threatening bleeding or invasive procedure is imminent — transfusions can fuel thrombosis in HIT.
   - Monitor for hemolysis/DIC and treat organ‑specific complications; transfuse RBCs as needed for anemia.
5. Practical pitfalls
   - Don’t assume a therapeutic anticoagulant guarantees control — lab trends may lag behind or show persistent activity.
   - Post‑op fibrinogen dynamics are noisy: contextualize trends against the expected early postoperative rise.
   - D‑dimer is sensitive but nonspecific; interpret trends, not single values.
   - Peripheral smear matters — ask lab to report schistocyte percentage when concerned (and get the smear reviewed by a hematologist if present).

Clinical pearls
- Platelet recovery can be slow in aHIT; use fibrinogen, D‑dimer and smear changes to judge control earlier than platelet counts allow.
- Falling fibrinogen in a post‑op HIT patient is not always “good” — after the initial postoperative rise, further fall suggests consumption from ongoing thrombosis.
- A rising D‑dimer while on therapeutic anticoagulation is a red flag for uncontrolled thrombus formation.
- New schistocytes indicate microvascular thrombosis/MAHA — escalate and consult.
- If the clinical/lab picture points to ongoing thrombosis despite anticoagulation, escalate: check drug dosing, consider agent switch, give IVIG, and involve hematology early.

Take‑home messages
- In HIT (especially autoimmune HIT), don’t rely on platelet count alone to assess anticoagulant efficacy.
- Serial fibrinogen, D‑dimer, and peripheral smear are practical, bedside barometers of ongoing consumptive coagulopathy.
- Rising D‑dimer + falling fibrinogen ± new schistocytes = rethink your strategy: confirm therapeutic anticoagulation, consider changing agents, add immune modulation, and get hematology involved early.

This case shows how watching the lab trends — not just the platelet number — can change management and, ultimately, outcomes. Read the tea leaves early.



# Stroke in HIT: Beyond DVT—Recognizing and Treating Arterial Events

What makes this case arresting is how a classic postoperative problem—delirium after cardiac surgery—turned out to be an arterial stroke from autoimmune heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT). We tend to think of HIT as a venous thrombotic disease (DVT/PE) or line‑related clots, but HIT can and does cause arterial events, including cortical infarcts that may present as confusion, focal deficits, or simply “not waking up right” after surgery. This case also highlights several practical challenges: very early post‑op onset when there was preoperative heparin exposure, profound thrombocytopenia, heparin‑independent (autoimmune) platelet activation, and an awkward therapeutic balance between preventing further clotting and avoiding hemorrhagic conversion of the stroke.

Below are the clinical lessons I would want in my pocket when seeing a similar patient.

## Recognize HIT as a cause of postoperative arterial thrombosis
- Timeline can be misleading. In cardiac surgery patients HIT usually appears POD5–10, but prior recent heparin exposure (even a small procedural dose) can shift the onset earlier. If platelet decline is unexplained and progressive, think HIT regardless of “typical” timing.
- HIT can cause arterial thrombosis (stroke, limb ischemia, myocardial infarction). In this case a left parietal cortical infarct presented as worsening delirium with dropping platelets.
- Consider HIT when you have the triad: falling platelets (usually >50% drop from baseline), new thrombotic event (venous or arterial), and recent heparin exposure — even small intra‑arterial or procedural doses.

## When to image the brain: a low threshold after platelet fall + neuro change
- Postop delirium is common, but new or worsening neuro change or any focal sign with concurrent, unexplained thrombocytopenia should prompt neuroimaging. The cost of missing an acute infarct from HIT is high—ongoing hypercoagulability will worsen ischemia.
- Noncontrast head CT is the fastest initial test to exclude hemorrhage and to detect large infarcts; follow with MRI if uncertainty remains or to better define ischemia.
- Early imaging matters for management decisions (anticoagulation choice/timing, neurology/neurosurgery consults, avoidance of thrombolysis).

## Anticoagulation when platelets are critically low and stroke present
The guiding principles: stop all heparin products; anticoagulate with a non‑heparin agent as soon as safe to prevent propagation; avoid platelet transfusion unless bleeding or invasive procedure risk requires it.

Choice of anticoagulant
- Direct thrombin inhibitors: argatroban is a common early choice (IV, short half‑life, titratable with aPTT). Good for unstable postop patients or when you want rapid reversal by stopping infusion.
- Fondaparinux: effective off‑label for HIT and convenient (once‑daily SC). Use is reasonable when bleeding risk is lower or when parenteral infusion logistics are limiting. Be aware it’s AT‑dependent; low antithrombin levels can reduce efficacy—consider AT concentrate if AT is low and you plan fondaparinux.
- DOACs (rivaroxaban/apixaban): increasingly used once platelet counts have recovered and the patient is clinically stable, or for outpatient management after acute control. Not for initial unstable patients.
- Avoid platelet transfusions unless life‑threatening bleeding or invasive procedure needs >50×10^9/L (discuss with hematology). Platelet transfusion may worsen thrombosis in HIT.
- Thrombolysis: generally contraindicated in HIT because of thrombocytopenia and high bleeding risk; decisions need multidisciplinary discussion with neurology/stroke/hematology.

Anticoagulation when platelets are very low
- There is no absolute platelet number that universally forbids anticoagulation for HIT; decisions are individualized.
- Practical approach used in this case and frequently in practice:
  - Platelets <20×10^9/L: very high bleeding risk — start non‑heparin anticoagulation only after discussion; consider IVIG to increase platelets first.
  - Platelets 20–50×10^9/L: many clinicians will anticoagulate with a short‑acting, titratable agent (argatroban) if there is thrombosis, accepting bleeding risk because the risk of new/propagating thrombosis from HIT is higher than bleeding risk in many cases.
  - Platelets >50×10^9/L: anticoagulate standardly with non‑heparin agents.
- In this case argatroban was chosen early despite platelets as low as 13×10^9/L because of active thrombotic disease; IVIG was used to try to blunt autoantibody platelet activation and to hasten platelet recovery.

## Role of IVIG in autoimmune HIT (aHIT)
- IVIG (high‑dose) can rapidly inhibit Fc‑mediated platelet activation in many cases of aHIT and is recommended when there is severe thrombocytopenia, ongoing thrombosis despite anticoagulation, or life‑ or limb‑threatening disease.
- Response can be brisk, but not uniformly: refractory or unusually severe aHIT may show only partial recovery, as in this case.
- IVIG does not replace anticoagulation; it’s adjunctive therapy to blunt immune platelet activation.

## Managing hemorrhagic risk after ischemic stroke in HIT
- Hemorrhagic conversion risk after ischemic stroke is related to infarct size, reperfusion status, anticoagulation intensity, and platelet level.
- Large cortical infarcts (as in this case) raise concern for hemorrhagic transformation; that risk must be balanced against the high risk of ongoing thrombosis without anticoagulation in HIT.
- Practical measures:
  - Use a short‑half‑life, titratable IV anticoagulant (argatroban) initially so therapy can be stopped if bleeding develops.
  - Target modest anticoagulation (avoid supratherapeutic targets) and monitor aPTT closely.
  - Repeat neuroimaging early and if any deterioration.
  - Involve neurology/stroke and hematology early; consider neurosurgical input if hemorrhagic conversion occurs.
  - Consider delaying therapeutic anticoagulation for a short window only if bleeding risk overwhelmingly outweighs thrombosis risk — rare in HIT with active thrombosis.

## Laboratory confirmation and nuances that affect management
- ELISA (PF4/polyanion) is a sensitive screen; high OD values support HIT but aren’t perfectly specific.
- Functional assays (SRA) define platelet‑activating antibodies. aHIT may show heparin‑independent platelet activation (serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin).
- Be aware of atypical serologic patterns (partial resistance to Fc‑blockade) that can reflect unusually severe autoimmune HIT; do not dismiss indeterminate/odd SRA results if the clinical picture fits.
- Serial testing can change over time as antibody mix evolves; treat the patient, not the lab alone.

## Practical workflow at the bedside
1. Any unexplained ≥30–50% platelet fall or platelets <100×10^9/L after recent heparin exposure → calculate 4Ts score and send HIT testing (ELISA ± functional assay).
2. Stop all heparin (including LMWH/fondaparinux if strongly suspected) and remove heparin‑coated lines.
3. Start non‑heparin anticoagulation promptly if thrombosis is present or HIT is highly likely. Choose agent by clinical context (argatroban for unstable/bleeding‑risk cases; fondaparinux or DOAC later).
4. If arterial thrombosis/stroke suspected: immediate noncontrast head CT; involve neurology. If anticoagulating with recent large infarct, prefer short‑acting IV agent and close monitoring.
5. Use IVIG for severe aHIT (profound thrombocytopenia, ongoing thrombosis despite anticoagulation), but continue anticoagulation.
6. Avoid platelet transfusion unless life‑threatening bleeding or needed for essential procedures.
7. Reassess frequently with labs (platelets, fibrinogen, d‑dimer, aPTT/anti‑Xa as appropriate) and repeat imaging if any neuro change.

## Final clinical pearls
- HIT can cause arterial strokes; don’t assume postoperative delirium is benign—image early if platelets are falling.
- Prior small heparin exposures (e.g., cath lab) can precipitate early post‑op HIT.
- Stop heparin, start a non‑heparin anticoagulant even with low platelets if thrombosis is present—argatroban is useful when you want a short‑acting, titratable option.
- Use high‑dose IVIG for autoimmune HIT with severe thrombocytopenia or refractory hypercoagulability, but expect variable responses.
- Manage the bleeding vs thrombosis tension with multidisciplinary input; short half‑life agents and serial imaging give you agility.

This case is a reminder: HIT is a hypercoagulable disease that can present as an arterial catastrophe. Keep the diagnosis in mind when platelets drop after any heparin exposure and act early—because the biggest harm in HIT is untreated thrombosis, not the low platelet count itself.



# Post‑Cardiac Surgery Thrombocytopenia: Don’t Miss HIT Among the Usual Suspects

What makes this case interesting? An elderly patient after routine CPB-developed severe thrombocytopenia, a parietal stroke, and an immunoassay strongly positive for HIT antibodies — but an unusual platelet activation profile. The platelet count fall began earlier than the classic POD5–10 window, nadired at 13×10^9/L, and the serotonin-release assay showed heparin‑independent activation with only partial inhibition by an Fc receptor‑blocking antibody. This is autoimmune HIT (aHIT) — a severe, often heparin‑independent variant — and it exposes three practical traps emergency and perioperative clinicians should know: timing can be shifted by pre‑op heparin exposure, thrombocytopenia depth and consumptive coagulopathy point away from uncomplicated post‑CPB nadirs, and immunoassays need interpretation in the context of platelet activation testing.

## The diagnostic challenge after cardiac surgery: the usual differential

After CPB, platelets commonly fall. But not every post‑op thrombocytopenia is HIT. Think about:

- CPB‑related platelet nadir: expect an immediate postoperative drop that usually recovers by POD3–5 as consumption/dilution effects resolve.
- Infection / sepsis / DIC: look for hemodynamic instability, bleeding, low fibrinogen, high PT/PTT, high D‑dimer.
- Drug‑induced immune thrombocytopenia: typically abrupt, often after first exposures or with a characteristic temporal pattern.
- Post‑transfusion purpura (PTP): requires a transfusion history.
- Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP): look for microangiopathic hemolysis (schistocytes, low haptoglobin), severe ADAMTS13 deficiency.

This case steered away from many of these: hemodynamics were stable, cultures were negative, there were no transfusions, and early labs showed normal haptoglobin, normal PT/INR, high fibrinogen (an acute phase response), and later evolving schistocytes consistent with ongoing consumptive thrombosis rather than primary TTP.

## Timing is everything — but watch for modifiers

Classic HIT timing after heparin exposure is 5–10 days. But exceptions matter:

- Rapid‑onset HIT (within 24 hours) or early onset (before POD5) can occur when there was recent heparin exposure in the prior 100 days. In this case the patient received UFH during a pre‑op radial cath 1 week before surgery — enough to prime anti‑PF4 immunity and produce early postoperative HIT.
- In cardiac surgery the baseline expectation of early platelet drops can mask HIT unless you observe a continuing fall or a second drop after an initial recovery.

If the platelet count keeps falling after POD1–3, or drops profoundly beyond expected CPB nadirs, raise HIT on your differential — especially with heparin exposure in the preceding days.

## Depth of thrombocytopenia and thrombotic picture point toward aHIT

Typical HIT platelet nadirs often remain >20–50×10^9/L; profound thrombocytopenia (e.g., nadirs <20×10^9/L) and evidence of consumptive coagulopathy (falling fibrinogen, rising D‑dimer, new schistocytes) should make you think autoimmune HIT. aHIT antibodies can activate platelets even without heparin and cause severe, persistent thrombocytopenia and thrombosis — as in this patient who suffered a parietal stroke.

## Lab strategy: immunoassay first, but activation testing guides the diagnosis

- PF4/polyanion ELISA is sensitive. A strong positive (high optical density) increases pretest probability but is not diagnostic alone.
- Functional platelet activation assays (eg, serotonin‑release assay, SRA) confirm pathogenic antibodies. Classic HIT pattern: activation at low‑intermediate heparin concentrations (eg, 0.1–0.3 U/mL) with inhibition at very high heparin (100 U/mL).
- aHIT shows heparin‑independent platelet activation — i.e., serotonin release even at 0 U/mL heparin.
- Some aHIT sera may be unusually potent or show atypical features, such as incomplete inhibition by Fc receptor‑blocking monoclonal antibodies (IV.3), so an “indeterminate” SRA needs careful interpretation in light of clinical features and ELISA OD.

In this case the POD5 serum caused near‑complete serotonin release at 0, 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL heparin with loss of activity at 100 U/mL, and IV.3 only partially inhibited activation — a novel serologic phenotype of aHIT.

## Management principles — fast and decisive

When you suspect HIT (including aHIT):

1. Immediately stop all heparin exposure — this includes intraoperative UFH remnants, LMWH prophylaxis, and heparinized lines.
2. Do NOT give platelet transfusions unless there is life‑threatening bleeding. Platelets can worsen thrombotic risk in HIT.
3. Start a non‑heparin anticoagulant empirically while awaiting diagnostic confirmation. Options:
   - Direct thrombin inhibitors (argatroban, bivalirudin) — useful with short half‑life and monitoring needs.
   - Fondaparinux may be used off‑label but can be effective; consider antithrombin status when switching to indirect Xa inhibitors.
   - DOACs (eg, apixaban) are an option in stable patients once platelet count recovers.
4. For severe aHIT with profound thrombocytopenia or ongoing thrombosis despite anticoagulation, consider high‑dose IVIG to block Fc‑mediated platelet activation.
5. Monitor for consumptive coagulopathy (fibrinogen, D‑dimer), peripheral smear for schistocytes, and serial platelet counts. Titrate anticoagulant intensity to thrombotic risk and bleeding status.

In the reported patient, prophylactic LMWH was stopped, argatroban was started, high‑dose IVIG given (with only modest platelet rise), and ultimately fondaparinux with antithrombin supplementation led to platelet recovery and clinical improvement.

## Clinical pearls

- Don’t be reassured by an early CPB platelet drop alone — persistent/progressive decline or a second fall should trigger HIT evaluation.
- A recent pre‑op heparin exposure (eg, cath lab UFH) can lead to early postoperative HIT; always check the procedural heparin history.
- Very low platelet nadirs (<20×10^9/L) and lab evidence of consumptive coagulopathy suggest autoimmune HIT rather than routine post‑CPB thrombocytopenia.
- PF4 ELISA is sensitive; a strongly positive OD plus compatible clinical findings increases likelihood of pathogenic HIT antibodies.
- Functional assays matter: heparin‑independent serotonin release = aHIT. Beware atypical SRA features (eg, incomplete IV.3 inhibition) — interpret in clinical context.
- Stop heparin, anticoagulate with a non‑heparin agent, avoid platelet transfusion unless bleeding, and add IVIG for severe aHIT.

Key take‑home messages:
- Always review preoperative heparin exposures (cath lab or other) when you see early post‑op thrombocytopenia.
- Persistent or progressive thrombocytopenia after CPB, especially with profound nadirs and thrombotic events, should make HIT/aHIT top of your differential.
- Use PF4 ELISA for screening; rely on functional assays (SRA) and clinical context for confirmation — recognize heparin‑independent activation as a red flag for aHIT.
- Stop heparin immediately, start a non‑heparin anticoagulant, consider IVIG for severe aHIT, and monitor fibrinogen/D‑dimer/smear for ongoing consumptive coagulopathy.

Bottom line: In post‑cardiac surgery thrombocytopenia, don’t let routine CPB‑related explanations lull you into missing HIT. Timing can be shifted by prior heparin exposure, the depth of the fall and associated coagulation markers clue you toward aHIT, and early targeted management can prevent catastrophic thrombosis.



# Antibody Evolution in HIT: From Autoimmune to Heparin‑Dependent and the Implications

What makes this case interesting
A man in his 70s developed severe, early-onset heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery. His serology showed classic extremes of autoimmune HIT (aHIT): strong heparin‑independent platelet activation on the serotonin‑release assay (SRA), very profound thrombocytopenia, thrombotic stroke, partial resistance of activation to FcγRIIa blockade (IV.3), and an unusually slow platelet recovery despite high‑dose IVIG and non‑heparin anticoagulation. Over weeks his antibody phenotype evolved: SRA reactivity changed from predominantly heparin‑independent to a more heparin‑dependent pattern. That serologic evolution—documented in the case report—frames practical questions we face in the ED, ICU and ward: what does persistent SRA positivity mean, how long should non‑heparin anticoagulation continue, and how cautious should we be about future heparin exposure?

Why serology phenotype matters (quick review)
- Classic HIT: pathogenic IgG antibodies form immune complexes with PF4 when PF4 is cross‑linked by heparin (or other polyanions). On functional testing (SRA), you see strong platelet activation at therapeutic heparin concentrations (eg 0.1–0.3 U/mL) and suppression at very high heparin (100 U/mL).
- Autoimmune HIT (aHIT): some antibodies can activate platelets without heparin (heparin‑independent activation) and often cause more severe, prolonged, and heparin‑resistant clinical disease. These antibodies may behave differently in vitro (eg activation at 0 U/mL heparin on SRA).
- Functional assays (SRA, HIPA, P‑selectin) detect platelet activation. Immunoassays (PF4/polyanion ELISA) detect antibody binding but not pathogenicity.

Lessons from this case
1) Phenotype can evolve over time
This patient’s early sera caused strong serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin (aHIT pattern) and were only partially inhibited by the FcγRIIa‑blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3—an unusual finding suggesting very aggressive antibody activity. Serial testing months later showed conversion toward a heparin‑dependent SRA pattern (eg some samples with low/no release at 0 U/mL but high release at 0.3 U/mL). Clinically, that meant the initial hypercoagulable, treatment‑resistant phase gradually waned and antibody properties changed as the immune response evolved.

2) Persistent SRA positivity does not equal ongoing identical risk
Functional positivity can persist for weeks–months after clinical recovery. In this case SRA remained positive for several months even as the platelet count recovered and clinical thrombosis risk waned. Key point: a positive SRA indicates presence of platelet‑activating antibodies, but the magnitude, heparin dependence, and clinical context determine ongoing thrombotic risk. Serial clinical correlation (platelets, D‑dimer, fibrinogen, schistocytes, bleeding/thrombosis status) is essential.

3) Management implications
- Non‑heparin anticoagulation should be continued while there is clinical or laboratory evidence of active HIT-associated hypercoagulability. In practice that means until platelet recovery (generally >150×10^9/L or substantial sustained rise), clinical stabilization, and decreasing markers of consumptive coagulopathy. In the case, fondaparinux was continued until platelet recovery and apixaban resumed after clinical improvement.
- Choice of agent matters in severe aHIT. The team initially used argatroban (short half‑life, titratable) then switched to fondaparinux when argatroban seemed insufficient to control consumptive coagulopathy. High‑dose IVIG is a recognized adjunct for aHIT; in this case the platelet response was blunted, emphasizing variable responses in severe aHIT.
- Persistent functional antibodies on SRA do not mandate lifelong non‑heparin anticoagulation, but they do argue for caution about re‑exposure to heparin and for individualized timing of switching back to oral anticoagulants when safe.

Practical approach to persistent SRA positivity and duration of non‑heparin anticoagulation
- Treat the patient, not the lab. If the platelet count has recovered, clinical hypercoagulability has resolved (falling D‑dimer, rising fibrinogen normalization), and there are no new thromboses, the immediate high thrombotic risk has likely passed—even if SRA remains positive.
- Continue non‑heparin parenteral anticoagulation until:
  - Platelet count has substantially recovered (commonly >150×10^9/L or at least a robust sustained rise), AND
  - There is no evidence of ongoing consumptive coagulopathy (normalizing D‑dimer/fibrinogen, no new schistocytes), AND
  - Clinical picture is stable.
- Transition to an oral non‑heparin anticoagulant (DOAC or VKA after overlap/recovery) can be considered once the above are met. In the case, apixaban was resumed after platelet recovery (POD65).
- Duration of therapeutic anticoagulation for HIT with thrombosis follows standard recommendations (commonly ≥3 months), adjusted for provoking factors and clinical course.

Strategies and cautions around future heparin exposure
- Avoid heparin if possible while pathogenic antibodies persist. Even if SRA converts to heparin‑dependent phenotype, re‑exposure can boost immune response.
- If heparin must be used (eg cardiac surgery with CPB, urgent obstetric situations), consider:
  - Expert hematology involvement and multidisciplinary planning,
  - Preprocedural testing (functional assay and immunoassay) to assess residual antibodies—though results may take time and interpretation is nuanced,
  - Strategies to mitigate risk when heparin is deemed necessary: perioperative high‑dose IVIG or plasmapheresis have been used anecdotally to reduce antibody burden before re‑exposure, but evidence is limited and decisions must be individualized.
- Document the HIT diagnosis clearly in the chart and issue clear instructions to avoid heparin until cleared by hematology.

Interpreting unusual SRA features (like IV.3 partial resistance)
- Partial resistance to FcγRIIa blockade (incomplete inhibition by IV.3) is rare. It may reflect very high‑affinity or high‑titer antibody complexes or additional activation pathways. Awareness of atypical inhibition patterns helps avoid dismissing pathogenic serology as “indeterminate.” Combine functional results with clinical context and immunoassay OD values (this patient had a strongly positive PF4 ELISA, OD 2.2).

Clinical pearls
- aHIT can present unusually early after cardiac surgery if there was recent preoperative heparin exposure (eg catheterization heparin).
- Heparin‑independent platelet activation on SRA predicts a more severe course and often slower response to therapy (including IVIG).
- Serial functional testing can show antibody phenotype evolution; interpret serial SRA results in the context of platelet trend and coagulation markers.
- Persistent SRA positivity alone is not an automatic contraindication to stopping non‑heparin anticoagulation; clinical recovery and normalization of coagulation markers are key.
- If future heparin exposure is contemplated, seek hematology input. Consider antibody testing and risk mitigation strategies; treat decisions as case‑by‑case.

Take‑home messages
- HIT antibodies can evolve: aHIT (heparin‑independent activation) may convert over weeks to a more heparin‑dependent profile as the immune response wanes.
- Persistent SRA positivity can persist for months; manage based on clinical and laboratory evidence of ongoing hypercoagulability, not SRA alone.
- Continue non‑heparin anticoagulation until platelet recovery and resolution of consumptive markers; then consider transition to oral therapy guided by hematology.
- Avoid routine re‑exposure to heparin while pathogenic antibodies persist; if unavoidable, plan meticulously with hematology and the procedural team.

This case is a useful reminder that HIT serology is dynamic. The lab tells part of the story — the patient’s platelets, coagulation markers, and clinical trajectory tell the rest.



## A Practical ICU Pathway for Suspected autoimmune HIT (aHIT)

What makes this case interesting
An elderly post‑cardiac surgery patient developed an unusually severe, early thrombocytopenia and a thrombotic stroke. Lab testing showed very strong PF4/polyanion ELISA positivity and a serotonin‑release assay (SRA) that activated platelets without heparin (classic aHIT) — and, uniquely, only partial inhibition with an FcγRIIa‑blocking antibody. In short: he had bona fide autoimmune HIT (aHIT), unusually aggressive and partially resistant in vitro. The case reminds us that aHIT can present earlier than the usual POD5–10 window (if there was recent prior heparin exposure), may be profoundly thrombocytopenic, can be hypercoagulable despite anticoagulation, and may not behave exactly like textbook HIT in functional tests.

Why you need a simple bedside pathway
aHIT is both prothrombotic and paradoxically low‑platelet. Decisions are time‑sensitive: continue heparin and you risk worsening thrombosis; stop anticoagulation and you risk propagation of HIT‑associated thrombosis. You need a pragmatic, repeatable approach to: stop heparins, anticoagulate safely, send the right tests, identify occult thrombosis, consider IVIG for severe aHIT, monitor response, and plan transitions to outpatient anticoagulation.

A stepwise bedside algorithm for suspected aHIT

1) Stop all heparin immediately
- Remove all sources of UFH/LMWH (including heparin flushes, coated catheters if possible).
- Do not await laboratory confirmation if clinical suspicion is moderate–high (e.g., falling platelets with recent heparin exposure ± thrombosis).

2) Start a non‑heparin anticoagulant empirically
- Choice depends on bleeding risk, renal/hepatic function, and ease of monitoring.
  - Argatroban (direct thrombin inhibitor; hepatic clearance) — good when rapid on/off control is desirable (recent surgery, stroke). Example target PTT 1.5–2.5× baseline (case used 55–85 s).
  - Bivalirudin (direct thrombin inhibitor) is an alternative in some centres.
  - Fondaparinux (anti‑Xa, synthetic) can be used off‑label for HIT if bleeding risk is lower and renal function is adequate; easier once patient is stable. Therapeutic dose is commonly 7.5–10 mg daily depending on weight (case used 10 mg).
- Practical tip from the case: start argatroban when bleeding risk is significant or short half‑life is preferred; consider switching to fondaparinux later if anticoagulation control is inadequate or for ease of outpatient transition.

3) Send the right labs — PF4 ELISA + functional assay
- Send a PF4/polyanion immunoassay (ELISA) for sensitivity.
- Send a functional platelet activation test (SRA or equivalent) to demonstrate platelet activation and to detect heparin‑independent activation (aHIT).
- Tell the lab about clinical suspicion of aHIT and any recent heparin exposures — this helps interpretation (e.g., strong ELISA + serotonin release at 0 U/mL = aHIT).
- Don’t over‑interpret an “indeterminate” SRA without discussion — some aHIT antibodies have atypical profiles (partial inhibition by Fc blockade, strong heparin‑independent activation).

4) Consider high‑dose IVIG early for suspected aHIT with severe features
- Indications to consider IVIG: very low platelets (eg, <20–30×10^9/L), ongoing thrombosis, heparin‑independent activation on functional testing, or failure of platelets to respond to non‑heparin anticoagulation.
- IVIG can block FcγRIIa‑mediated platelet activation and often produces a brisk platelet rise in aHIT; but deeply severe cases may have a blunted response (case required three 100 g doses and only had a modest rise).
- Consult hematology early before and after IVIG (dosing/regimen, contraindications).

5) Screen aggressively for occult thrombosis
- aHIT has a high thrombotic risk. Low threshold for imaging:
  - Duplex ultrasound of legs for DVT.
  - CT head if any neurologic change (case had parietal infarct despite no focal deficits initially).
  - CT pulmonary angiography if hypoxia/right heart strain.
  - Consider other targeted imaging based on symptoms/exam.
- If thrombosis is found, continue therapeutic non‑heparin anticoagulation.

6) Use fibrinogen, D‑dimer, and peripheral film to monitor disease activity and anticoagulant efficacy
- In this case falling fibrinogen and rising D‑dimer plus schistocytes despite argatroban signalled ongoing consumptive hypercoagulability.
- Trending values:
  - D‑dimer high or rising → ongoing thrombin generation.
  - Falling fibrinogen → consumption/coagulopathy.
  - New schistocytes → microangiopathic hemolysis from consumptive thrombosis.
- These labs are adjuncts — interpret them with clinical context and imaging.

7) Plan transitions: when and how to switch anticoagulants
- From acute DTI (argatroban/bivalirudin) to fondaparinux:
  - Consider switching once bleeding risk decreases and if argatroban is not controlling hypercoagulability or for practical outpatient dosing.
  - Ensure renal function adequate for fondaparinux; consider antithrombin level (case gave AT concentrate because fondaparinux is AT‑dependent and AT was mildly low).
- From parenteral/non‑heparin to DOAC (eg, apixaban, rivaroxaban):
  - Timing varies. In the published case, apixaban was resumed once platelets recovered to 155×10^9/L (POD65) and the patient was clinically stable.
  - Practical considerations: platelet recovery (generally into normal range), stable clinical course, and hematology guidance. Some centres will require platelet >100–150×10^9/L before switching.
  - Confirm functional tests are improving/waning if available; however, decisions are often clinical.
- Document anticoagulant allergies/suspicions clearly in the chart and discharge summary.

8) Clear consult triggers
- Call hematology/platelet immunology when:
  - PF4 ELISA strongly positive or SRA shows heparin‑independent activation.
  - Platelets are very low (<30×10^9/L) or falling rapidly.
  - New thrombosis or suspected occult thrombosis.
  - Lab evidence of consumptive coagulopathy (falling fibrinogen, rising D‑dimer, schistocytes).
  - Considering IVIG, AT concentrate, plasma exchange, or long‑term anticoagulation strategy.
- Engage neurology, vascular surgery, interventional radiology as needed for stroke, limb ischemia, or large vessel thrombosis.
- Notify transfusion medicine if platelet transfusion or blood products might be considered — platelets are generally avoided unless life‑threatening bleeding.

Clinical pearls from the case
- aHIT can present earlier than POD5 if there was recent preoperative heparin exposure (eg, cath lab heparin).
- A very strong PF4 ELISA plus platelet activation without heparin on SRA is a red flag for aHIT.
- Some aHIT antibodies may partially resist FcγRIIa blockade in vitro — an “indeterminate” functional assay should prompt further expert review rather than dismissal.
- IVIG is a powerful adjunct in aHIT but may have a variable or blunted effect in the most severe cases — expect to monitor clinical and lab response and be ready to adjust anticoagulation.
- Monitor fibrinogen and D‑dimer as bedside clues to ongoing HIT‑driven hypercoagulability, especially when imaging is non‑diagnostic or when anticoagulation choices are being reassessed.

Key take‑home messages
- Stop all heparins immediately for suspected HIT/aHIT and start a non‑heparin anticoagulant without delay.
- Send PF4 ELISA and a functional assay (SRA or equivalent); discuss atypical results with the lab.
- Consider early IVIG for severe aHIT (profound thrombocytopenia, ongoing thrombosis, or heparin‑independent activation).
- Screen aggressively for occult thrombosis and use fibrinogen/D‑dimer and peripheral film to judge disease activity and anticoagulant control.
- Transition anticoagulants thoughtfully (DTI → fondaparinux → DOAC) once platelets recover and clinical stability is achieved; involve hematology early.

A final note: aHIT is rare but can be devastating and protean. Keep a low threshold to act when the clinical pattern fits — prompt cessation of heparin, institution of non‑heparin anticoagulation, appropriate testing, and early hematology involvement save limbs and lives.
