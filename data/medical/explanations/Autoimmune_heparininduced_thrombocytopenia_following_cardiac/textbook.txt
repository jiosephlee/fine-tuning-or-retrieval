Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

## Clinical Approach to Post–Cardiac Surgery Thrombocytopenia and Thrombosis

Cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) produces a predictable, transient fall in platelet count in most patients; separating that expected post‑CPB thrombocytopenia from pathologic causes—most importantly heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) and the more aggressive autoimmune form (aHIT)—is a common and time‑sensitive diagnostic challenge. The case just presented illustrates many teaching points: an initially modest post‑operative fall in platelets followed by progressive decline, new ischemic stroke, strong PF4/polyanion serology and an atypical functional assay profile that signalled a heparin‑independent, FcγRIIa‑mediated platelet‑activating antibody. Below is a practical clinical framework to evaluate and manage thrombocytopenia and thrombosis after cardiac surgery, with emphasis on recognizing aHIT and initiating urgent therapy.

Distinguish expected CPB‑related thrombocytopenia from HIT/aHIT
- Typical post‑CPB thrombocytopenia is early (largest decline by POD1–2), modest (often 20–50% fall but usually remaining >100×10^9/L), and recovers over the first week. Mechanisms include hemodilution, platelet activation and sequestration during bypass, and contact activation on artificial surfaces.
- HIT classically shows a biphasic or delayed pattern: relative preservation early after surgery, with a new or progressive platelet fall beginning typically between POD5 and POD10 (though this window shifts earlier with recent heparin exposure). Thrombosis (venous or arterial) is common and often paradoxical in the setting of thrombocytopenia.
- aHIT is an atypical, often more severe variant in which antibodies can activate platelets independent of heparin; clinical hallmarks include profound thrombocytopenia, progressive consumptive coagulopathy, marked hypercoagulability with thrombosis despite cessation of heparin, and sometimes an earlier onset if there was recent heparin exposure (as in preoperative catheterization). In practice, any progressive platelet drop after POD1, particularly when accompanied by new thrombosis, should raise immediate concern for HIT/aHIT.

Early clinical evaluation—history, pattern and differential
- Time course is critical. Ask about all recent heparin exposures (intraoperative UFH, catheterization heparin boluses, heparin flushes, prophylactic LMWH). Early onset (before POD5) should prompt review for a prior recent heparin exposure in the preceding 5–10 days that can “prime” the immune response.
- Characterize the platelet trajectory: percent fall from baseline, nadir, and speed of decline. Profound nadirs (<30×10^9/L) and continued decline despite stopping heparin are worrisome for aHIT.
- Review other causes of post‑operative thrombocytopenia: dilutional effects, sepsis/DIC, mechanical destruction (prosthetic valves, ECMO), drug‑induced immune thrombocytopenia, thrombotic microangiopathies (TTP/HUS), post‑transfusion purpura, platelet clumping/artifact. The peripheral blood film (look for schistocytes, platelet clumps) and basic labs (CBC trend, coagulation panel, hemolysis markers, cultures) help exclude many alternatives. In the case, absence of schistocytes initially, stable hemoglobin and WBCs, and a strong PF4 ELISA pointed to HIT.

Use pretest probability (4Ts) but appreciate limitations after CPB
- Apply a structured score (4Ts: Thrombocytopenia degree, Timing of fall, Thrombosis or other sequelae, oTher causes) to stratify pretest probability. A moderate or high 4Ts score justifies immediate action.
- Caveat: the 4Ts has reduced specificity after cardiac surgery because many non‑HIT mechanisms produce early platelet falls; nevertheless, a rising score driven by progressive fall and new thrombosis should not be ignored. In practice, treat clinical probability and trajectory rather than absolute POD cutoffs.

Laboratory testing strategy—what to order and how to interpret results
- Order a high‑sensitivity PF4/polyanion immunoassay (ELISA) and, in parallel, send or arrange a functional platelet activation assay (serotonin‑release assay, SRA, or heparin‑induced platelet activation assay) to a reference laboratory. Do not wait for confirmatory testing before initiating therapy if clinical probability is moderate/high.
- Interpret results in clinical context:
  - PF4 ELISA is very sensitive but less specific; high optical density (OD) values (for example, >1.0–2.0) increase the likelihood of pathogenic antibodies.
  - Functional assays (SRA) are highly specific: classic HIT shows heparin‑dependent platelet activation (strong release at low heparin [0.1–0.3 U/mL], inhibition at very high heparin). aHIT often demonstrates heparin‑independent platelet activation (serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin) or strong activation across a range of heparin concentrations.
  - Specialized testing (e.g., inhibition with FcγRIIa‑blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3) can confirm Fc receptor dependence. However, as this case demonstrates, atypical serologic patterns can occur—partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade or unusual SRA inhibition profiles—so close liaison with an experienced platelet immunology laboratory is essential.
- Repeat or serial testing may be informative: antibody reactivity can change over weeks (aHIT features may wane to more heparin‑dependent profiles as antibodies decline).

Immediate management steps when HIT/aHIT is suspected
- Stop all heparin exposures immediately: discontinue UFH and LMWH, remove heparin‑containing catheters or locks if feasible, and avoid heparinized flushes. This is necessary but by itself insufficient for HIT/aHIT because thrombosis risk is high.
- Start a non‑heparin parenteral anticoagulant without waiting for functional confirmation when clinical probability is intermediate or high. Choice depends on clinical context:
  - Argatroban (direct thrombin inhibitor) is commonly used in the postoperative setting, has a short half‑life and is hepatically metabolized (advantageous in renal dysfunction; needs PTT monitoring and dose adjustment in hepatic impairment).
  - Bivalirudin (direct thrombin inhibitor) is often used in cardiac procedural settings and is an option for perioperative anticoagulation; it is partially renally cleared.
  - Fondaparinux (indirect anti‑Xa via antithrombin) has been used off‑label for HIT and is given subcutaneously once daily; it is renally cleared and requires sufficient antithrombin levels and renal function. In the case, fondaparinux was used after initial argatroban when practical once bleeding risk and AT level were assessed.
  - Note: direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) are increasingly used for outpatient management of HIT once platelet counts have recovered, but they are not the first choice for immediate inpatient therapy when bleeding risk or renal function are concerns.
- Avoid platelet transfusion unless there is life‑threatening bleeding or an urgent invasive procedure; transfusion can exacerbate thrombosis in HIT.

Adjunctive and rescue therapies for aHIT
- High‑dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) can be effective in aHIT by blocking Fcγ receptor‑mediated platelet activation; typical regimens used in reports include 1 g/kg daily for 2 days or single doses of 100 g in severe cases as adjunctive therapy. IVIG can raise thrombotic risk transiently and must be balanced against the severity of aHIT; involve hematology early.
- Plasma exchange (therapeutic plasmapheresis) has been used in extreme or refractory cases to remove pathogenic antibodies, especially when urgent cardiac surgery is required.
- Monitor coagulation biomarkers (d‑dimer, fibrinogen) and peripheral smear serially; worsening consumptive coagulopathy despite anticoagulation suggests uncontrolled HIT‑associated thrombosis and should prompt reassessment of anticoagulant choice/intensity and consideration of adjunctive measures.

Diagnosis and treatment of thrombotic complications
- Maintain a low threshold for imaging when neurologic changes, new limb symptoms, or unexplained organ ischemia occur; delirium or altered mental status in postoperative patients can herald an ischemic stroke and should prompt CT/MRI as clinically appropriate. Detection of arterial thrombosis (as in the parietal infarct in the case) confirms HIT‑associated thrombosis and necessitates therapeutic anticoagulation.
- Treat proven HIT‑associated thrombosis as provoked: anticoagulate with a non‑heparin agent for at least 3 months. If HIT occurs without thrombosis, a common recommendation is approximately 4 weeks of non‑heparin anticoagulation.
- Transition to long‑term oral anticoagulation: warfarin should not be started until platelet count has recovered to a safe threshold (commonly >150×10^9/L) and the patient has been adequately anticoagulated with a non‑heparin agent, because initiating warfarin during active HIT increases risk of venous limb gangrene due to rapid fall in protein C. DOACs (e.g., apixaban, rivaroxaban) are increasingly used as outpatient alternatives once platelets have recovered; timing and choice should be individualized.

Practical algorithm (summary)
1. Recognize pattern: obtain baseline platelet count and follow daily. Any progressive drop after POD1 or new thrombosis should prompt HIT evaluation.
2. Review recent heparin exposures (including preoperative cath) and calculate 4Ts to assess pretest probability, knowing the 4Ts has limits after CPB.
3. If 4Ts is intermediate/high or clinical suspicion is strong:
   - Stop all heparin immediately.
   - Start a non‑heparin anticoagulant (argatroban, bivalirudin or fondaparinux as appropriate to renal/hepatic function and bleeding risk).
   - Order PF4/polyanion ELISA and functional platelet activation testing in parallel; contact a reference platelet immunology laboratory early if results are atypical.
   - Obtain imaging as indicated for suspected thrombosis (brain CT/MRI for neurologic changes).
   - In cases suggestive of aHIT (heparin‑independent activation, profound thrombocytopenia, consumptive coagulopathy), add hematology consultation, consider IVIG and, rarely, plasmapheresis.
4. Monitor platelets, coagulation parameters, hemolysis markers and clinical signs of thrombosis; avoid platelet transfusion unless bleeding.
5. Once platelet counts recover, plan long‑term anticoagulation (typically 3 months for HIT with thrombosis, 4 weeks for isolated HIT) and arrange follow‑up testing and documentation in the medical record to avoid future heparin exposure.

Closing practical notes from the case
- The presented patient’s early onset of HIT before POD5 emphasizes the importance of documenting and asking about any preoperative heparin (he received UFH during cardiac catheterization). Small doses given days earlier can prime an immune response and produce an earlier clinical HIT after surgery.
- Strong ELISA reactivity (high OD) together with an SRA demonstrating heparin‑independent platelet activation should alert clinicians to aHIT. Do not dismiss “indeterminate” SRA patterns without consulting the reference laboratory; atypical profiles—partial resistance to Fc blockade, strong activation at 0 U/mL heparin—are consistent with severe aHIT and mandate aggressive therapy.
- Timely cessation of heparin, immediate non‑heparin anticoagulation, early hematology involvement, and consideration of IVIG or plasmapheresis when aHIT is suspected can be life‑ and limb‑saving.

## Pathophysiology: From Classic HIT to Autoimmune HIT

Heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) is fundamentally an immune‑mediated, prothrombotic disorder that arises when antibodies of the IgG class recognise complexes of platelet factor 4 (PF4) bound to polyanions such as unfractionated heparin (UFH). PF4 is a small, highly cationic tetramer released from platelet alpha granules that avidly binds to anionic molecules on bacterial surfaces, endothelial glycocalyx, and therapeutic polyanions such as heparin. When PF4 and heparin associate they form multimolecular complexes that expose neoepitopes not present on PF4 alone. B cells recognising these neoepitopes, with T‑cell help, differentiate and class‑switch to make anti‑PF4/polyanion IgG. Pathogenic IgG antibodies form immune complexes with PF4–heparin on platelet surfaces; the Fc portion of these IgG molecules engages and clusters platelet FcγRIIa receptors, triggering platelet activation, release of procoagulant microparticles, and further PF4 release. The net effect is a powerful amplification loop in which antibody‑mediated platelet activation generates thrombin and fibrin — producing thrombosis even in the face of thrombocytopenia.

The classical laboratory and mechanistic hallmark of heparin‑dependent HIT is that platelet activation occurs at low to therapeutic heparin concentrations (for example 0.1–0.3 U/mL in functional assays such as the serotonin‑release assay, SRA) and is suppressed at very high heparin concentrations (e.g., 100 U/mL). High heparin levels disrupt the lattice of PF4–heparin multimers and thereby prevent formation of the immune complexes required for FcγRIIa clustering; this property is exploited diagnostically. Clinically, classic HIT typically appears within 5–10 days after heparin exposure in patients without recent heparin exposure, although an anamnestic, rapid‑onset HIT can occur within hours in patients previously sensitised in the preceding few weeks.

Autoimmune HIT (aHIT) denotes a distinct and clinically important subgroup in which the antibodies display qualitatively different serologic and functional behaviour: they can activate platelets even in the absence of added heparin. Mechanistically, aHIT antibodies either recognise PF4 alone, PF4 in complexes with endogenous polyanions that decorate platelet, monocyte, or endothelial surfaces, or induce PF4 to adopt conformations that mimic the heparin‑bound state. These antibodies are often higher affinity and/or recognise broader or more exposed neoepitopes on PF4, permitting immune complex formation on cell surfaces without exogenous heparin. In functional assays this manifests as strong platelet activation at 0 U/mL heparin. Clinically, aHIT presents with more severe and protracted thrombocytopenia, frequent and sometimes atypical thrombotic events (including arterial thromboses such as stroke), delayed‑onset or persisting HIT after heparin cessation, and in extreme cases a consumptive coagulopathy with falling fibrinogen, rising D‑dimer, and schistocytes due to widespread thrombin generation and microvascular fibrin formation.

Cellular amplification beyond platelets explains much of the hypercoagulability seen in aHIT. PF4–antibody immune complexes deposit on monocytes and endothelial cells, inducing tissue factor expression and release of proinflammatory cytokines. Complement activation can occur via classical pathway engagement of C1q by IgG within immune complexes, further opsonising cell surfaces and recruiting neutrophils. Neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation, platelet–neutrophil aggregates, and endothelial perturbation all contribute to a milieu that amplifies coagulation. The downstream effect is excessive thrombin generation that consumes fibrinogen and platelets and can produce microangiopathic haemolysis (schistocytes) — a consumptive picture that can be misread as disseminated intravascular coagulation or thrombotic microangiopathy unless HIT is considered.

Laboratory correlates reflect this biology. Strong positive PF4/polyanion ELISA readings (high optical density) correlate with likelihood of clinically relevant, platelet‑activating antibodies, and functional assays such as the SRA help define dependence on heparin. In aHIT, the SRA typically shows marked serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin and preserved inhibition at very high heparin concentrations (confirming PF4 involvement). One caveat illustrated by the present case is that some highly potent aHIT antibodies may show only partial inhibition by FcγRIIa‑blocking agents in vitro (for example monoclonal IV.3). Partial resistance to FcγRIIa blockade does not exclude HIT: it most likely reflects extremely avid immune complexes that either overwhelm the blocking reagent, interact with FcγRIIa in a way not fully prevented by a particular blocker, or recruit additional receptors/co‑factors (complement receptors, other Fc receptors) contributing to activation. Thus, incomplete IV.3 inhibition should prompt consideration of aHIT rather than premature exclusion of the diagnosis.

Temporal dynamics of the antibody response are also important. In many patients with aHIT the initial dominant serologic phenotype is heparin‑independent activation; over weeks to months, as antibody titres fall and affinity maturation changes, the reactivity often evolves toward a more classic heparin‑dependent pattern or eventually becomes undetectable. This evolution explains clinical scenarios in which early, fulminant disease with heparin‑independent SRA positivity later gives way to more conventional SRA profiles. It also underlies the clinical observation that recent preoperative heparin exposure (for example, during angiography) can prime an anamnestic response, causing unusually early postoperative HIT manifestations.

Understanding these mechanistic distinctions has practical implications. aHIT is highly prothrombotic and often requires prompt non‑heparin anticoagulation and consideration of adjunctive therapies aimed at interrupting Fc receptor‑mediated activation — high‑dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) is commonly used because it can saturate Fc receptors and neutralise pathogenic antibodies, but exceptionally potent aHIT antibodies may respond incompletely, as in the reported case. Alternative options in refractory or life‑threatening presentations include plasmapheresis to remove pathogenic IgG and targeted strategies to blunt downstream coagulation (carefully chosen non‑heparin anticoagulants). Finally, awareness that a negative or “indeterminate” functional assay does not rule out aHIT — and that serologic evolution over time occurs — is essential for interpreting serial tests and guiding ongoing management.

In summary, classic HIT results from heparin‑dependent PF4 immune complexes that activate platelets via FcγRIIa clustering, with high heparin concentrations being inhibitory. Autoimmune HIT represents a spectrum of more aggressive anti‑PF4 responses in which antibodies can activate platelets and other blood and vascular cells without heparin, provoke marked thrombin generation and consumptive coagulopathy, and in some cases partially resist FcγRIIa blockade in vitro. Recognition of these mechanistic nuances is critical to diagnosis, risk stratification, interpretation of laboratory testing, and selection of appropriate therapeutic interventions.

## Differential Diagnosis of Early Postoperative Thrombocytopenia After CPB

A falling platelet count after cardiac surgery is common and has a wide differential. The case above illustrates how a relatively dramatic, early postoperative thrombocytopenia can be misattributed to routine post-cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) effects unless a systematic approach is taken. The aim of this section is to provide a pragmatic, pathophysiology‑based differential diagnosis for significant platelet declines after CPB and to highlight the clinical and laboratory features that point toward heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) and its autoimmune variant (aHIT).

Common non‑immune causes after CPB
Cardiopulmonary bypass itself is the most frequent cause of an early postoperative platelet fall. Mechanical shear, contact activation on foreign surfaces, haemodilution from priming solutions and intraoperative blood loss and transfusion combine to lower platelet counts in most patients. Typically these declines occur immediately or within the first 24 hours, are modest (often 30%–50% from baseline), and stabilize or begin to recover by postoperative day (POD) 3–4 as platelet production and redistribution normalize. Platelet sequestration in a hypothermic spleen, re‑equilibration of volume status, and consumption related to surgical bleeding also produce predictable, early declines. In most patients there is an uncomplicated postoperative course with stable haemodynamics, no new thrombosis, and recovery of platelet count without additional intervention—this pattern favors CPB‑related, nonimmune etiologies.

Critical illness, sepsis and DIC
When thrombocytopenia occurs in the setting of hemodynamic instability, persistent fever or organ dysfunction, sepsis and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) must be considered. Laboratory hallmarks that distinguish these processes from uncomplicated CPB‑related platelet loss include prolonged prothrombin time (PT)/INR and/or aPTT, marked elevation of D‑dimer with falling fibrinogen (hypofibrinogenemia in consumptive DIC), and biochemical evidence of organ dysfunction. Peripheral smear often shows schistocytes when microangiopathic destruction is present. Sepsis‑associated thrombocytopenia tends to evolve with systemic inflammatory signs and is accompanied by progressive coagulopathy rather than the isolated, profound platelet drop seen with immune causes.

Immune‑mediated thrombocytopenias
Immune mechanisms produce some of the most clinically important postoperative thrombocytopenias because they carry a risk of thrombosis and often require urgent change in management.

- Heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) and autoimmune HIT (aHIT). Classical HIT is antibody mediated (IgG antibodies against PF4/heparin complexes) and typically manifests between POD5 and POD10 after an initiating heparin exposure. After cardiac surgery the incidence is higher than in many settings because of extensive intraoperative UFH use. Typical HIT causes an intermediate platelet fall (often 30%–70% from baseline) with a strong predilection for thrombosis rather than bleeding. aHIT denotes a subset of antibodies that can activate platelets independently of added heparin; clinically these patients may have more severe thrombocytopenia, more aggressive and persistent hypercoagulability, and unusual serologic test patterns (for example, platelet activation in functional assays even without heparin). Important clinical clues favoring HIT/aHIT in the postoperative setting are a new thrombotic event (arterial or venous), a strong‑positive PF4/polyanion ELISA (high optical density, e.g. >1.0–2.0), and functional assay evidence of heparin‑independent platelet activation. Rapid‑onset HIT (within 24 hours of re‑exposure to heparin) occurs in patients with recent prior heparin exposure (for example, the low‑dose UFH given at catheterization in the case) and can produce an apparently “early” postoperative HIT picture—even before POD5.

- Drug‑induced immune thrombocytopenia (DITP). Many drugs can cause an immune thrombocytopenia, producing severe platelet reductions. The characteristic temporal pattern is 5–10 days after first exposure, but earlier onset can occur with prior sensitization. Postoperative DITP should be considered when a new drug with known association (e.g., certain antibiotics, glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors, quinine derivatives) has been started and the timing fits. In the cardiac surgical setting, many of the commonly used perioperative drugs are not classic causes, and DITP is less likely if the timing is very early and no new culprit drug was introduced.

- Post‑transfusion purpura (PTP). PTP requires prior blood product exposure and is characterized by severe thrombocytopenia occurring about 5–10 days after transfusion; it is therefore unlikely in patients who received no transfusion or when the onset is very early.

Thrombotic microangiopathies (TTP/HUS)
Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) and hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS) cause thrombocytopenia from platelet microthrombi and are classically accompanied by microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA): schistocytes on smear, low haptoglobin, elevated LDH, and indirect hyperbilirubinemia. Neurologic symptoms and renal dysfunction are common. If the clinical picture and laboratory evidence support MAHA, ADAMTS13 activity testing and urgent hematology consultation are indicated because TTP requires plasma‑directed therapy (PEX) rather than the anticoagulant strategies used for HIT.

Device‑related and mechanical causes
Mechanical devices that remain in place after surgery—such as intra‑aortic balloon pumps, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) circuits or ventricular assist devices—can cause continuous platelet activation and consumption. These sources produce a pattern of ongoing platelet loss often accompanied by evidence of hemolysis and elevated LDH. Device malfunction or high shear states from valvular prostheses can produce similar features. A focused device inspection and consideration of flow dynamics are necessary when these devices are present.

Pseudo‑thrombocytopenia and laboratory artifacts
Before escalating therapy, confirm true thrombocytopenia. EDTA‑induced platelet clumping can spuriously lower automated platelet counts; a peripheral smear will show clumps and repeating the count in a citrate or heparin tube usually resolves the discrepancy. Other preanalytical errors (sample clotting, platelet satellitism) should be excluded early.

Pattern recognition and laboratory clues that favor HIT/aHIT
Timing. Classical HIT post‑CPB tends to appear in the POD5–10 window. Two exceptions are important: (1) rapid‑onset HIT occurs within hours to 1 day after heparin re‑exposure in patients with recent heparin exposure (within the past 100 days or so), and (2) aHIT can produce earlier and more severe platelet falls because of preformed or heparin‑independent antibodies. Thus an “early” platelet drop should prompt a careful review of heparin exposures within the preceding weeks.

Degree of thrombocytopenia. Typical HIT produces moderate thrombocytopenia (nadir often >20–30×10^9/L). Very profound nadirs (e.g., <20×10^9/L), especially when accompanied by laboratory evidence of consumptive coagulopathy (falling fibrinogen, rising D‑dimer, schistocytes), should raise concern for aHIT or another fulminant consumptive process.

Thrombosis and clinical course. New arterial or venous thrombosis—particularly unusual sites or arterial events such as ischemic stroke soon after surgery—strongly supports HIT/aHIT. Progressive thrombocytopenia despite stopping heparin, or poor response to usual measures, suggests an autoimmune phenotype with sustained platelet‑activating antibodies.

Laboratory testing strategy
- Confirm true thrombocytopenia with a smear and repeat counts in another anticoagulant if clumping is suspected.  
- Obtain coagulation studies (PT/INR, aPTT), fibrinogen, D‑dimer, LDH, haptoglobin and bilirubin to screen for DIC or MAHA.  
- If HIT is suspected, order a PF4/polyanion immunoassay (ELISA) and request that the lab report the optical density; high OD values correlate with clinically significant (platelet‑activating) antibodies. Immunoassays are sensitive but not specific—false positives are common after surgery—so functional testing (serotonin‑release assay, SRA, or other platelet activation assays) is used for confirmation.  
- Interpret functional assays in context: classic HIT shows platelet activation at low heparin concentrations (0.1–0.3 U/mL) with inhibition at very high heparin (100 U/mL). aHIT can produce activation without added heparin (0 U/mL), consistent with heparin‑independent platelet activation; such activation is also typically inhibited at supratherapeutic heparin concentrations. Laboratories sometimes perform FcγRIIa blockade (e.g., with monoclonal antibody IV.3) to show Fc receptor–dependent activation: complete inhibition supports a classic FcγRIIa‑mediated mechanism. Partial or absent inhibition—as in the presented case—is unusual and suggests an unusually potent or atypical mechanism of platelet activation that still may represent aHIT. Because serologic patterns can evolve, repeat testing on later sera can be informative.

Putting it together: a practical approach
1. Immediately confirm platelet count and review the peripheral smear. Exclude clumping and gross hemolysis.  
2. Rapidly assess timing and heparin exposures in the prior days to weeks (operative UFH, catheterization heparin doses, prophylactic LMWH). Early platelet fall does not exclude HIT if there was recent preoperative exposure.  
3. Look for red flags that increase the pretest probability of HIT/aHIT: new thrombosis, severe nadir, consumptive coagulopathy, high OD PF4 ELISA, and functional assay activation at 0 U/mL.  
4. If HIT is reasonably suspected, stop all heparin (including heparin flushes) and begin an alternative non‑heparin anticoagulant while testing is pending; consult hematology early. For suspected aHIT with severe thrombocytopenia or ongoing thrombosis, consider adjunctive therapies such as high‑dose IVIG under specialist guidance.  
5. If features favor sepsis, DIC or TTP, the management diverges (antibiotics and supportive care for sepsis/DIC; urgent plasma exchange for TTP) and these diagnoses must be rapidly excluded or confirmed.

Conclusion
Early postoperative thrombocytopenia after CPB is most often benign and related to CPB, haemodilution and blood loss, but a systematic assessment will identify the minority of patients with immune‑mediated and consumptive causes that mandate very different therapies. HIT and especially aHIT should be suspected when platelet counts fall substantially and/or when thrombosis develops, particularly if there is recent heparin exposure before surgery. A careful combination of timing, clinical features, smear review and targeted laboratory testing (PF4 ELISA with OD and functional assay interpretation) allows discrimination among causes and guides urgent, sometimes lifesaving, therapeutic decisions. The case reported above underscores the importance of this structured approach: an apparently “early” thrombocytopenia was in fact HIT/aHIT triggered by preoperative heparin exposure, and recognition of the atypical serologic profile guided appropriate therapy.

## Diagnostic Workup and Interpretation of HIT/aHIT Testing

Making the diagnosis of heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) is an exercise in integrating clinical probability with laboratory data. In the postoperative cardiac surgical setting this is especially important because cardiopulmonary bypass, surgical blood loss and routine postoperative anticoagulants all commonly change platelet counts and generate PF4‑reactive antibodies that are frequently nonpathogenic. The first step in any patient with a falling platelet count after heparin exposure is therefore to estimate the pretest probability of HIT using a structured clinical score (for example, the 4Ts: Thrombocytopenia magnitude, Timing of onset, Thrombosis or other sequelae, and oTher causes). Use of the 4Ts guides immediate management: patients with a low pretest probability rarely have HIT and generally do not require empiric non‑heparin anticoagulation, whereas those with an intermediate or high probability should have heparin discontinued and a non‑heparin anticoagulant started while laboratory testing proceeds. Importantly, in post‑CPB patients the timing element must be applied with caution—an early platelet fall in the first 1–3 postoperative days is common after surgery, but a platelet decline consistent with HIT that begins prior to postoperative day 5 can occur if there was a recent heparin exposure (for example, catheterization or other heparin use within the preceding days to weeks). The case above illustrates this point: a modest intra‑procedural heparin dose a week before surgery explained the unusually early onset of true HIT.

Laboratory testing falls into two complementary categories: immunoassays that detect anti‑PF4/polyanion antibodies and functional platelet activation assays that confirm pathogenicity. Immunoassays are fast and sensitive; functional assays are more specific and establish that antibodies in the patient’s serum actually activate platelets. The most widely used immunoassay is the PF4/polyanion ELISA. IgG‑specific assays are preferred because pathogenic HIT antibodies are almost always IgG — assays that also detect IgM or IgA will increase false‑positive rates, particularly after surgery. Optical density (OD) on ELISA correlates with clinical significance: low OD values (below the laboratory cutoff, often ~0.4) are unlikely to represent pathogenic antibodies; intermediate values (≈0.4–1.0) are equivocal; and higher ODs (≥1.0, and especially >2.0) are strongly associated with platelet‑activating antibodies and with thrombotic risk. In cardiac surgical populations many patients will be ELISA‑positive with low ODs but most of these antibodies are nonpathogenic — thus an isolated positive ELISA after cardiopulmonary bypass should not by itself be taken as diagnostic of HIT without consideration of clinical context and functional testing.

Functional assays demonstrate whether the patient’s antibodies can cross‑link platelet FcγRIIa and trigger activation. The serotonin‑release assay (SRA) is the historical gold standard: washed donor platelets loaded with radiolabeled serotonin are incubated with patient serum and varying concentrations of heparin; serotonin release indicates platelet activation. A classical HIT SRA pattern is strong activation at therapeutic heparin concentrations (commonly at 0.1–0.3 U/mL), with marked inhibition at very high heparin concentrations (for example 100 U/mL) — the high‑heparin inhibition occurs because excess heparin disrupts the PF4‑heparin antigenic complexes. The heparin‑induced platelet aggregation (HIPA) assay or flow cytometric assays of P‑selectin or platelet microaggregation are alternative functional tests available in specialized centers. Because functional assays rely on fresh washed platelets from susceptible donors and specialized lab expertise, turnaround is often delayed and testing must be sent to reference laboratories; therefore empiric treatment decisions are frequently made before a functional result is available.

Autoimmune HIT (aHIT) represents a subgroup of patients whose antibodies acquire the ability to activate platelets independent of added heparin; recognizing the laboratory signature of aHIT is crucial because these patients often have more severe thrombocytopenia and more fulminant thrombotic or consumptive coagulopathy. The hallmark SRA pattern of aHIT is substantial platelet activation even in the absence of heparin (0 U/mL), often accompanied by the typical pattern of activation at therapeutic heparin concentrations and inhibition at supratherapeutic heparin. Thus, a positive SRA at 0 U/mL (strong serotonin release without added heparin) is highly suggestive of autoimmune HIT. In many aHIT sera, platelet activation is still suppressed by high heparin concentrations, reflecting disruption of PF4‑containing immune complexes; however, atypical serologic behaviors can occur. One such atypical feature — illustrated by the present case — is partial resistance to blockade by an anti‑FcγRIIa monoclonal antibody (IV.3). Under usual circumstances the addition of IV.3 to functional assays markedly attenuates or abolishes serotonin release from HIT antibodies because platelet activation is FcγRIIa‑dependent. If inhibition is incomplete, this suggests unusually strong immune complexes or, rarely, alternative platelet‑activating mechanisms; partial IV.3 resistance does not exclude HIT/aHIT but marks an aggressive serologic phenotype and should prompt careful clinical correlation and consideration of adjunct testing.

When the SRA is negative or indeterminate despite a high clinical probability or a strongly positive ELISA, several strategies can increase diagnostic sensitivity for aHIT. Laboratories experienced in HIT testing can perform PF4‑enhanced functional assays (for example, adding excess PF4 to the SRA or using a PF4‑dependent P‑selectin expression assay), which unmask antibodies that preferentially bind PF4 rather than PF4/heparin complexes. PF4‑modified assays and platelet‑activation tests measured by flow cytometry tend to be more sensitive for detecting heparin‑independent activity. Repeat testing on a separate specimen or testing sera obtained serially over days may also reveal evolving patterns — aHIT serology can change with time, sometimes shifting to a more typical heparin‑dependent pattern as antibodies mature or wane, as occurred in the case described. If a local laboratory reports an “indeterminate” SRA, request a detailed report and consider referral of the specimen to a reference laboratory where these enhanced or alternative functional assays can be performed, and where IV.3 blockade experiments can be explicitly reported.

Practical laboratory and clinical considerations should be emphasized. Communicate to the testing laboratory the clinical context (recent CPB, recent heparin exposures including catheterization heparin, timing of platelet fall, current anticoagulants), because these details inform assay selection and interpretation. Be aware that rapid immunoassays (lateral flow or particle‑gel) and polyspecific ELISAs may give frequent false‑positive results after cardiac surgery; when possible, use an IgG‑specific ELISA and interpret OD magnitude rather than a binary positive/negative result. Because functional assays can have limited availability and require technical expertise, management often proceeds empirically — for patients with intermediate or high pretest probability, heparin should be stopped and an appropriate non‑heparin anticoagulant begun while testing occurs.

Finally, use ancillary coagulation and hematologic tests both as severity markers and as gauges of therapeutic response. In severe HIT or aHIT, consumptive coagulopathy may develop: falling fibrinogen, rising D‑dimer, development of schistocytes, and progressive anemia are warning signals of ongoing platelet and fibrin consumption and increased thrombotic burden. These trends, together with serial platelet counts, help assess whether anticoagulation and interventions such as high‑dose intravenous immunoglobulin are controlling the immune thrombotic process. In summary, a thoughtful diagnostic approach to suspected HIT/aHIT combines careful clinical assessment (4Ts and documentation of recent heparin exposures), judicious use of IgG‑specific immunoassays with attention to OD levels, and functional confirmation with SRA or alternative platelet activation assays — and, in cases of discordant or indeterminate results, referral for PF4‑enhanced testing and serial specimen analysis is often necessary to resolve diagnosis and guide therapy.

## Management of HIT and Autoimmune HIT After Cardiac Surgery

Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) and its more aggressive variant, autoimmune HIT (aHIT), are clinical emergencies in the postoperative cardiac surgery patient because they combine severe thrombocytopenia with a high thrombotic risk. Management has two simultaneous goals: (1) immediately stop the immunologic stimulus (all heparin exposures) and provide effective non‑heparin anticoagulation to prevent and treat thrombosis, and (2) modulate or interrupt pathogenic antibody‑mediated platelet activation when disease is severe or refractory. The case presented illustrates several practical principles: heparin exposure shortly before surgery can produce an unusually early HIT onset; aHIT may be heparin‑independent and persistently platelet‑activating; and aggressive combined anticoagulant and immunomodulatory therapy (argatroban, fondaparinux and high‑dose IVIG in this case) is often required.

Immediate steps
- Discontinue all heparin products immediately (unfractionated heparin, low‑molecular‑weight heparin), including heparin flushes, coated catheters and heparin‑containing lines. This is the first, indispensable action.
- Do not wait for confirmatory testing to anticoagulate: initiate a non‑heparin anticoagulant on clinical suspicion of HIT/aHIT, even when platelet counts are very low or when arterial thrombosis has already occurred. The risk of new or extending thrombosis is high and outweighs the bleeding risk in most cases.
- Obtain rapid testing: a PF4/polyanion immunoassay is sensitive and useful for screening; a functional platelet activation assay (eg, serotonin‑release assay, SRA) is more specific and helps characterize heparin dependence and aHIT behaviour. However, management should begin based on clinical probability and the screening result.

Choice of non‑heparin anticoagulant and practical considerations
There are several effective non‑heparin agents; selection is individualized by renal and hepatic function, clinical setting (ICU, impending surgery/CPB, need for rapid reversibility), and local experience.

- Argatroban (direct thrombin inhibitor)
  - Advantages: hepatic clearance (preferable in renal impairment), short half‑life, rapid offset after stopping the infusion—useful in patients at bleeding risk or who may need urgent procedures.
  - Dosing and monitoring: typical initial infusion in otherwise well patients is 2 mcg/kg/min with a target aPTT 1.5–3.0× baseline (commonly 55–85 s). In critically ill or elderly patients, lower initial rates (eg, 0.5–1.0 mcg/kg/min) are often used and then titrated to aPTT; the case patient received 0.75 mcg/kg/min given recent stroke and severe thrombocytopenia. Reduce dose in hepatic impairment.
  - Limitations: aPTT may be affected by other coagulopathies; argatroban artificially elevates INR complicating warfarin transition.

- Bivalirudin (direct thrombin inhibitor)
  - Advantages: mixed enzymatic and renal clearance, short half‑life, and predictable anticoagulant effect. It is commonly used for percutaneous coronary intervention and is an option for CPB anticoagulation in patients who cannot receive heparin.
  - Dosing and monitoring: institutional protocols vary; non‑CPB systemic anticoagulation usually relies on aPTT targets similar to argatroban. For CPB, dosing strategies and ACT targets differ and require coordination between cardiac surgery, perfusion and hematology teams.
  - Limitations: dose adjustment with renal impairment; no specific antidote.

- Fondaparinux (synthetic indirect factor Xa inhibitor)
  - Advantages: once‑daily subcutaneous dosing facilitates outpatient care; low immunogenicity and increasing evidence for off‑label use in HIT/aHIT.
  - Dosing: treatment doses (weight‑based) rather than prophylactic doses must be used when treating HIT. Typical therapeutic dosing (commonly applied in DVT/PE) is 5 mg daily for ≤50 kg, 7.5 mg daily for 50–100 kg, and 10 mg daily for >100 kg; many centers use 7.5–10 mg in HIT. The case used 10 mg daily in a patient who required therapeutic anticoagulation.
  - Caveats: renally cleared—avoid if CrCl <30 mL/min. Because fondaparinux acts via antithrombin (AT), low AT levels may impair its efficacy; some teams give AT concentrate (eg, 1000 U) prior to starting fondaparinux if AT is low, as was done in the case. Evidence for this practice is limited and should be individualized.
  - Monitoring: routine anti‑Xa assays are not standardized for fondaparinux; clinical and laboratory assessment (see below) guides efficacy.

Other agents and considerations
- Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) such as apixaban and rivaroxaban have growing evidence as post‑acute transition agents for HIT and may be preferred for outpatient long‑term anticoagulation once platelet counts have recovered and the patient is clinically stable.
- Intravenous direct thrombin inhibitors are preferable in unstable patients or when rapid reversal is desirable; fondaparinux is favored for stability and convenience when renal function allows.
- Choice must consider concomitant organ dysfunction, recent procedures and institutional familiarity.

Immunomodulation for aHIT and refractory HIT
aHIT is characterized by antibodies that can activate platelets even in the absence of heparin and often produces more severe and persistent thrombocytopenia and hypercoagulability. When there is evidence of severe disease (profound thrombocytopenia, clinical or laboratory evidence of consumptive coagulopathy, new thrombotic events despite anticoagulation, or a heparin‑independent, strongly platelet‑activating SRA), add immunomodulatory therapy promptly.

- Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG)
  - Rationale: high‑dose IVIG can block Fcγ receptor–mediated platelet activation by competing with pathogenic IgG for Fcγ receptor binding and by other immunomodulatory mechanisms.
  - Dosing: commonly 1 g/kg daily for 2 days (total 2 g/kg) or 0.4 g/kg daily for 5 days (total 2 g/kg); many reports of aHIT use 1–2 g/kg total, often given as 1 g/kg ×2 days. In refractory cases repeat dosing may be considered.
  - Monitoring and risks: IVIG frequently produces a brisk rise in platelet count within days in many aHIT patients; failure to rise suggests more aggressive or persistent antibody activity. IVIG carries risks—thrombotic complications, fluid overload, headache, aseptic meningitis, renal dysfunction and hemolysis (especially in non‑O blood groups). Be mindful that IVIG can cause transient false‑negative functional platelet assays.
  - The case demonstrates that some aHIT patients may only partially respond to IVIG, requiring continued attention to anticoagulant choice and duration.

- Plasma exchange (plasmapheresis)
  - Consider for life‑ or limb‑threatening thrombosis or when IVIG is contraindicated or ineffective. Plasmapheresis can remove pathogenic antibodies and is sometimes used preoperatively to reduce antibody burden before urgent cardiac surgery.

- Corticosteroids and other immunosuppressives
  - Not routinely recommended as primary therapy but may be considered in select refractory cases in consultation with hematology.

Monitoring response and laboratory markers
Clinical decisions should be guided by serial laboratory and bedside assessments rather than a single test.

- Platelet count: the most useful and readily available marker. Expect stabilization and then a sustained upward trend over days once pathogenic heparin exposure is stopped and effective therapy instituted. In typical HIT platelets usually begin to rise within 4–10 days; in aHIT they may take longer and rise more slowly.
- Markers of coagulation activation/consumption:
  - D‑dimer: falling values generally indicate control of thrombosis/hypercoagulability.
  - Fibrinogen: stabilization or recovery is reassuring; falling fibrinogen suggests ongoing consumption and uncontrolled thrombosis.
  - A peripheral smear for schistocytes and hemolysis markers (haptoglobin, LDH) can detect microangiopathic hemolysis from consumptive coagulopathy.
- Functional assays (SRA): serial testing can document waning platelet‑activating antibodies. Persistent strong platelet activation (including heparin‑independent activation) suggests ongoing risk and longer need for non‑heparin anticoagulation and possibly additional immunotherapy.
- Clinical surveillance for new thrombi: serial examinations and appropriate imaging when symptoms warrant.

Transfusion and procedural considerations
- Platelet transfusions are strongly discouraged in active HIT/aHIT because they can provide substrate for further thrombin generation and worsen thrombosis; transfuse only for life‑threatening bleeding or immediately prior to/during critical invasive procedures when benefits outweigh risks, and in such cases use the minimum required and continue therapeutic anticoagulation when possible.
- For urgent invasive procedures or surgery requiring anticoagulation strategies, coordinate closely with hematology and surgery. If platelets must be raised for surgery, consider IVIG or plasmapheresis rather than platelet transfusion where appropriate.

Transition to oral anticoagulation and duration of therapy
- Warfarin:
  - Avoid initiating warfarin during the acute phase of HIT because rapid decline of protein C can create a prothrombotic state and cause venous limb gangrene or skin necrosis.
  - If warfarin is to be used, only start after platelet recovery (many experts recommend ≥150 × 10^9/L) and after the patient has been therapeutic on a non‑heparin parenteral anticoagulant for at least 5 days. Overlap the parenteral anticoagulant with warfarin until INR is therapeutic for at least 24 hours.
  - Be aware that argatroban increases INR; specialized transition guidance is required.
- Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs):
  - DOACs (for example, apixaban) are increasingly used to complete HIT treatment or for long‑term anticoagulation once the patient is stable and platelet counts have recovered. They avoid the complexity of warfarin bridging and are a reasonable option in many patients, including the case patient who resumed apixaban.
- Duration of therapy:
  - If HIT is complicated by thrombosis (HITT), treat for at least 3 months from the thrombotic event.
  - For isolated HIT without documented thrombosis, 4–6 weeks of therapeutic anticoagulation is typical.
  - If a separate indication for long‑term anticoagulation exists (eg, atrial fibrillation), duration should be dictated by that indication after careful assessment of bleeding risk and ongoing antibody activity.

Special situations: future cardiac surgery and CPB
- Re‑exposure to heparin for future procedures requires careful planning. Avoid heparin if possible; if surgery is urgent and heparin cannot be avoided, consult hematology and surgery.
- Options when heparin would ordinarily be used:
  - Delay elective surgery until pathogenic antibodies are no longer detectable by functional testing (timing varies; antibodies often wane over weeks to months but immunoassays may remain positive longer).
  - Use alternative anticoagulation strategies for CPB (eg, bivalirudin protocols tailored to CPB) if feasible and supported by institutional experience.
  - If heparin must be used despite recent HIT/aHIT, consider plasmapheresis or high‑dose IVIG preoperatively to reduce antibody burden—these are specialized strategies that require multidisciplinary planning.
- Document HIT history clearly in the medical record and patient wallet card and communicate to all future treating teams.

When to escalate therapy
- Escalate or change therapy when there is evidence of ongoing consumptive coagulopathy or new thrombosis despite an appropriate dose of non‑heparin anticoagulant. In the presented case falling fibrinogen, rising D‑dimer, and new schistocytes prompted reconsideration of anticoagulant choice and the addition of IVIG and later a switch to fondaparinux plus AT concentrate.
- Consider repeat or adjunctive immunomodulation (additional IVIG dosing, plasmapheresis) for refractory aHIT.

Clinical pearls from the case
- Early postoperative onset of thrombocytopenia (before POD5) after cardiac surgery should prompt review of all recent perioperative heparin exposures, including catheterization procedures days earlier.
- In aHIT, the SRA can show heparin‑independent platelet activation; incomplete inhibition by FcγR‑blocking agents (eg, IV.3) may indicate unusually strong or Fc‑independent mechanisms and should not be used to exclude the diagnosis.
- Therapeutic fondaparinux may be a practical and effective option for prolonged anticoagulation, but be sure to use therapeutic, not prophylactic, doses and account for renal function and antithrombin status.
- IVIG is a valuable adjunct in aHIT but may not always produce an immediate or complete platelet response; persistent laboratory evidence of hypercoagulability mandates ongoing reassessment and possible escalation.

Summary
Management of HIT and aHIT after cardiac surgery requires prompt cessation of heparin exposures, immediate institution of an appropriate non‑heparin anticoagulant, and close clinical and laboratory monitoring. In aHIT or refractory cases, add immunomodulatory therapy—most commonly high‑dose IVIG—and consider plasmapheresis when necessary. Transition to oral anticoagulation should be delayed until platelet recovery and guided by the presence or absence of thrombosis and by the patient’s longer‑term anticoagulation needs. Multidisciplinary coordination between hematology, cardiology/cardiac surgery, and critical care is essential for complex or high‑risk patients.

## Prognosis, Follow-up, and Clinical Pearls

Prognosis in HIT is determined less by the platelet nadir per se than by recognition of the diagnosis, prompt removal of all heparin exposures, institution of an appropriate non‑heparin anticoagulant, and control of the antibody‑mediated hypercoagulable process. In most typical HIT cases the platelet count begins to rise within days of stopping heparin and initiating alternative anticoagulation; recovery to the normal range commonly occurs over 1–3 weeks. Autoimmune HIT (aHIT), however, behaves differently. As illustrated by this patient, platelet recovery can be markedly delayed despite correct therapy and aggressive immunomodulation: his count fell to 13 × 10^9/L and required many weeks to recover (platelets normalizing by POD62). aHIT antibodies often have heparin‑independent platelet‑activating properties and may persist for weeks to months. Functional assay profiles can therefore evolve over time as antibody concentration and functional potency fall — for example, sera that initially activate platelets in the absence of heparin may later show primarily heparin‑dependent activity or lose activity altogether as titers decline. Clinically, the period of highest thrombotic risk parallels the presence of pathogenic, platelet‑activating antibodies and the phase of thrombocytopenia; this risk generally diminishes as platelets recover and laboratory markers of coagulation normalize.

Antibody kinetics and laboratory follow‑up require realistic expectations. Anti‑PF4/polyanion IgG ELISAs often remain positive for many weeks; functional assays such as the serotonin‑release assay (SRA) may likewise stay positive but can change in pattern (from heparin‑independent to heparin‑dependent or to negative) as in this case. Persistently positive laboratory tests do not necessarily mean ongoing clinical risk once the platelet count has recovered and no ongoing thrombosis is apparent, but they warrant caution. Therefore follow‑up testing is most useful when interpreted alongside the clinical picture (platelet trend, evidence of thrombosis, fibrinogen and D‑dimer trends). In practice, serial daily platelet counts are obtained while a patient is in the acute phase; once improving, monitoring may be spaced to every few days and then weekly until stable.

Thrombosis risk and duration of anticoagulation. Patients with HIT complicated by thrombosis (HIT‑TS) require therapeutic, non‑heparin anticoagulation. Anticoagulation should generally be continued until platelet recovery and for a total minimum period appropriate for venous or arterial thromboembolism (commonly at least 3 months for documented thrombosis). In isolated HIT without thrombosis, anticoagulation with a non‑heparin agent is continued for at least 4 weeks after diagnosis. Choice of agent should be individualized: parenteral direct thrombin inhibitors (argatroban, bivalirudin), parenteral factor Xa inhibitors where available, and fondaparinux (off‑label) have been used. Important practical points illustrated by the case include that prophylactic‑dose fondaparinux (2.5 mg daily) is inadequate to treat suspected HIT; therapeutic dosing (eg, 7.5–10 mg daily depending on weight and renal function) is required when fondaparinux is chosen. If fondaparinux (an antithrombin‑dependent indirect Xa inhibitor) is used and antithrombin levels are low, consider antithrombin supplementation because low AT can blunt the drug’s efficacy — the treating team administered AT concentrate before starting fondaparinux when AT activity was mildly reduced. When balancing anticoagulation after an arterial event such as ischemic stroke, collaboration with neurology is essential; decisions about timing, agent choice, and intensity must weigh bleeding risk (particularly intracranial) against the high ongoing thrombotic risk of HIT. In this case the team preferred argatroban initially because of its short half‑life and rapid titratability in the event of bleeding.

Immunomodulation and refractory disease. High‑dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) is an effective adjunct in many aHIT cases and often produces a brisk platelet response by blocking FcγRIIa‑mediated platelet activation. However, as seen here, not all patients have a prompt or complete response to IVIG — some have unusually potent autoantibodies that are only partially inhibited. For refractory cases, additional interventions such as plasma exchange, repeat or higher IVIG doses, or other immunosuppressive strategies have been used in selected patients; these measures are best undertaken in consultation with hematology and centres experienced in immune cytopenias.

Interpreting laboratory assays and the limits of testing. Functional assays remain the most specific tests for platelet‑activating anti‑PF4 antibodies, but atypical patterns occur in aHIT. Strong platelet activation in the absence of heparin (0 U/mL) is characteristic of aHIT; conversely, loss of activation at very high heparin concentrations (>100 U/mL) is typical of HIT biology. Importantly, incomplete inhibition of patient sera by FcγRIIa‑blocking reagents (eg, monoclonal antibody IV.3) does not exclude aHIT — partial resistance can reflect highly avid antibodies or mixed populations of antibodies with differing mechanisms. When assay results are indeterminate or discordant with the clinical picture, consult an experienced platelet immunology laboratory for additional testing (PF4‑enhanced functional assays, concentration‑response curves, serial testing). Do not rely on a single laboratory result in isolation when the clinical probability is high.

Prevention of recurrence and perioperative planning. All heparin products (unfractionated heparin and LMWH) should be avoided after a confirmed HIT episode. Heparin exposures include not only therapeutic and prophylactic doses but also heparin flushes, heparin‑bonded catheters, and heparinized coatings on extracorporeal circuits. Document the reaction prominently in the medical record, provide the patient with written information and allergy alerts, and ensure the primary care team, cardiology, anesthesia and surgical teams are informed. For patients who may later require cardiac surgery or other procedures for which heparin is the usual intraoperative anticoagulant, early multidisciplinary planning is essential. Options to permit future cardiopulmonary bypass in patients with a history of HIT include delaying elective procedures until antibodies have cleared (typically several months), using bivalirudin for CPB, or in urgent situations considering plasmapheresis or IVIG to reduce antibody levels, followed by careful intraoperative management; each strategy has risks and benefits that must be weighed on a case‑by‑case basis.

Patient education, documentation and long‑term follow‑up. Patients should be educated that HIT is an immune‑mediated sensitivity to heparin and that re‑exposure to any heparin formulation can precipitate recurrent HIT, often more rapidly and more severely. They should carry documentation (wallet card, allergy bracelet) that lists heparin as an allergy, and instructions to notify care providers and to seek hematology input if heparin is being considered. After hospital discharge, follow‑up should include review with hematology to determine duration of anticoagulation, timing of laboratory reassessment (platelet counts until stable, and functional/ELISA testing only as indicated), and planning for any future procedures. For patients who had HIT with thrombosis like this case, a minimum of three months of anticoagulation is typical; for isolated HIT, four weeks is the usual minimum.

Clinical outcome expectations. With rapid recognition, cessation of heparin, initiation of an appropriate non‑heparin anticoagulant, and use of immunomodulatory therapy when indicated, most patients ultimately have favorable outcomes. Arterial events such as ischemic stroke are uncommon but recognized complications of aHIT and require close coordination between hematology and relevant specialty services to optimize recovery while minimizing bleeding. The case presented exemplifies both the potential severity of aHIT — profound thrombocytopenia, consumptive coagulopathy, arterial thrombosis — and the potential for complete recovery with attentive, multidisciplinary care. Awareness of the atypical laboratory signatures of aHIT (early onset after recent heparin exposure, heparin‑independent platelet activation, and unusual resistance to FcγR blockade) will help avoid diagnostic delays that can adversely affect prognosis.
