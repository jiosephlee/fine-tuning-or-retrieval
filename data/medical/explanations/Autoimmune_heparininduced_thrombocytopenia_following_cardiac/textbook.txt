# Autoimmune Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia After Cardiac Surgery: Early-Onset Severe Thrombocytopenia With Stroke

## The Case as a Teaching Vehicle

An elderly man underwent elective cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) using unfractionated heparin (UFH). His immediate postoperative platelet count fell modestly, as expected, but then declined steadily to 50 × 10⁹/L by postoperative day (POD) 5 and to a nadir of 13 × 10⁹/L, accompanied by an ischemic parietal stroke. A PF4/polyanion enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was strongly positive (optical density [OD] 2.2). The serotonin-release assay (SRA) showed strong platelet activation not only at low heparin concentrations but also in the absence of heparin, and inhibition with an FcγRIIa-blocking antibody (IV.3) was only partial—an atypical pattern. Heparin was stopped and alternative anticoagulation started (argatroban, then fondaparinux), with adjunctive high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg). The course was marked by laboratory features of consumptive coagulopathy before gradual recovery of platelet count and full neurologic recovery. Subsequent testing confirmed antibodies with heparin-independent platelet-activating properties, consistent with autoimmune HIT (aHIT), with eventual waning to a more conventional heparin-dependent SRA profile.

This case illustrates key themes in the evaluation and management of thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery, the pathobiology of HIT across a spectrum that includes autoimmune variants, and the interpretation of complex laboratory data to guide high-stakes therapy.

## Clinical Approach to Thrombocytopenia After Cardiac Surgery

Thrombocytopenia is common after CPB. An approach anchored in time course, magnitude, associated clinical events, and pretest probability (such as the 4Ts score) helps separate expected postoperative changes from dangerous immune thrombocytopenias.

- Expected postoperative pattern: CPB causes immediate hemodilution, platelet activation/consumption, and splenic sequestration. Platelets commonly fall 30–50% on POD1–2 and recover by POD3–4. Inflammatory acute phase responses drive high fibrinogen. Mild anemia without schistocytosis is expected.

- Red flags: A second fall in platelets after initial recovery (a biphasic pattern), a greater-than-50% decline from peak postoperative value, timing consistent with immune sensitization (classically POD5–10), new thrombosis, skin necrosis at heparin injection sites, or global hypercoagulability (rising D-dimer, falling fibrinogen) should raise suspicion for HIT.

- Confounders and alternatives: Sepsis, cardiogenic shock, intra-aortic balloon pump, massive transfusion, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), post-transfusion purpura, thrombotic microangiopathy (TTP/HUS), and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia must be considered. Peripheral smear, hemolysis markers, coagulation studies, cultures, and medication review are essential.

- When early HIT occurs: “Rapid-onset” HIT can present within 24–48 hours of heparin re-exposure in a patient primed by recent heparin (within ∼100 days). After cardiac surgery, recent preoperative UFH exposure (e.g., cardiac catheterization) compresses the timeline so that the postoperative platelet fall begins unusually early, as in this case. Early-onset severe thrombocytopenia with thrombosis after CPB should prompt aggressive evaluation for HIT, even before POD5, if there was preoperative heparin.

Applying the 4Ts (thrombocytopenia, timing, thrombosis, other causes) remains helpful: a >50% fall to nadir <20–50 × 10⁹/L, rapid timing after recent heparin, documented stroke, and lack of strong alternative explanations yields an intermediate-to-high pretest probability, warranting immediate cessation of heparin and initiation of non-heparin anticoagulation while awaiting laboratory results.

## Pathophysiology: From Classic HIT to Autoimmune HIT

Classic HIT is an antibody-mediated, paradoxically prothrombotic condition triggered by heparin exposure. Autoimmune HIT lies on the same immunologic spectrum but is distinguished by heparin-independent platelet activation and often a more severe clinical phenotype.

- The PF4–heparin antigen: Platelet factor 4 (PF4) is a cationic chemokine stored in platelet α-granules and released upon platelet activation. PF4 forms tetramers that avidly bind polyanions, including heparin. At certain stoichiometries, PF4–heparin complexes expose neoepitopes that elicit high-affinity IgG (often IgG1/IgG3) anti-PF4/polyanion antibodies. Complex size and antigen density are crucial; both under- and over-saturation with heparin reduce antigen formation.

- Effector mechanisms: Immune complexes bind FcγRIIa (CD32a) on platelets, triggering powerful activation with further PF4 release, granule secretion, and microparticle generation. Monocyte Fcγ receptors are engaged, inducing tissue factor expression and amplifying thrombin generation. Endothelial cells may be activated via Fcγ receptors and complement. Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) and complement deposition further propagate thrombosis. The net effect is a consumptive, hypercoagulable state with striking thrombin generation and a propensity for both venous and arterial thrombosis.

- Why classic HIT is heparin-dependent: In typical HIT, pathogenic antibodies require polyanion-bound PF4 to form the immunogenic complex and to cluster FcγRIIa. High concentrations of heparin disrupt antigen formation, explaining the diagnostic phenomenon of “excess heparin inhibition” in platelet activation assays.

- Autoimmune HIT (aHIT): aHIT is defined by antibodies that can activate platelets even in the absence of heparin. Antigen formation is driven by PF4 alone or PF4 bound to endogenous polyanions (e.g., glycosaminoglycans, extracellular DNA/NETs, polyphosphates) without exogenous heparin. Phenotypes include delayed-onset HIT (thrombocytopenia and thrombosis emerging days after heparin cessation), persistent HIT with protracted thrombocytopenia, and “spontaneous HIT” after certain surgeries or infections with minimal or no heparin exposure. VITT (vaccine-induced immune thrombotic thrombocytopenia) is an extreme PF4-autoimmune syndrome sharing mechanistic features but with distinct triggers.

- Clinical severity in aHIT: Heparin-independent activation sustains platelet consumption and thrombin generation despite heparin withdrawal. Marked thrombocytopenia, laboratory features mimicking DIC (falling fibrinogen, elevated D-dimer), and refractoriness to standard therapies are more common. Many aHIT antibodies show strong activation in PF4-enhanced functional assays and often respond briskly to IVIg, which blocks Fcγ receptors and modulates pathogenic Fc interactions.

- Partial resistance to FcγRIIa blockade: In classic assays, IV.3 monoclonal antibody blocks FcγRIIa and suppresses antibody-induced platelet activation. The present case showed only partial inhibition, suggesting unusually potent immune complexes, involvement of additional Fcγ receptors or co-receptors, or exceptionally high-affinity antibodies that sustain activation despite partial receptor blockade. This highlights mechanistic heterogeneity within aHIT and cautions against overreliance on any single in vitro criterion.

## Diagnostic Workup and Interpretation

Diagnosis integrates clinical probability and laboratory confirmation, with attention to the peculiarities of the post-cardiac surgery setting and the aHIT spectrum.

- Initial labs and bedside assessment: Full blood count and trend; peripheral smear (to exclude schistocytosis and platelet clumping); hemolysis markers (LDH, haptoglobin, bilirubin); coagulation tests (PT/INR, aPTT), fibrinogen, and D-dimer. Early postoperative inflammatory states elevate fibrinogen; a falling fibrinogen in a thrombocytopenic postoperative patient is worrisome for consumptive coagulopathy. Screen for infection, hemodynamic instability, and drug exposures.

- Immunoassays:
  - PF4/polyanion ELISA: Highly sensitive; OD magnitude correlates with likelihood of functional positivity and clinical HIT. OD >1.0 is strongly associated with pathogenic antibodies; OD 2.2 in this case supports true HIT. IgG-specific ELISAs increase specificity relative to polyspecific assays.
  - Rapid immunoassays (particle gel immunoassay, lateral flow): Useful for triage but less sensitive after cardiac surgery and less informative than ELISA OD.

- Functional platelet activation assays:
  - Serotonin-release assay (SRA) and heparin-induced platelet activation (HIPA) test are reference methods. Hallmarks of classic HIT in SRA include strong activation at low heparin (0.1–0.3 U/mL) with suppression at high heparin (100 U/mL) and inhibition by FcγRIIa blockade (IV.3).
  - aHIT clues: Platelet activation at 0 U/mL heparin; robust activation enhanced by exogenous PF4; and sometimes incomplete inhibition by IV.3. PF4-enhanced SRA or PF4-dependent P-selectin expression assay (PEA) increase sensitivity for aHIT. When an SRA is reported “indeterminate,” request full reaction curves (0 heparin, low heparin, high heparin, ±Fc blockade) and consider PF4-enhanced assays.

- Imaging for thrombosis: Directed by symptoms/signs. HIT is highly prothrombotic; screen actively for limb DVT, catheter-associated thrombosis, graft thrombosis, and, in this case, neuroimaging for delirium uncovered a cortical infarct.

- Distinguishing mimics: TTP (severe ADAMTS13 deficiency) produces microangiopathic hemolysis with many schistocytes, severe hemolysis, and neurologic findings; coagulation panel often normal. DIC shows prolonged PT/aPTT, low fibrinogen, and diffuse bleeding. Drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia typically produces abrupt severe thrombocytopenia but without the distinctive heparin exposure pattern or PF4-dependent antibodies.

In this patient, the strong ELISA, SRA activation at 0 heparin and low heparin with high-heparin suppression, and partial Fc blockade, together with clinical severity and recent preoperative heparin exposure, established aHIT despite the atypical SRA comment.

## Management Principles

HIT is a hematologic emergency where under-treatment risks thrombosis and organ loss, and over-treatment risks bleeding. aHIT adds urgency because heparin withdrawal alone does not turn off pathogenic platelet activation.

- Stop all heparin immediately: Discontinue UFH, LMWH, heparin catheter flushes, and heparin-coated devices. LMWH is contraindicated due to cross-reactivity.

- Initiate therapeutic-intensity non-heparin anticoagulation without delay:
  - Parenteral agents: Argatroban (hepatic clearance; aPTT target 1.5–3× baseline, often 50–80 s), bivalirudin (renal and proteolytic clearance; monitor with aPTT or ACT intraoperatively), or fondaparinux (factor Xa inhibitor; once-daily SQ; anti-Xa monitoring not standardized). Choice is individualized: argatroban is attractive in the immediate post-stroke period due to short half-life; fondaparinux is convenient for ward care but relies on antithrombin (AT).
  - Antithrombin supplementation: In severe HIT with consumptive coagulopathy or low AT levels, AT concentrate can optimize fondaparinux efficacy, as in this case.
  - Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs): Rivaroxaban or apixaban are increasingly used once the patient is clinically stable and platelet count is recovering; they are suitable for subacute and long-term management.

- Adjunctive immunomodulation in aHIT:
  - IVIg (1–2 g/kg total dose over 1–2 days) can rapidly blunt FcγR-mediated platelet activation, facilitating platelet count recovery and reducing thrombin generation. It is especially useful in aHIT phenotypes with heparin-independent activation, delayed-onset, or refractory thrombocytopenia. Thrombotic risk exists; weigh benefits and monitor closely. In this case, an initially blunted response likely reflected unusually potent antibodies and ongoing hypercoagulability.
  - Therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) can remove pathogenic IgG and is considered for life-threatening thrombosis, need for urgent cardiac surgery, or refractory aHIT. It can be combined with IVIg.

- Monitor coagulation and inflammatory markers: Serial platelet counts, fibrinogen, and D-dimer provide a bedside index of thrombin generation and consumptive coagulopathy. Worsening D-dimer and falling fibrinogen despite anticoagulation suggest inadequate control and may prompt therapy adjustment.

- Duration and transitions:
  - HIT with thrombosis (HITT): Treat for at least 3 months.
  - Isolated HIT (no thrombosis): Treat for 4–6 weeks because thrombosis risk persists after platelet recovery.
  - Transition to oral therapy: DOACs are preferred in many settings. If warfarin is chosen, delay initiation until platelets ≥150 × 10⁹/L and patient is therapeutically anticoagulated with a non-heparin agent; overlap for ≥5 days and until INR therapeutic. Argatroban elevates INR; chromogenic factor X levels can guide safe transition.

- Platelet transfusion: Avoid routine transfusion in HIT due to concern for fueling thrombosis; reserve for clinically significant bleeding or urgent invasive procedures, ideally after non-heparin anticoagulation has begun.

- Managing stroke in HIT: Arterial thrombosis is a recognized HIT complication. Balancing hemorrhagic transformation risk with uncontrolled thrombosis is nuanced; short–half-life agents (argatroban, bivalirudin) provide titratability. Early involvement of neurology and hematology is prudent.

- Re-exposure to heparin: Avoid heparin for at least 100 days; many clinicians favor permanent avoidance after aHIT due to risk and available alternatives. If cardiac surgery is urgently needed in a patient with circulating antibodies, consider bivalirudin for CPB, often with perioperative IVIg or TPE in severe aHIT. In exceptional circumstances where heparin must be used (e.g., emergent CPB, no alternative), specialized protocols with TPE and IVIg mitigate risk but do not eliminate it.

In the present case, argatroban plus IVIg was an appropriate early strategy; rising D-dimer, falling fibrinogen, and schistocytes—signs of ongoing consumptive coagulopathy—prompted a switch to fondaparinux with AT supplementation, followed by gradual platelet recovery and full neurologic recovery. Long-term anticoagulation with apixaban was reasonable for atrial fibrillation and HIT convalescence.

## Special Considerations in Post-Cardiac Surgery HIT

- Incidence and risk modification: HIT after cardiac surgery occurs in approximately 0.5–1.0% of patients, influenced by postoperative prophylaxis choice (UFH > LMWH). Preoperative heparin exposure primes for early-onset HIT after CPB.

- Recognizing the biphasic pattern: A modest fall on POD1–2 with recovery by POD3–4 is typical. A new or progressive fall thereafter (especially with thrombosis) should trigger HIT evaluation. Early-onset HIT before POD5 is unusual without recent heparin exposure; a careful preoperative history (e.g., heparin during cardiac catheterization) is essential.

- Laboratory subtleties: The acute phase response raises fibrinogen; a declining fibrinogen in the first 1–2 postoperative weeks alongside thrombocytopenia and elevated D-dimer suggests HIT-associated consumptive coagulopathy rather than uncomplicated postoperative inflammation.

- Functional testing in aHIT: Activation at 0 U/mL heparin and only partial FcγR blockade are not “false positives”; they reflect the biology of aHIT. PF4-enhanced assays increase detection when conventional SRA is equivocal. High-heparin suppression (100 U/mL) remains a helpful internal control.

## Clinical Pearls and Take-Home Points

- After CPB, a second platelet fall or a progressive decline beyond POD3–4, particularly with thrombosis, argues for HIT over expected postoperative thrombocytopenia.

- Recent (within ∼100 days) preoperative heparin exposure can compress the timeline and produce early-onset HIT after cardiac surgery. Ask specifically about heparin given during diagnostic procedures.

- Strong ELISA OD values (>1.0) correlate with pathogenicity. A strongly positive ELISA plus compatible clinical picture warrants treatment while awaiting functional assay results.

- aHIT is characterized by heparin-independent platelet activation, more severe thrombocytopenia, DIC-like laboratory features, and a higher likelihood of refractory disease. IVIg can be decisive; consider early in severe cases.

- In functional assays, platelet activation at 0 U/mL heparin and partial resistance to FcγRIIa blockade are clues to aHIT. High-heparin suppression supports PF4-mediated specificity even when other features are atypical.

- Stop all forms of heparin immediately and start therapeutic-intensity non-heparin anticoagulation. Choose agents with consideration of organ function, bleeding risk, and logistics; argatroban’s short half-life is advantageous after stroke.

- Monitor fibrinogen and D-dimer to gauge control of HIT-associated hypercoagulability; a falling fibrinogen and rising D-dimer despite therapy suggest the need to adjust treatment.

- Avoid warfarin initiation in acute HIT; transition to oral agents only after platelet recovery. DOACs offer a practical and effective long-term option.

- Heparin re-exposure is best avoided after aHIT. If unavoidable, involve experts and consider TPE and/or IVIg, with bivalirudin preferred for CPB.

This case underscores that in the postoperative cardiac surgery setting, early recognition of HIT—particularly the autoimmune variant—depends on integrating careful clinical observation with nuanced laboratory interpretation. Prompt heparin cessation, appropriate alternative anticoagulation, and timely immunomodulation can reverse a life-threatening hypercoagulable state and lead to full recovery.
