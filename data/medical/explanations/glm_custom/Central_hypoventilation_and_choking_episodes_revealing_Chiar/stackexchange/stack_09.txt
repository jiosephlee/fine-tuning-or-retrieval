Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

### [Clinical Pearls] What is the cardinal headache symptom of Chiari malformation type I, and how did this patient's headache pattern differ from the classic presentation in terms of location and provoking factors?

The cardinal headache symptom of Chiari malformation type I is **paroxysmal occipital headache precipitated by Valsalva manoeuvres**, present in over 80% of symptomatic cases. The pathophysiology relates to the developmentally small posterior fossa causing volumetric mismatch between neural structures and available space; when intrathoracic or intra-abdominal pressure rises during Valsalva, CSF outflow obstruction at the crowded foramen magnum transiently increases intracranial pressure, producing the characteristic suboccipital pain.

This patient's headache differed from the classic presentation in two important ways. First, regarding **location**, while she did have occipital component, her pain was also prominently localized to the **vertex**, described as constant, pressure-like, and concentrated in a coin-sized area — a pattern that led multiple physicians to diagnose nummular headache. Second, regarding **provoking factors**, her headaches were **not exacerbated by coughing, straining, or other Valsalva manoeuvres**. Instead, the most distinctive provoking feature was **marked cutaneous allodynia at the vertex**, where even the weight of her hair triggered discomfort — leading her to cut her hair short, which reduced baseline pain from 6/10 to 3/10. This allodynia pattern may reflect occipital neuralgia associated with CMI, a phenomenon that meets diagnostic criteria requiring "dysesthesia and/or allodynia apparent during innocuous stimulation of the scalp and/or hair," though the authors note this association remains understudied and incompletely characterized.

This case underscores that CMI can present with headache patterns that deviate substantially from the textbook Valsalva-provoked occipital model, contributing to the 14-year diagnostic delay.
