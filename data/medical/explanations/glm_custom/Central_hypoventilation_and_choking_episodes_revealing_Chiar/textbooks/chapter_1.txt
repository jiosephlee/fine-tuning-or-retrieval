Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

Chapter 1: ## Case Presentation and Clinical History

Understanding a complex case like this one requires careful attention to the chronology of symptoms, because the sequence in which findings appear often reveals more about the underlying pathology than any single symptom in isolation. This patient's story unfolded over 14 years, and the delays in diagnosis stemmed partly from the fact that her symptoms were evaluated in isolation by different specialists rather than as components of a single neurological process.

### Initial Presentation: Motor Vehicle Accident and Headache Onset

The patient was a woman in her early 40s when she presented to a family medicine clinic, but her symptoms had begun 14 years earlier, shortly after a minor motor vehicle accident that involved a brief loss of consciousness. The immediate aftermath of the trauma was marked by the onset of persistent occipital and vertex headaches. These headaches were described as constant and pressure-like, often localized to a coin-sized area at the vertex of the skull. Notably, the headaches were not exacerbated by coughing, straining, or other Valsalva maneuvers. This absence of Valsalva exacerbation is clinically important because it represents a departure from the classic headache pattern of Chiari malformation type I (CMI), in which paroxysmal occipital headaches precipitated by Valsalva maneuvers occur in over 80% of symptomatic patients. The absence of this hallmark feature likely contributed to the diagnostic delay, as clinicians repeatedly evaluated her for primary headache syndromes rather than structural posterior fossa pathology. Several physicians diagnosed her with nummular headache, a distinct primary headache disorder characterized by localized, coin-shaped head pain. She underwent multiple local scalp injections that provided only transient relief, which is consistent with the variable and often short-lived response of nummular headache to local anesthetic blocks. The temporal relationship between the accident and symptom onset raises the important question of whether her CMI was congenital and merely unmasked by trauma, or whether the trauma itself induced tonsillar herniation. Both mechanisms are recognized in contemporary etiological classifications of Chiari malformations, which distinguish primary (congenital) forms from secondary or acquired forms resulting from head trauma or increased intracranial pressure.

### Evolution of Choking Episodes

Seven years before her eventual neurological evaluation, the patient developed a new and distressing symptom: recurrent choking episodes that occurred unpredictably during both meals and sleep. The episodes affected solids and liquids equally, a feature that carries significant diagnostic weight. Dysphagia that affects all consistencies uniformly suggests a neurogenic or muscular origin rather than a mechanical obstruction, which typically affects solids first and progresses to liquids only as the lumen narrows further. The episodes were described as sudden and distressing, often interrupting meals or awakening her from sleep with violent coughing and gasping, though they never resulted in complete airway obstruction. Nocturnal episodes occurred three to four times per week, occasionally twice in a single night. This frequency and pattern are consistent with lower cranial nerve dysfunction, which is reported in 15% to 26% of CMI patients, typically presenting with oropharyngeal dysfunction such as palatal weakness, tongue atrophy, or cricopharyngeal achalasia. The brainstem and lower cranial nerve nuclei (nucleus ambiguus, glossopharyngeal and vagal nuclei) reside at the cranio-cervical junction, precisely where tonsillar descent exerts its compressive effect. Over the course of 7 years, the patient underwent extensive evaluation for these choking episodes, including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies, all of which were reported as normal. The persistence of symptoms despite normal endoscopic findings is itself an important diagnostic clue: when structural laryngeal and esophageal evaluations are repeatedly normal in the setting of consistent dysphagia, the evaluation should shift toward neurological causes.

### Allodynia at the Vertex

One of the most distinctive features of this patient's history was marked cutaneous allodynia localized to the vertex of the scalp, where even the weight of her hair triggered discomfort. Allodynia—the perception of pain from a normally non-painful stimulus—is a phenomenon involving sensitization of nociceptive pathways and can occur with peripheral nerve injury, central sensitization, or both. The patient ultimately discovered that cutting her hair short reduced her baseline pain from 6/10 to 3/10, a pragmatic but telling self-intervention. This finding is difficult to categorize within established headache classifications. Occipital neuralgia, which occurs in association with CMI, can cause scalp hypersensitivity, and diagnostic criteria include dysesthesia or allodynia apparent during innocuous stimulation of the scalp or hair. However, the pain was localized to the vertex rather than the occipital region, which is atypical for occipital neuralgia. An alternative possibility is the recently described "burning vertex syndrome," which presents with scalp pain and cutaneous allodynia that does not fit neatly into established primary headache classifications. The hypothesized mechanism involves small fiber pathways originating in the scalp. In this patient, the vertex symptoms could represent occipital neuralgia related to CMI, burning vertex syndrome occurring coincidentally, or an entirely separate primary headache disorder. The decision to cut her hair short, while therapeutically effective, conflicted with cultural expectations and contributed to emotional distress—a reminder that clinical symptoms can carry consequences extending well beyond their physiological impact.

### Incidental Discovery of Oxygen Desaturation

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient began self-monitoring her oxygen saturation using a home pulse oximeter. Over several months, she repeatedly documented asymptomatic episodes of oxygen saturation dropping to the 70% to 80% range. These episodes were often unrecognized until she checked her device, meaning she experienced no respiratory distress, no dyspnea, and no subjective sensation of air hunger during the desaturation events. This finding is remarkable and clinically alarming. Oxygen saturations in the 70% to 80% range correspond to arterial partial pressures of oxygen well below 50 mm Hg, a level that under normal physiological conditions should trigger potent ventilatory drive and an intense sensation of breathlessness mediated through peripheral chemoreceptors (carotid bodies) and central chemoreceptors medullary respiratory centers, with afferent signals ascending through brainstem pathways to the cortex. The absence of dyspnea during such profound hypoxemia indicates a failure of this respiratory control circuit, and specifically points toward brainstem pathology interrupting the afferent neural pathways that must ascend through the brainstem to reach the cortex. In CMI, cerebellar tonsillar descent compresses medullary structures, which can disrupt both the chemoreceptor response and the ascending interoceptive signals that produce the conscious sensation of air hunger. Multiple emergency evaluations at this stage attributed her symptoms to anxiety, which represents a common diagnostic pitfall when patients present with subjective symptoms that are not immediately supported by routine objective testing.

### The Pivotal Airport Episode

A turning point in the patient's diagnostic journey occurred at an international airport. While waiting for her flight, she experienced acute dizziness and nausea. On-site medical personnel documented an oxygen saturation of 78%, and she was denied boarding. Administration of supplemental oxygen led to complete symptom resolution within minutes. This episode is instructive for several reasons. First, it provided an objective, externally documented measurement of profound hypoxemia during an acute symptomatic event, eliminating the possibility that the home pulse oximetry readings were artifactual. Second, the rapid resolution with supplemental oxygen confirms that the symptoms were directly related to hypoxemia rather than to anxiety or another cause. Third, the fact that the episode occurred during what was likely a period of reduced vigilance—waiting passively in an airport—illustrates a key feature of CMI-related respiratory dysfunction: the selective impairment of automatic breathing while voluntary (cortical) breathing remains intact. During moments of distraction, reduced arousal, or drowsiness, the brainstem-driven automatic respiratory control system should maintain ventilation, but in this patient, that system was compromised. The subsequent pulmonary evaluation, including chest imaging and pulmonary function testing, was entirely normal, effectively excluding intrinsic pulmonary disease and redirecting the investigation toward central causes.

### Prior Evaluations and Misattribution to Anxiety

The patient's diagnostic history illustrates the fragmented nature of care that patients with rare or atypical presentations often experience. Over the course of 14 years, she underwent three flexible laryngoscopies, two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies, multiple local scalp injections for headache, multiple emergency department visits for hypoxia, and a full pulmonary evaluation. All of these were either normal or non-diagnostic. The repeated attribution of her symptoms to anxiety is a recurrent theme worth examining. Anxiety can produce hyperventilation, choking sensations, and subjective dyspnea, but it does not produce asymptomatic oxygen saturations in the 70% to 80% range, nor does it produce dysphagia affecting all consistencies with normal endoscopic findings. The misattribution likely occurred because each specialist evaluated the symptom within their own domain: the otolaryngologist saw normal laryngoscopy and concluded there was no structural cause; the gastroenterologist saw normal endoscopy and excluded reflux; the emergency physician saw a normal-appearing patient with a pulse oximeter reading and considered anxiety. What was missing throughout was a unifying neurological diagnosis that could account for all symptoms simultaneously.

### Social and Emotional Impact

The social consequences of the patient's symptoms were substantial. She avoided restaurants and social gatherings due to fear of public choking episodes, leading to progressive social withdrawal and emotional distress. This is a clinically significant feature because the psychosocial burden of chronic undiagnosed illness can amplify symptom severity and complicate the clinical picture, potentially reinforcing the misattribution to psychiatric causes. The decision to cut her hair short, while providing meaningful pain relief, further contributed to distress because it conflicted with cultural expectations. She was a divorced mother of one teenage son and worked as a government administrator, details that contextualize the functional impact of her illness on her daily life and underscore the importance of arriving at a correct diagnosis not only for disease management but also for restoration of quality of life.

### Physical Examination Findings

At the time of neurological consultation, the patient's vital signs were notable for a blood pressure of 118/76 mm Hg, heart rate of 72 beats per minute, respiratory rate of 14 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation of 96% on room air. The normal oxygen saturation at rest is consistent with the intermittent nature of her desaturation episodes, which occurred during sleep or distraction rather than during active wakeful examination. The neurological examination findings, as documented in the case report, are presented in the investigations section with the corresponding imaging. The pertinent point for the clinical history is that the examination did not reveal overt cranial nerve palsies, motor deficits, or sensory findings that would have immediately localized the lesion, which is consistent with the often subtle neurological presentation of CMI in adults, where lower cranial nerve involvement may be functional rather than detectable on routine bedside examination.

### Past Medical and Family History, and the Significance of the Old CT Scan

The patient's past medical history was notable only for the motor vehicle accident that had prompted a non-contrast CT scan of the head 14 years earlier. This scan had been reported at the time as normal. Family history was negative for neurological conditions, which makes a purely genetic congenital explanation less straightforward, although CMI is typically sporadic and does not follow a simple Mendelian pattern. The retrieval and re-review of the 14-year-old CT scan proved pivotal. Although CT is less sensitive than MRI for evaluating the posterior fossa due to bone artifact, the scan clearly showed a crowded foramen magnum with cerebellar tissue extending to the level of the opisthion and loss of cerebrospinal fluid space at the cranio-cervical junction. These findings were suggestive of CMI but had been overlooked at the time of initial interpretation. This oversight carries an important lesson: the posterior fossa and craniocervical junction are anatomical regions that require deliberate attention on every head imaging study, particularly in trauma patients who may have subtle structural abnormalities. The missed finding on the CT scan resulted in a 14-year delay in diagnosis, during which the patient's symptoms progressed from headaches to bulbar dysfunction to respiratory compromise. The subsequent MRI of the brain and cervical spine, which confirmed 11 mm cerebellar tonsillar descent, was prompted not by the original CT findings but by the eventual clinical recognition that a unifying neurological diagnosis was needed.
