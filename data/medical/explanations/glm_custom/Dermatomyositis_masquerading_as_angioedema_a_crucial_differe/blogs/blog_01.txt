Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

## When Angioedema Isn't: A Framework for Subacute Neck Swelling That Defies Classic Pathways

A man in his 40s walks into your emergency department with three weeks of progressive neck swelling. The first thought? Ludwig's angina or a deep neck space infection. CT neck shows diffuse soft tissue edema involving the strap muscles and extending into the retropharyngeal space — but no abscess, no source. ENT scopes him, finds nothing structural. The baton passes to immunology: "suspected idiopathic angioedema." But here's the catch — he's not on an ACE inhibitor, not on a DPP-4 inhibitor, has no family history, and his complement levels and C1 esterase function come back completely normal. So what do you do when the angioedema pathway doesn't close? This case, published by Chan and colleagues, is a masterclass in what happens when you stop anchoring on the obvious and start looking at what's actually in front of you.

## The Case: What They Almost Missed

This previously well, non-smoking patient presented with subacute neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness in his hip flexors with mild myalgia — a symptom that was initially treated as a footnote rather than a clue. He was afebrile, vitally stable, with a CRP of 55.6 mg/L and a white cell count of 9.1. After ENT excluded a surgical cause, the immunology team took a careful second look. What they found was hiding in plain sight: subtle periungual erythema and nailfold microhaemorrhages on capillaroscopy, generalized facial erythema over the nasolabial folds, a rash on his right lateral thigh, and hip flexor power reduced to 4/5. No heliotrope rash. No Gottron's papules. The classic dermatomyositis textbook features were absent.

The turning point was a creatine kinase level. It came back at 16,184 U/L. That single test reframed the entire presentation.

## Beyond Histamine and Bradykinin: When the Complement Pathway Is Normal

Angioedema is one of those diagnoses that can become a diagnostic wastebasket. When you see neck or facial swelling and the airway is intact, it's easy to default to "idiopathic angioedema" and move on. But this case forces us to think differently. The standard angioedema workup — medication history (ACE inhibitors, DPP-4 inhibitors, NSAIDs), family history, complement C3/C4, C1 esterase inhibitor function and quantification — was unrevealing. No trigger. No pathway. No diagnosis. That should be your signal to stop and reconsider, not to label it idiopathic and discharge.

The key question is: what else causes subacute neck soft tissue swelling? The answer is broader than most of us carry in our default workup. This case reminds us to consider inflammatory myopathies, particularly dermatomyositis, where subcutaneous edema — sometimes called pseudoangioedema — is rare but well described. In a cohort of 121 dermatomyositis patients, only 8 had angioedema-like swelling. In SAE1-positive dermatomyositis specifically, a literature review of 10 cases found only three described edema, and just one mentioned throat-region swelling. It is uncommon, but it happens, and when it does, the stakes are high. This patient had severe pharyngeal dysphagia with post-swallow penetration to the vocal cords on barium swallow, aspiration pneumonitis on CT chest, and active myositis across multiple muscle groups on MRI and PET imaging. Delayed diagnosis could have meant airway compromise or ongoing aspiration.

## The Subtle Signs That Change Everything

What makes this case instructive is how easily the cutaneous and muscular clues could have been overlooked. The patient lacked the hallmark heliotrope eruption and Gottron's papules that most clinicians associate with dermatomyositis. Instead, he had periungual erythema, a positive Holster sign (erythema on the lateral thighs), nailfold microhaemorrhages with avascular areas on capillaroscopy, and proximal weakness. These are the signs that sit in the footnotes of the textbook chapter — present enough to clinch the diagnosis if you look for them, subtle enough to miss entirely if you don't.

The laboratory pattern was equally telling. Beyond the dramatically elevated CK, the patient had elevated transaminases (ALT 143, AST 396) and LDH of 692, reflecting muscle-derived enzyme leakage rather than hepatic dysfunction. His ANA was positive at 1:1280 with a speckled pattern. The myositis antibody panel came back strongly positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies, and the muscle biopsy showed perifascicular necrosis, myophagocytosis, and complement C5b-9 deposition in perifascicular endomysial capillaries — the histological signature of dermatomyositis.

## A Practical Framework for Subacute Neck Swelling

When faced with neck or facial swelling that doesn't fit classic angioedema, a systematic approach is essential. First, confirm the swelling is truly edematous and not infectious — CT imaging and ENT evaluation remain critical first steps. If infection is excluded and the angioedema workup is negative (no offending medications, no family history, normal complement and C1 esterase), the next question should be: are there systemic features? Ask about and examine for proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, dyspnea, skin changes in sun-exposed and extensor surfaces, nailfold abnormalities, and constitutional symptoms. If any of these are present, send a CK. It's cheap, it's fast, and in this case it was the single most important diagnostic test.

The broader differential for non-angioedema neck swelling includes superior vena cava syndrome, orofacial granulomatosis, hypocomplementemic urticarial vasculitis, deep neck space infections that may not be immediately obvious on initial imaging, cellulitis or erysipelas, and drug hypersensitivity reactions such as DRESS. Each of these has a distinct clinical signature, but they share one thing: they won't be found if you stop at "idiopathic angioedema."

## Treatment and the Importance of Speed

This patient received pulse-dose intravenous methylprednisolone (1000 mg daily for 3 days), high-dose prednisone taper, loading IVIG at 2 g/kg over 5 days followed by 5 additional monthly cycles, mycophenolate mofetil, and two doses of rituximab. Methotrexate was deliberately avoided due to concerns about interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis given his abnormal pulmonary function tests. He required nasogastric feeding for persistent dysphagia for two months post-discharge. At three weeks, his CK normalized. At three months, neck swelling fully resolved. At seven months, prednisone was discontinued and dysphagia had fully resolved. This was not a mild case — the speed of immunosuppression mattered.

A critical additional consideration: this patient carried multiple malignancy risk factors. Per IMACS guidelines, dermatomyositis itself and dysphagia are high-risk features, while male sex and SAE1 antibody positivity are intermediate-risk features. He underwent CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, PET scan, tumor markers, and was scheduled for gastroscopy and colonoscopy. In SAE1-positive dermatomyositis, malignancy has been reported in approximately 18% of cases. This is not a diagnosis you make and walk away from — ongoing surveillance is part of the package.

## Key Take-Home Points

- When angioedema workup is negative — no trigger medications, no family history, normal complement and C1 esterase — do not label it idiopathic. Broaden the differential.
- Send a creatine kinase level in any patient with unexplained neck or facial swelling plus weakness, myalgia, dysphagia, or skin changes. It may be the single most important test you order.
- Dermatomyositis can present without classic heliotrope rash or Gottron's papules. Look for periungual erythema, nailfold capillary changes, Holster sign, shawl sign, and subtle proximal weakness.
- Pseudoangioedema in dermatomyositis is rare but real. It reflects muscle and soft tissue inflammation, not histamine or bradykinin-mediated pathways, and it requires immunosuppression, not antihistamines or bradykinin blockers.
- Dysphagia in dermatomyositis is high risk — for aspiration, for malnutrition, and for malignancy. Assess swallowing early and aggressively.
- SAE1-positive dermatomyositis carries a malignancy risk of approximately 18%. Screen comprehensively and surveil longitudinally.
- Treat aggressively and early. This patient needed pulse steroids, IVIG, mycophenolate, and rituximab — and still required NG feeding for two months. Delayed treatment risks irreversible muscle damage and aspiration complications.
