Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Differential Diagnosis] How do clinical history, medication review, complement studies (C3, C4, C1 esterase inhibitor function and quantity), and the presence or absence of urticaria help distinguish histaminergic angioedema, hereditary angioedema due to C1 inhibitor deficiency, acquired C1 inhibitor deficiency, and non-mediator-mediated swelling such as the pseudoangioedema of dermatomyositis?

**Distinguishing Histaminergic Angioedema, Hereditary Angioedema, Acquired C1 Inhibitor Deficiency, and Pseudoangioedema**

The evaluation of angioedema begins with three key clinical anchors: the presence or absence of urticaria, the medication history, and the tempo and associated symptoms of the swelling. Together with complement studies, these features narrow the differential into histamine-mediated, bradykinin-mediated, and non-mediator-mediated categories.

**Histaminergic angioedema** is typically accompanied by urticaria and pruritus, reflects mast cell degranulation, and may be triggered by allergens, medications, or physical stimuli. It responds to antihistamines, epinephrine, and corticosteroids. Complement studies — C3, C4, and C1 esterase inhibitor (C1-INH) function and quantity — are characteristically normal. The swelling is episodic, self-limited (usually resolving within 24–48 hours), and lacks a family history of angioedema.

**Hereditary angioedema due to C1-INH deficiency** is bradykinin-mediated and distinguished by the *absence* of urticaria and the *failure* to respond to antihistamines or epinephrine. It is autosomal dominant, so a careful family history is critical. Attacks are typically episodic and may be provoked by trauma, dental procedures, stress, estrogen-containing medications, or ACE inhibitors. Complement studies show a low C4 (often even between attacks), normal or low C3, and reduced C1-INH function and/or quantity. C1q is typically normal.

**Acquired C1-INH deficiency** shares the bradykinin-mediated, non-urticarial phenotype but differs in lacking a family history and presenting later in life, typically in association with B-cell lymphoproliferative disorders or autoantibodies against C1-INH. The distinguishing laboratory feature is a low C1q (in contrast to hereditary angioedema, where C1q is normal), alongside low C4, low C1-INH function and quantity, and usually normal C3.

**Pseudoangioedema of dermatomyositis**, as illustrated by this case, is neither histamine- nor bradykinin-mediated. The swelling arises from inflammation of subcutaneous tissue and muscle rather than from vasoactive mediator release. Several features in this patient pointed away from mediator-mediated angioedema and toward an inflammatory myopathy:

- **Clinical history:** The swelling was subacute and progressive over three weeks, not episodic. He had no personal or family history of angioedema, which argued against hereditary angioedema. The absence of urticaria ruled against histaminergic angioedema.
- **Medication review:** He was not taking ACE inhibitors, DPP-4 inhibitors, NSAIDs, or any other agents known to precipitate bradykinin-mediated or histaminergic angioedema.
- **Complement studies:** C3, C4, and C1-INH function and quantity were all normal, excluding both hereditary and acquired C1-INH deficiency.
- **Associated features:** The key clues were proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, periungual erythema, nailfold microhaemorrhages on capillaroscopy, facial erythema, and an elevated CK of 16,184 U/L — none of which are features of histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema. The neck swelling was caused by myositis and soft-tissue inflammation, as confirmed by MRI, FDG-PET, and muscle biopsy showing perifascicular necrosis and C5b-9 deposition in endomysial capillaries, consistent with dermatomyositis.

In summary, urticaria points toward histaminergic angioedema; its absence shifts the focus toward bradykinin-mediated or non-mediator-mediated causes. A family history and low C4 with reduced C1-INH function point to hereditary angioedema, while low C1q and adult onset without family history suggest acquired C1-INH deficiency. When all complement studies are normal, urticaria is absent, the swelling is progressive rather than episodic, and there are features of systemic inflammation — particularly muscle weakness, dysphagia, and cutaneous signs such as periungual changes — the diagnosis of pseudoangioedema from an inflammatory myopathy such as dermatomyositis should be actively considered. A CK level is a rapid, inexpensive, and decisive test in this setting.
