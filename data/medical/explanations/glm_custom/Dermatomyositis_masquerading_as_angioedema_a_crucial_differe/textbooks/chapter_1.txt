Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and Initial Diagnostic Challenge

The patient, a previously well male in his 40s with no significant medical history, presented to the emergency department with a 3-week history of progressive neck swelling accompanied by proximal muscle weakness in his hip flexors and mild myalgia. He was a lifelong non-smoker and was not taking any medications, notably including a 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitor (statin), the absence of which is an important detail because statins are a well-recognized cause of drug-induced myopathy and muscle enzyme elevation. The insidious onset and subacute progression over three weeks already hinted at an inflammatory or autoimmune process rather than an acute infectious or allergic event, but the prominent neck swelling initially dominated the clinical picture and drew attention away from the muscle symptoms.

On initial assessment, the patient was afebrile, saturating 99% on room air, and not tachypnoeic—features arguing against an acute septic process. His white cell count was within normal limits at 9.1×10⁹/L, though his C-reactive protein (CRP) was elevated at 55.6 mg/L, indicating a measurable inflammatory response. The combination of neck swelling and an elevated CRP understandably raised concern for a deep neck space infection, and the initial working diagnosis was Ludwig's angina, a rapidly spreading cellulitis of the submandibular and sublingual spaces usually of odontogenic origin. A CT scan of the neck was performed to evaluate for abscess or deep space infection. The scan revealed diffuse neck oedema involving the strap muscles and inflammation extending into the retropharyngeal space. Notably, no discrete fluid collection or abscess was identified to account for the swelling, and the patient's lack of fever and only modest inflammatory marker elevation were not fully consistent with a deep neck space infection. Nevertheless, the imaging findings were concerning enough to warrant transfer to a quaternary hospital with ear, nose, and throat (ENT) surgery services for further specialist evaluation.

At the quaternary centre, ENT performed a flexible nasendoscopy to directly visualize the upper aerodigestive tract. The examination found no significant anatomical abnormalities in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid space, effectively excluding structural obstruction or a visible infectious source. However, increased secretions and coughing were noted during the procedure. This finding is clinically relevant in retrospect: in a patient with dysphagia from inflammatory myopathy, pharyngeal muscle dysfunction leads to impaired swallowing and pooling of secretions, which predisposes to micro-aspiration. At the time, however, the coughing was interpreted as a non-specific finding, and because no structural or infectious cause for the neck swelling had been identified, ENT consulted the on-call immunologist for suspected angioedema.

At this point, it is useful to review the mechanisms of angioedema, as understanding them clarifies why this patient's presentation did not fit. Angioedema is characterized by non-pitting, non-dependent swelling of the deep dermal, subcutaneous, or submucosal tissues resulting from increased vascular permeability. Two principal pathophysiological mechanisms are recognized. Histamine-mediated (allergic) angioedema occurs when mast cell degranulation releases histamine and other vasoactive mediators, typically in the setting of allergen exposure, and is usually accompanied by urticaria, pruritus, and a rapid response to antihistamines and epinephrine. Bradykinin-mediated angioedema involves excessive bradykinin activity, which directly increases vascular permeability without involving mast cells, and therefore presents without urticaria or pruritus and does not respond to antihistamines, epinephrine, or corticosteroids. The major bradykinin-mediated subtypes include hereditary angioedema (due to C1 esterase inhibitor deficiency or dysfunction), acquired C1 esterase inhibitor deficiency, and angioedema associated with angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors, which impair bradykinin degradation. When no trigger or complement abnormality is identified, the term "idiopathic angioedema" is used, which itself is a diagnosis of exclusion.

When the immunologist reviewed the patient, several features immediately made the presentation atypical for any form of angioedema. The patient was not taking any medications associated with bradykinin-mediated angioedema, specifically ACE inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs). He had no personal or family history of angioedema episodes, which would be expected in hereditary angioedema, where autosomal dominant transmission typically produces a positive family history and recurrent episodes beginning in childhood or adolescence. The swelling itself was erythematous and non-tender and involved the neck rather than the face, lips, tongue, or larynx—the sites most commonly affected in both histamine- and bradykinin-mediated angioedema. Furthermore, angioedema does not produce muscle weakness, myalgia, or the skin changes that were evident on careful examination. The subacute 3-week progression was also inconsistent with angioedema, which typically develops over minutes to hours.

A thorough physical examination revealed findings that shifted the diagnostic reasoning toward a connective tissue disease. The patient had generalized erythema of the face, including over the nasolabial folds, and an erythematous, swollen, non-tender neck. Subtle nailfold changes were present, with periungual erythema suggestive of capillary abnormalities. A rash was noted on the right lateral thigh, which would later be recognized as a Holster sign, a cutaneous manifestation of dermatomyositis. Proximal muscle power was reduced, with hip flexor strength graded at 4 out of 5. Notably, the two hallmark cutaneous features of dermatomyositis—Gottron's papules over the metacarpophalangeal joints and the heliotrope eruption of the periorbital skin—were absent, which contributed to the initial diagnostic uncertainty and illustrates that the absence of classic findings does not exclude the diagnosis.

Nailfold capillaroscopy, performed during the initial evaluation, provided an important early clue. This non-invasive technique uses magnification to visualize the capillary loops at the nailfold, where characteristic morphological changes occur in several connective tissue diseases, particularly systemic sclerosis and dermatomyositis. In dermatomyositis, the underlying microvasculopathy produces dilated, tortuous capillary loops interspersed with avascular areas where capillaries have been lost, along with microhaemorrhages from compromised capillary wall integrity. This patient's capillaroscopy demonstrated periungual microhaemorrhages and avascular areas, findings consistent with the capillary dropout and microvascular injury that are now understood to be central to the pathophysiology of dermatomyositis. In a patient presenting with otherwise unexplained neck swelling, the presence of these nailfold abnormalities should prompt consideration of a connective tissue disorder and, combined with the proximal weakness and elevated inflammatory markers, makes an inflammatory myopathy a leading diagnostic possibility.

The decisive clinical action was requesting a creatine kinase (CK) level. This test was prompted specifically by the atypical nature of the neck oedema, which was inconsistent with angioedema, combined with the skin and nail changes on examination. CK is an enzyme found predominantly in skeletal and cardiac muscle, and its serum level rises when muscle fibres are damaged, as occurs in inflammatory myopathies, muscular dystrophies, rhabdomyolysis, and certain drug-induced myopathies. The result, available the following day, was markedly elevated at 16,184 U/L—a level far exceeding normal and strongly supporting active muscle destruction. This single result effectively reoriented the entire diagnostic pathway: the neck swelling was not angioedema at all but rather reflected inflammatory oedema of the cervical musculature in the setting of dermatomyositis. Additional laboratory findings supported widespread muscle injury, with elevated transaminases (alanine aminotransferase 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase 396 U/L) and lactate dehydrogenase (692 U/L), all enzymes found in muscle that rise in parallel with CK during myocyte injury. The normal renal function and negative urine myoglobin screen were reassuring, indicating that despite the massive CK elevation, there was no evidence of myoglobinuric acute kidney injury.

In retrospect, the diagnostic journey from Ludwig's angina to idiopathic angioedema and ultimately to dermatomyositis underscores a critical clinical lesson: when a presenting symptom does not conform to the expected pattern for the suspected diagnosis, the clinician must return to the complete history and physical examination for overlooked clues. In this case, the proximal muscle weakness, mild myalgia, facial erythema, thigh rash, periungual changes, and nailfold capillaroscopy abnormalities were all present at the initial evaluation. Their significance was appreciated only when the angioedema hypothesis was scrutinized and found wanting. The elevated CK then provided objective confirmation of muscle injury and crystallized the provisional diagnosis of dermatomyositis, setting the stage for confirmatory antibody testing and muscle biopsy.
