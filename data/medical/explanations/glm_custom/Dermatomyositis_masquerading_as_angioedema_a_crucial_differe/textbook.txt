Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Diagnostic Challenge

The patient, a previously well male in his 40s with no significant medical history, presented to the emergency department with a 3-week history of progressive neck swelling accompanied by proximal muscle weakness in his hip flexors and mild myalgia. He was a lifelong non-smoker and was not taking any medications, notably including a 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitor (statin), the absence of which is an important detail because statins are a well-recognized cause of drug-induced myopathy and muscle enzyme elevation. The insidious onset and subacute progression over three weeks already hinted at an inflammatory or autoimmune process rather than an acute infectious or allergic event, but the prominent neck swelling initially dominated the clinical picture and drew attention away from the muscle symptoms.

On initial assessment, the patient was afebrile, saturating 99% on room air, and not tachypnoeic—features arguing against an acute septic process. His white cell count was within normal limits at 9.1×10⁹/L, though his C-reactive protein (CRP) was elevated at 55.6 mg/L, indicating a measurable inflammatory response. The combination of neck swelling and an elevated CRP understandably raised concern for a deep neck space infection, and the initial working diagnosis was Ludwig's angina, a rapidly spreading cellulitis of the submandibular and sublingual spaces usually of odontogenic origin. A CT scan of the neck was performed to evaluate for abscess or deep space infection. The scan revealed diffuse neck oedema involving the strap muscles and inflammation extending into the retropharyngeal space. Notably, no discrete fluid collection or abscess was identified to account for the swelling, and the patient's lack of fever and only modest inflammatory marker elevation were not fully consistent with a deep neck space infection. Nevertheless, the imaging findings were concerning enough to warrant transfer to a quaternary hospital with ear, nose, and throat (ENT) surgery services for further specialist evaluation.

At the quaternary centre, ENT performed a flexible nasendoscopy to directly visualize the upper aerodigestive tract. The examination found no significant anatomical abnormalities in the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid space, effectively excluding structural obstruction or a visible infectious source. However, increased secretions and coughing were noted during the procedure. This finding is clinically relevant in retrospect: in a patient with dysphagia from inflammatory myopathy, pharyngeal muscle dysfunction leads to impaired swallowing and pooling of secretions, which predisposes to micro-aspiration. At the time, however, the coughing was interpreted as a non-specific finding, and because no structural or infectious cause for the neck swelling had been identified, ENT consulted the on-call immunologist for suspected angioedema.

At this point, it is useful to review the mechanisms of angioedema, as understanding them clarifies why this patient's presentation did not fit. Angioedema is characterized by non-pitting, non-dependent swelling of the deep dermal, subcutaneous, or submucosal tissues resulting from increased vascular permeability. Two principal pathophysiological mechanisms are recognized. Histamine-mediated (allergic) angioedema occurs when mast cell degranulation releases histamine and other vasoactive mediators, typically in the setting of allergen exposure, and is usually accompanied by urticaria, pruritus, and a rapid response to antihistamines and epinephrine. Bradykinin-mediated angioedema involves excessive bradykinin activity, which directly increases vascular permeability without involving mast cells, and therefore presents without urticaria or pruritus and does not respond to antihistamines, epinephrine, or corticosteroids. The major bradykinin-mediated subtypes include hereditary angioedema (due to C1 esterase inhibitor deficiency or dysfunction), acquired C1 esterase inhibitor deficiency, and angioedema associated with angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors, which impair bradykinin degradation. When no trigger or complement abnormality is identified, the term "idiopathic angioedema" is used, which itself is a diagnosis of exclusion.

When the immunologist reviewed the patient, several features immediately made the presentation atypical for any form of angioedema. The patient was not taking any medications associated with bradykinin-mediated angioedema, specifically ACE inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs). He had no personal or family history of angioedema episodes, which would be expected in hereditary angioedema, where autosomal dominant transmission typically produces a positive family history and recurrent episodes beginning in childhood or adolescence. The swelling itself was erythematous and non-tender and involved the neck rather than the face, lips, tongue, or larynx—the sites most commonly affected in both histamine- and bradykinin-mediated angioedema. Furthermore, angioedema does not produce muscle weakness, myalgia, or the skin changes that were evident on careful examination. The subacute 3-week progression was also inconsistent with angioedema, which typically develops over minutes to hours.

A thorough physical examination revealed findings that shifted the diagnostic reasoning toward a connective tissue disease. The patient had generalized erythema of the face, including over the nasolabial folds, and an erythematous, swollen, non-tender neck. Subtle nailfold changes were present, with periungual erythema suggestive of capillary abnormalities. A rash was noted on the right lateral thigh, which would later be recognized as a Holster sign, a cutaneous manifestation of dermatomyositis. Proximal muscle power was reduced, with hip flexor strength graded at 4 out of 5. Notably, the two hallmark cutaneous features of dermatomyositis—Gottron's papules over the metacarpophalangeal joints and the heliotrope eruption of the periorbital skin—were absent, which contributed to the initial diagnostic uncertainty and illustrates that the absence of classic findings does not exclude the diagnosis.

Nailfold capillaroscopy, performed during the initial evaluation, provided an important early clue. This non-invasive technique uses magnification to visualize the capillary loops at the nailfold, where characteristic morphological changes occur in several connective tissue diseases, particularly systemic sclerosis and dermatomyositis. In dermatomyositis, the underlying microvasculopathy produces dilated, tortuous capillary loops interspersed with avascular areas where capillaries have been lost, along with microhaemorrhages from compromised capillary wall integrity. This patient's capillaroscopy demonstrated periungual microhaemorrhages and avascular areas, findings consistent with the capillary dropout and microvascular injury that are now understood to be central to the pathophysiology of dermatomyositis. In a patient presenting with otherwise unexplained neck swelling, the presence of these nailfold abnormalities should prompt consideration of a connective tissue disorder and, combined with the proximal weakness and elevated inflammatory markers, makes an inflammatory myopathy a leading diagnostic possibility.

The decisive clinical action was requesting a creatine kinase (CK) level. This test was prompted specifically by the atypical nature of the neck oedema, which was inconsistent with angioedema, combined with the skin and nail changes on examination. CK is an enzyme found predominantly in skeletal and cardiac muscle, and its serum level rises when muscle fibres are damaged, as occurs in inflammatory myopathies, muscular dystrophies, rhabdomyolysis, and certain drug-induced myopathies. The result, available the following day, was markedly elevated at 16,184 U/L—a level far exceeding normal and strongly supporting active muscle destruction. This single result effectively reoriented the entire diagnostic pathway: the neck swelling was not angioedema at all but rather reflected inflammatory oedema of the cervical musculature in the setting of dermatomyositis. Additional laboratory findings supported widespread muscle injury, with elevated transaminases (alanine aminotransferase 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase 396 U/L) and lactate dehydrogenase (692 U/L), all enzymes found in muscle that rise in parallel with CK during myocyte injury. The normal renal function and negative urine myoglobin screen were reassuring, indicating that despite the massive CK elevation, there was no evidence of myoglobinuric acute kidney injury.

In retrospect, the diagnostic journey from Ludwig's angina to idiopathic angioedema and ultimately to dermatomyositis underscores a critical clinical lesson: when a presenting symptom does not conform to the expected pattern for the suspected diagnosis, the clinician must return to the complete history and physical examination for overlooked clues. In this case, the proximal muscle weakness, mild myalgia, facial erythema, thigh rash, periungual changes, and nailfold capillaroscopy abnormalities were all present at the initial evaluation. Their significance was appreciated only when the angioedema hypothesis was scrutinized and found wanting. The elevated CK then provided objective confirmation of muscle injury and crystallized the provisional diagnosis of dermatomyositis, setting the stage for confirmatory antibody testing and muscle biopsy.

## Differential Diagnosis and Diagnostic Reasoning

The diagnostic challenge in this case began with a deceptively straightforward complaint: three weeks of progressive neck swelling. In the emergency department setting, subacute neck swelling accompanied by an elevated CRP of 55.6 mg/L immediately raises concern for infectious etiologies, and the initial clinical impression of Ludwig's angina was a reasonable starting point. Ludwig's angina is a rapidly spreading cellulitis of the submandibular, sublingual, and submental spaces, typically arising from dental infection, and it classically produces firm, brawny induration of the floor of the mouth with potential airway compromise. Deep neck space infections—retropharyngeal or parapharyngeal abscesses—similarly present with neck swelling, pain, fever, and often elevated inflammatory markers. Cellulitis or erysipelas of the neck and bacterial sialadenitis (infection of the submandibular or parotid glands) were also viable infectious considerations. However, several features argued against infection: the patient was afebrile, his white cell count was normal at 9.1×10⁹/L, the swelling was non-tender, and the CT neck demonstrated diffuse edema involving strap muscles and retropharyngeal inflammation rather than a discrete abscess or gas-forming infection. Flexible nasendoscopy by ENT revealed no anatomical obstruction or purulence, and no source of infection was identified, effectively excluding a deep neck space infection as the cause.

When infectious causes were excluded, the diagnostic focus shifted to angioedema. Angioedema is characterized by episodic, non-pitting swelling of the subcutaneous or submucosal tissues, most commonly affecting the face, lips, tongue, and larynx. The pathophysiology is divided into histamine-mediated (allergic, often associated with urticaria and pruritus) and bradykinin-mediated (non-allergic) mechanisms. Hereditary angioedema results from C1 esterase inhibitor deficiency or dysfunction, leading to unregulated bradykinin production, and is inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern. Acquired angioedema involves similar complement dysregulation but is associated with B-cell lymphoproliferative disorders or autoimmune disease. Drug-induced angioedema is most classically linked to angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors, which potentiate bradykinin, and less commonly to dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibitors or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs). Idiopathic angioedema is a diagnosis of exclusion applied when no allergic, hereditary, or drug trigger is identified. In this case, the immunology consultation systematically evaluated these possibilities. The patient denied use of ACE inhibitors, DPP-4 inhibitors, or NSAIDs, and had no personal or family history of angioedema. Critically, his complement studies—including C3, C4, and C1 esterase function and quantification—were all normal, ruling out hereditary and acquired angioedema due to C1 inhibitor deficiency. The clinical profile was also atypical: angioedema usually produces episodic swelling that resolves over hours to days, whereas this patient's swelling was progressive over three weeks and accompanied by proximal muscle weakness and skin changes—features not explained by any angioedema subtype.

The differential diagnosis was further broadened to include several less common entities. Orofacial granulomatosis, a granulomatous inflammation of the oral and facial soft tissues that can cause persistent lip and facial swelling, was considered. Hypocomplementemic urticarial vasculitis, characterized by urticaria-like lesions, complement consumption, and systemic involvement, can cause facial swelling but is typically accompanied by urticarial rash and low complement levels—neither of which were present. Superior vena cava syndrome, which produces facial and neck swelling due to venous obstruction, was considered but was not supported by the imaging findings. Neuromuscular causes such as myasthenia gravis were entertained because of the patient's dysphagia and muscle weakness, though the distribution and degree of weakness, along with the markedly elevated muscle enzymes, did not fit a disorder of the neuromuscular junction. Drug hypersensitivity reactions, particularly drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS), and acute contact dermatitis were also in the differential given the facial erythema, but the absence of eosinophilia, the deeply elevated CK, and the specific pattern of cutaneous findings steered the reasoning away from these diagnoses.

The turning point in this case was the decision to check a creatine kinase (CK) level. This single test fundamentally redirected the diagnostic trajectory. The request was prompted by the atypical nature of the neck swelling—progressive rather than episodic, involving strap muscles rather than subcutaneous tissue alone—and by the recognition of subtle but meaningful cutaneous and muscular signs. The CK returned at 16,184 U/L, a level far exceeding any value attributable to angioedema, infection, or drug reaction, and immediately signaled ongoing skeletal muscle injury. This was not a borderline elevation; it was a magnitude consistent with active inflammatory myopathy.

Interpreting this CK elevation in context with the other laboratory abnormalities solidified the diagnostic picture. The patient's AST was elevated at 396 U/L, ALT at 143 U/L, and LDH at 692 U/L. While these transaminases are commonly associated with liver injury, both AST and ALT are also present in skeletal muscle, and in the setting of a massively elevated CK, these enzyme elevations reflect muscle breakdown rather than hepatocellular damage. LDH, a nonspecific marker of tissue injury, was similarly consistent with myocyte necrosis. The normal renal function and negative urine myoglobin screen were reassuring, indicating that despite the extent of muscle injury, there was no evidence of rhabdomyolysis-induced acute kidney injury—a clinically important distinction that influenced the urgency and intensity of immunosuppressive treatment rather than volume-based rhabdomyolysis management.

The cutaneous findings, though subtle, were the second critical piece of evidence. On careful physical examination, the patient had periungual erythema with nailfold microhaemorrhages and avascular areas on capillaroscopy, generalized facial erythema involving the nasolabial folds, and a rash on the right lateral thigh consistent with a Holster sign. He lacked the classic Gottron's papules and heliotrope eruption that are the most pathognomonic cutaneous features of dermatomyositis, which initially made the diagnosis less obvious. However, periungual erythema and nailfold capillary changes are well-recognized manifestations of dermatomyositis, reflecting the microvascular pathology that underlies the disease. The Holster sign—erythematous rash on the lateral thighs—is another characteristic but less commonly emphasized cutaneous feature. When these findings were considered alongside proximal hip flexor weakness rated 4/5 and the markedly elevated CK, a coherent diagnostic picture of inflammatory myopathy emerged.

The concept of pseudoangioedema is central to understanding how dermatomyositis can masquerade as angioedema. Unlike true angioedema, which is mediated by vasoactive mediators—histamine in allergic reactions or bradykinin in hereditary and acquired forms—pseudoangioedema in dermatomyositis arises from inflammation of skeletal muscle and surrounding soft tissues. In this patient, the CT neck demonstrated edema involving the strap muscles with inflammation extending to the retropharyngeal space, and the FDG-PET scan later confirmed diffuse abnormal uptake in the head and neck musculature. The neck swelling was therefore a direct consequence of myositis affecting the cervical and strap muscles, not a result of vasodilation and vascular leak from histamine or bradykinin. This mechanistic distinction has important clinical implications: pseudoangioedema will not respond to antihistamines, bradykinin receptor antagonists, or C1 esterase inhibitor replacement, and its treatment requires immunosuppression targeting the underlying myopathy.

A key lesson from this case is that dysphagia and muscle weakness may be subtle or overlooked when prominent neck swelling dominates the clinical picture. The patient's dysphagia was not the chief complaint and was not initially emphasized; the ENT examination noted increased secretions and coughing, which in retrospect reflected impaired swallowing from pharyngeal muscle involvement. Only when the immunology team performed a thorough systematic examination did the combination of proximal weakness, subtle skin changes, and dysphagia coalesce into a pattern recognizable as dermatomyositis. This case illustrates that when neck swelling does not conform to the expected clinical course or treatment response of angioedema, clinicians must actively search for alternative explanations, examine for cutaneous and muscular signs of connective tissue disease, and obtain a CK level early in the evaluation.

## Pathophysiology of Dermatomyositis and Anti-SAE1 Antibody Disease

Dermatomyositis is an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy, a group of acquired autoimmune disorders characterised by immune-mediated injury to skeletal muscle. It has an estimated incidence of approximately 10 cases per million population and follows a bimodal age distribution, with one peak in children aged 5–14 years (juvenile dermatomyositis) and a second in adults aged 45–64 years. The patient in this case—a male in his 40s—falls squarely within the adult-onset peak. Although dermatomyositis principally affects skin and skeletal muscle, it is a systemic disease that can involve the gastrointestinal tract (particularly through dysphagia), joints, lungs (interstitial lung disease), and heart. Understanding the diversity of organ involvement is essential, because this patient's presenting feature—neck swelling—was initially attributed to a localised process rather than a systemic autoimmune one.

The hallmark immunopathology of dermatomyositis is a complement-mediated microvasculopathy. The earliest observable event is deposition of complement component C5b-9 (the membrane attack complex) on endomysial capillaries, which triggers capillary injury, reduced capillary density, and downstream ischaemic and inflammatory changes in muscle. This process is distinctly different from the immunopathology of polymyositis or immune-mediated necrotising myopathy, where the primary target is the muscle fibre itself via CD8+ T-cell cytotoxicity or autoantibody-mediated necrosis. In dermatomyositis, the vasculopathic process produces a characteristic perifascicular pattern of muscle fibre injury—necrosis, regeneration, and atrophy concentrated at the periphery of muscle fascicles, where the capillary supply is most vulnerable. The importance of this pattern is directly illustrated by this patient's muscle biopsy, which showed foci of necrotic, vacuolated, and regenerative myofibres in a perifascicular distribution, along with scattered perimysial inflammation. Crucially, immunohistochemistry demonstrated C5b-9 deposition in perifascicular endomysial capillaries—a finding the pathologist identified as typical of dermatomyositis. This biopsy result was central to confirming the diagnosis, which had been clinically suspected after the elevated creatine kinase (CK) and identification of cutaneous signs.

A second key immunological feature is a prominent type I interferon (IFN) signature. In dermatomyositis, there is upregulation of type I IFN-inducible genes in both muscle and peripheral blood, reflecting activation of innate antiviral signalling pathways. The precise trigger for this aberrant IFN activity is not fully established, but it contributes to several clinical features of the disease. The skin is exquisitely sensitive to type I IFN signalling, which helps explain why cutaneous manifestations are so prominent and often precede overt muscle weakness. It also provides a mechanistic basis for why dermatomyositis rashes can be refractory and why even skin findings that appear subtle—such as the periungual erythema, facial erythema, and Holster sign identified in this patient on careful examination—can carry significant diagnostic weight.

Myositis-specific antibodies (MSAs) are central to modern classification and phenotyping of dermatomyositis. These antibodies are directed against intracellular antigens involved in transcription, translation, and post-translational modification, and each is associated with a distinct clinical syndrome. The presence of a particular MSA is generally mutually exclusive of others and helps predict patterns of organ involvement, malignancy risk, and prognosis. In this case, a 16-antigen myositis panel was strongly positive for anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies, with a signal intensity of 173 (normal <35), while other MSAs including anti-Mi-2α, anti-TIF1-γ, anti-Jo-1, and anti-OJ were negative. This result was pivotal in confirming the diagnosis and shaping expectations regarding associated complications and malignancy surveillance.

Anti-SAE1 antibodies are directed against the small ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, a protein structurally similar to ubiquitin that participates in post-translational modification of target proteins through a process called sumoylation. Sumoylation regulates diverse cellular processes including transcription, DNA repair, and protein stability. How autoimmunity against SAE1 produces the clinical syndrome of dermatomyositis is not fully understood, but the association is now well characterised. In a large literature review of 215 patients with anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis, cutaneous disease was typically prominent, with features such as heliotrope rash, Gottron's papules, V-neck sign, and shawl sign. Dysphagia was reported in approximately 46% and interstitial lung disease in 43%, while malignancy occurred in 18%. These figures directly informed the approach to this patient: his severe pharyngeal dysphagia was consistent with the known anti-SAE1 phenotype, and his categorisation as high-risk for malignancy—with adult-onset dermatomyositis and dysphagia as high-risk factors, and male sex and SAE1 antibody positivity as intermediate-risk factors—prompted comprehensive screening including CT imaging, tumour markers, FDG-PET, and planned upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy.

The characteristic cutaneous manifestations of dermatomyositis result from the same vasculopathic and interferon-driven processes that affect muscle. Gottron's papules (erythematous, scaly papules over the metacarpophalangeal and interphalangeal joints), heliotrope eruption (violaceous discolouration of the periorbital skin), shawl sign (erythema over the posterior shoulders and neck), V-neck sign, and Holster sign (erythema on the lateral thighs) are well-established diagnostic features. Periungual changes—including periungual erythema, nailfold telangiectasias, and microhaemorrhages visible on capillaroscopy—reflect the same microvascular injury that characterises muscle pathology. In this patient, Gottron's papules and a heliotrope eruption were absent, and it was only through careful physical examination that subtle but diagnostically crucial signs were identified: periungual erythema, a rash on the right lateral thigh (consistent with a Holster sign), generalised facial erythema, and proximal nailfold microhaemorrhages with avascular areas on capillaroscopy. Their subtlety underscores the diagnostic challenge: these findings could easily be missed when the presenting complaint points toward an ENT or immunological process.

The neck swelling in this patient deserves particular attention because it is uncommon and diagnostically treacherous. True angioedema is caused by vascular leakage mediated by either histamine (allergic or mast-cell driven) or bradykinin (hereditary or acquired, often related to C1 esterase inhibitor deficiency). Workup for these mechanisms was unrevealing in this patient: C3 and C4 were normal, and C1 esterase function and quantification were within the normal range. The swelling instead represented what has been termed pseudoangioedema—subcutaneous tissue oedema arising not from vascular permeability but from the intense muscle inflammation of myositis itself. The CT scan of the neck at presentation showed diffuse oedema involving the strap muscles, with inflammation extending to the retropharyngeal space, and the FDG-PET scan later demonstrated marked FDG uptake in head and neck muscle groups. The mechanism is therefore local inflammatory infiltration of cervical musculature producing soft tissue swelling that mimics angioedema clinically.

Pseudoangioedema is rare in dermatomyositis. In a cohort study of 121 patients, only 8 (6.6%) had angioedema, and a literature review from 1985 to 2013 identified only 19 reported cases. Among myositis-specific antibodies, anti-MDA-5 and anti-TIF1-γ antibodies have been more commonly associated with oedematous presentations. Anti-SAE1 disease is particularly rarely linked to this phenomenon: in a review of 10 reported cases of SAE1-positive dermatomyositis, only three described oedema, and only one mentioned swelling in the throat region. This rarity explains why the initial treating teams focused on deep neck space infection and idiopathic angioedema, and why recognition of dermatomyositis as a differential for otherwise unexplained neck swelling is emphasised by the authors as a critical clinical lesson. The diagnosis ultimately turned on three elements: the atypical distribution of the neck swelling for true angioedema, the identification of subtle cutaneous and nailfold signs, and the prompt measurement of CK, which returned markedly elevated at 16,184 U/L and immediately redirected the diagnostic reasoning.

## Confirmatory Investigations and Disease Characterization

Once the clinical immunology team recognized that this patient's neck swelling was atypical for angioedema—supported by the absence of triggering medications, no personal or family history of angioedema, and the presence of subtle cutaneous and muscular signs—a structured diagnostic pathway was pursued. The goals of investigation were threefold: to definitively exclude hereditary and acquired angioedema, to confirm the suspected diagnosis of dermatomyositis through autoantibody profiling and tissue pathology, and to characterize the full extent of organ involvement to guide treatment intensity and anticipate complications.

### Excluding Angioedema: Complement Studies

Because the patient's initial presentation centered on neck swelling, complement studies were performed to rule out bradykinin-mediated forms of angioedema. Hereditary angioedema, caused by deficiency or dysfunction of C1 esterase inhibitor, is characterized by low C4 levels and reduced C1 esterase inhibitor function or quantity. Acquired angioedema, seen in association with B-cell lymphoproliferative disorders or autoimmune C1 esterase inhibitor consumption, similarly produces low C4 and low C1 esterase inhibitor levels, often with reduced C1q. In this patient, C3 and C4 were normal, and both C1 esterase inhibitor function and quantification were within the normal range. These results effectively excluded hereditary and acquired angioedema and redirected the diagnostic focus toward a myositis-associated process. The normal complement levels also argued against hypocomplementemic urticarial vasculitis, another condition that can produce angioedema-like swelling.

### Autoantibody Profiling

The patient's antinuclear antibody (ANA) was positive at a titre of 1:1280 with a speckled pattern, indicating a systemic autoimmune process. ANCA was negative, reducing the likelihood of ANCA-associated vasculitis as a contributor to the clinical picture. A comprehensive myositis antibody panel of 16 antigens was performed using the Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay, a line immunoassay that simultaneously tests for multiple myositis-specific and myositis-associated autoantibodies. Each myositis-specific antibody tends to correlate with distinct clinical phenotypes: for example, anti-Jo-1 and other anti-synthetase antibodies are associated with interstitial lung disease and antisynthetase syndrome, anti-TIF1-γ and anti-NXP2 antibodies are linked to malignancy-associated dermatomyositis, and anti-MDA-5 antibodies are associated with rapidly progressive interstitial lung disease and amyopathic disease. In this case, the panel returned strongly positive for anti-SAE1 (small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1) antibodies, with a signal intensity of 173 (normal range <35). All other myositis-specific antibodies, including anti-Mi-2α, anti-TIF1-γ, anti-Jo-1, and anti-OJ, were negative. This serological profile effectively narrowed the subtype of dermatomyositis and had direct implications for prognosis and associated risks.

### Significance of Anti-SAE1 Antibodies

Anti-SAE1 antibodies target the SUMO activating enzyme, a protein structurally homologous to ubiquitin that mediates post-translational modification of target proteins through a process called sumoylation. SAE1-positive dermatomyositis is a rare subtype that, based on a literature review of 215 patients, is typically characterized by classic cutaneous features such as heliotrope rash, Gottron's papules, V-neck sign, and shawl sign. Dysphagia is reported in approximately 46% of cases and interstitial lung disease in approximately 43%, while malignancy occurs in roughly 18%. This patient's presentation was somewhat atypical in that the dominant cutaneous signs were subtle periungual erythema and a positive Holster sign, while the classic heliotrope eruption and Gottron's papules were absent. The prominence of neck swelling as the chief complaint further distinguished this case from the usual SAE1 phenotype, underscoring the variability with which dermatomyositis can present and the value of antibody testing in establishing the diagnosis when classical skin findings are incomplete.

### Muscle Imaging: MRI and FDG-PET

MRI of the thighs demonstrated changes consistent with active myositis, with the most prominent inflammation in the bilateral obturator internus and externus muscles, and milder involvement of the hip adductors, bilateral gracilis muscles, and distal iliopsoas muscles. MRI is the preferred imaging modality for assessing muscle inflammation in inflammatory myopathy, as it detects edema within inflamed muscle through fluid-sensitive sequences such as short tau inversion recovery (STIR) or T2 fat-suppressed images. The pattern of muscle involvement on MRI helps distinguish dermatomyositis from other inflammatory myopathies: dermatomyositis classically shows perifascicular predominance on histology, but imaging may reveal more generalized muscle edema. In this case, the MRI findings corroborated the clinical observation of proximal hip flexor weakness and provided objective evidence of disease activity to justify aggressive immunosuppression.

A whole-body FDG-PET scan was also performed and demonstrated diffuse abnormal FDG accumulation across multiple muscle groups, most prominently in the head and neck, bilateral deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdomen, and pelvis. FDG-PET served a dual purpose. First, it confirmed the widespread extent of muscle inflammation, which was particularly important given that the neck swelling itself resulted from inflamed strap muscles and adjacent soft tissue rather than from a vascular or allergic process. Second, it functioned as a malignancy screening tool. The association between dermatomyositis and underlying malignancy is well established, and FDG-PET is valuable in this setting because it can detect metabolically active tumors that may not yet be structurally apparent on conventional CT. In this patient, no metabolically active disease was identified outside the muscular inflammation, providing reassurance that occult malignancy was not driving the myositis at the time of evaluation.

### Muscle Biopsy Histopathology

A muscle biopsy of the thigh provided the definitive pathological confirmation of dermatomyositis. Histological examination revealed foci of necrotic, vacuolated degenerate, myophagocytosed, and regenerative myofibers in a perifascicular distribution, along with scattered perimysial inflammation consisting predominantly of histiocytes. The perifascicular distribution of myofiber damage is the pathological hallmark of dermatomyositis and reflects the disease's primary pathophysiology: a complement-mediated microangiopathy targeting endomysial capillaries. This mechanism was directly demonstrated in this biopsy by immunohistochemical staining for the membrane attack complex, which showed deposition of complement component C5b-9 in perifascicular endomysial capillaries. The deposition of C5b-9 in capillaries is among the earliest observable abnormalities in dermatomyositis and represents the downstream consequence of immune complex deposition and complement activation. This capillary injury leads to ischemic injury of perifascicular myofibers, which are located at the periphery of the fascicle and are most vulnerable to reduced perfusion. The biopsy findings thus confirmed the diagnosis at a tissue level and distinguished dermatomyositis from immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy, which characteristically shows necrosis and regeneration without prominent perifascicular patterning or complement deposition, and from inclusion body myositis, which features rimmed vacuoles and endomysial inflammation.

### Assessment of Dysphagia

A video fluoroscopic (barium) swallow study was performed to formally characterize the dysphagia that had been suggested by increased secretions and coughing during flexible nasendoscopy. The study demonstrated severe pharyngeal dysphagia with significant pyriform fossae residue and post-swallow penetration of thin and mildly thick fluids to the level of the vocal cords. This finding has important clinical implications. Pharyngeal dysphagia in dermatomyositis results from involvement of the striated muscles of the pharynx and upper esophageal sphincter, which share embryological and structural characteristics with skeletal muscle. The severity of dysphagia in this patient was a major determinant of treatment intensity, as it indicated that myositis was affecting muscles critical for airway protection. The documented penetration of fluids to the vocal cords also explained the peribronchial consolidation seen on chest CT, which was attributed to micro-aspiration. This finding necessitated nasogastric tube feeding to maintain nutrition and prevent further aspiration while immunosuppressive therapy was initiated.

### Pulmonary Evaluation

Pulmonary function tests revealed an extrathoracic restrictive pattern with no significant bronchodilator response. This pattern was consistent with restriction originating from upper airway and pharyngeal muscle dysfunction rather than intrinsic lung disease. CT of the chest showed multiple non-cavitating pulmonary nodules with a mid to lower zone distribution, the largest measuring 15 mm in the right lower lobe, as well as patchy areas of peribronchial consolidation attributed to aspiration pneumonitis. Importantly, there was no radiological evidence of pulmonary fibrosis. The absence of interstitial lung disease was significant, as it influenced the choice of immunosuppressive agents and prognostic expectations. The pulmonary nodules, while requiring ongoing surveillance, were assessed in the context of the overall malignancy screening strategy.

### Malignancy Risk Stratification and Screening

The association between dermatomyositis and malignancy is a central concern in the management of affected patients. According to the International Guideline for Idiopathic Inflammatory Myopathy-Associated Cancer Screening by the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS), patients are stratified into risk categories based on specific clinical and serological factors. This patient carried two high-risk factors (dermatomyositis itself and dysphagia) and two intermediate-risk factors (SAE1 antibody positivity and male sex). This combination placed him in a high-risk category for malignancy, justifying a comprehensive and proactive screening approach.

The malignancy screening strategy incorporated CT imaging of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis, which showed no evidence of primary or metastatic malignancy and no lymphadenopathy. A whole-body FDG-PET scan, as discussed above, detected no metabolically active disease outside the inflamed muscles. Tumor markers, including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA 19-9), cancer antigen 125 (CA-125), and prostate-specific antigen (PSA), were all within normal limits. Based on the high-risk stratification, plans were made for upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy to exclude occult gastrointestinal or colorectal malignancy. This multi-modal screening strategy reflects the principle that the type and intensity of malignancy surveillance in dermatomyositis should be tailored to individual risk, and that screening should continue longitudinally rather than being a one-time assessment, as malignancies can manifest months to years after the initial diagnosis.

### Pre-Immunosuppression Infection Screening

Before initiating intensive immunosuppressive therapy, patients must be screened for latent or chronic infections that could reactivate under immunosuppression. This patient's screening included serology for hepatitis B (HBsAg, HBcAb, HBeAb), hepatitis C, and HIV, all of which were negative. Varicella zoster virus IgG was negative, indicating no prior immunity and a potential need for vaccination before immunosuppression. Cytomegalovirus IgG and IgM were negative. An indeterminate QuantiFERON-TB Gold result prompted further evaluation with three sputum samples for acid-fast bacilli, all of which were negative for Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Immunoglobulin G levels and subclasses were normal, providing reassurance that the patient was not immunodeficient at baseline and could safely tolerate the planned immunosuppressive regimen. This infection screening is an essential component of the pre-treatment evaluation in any patient anticipated to receive high-dose corticosteroids, rituximab, mycophenolate mofetil, and intravenous immunoglobulin, as these agents carry variable risks of viral reactivation, opportunistic infection, and impaired humoral defense.

In summary, the confirmatory investigations in this patient served to close the diagnostic loop opened by the atypical presentation of neck swelling. Complement studies excluded angioedema, the anti-SAE1 antibody and muscle biopsy established dermatomyositis at a serological and pathological level, imaging and functional studies mapped the extent of muscular and pharyngeal involvement, and malignancy risk stratification guided a surveillance strategy that reflected the high-risk profile of this patient. Together, these investigations provided the evidence base for the aggressive, multi-agent immunosuppressive treatment that followed.

## Management, Outcome, and Clinical Lessons

The management of this patient's dermatomyositis required an aggressive, multidisciplinary approach driven by the severity and distribution of his muscle involvement. The decision to pursue intensive immunosuppression was predicated on several alarming features: clinically significant dysphagia with documented aspiration on fluoroscopic swallow study, proximal muscle weakness, and a markedly elevated creatine kinase (CK) of 16,184 U/L. In dermatomyositis, the severity of muscle enzyme elevation generally correlates with the degree of active muscle inflammation, and levels in the tens of thousands indicate widespread myofiber necrosis. The involvement of pharyngeal and cervical musculature was particularly concerning, as these muscles are essential for airway protection and swallowing, and their compromise places the patient at risk for aspiration pneumonitis—a potentially life-threatening complication that this patient had already begun to demonstrate on imaging. This clinical picture demanded rapid suppression of the autoimmune inflammatory process to halt further muscle destruction and prevent irreversible damage or airway compromise.

Corticosteroids form the cornerstone of initial treatment for moderate to severe dermatomyositis, and this patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for three days. Pulse-dose intravenous corticosteroids are favored in severe inflammatory myopathy when rapid, high-level immunosuppression is needed, particularly when bulbar or respiratory muscles are involved. The mechanism is broad suppression of both cellular and humoral immune pathways, reducing the inflammatory infiltrate in muscle tissue and the complement-mediated microangiopathy that characterizes dermatomyositis. After the pulse phase, the patient was transitioned to oral prednisone at 60 mg daily, with a gradual taper of 5 mg every two to three weeks. This tapering schedule reflects the standard approach of starting at a weight-appropriate high dose (typically 0.5–1 mg/kg/day) and reducing slowly over months, balancing the need to maintain disease control against the well-known adverse effects of prolonged glucocorticoid therapy, including osteoporosis, glucose intolerance, adrenal suppression, and increased infection risk.

Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was incorporated into the regimen given the severity of the presentation, particularly the dysphagia and aspiration risk. IVIG is thought to work through multiple immunomodulatory mechanisms, including blockade of Fc receptors on macrophages, modulation of complement activation, neutralization of autoantibodies through anti-idiotypic interactions, and regulation of B- and T-cell function. It is especially valuable in dermatomyositis because the disease involves a complement-mediated microangiopathy, and IVIG can interrupt the deposition of membrane attack complex on endomysial capillaries. The patient received a loading dose of 2 g/kg (165 g total, administered as 35 g daily over five days), followed by maintenance cycles of 2 g/kg every four weeks for five additional cycles. IVIG is particularly beneficial in cases with prominent cutaneous disease, dysphagia, or esophageal involvement, and its relatively rapid onset compared to oral immunosuppressants makes it valuable as bridge therapy while slower-acting agents take effect.

For long-term steroid-sparing immunosuppression, mycophenolate mofetil was selected at a dose of 1 g twice daily. Mycophenolate inhibits inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase, an enzyme critical for de novo guanine nucleotide synthesis in lymphocytes, which are particularly dependent on this pathway. The rationale for choosing mycophenolate over alternative agents involved several case-specific considerations. Methotrexate was avoided due to its known association with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, a concern heightened by this patient's abnormal pulmonary function tests showing a restrictive pattern. This is a critical point: while methotrexate is one of the most commonly used first-line steroid-sparing agents in dermatomyositis, its pulmonary toxicity makes it a poor choice in any patient with existing or suspected lung involvement. Azathioprine and tacrolimus were not pursued because mycophenolate was well tolerated, and switching agents in a clinically stable patient would introduce unnecessary risk. The decision was also informed by patient preference for a daily oral regimen, highlighting the importance of shared decision-making in chronic disease management.

Rituximab, a monoclonal antibody targeting CD20 on B lymphocytes, was administered as two infusions of 1000 mg intravenously, given two weeks apart. This regimen is the standard dosing schedule adapted from rheumatoid arthritis protocols and has been studied extensively in the Rituximab in Myositis (RIM) trial. B-cell depletion is rational in dermatomyositis because B cells contribute to disease pathogenesis through production of autoantibodies—such as the anti-SAE1 antibody in this patient—and through antigen presentation and cytokine secretion that sustain the inflammatory cascade. In severe or refractory disease, rituximab is often combined with other immunosuppressants, and in this case it was used adjunctively with mycophenolate and IVIG to maximize immunosuppressive depth. The limited evidence specific to anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis means that treatment approaches remain heterogeneous, but the combination strategy employed here reflects the general principle that severe, multi-system inflammatory myopathy warrants multi-agent therapy.

Because the patient was receiving high-dose corticosteroids in combination with multiple immunosuppressive agents, prophylaxis against *Pneumocystis jirovecii* pneumonia was indicated. He was initially started on sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim, the standard first-line agent. However, this was complicated by the development of hemolytic anemia, an adverse event that occurred despite normal glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) activity. Drug-induced hemolytic anemia in the absence of G6PD deficiency is a recognized but less common complication of sulfonamide therapy and can arise through immune-mediated mechanisms. The prophylaxis was switched to nebulized pentamidine 300 mg every four weeks, which delivers the drug directly to the pulmonary epithelium and is a reasonable alternative for patients intolerant of systemic prophylactic agents, though it may be less effective against extrapulmonary disease. This complication illustrates the practical challenges of managing immunosuppressed patients, where the prevention of one infection can introduce new toxicities that require ongoing vigilance and flexibility in management.

The patient's inpatient course lasted 21 days, reflecting the complexity of his presentation and the need to coordinate multiple immunosuppressive infusions, diagnostic workup, and rehabilitation. Persistent dysphagia at the time of discharge necessitated nasogastric tube feeding, underscoring that improvement in bulbar muscle function often lags behind that of limb muscles in dermatomyositis. This is clinically important because dysphagia is both a marker of severe disease and a major determinant of quality of life and nutritional status during recovery. The nasogastric tube was required for an additional two months after discharge before swallowing function improved sufficiently for oral intake.

The short-term outcomes demonstrated a meaningful but incomplete response. After three weeks of treatment, the patient showed moderate improvement in hip flexor weakness and marked improvement in cervical soft tissue swelling, though the latter had not fully resolved. This pattern is consistent with the understanding that subcutaneous edema in dermatomyositis, arising from underlying muscle inflammation rather than from histamine- or bradykinin-mediated vascular permeability, responds to immunosuppression on a timescale that differs from true angioedema. The persistence of dysphagia at discharge highlights that pharyngeal muscle recovery can be particularly slow, requiring prolonged supportive care.

By the medium-term follow-up at three weeks post-discharge, proximal muscle strength had fully recovered and CK had normalized to 37 U/L, a dramatic reduction from the initial 16,184 U/L. The normalization of CK is a reliable biochemical marker of disease quiescence in inflammatory myopathy and provides objective evidence that the immunosuppressive regimen has effectively halted active muscle fiber necrosis. At two months, cervical swelling continued to improve, residual facial erythema and periungual changes were the remaining cutaneous findings, and the nasogastric tube was removed as swallowing became adequate for soft solids. By three months, cervical swelling had completely resolved. This trajectory demonstrates that while dermatomyositis can present with dramatic and alarming physical findings, appropriate aggressive treatment can achieve substantial recovery over weeks to months.

Long-term outcomes were favorable. Prednisone was successfully discontinued at seven months, representing effective steroid sparing through the use of mycophenolate and IVIG as maintenance therapy. A follow-up CT chest at 11 months showed near-complete resolution of the basal ground-glass opacities and airspace consolidation that had been attributed to aspiration, and pulmonary function tests showed mild improvement with a residual restrictive pattern thought to reflect the patient's baseline physiology rather than active disease. At 12 months, respiratory function remained stable with good exercise tolerance. The ongoing management plan involves continuation of IVIG, gradual tapering of mycophenolate over the subsequent six months depending on clinical progress, and continued malignancy surveillance. The latter is particularly important given that this patient carries multiple malignancy risk factors under the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) framework: two high-risk factors (dermatomyositis itself and dysphagia) and two intermediate-risk factors (anti-SAE1 antibody positivity and male sex). He will undergo gastroscopy and colonoscopy to exclude occult gastrointestinal malignancy, and ongoing surveillance will be guided by symptoms, examination, blood tests, and serial CT chest imaging. Anti-SAE1–positive dermatomyositis has been associated with malignancy in approximately 18% of cases, making structured cancer screening essential.

Several clinical lessons emerge from this case that are directly applicable to clinical practice. First, dermatomyositis should be included in the differential diagnosis of unexplained neck swelling, particularly when the swelling is atypical for angioedema in its distribution, duration, or response to therapy. True angioedema—whether histaminergic (allergic) or bradykinin-mediated (hereditary or drug-induced)—typically produces transient, well-demarcated swelling of subcutaneous and submucosal tissues. This patient's swelling was persistent, involved the strap muscles and retropharyngeal space on imaging, and was accompanied by erythema and systemic inflammation, none of which fit neatly into the angioedema framework. Second, a creatine kinase level is a simple, inexpensive, and rapidly available test that should be requested early when the clinical picture is atypical for angioedema, particularly when muscle weakness, skin changes, or dysphagia are present. In this case, the CK result was the pivotal laboratory finding that redirected the diagnostic workup away from angioedema and toward inflammatory myopathy. Third, careful physical examination focusing on subtle cutaneous and nailfold findings—periungual erythema, nailfold microhaemorrhages on capillaroscopy, a rash on the lateral thigh consistent with a Holster sign—provided important diagnostic clues even in the absence of the classic Gottron's papules and heliotrope eruption. These findings should be actively sought in any patient with unexplained soft tissue swelling and systemic symptoms. Fourth, this case demonstrates the value of multidisciplinary collaboration. The patient was initially evaluated by emergency physicians and ENT surgeons, and the eventual diagnosis required input from immunology, radiology, pathology, and speech-language therapy. The transition from an initial impression of Ludwig's angina to a working diagnosis of angioedema and finally to dermatomyositis illustrates how the differential diagnosis evolves as information accumulates, and how each specialty contributes critical data—nasendoscopy excluding structural pathology, capillaroscopy revealing microvascular changes, and muscle biopsy confirming the histologic pattern of dermatomyositis. Finally, the case underscores that when dermatomyositis presents with severe muscle involvement, particularly affecting bulbar and respiratory muscles, prompt and aggressive multidrug immunosuppression is necessary to prevent disease progression and multiorgan damage, and that favorable outcomes are achievable with timely, coordinated care.
