Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# IMNM vs. Rhabdomyolysis: When CK Elevation Isn't What It Seems

## The Setup

A woman in her 60s gets a single dose of semaglutide. Within hours, she's vomiting. Within days, she's in the ICU with acute kidney injury and metabolic acidosis. But here's where it gets interesting: her CK was only 337 U/L on that initial admission. Four months later — long after the drug was stopped — her CK is 3,992 U/L, she can't stand without assistance, and she's having trouble swallowing. She got one dose. One. And the muscle destruction didn't just persist — it *accelerated* over months.

That temporal pattern is the whole story here, and it's the key to distinguishing immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) from the drug-induced rhabdomyolysis we're all more familiar with. This case, published by Lam and colleagues, is the first reported case of IMNM probably linked to a GLP-1 receptor agonist, and it offers a masterclass in why not all CK elevation is created equal.

## Two Diseases, One Biomarker

Let's put this case side by side with the previously reported tirzepatide-associated rhabdomyolysis case referenced in the paper. Both involve GLP-1 receptor agonists. Both involve CK elevation and muscle injury. And that's where the similarities end.

The tirzepatide patient presented with a CK of 57,014. Massive. Classic rhabdomyolysis territory. But here's the thing: within *days* of drug discontinuation and hydration, the CK started falling. No immunosuppression needed. That's toxic muscle injury — the drug damages muscle directly, you remove the drug, the muscle recovers. Clean, predictable, and consistent with everything we know about drug-induced rhabdomyolysis from statins, colchicine, and other culprits.

Now look at the semaglutide patient. Her CK at initial presentation was 337 — barely above normal. She got no further doses of the drug. Yet over the next four months, her CK climbed 12-fold to nearly 4,000. Her weakness progressed to the point where she couldn't sit upright unassisted. She developed dysphagia and dysphonia. This isn't toxicity. This is an autoimmune process that was *triggered* by the drug but then took on a life of its own.

That distinction — direct toxicity versus immune triggering — changes everything about how you manage the patient.

## The Timeline Tells You Everything

If you take one thing from this post, let it be this: the CK trajectory is the single most important clinical clue in separating these two entities.

In drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, the CK peaks at presentation or shortly after, and then falls within days of drug withdrawal. The injury is happening *now*, and removing the cause stops the damage. You support the patient with fluids, manage complications like kidney injury and electrolyte disturbances, and the muscle recovers.

In IMNM, the CK may be modest initially — or even normal — and then *rises* over weeks to months, even after the drug is gone. The drug didn't poison the muscle. It flipped a switch in the immune system, and now the immune system is destroying the muscle on its own. Stopping the drug doesn't help because the drug was just the trigger, not the ongoing driver. This patient's CK was climbing for four months after a single dose. No further exposure. No recovery. That's the signature of an immune-mediated process, not a toxic one.

## What the Biopsy Shows

The muscle biopsy in this patient sealed the diagnosis. She had necrotic and regenerating muscle fibres, CD68-positive macrophages with myophagocytosis, and membrane attack complex staining on both necrotising and non-necrotising myofibres. There were only rare CD3-positive T-cells.

This is the histologic face of IMNM: necrosis with a paucity of lymphocytic infiltrate. It's not polymyositis with its dense endomysial T-cell infiltration. It's necrotising myopathy — muscle fibres are dying, macrophages are cleaning up the debris, and complement is depositing on muscle fibres. The relative absence of lymphocytes is actually a key diagnostic feature, not evidence that inflammation is absent.

Contrast this with rhabdomyolysis on biopsy, where you'd see necrosis but without the immune signatures — no complement deposition on non-necrotic fibres, no evidence of an ongoing immunologic attack on otherwise healthy muscle.

## The Antibody Problem

Here's where it gets tricky. Anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP are the two autoantibodies most associated with IMNM, and anti-HMGCR in particular is strongly linked to statin exposure. This patient had never taken statins. Her entire myositis panel — SRP, HMGCR, Jo-1, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-γ, NXP-2, and the rest — was negative.

But here's the pearl: roughly one-third of IMNM patients are seronegative. The absence of these antibodies doesn't rule out IMNM, and the authors make an important point — there are likely undiscovered autoantibodies and triggers out there. Statin-associated IMNM wasn't recognised as autoimmune until anti-HMGCR was identified. Before that, many of these patients were labelled as having toxic myopathy or polymyositis. We may be at the same crossroads now with GLP-1 receptor agonists.

She did have a low-titre positive SS-B antibody with negative ANA and SSA, which was unexpected and not clearly relevant. The authors couldn't find a CK from before semaglutide initiation, so they can't fully exclude a pre-existing smouldering autoimmune process. But the 12-fold CK rise after the drug, the progressive weakness, and the response to immunosuppression all point toward the drug as the trigger.

## Response to Treatment Confirms the Diagnosis

The treatment response further distinguishes IMNM from rhabdomyolysis. This patient received pulse methylprednisolone for five days, then oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg, plus a single course of IVIG at 2 g/kg. Her CK dropped markedly within two weeks. She was weaned off prednisone over just two months, received no additional IVIG, and was back at baseline functional status within ten months — off all immunosuppression for eight of those months.

Drug-induced rhabdomyolysis doesn't respond to immunosuppression. You don't need steroids or IVIG for rhabdomyolysis — you need fluids and time. The fact that this patient required and responded to immunosuppression is itself diagnostic evidence for an immune-mediated process.

Equally important: her rapid taper and sustained remission off immunosuppression is more consistent with drug-triggered IMNM than idiopathic IMNM, which often requires prolonged, intensive immunosuppression. When you remove the trigger and give a short course of immune suppression to break the cycle, drug-triggered IMNM can stay in remission. Idiopathic IMNM tends to relapse.

## A Note on the Cardiac Findings

The patient had a mildly elevated high-sensitivity troponin (62 pg/mL), a proBNP of 4,358 pg/mL, and a cardiac MRI showing mild mid-anteroseptal and anterior wall hypokinesis with myocardial oedema but no late gadolinium enhancement. The authors couldn't definitively confirm myocarditis — stress-induced cardiomyopathy was also considered. But both the echocardiogram and cardiac MRI abnormalities normalised on follow-up, which is reassuring and consistent with either a transient inflammatory process or stress cardiomyopathy in the setting of her severe systemic illness.

The broader point: IMNM can involve cardiac muscle, and any patient with significant IMNM deserves a cardiac evaluation — troponin, ECG, echocardiogram, and consider cardiac MRI if there's any signal.

## Why This Matters Now

GLP-1 receptor agonists are everywhere. Semaglutide, tirzepatide, liraglutide — these are among the most prescribed medications in the world, and their use is growing exponentially. We already know they cause loss of skeletal muscle mass. We have a published case of rhabdomyolysis with tirzepatide. And now we have what appears to be the first case of IMNM triggered by semaglutide.

The authors are appropriately cautious about causality — using WHO-UMC criteria, they classify this as *probable* causality. A rechallenge would be unethical. But the temporal relationship, the absence of alternative explanations, and the response to withdrawal and immunosuppression all align.

We don't yet know what predisposes patients to this reaction. We don't know if it's dose-dependent, if it requires sustained exposure, or if a single dose can trigger it in the right host — though this case suggests it can. We don't know if there's an as-yet unidentified autoantibody that marks susceptibility. What we do know is that if a patient on a GLP-1 receptor agonist presents with progressive weakness and CK elevation that *worsens* after drug discontinuation, you need to be thinking IMNM, not rhabdomyolysis — and you need rheumatology and neurology involved early.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **CK trajectory is the critical discriminator.** Rhabdomyolysis peaks at or near presentation and falls within days of drug withdrawal. IMNM may start with modest CK elevation and *rise* over months, even after the drug is stopped. A rising CK after discontinuation should shift your thinking toward an immune-mediated process.

- **One dose was enough.** This patient developed IMNM after a single 0.25 mg dose of semaglutide. The trigger doesn't require sustained exposure.

- **Seronegativity doesn't rule out IMNM.** One-third of IMNM patients are negative for anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP. A negative myositis panel in the setting of progressive proximal weakness and CK elevation does not exclude the diagnosis — biopsy is essential.

- **Response to immunosuppression is diagnostic evidence.** If the CK falls with steroids and IVIG after drug withdrawal, you're dealing with an immune-mediated process, not direct toxicity. Rhabdomyolysis doesn't need or respond to immunosuppression.

- **Drug-triggered IMNM may need only a short course of immunosuppression.** Unlike idiopathic IMNM, which often requires prolonged treatment, drug-triggered IMNM may resolve with withdrawal of the trigger plus a brief course of steroids ± IVIG. This patient was off all immunosuppression within two months and remained in remission.

- **Evaluate the heart.** IMNM can involve cardiac muscle. Check troponin, get an echocardiogram, and consider cardiac MRI in any patient with significant IMNM.

- **Watch for this.** With the explosive growth of GLP-1 receptor agonist prescribing, this won't be the last case. When you see progressive weakness and CK elevation in a patient on semaglutide or tirzepatide, ask whether the trajectory fits rhabdomyolysis — or whether the immune system has taken over.



# GLP-1 Receptor Agonists and Skekeletal Muscle: Beyond the GI Side Effects We Talk About

## One Dose. Four Months of Decline. A New Adverse Effect Hiding in Plain Sight?

You've prescribed semaglutide. Your patients are on it. Your colleagues are on it. GLP-1 receptor agonists have become one of the most widely prescribed drug classes in modern medicine, and for good reason — they work. We talk endlessly about the GI side effects: nausea, vomiting, delayed gastric emptying. But what about skeletal muscle? We know these medications cause measurable loss of lean muscle mass. There's a published case of tirzepatide-associated rhabdomyolysis. And now, we have what appears to be the **first reported case of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) probably triggered by a single dose of semaglutide.**

Let that sink in for a moment. Not rhabdomyolysis from a direct toxic effect. An autoimmune myopathy — necrotising, progressive, requiring immunosuppression — after *one dose*.

This case should give us pause not because GLP-1 agonists are bad drugs, but because we may be at the very beginning of a recognition curve that we've seen before with another wildly popular drug class: statins.

## The Case: A Single Dose, Then a Slow Unraveling

A woman in her 60s with obesity, NAFLD, hypertension, and hypothyroidism was started on semaglutide for persistent fatty liver disease and obesity. Within hours of her first and only 0.25 mg subcutaneous dose, she developed nausea and vomiting. Two days later, she was hospitalized with severe GI symptoms, acute renal failure, and metabolic acidosis requiring brief non-invasive ventilation in the ICU. Her CK on admission was 337 U/L — elevated but nowhere near rhabdomyolysis territory. She was hydrated, stabilized, and discharged after five days with resolved GI symptoms but noticeable deconditioning.

Then things got worse. Much worse. Over the next four months, she developed progressive proximal muscle weakness to the point where she could no longer stand or sit without assistance. She developed dysphagia to both liquids and solids and dysphonia. Her CK climbed to 3,992 U/L — a 12-fold increase from her admission CK — despite having been off semaglutide for the entire four-month period. She had no prior statin use, no red yeast rice or mushroom supplements, no family history of myopathy, no excessive exercise, no illicit drug use.

Her workup at a tertiary center revealed a negative full myositis panel including anti-SRP, anti-HMGCR, Jo-1, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-γ, NXP-2, and all others tested. ANA was negative. She had a low-titer SS-B antibody (2.4, normal <1.0) with negative SS-A, which was unexpected and of unclear significance. MRI of bilateral femurs showed symmetric muscular edema in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps. Cardiac MRI showed mild mid-anteroseptal and anterior wall hypokinesis with myocardial edema — potentially early myocarditis or stress cardiomyopathy. A muscle biopsy of the right vastus lateralis was the key: scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibres, CD68+ macrophage infiltration with myophagocytosis, membrane attack complex deposition on both necrotising and non-necrotising myofibres, and only rare CD3+ T-cells. This was textbook IMNM.

## Why This Isn't Rhabdomyolysis — And Why That Distinction Matters

The temporal course here is the critical clue. This patient's CK *rose* over four months after a single dose of the drug. In drug-induced rhabdomyolysis from a direct toxic effect, you expect a rapid CK spike while the drug is on board and a prompt fall within days of discontinuation and supportive care. That's exactly what was seen in the previously reported tirzepatide case — CK of 57,014 at presentation with improvement within days of withdrawal and hydration, no immunosuppression needed.

This patient's course was the opposite. Her CK was modestly elevated (337 U/L) at presentation shortly after the dose, then climbed steadily to nearly 4,000 U/L over four months *while off the drug entirely*. Her muscle biopsy showed an immune-mediated necrotising process with complement activation, not a toxic myopathy. And critically, her condition improved with immunosuppression — pulse methylprednisolone followed by oral prednisone and a single course of IVIG — plus continued avoidance of semaglutide. Her CK normalized within two weeks of treatment. She was weaned off prednisone over just two months, received no additional IVIG, and ten months later had regained full baseline function off all immunosuppression for seven to eight months.

This clinical pattern — persistent progression after drug cessation, response to immunosuppression, and sustained remission after a short course — aligns with drug-triggered IMNM rather than drug-induced rhabdomyolysis. The WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre criteria classify this as *probable* causality, acknowledging that a rechallenge would be unethical given the severity of the reaction.

## The Statin Parallel: Are We Seeing the Same Movie Again?

Here's where this case gets genuinely thought-provoking. Consider the history of statin-associated myopathy. Before the discovery of anti-HMGCR autoantibodies, patients with statin-associated necrotising myopathy were often classified as having toxic myopathy or polymyositis. Many had persistent myopathy and CK elevation despite statin cessation — a pattern inconsistent with a pure toxic effect. It was only after recognizing that these patients responded to immunosuppression and that autoantibodies were present that statin-associated IMNM was identified as a distinct autoimmune entity.

Importantly, about one-third of IMNM patients are seronegative for both anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP. This patient was seronegative on a comprehensive myositis panel. The absence of a known autoantibody doesn't exclude IMNM — it suggests there are associated autoantibodies and triggers we haven't yet discovered. GLP-1 receptor agonists could be one such trigger, and the relevant autoantibody may not yet exist in our testing arsenal.

The authors make a compelling point: given the exponential adoption of GLP-1 agonists, if even a tiny fraction of patients develop immune-mediated muscle disease, we will see more cases. The question is whether we'll recognize them or attribute progressive weakness to "deconditioning," "aging," or "weight loss–related sarcopenia" — all of which are expected with these medications.

## What About the Muscle Mass Loss We Already Know About?

The skeletal muscle story with GLP-1 agonists isn't new. These medications cause significant loss of lean body mass as part of their weight loss effect — in some trials, up to 25-40% of total weight lost is lean mass. This is generally framed as a "known, expected" effect rather than an adverse event. But here's the concern: if patients are already losing muscle mass from the drug's metabolic effects, and a subset develop an immune-mediated necrotising process on top of that, the clinical picture could be muddy. A patient on semaglutide who reports weakness could be experiencing sarcopenia from weight loss, or they could be developing IMNM. Without checking a CK, you won't distinguish them. And without a muscle biopsy, you won't know if it's autoimmune.

## What Should Clinicians Actually Watch For?

This is a single case report, and correlation is not causation. But the temporal relationship, the biopsy-proven IMNM pathology, the progressive course despite drug cessation, and the complete response to immunosuppression with drug avoidance make this a credible signal worth taking seriously. Here's what I'd take away:

## Key Take-Home Points

- **GLP-1 agonists may be associated with immune-mediated necrotising myopathy, not just direct toxic rhabdomyolysis or expected lean mass loss.** This is the first reported probable case, but given the massive prescribing volume, it likely won't be the last.

- **The temporal pattern distinguishes IMNM from rhabdomyolysis.** Rhabdomyolysis = acute CK spike while on drug, rapid improvement with cessation. IMNM = progressive CK rise and weakness *after* drug cessation, requiring immunosuppression. If you see progressive weakness and rising CK in a patient who stopped a GLP-1 agonist weeks or months ago, think IMNM, not delayed rhabdo.

- **Check a CK in any patient on a GLP-1 agonist with unexplained weakness.** Don't assume it's deconditioning or sarcopenia from weight loss. The CK is cheap, fast, and may be the only clue that something autoimmune is brewing.

- **Seronegative IMNM is common.** About one-third of IMNM patients are negative for anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP. A negative myositis panel does not exclude IMNM. Muscle biopsy is the gold standard.

- **Biopsy findings in IMNM are distinct.** Look for necrotic and regenerating fibres, macrophage-predominant infiltrate (CD68+), membrane attack complex positivity, and a paucity of T-cells. This is not polymyositis and not a toxic myopathy.

- **The statin-IMNM story is a cautionary parallel.** It took years to recognize that persistent myopathy after statin cessation was autoimmune, not toxic. We should be vigilant that a similar recognition curve may be starting with GLP-1 agonists.

- **Drug-triggered IMNM may require only short-course immunosuppression if the trigger is withdrawn.** This patient responded to pulse steroids, a single IVIG course, and brief prednisone taper — and remained in remission off all immunosuppression. Idiopathic IMNM typically requires prolonged, intensive immunosuppression. This difference supports a drug-triggered mechanism.

- **Pharmacovigilance matters more than ever.** Rare autoimmune adverse effects may not appear in pre-marketing trials but can emerge with population-level prescribing at this scale. Report suspected cases. The signal only becomes real when enough dots are connected.

## Bottom Line

We're prescribing GLP-1 receptor agonists at an unprecedented scale. We know about the GI effects, the pancreatitis risk, the muscle mass loss. We're learning about rhabdomyolysis. And now we have a credible first case of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy probably triggered by a single dose of semaglutide. Is this a one-in-a-million idiosyncratic reaction, or the tip of an iceberg we can't yet see? We don't know. But the history of statin-associated IMNM tells us that the answer only comes when clinicians are alert enough to check the CK, biopsy the muscle, and report the case. Consider this your alert.



# The Seronegative Myopathy Dilemma: When the Autoantibody Panel Comes Back Empty

## The Case That Refuses to Fit the Box

Picture this: a woman in her 60s presents with four months of progressive proximal weakness so severe she can no longer stand or sit without assistance. She's also developed dysphagia and dysphonia. Her CK is 3,992 U/L. You order a myositis panel — the full buffet: anti-SRP, anti-HMGCR, anti-Jo-1, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-γ, NXP-2, and on down the list. Every single one comes back negative. ANA? Negative. So what do you do with a patient who has all the clinical hallmarks of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) and none of the serologic signatures?

This is exactly the scenario described in a recent case report by Lam and colleagues, and it's a scenario that should make every clinician uncomfortable — because approximately one-third of IMNM patients are seronegative. That's not a rounding error. That's a third of patients who will be missed if you anchor on antibody testing.

## Why Seronegative IMNM Is a Diagnostic Trap

The two antibodies most associated with IMNM — anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP — have become the cornerstones of diagnosis, and understandably so. Anti-HMGCR is classically linked to statin exposure, and its discovery helped reclassify patients who had previously been lumped into "toxic myopathy" or "polymyositis" categories for years. But here's the critical lesson from the statin story: before anti-HMGCR was identified, those patients had persistent myopathy and CK elevation despite drug cessation, and they improved with immunosuppression. The clinical behavior pointed to an autoimmune process before the antibody confirmed it. The same logic must be applied to seronegative patients today. The absence of a known antibody does not exclude IMNM — it means we haven't yet discovered the relevant antibody (or trigger) for that subset of patients.

In this case, the patient had never taken statins, denied red yeast rice or mushroom consumption, and had no family history of myopathy. Her myositis panel was completely negative. Yet her clinical course — progressive proximal weakness, CK climbing 12-fold over four months, histologic confirmation on biopsy, and response to immunosuppression — was textbook IMNM.

## The Muscle Biopsy: Where the Diagnosis Actually Lives

When the antibody panel is empty, muscle biopsy shifts from confirmatory to essential. The histologic hallmark of IMNM is necrotising myofibres with a paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates — and that distinction from polymyositis matters enormously. Polymyositis classically shows endomysial inflammation with CD8+ T cells surrounding and invading non-necrotic fibres. IMNM, by contrast, is dominated by necrosis and regeneration with macrophage-predominant infiltrates. The inflammatory cells you see are mostly there to clean up dead muscle, not to attack living fibres.

In this patient's biopsy of the right vastus lateralis, the findings were archetypal: dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibres, CD68-positive macrophages with myophagocytosis, and membrane attack complex deposition on both necrotising and non-necrotising myofibres. Rare CD3-positive T cells were present but sparse. This is the histologic fingerprint of IMNM — and it is what separated this diagnosis from a toxic myopathy, where you would expect to see regenerative changes without the complement-mediated attack pattern.

## Distinguishing IMNM from Drug-Induced Rhabdomyolysis

The temporal relationship to semaglutide in this case is striking — the patient received a single dose and within hours developed nausea, vomiting, and metabolic derangement. But the muscle story didn't unfold like rhabdomyolysis. A prior case report of tirzepatide-associated rhabdomyolysis showed the classic pattern: CK in the tens of thousands at presentation, with rapid improvement within days of drug withdrawal and hydration alone, no immunosuppression needed. That is the signature of direct toxic injury.

This patient's CK was only modestly elevated (337 U/L) at initial presentation and then climbed to 3,992 U/L over four months — while she was off the drug entirely. That trajectory — worsening after withdrawal, requiring immunosuppression to resolve — is incompatible with a purely toxic mechanism. It is, however, entirely consistent with an immune-mediated process that was triggered (or unmasked) by the drug but then became self-sustaining. This is the same pattern that was observed with statin-associated IMNM before anti-HMGCR was discovered, and it is the clinical reasoning that allowed the authors to classify this as probable drug-induced IMNM using WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre causality criteria.

## The SS-B Red Herring

Here's a detail that could have derailed the diagnosis: the patient had a low-titer positive SS-B antibody (2.4, normal <1.0) in the setting of a negative ANA and negative SS-A. There are no published reports linking isolated SS-B positivity — without ANA or SS-A — to IMNM. The patient had no sicca symptoms, no synovitis, no other features of Sjögren's syndrome or connective tissue disease. In context, this appears to be an incidental finding, the kind of low-titer autoantibody that can appear in healthy individuals and muddle an already complex picture. The lesson is straightforward: do not let a weakly positive, contextually isolated autoantibody pull you away from a clinical and histologic diagnosis that fits.

## How to Approach the Seronegative Patient

The reasoning chain in this case provides a template for approaching any patient with progressive proximal weakness, significant CK elevation, and a negative antibody panel. First, confirm the temporal course — is the CK rising despite withdrawal of any potential trigger? That distinguishes immune-mediated necrotising myopathy from direct toxic injury. Second, obtain a muscle biopsy and look for the specific pattern: necrotising and regenerating fibres, macrophage-predominant infiltrates, membrane attack complex deposition, and sparse T-cell infiltration. Third, assess the response to immunosuppression — IMNM should respond to corticosteroids and IVIG, and drug-induced IMNM may resolve with a shorter course than idiopathic disease. Finally, consider the possibility that an as-yet unidentified autoantibody or trigger is at play, and maintain vigilance for pharmacovigilance as new medications like GLP-1 receptor agonists are used at exponentially increasing rates.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **One-third of IMNM patients are seronegative.** A negative myositis panel does not exclude the diagnosis. The absence of a known antibody means the relevant antibody or trigger hasn't been discovered yet.
- **Muscle biopsy is essential when serology is negative.** Look for necrotising myofibres, macrophage-predominant infiltrates (CD68+), and membrane attack complex deposition — with a paucity of T-cell inflammation. This distinguishes IMNM from polymyositis and toxic myopathy.
- **The clinical course distinguishes IMNM from rhabdomyolysis.** Toxic injury produces a rapid CK spike that normalizes within days of drug withdrawal. IMNM produces a CK that climbs over weeks to months despite drug cessation and requires immunosuppression to resolve.
- **A drug trigger doesn't mean a toxic mechanism.** Drug-induced IMNM is an immune process that becomes self-sustaining after the trigger — the same pattern seen with statin-associated anti-HMGCR myopathy before the antibody was discovered.
- **Don't anchor on isolated weak autoantibodies.** A low-titer SS-B with negative ANA and SS-A, in the absence of any clinical features of connective tissue disease, should not redirect your diagnostic reasoning.
- **GLP-1 receptor agonists may be a new trigger for IMNM.** With exponentially increasing use of semaglutide and related agents, clinicians should be aware of this potential association and maintain pharmacovigilance. Rechallenge would be unethical given the severity of the reaction.



# Drug-Induced vs. Idiopathic IMNM: Does the Trigger Change the Treatment Plan?

## The Case That Stops You in Your Tracks

A woman in her 60s receives a single dose of semaglutide. Within hours, she's vomiting. Within days, she's in the ICU with acute kidney injury and metabolic acidosis. And within four months, she can't stand, can't sit upright without assistance, can't swallow — with a CK of 3992 U/L and a muscle biopsy showing necrotising myopathy with macrophage-mediated myophagocytosis and membrane attack complex deposition. One dose. That's all it took.

What makes this case from Lam and colleagues so striking isn't just the severity — it's the temporal relationship. A single 0.25 mg subcutaneous dose of semaglutide preceded a 12-fold rise in CK over four months, culminating in immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) severe enough to cause dysphagia, dysphonia, and profound proximal weakness. And here's the part that should make you sit up: after pulse methylprednisolone, oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg, and a single course of IVIG at 2 g/kg, she was weaned off all immunosuppression within two months and remained in remission at ten months. Off everything. Normal CK. Baseline functional status.

That is not the typical IMNM trajectory we've been trained to expect.

## Why This Matters: The Treatment Duration Question

When we think about IMNM, we think about a disease that fights back. Idiopathic IMNM — particularly the anti-SRP and anti-HMGCR subtypes — is notorious for requiring prolonged, aggressive immunosuppression. We're talking years of combination therapy, often steroids plus an additional agent, with high relapse rates when immunosuppression is tapered. The muscle necrosis is relentless, the functional impairment can be devastating, and patients frequently need repeated courses of IVIG or even rituximab to maintain disease control.

So when this patient walked (literally, eventually) off all immunosuppression after a two-month course and stayed in remission, it challenges us to think about what drives the disease course. Is it the underlying immunopathology alone, or does the trigger matter?

The argument here is biologically plausible. If an exogenous agent triggers an immune response against muscle fibres, and that agent is removed, the immune system may lose its ongoing stimulus. The self-amplifying loop of necrosis, antigen release, and further immune activation could theoretically be interrupted more easily when the inciting trigger is gone. In contrast, idiopathic IMNM may represent a self-sustaining autoimmune process without an identifiable external driver — meaning the immune system has crossed the threshold into autonomous self-destruction, and removing a trigger isn't an option because there isn't one to remove.

## The Statin Paradox: When Trigger Removal Isn't Enough

But before we get too comfortable with this framework, we need to talk about the elephant in the room: statin-associated HMGCR-positive IMNM.

Statin-associated IMNM is the classic example of drug-induced IMNM, and it's the one that complicates the "remove the trigger and the disease resolves" narrative. We know that statins can trigger HMGCR autoantibody-positive IMNM, and we also know that stopping the statin does not reliably stop the disease. These patients frequently require sustained immunosuppression — often for years — even after statin cessation. Some never fully come off treatment. The autoantibody persists, the immune response is self-directed, and the disease behaves more like idiopathic IMNM than a self-limited drug reaction.

Why the difference? One possibility is that statins, by upregulating HMGCR expression in muscle, create a sustained antigenic target that persists even after the drug is gone. The autoantibody is directed against the enzyme itself, not the drug. Once the immune system has launched this response, it may not need the drug anymore — the muscle is the target, and the muscle is still there.

In our semaglutide case, the patient was seronegative for HMGCR, SRP, and the full myositis panel. No identifiable autoantibody. This could be the key distinction. Perhaps drug-induced IMNM without a persistent autoantibody target truly can be more self-limited — the immune response is driven by the drug or a drug-altered antigen, and once the drug is gone, the stimulus disappears. The one-third of IMNM patients who are seronegative may include a subset whose disease is genuinely trigger-dependent and potentially more curable.

## Clinical Reasoning: How Do We Decide How Long to Treat?

Here's the practical question that matters at the bedside: when you diagnose IMNM in a patient with a clear drug trigger, how aggressive do you need to be, and for how long?

In this case, the treating team made several reasonable decisions. First, they used pulse methylprednisolone — 1000 mg IV daily for five days — to rapidly suppress the immune response. Then they transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg. And because of persistent severe weakness, they added IVIG at 2 g/kg over four days. This is a solid induction regimen. It's not minimal, but it's also not the open-ended, multi-agent approach we often default to with idiopathic IMNM.

What's notable is what they did next: they watched. They followed the CK. They followed the functional status. And when both normalised, they tapered — quickly. No long-term steroid-sparing agent was added. No maintenance IVIG. The patient's CK stayed normal. She regained baseline function. She stayed in remission.

The contrast with statin-associated HMGCR-positive IMNM is instructive. In those patients, CK normalisation on treatment doesn't guarantee remission off treatment. Relapse rates are high, and many clinicians maintain immunosuppression for extended periods. The autoantibody serves as both a disease marker and a warning that the immune system has committed to a self-sustaining response.

So the clinical reasoning might go something like this: for drug-induced IMNM with an identified trigger that has been removed, particularly in seronegative patients, a finite induction course with close monitoring may be appropriate. For drug-induced IMNM with a persistent autoantibody (HMGCR-positive, statin-associated), prolonged treatment is more likely necessary. And for idiopathic IMNM, plan for the long haul.

## What About Rechallenge?

Nobody is going to rechallenge this patient with semaglutide. The authors explicitly state this would be unethical, and they're right. But the absence of rechallenge data is a limitation that's worth acknowledging. The WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre criteria classify this as "probable" causality — reasonable time relationship, unlikely attributable to other diseases or drugs, and clinical response upon withdrawal. But probable is not certain. We cannot exclude that this was idiopathic IMNM that happened to follow a single semaglutide dose by coincidence, though the 12-fold CK rise in temporal association makes that less likely.

The broader pharmacovigilance point is critical. GLP-1 receptor agonists are being prescribed at an extraordinary rate. A prior case report described rhabdomyolysis with necrotising features associated with tirzepatide, but that patient's course was classic drug-induced rhabdomyolysis — CK of 57,014, rapid improvement within days of withdrawal, no immunosuppression needed. Our patient's course was fundamentally different: a slower, progressive immune-mediated process requiring immunosuppression. These are distinct entities, and clinicians should be aware that GLP-1 agonists may be associated with both.

## Cardiac Involvement: Don't Forget the Myocardium

One more pearl from this case worth flagging. This patient had cardiac MRI findings concerning for myocardial inflammation — mild hypokinesis of the mid-anteroseptal and anterior wall with myocardial oedema, no late gadolinium enhancement. Her pro-BNP was markedly elevated at 4358 pg/mL. She also developed paroxysmal atrial fibrillation during her initial hospitalisation. IMNM can affect cardiac muscle, and while the cardiac findings here were inconclusive for definitive myocarditis, they normalised on repeat imaging after treatment. The point: when you're evaluating IMNM, think about the heart. It's not just skeletal muscle.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Drug-induced IMNM may follow a more self-limited course than idiopathic IMNM when the offending agent is withdrawn**, particularly in seronegative patients. This case achieved sustained remission after a finite course of pulse steroids, oral prednisone, and a single IVIG cycle — with complete weaning off immunosuppression within two months.
- **Statin-associated HMGCR-positive IMNM is the exception that proves the rule.** Despite being drug-triggered, it often requires prolonged immunosuppression because the autoantibody targets muscle antigen that persists after drug withdrawal. The presence or absence of a persistent autoantibody may be the critical determinant of disease self-limitation.
- **GLP-1 receptor agonists may be associated with both toxic rhabdomyolysis and immune-mediated necrotising myopathy** — two mechanistically distinct entities. Rhabdomyolysis resolves rapidly with drug withdrawal and supportive care; IMNM requires immunosuppression and may progress over months.
- **Seronegative IMNM represents about one-third of cases and remains diagnostically challenging.** This case suggests that at least some seronegative cases may be trigger-dependent and potentially more curable with finite treatment — but more data are needed.
- **When managing IMNM, monitor CK trajectory and functional status closely.** A normalising CK on treatment, combined with functional recovery, may support earlier tapering in drug-induced cases — but only with vigilant follow-up, as relapse can occur.
- **Evaluate for cardiac involvement in IMNM.** Cardiac MRI and troponin/BNP should be part of the workup, even if echocardiography is reassuring. Myocardial involvement can be subtle and may normalise with treatment of the underlying myopathy.

## Final Thought

This case raises more questions than it answers, and the authors are honest about that. One case doesn't establish a pattern, and the absence of a baseline CK before semaglutide means we can't fully exclude a smouldering process that was already underway. But with GLP-1 receptor agonist use exploding globally, this report is a bellwether. If you see progressive proximal weakness with CK elevation in a patient on a GLP-1 agonist — even one who took only a single dose — think IMNM, not just rhabdomyolysis. And consider whether the trigger you identify might change not just the diagnosis, but the duration of the treatment plan.



# Myositis and the Heart: Making Sense of Cardiac Findings in Inflammatory Myopathy

## When the Heart Confuses the Picture

A woman in her 60s presents with four months of progressive proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia. Her CK is nearly 4,000. Her muscle biopsy confirms immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). But here's where it gets interesting — she also has a mildly elevated high-sensitivity troponin (62 pg/mL, normal ≤53), a proBNP through the roof at 4,358 pg/mL, an echo showing a borderline RVSP of 46 mmHg, and a cardiac MRI with myocardial edema and mid-anteroseptal hypokinesis but no late gadolinium enhancement (LGE). So does she have myocarditis? Stress cardiomyopathy? Or is the troponin just noise from severe skeletal muscle injury?

This case — the first reported IMNM probably associated with semaglutide — forces us to confront one of the most vexing scenarios in the acute care of myopathy patients: what do we do with ambiguous cardiac findings when the skeletal muscle is on fire?

## The Troponin Problem in Severe Myopathy

Let's start with the troponin. Her hs-troponin was 62 pg/mL — only mildly above the reference range. In a patient with a CK of 3,992 U/L and active necrotising myopathy, how worried should we be?

This is where clinical context matters enormously. The traditional teaching is that cardiac troponin assays are highly cardiac-specific, and for the most part, that holds — modern hs-troponin assays detect cardiac-specific isoforms that are not typically expressed in significant quantities by skeletal muscle. However, there is accumulating evidence that skeletal muscle can express cardiac troponin T in certain disease states, particularly in regenerating skeletal muscle, and patients with severe myopathy or rhabdomyolysis can have troponin elevations without true myocardial injury. The key distinction here is magnitude: a troponin of 62 pg/mL in the setting of a CK near 4,000 is proportionally quite modest. If this were true active myocarditis with enough necrosis to produce MRI-visible edema and wall motion abnormalities, you would often expect a more pronounced troponin elevation. The discordance — massive skeletal muscle injury with only a whisper of troponin elevation — should make you pause before calling this primary myocarditis.

That said, a mildly elevated troponin in this setting is not something to dismiss entirely. You still need the imaging to sort it out.

## The Cardiac MRI: Edema Without LGE — What Does It Mean?

Her cardiac MRI showed mild hypokinesis of the mid-anteroseptal and anterior wall with associated myocardial edema but no definitive LGE. This is a genuinely ambiguous finding, and the authors of the case report appropriately acknowledge the uncertainty — listing both "myocardial inflammation/early myocarditis" and "focal stress-induced cardiomyopathy" as possibilities.

Here's how to think about it. In classic acute myocarditis, the Lake Louise Criteria call for both tissue edema (T2-based) and non-ischemic LGE to establish the diagnosis with higher confidence. The typical LGE pattern in myocarditis is mid-wall or subepicardial, often in the lateral or inferolateral walls, reflecting the epicardial-to-endocardial spread of inflammation. The mid-anteroseptal location in this case is somewhat atypical for classic myocarditis, and the complete absence of LGE makes a definitive diagnosis of myocarditis harder to sustain — though early or mild myocarditis can present without LGE, particularly if the necrosis has not progressed enough to produce a detectable fibrotic or necrotic signal on imaging.

Stress-induced cardiomyopathy (Takotsubo) is the other serious consideration. This patient had been critically ill — acute renal failure, metabolic acidosis, ICU-level care, new atrial fibrillation — all triggered within days of her semaglutide dose. Stress cardiomyopathy can produce regional wall motion abnormalities, myocardial edema on MRI, and troponin and natriuretic peptide elevations, often with minimal or no LGE initially. The mid-ventricular or anteroseptal distribution, while not the classic apical ballooning pattern, is a recognized variant. The markedly elevated proBNP is also consistent with stress cardiomyopathy, which characteristically produces disproportionate natriuretic peptide elevation relative to troponin.

## What About the proBNP and the RVSP?

The proBNP of 4,358 pg/mL is striking. While this could reflect LV dysfunction from myocarditis or stress cardiomyopathy, the echo showed a preserved LVEF of 65–70%. So where is the proBNP coming from? Several contributors are plausible in this patient: new-onset paroxysmal atrial fibrillation during her initial hospitalization, RV strain from the elevated RVSP of 46 mmHg, volume overload related to her acute kidney injury, and the general systemic stress of critical illness. The RVSP itself is borderline-mild and could reflect left heart disease, pulmonary disease, deconditioning, or even her prior history of DVT/PE, though no acute PE was documented in this presentation.

The point is that in a critically ill patient with multi-system involvement, proBNP and a borderline RVSP are not specific for myocarditis. They are part of the systemic stress picture and need to be interpreted in that light.

## Cardiac Involvement in IMNM Specifically — What Do We Know?

This is an important nuance. IMNM is a distinct entity from polymyositis and dermatomyositis, characterized by muscle fiber necrosis with minimal lymphocytic infiltration and macrophage-predominant pathology — exactly what was seen on this patient's biopsy (CD68+ macrophages with myophagocytosis, membrane attack complex deposition, rare CD3+ T cells). Cardiac involvement has been described across the inflammatory myopathy spectrum, but the data specific to IMNM are more limited. Anti-SRP positive IMNM, in particular, has been associated with higher rates of cardiac involvement, including myocarditis and arrhythmia. This patient was seronegative for both anti-SRP and anti-HMGCR, placing her in the approximately one-third of IMNM patients who are autoantibody-negative — a group about whom we know even less regarding cardiac risk.

The practical takeaway: IMNM patients can have cardiac involvement, but it is not the norm, and when cardiac abnormalities are present, they need to be interpreted alongside the entire clinical picture — the severity of muscle injury, the temporal relationship to drug exposure, the presence of critical illness, and the imaging pattern.

## Follow-Up Imaging: The Great Clarifier

Perhaps the most instructive part of this case is what happened on follow-up. Her repeat echocardiogram and cardiac MRI after treatment and recovery showed complete normalization of all prior cardiac abnormalities. She also had no recurrence of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

This resolution pattern is diagnostically powerful. Active autoimmune myocarditis — particularly in the context of an ongoing systemic autoimmune process like IMNM — would be expected to require targeted cardiac immunosuppression and would carry a risk of residual fibrosis or persistent dysfunction, often detectable as persistent LGE even after functional recovery. The rapid and complete normalization without cardiac-specific immunosuppression, in a patient who was weaned off prednisone over just two months and received only a single course of IVIG, is more consistent with a transient, stress-related process or a self-limited inflammatory insult than with a committed autoimmune myocarditis. The absence of LGE on the initial MRI and the absence of residual findings on follow-up both support this interpretation.

## Putting It Together: A Framework for the Bedside

When you encounter a patient with severe inflammatory myopathy and ambiguous cardiac findings, here is a practical approach:

First, contextualize the troponin. A mild elevation in the setting of massive CK elevation is less alarming than the same troponin in a patient with minimal muscle injury. Proportionality matters. But do not ignore it — get the imaging.

Second, interpret the cardiac MRI pattern carefully. Edema without LGE in an atypical location for myocarditis, in a patient who has been critically ill, should raise stress cardiomyopathy as a serious diagnostic contender. The Lake Louise Criteria require both edema and non-ischemic LGE for a confident MRI diagnosis of myocarditis; absence of LGE does not exclude myocarditis but makes it less certain and should prompt you to consider alternatives.

Third, do not over-attribute proBNP and RVSP to myocarditis. In the critically ill myopathy patient, these values are frequently driven by arrhythmia, RV strain, volume status, and systemic stress.

Fourth, let time and follow-up imaging be your adjudicator. If the cardiac findings resolve completely after treatment of the underlying myopathy and withdrawal of the offending agent — as in this case — the transient, stress-related explanation gains substantial credibility.

## Key Take-Home Points

- **Mild troponin elevation in severe myopathy is common and may reflect skeletal muscle injury or critical illness rather than primary myocarditis, but it still warrants cardiac imaging to adjudicate.**
- **Cardiac MRI with edema but no LGE is diagnostically ambiguous — it can represent early myocarditis, stress cardiomyopathy, or a transient inflammatory insult. Location and clinical context help differentiate.**
- **Stress-induced cardiomyopathy is a serious consideration in IMNM patients who have been critically ill, and can produce wall motion abnormalities, edema, and disproportionate proBNP elevation.**
- **IMNM can involve the heart, but cardiac involvement is not universal; anti-SRP positive disease carries higher cardiac risk, and seronegative IMNM (as in this case) remains poorly characterized.**
- **Follow-up imaging is the key adjudicator — complete resolution without cardiac-specific immunosuppression favors a transient process over committed autoimmune myocarditis.**
- **If the initial MRI lacks LGE and the follow-up MRI is clean, think twice before labeling it myocarditis — the pattern is more consistent with stress cardiomyopathy or a self-limited inflammatory insult.**

## Final Thought

This case is a reminder that in the era of ubiquitous GLP-1 receptor agonist use, we need to keep IMNM on our differential when patients develop progressive proximal weakness and soaring CKs — and when the cardiac workup muddies the waters, a systematic, context-driven approach will keep you from over-diagnosing myocarditis while not missing it when it is truly there. The follow-up image, not the first one, often tells the real story.
