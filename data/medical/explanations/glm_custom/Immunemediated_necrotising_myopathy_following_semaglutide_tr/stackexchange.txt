Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Morning Report] A woman in her 60s presents with 4 months of progressive proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, dysphonia, and CK elevation to ~4000 U/L. What diagnoses should be considered, and what key elements of the history, examination, and laboratory workup would help narrow the differential?

**Clinical Teaching Answer**

This presentation — subacute, progressive proximal weakness with bulbar involvement (dysphagia, dysphonia) and CK in the thousands — should immediately broaden the differential beyond ordinary toxic myopathy or rhabdomyolysis and prompt consideration of an inflammatory myopathy, with **immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM)** at the top of the list.

**Key differential diagnoses:**

- **IMNM** (anti-HMGCR, anti-SRP, or seronegative): Characterized by severe proximal weakness, marked CK elevation (often >10× upper limit of normal), necrotic fibers with paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates on biopsy, and macrophage-predominant myophagocytosis with membrane attack complex (MAC) deposition. About one-third of patients are seronegative, as in this case.
- **Polymyositis**: Endomysial T-cell infiltrate on biopsy; less necrosis-predominant than IMNM.
- **Dermatomyositis**: Would be suggested by Gottron's papules, heliotrope rash, or perifascicular atrophy on biopsy — none of which were present here.
- **Inclusion body myositis**: Typically slower (years, not months), with early distal weakness (finger flexors, knee extensors) and relatively modest CK elevation.
- **Drug-induced toxic myopathy/rhabdomyolysis**: Typically acute, with rapid CK rise and rapid normalization upon drug withdrawal — inconsistent with this patient's 4-month progressive rise.
- **Endocrine myopathy** (hypothyroid): Usually modest CK elevation; excluded by normal TSH here.
- **Overlap myositis / connective tissue disease–associated myopathy**: Less likely with negative ANA, normal complement, and absence of synovitis, sicca, or rash.

**History elements that narrow the differential:**

The temporal relationship to a medication exposure is critical. This patient received a single dose of **semaglutide**, followed within days by acute illness and then 4 months of progressive CK rise (from 337 to nearly 4000 U/L) and worsening weakness — a course inconsistent with direct toxic rhabdomyolysis, which typically shows rapid CK normalization after drug cessation. Her **lack of statin exposure**, mushroom or red yeast rice use, family history of myopathy, or excessive exertion helps exclude common IMNM triggers and toxic etiologies.

**Examination elements:**

The **proximal-predominant weakness** without rash, synovitis, neuropathy, or extraocular involvement points toward a myopathic process rather than neuromuscular junction disease, motor neuron disease, or dermatomyositis. Bulbar involvement (dysphagia, dysphonia) indicates significant disease burden and raises concern for respiratory muscle compromise — an important prognostic consideration.

**Laboratory and diagnostic workup:**

- **CK**: The magnitude (>3000 U/L) and progressive trajectory strongly support an active necrotizing process.
- **Myositis-specific autoantibodies**: Anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP should be checked; their presence would confirm IMNM. In this case, a full myositis panel was negative, placing her in the seronegative IMNM subset — a group where novel triggers and autoantibodies are still being characterized.
- **ANA and overlap antibodies**: To screen for connective tissue disease–associated myositis; their absence, as here, supports a primary necrotizing myopathy.
- **TSH**: Excludes hypothyroid myopathy.
- **EMG and muscle MRI**: MRI can demonstrate symmetric muscular edema in proximal muscle groups, as seen here, supporting an inflammatory process.
- **Muscle biopsy**: This is the **diagnostic gold standard**. The finding of scattered necrotic and regenerating fibers with MAC deposition on non-necrotic fibers, CD68+ macrophages with myophagocytosis, and only rare T cells is the histologic signature of IMNM and distinguishes it from polymyositis (endomysial T-cell inflammation) and dermatomyositis (perifascicular pathology).

**Clinical reasoning specific to this case:**

The patient's CK continued to rise 12-fold over 4 months after a single drug exposure, with progressive weakness and bulbar involvement — a course incompatible with transient toxic injury but consistent with a self-perpetuating immune-mediated process triggered by the drug. Her **response to a short course of corticosteroids and IVIG**, with sustained remission off immunosuppression and continued avoidance of the suspected trigger, further distinguishes drug-provoked IMNM from idiopathic IMNM, which typically requires prolonged immunosuppression. This mirrors the historical recognition of statin-associated IMNM: what was once labeled "toxic myopathy" was later understood as an autoimmune process often persisting after drug withdrawal and responding to immunosuppression — a paradigm now extended, in this report, to GLP-1 receptor agonists as a potential novel trigger.

**Bottom line for the bedside:** In any patient with subacute proximal weakness and CK in the thousands, obtain a myositis antibody panel, TSH, and ANA; pursue MRI and muscle biopsy; and carefully review medication exposures — including statins and, as this case suggests, GLP-1 receptor agonists — as potential immune triggers rather than simple toxins. Biopsy confirmation is essential, because the distinction between IMNM and other inflammatory myopathies fundamentally changes prognosis and treatment strategy.

### [Pathophysiology] What are the characteristic histopathological findings on muscle biopsy that distinguish immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) from polymyositis, and what does the presence of membrane attack complex (MAC/C5b-9) deposition on non-necrotic muscle fibers suggest about the pathogenic mechanism?

## Histopathological Distinction Between IMNM and Polymyositis

The hallmark histopathological feature that distinguishes immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) from polymyositis is the **predominance of muscle fiber necrosis with a paucity of lymphocytic infiltration**. In polymyositis, the classic finding is **endomysial CD8+ T-cell infiltration surrounding and invading non-necrotic muscle fibers**, reflecting a direct, T-cell–mediated cytotoxic process. In contrast, IMNM is characterized by **scattered necrotic and regenerating fibers with minimal lymphocytic infiltrate**, and the inflammatory cells present are predominantly **macrophages** rather than T cells, accompanying myophagocytosis rather than directing cytotoxic attack.

This distinction is clearly illustrated in the present case. The biopsy of the patient's vastus lateralis demonstrated **dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers** with **CD68-positive macrophages and acid phosphatase staining showing myophagocytosis**, while **CD3-positive T cells were only rare**. This pattern — prominent necrosis with macrophage-predominant infiltrate and few T cells — is the histological signature of IMNM and would be atypical for polymyositis, where T-cell invasion of non-necrotic fibers would be expected as the dominant feature.

## Significance of MAC/C5b-9 Deposition on Non-Necrotic Fibers

The deposition of **membrane attack complex (MAC/C5b-9) on non-necrotic muscle fibers** is a critical finding with important mechanistic implications. In this case, MAC staining was positive on **both necrotic and non-necrotic myofibers**, indicating that **complement activation is occurring on intact muscle fiber membranes before overt necrosis develops**. This finding supports a **complement-mediated, antibody-dependent pathogenic mechanism** in IMNM, in which autoantibodies — such as anti-SRP or anti-HMGCR, when present — activate the classical complement cascade on the muscle fiber surface, leading to assembly of the MAC and formation of membrane pores. The resulting osmotic injury and membrane disruption drive fiber necrosis. The presence of MAC on non-necrotic fibers suggests these fibers are in a **pre-necrotic stage of immune injury**, with complement deposition as the initiating event rather than a secondary consequence of necrosis.

This mechanism stands in contrast to the **direct T-cell cytotoxicity** that drives fiber injury in polymyositis. Importantly, in this patient's case, both anti-SRP and anti-HMGCR antibodies were negative, placing her in the seronegative IMNM subset, which comprises roughly one-third of IMNM cases. The demonstration of complement deposition on non-necrotic fibers nonetheless supports an immune-mediated process — even in the absence of a identified autoantibody — and reinforces the rationale for immunosuppressive therapy, to which she responded with rapid CK normalisation and sustained clinical recovery.

### [Clinical Pearls] Which two myositis-specific autoantibodies are most strongly associated with IMNM? What medication class is a well-known trigger for one of these antibody-associated forms, and approximately what proportion of IMNM patients are seronegative for both?

The two myositis-specific autoantibodies most strongly associated with immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) are **anti–hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (anti-HMGCR)** and **anti–signal recognition particle (anti-SRP)** antibodies. These antibodies define the major serologic subgroups of IMNM and are important not only for diagnosis but also for understanding the pathogenesis and potential triggers of the disease.

**Statin medications** are the well-known trigger for the anti-HMGCR–associated form. As described in the case report, the recognition of this association emerged from clinical observation: patients treated with statins for metabolic disorders developed necrotising myopathy with persistent CK elevation and progressive weakness even after statin discontinuation — features inconsistent with simple toxic myopathy. Their clinical improvement with immunosuppression ultimately led to the identification of anti-HMGCR autoantibody as a hallmark of this disease entity. This pattern — an immune-mediated process triggered by a medication but persisting after drug withdrawal — is a key diagnostic principle that distinguishes IMNM from direct drug-induced toxic myopathy or rhabdomyolysis.

Importantly, approximately **one-third of IMNM patients are seronegative** for both anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP antibodies. This seronegative subgroup poses significant diagnostic challenges and suggests that additional, yet-to-be-identified autoantibodies and environmental or pharmacologic triggers remain to be discovered. The presented case exemplifies this gap: the patient had a complete myositis panel negative for anti-HMGCR, anti-SRP, and all other known myositis-specific autoantibodies, yet her clinical course — progressive proximal weakness, marked CK elevation, necrotising myopathy on biopsy with macrophage-predominant infiltrate and membrane attack complex deposition, and sustained response to immunosuppression — was fully consistent with IMNM. Her temporal association with semaglutide raises the possibility that GLP-1 receptor agonists may represent a novel trigger in a seronegative IMNM subset, though causality remains unproven.

This case reinforces several practical clinical principles: (1) IMNM should be suspected in patients with severe proximal weakness, marked CK elevation, and necrotising myopathy on biopsy even when the autoantibody panel is negative; (2) a paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates on histology — with macrophage-predominant myophagocytosis and complement activation — distinguishes IMNM from polymyositis; (3) persistent or progressive myopathy after withdrawal of a potential trigger, rather than rapid improvement, favors an immune-mediated process requiring immunosuppression; and (4) the expanding therapeutic landscape for metabolic disease may introduce new pharmacologic triggers for IMNM that clinicians should remain vigilant about.

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a patient who develops muscle weakness and CK elevation after exposure to a medication, what clinical and laboratory features distinguish drug-induced immune-mediated necrotising myopathy from direct toxic rhabdomyolysis, and how does the CK trajectory differ between the two conditions?

## Distinguishing Drug-Induced IMNM from Direct Toxic Rhabdomyolysis

The critical distinction lies in the **temporal relationship between drug discontinuation and the CK trajectory**, and this case illustrates it well.

In **direct toxic rhabdomyolysis**, CK rises acutely during drug exposure and normalizes within days to weeks after drug withdrawal and supportive hydration—no immunosuppression is required. The report references a prior case of tirzepatide-associated rhabdomyolysis in which the patient presented with a CK of 57,014 U/L that began improving within days of drug discontinuation, following a classic toxic pattern.

In **drug-induced immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM)**, the mechanism is not direct toxicity but rather an autoinflammatory process triggered by the medication. The hallmark is **progressive CK elevation and worsening weakness that continue despite drug cessation**, because the immune response is self-sustaining. In this patient, CK was only mildly elevated at 337 U/L at initial presentation after a single semaglutide dose, yet it **rose 12-fold to 3,992 U/L over the following 4 months while she was entirely off the drug**. This trajectory is fundamentally incompatible with a direct toxic effect and is the single most informative feature.

Several additional features support IMNM over toxic rhabdomyolysis in this case:

- **Progressive proximal weakness with dysphagia and dysphonia**, evolving subacutely over months rather than resolving after drug withdrawal.
- **Histopathology** showing necrotic myofibers with macrophage-predominant inflammation (CD68 positivity, myophagocytosis), membrane attack complex deposition on both necrotic and non-necrotic fibers, and only rare T-cell infiltrates—the characteristic pattern of IMNM rather than purely toxic necrosis.
- **Sustained improvement with immunosuppression** (pulse methylprednisolone, oral prednisone, and a single course of IVIG) rather than with hydration alone, with normalization of CK and return to baseline function off all immunosuppression.

Autoantibodies such as anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP support a diagnosis of IMNM when present, but approximately one-third of IMNM patients are seronegative—as this patient was—so their absence does not exclude the diagnosis.

In summary, when a patient develops CK elevation after medication exposure, the key distinguishing question is whether CK normalizes promptly after drug withdrawal (favoring direct toxic rhabdomyolysis) or continues to rise with progressive weakness (favoring immune-mediated necrotising myopathy). The latter mandates immunosuppressive therapy, not merely supportive care.

### [Management] What is the standard treatment approach for IMNM, and how might the treatment course differ between drug-induced IMNM (after withdrawal of the offending agent) and idiopathic IMNM in terms of intensity and duration of immunosuppression?

# Treatment of IMNM: Drug-Induced vs. Idiopathic

The standard treatment approach for immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) centers on immunosuppression, typically beginning with high-dose corticosteroids — oral prednisone at 0.5–1 mg/kg/day, or pulse intravenous methylprednisolone in severe presentations — often in combination with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), particularly when weakness is profound, dysphagia is present, or CK elevation is marked. In this case, the patient received both pulse methylprednisolone (1000 mg daily for 5 days) and IVIG at 2 g/kg over 4 days, reflecting the severity of her presentation with progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, dysphonia, and a peak CK of nearly 4000 U/L. Additional steroid-sparing agents — such as methotrexate, azathioprine, or mycophenolate mofetil, and in refractory cases rituximab — are frequently required, particularly for idiopathic IMNM.

The critical distinction lies in the anticipated duration of therapy. Idiopathic IMNM, particularly when associated with anti-SRP or anti-HMGCR antibodies, typically follows a relapsing or chronic course and often demands prolonged, intensive immunosuppression with sequential or combination agents to achieve and sustain remission. In contrast, drug-induced IMNM — as illustrated by this case, where the putative trigger was a single dose of semaglutide — may resolve with a comparatively short course of immunosuppression provided the offending agent is withdrawn and avoided. After her initial treatment, this patient was rapidly weaned off prednisone over approximately two months, received no additional IVIG courses, and remained off all immunosuppression for eight months with sustained normal CK levels and full return to baseline function. The persistent rise in CK over four months despite only a single drug exposure — rather than rapid normalization expected with direct toxic rhabdomyolysis — supported an immune-mediated mechanism triggered by the drug, and the durable response to brief immunosuppression after drug cessation is characteristic of this drug-induced phenotype. This clinical trajectory contrasts sharply with the prolonged treatment requirements expected in idiopathic IMNM, where ongoing immunosuppression is usually necessary to prevent relapse.

### [Clinical Pearls] What adverse effects on skeletal muscle have been described with GLP-1 receptor agonists? How does the clinical course of the previously reported tirzepatide-associated rhabdomyolysis case differ from the semaglutide-associated IMNM described in this report?

## Adverse Skeletal Muscle Effects of GLP-1 Receptor Agonists and Distinguishing Tirzepatide-Associated Rhabdomyolysis from Semaglutide-Associated IMNM

GLP-1 receptor agonists have been associated with two distinct categories of skeletal muscle adverse effects. The first is a loss of skeletal muscle mass, which has been observed as a class effect and is thought to relate to the substantial weight loss these agents produce, potentially reducing lean body mass alongside fat mass. The second category includes clinically significant myotoxicity, encompassing rhabdomyolysis and, as described in this report, immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM).

The previously reported tirzepatide-associated rhabdomyolysis case and the semaglutide-associated IMNM described here differ fundamentally in their temporal profile, mechanism, and treatment requirements, illustrating the critical distinction between direct toxic injury and immune-mediated disease.

In the tirzepatide case, the patient developed an acute, marked creatine kinase (CK) elevation—approximately 57,000 U/L at presentation—while on active treatment. Critically, the CK began improving within days of drug discontinuation and supportive hydration, and no immunosuppression was required. This pattern is consistent with drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, in which the offending agent causes direct myofiber injury and the process resolves once the toxic exposure is removed.

In contrast, the patient in this report received only a single dose of semaglutide and had a modestly elevated CK of 337 U/L at her initial hospitalization. Over the subsequent 4 months—entirely off the medication—her CK increased 12-fold to nearly 4,000 U/L, accompanied by progressive proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia. This continued deterioration after drug withdrawal is incompatible with a purely toxic mechanism, which would be expected to stabilize or improve once the agent is discontinued. Instead, her course reflects an autoimmune process: the muscle biopsy demonstrated necrotizing myofibers with macrophage-predominant inflammation (CD68-positive myophagocytosis), membrane attack complex deposition on both necrotizing and non-necrotizing fibers, and rare T-cell infiltrates—features diagnostic of IMNM. Furthermore, her sustained response to a short course of corticosteroids and IVIG, with normalization of CK and return to baseline function off all immunosuppression for months, aligns with drug-triggered autoimmune myopathy rather than idiopathic IMNM, which typically requires prolonged immunosuppression.

In summary, the tirzepatide case exemplifies direct toxic rhabdomyolysis with rapid CK resolution upon drug withdrawal and no need for immunosuppression, whereas this semaglutide case represents a drug-triggered immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy characterized by progressive CK elevation and weakness despite drug discontinuation, necessitating immunosuppressive therapy. This distinction mirrors the historical evolution in understanding of statin-associated myopathy, where cases initially classified as toxic myopathy were later recognized as autoimmune IMNM when muscle weakness and CK elevation persisted despite statin withdrawal and improved with immunosuppression.

### [Differential Diagnosis] A patient with known hepatic steatosis and an inflammatory myopathy has elevated transaminases (AST and ALT) and a mildly elevated high-sensitivity troponin. How would you determine whether these abnormalities are due to the myopathy itself versus hepatic and cardiac disease, and what additional testing might help clarify the source?

# Differentiating Myopathic vs. Hepatic and Cardiac Sources of Elevated Transaminases and Troponin

## Key Clinical Reasoning

The critical principle is that **aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) are not liver-specific enzymes.** Both are found in skeletal muscle, cardiac muscle, and hepatic tissue, though ALT is present in relatively lower concentrations in skeletal muscle compared to AST. In the setting of significant muscle injury — particularly necrotizing myopathy with CK levels in the thousands — elevations in AST and ALT are expected and do not necessarily indicate concurrent hepatic dysfunction.

### Distinguishing Hepatic from Myopathic Transaminitis

Several features help separate the two sources:

1. **Magnitude and ratio of enzyme elevation.** In myopathy, AST tends to be disproportionately elevated relative to ALT, reflecting the higher AST content in skeletal muscle. In pure hepatic disease, particularly steatohepatitis, ALT is often greater than AST, as was documented in this patient's liver evaluation three years prior to her myopathy onset (ALT greater than AST, both approximately twice the upper limit of normal).

2. **Correlation with creatine kinase.** CK is the most specific serum marker for skeletal muscle injury. When AST and ALT elevations parallel marked CK elevations, a myopathic contribution is likely. In this case, at the time of her acute presentation with CK of 1,237 U/L (and a recent peak of 3,992 U/L), her AST was 191 U/L and ALT was 165 U/L — elevations that are fully consistent with muscle breakdown.

3. **Persistence after CK normalization.** This is the most discriminating feature in this case. The authors specifically note that her borderline AST and ALT elevations **persisted even after her CK levels normalized** following immunosuppressive therapy. This observation strongly supports a hepatic source for the residual transaminitis rather than ongoing muscle injury. Had the transaminase elevation been purely myopathic, one would expect them to normalize in concert with CK.

4. **Liver biopsy.** The repeat liver biopsy performed during her admission demonstrated macrovesicular steatosis encompassing approximately 70% of the parenchyma with progression to NAFLD Activity Score grade 5/8, stage 1 — confirming active, ongoing steatohepatitis as the substrate for her hepatic enzyme elevation.

### Evaluating the Cardiac Source

The mildly elevated high-sensitivity troponin (62 pg/mL; normal ≤53 pg/mL) and markedly elevated proBNP (4,358 pg/mL) warranted careful cardiac evaluation, particularly given that high-sensitivity troponin assays — while highly specific for cardiac troponin — can be elevated in the setting of skeletal myopathy due to shared epitopes or renal clearance issues, though true cross-reactivity is minimal with modern assays.

The additional testing that clarified the cardiac picture included:

- **Echocardiography**, which showed preserved left ventricular systolic function (LVEF 65%–70%) but elevated right ventricular systolic pressure (46 mm Hg), the latter potentially reflecting volume status or pulmonary considerations.
- **Cardiac MRI**, which demonstrated mild hypokinesis of the mid-anteroseptal and anterior walls with myocardial edema but **no late gadolinium enhancement**, no significant scarring or infarction, and no significant valvular dysfunction. These findings were interpreted as potentially representing early myocarditis or focal stress-induced cardiomyopathy.
- **Repeat echocardiography and cardiac MRI** during follow-up, which showed **complete normalization** of the previously documented abnormalities. This resolution, combined with the absence of definitive late gadolinium enhancement on the initial study, made active myocarditis less likely and favored a transient, stress-related phenomenon — particularly given the severity of her systemic illness and deconditioning.

## Summary of Approach

In a patient with inflammatory myopathy and known hepatic steatosis, the approach to elevated transaminases and troponin should be **multimodal and longitudinal**:

| Assessment | What It Clarifies |
|---|---|
| CK level and trend | Confirms and quantifies muscle injury |
| AST-to-ALT ratio | Myopathy typically yields AST > ALT; hepatic disease often yields ALT > AST |
| Persistence of transaminase elevation after CK normalization | If transaminases persist, hepatic source is implicated |
| Liver biopsy | Definitively characterizes hepatic pathology |
| Echocardiography | Assesses ventricular function and pulmonary pressures |
| Cardiac MRI with late gadolinium enhancement | Distinguishes myocarditis (edema ± LGE) from stress cardiomyopathy (typically no LGE) |
| Repeat cardiac imaging | Normalization supports transient, reversible process over ongoing myocarditis |

In this case, the combination of persistent transaminitis after CK normalization, biopsy-confirmed progressive steatohepatitis, and reversible cardiac imaging abnormalities ultimately supported the conclusion that the **hepatic enzyme elevation was primarily of hepatic origin** (from NAFLD) and that the **cardiac findings likely represented a transient stress-related phenomenon** rather than active myocarditis — while the **marked CK elevation and proximal muscle weakness** were the hallmarks of her immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy.

### [Management] According to the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre causality assessment criteria, what factors in this case support 'probable' (as opposed to 'possible' or 'certain') causality between semaglutide and IMNM, and why was rechallenge not performed?

## Probable Causality per WHO-UMC Criteria: Semaglutide and IMNM

The WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) system defines **probable** causality as an adverse event with a reasonable temporal relationship to drug exposure, unlikely attributable to alternative diseases or medications, and a clinically reasonable response upon drug withdrawal — without requiring rechallenge. This case satisfies each of these criteria while falling short of "certain" on two explicit grounds.

**Reasonable time relationship.** The patient developed progressive proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia with a 12-fold CK rise (from 337 U/L at initial presentation to a peak of 3992 U/L) over the 4 months following her single dose of semaglutide. The temporal sequence — drug exposure followed by insidious but progressive myopathy — is consistent with an immune-mediated process rather than acute direct toxicity, which would be expected to resolve within days of drug cessation, as illustrated by the previously reported tirzepatide rhabdomyolysis case (CK >57,000 with rapid improvement on withdrawal alone).

**Unlikely due to other diseases or drugs.** The patient had no prior statin use, no consumption of supplements associated with IMNM (such as red yeast rice or mushrooms), no family history of myopathy, and no excessive physical activity. Her myositis panel was negative for anti-SRP, anti-HMGCR, and all other known myositis-specific antibodies. She had no clinical features of a connective tissue disease, and her isolated low-titer anti-SSB positivity (with negative ANA and anti-SSA) has no known association with IMNM. The authors acknowledge they cannot definitively exclude a pre-existing smoldering IMNM because no baseline CK was available before semaglutide initiation — a limitation that also contributes to the assessment remaining at "probable" rather than "certain."

**Clinically reasonable response on withdrawal.** Following cessation of semaglutide and treatment with pulse methylprednisolone, oral prednisone, and a single course of IVIG, the patient's CK normalized, her strength improved markedly, and she returned to baseline functional status. She remained well off all immunosuppression for 8 months with continued avoidance of GLP-1 receptor agonists. This sustained remission after a short immunosuppression course is more characteristic of drug-induced IMNM than idiopathic IMNM, which typically requires prolonged, intensive immunosuppression.

**Why not "possible"?** The "possible" category applies when the event could also be reasonably explained by the underlying disease or concomitant drugs. Here, the extensive negative workup effectively excluded alternative etiologies, and the clinical course aligned specifically with a drug-triggered immune mechanism, thereby supporting a higher confidence level.

**Why not "certain" and why no rechallenge?** The "certain" category requires that the event be confirmed by reappearance upon rechallenge. Rechallenge was deemed unethical given the severity of the initial reaction — the patient developed profound quadriparesis with dysphagia requiring hospitalization and aggressive immunosuppression. Deliberately re-exposing her to semaglutide would risk recurrence of a potentially life-threatening myopathy. The absence of rechallenge, combined with the lack of a pre-drug CK measurement, precludes assignment of "certain" causality and appropriately caps the assessment at "probable."
