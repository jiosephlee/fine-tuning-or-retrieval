Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Differential Diagnosis] A patient with known hepatic steatosis and an inflammatory myopathy has elevated transaminases (AST and ALT) and a mildly elevated high-sensitivity troponin. How would you determine whether these abnormalities are due to the myopathy itself versus hepatic and cardiac disease, and what additional testing might help clarify the source?

# Differentiating Myopathic vs. Hepatic and Cardiac Sources of Elevated Transaminases and Troponin

## Key Clinical Reasoning

The critical principle is that **aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) are not liver-specific enzymes.** Both are found in skeletal muscle, cardiac muscle, and hepatic tissue, though ALT is present in relatively lower concentrations in skeletal muscle compared to AST. In the setting of significant muscle injury — particularly necrotizing myopathy with CK levels in the thousands — elevations in AST and ALT are expected and do not necessarily indicate concurrent hepatic dysfunction.

### Distinguishing Hepatic from Myopathic Transaminitis

Several features help separate the two sources:

1. **Magnitude and ratio of enzyme elevation.** In myopathy, AST tends to be disproportionately elevated relative to ALT, reflecting the higher AST content in skeletal muscle. In pure hepatic disease, particularly steatohepatitis, ALT is often greater than AST, as was documented in this patient's liver evaluation three years prior to her myopathy onset (ALT greater than AST, both approximately twice the upper limit of normal).

2. **Correlation with creatine kinase.** CK is the most specific serum marker for skeletal muscle injury. When AST and ALT elevations parallel marked CK elevations, a myopathic contribution is likely. In this case, at the time of her acute presentation with CK of 1,237 U/L (and a recent peak of 3,992 U/L), her AST was 191 U/L and ALT was 165 U/L — elevations that are fully consistent with muscle breakdown.

3. **Persistence after CK normalization.** This is the most discriminating feature in this case. The authors specifically note that her borderline AST and ALT elevations **persisted even after her CK levels normalized** following immunosuppressive therapy. This observation strongly supports a hepatic source for the residual transaminitis rather than ongoing muscle injury. Had the transaminase elevation been purely myopathic, one would expect them to normalize in concert with CK.

4. **Liver biopsy.** The repeat liver biopsy performed during her admission demonstrated macrovesicular steatosis encompassing approximately 70% of the parenchyma with progression to NAFLD Activity Score grade 5/8, stage 1 — confirming active, ongoing steatohepatitis as the substrate for her hepatic enzyme elevation.

### Evaluating the Cardiac Source

The mildly elevated high-sensitivity troponin (62 pg/mL; normal ≤53 pg/mL) and markedly elevated proBNP (4,358 pg/mL) warranted careful cardiac evaluation, particularly given that high-sensitivity troponin assays — while highly specific for cardiac troponin — can be elevated in the setting of skeletal myopathy due to shared epitopes or renal clearance issues, though true cross-reactivity is minimal with modern assays.

The additional testing that clarified the cardiac picture included:

- **Echocardiography**, which showed preserved left ventricular systolic function (LVEF 65%–70%) but elevated right ventricular systolic pressure (46 mm Hg), the latter potentially reflecting volume status or pulmonary considerations.
- **Cardiac MRI**, which demonstrated mild hypokinesis of the mid-anteroseptal and anterior walls with myocardial edema but **no late gadolinium enhancement**, no significant scarring or infarction, and no significant valvular dysfunction. These findings were interpreted as potentially representing early myocarditis or focal stress-induced cardiomyopathy.
- **Repeat echocardiography and cardiac MRI** during follow-up, which showed **complete normalization** of the previously documented abnormalities. This resolution, combined with the absence of definitive late gadolinium enhancement on the initial study, made active myocarditis less likely and favored a transient, stress-related phenomenon — particularly given the severity of her systemic illness and deconditioning.

## Summary of Approach

In a patient with inflammatory myopathy and known hepatic steatosis, the approach to elevated transaminases and troponin should be **multimodal and longitudinal**:

| Assessment | What It Clarifies |
|---|---|
| CK level and trend | Confirms and quantifies muscle injury |
| AST-to-ALT ratio | Myopathy typically yields AST > ALT; hepatic disease often yields ALT > AST |
| Persistence of transaminase elevation after CK normalization | If transaminases persist, hepatic source is implicated |
| Liver biopsy | Definitively characterizes hepatic pathology |
| Echocardiography | Assesses ventricular function and pulmonary pressures |
| Cardiac MRI with late gadolinium enhancement | Distinguishes myocarditis (edema ± LGE) from stress cardiomyopathy (typically no LGE) |
| Repeat cardiac imaging | Normalization supports transient, reversible process over ongoing myocarditis |

In this case, the combination of persistent transaminitis after CK normalization, biopsy-confirmed progressive steatohepatitis, and reversible cardiac imaging abnormalities ultimately supported the conclusion that the **hepatic enzyme elevation was primarily of hepatic origin** (from NAFLD) and that the **cardiac findings likely represented a transient stress-related phenomenon** rather than active myocarditis — while the **marked CK elevation and proximal muscle weakness** were the hallmarks of her immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy.
