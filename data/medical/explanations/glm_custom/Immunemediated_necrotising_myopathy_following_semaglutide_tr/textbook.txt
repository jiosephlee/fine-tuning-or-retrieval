Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

## Background and Clinical Presentation

The patient was a woman in her 60s whose medical history was notable for obesity, hepatosteatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a remote history of venous thromboembolism. She had been fully independent in her activities of daily living and in good overall health prior to the events described in this report. Three years before her presentation with myopathy, she underwent evaluation for chronically elevated transaminases—AST and ALT approximately twice the upper limit of normal, with ALT greater than AST. Her viral hepatitis serologies, antinuclear antibodies, anti-mitochondrial antibodies, anti-smooth muscle antibodies, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies were all negative, and she denied alcohol use or hepatotoxic medications. Abdominal ultrasound demonstrated hepatomegaly with diffusely increased echotexture, and liver biopsy confirmed steatohepatitis consistent with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), with an NAFLD Activity Score of 4/8 and stage 0 fibrosis. This background of metabolic disease—obesity and biopsy-proven NAFLD—prompted her subsequent treatment with a glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist.

GLP-1 receptor agonists such as semaglutide have become widely prescribed for type 2 diabetes and obesity. Their common adverse effects are predominantly gastrointestinal, including nausea and vomiting. However, emerging reports have raised concern about skeletal muscle effects, including loss of muscle mass and, more rarely, rhabdomyolysis. A prior case report described rhabdomyolysis with necrotising features associated with tirzepatide, another agent in this class, characterized by a markedly elevated CK in the thousands that improved within days of drug discontinuation—a pattern typical of direct toxic injury rather than autoimmune myopathy. Understanding this distinction is critical, as the present case illustrates a fundamentally different process.

Two and a half years after her NAFLD diagnosis, the patient was initiated on semaglutide for persistent obesity and fatty liver. Within hours of her first and only subcutaneous dose (0.25 mg), she developed nausea and vomiting. Two days later she was hospitalized with poor oral intake, dyspnea, acute renal failure, and severe metabolic acidosis. She required intravenous hydration, bicarbonate, antiemetics, and a brief period of non-invasive ventilation in the intensive care unit. Her renal function improved with supportive care alone, without the need for dialysis. Notably, her CK on admission was only modestly elevated at 337 U/L—well below the range typically seen in rhabdomyolysis. She had no rash, muscle pain, or dark urine. She also developed paroxysmal atrial fibrillation during this admission, although troponin was normal. After five days, she was discharged with resolution of gastrointestinal symptoms but was described as deconditioned.

Over the following four months, her clinical course diverged sharply from what would be expected after a simple drug intolerance reaction. She developed progressive, severe proximal muscle weakness, eventually becoming unable to stand or sit upright without assistance. She also developed dysphagia to both liquids and solids and dysphonia. This pattern of subacute, progressive proximal weakness with bulbar involvement is characteristic of inflammatory myopathy rather than transient toxic muscle injury. At the outside hospital, her CK was found to be 3992 U/L (normal range 12–191 U/L), reflecting a twelve-fold rise from her admission CK of 337 U/L measured four months earlier.

This clinical trajectory brings into focus the entity of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). IMNM is a distinct subclassification of idiopathic inflammatory myopathies defined by aggressive proximal muscle weakness, markedly elevated CK, and histologic features of muscle fiber necrosis and regeneration with a notable paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates. Unlike polymyositis or dermatomyositis, IMNM lacks prominent endomysial mononuclear cell infiltration; instead, macrophage-mediated myophagocytosis and complement-mediated necrosis predominate. Two myositis-specific autoantibodies are strongly associated with IMNM: anti-signal recognition particle (anti-SRP) and anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (anti-HMGCR). The latter is of particular clinical relevance because statin use is strongly linked to anti-HMGCR-positive IMNM. The historical recognition of this entity emerged from patients who developed necrotising myopathy on statins but whose weakness and CK elevation persisted despite statin discontinuation and improved only with immunosuppression—a pattern that distinguished an autoimmune process from direct toxic injury. Importantly, approximately one-third of IMNM patients are seronegative for known autoantibodies, suggesting that additional autoantibodies and triggers remain to be identified.

The patient in this report denied any prior statin use, consumption of supplements associated with myopathy such as red yeast rice or mushrooms, excessive physical activity, alcohol or illicit drug use, or family history of myopathy. These negatives are essential context: they help exclude common causes of toxic or hereditary myopathy and direct attention toward a possible medication-triggered immune mechanism. The temporal relationship between a single dose of semaglutide and the subsequent twelve-fold CK rise over four months, accompanied by progressive proximal weakness, forms the core of the diagnostic reasoning that follows.

## Diagnostic Workup and Investigations

The diagnostic evaluation of a patient with suspected myopathy must systematically answer three questions: Is there muscle damage? What is the pattern and severity of involvement? And what is the underlying etiology? In this case, a coordinated workup of laboratory studies, imaging, and tissue biopsy allowed the clinical team to confirm immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) while excluding alternative diagnoses.

### Laboratory Findings

Creatine kinase (CK) is the single most informative serum marker for muscle injury. CK is an intracellular enzyme abundant in skeletal muscle, and its extracellular presence reflects disruption of the myocyte membrane. In this patient, CK was markedly elevated at 3992 U/L (normal range 12–191 U/L) at the outside hospital, and remained elevated at 1237 U/L at the time of transfer to the tertiary center. This degree of CK elevation is well beyond what would be expected from mild toxic injury or deconditioning, and is consistent with an active necrotising process. The continued rise in CK over 4 months—despite the patient having received only a single dose of semaglutide and having no ongoing exposure to known myotoxic drugs—was a critical observation that distinguished a self-limited toxic rhabdomyolysis from a sustained immune-mediated process.

Serum aminotransferases were also elevated (AST 191 U/L, ALT 165 U/L). Both AST and ALT are found in skeletal muscle as well as liver, and their elevation in the setting of a markedly abnormal CK raises the important question of whether transaminitis reflects muscle breakdown, hepatic dysfunction, or both. This patient's prior biopsy-proven non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and persistently borderline transaminase elevation years before her myopathy developed indicated a hepatic contribution. The ALT-greater-than-AST pattern noted 3 years earlier, together with a normal platelet count, albumin, bilirubin, and alkaline phosphatase at that time, was consistent with steatohepatitis rather than muscle injury. The key teaching point is that in any patient with a myopathy and elevated transaminases, the clinician must determine whether the liver enzymes are rising from muscle or liver. A muscle source typically produces an AST-to-ALT ratio greater than 3 and a CK that is disproportionately elevated relative to the transaminases, whereas a hepatic source produces a pattern dominated by ALT and is independent of CK. In this case, both processes likely contributed, and the liver biopsy obtained during admission helped confirm this.

The autoantibody workup is central to subclassifying inflammatory myopathies and identifying patients with IMNM. Anti-signal recognition particle (anti-SRP) and anti-3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (anti-HMGCR) are the two myositis-specific autoantibodies most strongly associated with IMNM. Anti-HMGCR antibodies are particularly linked to statin exposure, and their discovery helped reclassify many patients previously labeled as having toxic myopathy or polymyositis. In this patient, a comprehensive myositis panel was negative for anti-SRP and anti-HMGCR, as well as for the antisynthetase antibodies (Jo-1, PL-7, PL-12, EJ, OJ), anti-Mi-2, anti-MDA-5, anti-TIF1-γ, anti-NXP-2, and anti-cN-1A. A negative ANA and negative anti-SSA were also documented. She did have a low-titer anti-SSB antibody (2.4, normal <1.0), but in the absence of ANA and anti-SSA positivity and without clinical features of Sjögren syndrome, this isolated finding was considered incidental and not contributory. This case illustrates an important diagnostic principle: approximately one-third of patients with IMNM are seronegative for known autoantibodies. A negative myositis panel does not exclude IMNM when the clinical and histological features are supportive, and the existence of seronegative cases suggests that additional autoantibodies and triggers remain to be discovered.

Inflammatory markers were mildly elevated, with an ESR of 22 mm/hour and a C-reactal protein of 22.8 mg/L. These modest elevations are consistent with IMNM, which characteristically produces less systemic inflammation than other inflammatory myopathies such as dermatomyositis or antisynthetase syndrome. Complement levels, creatinine, and complete blood counts were normal, helping to exclude concurrent renal failure, cytopenias, or a systemic autoimmune disorder. TSH was normal, ruling out hypothyroid myopathy, a common and reversible cause of CK elevation.

Cardiac biomarkers warrant separate discussion. A high-sensitivity troponin of 62 pg/mL (normal ≤53 pg/mL) and a proBNP of 4358 pg/mL (normal 0–125 pg/mL) were elevated, raising concern for myocardial involvement. Troponin can be elevated from skeletal muscle injury alone, as cardiac and skeletal muscle troponin isoforms share epitopes detected by some assays. However, the magnitude of proBNP elevation suggested possible cardiac strain or dysfunction, and prompted dedicated cardiac imaging, discussed below.

### Musculoskeletal Imaging

MRI of the bilateral femurs revealed mild, symmetric muscular edema most evident in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps muscles, with associated subcutaneous edema along the lateral thighs. Muscle MRI is a valuable noninvasive tool in the evaluation of myopathy because it can detect edema, fatty replacement, and atrophy before these changes are clinically apparent. Edema on T2-weighted sequences with fat suppression or short tau inversion recovery (STIR) indicates active inflammation or necrosis. The proximal and symmetric distribution in this patient's MRI paralleled her clinical proximal weakness, supporting a diffuse myopathic process rather than a focal injury. The degree of edema was described as mild, which is notable because IMNM can sometimes produce striking MRI changes due to extensive necrosis; however, the severity of MRI edema does not always correlate with the degree of histological necrosis, and biopsy remains the definitive diagnostic step.

### Cardiac Evaluation

Given the elevated troponin and proBNP, cardiac evaluation was essential to determine whether the myopathic process extended to the myocardium. An echocardiogram showed a preserved left ventricular ejection fraction of 65%–70% but an elevated right ventricular systolic pressure of 46 mm Hg, suggesting mild pulmonary hypertension. A cardiac MRI demonstrated mild hypokinesis of the mid-anteroseptal and anterior walls with associated myocardial edema but no definite late gadolinium enhancement (LGE). The absence of LGE argued against established myocardial scarring or infarction. Myocardial edema without LGE can represent early myocarditis, but in this clinical context—severe systemic illness, deconditioning, and a recent intensive care unit admission—focal stress-induced cardiomyopathy was also a plausible explanation. The cardiac findings were therefore inconclusive for active myocarditis. The teaching point is that inflammatory myopathies, particularly IMNM and dermatomyositis, can involve the heart, and cardiac MRI is the preferred modality for evaluating myocardial inflammation, edema, and fibrosis. In this patient, the cardiac abnormalities later normalized on repeat echocardiogram and cardiac MRI, which favored a transient process rather than ongoing myocarditis.

### Histopathological Confirmation

The definitive diagnostic procedure in this case was a muscle biopsy of the right vastus lateralis. Histopathology is the cornerstone for diagnosing IMNM and distinguishing it from other inflammatory myopathies and toxic muscle injury.

The biopsy demonstrated dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers, the hallmark of a necrotising myopathy. Unlike polymyositis or dermatomyositis, IMNM is characterized by muscle fiber necrosis as the predominant finding, with only a sparse lymphocytic infiltrate. This paucity of lymphocytes is a key histological distinction; the immune attack in IMNM is thought to be mediated primarily by complement activation and macrophage recruitment rather than direct T-cell cytotoxicity.

Immunohistochemistry provided further critical detail. CD68 staining, a macrophage marker, was positive with evidence of myophagocytosis—macrophages actively engulfing necrotic muscle fiber debris. Acid phosphatase staining, a lysosomal enzyme enriched in macrophages, was also positive. These findings confirm active macrophage-mediated cleanup of necrotic fibers, a process common to many myopathies but expected in IMNM. The membrane attack complex (MAC, C5b-9) deposition was positive on both necrotic and non-necrotic myofibers. MAC deposition on non-necrotic fibers is particularly significant because it indicates complement-mediated injury is actively damaging intact muscle cells, supporting an immune-mediated process rather than purely primary toxic necrosis. Rare CD3-positive T cells were present, and there were perivascular inflammatory cells. The sparse T-cell infiltrate, the presence of MAC deposition, and the predominance of macrophages over lymphocytes collectively established the histological pattern of IMNM.

This biopsy was essential because it established the disease category that directed treatment. A toxic myopathy would be expected to show necrosis with minimal or no immune activation. Polymyositis would show endomysial CD8-positive T cells invading non-necrotic fibers. Dermatomyositis would show perifascicular atrophy and perivascular inflammation with MAC deposition on capillaries. The pattern here—necrosis with macrophage predominance, complement deposition, and minimal lymphocytic infiltration—fit IMNM.

### Liver Biopsy and Transaminitis

A repeat liver biopsy was performed during the admission to clarify the source of the patient's transaminitis and to assess progression of her known NAFLD. The biopsy showed macrovesicular steatosis involving approximately 70% of the submitted parenchyma, with no significant portal inflammation or interface hepatitis. These findings were consistent with progression of her underlying steatohepatitis (NAFLD Activity Score grade 5/8, stage 1) and showed no evidence of autoimmune hepatitis or drug-induced liver injury.

This biopsy was diagnostically valuable for two reasons. First, it confirmed that the patient's transaminase elevation had a substantial hepatic component independent of her muscle disease. This is important because transaminase elevation in a myopathy patient can be misattributed entirely to muscle breakdown, potentially leading to unnecessary hepatologic evaluation or delayed recognition of a myopathy if CK is not simultaneously checked. Second, the absence of interface hepatitis, autoimmune serology, and other features of autoimmune liver disease narrowed the evaluation and supported the conclusion that her hepatic and muscular processes were distinct. The teaching point is that when evaluating a patient with both transaminitis and myopathy, the clinician should always consider dual pathology and use CK-to-transaminase ratios, imaging, and biopsy when necessary to disentangle the contributions of each organ.

### Integrating the Workup

Taken together, the diagnostic workup established IMNM through the convergence of characteristic clinical features (progressive proximal weakness, 12-fold CK rise), compatible imaging (symmetric proximal muscle edema), and confirmatory histology (necrotic fibers, macrophage predominance, complement deposition). The negative myositis panel placed this patient in the seronegative IMNM subset, which does not preclude the diagnosis. The cardiac and hepatic evaluations were essential not to diagnose IMNM itself, but to define the extent of involvement and exclude alternative or overlapping processes. The comprehensive workup allowed the team to proceed with confidence to immunosuppressive therapy, which is discussed in the treatment section.

## Differential Diagnosis and Causality Assessment

Establishing the correct diagnosis in this case required the care team to distinguish immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) from several other forms of muscle injury, to exclude alternative etiologies, and to critically assess whether semaglutide was causally related to the patient's illness. Each of these steps involved a distinct line of clinical reasoning that illustrates how myopathies are evaluated in practice.

### Differentiating IMNM from Drug-Induced Rhabdomyolysis

The most immediate alternative diagnosis to consider was drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, particularly given the temporal proximity to semaglutide exposure and the known association between GLP-1 receptor agonists and skeletal muscle toxicity. Rhabdomyolysis is a syndrome of acute skeletal muscle injury characterized by rapid release of muscle cell contents into the circulation, typically producing a markedly elevated creatine kinase (CK), often in the tens of thousands, along with myoglobinuria, acute kidney injury, and systemic toxicity. In the previously reported case of rhabdomyolysis associated with tirzepatide (another GLP-1 receptor agonist), the patient presented with a CK of approximately 57,000 U/L, and CK levels began to normalize within days of drug discontinuation and supportive hydration alone, without any need for immunosuppression.

This patient's course differed in several critical respects. At the time of her initial hospitalization two days after receiving semaglutide, her CK was only modestly elevated at 337 U/L—far below the range expected for acute rhabdomyolysis. She had no myalgias, no cola-colored urine, and no clinical features of acute muscle destruction. Rather than resolving after drug withdrawal, her CK rose progressively over the subsequent four months to a peak of 3992 U/L, accompanied by worsening proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia. This pattern—a subacute rise in CK over months with progressive weakness despite removal of the suspected trigger—is fundamentally incompatible with a purely toxic rhabdomyolytic process and instead points toward an ongoing immune-mediated injury.

The muscle biopsy further solidified the distinction. Drug-induced rhabdomyolysis typically produces widespread myofiber necrosis without significant inflammatory infiltration, as the injury is a direct toxic effect on muscle membranes. In contrast, this patient's biopsy demonstrated necrotic and regenerating fibers with macrophage-mediated myophagocytosis (CD68 positivity), membrane attack complex deposition on both necrotic and non-necrotic fibers, and rare perivascular T-cell infiltrates. The presence of complement-mediated injury on non-necrotic fibers is especially informative, as it indicates immune activation beyond the boundaries of already-dead tissue—a hallmark of IMNM rather than simple toxic necrosis.

### Ruling Out Other Etiologies

Several other causes of necrotizing myopathy required explicit consideration. Statin-associated autoimmune necrotizing myopathy is among the most well-characterized forms of IMNM and shares important parallels with this case. In statin-associated IMNM, the initial muscle injury may resemble a toxic myopathy, but the process becomes self-sustaining through autoimmune mechanisms, persisting and often worsening even after the statin is discontinued. Many such patients are positive for anti-HMGCR antibodies, and their myopathy requires immunosuppression rather than simple drug withdrawal. This patient denied any prior statin use, and her myositis panel was negative for anti-HMGCR antibodies. However, the conceptual framework of statin-associated IMNM—a medication triggering a self-perpetuating immune myopathy that outlasts drug exposure—provides a useful model for understanding how semaglutide might act as a similar trigger. It is also notable that an estimated one-third of patients with IMNM are seronegative for known myositis-specific antibodies, and the existence of yet-uncharacterized autoantibodies and triggers is an active area of investigation.

Toxic myopathy from other substances was considered as well. The patient denied consumption of mushrooms, red yeast rice (which contains a naturally occurring statin-like compound), or any other nutritional supplements associated with muscle injury. She had no history of excessive or unusual physical exertion that might produce exertional rhabdomyolysis, and she denied alcohol or illicit drug use. Her thyroid-stimulating hormone was within normal limits, excluding hypothyroid myopathy, which can produce CK elevation and proximal weakness.

Idiopathic autoimmune IMNM remained a diagnostic possibility, particularly given her seronegative status for anti-SRP and anti-HMGCR. However, several features argued against a de novo idiopathic process. The close temporal relationship between semaglutide exposure and the onset of progressive muscle injury, the absence of any prior myopathic symptoms, and—most importantly—the clinical course all favored a drug-triggered process. Idiopathic IMNM typically follows a chronic, relapsing course that requires prolonged, intensive immunosuppression, often with multiple agents over months to years. In contrast, this patient achieved complete and sustained remission after a single short course of pulse corticosteroids, oral prednisone tapered over approximately two months, and one course of IVIG, while remaining off semaglutide. This pattern of rapid, durable response to brief immunosuppression following withdrawal of the suspected trigger is more consistent with drug-induced IMNM, analogous to the clinical course seen in some patients with statin-associated IMNM after statin cessation and limited immunosuppression.

A final diagnostic consideration was whether the patient might have had a pre-existing, smolderering autoimmune myopathy before receiving semaglutide that was simply unmasked or exacerbated by the drug. The authors acknowledge this limitation directly: no CK measurement was available prior to semaglutide initiation, so a low-level pre-existing process cannot be definitively excluded. However, the patient was previously fully independent in all activities of daily living, and the 12-fold rise in CK from 337 U/L to 3992 U/L over four months after drug exposure, with progressive functional decline from full independence to inability to stand or sit without assistance, makes a clinically significant pre-existing myopathy unlikely.

### The Isolated SS-B Antibody

The patient's autoantibody profile was notable for a low-titer positive anti-SS-B antibody at 2.4 (normal <1.0) in the setting of a negative ANA, negative anti-SS-A, and an otherwise unremarkable connective tissue disease serology. Anti-SS-B (also known as anti-La) is classically associated with Sjögren syndrome and systemic lupus erythematosus, typically in conjunction with anti-SS-A (anti-Ro) and a positive ANA. Isolated anti-SS-B positivity with negative ANA and negative anti-SS-A is unusual and diagnostically challenging. There are no published reports linking this isolated serologic pattern to IMNM, and the patient lacked clinical features of Sjögren syndrome (no sicca symptoms, no synovitis, no rashes). Given the low titer and the absence of corroborating autoantibodies or clinical features, the treating team reasonably concluded that this finding was not central to the myopathy and may represent nonspecific low-level autoantibody production. This case illustrates an important principle for trainees: isolated low-titer autoantibodies, particularly when they do not fit a recognizable clinical pattern, should not automatically redirect the diagnostic approach or trigger broad immunosuppression for a connective tissue disease the patient does not have.

### Cardiac Findings: Myocarditis versus Stress-Induced Cardiomyopathy

The patient's evaluation revealed cardiac abnormalities that required careful interpretation. Her high-sensitivity troponin was mildly elevated at 62 pg/mL (normal ≤53 pg/mL), and her pro-BNP was markedly elevated at 4358 pg/mL (normal 0–125 pg/mL). The cardiac MRI demonstrated mild hypokinesis of the mid-anteroseptal and anterior walls with myocardial edema but no definite late gadolinium enhancement, no significant scarring or infarction, and no significant valvular dysfunction. The echocardiogram showed preserved left ventricular ejection fraction of 65%–70%.

Two interpretations were considered. Cardiac involvement in IMNM, including myocarditis, has been described, and the myocardial edema on cardiac MRI could reflect active myocardial inflammation extending the necrotizing process to cardiac muscle. Alternatively, stress-induced (takotsubo-type) cardiomyopathy could produce regional wall motion abnormalities and myocardial edema in the setting of the patient's severe acute illness, critical care requirements, and emotional and physiologic stress. The absence of late gadolinium enhancement—which would be expected in true myocarditis with myocyte necrosis—and the preservation of ejection fraction made definitive myocarditis difficult to confirm. The authors noted that stress-induced cardiomyopathy was a reasonable alternative explanation given the clinical setting. Importantly, the patient's cardiac abnormalities normalized on repeat echocardiogram and cardiac MRI at 10-month follow-up, and she had no recurrence of the paroxysmal atrial fibrillation that first appeared during her initial hospitalization. This resolution is consistent with both transient myocarditis and stress-induced cardiomyopathy, and the case demonstrates the difficulty of definitively distinguishing these entities when imaging features are equivocal.

### Applying WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre Causality Criteria

To formally assess the relationship between semaglutide and IMNM, the authors applied the World Health Organization–Uppsala Monitoring Centre (WHO-UMC) causality assessment criteria. This standardized framework is widely used in pharmacovigilance to evaluate suspected adverse drug reactions and assigns causality to one of several categories: certain, probable, possible, unlikely, conditional, or unassessable.

The WHO-UMC system considers several key elements: the temporal relationship between drug exposure and the adverse event, whether alternative etiologies have been adequately excluded, the response to drug withdrawal (dechallenge), and the response to re-exposure (rechallenge). A "probable" categorization requires a reasonable time relationship between drug exposure and the event, that the event is unlikely to be attributable to other diseases or drugs, and that there is a clinically reasonable response to withdrawal of the suspected agent.

In this case, the authors classified the causality as "probable." The reasoning supporting this assessment is as follows. First, there was a reasonable temporal relationship: the patient's clinical decline began after a single dose of semaglutide, and progressive CK elevation and weakness developed over the subsequent four months, a timeline consistent with an immune-mediated process triggered by drug exposure. Second, alternative etiologies were unlikely: the patient had no statin exposure, no other toxins or supplements, no connective tissue disease, normal thyroid function, and no other identifiable cause of necrotizing myopathy. Third, there was a clinically reasonable response to withdrawal: the patient improved after semaglutide discontinuation combined with immunosuppression, and she remained well off all immunosuppressive therapy for months while continuing to avoid GLP-1 receptor agonists.

A rechallenge would have provided the strongest evidence for a "certain" categorization, but the authors appropriately noted that re-exposing the patient to semaglutide after such a severe adverse reaction would be unethical. Thus, "probable" rather than "certain" is the highest causality category achievable under these circumstances. The authors also acknowledged that the causal relationship is not definitively proven, particularly given the absence of a pre-semaglutide CK measurement and the possibility of a pre-existing subclinical autoimmune process. This transparency about diagnostic uncertainty is essential to sound pharmacovigilance reporting, and the case serves as a model for how clinicians should think about drug causality: using structured criteria, recognizing the limits of the available evidence, and weighing the totality of clinical, histologic, laboratory, and longitudinal data.

## Management and Clinical Course

The management of this patient's immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) illustrates several important principles in the treatment of severe, autoimmune muscle disease, while also highlighting how medication-associated IMNM may follow a more abbreviated treatment course than idiopathic forms.

### Acute Immunomodulatory Therapy

Upon recognition of IMNM at the outside hospital, the patient was started on high-dose intravenous corticosteroids — 1000 mg of methylprednisolone daily for 5 days — under neurology guidance via telemedicine consultation. This approach, often referred to as "pulse" steroid therapy, is a standard initial strategy for severe inflammatory myopathies. The rationale is to achieve rapid, high-level immunosuppression to halt ongoing complement-mediated necrosis of myofibres and macrophage-driven myophagocytosis, the histological hallmarks demonstrated on this patient's biopsy. High-dose IV administration is preferred in acutely ill patients with severe weakness because it ensures complete bioavailability and may offer more potent anti-inflammatory effects than oral equivalents in the acute setting.

After transfer to the tertiary hospital for rheumatology evaluation, the patient was transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg daily. This transition to high-dose oral corticosteroids maintains sustained immunosuppression after the initial pulse period and serves as the backbone of therapy while slower-acting adjunctive strategies take effect. In this case, the prednisone dose of 1 mg/kg reflects a weight-based approach aimed at delivering adequate immunomodulation for a necrotising myopathy that had progressed to profound proximal weakness — the patient could no longer stand or sit upright without assistance — and bulbar involvement with dysphagia.

### Adjunctive Therapy with IVIG

Given the severity of the patient's presentation — progressive weakness over 4 months, dysphagia, dysphonia, and a CK exceeding 3992 U/L — intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days. IVIG is commonly used as an adjunctive agent in inflammatory myopathies, particularly in patients with severe or rapidly progressive disease, significant weakness, or bulbar/respiratory involvement. Its mechanisms of action are multifactorial and include neutralization of pathogenic autoantibodies, inhibition of complement activation (relevant here given the membrane attack complex deposition seen on biopsy), modulation of Fc receptor function on macrophages, and broad downregulation of inflammatory cytokine networks. The decision to add IVIG to high-dose corticosteroids in this patient reflects the clinical concern that corticosteroids alone might not sufficiently or rapidly enough suppress the necrotising process, especially given the degree of functional impairment and the bulbar symptoms that portended potential respiratory compromise.

### Monitoring Treatment Response

The patient's response to combined pulse corticosteroids and IVIG was notable for a marked and relatively rapid decline in CK levels over the course of approximately 2 weeks. CK served as the principal biochemical marker of ongoing muscle necrosis in this case, and its trajectory — falling from a peak of 3992 U/L to within the normal range — provided objective evidence that the immunosuppressive regimen was effectively halting the necrotising process. This biochemical improvement was accompanied by clinically appreciable gains in muscle strength and functional capacity, though the precise timeline of strength recovery relative to CK normalization is not detailed. In clinical practice, CK often normalizes faster than strength returns, as regeneration of necrotic myofibres and reconstitution of the contractile apparatus require additional time beyond cessation of active inflammation.

The patient was discharged after a hospitalization lasting nearly 4 weeks, reflecting the severity of her initial presentation and the time required for stabilization and initiation of a durable treatment plan.

### Management of Complications

Twelve days after discharge, the patient was rehospitalized for 10 days due to septic shock from a pseudomonal urinary tract infection. This complication is an important reminder of the infectious risks associated with high-dose corticosteroid therapy, which impairs neutrophil function, T-cell activation, and mucosal barrier integrity. Pseudomonas aeruginosa is a particular concern in immunocompromised hosts and in the setting of healthcare exposure. Notably, her CK was found to be normal during this admission, indicating that the episode of IMNM remained in biochemical remission despite the acute intercurrent infection. The absence of CK re-elevation during sepsis is reassuring, as it suggests that the immunosuppressive course had effectively controlled the underlying myopathy and that the infection did not trigger a disease flare. The infection was managed and resolved without apparent long-term sequelae.

### Outpatient Follow-up and Sustained Remission

Following discharge from the second hospitalization, the patient was followed in the outpatient setting with continued improvement in functional status and persistently normal CK levels. This sustained response allowed for a rapid tapering of oral prednisone, completed over approximately 2 months. The rapidity of the steroid taper is noteworthy: idiopathic IMNM, particularly cases associated with anti-SRP or anti-HMGCR antibodies, frequently requires prolonged immunosuppression — often with steroid-sparing agents such as methotrexate, azathioprine, or rituximab — and relapses are common during tapering. The fact that this patient's disease was controlled after a relatively brief course of corticosteroids and a single cycle of IVIG, without the need for additional maintenance immunosuppression, is clinically consistent with a drug-induced IMNM that improved following withdrawal of the inciting agent. As the authors note, this course aligns more closely with drug-induced IMNM, which can resolve with short-term immunosuppression after removal of the offending drug, than with idiopathic IMNM, which typically demands more intensive and prolonged therapy.

The patient did not receive additional IVIG beyond the initial inpatient course, a decision influenced by both the high cost of IVIG and her sustained clinical improvement off semaglutide. At her last outpatient visit, 10 months after initial evaluation and treatment initiation, she had regained her baseline functional status and had been off immunosuppression for approximately 7–8 months. Her CK remained within the normal range. Repeat echocardiography and cardiac MRI demonstrated normalization of the previously observed myocardial abnormalities (mild mid-anteroseptal and anterior wall hypokinesis with associated edema), and she experienced no recurrence of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation. These cardiac findings, which had been inconclusive for active myocarditis during the index admission, resolved in parallel with the myopathy, raising the possibility that they represented either transient myocardial involvement in the same immune process or stress-induced cardiomyopathy related to the acute illness.

The complete and durable remission achieved in this patient — without ongoing immunosuppression and with continued avoidance of GLP-1 receptor agonists — reinforces several key clinical lessons. First, drug-induced IMNM should be suspected when necrotising myopathy follows a close temporal relationship to a new medication, particularly when CK continues to rise rather than normalize after drug discontinuation (distinguishing it from simple toxic rhabdomyolysis). Second, withdrawal of the suspected offending agent is an essential component of management alongside immunosuppression. Third, the trajectory of recovery — including the ability to taper immunosuppression relatively quickly — can itself serve as a diagnostic clue that the myopathy was medication-triggered rather than idiopathic. Finally, vigilant monitoring for treatment-related complications, particularly infections in the setting of high-dose corticosteroids, remains a critical component of care.

## Clinical Lessons and Pharmacovigilance

This case offers several important lessons for clinicians who prescribe GLP-1 receptor agonists or evaluate patients with myopathy. The most immediate lesson is the need to distinguish toxic rhabdomyolysis from immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) when a patient on one of these medications presents with muscle symptoms or creatine kinase (CK) elevation. The previously reported case of rhabdomyolysis associated with tirzepatide followed a classic toxic pattern: a marked acute CK elevation exceeding 50,000 U/L, rapid improvement within days of drug withdrawal and hydration, and no need for immunosuppression. In contrast, the present patient had only a modestly elevated CK of 337 U/L at the time of her initial hospitalization for gastrointestinal and renal complications, followed by a progressive 12-fold rise in CK over the subsequent 4 months despite having received only a single dose of semaglutide. This delayed, progressive course—continuing to worsen long after the drug was discontinued—is incompatible with a direct toxic effect and instead points toward an immune-mediated process that became self-sustaining after the initial trigger. Recognizing this temporal pattern is critical, because it changes the therapeutic approach from supportive care and drug cessation alone to active immunosuppression.

A second lesson concerns the expected treatment duration and prognosis of drug-induced IMNM compared with idiopathic IMNM. Idiopathic IMNM, particularly cases associated with anti-SRP or anti-HMGCR autoantibodies, typically requires prolonged and intensive immunosuppressive regimens, often combining corticosteroids with steroid-sparing agents, and relapses are common when immunosuppression is tapered. In this case, the patient improved dramatically with a single course of pulse methylprednisolone, approximately 2 months of oral prednisone, and one course of intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) at 2 g/kg. She was weaned off prednisone rapidly, did not receive additional IVIG, and remained in remission with normal CK levels 8 months after discontinuing all immunosuppression. This clinical course—resolution after brief immunosuppression combined with withdrawal of the suspected offending agent—is more consistent with drug-induced IMNM than with idiopathic disease. The lesson for clinicians is that while drug-induced IMNM can be severe and may require aggressive initial treatment, the long-term prognosis may be more favorable than idiopathic IMNM, provided the trigger is identified and avoided. This distinction has practical implications for counseling patients about prognosis and for decisions regarding the duration of maintenance immunosuppression.

The case also underscores the importance of pharmacovigilance as GLP-1 receptor agonists are prescribed to an exponentially growing patient population. Semaglutide and related medications are now used broadly for type 2 diabetes and obesity, meaning that even exceedingly rare adverse events will be encountered in clinical practice. The authors applied the World Health Organization–Uppsala Monitoring Centre causality framework to assess the relationship between semaglutide and IMNM, concluding that the association was "probable" based on a reasonable temporal relationship, the unlikelihood of alternative explanations, and the clinical response upon drug withdrawal. At the same time, they appropriately acknowledged that causality is not certain, since a baseline CK before semaglutide exposure was not available and a smolderering autoimmune process cannot be definitively excluded. This balanced approach—reporting a temporally associated case while explicitly stating the limitations—is a model for how clinicians can contribute to pharmacovigilance through careful case documentation. The report also raises the question of what predisposing factors may have placed this patient at risk. She had no known myositis-specific autoantibodies, no statin exposure, no family history of myopathy, and no other obvious triggers, though the isolated low-titer anti-SSB positivity with negative ANA and anti-SSA was unexplained. As GLP-1 receptor agonist use continues to expand, identifying host factors—genetic, immunologic, or metabolic—that predispose to rare immune-mediated reactions will become a research priority, particularly if additional cases with a close temporal relationship to semaglutide are reported.

Finally, this case raises an ethical point that clinicians should recognize: drug rechallenge, which could provide stronger evidence for causality, is not justified when a severe adverse reaction has occurred. The patient experienced life-threatening weakness with dysphagia, profound proximal muscle impairment, and CK elevation nearing 4,000 U/L, requiring nearly 4 weeks of hospitalization and combined immunosuppressive therapy. Re-exposing her to semaglutide to confirm causation would have placed her at unacceptable risk of recurrence, potentially with greater severity. This ethical constraint means that causality for rare drug-associated immune reactions will often remain at the level of "probable" rather than "confirmed," and clinicians must make practical management decisions—permanent avoidance of the suspected agent and vigilance for similar cases—based on the best available evidence rather than definitive proof.
