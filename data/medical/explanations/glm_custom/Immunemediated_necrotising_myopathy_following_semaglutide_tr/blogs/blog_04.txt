Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# Drug-Induced vs. Idiopathic IMNM: Does the Trigger Change the Treatment Plan?

## The Case That Stops You in Your Tracks

A woman in her 60s receives a single dose of semaglutide. Within hours, she's vomiting. Within days, she's in the ICU with acute kidney injury and metabolic acidosis. And within four months, she can't stand, can't sit upright without assistance, can't swallow — with a CK of 3992 U/L and a muscle biopsy showing necrotising myopathy with macrophage-mediated myophagocytosis and membrane attack complex deposition. One dose. That's all it took.

What makes this case from Lam and colleagues so striking isn't just the severity — it's the temporal relationship. A single 0.25 mg subcutaneous dose of semaglutide preceded a 12-fold rise in CK over four months, culminating in immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) severe enough to cause dysphagia, dysphonia, and profound proximal weakness. And here's the part that should make you sit up: after pulse methylprednisolone, oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg, and a single course of IVIG at 2 g/kg, she was weaned off all immunosuppression within two months and remained in remission at ten months. Off everything. Normal CK. Baseline functional status.

That is not the typical IMNM trajectory we've been trained to expect.

## Why This Matters: The Treatment Duration Question

When we think about IMNM, we think about a disease that fights back. Idiopathic IMNM — particularly the anti-SRP and anti-HMGCR subtypes — is notorious for requiring prolonged, aggressive immunosuppression. We're talking years of combination therapy, often steroids plus an additional agent, with high relapse rates when immunosuppression is tapered. The muscle necrosis is relentless, the functional impairment can be devastating, and patients frequently need repeated courses of IVIG or even rituximab to maintain disease control.

So when this patient walked (literally, eventually) off all immunosuppression after a two-month course and stayed in remission, it challenges us to think about what drives the disease course. Is it the underlying immunopathology alone, or does the trigger matter?

The argument here is biologically plausible. If an exogenous agent triggers an immune response against muscle fibres, and that agent is removed, the immune system may lose its ongoing stimulus. The self-amplifying loop of necrosis, antigen release, and further immune activation could theoretically be interrupted more easily when the inciting trigger is gone. In contrast, idiopathic IMNM may represent a self-sustaining autoimmune process without an identifiable external driver — meaning the immune system has crossed the threshold into autonomous self-destruction, and removing a trigger isn't an option because there isn't one to remove.

## The Statin Paradox: When Trigger Removal Isn't Enough

But before we get too comfortable with this framework, we need to talk about the elephant in the room: statin-associated HMGCR-positive IMNM.

Statin-associated IMNM is the classic example of drug-induced IMNM, and it's the one that complicates the "remove the trigger and the disease resolves" narrative. We know that statins can trigger HMGCR autoantibody-positive IMNM, and we also know that stopping the statin does not reliably stop the disease. These patients frequently require sustained immunosuppression — often for years — even after statin cessation. Some never fully come off treatment. The autoantibody persists, the immune response is self-directed, and the disease behaves more like idiopathic IMNM than a self-limited drug reaction.

Why the difference? One possibility is that statins, by upregulating HMGCR expression in muscle, create a sustained antigenic target that persists even after the drug is gone. The autoantibody is directed against the enzyme itself, not the drug. Once the immune system has launched this response, it may not need the drug anymore — the muscle is the target, and the muscle is still there.

In our semaglutide case, the patient was seronegative for HMGCR, SRP, and the full myositis panel. No identifiable autoantibody. This could be the key distinction. Perhaps drug-induced IMNM without a persistent autoantibody target truly can be more self-limited — the immune response is driven by the drug or a drug-altered antigen, and once the drug is gone, the stimulus disappears. The one-third of IMNM patients who are seronegative may include a subset whose disease is genuinely trigger-dependent and potentially more curable.

## Clinical Reasoning: How Do We Decide How Long to Treat?

Here's the practical question that matters at the bedside: when you diagnose IMNM in a patient with a clear drug trigger, how aggressive do you need to be, and for how long?

In this case, the treating team made several reasonable decisions. First, they used pulse methylprednisolone — 1000 mg IV daily for five days — to rapidly suppress the immune response. Then they transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg. And because of persistent severe weakness, they added IVIG at 2 g/kg over four days. This is a solid induction regimen. It's not minimal, but it's also not the open-ended, multi-agent approach we often default to with idiopathic IMNM.

What's notable is what they did next: they watched. They followed the CK. They followed the functional status. And when both normalised, they tapered — quickly. No long-term steroid-sparing agent was added. No maintenance IVIG. The patient's CK stayed normal. She regained baseline function. She stayed in remission.

The contrast with statin-associated HMGCR-positive IMNM is instructive. In those patients, CK normalisation on treatment doesn't guarantee remission off treatment. Relapse rates are high, and many clinicians maintain immunosuppression for extended periods. The autoantibody serves as both a disease marker and a warning that the immune system has committed to a self-sustaining response.

So the clinical reasoning might go something like this: for drug-induced IMNM with an identified trigger that has been removed, particularly in seronegative patients, a finite induction course with close monitoring may be appropriate. For drug-induced IMNM with a persistent autoantibody (HMGCR-positive, statin-associated), prolonged treatment is more likely necessary. And for idiopathic IMNM, plan for the long haul.

## What About Rechallenge?

Nobody is going to rechallenge this patient with semaglutide. The authors explicitly state this would be unethical, and they're right. But the absence of rechallenge data is a limitation that's worth acknowledging. The WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre criteria classify this as "probable" causality — reasonable time relationship, unlikely attributable to other diseases or drugs, and clinical response upon withdrawal. But probable is not certain. We cannot exclude that this was idiopathic IMNM that happened to follow a single semaglutide dose by coincidence, though the 12-fold CK rise in temporal association makes that less likely.

The broader pharmacovigilance point is critical. GLP-1 receptor agonists are being prescribed at an extraordinary rate. A prior case report described rhabdomyolysis with necrotising features associated with tirzepatide, but that patient's course was classic drug-induced rhabdomyolysis — CK of 57,014, rapid improvement within days of withdrawal, no immunosuppression needed. Our patient's course was fundamentally different: a slower, progressive immune-mediated process requiring immunosuppression. These are distinct entities, and clinicians should be aware that GLP-1 agonists may be associated with both.

## Cardiac Involvement: Don't Forget the Myocardium

One more pearl from this case worth flagging. This patient had cardiac MRI findings concerning for myocardial inflammation — mild hypokinesis of the mid-anteroseptal and anterior wall with myocardial oedema, no late gadolinium enhancement. Her pro-BNP was markedly elevated at 4358 pg/mL. She also developed paroxysmal atrial fibrillation during her initial hospitalisation. IMNM can affect cardiac muscle, and while the cardiac findings here were inconclusive for definitive myocarditis, they normalised on repeat imaging after treatment. The point: when you're evaluating IMNM, think about the heart. It's not just skeletal muscle.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Drug-induced IMNM may follow a more self-limited course than idiopathic IMNM when the offending agent is withdrawn**, particularly in seronegative patients. This case achieved sustained remission after a finite course of pulse steroids, oral prednisone, and a single IVIG cycle — with complete weaning off immunosuppression within two months.
- **Statin-associated HMGCR-positive IMNM is the exception that proves the rule.** Despite being drug-triggered, it often requires prolonged immunosuppression because the autoantibody targets muscle antigen that persists after drug withdrawal. The presence or absence of a persistent autoantibody may be the critical determinant of disease self-limitation.
- **GLP-1 receptor agonists may be associated with both toxic rhabdomyolysis and immune-mediated necrotising myopathy** — two mechanistically distinct entities. Rhabdomyolysis resolves rapidly with drug withdrawal and supportive care; IMNM requires immunosuppression and may progress over months.
- **Seronegative IMNM represents about one-third of cases and remains diagnostically challenging.** This case suggests that at least some seronegative cases may be trigger-dependent and potentially more curable with finite treatment — but more data are needed.
- **When managing IMNM, monitor CK trajectory and functional status closely.** A normalising CK on treatment, combined with functional recovery, may support earlier tapering in drug-induced cases — but only with vigilant follow-up, as relapse can occur.
- **Evaluate for cardiac involvement in IMNM.** Cardiac MRI and troponin/BNP should be part of the workup, even if echocardiography is reassuring. Myocardial involvement can be subtle and may normalise with treatment of the underlying myopathy.

## Final Thought

This case raises more questions than it answers, and the authors are honest about that. One case doesn't establish a pattern, and the absence of a baseline CK before semaglutide means we can't fully exclude a smouldering process that was already underway. But with GLP-1 receptor agonist use exploding globally, this report is a bellwether. If you see progressive proximal weakness with CK elevation in a patient on a GLP-1 agonist — even one who took only a single dose — think IMNM, not just rhabdomyolysis. And consider whether the trigger you identify might change not just the diagnosis, but the duration of the treatment plan.
