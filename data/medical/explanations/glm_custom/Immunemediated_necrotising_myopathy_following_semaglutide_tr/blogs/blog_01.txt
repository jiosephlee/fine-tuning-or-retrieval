Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# IMNM vs. Rhabdomyolysis: When CK Elevation Isn't What It Seems

## The Setup

A woman in her 60s gets a single dose of semaglutide. Within hours, she's vomiting. Within days, she's in the ICU with acute kidney injury and metabolic acidosis. But here's where it gets interesting: her CK was only 337 U/L on that initial admission. Four months later — long after the drug was stopped — her CK is 3,992 U/L, she can't stand without assistance, and she's having trouble swallowing. She got one dose. One. And the muscle destruction didn't just persist — it *accelerated* over months.

That temporal pattern is the whole story here, and it's the key to distinguishing immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) from the drug-induced rhabdomyolysis we're all more familiar with. This case, published by Lam and colleagues, is the first reported case of IMNM probably linked to a GLP-1 receptor agonist, and it offers a masterclass in why not all CK elevation is created equal.

## Two Diseases, One Biomarker

Let's put this case side by side with the previously reported tirzepatide-associated rhabdomyolysis case referenced in the paper. Both involve GLP-1 receptor agonists. Both involve CK elevation and muscle injury. And that's where the similarities end.

The tirzepatide patient presented with a CK of 57,014. Massive. Classic rhabdomyolysis territory. But here's the thing: within *days* of drug discontinuation and hydration, the CK started falling. No immunosuppression needed. That's toxic muscle injury — the drug damages muscle directly, you remove the drug, the muscle recovers. Clean, predictable, and consistent with everything we know about drug-induced rhabdomyolysis from statins, colchicine, and other culprits.

Now look at the semaglutide patient. Her CK at initial presentation was 337 — barely above normal. She got no further doses of the drug. Yet over the next four months, her CK climbed 12-fold to nearly 4,000. Her weakness progressed to the point where she couldn't sit upright unassisted. She developed dysphagia and dysphonia. This isn't toxicity. This is an autoimmune process that was *triggered* by the drug but then took on a life of its own.

That distinction — direct toxicity versus immune triggering — changes everything about how you manage the patient.

## The Timeline Tells You Everything

If you take one thing from this post, let it be this: the CK trajectory is the single most important clinical clue in separating these two entities.

In drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, the CK peaks at presentation or shortly after, and then falls within days of drug withdrawal. The injury is happening *now*, and removing the cause stops the damage. You support the patient with fluids, manage complications like kidney injury and electrolyte disturbances, and the muscle recovers.

In IMNM, the CK may be modest initially — or even normal — and then *rises* over weeks to months, even after the drug is gone. The drug didn't poison the muscle. It flipped a switch in the immune system, and now the immune system is destroying the muscle on its own. Stopping the drug doesn't help because the drug was just the trigger, not the ongoing driver. This patient's CK was climbing for four months after a single dose. No further exposure. No recovery. That's the signature of an immune-mediated process, not a toxic one.

## What the Biopsy Shows

The muscle biopsy in this patient sealed the diagnosis. She had necrotic and regenerating muscle fibres, CD68-positive macrophages with myophagocytosis, and membrane attack complex staining on both necrotising and non-necrotising myofibres. There were only rare CD3-positive T-cells.

This is the histologic face of IMNM: necrosis with a paucity of lymphocytic infiltrate. It's not polymyositis with its dense endomysial T-cell infiltration. It's necrotising myopathy — muscle fibres are dying, macrophages are cleaning up the debris, and complement is depositing on muscle fibres. The relative absence of lymphocytes is actually a key diagnostic feature, not evidence that inflammation is absent.

Contrast this with rhabdomyolysis on biopsy, where you'd see necrosis but without the immune signatures — no complement deposition on non-necrotic fibres, no evidence of an ongoing immunologic attack on otherwise healthy muscle.

## The Antibody Problem

Here's where it gets tricky. Anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP are the two autoantibodies most associated with IMNM, and anti-HMGCR in particular is strongly linked to statin exposure. This patient had never taken statins. Her entire myositis panel — SRP, HMGCR, Jo-1, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-γ, NXP-2, and the rest — was negative.

But here's the pearl: roughly one-third of IMNM patients are seronegative. The absence of these antibodies doesn't rule out IMNM, and the authors make an important point — there are likely undiscovered autoantibodies and triggers out there. Statin-associated IMNM wasn't recognised as autoimmune until anti-HMGCR was identified. Before that, many of these patients were labelled as having toxic myopathy or polymyositis. We may be at the same crossroads now with GLP-1 receptor agonists.

She did have a low-titre positive SS-B antibody with negative ANA and SSA, which was unexpected and not clearly relevant. The authors couldn't find a CK from before semaglutide initiation, so they can't fully exclude a pre-existing smouldering autoimmune process. But the 12-fold CK rise after the drug, the progressive weakness, and the response to immunosuppression all point toward the drug as the trigger.

## Response to Treatment Confirms the Diagnosis

The treatment response further distinguishes IMNM from rhabdomyolysis. This patient received pulse methylprednisolone for five days, then oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg, plus a single course of IVIG at 2 g/kg. Her CK dropped markedly within two weeks. She was weaned off prednisone over just two months, received no additional IVIG, and was back at baseline functional status within ten months — off all immunosuppression for eight of those months.

Drug-induced rhabdomyolysis doesn't respond to immunosuppression. You don't need steroids or IVIG for rhabdomyolysis — you need fluids and time. The fact that this patient required and responded to immunosuppression is itself diagnostic evidence for an immune-mediated process.

Equally important: her rapid taper and sustained remission off immunosuppression is more consistent with drug-triggered IMNM than idiopathic IMNM, which often requires prolonged, intensive immunosuppression. When you remove the trigger and give a short course of immune suppression to break the cycle, drug-triggered IMNM can stay in remission. Idiopathic IMNM tends to relapse.

## A Note on the Cardiac Findings

The patient had a mildly elevated high-sensitivity troponin (62 pg/mL), a proBNP of 4,358 pg/mL, and a cardiac MRI showing mild mid-anteroseptal and anterior wall hypokinesis with myocardial oedema but no late gadolinium enhancement. The authors couldn't definitively confirm myocarditis — stress-induced cardiomyopathy was also considered. But both the echocardiogram and cardiac MRI abnormalities normalised on follow-up, which is reassuring and consistent with either a transient inflammatory process or stress cardiomyopathy in the setting of her severe systemic illness.

The broader point: IMNM can involve cardiac muscle, and any patient with significant IMNM deserves a cardiac evaluation — troponin, ECG, echocardiogram, and consider cardiac MRI if there's any signal.

## Why This Matters Now

GLP-1 receptor agonists are everywhere. Semaglutide, tirzepatide, liraglutide — these are among the most prescribed medications in the world, and their use is growing exponentially. We already know they cause loss of skeletal muscle mass. We have a published case of rhabdomyolysis with tirzepatide. And now we have what appears to be the first case of IMNM triggered by semaglutide.

The authors are appropriately cautious about causality — using WHO-UMC criteria, they classify this as *probable* causality. A rechallenge would be unethical. But the temporal relationship, the absence of alternative explanations, and the response to withdrawal and immunosuppression all align.

We don't yet know what predisposes patients to this reaction. We don't know if it's dose-dependent, if it requires sustained exposure, or if a single dose can trigger it in the right host — though this case suggests it can. We don't know if there's an as-yet unidentified autoantibody that marks susceptibility. What we do know is that if a patient on a GLP-1 receptor agonist presents with progressive weakness and CK elevation that *worsens* after drug discontinuation, you need to be thinking IMNM, not rhabdomyolysis — and you need rheumatology and neurology involved early.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **CK trajectory is the critical discriminator.** Rhabdomyolysis peaks at or near presentation and falls within days of drug withdrawal. IMNM may start with modest CK elevation and *rise* over months, even after the drug is stopped. A rising CK after discontinuation should shift your thinking toward an immune-mediated process.

- **One dose was enough.** This patient developed IMNM after a single 0.25 mg dose of semaglutide. The trigger doesn't require sustained exposure.

- **Seronegativity doesn't rule out IMNM.** One-third of IMNM patients are negative for anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP. A negative myositis panel in the setting of progressive proximal weakness and CK elevation does not exclude the diagnosis — biopsy is essential.

- **Response to immunosuppression is diagnostic evidence.** If the CK falls with steroids and IVIG after drug withdrawal, you're dealing with an immune-mediated process, not direct toxicity. Rhabdomyolysis doesn't need or respond to immunosuppression.

- **Drug-triggered IMNM may need only a short course of immunosuppression.** Unlike idiopathic IMNM, which often requires prolonged treatment, drug-triggered IMNM may resolve with withdrawal of the trigger plus a brief course of steroids ± IVIG. This patient was off all immunosuppression within two months and remained in remission.

- **Evaluate the heart.** IMNM can involve cardiac muscle. Check troponin, get an echocardiogram, and consider cardiac MRI in any patient with significant IMNM.

- **Watch for this.** With the explosive growth of GLP-1 receptor agonist prescribing, this won't be the last case. When you see progressive weakness and CK elevation in a patient on semaglutide or tirzepatide, ask whether the trajectory fits rhabdomyolysis — or whether the immune system has taken over.
