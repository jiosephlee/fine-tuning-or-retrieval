Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

Chapter 4: ## Treatment Strategy and Management of Severe Autoimmune HIT

The management of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) rests on several foundational principles that must be applied simultaneously and urgently: immediate discontinuation of all heparin products (including low-molecular-weight heparin), initiation of a rapid-acting non-heparin anticoagulant, avoidance of platelet transfusions even when thrombocytopenia is severe, and careful clinical and laboratory monitoring for thrombosis and consumptive coagulopathy. In autoimmune HIT (aHIT), an additional therapeutic modality—high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG)—is employed to interrupt the platelet-activating effects of heparin-independent antibodies. This case illustrates each of these principles while also demonstrating how treatment decisions must be dynamically adjusted when the patient's clinical course proves unusually severe or refractory.

The first therapeutic intervention in this patient occurred on postoperative day 5 (POD5), when the platelet count had fallen to 50×10⁹/L and HIT was clinically suspected. Dalteparin, the low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) being used for postoperative thromboprophylaxis, was immediately discontinued. This step is non-negotiable in suspected HIT: continued exposure to any heparin product, whether unfractionated heparin (UFH) or LMWH, will fuel ongoing platelet activation and thrombin generation. Prophylactic-dose fondaparinux (2.5 mg subcutaneously) was begun on POD5 at 18:00 as an interim measure. Fondaparinux, a synthetic pentasaccharide factor Xa inhibitor, does not cross-react with HIT antibodies and is a reasonable option for prophylaxis or treatment of HIT, though it is used off-label for this indication. However, once the diagnosis of HIT was supported by a strong-positive PF4/polyanion ELISA and the clinical picture was further complicated by the discovery of an acute cerebral infarct on POD6 imaging, a more aggressive therapeutic strategy was required.

Therapeutic-dose argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, was selected as the primary anticoagulant at a dose of 0.75 mcg/kg/min, with a partial thromboplastin time (PTT) target of 55–85 seconds. The treating haematologist specifically chose argatroban over therapeutic-dose fondaparinux at this juncture for two reasons. First, argatroban has a short half-life (approximately 40–50 minutes), allowing for rapid dose titration and rapid reversal of anticoagulant effect if bleeding occurred—a critical consideration given the patient's recent ischemic stroke and the theoretical risk of hemorrhagic transformation. Second, the patient's platelet count had fallen to a profoundly low level (26×10⁹/L on POD6), increasing the baseline bleeding risk. In a patient this thrombocytopenic with a fresh cerebral infarct, an anticoagulant that can be quickly turned off is safer than a longer-acting agent given by subcutaneous injection. Argatroban is also hepatically cleared, making it suitable for patients with normal liver function, as was the case here. The PTT-adjusted dosing strategy requires close laboratory monitoring, but this is feasible in the inpatient setting and provides real-time assessment of anticoagulant intensity.

In parallel with anticoagulant management, high-dose IVIG was administered. IVIG has emerged as a specific therapy for aHIT because it is thought to block the platelet Fcγ receptors through which HIT antibodies activate platelets, thereby interrupting the platelet consumption and thrombin generation that characterize the syndrome. In typical aHIT, IVIG often produces a brisk platelet count rise within several days, reflecting rapid cessation of antibody-mediated platelet activation. The standard dosing employed is 1 g/kg per day (rounded to 100 g for practical purposes in this patient) for two consecutive days, which was the initial course given here on POD7 and POD8. However, because the platelet count did not rise substantially after the initial two doses, a third 100 g dose was administered. The need for a third dose, and the suboptimal magnitude of the platelet count response—nadir of 13×10⁹/L on POD7, with an interim peak of only 54×10⁹/L by POD14—indicated that this patient's aHIT was unusually severe and resistant to IVIG. This blunted response is itself a clinical marker of severe aHIT and correlates with the unique laboratory finding of partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade by the monoclonal antibody IV.3, suggesting that the patient's antibodies activated platelets through mechanisms not fully inhibited by Fc receptor saturation.

To assess whether the anticoagulant regimen was controlling the HIT-associated consumptive coagulopathy, serial fibrinogen levels, d-dimer levels, and peripheral blood films were monitored. This approach is important because the PTT, used to titrate argatroban, may be influenced by the consumptive coagulopathy itself and does not directly reflect whether the underlying prothrombotic process is being suppressed. Falling fibrinogen levels and rising d-dimer levels indicate ongoing thrombin generation and consumption of clotting factors, while the appearance of schistocytes on the peripheral blood film suggests microvascular platelet-fibrin deposition with shear-induced red cell fragmentation—evidence that the thrombotic microangiopathy of severe HIT is not yet controlled.

On argatroban, these markers worsened progressively. Fibrinogen fell from elevated postoperative levels to a nadir of 2.49 g/L by POD17, d-dimer peaked around POD12, and schistocytes were detectable on peripheral blood films from POD9 through POD16. Together, these findings indicated that despite therapeutic argatroban dosing, the patient's HIT-associated hypercoagulability was insufficiently controlled. This is a clinically important observation: argatroban, while an effective direct thrombin inhibitor, may not adequately suppress the downstream consumptive coagulopathy in severe aHIT, where massive platelet activation generates thrombin at a rate that exceeds the capacity of a single anticoagulant mechanism. The progressive laboratory abnormalities, combined with the suboptimal platelet count response to IVIG, prompted a change in anticoagulant strategy.

On POD14, argatroban was discontinued and therapeutic-dose fondaparinux (10 mg subcutaneously once daily) was initiated. The rationale for this switch was twofold. First, fondaparinux offers practical advantages over argatroban: it is administered as a once-daily subcutaneous injection rather than a continuous intravenous infusion with PTT monitoring, simplifying care as the patient transitioned toward recovery and eventual outpatient management. Second, and more importantly, the persistent hypercoagulability on argatroban—evidenced by the falling fibrinogen, rising d-dimer, and schistocytosis—suggested that a different anticoagulant mechanism might provide better control. Fondaparinux, as an indirect antithrombin-dependent factor Xa inhibitor, reduces thrombin generation by inhibiting the proximal portion of the coagulation cascade, and this mechanism may be more effective in controlling the intense platelet-driven thrombin generation of severe aHIT.

Because fondaparinux exerts its anticoagulant effect through potentiation of antithrombin (AT), adequate AT levels are essential for therapeutic efficacy. The patient's pre-infusion AT level was mildly reduced at 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL), likely reflecting consumption during the preceding days of uncontrolled HIT-associated coagulopathy. To ensure therapeutic fondaparinux activity, supplemental AT concentrate (1000 U) was administered prior to the first fondaparinux dose. This intervention highlights an important pharmacological consideration: in the setting of ongoing consumptive coagulopathy, AT levels may be depleted, and a cofactor-dependent anticoagulant such as fondaparinux will be less effective if the cofactor is not repleted. Monitoring and supplementing AT is a practical step that can optimize the transition to fondaparinux in severe HIT.

The combined approach—therapeutic fondaparinux anticoagulation with AT repletion, following IVIG for immune modulation—ultimately proved effective. After the switch to fondaparinux, the markers of consumptive coagulopathy stabilized, the platelet count gradually recovered to 155×10⁹/L by POD62 and 202×10⁹/L by POD87, and the patient made a complete neurological recovery. Fondaparinux was continued until POD65, at which point long-term anticoagulation with oral apixaban (5 mg twice daily) was resumed. The prolonged recovery course—spanning more than two months—reflects the severity of this patient's aHIT and the time required for the pathogenic antibodies to wane, as confirmed by serial SRA testing that eventually showed evolution toward a more typical heparin-dependent antibody profile by POD124.

This case underscores several practical lessons in HIT management. First, when HIT is suspected, all heparin products must be stopped immediately and a non-heparin anticoagulant started at therapeutic intensity; prophylactic-dose fondaparinux is acceptable as an interim step but should be escalated to therapeutic dosing once the diagnosis is established. Second, the choice of anticoagulant should be individualized: argatroban's short half-life is advantageous when bleeding risk is high, but if the consumptive coagulopathy persists, switching to a factor Xa inhibitor such as fondaparinux may provide better control. Third, IVIG is a key adjunct in aHIT, but a suboptimal platelet count response should raise concern for an unusually severe antibody phenotype and prompt intensification of anticoagulant monitoring. Fourth, serial fibrinogen, d-dimer, and peripheral blood film review provide essential feedback on whether the anticoagulant is controlling the disease process. Fifth, AT levels should be checked before initiating fondaparinux in the setting of ongoing consumption, and supplemented if reduced. Finally, platelet transfusions should be avoided in HIT regardless of the degree of thrombocytopenia, as transfused platelets become targets for antibody-mediated activation and may worsen thrombosis.
