Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

## Clinical Presentation and Preoperative Context

The patient was a man in his 70s who presented with a two-year history of exertional dyspnea and longstanding atrial fibrillation, for which he was anticoagulated with oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily. A previously undetected systolic murmur prompted further cardiac evaluation. Diagnostic heart catheterization revealed 60% stenosis in the obtuse marginal coronary artery, moderate aortic stenosis, and a dilated ascending aorta—a constellation of findings that indicated the need for combined cardiac surgery. This patient's comorbidities and the planned complexity of his surgery are relevant context, as cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) is the clinical setting in which heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) most commonly occurs, with an estimated frequency of 0.5%–1.0% depending on the type and duration of postoperative heparin thromboprophylaxis.

A critical detail in the preoperative course emerged from the heart catheterization. The patient had not held his apixaban before the procedure, which influenced the cardiologist's approach in two important ways. First, the procedure was performed via a transradial artery approach rather than the more common femoral approach, likely to reduce bleeding risk in the setting of continued anticoagulation. Second, a lower-than-usual intraprocedural dose of unfractionated heparin (UFH)—only 2000 units—was administered. While this dose was small compared with the heparin doses used during cardiac surgery, it was sufficient to serve as an immunizing exposure. This preoperative UFH administration would prove to be the key to understanding the unusually early onset of thrombocytopenia that followed, as it primed the patient's immune system before the much larger intraoperative heparin exposure one week later.

The cardiac surgery was performed one week after catheterization using CPB, with anticoagulation achieved using 45,000 units of UFH—a standard high-dose regimen for cardiac surgery. The procedure included aortic valve replacement with a bioprosthesis, replacement of the ascending aorta with a Dacron graft, a single coronary artery bypass using a saphenous vein graft to the obtuse marginal coronary artery, and left atrial appendage excision. The operation proceeded without major blood loss or transfusion requirement, and the patient was easily separated from cardiopulmonary bypass with excellent hemodynamics. The uncomplicated intraoperative course makes the subsequent dramatic platelet decline all the more notable, as it argues against perioperative events such as major hemorrhage or protamine-related platelet activation as explanations for the thrombocytopenia.

The postoperative platelet count trajectory is central to understanding this case. The immediate preoperative platelet count was 210×10⁹/L, within the normal reference range of 150–400×10⁹/L. On postoperative day 1 (POD1), the platelet count was 155×10⁹/L, representing a relatively modest early decline consistent with the expected hemodilution and consumption associated with cardiac surgery and CPB. Routine postoperative thromboprophylaxis with low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) was commenced on POD1, with 5000 units of dalteparin administered subcutaneously, followed by higher doses (7500 units) on POD2, POD3, and POD4. However, from POD1 onward, the platelet count progressively and unexpectedly declined, reaching 50×10⁹/L on POD5—a level far below what would be expected from routine post-cardiac surgery platelet count recovery alone. This pattern of a precipitous fall to a profoundly low platelet count by POD5, accompanied by thrombotic stroke, is characteristic of autoimmune HIT rather than classic HIT, as discussed in subsequent sections.

The timing of the platelet count decline deserves special attention. Classic HIT typically manifests between POD5 and POD10 in patients who have undergone cardiac surgery, reflecting the approximately 5-day delay required for a primary immune response against PF4/heparin complexes. Onset before POD5 is unusual, occurring in only about 10% of cardiac surgery patients who develop HIT. When early-onset HIT does occur, it is characteristically linked to recent preoperative heparin exposure, which pre-sensitizes the patient's immune system. In this patient, the 2000-unit UFH dose during heart catheterization one week before surgery served as the sensitizing event, enabling a rapid anamnestic immune response upon re-exposure to the large intraoperative heparin dose. This temporal relationship between preoperative heparin exposure and early postoperative platelet decline is a critical diagnostic clue that the clinician must actively seek when evaluating suspected early-onset HIT.

On POD5, the absence of new focal neurological findings initially provided reassurance, but worsening postoperative delirium combined with a further platelet count decline to 26×10⁹/L on POD6 (confirmed at 24×10⁹/L on repeat testing) prompted computed tomography (CT) imaging of the brain. This imaging revealed an acute cerebral infarct in the left parietal lobe, with an area of hypodensity measuring approximately 3.2 cm × 1.8 cm and extending to the cerebral cortex. The discovery of thrombotic stroke in the setting of severe, otherwise unexplained thrombocytopenia is a hallmark clinical presentation of HIT. HIT is paradoxical among drug-induced thrombocytopenias because it is strongly associated with thrombosis rather than bleeding, with a relative risk for thrombosis of approximately 15. The combination of a falling platelet count and new thrombosis—whether arterial, venous, or microvascular—should always raise clinical suspicion for HIT, even when the temporal pattern is atypical, as it was in this case with onset before POD5. The parietal stroke observed here represents the thrombotic complication that, together with the platelet count decline, solidified the clinical suspicion for HIT and prompted laboratory testing for HIT antibodies.

## Differential Diagnosis and Initial Laboratory Evaluation

When a patient develops severe thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery, the clinician faces a broad differential diagnosis that spans benign expected postoperative changes, common serious complications, and rare but dangerous immune-mediated disorders. In this case, the platelet count fell from 155×10⁹/L on postoperative day 1 (POD1) to 50×10⁹/L on POD5 — a decline too steep and too rapid to dismiss as routine. The systematic evaluation that followed illustrates a logical, stepwise approach to post-cardiac-surgery thrombocytopenia, one that integrates the clinical picture, routine laboratory data, and careful temporal reasoning to arrive at the correct diagnosis.

### Ruling Out Common Causes

The most frequent explanations for severe thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery are those related to the patient's overall clinical condition. Cardiogenic shock and critical illness can produce thrombocytopenia through mechanisms such as disseminated intravascular coagulation, platelet consumption at sites of endothelial injury, or bone marrow suppression from systemic inflammation. Infection — whether bloodstream, urinary, or pulmonary — can similarly drive thrombocytopenia through sepsis-related consumptive processes. Both of these explanations were quickly evaluated and discarded in this patient. His hemodynamics were excellent: he had come off cardiopulmonary bypass without difficulty, and he was clinically stable apart from postoperative delirium. Blood and urine cultures were drawn and ultimately returned negative, and the chest X-ray showed no major abnormalities. With normal hemodynamics, absence of fever or other infectious signs, and negative cultures, both critical-illness thrombocytopenia and infection-related thrombocytopenia were effectively excluded.

### Ruling Out Rare Causes

Several rare entities were also considered and systematically ruled out. Post-transfusion purpura (PTP) is a dramatic immune-mediated thrombocytopenia that typically occurs 5–10 days after transfusion, caused by alloantibodies against platelet antigens such as HPA-1a. However, this patient received no blood transfusions during or after surgery, making PTP impossible. Post-surgical thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) is another rare postoperative complication, characterized by severe ADAMTS13 deficiency leading to microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and organ damage. In this patient, however, the initial laboratory markers did not support hemolysis: haptoglobin was elevated (3.19 g/L; reference range 0.32–1.97 g/L), the reticulocyte count was unremarkable, and the early peripheral blood film showed no schistocytes. The elevated haptoglobin is consistent with the early postoperative state, where haptoglobin rises as part of the acute-phase response and actually argues against active intravascular hemolysis. Drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia was also considered, but the temporal pattern excluded this possibility: drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia typically requires 5–10 days of exposure to a newly introduced drug before antibodies form and destroy platelets. The platelet count fall began on POD1, far too early to be attributable to any postoperative medication. The drugs given only on the day of surgery (vancomycin, cefazolin, tranexamic acid) and those given postoperatively (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, midodrine) were all newly started within a timeframe that would not allow an immune-mediated drug reaction to manifest by POD1–POD5.

### Initial Laboratory Workup

The complete blood count on POD5 provided essential diagnostic information beyond the platelet count itself. The hemoglobin was 97 g/L, similar to values obtained over the preceding three days, consistent with expected early surgical blood losses without ongoing hemorrhage. The white blood cell count was normal at 7.8×10⁹/L with a normal differential, which helped exclude systemic infection or a leukemoid reaction. A normal white cell count and differential in the setting of isolated severe thrombocytopenia narrows the differential considerably — it makes marrow failure syndromes and systemic infection less likely and points toward a process selectively consuming or destroying platelets.

### Coagulation Studies

Coagulation studies were performed to assess whether the thrombocytopenia might be part of a broader consumptive coagulopathy. The prothrombin time was normal, corresponding to an international normalized ratio (INR) of 1.1, and the partial thromboplastin time (PTT) was normal at 34 seconds. These normal results indicated that the clotting factor pathways were intact and that there was no significant disseminated intravascular coagulation at this early stage. The fibrinogen level, however, was elevated at 6.59 g/L (reference range 2.00–3.93 g/L). This is an expected finding in the early postoperative period because fibrinogen is an acute-phase reactant, and levels typically rise substantially after major surgery. The elevated fibrinogen is therefore not pathological in isolation but serves as an important baseline value against which subsequent changes can be measured — as will be seen later, the progressive fall in fibrinogen during the following days provided critical evidence of worsening consumptive coagulopathy.

### Blood Film Review

On POD7, peripheral blood film review confirmed the presence of true thrombocytopenia by demonstrating the absence of platelet clumps, thereby excluding pseudothrombocytopenia — a spurious low platelet count caused by EDTA-dependent platelet agglutination in the collection tube. Importantly, the film at this early stage showed no schistocytes or "toxic" white blood cell changes. The absence of schistocytes initially argued against microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (as seen in TTP or disseminated intravascular coagulation) at this point in the illness, though schistocytes did appear later (POD9–POD16) as evidence of severe HIT-associated consumptive coagulopathy developed.

### Temporal Reasoning and the Key Insight: Preoperative Heparin Exposure

With the common and rare causes of postoperative thrombocytopenia systematically excluded, attention turned to HIT. However, a critical diagnostic challenge remained: the temporal profile of the platelet count decline did not fit the expected pattern for classic HIT after cardiac surgery. Classic HIT typically begins between POD5 and POD10, reflecting the 5–10 days required for the immune response to generate clinically significant levels of anti-PF4/heparin IgG antibodies after a new heparin exposure. This patient's platelet count was already falling on POD1 — too early to be explained by the large dose of unfractionated heparin (45,000 U) given intraoperatively during cardiopulmonary bypass on the day of surgery.

The resolution of this puzzle came from careful review of the patient's preoperative history. One week before cardiac surgery, the patient had undergone heart catheterization, during which he received 2000 U of UFH via the transradial artery approach. This preoperative heparin exposure provided the necessary antecedent immune priming event. The concept of "early-onset HIT" in patients with recent preoperative heparin exposure is well established: when a patient has been exposed to heparin within the preceding 5–100 days, HIT antibodies may already be present or rapidly boosting at the time of subsequent heparin re-exposure, so the platelet count fall can begin immediately — even before POD5. Only about 10% of post-cardiac-surgery HIT cases exhibit such early onset, and it is invariably linked to recent preoperative heparin exposure, as was documented here. The combination of early-onset thrombocytopenia, the strong clinical suspicion of thrombotic stroke on imaging, and this identifiable preoperative heparin exposure made HIT the most probable diagnosis.

### Decision to Send HIT Antibody Testing

Based on this integrated reasoning, a blood sample drawn on POD5 was sent to a reference laboratory for HIT antibody testing. This decision followed from the convergence of three factors: (1) exclusion of alternative explanations for postoperative thrombocytopenia; (2) identification of the temporal pattern as consistent with early-onset HIT given the preoperative heparin exposure; and (3) the concurrent finding of an acute cerebral infarct on brain imaging, which raised the clinical stakes and strengthened the need to confirm or exclude a thrombophilic disorder. The workup now shifted from the broad differential diagnosis of postoperative thrombocytopenia to specific laboratory confirmation of HIT — a step that, as the subsequent investigations revealed, would itself yield surprising and diagnostically challenging results.

## Pathophysiology and Laboratory Diagnosis of Classic HIT vs. Autoimmune HIT

### From Immune Complex Formation to Platelet Activation: The Pathophysiology of Classic HIT

In classic heparin-induced thrombocytopenia, the central immunological event is the formation of antibodies directed against a complex of platelet factor 4 (PF4) bound to heparin. PF4 is a cationic protein stored in platelet alpha-granules and released upon platelet activation. When unfractionated heparin (UFH)—a polyanion—is introduced into the circulation, it binds PF4 through charge interactions, creating a neoepitope on PF4 that is recognized by immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies in susceptible patients. These IgG antibodies form immune complexes with PF4-heparin on platelet surfaces. The Fc portion of the IgG then engages the platelet Fcγ receptor, FcγRIIa (CD32), and cross-linking of multiple Fc receptors by the immune complexes triggers potent platelet activation. This activation cascade leads to platelet aggregation, thrombocytopenia from platelet consumption, and a striking prothrombotic state—the hallmark of HIT, with a relative risk for thrombosis of approximately 15. Critically, the formation of the PF4-heparin complex is heparin-dependent: at therapeutic heparin concentrations, the complex assembles and the antibody binds, whereas at very high (supratherapeutic) heparin concentrations—approximately 100 U/mL—the excess polyanion saturates PF4 and disrupts the immune complex, preventing antibody binding and abolishing platelet activation. This heparin-dependence and its loss at supratherapeutic concentrations is the serological signature of classic HIT, and it forms the conceptual baseline against which autoimmune HIT must be contrasted.

### The Concept of Autoimmune HIT: Heparin-Independent Platelet Activation

Autoimmune HIT (aHIT) represents a recently recognized subgroup of HIT in which the antibodies possess the capacity to activate platelets even in the complete absence of heparin. Rather than requiring PF4-heparin immune complexes, these antibodies recognize PF4 in a conformation that is already adoptable on the platelet surface or in solution, independent of heparin binding. The clinical importance of this distinction is considerable: because the antibodies do not require ongoing heparin exposure to trigger platelet activation, simply stopping heparin—as is the standard management for classic HIT—does not reliably halt the thrombotic process. This heparin-independent activation accounts for the unusually severe clinical courses observed in aHIT, including profound thrombocytopenia, delayed platelet count recovery, and features of a consumptive coagulopathy resembling disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). In the case under discussion, the patient's platelet count fell to a nadir of only 13×10⁹/L—far below the typical nadir of classic HIT, which usually bottoms out in the 40–80×10⁹/L range—and was accompanied by falling fibrinogen levels, rising d-dimer levels, and the transient appearance of schistocytes on the peripheral blood film from postoperative day (POD) 9 to POD16, all of which indicated severe, progressive HIT-associated consumptive coagulopathy. Furthermore, the patient's platelet count rose only modestly after high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (from 13×10⁹/L on POD7 to a peak of 54×10⁹/L on POD14), a much blunter response than is typically seen when IVIg is administered for aHIT, further underscoring the unusual severity of this patient's autoimmune phenotype.

### PF4/Polyanion ELISA: Detecting HIT Antibodies

The diagnostic workup of HIT typically begins with a screening immunoassay, most commonly the PF4/polyanion enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). In this assay, a PF4-polyanion complex is immobilized on a microtiter plate. Patient serum is added, and if IgG antibodies against the PF4-polyanion complex are present, they bind to the immobilized antigen. After washing, an enzyme-conjugated anti-human IgG is added, followed by a substrate whose color change is measured spectrophotometrically and reported in optical density (OD) units. A strongly positive ELISA result—generally defined as OD >1.0—correlates with a high probability of clinically significant HIT, because the OD roughly reflects the antibody concentration and, to some extent, the functional potency of the antibodies. In this case, the ELISA returned a strongly positive result of 2.2 OD units (reference range <0.4 OD units), providing strong presumptive evidence that the patient's thrombocytopenia and stroke were attributable to HIT. However, the ELISA detects the presence of antibodies regardless of their functional capacity to activate platelets; it cannot, by itself, distinguish classic HIT from aHIT, nor can it confirm that the antibodies are pathogenic. For this reason, a confirmatory functional assay—the serotonin-release assay (SRA)—is essential.

### The Serotonin-Release Assay: Principle and Typical HIT Profile

The SRA is the reference standard functional assay for HIT because it directly measures the capacity of patient antibodies to activate platelets. In this assay, washed donor platelets are preloaded with radiolabeled serotonin and then incubated with patient serum in the presence of varying concentrations of UFH. If pathogenic HIT antibodies are present, they form immune complexes that cross-link platelet FcγRIIa, triggering platelet activation and degranulation, which releases the radiolabeled serotonin into the supernatant. The percentage of serotonin released is measured, with <20% release considered negative and ≥20% considered positive.

A typical SRA profile for classic HIT shows minimal or no serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin (no heparin-independent activation), strong release at therapeutic heparin concentrations of 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL, and return to <20% release at the supratherapeutic concentration of 100 U/mL. This extinguishment at high heparin concentration is a pathognomonic feature of classic HIT, reflecting the dissolution of PF4-heparin immune complexes when heparin is in vast excess, as discussed above. The SRA, therefore, simultaneously confirms the presence of functionally relevant HIT antibodies and demonstrates their heparin-dependence.

### SRA Results in This Case: Confirming HIT but Revealing Atypical Features

In this patient, the SRA performed on a POD5 serum sample demonstrated strong serotonin release at 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL UFH (97% and 99%, respectively), consistent with the presence of potent platelet-activating HIT antibodies. The absence of serotonin release at 100 U/mL UFH (0%) was likewise consistent with classic HIT, confirming that high heparin concentrations disrupted the immune complexes. Together with the strongly positive ELISA and the clinical context of postoperative thrombocytopenia with thrombotic stroke, these results firmly established the diagnosis of HIT. However, two additional features of the SRA profile deviated from the classic pattern and ultimately proved decisive in classifying this case as autoimmune HIT.

### Atypical SRA Feature 1: Heparin-Independent Platelet Activation

The first atypical finding was strong serotonin release in the complete absence of heparin. At 0 U/mL UFH, the patient's serum produced 96% serotonin release, far exceeding the reference range of <20%. This heparin-independent platelet activation is the defining laboratory hallmark of aHIT, indicating that the patient's antibodies could activate platelets through FcγRIIa clustering without requiring the formation of PF4-heparin immune complexes. In classic HIT, by contrast, the absence of heparin means no PF4-heparin complex formation, no immune complex assembly, and therefore no platelet activation; release at 0 U/mL should be minimal. This single result immediately distinguished the patient's condition from classic HIT and placed it within the aHIT spectrum, explaining the severity of the clinical course and the resistance to routine management.

### Atypical SRA Feature 2: Partial Resistance to Fc Receptor Blockade

The second atypical finding was novel and, to the authors' knowledge, previously unreported. The SRA protocol includes a critical control step: the addition of monoclonal antibody IV.3, which blocks the platelet FcγRIIa receptor. In classic HIT, IV.3 should nearly completely inhibit serotonin release because the antibodies require Fc receptor engagement to activate platelets; with the receptor blocked, the immune complexes can bind but cannot trigger the activation cascade, and release should fall below 20%. In this case, however, the addition of IV.3 at 0.1 U/mL UFH reduced serotonin release only partially, to 76%—well above the expected <20% threshold. This partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade indicated that the patient's HIT antibodies were unusually reactive, possessing a capacity to activate platelets that exceeded what could be fully blocked by IV.3 under standard assay conditions. The mechanism underlying this phenomenon remains speculative, but it may relate to the exceptional affinity or density of antibody binding, allowing residual Fc receptor engagement despite partial blockade. Regardless of mechanism, this novel serological phenotype represented a uniquely severe subtype of aHIT and provided an important clue to understanding the patient's unusually profound thrombocytopenia and delayed recovery.

### Reconciling the "Indeterminate" SRA Result

Because of the partial resistance to IV.3 blockade, the reporting laboratory initially designated the SRA result as "indeterminate," reflecting uncertainty about whether the pattern represented true HIT or an artifact. This designation prompted the treating team to request expert review from the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory. Upon critical analysis, the full SRA profile was recognized as consistent with aHIT: the strong heparin-dependent release at 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL, the extinguishment at 100 U/mL, the strong heparin-independent release at 0 U/mL, and the partial (rather than complete) inhibition by IV.3 were all reconcilable under the unifying diagnosis of aHIT with an unusually severe serological phenotype. The "indeterminate" label, therefore, did not reflect a failure to meet diagnostic criteria but rather the limitations of a standard interpretive framework that did not yet encompass this extreme pattern of Fc receptor blockade resistance. Subsequent serial testing supported the diagnosis: later SRA profiles obtained during the prolonged recovery period showed more characteristic aHIT patterns, and the final sample tested at POD124—after platelet count recovery—demonstrated a predominantly heparin-dependent SRA profile (15% release at 0 U/mL, 100% release at 0.3 U/mL), reflecting the waning of aHIT antibodies and evolution toward a classic HIT serological pattern. This serial evolution provided further confirmation that the patient had indeed experienced aHIT.

### Clinical Importance of Recognizing Atypical SRA Profiles

This case illustrates a critical diagnostic principle: failure to recognize atypical SRA profiles can lead to a missed diagnosis of aHIT with potentially devastating consequences. The initial "indeterminate" label, if accepted at face value without expert review, might have cast doubt on the diagnosis of HIT and delayed or derailed the initiation of appropriate therapy—namely, cessation of all heparin, initiation of a non-heparin anticoagulant, and administration of high-dose IVIg, which specifically targets the Fc receptor-mediated platelet activation central to aHIT. The lesson is that the SRA must be interpreted in its entirety, integrating the heparin-dependent release pattern, the degree of heparin-independent release at 0 U/mL, and the extent of IV.3 inhibition, rather than relying on a single binary criterion. Strong serotonin release at 0 U/mL should always raise the question of aHIT, and partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade, while rare, is compatible with a uniquely severe form of aHIT rather than excluding the diagnosis. Clinicians and laboratory specialists must maintain a low threshold to seek expert consultation when the SRA profile does not fit the classic mold, because the therapeutic implications—particularly the need for IVIg and the potential for prolonged, severe consumptive coagulopathy—differ substantially from those of classic HIT.

## Treatment Strategy and Management of Severe Autoimmune HIT

The management of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) rests on several foundational principles that must be applied simultaneously and urgently: immediate discontinuation of all heparin products (including low-molecular-weight heparin), initiation of a rapid-acting non-heparin anticoagulant, avoidance of platelet transfusions even when thrombocytopenia is severe, and careful clinical and laboratory monitoring for thrombosis and consumptive coagulopathy. In autoimmune HIT (aHIT), an additional therapeutic modality—high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG)—is employed to interrupt the platelet-activating effects of heparin-independent antibodies. This case illustrates each of these principles while also demonstrating how treatment decisions must be dynamically adjusted when the patient's clinical course proves unusually severe or refractory.

The first therapeutic intervention in this patient occurred on postoperative day 5 (POD5), when the platelet count had fallen to 50×10⁹/L and HIT was clinically suspected. Dalteparin, the low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) being used for postoperative thromboprophylaxis, was immediately discontinued. This step is non-negotiable in suspected HIT: continued exposure to any heparin product, whether unfractionated heparin (UFH) or LMWH, will fuel ongoing platelet activation and thrombin generation. Prophylactic-dose fondaparinux (2.5 mg subcutaneously) was begun on POD5 at 18:00 as an interim measure. Fondaparinux, a synthetic pentasaccharide factor Xa inhibitor, does not cross-react with HIT antibodies and is a reasonable option for prophylaxis or treatment of HIT, though it is used off-label for this indication. However, once the diagnosis of HIT was supported by a strong-positive PF4/polyanion ELISA and the clinical picture was further complicated by the discovery of an acute cerebral infarct on POD6 imaging, a more aggressive therapeutic strategy was required.

Therapeutic-dose argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, was selected as the primary anticoagulant at a dose of 0.75 mcg/kg/min, with a partial thromboplastin time (PTT) target of 55–85 seconds. The treating haematologist specifically chose argatroban over therapeutic-dose fondaparinux at this juncture for two reasons. First, argatroban has a short half-life (approximately 40–50 minutes), allowing for rapid dose titration and rapid reversal of anticoagulant effect if bleeding occurred—a critical consideration given the patient's recent ischemic stroke and the theoretical risk of hemorrhagic transformation. Second, the patient's platelet count had fallen to a profoundly low level (26×10⁹/L on POD6), increasing the baseline bleeding risk. In a patient this thrombocytopenic with a fresh cerebral infarct, an anticoagulant that can be quickly turned off is safer than a longer-acting agent given by subcutaneous injection. Argatroban is also hepatically cleared, making it suitable for patients with normal liver function, as was the case here. The PTT-adjusted dosing strategy requires close laboratory monitoring, but this is feasible in the inpatient setting and provides real-time assessment of anticoagulant intensity.

In parallel with anticoagulant management, high-dose IVIG was administered. IVIG has emerged as a specific therapy for aHIT because it is thought to block the platelet Fcγ receptors through which HIT antibodies activate platelets, thereby interrupting the platelet consumption and thrombin generation that characterize the syndrome. In typical aHIT, IVIG often produces a brisk platelet count rise within several days, reflecting rapid cessation of antibody-mediated platelet activation. The standard dosing employed is 1 g/kg per day (rounded to 100 g for practical purposes in this patient) for two consecutive days, which was the initial course given here on POD7 and POD8. However, because the platelet count did not rise substantially after the initial two doses, a third 100 g dose was administered. The need for a third dose, and the suboptimal magnitude of the platelet count response—nadir of 13×10⁹/L on POD7, with an interim peak of only 54×10⁹/L by POD14—indicated that this patient's aHIT was unusually severe and resistant to IVIG. This blunted response is itself a clinical marker of severe aHIT and correlates with the unique laboratory finding of partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade by the monoclonal antibody IV.3, suggesting that the patient's antibodies activated platelets through mechanisms not fully inhibited by Fc receptor saturation.

To assess whether the anticoagulant regimen was controlling the HIT-associated consumptive coagulopathy, serial fibrinogen levels, d-dimer levels, and peripheral blood films were monitored. This approach is important because the PTT, used to titrate argatroban, may be influenced by the consumptive coagulopathy itself and does not directly reflect whether the underlying prothrombotic process is being suppressed. Falling fibrinogen levels and rising d-dimer levels indicate ongoing thrombin generation and consumption of clotting factors, while the appearance of schistocytes on the peripheral blood film suggests microvascular platelet-fibrin deposition with shear-induced red cell fragmentation—evidence that the thrombotic microangiopathy of severe HIT is not yet controlled.

On argatroban, these markers worsened progressively. Fibrinogen fell from elevated postoperative levels to a nadir of 2.49 g/L by POD17, d-dimer peaked around POD12, and schistocytes were detectable on peripheral blood films from POD9 through POD16. Together, these findings indicated that despite therapeutic argatroban dosing, the patient's HIT-associated hypercoagulability was insufficiently controlled. This is a clinically important observation: argatroban, while an effective direct thrombin inhibitor, may not adequately suppress the downstream consumptive coagulopathy in severe aHIT, where massive platelet activation generates thrombin at a rate that exceeds the capacity of a single anticoagulant mechanism. The progressive laboratory abnormalities, combined with the suboptimal platelet count response to IVIG, prompted a change in anticoagulant strategy.

On POD14, argatroban was discontinued and therapeutic-dose fondaparinux (10 mg subcutaneously once daily) was initiated. The rationale for this switch was twofold. First, fondaparinux offers practical advantages over argatroban: it is administered as a once-daily subcutaneous injection rather than a continuous intravenous infusion with PTT monitoring, simplifying care as the patient transitioned toward recovery and eventual outpatient management. Second, and more importantly, the persistent hypercoagulability on argatroban—evidenced by the falling fibrinogen, rising d-dimer, and schistocytosis—suggested that a different anticoagulant mechanism might provide better control. Fondaparinux, as an indirect antithrombin-dependent factor Xa inhibitor, reduces thrombin generation by inhibiting the proximal portion of the coagulation cascade, and this mechanism may be more effective in controlling the intense platelet-driven thrombin generation of severe aHIT.

Because fondaparinux exerts its anticoagulant effect through potentiation of antithrombin (AT), adequate AT levels are essential for therapeutic efficacy. The patient's pre-infusion AT level was mildly reduced at 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL), likely reflecting consumption during the preceding days of uncontrolled HIT-associated coagulopathy. To ensure therapeutic fondaparinux activity, supplemental AT concentrate (1000 U) was administered prior to the first fondaparinux dose. This intervention highlights an important pharmacological consideration: in the setting of ongoing consumptive coagulopathy, AT levels may be depleted, and a cofactor-dependent anticoagulant such as fondaparinux will be less effective if the cofactor is not repleted. Monitoring and supplementing AT is a practical step that can optimize the transition to fondaparinux in severe HIT.

The combined approach—therapeutic fondaparinux anticoagulation with AT repletion, following IVIG for immune modulation—ultimately proved effective. After the switch to fondaparinux, the markers of consumptive coagulopathy stabilized, the platelet count gradually recovered to 155×10⁹/L by POD62 and 202×10⁹/L by POD87, and the patient made a complete neurological recovery. Fondaparinux was continued until POD65, at which point long-term anticoagulation with oral apixaban (5 mg twice daily) was resumed. The prolonged recovery course—spanning more than two months—reflects the severity of this patient's aHIT and the time required for the pathogenic antibodies to wane, as confirmed by serial SRA testing that eventually showed evolution toward a more typical heparin-dependent antibody profile by POD124.

This case underscores several practical lessons in HIT management. First, when HIT is suspected, all heparin products must be stopped immediately and a non-heparin anticoagulant started at therapeutic intensity; prophylactic-dose fondaparinux is acceptable as an interim step but should be escalated to therapeutic dosing once the diagnosis is established. Second, the choice of anticoagulant should be individualized: argatroban's short half-life is advantageous when bleeding risk is high, but if the consumptive coagulopathy persists, switching to a factor Xa inhibitor such as fondaparinux may provide better control. Third, IVIG is a key adjunct in aHIT, but a suboptimal platelet count response should raise concern for an unusually severe antibody phenotype and prompt intensification of anticoagulant monitoring. Fourth, serial fibrinogen, d-dimer, and peripheral blood film review provide essential feedback on whether the anticoagulant is controlling the disease process. Fifth, AT levels should be checked before initiating fondaparinux in the setting of ongoing consumption, and supplemented if reduced. Finally, platelet transfusions should be avoided in HIT regardless of the degree of thrombocytopenia, as transfused platelets become targets for antibody-mediated activation and may worsen thrombosis.

## Outcome, Follow-up, and Clinical Lessons

Following the switch to therapeutic-dose fondaparinux on postoperative day 14 (POD14), the patient's clinical course improved gradually but decisively. The platelet count, which had fallen to a nadir of 13×10⁹/L on POD7 and had risen only modestly to an interim peak of 54×10⁹/L on POD14 despite high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), continued a slow recovery thereafter. By POD62, the platelet count had reached 155×10⁹/L—within the normal reference range—and by POD87 it had fully normalized at 202×10⁹/L. This protracted recovery trajectory, spanning nearly two months, contrasts with the more rapid platelet count rebound typically observed in classic heparin-dependent HIT once heparin is discontinued and alternative anticoagulation is established, and it reflects the persistent, heparin-independent platelet activation that characterizes autoimmune HIT (aHIT).

Neurologically, the patient made a complete recovery despite the acute left parietal cerebral infarct identified on CT imaging on POD6. The stroke, which had been discovered incidentally in the setting of worsening postoperative delirium and further platelet count decline, left no residual neurological abnormalities. This favorable neurological outcome is particularly noteworthy given that thrombosis in the setting of HIT can carry significant morbidity, and it underscores the importance of prompt anticoagulant management even in the face of profound thrombocytopenia.

Fondaparinux was continued at therapeutic dosing (10 mg subcutaneously once daily) until POD65, at which point the patient was transitioned back to oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily for long-term anticoagulation. The rationale for resuming apixaban—the patient's preoperative anticoagulant for atrial fibrillation—was that the acute HIT episode had resolved, as evidenced by platelet count recovery, and the risk of recurrent thrombosis had diminished. The total duration of non-heparin anticoagulant therapy (argatroban followed by fondaparinux) thus spanned approximately 51 days, reflecting the prolonged time to recovery in this unusually severe case.

Serial serotonin-release assay (SRA) testing played a critical role in confirming the diagnosis retrospectively and in documenting the immunological evolution of the antibody response over time. SRA results remained strongly positive for several months after the acute episode. Importantly, several SRA profiles obtained during the prolonged recovery period exhibited a more characteristic aHIT pattern—that is, strong serotonin release in the absence of heparin, with preserved release at therapeutic heparin concentrations and inhibition at supratherapeutic concentrations. This evolution toward a more typical aHIT profile provided corroborative evidence for the diagnosis, as the initial SRA had yielded an atypical result due to partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade by monoclonal antibody IV.3.

The final blood sample tested, obtained on POD124 after complete platelet count recovery, showed an SRA profile consistent with predominantly heparin-dependent HIT antibodies: only 15% serotonin release in the absence of UFH (near the negative threshold of <20%), but 100% release in the presence of 0.3 U/mL UFH. This shift from heparin-independent to heparin-dependent reactivity reflects the natural waning of the autoimmune component of the antibody response. As the pathogenic autoantibodies declined in titer, the remaining antibodies exhibited the classic heparin-dependent binding and activation properties seen in typical HIT. This serial evolution of SRA profiles—from an initially atypical pattern with partial IV.3 resistance, to more characteristic aHIT profiles during recovery, and finally to a classic heparin-dependent pattern—provided the strongest laboratory confirmation that the patient had indeed experienced aHIT.

The diagnosis of aHIT was thus confirmed on three interlocking grounds. First, subsequent SRA profiles during the recovery period demonstrated reactivity patterns more characteristic of aHIT, validating the initial suspicion. Second, the final sample showed the expected transition to a heparin-dependent HIT pattern, reflecting waning of the autoantibody population. Third, no alternative diagnosis could plausibly explain the patient's entire clinical course, including the early-onset severe thrombocytopenia, thrombotic stroke, consumptive coagulopathy with falling fibrinogen and rising d-dimer, schistocytosis, and the unique SRA profile. The combination of evolving serology, expected immunological maturation, and diagnostic exclusion constitutes a robust framework for confirming aHIT in cases where initial laboratory results are ambiguous.

Several clinical and diagnostic lessons emerge from this case that are directly applicable to the evaluation and management of postoperative thrombocytopenia in heparin-exposed patients.

First, preoperative heparin exposure can precipitate early-onset HIT before the conventional diagnostic window of POD5–POD10. In this patient, the administration of 2000 U of unfractionated heparin (UFH) during preoperative transradial heart catheterization—one week before cardiac surgery—primed the immune system, such that the subsequent large intraoperative heparin dose during cardiopulmonary bypass triggered an anamnestic antibody response. Only about 10% of cardiac surgery patients who develop HIT exhibit a platelet count fall before POD5, and this phenomenon is invariably linked to recent preoperative heparin exposure. Clinicians evaluating early postoperative thrombocytopenia must therefore elicit a careful history of all heparin exposures in the preceding weeks, not only those occurring during the index surgical admission.

Second, severe thrombocytopenia with a nadir as low as 13×10⁹/L, accompanied by features of consumptive coagulopathy—falling fibrinogen, rising d-dimer, and schistocytosis—should raise suspicion for aHIT rather than classic HIT. In typical HIT, the platelet count nadir usually remains above 20–30×10⁹/L, and frank DIC-like laboratory abnormalities are uncommon. The profound thrombocytopenia and progressive coagulopathy observed in this patient reflect intense, heparin-independent platelet activation with secondary coagulation consumption, a hallmark of the autoimmune subtype. When these features are encountered, particularly in the setting of a strong-positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, the possibility of aHIT should be actively considered, and the SRA should be scrutinized for evidence of heparin-independent platelet activation.

Third, partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade on the SRA is a novel serological phenotype of aHIT that should not be misinterpreted as excluding the diagnosis. In classic HIT, the addition of monoclonal antibody IV.3—which blocks the platelet FcγRIIa receptor—reliably inhibits serotonin release to below 20%, confirming that platelet activation is Fc receptor-mediated. In this patient, IV.3 only partially inhibited serotonin release (76% residual release at 0.1 U/mL UFH despite IV.3), initially yielding an "indeterminate" SRA result. This partial resistance likely reflects an unusually high antibody titer or an unusually high-affinity antibody capable of overcoming competitive Fc receptor blockade under standard assay conditions. Awareness of this atypical serological profile is essential, as a missed or delayed diagnosis of aHIT could lead to continued heparin administration, inadequate anticoagulation, and potentially catastrophic thrombotic complications.

Fourth, multimodal therapy combining a non-heparin anticoagulant with IVIG may be necessary for severe aHIT, and fondaparinux can be an effective option for refractory cases. In this patient, argatroban was initially chosen for its short half-life and titratability, given the concern for bleeding in the setting of recent stroke and profound thrombocytopenia. However, persisting hypercoagulability—evidenced by falling fibrinogen, rising d-dimer, and schistocytes despite therapeutic argatroban—prompted a switch to therapeutic-dose fondaparinux on POD14. Fondaparinux, a synthetic pentasaccharide factor Xa inhibitor, does not cross-react with HIT antibodies and has been used successfully as an off-label treatment for HIT. The supplemental antithrombin concentrate given prior to initiating fondaparinux addressed the patient's mildly reduced antithrombin level and ensured adequate anticoagulant activity. The eventual platelet count recovery following this switch supports the use of fondaparinux as a viable alternative when direct thrombin inhibitors fail to adequately control HIT-associated consumptive coagulopathy.

Fifth and finally, awareness of atypical SRA profiles is essential to avoid missed diagnoses of aHIT. The initial "indeterminate" SRA result in this case—featuring both heparin-independent platelet activation and partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade—could have been misinterpreted as a negative or inconclusive test, potentially leading to diagnostic uncertainty and inappropriate management. Clinicians and laboratory personnel must recognize that aHIT can produce SRA profiles that deviate from the classic HIT pattern in two key ways: significant platelet activation in the absence of therapeutic-dose heparin, and incomplete inhibition by IV.3. When these atypical features are encountered, the diagnosis of aHIT should be actively considered rather than dismissed, and repeat testing should be pursued to track the evolution of antibody reactivity over time. The longitudinal SRA data in this case—showing transition from an atypical aHIT profile to a classic heparin-dependent profile as antibodies waned—demonstrate the diagnostic value of serial testing in confirming aHIT and in documenting the natural immunological history of the disorder.

In summary, this case illustrates the full clinical arc of aHIT from acute presentation through prolonged recovery and ultimate resolution. The favorable outcome—despite severe thrombocytopenia, thrombotic stroke, and an initially confusing laboratory profile—demonstrates that prompt recognition of atypical HIT, immediate cessation of all heparin products, individualized non-heparin anticoagulation, and judicious use of IVIG can lead to complete recovery even in the most challenging cases. The novel serological features described here expand the recognized spectrum of aHIT and provide important diagnostic lessons for clinicians and laboratory specialists alike.
