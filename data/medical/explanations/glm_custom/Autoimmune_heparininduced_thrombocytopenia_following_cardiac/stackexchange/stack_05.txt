Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

### [Differential Diagnosis] What are the major diagnostic considerations for severe thrombocytopenia occurring after cardiac surgery, and what specific clinical and laboratory features of this case made alternative diagnoses such as cardiogenic shock, infection, post-transfusion purpura, post-surgical TTP, and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia less likely?

## Major Diagnostic Considerations for Severe Thrombocytopenia After Cardiac Surgery

Severe thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery demands a systematic differential diagnosis. The major considerations include cardiogenic shock or critical illness–associated thrombocytopenia, infection/sepsis, post-transfusion purpura (PTP), post-surgical thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia (DIT), and heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) — including its autoimmune subtype (aHIT). In this case, several clinical and laboratory features systematically narrowed the differential and pointed toward HIT, specifically aHIT triggered by preoperative heparin exposure.

**Cardiogenic shock and critical illness** were effectively excluded by the patient's stable postoperative course. He came off cardiopulmonary bypass with excellent hemodynamics, and there was no clinical evidence of hypoperfusion or multiorgan dysfunction to explain a platelet count falling to 13 × 10⁹/L.

**Infection** was unlikely given negative blood and urine cultures, a normal white blood cell count (7.8 × 10⁹/L) with normal differential, and an unremarkable chest X-ray. No fever or other clinical signs of sepsis were present.

**Post-transfusion purpura** was excluded by the explicit history that no blood transfusions were administered during or after surgery, eliminating the requisite antigen exposure for this antibody-mediated syndrome.

**Post-surgical TTP** was considered but made unlikely by an elevated haptoglobin (3.19 g/L) and the initial absence of schistocytes on the peripheral blood film. While schistocytes later appeared from POD9 to POD16, these were attributed to severe HIT-associated consumptive coagulopathy — supported by concurrently falling fibrinogen and rising D-dimer levels — rather than to a primary thrombotic microangiopathy.

**Drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia** was unlikely on temporal grounds. The platelet count decline was already underway from POD1, far too early for immune-mediated destruction from newly introduced postoperative medications (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, midodrine) or even intraoperative agents (vancomycin, cefazolin, tranexamic acid), which typically require days to a week of exposure before triggering thrombocytopenia.

The remaining diagnosis — HIT — was strongly supported by three converging features: the strong-positive PF4/polyanion ELISA (2.2 OD units), the occurrence of thrombotic stroke (consistent with HIT's prothrombotic nature), and the characteristic SRA pattern showing maximal platelet activation at therapeutic heparin concentrations with inhibition at supratherapeutic levels. Although the onset before POD5 was earlier than expected for typical post-cardiac-surgery HIT, subsequent review identified preoperative UFH exposure during transradial cardiac catheterization one week prior, providing the sensitizing event. The atypical SRA features — strong serotonin release even in the absence of heparin and only partial inhibition by Fc receptor blockade with monoclonal antibody IV.3 — ultimately established the diagnosis of autoimmune HIT, a severe subtype capable of producing the profound thrombocytopenia and consumptive coagulopathy observed in this patient.
