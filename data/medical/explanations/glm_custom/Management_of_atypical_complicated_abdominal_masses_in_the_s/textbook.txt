Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Assessment

The patient was a nulliparous woman in her 40s, classified as G1P0A1, who presented at 21 weeks of gestational age. Her body mass index was 33, placing her in the obese category—a factor that would later complicate both physical examination and imaging interpretation. Advanced maternal age and nulliparity are important contextual features, as both are associated with a higher likelihood of uterine leiomyomas and adnexal pathology. Her pregnancy had been uncomplicated to this point: first- and second-trimester aneuploidy screening reported low risk, non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT) was reassuring, and fetal morphology scans were normal. This unremarkable obstetric history meant that the acute presentation of abdominal pain and dyspnea represented a departure from an otherwise low-risk pregnancy and warranted thorough evaluation for non-obstetric causes.

The patient's chief complaints were severe, acute abdominal pain localized to both the upper left quadrant and the pelvis, each rated 5 on the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), accompanied by mild dyspnea. The VAS is a validated 0–10 scale where a score of 5 corresponds to moderate pain that typically interferes with daily activity and may require pharmacologic intervention. The combination of abdominal pain and dyspnea in a pregnant patient raises several important diagnostic considerations: the pain could reflect obstetric complications such as preterm labor, placental abruption, or uterine rupture, or non-obstetric causes such as ovarian torsion, degenerating leiomyoma, appendicitis, or biliary disease. Dyspnea in the second trimester can be physiologic—driven by progesterone-mediated increases in respiratory drive and the beginning of mechanical diaphragmatic elevation from the enlarging uterus—but when associated with acute abdominal pain, it raises concern for a pathologic process producing mass effect on the diaphragm, restriction of pulmonary excursion, or, less commonly, a cardiopulmonary event.

Upon arrival at the emergency department, the initial evaluation focused on excluding immediately life-threatening conditions. Vital signs were reassuring: oxygen saturation was 97% on room air, heart rate was 74 bpm, and blood pressure was 100/60 mmHg. An electrocardiogram was normal. These findings effectively ruled out hemodynamic instability and primary cardiopulmonary pathology, allowing the clinical focus to shift toward the abdominal compartment as the source of symptoms. After this initial stabilization and triage, the patient was transferred to the Obstetrical and Gynaecological Division for more specialized evaluation.

The abdominal examination revealed enlargement greater than expected for 21 weeks of gestation, with a tender swelling in the upper left quadrant and a taut-elastic mass measuring approximately 18 cm. There were no clinical signs of ascites, which helped narrow the differential away from conditions such as ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome, advanced ovarian malignancy with peritoneal spread, or severe hepatic decompensation. In pregnancy, abdominal examination is inherently limited by the enlarging uterus, and distinguishing a pathologic mass from normal uterine enlargement requires careful correlation with gestational age, fundal height, and imaging. The taut-elastic quality of the mass on palpation suggested a cystic or fluid-filled structure rather than a solid neoplasm, though this impression would require imaging confirmation.

Bimanual vaginal examination provided critical additional information. A fixed, rounded, hard mass of approximately 9 cm was palpated in the lesser pelvis, partially bulging at the posterior fornix. The fixation and hardness of this mass on vaginal examination raised concern for a solid lesion, potentially a leiomyoma, whereas the cranial mass's taut-elastic quality suggested a cystic process. The posterior fornix bulging is notable because it can indicate a mass occupying the pouch of Douglas, the most dependent portion of the female pelvis. The discovery of two anatomically distinct masses—a large upper abdominal lesion and a separate pelvic lesion—immediately broadened the differential diagnosis and underscored the need for cross-sectional imaging to define their origin, relationship to the uterus and adnexa, and potential connection to one another.

Initial laboratory testing revealed several abnormalities. The patient had iron deficiency anemia with a hemoglobin of 9.9 g/L (normal range 12.0–16.6 g/L) and serum ferritin of 25 mcg/L (normal range 15–300 mcg/L). Iron deficiency anemia is common in pregnancy due to the expanded plasma volume and increased fetal iron demand, but a hemoglobin below 10 g/L is significant because it increases the risk of adverse maternal and fetal outcomes and reduces physiologic reserve in the event of surgical intervention or hemorrhage. C-reactive protein was mildly elevated at 1.39 mg/dL (normal range 0–0.3 mg/dL), and the white blood cell count was 11.29×10³/µL with a neutrophil count of 9.27×10³/µL. Leukocytosis with neutrophilia is a well-described physiologic change in pregnancy, driven by estrogen-mediated demargination of neutrophils from the vascular endothelium; values up to 13×10³/µL are considered within normal limits in the second and third trimesters. The mild CRP elevation, however, is not explained by pregnancy alone and suggested an underlying inflammatory or infectious process.

During the hospital stay, the patient developed a low-grade fever. A SARS-CoV-2 nasopharyngeal swab was negative, but urine culture detected *Klebsiella pneumoniae*. Asymptomatic bacteriuria and symptomatic urinary tract infections are more common in pregnancy due to progesterone-mediated ureteral smooth muscle relaxation, mechanical compression of the ureters by the gravid uterus, and glucosuria promoting bacterial growth. *Klebsiella pneumoniae* is a common uropathogen in pregnancy, and its identification had both diagnostic and therapeutic implications: it provided a plausible explanation for the low-grade fever and CRP elevation, and it would guide postoperative antibiotic selection. Importantly, however, the urinary tract infection did not fully explain the abdominal pain, the large palpable masses, or the dyspnea, and the clinical team correctly pursued further evaluation of the abdominal pathology rather than attributing all symptoms to the urinary infection.

Initial symptomatic management consisted of intravenous paracetamol at 1000 mg twice daily and placement in a semi-orthostatic position. The rationale for semi-orthostatic positioning was to reduce diaphragmatic compression from the gravid uterus and the large abdominal masses, thereby alleviating dyspnea. This approach decreased the pain from VAS 5 to VAS 1 without requiring supplemental oxygen or ventilatory support, which both provided symptomatic relief and supported the hypothesis that the dyspnea was mechanical in origin rather than due to intrinsic cardiopulmonary disease. The clinical improvement with conservative measures, while meaningful, did not eliminate the need for definitive diagnosis, as the masses remained and carried risks of rupture, torsion, infection, or malignant transformation—all of which would inform the subsequent decision to pursue imaging and ultimately surgical management.

## Imaging Evaluation and Tumor Marker Assessment

The diagnostic workup of abdominal masses in pregnancy requires a careful balance between obtaining sufficient information for clinical decision-making and minimizing fetal risk. In this case, the patient presented at 21 weeks' gestation with severe abdominal pain, dyspnea, and two palpable masses—a tender 18 cm swelling in the left upper quadrant and a fixed 9 cm mass in the lesser pelvis. The diagnostic strategy relied on two complementary imaging modalities—ultrasound and non-contrast MRI—alongside a serum tumor marker panel, each of which contributed to the differential diagnosis but also illustrated the inherent limitations of evaluating masses in a gravid abdomen.

**Ultrasound Findings and Their Interpretation**

Transvaginal and transabdominal ultrasound demonstrated two large, unilocular cystic masses with regular margins and peripheral vascularization. The upper mass measured 18 × 12 × 7 cm and extended from the left subphrenic region to the uterine fundus, occupying the entire left subphrenic space. The lower mass measured 9.5 × 8 × 8 cm and appeared adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma with cystic degeneration, the myoma itself measuring 58 × 71 mm. The unilocular appearance and regular margins initially suggested benign cystic lesions, and the peripheral vascularization pattern was consistent with encapsulated masses rather than infiltrative neoplasms. However, the adherence of the caudal mass to a uterine myoma introduced diagnostic ambiguity, as it remained unclear whether the cystic component represented a degenerating leiomyoma, an endometrioma adherent to the uterine wall, or a true adnexal cyst. Ultrasound remains the first-line imaging modality in pregnancy due to its safety, availability, and ability to characterize cyst architecture, but in this case, the massive size of the lesions and the enlarging gravid uterus limited the ability to identify connective stalks or feeding vessels, particularly for the upper mass. This "steric bulk" effect—the physical obstruction of visualization by the pregnant uterus and the huge masses—prevented definitive identification of the origin of the upper lesion and oriented the initial differential diagnosis toward adnexal pathology, specifically endometriomas.

**Rationale and Safety of Non-Contrast MRI in Pregnancy**

Given the diagnostic uncertainty on ultrasound, the multidisciplinary team proceeded to MRI without contrast medium. This decision reflects established safety principles in pregnancy: gadolinium-based contrast agents cross the placenta and have been associated with rheumatological, inflammatory, and infiltrative skin conditions in exposed offspring, as well as an increased risk of stillbirth and neonatal death. Therefore, gadolinium-enhanced MRI is avoided during pregnancy unless strictly necessary and the potential benefit clearly outweighs the fetal risk. Non-contrast MRI, by contrast, provides superior soft tissue characterization compared with ultrasound and is considered reasonably safe after the first trimester at field strengths of 1.5 T or lower. In this case, the patient was at 21 weeks, well beyond the period of organogenesis, and non-contrast 1.5-T MRI was deemed appropriate for further evaluation.

**MRI Findings**

MRI demonstrated two rounded, unilocular adnexal cysts with hypertrophied vessels and inhomogeneous fluid content. The cyst walls were notably thick, measuring 1.4 cm, a feature that raised concern for both inflammatory and neoplastic etiologies. T1 and T2 fat-sat sequences revealed inhomogeneous fluid content and focal hyperintense T1-T2 weighted solid components, suggesting the presence of blood products, proteinaceous material, or solid tissue within the cysts—findings compatible with decidualized endometriomas, cystic degenerating leiomyomas, or, less likely, malignant cystic neoplasms. The caudal mass showed unclear cleavage from the posterior uterine wall, appearing in continuity with the previously identified intramural-subserosal leiomyoma, which supported a myomatous origin for this lesion. Importantly, MRI identified no adenopathy, a finding that argued against a malignant process. Despite these additional details, MRI could not fully resolve the origin of the upper mass because no connective stalk or feeding vessel was identified, again due to the spatial interference from the gravid uterus and the large lesion size.

**Tumor Marker Panel and CA-125 Interpretation**

The serum tumor marker panel revealed normal values for CA 19-9, CA 15-3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH, which provided reassurance against several gastrointestinal, breast, and hematologic malignancies. However, CA-125 was elevated at 41.8 U/mL, exceeding the standard cutoff of 35 U/mL and showing an increasing trend from 36 U/mL measured two weeks earlier. CA-125 is the most commonly used serum marker for ovarian cancer surveillance, particularly in women over 50, but its diagnostic specificity is limited by the fact that it is produced by mesothelial cells and can be elevated in numerous benign conditions. These include endometriosis and adenomyosis (with elevations above 19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, early pregnancy, risk of miscarriage, and uterine myomas. The isolated slight elevation of CA-125 in this patient therefore remained a nonspecific finding that could support the hypothesis of decidualized endometriomas but could not confirm it.

**Physiological Variations of CA-125 During Pregnancy**

Understanding the normal dynamics of CA-125 during pregnancy is essential to avoid overinterpreting isolated elevations. Physiologically, CA-125 reaches two peaks: one during the first trimester, with a median value of approximately 23 U/mL, and another during the third trimester, with a median of approximately 21 U/mL. Between these peaks, CA-125 declines to its lowest pregnancy values during the second trimester, with a median of approximately 14 U/mL. The patient's value of 41.8 U/mL was therefore substantially higher than the expected second-trimester median, representing a meaningful deviation from the physiologic nadir rather than a trivial variation. However, because CA-125 physiologically rises in the first trimester and then normalizes, isolated measurements are not diagnostic; serial evaluations that track the trend over time provide more clinically useful information. In this case, the rising trend from 36 to 41.8 U/mL over two weeks added a modest degree of concern for ongoing pathology, but it remained insufficient to distinguish between benign and malignant etiologies.

**Synthesis of Diagnostic Information**

Taken together, the imaging and laboratory data supported a differential diagnosis that included decidualized endometriomas and cystic degenerating leiomyomas. The slight CA-125 elevation and the unilocular cystic appearance with inhomogeneous content favored endometrioma for the upper cyst, while the MRI-demonstrated continuity with uterine tissue suggested a myomatous origin for the caudal mass. The inability to identify a pedicle or feeding vessel for the upper mass on either ultrasound or MRI—a direct consequence of the steric bulk of the gravid uterus and the enormous lesion size—left its origin undetermined preoperatively. No imaging modality available in the setting of pregnancy could definitively establish whether the upper mass was ovarian or uterine in origin. This diagnostic uncertainty, combined with the patient's worsening symptoms, the risk of complications such as rupture or torsion, and the anticipated growth of the masses with advancing gestation, ultimately drove the decision to proceed with surgical exploration, which would at once provide a definitive diagnosis and therapeutic intervention.

## Differential Diagnosis and Multidisciplinary Decision-Making

Constructing an accurate differential diagnosis for abdominal masses in pregnancy is complicated by the physiological enlargement of the gravid uterus, which distorts normal anatomy and limits the sensitivity of physical examination. In this case, the patient presented at 21 weeks' gestation with two large cystic masses—one in the left upper quadrant measuring 18 cm and one in the pouch of Douglas measuring approximately 9 cm—along with a known posterior intramural-subserosal leiomyoma with cystic degeneration. The diagnostic challenge was to determine whether these masses represented ovarian pathology, uterine pathology, or a combination of both, a distinction with critical implications for surgical planning and obstetric management.

### Epidemiologic Framework

The differential diagnosis begins with an appreciation of the relative prevalence of uterine and adnexal masses during pregnancy. Uterine leiomyomas are detected in 0.3–10.1% of pregnancies, while adnexal masses are identified in approximately 1–6%. Although leiomyomas are more common, their identification is not always straightforward, particularly when they undergo cystic degeneration and acquire imaging characteristics that overlap with adnexal cysts. This epidemiologic context framed the initial diagnostic considerations: both categories of pathology were plausible, and the team had to use imaging characteristics, tumor markers, and anatomical relationships to distinguish between them.

### Imaging-Based Differential: Endometriomas versus Degenerating Leiomyomas

Ultrasound demonstrated two large, unilocular cystic masses with regular margins and peripheral vascularization. The upper mass extended from the left subphrenic region to the uterine fundus, while the lower mass appeared adherent to the posterior intramural-subserosal myoma. Non-contrast MRI was subsequently obtained to improve diagnostic accuracy, given that gadolinium-enhanced MRI is contraindicated in pregnancy except in strictly necessary circumstances. The MRI revealed thick walls (1.4 cm), inhomogeneous fluid content on T1 and T2 fat-sat sequences, and focal hyperintense solid components, but it could not definitively identify a connecting stalk or feeding vessel for the upper mass.

The imaging features initially suggested two large decidualized endometriomas. Endometriomas in pregnancy can undergo decidualization, a process in which ectopic endometrial stroma responds to high progesterone levels and transforms into decidual tissue, altering the cyst's sonographic and MRI appearance. Decidualized endometriomas may display thickened walls, heterogeneous internal echoes, and hypervascular peripheral walls, features that overlap substantially with the findings in this case. The slight elevation of CA-125 (41.8 U/mL, normal <35 U/mL) further supported this interpretation, as CA-125 is frequently elevated in endometriosis. However, the caudal mass demonstrated apparent continuity with the posterior uterine wall on both ultrasound and MRI, a critical finding that favored a myomatous origin with cystic degeneration over an ovarian origin.

### Diagnostic Ambiguity of the Upper Mass

The upper mass proved more difficult to characterize. Despite its large size and extension from the left subphrenic space to the uterine fundus, no connecting stalk or tumor-feeding vessel could be identified at either ultrasound or MRI. The gravid uterus occupied significant abdominal space and may have compressed or obscured potential vascular connections. Without evidence of continuity with the uterine wall, the diagnostic reasoning defaulted toward an ovarian origin, with endometrioma representing the leading hypothesis given the cystic appearance and slight CA-125 elevation. This case illustrates a key diagnostic limitation: when imaging cannot establish the organ of origin due to mass effect from the gravid uterus, the differential diagnosis must remain broad, and definitive characterization may require direct surgical inspection.

### Diagnostic Reasoning for the Caudal Mass

In contrast to the upper mass, the caudal mass demonstrated a clear anatomical relationship to the uterus. Both ultrasound and MRI showed the mass in continuity with the previously documented posterior intramural-subserosal leiomyoma. This continuity was the decisive imaging feature that oriented the diagnosis toward a degenerating uterine myoma rather than an adnexal cyst. Leiomyomas can undergo cystic degeneration, particularly during pregnancy when rapid growth outstrips the vascular supply, leading to ischemia, central necrosis, and cystic transformation. The appearance of a thick-walled cystic structure adherent to uterine tissue is therefore consistent with this pathophysiologic process. The inability to clearly define a cleavage plane between the caudal mass and the uterus on MRI further reinforced the myomatous interpretation.

### Attribution of Dyspnea

The patient presented with mild dyspnea, but her oxygen saturation was normal (97%), heart rate and blood pressure were within normal limits, and the ECG was unremarkable. With no evidence of pulmonary or cardiac dysfunction, the dyspnea was attributed to mechanical compression from the massive abdominal masses. The upper lesion, occupying the entire left subphrenic space and extending to the uterine fundus, likely restricted diaphragmatic excursion. This mechanical explanation was further supported by the observation that a semi-orthostatic position partially relieved her symptoms, reducing pain from 5 to 1 on the Visual Analogue Scale. Recognizing dyspnea as a mass effect rather than a primary cardiopulmonary problem was essential, because it identified the masses themselves as the therapeutic target.

### Multidisciplinary Team and Decision-Making

The patient's evaluation and management involved a multidisciplinary team including surgeons, gynecologists, obstetricians, and radiologists. This collaboration was critical given the complexity of the case, the absence of definitive management guidelines for atypical pelvic masses in pregnancy, and the need to balance maternal and fetal considerations. The team's decision-making incorporated the imaging findings, tumor marker trends, clinical symptomatology, and anticipated trajectory of pregnancy. Serial CA-125 measurements showed an increasing trend (from 36 to 41.8 U/mL over two weeks), which, although not diagnostic, contributed to the collective risk assessment. The team recognized that isolated tumor marker values during pregnancy are unreliable, as CA-125 physiologically rises during the first trimester and can be elevated in numerous benign conditions, but the upward trend provided additional data supporting further investigation and intervention.

### Risk Factors Favoring Surgical Intervention

Several factors collectively justified proceeding to laparotomy despite the absence of definitive guidelines. First, the patient's clinical condition was worsening, with persistent abdominal pain and dyspnea from restrictive lung compression. Second, the masses carried real risks of acute complications—rupture, torsion, and infection—any of which could precipitate an obstetric emergency. The patient did in fact develop a low-grade fever during her hospital stay, later found at surgery to correlate with a twisted, leaking pedunculated leiomyoma. Third, the masses were large and vascularized with mixed solid and cystic components, features associated with potential growth, hypoxia, necrosis, and further degeneration as pregnancy advanced. Fourth, the slight elevation of CA-125 introduced uncertainty regarding possible neoplastic pathology that could not be excluded noninvasively. Fifth, the sheer size of the masses posed a spatial problem: further uterine enlargement with fetal growth would increase compression and the risk of complications, and the caudal mass would likely obstruct labor. Finally, the inability to definitively characterize the upper mass noninvasively meant that surgical exploration offered both diagnostic and therapeutic value.

### Individualized Risk Assessment in the Absence of Guidelines

There are no definitive management guidelines for atypical or complicated pelvic masses in pregnancy, making individualized, multidisciplinary risk assessment essential. The general principle is to favor conservative, expectant management when possible, reserving surgery for cases with oncologic suspicion or clinical complications, always with the goal of preserving pregnancy and avoiding overtreatment. In this case, the convergence of worsening symptoms, mass-related complication risk, diagnostic uncertainty, and space limitations shifted the balance toward intervention. The team opted for a longitudinal laparotomic approach, which provided the exposure necessary for a mass of this size and allowed direct inspection of both lesions. The decision to remove only the upper mass while leaving the caudal mass in situ exemplified the principle of minimizing obstetric risk: the upper pedunculated myoma was twisted and leaking, posing an immediate threat, while the caudal intramural-subserosal myoma was fixed within the uterine wall, and its removal would have carried unacceptable risk of hemorrhage, preterm labor, or uterine rupture. This targeted surgical strategy reflected the core lesson of the case: in pregnancy, surgical management of abdominal masses should be guided by the specific anatomy, clinical risk, and feasibility of safe resection rather than by a uniform protocol.

## Surgical Management and Intraoperative Findings

### Timing and Indications for Surgery

The decision to proceed with surgery during pregnancy is never taken lightly, and this case illustrates the complex risk-benefit analysis required when conservative management fails. The patient underwent laparotomy at 21 weeks and 6 days of gestation, a timing driven by several converging clinical concerns. Her abdominal pain and dyspnoea were worsening despite analgesia and positional manoeuvres, indicating that the masses were becoming functionally intolerable. The dyspnoea was attributed to mechanical restriction of diaphragmatic excursion by the large cranial mass, which occupied the entire left subphrenic space — a problem that would only worsen as the gravid uterus enlarged. Additionally, the mixed solid and cystic nature of the masses, combined with their high vascularity, raised concern for potential complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection. The patient's low-grade fever during hospitalisation, later attributed to a leaking and twisted pedicle, underscored this risk. Finally, the inability to reach a definitive diagnosis preoperatively — imaging and tumour markers remained equivocal — meant that the surgical team could not exclude malignancy with confidence. Together, these factors justified the risks of surgical intervention at this gestational age, recognising that delaying surgery could expose both mother and fetus to greater danger from acute complications or progressive mass enlargement.

### Selection of Surgical Approach

The multidisciplinary team chose a longitudinal xiphopubic laparotomy rather than a laparoscopic approach. This decision reflects important principles in surgical planning for large masses in pregnancy. Laparoscopy during pregnancy is feasible and is generally preferred in selected cases, particularly in the first or early second trimester, when the mass is small, solitary, and not intramural, and when an open Hasson technique can be used for safe abdominal entry. However, several features of this case favoured laparotomy: the masses were exceptionally large (18 cm cranially and 9.5 cm caudally), there were two lesions, the caudal mass appeared intramural-subserosal in location, and the pregnancy was at nearly 22 weeks, when uterine size begins to significantly limit laparoscopic working space and visibility. The laparotomic approach provided the broadest exposure for safe exploration, allowed simultaneous assessment of both masses and their relationships to the uterus and adnexa, and facilitated rapid intervention if a complication such as torsion or hemorrhage was encountered. This case demonstrates a common selection bias in surgical decision-making: when masses are large, multiple, intramural, or critically located, laparotomy is often the safer and more pragmatic choice.

### Intraoperative Steps

The surgical procedure followed a logical sequence designed to maximise diagnostic yield while protecting the pregnancy. Before manipulating any mass, the team collected two samples of free peritoneal fluid for bacterial culture and cytological examination. This step is essential when the nature of an abdominal mass is uncertain, as peritoneal fluid analysis can reveal malignant cells or infectious organisms that would alter management. The abdominal cavity was then systematically explored, and the surgeons first confirmed fetal cardiac activity, establishing a baseline for fetal wellbeing before proceeding with any operative manipulation. Throughout the procedure, serial transvaginal ultrasounds were used to monitor fetal viability intermittently, consistent with recommendations from multicentric studies emphasising that fetal heart rate must be checked and documented before, during, and after surgery in pregnant patients.

### The Surprise Intraoperative Finding

The most striking aspect of this case was the discrepancy between the preoperative and intraoperative diagnoses. Preoperatively, the multidisciplinary team had favoured a diagnosis of two decidualised endometriomas, with the caudal mass possibly representing a cystic degenerating leiomyoma. At laparotomy, however, the cranial mass — which had been interpreted on imaging as a left adnexal cyst extending from the subphrenic region to the uterine fundus — was found to be a huge pedunculated cystic leiomyoma of the uterus, not an ovarian lesion. This distinction is clinically significant. Endometriomas are ovarian in origin and would require adnexal surgery, potentially compromising ovarian function and vascular supply. A pedunculated leiomyoma, by contrast, arises from the uterine serosal surface via a connective stalk and can be removed with minimal uterine trauma. The failure to identify a connecting stalk or feeding vessel on preoperative imaging was due to the massive size of the gravid uterus and the masses themselves, which obscured the anatomical relationships — a limitation the surgeons anticipated given the steric bulk of the pregnant abdomen.

Furthermore, the cranial mass was found to be initially twisted and leaking. Torsion of a pedunculated leiomyoma is an acute surgical emergency: the twisting of the pedicle compromises the arterial supply and venous drainage, leading to ischaemia, necrosis, and potentially peritonitis from leakage of necrotic material. This finding retrospectively explained the patient's low-grade fever during the hospital stay, which had initially been attributed to the Klebsiella urinary tract infection. The leaking of the twisted mass likely produced a low-grade inflammatory response. This intraoperative finding validated the decision to operate, as an undiagnosed torsion left untreated could have progressed to severe complications, including peritonitis and sepsis, with catastrophic consequences for both the mother and the fetus.

### Surgical Technique for the Upper Myomectomy

Once the cranial mass was identified as a pedunculated leiomyoma, the surgical team proceeded with myomectomy. The technique employed was deliberately conservative. The mass was gently excised from its uterine implant using a double clamp placed on the pedicle. The double-clamp technique provides vascular control at the base of the stalk, preventing significant hemorrhage when the pedicle is transected. After the stalk was cut, the exposed root on the uterine serosa was carefully closed with a single absorbable suture. This minimalist approach is appropriate for pedunculated myomas, where the uterine myometrium is not deeply breached, and extensive suturing is unnecessary. The surgery lasted 2 hours and 25 minutes — a duration that reflects the complexity of the case while remaining within a range that minimises anaesthetic exposure to the fetus.

### Decision to Leave the Caudal Mass In Situ

The caudal mass, which originated from the uterine torus and appeared as a fixed intramural-subserosal leiomyoma with a smooth surface and soft cystic texture, was intentionally left in situ. This decision reflects a fundamental principle of surgery during pregnancy: limit the intervention to what is necessary to address the immediate clinical problem. Removing an intramural myoma from the posterior uterine wall at 22 weeks would have required a deep myometrial incision on the gravid uterus, carrying substantial risks of bleeding, uterine rupture, pregnancy loss, and preterm labour. Since the caudal mass was not acutely complicated — it was neither twisted nor leaking — there was no immediate indication for its removal. The team recognised that the intramural-subserosal location of this mass would eventually obstruct labour, necessitating a caesarean delivery, but this could be planned electively. By leaving the mass, the surgeons prioritised pregnancy preservation and avoided overtreatment, a principle the case report explicitly endorses.

### Perioperative Tocolytic Management

Tocolytic prophylaxis was a key component of the perioperative strategy. The patient received progesterone 100 mg intramuscularly starting intraoperatively, continued daily until discharge, followed by intramuscular hydroxyprogesterone caproate 341 mg every 3 days until delivery. Progesterone and its synthetic analogues play a role in maintaining uterine quiescence during pregnancy by reducing myometrial contractility. Although the evidence base for tocolytic prophylaxis during non-obstetric surgery in pregnancy is limited, the use of progesterone in this context reflects a precautionary approach to counteract the uterine irritability that surgical manipulation and anaesthesia can provoke. The transition from daily progesterone to longer-acting hydroxyprogesterone caproate after discharge provided sustained tocolytic coverage through the remainder of the pregnancy, minimising the risk of preterm labour as a consequence of the surgical intervention.

### Antibiotic Management

Antibiotic therapy in this case served both prophylactic and therapeutic purposes. Intraoperative prophylaxis with cefazolin 2 g intravenously followed standard practice for preventing surgical site infections. Postoperatively, the regimen was extended with cefazolin 1 g three times daily plus clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily during the hospital stay. This combination was guided by the urine culture antibiogram, which identified Klebsiella pneumoniae sensitive to cephalosporins and clarithromycin. The decision to incorporate the urinary tract pathogen into the antibiotic plan reflects the importance of integrating all available microbiological data into perioperative management, particularly in pregnant patients where untreated infection can precipitate preterm labour or chorioamnionitis.

### Pathological Examination Results

The pathological examination of the excised cranial mass confirmed the diagnosis of benign uterine leiomyoma with areas of ischaemic necrosis near the twisted pedicle. This histological finding is the expected consequence of torsion: interruption of the vascular supply leads to coagulative necrosis of the smooth muscle tissue at the pedicle. Importantly, the peritoneal fluid collected intraoperatively was negative on both cultural and cytological analysis, excluding peritonitis, malignant cells, and infectious contamination. These results provided definitive reassurance that the mass was benign and that no further oncological staging or treatment was required. The pathological confirmation closed the diagnostic loop that had remained open throughout the preoperative evaluation, demonstrating that when imaging and biomarkers are inconclusive, surgical exploration with histopathological examination remains the definitive diagnostic standard.

## Postoperative Course, Pregnancy Follow-up, and Delivery Planning

The immediate postoperative period for this patient was complicated by a significant drop in hemoglobin to 7.5 g/L (hematocrit 24.3%), reflecting the combined effects of intraoperative blood loss from a highly vascularized pedunculated myoma and her preexisting iron deficiency anemia. This degree of anemia in a pregnant patient warrants prompt correction, as inadequate oxygen-carrying capacity can compromise both maternal recovery and fetal oxygenation. The team administered intravenous ferric carboxymaltose 1 g to replete iron stores and two units of packed red blood cells, achieving a post-transfusion hemoglobin of 10.0 g/L and hematocrit of 30.4%. In pregnant patients with moderate to severe anemia after surgery, the combination of parenteral iron and transfusion is appropriate when hemoglobin falls below 7–8 g/L, particularly when ongoing obstetric needs demand adequate reserve. The patient's hospital course was otherwise uncomplicated, and she was discharged 10 days after surgery on progesterone prophylaxis, having received intramuscular hydroxyprogesterone caproate 341 mg every 3 days to continue until delivery.

Obstetric follow-up was structured around close surveillance of fetal growth and well-being. The patient underwent ultrasound monitoring every 3 weeks, tracking fetal growth along the 86th percentile according to WHO fetal growth charts, as well as amniotic fluid index assessments. This protocol reflects the standard principle that pregnancies complicated by maternal surgery, large uterine masses, or myomectomy scars require serial surveillance for growth restriction, oligohydramnios, and signs of preterm labor. The progesterone supplementation was maintained to reduce uterine irritability and preterm delivery risk, consistent with the rationale that progesterone lowers smooth muscle contractility and may support cervical integrity after an interventional procedure during pregnancy.

The mode of delivery required careful multidisciplinary planning. The caudal myoma, left in situ during the laparotomy, originated from the uterine torus and measured approximately 9.5 cm. Its posterior location in the lower uterine segment raised concern that it would obstruct fetal head descent through the birth canal during the expulsive phase of labor. When a pelvic mass occupies the lower uterine segment or pouch of Douglas and is fixed and immobile, it can function as a mechanical barrier to vaginal delivery, increasing the risk of prolonged labor, malpresentation, and cesarean delivery. Additionally, the prior myomectomy site on the uterine fundus introduced a theoretical risk of scar dehiscence during labor contractions, though the pedunculated nature of the excised myoma and the minimal myometrial disruption from a stalk ligation likely lowered this risk compared with a deep intramural myomectomy. These factors collectively justified the decision to proceed with an elective cesarean delivery at 37 weeks of gestation. Performing the cesarean at 37 weeks balanced the benefits of reaching term gestational age against the risks of continuing pregnancy with a large residual pelvic mass and a prior uterine surgical site. The surgical team utilized the same longitudinal xiphos-pubic scar to minimize additional uterine and abdominal wall trauma and reduce the risk of scar rupture.

The maternal and neonatal outcomes were excellent. The neonate was delivered at term without complications, and the mother breastfed successfully. The pathological examination of the excised cranial mass confirmed a benign uterine leiomyoma with areas of ischaemic necrosis near the twisted pedicle, consistent with the intraoperative finding of torsion. Peritoneal fluid cytology and cultures were negative, excluding malignant or infectious etiologies. These results validated the decision to limit surgery to the cranial mass, avoiding unnecessary intervention on the caudal lesion.

Postpartum, the residual pelvic myoma partially regressed to 7.5 cm, and the patient remained asymptomatic. This outcome illustrates the well-documented natural history of uterine leiomyomas in the puerperium. Leiomyomas exhibit a non-linear growth pattern during pregnancy, with the greatest increase occurring in the first trimester and a slower rate in the second and third trimesters. After delivery, involution of the uterus and the postpartum decline in estrogen and progesterone lead to significant myoma regression, with studies reporting a mean size reduction of approximately 44% by 6 weeks postpartum. This physiological regression supports a conservative approach to residual asymptomatic myomas after pregnancy, particularly when the patient has completed childbearing or when symptoms have resolved. In this case, the patient elected to defer further surgical management, citing partial postpartum regression, absence of symptoms, and fulfillment of motherhood. This decision aligns with the broader principle that asymptomatic leiomyomas in the puerperium can be managed expectantly, reserving surgical intervention for persistent symptomatic masses, recurrent complications, or concern for malignancy.

## Pathophysiology of Leiomyomas in Pregnancy and Clinical Lessons

Uterine leiomyomas are common in pregnancy, with an incidence ranging from 0.3% to 10.1%, yet their behavior during gestation is unpredictable and often misunderstood. Understanding the biological forces that drive myoma growth, degeneration, and complication during pregnancy is essential for interpreting atypical presentations such as the one described in this case, where two large masses masqueraded as adnexal cysts and ultimately proved to be uterine leiomyomas with cystic degeneration and torsion.

### Leiomyoma Growth Patterns During Pregnancy

Leiomyomas do not grow linearly during pregnancy. The literature describes a non-linear trend in which myoma size increases by approximately 6.90% per week overall, but the rate is not uniform across trimesters. The most rapid growth occurs between the first and second trimesters, with a mean increase of 6.12% per week. Between the second and third trimesters, the growth rate slows substantially to approximately 3.10% per week. This deceleration is clinically relevant because it means that masses discovered in the second trimester, as in this case, may still enlarge but at a reduced pace compared with earlier gestational ages. Importantly, a significant proportion of leiomyomas regress in the third trimester and postpartum, with mean size reductions of approximately 44% by six weeks after delivery. This regression is driven by the dramatic fall in estrogen and progesterone levels following delivery and involution of the uterine vasculature. For the patient in this case, the residual pelvic myoma decreased from approximately 9 cm to 7.5 cm postpartum, consistent with this expected pattern and supporting the team's decision to leave the caudal mass in situ.

### Pathophysiology of Cystic Degeneration

The rapid growth of leiomyomas during early pregnancy is supported by high levels of estrogen and progesterone, which stimulate both cellular proliferation and extracellular matrix deposition. However, this growth can outstrip the tumor's blood supply, particularly in large or rapidly enlarging myomas. The resulting ischemia leads to a cascade of tissue injury: initial hypoxia is followed by ischemic necrosis, and the necrotic tissue eventually undergoes cystic change as liquefaction produces fluid-filled cavities within the lesion. This cystic degeneration transforms a solid tumor into a complex cystic structure that can closely resemble an ovarian cyst or endometrioma on imaging. In this case, the caudal mass appeared at both ultrasound and MRI as a cystic lesion adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma with cystic degeneration, while the cranial mass—eventually found to be a pedunculated leiomyoma—also exhibited cystic features. The pathological examination confirmed areas of ischemic necrosis near the twisted pedicle of the excised cranial mass, corroborating the clinical hypothesis that vascular compromise drove the degenerative changes.

### Torsion of Pedunculated Leiomyomas

Pedunculated leiomyomas carry a unique risk during pregnancy: torsion of the vascular stalk. As the gravid uterus grows and changes position, the stalk of a pedunculated myoma can twist, compromising both arterial inflow and venous outflow. The resulting ischemia produces acute pain and, if persistent, infarction and necrosis. Necrotic tissue may leak inflammatory fluid into the peritoneal cavity, producing fever and leukocytosis. In this case, the cranial mass was found intraoperatively to be a huge pedunculated leiomyoma that was initially twisted and leaking, which almost certainly explained the low-grade fever the patient developed during her hospital stay. The Klebsiella urinary tract infection was a separate finding, but the fever pattern and the intraoperative observation of torsion and leakage illustrate how pedunculated myomas can generate both acute symptoms and systemic inflammatory signs that mimic other complications of pregnancy.

### Why Leiomyomas Mimic Adnexal Masses

The diagnostic confusion in this case is a well-recognized phenomenon. Several factors conspire to make large leiomyomas during pregnancy resemble ovarian or adnexal masses. First, the sheer size of the masses—18 cm and 9.5 cm in this case—makes it difficult to identify their organ of origin, particularly when the gravid uterus occupies much of the abdominal cavity and displaces adnexal structures. Second, cystic degeneration alters the imaging characteristics of a myoma, replacing the expected homogeneous solid appearance with fluid-filled cavities, thick walls, and heterogeneous internal echoes that overlap with the features of endometriomas or other ovarian cysts. Third, the "steric bulk" of the pregnant uterus can obscure the connective stalk or feeding vessels that would otherwise identify a mass as pedunculated and uterine in origin. In this case, no connecting stalk or tumor-feeding vessel was identified for the upper mass at ultrasound or MRI, which steered the differential diagnosis toward ovarian masses, particularly endometriomas. Only at laparotomy did the true uterine origin of both masses become apparent.

### Decidualized Endometriomas Versus Cystic Leiomyomas

The distinction between decidualized endometriomas and cystic leiomyomas is one of the most challenging differential diagnoses in pregnant patients with large cystic masses. Endometriomas undergo decidualization during pregnancy under the influence of progesterone, producing thick, vascular walls and internal solid components that can mimic a degenerating myoma. Both entities can show unilocular cystic structure, peripheral vascularization on color Doppler, and heterogeneous fluid content on MRI. In this case, the slight elevation of CA-125 (41.8 U/mL, above the normal cutoff of 35 U/mL and higher than the second-trimester median of approximately 14 U/mL) initially favored endometrioma for the upper cyst, since CA-125 can be elevated in endometriosis. However, the continuity of the caudal mass with uterine tissue on MRI favored a myomatous origin. MRI without contrast provided superior anatomical detail, showing thick walls, inhomogeneous fluid content, and the relationship between the caudal mass and the posterior uterine wall, but even MRI could not definitively establish the origin of the cranial mass preoperatively. This case illustrates that imaging can narrow but not always resolve the differential diagnosis, and that surgical exploration may be necessary when clinical complications demand a conclusive answer.

### Evidence on Myomectomy During Pregnancy

The default management of uterine myomas diagnosed during pregnancy is conservative, because myomectomy carries risks of severe bleeding, pregnancy loss, preterm labor, and uterine rupture. However, surgical intervention becomes warranted when complications such as torsion of a pedunculated myoma, severe persistent pain unresponsive to conservative measures, or suspicion of malignancy are present. In this case, the multidisciplinary team proceeded with laparotomy at 21 weeks plus 6 days because of worsening pain, dyspnea from mass effect, the risk of rupture or torsion, the slight CA-125 elevation, and concern that further uterine enlargement would exacerbate mass-related complications. The decision to remove only the cranial mass was guided by the intraoperative finding of a twisted pedicle, which carried an immediate risk of infarction and peritoneal leakage. The caudal mass was left in situ to minimize obstetric risk. This approach reflects a fundamental principle: surgery during pregnancy should be as limited as possible, targeting only the mass responsible for complications while preserving the pregnancy.

The choice between laparotomy and laparoscopy is influenced by selection bias. Laparoscopy, ideally with an open Hasson technique, may be feasible in the first or early second trimester when the mass is smaller, not intramural, and located favorably. However, larger, numerous, intramural, or critically located masses—such as a huge myoma adherent to the posterior isthmic uterine wall—more often warrant laparotomy, as in this case. This bias means that the populations managed by the two approaches are not comparable, and outcomes cannot be directly extrapolated between them.

### Evidence on Cesarean Myomectomy

When a leiomyoma persists to the time of delivery, the question arises whether to remove it at cesarean section. A multicentric study by Sakinci and colleagues showed no statistically significant differences in preoperative and postoperative hemoglobin values or blood transfusion rates between women undergoing cesarean section with myomectomy and those undergoing cesarean alone, suggesting that cesarean myomectomy can be hemodynamically safe in selected cases. However, both hospitalization duration and surgery duration were significantly longer in the myomectomy group. The study also demonstrated a statistically significant inverse correlation between myoma size and delivery week, meaning that larger myomas are associated with earlier delivery, likely due to complications or planned early delivery. In this case, the caudal myoma was left in situ during the antepartum laparotomy, and an elective cesarean was performed at 37 weeks using the same surgical scar. The residual pelvic myoma would have obstructed the fetal head during vaginal delivery, justifying the planned cesarean approach.

### Principles of Comprehensive Evaluation

This case underscores the importance of a structured, multimodal evaluation for atypical abdominal masses in pregnancy. Bimanual vaginal examination remains a cornerstone, as it was in this case, where it revealed a fixed, hard, rounded mass in the lesser pelvis that bulged at the posterior fornix. Ultrasound with color Doppler provides the first-line imaging assessment, identifying cystic versus solid components, vascularity, and the relationship of masses to the gravid uterus. Non-contrast MRI offers superior soft-tissue resolution and is considered reasonably safe after the first trimester at field strengths of 1.5 T or lower; gadolinium should be avoided unless strictly necessary. Serial CA-125 measurements, rather than isolated values, can provide useful trend information, since CA-125 rises physiologically in the first trimester and varies with many benign conditions including endometriosis, adenomyosis, pregnancy, and even myomas. The multidisciplinary team must integrate all of these data—clinical, imaging, and laboratory—to formulate a management plan that balances diagnostic certainty with the imperative to preserve the pregnancy.

### Key Clinical Lessons

Several lessons emerge from this case and the supporting literature. First, surgical management of abdominal masses during pregnancy should be considered only in the presence of oncological suspicion or clinical complications—worsening pain, dyspnea, risk of torsion or rupture, or infection—not simply because a mass is large. Second, the priority is always to preserve the pregnancy, and surgery should be as limited as possible, targeting only the mass responsible for the complication. Third, avoidance of overtreatment is essential: the caudal myoma in this case was left in situ and subsequently regressed postpartum, validating a conservative approach for the uncomplicated mass. Fourth, the mode of delivery should depend on the size, number, and anatomical location of any residual uterine masses; in this case, the pelvic myoma would have obstructed labor, justifying elective cesarean. Finally, this case illustrates that even with modern imaging, the preoperative diagnosis of atypical masses in pregnancy may remain uncertain, and the surgeon must be prepared to adapt the intraoperative plan based on actual findings—such as the unexpected torsion of the cranial pedunculated myoma—while maintaining fetal monitoring and uterine tocolytic prophylaxis throughout the procedure.
