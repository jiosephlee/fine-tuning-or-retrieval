Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

Chapter 4: ## Surgical Management and Intraoperative Findings

### Timing and Indications for Surgery

The decision to proceed with surgery during pregnancy is never taken lightly, and this case illustrates the complex risk-benefit analysis required when conservative management fails. The patient underwent laparotomy at 21 weeks and 6 days of gestation, a timing driven by several converging clinical concerns. Her abdominal pain and dyspnoea were worsening despite analgesia and positional manoeuvres, indicating that the masses were becoming functionally intolerable. The dyspnoea was attributed to mechanical restriction of diaphragmatic excursion by the large cranial mass, which occupied the entire left subphrenic space — a problem that would only worsen as the gravid uterus enlarged. Additionally, the mixed solid and cystic nature of the masses, combined with their high vascularity, raised concern for potential complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection. The patient's low-grade fever during hospitalisation, later attributed to a leaking and twisted pedicle, underscored this risk. Finally, the inability to reach a definitive diagnosis preoperatively — imaging and tumour markers remained equivocal — meant that the surgical team could not exclude malignancy with confidence. Together, these factors justified the risks of surgical intervention at this gestational age, recognising that delaying surgery could expose both mother and fetus to greater danger from acute complications or progressive mass enlargement.

### Selection of Surgical Approach

The multidisciplinary team chose a longitudinal xiphopubic laparotomy rather than a laparoscopic approach. This decision reflects important principles in surgical planning for large masses in pregnancy. Laparoscopy during pregnancy is feasible and is generally preferred in selected cases, particularly in the first or early second trimester, when the mass is small, solitary, and not intramural, and when an open Hasson technique can be used for safe abdominal entry. However, several features of this case favoured laparotomy: the masses were exceptionally large (18 cm cranially and 9.5 cm caudally), there were two lesions, the caudal mass appeared intramural-subserosal in location, and the pregnancy was at nearly 22 weeks, when uterine size begins to significantly limit laparoscopic working space and visibility. The laparotomic approach provided the broadest exposure for safe exploration, allowed simultaneous assessment of both masses and their relationships to the uterus and adnexa, and facilitated rapid intervention if a complication such as torsion or hemorrhage was encountered. This case demonstrates a common selection bias in surgical decision-making: when masses are large, multiple, intramural, or critically located, laparotomy is often the safer and more pragmatic choice.

### Intraoperative Steps

The surgical procedure followed a logical sequence designed to maximise diagnostic yield while protecting the pregnancy. Before manipulating any mass, the team collected two samples of free peritoneal fluid for bacterial culture and cytological examination. This step is essential when the nature of an abdominal mass is uncertain, as peritoneal fluid analysis can reveal malignant cells or infectious organisms that would alter management. The abdominal cavity was then systematically explored, and the surgeons first confirmed fetal cardiac activity, establishing a baseline for fetal wellbeing before proceeding with any operative manipulation. Throughout the procedure, serial transvaginal ultrasounds were used to monitor fetal viability intermittently, consistent with recommendations from multicentric studies emphasising that fetal heart rate must be checked and documented before, during, and after surgery in pregnant patients.

### The Surprise Intraoperative Finding

The most striking aspect of this case was the discrepancy between the preoperative and intraoperative diagnoses. Preoperatively, the multidisciplinary team had favoured a diagnosis of two decidualised endometriomas, with the caudal mass possibly representing a cystic degenerating leiomyoma. At laparotomy, however, the cranial mass — which had been interpreted on imaging as a left adnexal cyst extending from the subphrenic region to the uterine fundus — was found to be a huge pedunculated cystic leiomyoma of the uterus, not an ovarian lesion. This distinction is clinically significant. Endometriomas are ovarian in origin and would require adnexal surgery, potentially compromising ovarian function and vascular supply. A pedunculated leiomyoma, by contrast, arises from the uterine serosal surface via a connective stalk and can be removed with minimal uterine trauma. The failure to identify a connecting stalk or feeding vessel on preoperative imaging was due to the massive size of the gravid uterus and the masses themselves, which obscured the anatomical relationships — a limitation the surgeons anticipated given the steric bulk of the pregnant abdomen.

Furthermore, the cranial mass was found to be initially twisted and leaking. Torsion of a pedunculated leiomyoma is an acute surgical emergency: the twisting of the pedicle compromises the arterial supply and venous drainage, leading to ischaemia, necrosis, and potentially peritonitis from leakage of necrotic material. This finding retrospectively explained the patient's low-grade fever during the hospital stay, which had initially been attributed to the Klebsiella urinary tract infection. The leaking of the twisted mass likely produced a low-grade inflammatory response. This intraoperative finding validated the decision to operate, as an undiagnosed torsion left untreated could have progressed to severe complications, including peritonitis and sepsis, with catastrophic consequences for both the mother and the fetus.

### Surgical Technique for the Upper Myomectomy

Once the cranial mass was identified as a pedunculated leiomyoma, the surgical team proceeded with myomectomy. The technique employed was deliberately conservative. The mass was gently excised from its uterine implant using a double clamp placed on the pedicle. The double-clamp technique provides vascular control at the base of the stalk, preventing significant hemorrhage when the pedicle is transected. After the stalk was cut, the exposed root on the uterine serosa was carefully closed with a single absorbable suture. This minimalist approach is appropriate for pedunculated myomas, where the uterine myometrium is not deeply breached, and extensive suturing is unnecessary. The surgery lasted 2 hours and 25 minutes — a duration that reflects the complexity of the case while remaining within a range that minimises anaesthetic exposure to the fetus.

### Decision to Leave the Caudal Mass In Situ

The caudal mass, which originated from the uterine torus and appeared as a fixed intramural-subserosal leiomyoma with a smooth surface and soft cystic texture, was intentionally left in situ. This decision reflects a fundamental principle of surgery during pregnancy: limit the intervention to what is necessary to address the immediate clinical problem. Removing an intramural myoma from the posterior uterine wall at 22 weeks would have required a deep myometrial incision on the gravid uterus, carrying substantial risks of bleeding, uterine rupture, pregnancy loss, and preterm labour. Since the caudal mass was not acutely complicated — it was neither twisted nor leaking — there was no immediate indication for its removal. The team recognised that the intramural-subserosal location of this mass would eventually obstruct labour, necessitating a caesarean delivery, but this could be planned electively. By leaving the mass, the surgeons prioritised pregnancy preservation and avoided overtreatment, a principle the case report explicitly endorses.

### Perioperative Tocolytic Management

Tocolytic prophylaxis was a key component of the perioperative strategy. The patient received progesterone 100 mg intramuscularly starting intraoperatively, continued daily until discharge, followed by intramuscular hydroxyprogesterone caproate 341 mg every 3 days until delivery. Progesterone and its synthetic analogues play a role in maintaining uterine quiescence during pregnancy by reducing myometrial contractility. Although the evidence base for tocolytic prophylaxis during non-obstetric surgery in pregnancy is limited, the use of progesterone in this context reflects a precautionary approach to counteract the uterine irritability that surgical manipulation and anaesthesia can provoke. The transition from daily progesterone to longer-acting hydroxyprogesterone caproate after discharge provided sustained tocolytic coverage through the remainder of the pregnancy, minimising the risk of preterm labour as a consequence of the surgical intervention.

### Antibiotic Management

Antibiotic therapy in this case served both prophylactic and therapeutic purposes. Intraoperative prophylaxis with cefazolin 2 g intravenously followed standard practice for preventing surgical site infections. Postoperatively, the regimen was extended with cefazolin 1 g three times daily plus clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily during the hospital stay. This combination was guided by the urine culture antibiogram, which identified Klebsiella pneumoniae sensitive to cephalosporins and clarithromycin. The decision to incorporate the urinary tract pathogen into the antibiotic plan reflects the importance of integrating all available microbiological data into perioperative management, particularly in pregnant patients where untreated infection can precipitate preterm labour or chorioamnionitis.

### Pathological Examination Results

The pathological examination of the excised cranial mass confirmed the diagnosis of benign uterine leiomyoma with areas of ischaemic necrosis near the twisted pedicle. This histological finding is the expected consequence of torsion: interruption of the vascular supply leads to coagulative necrosis of the smooth muscle tissue at the pedicle. Importantly, the peritoneal fluid collected intraoperatively was negative on both cultural and cytological analysis, excluding peritonitis, malignant cells, and infectious contamination. These results provided definitive reassurance that the mass was benign and that no further oncological staging or treatment was required. The pathological confirmation closed the diagnostic loop that had remained open throughout the preoperative evaluation, demonstrating that when imaging and biomarkers are inconclusive, surgical exploration with histopathological examination remains the definitive diagnostic standard.
