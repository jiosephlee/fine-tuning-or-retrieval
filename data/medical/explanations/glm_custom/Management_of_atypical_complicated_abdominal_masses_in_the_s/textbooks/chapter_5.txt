Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

Chapter 5: ## Postoperative Course, Pregnancy Follow-up, and Delivery Planning

The immediate postoperative period for this patient was complicated by a significant drop in hemoglobin to 7.5 g/L (hematocrit 24.3%), reflecting the combined effects of intraoperative blood loss from a highly vascularized pedunculated myoma and her preexisting iron deficiency anemia. This degree of anemia in a pregnant patient warrants prompt correction, as inadequate oxygen-carrying capacity can compromise both maternal recovery and fetal oxygenation. The team administered intravenous ferric carboxymaltose 1 g to replete iron stores and two units of packed red blood cells, achieving a post-transfusion hemoglobin of 10.0 g/L and hematocrit of 30.4%. In pregnant patients with moderate to severe anemia after surgery, the combination of parenteral iron and transfusion is appropriate when hemoglobin falls below 7–8 g/L, particularly when ongoing obstetric needs demand adequate reserve. The patient's hospital course was otherwise uncomplicated, and she was discharged 10 days after surgery on progesterone prophylaxis, having received intramuscular hydroxyprogesterone caproate 341 mg every 3 days to continue until delivery.

Obstetric follow-up was structured around close surveillance of fetal growth and well-being. The patient underwent ultrasound monitoring every 3 weeks, tracking fetal growth along the 86th percentile according to WHO fetal growth charts, as well as amniotic fluid index assessments. This protocol reflects the standard principle that pregnancies complicated by maternal surgery, large uterine masses, or myomectomy scars require serial surveillance for growth restriction, oligohydramnios, and signs of preterm labor. The progesterone supplementation was maintained to reduce uterine irritability and preterm delivery risk, consistent with the rationale that progesterone lowers smooth muscle contractility and may support cervical integrity after an interventional procedure during pregnancy.

The mode of delivery required careful multidisciplinary planning. The caudal myoma, left in situ during the laparotomy, originated from the uterine torus and measured approximately 9.5 cm. Its posterior location in the lower uterine segment raised concern that it would obstruct fetal head descent through the birth canal during the expulsive phase of labor. When a pelvic mass occupies the lower uterine segment or pouch of Douglas and is fixed and immobile, it can function as a mechanical barrier to vaginal delivery, increasing the risk of prolonged labor, malpresentation, and cesarean delivery. Additionally, the prior myomectomy site on the uterine fundus introduced a theoretical risk of scar dehiscence during labor contractions, though the pedunculated nature of the excised myoma and the minimal myometrial disruption from a stalk ligation likely lowered this risk compared with a deep intramural myomectomy. These factors collectively justified the decision to proceed with an elective cesarean delivery at 37 weeks of gestation. Performing the cesarean at 37 weeks balanced the benefits of reaching term gestational age against the risks of continuing pregnancy with a large residual pelvic mass and a prior uterine surgical site. The surgical team utilized the same longitudinal xiphos-pubic scar to minimize additional uterine and abdominal wall trauma and reduce the risk of scar rupture.

The maternal and neonatal outcomes were excellent. The neonate was delivered at term without complications, and the mother breastfed successfully. The pathological examination of the excised cranial mass confirmed a benign uterine leiomyoma with areas of ischaemic necrosis near the twisted pedicle, consistent with the intraoperative finding of torsion. Peritoneal fluid cytology and cultures were negative, excluding malignant or infectious etiologies. These results validated the decision to limit surgery to the cranial mass, avoiding unnecessary intervention on the caudal lesion.

Postpartum, the residual pelvic myoma partially regressed to 7.5 cm, and the patient remained asymptomatic. This outcome illustrates the well-documented natural history of uterine leiomyomas in the puerperium. Leiomyomas exhibit a non-linear growth pattern during pregnancy, with the greatest increase occurring in the first trimester and a slower rate in the second and third trimesters. After delivery, involution of the uterus and the postpartum decline in estrogen and progesterone lead to significant myoma regression, with studies reporting a mean size reduction of approximately 44% by 6 weeks postpartum. This physiological regression supports a conservative approach to residual asymptomatic myomas after pregnancy, particularly when the patient has completed childbearing or when symptoms have resolved. In this case, the patient elected to defer further surgical management, citing partial postpartum regression, absence of symptoms, and fulfillment of motherhood. This decision aligns with the broader principle that asymptomatic leiomyomas in the puerperium can be managed expectantly, reserving surgical intervention for persistent symptomatic masses, recurrent complications, or concern for malignancy.
