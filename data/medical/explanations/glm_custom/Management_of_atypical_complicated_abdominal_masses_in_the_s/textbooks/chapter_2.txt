Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

Chapter 2: ## Imaging Evaluation and Tumor Marker Assessment

The diagnostic workup of abdominal masses in pregnancy requires a careful balance between obtaining sufficient information for clinical decision-making and minimizing fetal risk. In this case, the patient presented at 21 weeks' gestation with severe abdominal pain, dyspnea, and two palpable masses—a tender 18 cm swelling in the left upper quadrant and a fixed 9 cm mass in the lesser pelvis. The diagnostic strategy relied on two complementary imaging modalities—ultrasound and non-contrast MRI—alongside a serum tumor marker panel, each of which contributed to the differential diagnosis but also illustrated the inherent limitations of evaluating masses in a gravid abdomen.

**Ultrasound Findings and Their Interpretation**

Transvaginal and transabdominal ultrasound demonstrated two large, unilocular cystic masses with regular margins and peripheral vascularization. The upper mass measured 18 × 12 × 7 cm and extended from the left subphrenic region to the uterine fundus, occupying the entire left subphrenic space. The lower mass measured 9.5 × 8 × 8 cm and appeared adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma with cystic degeneration, the myoma itself measuring 58 × 71 mm. The unilocular appearance and regular margins initially suggested benign cystic lesions, and the peripheral vascularization pattern was consistent with encapsulated masses rather than infiltrative neoplasms. However, the adherence of the caudal mass to a uterine myoma introduced diagnostic ambiguity, as it remained unclear whether the cystic component represented a degenerating leiomyoma, an endometrioma adherent to the uterine wall, or a true adnexal cyst. Ultrasound remains the first-line imaging modality in pregnancy due to its safety, availability, and ability to characterize cyst architecture, but in this case, the massive size of the lesions and the enlarging gravid uterus limited the ability to identify connective stalks or feeding vessels, particularly for the upper mass. This "steric bulk" effect—the physical obstruction of visualization by the pregnant uterus and the huge masses—prevented definitive identification of the origin of the upper lesion and oriented the initial differential diagnosis toward adnexal pathology, specifically endometriomas.

**Rationale and Safety of Non-Contrast MRI in Pregnancy**

Given the diagnostic uncertainty on ultrasound, the multidisciplinary team proceeded to MRI without contrast medium. This decision reflects established safety principles in pregnancy: gadolinium-based contrast agents cross the placenta and have been associated with rheumatological, inflammatory, and infiltrative skin conditions in exposed offspring, as well as an increased risk of stillbirth and neonatal death. Therefore, gadolinium-enhanced MRI is avoided during pregnancy unless strictly necessary and the potential benefit clearly outweighs the fetal risk. Non-contrast MRI, by contrast, provides superior soft tissue characterization compared with ultrasound and is considered reasonably safe after the first trimester at field strengths of 1.5 T or lower. In this case, the patient was at 21 weeks, well beyond the period of organogenesis, and non-contrast 1.5-T MRI was deemed appropriate for further evaluation.

**MRI Findings**

MRI demonstrated two rounded, unilocular adnexal cysts with hypertrophied vessels and inhomogeneous fluid content. The cyst walls were notably thick, measuring 1.4 cm, a feature that raised concern for both inflammatory and neoplastic etiologies. T1 and T2 fat-sat sequences revealed inhomogeneous fluid content and focal hyperintense T1-T2 weighted solid components, suggesting the presence of blood products, proteinaceous material, or solid tissue within the cysts—findings compatible with decidualized endometriomas, cystic degenerating leiomyomas, or, less likely, malignant cystic neoplasms. The caudal mass showed unclear cleavage from the posterior uterine wall, appearing in continuity with the previously identified intramural-subserosal leiomyoma, which supported a myomatous origin for this lesion. Importantly, MRI identified no adenopathy, a finding that argued against a malignant process. Despite these additional details, MRI could not fully resolve the origin of the upper mass because no connective stalk or feeding vessel was identified, again due to the spatial interference from the gravid uterus and the large lesion size.

**Tumor Marker Panel and CA-125 Interpretation**

The serum tumor marker panel revealed normal values for CA 19-9, CA 15-3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH, which provided reassurance against several gastrointestinal, breast, and hematologic malignancies. However, CA-125 was elevated at 41.8 U/mL, exceeding the standard cutoff of 35 U/mL and showing an increasing trend from 36 U/mL measured two weeks earlier. CA-125 is the most commonly used serum marker for ovarian cancer surveillance, particularly in women over 50, but its diagnostic specificity is limited by the fact that it is produced by mesothelial cells and can be elevated in numerous benign conditions. These include endometriosis and adenomyosis (with elevations above 19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, early pregnancy, risk of miscarriage, and uterine myomas. The isolated slight elevation of CA-125 in this patient therefore remained a nonspecific finding that could support the hypothesis of decidualized endometriomas but could not confirm it.

**Physiological Variations of CA-125 During Pregnancy**

Understanding the normal dynamics of CA-125 during pregnancy is essential to avoid overinterpreting isolated elevations. Physiologically, CA-125 reaches two peaks: one during the first trimester, with a median value of approximately 23 U/mL, and another during the third trimester, with a median of approximately 21 U/mL. Between these peaks, CA-125 declines to its lowest pregnancy values during the second trimester, with a median of approximately 14 U/mL. The patient's value of 41.8 U/mL was therefore substantially higher than the expected second-trimester median, representing a meaningful deviation from the physiologic nadir rather than a trivial variation. However, because CA-125 physiologically rises in the first trimester and then normalizes, isolated measurements are not diagnostic; serial evaluations that track the trend over time provide more clinically useful information. In this case, the rising trend from 36 to 41.8 U/mL over two weeks added a modest degree of concern for ongoing pathology, but it remained insufficient to distinguish between benign and malignant etiologies.

**Synthesis of Diagnostic Information**

Taken together, the imaging and laboratory data supported a differential diagnosis that included decidualized endometriomas and cystic degenerating leiomyomas. The slight CA-125 elevation and the unilocular cystic appearance with inhomogeneous content favored endometrioma for the upper cyst, while the MRI-demonstrated continuity with uterine tissue suggested a myomatous origin for the caudal mass. The inability to identify a pedicle or feeding vessel for the upper mass on either ultrasound or MRI—a direct consequence of the steric bulk of the gravid uterus and the enormous lesion size—left its origin undetermined preoperatively. No imaging modality available in the setting of pregnancy could definitively establish whether the upper mass was ovarian or uterine in origin. This diagnostic uncertainty, combined with the patient's worsening symptoms, the risk of complications such as rupture or torsion, and the anticipated growth of the masses with advancing gestation, ultimately drove the decision to proceed with surgical exploration, which would at once provide a definitive diagnosis and therapeutic intervention.
