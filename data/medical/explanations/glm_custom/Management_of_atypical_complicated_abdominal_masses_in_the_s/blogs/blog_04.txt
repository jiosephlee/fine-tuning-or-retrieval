Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# Blind Spots: Navigating Imaging Limitations in the Gravid Abdomen

A nulliparous woman in her 40s, 21 weeks pregnant, walks into your emergency department with severe abdominal pain and dyspnea. Ultrasound reveals two massive cystic masses — one 18 cm, one 9.5 cm — compressing everything in sight. MRI without contrast shows thick-walled, inhomogeneous cysts with solid components. CA-125 is mildly elevated and trending upward. You need to figure out what these are, whether they're dangerous, and whether to operate on a gravid uterus — all while your imaging options are deliberately handcuffed by pregnancy. This case, published by De Nola and colleagues, is a masterclass in just how blind we can be when the gravid abdomen defies even our best non-invasive tools.

## The Setup: Two Masses, Zero Certainty

This patient had two large unilocular cystic masses with peripheral vascularisation on ultrasound. The upper lesion extended from the left subphrenic space to the uterine fundus; the lower one sat in the pouch of Douglas, apparently adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma with cystic degeneration. Both ultrasound and non-contrast MRI suggested these were decidualised endometriomas — the slight CA-125 elevation (41.8 U/mL, up from 36 two weeks prior) supported that interpretation for the cranial mass, while the caudal lesion's continuity with uterine tissue pointed toward a myomatous origin.

Here is where it gets humbling: at laparotomy, the upper "endometrioma" turned out to be a huge pedunculated cystic leiomyoma — twisted, leaking, and likely the source of the patient's low-grade fever. The imaging couldn't identify the connecting stalk or feeding vessel because the pregnant uterus created so much steric bulk that vascular pedicles were simply invisible. The lower mass was also a cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma, left in situ to protect the pregnancy.

The preoperative diagnosis was wrong for the cranial mass. And that is the central lesson: in pregnancy, even a careful multimodal workup can mislead you.

## Why Imaging Falls Short in Pregnancy — and What We Can Still Do

The imaging toolkit in pregnancy is intentionally limited. Ultrasound with colour Doppler is your frontline modality — it is safe, repeatable, and excellent for characterising anterior wall, submucosal, or pedunculated leiomyomas on the gravid uterus. But when masses are posterior, multiple, intramural, or buried behind a 21-week uterus in a patient with a BMI of 33, ultrasound hits a wall. In this case, no feeding vessel or stalk was identified for the upper mass, which would have immediately clarified its uterine origin.

Non-contrast MRI is the recommended second-line modality and offers superior diagnostic accuracy for large, multiple, or posterior myomas — particularly because it can precisely delineate the distance between a myoma and the uterine cavity. MRI in this case added real value: it characterised the thick walls (1.4 cm), the inhomogeneous fluid content, the focal solid components, and confirmed the caudal mass's continuity with the posterior uterine wall. But it could not resolve the cranial mass's origin.

Gadolinium-enhanced MRI would have been the logical next step to characterise vascular supply and solid components — but it is avoided in pregnancy due to associations with rheumatological, inflammatory, and infiltrative skin conditions in the neonate, as well as risk of stillbirth and neonatal death. The authors used a 1.5-T non-enhanced MRI, which is considered reasonably safe after the first trimester, and is the current standard when MRI is indicated during pregnancy. Avoiding gadolinium is not a trivial sacrifice — it directly reduces your ability to characterise complex cystic masses with solid components, which is exactly the scenario where you need the most clarity.

So you are left with a deliberate trade-off: you protect the fetus by accepting reduced diagnostic certainty. The question is what to do with that uncertainty.

## When the Clinical Picture Forces Your Hand

The multidisciplinary team here did not operate because of the imaging findings — they operated despite them. The decision-making hinged on several converging factors. First, the masses were huge and vascularised with mixed solid and cystic components, carrying real risk of rupture, torsion, or infection. Second, the clinical picture was worsening — pain and restrictive dyspnea from mass effect, plus a fever that, in retrospect, was likely from the twisted, leaking pedunculated myoma. Third, as the uterus continued to grow, the masses would grow too — uterine leiomyomas increase by roughly 6.9% per week during pregnancy, with the fastest growth in the first trimester — increasing the risk of ischaemia, necrosis, and degeneration. Fourth, there was a slight but rising CA-125, which, while not diagnostic in pregnancy, added to the oncological uncertainty.

The surgical team performed a longitudinal xiphos-pubic laparotomy at 21 weeks + 6 days. They collected peritoneal fluid for culture and cytology, confirmed fetal cardiac activity, and discovered that the cranial mass was a twisted pedunculated leiomyoma — not an ovarian cyst. They removed only the upper mass and left the lower one in situ to minimise obstetric risk. The surgery lasted 2 hours and 25 minutes, and the patient received intraoperative and postoperative progesterone prophylaxis to reduce uterine irritability.

The pathology confirmed benign uterine leiomyomas with ischaemic necrosis near the twisted pedicle. Peritoneal fluid was negative. The patient was discharged 10 days later after requiring two blood transfusions for postoperative anaemia. She delivered via elective repeat cesarean at 37 weeks on the same surgical scar, with an excellent neonatal outcome.

## Integrating What You Have When You Can't See Everything

The practical takeaway is that when imaging cannot give you a definitive answer in pregnancy, you integrate what you have and make a risk-benefit calculation. Here is how the pieces fit together in this case and how they should inform your approach.

Bimanual examination remains valuable and underused. The vaginal exam here revealed a fixed, hard, rounded 9 cm mass in the pelvis — a finding that contributed to the decision to leave it in situ, because it would clearly obstruct vaginal delivery regardless of its exact histology. Physical examination also flagged the 18 cm upper quadrant mass and the absence of ascites, which helped frame the differential away from advanced malignancy.

Tumour markers in pregnancy require serial interpretation, not isolated cutoffs. CA-125 physiologically peaks in the first and third trimesters (medians of 23 and 21 U/mL respectively) and is lowest in the second trimester (median 14 U/mL). This patient's value of 41.8 U/mL in the second trimester was above the expected median and showed an upward trend — not diagnostic of malignancy, but a signal worth incorporating. Other markers (CA 19-9, CA 15-3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, LDH) were normal, which provided some reassurance. The key point: never hang your hat on a single CA-125 in pregnancy. Trend it.

The decision to operate during pregnancy should be driven by complications and risk trajectory, not by the imaging diagnosis itself. Conservative management is the default for uterine myomas in pregnancy, given risks of bleeding, pregnancy loss, preterm labour, and uterine rupture. But when you have worsening pain, mass effect, a twisted or degenerating pedunculated myoma, or oncological suspicion you cannot exclude, the calculus shifts. The goal is the shortest, safest intervention possible — remove what is dangerous, leave what is stable, and preserve the pregnancy.

## Key Take-Home Points

- **Ultrasound with colour Doppler is first-line, but recognise its limits.** Posterior, intramural, or large masses in a gravid abdomen may have invisible feeding vessels and pedicles — exactly the features that distinguish a pedunculated myoma from an ovarian cyst.

- **Non-contrast 1.5-T MRI after the first trimester is your best second-line tool.** It adds spatial resolution and can characterise myoma location relative to the uterine cavity, but it cannot replace gadolinium-enhanced sequences for complex cystic masses with solid components. Accept that gap.

- **Gadolinium is avoided in pregnancy for good reason.** Do not order it unless absolutely necessary — the fetal risks are documented. Work with what you have.

- **Trend your tumour markers.** A single CA-125 is meaningless in pregnancy. Serial values that rise against the expected second-trimester nadir warrant attention but are not diagnostic alone.

- **Do not let imaging uncertainty delay action when the clinical picture is deteriorating.** This patient had worsening pain, dyspnea, fever, and masses large enough to threaten complication — the surgical team operated because the risk of doing nothing exceeded the risk of uncertainty.

- **Operate selectively and minimally.** Remove the mass that is dangerous; leave the stable one. Use progesterone prophylaxis, monitor fetal heart rate before, during, and after surgery, and keep the operation as short as possible.

- **Plan delivery around what remains.** The caudal myoma stayed in the pelvis and would have obstructed labour — an elective cesarean at 37 weeks on the existing scar was the right call. The mode of delivery should depend on the size, number, and anatomical location of any remaining uterine masses.

- **Myomas regress postpartum.** Leiomyomas decrease by a mean of 44% in the third trimester and 6 weeks postpartum, which is why conservative management of the residual mass — rather than immediate re-operation — is often appropriate.

## The Bottom Line

Pregnancy forces you to accept diagnostic compromise. Your job is to integrate ultrasound, non-contrast MRI, bimanual exam, serial tumour markers, and the clinical trajectory into a coherent risk assessment — and to know when that risk justifies operating on a gravid uterus. This team got it right: they removed the dangerous mass, preserved the pregnancy, and planned delivery around what remained. The lesson is not that imaging failed — it is that imaging in pregnancy will always have blind spots, and your clinical judgment has to fill them.
