Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Management] A pregnant patient has a 9.5 cm posterior intramural-subserosal uterine leiomyoma in the pouch of Douglas that was managed conservatively and left in situ during a second-trimester myomectomy of a separate pedunculated fibroid. What factors should influence the decision regarding the mode and timing of delivery in a patient with a residual pelvic uterine myoma?

## Clinical Teaching Answer

In a pregnant patient with a residual pelvic uterine myoma, the decision regarding mode and timing of delivery should be guided by three principal factors: **the size, number, and anatomical location of the myoma** — specifically, whether it will mechanically obstruct labor — balanced against the risk of uterine rupture at the prior myomectomy scar.

**Mode of delivery** is determined chiefly by the relationship of the residual myoma to the birth canal. In this case, the 9.5 cm posterior intramural-subserosal leiomyoma occupying the pouch of Douglas was adherent to the posterior uterine wall at the isthmic site. As the authors note, this lesion "would have represented a huge obstacle for the fetal head progression during the expulsive phase of labour," functioning as a fixed pelvic mass that could impede descent and rotation. For this reason, an elective cesarean delivery was selected rather than a trial of labor. More broadly, anterior or fundal myomas that do not encroach on the lower uterine segment or cervix may not preclude vaginal delivery, whereas posterior, lower-segment, or cervical myomas — particularly those of large diameter — are more likely to cause mechanical dystocia and warrant cesarean delivery.

**Timing of delivery** must account for both fetal maturity and the integrity of the uterine wall following antepartum myomectomy. In this patient, the pedunculated fundal leiomyoma had been excised at 21 weeks + 6 days via longitudinal laparotomy, leaving a uterine scar. To minimize the risk of scar rupture while ensuring fetal maturity, the team performed the elective cesarean at 37 weeks' gestation — the earliest acceptable full-term threshold — using the same surgical incision. This approach reflects the general principle that delivery timing should balance the increasing risk of uterine rupture or dehiscence with advancing gestation against the benefits of achieving term gestational age. The case report also references the inverse correlation between myoma size and delivery week, suggesting that larger or more numerous myomas may necessitate earlier delivery planning.

Additional considerations include the potential for myoma growth during pregnancy — leiomyomas increase in size by approximately 6–7% per week, with continued growth into the third trimester — and the partial postpartum regression that may occur, which the authors observed as the residual myoma decreased from 9.5 cm to 7.5 cm. These dynamics underscore the value of serial ultrasound surveillance throughout pregnancy to reassess myoma size and location and to refine the delivery plan accordingly. In summary, the mode and timing of delivery in such patients should be individualized through multidisciplinary assessment, integrating serial imaging of myoma characteristics with evaluation of prior uterine scars and the mechanical relationship of the mass to the pelvic birth canal.
