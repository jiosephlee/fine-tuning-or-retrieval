Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Pathophysiology] How does serum CA-125 typically fluctuate throughout the trimesters of an uncomplicated pregnancy, and what benign gynecologic conditions besides ovarian cancer can cause an elevated CA-125? In the presented case, the CA-125 was 41.8 U/mL during the second trimester — how should this be interpreted?

**Serum CA-125 in Pregnancy and Interpretation of the Case Value**

CA-125 is a glycoprotein antigen expressed by tissues of coelomic and Müllerian origin, and its serum levels are influenced by a variety of physiologic and pathologic processes beyond ovarian malignancy. In an uncomplicated pregnancy, CA-125 characteristically exhibits two physiologic peaks: one during the first trimester (median approximately 23 U/mL) and another during the third trimester (median approximately 21 U/mL). The second trimester is associated with the lowest values (median approximately 14 U/mL), making elevations during this period more noteworthy — though still not diagnostic of malignancy.

Numerous benign gynecologic and non-gynecologic conditions can elevate CA-125. These include endometriosis and adenomyosis (often with levels above 19 U/mL), pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, early pregnancy, near-term pregnancy, risk of miscarriage, and uterine leiomyomas. Non-gynecologic causes such as peritoneal inflammation, hepatic disease with ascites, pleural effusion, and even benign peritoneal irritation can also raise levels. Thus, CA-125 lacks specificity, particularly in reproductive-age women and during pregnancy, where the physiologic milieu itself can alter marker expression.

In the presented case, the CA-125 of 41.8 U/mL — measured at 21 weeks of gestation — exceeded both the standard non-pregnant cutoff of 35 U/mL and the expected second-trimester median of approximately 14 U/mL. Furthermore, serial measurement showed a rising trend from 36 U/mL two weeks prior. This mild but progressive elevation initially oriented the differential diagnosis toward a decidualized endometrioma, a known benign cause of CA-125 elevation. However, the ultimate surgical pathology revealed benign uterine leiomyomas with cystic degeneration and ischemic necrosis near a twisted pedicle — another benign process capable of producing CA-125 release, likely via peritoneal irritation from the degenerating, leaking myoma.

The critical teaching point is that an isolated or mildly elevated CA-125 in pregnancy — even when it exceeds 35 U/mL — must never be interpreted in isolation. As the authors emphasize, serial measurements providing trend data are more informative than single values, and clinical decision-making must integrate imaging characteristics, symptomatology, and risk of mechanical or ischemic complications. In this case, the slightly elevated and rising CA-125 contributed to the multidisciplinary decision to proceed with surgical exploration, but the driving indications were the large, symptomatic, and potentially complicated masses — not the marker value alone. The benign final pathology underscores the limited specificity of CA-125 in pregnancy and reinforces the principle that elevated tumor markers in gravid patients warrant cautious, contextual interpretation rather than automatic assumption of malignancy.
