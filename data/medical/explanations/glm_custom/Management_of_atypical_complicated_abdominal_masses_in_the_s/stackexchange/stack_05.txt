Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Management] A pregnant patient at 21 weeks gestation has a large, symptomatic pedunculated uterine leiomyoma causing worsening pain, dyspnea from mass effect, and intermittent low-grade fever. What are the indications for surgical intervention (myomectomy) for uterine leiomyomas during pregnancy, and what intraoperative measures should be taken to preserve the pregnancy?

# Indications and Intraoperative Considerations for Myomectomy During Pregnancy

## Indications for Surgical Intervention

Uterine leiomyomas are common in pregnancy, with an incidence of 0.3–10.1%, and the overwhelming majority are managed expectantly. Myomectomy during pregnancy is reserved for well-defined clinical scenarios, as surgical intervention carries inherent risks of severe hemorrhage, pregnancy loss, preterm labor, and uterine rupture. The threshold for operative management should be driven by specific clinical indications, not by the mere presence of a leiomyoma.

As illustrated in this case of a nulliparous woman at 21 weeks' gestation, the key indications for surgical intervention are:

1. **Refractory or progressive symptomatic mass effect.** Despite conservative management with analgesia and positional maneuvers, the patient's abdominal pain worsened, and she developed restrictive dyspnea from mechanical compression by an 18 cm cranial mass. Failure of conservative measures — not the size of the myoma alone — established the need for intervention.

2. **Impending or actual complications of a pedunculated myoma.** At laparotomy, the cranial mass was found to be a pedunculated leiomyoma that had undergone torsion with leakage, explaining the patient's intermittent low-grade fever. Torsion of a pedunculated myoma, with its attendant risk of ischemic necrosis, infarction, and peritonitis, is among the clearest indications for surgical removal during pregnancy. The pathological examination ultimately confirmed areas of ischaemic necrosis near the twisted pedicle, validating the decision to operate.

3. **Clinical complications: rupture, torsion, or infection.** The authors explicitly identify these as indications for surgical consideration. In this case, the risk of torsion had already materialized, and the leaking mass posed an ongoing infectious risk.

4. **Diagnostic uncertainty with oncologic concern.** The slight CA-125 elevation (41.8 U/mL, exceeding the normal <35 U/mL threshold and higher than the expected physiologic second-trimester median of ~14 U/mL), combined with imaging features of large mixed solid-cystic masses, necessitated a conclusive histologic diagnosis. The authors note that surgical management should be considered in the setting of oncological suspicion or clinical complications, always aiming to preserve pregnancy and avoid overtreatment.

5. **Anticipated compromise of uterine enlargement.** The prospect of continued uterine and myoma growth — leiomyomas increase by approximately 6.12% between the first and second trimesters and 3.10% between the second and third — threatened further mass effect and potential degeneration, necrosis, or rupture, supporting the decision to intervene at 21 weeks + 6 days rather than delay.

Notably, the caudal 9.5 cm mass, which was a posterior intramural-subserosal leiomyoma with cystic degeneration, was deliberately left in situ. This illustrates an essential principle: only the mass causing complications or posing an immediate threat should be removed. The intramural location of the caudal myoma would have carried a substantially higher risk of hemorrhage and uterine rupture, and its removal was not clinically justified at that time.

## Intraoperative Measures to Preserve the Pregnancy

Once the decision to operate is made, several intraoperative strategies are critical to minimize obstetric risk:

**Fetal monitoring.** Fetal cardiac activity should be verified at the start of the procedure and documented before, during, and after surgery, as emphasized by Zhao et al. In this case, the surgical team verified fetal cardiac activity immediately upon entering the abdomen and employed serial and intermittent transvaginal ultrasound monitoring throughout the operation.

**Tocolytic prophylaxis.** Progesterone prophylaxis was initiated intraoperatively (100 mg intramuscularly) and continued daily through discharge, followed by hydroxyprogesterone caproate (341 mg IM every 3 days) until delivery. This reflects the principle that uterine quiescence should be pharmacologically supported from the intraoperative period onward.

**Minimizing operative time and uterine manipulation.** The authors stress that surgery must be as short as possible to preserve the pregnancy. In this case, the procedure lasted 2 hours 25 minutes — relatively brief given the complexity. The surgeon used a double clamp technique to gently excise the pedunculated mass from its uterine implant, secured the stalk with a single absorbable suture at the exposed root, and avoided any manipulation of the gravid uterus itself. No incision into the uterine wall was required because the myoma was pedunculated, which significantly reduced the risk of hemorrhage, uterine rupture, and pregnancy loss.

**Limiting the extent of resection.** The decision to remove only the torsed, symptomatic pedunculated myoma while leaving the caudal intramural-subserosal myoma in situ was deliberate. This conservative surgical philosophy minimizes obstetric risk and is consistent with the principle that only the complication-driving lesion warrants intervention. The residual myoma was managed expectantly and ultimately required only cesarean delivery planning due to its potential to obstruct labor.

**Antibiotic prophylaxis and infection control.** Intraoperative cefazolin was administered, followed by postoperative antibiotics tailored to the patient's Klebsiella pneumoniae urinary tract infection, which had been identified preoperatively. This underscores the importance of screening for and treating concurrent infections that could threaten pregnancy viability in the perioperative period.

**Blood loss anticipation and management.** Although the pedunculated approach minimized bleeding, the patient still developed significant postoperative anemia (hemoglobin 7.5 g/L), requiring two transfusions and intravenous iron supplementation. This highlights that even seemingly straightforward myomectomies during pregnancy carry substantial hemorrhagic risk, and blood products should be available and anticipated.

## Key Teaching Point

Myomectomy during pregnancy is not a routine procedure — it is a carefully justified intervention reserved for pedunculated myomas with torsion, refractory symptomatic mass effect, or diagnostic uncertainty with oncologic concern. The decision to operate should always follow a failed trial of conservative management and multidisciplinary consensus. When surgery is indicated, the operative strategy should prioritize the shortest possible duration, removal of only the complicating lesion, continuous fetal monitoring, progesterone prophylaxis, and meticulous hemostasis — precisely the approach that yielded an excellent maternal and neonatal outcome in this case, with elective cesarean delivery at 37 weeks.
