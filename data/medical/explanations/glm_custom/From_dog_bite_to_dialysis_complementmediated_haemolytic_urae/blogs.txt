Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# Don't Forget the Dog: Capnocytophaga canimorsus and the Septic Patient with a Bite History

## The Dog Bite That Didn't Look Like Much — Until It Did

A previously healthy woman in her late 40s gets bitten on the upper lip by her own dog. She gets a tetanus shot and a prescription for phenoxymethylpenicillin. She never picks up the prescription. Four days later, she's in septic shock with DIC, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury, refractory hypoglycemia (glucose 0.6 mmol/L), and mottled extremities. She ends up dialysis-dependent with a kidney biopsy showing thrombotic microangiopathy. The culprit? *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* — a slow-growing, encapsulated gram-negative rod that lives harmlessly in the mouths of dogs and cats but can cause absolute havoc when it gets into human tissue.

This case is a masterclass in why bite history matters, why empiric broad-spectrum coverage can't wait for culture results, and how a septic patient who appears to be improving can then develop a completely separate, life-threatening complement-mediated process that you cannot afford to miss.

## The Pathogen You Need to Have on Your Radar

*Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is part of the normal oropharyngeal flora in dogs and cats. Transmission to humans occurs via bites, scratches, or even close contact. Here's what makes it clinically treacherous: it is fastidious and slow-growing in culture, meaning your blood cultures may not flag positive for days — in this case, confirmation came at day 7. That delay can be lethal if you're waiting for cultures to narrow your antibiotic coverage. Mortality approaches 30% despite the organism's broad antibiotic susceptibility, which tells you everything you need to know about how quickly these patients decompensate while you wait for the lab to call.

The clinical spectrum is remarkably wide — from isolated meningitis to fulminant sepsis with DIC, multiorgan failure, and thrombotic microangiopathy. Our patient landed at the severe end: septic shock with severe leukopenia, coagulopathy consistent with DIC, liver enzyme elevation, lactic acidosis, and progression to complete anuria requiring continuous renal replacement therapy. But even in a fulminant presentation, the organism may not be immediately suspected unless you specifically ask about animal exposure.

This is why empiric coverage matters. When this patient arrived, the team suspected *C. canimorsus* from the outset based on the dog bite history and initiated piperacillin-tazobactam — a decision that likely saved her life before cultures confirmed the diagnosis a week later. The lesson is straightforward: when a septic patient has a bite history (dog, cat, or otherwise), your empiric regimen needs to cover *Capnocytophaga* species from minute one. You cannot wait for the culture to tell you what you already clinically suspect.

## The Diagnostic Twist: When Sepsis Improves but Hemolysis Begins

Here's where this case gets genuinely fascinating and where most clinicians could get caught out. After eight days, the patient was stable enough to transfer to the nephrology ward. Inflammatory markers were improving, leukocytes were recovering, coagulation parameters had normalized, and antibiotics were being de-escalated. Then, suddenly, her hemoglobin dropped from 111 to 68 g/L over six days without any evidence of bleeding. Platelets stayed stuck at 30–40 × 10⁹/L. Haptoglobin became undetectable. LDH rose.

The initial DAT (Coombs test) was positive for IgG, and the team reasonably started high-dose prednisolone for suspected autoimmune hemolytic anemia. But the hemolysis didn't respond to steroids, and a peripheral smear told the real story: 15–20% schistocytes. This was microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA) — thrombotic microangiopathy had entered the building.

This is the critical inflection point. A patient who is recovering from sepsis suddenly develops new MAHA with persistent thrombocytopenia and worsening kidney function. The differential now includes TTP, STEC-HUS, and complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS). Getting this distinction right changes everything about your management.

## Navigating the TMA Differential

The team worked through this systematically. ADAMTS13 activity came back at 43.5% — TTP was effectively ruled out, as it typically presents with activity below 10%. The PLASMIC score was 4/7, also suggesting low pretest probability. STEC-HUS was excluded with a negative stool PCR for Shiga toxin and no prodromal enteric illness. A kidney biopsy showed severe acute tubular necrosis, ischemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi, and scattered crescent formation — consistent with TMA.

The diagnosis was complement-mediated HUS. And here's a pearl that's easy to miss: **basic complement levels (C3/C4) may be completely normal in CM-HUS.** In this patient, standard complement tests and functional pathway assays were within normal limits. The diagnosis is fundamentally clinicopathological and one of exclusion — you rule out TTP with ADAMTS13, rule out STEC with stool PCR, and then CM-HUS becomes the working diagnosis, particularly when supported by biopsy findings and a clinical response to complement blockade.

## The Treatment Dilemma: Complement Blockade During and After Sepsis

The management of CM-HUS in the context of recent bacteraemia presents a genuine therapeutic tightrope. Eculizumab — a monoclonal antibody against terminal complement component C5 — is the standard of care for CM-HUS, and early initiation is the single most important predictor of renal recovery. Delayed initiation means incomplete renal recovery and higher risk of progression to end-stage kidney disease. But terminal complement components are critical for host defence against encapsulated organisms, which means complement blockade carries real infectious risk — particularly against *Neisseria* and, ironically, against *Capnocytophaga* itself.

The team navigated this by initiating eculizumab only after blood cultures had become negative. Plasma exchange was used as bridging therapy before the first eculizumab dose. Notably, the patient's diuresis returned after the second and third PEX sessions — but before eculizumab — reflecting only transient stabilisation, not durable disease control. Sustained haematological and renal recovery occurred only after complement blockade was established, reinforcing that PEX alone is insufficient for CM-HUS.

Meningococcal vaccination (both Menveo and Bexsero) was administered before eculizumab induction, with prophylactic phenoxymethylpenicillin given because the vaccines had not been completed two weeks prior to complement blockade. Extended complement genetic testing did not identify pathogenic variants, supporting infection-triggered CM-HUS rather than an inherited complement defect. This parallels a prior report by Bjørkto et al. of *C. canimorsus*-induced CM-HUS, though that patient harboured a complement factor B variant — our patient did not.

## Key Take-Home Points

- **Ask about animal bites in every septic patient.** *C. canimorsus* is rare but devastating, with mortality approaching 30% despite broad antibiotic susceptibility. A dog or cat bite, scratch, or even close contact history should immediately broaden your empiric coverage.
- **Start broad empiric antibiotics immediately.** Piperacillin-tazobactam is a reasonable choice for dog bite–associated sepsis covering *Capnocytophaga* species. Do not wait for culture confirmation — this organism is slow-growing and fastidious, and culture confirmation may take a week.
- **Consider molecular diagnostics.** Given the fastidious nature of *C. canimorsus*, real-time nanopore sequencing and other molecular methods are increasingly valuable for accelerating diagnosis when cultures are slow to flag.
- **Watch for TMA after sepsis improves.** New MAHA with schistocytes, persistent thrombocytopenia, and worsening AKI in a recovering septic patient should trigger a TMA workup — do not attribute it to ongoing DIC or autoimmune hemolysis without investigating.
- **Normal complement levels do not exclude CM-HUS.** C3 and C4 can be within reference ranges. CM-HUS is a clinicopathological diagnosis of exclusion after ruling out TTP (ADAMTS13 <10%) and STEC-HUS (stool Shiga toxin PCR).
- **Eculizumab early, but not during active bacteraemia.** Initiate complement blockade promptly once TTP and STEC-HUS are excluded, but ensure blood cultures are negative first. Vaccinate against meningococcus and provide antibiotic prophylaxis.
- **PEX is a bridge, not a destination.** Plasma exchange may provide transient stabilisation in CM-HUS, but sustained recovery requires terminal complement blockade with eculizumab.
- **Consider infectious triggers for CM-HUS even without genetic predisposition.** Not every CM-HUS patient has an identifiable complement gene variant. Infection-triggered CM-HUS can occur in immunocompetent adults — *C. canimorsus* is now part of a small but growing list of reported triggers.

## The Bottom Line

*Capnocytophaga canimorsus* sepsis following a dog bite is rare but carries a mortality approaching 30%. The diagnosis is often delayed by the organism's fastidious, slow-growing nature in culture, making empiric broad-spectrum coverage critical from the moment you hear "dog bite." And in a subset of these patients, what follows sepsis is not straightforward recovery but a complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy that will quietly destroy the kidneys unless you recognise it, work through the TMA differential, and initiate complement blockade. Ask about the dog. Cover empirically. And when the sepsis improves but the hemolysis starts — think CM-HUS.



# When Sepsis Improves But Haemolysis Worsens: Recognizing Superimposed Thrombotic Microangiopathy

## The Pattern That Should Make You Stop and Rethink

Your septic patient is turning the corner. Inflammatory markers are settling, white cells are recovering, coagulopathy has resolved, and you're starting to feel like the hard part is over. Then the haemoglobin tanks — 111 down to 68 g/L in six days — with no bleeding source. Platelets stay stuck in the basement. Haptoglobin disappears. What do you do?

This is exactly the scenario that unfolded in a previously healthy woman in her late 40s, four days after a dog bite to the upper lip. Her story is a masterclass in how the initial clinical narrative — fulminant sepsis with DIC — can obscure a second, entirely different pathological process that declares itself only when the first is resolving. The teaching point isn't just about Capnocytophaga canimorsus or even complement-mediated HUS. It's about temporal pattern recognition: when a new problem emerges that doesn't fit the trajectory of the original diagnosis, you need to broaden your differential, send the right tests, and resist anchoring.

## The Case: From Dog Bite to Dialysis

Four days after being bitten by her dog on the upper lip, this previously healthy woman presented in septic shock with fever over 40°C, mottled extremities, refractory hypoglycaemia (nadir 0.6 mmol/L), pancytopenia, AKI, liver enzyme elevation, and coagulopathy consistent with DIC. She had been prescribed phenoxymethylpenicillin at the time of the bite but never collected it. Broad-spectrum piperacillin-tazobactam was started empirically in the ED, covering dog-associated pathogens including Capnocytophaga. Blood cultures confirmed *C. canimorsus* bacteraemia seven days later.

She became completely anuric, required continuous renal replacement therapy, and developed diffuse ecchymoses with distal cyanotic changes to toes and fingers — microthrombotic phenomena in the context of DIC. She never required vasopressors beyond stress-dose steroids. After eight days, she was transferred to nephrology, dialysis-dependent but clinically stable.

Then the picture shifted. Her inflammatory profile improved. Coagulation normalised. Platelets had started to recover. But then: sudden isolated haemoglobin drop, persistent thrombocytopaenia (30–40 × 10⁹/L), undetectable haptoglobin, and rising LDH. This was not a continuation of the sepsis story. This was something new.

## The DAT Trap: A False-Positive Coombs Test Leading Down the Wrong Path

Here's where the case takes a turn that should make every clinician pause. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT / Coombs) came back positive for IgG. The team reasonably suspected autoimmune haemolytic anaemia and started high-dose prednisolone. It didn't work. Haemolysis continued. Platelets stayed low.

The repeat DAT was negative. The initial positive result was almost certainly a false positive — likely driven by penicillin exposure (penicillin can coat red cells and cause a positive DAT without true autoimmune haemolysis) or by recent transfusions. This is a critical pitfall. A single positive DAT in a patient who has been on beta-lactams and received blood products should be interpreted with extreme caution, especially when the clinical picture doesn't fully fit autoimmune haemolytic anaemia — and in this case, the persistent thrombocytopaenia should have been the clue that something else was going on.

The team then did the test that should have been done earlier: a peripheral blood smear. It showed 15–20% schistocytes. This was microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA). The diagnosis was thrombotic microangiopathy, not autoimmune haemolysis. The steroids were a detour.

## The TMA Diagnostic Algorithm: Exclude, Exclude, Then Commit

Once schistocytes are identified, the question becomes: what type of TMA? The algorithm is straightforward but requires discipline, especially when you're managing a critically ill patient and waiting on send-out labs.

**Exclude TTP first.** Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura is defined by severe ADAMTS13 deficiency, typically below 10%. This patient's ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%, and her PLASMIC score was only 4/7 — both arguing strongly against TTP. While waiting for ADAMTS13 results, plasma exchange was appropriately initiated as bridging therapy, because the downside of delaying PEX in possible TTP is catastrophic.

**Exclude STEC-HUS.** Shiga toxin-producing E. coli HUS was ruled out with a negative stool PCR, and there was no prodromal enteric illness.

**Then consider complement-mediated HUS.** The constellation of persistent MAHA, dialysis-dependent AKI, schistocytes on smear, and kidney biopsy showing acute tubular necrosis with ischaemic glomeruli and fibrin thrombi cemented the diagnosis. Crucially, basic complement levels (C3 and C4) were normal — and this is a point that cannot be overstated. Normal C3 and C4 do not exclude CM-HUS. Complement-mediated TMA is a clinicopathological diagnosis of exclusion, not a diagnosis that requires abnormal standard complement panels.

## The Treatment Dilemma: Complement Blockade After Sepsis

Eculizumab — a monoclonal antibody targeting terminal complement component C5 — is the standard of care for CM-HUS, and early initiation is the single most important predictor of renal recovery. Delayed initiation is associated with incomplete renal recovery and higher risk of progression to end-stage kidney disease.

But here's the tension: terminal complement components are critical for defence against encapsulated bacteria, including *Neisseria* and, notably, *Capnocytophaga*. Starting eculizumab during active bacteraemia could risk uncontrolled infection. The team waited until blood cultures were negative before initiating complement blockade. Meningococcal vaccination (Menveo and Bexsero) was administered before the first dose, with prophylactic penicillin given because the vaccines hadn't been completed two weeks prior to starting eculizumab — the standard requirement for adequate antibody response.

Plasma exchange was used as bridging therapy — three sessions with Octaplas. Diuresis returned after the final PEX sessions but before eculizumab, reflecting only transient stabilisation. Sustained haematological and renal recovery occurred only after complement blockade was initiated. This is consistent with the evidence: PEX removes circulating complement fragments and inflammatory mediators but doesn't correct the underlying complement dysregulation. It buys time; eculizumab fixes the problem.

## What Makes This Case Unique: Infection-Triggered CM-HUS Without a Genetic Predisposition

Most cases of CM-HUS occur in patients with identifiable genetic variants or autoantibodies affecting complement regulatory proteins — factors H, I, B, or membrane cofactor protein. Disease penetrance is incomplete, and a trigger is usually needed: infection, pregnancy, autoimmune disease, or certain medications.

This patient had no pathogenic complement gene variants identified on extended genetic testing. She had no significant medical history. Her CM-HUS appears to have been purely infection-triggered — *C. canimorsus* activating the alternative complement pathway in a host without a demonstrable inherited predisposition. This is rare but not unprecedented; a prior report by Bjørkto et al. described *C. canimorsus*-induced CM-HUS in a patient harbouring a complement factor B variant. This case adds to that literature but suggests the trigger alone can be sufficient, even without a genetic "first hit."

The clinical paradox is worth reflecting on: complement activation helps clear *C. canimorsus* (many isolates are complement-sensitive, though capsule-expressing strains show partial resistance), but in susceptible hosts — or perhaps even in some apparently non-susceptible hosts — that same activation may trigger uncontrolled endothelial damage and TMA.

## Clinical Pearls

- **Temporal pattern recognition is a diagnostic tool.** When haemolysis emerges as sepsis resolves, it's not the same disease getting worse — it's a new process. Stop and reassess.
- **Send a peripheral smear early when haemolysis doesn't fit the clinical trajectory.** Schistocytes change the entire diagnostic framework. This patient's smear was the turning point.
- **A positive DAT in a patient on beta-lactams or with recent transfusions may be a false positive.** If steroids aren't working and platelets are also low, rethink — the combination of MAHA and thrombocytopaenia points toward TMA, not autoimmune haemolysis.
- **Normal C3 and C4 do not exclude CM-HUS.** This is a clinicopathological diagnosis of exclusion. Don't be reassured by a normal complement panel.
- **Exclude TTP first (ADAMTS13 < 10%), then STEC-HUS (stool PCR), then commit to CM-HUS.** Use PLASMIC scoring to triage while awaiting results, but confirm with labs.
- **Plasma exchange is bridging therapy in CM-HUS, not definitive treatment.** Sustained recovery requires complement blockade with eculizumab.
- **Initiate eculizumab early.** Time-to-initiation is the main determinant of renal recovery. Vaccinate, give prophylactic antibiotics, and start complement blockade once bacteraemia has cleared.
- **Consider unusual infectious triggers for TMA.** *C. canimorsus* is rare, but dog bites are not. When a patient presents with sepsis after a dog bite and later develops TMA, this pathogen should be on your radar.

## The Bottom Line

This case illustrates one of the hardest things in medicine: recognising when a patient who is "getting better" is actually getting worse from a different process. The sepsis-to-CM-HUS transition is subtle, the initial DIC picture is a convincing narrative, and a false-positive DAT can send you down the wrong path for days. The peripheral smear is the key that unlocks the correct diagnosis. When haemolysis appears after sepsis improves, think TMA, send the smear, run the exclusion algorithm, and don't let a normal C3 or C4 talk you out of complement-mediated HUS.



# MAHA + Thrombocytopenia + AKI: Navigating the TMA Diagnostic Maze

## When Sepsis Gets Better But Hemolysis Gets Worse

A previously healthy woman in her late 40s gets bitten by her dog on the upper lip. Four days later, she arrives in septic shock with pancytopenia, DIC, lactic acidosis, and refractory hypoglycemia. Blood cultures grow *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*. Antibiotics and supportive care kick in, inflammatory markers improve, and coagulopathy resolves. You think you're winning.

Then her hemoglobin drops from 111 to 68 g/L over six days without any bleeding source. Her platelets stay stuck in the 30s. Haptoglobin becomes undetectable. LDH climbs. A peripheral smear shows 15%–20% schistocytes. She remains dialysis-dependent and anuric.

This is the moment where the case pivots from straightforward sepsis management to one of the most challenging diagnostic mazes in clinical medicine: the thrombotic microangiopathies.

## The TMA Framework: A Structured Approach

When you encounter the triad of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury, the umbrella term is thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). But TMA is not a diagnosis — it's a histopathological pattern of endothelial injury and microvascular thrombus formation that can be triggered by a remarkably diverse set of conditions. Your job is to rapidly sort through the differential because the specific subtype dictates fundamentally different treatments. Give plasma exchange for TTP and you save a life. Give it for CM-HUS and you're mostly wasting time and plasma.

The major entities to systematically exclude are:

1. **Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP)** — deficiency of ADAMTS13
2. **Shiga toxin-associated HUS (STEC-HUS)** — post-enteric infection
3. **Complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS)** — dysregulation of alternative complement pathway
4. **Secondary TMAs** — drugs, autoimmune disease, malignant hypertension, pregnancy, malignancy

Let's walk through how this case team navigated each branch.

## Step 1: Exclude TTP First — It's the One You Cannot Miss

TTP is the first priority because untreated mortality approaches 90%, and plasma exchange is lifesaving. The pathophysiology is severe deficiency of ADAMTS13 (<10%), the metalloprotease that cleaves ultra-large von Willebrand factor multimers. Without it, ULVWF accumulates on endothelial surfaces, causing platelet-rich microthrombi.

In this case, the team used the PLASMIC score to triage while awaiting lab results. Her score was 4/7 — low pretest probability for TTP. The critical result came back: ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%. That effectively rules out TTP, which requires activity below 10%. The intermediate range (10%–60%) represents deficiency but not severe enough to support a TTP diagnosis, though some experts would still monitor carefully.

An important clinical pearl emerges here: this team initiated plasma exchange as a bridging therapy *before* the ADAMTS13 result returned. This is a pragmatic approach — PEX is lifesaving in TTP and relatively low-risk, so when the diagnosis is uncertain and the clinical picture fits MAHA with thrombocytopenia, empiric PEX while waiting for results is a reasonable strategy. The PLASMIC score can help you decide whether to start empirically, but if there's clinical uncertainty, treat first and sort it out later. TTP does not wait for the lab.

## Step 2: Rule Out STEC-HUS — Check for the Prodrome and the Toxin

STEC-HUS is the most common form of TMA in children but remains relevant in adults. The classic presentation is bloody diarrhea followed by MAHA and AKI within 5–13 days. The underlying mechanism is Shiga toxin-mediated endothelial damage, primarily from Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* (STEC), most commonly O157:H7.

For this patient, two factors argued against STEC-HUS. First, there was no prodromal enteric illness — no diarrhea, no bloody stools. Second, stool PCR for Shiga toxin was negative. In the absence of both the clinical prodrome and microbiological confirmation, STEC-HUS was effectively excluded.

Don't forget, however, that a subset of patients with STEC-HUS do not have preceding diarrhea, so the negative stool PCR is the more definitive exclusion here. If you have high clinical suspicion despite a negative initial PCR, repeat testing or additional methods may be warranted.

## Step 3: Consider Secondary TMA — Drugs, Autoimmunity, and Beyond

Before landing on CM-HUS, secondary causes of TMA need consideration. This is where the clinical history becomes paramount.

**Drug-induced TMA** can occur through two mechanisms: direct endothelial toxicity (e.g., chemotherapy agents like mitomycin C, gemcitabine) or immune-mediated (e.g., quinine, calcineurin inhibitors). This patient had no relevant drug exposures — no chemotherapy, no quinine, no calcineurin inhibitors.

**Autoimmune disease**, particularly antiphospholipid syndrome and systemic lupus erythematosus, can cause secondary TMA. The workup here included ANA and ANCA testing, both of which were negative. No clinical features of SLE or APS were present.

**Malignant hypertension** can cause a TMA-like syndrome, but this patient did not present with hypertensive emergency — her blood pressure was not the driving issue.

**Pregnancy-associated TMA** was irrelevant given her clinical context.

With secondary causes effectively excluded, and with no history of pregnancy, autoimmune disease, or offending medications, the diagnostic net tightened.

## The Red Herring: A Positive DAT That Wasn't What It Seemed

One of the most instructive diagnostic moments in this case was the initially positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT/Coombs), positive for IgG. This raised the specter of autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA), and high-dose prednisolone was started. But the hemolysis didn't respond to corticosteroids, and platelets remained low — inconsistent with AIHA, which typically doesn't cause severe thrombocytopenia to this degree.

The peripheral blood smear told the real story: schistocytes, not spherocytes, dominated. This was MAHA, not immune hemolysis. The repeat DAT was negative. The initial false positive was attributed to penicillin exposure and prior transfusions — both known causes of transient DAT positivity.

This is a critical teaching point: when hemolysis and thrombocytopenia coexist, always get the peripheral smear early. Schistocytes point to mechanical fragmentation (TMA, DIC, HELLP), while spherocytes point to immune-mediated destruction. The smear can redirect your entire workup within minutes.

## The Kidney Biopsy: Why Tissue Matters in TMA

When the clinical and laboratory picture suggested TMA but the subtype remained unclear, the team performed a renal biopsy — once platelets had recovered enough (77 × 10⁹/L) to make the procedure safe.

The biopsy findings were striking: severe acute tubular necrosis, ischemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi, and scattered crescent formation. Immunofluorescence showed no immune complex deposition, and electron microscopy excluded a primary glomerulopathy. These findings are pathognomonic for TMA — the fibrin thrombi within glomerular capillaries represent the histological fingerprint of endothelial injury and microvascular coagulation.

A kidney biopsy serves several critical functions in the TMA workup:

- **Confirms the diagnosis** when clinical and lab findings are ambiguous
- **Excludes alternative diagnoses** such as glomerulonephritis with crescents, which can mimic TMA clinically
- **Assesses the extent and chronicity of damage**, which has prognostic implications for renal recovery
- **Guides subtype classification** — while the biopsy cannot distinguish between TMA subtypes on histology alone (the pattern is shared across TTP, STEC-HUS, and CM-HUS), it confirms that the renal injury is indeed TMA-related rather than from another cause of AKI

In this case, the crescent formation is worth noting. Crescents in TMA represent severe glomerular injury from rupture of the glomerular capillary loop, with extracapillary cellular proliferation. Their presence indicates aggressive disease but does not necessarily predict poor outcomes if the underlying process is treated promptly.

## CM-HUS: A Diagnosis of Exclusion With Normal Complement Levels

After systematically excluding TTP (ADAMTS13 >10%), STEC-HUS (negative stool PCR, no prodrome), and secondary TMA (no drugs, no autoimmunity), the remaining diagnosis was complement-mediated HUS.

This is where the case teaches a crucial lesson: **normal complement levels do not exclude CM-HUS.** The standard C3 and C4 were within normal limits. Functional assays of the classical, alternative, and lectin pathways were normal. Extended testing later showed markedly reduced alternative pathway activity (AH50 at 6%), but this was performed after plasma exchange and eculizumab, so it most likely reflected treatment effects rather than intrinsic complement consumption.

CM-HUS arises from dysregulation of the alternative complement pathway, but in many cases, standard complement panels remain normal during the acute illness. The diagnosis is clinicopathological — based on the exclusion of other TMA subtypes, compatible biopsy findings, and the clinical response to complement inhibition.

In this case, the diagnosis was further strengthened by the response to eculizumab: sustained hematological remission and partial renal recovery following initiation of complement blockade. No pathogenic complement gene variants were identified on extended genetic testing, supporting an infection-triggered mechanism rather than a pre-existing inherited complement defect.

## The Infection Trigger: When the Pathogen and the Complement System Collide

What makes this case particularly interesting is the trigger: *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*. This gram-negative rod from canine oral flora is a rare but increasingly recognized cause of severe sepsis, particularly in asplenic or immunocompromised patients — though this patient was immunocompetent.

The complement paradox here is fascinating. *C. canimorsus* shows variable serum susceptibility — many strains are complement-sensitive, but capsule-expressing strains show partial resistance. Complement activity is important for host defense against this organism. But in a susceptible host, the infection can trigger uncontrolled complement activation on the endothelial surface, producing TMA. The infection both activates the complement system and, in some cases, is partially resistant to its effects.

This case adds to a small but growing literature linking *C. canimorsus* to CM-HUS. A previous report by Bjørkto et al. described a patient with the same infection-triggered CM-HUS who harbored a complement factor B variant. This patient, however, had no identifiable complement gene variants, suggesting that severe infection alone — without a demonstrable genetic predisposition — can trigger CM-HUS in some patients.

## The Therapeutic Dilemma: Blocking Complement During Infection

Initiating eculizumab during or shortly after bacteremia presents a genuine dilemma. Eculizumab blocks terminal complement component C5, impairing defense against encapsulated organisms — particularly *Neisseria* but potentially also *Capnocytophaga*. The team waited until blood cultures were negative before initiating eculizumab, which is a prudent approach.

Meningococcal vaccination (both serogroups ACYW and B) was initiated before the first eculizumab dose, with prophylactic penicillin provided since the vaccines had not been given at least two weeks prior to complement blockade. This reflects best practice — patients on eculizumab require vaccination, antibiotic prophylaxis, and ongoing vigilance for encapsulated organism infections.

Plasma exchange was used as bridging therapy, and interestingly, spontaneous diuresis returned after the final PEX sessions but before eculizumab took effect. This likely represents temporary stabilization from removal of circulating complement fragments and inflammatory mediators rather than disease modification. Sustained remission occurred only after complement blockade, reinforcing that PEX is not definitive therapy for CM-HUS.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **When MAHA + thrombocytopenia + AKI appear together, work through the TMA differential systematically.** The specific subtype dictates fundamentally different treatments — PEX for TTP, eculizumab for CM-HUS, supportive care for STEC-HUS.

- **Exclude TTP first.** ADAMTS13 activity <10% confirms TTP. Use the PLASMIC score to triage while waiting. Start empiric PEX if clinical suspicion is high — TTP does not wait for the lab.

- **Normal complement levels (C3, C4) do not exclude CM-HUS.** The diagnosis is clinicopathological, based on exclusion of other TMA subtypes and compatible biopsy findings.

- **Get the peripheral smear early.** Schistocytes point to TMA, spherocytes to immune hemolysis. A false-positive DAT can mislead you — this patient's initial IgG-positive DAT was likely from penicillin and transfusions, and repeat testing was negative.

- **Kidney biopsy confirms TMA and excludes mimics.** Ischemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi are the histological hallmark. Crescents indicate severe injury. Biopsy cannot distinguish TMA subtypes but confirms the process and excludes alternative diagnoses.

- **Unusual infections can trigger CM-HUS even without a genetic predisposition.** *C. canimorsus* is a rare but recognized trigger. Consider infection-triggered CM-HUS when TMA develops during or after bacteremia, even in immunocompetent patients.

- **Eculizumab should be initiated promptly once CM-HUS is diagnosed.** Delayed initiation correlates with incomplete renal recovery and progression to end-stage kidney disease. Wait for negative blood cultures if possible, vaccinate against meningococcus, and provide antibiotic prophylaxis.

- **Plasma exchange is bridging therapy in CM-HUS, not definitive treatment.** Diuresis may return with PEX, but sustained remission requires complement blockade.



# Normal C3 and C4 Do Not Exclude Complement-Mediated HUS: A Critical Diagnostic Pitfall

## The Case That Stops You in Your Tracks

A previously healthy woman in her late 40s gets bitten by her dog on the upper lip. Four days later she's in septic shock with pancytopenia, DIC, lactic acidosis, and refractory hypoglycemia. Blood cultures grow *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*. She survives the septic phase with antibiotics and supportive care — but then, just as things seem to be improving, she develops sudden isolated haemolysis and persistent thrombocytopenia. Her C3 and C4 are normal. So... is this CM-HUS, or not?

This case, published by Fredriksen and colleagues, is a masterclass in why **normal complement levels do not exclude complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome** — and why timing of extended complement testing matters enormously when you've already started treating.

## The Diagnostic Labyrinth

Let's walk through how this unfolded, because the diagnostic pathway here is where the real lessons live.

After surviving the initial septic phase (which included complete anuria, CRRT, and ischaemic digits from microthrombi), the patient was transferred to nephrology, stable but dialysis-dependent. Then the haemoglobin dropped from 111 to 68 g/L over 6 days without any bleeding source. Haptoglobin became undetectable. LDH rose. Platelets stayed stuck in the 30–40 range. The peripheral smear showed 15–20% schistocytes. This was microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) — a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) was underway.

Now comes the critical branching point: **which TMA?**

The team worked through the differential systematically. ADAMTS13 came back at 43.5%, effectively ruling out TTP (which requires activity below 10%). The PLASMIC score was 4/7, reinforcing low pretest probability. Shiga toxin PCR on stool was negative, and there was no prodromal enteric illness — ruling out STEC-HUS. A kidney biopsy showed severe acute tubular necrosis, ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi, and scattered crescents — consistent with TMA. That left complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS).

But here's the rub: **C3 and C4 were within normal limits.** And not just slightly — the standard complement panel, including functional assays of the classical, alternative, and lectin pathways, was all normal. If you had stopped there and said "complement's fine, can't be CM-HUS," you would have been wrong.

## Normal C3/C4: The Pitfall

This is the critical teaching point of the entire case. CM-HUS is a **clinicopathological diagnosis of exclusion**. Normal C3 and C4 levels do not exclude it. The authors state this explicitly, and it bears repeating because it is a well-documented but underappreciated fact in clinical practice.

CM-HUS arises from dysregulation of the **alternative complement pathway** — but that dysregulation doesn't necessarily manifest as depressed C3 or C4 on standard assays. The alternative pathway can be locally dysregulated at the endothelial surface without producing a measurable systemic depletion of complement proteins. Think of it as a localized runaway reaction rather than a global complement consumption. The damage is happening at the tissue level, not necessarily in the serum.

This is why the diagnosis requires assembling the full clinical picture: MAHA on smear, persistent thrombocytopenia, AKI consistent with TMA on biopsy, and exclusion of TTP (ADAMTS13 >10%) and STEC-HUS (negative Shiga toxin PCR, no diarrheal prodrome). When all of those pieces fit, CM-HUS is your diagnosis — even if C3 and C4 look completely unremarkable.

## The Extended Complement Testing Problem

The case also illustrates a problem that many clinicians will recognize but few discuss openly: **what happens when you send extended complement testing after you've already started treating?**

In this case, the team did the right thing clinically. They initiated plasma exchange (PEX) as bridging therapy before eculizumab. They vaccinated against meningococcus. They gave prophylactic antibiotics. They started eculizumab (900 mg IV weekly × 4 weeks, then 1200 mg IV every 2 weeks for maintenance).

But the extended complement testing — sent to a reference laboratory in Sweden — was performed 8 days after the final PEX session and 4 days after the first dose of eculizumab. The alternative pathway activity (AH50) came back at 6% — markedly reduced. Complement factors H, I, and B were normal, and anti-factor H antibodies were negative. Genetic testing ultimately identified no pathogenic variants.

So what does that AH50 of 6% actually mean? The authors are appropriately cautious: **the reduced AH50 is most consistent with treatment effects rather than intrinsic complement dysregulation.** Eculizumab blocks terminal complement component C5, and PEX alters the complement protein milieu. By the time the sample was drawn, the patient had been through three PEX sessions and one dose of eculizumab. You simply cannot interpret that AH50 as evidence of a pre-existing complement deficiency.

This is a real-world dilemma. You cannot always wait for extended complement testing before initiating therapy — the evidence is clear that delayed eculizumab initiation is associated with worse renal outcomes and higher progression to end-stage kidney disease. But if you treat first and test later, you've confounded your own diagnostic workup. The authors acknowledge this tension explicitly, and it's worth flagging for anyone who manages these patients: **treatment timing and diagnostic testing are in direct conflict, and patient safety rightly wins.**

## Infection-Triggered CM-HUS Without a Genetic Predisposition

Another fascinating aspect of this case is that the patient had **no identified pathogenic complement gene variants.** Extended genetic testing was negative. This supports an infection-triggered CM-HUS without a demonstrable inherited complement predisposition — something that challenges the traditional teaching that CM-HUS requires an underlying genetic defect.

*Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is a particularly intriguing trigger. It's a slow-growing, encapsulated gram-negative rod found in dog and cat oropharyngeal flora. The authors describe a "clinical paradox": complement activation is important for clearing *C. canimorsus* (many isolates are complement-sensitive, though capsule-expressing strains show partial resistance), but that same complement activation may trigger uncontrolled endothelial damage and TMA in susceptible hosts. The infection may be both cleared by complement and the very thing that dysregulates it.

This adds to a small but growing literature. The authors reference a case by Bjørkto et al. in which *C. canimorsus*-induced CM-HUS was associated with a complement factor B variant — a case where a genetic predisposition *was* identified. This patient, by contrast, had none. The implication is that **infection alone may be sufficient to trigger CM-HUS even without a genetic "second hit"** — which broadens the differential for any patient presenting with TMA after a significant infection.

## The PEX Question: Bridge, Not Destination

One more clinical pearl worth pulling out: the response to PEX in this case was transient. The patient's spontaneous diuresis returned after the second and third PEX sessions but before eculizumab — reflecting temporary stabilization only. Sustained haematological and renal recovery occurred only after complement blockade was initiated. This is consistent with the known pathophysiology: PEX may remove circulating complement fragments and inflammatory mediators, but it does not correct the underlying complement dysregulation. In TTP, PEX is lifesaving and definitive. In CM-HUS, it's a bridge — not a destination.

The false-positive DAT (Coombs test) is also worth noting. The initial result was positive for IgG, raising suspicion for autoimmune haemolytic anaemia and prompting a course of high-dose prednisolone that did nothing. The repeat test was negative, and the authors attribute the initial result to penicillin or prior transfusions. **A single positive DAT in a sick, multi-transfused patient on antibiotics should be interpreted with caution** — and should not distract from the smear findings showing schistocytes, which point toward MAHA, not autoimmune haemolysis.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Normal C3 and C4 do not exclude CM-HUS.** This is a clinicopathological diagnosis of exclusion. If you have MAHA, thrombocytopenia, AKI with biopsy-confirmed TMA, and you've excluded TTP (ADAMTS13 >10%) and STEC-HUS (negative Shiga toxin PCR), CM-HUS is your diagnosis — regardless of what C3 and C4 show.
- **Extended complement testing performed after PEX and eculizumab cannot be reliably interpreted.** A markedly reduced AH50 after treatment may reflect eculizumab and PEX effects rather than intrinsic complement deficiency. When clinical urgency mandates treating before testing, acknowledge that downstream complement assays are confounded.
- **CM-HUS can be triggered by infection alone, without a demonstrable genetic predisposition.** *C. canimorsus* is a rare but increasingly recognized trigger. Don't assume a patient needs a complement gene variant to develop CM-HUS — infection-triggered disease without genetic susceptibility is real.
- **PEX is a bridge to eculizumab in CM-HUS, not definitive therapy.** Transient improvement after PEX alone does not confirm the diagnosis or substitute for complement blockade. Sustained recovery requires terminal complement inhibition.
- **Early eculizumab initiation matters.** Delayed initiation is associated with incomplete renal recovery and higher risk of end-stage kidney disease. The main determinants of prognosis are residual renal function at baseline and time-to-initiation of complement blockade.
- **A positive DAT in a multi-transfused patient on antibiotics may be a false positive.** Don't let autoimmune haemolytic anaemia become a diagnostic anchor when the smear shows schistocytes. Repeat the test, and trust the peripheral smear.



# Complement Blockade in the Face of Active Bacteraemia: Walking the Therapeutic Tightrope

## The Paradox at the Bedside

You have a patient with biopsy-confirmed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), persistent haemolysis, dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury, and ADAMTS13 activity that rules out TTP. You know the diagnosis is complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). You know the standard of care is eculizumab—and you know that every day you delay, the odds of meaningful renal recovery shrink. But your patient has *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteraemia, an encapsulated gram-negative rod against which terminal complement is a critical line of host defence. Block complement, and you risk uncontrolled infection. Don't block it, and you risk irreversible kidney damage.

This is the therapeutic tightrope walked by the team managing a previously healthy woman in her late 40s who presented with fulminant sepsis four days after a dog bite, progressed to complete anuria and dialysis dependence, and then—just as her sepsis began to improve—developed sudden isolated haemolysis and persistent thrombocytopenia that signalled a superimposed TMA. Her case, published by Fredriksen and colleagues, is a masterclass in navigating the tension between urgent complement blockade and infectious risk, and it offers hard-won lessons applicable to anyone who may encounter CM-HUS in the setting of active or recent infection.

## The Clinical Course: How We Got Here

Let me briefly set the scene, because the timeline matters enormously for the therapeutic decisions that follow. Four days after being bitten on the upper lip by her dog, this patient presented in septic shock with pancytopenia, coagulopathy, metabolic acidosis, refractory hypoglycaemia, and acute kidney injury. *C. canimorsus* was suspected from the outset and confirmed by blood cultures seven days later. She was started on broad-spectrum piperacillin-tazobactam, later de-escalated to ampicillin and then oral penicillin V. Her early course was dominated by DIC, multiorgan involvement, and septic cardiomyopathy requiring continuous renal replacement therapy.

The critical pivot came around day 8 of hospitalisation. Inflammatory markers were improving, coagulopathy was resolving, and she seemed to be on the mend. Then her haemoglobin dropped from 111 to 68 g/L over six days without any bleeding source. Haptoglobin became undetectable, LDH rose, and platelets remained stuck at 30–40 × 10⁹/L. A peripheral smear showed 15–20% schistocytes. ADAMTS13 came back at 43.5%, ruling out TTP. Shiga toxin PCR was negative. A kidney biopsy showed ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi and acute tubular necrosis—consistent with TMA. The diagnosis was CM-HUS, triggered by *C. canimorsus* infection.

## Why This Case Forces the Dilemma into Sharp Relief

Here is what makes this case so instructive. *C. canimorsus* is an encapsulated organism. Many isolates are complement-sensitive, meaning terminal complement activity is genuinely important for clearing the pathogen—but some capsule-expressing strains show partial resistance, which is precisely why uncontrolled complement activation can occur in susceptible hosts. The infection triggers the complement dysregulation that drives TMA, yet the same complement pathway you are about to blockade is part of the host defence against the organism that caused the problem in the first place. It is a genuine clinical paradox, not a theoretical one.

The team's approach was methodical. Eculizumab was not initiated until blood cultures were confirmed negative. This is a critical point and arguably the single most important risk-mitigation step they took. Initiating terminal complement blockade during active bacteraemia with an encapsulated organism would have been extraordinarily dangerous—the patient could have developed uncontrolled, disseminated infection with an organism that is already capable of fulminant sepsis with mortality approaching 30%.

## Vaccination and Prophylaxis: Non-Negotiable but Logistically Messy

The infectious risk of eculizumab is primarily mediated through increased susceptibility to *Neisseria meningitidis*, but the principle extends to other encapsulated organisms. The standard mitigation strategy is meningococcal vaccination at least two weeks before the first dose, supplemented by antibiotic prophylaxis. In this case, the team administered Menveo (serogroups A, C, Y, W) and Bexsero (serogroup B), with the second doses planned for eight weeks later on an outpatient basis. However, the clinical situation did not allow a two-week window between vaccination and the first eculizumab dose—the patient had biopsy-confirmed TMA, ongoing haemolysis, and dialysis-dependent AKI, and every day of delay threatened further irreversible renal damage.

This is the reality of CM-HUS in practice. The textbook sequence—vaccinate, wait two weeks, then treat—is often impossible when the disease itself is actively destroying kidneys. The team recognised this and covered the gap with prophylactic penicillin V, acknowledging that the vaccines had not been given the full lead-in time. This is a pragmatic, defensible approach, and it is worth noting that antibiotic prophylaxis should continue for the duration of complement blockade regardless of vaccination status, given the possibility of vaccine failure and the existence of non-vaccine serogroups.

## Plasma Exchange as a Bridge: What It Can and Cannot Do

One of the most clinically useful insights from this case is the role of plasma exchange (PEX) as a bridging strategy. The team initiated PEX before the first dose of eculizumab, and the patient's spontaneous diuresis returned after the second and third PEX sessions—before eculizumab had been given. This might tempt you to conclude that PEX alone was sufficient. It was not. Sustained haematological and renal recovery occurred only after complement blockade was initiated.

This distinction matters. PEX can remove circulating complement fragments, inflammatory mediators, and autoantibodies, and it may provide temporary stabilisation. But it does not correct the underlying complement dysregulation. In TTP, PEX is disease-modifying and lifesaving. In CM-HUS, it is a stopgap—a way to buy time while you confirm the diagnosis, clear the bacteraemia, vaccinate, and arrange eculizumab. The case report is explicit on this point: diuresis returned after PEX but before eculizumab, reflecting "transient stabilisation." The durable recovery came only with terminal complement blockade.

There is also a diagnostic sequencing consideration here. Basic complement studies (C3, C4) and ADAMTS13 were obtained immediately before the first PEX session. Extended complement testing—including alternative pathway activity (AH50), complement factors H, I, and B, and anti-factor H antibodies—was performed eight days after the final PEX session and four days after the first eculizumab dose. The AH50 came back at 6%, but the authors interpret this as most consistent with treatment effect rather than intrinsic complement depletion, which is a critical and honest interpretive caveat. If you are sending these tests, think carefully about what your patient has already received—PEX and eculizumab will both confound the results.

## Time-to-Eculizumab and the Renal Recovery Clock

The evidence is clear and was reinforced by this patient's outcome: time-to-initiation of eculizumab is one of the strongest predictors of renal recovery. Delayed initiation is associated with incomplete renal recovery and a higher risk of progression to end-stage kidney disease. This patient achieved haematological remission and partial renal recovery (serum creatinine stabilised at 175 µmol/L), which is consistent with the broader literature showing that renal function at the time of initiation and the duration of untreated disease are the main determinants of long-term outcome.

The tension, then, is real and quantifiable. Every day you wait for culture clearance, vaccination lead-in, or confirmatory biopsy results, you are trading infectious safety for renal parenchyma. There is no formula that resolves this. What this case demonstrates is a reasonable framework: confirm culture negativity, vaccinate even if you cannot wait the full two weeks, initiate antibiotic prophylaxis, use PEX as a bridge, and start eculizumab as soon as the infectious risk is acceptable. The team also performed the kidney biopsy only once platelets had recovered to 77 × 10⁹/L, which is a practical point about procedural safety in the context of active TMA.

## A Note on Complement Testing and Genetic Workup

One additional nuance worth highlighting: standard complement tests (C3 and C4) were within normal limits in this patient, and functional assays of the classical, alternative, and lectin pathways were also normal at baseline. This is an important pearl—normal C3 and C4 do not exclude CM-HUS. The diagnosis is clinicopathological and is made by confirming MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and end-organ damage (typically renal) after excluding TTP and STEC-HUS. Extended genetic testing in this patient did not identify pathogenic complement gene variants, which supports infection-triggered CM-HUS without a demonstrable inherited predisposition. The team plans to reassess for discontinuation if renal function stabilises, which is a reasonable approach given the absence of identified genetic variants, though they rightly note that close monitoring for relapse is essential.

## Take-Home Messages

- **Confirm culture negativity before initiating eculizumab in the setting of recent bacteraemia.** Terminal complement is critical for defence against encapsulated organisms, including *Capnocytophaga* and *Neisseria*. Starting complement blockade during active bacteraemia risks uncontrolled infection.

- **Vaccinate early, but do not let incomplete vaccination lead-in delay lifesaving therapy.** Administer meningococcal vaccines (covering serogroups A, C, Y, W and B) as soon as CM-HUS is on the differential. If you cannot wait two weeks, cover with antibiotic prophylaxis—which should continue for the duration of complement blockade regardless.

- **Use plasma exchange as a bridge, not a cure.** PEX may provide temporary stabilisation and can restore diuresis, but sustained haematological and renal recovery in CM-HUS requires terminal complement blockade. Do not be falsely reassured by early PEX response.

- **Normal C3 and C4 do not exclude CM-HUS.** The diagnosis is clinicopathological—confirm MAHA with schistocytes on smear, rule out TTP with ADAMTS13 (>10% effectively excludes it), rule out STEC-HUS with stool PCR, and consider biopsy when safe.

- **Time-to-eculizumab is a major determinant of renal recovery.** Every day of delay trades infectious safety for irreversible renal damage. The framework is: clear the infection, vaccinate, prophylax, bridge with PEX, and initiate eculizumab as soon as infectious risk is acceptable.

- **Think about complement testing timing.** PEX and eculizumab will both confound functional complement assays. Send baseline studies before initiating treatment whenever possible, and interpret post-treatment results with caution.

## The Bottom Line

This case is a reminder that CM-HUS can be triggered by organisms you do not see every day, that it can masquerade as sepsis-related DIC until the haemolysis pattern changes, and that treating it requires you to simultaneously fight an infection and block the very immune pathway that helps fight it. The team's approach—methodical, sequenced, and willing to accept imperfect timing in the service of both safety and renal recovery—is a model for how to walk this tightrope when you find yourself on it. Because eventually, you will.



# Infection-Triggered CM-HUS Without a Genetic Predisposition: Rethinking the 'Second Hit' Model

## The Dog Bite That Broke the Textbook Narrative

A previously healthy woman in her late 40s gets bitten on the upper lip by her own dog. Four days later, she's in septic shock with pancytopenia, DIC, refractory hypoglycaemia, and anuria. Blood cultures grow *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*. You treat the sepsis, the coagulopathy improves, the inflammatory markers settle—and then, just when things should be getting better, her haemoglobin drops from 111 to 68 g/L over six days with no bleeding source, her platelets stay stuck in the 30s, and her LDH rockets upward. She's not relapsing. She's developed something new: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome. But here's what should stop you in your tracks: extended genetic testing found no pathogenic complement variants whatsoever.

This case, published by Fredriksen and colleagues, forces us to confront an uncomfortable question about CM-HUS pathophysiology. The classical teaching is that infection-triggered CM-HUS occurs in genetically predisposed hosts—someone harbouring a variant in complement factor H, I, or B, or membrane cofactor protein, who then experiences a "second hit" from an infection, pregnancy, or other trigger. But this patient had none of that. No significant medical history. Two prior uncomplicated pregnancies. No autoimmune disease. Negative ANA and ANCA. And when the reference laboratory at Lund University ran the extended complement genetic panel, they found nothing.

So what do we do with a patient who develops CM-HUS from an infection without any identifiable genetic substrate? The answer may reshape how we think about triggers, penetrance, and the necessity of a predisposing defect for disease manifestation.

## The 'Second Hit' Model: What We Thought We Knew

The prevailing framework for CM-HUS—historically and somewhat unhelpfully termed "atypical HUS"—rests on two pillars. First, dysregulation of the alternative complement pathway, often due to inherited or acquired defects in complement regulatory proteins. Second, incomplete disease penetrance: many individuals carry pathogenic variants but never develop clinical disease. This incomplete penetrance is what gives the "second hit" concept its weight. The genetic variant creates vulnerability, but an external trigger—classically an infection—is needed to tip the system into uncontrolled complement activation and endothelial destruction.

Between 50% and 70% of CM-HUS patients harbour genetic variants or autoantibodies affecting complement regulation. That's a substantial majority, and it has reinforced the mental model that there's always something lurking in the genome or the immune repertoire. When you diagnose CM-HUS, you send the genetic panel expecting to find a culprit, and often you do. The infection is the match; the genetic predisposition is the dry timber.

But what about the other 30% to 50%? Are those patients simply carrying variants we haven't discovered yet? Is our genetic testing incomplete? Or is the framework itself incomplete?

## When the Infection Is Both Match and Timber

This case suggests that under certain circumstances, the infection may be sufficient on its own—or at least sufficient in combination with transient complement perturbations that don't require a germline variant. *C. canimorsus* is a fascinating organism in this regard. It's an encapsulated gram-negative rod, and its capsule confers variable resistance to complement-mediated killing. Some isolates are complement-sensitive; capsule-expressing strains show partial resistance. This means complement activation is a critical arm of host defence against this pathogen. The complement system is actively engaged in clearing the organism from the bloodstream.

But here's the paradox that the authors highlight so well: the very complement activation that helps clear the pathogen may simultaneously drive uncontrolled endothelial damage and thrombotic microangiopathy. The alternative pathway, once activated, creates a feedback loop of C3b deposition, amplification on cell surfaces, and—if regulation is even transiently insufficient—endothelial injury. You don't necessarily need a genetic defect in factor H or I for this to spiral out of control. You need an organism that activates complement robustly enough, in a host whose regulatory capacity is overwhelmed, even if that regulatory capacity is genetically normal.

The previously reported case by Bjørkto and colleagues described *C. canimorsus*-induced CM-HUS in a patient who harboured a complement factor B variant—a textbook example of the two-hit model. The contrast with this case is striking. Same organism, same clinical syndrome, same therapeutic response to eculizumab—but one patient had a genetic predisposition and the other did not. This doesn't invalidate the two-hit model; it tells us the model is incomplete.

## The Diagnostic Tightrope: When CM-HUS Hides Inside Sepsis

One of the most valuable clinical lessons from this case is how CM-HUS was nearly missed because it was buried inside severe sepsis with DIC. The initial presentation—fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy, AKI, lactic acidosis—was entirely consistent with DIC from bacteraemia. HUS was considered early but reasonably discounted because of the leukopaenia, inflammatory profile, normal haptoglobin, and non-compensatory reticulocytes. That's a sound clinical reasoning process at that stage.

The critical pivot came when the patient's sepsis and coagulopathy were improving but haemolysis suddenly appeared in isolation. The haemoglobin dropped without bleeding. Platelets stayed low. Haptoglobin became undetectable. LDH rose. This was not DIC—that was resolving. This was something new. The peripheral smear showing 15% to 20% schistocytes confirmed microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia, and the diagnostic pathway shifted from haematology to nephrology to complement.

A few diagnostic pearls deserve emphasis. The initial DAT was positive for IgG, which led to a diagnostic detour toward autoimmune haemolytic anaemia and a trial of corticosteroids that predictably failed. This was almost certainly a false positive from penicillin exposure or prior transfusions, and the repeat test was negative. In the context of suspected TMA, a positive DAT should be interpreted with caution and not allowed to derail the workup. Similarly, the PLASMIC score was 4/7—low pretest probability for TTP—which, combined with an ADAMTS13 activity of 43.5%, effectively excluded TTP and kept the diagnostic momentum moving toward CM-HUS.

Perhaps the most important laboratory pearl: basic complement levels (C3 and C4) were normal throughout. Normal complement levels do not exclude CM-HUS. The diagnosis is clinicopathological, built on the constellation of MAHA, thrombocytopenia, AKI, renal biopsy showing TMA, and exclusion of alternative diagnoses (TTP, STEC-HUS). In this case, the alternative pathway activity (AH50) was markedly reduced at 6%, but this was measured after plasma exchange and eculizumab, so it reflected treatment effect rather than intrinsic dysregulation. The message is clear: don't wait for abnormal complement levels to pursue the diagnosis.

## Treatment: Bridge, Block, and Mind the Infection

The therapeutic strategy in this case deserves careful study because it navigates a genuine clinical dilemma. Plasma exchange was initiated as bridging therapy while awaiting ADAMTS13 results and before eculizumab could be started. The authors note that diuresis returned after the second and third PEX sessions but before eculizumab, reflecting the transient stabilisation that PEX can provide in CM-HUS by removing circulating complement fragments and inflammatory mediators. But PEX doesn't correct the underlying complement dysregulation. Sustained haematological and renal recovery occurred only after complement blockade with eculizumab was initiated.

This is a critical distinction from TTP, where PEX is the definitive, lifesaving therapy. In CM-HUS, PEX is a bridge, not a destination. If you're managing a patient with suspected TMA and you're not yet sure whether it's TTP or CM-HUS, starting PEX while awaiting ADAMTS13 is reasonable, but you should not delay eculizumab once TTP is excluded and CM-HUS is confirmed.

The infection risk associated with eculizumab is well known—terminal complement blockade impairs defence against encapsulated organisms, particularly *Neisseria*. The authors handled this carefully by ensuring blood cultures were negative before initiating eculizumab and by administering meningococcal vaccinations (Menveo and Bexsero) before the first dose. Prophylactic penicillin was given because the vaccines had not been administered two weeks prior to complement blockade, which is the minimum interval needed for adequate vaccine response. This is a pragmatic approach worth emulating: in the acutely ill patient where you cannot wait two weeks for vaccine response, antibiotic prophylaxis fills the gap.

## Prognosis and the Question of Maintenance Therapy

The patient achieved haematological remission and partial renal recovery with a serum creatinine stabilising around 175 µmol/L. She continues maintenance eculizumab at 1200 mg IV every two weeks. The authors plan to reassess for potential discontinuation if renal function stabilises, which raises the central question for infection-triggered CM-HUS without genetic variants: what is the relapse risk, and how long should complement blockade continue?

In patients with identified pathogenic complement variants, the relapse risk is substantial and long-term therapy is often recommended with close monitoring. In patients without identified variants, the calculus shifts. If the trigger was a transient infection with no underlying genetic susceptibility, the risk of relapse may be lower—particularly if the triggering infection has been fully cleared and there is no ongoing source of complement activation. However, the absence of identified variants does not guarantee absence of risk. Our genetic testing is not exhaustive; some regulatory variants, epigenetic factors, or transient acquired defects may not be captured by current panels. The authors' plan to reassess for discontinuation is reasonable, but it should be guided by careful monitoring for evidence of recurrent TMA, not by an assumption that "no variant means no risk."

The broader point about prognosis is that time-to-eculizumab is one of the most important determinants of renal recovery. Delayed initiation is associated with incomplete renal recovery and higher risk of progression to end-stage kidney disease. In this case, eculizumab was initiated relatively promptly after the diagnosis was confirmed, and the partial renal recovery is consistent with what the evidence predicts.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **CM-HUS can occur without an identifiable genetic predisposition.** The classical two-hit model—genetic vulnerability plus infection trigger—does not explain all cases. Infection alone, particularly with complement-activating organisms like *C. canimorsus*, may be sufficient in some hosts.
- **Normal C3 and C4 do not exclude CM-HUS.** The diagnosis is clinicopathological: MAHA, thrombocytopenia, AKI, biopsy-confirmed TMA, and exclusion of TTP (ADAMTS13 > 10%) and STEC-HUS (negative Shiga toxin PCR).
- **CM-HUS can be obscured by sepsis and DIC.** Watch for the patient whose sepsis is improving but who develops new isolated haemolysis and persistent thrombocytopaenia—that's not DIC, that's a new process.
- **A positive DAT can be a false positive in TMA.** Penicillin exposure and recent transfusions can cause a positive IgG DAT. If steroids don't work and the smear shows schistocytes, redirect toward TMA.
- **Plasma exchange is a bridge, not a destination, in CM-HUS.** Sustained recovery requires complement blockade with eculizumab. Don't delay eculizumab once TTP is excluded.
- **Eculizumab during or after active bacteraemia requires careful risk management.** Ensure cultures are negative, vaccinate against *Neisseria*, and use antibiotic prophylaxis if vaccination timing is suboptimal.
- **Absence of identified complement variants does not guarantee low relapse risk.** Decisions about discontinuing eculizumab should be individualised and guided by monitoring, not by the assumption that no variant means no risk.
- **Consider unusual infectious triggers in new-onset TMA.** *C. canimorsus* from dog bites is rare but increasingly recognised. A thorough exposure history—including animal bites and scratches—matters.
