Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

Chapter 4: ## Management of Pancreatopleural Fistula

The management of pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) follows a stepwise algorithm that escalates from conservative medical therapy to endoscopic intervention and, ultimately, to surgery if less invasive measures fail. Because PPF is exceedingly rare in children—only 47 pediatric cases have been reported in the literature—no standardized treatment guidelines exist. Instead, management principles are derived from adult data, small case series, and individual case reports, all adapted to the pediatric context. In this case, the child was successfully managed conservatively, but understanding the full treatment ladder is essential for any clinician who may encounter this condition.

### Principles of Conservative Management

The central goal of conservative therapy is to reduce pancreatic exocrine secretion to a minimum, thereby decreasing the volume of fluid traversing the fistulous tract and allowing the tract to close spontaneously. This is achieved through a combination of bowel rest, pharmacologic suppression of pancreatic secretion, pancreatic enzyme replacement, and drainage of the pleural collection. The rationale is mechanistic: a pancreatopleural fistula functions like a leaky pipe, constantly delivering enzyme-rich pancreatic fluid into the pleural cavity. If the output of that pipe can be suppressed, the fistula may seal on its own without the need for invasive procedures. Conservative measures are generally recommended as the initial approach when there is no significant anomaly of the pancreatic duct, and they should be pursued for 2–3 weeks before declaring failure and escalating to endoscopic or surgical therapy.

### Bowel Rest and Total Parenteral Nutrition

Keeping the patient nil per oral (NPO) is a foundational component of conservative management. Any oral intake—particularly fats and proteins—stimulates the exocrine pancreas to secrete digestive enzymes, which would increase flow through the fistula and impair closure. By eliminating enteral stimulation, bowel rest allows the pancreas to enter a quiescent state. Because the child cannot eat, total parenteral nutrition (TPN) is required to meet caloric and nutritional needs during what may be a prolonged treatment course. In this case, the child was placed NPO and started on TPN immediately after the diagnosis was confirmed by MRCP. TPN ensures that the growing child receives adequate macronutrients and micronutrients while the pancreas is rested, preventing the malnutrition that would otherwise complicate weeks of fasting.

### Octreotide: Mechanism, Dosing, and Monitoring

Octreotide is a synthetic somatostatin analogue that plays a key pharmacologic role in conservative management. Somatostatin is a naturally occurring inhibitory peptide that suppresses the release of multiple gastrointestinal hormones and directly inhibits exocrine pancreatic secretion. Octreotide, with its longer half-life compared to native somatostatin, reduces the volume of pancreatic juice and the concentration of digestive enzymes within it, effectively diminishing the driving force behind the fistulous flow. In this case, octreotide was administered as a continuous infusion at a dose of 30 µg/kg/day, which falls within the recommended pediatric range of 10–40 µg/kg/day. Monitoring during octreotide therapy includes tracking the daily output from the intercostal drain, watching for adverse effects such as hyperglycemia, abdominal cramping, and steatorrhea, and ensuring that nutritional status is maintained through TPN. The gradual decrease in drain output over the course of treatment serves as the primary clinical marker of response.

### Pancreatic Enzyme Replacement Therapy

Pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT) was also initiated in this case. At first glance, administering pancreatic enzymes to a patient whose problem is pancreatic enzyme leakage may seem counterintuitive. However, the rationale is twofold. First, when the pancreas is not delivering enzymes to the intestinal lumen because the secretions are being diverted through the fistula, the patient may develop pancreatic exocrine insufficiency with malabsorption and steatorrhea. PERT supplies these enzymes enterally to maintain digestive function. Second, enteral pancreatic enzymes can provide negative feedback inhibition of pancreatic exocrine secretion through the cholecystokinin-mediated pathway, further reducing the stimulus for pancreatic output. PERT is therefore a complementary component of the conservative regimen rather than a contradictory one.

### Thoracic Drainage

Although the primary pathology is in the pancreas, the immediate threat to the patient is respiratory compromise from the massive pleural effusion. In this case, the child presented three times with right-sided pleural effusions causing tachypnea, chest pain, and hypoxemia, each time requiring drainage of approximately one liter of dark red fluid. After two episodes of therapeutic thoracocentesis, an intercostal drainage (ICD) tube was inserted on the third admission. The ICD serves two purposes: it relieves the respiratory distress by evacuating the pleural collection, and it provides a measurable output that allows the clinicians to track the fistula's activity over time. As the conservative measures reduce pancreatic secretion, the daily ICD output should progressively diminish, which is exactly what happened in this case—drainage decreased over 10 days and eventually stopped, at which point the ICD was removed.

### Timeline and Expected Response

Conservative management of PPF requires patience. The expected timeline for response is 2–3 weeks of sustained therapy before the fistula closes. During this period, the patient remains NPO on TPN with continuous octreotide infusion and ICD in place. The clinical team monitors daily drain output, serial imaging as needed, and nutritional status. In this case, the response was relatively prompt—drainage ceased within 10 days, allowing ICD removal and discharge. This favorable outcome is not universal, however, and the clinician must be prepared to escalate if output persists beyond the expected window.

### Success Rate of Conservative Management in Pediatric PPF

It is important to set realistic expectations. The reported success rate of conservative management alone in pediatric PPF is only approximately 12%. This low rate means that the majority of children with PPF will ultimately require either endoscopic or surgical intervention to achieve fistula closure. Nevertheless, because conservative therapy is noninvasive and carries minimal morbidity, it remains the appropriate first step unless there is a clear ductal anomaly that would predict failure. In this case, conservative management succeeded despite the low overall success rate, likely because the fistula was related to acute traumatic pancreatitis rather than chronic ductal obstruction or hereditary pancreatitis with structural ductal disease.

### Endoscopic Intervention: ERCP and Transpapillary Stenting

When conservative management fails after 2–3 weeks, endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) with transpapillary stenting is the next step. ERCP serves both diagnostic and therapeutic functions: it allows direct visualization of the pancreatic duct anatomy, identification of the fistula site and any ductal strictures, and placement of a stent across the region of duct disruption or stenosis. The stent works by reducing intraductal pressure and rerouting pancreatic secretions through the papilla rather than through the fistulous tract, allowing the fistula to heal. Endoscopic intervention can also include balloon dilatation of ductal strictures if present. In pediatric cases, ERCP has a fistula visualization rate of 38–78% and a reported success rate of 100% in published series, with long-term success exceeding 90% in mixed-age cohorts. Stents typically remain in place for 4 weeks to 6 months. Because of its minimal morbidity and mortality, ERCP is preferred over surgery when stent placement is feasible—particularly when ductal stenosis is identified, as in the MRCP findings of this case, which showed narrowing of the pancreatic duct proximal to the fistula communication.

### Surgical Options

Surgery is reserved for cases where endoscopic stent placement is not possible—such as severe ductal obstruction, a distally located fistula, or failure of both conservative and endoscopic management after 3–4 weeks. The surgical options include distal pancreatectomy, which removes the segment of pancreas containing the fistula; the Puestow procedure (pancreatojejunostomy), which decompresses the pancreatic duct by draining it into a Roux-en-Y loop of jejunum; and cystojejunostomy, which internalizes a pseudocyst into the bowel lumen. In the pediatric literature, surgery has demonstrated a 93% success rate across 15 reported cases, with operative mortality below 5% and a recurrence rate of approximately 11%. While these outcomes are favorable, surgery carries greater morbidity than endoscopic approaches and is appropriately held in reserve as a last resort.

### Stepwise Treatment Algorithm

The treatment algorithm for pediatric PPF can be summarized as follows. First, establish the diagnosis with MRCP and pleural fluid amylase/lipase levels. Begin conservative management immediately: NPO, TPN, octreotide infusion (10–40 µg/kg/day), PERT, and ICD placement for thoracic drainage. Monitor daily drain output for 2–3 weeks. If the fistula closes and output ceases, remove the ICD and discharge with follow-up. If output persists, proceed to ERCP for ductal evaluation and transpapillary stenting, particularly if ductal stenosis or disruption is identified. If stent placement is not feasible or if the fistula persists despite stenting for 3–4 weeks, proceed to surgery—distal pancreatectomy, Puestow procedure, or cystojejunostomy depending on the ductal anatomy and fistula location.

### Why Conservative Management Succeeded in This Case

Several factors likely contributed to the success of conservative management in this child. The underlying cause was blunt abdominal trauma rather than chronic pancreatitis or hereditary ductal disease, meaning the ductal disruption was acute and potentially self-limited once pancreatic secretion was suppressed. The child had no evidence of chronic ductal obstruction, calcification, or pseudocyst that would sustain fistula flow. Prompt initiation of the full conservative regimen—NPO, TPN, octreotide, and PERT—immediately after MRCP confirmation likely minimized the duration of active fistula flow. The MRCP did reveal narrowing of the pancreatic duct proximal to the fistula communication, which in other cases might have prompted early ERCP, but the rapid decline in drain output within 10 days indicated that the fistula was closing spontaneously, obviating the need for endoscopic intervention. This case illustrates that while conservative management has a low overall success rate in pediatric PPF, it can be effective in carefully selected patients—particularly those with acute, post-traumatic fistulas—and its initial trial is justified before escalating to more invasive interventions.
