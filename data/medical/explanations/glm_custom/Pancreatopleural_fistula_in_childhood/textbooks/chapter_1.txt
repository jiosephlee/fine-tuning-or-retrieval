Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and Initial Evaluation of Recurrent Pleural Effusion

### Patient Demographics, Presenting Symptoms, and Vital Signs

The case involves a boy in late childhood who presented to the outpatient department accompanied by his mother with a one-week history of progressively worsening difficulty in breathing and right-sided chest pain. The child's body mass index was 35, calculated from a weight of 44 kg and height of 112 cm, placing him in the obese category for his age. This metabolic background is relevant because obesity can predispose to pancreatic pathology and may also accentuate respiratory symptoms by reducing chest wall compliance. On initial evaluation, his respiratory rate was 20 breaths per minute with mild tachypnoea, his heart rate was 110 beats per minute, and his oxygen saturation was 88%–89% on room air. These vital signs indicated significant respiratory compromise and hypoxia, necessitating admission to the pediatric intensive care unit and supplemental oxygen at 5 L/min via mask. The combination of hypoxia, tachycardia, and tachypnoea in a child with a unilateral chest finding immediately raised concern for a large pleural effusion compromising respiratory function.

### Physical Examination Findings

The respiratory system examination revealed a constellation of classic physical signs consistent with a large right-sided pleural effusion. Chest wall movements were reduced on the right side. Percussion over the right subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary regions produced a stony dull note, a hallmark of fluid accumulation in the pleural space. Vocal fremitus was significantly weaker on the right compared with the left, and breath sounds were decreased in the same regions. These findings together — reduced movement, stony dullness, diminished fremitus, and decreased breath sounds — form the traditional physical diagnostic triad for pleural effusion. The pathophysiology is straightforward: fluid in the pleural space dampens the transmission of sound and vibration from the lung to the chest wall, while simultaneously restricting lung expansion on the affected side.

Importantly, the abdominal examination was unremarkable. The abdomen was soft with no hepatosplenomegaly, and there was no tenderness or guarding. There was no pallor, cyanosis, icterus, clubbing, or lymphadenopathy. The absence of abdominal findings at this stage was clinically misleading, as it provided no clue to the pancreatic origin of the problem. The cardiovascular examination revealed only tachycardia without murmurs. The child was conscious and oriented. The absence of jaundice or abdominal tenderness was particularly significant in retrospect, because it meant there was no clinical pointer toward a hepatobiliary or pancreatic process at the bedside, allowing the diagnostic focus to remain entirely on the chest.

### Initial Investigations: Chest X-ray and Bedside Lung Ultrasound

A chest X-ray was ordered immediately and revealed a right-sided pleural effusion with blunting of the right costophrenic angle. Blunting of the costophrenic angle is the earliest radiographic sign of pleural effusion and typically appears when approximately 200–500 mL of fluid has accumulated in an adult; in children, smaller volumes may produce this sign. The X-ray confirmed what the physical examination had already suggested — a significant unilateral pleural collection.

Bedside lung ultrasound was then performed and demonstrated a pleural effusion of approximately one litre. Ultrasound is now recognized as a superior bedside tool for estimating pleural fluid volume and characterizing its nature, as it can distinguish between anechoic simple fluid and complex or septated collections. The ability to perform this rapidly at the bedside in a critically ill child makes it invaluable for guiding thoracocentesis. The large volume of approximately one litre in a child was remarkable and indicated that the underlying process was producing fluid at an unusually high rate, a feature that should have prompted early consideration of an uncommon cause. In addition, empirical intravenous antibiotics (piperacillin-tazobactam) were started after sending blood cultures, reflecting the clinical team's initial assumption that an infectious process — possibly bacterial pneumonia with parapneumonic effusion or empyema — was responsible.

### First Admission: Thoracocentesis, Pleural Fluid Appearance, and Temporary Improvement

Right-sided pleural aspiration was performed under aseptic precautions, and approximately one litre of dark red pleural fluid was drained. The appearance of the fluid was a critical observation. A dark red pleural effusion — sometimes called a hemorrhagic pleural effusion — raises a broad differential diagnosis that includes malignancy, tuberculosis, pulmonary embolism with infarction, trauma, and pancreaticopleural fistula. The pathophysiology of the dark red appearance in this case was related to the enzymatic action of pancreatic juices on pleural tissues: pancreatic enzymes leaking into the pleural space cause irritation, inflammation, and breakdown of small blood vessels, producing a serosanguineous or hemorrhagic appearance. This mechanism is distinct from true hemothorax, in which frank blood enters the pleural space from a vascular source.

Following thoracocentesis, the child's tachypnoea resolved, and his oxygen saturation improved to 95%–98% on room air. His chest pain improved, and he was weaned off oxygen support within 24 hours. He was shifted to the ward and discharged. This dramatic but temporary improvement after drainage is a characteristic pattern in recurrent pleural effusions: drainage relieves the mechanical compromise of lung expansion and improves gas exchange, but if the underlying cause of fluid production persists, the effusion will reaccumulate. The failure to identify the underlying cause at this first admission set the stage for recurrence.

### Second Admission: Recurrence, Elevated ADA, and Empirical Antitubercular Therapy

The child returned after six days with the same complaints of right-sided chest pain, tachypnoea, and difficulty breathing. Chest X-ray again confirmed a right-sided pleural effusion, and thoracocentesis was repeated, again draining approximately one litre of dark red fluid. At this point, the pleural fluid analysis revealed a mildly elevated adenosine deaminase (ADA) level. ADA is an enzyme involved in purine metabolism, and its elevation in pleural fluid is most strongly associated with tuberculous pleuritis. In regions where tuberculosis is endemic, an elevated ADA in the setting of a lymphocytic exudative pleural effusion has a high positive predictive value for tuberculosis.

The child also had a history of household contact with tuberculosis — his grandfather had been treated for pulmonary tuberculosis. The combination of a mildly elevated ADA and a known Koch's contact led the treating team to start empirical antitubercular therapy. This decision illustrates a common clinical pitfall: anchoring on a plausible but ultimately incorrect diagnosis when faced with incomplete data. In retrospect, the mildly elevated ADA was a nonspecific finding. ADA can be elevated in any inflammatory or infectious pleural process involving lymphocyte activation, including empyema, malignancy, and autoimmune pleuritis. A GeneXpert test for Mycobacterium tuberculosis was ultimately negative, and the child had no history of fever, chronic cough, or weight loss — features that would have been expected in genuine tuberculous pleural effusion. The dark red color of the fluid was also atypical for tuberculosis, which more commonly produces a straw-colored or serosanguineous exudate rather than dark red fluid.

### Third Admission: Recurrent Effusion, Intercostal Drainage, and the Critical History of Abdominal Trauma

Five days after the second discharge, the child was admitted for the third time with identical symptoms. The pattern of rapid reaccumulation after each drainage — approximately one litre each time within days — was itself an important diagnostic clue. Simple infectious effusions do not typically reaccumulate at this rate once drained, nor do tuberculous effusions re-form so quickly. This pattern pointed toward a continuous active source of fluid production.

Before inserting an intercostal drainage (ICD) tube this time, a contrast-enhanced CT of the chest was ordered. This decision marked the critical turning point in the case. The CT revealed patchy areas of airspace consolidation in the right lung, a right-sided hydropneumothorax, and — most importantly — an elongated, lobulated, multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right pleural space through the right paraesophageal region to the upper left retroperitoneum, terminating at the tail of the pancreas. This tracking collection was the radiographic fingerprint of a fistulous connection between the pancreas and the pleural space.

In parallel, the clinical team revisited the history with the child during this third admission, and the boy revealed that he had suffered blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle one month earlier. This was the missing piece. Blunt abdominal trauma — particularly from handlebar injuries — is a well-recognized cause of pancreatic injury in children. The handlebar compresses the pancreas against the vertebral column, typically injuring the pancreatic body or tail. The injury may initially be occult, with no immediate abdominal symptoms, but can lead to post-traumatic pancreatitis, pseudocyst formation, and ultimately ductal disruption. In this case, the timeline of approximately one month between the trauma and the onset of respiratory symptoms is consistent with the gradual development of a pancreatic pseudocyst or ductal leak that eventually eroded superiorly through the retroperitoneum and diaphragmatic hiatus into the pleural space.

An intercostal drain was inserted and again drained approximately one litre of dark red fluid. With the CT findings, the history of abdominal trauma, and the characteristic dark red pleural aspirate now converging, the diagnosis of a pancreatopleural fistula was strongly suspected. The clinical team then proceeded to confirm this with magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) and by sending serum and pleural fluid amylase and lipase levels, which revealed markedly elevated pleural fluid amylase (6118 U/L) and lipase (7211 U/L). Antitubercular drugs were stopped.

### The Concept of the "Pulmonary Mask" and Diagnostic Delay

This case powerfully illustrates what the authors describe as a "pulmonary mask" — a situation in which the presenting symptoms point so convincingly toward a thoracic process that the clinician's entire diagnostic effort is directed at the chest, while the actual pathology resides in the abdomen. The child had chest pain, respiratory distress, hypoxia, physical signs of pleural effusion, and abnormal chest imaging. There were no abdominal symptoms, no abdominal findings on examination, and no initial historical clue to pancreatic disease. The natural and understandable response to this presentation is to pursue pulmonary diagnoses — parapneumonic effusion, empyema, tuberculosis, malignancy — which is exactly what happened.

The concept of a pulmonary mask is not unique to pancreatopleural fistula. It reflects a broader clinical principle: when a pleural effusion is recurrent, large, and atypical in appearance or behavior, the clinician must broaden the differential beyond primary thoracic causes and systematically consider distant sources of fluid. In this case, several features should have triggered earlier suspicion: the unusually large volume of fluid for a child, the rapidity of reaccumulation after drainage, the consistently dark red appearance, and the lack of systemic symptoms such as fever or weight loss that would be expected with infection or malignancy. The mildly elevated ADA, rather than being a definitive pointer to tuberculosis, should have been interpreted in the context of these atypical features.

The diagnostic delay in this case — spanning three admissions over approximately two weeks — is characteristic of pancreatopleural fistula. In the published literature, PPF is consistently described as a diagnosis of delayed recognition. The majority of reported cases underwent extensive pulmonary workup before the pancreatic origin was suspected, with some patients subjected to multiple thoracocenteses, prolonged empirical antibiotic or antitubercular therapy, and even invasive thoracic procedures before the true cause was identified. The lesson from this case is that a recurrent pleural effusion that does not behave as expected — whether because of rapid reaccumulation, atypical fluid appearance, failure to respond to targeted therapy, or absence of expected systemic features — demands a deliberate widening of the diagnostic lens to include abdominal pathology, particularly the pancreas.
