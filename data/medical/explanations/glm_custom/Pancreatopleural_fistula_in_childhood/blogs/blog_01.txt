Title: Blog about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

# The Pulmonary Mask: When the Pancreas Hides Behind the Pleura

## The Setup

A child presents with recurrent right-sided pleural effusions. Each time you drain it, you get a liter of dark red fluid. He improves temporarily, then bounces back days later with the same complaint. You've got a positive TB contact history and a mildly elevated pleural fluid ADA — so you start antitubercular therapy. But the effusion keeps coming back. After the third admission, someone finally sits down with the kid and asks the right question. Turns out, he took a bicycle handle to the abdomen a month ago. The pancreas has been quietly leaking into his chest the entire time.

This is pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) — and it is one of the great imposters in pediatric medicine.

## What Makes This Case Dangerous

The temptation in a case like this is to stay locked into a thoracic differential. The patient has chest pain, respiratory distress, and a unilateral pleural effusion — everything points you toward the lungs. But the pathology is in the abdomen, and unless you ask the right question and send the right test, you can spiral into unnecessary procedures, unnecessary medications, and unnecessary delay.

In this case, the child was started on empiric antitubercular therapy based on a contact history and a mildly elevated ADA. It wasn't until the third admission that a careful re-history revealed blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle one month prior. A CT chest showed a multiloculated collection tracking from the right paraesophageal region to the tail of the pancreas. MRCP confirmed a fistulous tract from the pancreatic duct, through the retroperitoneum, through the esophageal hiatus, and into the right pleural cavity. Pleural fluid amylase was 6118 U/L and lipase was 7211 U/L. The antitubercular drugs were stopped. The actual disease was found.

## The Anatomic Mechanism: How Pancreas Gets Into the Chest

This is where the anatomy matters enormously. When the pancreatic duct or a pseudocyst ruptures anteriorly, pancreatic fluid leaks into the peritoneal cavity and you get ascites. But when the rupture occurs posteriorly — as it did in this child — pancreatic fluid enters the retroperitoneum and follows the path of least resistance superiorly. It tracks upward through natural diaphragmatic openings, most commonly the esophageal or aortic hiatus, enters the mediastinum, and then ruptures through the pleura to create a pleural effusion.

Think of it as a leaky pipe. The pancreas is the source, the retroperitoneum is the conduit, and the pleural cavity is the sink. The fluid travels a remarkable distance to get there, which is precisely why the clinical presentation is so misleading. The CT in this case captured this beautifully — an elongated, lobulated, multiloculated hypodense collection extending from the right pleural space, through the right paraesophageal region, to the upper retroperitoneum and the pancreatic tail.

## The Pulmonary Mask: Why Everyone Gets Fooled

The data is striking. In a literature review of PPF cases, 68% presented with predominant respiratory symptoms — massive recurrent unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, dyspnea, and cough. Fewer than 20% had abdominal symptoms like epigastric pain, vomiting, or weight loss. About 24% had constitutional symptoms like fever. In adults, the pattern is the same — Uchiyama et al.'s landmark review of 113 Japanese cases found chest symptoms predominated in 68% of patients.

This makes sense anatomically. The fistula drains cephalad, away from the abdomen. The pancreas may be injured, but the symptoms manifest where the fluid accumulates — in the chest. The child in this case had no abdominal pain, no vomiting, no epigastric tenderness. He had tachypnea, hypoxia to 88–89% on room air, decreased breath sounds, and stony dullness on the right. Everything about the presentation said "pleural disease." Nothing about it said "pancreas."

## When to Suspect PPF

The key clinical trigger is recurrence. A pleural effusion that reforms rapidly after drainage — especially a unilateral hemorrhagic one — should make you pause and think beyond the chest. In this case, the child was aspirated three times, each time yielding approximately one liter of dark red fluid, with symptom recurrence within 5–6 days.

The specific diagnostic anchor is pleural fluid amylase. A pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L is considered pathognomonic for PPF. In this case, it was 6118 U/L with a lipase of 7211 U/L. But here is the nuance — other conditions can also produce amylase-rich pleural fluid, including esophageal perforation, malignancy (lung, pancreatic, or metastatic), and even tuberculosis. What distinguishes PPF is that the amylase is pancreatic-type, whereas salivary-type amylase elevation is seen in malignancy and esophageal rupture. If your lab can perform amylase isoenzyme typing, it can help you narrow the differential before committing to advanced imaging.

Serum amylase and lipase may also be elevated, as they were in this case, but they are not always reliable — the fistula can drain directly into the pleural space without significant systemic elevation.

## Imaging: What to Order and What It Shows

MRCP is the diagnostic modality of choice for suspected PPF, offering 70–80% sensitivity for visualizing the fistulous tract and pancreatic duct anatomy. In this case, MRCP demonstrated the tubular cystic collection in the distal pancreatic body and tail, tracking posteriorly through the retroperitoneum and superiorly through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity, with a narrow communication to the left pleural cavity as well. It also showed communication with the distal pancreatic duct and proximal ductal narrowing.

CT is useful as an initial investigation and may show the collection tracking from the pleura toward the pancreas, but it only depicts the actual fistula in 33–47% of cases. ERCP remains the gold standard for therapeutic planning, with a fistula visualization rate of 38–78% in children — but it is invasive and typically reserved for cases where intervention is planned, not just diagnosis.

## Management: A Stepwise Approach

PPF lacks standardized treatment guidelines in pediatrics — only 47 cases have been reported in the pediatric literature. Management is stratified based on whether there is ductal obstruction.

Conservative therapy is first-line when there is no pancreatic duct anomaly. This includes bowel rest (nil per oral), total parenteral nutrition, somatostatin analogues (octreotide at 10–40 µg/kg/day, this child received 30 µg/kg/day), pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy, and thoracic drainage via intercostal tube. The rationale is straightforward: octreotide suppresses exocrine pancreatic secretion, bowel rest reduces pancreatic stimulation, and the fistula is given time to close. However, the success rate of conservative management in pediatric PPF is only about 12%.

If conservative therapy fails after 2–3 weeks, endoscopic intervention via ERCP is the next step. Transpapillary stenting reduces intraductal pressure and allows the fistula to close, typically within 2–3 weeks. ERCP has shown a 100% success rate in reported pediatric cases, with long-term success exceeding 90% in mixed-age cohorts.

Surgery is reserved for cases where stent placement is not possible due to severe duct obstruction or distally located fistulas, or when there is no response to medical or endoscopic management over 3–4 weeks. Options include distal pancreatectomy, pancreatojejunostomy (Puestow procedure), or cystojejunostomy. Surgery carries a 93% success rate in the 15 reported pediatric cases, with operative mortality under 5% and recurrence around 11%.

In this child, conservative management worked. Over 10 days of NPO, TPN, octreotide, and PERT, drainage from the intercostal tube gradually decreased and stopped. The tube was removed and the child was discharged with no recurrent effusions on follow-up.

## Clinical Pearls

- **Always think pancreas in recurrent unilateral hemorrhagic pleural effusion.** The fluid is dark red because it is hemorrhagic and enzyme-rich — not because of infection or malignancy until proven otherwise.
- **Send pleural fluid amylase and lipase early.** A pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L is pathognomonic for PPF. If you do not send it, you cannot make the diagnosis.
- **Take a trauma history repeatedly.** The child in this case concealed his bicycle injury initially. Pediatric histories are unreliable, especially when bullying or embarrassment is involved. Ask again. Ask differently.
- **Do not be anchored by TB contact history and mildly elevated ADA.** This child was started on antitubercular therapy before the diagnosis was reconsidered. A mildly elevated ADA in a hemorrhagic effusion is not diagnostic of tuberculosis.
- **MRCP over CT for fistula visualization.** CT may show the collection tracking toward the pancreas, but MRCP is the modality that defines the fistulous anatomy and ductal anatomy you need for management decisions.
- **Posterior rupture goes up, anterior rupture goes down.** If the pancreatic duct ruptures posteriorly, fluid tracks retroperitoneally through the hiatus and presents as a pleural effusion. If it ruptures anteriorly, you get ascites. This anatomic distinction explains the entire clinical presentation.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- Pancreatopleural fistula is rare in children (only 47 reported cases) but should be suspected in any child with recurrent unilateral pleural effusion of unclear etiology, especially with a history of abdominal trauma or pancreatitis.
- Over 68% of PPF cases present with predominant respiratory symptoms and fewer than 20% with abdominal symptoms — the pancreas hides behind the pleura.
- Pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L is pathognomonic; always send it in recurrent or hemorrhagic effusions.
- MRCP (70–80% sensitivity) is the diagnostic modality of choice; CT depicts the fistula in only 33–47% of cases.
- Conservative management (NPO, TPN, octreotide, PERT, chest drainage) is first-line but succeeds in only ~12% of pediatric cases. ERCP with stenting has a 100% success rate in reported pediatric cases and should be considered early if conservative therapy fails.
- Ask about abdominal trauma repeatedly in children — the history may not surface on the first encounter.

## The Bottom Line

PPF is a disease that wears a pulmonary mask. The child presents to you with respiratory distress, chest pain, and a pleural effusion — and you will be tempted to look only at the chest. But if the effusion keeps coming back, if the fluid is dark red, and if the ADA is only mildly elevated in the setting of a TB contact, stop and ask: could this be the pancreas? Send the amylase. Get the MRCP. And ask about trauma — again.
