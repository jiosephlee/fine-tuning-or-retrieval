Title: Blog about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

# Pediatric PPF: Why Children Are a Different Animal Than Adults

## The Case That Almost Went Down the Tuberculosis Rabbit Hole

A boy in late childhood — BMI of 35, frequent consumer of processed foods — shows up to the outpatient department with a week of progressive dyspnea, right-sided chest pain, and oxygen saturations of 88–89% on room air. Exam reveals classic right-sided pleural effusion findings: stony dullness, reduced fremitus, diminished breath sounds. Bedside ultrasound shows roughly a liter of fluid. You tap it. Dark red pleural fluid. One liter. He improves, goes home, and comes back six days later with the exact same problem. You tap it again. Another liter of dark red fluid. This time the ADA is mildly elevated and there's a known TB contact in the family — so empiric antitubercular therapy gets started. Five days later, he's back a third time.

It wasn't until the third admission that someone went back and took a really careful history. The boy recalled blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handlebar one month earlier. A CT chest showed a multiloculated hypodense collection tracking from the right paraesophageal region all the way to the tail of the pancreas. Pleural fluid amylase came back at 6118 U/L and lipase at 7211 U/L. MRCP confirmed bilateral pancreatopleural fistulas — right greater than left — with a pancreatic duct communication. This was post-traumatic pancreatopleural fistula (PPF), and the antitubercular drugs were stopped immediately.

What makes this case so instructive is not just the diagnosis itself — it's that it illustrates perfectly why pediatric PPF is a completely different clinical animal than what most of us learned about in training, where the entity is almost exclusively discussed in the context of adults with chronic alcoholic pancreatitis.

## The Adult Paradigm Doesn't Translate

If you read the adult literature on PPF, the story is remarkably consistent. The landmark single-country review by Uchiyama et al documented 113 cases from the Japanese literature alone, and a striking 99% were related to chronic alcoholic pancreatitis. The adult patient who walks in with a massive recurrent pleural effusion and a history of chronic pancreatitis is a pattern recognition success — the diagnosis may be delayed, but the pancreas is at least on the differential.

In children, the etiologic landscape is almost entirely different. Genetic mutations — PRSS1, SPINK1, CFTR, and CTRC — are the dominant drivers of chronic pancreatitis leading to PPF. SPINK1 and PRSS1 mutations alone account for up to 30% of pediatric chronic pancreatitis cases. The representative mutations are R122H and N291 in exon 3 and exon 2 of the PRSS1 gene, respectively. Blunt abdominal trauma is the other major non-hereditary trigger, comprising 32% of the 25-patient cohort analyzed by Yang et al. Idiopathic cases continue to appear, likely reflecting under-recognized genetic or ductal variants that haven't been characterized yet.

This etiologic divergence matters enormously at the bedside. You're not going to get the diagnosis of pediatric PPF by asking about alcohol use. You need to be asking about family history of pancreatitis, recurrent abdominal pain, and — as this case powerfully demonstrates — seemingly minor blunt abdominal trauma that the child may not even volunteer unless you ask directly and repeatedly.

## Why Pediatric PPF Hides Behind a Pulmonary Mask

The numbers tell the story of why this diagnosis is so easy to miss. Only 47 pediatric cases of PPF have been reported in the entire literature, compared with over 300 in adults. In those pediatric reports, 68% of cases presented with predominant respiratory symptoms — massive recurrent unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions, dyspnea, cough. Less than 20% had abdominal symptoms. About 24% had constitutional symptoms like fever.

Think about what that means for the clinician. A child comes in with respiratory distress and a pleural effusion. You're going to work up TB, empyema, malignancy, para-pneumonic effusion. You're going to send pleural fluid for cell count, culture, cytology, ADA. You are probably not going to send amylase and lipase on that fluid unless something tips you off. And in children, there's often nothing abdominal in the history to tip you off — the abdominal symptoms that would prompt a pancreatic workup are absent in over 80% of cases.

The pathophysiology explains the clinical presentation. When the pancreatic duct or a pseudocyst ruptures posteriorly, pancreatic fluid follows the path of least resistance into the retroperitoneum, tracks superiorly through the aortic or esophageal hiatus, and breaks through the pleura. The pancreas is essentially a leaky pipe diverting its secretions into the chest. If the rupture is anterior, you get ascites. If it's posterior, you get a pleural effusion — and the pleural effusion is what the patient presents with.

## When to Chase the Pancreas in a Child With a Pleural Effusion

The diagnostic threshold that should change your practice is straightforward: pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L is considered pathognomonic for PPF. In this case, the pleural fluid amylase was 6118 U/L. But the key is that you have to send it. If you have a child with a recurrent pleural effusion — especially a hemorrhagic or dark-colored effusion — and the standard infectious and malignant workup is not yielding an answer, pleural fluid amylase and lipase need to be on your panel.

There's a nuance here that matters. Other conditions can give you elevated pleural fluid amylase — esophageal perforation (Boerhaave syndrome), malignancy (lung, leukemia, lymphoma, metastatic disease). But PPF produces pancreatic-type amylase, while these other conditions produce salivary-type amylase. Pleural fluid amylase isoenzyme typing can distinguish PPF from its mimics if the diagnosis is unclear.

Imaging follows a specific hierarchy. MRCP offers 70–80% sensitivity for visualizing the fistula and duct anatomy. CT is excellent for showing pancreatic calcifications, pseudocysts, and ductal dilation, but only depicts the actual fistulous tract in 33–47% of cases. ERCP remains the gold standard for therapeutic planning, with fistula visualization rates in children ranging from 38–78%.

## Genetic Testing: Who, When, and Why

The case report doesn't mention whether this child underwent genetic testing — and given that his PPF was clearly post-traumatic, that may be entirely appropriate. But the broader pediatric pancreatitis literature makes a strong case for genetic workup in specific scenarios. If a child presents with recurrent pancreatitis, features of chronic pancreatitis, or a family history of pancreatitis, the yield of genetic testing is substantial. PRSS1 mutations cause hereditary pancreatitis with autosomal dominant inheritance, so a thorough family history across multiple generations is essential. SPINK1 mutations, CFTR mutations, and CTRC mutations each contribute to the genetic architecture of pediatric pancreatitis in different ways. In any child with unexplained or recurrent pancreatitis — and certainly in any child who develops PPF without a clear traumatic trigger — genetic testing and referral to pediatric gastroenterology or medical genetics is appropriate.

## Treatment: Conservative First, But Know the Numbers

PPF lacks standardized treatment guidelines in children due to its rarity, so management is built from case reports and small series. The conservative approach — bowel rest, total parenteral nutrition, somatostatin analogues (octreotide at 10–40 µg/kg/day), pancreatic enzyme replacement, and thoracic drainage — is the recommended initial strategy when there's no major pancreatic duct anomaly. The mechanism is logical: octreotide suppresses exocrine pancreatic function, bowel rest reduces pancreatic stimulation, and the fistulous tract is given the opportunity to close.

But here's the number that should temper your enthusiasm for prolonged conservative management: the success rate of conservative methods in pediatric PPF is only about 12%. In this case, it worked — drainage decreased over 10 days, the chest tube came out, and the child remained effusion-free at follow-up. But you should not be surprised when conservative management fails, and you should escalate promptly.

Endoscopic intervention via ERCP — transpapillary stenting, balloon dilatation for ductal stenosis — is the next step. Stent insertion reduces intraductal pressure and leakage, and endoscopic intervention generally closes the fistula within 2–3 weeks. ERCP had a 100% success rate in the reported pediatric cases, with a long-term success rate exceeding 90% in mixed-age cohorts. If stent placement isn't feasible due to severe duct obstruction or a distally located fistula, or if there's no response to medical management after 3–4 weeks, surgical options include distal pancreatectomy, pancreatojejunostomy (Puestow procedure), or cystojejunostomy, with a reported 93% success rate and operative mortality under 5%.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Pediatric PPF is rare but real.** Only 47 cases reported in children. The adult paradigm of alcoholic chronic pancreatitis does not apply — think genetic mutations (PRSS1, SPINK1, CFTR, CTRC) and blunt abdominal trauma instead.
- **PPF wears a pulmonary mask.** Over two-thirds of pediatric cases present with respiratory symptoms and fewer than 20% have abdominal symptoms. The pancreas is the pathology, but the chest is the presentation.
- **Send pleural fluid amylase and lipase on any recurrent or unexplained pleural effusion in a child.** Pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L is pathognomonic. If the standard infectious and malignant workup is negative, this is the test that unlocks the diagnosis.
- **Ask about abdominal trauma — and ask again.** In this case, the history of bicycle handlebar trauma only emerged on the third admission after repeated questioning. Children may not volunteer this information, particularly if they were concealing it for social reasons.
- **Pursue genetic testing in children with recurrent or chronic pancreatitis, especially with a positive family history.** PRSS1, SPINK1, CFTR, and CTRC mutations account for a significant proportion of pediatric chronic pancreatitis.
- **Start conservative management but know that it fails in the majority of pediatric cases.** Octreotide, bowel rest, TPN, and thoracic drainage have only a ~12% success rate in children. Move to ERCP with stenting early if conservative management isn't working — pediatric ERCP success rates approach 100%.
