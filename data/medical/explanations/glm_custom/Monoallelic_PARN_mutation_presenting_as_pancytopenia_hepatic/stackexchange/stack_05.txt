Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Clinical Pearls] Hepatic fibrosis and cirrhosis are increasingly recognized as manifestations of telomere biology disorders, yet they remain under-diagnosed. What evidence from registries and prior case reports supports hepatic fibrosis as part of the TBD phenotype, and what proportion of patients with pathogenic TERT or TERC mutations show significant hepatic fibrosis? Why might hepatic involvement be overlooked in patients with TBDs?

## Clinical Teaching Answer

Hepatic fibrosis and cirrhosis are now recognized as integral manifestations of telomere biology disorders (TBDs), though they remain underdiagnosed in clinical practice.

**Evidence from registries and case reports.** The strongest systematic evidence comes from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry, which used transient elastography to screen adult patients with pathogenic *TERT* or *TERC* mutations. In that cohort, **88.8% of patients had at least moderate hepatic fibrosis, and 33.3% had established cirrhosis**. This is a strikingly high prevalence that firmly establishes hepatic fibrosis as part of the TBD phenotype rather than an incidental finding. Additional case reports reinforce this association: one described a patient with pancytopenia and hepatic fibrosis who carried monoallelic variants in both *PARN* (c.1613C>G) and *TERT* (c.2476G>A). The present case extends this spectrum further by demonstrating hepatic fibrosis—confirmed by transient elastography showing a liver stiffness of 18.1 kPa, consistent with cirrhosis—in a patient with a monoallelic *PARN* variant alone, even in the absence of a concurrent *TERT* or *TERC* mutation. Collectively, these observations support the concept that impaired telomere maintenance, whether through telomerase core complex genes (*TERT*, *TERC*) or telomerase regulatory genes such as *PARN*, can produce clinically significant hepatic fibrosis.

**Why hepatic involvement is overlooked.** Several factors contribute. First, patients with TBDs frequently lack the classical mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita—oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation—especially when they present in adulthood. As in the present case, the patient had no mucocutaneous features, and suspicion for a genetic disorder arose only after multiple organ systems were found to be involved simultaneously. Second, hepatic fibrosis in TBDs is clinically silent until advanced; it may be discovered incidentally or attributed to more common etiologies. In this case, the initial hepatic evaluation focused on autoimmune hepatitis and viral hepatitis, both of which were excluded, before a syndromic cause was considered. Third, because TBDs are rare and exhibit variable penetrance, incomplete expression, and organ-specific predominance (e.g., pulmonary fibrosis or bone marrow failure presenting first), the hepatic component may be missed unless specifically screened for—particularly with noninvasive tools such as transient elastography. The high prevalence of hepatic fibrosis found in the Aachen registry only when actively screened underscores this point: without systematic assessment, hepatic involvement goes unrecognized.

In summary, hepatic fibrosis is a common but underappreciated feature of TBDs. Registry data show that nearly nine in ten adults with pathogenic *TERT* or *TERC* mutations have at least moderate hepatic fibrosis, yet this manifestation is frequently overlooked because TBDs lack pathognomonic features in many patients, hepatic fibrosis is subclinical until late, and alternative diagnoses are pursued first. Active screening with transient elastography in patients with known or suspected TBDs is therefore warranted.
