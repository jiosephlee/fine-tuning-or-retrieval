Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

Chapter 4: ## Genetic Diagnosis and Variant Interpretation

After the extensive clinical workup revealed a multisystem disorder affecting the bone marrow, liver, and lungs—without a unifying acquired explanation—the diagnostic focus shifted toward a genetic etiology. Clinical exome sequencing was pursued and identified a monoallelic missense variant in the PARN gene: c.613T>C, resulting in an amino acid substitution of cysteine to arginine at position 205 (p.Cys205Arg) in exon 8. This nucleotide change alters a residue within the R3H domain (spanning amino acids 178–245), a region of the PARN protein that is highly conserved across species. Conservation at a given residue suggests that evolutionary pressure has maintained it because it is critical to protein structure or function; a substitution at such a site is therefore more likely to be deleterious. In this case, the cysteine at position 205 was replaced by arginine—a change that disrupts the chemical character of the residue, as cysteine's unique thiol side chain can participate in disulfide bonding and metal coordination, functions arginine cannot fulfill. The location of this variant within a well-characterized functional domain, combined with its evolutionary conservation, provided early biological signals that it could be clinically meaningful.

To estimate the likely functional impact of the variant, four of five in silico missense prediction tools—LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT—predicted a damaging effect. These computational algorithms evaluate factors such as amino acid conservation, physicochemical properties of the substitution, and protein domain context to generate probability scores for pathogenicity. While none of these tools is diagnostic in isolation, their convergence on a deleterious prediction strengthens the case that a variant disrupts protein function. In clinical practice, in silico predictions serve as supportive evidence rather than primary proof, and they must always be interpreted in the context of the patient's phenotype and population-level data.

The formal framework used to classify this variant was the ACMG/AMP (American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics/Association for Molecular Pathology) standards, which provide a standardized, evidence-based system for categorizing sequence variants along a spectrum from benign to pathogenic. The PARN c.613T>C variant met several supportive criteria. **PM1** was applied because the substitution occurs within the R3H domain, a critical and well-established functional region of PARN with no known benign variation—meaning no healthy individuals in databases carry alterations at this site. **PM2** was satisfied because the variant is absent or extremely rare in large population databases such as gnomAD, indicating it is not a common polymorphism. **PP3** was met because multiple computational tools predicted a damaging effect on the gene product. Finally, **PP2** was applied because PARN has a low rate of benign missense variation overall, and missense variants are a recognized mechanism of disease in this gene—consistent with the observation that PARN is among the 20% of human genes least tolerant to rare, disruptive variants, supporting the concept of haploinsufficiency in carriers. Despite meeting these criteria, the evidence was insufficient to classify the variant as likely pathogenic or pathogenic under the ACMG/AMP framework, and it was therefore designated a **Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS)**.

A VUS represents a category of genetic variants for which the available evidence is inadequate to determine whether the variant is benign or pathogenic. This designation is a deliberate acknowledgment of uncertainty: it prevents clinicians from overcalling pathogenicity based on incomplete data, but it simultaneously creates a challenge for clinical decision-making. A VUS cannot be used to confirm a molecular diagnosis in isolation, nor should it be used to guide cascade family testing or reproductive counseling as a pathogenic variant would. In practice, the significance of a VUS must be assessed through clinical correlation—asking whether the patient's phenotype is consistent with the gene's known disease spectrum—and through ongoing reevaluation as new cases, functional studies, and population data accumulate over time.

To contextualize the potential pathogenicity of this variant, the authors drew parallels with other PARN missense variants reported in the ClinVar database. Two specific variants—p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1)—are classified as likely pathogenic. These variants, like p.Cys205Arg, are single missense changes in PARN identified in patients with clinical features suggestive of a telomere biology disorder. The existence of other likely pathogenic missense variants in the same gene supports the principle that missense alterations in PARN can be disease-causing, and it provides a comparative framework for evaluating the plausibility of the current variant's pathogenicity. However, it should be noted that each variant must be assessed on its own merits, as different missense changes within the same gene can have vastly different functional consequences depending on their location and the nature of the amino acid substitution.

The critical step in reaching a diagnostic conclusion in this case was **clinical-genetic correlation**. The patient presented with a constellation of findings—pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying, osteoporosis, and neuropathy—that collectively aligned with the known spectrum of telomere biology disorders (TBDs). PARN has an established role in this disease family: biallelic PARN variants cause autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), while monoallelic variants are increasingly recognized in telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4). Although the specific variant identified in this patient remained classified as a VUS by formal ACMG/AMP criteria, the strong phenotypic overlap with PARN-associated TBDs provided the clinical context necessary to support a diagnosis. This case illustrates a fundamental principle in clinical genetics: variant classification and clinical diagnosis are related but distinct processes. A VUS does not exclude a genetic disease, and when the clinical picture strongly suggests a particular disorder, the combination of a plausible candidate gene variant and a matching phenotype can justify a clinical diagnosis—even when the variant itself cannot yet be definitively classified as pathogenic.

The diagnostic gold standards for suspected TBDs include genetic testing through next-generation sequencing—of which clinical exome sequencing is one modality—and flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridization (flow-FISH) to directly measure telomere length in leukocyte subsets. Flow-FISH quantifies telomere fluorescence in individual cells and compares values to age-matched reference ranges, allowing clinicians to identify the abnormally short telomeres that are the molecular hallmark of TBDs. In this case, whole-exome sequencing was the primary tool that identified the PARN variant, and clinical correlation with the multisystem phenotype provided the diagnostic framework. The case underscores the complementary roles of genetic and functional testing: sequencing identifies the responsible variant, while telomere length measurement can provide independent confirmation of the downstream biological consequence. Together, these modalities form the foundation of modern TBD diagnostics, and their integration with clinical assessment is essential for accurate diagnosis, genetic counseling, and appropriate surveillance planning.
