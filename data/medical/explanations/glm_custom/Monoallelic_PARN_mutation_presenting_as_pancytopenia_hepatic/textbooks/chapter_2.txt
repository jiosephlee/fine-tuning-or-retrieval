Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

Chapter 2: ## Differential Diagnosis and Diagnostic Reasoning

The diagnostic journey in this case illustrates a fundamental principle of clinical reasoning: when a unifying explanation fails to account for the full breadth of a patient's presentation, the clinician must broaden the differential and reconsider whether seemingly unrelated findings share a common underlying cause. For this young woman, the initial clinical picture suggested several plausible explanations—nutritional deficiency, autoimmune disease, and chronic infection—but each proved insufficient upon systematic evaluation, ultimately redirecting attention toward a rare inherited disorder.

**Nutritional Deficiency as an Initial Explanation**

The patient's severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency provided a tempting initial diagnosis. Severe B12 deficiency can produce pancytopenia through ineffective erythropoiesis, in which hematopoietic precursors fail to mature properly due to impaired DNA synthesis. The peripheral smear findings—dimorphic erythrocytes, hypersegmented neutrophils, basophilic stippling, and Cabot rings—were classic megaloblastic features, and the bone marrow showed erythroid hyperplasia with increased iron stores, all consistent with this diagnosis. Her neurological findings, including polyneuropathy and bilateral thenar atrophy, were also compatible with B12 deficiency, which can cause subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord and peripheral neuropathy.

However, several features remained disproportionate to nutritional deficiency alone. Hepatic fibrosis and cirrhosis, pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying of hair, osteoporosis with recurrent fractures, and the persistent decline in functional capacity over five years are not explained by B12 or folate deficiency. Even after correction of these deficiencies with parenteral cyanocobalamin and oral folate, the patient's multisystem dysfunction persisted. This discrepancy between the identified deficiency and the clinical severity was an early signal that a more fundamental process was at work.

**Ineffective Erythropoiesis Versus Haemolysis**

A positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) for IgG initially raised concern for autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. This finding, combined with elevated lactate dehydrogenase and indirect hyperbilirubinaemia—both markers typically associated with haemolysis—could have led clinicians down an autoimmune pathway. However, the clinical reasoning correctly prioritized the reticulocyte count and bone marrow findings. In true immune-mediated haemolysis, the marrow responds with compensatory erythroid hyperplasia and a robust reticulocytosis as the body attempts to replace destroyed red cells. This patient's reticulocyte count was markedly low at 0.39%, indicating a hypoproliferative state rather than peripheral destruction. The bone marrow showed no evidence of dysplasia, malignancy, or infiltrative disease but did demonstrate ineffective erythropoiesis. The elevated iron and ferritin levels further supported this interpretation, as ineffective erythropoiesis traps iron within the marrow due to failed red cell maturation rather than releasing it through haemolysis. Thus, despite the positive DAT—a finding that can sometimes represent a nonspecific or incidental antibody—the weight of evidence pointed toward marrow dysfunction, not immune-mediated destruction.

**Evaluation of Hepatic Fibrosis Aetiology**

The discovery of cirrhosis on transient elastography in a woman in her late twenties prompted a thorough search for common hepatic causes. Autoimmune hepatitis was excluded by negative antinuclear antibodies and autoimmune hepatitis-specific serologies. Infectious causes were systematically ruled out: viral hepatitis panels, HIV, cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus testing were unrevealing. The only positive infectious marker was Epstein-Barr virus IgG, indicating past exposure rather than active infection. The inflammatory markers were elevated, but without a specific autoimmune or infectious target, this finding remained nonspecific. The absence of a clear hepatic aetiology in a young patient with cirrhosis was itself a diagnostic clue, as early-onset fibrosis without an identifiable cause should prompt consideration of metabolic, genetic, or syndromic disorders.

**Gastrointestinal Evaluation**

To evaluate possible malabsorption contributing to the B12 deficiency, upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was performed. The macroscopic appearance was normal, and duodenal biopsy showed only mild chronic gastritis with reduced parietal cells. While reduced parietal cell mass could impair intrinsic factor production and contribute to B12 malabsorption, this finding was too modest to explain the severity of the patient's deficiency or her multisystem involvement. No evidence of celiac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, or other significant gastrointestinal pathology was identified. The gastrointestinal workup thus reinforced the impression that nutritional deficiency was a contributing factor but not the primary driver of her illness.

**The Diagnostic Turning Point**

The critical shift in diagnostic reasoning occurred when the clinicians stepped back from individual organ findings and recognized the pattern connecting them. The constellation of premature greying of hair, osteoporosis with recurrent fractures, persistent cytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, and neuropathy in a young adult represented a syndrome of accelerated ageing and tissue failure. Individually, each finding had been evaluated and partially explained, but collectively they pointed toward a unifying diagnosis. Premature greying and osteoporosis in a patient under 30, combined with fibrosis of two organs (liver and lung) and bone marrow dysfunction, is not a pattern typical of nutritional deficiency, autoimmune disease, or infection. This recognition that the whole was greater—and more informative—than the sum of its parts redirected the diagnostic approach toward genetic testing.

**Absence of Family History and De Novo Mutation**

The lack of any family history of similar disease initially argued against a hereditary syndrome. However, this reasoning was appropriately challenged by the possibility of a de novo mutation, which can occur without any familial precedent. Additionally, variable penetrance in telomere biology disorders means that relatives carrying the same variant may be asymptomatic or present with milder, unrecognized manifestations. The absence of a family history, while relevant, does not exclude a genetic diagnosis and should not terminate that line of investigation when clinical suspicion is high.

**Hypothesis of a Telomere Biology Disorder**

The hypothesis of a telomere biology disorder (TBD) emerged from the recognition that TBDs can mimic acquired conditions and present with variable penetrance, often without the classical mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita—oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation. This patient lacked all three features, which is consistent with the increasingly recognized presentation of TBDs in adulthood with isolated or atypical manifestations. Telomere biology disorders may underlie what initially appear to be idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, aplastic anaemia, bone marrow failure syndromes, or cryptogenic cirrhosis. The key diagnostic insight was that these seemingly separate entities—cytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis—can share a single genetic basis in telomere dysfunction. This hypothesis was subsequently confirmed by clinical exome sequencing, which identified a monoallelic PARN missense variant, providing a unifying genetic explanation for the patient's multisystem disease.

The diagnostic process in this case underscores the importance of pattern recognition across organ systems, the willingness to reconsider an initial diagnosis when the clinical picture remains disproportionate, and the value of genetic testing when standard evaluations fail to provide an adequate explanation for early-onset multi-organ disease.
