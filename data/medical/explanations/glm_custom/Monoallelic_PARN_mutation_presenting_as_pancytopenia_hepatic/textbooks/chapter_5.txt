Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

Chapter 5: ## Management, Outcome, and Clinical Lessons

The management of this patient required addressing both the acute, reversible contributors to her clinical decline and the underlying telomere biology disorder (TBD) that unified her multisystem presentation. Because no curative therapy was immediately available or appropriate at the time of diagnosis, treatment was primarily supportive and targeted at the most clinically significant derangements.

Severe vitamin B12 deficiency was among the most immediately treatable abnormalities and likely exacerbated the patient's pancytopenia. She received intravenous cyanocobalamin at a dose of 1000 µg daily for five days, followed by weekly administration for four weeks and then monthly maintenance dosing. This parenteral repletion regimen is standard for severe, symptomatic B12 deficiency, particularly when intrinsic factor production is impaired, as was suggested by the reduced parietal cell density on duodenal biopsy. Oral folic acid (1 mg daily) was provided concurrently, because folate deficiency was also documented and because folate repletion alone in the setting of untreated B12 deficiency can precipitate or worsen neurological complications. The importance of correcting these deficiencies was twofold: it addressed a genuine nutritional deficit that contributed to ineffective erythropoiesis, and it served as a diagnostic test of sorts—persistent cytopenia and multisystem dysfunction despite adequate repletion strengthened the case for an underlying genetic disorder.

Supportive care measures extended beyond nutritional correction. The patient received whole blood transfusions for symptomatic anaemia, providing symptomatic relief while nutritional repletion took effect. Vitamin D deficiency and osteoporosis, confirmed on dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry and contributing to her recurrent bone pain and fractures, were managed with high-dose vitamin D (60,000 IU weekly) and elemental calcium (1000 mg daily). Secondary hyperparathyroidism and electrolyte disturbances, including hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia, were corrected with standard supplementation. Physiotherapy was incorporated to address the functional consequences of her neuropathy, thenar atrophy, and generalised deconditioning, all of which had progressed to the point of impairing self-care. These supportive interventions collectively improved her functional status even though they did not alter the underlying telomere pathology.

Following the genetic diagnosis, the patient and her family received genetic counselling. This is a critical component of management for TBDs, because the implications extend beyond the individual patient. Although no family history of similar illness was identified, variable penetrance, variable expressivity, and the possibility of a de novo mutation mean that relatives may carry the same variant without manifesting disease. Counselling addressed the nature of the monoallelic PARN variant, its classification as a Variant of Uncertain Significance, and the clinical–genetic correlation that nonetheless supported a TBD diagnosis. The patient demonstrated understanding of her condition and acceptance that curative options were not currently feasible, an important element of shared decision-making in rare genetic disease management.

At her two-week follow-up, the patient showed symptomatic improvement. Her blood counts had stabilised, fatigue had lessened, and no new clinical concerns were identified. While this short-term outcome is encouraging, it must be interpreted cautiously in the context of a progressive genetic disorder. The improvement likely reflects correction of the superimposed nutritional deficiencies and supportive measures rather than stabilisation of the underlying telomere biology defect.

Long-term surveillance is therefore essential and forms a central pillar of ongoing management. The patient continues to receive regular outpatient monitoring for hepatic function, pulmonary function, and haematological parameters. This surveillance strategy is designed to detect progression of bone marrow failure, worsening hepatic fibrosis or cirrhosis, and advancement of pulmonary fibrosis—each of which represents a potentially life-threatening complication of TBD. The monitoring schedule must be proactive rather than reactive, because early detection of organ-specific progression may create windows for intervention that would otherwise be missed.

Several future therapeutic considerations merit discussion, even if they were not immediately pursued in this case. Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) is the only potentially curative therapy for the haematological manifestations of TBD and is generally considered when bone marrow failure becomes progressive or severe. In this patient, cytopenia was largely attributable to B12 deficiency and had stabilised with repletion; however, the presence of a PARN variant places her at ongoing risk for evolving marrow failure, and the treating team must maintain vigilance for this possibility. It is also important to note that HSCT in TBD patients carries unique risks, because telomere shortening affects multiple organ systems and can complicate engraftment, increase transplant-related toxicity, and accelerate pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis post-transplant. Additionally, preclinical and small clinical studies have suggested that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone may promote telomere elongation and improve cytopenias in some TBD patients, but the long-term efficacy and safety of these agents remain uncertain, and their use is generally reserved for selected cases under specialist guidance.

Several broader clinical lessons emerge from this case that are directly relevant to the practising clinician. First, TBDs should be considered in young adults presenting with unexplained multisystem disease, particularly when the constellation includes cytopenia, organ fibrosis, and features of accelerated ageing such as premature greying and osteoporosis. The absence of a family history should not dissuade clinicians from suspecting a genetic disorder, because de novo mutations, variable penetrance, and somatic genetic rescue can obscure inheritance patterns. In this case, the initial presentation with fatigue and pancytopenia could easily have been attributed solely to severe B12 deficiency, and the positive DAT and elevated inflammatory markers could have led down an autoimmune diagnostic path. It was the persistence of multisystem findings—particularly hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis—after correction of nutritional deficiencies that ultimately shifted the diagnostic trajectory.

Second, TBDs can present without the classical mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita, which consists of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation. Adult-onset TBDs frequently manifest with isolated organ involvement, such as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, bone marrow failure, or hepatic fibrosis, and may never develop the cutaneous features that define classical DC. This patient had premature greying and knuckle hyperpigmentation but lacked the full triad, illustrating how the absence of textbook mucocutaneous findings can delay recognition. Clinicians should understand that the phenotypic spectrum of TBDs is broad and that relying on classical features as a prerequisite for genetic evaluation will result in missed diagnoses.

Third, monoallelic PARN mutations may present with a broader phenotype than idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis alone. While biallelic PARN variants are associated with autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita and the more severe Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome, monoallelic variants are more commonly linked to telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure. This case demonstrates that a single PARN allele may also be associated with hepatic fibrosis, a feature more typically attributed to TERT or TERC mutations. The expanding clinical spectrum of PARN-associated TBDs suggests that the downstream effects of impaired PARN-mediated TERC maturation can produce phenotypes resembling those caused by direct telomerase component defects. Clinicians and geneticists should therefore avoid anchoring on the canonical genotype–phenotype associations when evaluating patients with suspected TBDs.

Fourth, this case illustrates that TBDs may render the bone marrow more susceptible to haematopoietic stress, such as severe nutritional deficiency. Telomere shortening compromises the replicative reserve of haematopoietic stem cells, reducing their capacity to respond to increased demand. In a patient with intact telomere biology, severe B12 deficiency would cause megaloblastic anaemia but would typically resolve fully with repletion. In this patient, the persistence of cytopenia-related concerns and the severity of her marrow dysfunction may reflect the interaction between an exogenous stressor and an intrinsically compromised stem cell compartment. This concept has practical implications: patients with known or suspected TBDs who develop superimposed nutritional, infectious, or inflammatory insults should be monitored closely, because their haematological recovery may be incomplete or delayed, and these episodes may unmask an underlying marrow failure phenotype.

In summary, this case underscores that the recognition of TBDs requires a high index of suspicion, a willingness to pursue genetic testing when multisystem disease lacks a unifying conventional explanation, and an appreciation that the phenotypic boundaries of individual telomere-related genes are still being defined. Early diagnosis does not always lead to immediate disease-modifying therapy, but it enables surveillance planning, genetic counselling, and the timely consideration of transplant or investigational therapies as the clinical picture evolves.
