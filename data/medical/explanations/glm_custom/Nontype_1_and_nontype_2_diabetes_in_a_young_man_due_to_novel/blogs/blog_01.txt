Title: Blog about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

# Neither Type 1 Nor Type 2: When to Suspect MODY in Young-Onset Diabetes

## The Setup

A young adult walks into his annual health exam. He's got glycosuria, an HbA1c of 10.2%, fasting glucose of 12.4 mmol/L, and he's been losing weight with increased thirst and appetite. Your reflexive thought? Probably type 1 diabetes — he's young, symptomatic, and presenting with marked hyperglycemia. Or maybe early-onset type 2, given the BMI of 24.6 and the triglycerides of 4.31 mmol/L. Either way, you'd be wrong. This patient had a novel heterozygous mutation in HNF1A — a never-before-reported variant causing HNF1A-MODY, the most common form of monogenic diabetes. And the diagnosis didn't just satisfy academic curiosity — it fundamentally changed the treatment plan, allowing insulin to be discontinued entirely.

## The Diagnostic Dilemma That Won't Go Away

Here's the uncomfortable truth: MODY accounts for an estimated 1–6% of young-onset diabetes, and it is almost certainly underdiagnosed. The clinical features overlap so heavily with type 1 and type 2 that most patients get lumped into one of those two buckets and never get genetic testing. This case is a textbook example of why that happens — and why it matters.

The patient presented with symptoms we typically associate with type 1 diabetes: weight loss, polydipsia, polyphagia, and a markedly elevated HbA1c. But he also had a BMI in the overweight range, a lipid profile suggestive of possible metabolic syndrome, and no acanthosis nigricans. His autoantibodies (GADA and IA-2A) were negative. His stimulated C-peptide was low but preserved at 0.83 nmol/L. None of these findings individually clinches the diagnosis, but together they form a pattern that should make you pause before reaching for the insulin or metformin prescription pad.

## The Family History Is the loudest clue

The single most striking feature of this case is the family history: maternal grandmother diagnosed with diabetes in her 30s, mother in her late 20s, and a younger brother at age 20. That's three successive generations with early-onset diabetes — a classic autosomal dominant inheritance pattern. This is the kind of family history that should immediately trigger the question: "Is this really type 1 or type 2, or is this monogenic?"

Unfortunately, family history can be harder to interpret than it sounds. As the authors note, under-reporting, de novo mutations, and the high background prevalence of type 2 diabetes in the population can all muddy the waters. Still, when you see diabetes tracking through multiple generations with early onset and without the typical metabolic syndrome phenotype, MODY should be on your differential.

## The Clinical Approach: What Should Make You Suspect MODY?

The diagnostic algorithm, as illustrated by this case, comes down to a few key features that, taken together, build a compelling case for genetic testing.

First, early onset — MODY typically manifests before age 25, though as this case shows, it can be picked up later if screening is opportunistic. Second, negative islet autoantibodies — GADA, IA-2A, and ZnT8 should all be checked, and if they're negative, your suspicion for MODY should rise significantly. Third, preserved C-peptide — a stimulated C-peptide above 0.60 nmol/L suggests residual beta-cell function that is more consistent with MODY or type 2 than with classic type 1 diabetes, though early type 1 can still show preserved C-peptide initially, so serial monitoring matters. Fourth, the absence of insulin resistance markers — no acanthosis nigricans, no significant obesity — though as the authors rightly point out, rising global obesity rates are making BMI and metabolic syndrome less reliable discriminators than they used to be. And fifth, that multi-generational family history of early-onset diabetes.

The Exeter group's MODY probability calculator can help quantify suspicion, but as the authors note, it was derived from White European populations and may overestimate MODY probability in non-White groups. In this case, it returned a 62.4% probability — suggestive, but not definitive. That's why genetic testing via next-generation sequencing panels remains the gold standard for confirmation.

## Why the Diagnosis Changes Everything

Here's where this case really drives home the point. The patient was initially treated with basal insulin degludec, metformin, and saxagliptin — a reasonable empiric approach for a young patient with severe hyperglycemia where the diagnosis isn't yet clear. But once genetic testing confirmed HNF1A-MODY, insulin was discontinued and replaced with the GLP-1 receptor agonist dulaglutide. The result? HbA1c dropped from 10.2% to 6.0%, and the regimen was ultimately simplified to metformin and a DPP-4 inhibitor for maintenance.

This isn't just about avoiding injections. The treatment implications are specific to the molecular subtype. HNF1A-MODY classically responds to low-dose sulfonylureas as first-line therapy, but GLP-1 receptor agonists are emerging as a safer alternative with less hypoglycemia risk — a small randomized crossover trial showed equivalent glycemic control with liraglutide versus glimepiride in HNF1A-MODY patients, with fewer hypoglycemic events. And critically, SGLT-2 inhibitors should be used with extreme caution or avoided entirely in HNF1A-MODY, because HNF1A mutations already reduce SGLT2 expression in the renal tubules — adding an SGLT-2 inhibitor on top of that can precipitate severe dehydration and even diabetic ketoacidosis. That's a drug-class adverse effect you would never anticipate without knowing the molecular diagnosis.

The novel variant in this case — p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G) in exon 4 — was absent from ClinVar and VarSome, highlighting how much of the MODY genetic landscape remains uncharted. It was classified as likely pathogenic based on ACMG criteria, including a high AlphaMissense score, its location in the DNA-binding domain adjacent to known pathogenic residues, and segregation with diabetes across the family. As NGS becomes more accessible, we will undoubtedly uncover more variants like this, expanding the known spectrum of MODY mutations and refining our understanding of genotype-phenotype correlations.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Suspect MODY in any patient with early-onset diabetes, negative islet autoantibodies, preserved C-peptide, and a multi-generational family history of early-onset diabetes.** No single feature is diagnostic — it's the pattern that matters.
- **BMI is becoming a less reliable discriminator.** As obesity rates rise globally, MODY patients can overlap with type 2 diabetes phenotypically. Don't let a normal or elevated BMI talk you out of genetic testing when the rest of the picture fits.
- **Genetic testing is not academic — it changes treatment.** HNF1A-MODY patients can often transition off insulin onto oral agents or GLP-1 receptor agonists. SGLT-2 inhibitors may be dangerous in HNF1A-MODY due to intrinsic reduced SGLT2 expression.
- **GLP-1 receptor agonists are a viable alternative to sulfonylureas in HNF1A-MODY**, with comparable glycemic control and less hypoglycemia risk.
- **Next-generation sequencing panels are the diagnostic standard.** Novel variants like the one in this case won't appear in existing databases — pathogenicity assessment requires integration of computational predictions, segregation data, and clinical context using ACMG guidelines.
- **MODY is underdiagnosed.** If you're not thinking about it, you won't test for it — and your patient will be stuck on the wrong treatment plan indefinitely.

## The Bottom Line

This case isn't remarkable because the patient had an unusual presentation — it's remarkable precisely because the presentation was so ordinary. Young, symptomatic, elevated HbA1c, family history of diabetes — every clinic sees this. The difference here was that someone thought to ask whether this was really type 1 or type 2, ordered the genetic test, and then had the courage to stop insulin once the answer came back. In the era of precision medicine, the question isn't whether we can diagnose MODY — it's whether we remember to look for it.
