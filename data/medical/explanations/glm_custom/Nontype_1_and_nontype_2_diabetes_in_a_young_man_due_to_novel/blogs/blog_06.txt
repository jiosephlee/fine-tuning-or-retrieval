Title: Blog about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

# The Exeter MODY Calculator: A Useful Tool With a Population Problem

## A Young Man, a Strong Family History, and a Calculator That Said "Probably MODY"

A young adult walks into your clinic with an HbA1c of 10.2%, fasting glucose of 12.4 mmol/L, glycosuria, and a family history that reads like a generational curse: maternal grandmother diagnosed with diabetes in her 30s, mother in her late 20s, younger brother at age 20. No acanthosis nigricans, BMI of 24.6, negative GADA and IA-2A antibodies, and a stimulated C-peptide that is low but preserved at 0.83 nmol/L. You plug the clinical data into the Exeter MODY probability calculator, and it spits back a 62.4% probability — roughly a 1 in 1.6 chance. That is a compelling number. It certainly nudged the treating team toward genetic testing, which ultimately confirmed a novel heterozygous HNF1A variant (p.N270S, c.809A>G) and locked in a diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY.

But here is the wrinkle that should give every clinician pause: the very tool that helped flag this case was built almost entirely on White European data, and its performance in other populations is, to put it generously, unvalidated. This is not a knock on the Exeter group — their calculator is one of the most practically useful clinical decision aids in the monogenic diabetes space. It is a recognition that even excellent tools carry the biases of the populations they were trained on, and when we export them to genetically and phenotypically diverse settings, we need to interpret their outputs with appropriate humility.

## What the Exeter Calculator Actually Does

The MODY probability calculator, developed by the Exeter group, takes readily available clinical parameters — age at diagnosis, family history of diabetes, HbA1c at diagnosis, presence of islet autoantibodies, C-peptide levels, and features like BMI and insulin use — and generates an estimated probability that a given patient has MODY rather than type 1 or type 2 diabetes. It is accessible online, free, and designed to help clinicians decide who actually warrants expensive molecular genetic testing, which is the only way to definitively confirm a MODY diagnosis.

The logic is sound: MODY accounts for only an estimated 1%–6% of young-onset diabetes, and genetic testing is not cheap or universally available, so a pre-test probability tool serves as a critical gatekeeper. The problem is that the underlying model was derived and validated primarily in White European cohorts. When the authors of this case report entered their patient's clinical information, the calculator returned that 62.4% probability. On its face, that seems reassuringly high — and in this instance, the patient did indeed have MODY. But the authors themselves flag the concern: the tool may overestimate MODY probability in non-White groups because it was not calibrated using population-specific prevalence data or phenotypic distributions from those populations.

## Why Population Calibration Matters

Clinical prediction tools are not population-agnostic. The posterior probability that a patient has a condition depends critically on the pre-test probability, which is itself a function of disease prevalence in that population. If the baseline prevalence of MODY in a given ethnic group differs from the prevalence in the derivation cohort, the calculator's output will be miscalibrated — potentially substantially so. The Exeter calculator encodes not just the clinical features associated with MODY but also the relative prevalence of MODY versus type 1 and type 2 diabetes in the population from which its training data were drawn. Apply that same mathematical machinery to a population with a different prevalence of young-onset type 2 diabetes, different distributions of autoantibody positivity, or different familial clustering patterns of diabetes, and the resulting probability estimate may drift meaningfully from reality.

This is not a theoretical concern. The case report references several studies that underscore how much MODY prevalence and detection rates vary across populations. A study by Chen et al found that among 2,429 young Chinese adults with early-onset type 2 diabetes, the prevalence of MODY was 1.5%, with only 0.74% carrying pathogenic or likely pathogenic MODY variants. Møller et al demonstrated that in a Danish Caucasian population of patients with type 1 diabetes, 10% of 39 patients without high-risk HLA haplotypes harbored HNF1A mutations. A Russian population-based study screened 506 individuals suspected of having hereditary diabetes based on negative autoantibodies, preserved C-peptide, low insulin requirements, and absence of severe obesity — and identified pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in 46.4%, with HNF1A among the most commonly affected genes. These are dramatically different yields, reflecting differences in both true prevalence and selection criteria. A one-size-fits-all calculator cannot capture that variability.

## The Broader Problem: Algorithmic Export Without Validation

What we are really talking about here is a specific instance of a pervasive problem in clinical medicine: prediction models developed in one population are routinely applied to others without revalidation. We see this with cardiovascular risk scores, with sepsis prediction tools, with fracture risk calculators. The mathematical relationships between clinical features and outcomes may be broadly similar across populations, but the calibration — the mapping of a predicted probability to an actual observed frequency — frequently breaks down when you cross ethnic, geographic, or even temporal boundaries.

In the MODY context, the stakes are real. An overestimated MODY probability in a non-White population could lead to unnecessary genetic testing, misallocation of limited healthcare resources, and potential anxiety for patients and families told they "probably" have a monogenic disorder when the true pre-test probability in their population is considerably lower. Conversely, an underestimated probability — if, say, a population has a higher MODY prevalence than the derivation cohort — could mean missed diagnoses, with patients inappropriately placed on insulin when they might respond to sulfonylureas or GLP-1 receptor agonists, or vice versa.

## Back to the Case: Why the Diagnosis Still Mattered Enormously

Despite the calculator's limitations, the clinical suspicion in this case was well-founded. The autosomal dominant family history across three generations, the negative autoantibodies, the preserved-but-insufficient C-peptide, and the absence of classical type 2 features all pointed toward MODY independent of any algorithmic output. The genetic confirmation — a novel HNF1A variant in the DNA-binding domain, absent from ClinVar and VarSome but meeting ACMG criteria for likely pathogenic based on adjacency to known pathogenic residues, a high AlphaMissense score of 0.995, and family segregation — enabled a precision medicine approach that fundamentally changed management.

The patient was initially treated with basal insulin, metformin, and saxagliptin, reflecting a presumptive type 2 diabetes approach before genetic results were available. Once HNF1A-MODY was confirmed, insulin was discontinued and replaced with the GLP-1 receptor agonist dulaglutide, consistent with emerging evidence that GLP-1 receptor agonists can serve as an effective and safer alternative to sulfonylureas in HNF1A-MODY as beta-cell function declines, with lower hypoglycaemia risk. HbA1c plummeted from 10.2% to 6.0% within six months, and the regimen was subsequently simplified to metformin and saxagliptin for maintenance. That is a treatment trajectory directly enabled by a genetic diagnosis — not by a calculator estimate, but by the molecular confirmation that the calculator helped justify pursuing.

Notably, the case also reinforces a critical pharmacogenomic point: SGLT-2 inhibitors should be approached with caution in HNF1A-MODY. HNF1A transcriptionally controls SGLT2 expression in the renal tubules, and patients with HNF1A mutations already have reduced SGLT2 activity and lower renal glucose thresholds — explaining the glycosuria that can be an early clue to the diagnosis. Pharmacologically inhibiting SGLT2 in this population carries heightened risks of severe dehydration, euglycaemic diabetic ketoacidosis, and vascular collapse, as reported in the literature. The risks likely outweigh any glycaemic benefit.

## Where Do We Go From Here?

The Exeter MODY calculator remains a valuable starting point. It is accessible, transparent, and grounded in real data. It helps clinicians structure their thinking and provides a quantitative scaffold for what is otherwise an intuitive judgment call. The authors of this case report did exactly what you would want a thoughtful clinical team to do: they used the calculator as one input among several, weighed it against the broader clinical picture, and proceeded to genetic testing — which confirmed the diagnosis and transformed the treatment plan.

But the case also serves as a reminder that clinical prediction tools are not portable across populations without scrutiny. The calculator's 62.4% estimate may have been directionally correct in this instance, but we cannot assume it is calibrated for a Taiwanese patient, a Chinese cohort, a Russian cohort, or any other population that differs from its derivation sample. What we need are population-specific prevalence studies, locally validated prediction models, and clinician awareness that the number on the screen is a probability estimate conditioned on assumptions that may not hold for the patient sitting in front of you. The future of MODY diagnosis — and of precision medicine more broadly — depends not just on better sequencing technology but on prediction tools that reflect the full genetic and phenotypic diversity of the populations we actually serve.

## Key Take-Home Points

- The Exeter MODY probability calculator is a useful clinical decision aid but was derived and validated primarily in White European populations; its calibration in other ethnic groups is unknown and may overestimate or underestimate true MODY probability.
- Population-specific MODY prevalence data are essential for accurate pre-test probability estimation. Studies in Chinese (1.5% prevalence among young-onset T2D), Russian (46.4% yield in selected suspected hereditary diabetes cases), and Danish populations demonstrate markedly different detection rates.
- Despite the calculator's limitations, clinical features alone — autosomal dominant family history, negative autoantibodies, preserved C-peptide, absence of insulin resistance features — can and should drive the decision to pursue genetic testing.
- Genetic confirmation of HNF1A-MODY can fundamentally change management: this patient was transitioned from insulin to a GLP-1 receptor agonist with excellent glycaemic outcomes (HbA1c 10.2% → 6.0% in six months).
- Avoid SGLT-2 inhibitors in HNF1A-MODY: these patients already have reduced SGLT2 activity due to impaired HNF1A transcriptional control, and pharmacological inhibition increases the risk of severe dehydration, diabetic ketoacidosis, and vascular collapse.
- Novel HNF1A variants absent from standard databases can still be clinically pathogenic; ACMG framework analysis incorporating adjacency to known pathogenic residues, computational pathogenicity scores, and family segregation data is essential for interpretation.
- The future of MODY diagnosis requires population-calibrated prediction models, broader NGS screening strategies, and clinician awareness of the limitations of tools developed in narrow populations.
