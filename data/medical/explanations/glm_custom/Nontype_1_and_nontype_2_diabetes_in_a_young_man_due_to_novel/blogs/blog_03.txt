Title: Blog about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

# From Insulin to GLP-1: Precision Therapeutics in HNF1A-MODY

## The Case That Stops You in Your Tracks

A young adult walks into his annual physical. Routine labs show hyperglycemia and glycosuria. His HbA1c comes back at 10.2%. His fasting glucose is 12.4 mmol/L. He's lost weight, is thirsty all the time, and has polyphagia. You're thinking type 1 diabetes, maybe type 2 — except his BMI is 24.6, there's no acanthosis nigricans, and oh yeah, his mother was diagnosed with diabetes in her late 20s, his maternal grandmother in her 30s, and his younger brother at age 20. Three generations of early-onset diabetes. No autoantibodies. Moderately preserved C-peptide. This isn't type 1. It probably isn't classic type 2 either. This is MODY — specifically, HNF1A-MODY, caused by a novel mutation never before reported in any database. And here's where it gets interesting from a therapeutics standpoint: the genetic diagnosis didn't just satisfy academic curiosity — it fundamentally changed the treatment plan, allowed discontinuation of insulin, and got this patient to an HbA1c of 6.0% within months.

## The Diagnostic Dilemma

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young accounts for roughly 1–6% of young-onset diabetes cases, and HNF1A-MODY is the most common subtype, representing 30–65% of all MODY. Yet it remains vastly underdiagnosed. The clinical features overlap with both type 1 and type 2 diabetes, and in an era of rising obesity, you can no longer rely on a thin phenotype to clue you in. This patient had a BMI of 24.6, a triglyceride level of 4.31 mmol/L, and an HDL of 1.01 mmol/L — features suggesting possible metabolic syndrome. If you had used BMI alone to rule out MODY, you would have been wrong.

The key diagnostic clues here were the multi-generational family history of early-onset diabetes, negative islet autoantibodies (GADA and IA-2A), and a glucagon stimulation test showing a stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L — above the 0.60 nmol/L threshold suggesting preserved endogenous insulin secretion. The Exeter MODY probability calculator estimated a 62.4% probability of MODY for this patient, though it's worth noting this tool was derived from White European populations and may overestimate probability in other ethnic groups. Next-generation sequencing confirmed a heterozygous pathogenic variant in HNF1A: p.N270S (c.809A>G), located in the DNA-binding domain. This variant was absent from ClinVar, VarSome, and all published literature — a genuinely novel mutation, supported by a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), familial segregation with diabetes, and proximity to known pathogenic residues.

## The Treatment Pivot: Where Precision Medicine Meets the Bedside

Here's where the case becomes a practical lesson in therapeutics. At diagnosis, before genetic results were available, the team started empirically: basal insulin degludec (8 units/day), metformin (1500 mg/day), and saxagliptin (5 mg/day). Reasonable enough — you're staring at an HbA1c of 10.2% and you need control fast. One month in, the HbA1c dropped to 9.1%. But then the genetic confirmation came back, and the clinical reasoning shifted.

The team discontinued insulin entirely and initiated dulaglutide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist, at 1.5 mg weekly, continuing metformin. Within 6 months, the HbA1c dropped to 6.0%. The regimen was eventually simplified to metformin plus the DPP-4 inhibitor saxagliptin for long-term maintenance, with HbA1c consistently held below 6.5%. No microalbuminuria, no retinopathy, no signs of progressive complications on follow-up.

This transition deserves scrutiny. Why move away from insulin? Why a GLP-1 receptor agonist? And what's the evidence base?

## Evidence-Based Therapeutics in HNF1A-MODY

Traditional first-line therapy for HNF1A-MODY is low-dose sulfonylureas, which enhance insulin secretion by acting on ATP-sensitive potassium channels in beta cells. This approach has been validated over years of clinical experience and reflects the underlying pathophysiology — HNF1A mutations cause progressive beta-cell dysfunction with impaired insulin secretion, but the beta cells remain responsive to sulfonylurea stimulation. Many patients with HNF1A-MODY can be successfully managed on sulfonylureas for years before requiring insulin.

However, sulfonylureas carry a real hypoglycemia risk, and as beta-cell function declines over time, their effectiveness wanes while hypoglycemia risk persists or increases. This is where GLP-1 receptor agonists enter the conversation. A small randomized, double-blind, crossover trial demonstrated that liraglutide was as effective as glimepiride in reducing fasting plasma glucose and postprandial glucose fluctuations in HNF1A-MODY patients, with a notably lower risk of mild hypoglycemia. The mechanism makes sense — GLP-1 receptor agonists enhance glucose-dependent insulin secretion, meaning their effect is self-limiting at lower glucose levels, providing an intrinsic safeguard against hypoglycemia that sulfonylureas lack.

In this case, the patient's stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L indicated moderately preserved but insufficient beta-cell function — the sweet spot where a GLP-1 receptor agonist can augment endogenous insulin secretion without the need for exogenous insulin. The clinical decision-making process weighed three factors: residual beta-cell function (assessed via stimulated C-peptide), treatment burden (daily insulin injections versus weekly GLP-1 RA), and hypoglycemia risk. The result was a regimen that was not only more convenient but achieved superior glycemic control.

Metformin was maintained as adjunctive therapy throughout, which is appropriate given the patient's lipid profile suggesting some metabolic overlap, and because metformin's insulin-sensitizing effects complement the insulinotropic action of GLP-1 receptor agonists without additive hypoglycemia risk.

One critical pharmacologic caveat from the discussion: SGLT-2 inhibitors should be used with extreme caution, if at all, in HNF1A-MODY. HNF1A transcriptionally controls SGLT2 expression in the renal tubules, and patients with HNF1A-MODY already have reduced SGLT2 activity and glycosuria at lower glucose thresholds. Adding an SGLT-2 inhibitor on top of this can precipitate severe dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis. The risks — vascular collapse, genital infections, euglycemic DKA — likely outweigh any glycemic benefit in this specific population. This is a drug class that is broadly safe in type 2 diabetes but potentially dangerous in a monogenic form that looks like type 2 diabetes. If there's a stronger argument for genetic diagnosis before pharmacologic commitment, I haven't seen it.

## The Precision Medicine Lesson

This case is a concrete example of precision medicine in action — not as an abstract concept, but as a clinical decision that directly altered a patient's treatment trajectory. Molecular diagnosis informed the pharmacologic choice, reduced unnecessary treatment burden (daily insulin injections eliminated), potentially preserved endogenous beta-cell function by avoiding unnecessary exogenous insulin, and achieved a clinical outcome that was superior to the empiric regimen. The patient went from an HbA1c of 10.2% on insulin + metformin + DPP-4 inhibitor to 6.0% on a GLP-1 receptor agonist + metformin, and then maintained below 6.5% on metformin + DPP-4 inhibitor alone.

The broader lesson is that MODY is not rare — it is underdiagnosed. Approximately 1.5% of young Chinese adults with early-onset type 2 diabetes in one study carried pathogenic or likely pathogenic MODY variants. In a Danish study, 10% of patients with type 1 diabetes who lacked high-risk HLA haplotypes harbored HNF1A mutations. A Russian population-based study found pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in 46.4% of individuals suspected of having hereditary diabetes after autoimmune features were excluded. These numbers suggest that we are missing MODY cases routinely by not screening, and every missed case is a patient potentially on the wrong medication — sometimes for decades.

## Key Take-Home Points

- **Think MODY when:** early-onset diabetes with multi-generational family history, negative islet autoantibodies, and preserved C-peptide (stimulated >0.60 nmol/L). Don't let a normal or slightly elevated BMI talk you out of it — obesity rates in MODY are rising and BMI is no longer a reliable discriminator.

- **Genetic confirmation changes management.** In this case, it allowed discontinuation of insulin and transition to a GLP-1 receptor agonist, with superior glycemic outcomes. Without the genetic diagnosis, this patient might have remained on insulin indefinitely.

- **GLP-1 receptor agonists are a viable alternative to sulfonylureas in HNF1A-MODY.** Evidence supports equivalent glycemic efficacy with lower hypoglycemia risk, particularly as beta-cell function declines. The decision should be individualized based on residual C-peptide response, hypoglycemia risk, and treatment burden.

- **Avoid SGLT-2 inhibitors in HNF1A-MODY.** These patients already have reduced SGLT2 activity from the underlying transcription factor defect. Pharmacologic inhibition can precipitate severe dehydration and DKA. This is a precision medicine contraindication — safe in T2DM, dangerous in HNF1A-MODY.

- **Low-dose sulfonylureas remain the traditional first-line for HNF1A-MODY**, but GLP-1 receptor agonists are emerging as a safer alternative with growing evidence base. Stimulated C-peptide can help guide the choice — preserved beta-cell function supports GLP-1 RA use; severely depleted function may necessitate insulin.

- **MODY is underdiagnosed across populations.** Screening rates remain low, and clinical features overlap substantially with type 1 and type 2 diabetes. NGS-based MODY panels are increasingly accessible and can identify novel variants, as in this case, that standard databases do not yet contain. The absence of a variant in ClinVar does not mean it isn't pathogenic — ACMG guidelines integrate multiple lines of evidence including computational predictions, segregation data, and functional domain context.

- **Precision medicine isn't theoretical.** In this case, a single genetic result changed the prescription, reduced treatment burden, improved glycemic control, and spared a young patient years of unnecessary insulin therapy. That is the practical value of getting the diagnosis right.
