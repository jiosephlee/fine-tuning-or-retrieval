Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

Chapter 5: ## Clinical Outcome, Diagnostic Challenges, and Broader Implications

The patient's treatment trajectory vividly illustrates how genetic diagnosis can reshape clinical management. At the time of diagnosis, his glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) was markedly elevated at 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), prompting initiation of basal insulin degludec (eight units daily) alongside metformin (1500 mg/day) and the DPP-4 inhibitor saxagliptin (5 mg/day). This regimen reflected a preliminary assumption of type 2 diabetes, aiming to achieve rapid glycemic control while addressing potential insulin resistance. After one month, HbA1c decreased to 9.1%, indicating a partial but incomplete response. However, once genetic testing confirmed HNF1A-MODY, the clinical approach fundamentally shifted. Recognizing that the patient retained moderately preserved—though insufficient—β-cell function, as evidenced by a stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L on glucagon stimulation testing, clinicians discontinued insulin therapy and transitioned him to dulaglutide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist administered at 1.5 mg weekly. This decision was grounded in the pathophysiology of HNF1A-MODY: the disease involves a progressive decline in β-cell secretory capacity rather than complete autoimmune β-cell destruction, making incretin-based therapies a rational choice to support remaining endogenous insulin secretion while avoiding the risks associated with exogenous insulin. After six months of dulaglutide therapy combined with metformin, the patient's HbA1c decreased dramatically to 6.0%, representing a clinically meaningful reduction of over four percentage points from baseline. As glycemic control stabilized, the regimen was simplified to metformin (1500 mg/day) and saxagliptin (5 mg/day) for long-term maintenance. At the time of the report, HbA1c remained consistently below 6.5%, and ongoing surveillance had detected no microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy, though the patient remains under regular follow-up for long-term complication monitoring.

The avoidance of SGLT-2 inhibitors in this patient warrants attention, as it reflects a key pharmacogenomic consideration specific to HNF1A-MODY. HNF1A serves as a transcription factor for the SGLT2 gene in renal proximal tubules, and mutations in HNF1A reduce SGLT2 expression, lowering the renal threshold for glucose reabsorption and producing the glycosuria that can be an early clue to the diagnosis. Because SGLT-2 inhibitors further suppress residual SGLT2 activity, they can precipitate severe dehydration, euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis, and potential vascular collapse in HNF1A-MODY patients. This case therefore underscores how a precise genetic diagnosis not only opens therapeutic options—such as GLP-1 receptor agonists—but also closes doors to medications that might be harmful, a cornerstone of precision medicine.

Beyond the individual patient, this case illuminates the persistent diagnostic challenges surrounding MODY. Maturity-onset diabetes of the young accounts for approximately 1%–6% of all young-onset diabetes, yet it remains frequently misdiagnosed as type 1 or type 2 diabetes. The clinical overlap is substantial: patients with HNF1A-MODY can present with polydipsia, polyuria, polyphagia, and weight loss—symptoms indistinguishable from either type 1 or type 2 diabetes. In this case, the patient had a BMI of 24.6 kg/m², a lipid profile suggesting possible metabolic syndrome (low HDL at 1.01 mmol/L and elevated triglycerides at 4.31 mmol/L), and severely reduced sudomotor function on electrochemical skin conductance testing, findings that could easily steer a clinician toward a diagnosis of type 2 diabetes with early neuropathy. Historically, MODY was distinguished from type 2 diabetes by the absence of obesity and insulin resistance, but as obesity rates rise globally, BMI and metabolic markers have become less reliable discriminators. This erosion of phenotypic boundaries means that clinical features alone are increasingly insufficient to identify MODY, and clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion.

Several features of this case, taken together, should have prompted early consideration of MODY: the early age of onset, the strong matrilineal family history (grandmother diagnosed in her 30s, mother in her late 20s, younger brother at age 20), the absence of diabetic ketoacidosis at presentation despite marked hyperglycemia, negative islet autoantibodies (GADA and IA-2A), and preserved C-peptide secretion. The Exeter MODY probability calculator, when applied to this patient, yielded an estimated MODY probability of 62.4%. However, this tool was derived from White European populations and may overestimate probability in non-White groups, a limitation relevant here given that this patient was likely of Asian descent. This highlights a broader problem: much of the foundational MODY literature, diagnostic algorithms, and prevalence data originate from White European cohorts, and their applicability to other ethnic populations remains uncertain. Recent epidemiologic studies have begun to address this gap. A study by Chen et al found that among 2,429 young Chinese adults with early-onset type 2 diabetes, the prevalence of MODY was 1.5%, with 0.74% carrying pathogenic or likely pathogenic MODY variants. A Danish study by Møller et al revealed that among patients with a clinical diagnosis of type 1 diabetes who lacked high-risk HLA haplotypes, 10% harbored HNF1A mutations. A population-based Russian study screening 506 individuals suspected of having hereditary diabetes—selected based on negative autoantibodies, preserved C-peptide, low insulin requirements, and absence of severe obesity—identified pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in 46.4%, with HNF1A among the most commonly affected genes. These findings collectively suggest that MODY is underdiagnosed across diverse populations and that current clinical screening criteria may miss a substantial fraction of affected individuals.

The identification of a novel HNF1A variant in this case—p.N270S (c.809A>G)—further illustrates both the power and the complexity of modern genetic diagnostics. This heterozygous substitution, located in exon 4 within the DNA-binding domain of HNF1A, was absent from ClinVar, VarSome, and published literature at the time of the report. Its classification as likely pathogenic relied not on database precedent but on convergent evidence: the variant affects an amino acid residue adjacent to known pathogenic residues, carries a high AlphaMissense pathogenicity score (0.995), and segregates with diabetes in the patient's family. These criteria align with American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines for variant interpretation. The case thus demonstrates that next-generation sequencing (NGS) can identify novel variants that traditional database lookups would miss, but it also underscores the necessity of rigorous functional and segregation analysis to establish clinical relevance. Over 1,000 distinct HNF1A variants have been catalogued, and not all are pathogenic; some have minimal effects on protein function, and others may merely increase type 2 diabetes risk rather than cause monogenic diabetes. Distinguishing among these possibilities demands both molecular expertise and clinical correlation.

The therapeutic and prognostic implications of a confirmed HNF1A-MODY diagnosis extend well beyond the index patient. First-line therapy for HNF1A-MODY classically involves low-dose sulfonylureas, which enhance insulin secretion by closing ATP-sensitive potassium channels on β-cells. However, as β-cell function progressively declines, sulfonylureas carry an increasing risk of hypoglycemia. GLP-1 receptor agonists offer a compelling alternative, as demonstrated by a randomized crossover trial showing that liraglutide and glimepiride achieved comparable reductions in fasting and postprandial glucose in HNF1A-MODY patients, with GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy carrying a lower risk of mild hypoglycemia. The successful use of dulaglutide in this case, followed by simplification to oral agents, exemplifies a staged therapeutic approach calibrated to the patient's residual β-cell capacity and supports the expanding role of incretin therapies in MODY management. Beyond treatment selection, genetic confirmation also enables prognosis estimation, complication risk assessment, and cascade family screening. The autosomal dominant inheritance pattern means that first-degree relatives carry a 50% probability of the same mutation, and identification of the variant in this patient creates an opportunity for predictive testing and early intervention in family members, including his younger brother who was already diagnosed with diabetes at age 20.

Looking forward, the case highlights both the promise and the remaining barriers to precision medicine in diabetes. NGS technology now allows simultaneous analysis of multiple MODY-associated genes—over 15 have been identified—in a single reaction, dramatically improving screening feasibility compared with sequential Sanger sequencing. Yet significant obstacles persist: clinical awareness of MODY remains low, genetic testing is not universally accessible or reimbursed, and diagnostic algorithms calibrated in White European populations may not perform reliably in other ethnic groups. The development of population-specific screening strategies, broader NGS implementation, and improved cost-effectiveness analyses will be essential to close the diagnostic gap. As this case demonstrates, the stakes are tangible: a patient initially treated with insulin—appropriate for type 1 diabetes but unnecessary for HNF1A-MODY—was successfully transitioned to a regimen that preserved endogenous β-cell function, achieved sustained glycemic control, and simplified over time. The broader lesson is that in any young patient with diabetes, a family history of early-onset disease, negative autoantibodies, and preserved C-peptide, genetic testing should be pursued rather than deferred, because the resulting diagnosis can transform not only the treatment plan but also the trajectory of the patient's disease and the health of their family.
