Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

Chapter 2: ## Diagnostic Workup: From Clinical Suspicion to Genetic Confirmation

The diagnostic evaluation of early-onset diabetes demands a systematic approach that integrates clinical features, biochemical testing, and ultimately molecular genetics. In this case, a young adult presented with hyperglycemia and glycosuria detected on routine health examination, with a body mass index of 24.6 kg/m², no acanthosis nigricans, and a striking family history of early-onset diabetes across three generations. These features alone did not permit a definitive distinction between type 1 diabetes, type 2 diabetes, and monogenic forms such as maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY). The subsequent workup was therefore designed not merely to confirm diabetes but to determine its etiology, a distinction that would prove critical for treatment selection.

The first critical step in differentiating MODY from autoimmune type 1 diabetes is islet autoantibody testing. In this case, both glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA) and islet antigen-2 antibodies (IA-2A) were negative. Autoantibodies such as GADA, IA-2A, and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8) are markers of autoimmune beta-cell destruction, the hallmark of type 1 diabetes. Their absence in a patient with early-onset hyperglycemia effectively excludes autoimmune type 1 diabetes in the majority of cases and shifts the diagnostic consideration toward type 2 diabetes or MODY. It is important to recognize, however, that a small proportion of patients with HNF1A-MODY may harbor autoantibodies, as reported in several case series, meaning that a positive autoantibody result does not absolutely exclude MODY, though it significantly lowers the probability. In this patient, the negative autoantibody results, combined with the preserved but insufficient C-peptide response, provided strong evidence against autoimmune-mediated beta-cell destruction and supported further investigation for a monogenic etiology.

Assessment of residual beta-cell function is central to the diagnostic reasoning. The glucagon stimulation test was performed to evaluate endogenous insulin secretory capacity. In this test, glucagon is administered intravenously to stimulate insulin secretion from functioning beta cells, and serum C-peptide is measured at baseline and at 6 minutes. C-peptide is co-secreted with insulin in equimolar amounts and is the preferred clinical marker of endogenous insulin production because it does not undergo hepatic first-pass extraction. In clinical practice, a stimulated C-peptide level greater than 0.60 nmol/L is generally interpreted as indicating preserved endogenous insulin secretion, while lower values suggest significant beta-cell dysfunction, as seen in advanced type 1 diabetes. This patient's stimulated C-peptide was 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes, a value above the threshold for preserved secretion but notably lower than would be expected for fully normal beta-cell function. This result indicated that beta cells were present and partially functional but secreting insulin at an insufficient level, a pattern consistent with the progressive beta-cell dysfunction characteristic of HNF1A-MODY rather than the near-complete insulin deficiency of type 1 diabetes. The glucagon stimulation test should be regarded as a supportive rather than definitive investigation, particularly when autoantibody results are negative and the clinical presentation is atypical, as it helps situate the patient along the spectrum of beta-cell insufficiency without, by itself, establishing a specific diagnosis.

The oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) further characterized the patient's glucose physiology. After a 75-gram glucose load, the 2-hour plasma glucose was 19.8 mmol/L, far exceeding the diagnostic threshold of 11.1 mmol/L for diabetes. This marked postprandial hyperglycemia reflects the impaired insulin secretory response to a glucose challenge that is characteristic of HNF1A-MODY. In this condition, beta cells are unable to appropriately augment insulin secretion in response to rising glucose, leading to a steep postprandial glucose rise. The OGTT thus complemented the glucagon stimulation test by demonstrating the functional consequence of the beta-cell defect in vivo, while the initial fasting glucose of 12.4 mmol/L and HbA1c of 10.2% confirmed significant chronic hyperglycemia. Notably, the patient presented without diabetic ketoacidosis, which further supported the notion that some endogenous insulin remained, albeit insufficient for full metabolic control.

Additional investigations provided further clinical context and baseline assessment for long-term complications. Sudomotor function was evaluated using electrochemical skin conductance testing, which noninvasively assesses small unmyelinated autonomic nerve fibers through sweat gland function. The patient demonstrated severely reduced conductance in the hands, indicating significant autonomic dysfunction. This finding is notable given the relatively recent onset of symptomatic hyperglycemia, and it raises the possibility that subclinical neuropathy preceded the diagnosis, a phenomenon that has been described in early-onset diabetes. Microalbuminuria screening was negative, indicating no detectable early diabetic nephropathy at the time of diagnosis. These assessments established a baseline for longitudinal monitoring and reminded the clinical team that end-organ damage may already be present at diagnosis in young patients, even those with monogenic forms of diabetes.

The synthesis of clinical and biochemical data allowed the clinicians to apply established MODY diagnostic criteria. These criteria encompass early onset of diabetes, a strong family history consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance, postprandial glucose intolerance on OGTT, negative islet autoantibodies, and preserved C-peptide levels. The patient fulfilled each of these domains: diabetes was detected in a young adult, the maternal lineage showed diabetes onset in the grandmother in her 30s, the mother in her late 20s, and the younger brother at age 20; the OGTT demonstrated marked postprandial hyperglycemia; autoantibodies were negative; and stimulated C-peptide indicated partially preserved beta-cell function. The family history was particularly informative, as the pattern of affected individuals across three successive generations on the maternal side strongly suggested autosomal dominant transmission, the inheritance pattern of HNF1A-MODY. It is worth noting, however, that family history has limitations as a diagnostic tool, as it may be underreported, complicated by de novo mutations, or obscured by the high background prevalence of type 2 diabetes in many populations.

The Exeter group MODY probability calculator was used to quantify the clinical suspicion. This tool integrates clinical variables such as age at diagnosis, insulin use, family history, and metabolic features to generate a probability estimate that a patient has MODY rather than type 1 or type 2 diabetes. When this patient's clinical data were entered, the calculator returned an estimated MODY probability of 62.4%, corresponding to a 1 in 1.6 chance. This result strongly supported proceeding to genetic testing. Clinicians must interpret this tool with caution, however, because it was derived from data in White European populations and may overestimate or, in some contexts, misclassify MODY probability in non-White ethnic groups, among whom prevalence data and clinical presentations may differ. Despite this limitation, the high probability in this case reinforced the decision to pursue molecular genetic testing.

Genetic confirmation was achieved through next-generation sequencing (NGS) using a MODY gene panel. NGS enables simultaneous sequencing of multiple genes known to cause monogenic diabetes in a single reaction, making comprehensive MODY screening more clinically feasible than the sequential Sanger sequencing approach used in earlier years. One month after the initial diagnosis, the genetic test revealed a heterozygous variant in HNF1A, designated p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), in exon 4 of the gene. This variant had not been previously reported in the literature, the VarSome database, or the ClinVar database, establishing it as a novel variant. Its pathogenicity was assessed according to American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines, which evaluate evidence across multiple domains including computational prediction, population frequency data, segregation with disease in families, and functional consequences. The variant met criteria for likely pathogenic classification based on its high AlphaMissense score of 0.995, its location adjacent to known pathogenic residues within the DNA-binding domain, and its segregation with diabetes across the family. This genetic result confirmed the diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY and definitively distinguished it from both type 1 and type 2 diabetes.

The diagnostic journey in this case illustrates the layered reasoning required to identify monogenic diabetes. Each investigation served a specific diagnostic purpose: autoantibody testing excluded autoimmune type 1 diabetes, C-peptide assessment quantified residual beta-cell function, the OGTT characterized the physiological defect, and genetic testing provided the definitive molecular diagnosis. The case further demonstrates that clinical prediction tools, while imperfect in diverse populations, can effectively guide the decision to proceed with genetic testing, and that NGS panels are an essential tool for confirming MODY and enabling the precision medicine approach that followed.
