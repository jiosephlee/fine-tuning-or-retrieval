Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

Chapter 4: ## Treatment Strategy and Precision Medicine in HNF1A-MODY

At the time of diagnosis, this patient presented with markedly elevated glycemic parameters—an HbA1c of 10.2% (88 mmol/mol) and fasting glucose of 12.4 mmol/L—necessitating prompt intervention to achieve glycemic control. Because genetic testing had not yet been performed, the initial treatment strategy was empirically tailored to the working clinical suspicion of type 2 diabetes (T2D), which was the most likely diagnosis given the patient's BMI of 24.6 kg/m² and lipid profile suggestive of possible metabolic syndrome. The initial regimen combined basal insulin degludec (8 units/day), metformin (1500 mg/day), and the DPP-4 inhibitor saxagliptin (5 mg/day). This three-pronged approach addressed several therapeutic targets simultaneously: basal insulin provided exogenous coverage to rapidly suppress hepatic glucose output and correct severe hyperglycemia, metformin addressed potential insulin resistance, and saxagliptin augmented endogenous incretin-mediated insulin secretion. This approach is a pragmatic, evidence-based strategy for initial management of severe hyperglycemia when the definitive diabetes subtype has not yet been established and reflects the real-world clinical reality that molecular genetic results typically require weeks to obtain.

The rationale for initiating treatment before genetic confirmation is twofold. First, the degree of hyperglycemia present at diagnosis warranted immediate pharmacologic intervention to reduce glucotoxicity and prevent acute complications. Second, at this stage, the clinical picture was ambiguous: the patient had features that could be consistent with T2D (BMI, lipid abnormalities) alongside features suggestive of MODY (young age, strong family history of early-onset diabetes, absence of acanthosis nigricans, negative islet autoantibodies). The glucagon stimulation test demonstrated a stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L—above the 0.60 nmol/L threshold generally indicating preserved endogenous insulin secretion—yet still insufficient for normal glycemic regulation. This result was compatible with either early T2D or MODY and did not definitively resolve the diagnostic question. Initiating a regimen that could address multiple potential pathophysiologies was therefore clinically appropriate.

Following genetic confirmation of HNF1A-MODY one month later, the treatment strategy was fundamentally revised. The patient's HbA1c had already decreased to 9.1% on the initial regimen, reflecting a meaningful response. Given the genetic diagnosis, the patient's moderately preserved yet insufficient β-cell function, and the goal of preserving residual pancreatic function, insulin therapy was discontinued and replaced with dulaglutide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist administered at 1.5 mg weekly. After 6 months of this modified regimen, the patient's HbA1c decreased dramatically to 6.0%. As glycemic control stabilized, the regimen was further simplified to combination therapy with metformin (1500 mg/day) and saxagliptin (5 mg/day) for long-term maintenance, with HbA1c consistently maintained below 6.5% on follow-up.

The transition from insulin to a GLP-1 receptor agonist in this case is grounded in the evolving understanding of HNF1A-MODY pharmacotherapy. Traditionally, low-dose sulfonylureas have been considered first-line therapy for HNF1A-MODY. Sulfonylureas enhance insulin secretion by binding to and closing ATP-sensitive potassium (K_ATP) channels on the β-cell membrane, thereby depolarizing the cell and triggering calcium-mediated insulin granule exocytosis. Patients with HNF1A-MODY often exhibit particular sensitivity to sulfonylureas, which is thought to reflect the specific defect in glucose-stimulated insulin secretion pathways while K_ATP channel function remains relatively intact. However, as β-cell function progressively declines in HNF1A-MODY—a hallmark of the disease's natural history—sulfonylureas become less effective and carry a increasing risk of hypoglycemia, since their mechanism is independent of ambient glucose levels and they stimulate insulin secretion even when plasma glucose is low.

GLP-1 receptor agonists offer a mechanistically distinct and potentially superior alternative for HNF1A-MODY patients with declining β-cell function. These agents activate GLP-1 receptors on pancreatic β-cells, enhancing glucose-dependent insulin secretion—meaning their insulinotropic effects are amplified in the presence of elevated glucose and attenuated during hypoglycemia, thereby intrinsically reducing the risk of hypoglycemia. Clinical evidence supporting this approach includes a small randomized, double-blind, crossover trial demonstrating that liraglutide and glimepiride (a sulfonylurea) were comparably effective in reducing fasting plasma glucose and postprandial glucose fluctuations in HNF1A-MODY patients, but GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy was associated with a lower risk of mild hypoglycemia. This evidence base supported the decision in the present case to transition to dulaglutide, particularly given the patient's documented but insufficient β-cell reserve and the goal of preserving pancreatic function while minimizing hypoglycemic risk.

An important pharmacological consideration specific to HNF1A-MODY involves the use of SGLT-2 inhibitors, a drug class increasingly used in T2D management. In HNF1A-MODY, glycosuria occurs as a characteristic feature due to reduced expression of SGLT2 (sodium-glucose cotransporter 2) in the renal proximal tubule, where SGLT2 transcription is directly regulated by HNF1A. Because SGLT2-mediated glucose reabsorption is already functionally impaired in these patients, pharmacologic SGLT-2 inhibition risks compounding this defect, potentially leading to severe dehydration, euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis, and even vascular collapse. Case reports have documented HNF1A-MODY patients experiencing severe dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis due to high inhibition of residual SGLT2 activity. The risks of genital infections and ketoacidosis associated with SGLT-2 inhibitors may therefore outweigh potential benefits in HNF1A-MODY, and this drug class should generally be avoided. This consideration illustrates how knowledge of the specific molecular defect can prevent iatrogenic harm—another dimension of precision medicine beyond simply selecting the optimal therapy.

This case exemplifies the core principle of precision medicine in diabetes management: genetic diagnosis directly enabled treatment de-escalation from insulin therapy to an oral and injectable non-insulin regimen that was more appropriate for the underlying pathophysiology. Without genetic confirmation, the patient might have remained on insulin indefinitely, treated as having T2D with secondary failure or even as having slowly progressive type 1 diabetes. The ability to discontinue insulin after achieving initial glycemic control and transition to a GLP-1 receptor agonist that preserved β-cell function while maintaining excellent glycemic control (HbA1c from 10.2% to 6.0% within 6 months) underscores how molecular diagnosis can transform management. This case further demonstrates that precision medicine in diabetes operates bidirectionally—not only by guiding the selection of optimal therapy but also by identifying treatments to avoid, such as SGLT-2 inhibitors in HNF1A-MODY. The successful simplification of the long-term regimen to metformin and saxagliptin, with sustained HbA1c below 6.5% and no evidence of microvascular complications on follow-up, validates the treatment algorithm applied and reinforces the principle that accurate genetic diagnosis is not merely an academic exercise but a clinical tool with direct therapeutic consequences.
