Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

## Clinical Presentation and the Diagnostic Dilemma of Early-Onset Diabetes

A young adult was referred for evaluation of hyperglycemia and glycosuria detected during a routine annual health examination. At presentation, he reported increased thirst, increased appetite, and recent weight loss — the classic symptomatic triad of uncontrolled diabetes. Yet several features of his presentation did not fit neatly into the expected framework for either type 1 or type 2 diabetes. On physical examination, his body mass index was 24.6 kg/m², his blood pressure was 112/70 mmHg, and his heart rate was 78 beats per minute. Acanthosis nigricans, a cutaneous marker of insulin resistance often seen in type 2 diabetes, was notably absent. His BMI fell within the normal-to-borderline range, and he had no significant medical or surgical history. In other words, he did not exhibit the metabolic phenotype typically associated with type 2 diabetes, nor did he present with the acute, severe metabolic decompensation — such as diabetic ketoacidosis — that is often the initial manifestation of type 1 diabetes.

The family history, however, was striking and became a central clue. The patient's maternal grandmother was diagnosed with diabetes in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at age 20. This vertical transmission across three generations, with onset at a young age in each affected member and no known history of early-onset cardiovascular disease among them, strongly suggested an autosomal dominant pattern of inheritance rather than the multifactorial inheritance patterns characteristic of typical type 2 diabetes. For the clinician evaluating this patient, the family history alone raised the possibility of a monogenic form of diabetes.

Initial laboratory testing confirmed significant hyperglycemia. The patient's glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) was 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), fasting plasma glucose was 12.4 mmol/L, and the 2-hour glucose level after a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) was 19.8 mmol/L. There were no signs of diabetic ketoacidosis. The lipid profile revealed a high-density lipoprotein level of 1.01 mmol/L and triglycerides of 4.31 mmol/L, a pattern broadly suggestive of possible metabolic syndrome, though the absence of obesity and acanthosis nigricans made this interpretation less straightforward. Together, these findings presented a diagnostic dilemma: the patient had severe hyperglycemia with symptomatic onset, but his phenotype did not cleanly fit type 1 or type 2 diabetes.

To understand why this patient's presentation was puzzling, it is essential to place it in the context of maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY). MODY is defined as a group of dominantly inherited, non-autoimmune diabetes disorders characterized by a primary defect in the development and function of pancreatic beta cells. These conditions typically manifest and are diagnosed before the age of 25. MODY accounts for approximately 1%–6% of all young-onset diabetes cases, though this estimate may be conservative because genetic screening is typically limited to individuals selected based on clinical characteristics, and studies have largely focused on White European populations. Among the more than 15 genes linked to the MODY phenotype, mutations in hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A) are the most common cause, accounting for roughly 30%–65% of all MODY cases. HNF1A-MODY demonstrates a clearly observable autosomal dominant transmission of symptomatic diabetes across generations, which is precisely the pattern observed in this patient's family.

Differentiating MODY from type 1 and type 2 diabetes remains clinically challenging because of overlapping features. In early adolescence, HNF1A-MODY may be asymptomatic, with elevated blood glucose discovered incidentally on routine testing and without clinical evidence of insulin resistance. As in this patient, a postprandial glucose measurement or OGTT can reveal a marked rise (≥11.1 mmol/L), and glycosuria may be detected. Importantly, glycosuria in HNF1A-MODY is not simply a consequence of hyperglycemia exceeding the normal renal threshold; rather, it reflects a reduced renal threshold for glucose reabsorption due to compromised glucose transport in the renal tubules, a direct effect of HNF1A dysfunction. In later stages of the disease, however, patients may develop polydipsia, polyuria, polyphagia, and weight loss — symptoms indistinguishable from those of type 1 or type 2 diabetes, as occurred in this patient. This phenotypic convergence is what makes MODY a diagnostic trap: a young patient with symptomatic hyperglycemia can easily be labeled as having type 1 diabetes (if autoantibodies are assumed) or type 2 diabetes (if mild obesity or dyslipidemia is present), when the underlying etiology is in fact a monogenic beta-cell disorder. Recognizing this distinction at the point of initial presentation — as the family history and absence of insulin resistance markers prompted in this case — is the critical first step toward securing an accurate molecular diagnosis.

## Diagnostic Workup: From Clinical Suspicion to Genetic Confirmation

The diagnostic evaluation of early-onset diabetes demands a systematic approach that integrates clinical features, biochemical testing, and ultimately molecular genetics. In this case, a young adult presented with hyperglycemia and glycosuria detected on routine health examination, with a body mass index of 24.6 kg/m², no acanthosis nigricans, and a striking family history of early-onset diabetes across three generations. These features alone did not permit a definitive distinction between type 1 diabetes, type 2 diabetes, and monogenic forms such as maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY). The subsequent workup was therefore designed not merely to confirm diabetes but to determine its etiology, a distinction that would prove critical for treatment selection.

The first critical step in differentiating MODY from autoimmune type 1 diabetes is islet autoantibody testing. In this case, both glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA) and islet antigen-2 antibodies (IA-2A) were negative. Autoantibodies such as GADA, IA-2A, and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8) are markers of autoimmune beta-cell destruction, the hallmark of type 1 diabetes. Their absence in a patient with early-onset hyperglycemia effectively excludes autoimmune type 1 diabetes in the majority of cases and shifts the diagnostic consideration toward type 2 diabetes or MODY. It is important to recognize, however, that a small proportion of patients with HNF1A-MODY may harbor autoantibodies, as reported in several case series, meaning that a positive autoantibody result does not absolutely exclude MODY, though it significantly lowers the probability. In this patient, the negative autoantibody results, combined with the preserved but insufficient C-peptide response, provided strong evidence against autoimmune-mediated beta-cell destruction and supported further investigation for a monogenic etiology.

Assessment of residual beta-cell function is central to the diagnostic reasoning. The glucagon stimulation test was performed to evaluate endogenous insulin secretory capacity. In this test, glucagon is administered intravenously to stimulate insulin secretion from functioning beta cells, and serum C-peptide is measured at baseline and at 6 minutes. C-peptide is co-secreted with insulin in equimolar amounts and is the preferred clinical marker of endogenous insulin production because it does not undergo hepatic first-pass extraction. In clinical practice, a stimulated C-peptide level greater than 0.60 nmol/L is generally interpreted as indicating preserved endogenous insulin secretion, while lower values suggest significant beta-cell dysfunction, as seen in advanced type 1 diabetes. This patient's stimulated C-peptide was 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes, a value above the threshold for preserved secretion but notably lower than would be expected for fully normal beta-cell function. This result indicated that beta cells were present and partially functional but secreting insulin at an insufficient level, a pattern consistent with the progressive beta-cell dysfunction characteristic of HNF1A-MODY rather than the near-complete insulin deficiency of type 1 diabetes. The glucagon stimulation test should be regarded as a supportive rather than definitive investigation, particularly when autoantibody results are negative and the clinical presentation is atypical, as it helps situate the patient along the spectrum of beta-cell insufficiency without, by itself, establishing a specific diagnosis.

The oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) further characterized the patient's glucose physiology. After a 75-gram glucose load, the 2-hour plasma glucose was 19.8 mmol/L, far exceeding the diagnostic threshold of 11.1 mmol/L for diabetes. This marked postprandial hyperglycemia reflects the impaired insulin secretory response to a glucose challenge that is characteristic of HNF1A-MODY. In this condition, beta cells are unable to appropriately augment insulin secretion in response to rising glucose, leading to a steep postprandial glucose rise. The OGTT thus complemented the glucagon stimulation test by demonstrating the functional consequence of the beta-cell defect in vivo, while the initial fasting glucose of 12.4 mmol/L and HbA1c of 10.2% confirmed significant chronic hyperglycemia. Notably, the patient presented without diabetic ketoacidosis, which further supported the notion that some endogenous insulin remained, albeit insufficient for full metabolic control.

Additional investigations provided further clinical context and baseline assessment for long-term complications. Sudomotor function was evaluated using electrochemical skin conductance testing, which noninvasively assesses small unmyelinated autonomic nerve fibers through sweat gland function. The patient demonstrated severely reduced conductance in the hands, indicating significant autonomic dysfunction. This finding is notable given the relatively recent onset of symptomatic hyperglycemia, and it raises the possibility that subclinical neuropathy preceded the diagnosis, a phenomenon that has been described in early-onset diabetes. Microalbuminuria screening was negative, indicating no detectable early diabetic nephropathy at the time of diagnosis. These assessments established a baseline for longitudinal monitoring and reminded the clinical team that end-organ damage may already be present at diagnosis in young patients, even those with monogenic forms of diabetes.

The synthesis of clinical and biochemical data allowed the clinicians to apply established MODY diagnostic criteria. These criteria encompass early onset of diabetes, a strong family history consistent with autosomal dominant inheritance, postprandial glucose intolerance on OGTT, negative islet autoantibodies, and preserved C-peptide levels. The patient fulfilled each of these domains: diabetes was detected in a young adult, the maternal lineage showed diabetes onset in the grandmother in her 30s, the mother in her late 20s, and the younger brother at age 20; the OGTT demonstrated marked postprandial hyperglycemia; autoantibodies were negative; and stimulated C-peptide indicated partially preserved beta-cell function. The family history was particularly informative, as the pattern of affected individuals across three successive generations on the maternal side strongly suggested autosomal dominant transmission, the inheritance pattern of HNF1A-MODY. It is worth noting, however, that family history has limitations as a diagnostic tool, as it may be underreported, complicated by de novo mutations, or obscured by the high background prevalence of type 2 diabetes in many populations.

The Exeter group MODY probability calculator was used to quantify the clinical suspicion. This tool integrates clinical variables such as age at diagnosis, insulin use, family history, and metabolic features to generate a probability estimate that a patient has MODY rather than type 1 or type 2 diabetes. When this patient's clinical data were entered, the calculator returned an estimated MODY probability of 62.4%, corresponding to a 1 in 1.6 chance. This result strongly supported proceeding to genetic testing. Clinicians must interpret this tool with caution, however, because it was derived from data in White European populations and may overestimate or, in some contexts, misclassify MODY probability in non-White ethnic groups, among whom prevalence data and clinical presentations may differ. Despite this limitation, the high probability in this case reinforced the decision to pursue molecular genetic testing.

Genetic confirmation was achieved through next-generation sequencing (NGS) using a MODY gene panel. NGS enables simultaneous sequencing of multiple genes known to cause monogenic diabetes in a single reaction, making comprehensive MODY screening more clinically feasible than the sequential Sanger sequencing approach used in earlier years. One month after the initial diagnosis, the genetic test revealed a heterozygous variant in HNF1A, designated p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), in exon 4 of the gene. This variant had not been previously reported in the literature, the VarSome database, or the ClinVar database, establishing it as a novel variant. Its pathogenicity was assessed according to American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines, which evaluate evidence across multiple domains including computational prediction, population frequency data, segregation with disease in families, and functional consequences. The variant met criteria for likely pathogenic classification based on its high AlphaMissense score of 0.995, its location adjacent to known pathogenic residues within the DNA-binding domain, and its segregation with diabetes across the family. This genetic result confirmed the diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY and definitively distinguished it from both type 1 and type 2 diabetes.

The diagnostic journey in this case illustrates the layered reasoning required to identify monogenic diabetes. Each investigation served a specific diagnostic purpose: autoantibody testing excluded autoimmune type 1 diabetes, C-peptide assessment quantified residual beta-cell function, the OGTT characterized the physiological defect, and genetic testing provided the definitive molecular diagnosis. The case further demonstrates that clinical prediction tools, while imperfect in diverse populations, can effectively guide the decision to proceed with genetic testing, and that NGS panels are an essential tool for confirming MODY and enabling the precision medicine approach that followed.

## HNF1A-MODY Pathophysiology and the Novel Mutation

Understanding how a single gene mutation produces diabetes requires examining the molecular role of the affected protein. The *HNF1A* gene is located on the q24 region of chromosome 12 and spans 23,790 base pairs across 10 exons. It encodes a 631-amino-acid protein expressed in the liver, kidneys, and pancreas. In the pancreas, HNF1A functions as a transcription factor that regulates genes essential for beta-cell development and function. When HNF1A is mutated, these downstream genes are not properly transcribed, producing a progressive decline in beta-cell function. Specifically, the mutant HNF1A protein impairs expression of the insulin gene and the GLUT2 glucose transporter gene, and it reduces pyruvate kinase activity. Together, these molecular defects diminish the beta cell's ability to sense glucose and secrete insulin in response, leading to decreased insulin secretion and progressive glucose intolerance. This mechanism distinguishes HNF1A-MODY from type 1 diabetes, where autoimmune destruction eliminates beta cells, and from type 2 diabetes, where insulin resistance is the primary defect. In this patient, the glucagon stimulation test demonstrated a stimulated C-peptide level of 0.83 nmol/L—above the 0.60 nmol/L threshold suggesting preserved endogenous insulin secretion, yet insufficient for normal glycaemic control. This finding of partially preserved but inadequate beta-cell function is consistent with the progressive secretory failure that characterises HNF1A-MODY.

A distinctive clinical feature of HNF1A-MODY illustrated by this patient is glycosuria, detected on his routine health examination. In HNF1A-MODY, glycosuria is not simply a consequence of hyperglycaemia exceeding the renal threshold. Rather, HNF1A directly controls transcription of the SGLT2 gene, which encodes the sodium-glucose cotransporter responsible for the majority of glucose reabsorption in the renal proximal tubule. When HNF1A function is impaired, SGLT2 expression is reduced, lowering the renal threshold for glucose reabsorption and causing urinary glucose excretion at lower blood glucose levels than would otherwise occur. This mechanism also has therapeutic implications: SGLT2 inhibitors are potentially hazardous in HNF1A-MODY patients, as further inhibition of already compromised SGLT2 activity may precipitate severe dehydration and even diabetic ketoacidosis, as noted in prior case reports.

The patient's genetic testing revealed a heterozygous variant designated p.N270S (*HNF1A*:c.809A>G) in exon 4. This substitution replaces asparagine with serine at position 270 of the protein. The variant was not reported in the literature, the VarSome database, or the ClinVar database at the time of analysis, establishing it as a novel variant. Evaluating a previously unreported variant requires a systematic framework, and the clinicians applied the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines for variant interpretation. Under these guidelines, multiple lines of evidence supported classifying the variant as likely pathogenic. First, the variant affects an amino acid residue adjacent to previously established pathogenic residues, suggesting that position 270 resides in a region of the protein critical for function. Second, the variant carries a high AlphaMissense score of 0.995, indicating a high computational probability that the amino acid substitution is deleterious. Third, the variant segregates with diabetes in the family: the maternal grandmother, mother, and younger brother all had early-onset diabetes, consistent with autosomal dominant transmission. Together, these criteria satisfied ACMG standards for a likely pathogenic classification despite the variant's absence from major databases.

The location of this novel variant within the protein's functional architecture further informs its pathogenicity. The HNF1A protein contains two key functional regions: a DNA-binding domain and a transactivation domain. Studies have shown that the majority of pathogenic HNF1A mutations cluster in the DNA-binding domain, while the transactivation domain has a lower mutation rate. The p.N270S variant identified in this patient is situated in the DNA-binding domain, which is consistent with the overall distribution pattern of pathogenic mutations. This location is mechanistically meaningful because defects in the DNA-binding domain directly impair the transcription factor's ability to bind target gene promoters, disrupting transcription of insulin, GLUT2, pyruvate kinase, and SGLT2.

The identification of this novel variant also highlights an important distinction in HNF1A genetics. Not all HNF1A variants are equally pathogenic. Over 1,000 distinct HNF1A variants have been catalogued in databases such as ClinVar, with classifications ranging from pathogenic and likely pathogenic to benign, likely benign, and variants of uncertain significance. Some studies have shown that certain HNF1A mutations, while classified as pathogenic, have minimal effects on protein function, and some variants that are insufficient to cause MODY independently may instead be associated with an increased risk of type 2 diabetes. Distinguishing a truly pathogenic MODY-causing variant from a risk allele for type 2 diabetes requires integration of genetic evidence, functional domain analysis, computational prediction, and family segregation data—precisely the approach taken in this case. The discovery of this novel variant expands the known spectrum of HNF1A mutations relevant to precision medicine, as each new pathogenic variant improves the sensitivity of genetic screening panels and the accuracy of future variant classification.

## Treatment Strategy and Precision Medicine in HNF1A-MODY

At the time of diagnosis, this patient presented with markedly elevated glycemic parameters—an HbA1c of 10.2% (88 mmol/mol) and fasting glucose of 12.4 mmol/L—necessitating prompt intervention to achieve glycemic control. Because genetic testing had not yet been performed, the initial treatment strategy was empirically tailored to the working clinical suspicion of type 2 diabetes (T2D), which was the most likely diagnosis given the patient's BMI of 24.6 kg/m² and lipid profile suggestive of possible metabolic syndrome. The initial regimen combined basal insulin degludec (8 units/day), metformin (1500 mg/day), and the DPP-4 inhibitor saxagliptin (5 mg/day). This three-pronged approach addressed several therapeutic targets simultaneously: basal insulin provided exogenous coverage to rapidly suppress hepatic glucose output and correct severe hyperglycemia, metformin addressed potential insulin resistance, and saxagliptin augmented endogenous incretin-mediated insulin secretion. This approach is a pragmatic, evidence-based strategy for initial management of severe hyperglycemia when the definitive diabetes subtype has not yet been established and reflects the real-world clinical reality that molecular genetic results typically require weeks to obtain.

The rationale for initiating treatment before genetic confirmation is twofold. First, the degree of hyperglycemia present at diagnosis warranted immediate pharmacologic intervention to reduce glucotoxicity and prevent acute complications. Second, at this stage, the clinical picture was ambiguous: the patient had features that could be consistent with T2D (BMI, lipid abnormalities) alongside features suggestive of MODY (young age, strong family history of early-onset diabetes, absence of acanthosis nigricans, negative islet autoantibodies). The glucagon stimulation test demonstrated a stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L—above the 0.60 nmol/L threshold generally indicating preserved endogenous insulin secretion—yet still insufficient for normal glycemic regulation. This result was compatible with either early T2D or MODY and did not definitively resolve the diagnostic question. Initiating a regimen that could address multiple potential pathophysiologies was therefore clinically appropriate.

Following genetic confirmation of HNF1A-MODY one month later, the treatment strategy was fundamentally revised. The patient's HbA1c had already decreased to 9.1% on the initial regimen, reflecting a meaningful response. Given the genetic diagnosis, the patient's moderately preserved yet insufficient β-cell function, and the goal of preserving residual pancreatic function, insulin therapy was discontinued and replaced with dulaglutide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist administered at 1.5 mg weekly. After 6 months of this modified regimen, the patient's HbA1c decreased dramatically to 6.0%. As glycemic control stabilized, the regimen was further simplified to combination therapy with metformin (1500 mg/day) and saxagliptin (5 mg/day) for long-term maintenance, with HbA1c consistently maintained below 6.5% on follow-up.

The transition from insulin to a GLP-1 receptor agonist in this case is grounded in the evolving understanding of HNF1A-MODY pharmacotherapy. Traditionally, low-dose sulfonylureas have been considered first-line therapy for HNF1A-MODY. Sulfonylureas enhance insulin secretion by binding to and closing ATP-sensitive potassium (K_ATP) channels on the β-cell membrane, thereby depolarizing the cell and triggering calcium-mediated insulin granule exocytosis. Patients with HNF1A-MODY often exhibit particular sensitivity to sulfonylureas, which is thought to reflect the specific defect in glucose-stimulated insulin secretion pathways while K_ATP channel function remains relatively intact. However, as β-cell function progressively declines in HNF1A-MODY—a hallmark of the disease's natural history—sulfonylureas become less effective and carry a increasing risk of hypoglycemia, since their mechanism is independent of ambient glucose levels and they stimulate insulin secretion even when plasma glucose is low.

GLP-1 receptor agonists offer a mechanistically distinct and potentially superior alternative for HNF1A-MODY patients with declining β-cell function. These agents activate GLP-1 receptors on pancreatic β-cells, enhancing glucose-dependent insulin secretion—meaning their insulinotropic effects are amplified in the presence of elevated glucose and attenuated during hypoglycemia, thereby intrinsically reducing the risk of hypoglycemia. Clinical evidence supporting this approach includes a small randomized, double-blind, crossover trial demonstrating that liraglutide and glimepiride (a sulfonylurea) were comparably effective in reducing fasting plasma glucose and postprandial glucose fluctuations in HNF1A-MODY patients, but GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy was associated with a lower risk of mild hypoglycemia. This evidence base supported the decision in the present case to transition to dulaglutide, particularly given the patient's documented but insufficient β-cell reserve and the goal of preserving pancreatic function while minimizing hypoglycemic risk.

An important pharmacological consideration specific to HNF1A-MODY involves the use of SGLT-2 inhibitors, a drug class increasingly used in T2D management. In HNF1A-MODY, glycosuria occurs as a characteristic feature due to reduced expression of SGLT2 (sodium-glucose cotransporter 2) in the renal proximal tubule, where SGLT2 transcription is directly regulated by HNF1A. Because SGLT2-mediated glucose reabsorption is already functionally impaired in these patients, pharmacologic SGLT-2 inhibition risks compounding this defect, potentially leading to severe dehydration, euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis, and even vascular collapse. Case reports have documented HNF1A-MODY patients experiencing severe dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis due to high inhibition of residual SGLT2 activity. The risks of genital infections and ketoacidosis associated with SGLT-2 inhibitors may therefore outweigh potential benefits in HNF1A-MODY, and this drug class should generally be avoided. This consideration illustrates how knowledge of the specific molecular defect can prevent iatrogenic harm—another dimension of precision medicine beyond simply selecting the optimal therapy.

This case exemplifies the core principle of precision medicine in diabetes management: genetic diagnosis directly enabled treatment de-escalation from insulin therapy to an oral and injectable non-insulin regimen that was more appropriate for the underlying pathophysiology. Without genetic confirmation, the patient might have remained on insulin indefinitely, treated as having T2D with secondary failure or even as having slowly progressive type 1 diabetes. The ability to discontinue insulin after achieving initial glycemic control and transition to a GLP-1 receptor agonist that preserved β-cell function while maintaining excellent glycemic control (HbA1c from 10.2% to 6.0% within 6 months) underscores how molecular diagnosis can transform management. This case further demonstrates that precision medicine in diabetes operates bidirectionally—not only by guiding the selection of optimal therapy but also by identifying treatments to avoid, such as SGLT-2 inhibitors in HNF1A-MODY. The successful simplification of the long-term regimen to metformin and saxagliptin, with sustained HbA1c below 6.5% and no evidence of microvascular complications on follow-up, validates the treatment algorithm applied and reinforces the principle that accurate genetic diagnosis is not merely an academic exercise but a clinical tool with direct therapeutic consequences.

## Clinical Outcome, Diagnostic Challenges, and Broader Implications

The patient's treatment trajectory vividly illustrates how genetic diagnosis can reshape clinical management. At the time of diagnosis, his glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) was markedly elevated at 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), prompting initiation of basal insulin degludec (eight units daily) alongside metformin (1500 mg/day) and the DPP-4 inhibitor saxagliptin (5 mg/day). This regimen reflected a preliminary assumption of type 2 diabetes, aiming to achieve rapid glycemic control while addressing potential insulin resistance. After one month, HbA1c decreased to 9.1%, indicating a partial but incomplete response. However, once genetic testing confirmed HNF1A-MODY, the clinical approach fundamentally shifted. Recognizing that the patient retained moderately preserved—though insufficient—β-cell function, as evidenced by a stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L on glucagon stimulation testing, clinicians discontinued insulin therapy and transitioned him to dulaglutide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist administered at 1.5 mg weekly. This decision was grounded in the pathophysiology of HNF1A-MODY: the disease involves a progressive decline in β-cell secretory capacity rather than complete autoimmune β-cell destruction, making incretin-based therapies a rational choice to support remaining endogenous insulin secretion while avoiding the risks associated with exogenous insulin. After six months of dulaglutide therapy combined with metformin, the patient's HbA1c decreased dramatically to 6.0%, representing a clinically meaningful reduction of over four percentage points from baseline. As glycemic control stabilized, the regimen was simplified to metformin (1500 mg/day) and saxagliptin (5 mg/day) for long-term maintenance. At the time of the report, HbA1c remained consistently below 6.5%, and ongoing surveillance had detected no microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy, though the patient remains under regular follow-up for long-term complication monitoring.

The avoidance of SGLT-2 inhibitors in this patient warrants attention, as it reflects a key pharmacogenomic consideration specific to HNF1A-MODY. HNF1A serves as a transcription factor for the SGLT2 gene in renal proximal tubules, and mutations in HNF1A reduce SGLT2 expression, lowering the renal threshold for glucose reabsorption and producing the glycosuria that can be an early clue to the diagnosis. Because SGLT-2 inhibitors further suppress residual SGLT2 activity, they can precipitate severe dehydration, euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis, and potential vascular collapse in HNF1A-MODY patients. This case therefore underscores how a precise genetic diagnosis not only opens therapeutic options—such as GLP-1 receptor agonists—but also closes doors to medications that might be harmful, a cornerstone of precision medicine.

Beyond the individual patient, this case illuminates the persistent diagnostic challenges surrounding MODY. Maturity-onset diabetes of the young accounts for approximately 1%–6% of all young-onset diabetes, yet it remains frequently misdiagnosed as type 1 or type 2 diabetes. The clinical overlap is substantial: patients with HNF1A-MODY can present with polydipsia, polyuria, polyphagia, and weight loss—symptoms indistinguishable from either type 1 or type 2 diabetes. In this case, the patient had a BMI of 24.6 kg/m², a lipid profile suggesting possible metabolic syndrome (low HDL at 1.01 mmol/L and elevated triglycerides at 4.31 mmol/L), and severely reduced sudomotor function on electrochemical skin conductance testing, findings that could easily steer a clinician toward a diagnosis of type 2 diabetes with early neuropathy. Historically, MODY was distinguished from type 2 diabetes by the absence of obesity and insulin resistance, but as obesity rates rise globally, BMI and metabolic markers have become less reliable discriminators. This erosion of phenotypic boundaries means that clinical features alone are increasingly insufficient to identify MODY, and clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion.

Several features of this case, taken together, should have prompted early consideration of MODY: the early age of onset, the strong matrilineal family history (grandmother diagnosed in her 30s, mother in her late 20s, younger brother at age 20), the absence of diabetic ketoacidosis at presentation despite marked hyperglycemia, negative islet autoantibodies (GADA and IA-2A), and preserved C-peptide secretion. The Exeter MODY probability calculator, when applied to this patient, yielded an estimated MODY probability of 62.4%. However, this tool was derived from White European populations and may overestimate probability in non-White groups, a limitation relevant here given that this patient was likely of Asian descent. This highlights a broader problem: much of the foundational MODY literature, diagnostic algorithms, and prevalence data originate from White European cohorts, and their applicability to other ethnic populations remains uncertain. Recent epidemiologic studies have begun to address this gap. A study by Chen et al found that among 2,429 young Chinese adults with early-onset type 2 diabetes, the prevalence of MODY was 1.5%, with 0.74% carrying pathogenic or likely pathogenic MODY variants. A Danish study by Møller et al revealed that among patients with a clinical diagnosis of type 1 diabetes who lacked high-risk HLA haplotypes, 10% harbored HNF1A mutations. A population-based Russian study screening 506 individuals suspected of having hereditary diabetes—selected based on negative autoantibodies, preserved C-peptide, low insulin requirements, and absence of severe obesity—identified pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in 46.4%, with HNF1A among the most commonly affected genes. These findings collectively suggest that MODY is underdiagnosed across diverse populations and that current clinical screening criteria may miss a substantial fraction of affected individuals.

The identification of a novel HNF1A variant in this case—p.N270S (c.809A>G)—further illustrates both the power and the complexity of modern genetic diagnostics. This heterozygous substitution, located in exon 4 within the DNA-binding domain of HNF1A, was absent from ClinVar, VarSome, and published literature at the time of the report. Its classification as likely pathogenic relied not on database precedent but on convergent evidence: the variant affects an amino acid residue adjacent to known pathogenic residues, carries a high AlphaMissense pathogenicity score (0.995), and segregates with diabetes in the patient's family. These criteria align with American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines for variant interpretation. The case thus demonstrates that next-generation sequencing (NGS) can identify novel variants that traditional database lookups would miss, but it also underscores the necessity of rigorous functional and segregation analysis to establish clinical relevance. Over 1,000 distinct HNF1A variants have been catalogued, and not all are pathogenic; some have minimal effects on protein function, and others may merely increase type 2 diabetes risk rather than cause monogenic diabetes. Distinguishing among these possibilities demands both molecular expertise and clinical correlation.

The therapeutic and prognostic implications of a confirmed HNF1A-MODY diagnosis extend well beyond the index patient. First-line therapy for HNF1A-MODY classically involves low-dose sulfonylureas, which enhance insulin secretion by closing ATP-sensitive potassium channels on β-cells. However, as β-cell function progressively declines, sulfonylureas carry an increasing risk of hypoglycemia. GLP-1 receptor agonists offer a compelling alternative, as demonstrated by a randomized crossover trial showing that liraglutide and glimepiride achieved comparable reductions in fasting and postprandial glucose in HNF1A-MODY patients, with GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy carrying a lower risk of mild hypoglycemia. The successful use of dulaglutide in this case, followed by simplification to oral agents, exemplifies a staged therapeutic approach calibrated to the patient's residual β-cell capacity and supports the expanding role of incretin therapies in MODY management. Beyond treatment selection, genetic confirmation also enables prognosis estimation, complication risk assessment, and cascade family screening. The autosomal dominant inheritance pattern means that first-degree relatives carry a 50% probability of the same mutation, and identification of the variant in this patient creates an opportunity for predictive testing and early intervention in family members, including his younger brother who was already diagnosed with diabetes at age 20.

Looking forward, the case highlights both the promise and the remaining barriers to precision medicine in diabetes. NGS technology now allows simultaneous analysis of multiple MODY-associated genes—over 15 have been identified—in a single reaction, dramatically improving screening feasibility compared with sequential Sanger sequencing. Yet significant obstacles persist: clinical awareness of MODY remains low, genetic testing is not universally accessible or reimbursed, and diagnostic algorithms calibrated in White European populations may not perform reliably in other ethnic groups. The development of population-specific screening strategies, broader NGS implementation, and improved cost-effectiveness analyses will be essential to close the diagnostic gap. As this case demonstrates, the stakes are tangible: a patient initially treated with insulin—appropriate for type 1 diabetes but unnecessary for HNF1A-MODY—was successfully transitioned to a regimen that preserved endogenous β-cell function, achieved sustained glycemic control, and simplified over time. The broader lesson is that in any young patient with diabetes, a family history of early-onset disease, negative autoantibodies, and preserved C-peptide, genetic testing should be pursued rather than deferred, because the resulting diagnosis can transform not only the treatment plan but also the trajectory of the patient's disease and the health of their family.
