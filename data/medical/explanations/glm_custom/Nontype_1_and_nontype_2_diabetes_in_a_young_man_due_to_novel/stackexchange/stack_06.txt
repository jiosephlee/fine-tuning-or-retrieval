Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Morning Report] The glucagon stimulation test was used in this case to assess residual beta-cell function, showing a stimulated C-peptide level of 0.83 nmol/L. What does this value indicate about endogenous insulin secretion, and how does this test help differentiate MODY from type 1 diabetes?

## Clinical Teaching Answer

The glucagon stimulation test exploits the ability of glucagon to acutely stimulate insulin secretion from pancreatic β-cells, providing a dynamic measure of residual β-cell function. Because C-peptide is cosecreted with insulin on an equimolar basis but is not confounded by exogenous insulin or hepatic first-pass metabolism, it serves as the preferred biomarker for endogenous insulin secretion.

In this case, the patient's stimulated C-peptide level of 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes exceeds the commonly cited threshold of 0.60 nmol/L, indicating that endogenous insulin secretion is **preserved, albeit insufficient**. The β-cells retain measurable secretory reserve and can respond to a secretagogue stimulus, yet the magnitude of response is impaired relative to what would be expected in normal physiology. This profile is consistent with the pathophysiology of HNF1A-MODY, in which mutations in the HNF1A transcription factor lead to progressive but partial β-cell dysfunction—through impaired insulin gene expression, reduced GLUT2 expression, and decreased pyruvate kinase activity—rather than the complete β-cell destruction characteristic of type 1 diabetes.

This distinction is central to the differential diagnosis. In classic type 1 diabetes, autoimmune-mediated β-cell destruction typically results in severely depressed or undetectable stimulated C-peptide levels, particularly beyond the early honeymoon period. A stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L would be distinctly atypical for established type 1 diabetes and, when interpreted alongside the patient's negative islet autoantibodies (GADA and IA-2A), absence of diabetic ketoacidosis, and strong multigenerational family history of early-onset diabetes, strongly supported the clinical suspicion of a non-autoimmune monogenic form of diabetes.

However, as the report emphasizes, the glucagon stimulation test should be regarded as **supportive rather than definitive**. C-peptide levels may still be measurable in the early stages of type 1 diabetes, and a single value must always be interpreted within the full clinical context. It was the convergence of preserved but blunted C-peptide responsiveness, seronegativity for islet autoantibodies, an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern, and ultimately confirmation by next-generation sequencing that established the diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY in this patient.
