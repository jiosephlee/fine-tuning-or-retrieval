Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Differential Diagnosis] What clinical features in this young patient—including BMI, family history, autoantibody status, and C-peptide results—suggested MODY rather than type 1 or type 2 diabetes, and what features made the distinction from type 2 diabetes particularly challenging?

# Distinguishing HNF1A-MODY from Type 1 and Type 2 Diabetes

## Features Favouring MODY over Type 1 Diabetes

Several findings in this patient pointed away from autoimmune type 1 diabetes. First, islet autoantibodies—both GADA and IA-2A—were negative. Second, the glucagon stimulation test demonstrated a stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes, above the 0.60 nmol/L threshold generally used to indicate preserved endogenous insulin secretion. This degree of residual β-cell function is atypical for classic type 1 diabetes, particularly at presentation with an HbA1c of 10.2%. Third, the patient presented without diabetic ketoacidosis. Finally, the multigenerational family history—maternal grandmother diagnosed in her 30s, mother in her late 20s, and younger brother at age 20—strongly suggested autosomal dominant inheritance, a defining characteristic of MODY.

## Features Favouring MODY over Type 2 Diabetes

In classic HNF1A-MODY, patients are typically young, non-obese, and lack features of insulin resistance. The absence of acanthosis nigricans on physical examination supported this profile. The glycosuria noted on routine examination is also characteristic, as HNF1A mutations reduce expression of SGLT2 in renal tubules, lowering the threshold for glucose reabsorption—a feature not typical of type 2 diabetes.

## Why the Distinction from Type 2 Diabetes Was Particularly Challenging

Despite these supportive features, several elements made differentiation from type 2 diabetes difficult. The patient's BMI was 24.6 kg/m²—normal, but not as low as classically described in MODY. As the authors note, rising obesity rates have eroded the diagnostic utility of BMI for this purpose. Furthermore, the lipid profile—low HDL of 1.01 mmol/L and triglycerides of 4.31 mmol/L—suggested possible metabolic syndrome, a feature more commonly associated with type 2 diabetes. The patient also presented with classic hyperglycaemic symptoms of polydipsia, polyphagia, and weight loss, which overlap across all diabetes subtypes. Finally, family history of diabetes is common in type 2 diabetes as well, limiting its discriminative value, particularly given the high population prevalence of type 2 diabetes.

## Clinical Bottom Line

No single clinical feature reliably distinguishes HNF1A-MODY from type 1 or type 2 diabetes. The combination of negative autoantibodies, preserved C-peptide, and a multigenerational autosomal dominant inheritance pattern should raise clinical suspicion and prompt genetic testing. As this case demonstrates, molecular diagnosis via next-generation sequencing is the definitive step that enables precision management—in this case, discontinuation of insulin in favour of a GLP-1 receptor agonist and metformin, with excellent glycaemic outcome.

### [Pathophysiology] HNF1A encodes a transcription factor expressed in the liver, kidneys, and pancreas. How do loss-of-function mutations in HNF1A lead to progressive beta-cell dysfunction, and which specific downstream target genes and enzymatic activities are impaired?

## Answer

HNF1A encodes hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha, a transcription factor expressed in the liver, kidneys, and pancreas that regulates a network of genes essential for pancreatic β-cell function. As described in this case, loss-of-function mutations in HNF1A produce progressive β-cell dysfunction through impairment of several critical downstream targets:

**Impaired insulin gene expression.** HNF1A directly regulates transcription of the insulin gene. Reduced HNF1A activity diminishes insulin synthesis, leaving β-cells unable to meet secretory demand, particularly as glucose excursions worsen over time.

**Reduced GLUT2 expression.** GLUT2 is the glucose transporter responsible for glucose entry into β-cells and serves as the proximal sensor for glucose-stimulated insulin secretion. When HNF1A function is impaired, GLUT2 expression falls, blunting the β-cell's ability to sense and respond to ambient glucose. This explains the marked postprandial hyperglycemia observed on oral glucose tolerance testing in this patient (2-hour glucose of 19.8 mmol/L).

**Decreased pyruvate kinase activity.** Pyruvate kinase is a key glycolytic enzyme whose activity is regulated downstream of HNF1A. Reduced activity impairs glycolytic flux, limiting ATP generation from glucose metabolism. Since glucose-stimulated insulin secretion depends on ATP-mediated closure of ATP-sensitive potassium channels, depolarization, calcium influx, and insulin granule exocytosis, diminished pyruvate kinase activity directly attenuates this secretory cascade. This is consistent with the patient's glucagon stimulation test demonstrating insufficient C-peptide response (stimulated C-peptide 0.83 nmol/L), confirming impaired β-cell reserve.

**Reduced SGLT2 expression in renal tubules.** Beyond the pancreas, HNF1A transcriptionally controls SGLT2 in the proximal renal tubule. Reduced SGLT2 expression lowers the renal threshold for glucose reabsorption, producing glycosuria even at modestly elevated glucose levels—a feature noted at presentation in this patient and characteristic of HNF1A-MODY.

In this case, the novel heterozygous p.N270S variant resides in the DNA-binding domain of HNF1A. Mutations in this domain impair the protein's ability to bind promoter regions of its target genes, thereby reducing transcription of insulin, GLUT2, and other metabolic genes while diminishing pyruvate kinase activity. The cumulative effect is a progressive decline in glucose-stimulated insulin secretion—initially manifesting as postprandial hyperglycemia and glycosuria, and eventually progressing to overt fasting hyperglycemia and symptomatic diabetes, as observed across three generations in this family.

This molecular framework also explains the treatment rationale: GLP-1 receptor agonists amplify the remaining β-cell secretory capacity through cAMP-mediated potentiation of insulin exocytosis, bypassing the impaired glycolytic sensing pathway—a strategy that proved effective in this patient after genetic confirmation.

### [Clinical Pearls] Patients with HNF1A-MODY often present with glycosuria at lower blood glucose levels than expected. What molecular mechanism accounts for this reduced renal threshold for glucose, and how does it relate to the tissue expression pattern of HNF1A?

## Clinical Pearl

The reduced renal threshold for glucose in HNF1A-MODY is explained by the transcriptional control that HNF1A exerts over **SGLT2** (sodium-glucose cotransporter 2) expression in the proximal renal tubule. HNF1A is not confined to the pancreatic β-cell—it is a transcription factor expressed in the **liver, kidneys, and pancreas**, as emphasized in this case report. In the kidney, HNF1A normally drives expression of SGLT2, the primary transporter responsible for reabsorbing the bulk of filtered glucose from the tubular lumen. When HNF1A is mutated, SGLT2 expression is reduced, impairing renal glucose reabsorption and causing glucose to appear in the urine at blood glucose levels lower than the normal threshold of approximately 10 mmol/L.

This mechanism elegantly illustrates why glycosuria in HNF1A-MODY is not simply a downstream consequence of hyperglycemia, but rather a **direct tissue-specific effect** of the mutation in the kidney—parallel to the β-cell dysfunction caused by the same mutation in the pancreas, where HNF1A regulates insulin and GLUT2 expression. The patient in this report presented with glycosuria on routine examination, which served as an early clue.

Clinically, this has two important implications. First, glycosuria at modestly elevated blood glucose levels should raise suspicion for HNF1A-MODY, particularly in young, non-obese, autoantibody-negative patients with a family history of early-onset diabetes. Second, as the case report highlights, **SGLT2 inhibitors should be used with caution** in HNF1A-MODY, because these patients already have functionally reduced SGLT2 activity. Pharmacologic inhibition of residual SGLT2 function can precipitate severe dehydration, euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis, or vascular collapse—a risk underscored by published reports of HNF1A-MODY patients developing diabetic ketoacidosis with these agents.

### [Pathophysiology] SGLT2 inhibitors are generally avoided in patients with HNF1A-MODY. What is the pathophysiologic rationale for this caution, and what specific clinical complications have been described in this population?

## Pathophysiologic Rationale for Avoiding SGLT2 Inhibitors in HNF1A-MODY

HNF1A encodes a transcription factor expressed in the liver, kidneys, and pancreas that directly regulates the expression of the sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) in the renal proximal tubule. In HNF1A-MODY, loss-of-function mutations in HNF1A lead to **reduced SGLT2 expression** at the basolateral membrane of renal tubular cells. This is precisely why patients with HNF1A-MODY characteristically exhibit **glycosuria at lower plasma glucose concentrations** — they already have a reduced renal threshold for glucose reabsorption, a distinguishing clinical feature illustrated in the present case, where glycosuria was detected on routine screening without ketoacidosis.

The concern with prescribing an SGLT2 inhibitor in this population is that pharmacologic inhibition would further suppress the **already diminished residual SGLT2 activity**, producing near-complete blockade of renal glucose reabsorption. As noted in the case report's discussion, this can result in **severe dehydration, diabetic ketoacidosis, potential vascular collapse, and genital infections** — complications described in HNF1A-MODY patients given SGLT2 inhibitors. The volume depletion and ketoacidosis risk reflects the compounding effect of pharmacologic inhibition on a genetically impaired tubular transport system, where the physiologic reserve for glucose reabsorption is already compromised.

This stands in contrast to the treatment approach successfully employed in the present case. After genetic confirmation of the novel HNF1A variant (p.N270S), insulin was discontinued and the patient was transitioned to a GLP-1 receptor agonist (dulaglutide) with metformin, achieving an HbA1c reduction from 10.2% to 6.0%. This strategy leverages the preserved — though insufficient — β-cell function characteristic of HNF1A-MODY without imposing additional stress on the renal glucose transport pathway. The case underscores that **precision medicine in MODY extends beyond establishing a genetic diagnosis to selecting therapies compatible with the underlying molecular pathophysiology**, and that SGLT2 inhibitors represent a class whose mechanism directly conflicts with the renal tubular defect inherent to HNF1A-MODY.

### [Management] What is the recommended first-line pharmacologic treatment for HNF1A-MODY, and how does its mechanism of action directly address the underlying beta-cell defect?

## Answer

**Low-dose sulfonylureas are the recommended first-line pharmacologic therapy for HNF1A-MODY.** These agents directly address the core pathophysiologic defect—impaired insulin secretion from pancreatic beta cells—by binding to and closing ATP-sensitive potassium (K_ATP) channels on the beta-cell membrane. This closure depolarizes the membrane, opens voltage-gated calcium channels, and triggers calcium-dependent exocytosis of insulin granules, thereby augmenting the insufficient insulin secretion that characterizes this disorder.

This mechanism is particularly fitting because HNF1A-MODY is not a disease of insulin resistance but rather a primary beta-cell secretory defect. As detailed in the case report, mutations in HNF1A—a transcription factor expressed in the pancreas, liver, and kidneys—lead to progressive beta-cell dysfunction through impaired expression of insulin, GLUT2, and pyruvate kinase, ultimately resulting in decreased insulin secretion and glucose intolerance. The patient's glucagon stimulation test illustrated this clearly: stimulated C-peptide was only 0.83 nmol/L, confirming insufficient but residual beta-cell function. Sulfonylureas leverage this residual capacity, pharmacologically amplifying insulin release to compensate for the transcriptional defect.

It is worth emphasizing, as the case report does, that as beta-cell function declines over time, sulfonylureas carry an increasing risk of hypoglycemia. In such circumstances, GLP-1 receptor agonists may serve as a safer and equally effective alternative. A randomized crossover trial cited by the authors demonstrated comparable glycemic reduction between liraglutide and glimepiride in HNF1A-MODY, with a lower risk of mild hypoglycemia with the GLP-1 agonist. In this patient, after initial stabilization with insulin (started prior to genetic diagnosis), the confirming genetic result allowed discontinuation of insulin and transition to dulaglutide, which reduced HbA1c from 9.1% to 6.0% over six months. This sequence nicely illustrates the principle of precision medicine: once the monogenic diagnosis was established, therapy could be rationally redirected toward agents that support rather than replace residual beta-cell function.

**Clinical takeaway:** In HNF1A-MODY, low-dose sulfonylureas remain first-line because they pharmacologically overcome the beta-cell secretory defect. GLP-1 receptor agonists represent a valuable alternative or successor as beta-cell reserve diminishes, and SGLT-2 inhibitors should be avoided given the already reduced renal SGLT2 expression under HNF1A transcriptional control, which predisposes to glycosuria, dehydration, and potential ketoacidosis.

### [Morning Report] The glucagon stimulation test was used in this case to assess residual beta-cell function, showing a stimulated C-peptide level of 0.83 nmol/L. What does this value indicate about endogenous insulin secretion, and how does this test help differentiate MODY from type 1 diabetes?

## Clinical Teaching Answer

The glucagon stimulation test exploits the ability of glucagon to acutely stimulate insulin secretion from pancreatic β-cells, providing a dynamic measure of residual β-cell function. Because C-peptide is cosecreted with insulin on an equimolar basis but is not confounded by exogenous insulin or hepatic first-pass metabolism, it serves as the preferred biomarker for endogenous insulin secretion.

In this case, the patient's stimulated C-peptide level of 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes exceeds the commonly cited threshold of 0.60 nmol/L, indicating that endogenous insulin secretion is **preserved, albeit insufficient**. The β-cells retain measurable secretory reserve and can respond to a secretagogue stimulus, yet the magnitude of response is impaired relative to what would be expected in normal physiology. This profile is consistent with the pathophysiology of HNF1A-MODY, in which mutations in the HNF1A transcription factor lead to progressive but partial β-cell dysfunction—through impaired insulin gene expression, reduced GLUT2 expression, and decreased pyruvate kinase activity—rather than the complete β-cell destruction characteristic of type 1 diabetes.

This distinction is central to the differential diagnosis. In classic type 1 diabetes, autoimmune-mediated β-cell destruction typically results in severely depressed or undetectable stimulated C-peptide levels, particularly beyond the early honeymoon period. A stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L would be distinctly atypical for established type 1 diabetes and, when interpreted alongside the patient's negative islet autoantibodies (GADA and IA-2A), absence of diabetic ketoacidosis, and strong multigenerational family history of early-onset diabetes, strongly supported the clinical suspicion of a non-autoimmune monogenic form of diabetes.

However, as the report emphasizes, the glucagon stimulation test should be regarded as **supportive rather than definitive**. C-peptide levels may still be measurable in the early stages of type 1 diabetes, and a single value must always be interpreted within the full clinical context. It was the convergence of preserved but blunted C-peptide responsiveness, seronegativity for islet autoantibodies, an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern, and ultimately confirmation by next-generation sequencing that established the diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY in this patient.

### [Clinical Pearls] The MODY probability calculator from the Exeter group estimated a 62.4% probability of MODY in this patient. What are the key limitations of this calculator when applied to non-White populations, and why might BMI and metabolic syndrome be increasingly unreliable for distinguishing MODY from type 2 diabetes?

The Exeter MODY probability calculator is a clinically useful screening tool, but its key limitation is that it was derived and validated exclusively in White European populations. As the case report explicitly notes, when applied to non-White groups, the calculator may **overestimate** the probability of MODY. In this patient—a young Taiwanese man with a strong maternal family history of early-onset diabetes, negative islet autoantibodies, and preserved C-peptide—the calculator returned a 62.4% probability (1 in 1.6 chance). While this high probability correctly flagged the patient as a candidate for genetic testing, the authors caution that the tool's discriminative performance has not been validated in Asian or other non-White populations, where both the prevalence of specific MODY subtypes and the background prevalence of early-onset type 2 diabetes may differ substantially. This means the calculator can produce a high-probability result that may not translate into the same positive predictive value in non-European cohorts.

The unreliability of BMI and metabolic syndrome as distinguishing features reflects an important epidemiological shift. Historically, MODY patients were characterised by low BMI and absence of metabolic syndrome, which contrasted sharply with the typical T2D phenotype. However, as the case report observes, **rising obesity rates have eroded this distinction**. Patients with HNF1A-MODY can now present with BMI values in the overweight range and with dyslipidaemia suggestive of metabolic syndrome, as seen in this patient, whose BMI was 24.6 kg/m² and whose lipid profile showed low HDL (1.01 mmol/L) and elevated triglycerides (4.31 mmol/L). These features, which would have previously pointed toward T2D, coexisted with a pathogenic HNF1A variant. Similarly, family history—long considered a cornerstone of MODY suspicion—is increasingly confounded by the high background prevalence of T2D in many populations, under-reporting across generations, and the possibility of *de novo* mutations.

The practical takeaway is this: no single clinical feature—BMI, metabolic syndrome phenotype, or even family history—can reliably separate MODY from T2D in the modern era. The Exeter calculator should be used as a triage tool with the understanding that its probability estimates are population-dependent, and genetic testing via next-generation sequencing remains the definitive diagnostic step, particularly in non-White patients where clinical phenotypes increasingly overlap.

### [Management] After genetic confirmation of HNF1A-MODY, insulin was discontinued and replaced with dulaglutide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist. What clinical trial evidence supports the use of GLP-1 receptor agonists in HNF1A-MODY, and what advantages do they offer over sulfonylureas?

## Clinical Teaching Answer

The key trial evidence cited in this case report is a **small, randomised, double-blind, crossover trial** comparing the GLP-1 receptor agonist **liraglutide** with the sulfonylurea **glimepiride** in patients with genetically confirmed HNF1A-MODY. That trial demonstrated that both agents effectively reduced fasting plasma glucose and postprandial glucose fluctuations, with **no significant difference in glycaemic efficacy** between the two treatments. However, GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy was associated with a **lower risk of mild hypoglycaemia** compared with glimepiride.

This distinction matters because sulfonylureas have long been the first-line therapy for HNF1A-MODY, acting on ATP-sensitive potassium channels to enhance residual β-cell insulin secretion. While effective, sulfonylureas carry a dose-dependent hypoglycaemia risk that becomes increasingly problematic as β-cell function progressively declines—a hallmark of the natural history of HNF1A-MODY, which results from impaired insulin and GLUT2 gene expression and reduced pyruvate kinase activity. GLP-1 receptor agonists offer a mechanistically distinct approach: they potentiate glucose-dependent insulin secretion, meaning their glycaemic effect wanes as circulating glucose falls, thereby providing a built-in safeguard against hypoglycaemia. The trial data suggest that this safety advantage can be obtained without sacrificing glycaemic control.

In the present case, these principles were applied directly. After genetic confirmation of the novel HNF1A variant and recognition that the patient had moderately preserved but insufficient β-cell function (stimulated C-peptide 0.83 nmol/L), insulin was discontinued and dulaglutide was initiated. HbA1c fell from 9.1% to 6.0% within 6 months, and the regimen was subsequently simplified to metformin and a DPP-4 inhibitor for long-term maintenance. This outcome illustrates the practical value of the trial evidence: GLP-1 receptor agonists can serve as a safer and equally effective alternative to sulfonylureas, particularly in patients prone to hypoglycaemia or in those whose β-cell reserve is already compromised. It also underscores how genetic diagnosis enables precision medicine—transitioning this young patient away from lifelong insulin therapy toward an oral and injectable non-insulin regimen better suited to his underlying pathophysiology.

### [Pathophysiology] The novel HNF1A variant in this case (p.N270S, c.809A>G) was classified as likely pathogenic according to ACMG guidelines despite being absent from ClinVar and other variant databases. What specific lines of genetic and clinical evidence supported this pathogenicity classification?

## Answer

The classification of p.N270S (c.809A>G) as "likely pathogenic" was supported by three independent lines of evidence that together satisfy ACMG criteria, even though the variant had not been previously catalogued.

**First**, the variant affects an amino acid residue adjacent to known pathogenic residues within the DNA-binding domain of HNF1A — the functional domain in which the majority of disease-causing HNF1A mutations cluster. This proximity to established pathogenic residues in a critical and well-established functional domain supports the ACMG moderate-strength criterion PM1.

**Second**, computational predictive tools assigned a high AlphaMissense score of 0.995, strongly suggesting a deleterious effect on protein function. This satisfies ACMG supporting criterion PP3, which recognizes concordant in silico evidence across multiple predictive algorithms.

**Third**, the variant segregated with the diabetes phenotype across multiple generations: the patient's maternal grandmother was diagnosed with diabetes in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at age 20. This autosomal dominant pattern of early-onset diabetes is consistent with the known inheritance pattern of HNF1A-MODY and supports ACMG criterion PP1 (co-segregation with disease in multiple affected family members).

The clinical context further reinforced the classification: the patient was a young, non-obese adult with negative islet autoantibodies (GADA and IA-2A), insufficient but preserved β-cell function on glucagon stimulation testing, and glycosuria attributable to reduced renal tubular glucose reabsorption — all features characteristic of HNF1A-MODY. The absence of the variant from ClinVar and other databases speaks to its novelty rather than calling into question its clinical significance. Under ACMG guidelines, a novel variant can be classified as likely pathogenic when moderate and supporting levels of evidence converge across computational, functional-domain, and segregation data, even in the absence of prior reports.
