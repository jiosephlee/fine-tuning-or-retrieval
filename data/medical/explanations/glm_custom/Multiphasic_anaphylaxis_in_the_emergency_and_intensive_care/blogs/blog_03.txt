Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# Biphasic, Multiphasic, Refractory: Making Sense of Complex Anaphylaxis Terminology and Management

You're managing a straightforward anaphylaxis case. IM epinephrine given, symptoms resolve, patient looks great — until four hours later when they crash again. Then again. Then *again*. Four discrete episodes of anaphylactic shock in twelve hours, each requiring intramuscular epinephrine, despite no re-exposure to the trigger. Now what? Do you start a continuous epinephrine infusion preemptively? Do you watch and wait? Do you call allergology and hope they have an answer? As it turns out, nobody really knows — and the literature offers essentially zero guidance for multiphasic anaphylaxis. Let's unpack why.

## The Case: Four Episodes, One Trigger, Zero Roadmap

A woman in her 60s presented to the ED with anaphylaxis one hour after taking her first-ever dose of oral clarithromycin 500 mg. She'd been switched to clarithromycin five days into a clindamycin course after developing a delayed rash — and her only prior allergic history was a mild facial swelling reaction to amoxicillin in adolescence and a strong local reaction to a wasp sting. On presentation, she was hypotensive (74/50), hypoxemic (88% on room air), with generalized erythema, urticaria, facial swelling, and throat tightness. She received 0.3 mg IM epinephrine via auto-injector, IV clemastine, oxygen, and 500 mL normal saline — and responded well.

Then it came back. Over the next 12 hours, she had three additional discrete anaphylactic episodes, each requiring IM epinephrine. The first two doses were 0.3 mg via auto-injector; the next two were 0.5 mg drawn up individually. After the first recurrence, she was transferred to the ICU, where allergology was consulted. Corticosteroids were added — both prednisone 30 mg IV daily for five days *and* a single dose of dexamethasone 8 mg, thanks to conflicting protocols between the ward and ICU. Her symptoms gradually diminished in intensity with each episode, from deep hypoxaemia with cyanosis and hypotension initially to rash and pruritus with dyspnea by the final episode. After 24 hours without recurrence, she stepped down to a standard ward and was discharged after three days.

Further workup revealed elevated tryptase — both during the third episode and at baseline — along with hepatomegaly on abdominal ultrasound and a history of depression and memory problems. This pointed toward systemic mastocytosis, though she met only two WHO minor criteria and fell short of a formal diagnosis. Notably, tryptase sampling did not follow recommended timing protocols, limiting diagnostic certainty.

## Getting the Terminology Right

One of the biggest problems in this space is that the terms are used inconsistently, and that inconsistency has real clinical consequences. Let's clarify.

**Biphasic anaphylaxis** is a recurrence of symptoms after apparent and complete resolution, occurring in the absence of any new allergen exposure. The asymptomatic interval is what distinguishes it from sustained or refractory disease. Most biphasic reactions occur within 6–12 hours, but reports extend to 78 hours. The incidence is reported anywhere from 0.4% to 23.3%, though most credible estimates land around 4–6%.

**Multiphasic anaphylaxis** — as illustrated by this case — involves *multiple* recurrences: three, four, or more discrete episodes separated by asymptomatic intervals. This is distinct from biphasic and far less studied. To our knowledge, no research has specifically examined the role of systemic mastocytosis in biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis.

**Refractory anaphylaxis** is something different entirely: persistent anaphylaxis that does not respond — or responds inadequately — to correctly administered epinephrine. There is no asymptomatic interval. The patient is sick continuously.

The clinical challenge is that differentiating a true multiphasic course from sustained anaphylaxis with only transient response to epinephrine — or from ongoing allergen absorption, as with slow-release medications or retained food allergens — can be extraordinarily difficult. In this case, the clarithromycin was a single oral dose, and each episode was separated by a clear period of clinical stability, making a true multiphasic course the most plausible interpretation.

## The Management Void

Here's where things get uncomfortable. When this patient kept relapsing, the treating team and the allergology consultant weighed two strategies: start a **continuous IV epinephrine infusion** to preemptively treat before recurrence, or take an **expectant approach** and observe whether symptoms would resolve on their own given the decreasing intensity with each episode. Both carry genuine risks. Continuous epinephrine in a patient who may not need it introduces its own hazards — tachyarrhythmias, hypertension, myocardial ischemia — particularly in a patient with diabetes and hypertension. Expectant observation risks a severe recurrence occurring between nursing checks.

In the end, neither was needed — the patient simply stopped relapsing. But you cannot plan around that. The deeper problem is that there is essentially no evidence to guide this decision. Guidelines for biphasic anaphylaxis primarily address observation duration, not active management of ongoing recurrences. For multiphasic disease specifically, the literature is essentially silent. No trials, no consensus statements, no expert-based protocols. The team in this case was flying without instruments.

## Epinephrine Dosing: The 0.3 mg Problem

This case also highlights a dosing issue that deserves more attention than it gets. The first two epinephrine doses were delivered via auto-injector at 0.3 mg. Guidelines recommend 0.01 mg/kg up to a maximum of 0.5 mg for adults — and most adults weigh more than 50 kg. At 104 kg, this patient's ideal dose was 0.5 mg, yet she received 40% less on her first two episodes. A 2025 retrospective study showed that patients who initially received 0.3 mg epinephrine needed escalation of care — additional epinephrine injections — significantly more often than those who received 0.5 mg initially.

The rationale for using auto-injectors in-hospital is understandable: they minimize treatment delay and reduce hesitancy among staff who rarely administer vasoactive medications. But the tradeoff is a potentially critical underdose. If you're going to use an auto-injector in an adult over 50 kg, recognize that you may need to supplement with an additional dose drawn from a vial. Better yet, train your team to draw up 0.5 mg from a vial as the default — and save auto-injectors for the prehospital setting where they belong.

## The Steroid Question

This patient received both prednisone *and* dexamethasone due to conflicting ward and ICU protocols — a perfect illustration of how entrenched steroid use remains despite the evidence shifting. A 2017 systematic review of 22 studies found only one supporting corticosteroid use for preventing biphasic anaphylaxis, with the other 21 showing no benefit. A 2019 study demonstrated an *increased* likelihood of ICU or ward admission after prehospital corticosteroid treatment. Most contemporary guidelines now advise against routine corticosteroid administration in anaphylaxis. Yet protocols lag behind, and clinicians continue to reach for them reflexively — sometimes, as in this case, in duplicate.

## Mastocytosis: The Hidden Predisposition

The elevated baseline tryptase, hepatomegaly, and neuropsychiatric symptoms in this patient strongly suggest systemic mastocytosis — a condition involving abnormal mast cell proliferation and excessive mediator release that predisposes patients to severe and potentially recurrent anaphylaxis. She did not meet formal WHO diagnostic criteria, but the probability was high. This is clinically important because patients with mast cell disorders may behave differently in terms of recurrence risk and treatment response, and identifying them changes your long-term management and counseling.

One critical pearl from this case: **tryptase timing matters, and it was done wrong here.** Guidelines recommend a minimum of two, ideally three samples: the first as soon as feasible without delaying treatment, a second 1–2 hours after symptom onset (when tryptase peaks), and a third at least 24 hours after full resolution for baseline. In this case, the first sample was taken an hour after the *third* anaphylactic episode — missing the recommended window entirely for the first two episodes — and the second was taken 27 hours after the last recurrence. If you suspect mastocytosis or need to confirm anaphylaxis biochemically, get the timing right. Your allergology colleagues will thank you, and your patient's diagnostic workup will actually be interpretable.

## A Framework for Recurrent Anaphylaxis

Until we have evidence, here's a practical approach drawn from this case and the available literature:

- **Confirm you're dealing with true multiphasic disease.** Rule out ongoing allergen exposure — check that no slow-release formulation or retained allergen is continuing to drive the reaction. Verify that each episode is separated by genuine asymptomatic intervals, not just partial response to epinephrine.
- **Dose epinephrine correctly from the start.** 0.5 mg IM for adults over 50 kg. If you use an auto-injector, be prepared to supplement.
- **Escalate monitoring.** These patients belong in a high-acuity setting with continuous monitoring. Repeated IM epinephrine without telemetry is asking for trouble.
- **Engage allergology early.** Not just for discharge planning — for real-time management input on whether to start continuous infusion versus observe.
- **Get tryptase levels at the right times.** This is your best shot at identifying underlying mast cell disease and confirming the diagnosis.
- **Think twice about steroids.** The evidence does not support them for preventing biphasic reactions. If your protocol still includes them, it's probably time to update it.
- **Have an escalation plan.** Decide in advance: if recurrence happens again, what is the threshold for starting an epinephrine infusion? Document it so the next clinician isn't starting from scratch.

## The Bottom Line

- Multiphasic anaphylaxis — multiple discrete recurrences separated by asymptomatic intervals — is distinct from biphasic and refractory disease, and the literature provides almost no guidance for its management.
- Differentiating true multiphasic reactions from sustained anaphylaxis with transient epinephrine response or ongoing allergen absorption requires careful clinical assessment; a clear asymptomatic interval and excluded ongoing exposure support a multiphasic diagnosis.
- Adult epinephrine dosing should be 0.5 mg IM; 0.3 mg auto-injectors underdose most adults and are associated with more frequent need for escalation of care.
- Corticosteroids do not prevent biphasic anaphylaxis based on current evidence; their routine use should be abandoned, and conflicting institutional protocols should be harmonized.
- Systemic mastocytosis is a likely predisposing condition for complex anaphylaxis and should be considered when tryptase is elevated at baseline — but tryptase must be sampled at the recommended timepoints to be diagnostically useful.
- There is no evidence-based or expert consensus guidance for choosing between continuous IV epinephrine infusion and expectant observation in multiphasic anaphylaxis; clinical judgment, monitoring intensity, and early allergology involvement are your best tools.
- A national or international registry of complex anaphylaxis cases — capturing patient characteristics, management strategies, and outcomes — is urgently needed to generate the data required for meaningful expert-based recommendations.
