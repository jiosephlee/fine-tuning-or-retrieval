Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# When Anaphylaxis Won't Stop: Systemic Mastocytosis as a Hidden Driver of Severe, Recurrent Reactions

## The Case That Makes You Rethink "Standard" Anaphylaxis

You treated the anaphylaxis. The patient responded. You admitted for observation. Then it came back. And again. And again.

A woman in her 60s presented to the ED with anaphylaxis one hour after her first-ever dose of oral clarithromycin 500 mg — prescribed for a rash that developed during a course of clindamycin for erysipelas. She had a known beta-lactam allergy (facial swelling to amoxicillin in adolescence) and a strong local reaction to a wasp sting, but had never experienced anaphylaxis before.

At presentation: hypotensive (74/50), hypoxaemic (88% on room air), flushed, urticarial, diaphoretic, agitated, with facial swelling and throat tightness. She received IM epinephrine 0.3 mg via auto-injector, IV clemastine, oxygen, and 500 mL normal saline — and responded well. Blood pressure normalised. Swelling decreased. She was admitted to the acute internal ward.

Then the real challenge began.

Over the next 12 hours, she had three additional anaphylactic episodes — each requiring IM epinephrine — for a total of four discrete anaphylactic events from a single dose of clarithromycin. The intensity decreased with each episode, from profound hypoxaemia with cyanosis and hypotension initially, to dyspnoea, rash, and pruritus by the final episode. She was transferred to the ICU after the first relapse. Corticosteroids were added (prednisone 30 mg IV daily for 5 days plus a single dose of dexamethasone 8 mg IV — a discrepancy reflecting differing protocols between the general ward and ICU). No further relapses occurred after the fourth episode, and she was discharged after three days.

But the question that should keep you awake at night is: *why did this happen?*

## When "Severe Allergy" Isn't the Whole Story

The answer in this case — and one you should be actively looking for in your own refractory or recurrent anaphylaxis patients — is systemic mastocytosis.

Due to the atypical and prolonged clinical course, the treating team pursued further investigation. What they found was striking:

- **Elevated tryptase** during the third anaphylactic episode, with **persistently elevated baseline tryptase** on follow-up.
- **Hepatomegaly** on abdominal sonography.
- **Neuropsychiatric symptoms** including depression and memory problems.
- **Two WHO minor criteria** for systemic mastocytosis — but not enough for a formal diagnosis, as the bone marrow biopsy yielded insufficient material to assess the major criterion (multi-focal dense infiltrates of mast cells with tryptase staining).

This is a patient who almost certainly has systemic mastocytosis, but whose diagnosis was missed by the narrow margins of formal criteria. And that gap between "high probability" and "confirmed diagnosis" is exactly where many of these patients live — sometimes for years.

## What Is Systemic Mastocytosis and Why Does It Matter?

Systemic mastocytosis is a clonal mast cell disorder characterised by abnormal proliferation of mast cells, excessive mediator release, and multiorgan inflammation. The clinical consequence that brings these patients to the ED is straightforward and terrifying: they are predisposed to severe, recurrent, and sometimes refractory anaphylaxis.

Mast cells are the primary effector cells of anaphylaxis. When you have more of them — and when those cells are dysfunctional and primed for degranulation — the threshold for anaphylaxis drops, the severity of reactions escalates, and the response to treatment can become unpredictable. This is not a patient with a "strong allergy." This is a patient whose mast cell burden itself is the disease.

In the presented case, the patient had multiple features beyond the anaphylaxis itself that pointed toward a mast cell disorder: hepatomegaly (organ infiltration), neuropsychiatric symptoms (mast cell mediator effects on the central nervous system), and elevated baseline tryptase. The combination of high baseline tryptase with recurrent unprovoked-like anaphylactic episodes is the hallmark pattern clinicians need to recognise.

## Diagnostic Criteria — and Why So Many Patients Fall Through the Cracks

The WHO diagnostic criteria for systemic mastocytosis require either one major criterion plus one minor criterion, or three minor criteria. The major criterion is multi-focal dense infiltrates of mast cells (≥15 mast cells in aggregates) in bone marrow or other extracutaneous organs, confirmed by tryptase staining. The minor criteria include atypical mast cell morphology, aberrant CD25/CD2/CD30 expression, KIT D816V mutation, and elevated serum tryptase >20 ng/mL.

Here is the clinical reality: confirming the major criterion requires an adequate bone marrow biopsy. In this case, the biopsy did not yield enough material. The patient met two minor criteria but could not be formally diagnosed. This is not an unusual scenario. Bone marrow biopsies are invasive, not always definitive, and in many clinical settings are not pursued aggressively — especially when anaphylaxis has already resolved and the patient is being managed as an outpatient.

The practical implication is that **you will encounter patients who have systemic mastocytosis that you cannot formally prove**. Your clinical suspicion, supported by tryptase levels and organ involvement clues, may need to guide management in the absence of a tissue diagnosis.

## Tryptase: The Test You Are Probably Not Ordering Correctly

One of the most instructive failures in this case — and one that is almost certainly happening in your ED too — was the tryptase sampling.

Guidelines recommend a minimum of two, ideally three tryptase samples at specific intervals: an initial sample as soon as feasible without delaying treatment, a second sample 1–2 hours (but within 4 hours) of symptom onset when tryptase peaks, and a third sample at least 24 hours after full clinical resolution to establish baseline.

In this case, the first tryptase was drawn one hour after the *third* anaphylactic episode. The second was drawn 27 hours after the last recurrence. The recommended intervals were not adhered to — meaning the opportunity to document the dynamic rise and fall of tryptase was lost.

This matters enormously. A rise in acute tryptase of at least 20% plus 2 ng/mL above baseline supports the diagnosis of anaphylaxis. A persistently elevated baseline tryptase — as seen in this patient — points toward a mast cell disorder. But if you do not draw the samples at the right times, you lose both the acute anaphylaxis confirmation and the mastocytosis signal.

**If you are treating anaphylaxis and not drawing serial tryptase levels, you are flying blind.** And if you are drawing them but not at the right intervals, you are flying blind with the illusion of data.

## Clinical Clues That Should Trigger Suspicion

This case provides a practical checklist of features that should prompt you to think beyond "severe allergy" and toward a mast cell disorder:

- **Recurrent anaphylaxis** without re-exposure to the trigger — biphasic or multiphasic course.
- **Severe anaphylactic reaction to a trigger** that is not typically a high-risk allergen (clarithromycin is not a classic cause of life-threatening anaphylaxis).
- **Elevated baseline tryptase** — not just elevated during the acute event, but elevated on follow-up.
- **Organomegaly** — particularly hepatomegaly or splenomegaly, suggesting mast cell infiltration.
- **Neuropsychiatric symptoms** — depression, cognitive complaints, which are increasingly recognised as mast cell mediator effects.
- **History of severe local reactions** to insect stings — this patient had a strong local reaction to a wasp sting, which is a recognised red flag for mastocytosis in the allergy literature.
- **Treatment-resistant or recurrent course** despite standard management with appropriate IM epinephrine doses.

None of these in isolation is diagnostic. But the convergence of several should immediately shift your differential and your follow-up plan.

## How Identifying Mastocytosis Changes Everything

The long-term management of a patient with systemic mastocytosis is fundamentally different from the management of a patient with a "one-time" allergic reaction.

For the patient in this case, discharge planning included a new standing prescription for levocetirizine and an epinephrine auto-injector — which is appropriate for any anaphylaxis patient. But for a patient with mastocytosis, the stakes are higher. These patients need **perpetual trigger avoidance** with a heightened awareness that triggers may be unpredictable and that future reactions may be severe. They need **permanent epinephrine auto-injector carriage** — not just a single prescription, but ongoing education and reinforcement. And they need **specialist follow-up** with allergology and potentially haematology, because systemic mastocytosis is a disease with haematologic, dermatologic, gastrointestinal, and skeletal manifestations that extend far beyond the ED visit.

Interestingly, the team in this case elected not to perform formal allergy confirmation testing (e.g., skin testing for clarithromycin), reasoning that the risk of provoking another severe reaction was not proportional to the benefit, given that clarithromycin can be easily avoided. This is a reasonable and defensible approach — but it also underscores that in suspected mastocytosis, the specific trigger matters less than the underlying mast cell biology. Avoiding clarithromycin is necessary but not sufficient; the next trigger may be something entirely different.

## The Epinephrine Dosing Problem Hiding in Plain Sight

One more detail from this case deserves attention because it is relevant to every single anaphylaxis patient you treat: **epinephrine dosing.**

The first two epinephrine doses in this case were 0.3 mg via auto-injector. The second two were 0.5 mg drawn up individually. The recommended IM epinephrine dose for adults is 0.01 mg/kg, up to a maximum of 0.5 mg. At 104 kg, this patient's weight-based dose would have been approximately 0.5 mg — meaning the first two doses were significantly underdosed.

The case authors note that 0.3 mg auto-injectors are commonly used in adults despite the guideline recommendation, and a 2025 retrospective study showed that patients who initially received 0.3 mg required escalation of care significantly more often than those who received 0.5 mg. The convenience and reduced hesitancy of auto-injectors is real and valuable — but the trade-off is that many adults are receiving a subtherapeutic dose at the most critical moment.

If your department stocks only 0.3 mg auto-injectors for adult anaphylaxis, this should prompt a protocol review. The goal is not to eliminate auto-injector use but to ensure that when time permits — or when the first dose fails — you are giving the dose the guidelines actually recommend.

## The Corticosteroid Question

This patient received both prednisone and dexamethasone due to conflicting protocols between the general ward and ICU. The rationale was the traditional belief that corticosteroids prevent biphasic anaphylaxis. The evidence no longer supports this.

A 2017 systematic review of 22 studies found only one supporting corticosteroid use, with 21 showing no evidence of reduced biphasic risk. A 2019 study showed increased likelihood of ICU or hospital admission after prehospital corticosteroid treatment. Most contemporary guidelines now advise against routine corticosteroid administration for anaphylaxis — though clinical practice lags behind, as illustrated by the dual-protocol confusion in this very case.

The bottom line: this patient had multiphasic anaphylaxis despite receiving corticosteroids. Whether they helped, did nothing, or caused harm is unknowable from a single case — but the literature increasingly suggests the latter two are more likely than the first.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Multiphasic anaphylaxis is rare but real.** When symptoms recur without re-exposure, think beyond treatment failure — consider ongoing allergen absorption, refractory anaphylaxis, and underlying mast cell disorders.
- **Systemic mastocytosis should be on your differential for any patient with severe, recurrent, or atypically triggered anaphylaxis.** Clinical suspicion may need to guide management even when formal WHO diagnostic criteria are not met.
- **Draw serial tryptase levels at the right times.** Initial sample as soon as feasible, second at 1–2 hours (within 4) of symptom onset, third at ≥24 hours after resolution. If you miss the windows, you lose the data.
- **Elevated baseline tryptase is a red flag.** It is not just a marker of the acute event — it points toward a clonal mast cell disorder and warrants specialist follow-up.
- **Look for the extras.** Hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, neuropsychiatric symptoms, severe local reactions to insect stings, and cutaneous lesions (urticaria pigmentosa) are all clues that the anaphylaxis you are treating may be the tip of a much larger iceberg.
- **Give the right epinephrine dose.** Adults over 50 kg should receive 0.5 mg IM, not 0.3 mg. If your auto-injectors only deliver 0.3 mg, know when to switch to drawn-up dosing.
- **Rethink routine corticosteroids.** The evidence for preventing biphasic anaphylaxis is weak to absent, and the signal for harm is growing.
- **Identifying mastocytosis changes long-term management.** These patients need lifelong trigger vigilance, permanent epinephrine carriage, and multidisciplinary follow-up — not just discharge with an antihistamine and an auto-injector prescription.

## Final Thought

Anaphylaxis is common. Multiphasic anaphylaxis is not. But when you are standing at the bedside of a patient having their third anaphylactic episode in 12 hours, "rare" is not the word that comes to mind. The question is not just "how do I treat this episode?" but "why is this happening?" In a meaningful proportion of those cases, the answer may be systemic mastocytosis — a diagnosis you can suspect at the bedside, confirm with the right labs drawn at the right times, and act on with management that extends far beyond the ED doors.

The next time you see anaphylaxis that keeps coming back, ask yourself: have I checked the tryptase? And have I thought about the mast cells?
