Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# Observation After Anaphylaxis: How Long Is Long Enough When the Evidence Won't Commit?

Picture this: you've just successfully resuscitated a patient in anaphylactic shock. Epinephrine in, symptoms out, vitals normalized, skin cleared. You pat yourself on the back, admit for observation, and figure you're done. Then it happens again. And again. And again.

That's exactly what happened to a woman in her 60s who presented to the ED with anaphylaxis after her first-ever dose of clarithromycin—prescribed as a substitute for clindamycin, which had caused a rash five days into treatment for erysipelas. She had a known beta-lactam allergy from adolescence and a strong local reaction to a wasp sting, but had never experienced anaphylaxis before. Within an hour of taking 500 mg of clarithromycin orally, she developed pruritus, flushing, agitation, throat tightness, hypotension (74/50 mm Hg), and hypoxemia (88% on room air). Standard treatment with IM epinephrine, antihistamines, oxygen, and fluids produced a solid clinical response. She was admitted to the acute internal ward for monitoring. And then things got interesting.

## The Relapse Problem

Despite a full initial response, this patient went on to experience three additional anaphylactic episodes within the first 12 hours of admission, each requiring IM epinephrine. Four total episodes from a single dose of antibiotic. The intensity of each recurrence decreased progressively—from deep hypoxemia with cyanosis and severe hypotension in the first episode, down to dyspnea, rash, and pruritus in the fourth—but each one met criteria for anaphylactic shock. She was escalated to the ICU after the first relapse, and ultimately had no further episodes after the fourth. She was discharged after three days with an epinephrine auto-injector and a new prescription for levocetirizine, with no recurrences at follow-up.

This is what the authors call multiphasic anaphylaxis, and it sits at the extreme end of a clinical spectrum that we frankly do not understand well enough. Biphasic anaphylaxis—defined by the Resuscitation Council UK as a recurrence of symptoms after apparent resolution in the absence of re-exposure—is the more commonly recognized variant, with reported incidence ranging wildly from 0.4% to 23.3%, though most data cluster around 4–6%. Multiphasic reactions, with multiple relapses, are rarer still, and the literature is essentially silent on how to manage them prospectively.

## What makes this case particularly instructive is the timing

The patient's final anaphylactic episode occurred roughly 12 hours after initial presentation. If you had observed her for the traditionally recommended 4–6 hours after symptom resolution and discharged her, she could have been home when the second, third, or fourth episode struck. And while reports of biphasic reactions occurring up to 78 hours after initial allergen exposure exist, the bulk of recurrences happen in that 6–12 hour window—the very window where most guidelines go vague.

## What the Guidelines Say (and Don't Say)

Here's where it gets frustrating. The World Allergy Organization and the Dutch NHG guideline both acknowledge that approximately half of biphasic reactions occur within 6–12 hours after the initial event, but neither commits to a specific monitoring recommendation. The UK Resuscitation Council 2021 guideline is the most helpful, proposing a three-tier risk-stratified approach to observation, but even this is expert consensus, not evidence-based, and the evidence base it draws from is thin.

This matters because the tension here is real. On one side is patient safety: biphasic reactions can be severe, and we cannot reliably predict who will have one. On the other side is resource stewardship: with anaphylaxis being a common ED presentation and biphasic reactions occurring in only 4–6% of cases, prolonged observation for every patient is an enormous resource burden. We need risk stratification, and we need it to be better than what we have.

## Risk Predictors: The List That Doesn't Quite Deliver

Several predictors of biphasic anaphylaxis have been proposed: delayed epinephrine administration, severe initial presentation, need for multiple epinephrine doses, and prior history of biphasic reactions. These make pathophysiologic sense, and our patient hit several of them—severe initial presentation with hypotension and hypoxemia, multiple epinephrine doses required. But as the authors note, reliable predictors that allow targeted monitoring are not yet available. The sensitivity and specificity of these factors simply aren't good enough to safely triage patients into short versus long observation arms.

## The Steroid Question: Time to Let Go?

One particularly interesting detail in this case is the double corticosteroid regimen. After the first recurrence, the patient received both prednisone 30 mg IV daily for five days and dexamethasone 8 mg IV once, because the general ward anaphylaxis protocol and the ICU anaphylaxis protocol recommended different steroids. She still went on to have two more anaphylactic episodes.

This is consistent with the evolving evidence. A 2017 systematic review of 22 studies found only one supporting corticosteroid use, with the other 21 showing no evidence that steroids reduce the risk of biphasic anaphylaxis. A 2019 study even showed an increased likelihood of ICU or ward admission after prehospital corticosteroid treatment. Most contemporary guidelines now advise against routine corticosteroid administration in anaphylaxis, though entrenched practice habits die hard. This case is a sobering reminder that steroids did not prevent recurrence here, and the patient did fine once the mast cell degranulation cascade finally burned itself out.

## The Epinephrine Dosing Trap

A subtle but important point in this case involves epinephrine dosing. The first two episodes were treated with 0.3 mg IM epinephrine via auto-injector, while the subsequent two used 0.5 mg individually prepared doses. The guidelines recommend 0.01 mg/kg with a maximum of 0.5 mg for adults, simplified to 0.5 mg for most adults since the majority weigh over 50 kg. This patient weighed 104 kg, meaning she was significantly underdosed with the auto-injector for her first two episodes.

The authors make a compelling case for auto-injectors in time-sensitive or low-frequency settings, noting that they minimize treatment delay and reduce hesitancy among staff who rarely handle vasoactive medications. But the trade-off is clear: a 2025 retrospective study showed that patients who initially received 0.3 mg epinephrine needed escalation of care significantly more often than those who received 0.5 mg. The widespread availability of 0.3 mg auto-injectors—many marketed for adult use—creates a structural underdosing problem that we should be actively working to fix.

## The Elephant in the Room: Systemic Mastocytosis

The atypical and prolonged course of this patient's reaction prompted further investigation, which revealed a high probability of systemic mastocytosis. Her tryptase levels were elevated both during the third anaphylactic episode and at baseline, and remained elevated on follow-up. She had hepatomegaly on abdominal ultrasound and a history of depression and memory problems, both consistent with mast cell mediator effects. However, she did not meet formal WHO diagnostic criteria, with only two minor criteria fulfilled and the major criterion not assessable due to limited bone marrow biopsy material.

Systemic mastocytosis involves abnormal mast cell proliferation, excessive mediator release, and multiorgan inflammation, and it predisposes patients to severe and potentially atypical anaphylaxis. The connection to biphasic or multiphasic courses is logical—more mast cells, more mediators, longer cascades—but the authors note that no research has specifically addressed this relationship. This case suggests it should be on your differential when anaphylaxis follows an unusual trajectory, particularly when accompanied by elevated tryptase, hepatomegaly, or neuropsychiatric symptoms.

One important miss in this case: tryptase was not drawn at the recommended intervals. Guidelines call for an initial sample as soon as feasible, a second at 1–2 hours (within 4 hours) of symptom onset when tryptase peaks, and a third at least 24 hours after full resolution for baseline. In this case, the first sample was taken an hour after the third anaphylactic episode—far too late to capture the peak from the initial event. When you're managing a crashing anaphylaxis patient, the last thing on your mind may be serial tryptase draws, but the information is invaluable for retrospective diagnosis and for identifying underlying mast cell disorders.

## A Pragmatic Approach to Observation

So what do we do with all this uncertainty? The authors propose a registry for complex anaphylaxis cases to build the data infrastructure for evidence-based recommendations, and this is clearly needed. But in the meantime, here's a pragmatic synthesis:

For low-risk presentations—single epinephrine dose, rapid and complete response, mild initial presentation, no features suggesting mast cell disorder—observation for 4–6 hours after symptom resolution is probably adequate, though you should counsel patients about the small risk of late recurrence and ensure they have an epinephrine auto-injector and understand how to use it.

For higher-risk presentations—multiple epinephrine doses, severe initial reaction with hypotension or hypoxemia, delayed epinephrine, prior biphasic history, or any clinical features suggestive of systemic mastocytosis—extended observation for at least 12–24 hours is warranted. The UK three-tier approach is the best available framework, but it should be applied with the understanding that our risk prediction is imperfect.

For multiphasic reactions specifically, there is no evidence to guide you. The authors of this case considered two strategies: continuous IV epinephrine infusion to pre-emptively treat, versus expectant management given decreasing symptom intensity. Neither was needed in the end, as the patient stopped relapsing after her fourth episode. But the discussion itself highlights the clinical tightrope: too aggressive and you risk unnecessary epinephrine exposure and ICU resources; too conservative and you risk a life-threatening relapse on the ward or at home. Until we have better data, this decision will remain one of clinical judgment, serial reassessment, and honest conversation with the patient.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- Biphasic anaphylaxis occurs in roughly 4–6% of cases, with about half of recurrences happening in the 6–12 hour window. Traditional 4–6 hour observation periods may be inadequate for higher-risk patients.
- Proposed risk factors for biphasic reactions—delayed epinephrine, severe initial presentation, multiple epinephrine doses, prior biphasic history—make physiologic sense but lack the reliability needed for confident triage. The UK 2021 three-tier risk-stratified approach is the best available framework.
- Corticosteroids do not prevent biphasic anaphylaxis. The evidence is clear and accumulating, and contemporary guidelines are moving away from routine use. This case provides a vivid illustration: double steroids, still three more relapses.
- Watch your epinephrine dosing. 0.3 mg auto-injectors underdose most adults. The guideline-recommended dose is 0.5 mg IM for adults over 50 kg, and underdosing is associated with more frequent escalation of care.
- Consider systemic mastocytosis in any patient with an unusually severe, prolonged, or multiphasic anaphylactic course, especially with elevated tryptase, hepatomegaly, or neuropsychiatric symptoms. You won't always meet full WHO criteria, but the clinical implications for future management are significant.
- Draw serial tryptase levels. The schedule matters: initial, 1–2 hours, and 24+ hours post-resolution. Yes, it is hard to remember in the heat of the moment. Yes, it is worth it.
- When managing a multiphasic reaction, you are in uncharted territory. There is no evidence to guide continuous epinephrine infusion versus expectant management. Serial reassessment, early ICU involvement, and transparency with the patient about the uncertainty are your best tools.
- The bottom line: the evidence won't commit, so you have to. Use the risk factors we have, apply the most structured approach available, and when something about the case feels atypical—like anaphylaxis that keeps coming back—observe longer and investigate deeper.
