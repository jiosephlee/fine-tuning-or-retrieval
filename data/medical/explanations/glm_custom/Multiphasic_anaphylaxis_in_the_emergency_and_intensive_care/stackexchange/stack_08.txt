Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Morning Report] What risk factors have been proposed for predicting biphasic anaphylaxis, and why has reliable risk stratification proven difficult despite multiple proposed predictors?

# Risk Factors for Biphasic Anaphylaxis and the Challenge of Risk Stratification

Several predictors of biphasic anaphylaxis have been proposed, including **delayed epinephrine administration**, **severity of the initial presentation**, **requirement for multiple epinephrine doses**, and a **prior history of biphasic reactions**. Our patient exhibited several of these: she presented with severe initial anaphylactic shock (hypotension to 74/50 mm Hg, hypoxaemia to 88% on room air), required repeated intramuscular epinephrine across four discrete episodes, and had an underlying predisposition to severe anaphylaxis in the setting of likely systemic mastocytosis with persistently elevated tryptase levels. Yet despite possessing multiple proposed risk factors, her clinical course — multiphasic rather than simply biphasic, with gradually diminishing symptom intensity — illustrates precisely why validated risk stratification remains elusive.

The fundamental problem is threefold. **First**, biphasic anaphylaxis is uncommon, with an incidence likely between 4% and 6%, and multiphasic presentations are rarer still. This low event rate severely limits the statistical power of available studies and has precluded the development of well-validated predictive models. **Second**, definitions of biphasic anaphylaxis remain inconsistent across studies — varying in the required asymptomatic interval, the time window for recurrence (reported up to 78 hours), and whether reactions reflecting ongoing allergen absorption or transient epinephrine response are excluded — introducing heterogeneity that undermines comparison and synthesis of findings. **Third**, the proposed predictors themselves are correlates rather than independent drivers. Severe initial reactions and multiple epinephrine doses may simply reflect a more robust mast cell burden or ongoing mast cell activation rather than mechanistically causing recurrence, and they overlap considerably with one another, limiting their discriminative value for individual patients.

The role of systemic mastocytosis is particularly instructive here. It is well established that mast cell disorders predispose patients to severe and treatment-resistant anaphylaxis, yet to date no research has specifically examined the relationship between systemic mastocytosis and biphasic or multiphasic courses. Our patient's elevated baseline tryptase, hepatomegaly, and neuropsychiatric symptoms were consistent with systemic mastocytosis but fell short of formal WHO diagnostic criteria — a scenario that itself highlights the difficulty of identifying and studying relevant subpopulations.

In summary, while the proposed risk factors — delayed epinephrine, severe initial reaction, multiple epinephrine doses, and prior biphasic episodes — are clinically sensible and worth noting at the bedside, none has demonstrated sufficient sensitivity or specificity to reliably guide individual observation decisions. The rarity of the phenomenon, definitional inconsistency, and the lack of mechanistic understanding of what drives recurrence after apparent resolution collectively explain why current guidelines offer general observation timeframes rather than truly risk-stratified, patient-centred monitoring protocols. This gap underscores the authors' call for a prospective registry of complex anaphylaxis cases to generate the data necessary for evidence-based, individualised management recommendations.
