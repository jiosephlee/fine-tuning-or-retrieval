Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Morning Report] This patient had four anaphylactic episodes over 12 hours despite no re-exposure to the trigger, with diminishing symptom intensity over time. What management strategies were considered for ongoing multiphasic anaphylaxis, and what are the risks and potential benefits of each approach?

## Management Strategies for Ongoing Multiphasic Anaphylaxis

In this case, after the patient experienced four discrete anaphylactic episodes within 12 hours—each requiring intramuscular epinephrine and each with diminishing symptom intensity—the allergy service considered two management strategies: **continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion** versus an **expectant observation approach**.

### Continuous Intravenous Epinephrine

The rationale for a continuous epinephrine infusion is to move from a reactive to a preemptive strategy—maintaining therapeutic epinephrine levels to prevent recurrent mast cell mediator release rather than waiting for each recurrence and then rescuing the patient. This approach is conceptually analogous to using vasopressor infusions for refractory shock and is supported by extrapolation from guidelines for refractory anaphylaxis, where IV epinephrine is recommended when IM epinephrine fails to produce a sustained response.

However, the risks are substantial. Continuous IV epinephrine requires careful titration, ideally in an ICU with continuous telemetry and arterial monitoring, and carries a significant risk of tachyarrhythmias, hypertension, myocardial ischemia, and ventricular ectopy—particularly concerning in a patient with a history of hypertension. There is also a risk of overshooting treatment, leading to end-organ toxicity from prolonged high-dose catecholamine exposure, and conversely, if the infusion is tapered prematurely, a rebound recurrence could occur. In this patient, who weighed 104 kg, dose calculation and titration would need to account for both body weight and the unpredictable ongoing mast cell burden, particularly given the suspected underlying systemic mastocytosis.

### Expectant Observation

The alternative strategy was to continue close monitoring with intermittent IM epinephrine reserved for recurrent symptoms. The rationale here was grounded in an important clinical observation: **the intensity of symptoms diminished with each successive episode**, ranging from deep hypoxaemia with cyanosis and hypotension in the first episode to dyspnoea, rash, and pruritus in the fourth. This decremental pattern suggested that the ongoing mast cell degranulation was self-limited and waning, potentially making preemptive epinephrine unnecessary and exposing the patient to avoidable medication risks.

The obvious risk of this approach is that the next recurrence could be severe, unpredictable, and potentially life-threatening. It also demands intensive resource utilization—ICU-level monitoring with the capacity to immediately recognize and treat recurrence—which was available in this case but may not be in all settings. Furthermore, the psychological burden on the patient and clinical team of waiting for a possible relapse should not be underestimated.

### Clinical Decision and Outcome

Ultimately, neither aggressive strategy was required, as the patient did not relapse after the fourth episode. She was transferred to a standard ward after 24 hours without recurrence and discharged after 3 days. This outcome reinforces that multiphasic anaphylaxis can be self-limited, but it also highlights the central clinical challenge: there is no reliable predictor to distinguish patients who will have additional recurrences from those whose reaction has truly exhausted itself.

### Additional Considerations

It is worth noting that corticosteroids were administered after the first recurrence, consistent with traditional practice aimed at preventing biphasic reactions. However, as discussed in the case, a 2017 systematic review of 22 studies found no evidence that corticosteroids reduce biphasic anaphylaxis risk, and a 2019 study suggested increased likelihood of ICU or ward admission associated with prehospital corticosteroid use. Contemporary guidelines have consequently shifted away from routine corticosteroid administration in anaphylaxis.

The suspected systemic mastocytosis in this patient—supported by elevated baseline and acute tryptase levels, hepatomegaly, and neuropsychiatric symptoms—provides an important pathophysiological context. In mast cell disorders, the total mast cell burden is markedly increased, which may explain both the severity and the multiphasic nature of the reaction, as the larger mast cell pool allows for prolonged or repeated waves of mediator release even after the initial trigger has been cleared.

### Key Teaching Point

In multiphasic anaphylaxis, the choice between preemptive continuous epinephrine and expectant observation must be individualized, weighing the apparent trajectory of the reaction (improving vs. worsening with each episode), the patient's comorbidities, available monitoring resources, and suspected underlying mast cell pathology. The absence of evidence-based guidelines for this rare scenario means that clinical judgment, guided by the pattern of each individual patient's reaction, remains essential. A registry of such cases, as the authors suggest, would be invaluable for developing data-driven management recommendations.
