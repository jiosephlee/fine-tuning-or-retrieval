Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Clinical Pearls] What clinical criteria define anaphylaxis, and why can the diagnosis be challenging in patients who present without a clear-cut trigger or who lack classic cutaneous findings?

## Clinical Criteria for Anaphylaxis and Diagnostic Challenges

Anaphylaxis is a clinical diagnosis defined by the acute onset and rapid progression of symptoms involving the airway, breathing, circulation, and/or gastrointestinal tract, typically accompanied by cutaneous or mucosal manifestations such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. The widely accepted diagnostic criteria, as established by the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases and the Food Allergy and Anaphylaxis Network, require fulfillment of one of three scenarios: (1) acute involvement of skin or mucosal tissue plus either respiratory compromise or hypotension/end-organ dysfunction; (2) two or more of the following after exposure to a likely allergen—skin/mucosal involvement, respiratory compromise, reduced blood pressure, or persistent gastrointestinal symptoms; or (3) hypotension after exposure to a known allergen for that patient. Identification of a likely allergen supports the diagnosis but is not required, and in many cases a specific trigger cannot be determined.

The presented case illustrates both the straightforward and challenging aspects of diagnosis. The patient met criteria clearly at initial presentation: acute onset of flushing, generalized urticaria, and facial swelling accompanied by hypotension (74/50 mm Hg) and hypoxemia (oxygen saturation 88%), all developing within one hour of a new medication exposure. The cutaneous and mucosal findings, together with cardiovascular and respiratory compromise, made the initial diagnosis unambiguous.

However, the case also highlights why diagnosis can become challenging. First, in the biphasic and multiphasic phases, symptoms recurred without re-exposure to the allergen, and distinguishing true biphasic anaphylaxis from sustained anaphylaxis with transient response to epinephrine proved difficult—both were actively considered. Second, the underlying predisposition to severe, recurrent anaphylaxis was not immediately apparent. Elevated baseline tryptase and hepatomegaly raised suspicion for systemic mastocytosis, a condition known to predispose patients to severe anaphylaxis, yet formal diagnostic criteria were not met. This underscores that anaphylaxis can be the presenting manifestation of an occult mast cell disorder, and without a high index of suspicion, the recurrent nature of the reaction may be attributed solely to the allergen rather than to an underlying disease process.

The broader diagnostic challenge extends beyond this case. Cutaneous findings, though characteristic, are absent in up to 20% of anaphylaxis episodes, particularly in certain contexts such as food-dependent exercise-induced anaphylaxis, idiopathic anaphylaxis, and reactions in patients on beta-blockers. When hypotension or bronchospasm occurs without rash or urticaria, anaphylaxis may be mistaken for vasovagal syncope, asthma exacerbation, or cardiac events. Furthermore, when no clear trigger is identified—as can occur in patients with systemic mastocytosis, where anaphylaxis may appear idiomatic—clinicians may hesitate to diagnose anaphylaxis or may attribute symptoms to alternative etiologies, delaying epinephrine administration. As emphasized in the case discussion, while identification of a likely allergen supports the diagnosis, it is not essential; the diagnosis rests on recognition of the clinical pattern and prompt empiric treatment.
