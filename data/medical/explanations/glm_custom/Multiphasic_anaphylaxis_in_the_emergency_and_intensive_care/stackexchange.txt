Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Clinical Pearls] What clinical criteria define anaphylaxis, and why can the diagnosis be challenging in patients who present without a clear-cut trigger or who lack classic cutaneous findings?

## Clinical Criteria for Anaphylaxis and Diagnostic Challenges

Anaphylaxis is a clinical diagnosis defined by the acute onset and rapid progression of symptoms involving the airway, breathing, circulation, and/or gastrointestinal tract, typically accompanied by cutaneous or mucosal manifestations such as urticaria, angioedema, or flushing. The widely accepted diagnostic criteria, as established by the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases and the Food Allergy and Anaphylaxis Network, require fulfillment of one of three scenarios: (1) acute involvement of skin or mucosal tissue plus either respiratory compromise or hypotension/end-organ dysfunction; (2) two or more of the following after exposure to a likely allergen—skin/mucosal involvement, respiratory compromise, reduced blood pressure, or persistent gastrointestinal symptoms; or (3) hypotension after exposure to a known allergen for that patient. Identification of a likely allergen supports the diagnosis but is not required, and in many cases a specific trigger cannot be determined.

The presented case illustrates both the straightforward and challenging aspects of diagnosis. The patient met criteria clearly at initial presentation: acute onset of flushing, generalized urticaria, and facial swelling accompanied by hypotension (74/50 mm Hg) and hypoxemia (oxygen saturation 88%), all developing within one hour of a new medication exposure. The cutaneous and mucosal findings, together with cardiovascular and respiratory compromise, made the initial diagnosis unambiguous.

However, the case also highlights why diagnosis can become challenging. First, in the biphasic and multiphasic phases, symptoms recurred without re-exposure to the allergen, and distinguishing true biphasic anaphylaxis from sustained anaphylaxis with transient response to epinephrine proved difficult—both were actively considered. Second, the underlying predisposition to severe, recurrent anaphylaxis was not immediately apparent. Elevated baseline tryptase and hepatomegaly raised suspicion for systemic mastocytosis, a condition known to predispose patients to severe anaphylaxis, yet formal diagnostic criteria were not met. This underscores that anaphylaxis can be the presenting manifestation of an occult mast cell disorder, and without a high index of suspicion, the recurrent nature of the reaction may be attributed solely to the allergen rather than to an underlying disease process.

The broader diagnostic challenge extends beyond this case. Cutaneous findings, though characteristic, are absent in up to 20% of anaphylaxis episodes, particularly in certain contexts such as food-dependent exercise-induced anaphylaxis, idiopathic anaphylaxis, and reactions in patients on beta-blockers. When hypotension or bronchospasm occurs without rash or urticaria, anaphylaxis may be mistaken for vasovagal syncope, asthma exacerbation, or cardiac events. Furthermore, when no clear trigger is identified—as can occur in patients with systemic mastocytosis, where anaphylaxis may appear idiomatic—clinicians may hesitate to diagnose anaphylaxis or may attribute symptoms to alternative etiologies, delaying epinephrine administration. As emphasized in the case discussion, while identification of a likely allergen supports the diagnosis, it is not essential; the diagnosis rests on recognition of the clinical pattern and prompt empiric treatment.

### [Differential Diagnosis] How do you differentiate biphasic anaphylaxis from refractory anaphylaxis and from sustained anaphylaxis due to ongoing allergen absorption, and why is this distinction clinically important?

## Differentiating Biphasic, Refractory, and Sustained Anaphylaxis

**Biphasic anaphylaxis** is defined by a specific temporal pattern: initial anaphylaxis with subsequent *complete* symptom resolution, followed by an asymptomatic interval, and then recurrence of anaphylaxis **without** any re-exposure to the allergen. The key element is the interposed asymptomatic period. In the presented case, the patient exhibited a multiphasic variant of this phenomenon—four discrete anaphylactic episodes, each separated by periods of clinical stability, each responding to intramuscular epinephrine, and occurring without any additional clarithromycin ingestion beyond the single 500 mg dose.

**Refractory anaphylaxis** describes a fundamentally different scenario: persistent anaphylaxis that does **not** respond, or responds only incompletely, to correctly administered epinephrine at appropriate doses and intervals. There is no asymptomatic interval because symptoms never fully resolve. This entity typically requires rapid escalation to intravenous epinephrine infusion, adjunctive vasopressors, and airway management. Our patient did not have refractory anaphylaxis—each episode responded promptly to IM epinephrine, which is precisely what allowed the clinical team to observe the recurrent, episodic pattern.

**Sustained anaphylaxis from ongoing allergen absorption** occupies a third category. Here, symptoms persist or recur because the allergen continues to enter the systemic circulation—for example, from a retained food bolus, a slow-release drug formulation, or a persistent venom depot. The clinical picture may resemble biphasic anaphylaxis with fluctuating symptom intensity, but the mechanism is continuous antigen exposure rather than a de novo secondary mast cell degranulation. In our case, ongoing absorption was unlikely to explain the multiphasic course: clarithromycin was immediately discontinued after a single oral dose, and each recurrent episode was separated by clear resolution with normal hemodynamics, which would be atypical for continuous antigen delivery. However, this distinction is not always straightforward, and the authors explicitly note that differentiating true biphasic reactions from sustained anaphylaxis with transient epinephrine response or ongoing absorption "can be challenging."

**Why does this distinction matter clinically?**

The three entities demand different management strategies. Biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis require a **monitoring strategy**—a structured observation period after initial resolution, with readiness to administer repeat IM epinephrine for any recurrence. The core question is duration of observation, which remains debated, with most biphasic reactions occurring within 6–12 hours of the initial event. Refractory anaphylaxis, by contrast, demands **immediate escalation** to IV epinephrine infusion and advanced airway and hemodynamic support; continued IM dosing alone is inadequate. Sustained anaphylaxis from ongoing absorption requires **source control**—identifying and interrupting continuing antigen delivery—because repeated epinephrine alone will not break the cycle if the trigger remains bioavailable.

In our case, the allergology consultants considered two strategies for potential further episodes: preemptive continuous IV epinephrine infusion versus expectant management. The fact that the patient's symptom intensity decreased with each successive episode, and that she had complete inter-episode resolution, supported the biphasic/multiphasic framework rather than refractory or sustained anaphylaxis, and ultimately no further escalation was needed.

Finally, this case illustrates an important additional point: **host factors** can amplify susceptibility to multiphasic courses. The elevated baseline and acute tryptase levels, hepatomegaly, and neuropsychiatric symptoms raised high suspicion for systemic mastocytosis, a condition known to predispose to severe and potentially recurrent anaphylaxis due to the expanded mast cell burden. While formal WHO diagnostic criteria were not met, this association underscores that when the clinical course is atypical—particularly with multiple recurrences—investigation for underlying mast cell disorders is warranted, as it may inform long-term management including baseline antihistamine prophylaxis and patient education.

### [Management] What is the recommended weight-based intramuscular epinephrine dose for adults with anaphylaxis, and what are the potential consequences of using a 0.3 mg auto-injector in patients weighing more than 50 kg?

The recommended intramuscular epinephrine dose for adults with anaphylaxis is **0.01 mg/kg, with a maximum single dose of 0.5 mg**. Because the majority of adults weigh more than 50 kg, guidelines commonly simplify this to a fixed **0.5 mg IM dose** for adults.

In this case, the patient weighed **104 kg**, meaning her weight-based dose would have been approximately 1.04 mg, capped at the 0.5 mg maximum. Instead, her first two anaphylactic episodes were treated with a **0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector**—a dose intended for patients in the 30–50 kg range and representing only about 0.0029 mg/kg for this patient, roughly one-third of the recommended dose.

The clinical consequence is illustrated directly by her course. The authors note that use of a 0.3 mg auto-injector in adults over 50 kg leads to **potentially critical underdosing**, and reference a 2025 retrospective study demonstrating that patients who initially received 0.3 mg epinephrine required escalation of care—including additional epinephrine injections—significantly more often than those who initially received 0.5 mg. In this patient, the initial subtherapeutic dosing may have contributed to inadequate mast cell mediator suppression, and she ultimately required **four separate anaphylactic episodes** before the team switched from the 0.3 mg auto-injector to manually prepared 0.5 mg intramuscular doses for the third and fourth episodes. Notably, she had no further relapses after receiving the guideline-recommended 0.5 mg dose, although the natural history of her multiphasic course cannot be entirely attributed to dosing alone, given the suspected underlying systemic mastocytosis.

The key teaching point: while 0.3 mg auto-injectors reduce treatment delay and provider hesitancy—an important practical advantage in time-sensitive settings—they should not be considered an adequate adult dose for patients over 50 kg. When available, **0.5 mg should be administered** to adults meeting that weight threshold, and clinicians should not assume that a good initial response to a subtherapeutic epinephrine dose precludes recurrence.

### [Management] What is the current evidence regarding corticosteroid administration in anaphylaxis for prevention of biphasic reactions, and how has guideline practice shifted in recent years?

**Corticosteroids in Anaphylaxis: Evidence and Evolving Guideline Practice**

For decades, corticosteroids were administered routinely after anaphylaxis with the rationale that their anti-inflammatory effects might prevent biphasic reactions—recurrence of symptoms after an initial asymptomatic interval, without re-exposure to the trigger. However, the current evidence does not support this practice, and guideline recommendations have shifted accordingly.

A 2017 systematic review examined 22 studies addressing corticosteroid use in anaphylaxis; only one supported a benefit, while 21 found no evidence that corticosteroids reduce the risk of biphasic reactions. Subsequent studies have further eroded confidence in this intervention. A 2019 study demonstrated that prehospital corticosteroid administration was associated with an increased likelihood of ICU or hospital ward admission, highlighting the potential for harm. These findings reflect a broader concern that corticosteroids introduce side-effect burden without demonstrable benefit, while also potentially creating false reassurance regarding the risk of recurrence.

Consequently, most contemporary guidelines now advise against the routine administration of corticosteroids in anaphylaxis. The World Allergy Organization and other recent guideline bodies have moved away from recommending them as standard adjunctive therapy.

This shift is directly relevant to the presented case of multiphasic anaphylaxis to clarithromycin. After the first recurrence of anaphylactic shock, the patient received both prednisone (30 mg IV daily for 5 days) and a single dose of dexamethasone (8 mg IV), owing to discrepancies between the hospital's general ward and ICU anaphylaxis protocols. Despite this aggressive corticosteroid regimen, the patient experienced a fourth anaphylactic episode, and the relapses ultimately ceased without any clear relationship to corticosteroid timing. This clinical course is consistent with the evidence base: corticosteroids did not appear to alter the multiphasic trajectory, and symptom intensity instead decreased progressively with each episode.

The case also underscores a practical consequence of outdated protocols: the persistence of corticosteroid administration in clinical practice despite guideline changes. The existence of conflicting institutional protocols—as seen here between the general ward and ICU pathways—reflects the lag between evidence and implementation. The patient's care was further complicated by the concurrent administration of two different corticosteroid preparations, a practice with no evidence basis.

**Key teaching point:** Epinephrine, administered promptly intramuscularly at appropriate weight-based dosing (0.01 mg/kg, maximum 0.5 mg in adults), remains the cornerstone of anaphylaxis management for both the initial event and any recurrent biphasic or multiphasic episodes. Corticosteroids should not be routinely administered for the purpose of preventing biphasic reactions. The focus of post-anaphylaxis care should be on appropriate observation periods guided by risk stratification and on readiness to treat recurrence with epinephrine, rather than on adjunctive pharmacologic prophylaxis of unproven benefit.

### [Clinical Pearls] What is the recommended timing for serial serum tryptase sampling after an anaphylactic event, and how does comparing the peak tryptase to baseline help interpret the results?

# Serial Tryptase Sampling in Anaphylaxis: Timing and Interpretation

Current guidelines recommend obtaining a minimum of two, and ideally three, serum tryptase samples at defined intervals following an anaphylactic event:

1. **An initial sample** should be drawn as soon as feasible after symptom onset, without delaying treatment.
2. **A second sample** should be obtained 1–2 hours (and no later than 4 hours) after symptom onset, when tryptase levels typically reach their peak.
3. **A third sample** should be collected at least 24 hours after full clinical resolution to establish the patient's baseline tryptase level.

The rationale for comparing the **peak** tryptase to the **baseline** is twofold. First, a significant rise in peak tryptase relative to baseline supports the biochemical diagnosis of anaphylaxis, as it reflects acute mast cell degranulation. Second, and equally important, an elevated *baseline* tryptase raises suspicion for an underlying mast cell disorder, most notably systemic mastocytosis, which predisposes patients to severe, recurrent, or atypical anaphylactic reactions.

In this case, the recommended sampling intervals were not followed—tryptase was first measured one hour after the *third* anaphylactic episode, with a second sample drawn 27 hours after the final relapse. Nevertheless, both the acute and baseline tryptase levels were found to be elevated, and this persistent elevation — rather than a transient spike returning to a normal baseline — was a key finding that pointed toward a high probability of systemic mastocytosis. The patient did not meet full WHO diagnostic criteria (only two minor criteria were fulfilled, and the bone marrow biopsy was insufficient for assessment of the major criterion), but the biochemical pattern of chronically elevated tryptase, combined with hepatomegaly and neuropsychiatric symptoms, was clinically consistent with this diagnosis.

This case underscores a critical teaching point: **the diagnostic value of tryptase lies not just in its absolute level during the acute event, but in the relationship between the peak and the baseline.** A normal baseline with a sharp peak suggests a typical allergen-triggered mast cell activation. An elevated baseline — with or without a further rise during the event — should prompt investigation for systemic mastocytosis or another clonal mast cell disorder, particularly when anaphylaxis follows an atypical, prolonged, or multiphasic course. Early recognition of this pattern can fundamentally alter long-term management and counseling, including avoidance strategies, provision of epinephrine auto-injectors, and referral to an allergist or hematologist for further evaluation.

**Bottom line:** Draw tryptase early (at presentation), again at 1–2 hours (peak), and again ≥24 hours after resolution (baseline). Compare peak to baseline — the pattern of elevation is more informative than any single value.

### [Pathophysiology] How does systemic mastocytosis predispose patients to severe and recurrent anaphylaxis, and what are the WHO major and minor diagnostic criteria for systemic mastocytosis?

# Systemic Mastocytosis and Anaphylaxis: Pathophysiology and WHO Diagnostic Criteria

Systemic mastocytosis is characterized by a clonal proliferation of abnormal mast cells in extracutaneous tissues—most commonly the bone marrow, but also the liver, spleen, and gastrointestinal tract. The fundamental pathologic defect in most adult patients is the KIT D816V mutation, which confers constitutive activation of the KIT receptor tyrosine kinase, driving mast cell expansion and survival. The consequence is a markedly increased total body mast cell burden, and these abnormal mast cells exhibit a lower threshold for degranulation and enhanced mediator release upon stimulation. When triggered by an allergen, drug, or other stimulus, the sheer volume of mast cells available to degranulate produces a massive flood of mediators—histamine, tryptase, prostaglandin D2, leukotrienes, and platelet-activating factor—capable of producing anaphylaxis that is more severe, more rapid in onset, and more prone to recurrence than in patients with a normal mast cell compartment.

In the presented case, the patient experienced four distinct anaphylactic episodes following a single dose of clarithromycin, with each episode requiring intramuscular epinephrine. This multiphasic pattern is consistent with the pathophysiology of systemic mastocytosis: the expanded and hyperreactive mast cell pool may continue to release mediators in waves, sustaining recurrent anaphylactic reactions even after the initial trigger has been cleared. Notably, the intensity of symptoms decreased with each episode, possibly reflecting progressive depletion of the preformed mediator pool within the mast cell population.

Importantly, patients with systemic mastocytosis can experience anaphylaxis with triggers that would not provoke a reaction in healthy individuals—venom stings and certain drugs are classic examples, and this patient's history included a strong local skin reaction to a wasp sting, which can be an early clue. Furthermore, baseline serum tryptase is a surrogate marker of total mast cell burden, and persistently elevated baseline tryptase—demonstrated in this patient both during and between episodes—is a hallmark finding. The elevated acute tryptase during the third anaphylactic episode, combined with elevated baseline tryptase on follow-up, strongly supported the clinical suspicion. Additional findings consistent with mast cell infiltration included hepatomegaly on abdominal sonography and neuropsychiatric symptoms (depression and memory problems), which may reflect chronic mediator exposure.

The WHO diagnostic criteria for systemic mastocytosis are organized into one major and four minor criteria. The **major criterion** is the presence of multifocal, dense infiltrates of mast cells (≥15 mast cells per aggregate) in the bone marrow or other extracutaneous organ sections. The **minor criteria** are: (1) more than 25% of mast cells in the infiltrate display atypical morphology (spindle-shaped or pleomorphic), or, if fewer than 25% are atypical, detection of the KIT D816V mutation; (2) detection of the KIT D816V mutation in bone marrow, blood, or other extracutaneous tissue; (3) aberrant expression of CD25, CD2, and/or CD30 on mast cells by flow cytometry or immunohistochemistry (these markers are not normally expressed on resting mast cells); and (4) a persistently elevated serum tryptase level >20 ng/mL in the absence of an associated clonal myeloid disorder.

A formal diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis requires either the major criterion plus one minor criterion, or three minor criteria in the absence of the major criterion. In this case, only two minor criteria were met—elevated baseline tryptase and one additional finding—and the major criterion could not be assessed because the bone marrow biopsy yielded insufficient material to evaluate for multifocal dense mast cell infiltrates. Thus, the patient did not meet formal WHO diagnostic criteria, but the clinical picture was considered highly suggestive, illustrating an important practical point: a high clinical suspicion for systemic mastocytosis should be pursued even when strict criteria are not fulfilled, as limited biopsy material or early-stage disease may preclude definitive classification.

The take-home clinical lesson is straightforward: any patient presenting with severe, recurrent, or multiphasic anaphylaxis—particularly with an elevated baseline tryptase, hepatosplenomegaly, cutaneous mastocytosis lesions, or a history of severe venom reactions—should be evaluated for systemic mastocytosis. Early recognition has direct management implications, including avoidance of known mast cell degranulation triggers, provision of epinephrine auto-injectors, consideration of venom immunotherapy in appropriate cases, and referral to a specialist in mast cell disorders for definitive diagnosis and long-term management.

### [Morning Report] This patient had four anaphylactic episodes over 12 hours despite no re-exposure to the trigger, with diminishing symptom intensity over time. What management strategies were considered for ongoing multiphasic anaphylaxis, and what are the risks and potential benefits of each approach?

## Management Strategies for Ongoing Multiphasic Anaphylaxis

In this case, after the patient experienced four discrete anaphylactic episodes within 12 hours—each requiring intramuscular epinephrine and each with diminishing symptom intensity—the allergy service considered two management strategies: **continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion** versus an **expectant observation approach**.

### Continuous Intravenous Epinephrine

The rationale for a continuous epinephrine infusion is to move from a reactive to a preemptive strategy—maintaining therapeutic epinephrine levels to prevent recurrent mast cell mediator release rather than waiting for each recurrence and then rescuing the patient. This approach is conceptually analogous to using vasopressor infusions for refractory shock and is supported by extrapolation from guidelines for refractory anaphylaxis, where IV epinephrine is recommended when IM epinephrine fails to produce a sustained response.

However, the risks are substantial. Continuous IV epinephrine requires careful titration, ideally in an ICU with continuous telemetry and arterial monitoring, and carries a significant risk of tachyarrhythmias, hypertension, myocardial ischemia, and ventricular ectopy—particularly concerning in a patient with a history of hypertension. There is also a risk of overshooting treatment, leading to end-organ toxicity from prolonged high-dose catecholamine exposure, and conversely, if the infusion is tapered prematurely, a rebound recurrence could occur. In this patient, who weighed 104 kg, dose calculation and titration would need to account for both body weight and the unpredictable ongoing mast cell burden, particularly given the suspected underlying systemic mastocytosis.

### Expectant Observation

The alternative strategy was to continue close monitoring with intermittent IM epinephrine reserved for recurrent symptoms. The rationale here was grounded in an important clinical observation: **the intensity of symptoms diminished with each successive episode**, ranging from deep hypoxaemia with cyanosis and hypotension in the first episode to dyspnoea, rash, and pruritus in the fourth. This decremental pattern suggested that the ongoing mast cell degranulation was self-limited and waning, potentially making preemptive epinephrine unnecessary and exposing the patient to avoidable medication risks.

The obvious risk of this approach is that the next recurrence could be severe, unpredictable, and potentially life-threatening. It also demands intensive resource utilization—ICU-level monitoring with the capacity to immediately recognize and treat recurrence—which was available in this case but may not be in all settings. Furthermore, the psychological burden on the patient and clinical team of waiting for a possible relapse should not be underestimated.

### Clinical Decision and Outcome

Ultimately, neither aggressive strategy was required, as the patient did not relapse after the fourth episode. She was transferred to a standard ward after 24 hours without recurrence and discharged after 3 days. This outcome reinforces that multiphasic anaphylaxis can be self-limited, but it also highlights the central clinical challenge: there is no reliable predictor to distinguish patients who will have additional recurrences from those whose reaction has truly exhausted itself.

### Additional Considerations

It is worth noting that corticosteroids were administered after the first recurrence, consistent with traditional practice aimed at preventing biphasic reactions. However, as discussed in the case, a 2017 systematic review of 22 studies found no evidence that corticosteroids reduce biphasic anaphylaxis risk, and a 2019 study suggested increased likelihood of ICU or ward admission associated with prehospital corticosteroid use. Contemporary guidelines have consequently shifted away from routine corticosteroid administration in anaphylaxis.

The suspected systemic mastocytosis in this patient—supported by elevated baseline and acute tryptase levels, hepatomegaly, and neuropsychiatric symptoms—provides an important pathophysiological context. In mast cell disorders, the total mast cell burden is markedly increased, which may explain both the severity and the multiphasic nature of the reaction, as the larger mast cell pool allows for prolonged or repeated waves of mediator release even after the initial trigger has been cleared.

### Key Teaching Point

In multiphasic anaphylaxis, the choice between preemptive continuous epinephrine and expectant observation must be individualized, weighing the apparent trajectory of the reaction (improving vs. worsening with each episode), the patient's comorbidities, available monitoring resources, and suspected underlying mast cell pathology. The absence of evidence-based guidelines for this rare scenario means that clinical judgment, guided by the pattern of each individual patient's reaction, remains essential. A registry of such cases, as the authors suggest, would be invaluable for developing data-driven management recommendations.

### [Morning Report] What risk factors have been proposed for predicting biphasic anaphylaxis, and why has reliable risk stratification proven difficult despite multiple proposed predictors?

# Risk Factors for Biphasic Anaphylaxis and the Challenge of Risk Stratification

Several predictors of biphasic anaphylaxis have been proposed, including **delayed epinephrine administration**, **severity of the initial presentation**, **requirement for multiple epinephrine doses**, and a **prior history of biphasic reactions**. Our patient exhibited several of these: she presented with severe initial anaphylactic shock (hypotension to 74/50 mm Hg, hypoxaemia to 88% on room air), required repeated intramuscular epinephrine across four discrete episodes, and had an underlying predisposition to severe anaphylaxis in the setting of likely systemic mastocytosis with persistently elevated tryptase levels. Yet despite possessing multiple proposed risk factors, her clinical course — multiphasic rather than simply biphasic, with gradually diminishing symptom intensity — illustrates precisely why validated risk stratification remains elusive.

The fundamental problem is threefold. **First**, biphasic anaphylaxis is uncommon, with an incidence likely between 4% and 6%, and multiphasic presentations are rarer still. This low event rate severely limits the statistical power of available studies and has precluded the development of well-validated predictive models. **Second**, definitions of biphasic anaphylaxis remain inconsistent across studies — varying in the required asymptomatic interval, the time window for recurrence (reported up to 78 hours), and whether reactions reflecting ongoing allergen absorption or transient epinephrine response are excluded — introducing heterogeneity that undermines comparison and synthesis of findings. **Third**, the proposed predictors themselves are correlates rather than independent drivers. Severe initial reactions and multiple epinephrine doses may simply reflect a more robust mast cell burden or ongoing mast cell activation rather than mechanistically causing recurrence, and they overlap considerably with one another, limiting their discriminative value for individual patients.

The role of systemic mastocytosis is particularly instructive here. It is well established that mast cell disorders predispose patients to severe and treatment-resistant anaphylaxis, yet to date no research has specifically examined the relationship between systemic mastocytosis and biphasic or multiphasic courses. Our patient's elevated baseline tryptase, hepatomegaly, and neuropsychiatric symptoms were consistent with systemic mastocytosis but fell short of formal WHO diagnostic criteria — a scenario that itself highlights the difficulty of identifying and studying relevant subpopulations.

In summary, while the proposed risk factors — delayed epinephrine, severe initial reaction, multiple epinephrine doses, and prior biphasic episodes — are clinically sensible and worth noting at the bedside, none has demonstrated sufficient sensitivity or specificity to reliably guide individual observation decisions. The rarity of the phenomenon, definitional inconsistency, and the lack of mechanistic understanding of what drives recurrence after apparent resolution collectively explain why current guidelines offer general observation timeframes rather than truly risk-stratified, patient-centred monitoring protocols. This gap underscores the authors' call for a prospective registry of complex anaphylaxis cases to generate the data necessary for evidence-based, individualised management recommendations.

### [Management] After an initial anaphylactic reaction resolves, what observation period is traditionally recommended, and how does the UK 2021 guideline's three-tier risk-stratified approach attempt to individualize the observation duration?

**Observation After Anaphylaxis: Traditional Approach and the UK 2021 Risk-Stratified Framework**

Traditionally, an observation period of **4 to 6 hours** after full symptom resolution has been recommended following an anaphylactic episode. This practice stems from the recognition that approximately half of biphasic reactions occur within 6–12 hours of the initial event, and the 4–6 hour window is intended to capture the majority of early recurrences while balancing healthcare resource utilization. Notably, several guidelines—including those from the Dutch NHG and the World Allergy Organization—acknowledge this timing of biphasic recurrence but stop short of providing specific monitoring recommendations.

The **Resuscitation Council UK 2021 guideline** moves beyond this one-size-fits-all approach by proposing a **three-tier, risk-stratified observation strategy**. Rather than applying a uniform observation period to all patients, this framework seeks to individualize monitoring duration based on the patient's estimated risk of a biphasic reaction. Patients deemed **low risk**—for example, those with rapid and complete response to a single dose of epinephrine and no concerning features—may be suitable for shorter observation or even discharge with safety-netting advice. Those at **moderate risk** warrant a more prolonged observation period, typically in the range of 6–12 hours. Patients at **high risk**—characterized by features such as severe initial presentation requiring multiple epinephrine doses, delayed epinephrine administration, history of biphasic anaphylaxis, or limited access to emergency care—justify extended observation, potentially up to 24 hours or beyond.

This case powerfully illustrates the clinical relevance of such stratification. The patient experienced **four separate anaphylactic episodes within 12 hours**, each requiring intramuscular epinephrine, despite initial full clinical resolution between episodes. She was appropriately escalated to the ICU after the first recurrence, and only after approximately **24 hours without renewed symptoms** was she transferred back to a standard ward. Her course exemplifies the high-risk category: severe initial presentation with hypotension and hypoxemia, requirement for multiple epinephrine doses, and an underlying predisposing condition (high probability of systemic mastocytosis with persistently elevated tryptase). A fixed 4–6 hour observation would have been insufficient, as her relapses occurred well beyond that window.

The UK 2021 approach represents a necessary evolution from blanket observation protocols toward individualized risk assessment, acknowledging that the vast majority of anaphylaxis cases are single-episode events while ensuring that the small but clinically critical subset of patients at risk for biphasic or multiphasic courses receive appropriately extended monitoring. As this case demonstrates, the absence of reliable predictors for recurrence—beyond broad risk factors—remains a challenge, and further research, potentially through case registries, will be essential to refine risk stratification and optimize resource allocation.

### [Pathophysiology] By what mechanisms do mast cell mediators such as histamine, tryptase, and prostaglandins produce the multisystem manifestations of anaphylaxis, and how does persistent mast cell activation in systemic mastocytosis contribute to both acute severity and risk of recurrence?

## Mechanisms of Mast Cell Mediators in Anaphylaxis and the Role of Systemic Mastocytosis

Mast cell degranulation in anaphylaxis releases a constellation of preformed and newly synthesized mediators that act on multiple organ systems simultaneously, producing the characteristic multisystem phenotype. Understanding these mechanisms is essential for appreciating why anaphylaxis can be both acutely severe and prone to recurrence, particularly in the setting of an underlying mast cell disorder.

**Histamine**, the most abundant preformed mediator stored in mast cell granules, acts primarily through H1 and H2 receptors distributed across vascular endothelium, bronchial smooth muscle, and cardiac tissue. H1 receptor activation produces vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, bronchoconstriction, coronary artery spasm, and pruritus — accounting for the flushing, urticaria, angioedema, hypotension, and dyspnea observed in our patient. H2 receptor stimulation contributes additionally to vasodilation, myocardial irritability, and increased gastric acid secretion. The hypotension (74/50 mm Hg) and hypoxemia (88% on room air) at initial presentation, along with generalized erythema and urticaria, are hallmark manifestations of histamine's rapid endothelial and smooth muscle effects.

**Tryptase**, a serine protease stored exclusively in mast cell granules, serves as both a mediator and a diagnostic biomarker. While its precise pathophysiologic contributions — including complement activation and metalloprotease stimulation — are less clinically prominent than those of histamine, its release kinetics make it invaluable for confirming mast cell degranulation. Critically, baseline tryptase elevation suggests an underlying mast cell burden disorder. In this case, both event-related and baseline tryptase levels were elevated, supporting a high probability of systemic mastocytosis despite the patient not meeting formal WHO diagnostic criteria (only two minor criteria fulfilled, with the major criterion not assessable due to limited bone marrow biopsy material).

**Prostaglandins**, particularly PGD2, are newly synthesized mediators generated through arachidonic acid metabolism via the cyclooxygenase pathway following IgE-mediated mast cell activation. PGD2 is a potent bronchoconstrictor and vasodilator that contributes to airway narrowing, hypotension, and flushing. These lipid mediators are synthesized de novo after cell activation, meaning their effects may persist beyond the initial histamine burst, contributing to sustained or recurrent symptom phases.

**Systemic mastocytosis and the risk of multiphasic recurrence.** In systemic mastocytosis, there is clonal expansion of mast cells with excessive, often constitutive, mediator release and multiorgan inflammation. The elevated baseline mast cell burden creates a substantially larger reservoir of preformed mediators available for release upon triggering. When an allergen such as clarithromycin cross-links IgE on this expanded mast cell population, the magnitude of mediator release is proportionally greater, explaining why systemic mastocytosis predisposes patients to severe anaphylaxis. This patient's four discrete anaphylactic episodes — each requiring intramuscular epinephrine — despite discontinuation of the trigger after a single 500 mg dose, illustrate how an expanded and persistently activatable mast cell population can generate recurrent waves of mediator release without re-exposure. The gradually diminishing intensity of each successive episode may reflect progressive depletion of preformed mediator stores, though ongoing de novo synthesis of lipid mediators and continued mast cell reactivation could sustain further relapses if the stimulus were not fully cleared or the mast cell population were sufficiently large.

This pathophysiologic framework also explains why standard adjunctive therapies such as antihistamines and corticosteroids may be insufficient in this context: antihistamines block only histamine receptor–mediated effects while leaving prostaglandin, leukotriene, and tryptase pathways unaddressed, and corticosteroids — whose benefit in preventing biphasic reactions is increasingly questioned — act too slowly to interrupt acute mast cell degranulation. Epinephrine remains the cornerstone because it directly reverses vasodilation and bronchoconstriction via alpha- and beta-adrenergic receptor agonism while also stabilizing mast cells through beta-2 receptor–mediated inhibition of further degranulation. The possibility of underlying systemic mastocytosis should be actively investigated in any patient presenting with multiphasic anaphylaxis, as it carries direct implications for long-term management, including baseline tryptase monitoring, avoidance counseling, consideration of mast cell stabilizing agents, and specialist referral.
