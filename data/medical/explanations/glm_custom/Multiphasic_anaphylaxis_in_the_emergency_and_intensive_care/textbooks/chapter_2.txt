Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

Chapter 2: ## Multiphasic Course and Intensive Care Management

Anaphylaxis is typically a single, self-limited event that resolves with prompt epinephrine administration and supportive care. However, a subset of patients experience recurrent symptoms after an apparent complete resolution, a phenomenon termed biphasic anaphylaxis. The Resuscitation Council UK defines biphasic anaphylaxis as a recurrence of symptoms several hours after apparent symptom resolution in the absence of further allergen exposure. When multiple recurrences occur, the term multiphasic anaphylaxis is applied, though this entity is far less well characterized. It is important to distinguish biphasic and multiphasic reactions from refractory anaphylaxis, which refers to persistent anaphylaxis that does not respond, or responds insufficiently, to correctly administered epinephrine. This distinction matters clinically: a patient whose symptoms fully resolve before recurring is experiencing a biphasic or multiphasic process, while one whose symptoms never truly abate may have ongoing allergen absorption—such as from a slow-release medication—or an inadequate epinephrine response requiring continuous infusion. In the case under discussion, the patient demonstrated clear intervals of symptomatic resolution between episodes, confirming a true multiphasic course rather than refractory anaphylaxis.

After the patient's initial presentation and stabilization in the emergency department, she appeared to respond well to intramuscular epinephrine and ancillary therapy. Blood pressure normalized, facial swelling and erythema diminished, and supplemental oxygen was discontinued. She was admitted to the acute internal ward for monitoring. Despite this initial favorable response, she developed recurrent episodes of anaphylactic shock within the first 12 hours of admission, ultimately totaling four anaphylactic episodes including the original ED presentation. After the first relapse, she was transferred to the intensive care unit for closer monitoring. Each recurrence required intramuscular epinephrine for resolution. Notably, the intensity of symptoms progressively diminished with each episode: the initial presentation featured profound hypoxaemia with cyanosis, hypotension, and severe dyspnea, whereas the final episode was characterized primarily by dyspnea, recurrent rash, and pruritus. This gradual attenuation of severity, while reassuring, did not eliminate the need for active treatment of each episode.

The epinephrine dosing used in this case merits close attention. The first two episodes were treated with 0.3 mg of epinephrine administered via auto-injector, while the subsequent two episodes were treated with 0.5 mg doses individually prepared for intramuscular injection. The transition to the higher dose reflects the guideline-recommended dosing of 0.01 mg per kilogram of body weight, with a maximum adult dose of 0.5 mg. At a body weight of 104 kg, this patient's calculated dose would be approximately 1 mg, meaning even the 0.5 mg dose fell short of the weight-based target. The initial use of a 0.3 mg auto-injector represents a common but important clinical problem: many adult auto-injectors contain only 0.3 mg of epinephrine, which is subtherapeutic for adults weighing more than 50 kg. The treating team's rationale for using auto-injectors initially was pragmatic—they minimize treatment delay and reduce hesitancy among personnel who rarely administer vasoactive medications. However, this case illustrates the potential consequence of underdosing, as a retrospective study cited by the authors showed that patients initially receiving 0.3 mg epinephrine required escalation of care significantly more often than those receiving 0.5 mg. The switch to individually prepared 0.5 mg doses after ICU admission likely reflected both the availability of more resources for medication preparation and the recognition that the initial dose may have been insufficient.

In addition to epinephrine, corticosteroids were administered after the first recurrence of anaphylaxis, reflecting their traditional role in preventing biphasic reactions. The patient received both prednisone 30 mg intravenously daily for five days and a single dose of dexamethasone 8 mg intravenously, a discrepancy that arose from differences between the hospital's general ward and intensive care anaphylaxis protocols. This redundancy underscores a broader issue in anaphylaxis management: while corticosteroids were long recommended for biphasic prophylaxis, accumulating evidence has substantially undermined this practice. A 2017 systematic review of 22 studies found that only one study supported corticosteroid use, while the remaining 21 showed no evidence that corticosteroids reduce the risk of biphasic anaphylaxis. More recent data have suggested potential harm, with one 2019 study demonstrating an increased likelihood of ICU or hospital ward admission following prehospital corticosteroid administration. Contemporary guidelines have consequently shifted away from routine corticosteroid use in anaphylaxis, though their persistence in clinical protocols—as illustrated by the dual prescriptions in this case—remains a practical reality.

The early involvement of the allergology department reflects the complexity of managing multiphasic anaphylaxis, for which no evidence-based guidelines exist. Two strategic approaches were considered. The first was preemptive continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion, which would theoretically maintain therapeutic epinephrine levels and prevent further recurrences rather than waiting for symptoms to reappear. The second was an expectant approach, capitalizing on the observed trend of diminishing symptom severity, to allow potential spontaneous resolution while maintaining close monitoring and readiness to treat. Each strategy carried distinct risks: continuous epinephrine infusion requires careful titration and intensive monitoring, carries risks of tachyarrhythmia, hypertension, and myocardial ischemia, and had no established evidence base for multiphasic anaphylaxis. Conversely, the expectant approach risked undertreating a potentially life-threatening recurrence. Ultimately, neither strategy was required, as the patient experienced no further symptom relapse after the fourth episode. She was transferred back to a standard care ward after approximately 24 hours without renewed anaphylaxis and was discharged after three days of total admission. This outcome highlights a key clinical lesson: in multiphasic anaphylaxis, the natural history is poorly predictable, and management decisions must balance the risks of aggressive intervention against the risks of inadequate vigilance, often in the absence of reliable evidence to guide the choice.
