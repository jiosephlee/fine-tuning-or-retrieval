Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Clinical Pearls] This patient presented with persistent hiccups and an elevated creatine kinase of 1349 U/L. How can these findings serve as clinical clues to the severity of his underlying electrolyte disturbance, and what mechanism explains the relationship between profound hyponatremia and rhabdomyolysis?

## Clinical Teaching Answer

Persistent hiccups and an elevated creatine kinase (CK) level in this patient served as two important, though underappreciated, clinical markers of the severity of his hyponatremia. His serum sodium was 124 mmol/L—a profound degree of hypo-osmolar hyponatremia secondary to SIADH driven by central nervous system inflammation from acute neuro-Behçet's disease.

**Persistent hiccups** are a recognized but often overlooked sign of severe hyponatremia, particularly when due to SIADH in the context of CNS inflammation, including meningitis. Hiccups arise from irritation or dysfunction of brainstem structures along the medullary hiccup reflex arc. In profound hyponatremia, the osmotic shift of water into brain cells causes cerebral edema, which can irritate these brainstem pathways. In this patient, persistent hiccups were present from the outset alongside headache and vertigo—symptoms that in retrospect reflected both the neuroinflammatory process and the severity of the electrolyte disturbance.

**Elevated CK (1349 U/L)** reflected rhabdomyolysis, another recognized but infrequently investigated complication of profound hyponatremia. The mechanism relates to the rapid osmotic gradient created between the hypotonic extracellular fluid and the relatively hypertonic intracellular compartment of skeletal muscle cells. When serum sodium falls substantially, water shifts into myocytes, producing cellular swelling, increased membrane permeability, and eventual disruption of the sarcolemma. This osmotic stress impairs the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump, promotes intracellular calcium accumulation, activates proteases and phospholipases, and culminates in muscle cell necrosis with release of CK and myoglobin. Although this patient had no overt muscle weakness on examination, the markedly elevated CK indicated significant subclinical muscle injury—underscoring that rhabdomyolysis in hyponatremia can occur even without prominent muscular symptoms.

The key teaching point is that in any patient presenting with CNS symptoms and hyponatremia, persistent hiccups and an unexplained CK elevation should not be dismissed as incidental. Rather, they may signal that the electrolyte disturbance is profound enough to produce brainstem irritation and skeletal muscle cell injury, respectively. In this case, both findings resolved alongside correction of hyponatremia and treatment of the underlying neuro-Behçet's disease with colchicine, confirming their direct link to the severity of the SIADH-mediated hyponatremia. Clinicians should maintain a low threshold to measure CK in patients with profound hyponatremia, as rhabdomyolysis is a frequently missed complication that carries its own risks of acute kidney injury and pigment nephropathy.
