Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Clinical Pearls] HLA-B51 was positive in this patient but did not contribute meaningfully to establishing the diagnosis. What is the role of HLA-B51 in the diagnostic evaluation of Behçet's disease, and why do international criteria not require it for diagnosis?

## Role of HLA-B51 in Behçet's Disease Diagnosis

HLA-B51 is the strongest known genetic association with Behçet's disease (BD), and its presence supports the diagnosis in the appropriate clinical context. However, it is **not included as a diagnostic criterion** in the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease (ICBD) for several important reasons.

**Limited specificity.** HLA-B51 is prevalent in the general population, particularly in countries along the historic Silk Road — including Turkey, the Mediterranean basin, the Middle East, and East Asia — where BD is also most common. A positive HLA-B51 result is therefore not uncommon in healthy individuals from these populations, giving it low positive predictive value. Conversely, a substantial proportion of patients with definite BD are HLA-B51–negative, limiting its sensitivity as well. Because the test is neither sufficiently specific nor sensitive, it cannot reliably distinguish BD from other inflammatory or infectious conditions in the differential diagnosis.

**Clinical features remain the diagnostic gold standard.** The ICBD, revised in 2014, is a point-based system centered on objective clinical findings: oral aphthosis, genital aphthosis, ocular lesions, skin lesions, neurological manifestations, and vascular manifestations. A score of ≥4 establishes definite BD. This approach reflects the understanding that BD is fundamentally a clinical diagnosis — one of pattern recognition and exclusion — rather than a diagnosis anchored to any single laboratory or genetic marker.

**How this applies to the present case.** In this patient, the diagnosis of BD was established by mucocutaneous findings alone: oral aphthae (2 points), genital aphthosis (2 points), and folliculitis-like rash (1 point), yielding a total of 5 points — well above the threshold of 4. HLA-B51 was positive but, as the authors note, "did not contribute to establishing the diagnosis." Had the test been negative, the clinical criteria alone would still have secured the diagnosis. This illustrates precisely why international criteria do not require it: HLA-B51 may provide supportive or epidemiological context, but it does not meaningfully change the diagnostic determination when clinical features are sufficient, nor can it substitute for them when they are not.

In summary, HLA-B51 is best understood as a **risk-associated allele** rather than a diagnostic test. Its main utility lies in strengthening clinical suspicion in atypical or early presentations, but the diagnosis of BD — including neuro-BD — rests on recognizable clinical patterns and the exclusion of alternative causes.
