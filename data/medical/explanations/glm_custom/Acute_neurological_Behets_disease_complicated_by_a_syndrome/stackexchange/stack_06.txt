Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Management] Corticosteroids are considered the mainstay of treatment for acute neuro-BD. What was the rationale for using colchicine monotherapy in this patient, what is its mechanism of anti-inflammatory action, and what evidence supports or refutes its use as monotherapy for neuro-BD?

**Rationale, Mechanism, and Evidence for Colchicine Monotherapy in Acute Neuro-Behçet's Disease**

**Rationale for Colchicine Monotherapy in This Patient**

Several features of this case favored a conservative treatment approach. First, the patient's acute neuro-BD manifested as aseptic meningitis without parenchymal brain lesions—his brain MRI was normal, and he had no focal neurological deficits. As the authors note, spontaneous resolution has been described in cases of neuro-BD presenting solely with meningoencephalitis-like symptoms, suggesting a milder disease phenotype that may not automatically require corticosteroids. Second, the patient had poorly controlled type 2 diabetes mellitus (HbA1c 8.2%, plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L), a setting in which systemic corticosteroid therapy would be clinically undesirable and could exacerbate hyperglycemia and complicate management. Third, because BD and neuro-BD were diagnosed concurrently, the patient had no history of refractory or recurrent neurological disease that would mandate aggressive immunosuppression. These factors together provided a clinical rationale for a trial of colchicine monotherapy before committing to corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents.

**Mechanism of Anti-Inflammatory Action**

Colchicine exerts its anti-inflammatory effects through two principal mechanisms: inhibition of neutrophil migration and activation, and suppression of proinflammatory cytokine production, including interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and interleukin-6 (IL-6). This mechanism is particularly relevant to the pathophysiology proposed in this case. The authors highlight that aberrant monocyte activation in BD leads to spontaneous IL-6 production, and elevated IL-6 has been detected in the CSF of patients with acute neuro-BD. Furthermore, IL-6 has been associated with increased ADH release, providing a plausible link between neuro-BD–associated CNS inflammation and the development of SIADH. The markedly elevated CRP in this patient (180 mg/L) may have reflected this IL-6–driven inflammatory cascade. By suppressing IL-6 and IL-1β production, colchicine could theoretically attenuate both the CNS inflammatory process driving neuro-BD and the downstream neuroendocrine dysregulation responsible for SIADH. The temporal resolution of fever, neurological symptoms, mucocutaneous lesions, and hyponatremia following colchicine initiation is consistent with this proposed mechanism, though causality cannot be definitively established from a single case.

**Evidence Supporting or Refuting Colchicine Monotherapy for Neuro-BD**

The evidence predominantly refutes colchicine as adequate monotherapy for acute neuro-BD. As the authors acknowledge, systematic reviews have shown that colchicine is ineffective for acute neuro-BD and that immunosuppressive agents—typically corticosteroids, with or without additional agents such as azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, or infliximab—are generally required for adequate disease control. In previously reported cases, colchicine has been used as an adjunct to corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive therapies rather than as monotherapy. Corticosteroids remain the mainstay of treatment, and most patients respond favorably.

The evidence supporting colchicine monotherapy is limited to this single case report and analogous reports of spontaneous resolution in mild meningoencephalitis-predominant neuro-BD. The authors appropriately caution that "spontaneous recovery cannot be excluded in this case," meaning the observed improvement may have reflected the natural history of a self-limited acute neuro-BD episode rather than a direct therapeutic effect of colchicine. The absence of parenchymal brain involvement and the relatively indolent CSF profile (mild lymphocytic pleocytosis of 11 cells/µL, mildly elevated protein) are consistent with a milder phenotype that may resolve without aggressive immunosuppression.

**Clinical Bottom Line**

Colchicine monotherapy for acute neuro-BD is not supported by systematic evidence and should not be considered standard of care. Corticosteroids remain first-line for acute neuro-BD, particularly when parenchymal CNS lesions or significant neurological deficits are present. However, this case illustrates that in carefully selected patients with mild neuro-BD limited to aseptic meningitis without parenchymal involvement—especially when corticosteroids are contraindicated or undesirable—colchicine monotherapy may be a reasonable initial strategy, provided that close monitoring for clinical deterioration is maintained and escalation to corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants is readily available if symptoms progress. The 4-year follow-up without recurrence in this patient provides some reassurance but is insufficient to establish colchicine monotherapy as a broadly applicable treatment standard.
