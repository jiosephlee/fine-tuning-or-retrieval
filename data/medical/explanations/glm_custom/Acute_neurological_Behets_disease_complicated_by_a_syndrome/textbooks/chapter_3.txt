Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

Chapter 3: ## SIADH: Pathophysiology, Diagnosis, and Association with Neuro-BD

The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) is a central diagnostic and mechanistic feature of this case, linking the patient's profound hyponatremia to the central nervous system (CNS) inflammation of acute neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD). Understanding how SIADH was confirmed, what alternative diagnoses were excluded, and why it likely developed in this patient requires a systematic approach to hyponatremia evaluation and an appreciation of how CNS inflammation can disrupt the regulation of arginine vasopressin (ADH).

### Establishing the Diagnosis of SIADH

SIADH is defined by the non-physiologic, autonomous release of ADH, which impairs free water excretion and produces hypo-osmolar hyponatremia in a patient who is clinically euvolemic. The diagnosis is never made by a single laboratory value; rather, it requires a constellation of findings that together demonstrate impaired water excretion in the absence of an appropriate physiologic stimulus for ADH release. The essential criteria include hypo-osmolar hyponatremia (serum sodium below 135 mmol/L with serum osmolality below 275 mOsm/kg), clinical euvolemia (no signs of hypovolemia or hypervolemia), urine that is not maximally dilute (urine osmolality above 100 mOsm/kg), and elevated urinary sodium concentration (typically above 40 mmol/L) in the setting of normal dietary sodium intake and normal renal function. Additionally, the absence of other causes of hyponatremia—particularly adrenal insufficiency, hypothyroidism, and renal failure—is required, as these conditions can mimic SIADH biochemically.

This patient met each of these criteria. His serum sodium was 124 mmol/L, and his serum osmolality was low at 267 mOsm/kg, establishing hypo-osmolar hyponatremia. On physical examination, he had no peripheral edema, no signs of dehydration, and his volume status was assessed as euvolemic. His urine osmolality was markedly elevated at 900 mOsm/kg, far exceeding the threshold that would be expected if ADH were appropriately suppressed in the setting of low serum osmolality. His urinary sodium concentration was elevated at 88 mmol/L, consistent with ongoing renal sodium excretion despite hyponatremia—a hallmark of SIADH. Notably, his ADH level was elevated at 22.4 pg/mL, a finding that is pathologic in the context of hypo-osmolality, because low serum osmolality should normally suppress ADH secretion to near-undetectable levels. This inappropriately elevated ADH level provides direct evidence of non-physiologic vasopressin release and was a key confirmatory finding in this case.

### Excluding Alternative Causes of Hyponatremia

Before SIADH can be diagnosed, clinicians must systematically exclude other causes of hyponatremia that can produce a similar biochemical picture. The most important exclusions are adrenal insufficiency, hypothyroidism, and significant renal dysfunction. Adrenal insufficiency causes hyponatremia through a combination of reduced cortisol production—which increases ADH release—and mineralocorticoid deficiency leading to volume depletion; both mechanisms can produce euvolemic or hypovolemic hyponatremia with elevated urinary sodium, closely mimicking SIADH. Hypothyroidism can cause hyponatremia through reduced cardiac output and increased ADH secretion, and renal failure impairs the kidney's ability to dilute or concentrate urine, which can confound the interpretation of urinary indices.

In this patient, renal function was normal, ruling out a renal contribution to the electrolyte disturbance. The endocrine evaluation was particularly important: serum cortisol was 42 µg/dL, well above the reference threshold of greater than 6 µg/dL, effectively excluding adrenal insufficiency. Thyroid-stimulating hormone was 1.11 mIU/L (reference range 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), and free T4 was 1.25 ng/dL (reference range 0.90–1.70 ng/dL), both within normal limits, excluding hypothyroidism. These exclusionary tests were essential because treating SIADH in a patient who actually had adrenal insufficiency or hypothyroidism would not address the underlying cause and could delay critical therapy, such as glucocorticoid replacement. With these conditions excluded and no evidence of volume depletion, cardiac failure, or liver disease, the diagnosis of SIADH was firmly established.

### Pathophysiology: Linking CNS Inflammation to Dysregulated ADH Secretion

SIADH can be triggered by a diverse array of conditions, including malignancies (particularly small cell lung cancer through ectopic ADH production), pulmonary diseases, and numerous medications. However, CNS inflammation is a well-recognized cause, and the pathophysiology in this case is best understood through the lens of neuroinflammation. Under normal physiology, ADH secretion is tightly regulated by osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus and by baroreceptors in the carotid sinus and aortic arch. When serum osmolality falls, ADH release should be suppressed, allowing the kidney to excrete dilute urine and restore normal serum sodium. In SIADH secondary to CNS inflammation, this regulatory mechanism is disrupted, and ADH is released independent of osmotic signals.

The proposed molecular link in this case centers on interleukin-6 (IL-6), a proinflammatory cytokine that has been shown to stimulate ADH release from the posterior pituitary. Several lines of evidence support this mechanism. First, IL-6 is frequently elevated in neuroinflammatory disorders, and experimental studies have demonstrated that IL-6 can directly promote vasopressin secretion from hypothalamic neurons. Second, in Behçet's disease (BD), aberrant monocyte activation leads to spontaneous IL-6 production, and elevated IL-6 levels have been detected in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) of patients with acute neuro-BD. Third, this patient had a markedly elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) of 180 mg/L, which is an indirect marker of IL-6–mediated hepatic acute-phase response, suggesting a prominent IL-6–driven inflammatory milieu. Although IL-6 levels were not directly measured in the serum or CSF in this case, the clinical and laboratory context is consistent with IL-6–mediated stimulation of ADH as the mechanism linking CNS inflammation to SIADH.

It is important to note, as the authors of this case report acknowledge, that a definitive causal link between acute neuro-BD and SIADH cannot be established from a single case. The temporal association—the simultaneous presence of aseptic meningitis, elevated inflammatory markers, and SIADH, all of which resolved together—suggests a shared inflammatory mechanism but does not prove disease specificity. SIADH may represent a nonspecific response to CNS inflammation rather than a mechanism unique to neuro-BD. Nevertheless, the case highlights that clinicians evaluating patients with neuro-BD and hyponatremia should consider SIADH as a potential complication.

### Underrecognized Clinical Consequences of Profound Hyponatremia

Two clinical features in this case deserve special attention because they represent underrecognized manifestations of severe hyponatremia: persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis. The patient presented with persistent hiccups, which might appear to be an incidental nuisance symptom but has been reported as an important clinical clue to severe hyponatremia secondary to CNS inflammation. The mechanism likely involves irritation of CNS structures mediating the hiccup reflex, and in the context of meningitis and SIADH, persistent hiccups should prompt evaluation of serum sodium. This symptom was present from the outset alongside fever and headache, and its resolution paralleled the correction of hyponatremia, supporting its relationship to the electrolyte disturbance.

The patient also had an elevated creatine kinase (CK) of 1349 U/L, indicating rhabdomyolysis. Rhabdomyolysis is a recognized but frequently overlooked complication of profound hyponatremia. The mechanism involves osmotic shifts that disrupt skeletal muscle cell integrity, particularly when serum sodium falls rapidly or to very low levels. In this case, CK was measured as part of the initial evaluation of fever, arthralgia, and general fatigue, and the finding of rhabdomyolysis was not anticipated on clinical grounds alone. This underscores the importance of checking CK in patients with severe hyponatremia, even when myalgia or weakness is not a prominent complaint, because unrecognized rhabdomyolysis can lead to acute kidney injury and complicate fluid management. Both persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis resolved as the serum sodium normalized, further supporting their relationship to the hyponatremic state. For clinicians managing patients with neuro-BD, awareness of these apparently unrelated symptoms can facilitate earlier recognition of SIADH and its complications.
