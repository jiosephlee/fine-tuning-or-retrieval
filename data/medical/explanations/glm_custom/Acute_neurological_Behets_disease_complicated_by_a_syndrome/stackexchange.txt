Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Morning Report] What are the clinical features that contribute to the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease (ICBD) scoring system, and what score threshold establishes a diagnosis of definite BD?

The International Criteria for Behçet's Disease (ICBD), revised in 2014, assign weighted points to key clinical manifestations based on their specificity for BD. The scoring system is as follows:

- **Ocular lesions** (e.g., uveitis, retinal vasculitis): **2 points**
- **Genital aphthosis**: **2 points**
- **Oral aphthosis**: **2 points**
- **Skin lesions** (e.g., folliculitis-like rash, erythema nodosum, acneiform nodules): **1 point**
- **Neurological manifestations**: **1 point**
- **Vascular manifestations** (e.g., superficial phlebitis, deep vein thrombosis, arterial thrombosis/aneurysm): **1 point**
- **Positive pathergy test** (optional): **1 point**

A **total score of ≥4 points** establishes a diagnosis of definite BD.

In the case under discussion, the patient's diagnosis was supported by **oral aphthae (2 points)**, **genital aphthosis (2 points)**, and a **folliculitis-like rash (1 point)**, yielding a total score of **5 points**. Notably, although the patient had documented neurological involvement—aseptic meningitis consistent with acute neuro-BD—the systemic BD diagnosis itself was established purely on mucocutaneous features, which preceded or coincided with the neurological presentation. This underscores an important teaching point: the ICBD relies primarily on **clinical features** and does not require HLA-B51 positivity or radiological confirmation. In this patient, HLA-B51 was positive but contributed nothing additional to the diagnostic determination, consistent with the criteria's design. The concurrent neurological manifestations, while part of the ICBD scoring system (worth 1 point), were not needed to reach the diagnostic threshold here, though they were essential for establishing the separate diagnosis of acute neuro-BD.

### [Pathophysiology] This patient's SIADH was attributed to CNS inflammation from acute neuro-Behçet's disease. What proinflammatory cytokine has been implicated in the pathogenesis of ADH dysregulation in neuroinflammatory conditions, and how might it explain the markedly elevated CRP observed in this patient?

## Answer

**Interleukin-6 (IL-6)** is the proinflammatory cytokine implicated in ADH dysregulation in neuroinflammatory conditions.

In neuroinflammatory states, IL-6 has been shown to stimulate increased release of antidiuretic hormone (ADH) from the posterior pituitary, providing a mechanistic link between central nervous system inflammation and the development of SIADH. In Behçet's disease specifically, aberrant monocyte activation leads to spontaneous IL-6 production, and elevated IL-6 levels have been detected in the cerebrospinal fluid of patients with acute neuro-Behçet's disease. These observations formed the basis for attributing this patient's SIADH to IL-6–mediated inflammation within the CNS rather than to a medication effect or another secondary cause.

Regarding the markedly elevated CRP (180 mg/L), IL-6 is one of the principal upstream drivers of hepatic acute-phase reactant synthesis. IL-6 released during systemic and neuroinflammatory states directly stimulates hepatocytes to produce C-reactive protein, making the degree of CRP elevation a useful indirect marker of IL-6 activity. While IL-6 levels were not directly measured in this patient's serum or CSF, the authors specifically noted that the markedly elevated CRP may reflect an underlying IL-6–mediated inflammatory response. In this way, the same cytokine process proposed to cause ADH dysregulation—IL-6 driven CNS inflammation from acute neuro-BD—also accounts for the striking systemic inflammatory marker observed on presentation.

This dual role of IL-6 is clinically important: it suggests that in neuroinflammatory conditions, a single cytokine pathway may simultaneously produce CNS-specific endocrine dysfunction (SIADH) and systemic inflammatory signatures (elevated CRP). It also provides a rationale for why colchicine, which suppresses IL-6 (and IL-1β) production, was associated in this case with concurrent resolution of neurological symptoms, hyponatremia, and mucocutaneous manifestations.

### [Clinical Pearls] Neuro-Behçet's disease typically develops years after systemic BD is first diagnosed. Why was the concurrent diagnosis of systemic BD and acute neuro-BD in this patient considered unusual, and what proportion of BD patients develop neuro-BD overall?

Neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD) develops in approximately 10% to 20% of patients with Behçet's disease overall. In the vast majority of cases, neurological involvement is a delayed phenomenon: major organ involvement such as CNS lesions typically arises around 4 years after the initial diagnosis of BD, and neurological manifestations are present at disease onset in fewer than 5% of patients. For this reason, BD and neuro-BD are rarely diagnosed concurrently.

This patient's presentation was therefore unusual because he had no prior history of BD, yet he simultaneously exhibited the full mucocutaneous spectrum of systemic BD — oral aphthae, genital ulceration, and folliculitis-like rash — together with acute neurological involvement manifesting as headache, vertigo, and aseptic meningitis on cerebrospinal fluid analysis. The concurrent recognition of both systemic and neurological disease at the initial encounter is inherently challenging, because clinicians typically do not expect CNS involvement at BD onset. Indeed, in this case, the absence of genital ulcers early in the presentation initially broadened the differential diagnosis to include infection, malignancy, and sarcoidosis, and the normal brain MRI — which does not exclude early neuro-BD, particularly when presenting as aseptic meningitis without parenchymal lesions — further complicated recognition.

The key teaching point is that although neuro-BD is an uncommon complication of BD overall and almost always follows the systemic diagnosis by years, it can rarely coincide with the initial presentation. When a patient presents with mucocutaneous features suggestive of BD together with unexplained neurological symptoms and hyponatremia — as seen here with SIADH — clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for acute neuro-BD even in the absence of a prior BD diagnosis.

### [Differential Diagnosis] This patient had a normal brain MRI at presentation. Does a normal brain MRI exclude the diagnosis of acute neuro-BD, and what CSF findings supported the diagnosis of aseptic meningitis in this case?

A normal brain MRI does not exclude acute neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD). As illustrated by this case, acute neuro-BD may present as aseptic meningitis without parenchymal brain lesions, and radiological findings can be entirely normal, particularly early in the disease course. The absence of MRI abnormalities — even at typical sites such as the brainstem or hypothalamus — should therefore not dissuade clinicians from the diagnosis when clinical and CSF findings are suggestive.

The CSF findings supporting aseptic meningitis in this patient included a mildly elevated opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), increased protein (0.97 g/L), and a mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). Crucially, extensive microbiological evaluation was negative, including CSF cultures, acid-fast staining and tuberculosis DNA testing, and assays for HSV, VZV, and cryptococcal antigen. CSF cytology revealed no malignant cells. This pattern — inflammatory CSF profile with lymphocytic predominance and exclusion of infectious and neoplastic causes by negative cultures, molecular testing, and cytology — established the diagnosis of aseptic meningitis.

The key teaching point is that in neuro-BD, the diagnosis is fundamentally clinical and relies on the combination of characteristic systemic features (oral aphthae, genital ulcers, folliculitis-like rash, as seen here), compatible neurological symptoms, and supportive but non-specific CSF findings, after rigorous exclusion of infection and malignancy. MRI may corroborate the diagnosis when parenchymal lesions are present, but a normal scan does not exclude it.

### [Clinical Pearls] This patient presented with persistent hiccups and an elevated creatine kinase of 1349 U/L. How can these findings serve as clinical clues to the severity of his underlying electrolyte disturbance, and what mechanism explains the relationship between profound hyponatremia and rhabdomyolysis?

## Clinical Teaching Answer

Persistent hiccups and an elevated creatine kinase (CK) level in this patient served as two important, though underappreciated, clinical markers of the severity of his hyponatremia. His serum sodium was 124 mmol/L—a profound degree of hypo-osmolar hyponatremia secondary to SIADH driven by central nervous system inflammation from acute neuro-Behçet's disease.

**Persistent hiccups** are a recognized but often overlooked sign of severe hyponatremia, particularly when due to SIADH in the context of CNS inflammation, including meningitis. Hiccups arise from irritation or dysfunction of brainstem structures along the medullary hiccup reflex arc. In profound hyponatremia, the osmotic shift of water into brain cells causes cerebral edema, which can irritate these brainstem pathways. In this patient, persistent hiccups were present from the outset alongside headache and vertigo—symptoms that in retrospect reflected both the neuroinflammatory process and the severity of the electrolyte disturbance.

**Elevated CK (1349 U/L)** reflected rhabdomyolysis, another recognized but infrequently investigated complication of profound hyponatremia. The mechanism relates to the rapid osmotic gradient created between the hypotonic extracellular fluid and the relatively hypertonic intracellular compartment of skeletal muscle cells. When serum sodium falls substantially, water shifts into myocytes, producing cellular swelling, increased membrane permeability, and eventual disruption of the sarcolemma. This osmotic stress impairs the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump, promotes intracellular calcium accumulation, activates proteases and phospholipases, and culminates in muscle cell necrosis with release of CK and myoglobin. Although this patient had no overt muscle weakness on examination, the markedly elevated CK indicated significant subclinical muscle injury—underscoring that rhabdomyolysis in hyponatremia can occur even without prominent muscular symptoms.

The key teaching point is that in any patient presenting with CNS symptoms and hyponatremia, persistent hiccups and an unexplained CK elevation should not be dismissed as incidental. Rather, they may signal that the electrolyte disturbance is profound enough to produce brainstem irritation and skeletal muscle cell injury, respectively. In this case, both findings resolved alongside correction of hyponatremia and treatment of the underlying neuro-Behçet's disease with colchicine, confirming their direct link to the severity of the SIADH-mediated hyponatremia. Clinicians should maintain a low threshold to measure CK in patients with profound hyponatremia, as rhabdomyolysis is a frequently missed complication that carries its own risks of acute kidney injury and pigment nephropathy.

### [Management] Corticosteroids are considered the mainstay of treatment for acute neuro-BD. What was the rationale for using colchicine monotherapy in this patient, what is its mechanism of anti-inflammatory action, and what evidence supports or refutes its use as monotherapy for neuro-BD?

**Rationale, Mechanism, and Evidence for Colchicine Monotherapy in Acute Neuro-Behçet's Disease**

**Rationale for Colchicine Monotherapy in This Patient**

Several features of this case favored a conservative treatment approach. First, the patient's acute neuro-BD manifested as aseptic meningitis without parenchymal brain lesions—his brain MRI was normal, and he had no focal neurological deficits. As the authors note, spontaneous resolution has been described in cases of neuro-BD presenting solely with meningoencephalitis-like symptoms, suggesting a milder disease phenotype that may not automatically require corticosteroids. Second, the patient had poorly controlled type 2 diabetes mellitus (HbA1c 8.2%, plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L), a setting in which systemic corticosteroid therapy would be clinically undesirable and could exacerbate hyperglycemia and complicate management. Third, because BD and neuro-BD were diagnosed concurrently, the patient had no history of refractory or recurrent neurological disease that would mandate aggressive immunosuppression. These factors together provided a clinical rationale for a trial of colchicine monotherapy before committing to corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive agents.

**Mechanism of Anti-Inflammatory Action**

Colchicine exerts its anti-inflammatory effects through two principal mechanisms: inhibition of neutrophil migration and activation, and suppression of proinflammatory cytokine production, including interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and interleukin-6 (IL-6). This mechanism is particularly relevant to the pathophysiology proposed in this case. The authors highlight that aberrant monocyte activation in BD leads to spontaneous IL-6 production, and elevated IL-6 has been detected in the CSF of patients with acute neuro-BD. Furthermore, IL-6 has been associated with increased ADH release, providing a plausible link between neuro-BD–associated CNS inflammation and the development of SIADH. The markedly elevated CRP in this patient (180 mg/L) may have reflected this IL-6–driven inflammatory cascade. By suppressing IL-6 and IL-1β production, colchicine could theoretically attenuate both the CNS inflammatory process driving neuro-BD and the downstream neuroendocrine dysregulation responsible for SIADH. The temporal resolution of fever, neurological symptoms, mucocutaneous lesions, and hyponatremia following colchicine initiation is consistent with this proposed mechanism, though causality cannot be definitively established from a single case.

**Evidence Supporting or Refuting Colchicine Monotherapy for Neuro-BD**

The evidence predominantly refutes colchicine as adequate monotherapy for acute neuro-BD. As the authors acknowledge, systematic reviews have shown that colchicine is ineffective for acute neuro-BD and that immunosuppressive agents—typically corticosteroids, with or without additional agents such as azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, or infliximab—are generally required for adequate disease control. In previously reported cases, colchicine has been used as an adjunct to corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive therapies rather than as monotherapy. Corticosteroids remain the mainstay of treatment, and most patients respond favorably.

The evidence supporting colchicine monotherapy is limited to this single case report and analogous reports of spontaneous resolution in mild meningoencephalitis-predominant neuro-BD. The authors appropriately caution that "spontaneous recovery cannot be excluded in this case," meaning the observed improvement may have reflected the natural history of a self-limited acute neuro-BD episode rather than a direct therapeutic effect of colchicine. The absence of parenchymal brain involvement and the relatively indolent CSF profile (mild lymphocytic pleocytosis of 11 cells/µL, mildly elevated protein) are consistent with a milder phenotype that may resolve without aggressive immunosuppression.

**Clinical Bottom Line**

Colchicine monotherapy for acute neuro-BD is not supported by systematic evidence and should not be considered standard of care. Corticosteroids remain first-line for acute neuro-BD, particularly when parenchymal CNS lesions or significant neurological deficits are present. However, this case illustrates that in carefully selected patients with mild neuro-BD limited to aseptic meningitis without parenchymal involvement—especially when corticosteroids are contraindicated or undesirable—colchicine monotherapy may be a reasonable initial strategy, provided that close monitoring for clinical deterioration is maintained and escalation to corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants is readily available if symptoms progress. The 4-year follow-up without recurrence in this patient provides some reassurance but is insufficient to establish colchicine monotherapy as a broadly applicable treatment standard.

### [Management] This patient's SIADH was managed conservatively with fluid restriction (<800 mL/day) without hypertonic saline or vasopressin receptor antagonists. What is the primary concern with rapid correction of serum sodium in the setting of SIADH, and what is the generally recommended maximum rate of sodium correction per 24 hours?

## Clinical Teaching Answer

The primary concern with overly rapid correction of serum sodium in SIADH is the development of **osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS)**, also historically referred to as central pontine myelinolysis. This is a devastating and often irreversible neurologic complication characterized by demyelination, particularly in the pons, but also in extrapontine regions.

### Pathophysiology

In the setting of chronic or subacute hyponatremia, brain astrocytes adapt to the hypotonic extracellular environment by extruding osmolytes—principally myo-inositol, taurine, glutamate, and other organic osmolytes—to minimize cerebral edema. This adaptive downregulation takes hours to days. When serum sodium is corrected rapidly, the extracellular milieu becomes abruptly hypertonic relative to the adapted intracellular environment. Because brain cells cannot rapidly reacquire the lost osmolytes, water shifts out of astrocytes, causing cell shrinkage, disruption of the blood-brain barrier, and oligodendrocyte injury with subsequent demyelination. The clinical consequences can include quadriparesis, pseudobulbar palsy, locked-in syndrome, seizures, coma, and even death.

### Recommended Correction Rate

The generally recommended maximum rate of sodium correction is **8 to 12 mmol/L per 24 hours**, with many guidelines favoring the lower end of this range (≤8–10 mmol/L per 24 hours) as a safety margin. Correction should not exceed **18 mmol/L over any 48-hour period**. Patients at particularly high risk for ODS—including those with serum sodium ≤105 mmol/L, hypokalemia, alcohol use disorder, malnutrition, or advanced liver disease—warrant even more cautious correction, with a target of ≤8 mmol/L per 24 hours.

### Application to This Case

This patient's SIADH was managed conservatively with fluid restriction to less than 800 mL/day, without hypertonic saline or vasopressin receptor antagonists, and with explicit attention to avoiding rapid sodium correction. His initial serum sodium was 124 mmol/L—moderately severe, though above the threshold for highest ODS risk. Given the absence of severe symptoms such as seizures or coma, a conservative approach was appropriate. Over approximately 16 days, the serum sodium normalized to 136 mmol/L alongside treatment of the underlying inflammatory process with colchicine. This gradual trajectory reflects a correction rate well within safe limits and illustrates an important principle: when the underlying cause of SIADH is treated and the stimulus for excess ADH resolves, serum sodium can normalize without aggressive intervention, minimizing ODS risk.

### Key Teaching Point

The decision to use hypertonic saline should be reserved for patients with severe symptomatic hyponatremia (e.g., seizures, coma, or impending herniation) where the risk of ongoing cerebral edema outweighs the risk of ODS. In such scenarios, even rapid correction must be carefully capped, and serum sodium should be monitored frequently (every 2–4 hours initially). In less urgent settings—as in this case—fluid restriction, treatment of the underlying etiology, and close monitoring represent sound management and avoid the iatrogenic danger of overly rapid correction.

### [Differential Diagnosis] The initial differential diagnosis for this patient included viral meningoencephalitis (HSV, VZV), bacterial meningitis, tuberculous meningitis, primary CNS malignancy, neurosarcoidosis, and autoimmune conditions. What specific test results led to the exclusion of infectious causes and allowed the diagnosis of aseptic meningitis to be established?

The exclusion of infectious causes in this patient rested on a systematic CSF analysis and targeted microbiological testing, which together established the picture of aseptic meningitis.

CSF analysis revealed a mildly elevated opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), increased protein (0.97 g/L), and a **mild lymphocytic pleocytosis** (11 cells/µL). This cellular profile — lymphocytic rather than neutrophilic predominance — immediately shifted suspicion away from acute bacterial meningitis, a point further reinforced by **negative CSF cultures** for bacterial pathogens. Empirically initiated cefepime was therefore not continued on the basis of a bacterial etiology.

Viral meningoencephalitis, particularly HSV and VZV, was a leading early consideration given the combination of fever, headache, and worsening neurological symptoms. However, **CSF testing for HSV and VZV antigens and antibodies was negative**, leading to discontinuation of empirically started acyclovir. Tuberculous meningitis was excluded by **negative acid-fast staining and tuberculosis DNA testing** on CSF. Cryptococcal antigen testing was also negative. No malignant cells were seen on CSF cytology, helping to exclude leptomeningeal malignancy.

In summary, the combination of a lymphocytic-predominant pleocytosis with elevated protein but **negative bacterial, mycobacterial, viral, and cryptococcal studies** established the CSF profile as aseptic meningitis. Once infectious and neoplastic causes were excluded, and in the context of the patient's evolving mucocutaneous findings — oral aphthae, genital ulceration, and folliculitis-like rash — the diagnosis of acute neuro-Behçet's disease was established on clinical grounds using the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease. This case underscores that aseptic meningitis is a diagnosis of exclusion, and that neuro-BD should be considered when mucocutaneous features accompany CNS inflammation with a sterile CSF profile.

### [Clinical Pearls] HLA-B51 was positive in this patient but did not contribute meaningfully to establishing the diagnosis. What is the role of HLA-B51 in the diagnostic evaluation of Behçet's disease, and why do international criteria not require it for diagnosis?

## Role of HLA-B51 in Behçet's Disease Diagnosis

HLA-B51 is the strongest known genetic association with Behçet's disease (BD), and its presence supports the diagnosis in the appropriate clinical context. However, it is **not included as a diagnostic criterion** in the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease (ICBD) for several important reasons.

**Limited specificity.** HLA-B51 is prevalent in the general population, particularly in countries along the historic Silk Road — including Turkey, the Mediterranean basin, the Middle East, and East Asia — where BD is also most common. A positive HLA-B51 result is therefore not uncommon in healthy individuals from these populations, giving it low positive predictive value. Conversely, a substantial proportion of patients with definite BD are HLA-B51–negative, limiting its sensitivity as well. Because the test is neither sufficiently specific nor sensitive, it cannot reliably distinguish BD from other inflammatory or infectious conditions in the differential diagnosis.

**Clinical features remain the diagnostic gold standard.** The ICBD, revised in 2014, is a point-based system centered on objective clinical findings: oral aphthosis, genital aphthosis, ocular lesions, skin lesions, neurological manifestations, and vascular manifestations. A score of ≥4 establishes definite BD. This approach reflects the understanding that BD is fundamentally a clinical diagnosis — one of pattern recognition and exclusion — rather than a diagnosis anchored to any single laboratory or genetic marker.

**How this applies to the present case.** In this patient, the diagnosis of BD was established by mucocutaneous findings alone: oral aphthae (2 points), genital aphthosis (2 points), and folliculitis-like rash (1 point), yielding a total of 5 points — well above the threshold of 4. HLA-B51 was positive but, as the authors note, "did not contribute to establishing the diagnosis." Had the test been negative, the clinical criteria alone would still have secured the diagnosis. This illustrates precisely why international criteria do not require it: HLA-B51 may provide supportive or epidemiological context, but it does not meaningfully change the diagnostic determination when clinical features are sufficient, nor can it substitute for them when they are not.

In summary, HLA-B51 is best understood as a **risk-associated allele** rather than a diagnostic test. Its main utility lies in strengthening clinical suspicion in atypical or early presentations, but the diagnosis of BD — including neuro-BD — rests on recognizable clinical patterns and the exclusion of alternative causes.

### [Pathophysiology] SIADH has been previously reported in Behçet's disease patients receiving cyclophosphamide. How does the SIADH in this case differ from prior reports, and why is the temporal resolution of hyponatremia following colchicine therapy significant for understanding the relationship between neuro-BD and SIADH?

**How SIADH in This Case Differs from Prior Reports**

In previously reported cases of SIADH occurring in patients with Behçet's disease, the syndrome was attributed to cyclophosphamide therapy — a well-recognized pharmacologic trigger of inappropriate ADH release. In those cases, SIADH was fundamentally a drug-induced adverse effect, temporally and causally linked to medication exposure rather than to the underlying disease process itself.

This case is fundamentally different. The patient developed SIADH in the complete absence of cyclophosphamide or any other known pharmacologic trigger. No offending drug could be implicated. Instead, SIADH appeared concurrently with acute neuro-Behçet's disease, presenting as aseptic meningitis with lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated CSF protein, but without parenchymal brain lesions on MRI. The SIADH was therefore most plausibly linked to the CNS inflammatory process itself. The authors propose an IL-6–mediated mechanism: aberrant monocyte activation in BD drives spontaneous IL-6 production, IL-6 is elevated in the CSF of patients with acute neuro-BD, and IL-6 is known to stimulate ADH release. While IL-6 levels were not directly measured in this patient, the markedly elevated CRP (180 mg/L) is consistent with an IL-6–driven inflammatory response. Thus, this case represents SIADH arising from the disease's neuroinflammatory activity rather than from an iatrogenic trigger — a distinction that shifts the conceptual framework from a medication side effect to a potential direct complication of neuro-BD.

**Why the Temporal Resolution After Colchicine Is Significant**

The significance lies in what happened — and what did not happen — during treatment. The patient received colchicine monotherapy alone, without corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants. Colchicine inhibits neutrophil migration and suppresses cytokine production, including IL-1β and IL-6. Over the following two weeks, the patient's mucocutaneous lesions, neurological symptoms (headache, vertigo, hiccups), serum sodium (124 → 136 mmol/L), and ADH levels (22.4 → 1.8 pg/mL) all normalized in parallel.

This parallel resolution is important for two reasons. First, it provides temporal evidence that SIADH was mechanistically tied to the neuro-BD inflammatory process: as colchicine suppressed the inflammation driving the neurological disease, the stimulus for inappropriate ADH secretion abated, and water homeostasis normalized. If SIADH had been an independent or coincidental process, one would not necessarily expect it to resolve in lockstep with the neurological and mucocutaneous manifestations following a single anti-inflammatory intervention. Second, it demonstrates that treating the underlying inflammation — rather than fluid restriction alone, which was employed but is a supportive measure — addressed the root cause. While spontaneous resolution cannot be entirely excluded, the close temporal coupling between anti-inflammatory treatment and the simultaneous resolution of both neuro-BD and SIADH strongly supports a causal or at least mechanistically linked relationship between CNS inflammation in neuro-BD and dysregulated ADH secretion.

**Clinical Takeaway**

When you encounter a patient with Behçet's disease who presents with headache, fever, and hyponatremia, SIADH should be considered not merely as a drug effect but as a potential direct complication of neuro-BD–associated CNS inflammation. Recognizing this association expands the differential diagnosis for hyponatremia in BD beyond medication side effects and may guide treatment decisions, as controlling the neuroinflammatory process can address both the neurological and endocrine manifestations simultaneously.
