Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# Planning for the Third Valve at the Time of the First: Lifetime Management of Structural Valve Disease

## The 10-Year TAVI Journey That Should Make Us Rethink Everything

A man in his late 70s walks into your cath lab for his *third* transcatheter aortic valve. His first was a Lotus valve implanted in 2014. His second was an Evolut TAV-in-TAV in 2022. Between those two procedures, he survived prosthetic valve endocarditis from *Enterococcus faecalis*. Now he's back with severe restenosis, a mean gradient of 49 mmHg, and an aortic valve area of 0.5 cm². Two overlapping bioprosthetic frames sit in his aortic root. Three coronary arteries are stented. His EuroSCORE II is 27.36%. Open surgery would likely require total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement because those valve frames are deeply integrated into the aortic wall. What do you do?

This case, published by Merzulov and colleagues from the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center, is a 10-year case study in why the valve you choose on day one shapes every option you have on day three thousand six hundred and fifty. It's also a preview of what's coming for all of us.

## The Case: A Decade of Valve Decisions

Let's walk through the timeline, because it's instructive not just for what happened but for what each decision *foreclosed*.

In 2014, this patient received a 27 mm Lotus valve for severe aortic stenosis. He was high surgical risk with three-vessel coronary disease (multiple PCI interventions between 2009–2014), third-degree AV block requiring a pacemaker, COPD, diabetes, and recurrent GI bleeding. The Lotus was a reasonable choice at the time — it was a mechanically retractable, self-expanding valve that allowed precise positioning and repositioning. But it's worth noting that the Lotus platform has since been withdrawn from the market, and its unique frame geometry creates specific challenges when you need to stack another valve inside it.

By December 2021, the Lotus was failing. Mean gradient had climbed to 83 mmHg by early 2022, and EF had dropped from 54% to 42%. The team performed a TAV-in-TAV with a 23 mm Evolut valve. The procedure worked — EF recovered to 54% — but was complicated by a femoral pseudoaneurysm requiring vascular surgery.

Three months later, in September 2022, the patient presented with fever and heart failure. Blood cultures grew *Enterococcus faecalis*. TTE showed severe restenosis with a mean gradient of 35 mmHg. Prosthetic valve endocarditis was diagnosed. He received 6 weeks of IV ampicillin-based therapy followed by oral antibiotics. Repeated blood cultures, TEE, and CT confirmed resolution.

But the damage was done. By January 2024, the valve was failing again — mean gradient 49 mmHg, moderate-to-severe regurgitation. The decision was made to proceed with a third transcatheter valve.

## The Third Valve: CT-Guided Strategy in a Triple-Stented Root

This is where the case becomes a masterclass in preprocedural planning. The team used MSCT to measure coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, valve-to-coronary distance, and the interaction between the two existing valve frames. The minimum lumen diameter of the Evolut prosthesis was 13.7 mm — confirming severe compromise and defining the landing zone for the third valve.

The key strategic decisions were:

- **High implantation of the third valve** at the level of the Evolut leaflets, which pinned the pre-existing leaflets against the stent frame and prevented further inward leaflet folding that would narrow the effective orifice
- **Minimizing neo-skirt formation** below the coronary ostia to preserve coronary flow and future access
- **Commissural alignment** of the Acurate neo2 with the Evolut leaflets, guided by CT modelling and fluoroscopic landmarks
- **No coronary protection wires** were used — the CT planning demonstrated adequate coronary clearance, and this was confirmed with simultaneous aortography during balloon pre-dilation

An Acurate neo2 (size S) was chosen for its supra-annular leaflet positioning, relatively low frame height, and favorable hemodynamic performance in narrow anatomies. Balloon pre-dilation was performed with a 22/40 mm catheter under rapid ventricular pacing at 180 bpm. Post-dilation used a 20/40 mm balloon. Final aortography confirmed correct positioning, preserved coronary perfusion, and no regurgitation.

## The Immediate Post-Procedural Puzzle

Here's a detail that should give anyone pause. Immediately after the third valve was deployed, the mean gradient was **67 mmHg**. The AVA was 0.9 cm². The DVI was 0.24. This was severe patient-prosthesis mismatch by every definition.

But at 1 month, the mean gradient had dropped to 27 mmHg and the AVA had increased to 1.1 cm². At 1 year, the gradient was 26 mmHg, AVA 1.1 cm², EF 65%, and the patient was asymptomatic and active.

What happened? The authors attribute the improvement to a combination of optimized commissural alignment, effective post-dilation, and partial mechanical expansion of the prior Evolut frame. The high-pressure balloon post-dilation likely contributed to further radial expansion of the Evolut, improving leaflet motion and central flow dynamics. The valve continued to remodel over the first month.

## Why the First Valve Choice Matters More Than You Think

This is the broader point the case forces us to confront. When this patient received his Lotus valve in 2014, lifetime management was not the framing. TAVI was for patients who couldn't have surgery. The question was "can we do this safely?" not "what happens when this valve fails in 8 years?"

But that's no longer the world we live in. TAVI is now approved for low-risk patients. People in their 50s and 60s are receiving transcatheter valves. Many of them will need at least one reintervention. Some will need two or three.

The initial valve choice shapes everything downstream. The Lotus valve, with its unique frame geometry, is no longer commercially available — but the principle holds for every valve we implant today. Frame height, commissural alignment, leaflet position, and stent cell design all determine what's possible when you need to stack another valve inside. A valve that sits high with large cells may preserve coronary access. A valve with a tall frame and dense mesh may not.

## Commissural Alignment: Not Just a Nice-to-Have

In this case, the team attempted commissural alignment of the Acurate neo2 with the Evolut leaflets based on preprocedural CT. This wasn't cosmetic. In a triple-stented root, the cumulative mesh burden from three overlapping frames can make coronary cannulation nearly impossible. If the commissures of successive valves are misaligned, you create a lattice that blocks guide catheter access to the coronary ostia.

The reality is that commissural alignment is often treated as an afterthought during first-time TAVI. It shouldn't be. If there's even a chance this patient will need a second valve, the alignment of the first one matters — because the alignment of the second depends on it, and the third depends on both.

## The Endocarditis Question

The 2025 ESC guidelines recommend surgical valve replacement for prosthetic valve endocarditis, particularly with heart failure or uncontrolled infection. But this patient had completed antimicrobial therapy, had no evidence of active infection on TEE or CT, and had a EuroSCORE II of 27.36% with two deeply integrated valve frames. Surgery would have required root replacement. The team made a reasonable decision to proceed with transcatheter intervention after confirming infection resolution.

The mechanism of valve failure was likely multifactorial — leaflet injury from endocarditis plus progressive structural degeneration. Once the infection was treated and resolved, the management strategy aligned with that of any patient with prosthetic valve dysfunction: assess feasibility, hemodynamic severity, and surgical risk.

## The Sobering Reality of Bail-Out Options

Here's what should keep you up at night. If this Acurate neo2 fails prematurely, the options are essentially exhausted. Surgical explantation in a triple-stented root would be technically nightmarish. Bioprosthetic valve fracture — using high-pressure balloon dilation to crack the stent frame of a prior valve — has been described for surgical bioprostheses and some transcatheter valves, but in a triple-stented configuration with a Lotus frame at the base, this is untested territory. BASILICA (bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction) has been used to prevent coronary obstruction during TAV-in-TAV, but its feasibility in three overlapping frames is unknown.

The authors acknowledge this directly. Emerging catheter-based rescue technologies exist, but their safety and efficacy in triple-stented configurations remain uncertain. We are in uncharted waters.

## Where This Leaves Us

This patient is alive and asymptomatic at 1 year. The procedure was technically successful. The CT-guided strategy was meticulous. The team should be commended. But this case is also a warning about the trajectory we're on.

With expanding TAVI indications and increasing patient longevity, complex multi-prosthesis interventions will become more frequent. Each reintervention narrows the options for the next one. The stent frames accumulate. The coronary access gets harder. The effective orifice area gets smaller. The bail-out options get thinner.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Plan for the third valve at the time of the first.** Every TAVI decision should include a lifetime management assessment — frame geometry, commissural alignment, coronary access preservation, and the possibility of future reintervention. This is especially critical in younger and lower-risk patients.
- **Commissural alignment matters from valve one.** Misaligned commissures in successive valves create a mesh barrier to coronary access. Alignment at the first procedure sets the trajectory for all future interventions.
- **CT-guided planning is non-negotiable for TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV.** In this case, MSCT defined the landing zone (minimum lumen diameter 13.7 mm in the Evolut), confirmed coronary clearance, guided high implantation strategy, and enabled a procedure without coronary protection wires.
- **High implantation can preserve effective orifice area.** Deploying the third valve at the level of the Evolut leaflets pinned pre-existing leaflets against the stent frame, prevented inward folding, minimized neo-skirt formation, and preserved coronary flow.
- **Post-procedural gradient improvement can be delayed.** This patient had a mean gradient of 67 mmHg immediately post-deployment, which improved to 26 mmHg at 1 year — likely from frame remodeling and post-dilation effects. Don't panic at the initial gradient if the valve is well-positioned.
- **Endocarditis history does not preclude TAV-in-TAV if infection is resolved.** After completing antimicrobial therapy with documented resolution (negative cultures, TEE and CT confirmation), the procedural decision can be guided by anatomical feasibility and surgical risk — though this remains an individualized, multidisciplinary decision.
- **Bail-out options in triple-stented configurations are largely theoretical.** BASILICA, bioprosthetic valve fracture, and surgical explantation have uncertain feasibility when three valve frames overlap. This reality should factor into earlier decision-making about when to stop stacking and whether surgical conversion at an earlier stage might preserve more options.
- **We need better data on long-term TAVI trajectories.** Evidence regarding serial transcatheter reinterventions remains limited. As more patients live long enough to need second and third valves, systematic data collection on outcomes, complications, and procedural strategies is essential.
