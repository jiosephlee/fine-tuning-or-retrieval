Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Management] This patient developed prosthetic valve endocarditis caused by Enterococcus faecalis 3 months after his TAV-in-TAV procedure. What is the recommended antimicrobial treatment regimen for enterococcal prosthetic valve endocarditis, and what key differences exist compared to native valve endocarditis in terms of duration and agent selection?

### Answer

Enterococcal prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) demands a more aggressive and prolonged antimicrobial approach than native valve endocarditis (NVE), reflecting both the difficulty of eradicating infection from prosthetic material and the higher risk of relapse.

For **enterococcal PVE** caused by *E. faecalis*, the recommended regimen consists of **6 weeks of intravenous ampicillin (or penicillin G) combined with an aminoglycoside**—typically gentamicin—for synergistic bactericidal activity against the enterococcus. An alternative combination, **ampicillin plus ceftriaxone**, has emerged as an effective regimen that may be preferred when aminoglycoside toxicity is a concern, particularly in patients with renal dysfunction, and it has demonstrated comparable efficacy. In cases of penicillin allergy, **vancomycin** substitutes for ampicillin. When high-level aminoglycoside resistance is present, the ampicillin-ceftriaxone dual-β-lactam regimen becomes particularly valuable.

Several key differences distinguish PVE from NVE management:

**Duration:** PVE requires a **minimum of 6 weeks** of intravenous therapy, as was appropriately delivered in this patient. NVE may sometimes be treated with **4-week courses**, particularly with the ampicillin-ceftriaxone regimen in uncomplicated cases. The extended duration in PVE reflects the persistence of organisms within biofilm on prosthetic surfaces.

**Aminoglycoside synergy:** For PVE, **full-duration aminoglycoside synergistic therapy** is traditionally recommended throughout the 6-week course. For NVE, shorter aminoglycoside exposure (e.g., 2–4 weeks) or the ceftriaxone-based regimen without aminoglycosides may be sufficient, reducing nephrotoxicity risk.

**Post-antibiotic suppressive therapy:** PVE may require consideration of oral suppressive therapy following the IV course, particularly in patients with extensive prosthetic involvement. In this patient's case, following 6 weeks of IV ampicillin-based therapy, an oral course was administered per infectious disease recommendations, and suppressive therapy was ultimately not required—reflecting successful eradication as confirmed by negative blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and CT imaging.

The case well illustrates the principle that **timing of PVE also matters**: this patient developed endocarditis approximately 3 months post-TAV-in-TAV, falling within the **early PVE window** (≤1 year), which is more commonly associated with healthcare-associated or procedural infections. The prompt initiation of targeted, guideline-concordant therapy and confirmation of microbiological cure prior to any further intervention was essential to the feasibility of the subsequent TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedure, as active infection would have contraindicated transcatheter reintervention.
