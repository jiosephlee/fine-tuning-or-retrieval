Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Clinical Pearls] The procedural team deliberately employed a high implantation strategy for the third transcatheter valve, positioning it at the level of the Evolut valve leaflets. What is the rationale for this high implantation approach in terms of effective orifice area preservation, coronary flow maintenance, and prevention of leaflet folding, and what are the potential trade-offs of this strategy?

**Rationale for High Implantation of the Third Transcatheter Valve**

In a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV configuration, the cumulative stent frame burden and the spatial relationships between multiple overlapping valves create a uniquely constrained anatomy. The procedural team's decision to implant the Acurate neo2 at the level of the Evolut valve leaflets was a deliberate, CT-guided strategy addressing three interrelated mechanical problems.

**Effective Orifice Area Preservation:** By the time of the third intervention, the minimum lumen diameter within the Evolut prosthesis had narrowed to 13.7 mm, with a calculated AVA of 0.5 cm². Implanting the third valve at the leaflet level of the Evolut rather than deeper within the prior frame was intended to maximize the residual effective orifice area. A lower, more deeply nested implant would have positioned the new valve's waist within the narrowest portion of the existing frame, further restricting flow. The supra-annular leaflet design of the Acurate neo2, combined with high positioning, was selected to optimize hemodynamics in this severely compromised anatomy.

**Prevention of Leaflet Folding:** When a new valve is deployed within an existing prosthesis, the leaflets of the prior valve can be pushed inward by the expanding frame, creating a circumferential obstruction that reduces the effective orifice area. By aligning the third valve precisely at the level of the Evolut leaflets—guided by preprocedural CT modeling—the team pinned the pre-existing leaflets against the Evolut stent frame, preventing their inward folding and thereby avoiding additional flow limitation.

**Coronary Flow Maintenance:** High implantation minimized the formation of a neo-skirt below the coronary ostia. A neo-skirt forms when the leaflets of prior valves are displaced and create a tissue barrier between the new valve frame and the aortic wall, potentially obstructing coronary ostia. By positioning the third valve higher, the team kept this potential barrier at or above the coronary ostial level, preserving coronary perfusion and maintaining the feasibility of future coronary access. Preprocedural CT assessment of coronary ostial height, valve-to-coronary distance, and the anticipated neo-skirt location confirmed that coronary protection was unnecessary during the procedure.

**Potential Trade-offs:** Despite meticulous planning, this strategy did not fully eliminate prosthesis-patient mismatch. Immediately after implantation, the mean transvalvular gradient was 67 mm Hg with an indexed AVA of 0.4 cm²/m², consistent with severe PPM. Although these parameters improved substantially over the following month—likely due to post-dilatation effects, partial mechanical expansion of the Evolut frame, and ongoing remodeling—the persistent residual stenosis illustrates the inherent limitation of implanting a third valve within two already constrained frames. Additionally, high implantation carries inherent risks of valve instability or migration, and the cumulative mesh burden from three overlapping stent frames may still compromise future coronary access despite commissural alignment. Finally, should this prosthesis fail, further options are extremely limited, as surgical explantation in triple-stented anatomy would likely require aortic root and ascending aorta replacement.
