Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Clinical Pearls] In a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedure, the risk of coronary artery obstruction is a critical concern due to multiple overlapping stent frames and leaflet material. What specific CT-derived measurements are used to assess coronary obstruction risk, and what is the significance of the 'neo-skirt' concept in procedural planning?

In TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedures, coronary artery obstruction risk escalates with each successive layer of stent frames and displaced leaflet material. As illustrated in this case, the preprocedural CT assessment centered on several key measurements: **coronary ostial height**, **sinus of Valsalva dimensions**, **valve-to-coronary distance** (the distance from the aortic annular plane or the prosthetic valve frame to each coronary ostium), and the **interaction between the frames of previously implanted transcatheter valves**. The CT also identified the **minimum lumen diameter** within the existing Evolut prosthesis (13.7 mm in this case), confirming severe hemodynamic compromise and defining the anatomical constraints for a third valve. The relationship between the anticipated neo-skirt and the coronary ostia was specifically evaluated, as well as the feasibility of high implantation to preserve coronary flow and maintain future coronary access.

The **neo-skirt** concept is central to procedural planning in valve-in-valve interventions. When a new transcatheter valve is deployed within an existing prosthesis, the leaflets of the prior valve are displaced outward and pressed against the stent frame, creating a circumferential ring of tissue—functionally a "skirt"—that can extend above the annular plane. If this neo-skirt reaches the level of the coronary ostia, it can occlude coronary inflow, particularly when coronary ostial height is low or the sinus of Valsalva is shallow. In a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV configuration, the cumulative leaflet and frame material from multiple overlapping valves compounds this risk substantially.

In this case, the procedural strategy deliberately addressed the neo-skirt risk. **High implantation** of the Acurate neo2—positioned at the level of the Evolut valve leaflets—served two purposes: it fixed the pre-existing leaflets against the stent frame, preventing inward leaflet folding that would further reduce the effective orifice area, and it minimized neo-skirt formation *below* the coronary ostia, thereby preserving coronary perfusion and future coronary access. This approach, guided entirely by preprocedural CT modeling, allowed the procedure to be performed without active coronary protection (e.g., prophylactic guidewire placement or chimney stents). The case demonstrates that when CT-derived measurements confirm adequate valve-to-coronary distance and a favorable neo-skirt-to-ostium relationship, a strategy of high implantation can mitigate obstruction risk even in triple-stented anatomy.
