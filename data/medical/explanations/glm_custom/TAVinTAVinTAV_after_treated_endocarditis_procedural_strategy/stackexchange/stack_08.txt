Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Differential Diagnosis] If the third transcatheter prosthesis in this patient were to fail prematurely, treatment options would be extremely limited. What is the BASILICA (bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction) technique, and what are the theoretical advantages and limitations of this approach in a triple-stented aortic valve configuration?

**BASILICA and Its Role in Triple-Stented Aortic Valve Configurations**

The BASILICA technique — Bioprosthetic or native Aortic Scallop Intentional Laceration to prevent Iatrogenic Coronary Artery obstruction — is a catheter-based intervention designed to mitigate the risk of coronary artery obstruction during transcatheter aortic valve procedures. Using an electrified guidewire, the operator intentionally lacerates the aortic valve leaflet (scallop) that overlies the coronary ostium at risk. When a subsequent transcatheter valve is deployed, the pre-lacerated leaflet splays outward against the sinus wall rather than being displaced inward to occlude the coronary ostium. The technique was developed to address one of the most feared complications of TAV-in-TAV procedures, where the retained leaflets of a prior bioprosthesis are pushed toward the sinuses during deployment of a new valve, potentially obstructing coronary flow — particularly when coronary ostial height is low or the sinuses are shallow.

In the context of this patient's triple-stented aortic valve configuration, the theoretical advantage of BASILICA would relate to preserving the possibility of a future fourth transcatheter intervention. As the case report emphasizes, should the Acurate neo2 prosthesis fail prematurely, "treatment options would be extremely limited," and surgical explantation would be "technically demanding and high-risk," likely requiring total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement due to deep stent frame integration into the aortic wall. A catheter-based strategy that could prevent coronary obstruction during a hypothetical fourth valve implantation would therefore be conceptually attractive, particularly given this patient's prohibitive surgical risk (EuroSCORE II 27.36%) and extensive comorbidities, including three-vessel coronary disease with prior PCIs, COPD, diabetes, and recurrent GI bleeding.

However, the limitations of BASILICA in this specific anatomy are substantial and must be clearly understood. First, BASILICA was designed and validated for native or single bioprosthetic valve configurations, not for the scenario of three overlapping transcatheter valve frames. The procedure requires precise catheter navigation and electrosurgical laceration of a target leaflet — a task that becomes extraordinarily challenging when three layers of stent mesh, overlapping commissural posts, and densely packed metal struts create a physical labyrinth that must be traversed. The ability to steer an electrified wire through this cumulative mesh burden to reach and cleave the appropriate leaflet tissue is technically daunting, and the margin for error is minimal.

Second, the coronary obstruction risk in multi-layered TAV-in-TAV configurations is not solely a function of leaflet displacement. As the case report notes, the team was particularly concerned about the "neo-skirt" — the cylindrical structure formed by the retained leaflets and stent frames of prior valves that can extend below the coronary ostia. In a triple-stented configuration, this neo-skirt is a composite of multiple layers of prosthetic material, and BASILICA laceration of a single leaflet does not address the broader issue of frame-induced narrowing of the sinus and reduced coronary clearance from cumulative stent bulk.

Third, this patient already exhibits severe prosthesis-patient mismatch (indexed AVA 0.4 cm²/m², DVI 0.24 immediately post-procedure), and while gradients improved to a mean of 26 mm Hg at one year, the effective orifice area remains constrained at 1.1 cm². BASILICA does not address the problem of effective orifice area reduction — it is purely a coronary protection strategy. A fourth valve would face an even narrower landing zone, and even with successful coronary protection, the haodynamic result would likely be unacceptable.

Finally, as the authors themselves conclude, while emerging catheter-based solutions such as BASILICA "might be considered," their "feasibility and safety in triple-stented configurations remain uncertain." There is currently no clinical evidence supporting the use of BASILICA in patients with three overlapping transcatheter valve frames, and the technical complexity of such an undertaking cannot be overstated.

The key teaching point is this: BASILICA represents an important tool for coronary protection in selected valve-in-valve scenarios, but its applicability diminishes dramatically as the number of overlapping stent frames increases. In this patient, the most important strategy was the one the team actually employed — meticulous CT-guided preprocedural planning, high implantation of the third valve at the level of the Evolut leaflets to minimize neo-skirt formation, commissural alignment to preserve coronary access, and careful valve selection. These proactive measures at the time of each intervention, rather than reactive salvage strategies, represent the most meaningful approach to extending the longevity of transcatheter valve therapy in these increasingly complex patients.
