Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

## Triple Threat: Navigating the Anatomical Minefield of TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV

Picture this: a man in his late 70s walks into your cath lab with three layers of transcatheter valve frames stacked inside his aortic root, a history of prosthetic valve endocarditis, triple-vessel PCI, a pacemaker, COPD, diabetes, and recurrent GI bleeding. His EuroSCORE II is 27.36%. Surgery? That would mean root and ascending aorta replacement given how deeply those prior valve frames are embedded. Oh, and he's got severe symptoms. What do you do?

This is the world we're heading toward — and frankly, already in. As TAVI indications expand and treated patients live longer, structural valve deterioration and repeat interventions are no longer rare edge cases. They're becoming part of the structural heart landscape. And cases like this one, published by Merkulov and colleagues, give us a window into what the future of redo-redo transcatheter work looks like — the good, the bad, and the anatomically terrifying.

## The Case: A Decade of Valve Disease

Let me walk you through this patient's journey, because it's a timeline that structural heart teams will increasingly recognize.

In 2014, he received a 27 mm Lotus valve for severe aortic stenosis. The Lotus, if you recall, was a mechanically-expanded valve — Boston Scientific's answer to the self-expanding and balloon-expandable platforms. By late 2021, that valve was failing. Mean gradient had climbed to 83 mm Hg by early 2022, and his EF had dropped from 54% to 42%. A TAV-in-TAV was performed with a 23 mm Evolut (Medtronic), and his EF recovered to 54%. So far, a standard redo story.

But three months later, in September 2022, he presented with persistent fever and worsening heart failure. Blood cultures grew *Enterococcus faecalis*. TTE showed severe restenosis with a mean gradient of 35 mm Hg. Prosthetic valve endocarditis. He received six weeks of IV ampicillin-based therapy followed by oral antibiotics, and infection was declared resolved based on clinical assessment, repeat cultures, transesophageal echocardiography, and CT.

Then, in January 2024 — about a year and a half after completing antimicrobial therapy — his valve function worsened again. Mean gradient was 49 mm Hg, AVA had shrunk to 0.5 cm², and he had moderate-to-severe regurgitation. The mechanism was likely multifactorial: leaflet injury from endocarditis with subsequent mechanical restriction, plus progressive structural degeneration of two bioprosthetic valves. By the time of admission in June 2024, he was symptomatic walking 200–300 meters, with dizziness, fatigue, and exertional chest tightness.

The decision was made to proceed with a third transcatheter valve — a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV.

## Why This Case Is Not Just "Another Valve-in-Valve"

Here's what makes this case instructive beyond the novelty of a triple stack. Every layer of valve you add multiplies the technical complexity in ways that aren't simply additive — they're exponential. The challenges cluster around four domains: coronary access, effective orifice area, frame interaction, and infection-related structural change.

Let's take these one at a time.

**Coronary obstruction risk.** With two prior valve frames already in situ, a third prosthesis creates a "neo-skirt" — the folded leaflets of the existing valves get pushed outward against the stent frames and can form a barrier below the coronary ostia. The more valves you stack, the higher this neo-skirt sits relative to the coronaries. In this case, the team used preprocedural CT to measure coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, valve-to-coronary distance, and the interaction between the two existing frames. Based on this analysis, they determined that coronary protection was not necessary — a decision that could easily have gone the other way. The high implantation strategy (deploying the third valve at the level of the Evolut leaflets) was specifically chosen to minimize neo-skirt formation below the coronary ostia and preserve future coronary access.

**Effective orifice area.** Each valve-in-valve procedure narrows the functional orifice. The Lotus had an internal lumen, the Evolut went inside it, and now a third valve was going inside the Evolut. MSCT showed the Evolut's minimum lumen diameter was only 13.7 mm. That's a tight space. Valve selection had to account for this — and this is where the choice of prosthesis becomes critical, which we'll get to in a moment.

**Frame interaction and commissural alignment.** Three overlapping stent frames create a cumulative mesh burden that can make future coronary cannulation a nightmare. The team attempted commissural alignment during deployment of the third valve, guided by CT modeling and fluoroscopic landmarks. But as they honestly acknowledge, even with alignment, the cumulative mesh may still present challenges for future coronary interventions — particularly in a patient with known three-vessel disease and multiple prior PCIs.

**The endocarditis factor.** This is perhaps the most novel aspect. The 2025 ESC guidelines still recommend surgical valve replacement for PVE, particularly with heart failure or uncontrolled infection. But this patient had completed antimicrobial therapy, had no evidence of active infection on TEE or CT, and had prohibitive surgical risk. The team's position was that once infection is resolved, management of prosthetic dysfunction should align with non-endocarditis cases. That's a reasonable and increasingly defensible stance, but the evidence base is thin.

## The Procedural Strategy: CT-Guided Everything

This is where the case becomes a teaching artifact. The team didn't just show up and deploy a valve — they engineered the procedure.

Preprocedural MSCT was the linchpin. It confirmed the severity of stenosis (13.7 mm minimum lumen in the Evolut), assessed coronary obstruction risk, evaluated frame-to-frame interaction, and guided valve selection, implantation depth, and the decision to skip coronary protection. Three-dimensional modeling was used to plan commissural alignment.

The procedure was done under local anesthesia with left radial and left femoral access. After aortography confirmed severe regurgitation, a Safari wire was placed in the LV. Balloon pre-dilation was performed with a 22/40 mm catheter under rapid pacing at 180 bpm, with simultaneous aortography confirming unobstructed coronary flow. This step — checking coronary perfusion during pre-dilation — is a smart move in triple-stack anatomy where you're gambling on coronary obstruction with every maneuver.

The valve chosen was an Acurate neo2, size S. The rationale is worth understanding in detail because it speaks to the broader question of how to select valves for redo-redo procedures.

## Why the Acurate neo2?

The team selected this valve for three specific reasons:

First, supra-annular leaflet positioning. In a triple-stack configuration, the functional orifice is determined not by the annulus but by the narrowest point in the existing frame stack. Supra-annular leaflets sit higher in the flow stream, which can optimize hemodynamics in a constrained anatomy.

Second, relatively low frame height. A lower frame means less metal above the coronary ostia, reducing the mesh burden that could impede future coronary access. In a patient with triple-vessel disease and multiple stents, preserving coronary access isn't a luxury — it's a necessity.

Third, the design supports commissural alignment. The Acurate neo2 has stabilization elements and a top crown that allow controlled deployment, and the team used CT-derived landmarks to align the commissures with the Evolut valve leaflets. This alignment aimed to maximize the effective orifice area and reduce patient-prosthesis mismatch — which, given the 13.7 mm landing zone, was a real risk.

The valve was implanted with high positioning — deliberately at the level of the Evolut leaflets. This served a dual purpose: it pinned the existing leaflets against the stent frame (preventing them from folding inward and further narrowing the orifice) and it minimized neo-skirt formation below the coronaries.

Post-dilation was performed with a 20/40 mm balloon under rapid pacing. Final aortography showed correct positioning, preserved coronary perfusion, and no perivalvular or transvalvular regurgitation.

## The Frame Expansion Hypothesis

Here's a detail that deserves attention. Immediately after the procedure, the mean gradient was 67 mm Hg — actually worse than pre-procedure. The AVA was 0.9 cm² with a DVI of 0.24, consistent with severe patient-prosthesis mismatch. That's not what you want to see after a three-hour procedure in a high-risk patient.

But by one month, the mean gradient had dropped to 27 mm Hg and the AVA had increased to 1.1 cm². That's a substantial improvement that can't be explained by valve acclimatization alone. The authors propose that high-pressure balloon post-dilation contributed to partial mechanical expansion of the prior Evolut frame — essentially, the balloon didn't just expand the new Acurate neo2, it also pushed the Evolut frame outward. This improved leaflet mobility and central flow dynamics. Intentional frame fracture wasn't performed, but the hemodynamic improvement suggests that some degree of frame remodeling occurred.

This is an important observation. In valve-in-valve procedures, the existing frame is often treated as a fixed constraint. But high-pressure post-dilation may modify that constraint, particularly with self-expanding nitinol frames like the Evolut. Whether this is reproducible, safe, or generalizable remains unclear — but it's a hypothesis worth testing.

## The Outcome

At 1-year follow-up (June 2025), the patient remained asymptomatic and active. Mean gradient was 26 mm Hg, AVA 1.1 cm², EF 65%. No regurgitation, no recurrent endocarditis, no valve-related complications. That's a durable result in a patient who had exhausted most of his options.

But let's be clear about the ceiling here. If this third valve fails, the options are bleak. Surgical explantation in a triple-stented, triple-valved aortic root would be an anatomical nightmare. Emerging catheter-based techniques like bioprosthetic valve fracture or BASILICA might be considered, but their feasibility in a triple-stented configuration is entirely speculative. This patient is, in a sense, at the end of the transcatheter runway.

## What This Case Teaches Us

This case is a preview of what's coming. Patients who received TAVI a decade ago are now presenting with valve failure. Some have already had a TAV-in-TAV. As survival improves and indications extend to lower-risk and younger populations, the lifetime management of these patients becomes the central challenge of structural heart disease.

The key lessons here aren't just about technical execution — they're about planning philosophy. Every TAVI should be performed with an awareness of what comes next. Frame design, implantation depth, commissural alignment, and coronary access preservation all matter not just for the index procedure but for every future intervention that may follow. The concept of "lifetime management" isn't aspirational language anymore — it's operational reality.

### Key Take-Home Messages

- **TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV is technically feasible** in selected patients with prohibitive surgical risk, but evidence remains sparse and each case requires individualized, CT-guided planning.
- **Preprocedural CT is non-negotiable** — it drives valve selection, implantation depth, coronary obstruction risk assessment, and commissural alignment strategy.
- **High implantation of the third valve** (at the level of the existing valve leaflets) can pin prior leaflets against the frame, preserve effective orifice area, and minimize neo-skirt formation below the coronaries.
- **Valve selection in narrow stack anatomies should prioritize** supra-annular leaflet positioning, low frame height, and commissural alignment capability — the Acurate neo2 was chosen for exactly these reasons.
- **High-pressure balloon post-dilation may partially expand prior self-expanding frames**, improving hemodynamics beyond what the new valve alone achieves — but this observation needs further investigation.
- **Prior prosthetic valve endocarditis is not an absolute contraindication to transcatheter reintervention** once infection is fully treated and confirmed resolved by imaging and cultures — but multidisciplinary review and shared decision-making are essential.
- **Plan for the runway ending.** In triple-stack configurations, future options are severely limited. Lifetime management thinking must start at the first TAVI, not the third.



# TAVI After Treated Prosthetic Endocarditis: Where Do Guidelines End and Clinical Judgment Begin?

## The Case That Makes You Rethink "Contraindications"

Picture this: a man in his late 70s walks into your cath lab for his *third* transcatheter aortic valve. He's got two stacked valve frames in his aortic root, a history of prosthetic valve endocarditis, triple-vessel coronary disease with four prior PCIs, a pacemaker, COPD, diabetes, and recurrent GI bleeding. His EuroSCORE II is 27.36%. The 2025 ESC guidelines say surgery is the recommended strategy for prosthetic valve endocarditis. Surgery is not an option. What do you do?

This is exactly the scenario faced by the team at the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center in Moscow, and their published 1-year outcome forces us to confront a question we will face with increasing frequency: what happens when guideline-recommended therapy is genuinely impossible, and the alternative is not just "less ideal" but represents the only viable path forward?

## Guideline Tension: Surgery First, But At What Cost?

The 2025 ESC guidelines for the management of valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis are clear: surgical valve replacement remains the recommended strategy for prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), particularly when complicated by heart failure or uncontrolled infection. This recommendation is well-supported by evidence demonstrating that surgical intervention, when feasible, offers durable infection eradication and superior long-term outcomes compared to medical therapy alone.

But here is where the guideline language becomes critical: the guidelines also emphasize that management should be *individualised*, taking into account infection control, surgical risk, and anatomical feasibility. This is not a footnote — it is a deliberate acknowledgment that real patients do not always fit algorithmic pathways.

In this case, the patient's surgical risk was not merely elevated. It was prohibitive. Beyond the EuroSCORE II of 27.36%, the two previously implanted transcatheter valves — a 27 mm Lotus (2014) and a 23 mm Evolut (2022) — had become deeply integrated into the aortic wall over a combined decade. Surgical explantation would likely have required total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement, in a patient with COPD, diabetes, recurrent GI bleeding, and triple-stented coronary anatomy. The heart team concluded, reasonably, that surgery would carry mortality risk far exceeding the already staggering calculated score.

This is the reality of the modern TAVI population: we are seeing patients who were deemed high-risk for surgery a decade ago, have outlived their first prosthesis, and now present for reintervention with accumulated comorbidities that make the surgical option even less viable than it was originally. The guidelines were not written for these patients, and the authors know it.

## The Infection Question: When Does PVE Stop Being PVE?

One of the most clinically important decisions in this case was determining when the patient transitioned from "post-endocarditis" to "no active endocarditis" — because that distinction changed everything about how the team approached reintervention.

The timeline is instructive. In September 2022, three months after the TAV-in-TAV with an Evolut prosthesis, the patient presented with persistent fever and worsening heart failure. TTE showed severe restenosis with a mean gradient of 35 mm Hg. Blood cultures grew *Enterococcus faecalis*, and prosthetic valve endocarditis was diagnosed. He received 6 weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based therapy followed by oral antibiotics per infectious disease recommendations.

By the time the third TAVI was performed in June 2024 — nearly two years after completing antimicrobial therapy — the patient had no clinical signs of infection, repeated blood cultures were negative, transoesophageal echocardiography confirmed absence of vegetations or paravalvular complications, and MSCT showed no evidence of active infection. No suppressive antibiotic therapy was ongoing.

The team's reasoning here is worth emphasizing: once infection was considered resolved and no long-term suppressive therapy was required, the management strategy for prosthetic valve dysfunction aligned with that of patients *without* active or ongoing endocarditis. Procedural decision-making was then guided by anatomical feasibility, haemodynamic severity, and surgical risk — not by the prior history of infection alone.

This is a critical conceptual framework. The question is not "can we intervene on a patient with a history of PVE?" but rather "has the infection been adequately treated, and does the risk-benefit calculus now resemble that of a non-infected prosthesis?" In this case, the answer was yes, supported by multimodality imaging and microbiological confirmation.

## The Technical Challenge: Three Valves, One Root, Zero Margin for Error

If the infection question was the gatekeeper, the technical question was the execution. And this is where the case becomes a masterclass in CT-guided procedural planning for complex TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV.

The patient presented in January 2024 with severe prosthetic dysfunction: mean gradient of 49 mm Hg, AVA of 0.5 cm², and grade 2–3 intraprosthetic regurgitation. MSCT revealed a minimum lumen diameter of only 13.7 mm within the Evolut prosthesis — a critically narrowed space through which a third valve had to be deployed.

The preprocedural CT assessment addressed several specific concerns that anyone attempting this procedure must consider. Coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, valve-to-coronary distance, and the interaction between the two existing valve frames were all evaluated. Particular attention was paid to the anticipated "neo-skirt" — the tissue that forms around the prior valve frames and can progressively narrow the space below the coronary ostia — and its relationship to coronary flow.

Based on this planning, the team determined that coronary protection was not necessary. This is not a decision to be made casually in a triple-valve configuration, and it speaks to the quality of the preprocedural imaging. In a patient with triple-stented coronary anatomy and four prior PCIs, losing coronary access would be catastrophic.

## Valve Selection and Implantation Strategy: Every Choice Has Downstream Consequences

The team selected an Acurate neo2 (size S) for the third valve. The rationale is worth understanding because it reflects a lifetime management mindset rather than a procedure-by-procedure approach.

The Acurate neo2 was chosen for its supra-annular leaflet positioning, relatively low frame height, and favorable haemodynamic performance in narrow anatomies. Its design also supports commissural alignment, which was a deliberate goal to preserve future coronary access — even though, as the authors acknowledge, the cumulative mesh burden of three overlapping frames may still present challenges for future coronary interventions regardless of alignment.

The implantation strategy was equally deliberate. The third valve was implanted *high* — at the level of the Evolut valve leaflets. This served two purposes: it fixed the pre-existing leaflets against the stent frame, preventing further reduction of the effective orifice area from inward leaflet folding, and it minimized neo-skirt formation below the coronary ostia, preserving both coronary flow and future coronary access.

Post-dilation was performed with a 20/40 mm balloon under rapid pacing. The authors speculate that high-pressure post-dilation likely contributed to further radial expansion of the prior Evolut frame, improving leaflet motion and central flow dynamics. No intentional frame fracture was performed, but the haemodynamic improvement suggests that some degree of mechanical expansion of the prior frame occurred.

## The Haemodynamic Story: Patience Required

The immediate post-procedure echocardiogram is a moment that would make any operator pause. The mean gradient was 67 mm Hg. The AVA was 0.9 cm² (indexed 0.4 cm²/m²). The Doppler velocity index was 0.24. By any definition, this was severe patient-prosthesis mismatch.

But here is where the case teaches an important lesson about the timeline of haemodynamic optimization in complex valve-in-valve procedures. At 1 month, the mean gradient had dropped to 27 mm Hg and the AVA had increased to 1.1 cm². At 1 year, the mean gradient was 26 mm Hg, AVA 1.1 cm², EF 65%, and the patient was asymptomatic with good exercise tolerance.

The initial high gradient likely reflected incomplete expansion and frame interaction that partially resolved with left ventricular remodelling, ongoing frame expansion, and adaptation of the triple-valve complex. This is not dissimilar to what we see in other valve-in-valve contexts where early gradients overestimate the final haemodynamic result. The practical message: do not panic at the immediate post-procedure gradient in a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV, but plan for close surveillance to confirm improvement.

## The Ethical Dimension: What Happens When This Valve Fails?

Perhaps the most sobering aspect of this case is the acknowledgment of what happens next if the third prosthesis fails. The authors are candid: treatment options would be extremely limited. Surgical explantation in a triple-stented root would be technically demanding and carry enormous risk. Catheter-based solutions like bioprosthetic valve fracture or BASILICA have uncertain feasibility and safety in triple-stented configurations.

This raises a question that goes beyond the technical: is it ethical to offer a third TAVI knowing that future options are effectively exhausted? The counterargument is equally clear: the alternative was progressive heart failure and death in a symptomatic patient with severe stenosis and regurgitation. The patient was involved in shared decision-making with his family, and the case was reviewed in a multidisciplinary heart team meeting including interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, imaging specialists, and anaesthesiologists.

The 1-year outcome — an asymptomatic, active patient with preserved valve function — suggests the decision was correct *for now*. But this case is a stark reminder that every transcatheter intervention has downstream consequences, and the concept of "lifetime management" of structural valve disease must begin at the time of the first intervention, not when options are running out.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Guidelines are a framework, not a straitjacket.** The 2025 ESC guidelines recommend surgery for PVE, but explicitly call for individualized decision-making. In prohibitive surgical risk patients with treated PVE and no evidence of active infection, transcatheter reintervention is a reasonable strategy when guided by multidisciplinary heart team assessment.

- **Resolving PVE changes the calculus.** Once active infection is excluded by negative blood cultures, TEE, and CT imaging — and suppressive antibiotics are not ongoing — management of prosthetic dysfunction can align with non-endocarditis pathways. The prior infection history alone should not preclude transcatheter reintervention.

- **CT planning is non-negotiable for TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV.** Assessment of coronary ostial height, valve-to-coronary distance, neo-skirt implications, and frame interaction is essential for valve selection, implantation depth, and coronary protection decisions. In this case, CT planning allowed the procedure to proceed without coronary protection in a patient with critical coronary anatomy.

- **High implantation preserves options.** Implanting the third valve at the level of the prior Evolut leaflets fixed existing leaflets against the stent frame, minimized neo-skirt formation, and preserved coronary flow and future access. This strategy should be considered in multi-valve configurations.

- **Early post-procedure gradients may overestimate final severity.** The immediate mean gradient of 67 mm Hg improved to 26 mm Hg by 1 year, likely due to frame expansion, LV remodelling, and adaptation. Close surveillance is essential, but early gradients in complex valve-in-valve should be interpreted with caution.

- **Plan for the end from the beginning.** With expanding TAVI indications and increasing patient longevity, complex multi-prosthesis interventions will become more frequent. Commissural alignment, coronary access preservation, and residual effective orifice area should be considered at every stage — starting with the first valve implantation.



# Preserving Coronary Access Across Three Valve Frames: The Role of CT-Guided Neo-Skirt Planning

## The Triple-Valve Challenge

Here's a case that should make every structural heart team pause and think hard about lifetime valve management. A man in his late 70s presents with severe degeneration of two overlapping transcatheter aortic valves — and a history of treated prosthetic valve endocarditis in between. The question isn't just whether you *can* put a third valve in. It's whether you can do it without occluding the coronaries, without creating a functionally stenotic nightmare, and without foreclosing every future option the patient has. The answer, in this case, hinged almost entirely on one thing: preprocedural CT planning.

This patient's journey is a harbinger of what's coming. He received a 27 mm Lotus valve in 2014, underwent TAV-in-TAV with a 23 mm Evolut in 2022 for structural valve deterioration (mean gradient up to 83 mm Hg, EF dropped to 42%), then developed *Enterococcus faecalis* prosthetic valve endocarditis treated with 6 weeks of IV ampicillin-based therapy. By early 2024, after documented infection resolution, his mean gradient was back up to 49 mm Hg with moderate-to-severe regurgitation and an AVA of 0.5 cm². Two valve frames, three prior coronary stents, COPD, diabetes, recurrent GI bleeding, and a EuroSCORE II of 27.36%. Surgery would have required aortic root and ascending aorta replacement given the depth of stent integration into the aortic wall. The heart team went transcatheter — but the planning was everything.

## The Neo-Skirt Problem: Why Each Valve Makes the Next One Harder

Here is the core anatomical issue that escalates with every successive valve-in-valve. When you implant a new transcatheter valve inside a prior one, the leaflets of the existing prosthesis get pinned outward against its stent frame. Those pinned leaflets form a circular ridge of tissue — what the structural heart community calls the **neo-skirt** — that sits below the coronary ostia. With one valve-in-valve, this is manageable. With two overlapping frames, the neo-skirt becomes taller, wider, and more hemodynamically relevant. With a third valve, you are stacking neoskirts and metal frames in a spatial relationship to the coronaries that becomes increasingly unforgiving.

In this case, the team faced a triple challenge: the Lotus frame from 2014, the Evolut frame from 2022 sitting inside it, and now a third valve going inside both. Each frame adds metal and each set of pinned leaflets adds tissue bulk that can obstruct coronary flow or make future coronary cannulation impossible. The minimum lumen diameter inside the Evolut was 13.7 mm — severely compromised — which tells you how much real estate was already lost.

The critical insight from the CT planning was this: by implanting the third valve *high* — at the level of the Evolut leaflets rather than deeper in the annulus — the team could pin the Evolut leaflets against the Evolut frame rather than letting them fold further inward. This served two purposes. First, it prevented further reduction of the effective orifice area from inward leaflet folding. Second, it minimized the neo-skirt height below the coronary ostia, preserving both immediate coronary flow and future coronary access.

## CT Assessment: What Was Measured and Why It Mattered

The preprocedural multislice CT evaluation was the linchpin of the entire strategy. The team assessed:

- **Coronary ostial height** — How far above the annular plane do the coronary ostia sit, and how does that relate to the anticipated neo-skirt position?
- **Sinus of Valsalva dimensions** — Is there enough space in the sinuses to accommodate three overlapping frames without lateral compression compromising coronary flow?
- **Valve-to-coronary distance** — The straight-line distance from the tips of the pinned leaflets (the neo-skirt) to the coronary ostia. This is the number that tells you whether you are safe, borderline, or in trouble.
- **Frame interaction analysis** — How do the Lotus and Evolut frames overlap, and where will the third frame sit relative to both?
- **Landing zone assessment** — Where inside the Evolut can the Acurate neo2 anchor securely while maintaining optimal leaflet position?

The valve-to-coronary distance measurements, combined with the planned high implantation strategy, indicated sufficient coronary clearance that the procedure was performed **without coronary protection** — no guidewire or chimney stent in either coronary. That is a significant decision in a triple-frame anatomy, and it was justified by CT, not by intuition.

One important limitation: the team did not have access to dedicated 3D expansion analysis software. The CT assessment relied on standard multiplanar reconstructions and measurements rather than simulated three-dimensional valve expansion within the specific anatomical configuration. This is a real-world constraint that many centers face, and it means the planning, while thorough, involved inferential reasoning about how the new valve would expand rather than computational confirmation.

## Valve Selection and Commissural Alignment: The Acurate neo2 Choice

The team selected the Acurate neo2 (size S) for specific design reasons relevant to this anatomy. Its supra-annular leaflet positioning provides favorable hemodynamic performance in narrow anatomies — critical when you're working inside a 13.7 mm lumen. Its relatively low frame height reduces the amount of additional metal that could interfere with coronary access. And its design supports commissural alignment, which was a deliberate goal.

Commissural alignment matters increasingly with each successive valve. When commissures of overlapping valves are aligned, the commissural posts line up and leave the spaces between them — the inter-commissural windows — relatively open. These windows are the corridors through which you can pass a catheter to cannulate the coronaries in the future. When commissures are misaligned, the posts of one valve sit in front of the windows of another, creating a mesh that is extremely difficult to cross with a guide catheter. In a patient with three-vessel disease and four prior PCI interventions, preserving coronary access is not a theoretical concern — it is a clinical necessity.

The team used preprocedural CT modeling and fluoroscopic landmarks to plan alignment during deployment, rather than relying on arbitrary positioning. This is the level of precision that triple-valve procedures demand.

## The Hemodynamic Payoff — and the PPM Reality

The immediate post-procedure echo showed no aortic regurgitation — a technical success by VARC-3 criteria. But the mean gradient was 67 mm Hg with an AVA of 0.9 cm² and a Doppler velocity index of 0.24, consistent with severe prosthesis-patient mismatch. Before you throw your hands up, consider what happened next.

At 1 month, the mean gradient had dropped to 27 mm Hg and the AVA had increased to 1.1 cm². Left ventricular remodeling was already evident. At 1 year, the gradient remained stable at 26 mm Hg, AVA 1.1 cm², EF 65%, and the patient was asymptomatic and active. The early high gradient likely reflected incomplete expansion of the third valve and residual compression from the Evolut frame. The improvement over the first month is attributable to a combination of commissural alignment optimization, effective balloon post-dilation (20/40 mm balloon under rapid pacing), and partial mechanical expansion of the Evolut frame from the high-pressure post-dilation. The Evolut frame likely underwent further radial expansion, improving leaflet coaptation and central flow dynamics.

The take-home here is that early post-procedural gradients in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV may look alarming but can improve substantially as frames settle and expand. Don't judge the result solely on the immediate echo — the 1-month study is more informative.

## Why This Case Matters for Your Practice

This is not an isolated curiosity. With TAVI indications expanding to lower-risk and younger patients, and with structural valve deterioration accumulating over time, TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedures will become more common. The era of thinking about TAVI as a one-and-done intervention is ending. Every first TAVI in a younger patient should trigger a conversation about the lifetime trajectory — what happens at valve 2, valve 3, and beyond.

The history of treated endocarditis in this case is also noteworthy. The 2025 ESC guidelines still recommend surgical valve replacement for PVE, but this patient's anatomy and comorbidities made surgery prohibitive. Once infection was documented as resolved — confirmed by negative blood cultures, TEE without vegetations, and CT without paravalvular complications — the decision-making framework shifted to standard structural valve dysfunction assessment. The prior infection informed the evaluation but did not preclude a transcatheter strategy.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **CT is non-negotiable for TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV.** Valve-to-coronary distance, neo-skirt height estimation, frame interaction analysis, and landing zone planning are the foundation of procedural safety. Without this, you are guessing.
- **High implantation within the prior frame is a deliberate strategy** to pin existing leaflets outward, preserve effective orifice area, and minimize neo-skirt height below the coronies. It is not the default — it is a calculated choice based on CT measurements.
- **Commissural alignment must be planned, not left to chance.** In triple-frame anatomy, misaligned commissures can permanently foreclose coronary access. Use CT modeling and fluoroscopic landmarks.
- **Coronary protection may be avoidable with adequate CT clearance**, but the threshold for deploying protection should be low. This team felt comfortable without it based on their measurements — your threshold may differ depending on anatomy and software availability.
- **Early post-procedural gradients can be misleading.** Severe PPM on the immediate post-procedure echo improved dramatically by 1 month due to frame expansion and settling. The 1-month study is your real baseline.
- **Current CT assessment has limits.** Without dedicated 3D expansion simulation software, planning relies on inference. Advocate for better tools as these procedures become more common.
- **Start planning for valve 3 at valve 1.** Lifetime management of structural valve disease begins with the first intervention. Frame choice, implantation depth, and commissural alignment at the first TAVI all have downstream consequences decades later.



# The 67 mmHg to 26 mmHg Journey: Understanding Early Gradients and PPM After Triple Valve Implantation

## The Setup: When "Success" Looks Like Failure on Paper

Picture this: you've just completed a grueling TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedure on a man in his late 70s with prior infective endocarditis, three-vessel CAD with multiple stents, a pacemaker, COPD, diabetes, and recurrent GI bleeding. The aortography looks beautiful—no regurgitation, coronaries flowing, valve well-positioned. You're feeling good. Then the echo comes back: mean gradient 67 mmHg, aortic valve area 0.9 cm², indexed AVA 0.4 cm²/m², Doppler velocity index 0.24. That's severe prosthesis-patient mismatch by every definition. You've technically succeeded and hemodynamically failed—at least for now.

This case, published by Merkulov and colleagues from the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center, forces us to confront a question that structural heart teams will increasingly face: what do you do when your post-procedural numbers look terrible, but your patient looks fine? The answer, at least in this case, is remarkable. At one month, that gradient plummeted to 27 mmHg. At one year, it held at 26 mmHg with an AVA of 1.1 cm² and an ejection fraction of 65%. The patient remained asymptomatic and active. So what happened between that alarming immediate post-procedural echo and the one-month follow-up that rescued this outcome?

## The Case in Brief

Let me orient you to the anatomy of this intervention. The patient received a 27 mm Lotus valve in 2014 for severe aortic stenosis. By 2022, that valve had degenerated severely—mean gradient 83 mmHg, EF down to 42%—and he underwent TAV-in-TAV with a 23 mm Evolut prosthesis. EF recovered to 54%. Three months later, he was hospitalized with *Enterococcus faecalis* prosthetic valve endocarditis, treated with six weeks of IV ampicillin-based therapy followed by oral antibiotics. Infection was confirmed resolved by repeated blood cultures, transesophageal echocardiography, and CT.

By January 2024, the degenerative march continued. Mean gradient crept up to 49 mmHg with moderate-to-severe regurgitation, and by June 2024, AVA had fallen to 0.5 cm². CT showed the Evolut's minimum lumen diameter had narrowed to 13.7 mm. Given a EuroSCORE II of 27.36%, prior triple-stented coronary anatomy, and two deeply integrated valve frames that would likely require total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement if surgical explantation were attempted, the heart team elected for a third transcatheter valve. They chose an Acurate neo2 size S, deployed with a deliberate high-implantation strategy at the level of the Evolut leaflets, guided by preprocedural CT planning that confirmed adequate coronary clearance without the need for coronary protection.

## The 67 mmHg Problem

Here's where the story gets clinically fascinating. Immediately after deployment, the valve leaflets were mobile with complete opening and no regurgitation—mechanically, the valve was functioning. But the mean gradient was 67 mmHg, and the indexed AVA was 0.4 cm²/m². That's not mild PPM. That's severe PPM by VARC-3 criteria. In a single-valve TAVI, those numbers would prompt serious concern about valve undersizing, malposition, or leaflet tethering.

But this was not a single-valve TAVI. Three stent frames now occupied the aortic annulus—a Lotus, an Evolut, and an Acurate neo2—each with its own geometry, radial force, and leaflet dynamics. The immediate hemodynamic profile reflected the unresolved tension between these three structures at the moment of deployment, not necessarily the final result.

## What Actually Happened: Mechanisms of Gradient Resolution

The authors attribute the dramatic gradient improvement to several converging mechanisms, and these are worth understanding in detail because they will generalize to other multi-valve configurations.

First, the Acurate neo2 is a self-expanding valve. Unlike balloon-expandable platforms, self-expanding frames continue to exert radial force over time, gradually achieving more complete apposition against the surrounding tissue and stent frames. The 67 mmHg gradient represented an under-expanded state that improved as the frame reached its nominal diameter over the first month. This is the self-expanding advantage in a nutshell—patience pays off.

Second, commissural alignment was explicitly planned using preprocedural CT modeling. The Acurate neo2 was aligned with the Evolut valve leaflets to maximize the effective orifice area and reduce the risk of patient-prosthesis mismatch. When commissures are misaligned in a valve-in-valve configuration, leaflet coaptation is suboptimal and the effective orifice suffers. In a triple-valve stack, the penalty for misalignment is even steeper. The CT-guided alignment here was not a nice-to-have—it was essential.

Third, high-pressure balloon post-dilation with a 20/40 mm balloon likely contributed to partial mechanical expansion of the prior Evolut frame. While intentional frame fracture was not performed, the balloon post-dilation probably forced further radial expansion of the Evolut, which in turn improved leaflet mobility and central flow dynamics through the valve stack. This is an underappreciated point: post-dilation in valve-in-valve procedures doesn't just expand the new valve, it can reshape the old one.

Fourth, left ventricular remodeling was observed at one month, with reduction in chamber volumes. This is the reverse side of the hemodynamic equation. The gradient depends on flow, and flow depends on LV geometry. As the ventricle remodeled in response to the new valve—even before full frame expansion—the effective gradient improved.

Finally, the high-implantation strategy deserves emphasis. By placing the Acurate neo2 at the level of the Evolut leaflets rather than deeper in the annulus, the team pinned the pre-existing Evolut leaflets against the stent frame, preventing them from folding inward and further narrowing the effective orifice. This also minimized the formation of a neo-skirt below the coronary ostia, preserving coronary flow and future coronary access—a critical consideration in a patient with three stented coronary arteries and a strong likelihood of needing reintervention.

## The High-Implantation Calculus

The decision to implant high was not casual. In a triple-valve stack, every millimeter of depth matters. Low implantation would have buried the Acurate neo2 deeper into the annulus, potentially worsening PPM by creating a longer tunnel of stacked stent frames and folded leaflets. High implantation preserved the maximum achievable orifice area and kept the neo-skirt away from the coronary ostia. The trade-off is the risk of embolization or paravalvular leak, but in this case, post-dilation sealed the result with no residual regurgitation. This is the kind of strategic calculus that only works with meticulous CT planning—measuring coronary ostial height, sinus dimensions, valve-to-coronary distance, and frame interaction geometry before the procedure, not improvising in the cath lab.

## When to Worry vs. When to Wait

The obvious question: should we be comfortable sending a patient home with a 67 mmHg gradient? The honest answer is that this remains a judgment call, but several features of this case made watchful waiting appropriate rather than dangerous.

The valve leaflets were mobile with complete opening on the immediate post-procedural echo. There was no residual regurgitation. The patient had no symptoms of decompensation. The mechanism of the high gradient was predictable—self-expanding frame under-expansion in a triple-valve stack—and the expected trajectory was improvement, not deterioration. And improve it did, dramatically and within one month.

Contrast this with scenarios that should prompt immediate concern rather than patience. A fixed, non-improving gradient beyond the first few weeks would suggest mechanical obstruction from frame deformation, valve malposition, or leaflet dysfunction that will not self-correct. New or worsening regurgitation would raise the possibility of incomplete sealing or leaflet entrapment. Worsening functional capacity or signs of heart failure would indicate that the hemodynamic burden is clinically significant, not just a number on an echo. And in the context of prior endocarditis, any fever, bacteremia, or new vegetations would demand immediate reassessment for recurrent infection.

The key clinical message is that the early gradient in a self-expanding multi-valve stack is a snapshot of an evolving geometry, not a final verdict. But this assumption holds only when the procedural team has a mechanistic explanation for the high gradient and a clear expectation of improvement based on valve design and deployment strategy.

## The Endocarditis Question

One aspect of this case that deserves attention is the decision to implant a third transcatheter valve after treated prosthetic valve endocarditis. The 2025 ESC guidelines recommend surgical valve replacement for PVE, particularly with heart failure or uncontrolled infection. But the guidelines also emphasize individualized decision-making. Here, the infection was fully treated—blood cultures negative, TEE and CT showing no vegetations or paravalvular complications, no long-term suppressive antibiotics required. The surgical risk was prohibitive. The decision to proceed with TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV was grounded in the principle that once infection is resolved, management of prosthetic valve dysfunction follows the same logic as in patients without prior endocarditis: anatomy, hemodynamics, and surgical risk drive the decision.

At one year, there was no evidence of recurrent endocarditis. This is reassuring but not conclusive—a single case does not establish safety. The long-term infective risk of layering a third prosthetic valve on top of a prior endocarditis field remains an open question.

## Where Do We Go From Here?

This case also surfaces the broader issue of lifetime management. With expanding TAVI indications and improving survival, we are accumulating a population of patients with multiple transcatheter valves who will outlive their prostheses. Each reintervention narrows the effective orifice, complicates coronary access, and reduces future options. If this Acurate neo2 fails prematurely, the options are grim. Surgical explantation in a triple-stented aortic root would be extraordinarily hazardous. Catheter-based solutions like bioprosthetic valve fracture or BASILICA have uncertain feasibility in triple-stented configurations.

This reality should change how we think about the first TAVI in younger or high-risk patients. Commissural alignment, coronary access preservation, and frame compatibility with future valve-in-valve strategies are not afterthoughts—they should be part of the original procedural plan. The structural heart team's job does not end when the valve deploys; it begins.

## Key Take-Home Points

- **A high immediate post-procedural gradient after self-expanding valve-in-valve-in-valve implantation may reflect delayed frame expansion and can improve dramatically within one month.** In this case, the mean gradient fell from 67 mmHg to 27 mmHg at one month and remained stable at 26 mmHg at one year.
- **Multiple mechanisms contribute to early gradient resolution:** ongoing self-expansion of the Acurate neo2 frame, CT-guided commissural alignment, high-pressure balloon post-dilation expanding the prior Evolut frame, and left ventricular remodeling reducing flow-dependent gradient elevation.
- **High implantation at the level of the prior Evolut leaflets was a deliberate strategy** to pin existing leaflets against the stent frame, prevent inward leaflet folding, maximize effective orifice area, and minimize neo-skirt formation below the coronary ostia.
- **Preprocedural CT planning is non-negotiable in triple-valve configurations.** Assessment of coronary ostial height, sinus dimensions, valve-to-coronary distance, and frame interaction geometry guided valve selection, implantation depth, and the decision that coronary protection was unnecessary.
- **Watchful waiting for gradient improvement is appropriate only when specific criteria are met:** mobile leaflets with complete opening, no residual regurgitation, no symptoms of decompensation, a mechanistic explanation for the high gradient, and expected improvement based on valve design. Any deviation from these criteria should prompt reassessment, not patience.
- **TAVI after treated prosthetic valve endocarditis is feasible when infection is fully resolved and surgical risk is prohibitive**, but the long-term infective risk of additional prosthetic layers remains undefined.
- **Lifetime management starts at the first TAVI.** Commissural alignment, coronary access preservation, and frame compatibility with future reinterventions should inform initial procedural planning, not just the next one.



# Planning for the Third Valve at the Time of the First: Lifetime Management of Structural Valve Disease

## The 10-Year TAVI Journey That Should Make Us Rethink Everything

A man in his late 70s walks into your cath lab for his *third* transcatheter aortic valve. His first was a Lotus valve implanted in 2014. His second was an Evolut TAV-in-TAV in 2022. Between those two procedures, he survived prosthetic valve endocarditis from *Enterococcus faecalis*. Now he's back with severe restenosis, a mean gradient of 49 mmHg, and an aortic valve area of 0.5 cm². Two overlapping bioprosthetic frames sit in his aortic root. Three coronary arteries are stented. His EuroSCORE II is 27.36%. Open surgery would likely require total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement because those valve frames are deeply integrated into the aortic wall. What do you do?

This case, published by Merzulov and colleagues from the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center, is a 10-year case study in why the valve you choose on day one shapes every option you have on day three thousand six hundred and fifty. It's also a preview of what's coming for all of us.

## The Case: A Decade of Valve Decisions

Let's walk through the timeline, because it's instructive not just for what happened but for what each decision *foreclosed*.

In 2014, this patient received a 27 mm Lotus valve for severe aortic stenosis. He was high surgical risk with three-vessel coronary disease (multiple PCI interventions between 2009–2014), third-degree AV block requiring a pacemaker, COPD, diabetes, and recurrent GI bleeding. The Lotus was a reasonable choice at the time — it was a mechanically retractable, self-expanding valve that allowed precise positioning and repositioning. But it's worth noting that the Lotus platform has since been withdrawn from the market, and its unique frame geometry creates specific challenges when you need to stack another valve inside it.

By December 2021, the Lotus was failing. Mean gradient had climbed to 83 mmHg by early 2022, and EF had dropped from 54% to 42%. The team performed a TAV-in-TAV with a 23 mm Evolut valve. The procedure worked — EF recovered to 54% — but was complicated by a femoral pseudoaneurysm requiring vascular surgery.

Three months later, in September 2022, the patient presented with fever and heart failure. Blood cultures grew *Enterococcus faecalis*. TTE showed severe restenosis with a mean gradient of 35 mmHg. Prosthetic valve endocarditis was diagnosed. He received 6 weeks of IV ampicillin-based therapy followed by oral antibiotics. Repeated blood cultures, TEE, and CT confirmed resolution.

But the damage was done. By January 2024, the valve was failing again — mean gradient 49 mmHg, moderate-to-severe regurgitation. The decision was made to proceed with a third transcatheter valve.

## The Third Valve: CT-Guided Strategy in a Triple-Stented Root

This is where the case becomes a masterclass in preprocedural planning. The team used MSCT to measure coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, valve-to-coronary distance, and the interaction between the two existing valve frames. The minimum lumen diameter of the Evolut prosthesis was 13.7 mm — confirming severe compromise and defining the landing zone for the third valve.

The key strategic decisions were:

- **High implantation of the third valve** at the level of the Evolut leaflets, which pinned the pre-existing leaflets against the stent frame and prevented further inward leaflet folding that would narrow the effective orifice
- **Minimizing neo-skirt formation** below the coronary ostia to preserve coronary flow and future access
- **Commissural alignment** of the Acurate neo2 with the Evolut leaflets, guided by CT modelling and fluoroscopic landmarks
- **No coronary protection wires** were used — the CT planning demonstrated adequate coronary clearance, and this was confirmed with simultaneous aortography during balloon pre-dilation

An Acurate neo2 (size S) was chosen for its supra-annular leaflet positioning, relatively low frame height, and favorable hemodynamic performance in narrow anatomies. Balloon pre-dilation was performed with a 22/40 mm catheter under rapid ventricular pacing at 180 bpm. Post-dilation used a 20/40 mm balloon. Final aortography confirmed correct positioning, preserved coronary perfusion, and no regurgitation.

## The Immediate Post-Procedural Puzzle

Here's a detail that should give anyone pause. Immediately after the third valve was deployed, the mean gradient was **67 mmHg**. The AVA was 0.9 cm². The DVI was 0.24. This was severe patient-prosthesis mismatch by every definition.

But at 1 month, the mean gradient had dropped to 27 mmHg and the AVA had increased to 1.1 cm². At 1 year, the gradient was 26 mmHg, AVA 1.1 cm², EF 65%, and the patient was asymptomatic and active.

What happened? The authors attribute the improvement to a combination of optimized commissural alignment, effective post-dilation, and partial mechanical expansion of the prior Evolut frame. The high-pressure balloon post-dilation likely contributed to further radial expansion of the Evolut, improving leaflet motion and central flow dynamics. The valve continued to remodel over the first month.

## Why the First Valve Choice Matters More Than You Think

This is the broader point the case forces us to confront. When this patient received his Lotus valve in 2014, lifetime management was not the framing. TAVI was for patients who couldn't have surgery. The question was "can we do this safely?" not "what happens when this valve fails in 8 years?"

But that's no longer the world we live in. TAVI is now approved for low-risk patients. People in their 50s and 60s are receiving transcatheter valves. Many of them will need at least one reintervention. Some will need two or three.

The initial valve choice shapes everything downstream. The Lotus valve, with its unique frame geometry, is no longer commercially available — but the principle holds for every valve we implant today. Frame height, commissural alignment, leaflet position, and stent cell design all determine what's possible when you need to stack another valve inside. A valve that sits high with large cells may preserve coronary access. A valve with a tall frame and dense mesh may not.

## Commissural Alignment: Not Just a Nice-to-Have

In this case, the team attempted commissural alignment of the Acurate neo2 with the Evolut leaflets based on preprocedural CT. This wasn't cosmetic. In a triple-stented root, the cumulative mesh burden from three overlapping frames can make coronary cannulation nearly impossible. If the commissures of successive valves are misaligned, you create a lattice that blocks guide catheter access to the coronary ostia.

The reality is that commissural alignment is often treated as an afterthought during first-time TAVI. It shouldn't be. If there's even a chance this patient will need a second valve, the alignment of the first one matters — because the alignment of the second depends on it, and the third depends on both.

## The Endocarditis Question

The 2025 ESC guidelines recommend surgical valve replacement for prosthetic valve endocarditis, particularly with heart failure or uncontrolled infection. But this patient had completed antimicrobial therapy, had no evidence of active infection on TEE or CT, and had a EuroSCORE II of 27.36% with two deeply integrated valve frames. Surgery would have required root replacement. The team made a reasonable decision to proceed with transcatheter intervention after confirming infection resolution.

The mechanism of valve failure was likely multifactorial — leaflet injury from endocarditis plus progressive structural degeneration. Once the infection was treated and resolved, the management strategy aligned with that of any patient with prosthetic valve dysfunction: assess feasibility, hemodynamic severity, and surgical risk.

## The Sobering Reality of Bail-Out Options

Here's what should keep you up at night. If this Acurate neo2 fails prematurely, the options are essentially exhausted. Surgical explantation in a triple-stented root would be technically nightmarish. Bioprosthetic valve fracture — using high-pressure balloon dilation to crack the stent frame of a prior valve — has been described for surgical bioprostheses and some transcatheter valves, but in a triple-stented configuration with a Lotus frame at the base, this is untested territory. BASILICA (bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction) has been used to prevent coronary obstruction during TAV-in-TAV, but its feasibility in three overlapping frames is unknown.

The authors acknowledge this directly. Emerging catheter-based rescue technologies exist, but their safety and efficacy in triple-stented configurations remain uncertain. We are in uncharted waters.

## Where This Leaves Us

This patient is alive and asymptomatic at 1 year. The procedure was technically successful. The CT-guided strategy was meticulous. The team should be commended. But this case is also a warning about the trajectory we're on.

With expanding TAVI indications and increasing patient longevity, complex multi-prosthesis interventions will become more frequent. Each reintervention narrows the options for the next one. The stent frames accumulate. The coronary access gets harder. The effective orifice area gets smaller. The bail-out options get thinner.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Plan for the third valve at the time of the first.** Every TAVI decision should include a lifetime management assessment — frame geometry, commissural alignment, coronary access preservation, and the possibility of future reintervention. This is especially critical in younger and lower-risk patients.
- **Commissural alignment matters from valve one.** Misaligned commissures in successive valves create a mesh barrier to coronary access. Alignment at the first procedure sets the trajectory for all future interventions.
- **CT-guided planning is non-negotiable for TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV.** In this case, MSCT defined the landing zone (minimum lumen diameter 13.7 mm in the Evolut), confirmed coronary clearance, guided high implantation strategy, and enabled a procedure without coronary protection wires.
- **High implantation can preserve effective orifice area.** Deploying the third valve at the level of the Evolut leaflets pinned pre-existing leaflets against the stent frame, prevented inward folding, minimized neo-skirt formation, and preserved coronary flow.
- **Post-procedural gradient improvement can be delayed.** This patient had a mean gradient of 67 mmHg immediately post-deployment, which improved to 26 mmHg at 1 year — likely from frame remodeling and post-dilation effects. Don't panic at the initial gradient if the valve is well-positioned.
- **Endocarditis history does not preclude TAV-in-TAV if infection is resolved.** After completing antimicrobial therapy with documented resolution (negative cultures, TEE and CT confirmation), the procedural decision can be guided by anatomical feasibility and surgical risk — though this remains an individualized, multidisciplinary decision.
- **Bail-out options in triple-stented configurations are largely theoretical.** BASILICA, bioprosthetic valve fracture, and surgical explantation have uncertain feasibility when three valve frames overlap. This reality should factor into earlier decision-making about when to stop stacking and whether surgical conversion at an earlier stage might preserve more options.
- **We need better data on long-term TAVI trajectories.** Evidence regarding serial transcatheter reinterventions remains limited. As more patients live long enough to need second and third valves, systematic data collection on outcomes, complications, and procedural strategies is essential.
