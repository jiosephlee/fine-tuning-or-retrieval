Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

Chapter 1: ## 1. Clinical Presentation and Initial Assessment

The patient, a previously healthy male in his 40s, arrived at the emergency department with a **three‑week history of progressive neck swelling** accompanied by mild proximal myalgia and weakness of the hip flexors. He denied any recent infections, trauma, or new medications that might trigger angioedema. The absence of fever, dysphagia at presentation, and a lack of significant weight loss or constitutional symptoms suggested a non‑infectious process, but the rapid and symmetrical swelling of the cervical strap muscles raised immediate concern for a deep neck space infection, particularly Ludwig’s angina.

### History

On detailed questioning, the patient reported:

- **Gradual, painless swelling of the neck** expanding over the anterior and lateral aspects, with no obvious erythema or warmth.
- **Mild proximal muscle aches** predominantly in the lower limbs, described as a “sore feeling” rather than frank weakness.
- **No dysphagia, odynophagia, or voice changes** at the time of presentation.
- **No known allergies** or history of angiotensin‑converting‑enzyme inhibitor or non‑steroidal anti‑inflammatory drug use.
- **No history of febrile illness** or recent dental procedures that could precipitate a neck infection.

The patient’s past medical history was remarkable only for a lifelong non‑smoking status. He had not been on immunosuppressive therapy or biologics. The early timeline emphasized a subacute, non‑febrile illness with the hallmark of neck edema—an atypical domain for dermatomyositis.

### Physical Examination

A focused neck examination revealed:

- **Diffuse, soft tissue edema** of the anterior neck, involving strap muscles and extending to the retropharyngeal space. The swelling was firm, non‑tender, and not associated with overlying skin changes or ulceration.
- **Peri‑angular facial erythema** that appeared subtle but was noted to involve the nasolabial folds. The face was not markedly flushed or violaceous.
- **Nailfold capillaroscopy** demonstrated a mixture of micro‑haemorrhages and avascular areas, suggestive of peri‑ungual inflammation.
- **Musculoskeletal assessment** identified 4/5 strength in right and left hip flexors, with a mild decrement in the ability to perform rapid “hip flexor drills.” No distal weakness or wasting was evident.
- **Dermatological inspection** did not initially reveal classic Gottron’s papules, shawl sign, or heliotrope rash, which can sometimes be subtle or absent in early disease.

The ENT examination via flexible nasendoscopy was unremarkable for structural lesions but showed increased secretions and reflex coughing, raising concerns about aspiration potential. The neck swelling’s distribution and firmness, coupled with the lack of systemic infection signs, suggested a non‑infectious inflammatory process.

### Differential Diagnosis at Triage

Given the clinical picture, the primary differentials included:

1. **Ludwig’s angina**: A fulminant deep neck infection commonly presenting with fever, trismus, and odynophagia; however, the patient was afebrile and lacked trismus.
2. **Idiopathic angioedema**: Histamine‑mediated or bradykinin‑mediated swelling of the soft tissues. The absence of urticaria, positive family history, or medication triggers made this less likely.
3. **Deep neck space infection** (cellulitis, sialadenitis, abscess): Typically associated with fever, leukocytosis, and elevated inflammatory markers; the patient’s CRP was mildly elevated (55.6 mg/L) but WCC was normal.
4. **Autoimmune myopathy**: Dermatomyositis and polymyositis can present with proximal muscle weakness and systemic inflammation; subclinical skin findings may initially be absent.
5. **Other causes**: Cephalhematoma, cellulitis from insect or bite, necrotizing fasciitis (unlikely without severe systemic signs).

The early **CT scan of the neck** confirmed diffuse neck edema involving strap muscles and extension to the retropharyngeal space but did not identify a discrete abscess or pharyngeal perforation. The imaging findings reinforced the likelihood of a diffuse inflammatory process rather than focal infection.

### Early Laboratory Investigations

Initial blood tests provided critical clues:

- **Complete blood count**: Normal WCC (9.1 × 10^9/L) with no neutrophilia.
- **C-reactive protein**: Moderately elevated at 55.6 mg/L, indicating systemic inflammation but not necessarily infectious.
- **Serum creatine kinase (CK)**: Pending at the initial presentation; the subsequent dramatic rise to 16 184 U/L (normal <200 U/L) strongly suggested a muscular injury/necrosis.
- **Transaminases**: Elevated ALT (143 U/L) and AST (396 U/L), with a notable rise in LDH (692 U/L), supporting muscle breakdown.
- **Complement levels (C3, C4)**, **C1 esterase**: Normal, making hereditary angioedema less likely.

The combination of **neck edema, elevated CK, and transaminitis** from the outset tipped the balance toward an inflammatory myopathy rather than an infectious or histaminergic angioedema process. The presence of peri‑ungual erythema on capillaroscopy further pointed toward a systemic connective‑tissue disorder.

### Red Flags Prompting Re‑evaluation

1. **Lack of fever or systemic signs** in the setting of deep neck swelling—unexpected for Ludwig’s angina.
2. **Normal leukocyte count** despite significant neck inflammation.
3. **Absence of classic skin manifestations** initially, yet subtle peri‑ungual changes.
4. **Extreme CK elevation** (over 80× normal) in a patient without obvious trauma.

These red flags prompted early involvement of the immunology team and expedited the measurement of CK and myositis‑specific autoantibodies. The resultant diagnosis of **anti‑SAE1 antibody–positive dermatomyositis** reframed the entire clinical picture, explaining both the neck edema (as a manifestation of submucosal myositis) and the proximal muscle weakness.

--- 

In summary, the initial presentation of progressive neck swelling in a patient with mild proximal myalgia and normal systemic inflammatory markers, combined with subtle peri‑ungual changes, should prompt clinicians—especially in emergency and ENT settings—to consider dermatomyositis in the differential. A systematic bedside examination focused on skin, nailfold capillaries, and muscle strength, paired with early CK measurement and targeted imaging, can expedite correct diagnosis and avert potentially disordered treatment strategies for presumed angioedema or infection.
