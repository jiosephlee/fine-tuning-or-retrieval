# When Neck Swelling Is Not an Infection: Dermatomyositis as a Hidden Cause of Angioedema

## Why this case screams “look again”

Picture yourself in a busy ED or an on‑call ENT service: a male in his 40s with a three‑week history of progressive neck swelling, mild fevers, and no obvious source of infection. He gets a CT neck, the scan shows diffuse soft‑tissue edema of the strap muscles, and the provisional diagnosis is Ludwig’s angina (or, later, idiopathic angioedema). Nothing is found to be bacterial; cultures are negative, the patient is afebrile, and lab work is “whe‑whe” except an elevated CRP. You’re in the same boat as the team who first saw him.

But pause. A few bedside clues in the early assessment should have nudged you toward a different list: 1) a symmetric, non‑pitting swelling of the neck; 2) subtle nail‑fold erythema and telangiectasias; 3) an unremarkable mucosal exam but a rash on the lateral thigh; 4) early proximal muscle weakness (hip flexors 4/5); and 5) a normal white‑cell count but a CRP spike that does not align with classic infection pathways.

This patient turned out to have anti‑SAE1‑positive dermatomyositis—an autoimmune myopathy that rarely masquerades as “pseudo‑angioedema.” The neck swelling was not a histaminergic or bradykinin‑mediated reaction but an inflammatory myositis of the strap muscles and deep neck spaces. Recognising this can save time, prevent unnecessary antibiotics, and—most importantly—initiate life‑saving immunosuppression early.

## The clinical puzzle

| Symptom/Exam | Typical in dermatomyositis? | Typical in angioedema? |
|--------------|---------------------------|------------------------|
| Neck swelling (diffuse, non‑pitting) | Yes, but uncommon | Yes, common |
| Proximal weakness (hip flexors) | Classic | Rare |
| Nail‑fold telangiectasia | Classic | Rare |
| Elevated CK (≈16 000 U/L) | Classic | Not typical |
| Subtle facial erythema (non‑heliotrope) | Possible | Not typical |
| Normal complement (C3/4, C1‑esterase) | Often normal | Usually normal |

The overlap is minimal: a sudden, isolated angioedema episode would not usually present with massive CK elevation or proximal muscle weakness. Yet, in dermatomyositis, the “angioedema” can be a downstream sign of deep muscle inflammation that expands the subcutaneous space—an imaging misinterpretation if one is not looking for a muscle‑centric process.

## What to look for in the room

1. **Full skin exam** – Gottron’s papules and heliotrope rash are the hallmark, but absence does not rule out disease. Look for subtle peri‑ungual erythema, shawl or V‑neck sign, or a hyper‑pigmented thigh rash.
2. **Capillaroscopy** – Nail‑fold microhaemorrhages or avascular spots can be a quick bedside clue.
3. **Neighbors’ muscle power** – Ask the patient to flex knees, lift their arms overhead, and check hip flexors. A weakness grading of 4/5 or less should raise red flags.
4. **Dysphagia screen** – Even mild pharyngeal weakness can manifest as residue or aspiration on a brief swallowing test.
5. **“Get the CK”** – It only takes one look. A serum CK >10 000 U/L should prompt a prompt rheumatology consult.

These steps are quick, cheap, and can dramatically shift the differential from “Ludwig’s angina” to “inflammatory myopathy.”

## Testing strategy after the bedside triage

| Test | Why it matters |
|------|----------------|
| CK, AST, ALT, LDH | Quantify muscle breakdown and liver involvement. |
| ANA with speckled pattern | Sensitivity for myositis, often high in dermatomyositis. |
| Myositis panel (anti‑SAE1, anti‑MDA5, anti‑TIF1‑γ, etc.) | Detects the autoantibody that defines disease subsets and guides prognosis. |
| Complement levels (C3, C4, C1‑esterase) | Rules out hereditary or acquired angioedema. |
| Stool for occult blood, routine labs | Baseline to rule out infection or other causes. |
| Imaging: neck MRI/FDG‑PET | Clarifies muscle inflammation vs. abscess or tumor. |

In our case, the anti‑SAE1 antibody was the clincher, and muscle biopsy confirmed perifascicular necrosis with membrane attack complex deposition—pathognomonic for dermatomyositis.

## How we treated this patient (and why)

| Stage | Interventions | Rationale |
|-------|---------------|-----------|
| 1. IV methylprednisolone (1 g × 3 days) | Rapid control of severe airway muscle involvement and systemic inflammation. |
| 2. Oral prednisone taper (60 mg → 0) | Long‑term steroid backbone. |
| 3. IVIG (2 g/kg, 5 days) × 6 cycles | Modulates immune system, bridges between steroids and disease control. |
| 4. Mycophenolate mofetil (1 g BID) | Steroid‑sparing agent, good oral tolerability. |
| 5. Rituximab (2 × 1 000 mg) | B‑cell depletion for refractory or high‑risk disease. |
| 6. Pneumocystis prophylaxis (initially TMP/SMX) → nebulised pentamidine | Avoid immunosuppression‑related opportunistic infections. |

The patient’s CK normalized within three weeks, and dysphagia eventually resolved after 12 months of multimodal therapy. The fact that his pulmonary findings improved (pretending to be aspiration pneumonia) underscores the systemic impact of untreated dermatomyositis.

## Key pearls for the busy clinician

- **Swelling that looks like angioedema but lacks a clear cause deserves a muscle‑specific work‑up.** Think myositis earlier, not later.
- **Nail‑fold findings and subtle skin changes can be the only clues.** Don’t dismiss a scraped‑readony sign as “post‑caffeine’ DO NOT WIMP!</p>
- **CK > 10 000 U/L almost rules out infectious or allergic angioedema** and suggests muscle breakdown.
- **Anti‑SAE1 is a red flag for dysphagia and interstitial lung disease** and warrants aggressive early treatment.
- **Do not rely solely on imaging.** A CT neck will not differentiate between fluid pannus and inflamed muscle; MRI or FDG‑PET is more specific.
- **High‐risk for malignancy?** Dermatomyositis + dysphagia + anti‑SAE1 require thorough screening per IMACS guidelines: CT chest + abdominal imaging, tumor markers, and endoscopic surveillance.

## Take‑away

| Question | Action |
|----------|--------|
|“Patient with neck swelling—what do I do first?”|Perform a quick focused exam: skin, nails, proximal strength, swallow. | 
|“Elevated CK—what next?”|Invite rheumatology, order myositis panel, consider MRI of affected muscles.|
|“Suspect dermatomyositis, but no classic rash.”|Look for peri‑ungual changes, lip fissures, mild facial erythema; remember cutaneous findings can be subtle early on.|
|“Is aggressive therapy warranted?”|Yes—start corticosteroids promptly, add an immunosuppressant (mycophenolate, rituximab) and consider IVIG.|
|“Screen for cancer?”|Yes—get baseline CT chest, abdominal imaging, tumor markers, and schedule endoscopy per IMACS. |

### Bottom line

When neck swelling defies a tidy infectious narrative, broaden your differential. Dermatomyositis can mimic angioedema through deep muscle inflammation that expands the soft tissues. Early recognition—through a focused bedside survey and a quick CK—carries the dual benefit of averting unnecessary antibiotics and ushering in life‑saving immunosuppression. Keep the eye on the nailfold, the mind on the CK, and you’ll save patients from a cascade of missteps.



# SAE1 Autoantibodies: A Rare Fingerprint of Dermatomyositis With Unusual Features

## 1. What Makes This Case a “Show‑Stopper”

Dermatomyositis normally crashes into your practice with skin rashes, proximal muscle weakness, and maybe a chest X‑ray that says “interstitial lung disease, come on.” The case I’m calling out today belongs to that small, overlooked subset where the skin never quite gets the glamorous attention it deserves— instead, a patient presents to the emergency department with a swollen neck that looks like Ludwig’s angina or an episode of angioedema. He has no history of ACE inhibitors, no trigger drugs, no obvious infection, and he’s shouting “I cannot swallow!” Turns out—yes, he’s got dermatomyositis, and more specifically, anti‑SAE1 antibody–positive disease.

That scenario is a wake‑up call for ENT surgeons, emergency physicians, and immunologists alike: **“If you see unexplained neck swelling that’s not histamine‑mediated, think dermatomyositis.”** The neurologically silent neck swelling may be hiding active muscle inflammation that will abruptly cause dysphagia, myopathy, and even pulmonary compromise if you’re not on a watchlist.

## 2. A Primer on SAE1 – The “Why” Behind the Antibody

### 2.1 What is SAE1?

- **Small Ubiquitin‑Like Modifier Activating Enzyme 1 (SAE1)** is part of the SUMOylation pathway, a post‑translational modification that tweaks how proteins function.
- Anti‑SAE1 autoantibodies target a component of this machinery – a protein unlikely to be “secreted,” suggesting a spill‑over of nuclear antigens into the circulation.

### 2.2 Why Does the Body Turn Against SAE1?

The exact immunopathology remains elusive, but current thinking suggests that:

- **Cell death in muscle fibers releases nuclear antigens** that trigger a break in tolerance.
- **SUMOylated proteins** might become misfolded or aberrantly disposed, flagging the immune system.
- A **genetic predisposition** (e.g., HLA alleles) could amplify this response.

### 2.3 How Does SAE1‑Positive Dermatomyositis Differ Clinically?

| Feature | Typical Dermatomyositis | SAE1‑Positive Dermatomyositis |
|---------|------------------------|--------------------------------|
| Cutaneous rash | Helio‑tropic eye, Gottron’s papules, V‑neck | Often milder; heliotrope possible but may be absent in some (as in our neck‑swelling case) |
| Muscle weakness | Proximal, symmetric | Same, but severe dysphagia common |
| Dysphagia | 10–20% | 45–50% documented |
| Interstitial lung disease | 20–30% | ~43% (from large series) |
| Malignancy | 5–10% | 18% (moderate‑to‑high) |
| Oedema/angioedema | Rare | ~6% can present with facial or neck swelling |

> **Key take‑away:** If you see dysphagia or lung involvement in dermatomyositis, check SAE1. It’s a red flag for a higher ILD and potentially more aggressive disease.

## 3. Diagnostic Workflow – When to Drop the “SAE1” Flag

1. **Clinical suspicion** – Any patient with unexplained neck swelling + proximal myopathy + subtle cutaneous signs.
2. **Initial labs** – CBC, ESR/CRP, CK (‑edge!; <200 U/L might mislead), fasting glucose, immunoglobulins.
3. **Autoantibody panel** –  
   * **Myositis‐specific antibodies** (MSAs) using line‑assay or ELISA:  
     - Anti‑Jo‑1 (most common),  
     - Anti‑Mi‑2, anti‑MDA‑5, anti‑TIF1‑γ, **anti‑SAE1**, etc.
   * **Myositis‐associated antibodies** (MAAs) – e.g., anti‑Ro/SSA for overlap syndromes.
4. **Imaging** –  
   * **CT neck**: to rule out infectious or neoplastic processes.  
   * **FDG‑PET** or **MRI**: for muscle activity & discernment of active myositis.
5. **Muscle biopsy** – This is the gold standard; look for perifascicular atrophy, C5b‑9 deposition, and muscle necrosis.
6. **Screen for malignancy** – According to the IMACS guideline, high‑risk patients (dermatomyositis + dysphagia + SAE1 positivity + male sex) deserve CT chest/abdomen, PET‑CT, and age‑appropriate endoscopy.

> **Clinical pearl:** Screening labs for complement levels (C3, C4, C1q) and for evidence of angioedema (C1 esterase, bradykinin) can quickly rule out hormone‑mediated causes, saving you from unnecessary ED resources.

## 4. Malignancy Risk Stratification – Why SAE1 Matters

- **Risk categories (ICM‑IMACS)**  
  *Low:* <10 %  
  *Intermediate:* 10–30 %  
  *High:* >30 %
- SAE1 is an **intermediate** marker; however, when combined with dysphagia and male gender, the composite risk climbs into the **high** bracket.
- **Screening strategy:**  
  * Baseline CT chest/abdomen, PET‑CT, and routine tumor markers.  
  * Upper GI endoscopy and colonoscopy tailored to age/sex.  
  * Long‑term surveillance: repeat imaging at 6‑12 month intervals until 5 years.

## 5. Treatment Landscape – What Works, What’s Uncertain

### 5.1 First‑Line: Glucocorticoids
High‑dose IV methylprednisolone (1 g/day for 3 days) followed by a daily taper based on clinical response and CK trend.

### 5.2 Steroid‑Sparing Agents
| Agent | Evidence | Practical notes |
|-------|----------|------------------|
| **Mycophenolate mofetil** | 1 g BID; good for patients where MTX may worsen ILD | Usually better tolerated for long‑term use |
| **Methotrexate** | Most studied; risk of pulmonary fibrosis | Avoid if baseline PFTs abnormal |
| **Azathioprine** | Alternative when MMF not tolerated | Monitor TPMT genotype |
| **Rituximab** | Useful for refractory disease; evidence from case series | 1000 mg IV × 2 weeks, repeat if relapse |
| **IVIG** | Helpful in severe dysphagia or rapidly progressive disease | 2 g/kg over 5 days, repeat every 4–6 weeks |

> **Current evidence caveat:** Randomized trials are scarce; most data come from retrospective cohorts. Treatment remains highly individualized.

### 5.3 Emerging Therapies
- **Biosimilars of rituximab** → potential cost savings.  
- **Janus kinase inhibitors** (tofacitinib, baricitinib) – pilot data suggests benefit in refractory dermatomyositis, but data specific to SAE1 is lacking.  
- **Targeted complement inhibition** – split‑thickness doesn’t yet enter routine practice for dermatomyositis.

## 6. Putting the Puzzle Together – A Clinical Take‑Home

- **Suspicion is Key:** Anytime neck swelling appears “angioedema‑like” and no cause is found, check a creatine kinase and skin exam.  
- **Autoantibody panels matter:** SAE1 is not the most common, but when present it predicts a dysphagia‑heavy, ILD‑heavy phenotype.  
- **Screen aggressively for cancer** if you have high‑risk features (SAE1 + dysphagia + male).  
- **Treat early & aggressively:** Start steroids, consider MMF or rituximab, and use IVIG for dysphagia.  
- **Follow the evidence, not the hype:** There are no large RCTs yet; therapy is a do‑what‑works strategy.

## 7. Bottom Line – “Don’t Forget the Neck”

For the next time a patient comes in with neck swelling and a normal infection screen, picture the “neck‑swelling dermatomyositis” patient in your head. Check CK, look at the nails, order the MSA panel, and you may uncover SAE1‑positive dermatomyositis before the dysphagia becomes a crisis. Remember, this isn’t just a skin & muscle disease; it’s a systemic canary that can sound off poorly in a more serious choir—so listen closely.

# Muscle‑Targeted Swelling: How Dermatomyositis Can Produce True “Pseudo‑Angioedema”

## The moment that throws everyone off the track

Picture this: a man in his 40s rolls into the emergency department complaining of a three‑week, “puffy neck” that has expanded around his jaw and down his face. A CT scan shows diffuse edema of the strap muscles and a slight retropharyngeal infiltrate, but no abscess, abscess‑disposition, or obvious infectious focus. The patient is afebrile, CRP’s up at 56 mg/L, and his only other complaint is a vague “tightness” in his chest. The ENT officer in the room mentions the classic Ludwig’s angina possibility, pulls a neck‑pooling jug, and orders antibiotics. A few hours later, a rheumatologist runs a creatine kinase. The result? 16,000 U/L. Suddenly—yes—the story shifts from an infectious deep‑neck space problem to a diagnosis that was lurking in the shadows: dermatomyositis.

Dermatomyositis presenting as neck swelling is a *real* podshot of the disease, and it demands that we think beyond “histamine + bradykinin” in the context of angioedema. Let’s dive into: how it happens, how you spot it on imaging, and why timing your steroid pulse could be lifesaving.

---

## Pathophysiology: Muscle‑driven Edema vs. Classic Angioedema

| Feature | Classic Histamine‑/Bradykinin‑mediated Angioedema | Dermatomyositis‑related “Pseudo‑Angioedema” |
|---------|----------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------|
| **Mediators** | Histamine, tryptase, bradykinin | Complement activation (C5b‑9 MASP‑2), local cytotoxic inflammation |
| **Target** | Sub‐epidermal vessels, superficial soft tissue | Perifascicular muscle fibres, endomysial capillaries |
| **Onset** | Minutes to hours | Days to weeks (sub‑acute) |
| **Associated labs** | Normal C3/C4, normal CK | Markedly ↑ CK (often >10× ULN), ↑ LDH, ↑ transaminases |
| **Dermatologic clues** | Usually none | Gottron’s papules, heliotrope rash, periungual telangiectasia, nailfold micro‑haemorrhages |
| **Edema distribution** | Superficial, non‑tender, often localized | Diffuse, involves muscles, may be painful or tender, can track along fascia |

In dermatomyositis, the immune system attacks the endomysial capillary network particularly around the non‑contractile zones of muscle fibres. Because these vessels lie within the fascia that encloses the neck musculature, the local inflammatory cascade leads to capillary leakage and fluid accumulation **inside the muscle itself**, not the subcutaneous layer. That’s why you see “soft‑tissue swelling” that behaves like angioedema but actually tracks along the muscle, venturing into the retropharyngeal space and even impairing airway.

---

## Imaging hallmarks

### 1. CT Neck

- **Findings**: Fat stranding, diffuse swelling of the strap muscles, involvement of the retropharyngeal space.
- **Interpretive clue**: If the swelling “follows” the muscle planes rather than the superficial skin, consider myositis.
- **Tip**: A contrast‑enhanced scan may reveal linear enhancement along the fascia – more typical of active inflammation than an abscess.

### 2. MRI of the Neck / Thighs

- **T2/FLAIR hyperintensity** in the strap muscles, obturator internus/externus, adductors, and psoas – the classic “red‑blood‑sugar” signal of active edema/inflammation.
- **Short Tau Inversion Recovery (STIR)** sequences highlight edema‑rich tissue.
- The hallmark is the *perifascicular* pattern – the outer zones are more affected than the central muscle fibres.

### 3. PET‑FDG

- Diffuse FDG uptake in *multiple* muscle groups (head & neck, upper limbs, trunk).
- In dermatomyositis, you’ll see the “pink‑heart sign” – a diffuse, nearly homogeneous uptake in the muscles.
- **Clinical utility**: Rules out occult lymphoma or metastasis (which often have focal uptake) and confirms systemic muscle involvement.

**Bottom line:** If imaging shows diffuse, symmetrical muscle hyperintensity or FDG uptake, you’re not looking at cellulitis or a strict angioedema; you’re probably looking at myositis. That changes management from antibiotics to immunosuppression.

---

## Airway & Dysphagia: A Dangerous Combo

The neck swelling can encroach on the airway. In this case, the patient had *retropharyngeal* edema and a barium swallow demonstrating severe pharyngeal dysphagia with post‑swallow penetration. Why is that pivotal?

1. **Airway protective reflexes** get blunted when muscles controlling the pharynx are inflamed. 
2. Compromise of the hyoid and suprahyoid muscles can obstruct the upper airway, precipitating hypoxia.
3. Dysphagia is a red flag for *edematous* involvement of pharyngeal flexor muscles, indicating that early and aggressive immunosuppression may be required to preserve airway.

### What to do?

- **Early airway assessment**: laryngoscopy or fiber‑optic assessment if there is any suspicion of airway compromise, even if the patient is not tachypnoeic.
- **Nasogastric feeding** may be necessary until swallowing improves. 
- **Avoid sedatives** that could further depress respiratory drive.

---

## Urgent Steroid Pulse vs. Other Immunosuppressives

| Therapy | Rationale | Typical Timing | Front‑line? |
|---------|-----------|----------------|-------------|
| **IV methylprednisolone 1 g × 3 days** | Rapid blockade of cytokine surge, complement inhibition | Immediately on suspicion of severe muscle inflammation | Yes – first line for life‑threatening presentations |
| **IVIG (2 g/kg)** | Modulates Fc receptors, reduces autoantibody activity | Usually a few days after steroid pulse | Second line or adjunct in refractory cases |
| **Mycophenolate mofetil (1 g BID)** | T‑cell proliferation inhibitor; oral, long‑term maintenance | After initial induction | Long‑term maintenance |
| **Rituximab (1 g × 2)** | B‑cell depletion; useful in refractory or severe disease | Weeks after initiation of steroids | Add‑on for severe or refractory disease |
| **Low‑dose colchicine** | For complement‑driven edema? | Experimental | Still experimental |

The patient in the case we’re discussing received a classic 3‑day IV methylprednisolone pulse, followed by a rapid taper, then a rotating regimen of IVIG, mycophenolate, and rituximab. The overall strategy is two‑fold: one, seize the very window when inflammation is most intense; two, maintain long‑term immunosuppression to prevent relapses and airway compromise.

**Clinical pearl:** Do *not* wait for the CK to normalize before initiating steroids—guidelines advise treatment at the *onset* of significant muscle protein damage.

---

## Take‑away Checklist

- **Start early CK screening** in any patient with unexplained neck swelling that tracks along facial/neck muscles.
- **Look for subtle dermatomyositis clues**: periungual telangiectasias, nailfold micro‑haemorrhages, early skin changes even if more classic lesions are absent.
- **Check Complement Levels** – normal C3/C4 does *not* rule out dermatomyositis‑related edema.
- **Order CT neck, MRI of the involved area, and PET‑FDG** if clinical suspicion is high; look for symmetry and perifascicular pattern.
- **Airway assessment** (laryngoscopy) is essential if dysphagia or respiratory symptoms are present.
- **Initiate IV methylprednisolone 1 g × 3 days** within the first 24 h of diagnosis for severe presentations.
- **Adjunctive therapy**: IVIG and B‑cell depletion (rituximab) may be added in refractory or organ‑at‑risk cases.
- **Post‑acute monitoring**: CK should trend down within days; repeat imaging at 3–6 weeks to confirm resolution of edema.

---

## Closing thought

Dermatomyositis can masquerade as an angioedema crisis, and the earlier the diagnosis, the better the outcomes—especially when airway compromise looms. The key difference lies not in the skin rash (which may be subtle or delayed) but in the *location and nature* of the edema: muscle‑infiltrating, complement‑driven, and amenable to high‑dose steroids. Recognizing the unique imaging signature and rapidly initiating immunosuppression can turn a life‑threatening neck edema into a story of recovery, just like the man in his 40s who went from a puffy neck to a full‑rescue within months.

# Multidisciplinary Management of Aggressive Dermatomyositis:
## From IVIG to Rituximab

---

## Why this case should keep your phone screen on little white‑coat‑color mice

A previously healthy man in his 40s walked into the ED with a 3‑week history of neck swelling that looked like Ludwig’s angina. A CT scan showed diffuse subcutaneous edema of the strap muscles. He had a modest CRP, no fever, no obvious source of infection, and no tachypnoea. ENT cleared him of a deep‑neck space infection, and immunologists were called in for *idiopathic angioedema*. When the creatine kinase came back at 16,000 U/L, the puzzle pieces fell into place: anti‑SAE1–positive dermatomyositis presenting as pseudo‑angioedema.

It’s a classic narrative for “here’s a thick – we spell on steroids” but the fire‑walls of modern practice are more layered. How do we juggle high‑dose steroids, IVIG, rituximab, mycophenolate, infection prophylaxis, and a looming malignant risk without turning a good patient into a toxic‑waste‑pump? Below, I distill the algorithm that guided this gentleman to near‑complete recovery.

---

## The triad of failure: airway, dysphagia, and inflammation

1. **Airway compromise** – cervical subcutaneous edema from myositis, not histamine or bradykinin.  
2. **Dysphagia** – evidenced by video fluoroscopy and aspiration‑related consolidation on CT.  
3. **Marked muscle inflammation** – CK 16 184 U/L, PET avidity in multiple muscle groups, perifascicular necrosis on biopsy.

With all three present, the patient crossed the “organ‑involving” threshold for aggressive therapy.

---

## Step‑by‑step treatment algorithm

| Step | Intervention | Rationale & Timing | Key Points |
|------|--------------|--------------------|------------|
| **1️⃣ High‑dose IV methylprednisolone** | 1 g IV daily × 3 days | Rapid anti‑inflammatory effect, work‑up for neuromuscular involvement, bridge to other agents | Taper 5 mg off prednisone every 2–3 weeks; avoid a “steroid burst” without coverage |
| **2️⃣ First‑line immunosuppressant – Mycophenolate mofetil (MMF)** | 1 g BID (oral) | MTX avoided due to baseline restrictive PFTs (lung risk). MMF has shown benefit in DM and is orally convenient | Monitor CBC/renal function every 2–4 weeks |
| **3️⃣ Bypass the globin‑centred “busy” calcineurin drugs – avoid azathioprine, tacrolimus** | Not used | Both have high infection & GI risk; azathioprine can worsen ILD | Choose agents with better toxicity profile |
| **4️⃣ Adjunctive IVIG** | 2 g/kg over 5 days (first cycle) + 5 more cycles 4 weeks apart | Beneficial in refractory DM, can reduce steroid burden, addresses immune dysregulation | Watch for volume overload in heart‑failed patients; concomitant infection risk |
| **5️⃣ Second‑line biologic – Rituximab** | 1 000 mg IV × 2, 2 weeks apart | B‑cell depletion targets circulating autoantibody‑producing cells; evidence in DM & PM | Pre‑medicate with acetaminophen & antihistamine; monitor for infusion reactions |
| **6️⃣ Infection prophylaxis** | • PJP prophylaxis: start TMP‑SMX, but if hemolytic anemia → nebulised pentamidine 300 mg q4 wks. • ACE‑I or ARB‑avoid? Not relevant to DM but baseline blood pressure can worsen. | ΔT: suppression of macrophage function with steroids & MMF. | Check haptoglobin, LDH, and CBC before each TMP‑SMX cycle |
| **7️⃣ Monitoring** | • CK and transaminases bi‑weekly until <200 U/L, then monthly. • PFTs every 3 months. • Repeat PET or CT if worsening symptoms. | Capture fluctuations earlier; avoid overtreatment or delaying relapse | Document a V4 “flare score” (CK, strength, PFT, imaging) before taper |
| **8️⃣ Steroid taper** | Start at 60 mg PO daily → reduce 5 mg every 2–3 weeks if no relapse. | Target cumulative dose <2 g over 6 months; avoid re‑exposure to 10 mg/day for >4 weeks | If relapse, pause taper, re‑intensify IV steroids, and resume after control |
| **9️⃣ Malignancy surveillance** | Baseline chest CT, tumour markers, PET (already done). Follow with upper/lower GI endoscopy in 6 months, then biennial. | SAE1 positivity confers ~18 % malignancy risk. | Keep a high index for new rash, weight loss, night sweats. |

---

## Pearls & pitfalls

- **“Pseudo‑angioedema” can be myositis** – Always check CK in any unexplained, non‑radiologically documented neck or facial swelling.  
- **Don’t forget the nailfold capillaroscopy** – periungual erythema or microhaemorrhages can steer you toward dermatomyositis.  
- **Inflammatory armamentarium is not consensual** – The literature shows heterogeneity; select based on organ‑involvement, tolerability, and baseline comorbidity.  
- **TMP‑SMX → hemolytic anemia even without G6PD** – Monitor CBC, haptoglobin, LDH; switch to nebulised pentamidine if anaemia develops.  
- **PJP prophylaxis is mandatory until you’re below steroid 10 mg/day** – Even if you stop MMF or rituximab.  
- **Steroid taper is a negotiation** – Use a “taper‑with‑hit‑back” protocol if CK rises >2× ULN or strength wanes.  
- **Avoid methotrexate in interstitial lung disease** – It’s a known culprit for exacerbating ILD and fibrosis.  
- **EBV reactivation risk with rituximab** – Screen baseline EBV IGG/IgM; consider prophylactic ganciclovir if high‑risk.  

---

## Take‑home points

- **Treat aggressively but exactly** – Use high‑dose steroids to reverse airway compromise, pair with MMF for disease stabilization, add IVIG for rapid control, and rituximab for refractory cases.  
- **Watch the side‑effect profile** – Hemolytic anaemia from TMP‑SMX, PJP risk from steroids/MMF, infusion reactions from rituximab, and nephrotoxicity from MMF.  
- **Institutional protocols should be multidisciplinary** – SN, ENT, rheumatology, and infectious disease all need to agree on prophylaxis, monitoring schedules, and taper timelines.  
- **Early malignancy screening** – Immunogenic disease + dysphagia + SAE1 positivity → high‑risk. Adopt a structured IMACS‐style program.  
- **Measurable outcomes** – CK, strength, PFTs, imaging. Use these to guide tapering and to pre‑empt relapse.  

---

### Bottom line

The cascade from IV methylprednisolone to rituximab, coupled with vigilant infection prophylaxis and scheduled tapering, leads to an impressive recovery trajectory—even in the face of pseudo‑angioedema, dysphagia, and a high CK load. The muscle‑driven “white space” in the neck stayed healthy and the nerve‑gated swallowing apparatus finally normalised. This case underscores how fluid multidisciplinary collaboration and evidence‑guided escalation are vital for steering a severe dermatomyositis needle through the messy periphery of the immune system.



# Cancer Screening in Dermatomyositis: Who Needs What and Why

## The Hook
A 42‑year‑old man came into the ED with a 3‑week history of progressive neck swelling that the whole team kept calling “angioedema.” He was afebrile, had a mildly elevated CRP, and a constellation of subtle skin changes that were easily missed unless one knew the dermatomyositis “button‑hole” of nailfold haemorrhages and peri‑ungual erythema. Within 24 h of admission, a CK of 16 000 U/L and a positive anti‑SAE1 autoantibody tipped the scale toward dermatomyositis – and not just any dermatomyositis, but a subtype that is infamous for dysphagia, interstitial lung disease, and – crucially for us – a **high pre‑test probability of malignancy**.

This case highlights two things that often go hand‑in‑hand:  
1. The brutal masquerading of dermatomyositis as “pseudo‑angioedema” can delay diagnosis.  
2. Once you have a definitive diagnosis, deciding *what* cancer screening to perform is an art that depends on patient‑specific risk factors.  

Below, I walk through the evidence‑based approach to malignancy screening in dermatomyositis, illustrated by the SAE1‑positive case above.

## Why we care about cancer screening in dermatomyositis

Dermatomyositis (DM) is a classic paraneoplastic disease; up to 20‑30 % of adults with DM harbor an occult tumour. Early tumour detection can dramatically influence outcomes: a case‑controlled study showed a 5‑year survival benefit of 20 % when tumours were identified within 6 months of a DM diagnosis. Conversely, over‑testing can lead to unnecessary anxiety, radiation exposure, and delayed treatment of other comorbidities.

The American College of Rheumatology and the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) have issued guidelines that marry clinical features, autoantibody profiles, and demographic data to stratify patients into **low, intermediate, and high‑risk** categories for malignancy. The SAE1‑positive patient we just reviewed sits squarely in the high‑risk box:  

| Risk Factor | Patient’s Status |
|-------------|------------------|
| DM diagnosis | ✅ |
| Dysphagia | ✅ |
| SAE1‑positive autoantibody | ✅ |
| Male sex | ✅ |
| Age > 50 | ❌ (but still >40, borderline) |
| Rapid onset (<3 months) | ✅ |

With **four high‑risk** and **two intermediate‑risk** features, the IMACS algorithm flags the patient for *full‑body* screening – not just a chest CT or a CA‑125.

## The IMACS risk matrix in practice

| Risk Factor | Low | Intermediate | High |
|-------------|-----|--------------|------|
| DM alone | 15 % malignancy risk | 30 % | >50 % |
| Dysphagia |  | 30 % | >50 % |
| Myositis-specific antibodies (e.g., TIF1‑γ, MDA‑5, SAE1) |  | 30 % | >50 % |
| Onset < 3 months |  | 30 % | >50 % |
| Age ≥ 50 |  | 30 % | >50 % |
| Male sex |  | 30 % | >50 % |
| Cancer histories |  | 30 % | >50 % |

> **Take‑home:** If a patient has *two or more* high‑risk or *one high‑risk plus two intermediate* factors, you should adopt a “high‑risk” protocol.

## What does “high‐risk” screening mean?

1. **Chest CT (thin‑slice, low-dose)** – detects occult lung, mediastinal, or intrathoracic tumours. In the SAE1 case, a 4 mm ground‑glass opacity was clarified as aspiration‑related after a bronchoscopy; but it would be missed if you only did a chest X‑ray.  
2. **Whole‑body (or trunk‑to‑feet) PET‑CT** – functional imaging that flags metabolically active lesions outside the thorax. Our patient’s PET‑CT showed diffuse myositis activity but *no* metabolic focus suspicious for malignancy – a reassuring negative result.  
3. **Tumour markers panel** – CEA, CA‑19‑9, CA‑125, PSA (and for females: CA 15‑3, CA 153), and LDH. Useful mainly for tumor surveillance once a specific cancer is identified; rarely a screening tool alone.  
4. **Endoscopic surveillance** – upper GI endoscopy and flexible sigmoidoscopy/colonoscopy. Gastrointestinal cancers (particularly colorectal, pancreatic, gastric, and ovarian) are the most frequent occult tumours in DM patients.  
5. **Abdominal and pelvic CT or MRI** – to rule out pancreatic, ovarian, and other visceral tumours, especially if the patient reports abdominal pain, mass effect, or risk factors for endometrial cancer.  
6. **Additional targeted imaging** – e.g., mammography and breast MRI in women, PSA PSA in men, ultrasound of the thyroid.

**Tip:** In practice, you can combine the CT chest, whole‑body PET‑CT, and abdominal CT into a single “full‑body” imaging session (often in operating hours with a nuclear medicine team). This minimizes patient travel and cumulative radiation.

## Avoiding the “over‑screening trap”

Even high‑risk patients never require a **brain MRI** or **PET‑CT for every month**. Instead, adopt a stepwise approach:

| Step | Trigger | Test | Frequency |
|------|---------|------|-----------|
| 1 | Initial high‑risk assessment | Chest CT + whole‑body PET‑CT + abdominal/pelvic CT | Baseline |
| 2 | Baseline negative, low clinical suspicion | Repeat CT/PET‑CT | 6–12 months |
| 3 | New symptoms (e.g., unexplained weight loss, night sweats, new masses) | Targeted imaging (US, MRI) | On demand |
| 4 | Biopsy‑confirmed malignancy | Follow standard oncology surveillance | As per tumour type |

“Watch‑fully” means re‑evaluate at 6 months; if nothing emerges, you can safely consider tapering down imaging burden. This aligns with several expert consensus statements that recommend a 1 year surveillance interval for patients with no evidence of malignancy after baseline imaging.

## Case‑specific lessons

- **Early CK**: Look for myositis in any patient with unexplained neck swelling, especially when the swelling is non‑reducible, non‑tender, and with a mild systemic inflammatory picture.  
- **Autoantibodies guide suspicion**: SAE1 is strongly linked to dysphagia and ILD; it also signals a broader autoimmune milieu that may coexist with occult cancers.  
- **Persistent dysphagia**: A dysphagia symptom that lingers >3 months in DM should trigger a PET‑CT, as it predicts a 2‑fold increase in malignancy risk.  
- **Chemoprophylaxis**: Don’t forget PPIs or H2 blockers if you’re giving steroids – many DM patients have a history of gastro‑oesophageal reflux that may mask a GI malignancy.  

## Take‑home points (bullet‑point style)

- **Risk stratify** using the IMACS algorithm: identify high‑risk (≥2 high‑risk features) vs. intermediate/low.  
- **High‑risk protocol**: chest CT, whole‑body PET‑CT, abdominal/pelvic CT, and GI endoscopy ± tumour markers.  
- **Avoid routine brain MRI** or serial PET‑CTs unless new neurologic or systemic symptoms arise.  
- **Re‑evaluate at 6 months** if baseline imaging is clean; if unchanged, consider extending surveillance to 12 months.  
- **Keep an eye on dysphagia**: Persistent dysphagia is a red flag for occult malignancy.  
- **Tailor the plan**: In resource‑constrained settings, prioritize chest CT + abdominal CT with targeted PET‑CT only if high‑grade features persist.  
- **Document the plan**: Ensure the patient knows why each test was ordered – a conversation improves compliance and reduces anxiety.

## Bottom line

Dermatomyositis, especially the SAE1‑positive subset, should automatically trigger a “high‑risk” malignancy screen. The IMACS criteria give you a pragmatic framework, but the ultimate goal is to catch a hidden tumour early while sparing patients unnecessary tests. Think of this not as a one‑size‑fits‑all checklist but as a flow‑chart that scales with clinical nuance – just as we’d adjust steroid dosing based on CK trends.

If you’re a frontline clinician, remember that a patient who appears to have “angioedema” might actually be a DM patient wrestling with a silent cancer. The sooner you get the right work‑up, the sooner the patient can start immunosuppression *and* oncology care concurrently. Until then, keep your CK, autoantibody panel, and chest CT ready.



# From Dysphagia to Recovery: Long‑Term Follow‑Up and Outcomes in Dermatomyositis  

**Case in a nutshell**  
A previously healthy male in his 40s presented to the ED with a 3‑week history of progressive neck swelling. CT revealed diffuse soft‑tissue oedema, prompting a differential of Ludwig’s angina vs idiopathic angioedema. A nailfold capillaroscopy, subtle peri‑ungual erythema and an elevated CK (16 184 U/L) turned the diagnosis to anti‑SAE1 dermatomyositis. The patient underwent aggressive immunosuppression (IV methylprednisolone, IVIg, mycophenolate, rituximab) and required a nasogastric tube for 2 months due to severe dysphagia. By 12 months all major clinical indices (CK, pulmonary function, imaging) had returned to baseline and the patient was symptom‑free. The story is instructive for clinicians who manage airway compromise and dysphagia in inflammatory myopathy.

---

## Why This Case Stands Out  

* **Neck swelling as a red flag** – Dermatomyositis rarely presents with pseudo‑angioedema confined to the neck. That an otherwise well male presented with isolated cervical oedema could have led to unnecessary antibiotics or surgical drainage if not for the high CK and capillaroscopy findings.  
* **Rapid response to steroids + immunotherapy** – A 3‑day pulse of methylprednisolone was followed by a sustained taper with IVIg and mycophenolate, illustrating that aggressive early therapy can reverse even severe airway muscle involvement.  
* **Longitudinal monitoring** – Serial CK, PFTs and imaging were used to gauge remission and to epochally adjust therapy, demonstrating a pragmatic approach to disease activity.  

---

## The Natural History of Dysphagia in Dermatomyositis  

| Timepoint | Key Clinical Features | Management Milestone |
|-----------|-----------------------|----------------------|
| **Presentation** | Severe odynophagia, retropharyngeal oedema, neck swelling, proximal muscle weakness | Secure airway, rule out infection, obtain CK & autoantibody panel |
| **Early Hospitalization** | Progressive dysphagia to liquids; aspiration evident on swallow study | Initiate high‑dose IV steroids + consider IVIg; start enteral feeding (NG tube) |
| **Week 3–6** | CK trending down, neck oedema improves | Trial of oral liquids; repeat swallow; consider early swallow therapy |
| **Month 3** | CK normal, PFTs improving, swallow normal | Discharge with oral meds; wean steroids, taper immunosuppression |
| **Month 6–12** | Full muscular strength, no dysphagia, normal CK | Long‑term maintenance (mycophenolate ± IVIg, occasional steroid burst) |

**Take‑home points**

- **Early recognition** of dysphagia in myositis is crucial; a high CK or major antibody (e.g., anti‑SAE1) should prompt a swallow study even if oral intake seems fine.  
- **Enteral feeding** is not a last resort; in severe cases, an NG tube for 4–8 weeks can prevent aspiration pneumonia and give the immune system time to recover.  
- **Weaning decisions** should be guided by a combination of clinical exam, serial CK, and objective swallow testing.  
- **Pulmonary function testing** (PFTs) at baseline and every 3–6 months is valuable for detecting early ILD and for prognostication.  

---

## Serial CK: The “Neurospecific” Biomarker  

1. **Baseline CK** – Dramatically elevated in active myositis; in our case >16 000 U/L.  
2. **Weekly in acute phase** – Rapid drop (often >50 % by day 7) is a good sign of treatment response.  
3. **Post‑taper CK** – Levels should remain <4× upper limit of normal; a sudden rise may herald relapse.  
4. **Long‑term** – CK can be normal while ILD progresses; therefore CK alone is insufficient for monitoring.  

**Practical advice:**  
- For type I muscle fiber disease (like dermatomyositis), CK correlates closely with clinical activity.  
- If CK declines but patient continues to have dysphagia or weakness, consider imaging or repeat biopsy.  
- In patients on mycophenolate with prednisone taper, keep CBC and CMP in mind as mycophenolate can mask CK trends.

---

## Pulmonary Function Tests: Early Detection of ILD  

- **Baseline**: Exertional hypoxia and reduced DLCO are ominous. In this case, a restrictive pattern without significant bronchodilator response was noted.  
- **Follow‑up**: Repeat FVC, DLCO every 3–6 months for the first year, then annually if stable.  
- **Abnormal Trends**: FVC ↓ ≥ 10 % or DLCO ↓ ≥ 15 % should prompt high‑dose steroids or escalation to rituximab/IVIg.  
- **Radiological correlation**: PET or high‑resolution CT can pick up subclinical fibrosis before PFTs change.

**Take‑home points**

- Dermatomyositis patients with dysphagia are at higher risk for aspiration‑related lung injury; early PFT surveillance can differentiate ILD from aspiration.  
- A stable or improving PFT trajectory correlates with a better likelihood of long‑term remission.

---

## From “Dysphagia‑Risk” to “Dysphagia‑Free”  

| Milestone | Intervention | Clinical Reasoning |
|-----------|--------------|--------------------|
| **Day 1–7** | High‑dose IV methylprednisolone (1 g × 3 days) | Rapid suppression of immune-mediated muscle inflammation |
| **Day 5–10** | IVIg 2 g/kg over 5 days | Modulates immune complexes, complements membrane attack complex deposition |
| **Week 2** | Initiate mycophenolate 1 g BID | Steroid‑sparing; effective for chronic disease control |
| **Week 4** | First rituximab 1000 mg IV | Target CD20+ B‑cells; persistent high ANA titers appease relapse risk |
| **Month 2** | Repeat swallow study | Objective assessment for safe oral intake |
| **Month 3** | Taper prednisolone to 5 mg daily | Maintain disease remission; reduce steroid‑related morbidity |
| **Month 6–12** | Transition to monthly IVIg (if needed) | Maintain remission in high‑risk anti‑SAE1 patients |
| **Month 12** | Evaluate for reversal of ILD (HRCT, DLCO) | Confirm durability of pulmonary improvement |

---

## Counseling Patients: What to Expect

1. **Initial Severity** – “You may need a feeding tube for a few months; this is common and not a sign of permanent disability.”  
2. **Time to Full Recovery** – “In most anti‑SAE1 cases, CT, CK and PFTs normalize within 6–12 months.”  
3. **Long‑Term Monitoring** – “We’ll do annual CT chest scans for 3 years and repeat GI endoscopies if symptoms arise.”  
4. **Risks of Recurrence** – “A sudden rise in CK or new weakness warrants immediate evaluation.”  
5. **Lifestyle Modifications** – “Maintain a balanced diet, avoid hot or spicy foods that may trigger muscle pain, and adhere to the medication schedule.”

---

## Key Clinical Pearls  

- **Always order CK in patients with unexplained neck swelling or dysphagia; a high CK changes the entire differential.**  
- **Neck pseudo‑angioedema in dermatomyositis is usually muscle‑based, not histamine or bradykinin‑mediated; treat accordingly.**  
- **Combine immunosuppression with IVIg early when dysphagia is severe; delays correlate with longer NG tube dependence.**  
- **Serial PFTs and high‑resolution CT are vital for catching ILD early, especially in anti‑SAE1 disease which has a ~43 % ILD rate.**  
- **A robust multidisciplinary approach (ENT, rheumatology, pulmonology, nutrition) drives optimal outcomes.**  

---

## Bottom Line  

From a terrifying triad of neck swelling, dysphagia and extreme CK elevation to a full resolution at 12 months, this patient’s journey underscores the importance of high‑index suspicion, aggressive early therapy, vigilant monitoring (CK, PFTs, imaging), and patient‑centred counseling. In dermatomyositis, the airway can be compromised, but with timely diagnosis and a structured treatment algorithm, the prognosis is favorable—even in the most severe phenotypes.
