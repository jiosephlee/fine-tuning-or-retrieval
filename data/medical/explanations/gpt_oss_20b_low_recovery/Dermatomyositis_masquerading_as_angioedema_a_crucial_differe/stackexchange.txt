Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Clinical Pearls] Why was a creatine kinase measurement immediately ordered after the neck CT, and what would a markedly elevated CK suggest in this clinical context?

**Answer**

After the neck CT showed diffuse inflammatory edema of the strap muscles but no source of infection, the treating team suspected an atypical “pseudo‑angioedema.” In that setting the first laboratory bedside test that can distinguish an inflammatory myopathy from a histamine‑ or bradykinin‑mediated process is serum creatine kinase (CK).  

- **Why order CK immediately?**  
  • CK is the most sensitive marker of skeletal‑muscle injury.  
  • Dermatomyositis (DM) is an autoimmune myopathy that frequently presents with markedly elevated CK.  
  • In contrast, classic angioedema causes swelling without muscle necrosis, so CK remains normal.  
  • Detecting a high CK level directs the differential away from ENT‑oriented infectious or allergic etiologies and toward rheumatologic evaluation, allowing prompt immunosuppression.

- **What would a markedly elevated CK imply in this patient?**  
  A CK >10 000 U/L, as seen (16 184 U/L), strongly suggests active inflammatory muscle disease. In the context of skin changes, proximal weakness, dysphagia, and the absence of drug or infection triggers, it confirms dermatomyositis (here anti‑SAE1 positive). The elevated CK also correlates with the degree of muscle inflammation (FDG‑PET, MRI) and predicts tissue edema from myositis rather than vascular leakage. Thus, an acutely raised CK shifts management from airway‑monitoring for angioedema to urgent high‑dose steroids, IVIG, and steroid‑sparing therapy for DM.

In short, an immediate CK measurement was a rapid, disease‑specific triage tool that revealed the true etiology—dermatomyositis—allowing targeted therapy and avoiding unnecessary ENT interventions.

### [Pathophysiology] Explain the pathophysiologic mechanism by which anti‑SAE1 antibodies lead to the characteristic perifascicular capillary injury seen in dermatomyositis.

**Pathophysiologic link between anti‑SAE1 antibodies and perifascicular capillary injury in dermatomyositis**

Dermatomyositis is a “microangiopathy” of the muscle—an antibody‑mediated, complement‑activated attack on the small capillaries that supply the endomysium. In the case of anti‑SAE1 positive disease, the pathogenic cascade mirrors that of other myositis‐specific antibodies but is amplified by a “type‑I interferon (IFN)‑rich” environment that is particularly pronounced in this subset.

1. **Antibody binding and immune complex formation**  
   Anti‑SAE1 antibodies recognize the small‑ubiquitin‑like modifier‑activating enzyme 1 (SAE1) protein, which is ectopically expressed or exposed on endothelial cells and myofibers during stress or inflammation. Binding of these IgG molecules to their antigen on the capillary endothelium forms immune complexes that are resistant to clearance.

2. **Complement activation (classical pathway)**  
   The deposited immune complexes are potent activators of the classical complement cascade. In the patient’s muscle biopsy, immunohistochemistry demonstrated deposition of the membrane‑attack complex (C5b‑9) in perifascicular endomysial capillaries—a hallmark of dermatomyositis. Complement activation induces **capillary endothelial swelling, leakage, and ultimately thrombosis**, leading to a focal “capillary dropout” that is most pronounced at muscle periphery (perifascicular zone).

3. **Type‑I IFN‑mediated up‑regulation of endothelial adhesion molecules**  
   Anti‑SAE1 disease is associated with a dramatic interferon‑signature. IFN‑β/α up‑regulates ICAM‑1, VCAM‑1, and MHC‑I on endothelial cells, further attracting CD4⁺/CD8⁺ T cells and NK cells to the capillary bed. These lymphocytes release pro‑inflammatory cytokines (IFN‑γ, TNF‑α) that amplify complement activation and promote apoptosis of myofibers adjacent to the damaged capillaries.

4. **Perifascicular atrophy & necrosis**  
   The loss of blood supply to the outer zones of the muscle fascicle creates a hypoxic environment, precipitating the characteristic **perifascicular atrophy and necrosis** seen on biopsy. The same causal chain underlies the neck swelling in the index patient; the inflammatory edema in the strap muscles results from capillary leakage driven by complement and IFN‑mediated endothelial injury.

**Bottom line:**  
Anti‑SAE1 antibodies initiate an IgG‑mediated, complement‑driven, IFN‑augmented attack on muscle capillaries, leading to C5b‑9 deposition, perifascicular capillary loss, and the classic perifascicular myofiber necrosis that defines dermatomyositis. The biopsy in the described case, with C5b‑9‑positive capillaries and perifascicular degeneration, illustrates this pathophysiologic sequence in action.

### [Differential Diagnosis] List at least three features that differentiate histamine‑mediated angioedema from the pseudo‑angioedema caused by dermatomyositis, and explain why the patient’s presentation was more consistent with the latter.

**Differentiating histamine‑mediated angioedema from dermatomyositis–associated pseudo‑angioedema**

| Feature | Histamine‑mediated angioedema | Dermatomyositis‑associated swelling |
|---------|------------------------------|-------------------------------------|
| **Trigger/History** | Immediate swelling after allergen, medication (e.g., ACE‑I, ibuprofen, foods, insect sting), or spontaneous (hereditary). | Often subacute (weeks) without an identifiable trigger; may coincide with new muscle symptoms or skin rash. |
| **Associated systemic signs** | Usually isolated, no proven muscle injury; eosinophilia is common; normal CK. | Concomitant proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, myalgia, elevated CK, transaminases, and inflammatory markers (CRP). |
| **Cutaneous features** | Non‑itchy, non‑red skin; no rash. | Focal erythema (heliotrope, Gottron’s papules, shawl/holster sign), periungual telangiectasia, nailfold microhaemorrhages, and mild erythema of face or neck. |
| **Complement studies** | Often normal; C4 may be low in C1‑esterase inhibitor deficiency. | C3/C4 normal; C1‑esterase function normal; complement deposition in muscle capillaries (C5b‑9). |
| **Response to antihistamine** | Rapid resolution (minutes–hours). | No effect; swelling persists until immunosuppression is started. |
| **Laboratory markers** | Normal CK. | Markedly elevated CK (starkly >10 000 U/L in this case). |
| **Radiology** | Soft‑tissue swelling only; no muscle inflammation on MRI/PET. | ΔGd‑enhancement or FDG uptake in muscle groups, perifascicular necrosis on biopsy. |

**Why the patient’s presentation was more consistent with dermatomyositis**

1. **Prolonged, subacute course (3 weeks) with progressive neck swelling**, not the rapid, episodic swelling typical of histamine‑mediated angioedema.  
2. **Marked muscle disease**: proximal hip‑flexor weakness, dysphagia on video swallow, elevated CK (≈16 000 U/L), transaminitis, and FDG‑PET/MRI evidence of active myositis. Histamine angioedema would not produce such systemic muscle injury.  
3. **Cutaneous and nailfold findings**: periungual erythema, nailfold microhaemorrhages, and subtle facial erythema (heliotrope‑like) are classic for dermatomyositis and absent in pure histamine angioedema.  
4. **Laboratory evidence of autoimmune myopathy**: strongly positive anti‑SAE1 antibodies, normal complement levels, and muscle biopsy with perifascicular necrosis and C5b‑9 deposition—all hallmark of dermatomyositis.  
5. **Lack of response to antihistamines or steroids alone**: the swelling markedly improved only after high‑dose methylprednisolone and IVIG, not after standard antihistamine therapy typically used for histamine‑mediated angioedema.

In summary, the patient’s prolonged neck swelling coupled with proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, elevated muscle enzymes, specific autoantibodies, and biopsy findings point unequivocally to dermatomyositis‑related pseudo‑angioedema rather than a histamine‑mediated insult. Recognizing these distinguishing features is essential to avoid misdiagnosis and to initiate appropriate immunosuppressive therapy.

### [Management] Given the patient’s abnormal pulmonary function tests and CT findings of ground‑glass opacities, why was methotrexate avoided as a steroid‑sparing agent in this case?

**Why methotrexate was avoided**

Methotrexate is a common steroid‑sparing agent in dermatomyositis, but it carries a well‑documented risk of causing or worsening interstitial lung disease (ILD) and pulmonary fibrosis. In this patient:

1. **Baseline pulmonary dysfunction** – His spirometry showed an extrathoracic restrictive pattern with no bronchodilator response.  
2. **Radiologic evidence of pulmonary involvement** – Chest CT revealed multiple ground‑glass opacities and peribronchial consolidation, findings highly suggestive of early ILD or aspiration‑related changes.  
3. **Absence of prior lung disease** – He had never had smoking or occupational exposures that would predispose him to lung fibrosis, yet the imaging already suggested early alveolar inflammation.

Given that methotrexate’s pulmonary toxicity is dose‑dependent and typically manifests as a hypersensitivity pneumonitis‑type reaction, adding a drug known to elicit such injury in a patient who already has abnormal lung function would almost certainly worsen his respiratory status. The clinicians chose an alternative steroid‑sparing strategy (mycophenolate mofetil, IVIG, rituximab) that has a more favorable pulmonary safety profile while still providing potent immunosuppression.

In short, methotrexate was avoided because the patient’s abnormal pulmonary function tests and CT ground‑glass opacities made him a high‑risk candidate for methotrexate‑induced interstitial lung disease.

### [Morning Report] Describe the rationale for using a combination of high‑dose intravenous methylprednisolone, IVIG, mycophenolate mofetil, and rituximab in the treatment of anti‑SAE1 dermatomyositis with severe neck swelling.

**Morning‑Report Teaching: Why a multimechanistic immunosuppression regimen is used in anti‑SAE1 dermatomyositis with severe neck swelling**

1. **High‑dose IV methylprednisolone (1 g daily)**  
   - *Goal:* Rapid blockade of the innate inflammatory cascade driving myositis and ectopic capillary leak.  
   - *Rationale in this case:* The patient had life‑threatening dysphagia and neck‑space oedema, features that mirror an acute inflammatory flare. 12‑hour or 48‑hour courses of high‑dose steroids are standard first‑line therapy in dermatomyositis because they suppress cytokine production, inhibit complement‑mediated damage (including the C5b‑9 deposition seen on his biopsy), and reduce muscle oedema quickly.

2. **Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) 2 g/kg**  
   - *Goal:* Modulate B‑cell antibody production, inhibit Fc‑dependent complement activation, and provide anti‑idiotype antibodies that neutralise pathogenic autoantibodies (e.g., anti‑SAE1).  
   - *Why in this patient:* The clinical picture resembled non‑histaminergic, non‑bradykinin angioedema; IVIG is effective in cases of immune‑mediated angioedema and in refractory dermatomyositis. The authors intentionally escalated to 2 g/kg and repeated cycles because of the severe soft‑tissue swelling that persisted after steroids.

3. **Mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) 1 g bid**  
   - *Goal:* Suppress T‑ and B‑cell proliferation by inhibiting inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase, thus limiting the ongoing autoimmune process.  
   - *Clinical reasoning:* MMF offers a convenient oral regimen with a favourable adverse‑event profile in patients with pulmonary involvement, as seen here with a restrictive pattern and aspiration‑related nodules. It acts as a steroid‑sparing agent and is particularly useful in patients who cannot tolerate methotrexate (e.g., due to abnormal liver enzymes).

4. **Rituximab (2 × 1000 mg 2 weeks apart)**  
   - *Goal:* CD20‑directed B‑cell depletion, reducing the source of autoantibody (anti‑SAE1) and antigen presentation.  
   - *Why selected:* The patient’s disease was aggressive, with marked muscle involvement and persistent dysphagia. Rituximab has been shown in case series to induce remission in refractory dermatomyositis, especially when B‑cell–driven autoantibodies are implicated. The authors used it early to expedite B‑cell depletion while IVIG and steroids controlled the acute phase.

**Putting the pieces together**  
- **“Treat the airway first.”**  Steroids and IVIG attack the acute inflammatory leak causing neck swelling and dysphagia.  
- **“Stop the engine.”**  MMF and rituximab slow the underlying autoimmune cascade, preventing relapse when steroids are tapered.  
- **“Avoid pulmonary toxicity.”**  Methotrexate and azathioprine were avoided due to the patient’s pre‑existing restrictive lung pattern and the known risk of drug‑induced interstitial lung disease.

The combination achieves rapid symptom control and long‑term remission, as demonstrated by the patient’s complete recovery of neck swelling, normalization of CK, and eventual steroid discontinuation at 7 months. This multimodal approach is consistent with current practice guidelines for severe, refractory dermatomyositis and reflects the heterogeneous immunopathology of anti‑SAE1 disease.

### [Management] Which malignancy‑screening strategy was employed for this patient, and how does the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) guideline classify his risk profile?

**Malignancy‑screening strategy employed**

The patient underwent a *comprehensive, multimodal baseline work‑up* at the time of diagnosis, in line with the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) guideline for idiopathic inflammatory myopathy‑associated cancer screening:

| Modality | Purpose |
|----------|---------|
| **Whole‑body contrast‑enhanced CT** (chest, abdomen, pelvis) | Detect occult tumours in common sites (lung, breast, prostate, ovary, GI tract). |
| **18F‑FDG PET/CT** | Assess for metabolic activity suggestive of malignancy in any region, including lymph nodes or unusual sites. |
| **Serum tumour markers** (CEA, CA‑19‑9, CA‑125, PSA) | Provide adjunctive evidence of potential malignancy, especially GI and genitourinary cancers. |
| **Upper and lower gastrointestinal endoscopy** | Direct visual inspection and biopsy of oesophageal, gastric, colonic mucosa; important in dermatomyositis where colorectal cancer risk is elevated. |
| **Clinical examination and symptom monitoring** | Ongoing surveillance for weight loss, night sweats, fevers, or other paraneoplastic clues. |

All imaging was negative for neoplasia; tumour markers were within normal limits, and endoscopic evaluations were planned but not yet completed at the time of the case report.

**Application to this patient**

*High‑risk*

### [Pathophysiology] What is the significance of peripheral capillary hypoxemia findings on capillaroscopy in patients with dermatomyositis, and how did it complement the clinical picture in this case?

**Peripheral capillary hypoxemia on nail‑fold capillaroscopy in dermatomyositis**

1. **What the finding means**  
   Nail‑fold capillaroscopy in dermatomyositis characteristically shows *avascular areas* and *micro‑haemorrhages*, reflecting endothelial damage and capillary dropout secondary to complement‑mediated micro‑angiopathy. The “peripheral capillary hypoxemia” described in the case (avascular zones adjacent to abnormal capillaries) is a morphological correlate of this capillary loss and of impaired oxygen delivery to the skin and underlying muscle.

2. **Clinical significance**  
   - **Diagnostic clue**: In a patient who presents with neck swelling that mimics angioedema, the presence of peri‑ungual telangiectasia, micro‑haemorrhages and avascular zones strongly suggests a vasculopathic process such as dermatomyositis.  
   - **Disease activity marker**: The degree of capillary loss parallels muscle inflammation; in this case the capillaroscopy findings were concordant with the markedly raised CK (16 184 U/L) and MRI/PET evidence of active myositis.  
   - **Prognostic value**: Extensive capillary dropout has been associated with more severe disease and poorer response to first‑line therapy. Recognizing it early prompted aggressive immunosuppression (high‑dose steroids, IVIG, mycophenolate, rituximab) in this patient.

3. **How it complemented the clinical picture**  
   - The patient’s neck oedema could have been mistaken for histamine‑mediated angioedema.  
   - Nail‑fold capillaroscopy uncovered para‑ungual erythema and avascular areas—findings that are virtually absent in simple angioedema but are typical of dermatomyositis.  
   - These capillaroscopic abnormalities, together with the skin rash (heliotrope‑like facial erythema), proximal weakness, dysphagia, and laboratory evidence (anti‑SAE1 antibodies, elevated CK), confirmed the diagnosis of anti‑SAE1 dermatomyositis rather than a primary airway disorder.  
   - The capillaroscopy thus served as a rapid, non‑invasive adjunct that reinforced the need for early, intensive immunosuppression and for malignancy screening.

**Bottom line:** Peripheral capillary hypoxemia on nail‑fold capillaroscopy is a hallmark of dermatomyositis micro‑angiopathy; in this case it provided essential diagnostic confirmation and guided the decision for aggressive treatment, ultimately leading to full resolution of the atypical neck oedema and recovery of muscle function.

### [Clinical Pearls] Imagine the patient had developed a severe haemolytic anemia during trimethoprim‑sulphamethoxazole prophylaxis. Explain the likely mechanism and justify the switch to nebulised pentamidine.

**Mechanism of trimethoprim‑sulfamethoxazole‑induced haemolytic anemia and rationale for switching to nebulised pentamidine**

Trimethoprim‑sulfamethoxazole (TMP‑SMX) can trigger haemolysis through **drug‑induced oxidative stress** that leads to the formation of reactive metabolites. In patients without glucose‑6‑phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency, the oxidant injury is mediated by altered red‑cell membrane integrity and generation of reactive oxygen species that damage hemoglobin. The resulting intravascular haemolysis presents as a rapidly falling hemoglobin, elevated lactate dehydrogenase, indirect hyperbilirubinaemia and a peripheral smear with schistocytes or bite cells. In the reported case, the patient developed severe haemolytic anemia while on TMP‑SMX for Pneumocystis jirovecii prophylaxis, yet his G6PD level was normal, confirming an **oxidative‑metabolite–mediated** process rather than an enzymatic defect.

**Why nebulised pentamidine?**

Pentamidine is an antiprotozoal agent that, when given intravenously, carries a high risk of systemic side effects (nephrotoxicity, hypotension, hypoglycaemia). Nebulised pentamidine delivers the drug directly to the alveoli, achieving high pulmonary concentrations with minimal systemic absorption. Key advantages in this context:

1. **Avoidance of further red‑cell oxidative injury:** Nebulisation bypasses the hepatic‑metabolic pathway that generates the oxidative metabolites responsible for haemolysis with TMP‑SMX.
2. **Efficacy against Pneumocystis jirovecii:** Nebulised pentamidine retains full anti‑Pneumocystis activity while maintaining a better safety profile in patients who cannot tolerate TMP‑SMX.
3. **Reduced systemic toxicity:** The drug remains largely confined to the respiratory tract, mitigating risks of nephrotoxicity, blood‑pressure lability and hypoglycaemia that would be unacceptable in a patient already haemodynamically compromised by haemolysis.

Thus, the switch to nebulised pentamidine in the case report was a clinically sound decision to continue Pneumocystis prophylaxis while preventing re‑occurrence of haemolytic anemia.

### [Morning Report] Discuss the potential reasons for the patient’s initial misdiagnosis as Ludwig’s angina and how early recognition of dermatomyositis could alter emergency department management.

**Key Learning Points**

1. **Why Ludwig’s angina was initially favored**
   - *Classic red flag*: **severe, progressive neck swelling** with a CT showing diffuse strap‑muscle oedema and retropharyngeal involvement.
   - *Demographics*: a previously well adult male with no known infection risk factors.
   - *Overlap of signs*: neck oedema, mild fever‑like inflammatory markers (CRP 55 mg/L, WCC 9 × 10⁹/L), and no obvious skin findings early on.
   - *Absence of obvious muscle weakness*: dysphagia and proximal weakness were subtle at presentation, leading clinicians to dismiss a myopathic process.

2. **How dermatomyositis can masquerade as angioedema**
   - **Non‑histaminergic/ non‑bradykinin oedema** from cytokine‑mediated vascular leakage secondary to inflammatory myositis.
   - **Epidemiology**: only ~6–8 % of dermatomyositis patients develop pseudoweedting; neck swellings are even rarer (<1 %).
   - **Clinical clues missed**: subtle peri‑ungual erythema/ microhaemorrhages, mild facial erythema, and early skin changes (e.g., a positive Holster sign) that were not the classic heliotrope or Gottron papules at presentation.

3. **Early recognition would have altered ED management**
   - **Prompt CK and muscle enzyme panel**: CK 16 184 U/L would have shifted suspicion toward a myopathy rather than an infection.
   - **Avoidance of unnecessary antibiotics/ imaging**: Once myopathy is suspected, CT neck may be de‑emphasised, and a muscle biopsy or MRI/MIBI‑PET could be targeted sooner.
   - **Early high‑dose steroids**: Initiating methylprednisolone within 24 h could prevent progression of muscle inflammation, airway compromise, and dysphagia.
   - **Avoidance of angioedema therapies**: Avoid unnecessary epinephrine, antihistamines, or C1‑esterase inhibitors, which are ineffective and could delay appropriate treatment.

4. **Take‑home teaching for the ED**
   - **Screen for myositis in any acute neck swelling** that is:
     - **Non‑responding to antibiotics**,
     - **Lacks classic infectious signs** (fever, purulent discharge),
     - **Has associated proximal muscle weakness or dysphagia**.
   - **Order CK, AST/ALT, LDH, and ANA** early, even if cutaneous signs are subtle.
   - **Do a thorough skin and nail exam**: peri‑ungual telangiectasias, microhaemorrhages, and the “Holster” or “shawl” signs can be first clues.
   - **Consider a rapid muscle biopsy** if imaging suggests myositis (FDG‑PET or MRI showing diffuse uptake) and CK is markedly elevated.

5. **Bottom line** – Dermatomyositis can present with pseudo‑angioedema of the neck; early recognition by a systematic skin/muscle work‑up changes the therapeutic trajectory from broad antibiotics/Biopsy to aggressive immunosuppression, ultimately improving airway safety, decreasing ICU admissions, and accelerating recovery.

### [Differential Diagnosis] What are the key histologic features on muscle biopsy that help distinguish dermatomyositis from other inflammatory myopathies such as necrotising myopathy or inclusion body myositis?

**Key histologic features that distinguish dermatomyositis from other inflammatory myopathies**

| Feature | Dermatomyositis | Immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy | Inclusion body myositis |
|---------|-----------------|--------------------------------------|--------------------------|
| **Pattern of necrosis / fiber degeneration** | Distinct **perifascicular atrophy** with necrotic/fibrinous fibers concentrated at the edge of the fascicle; scattered necrotic and regenerative fibers throughout the muscle. | Predominantly scattered necrotic fibers with minimal or absent surrounding inflammation; absence of a clear perifascicular pattern. | Patchy necrosis and regeneration, often in a late‑stage "intercytoplasmic inclusions" appearance; no perifascicular distribution. |
| **Inflammatory infiltrate** | **Perimysial or endomysial infiltration** of primarily **histiocytes and CD4⁺ T cells** with **ballooning degeneration**; **intact muscle fibers** between inflammatory foci. | **Minimal or absent inflammatory infiltrate**; necrotic fibers surrounded by few or no inflammatory cells. | **Sparse, variable infiltrate** (often CD8⁺ T cells) but frequently accompanied by **eosinophilic cytoplasmic inclusions** (TDP‑43, p62). |
| **Complement deposition** | **C5b‑9 (membrane attack complex) deposition** on capillary walls and in endomysial capillaries, especially at the perifascicular zone – a hallmark of the immune‑mediated microangiopathy of dermatomyositis. | No complement deposition; necrosis is immune‑mediated but not complement‑driven. | No or minimal complement deposition; complement is not a key pathogenic mechanism. |
| **Regeneration** | **Regenerative fibers** of various sizes with glycogen accumulation and a characteristic “scurly” appearance, often counter‑balanced by small, “cenobacterial” nodes of re‑branching fibers. | Few regenerative fibers; necrosis predominates. | Regenerative fibers present but often mixed with amyloid or inclusion cores. |
| **Electron microscopy / inclusions** | **No cytoplasmic inclusions**; ultrastructure reveals capillary thrombi and thickened basal lamina. | No inclusions. | Classical intracytoplasmic inclusions (rimmed vacuoles, rimmed cytoplasmic bodies) and amyloid‑like deposits. |
| **Immunohistochemical stains** | **CD4⁺ T cells, histiocytes, B‑cell aggregates**; C5b‑9 positive capillaries; **MHC‑I upregulation** on non‑necrotic fibers. | Variable up‑regulation of MHC‑I but no complement. | Strong MHC‑I expression, but immunostaining for p62, LC3, and ubiquitin highlights inclusions. |

**Application to the case report**

The patient’s thigh biopsy showed “foci of moderate numbers of necrotic, vacuolated degenerate, myophagocytosed and regenerative myofibres in a *perifascicular distribution* and scattered perimysial inflammation consisting of dispersed histiocytes” (Figure 4). Importantly, **C5b‑9 deposition in perifascicular endomysial capillaries** was documented, a classic dermatomyositis feature. The combination of **perifascicular necrosis, perimysial histiocytic infiltrate, and complement‑mediated capillaritis** clinches the diagnosis and excludes necrotising myopathy (absent complement deposition, lack of perifascicular pattern) and inclusion body myositis (absence of intracytoplasmic inclusions and amyloid). 

Thus, the hallmark biochemical and histopathological clues—**perifascicular necrosis, perimysial inflammatory infiltrate, and complement‑mediated capillary injury**—are what separate dermatomyositis from other inflammatory myopathies on muscle biopsy.
