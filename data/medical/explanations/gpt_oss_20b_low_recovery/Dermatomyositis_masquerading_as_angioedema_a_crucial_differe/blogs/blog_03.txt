Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# Muscle‑Targeted Swelling: How Dermatomyositis Can Produce True “Pseudo‑Angioedema”

## The moment that throws everyone off the track

Picture this: a man in his 40s rolls into the emergency department complaining of a three‑week, “puffy neck” that has expanded around his jaw and down his face. A CT scan shows diffuse edema of the strap muscles and a slight retropharyngeal infiltrate, but no abscess, abscess‑disposition, or obvious infectious focus. The patient is afebrile, CRP’s up at 56 mg/L, and his only other complaint is a vague “tightness” in his chest. The ENT officer in the room mentions the classic Ludwig’s angina possibility, pulls a neck‑pooling jug, and orders antibiotics. A few hours later, a rheumatologist runs a creatine kinase. The result? 16,000 U/L. Suddenly—yes—the story shifts from an infectious deep‑neck space problem to a diagnosis that was lurking in the shadows: dermatomyositis.

Dermatomyositis presenting as neck swelling is a *real* podshot of the disease, and it demands that we think beyond “histamine + bradykinin” in the context of angioedema. Let’s dive into: how it happens, how you spot it on imaging, and why timing your steroid pulse could be lifesaving.

---

## Pathophysiology: Muscle‑driven Edema vs. Classic Angioedema

| Feature | Classic Histamine‑/Bradykinin‑mediated Angioedema | Dermatomyositis‑related “Pseudo‑Angioedema” |
|---------|----------------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------|
| **Mediators** | Histamine, tryptase, bradykinin | Complement activation (C5b‑9 MASP‑2), local cytotoxic inflammation |
| **Target** | Sub‐epidermal vessels, superficial soft tissue | Perifascicular muscle fibres, endomysial capillaries |
| **Onset** | Minutes to hours | Days to weeks (sub‑acute) |
| **Associated labs** | Normal C3/C4, normal CK | Markedly ↑ CK (often >10× ULN), ↑ LDH, ↑ transaminases |
| **Dermatologic clues** | Usually none | Gottron’s papules, heliotrope rash, periungual telangiectasia, nailfold micro‑haemorrhages |
| **Edema distribution** | Superficial, non‑tender, often localized | Diffuse, involves muscles, may be painful or tender, can track along fascia |

In dermatomyositis, the immune system attacks the endomysial capillary network particularly around the non‑contractile zones of muscle fibres. Because these vessels lie within the fascia that encloses the neck musculature, the local inflammatory cascade leads to capillary leakage and fluid accumulation **inside the muscle itself**, not the subcutaneous layer. That’s why you see “soft‑tissue swelling” that behaves like angioedema but actually tracks along the muscle, venturing into the retropharyngeal space and even impairing airway.

## Imaging hallmarks

### 1. CT Neck

- **Findings**: Fat stranding, diffuse swelling of the strap muscles, involvement of the retropharyngeal space.
- **Interpretive clue**: If the swelling “follows” the muscle planes rather than the superficial skin, consider myositis.
- **Tip**: A contrast‑enhanced scan may reveal linear enhancement along the fascia – more typical of active inflammation than an abscess.

### 2. MRI of the Neck / Thighs

- **T2/FLAIR hyperintensity** in the strap muscles, obturator internus/externus, adductors, and psoas – the classic “red‑blood‑sugar” signal of active edema/inflammation.
- **Short Tau Inversion Recovery (STIR)** sequences highlight edema‑rich tissue.
- The hallmark is the *perifascicular* pattern – the outer zones are more affected than the central muscle fibres.

### 3. PET‑FDG

- Diffuse FDG uptake in *multiple* muscle groups (head & neck, upper limbs, trunk).
- In dermatomyositis, you’ll see the “pink‑heart sign” – a diffuse, nearly homogeneous uptake in the muscles.
- **Clinical utility**: Rules out occult lymphoma or metastasis (which often have focal uptake) and confirms systemic muscle involvement.

**Bottom line:** If imaging shows diffuse, symmetrical muscle hyperintensity or FDG uptake, you’re not looking at cellulitis or a strict angioedema; you’re probably looking at myositis. That changes management from antibiotics to immunosuppression.

---

## Airway & Dysphagia: A Dangerous Combo

The neck swelling can encroach on the airway. In this case, the patient had *retropharyngeal* edema and a barium swallow demonstrating severe pharyngeal dysphagia with post‑swallow penetration. Why is that pivotal?

1. **Airway protective reflexes** get blunted when muscles controlling the pharynx are inflamed. 
2. Compromise of the hyoid and suprahyoid muscles can obstruct the upper airway, precipitating hypoxia.
3. Dysphagia is a red flag for *edematous* involvement of pharyngeal flexor muscles, indicating that early and aggressive immunosuppression may be required to preserve airway.

### What to do?

- **Early airway assessment**: laryngoscopy or fiber‑optic assessment if there is any suspicion of airway compromise, even if the patient is not tachypnoeic.
- **Nasogastric feeding** may be necessary until swallowing improves. 
- **Avoid sedatives** that could further depress respiratory drive.

## Urgent Steroid Pulse vs. Other Immunosuppressives

| Therapy | Rationale | Typical Timing | Front‑line? |
|---------|-----------|----------------|-------------|
| **IV methylprednisolone 1 g × 3 days** | Rapid blockade of cytokine surge, complement inhibition | Immediately on suspicion of severe muscle inflammation | Yes – first line for life‑threatening presentations |
| **IVIG (2 g/kg)** | Modulates Fc receptors, reduces autoantibody activity | Usually a few days after steroid pulse | Second line or adjunct in refractory cases |
| **Mycophenolate mofetil (1 g BID)** | T‑cell proliferation inhibitor; oral, long‑term maintenance | After initial induction | Long‑term maintenance |
| **Rituximab (1 g × 2)** | B‑cell depletion; useful in refractory or severe disease | Weeks after initiation of steroids | Add‑on for severe or refractory disease |
| **Low‑dose colchicine** | For complement‑driven edema? | Experimental | Still experimental |

The patient in the case we’re discussing received a classic 3‑day IV methylprednisolone pulse, followed by a rapid taper, then a rotating regimen of IVIG, mycophenolate, and rituximab. The overall strategy is two‑fold: one, seize the very window when inflammation is most intense; two, maintain long‑term immunosuppression to prevent relapses and airway compromise.

**Clinical pearl:** Do *not* wait for the CK to normalize before initiating steroids—guidelines advise treatment at the *onset* of significant muscle protein damage.

---

## Take‑away Checklist

- **Start early CK screening** in any patient with unexplained neck swelling that tracks along facial/neck muscles.
- **Look for subtle dermatomyositis clues**: periungual telangiectasias, nailfold micro‑haemorrhages, early skin changes even if more classic lesions are absent.
- **Check Complement Levels** – normal C3/C4 does *not* rule out dermatomyositis‑related edema.
- **Order CT neck, MRI of the involved area, and PET‑FDG** if clinical suspicion is high; look for symmetry and perifascicular pattern.
- **Airway assessment** (laryngoscopy) is essential if dysphagia or respiratory symptoms are present.
- **Initiate IV methylprednisolone 1 g × 3 days** within the first 24 h of diagnosis for severe presentations.
- **Adjunctive therapy**: IVIG and B‑cell depletion (rituximab) may be added in refractory or organ‑at‑risk cases.
- **Post‑acute monitoring**: CK should trend down within days; repeat imaging at 3–6 weeks to confirm resolution of edema.

---

## Closing thought

Dermatomyositis can masquerade as an angioedema crisis, and the earlier the diagnosis, the better the outcomes—especially when airway compromise looms. The key difference lies not in the skin rash (which may be subtle or delayed) but in the *location and nature* of the edema: muscle‑infiltrating, complement‑driven, and amenable to high‑dose steroids. Recognizing the unique imaging signature and rapidly initiating immunosuppression can turn a life‑threatening neck edema into a story of recovery, just like the man in his 40s who went from a puffy neck to a full‑rescue within months.
