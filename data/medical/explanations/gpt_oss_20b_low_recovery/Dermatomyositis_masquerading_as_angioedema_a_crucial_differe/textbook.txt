Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

## 1. Clinical Presentation and Initial Assessment

The patient, a previously healthy male in his 40s, arrived at the emergency department with a **three‑week history of progressive neck swelling** accompanied by mild proximal myalgia and weakness of the hip flexors. He denied any recent infections, trauma, or new medications that might trigger angioedema. The absence of fever, dysphagia at presentation, and a lack of significant weight loss or constitutional symptoms suggested a non‑infectious process, but the rapid and symmetrical swelling of the cervical strap muscles raised immediate concern for a deep neck space infection, particularly Ludwig’s angina.

### History

On detailed questioning, the patient reported:

- **Gradual, painless swelling of the neck** expanding over the anterior and lateral aspects, with no obvious erythema or warmth.
- **Mild proximal muscle aches** predominantly in the lower limbs, described as a “sore feeling” rather than frank weakness.
- **No dysphagia, odynophagia, or voice changes** at the time of presentation.
- **No known allergies** or history of angiotensin‑converting‑enzyme inhibitor or non‑steroidal anti‑inflammatory drug use.
- **No history of febrile illness** or recent dental procedures that could precipitate a neck infection.

The patient’s past medical history was remarkable only for a lifelong non‑smoking status. He had not been on immunosuppressive therapy or biologics. The early timeline emphasized a subacute, non‑febrile illness with the hallmark of neck edema—an atypical domain for dermatomyositis.

### Physical Examination

A focused neck examination revealed:

- **Diffuse, soft tissue edema** of the anterior neck, involving strap muscles and extending to the retropharyngeal space. The swelling was firm, non‑tender, and not associated with overlying skin changes or ulceration.
- **Peri‑angular facial erythema** that appeared subtle but was noted to involve the nasolabial folds. The face was not markedly flushed or violaceous.
- **Nailfold capillaroscopy** demonstrated a mixture of micro‑haemorrhages and avascular areas, suggestive of peri‑ungual inflammation.
- **Musculoskeletal assessment** identified 4/5 strength in right and left hip flexors, with a mild decrement in the ability to perform rapid “hip flexor drills.” No distal weakness or wasting was evident.
- **Dermatological inspection** did not initially reveal classic Gottron’s papules, shawl sign, or heliotrope rash, which can sometimes be subtle or absent in early disease.

The ENT examination via flexible nasendoscopy was unremarkable for structural lesions but showed increased secretions and reflex coughing, raising concerns about aspiration potential. The neck swelling’s distribution and firmness, coupled with the lack of systemic infection signs, suggested a non‑infectious inflammatory process.

### Differential Diagnosis at Triage

Given the clinical picture, the primary differentials included:

1. **Ludwig’s angina**: A fulminant deep neck infection commonly presenting with fever, trismus, and odynophagia; however, the patient was afebrile and lacked trismus.
2. **Idiopathic angioedema**: Histamine‑mediated or bradykinin‑mediated swelling of the soft tissues. The absence of urticaria, positive family history, or medication triggers made this less likely.
3. **Deep neck space infection** (cellulitis, sialadenitis, abscess): Typically associated with fever, leukocytosis, and elevated inflammatory markers; the patient’s CRP was mildly elevated (55.6 mg/L) but WCC was normal.
4. **Autoimmune myopathy**: Dermatomyositis and polymyositis can present with proximal muscle weakness and systemic inflammation; subclinical skin findings may initially be absent.
5. **Other causes**: Cephalhematoma, cellulitis from insect or bite, necrotizing fasciitis (unlikely without severe systemic signs).

The early **CT scan of the neck** confirmed diffuse neck edema involving strap muscles and extension to the retropharyngeal space but did not identify a discrete abscess or pharyngeal perforation. The imaging findings reinforced the likelihood of a diffuse inflammatory process rather than focal infection.

### Early Laboratory Investigations

Initial blood tests provided critical clues:

- **Complete blood count**: Normal WCC (9.1 × 10^9/L) with no neutrophilia.
- **C-reactive protein**: Moderately elevated at 55.6 mg/L, indicating systemic inflammation but not necessarily infectious.
- **Serum creatine kinase (CK)**: Pending at the initial presentation; the subsequent dramatic rise to 16 184 U/L (normal <200 U/L) strongly suggested a muscular injury/necrosis.
- **Transaminases**: Elevated ALT (143 U/L) and AST (396 U/L), with a notable rise in LDH (692 U/L), supporting muscle breakdown.
- **Complement levels (C3, C4)**, **C1 esterase**: Normal, making hereditary angioedema less likely.

The combination of **neck edema, elevated CK, and transaminitis** from the outset tipped the balance toward an inflammatory myopathy rather than an infectious or histaminergic angioedema process. The presence of peri‑ungual erythema on capillaroscopy further pointed toward a systemic connective‑tissue disorder.

### Red Flags Prompting Re‑evaluation

1. **Lack of fever or systemic signs** in the setting of deep neck swelling—unexpected for Ludwig’s angina.
2. **Normal leukocyte count** despite significant neck inflammation.
3. **Absence of classic skin manifestations** initially, yet subtle peri‑ungual changes.
4. **Extreme CK elevation** (over 80× normal) in a patient without obvious trauma.

These red flags prompted early involvement of the immunology team and expedited the measurement of CK and myositis‑specific autoantibodies. The resultant diagnosis of **anti‑SAE1 antibody–positive dermatomyositis** reframed the entire clinical picture, explaining both the neck edema (as a manifestation of submucosal myositis) and the proximal muscle weakness.

--- 

In summary, the initial presentation of progressive neck swelling in a patient with mild proximal myalgia and normal systemic inflammatory markers, combined with subtle peri‑ungual changes, should prompt clinicians—especially in emergency and ENT settings—to consider dermatomyositis in the differential. A systematic bedside examination focused on skin, nailfold capillaries, and muscle strength, paired with early CK measurement and targeted imaging, can expedite correct diagnosis and avert potentially disordered treatment strategies for presumed angioedema or infection.

## 2. Diagnostic Reasoning in Dermatomyositis

Diagnosing dermatomyositis, particularly the anti‑SAE1 antibody subset, requires a systematic approach that integrates the clinical picture with biochemical, serological, imaging and histopathologic data. The case of the 40‑year‑old male with neck swelling demonstrates how a seemingly unrelated presentation can be unraveled by keeping the red flag for an inflammatory myopathy in mind.

### Key clinical red flags for inflammatory myopathy
In the emergency setting, progressive proximal muscle weakness that is out of proportion to exertion, dysphagia, or a combination of muscle pain and systemic symptoms should prompt consideration of an inflammatory myopathy. Neck swelling, especially when it is bilateral, non‑tender, and associated with inflammatory markers, is an atypical but notable red flag, given the reported 6–8 % incidence of pseudo‑angioedema in dermatomyositis. Absence of fever, normal white cell count, and a lack of focal infectious signs further point toward a non‑infectious inflammatory process.

### Role of creatine kinase and inflammatory markers
Elevated serum creatine kinase (CK) is a hallmark of active muscle injury. In this patient, CK rose to 16,184 U/L, far above the normal upper limit, confirming significant myofiber damage. While the acute‑phase reactants, such as C‑reactive protein (CRP), were markedly elevated (55.6 mg/L), they lack specificity for myositis but support an inflammatory milieu. Normal renal function and absent blood myoglobin ruled out rhabdomyolysis with systemic consequences.

### Autoantibody testing: ANA, myositis panel, SAE1 specificity
A high‑titer anti‑nuclear antibody (ANA) with a speckled pattern is common in dermatomyositis but is nonspecific. The Euroimmun Euroline immunoassay, interrogating 16 myositis‑associated antigens, revealed a strongly positive anti‑SAE1 antibody (signal intensity 173 vs <35 normal). Anti‑SAE1 antibodies are pathogenic against the small ubiquitin‑like modifier activating enzyme, a protein involved in post‑translational modification by sumoylation. No other myositis‑specific antibodies (anti‑Mi‑2α, anti‑TIF1‑γ, anti‑Jo‑1, anti‑OJ) were detected, further narrowing the diagnosis to anti‑SAE1 dermatomyositis. Concurrently, the patient’s complement profile (C3, C4, C1 esterase activity) and C1q were within normal ranges, effectively excluding complement‑driven hereditary or acquired angioedema.

### Complement and angioedema work‑up (C1 esterase, C3/C4)
Idiopathic angioedema typically presents with episodic histamine‑ or bradykinin‑mediated swelling. Laboratory evaluation often shows reduced C1 esterase inhibitor activity or low C4 in hereditary angioedema. In this case, the normal C1 esterase activity and normal C4 supported the exclusion of a classic angioedema pathway, directing the diagnostic focus toward a different mechanism of edema—namely, inflammatory myositis‑related muscle swelling.

### Muscle imaging: MRI, FDG‑PET patterns
Muscle MRI is the imaging modality of choice for detecting active inflammation. T2‑weighted short tau inversion recovery (STIR) sequences revealed high signal intensity in the bilateral obturator internus and externus, with concomitant involvement of adductors, gracilis, and iliopsoas—findings typical of dermatomyositis and indicative of active myositis. FDG‑PET imaging corroborated these findings by showing diffuse, metabolically active uptake in multiple muscle groups, most prominently in the head and neck, deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdomen, and pelvis. This global hyper‑FDG pattern is characteristic of inflammatory myopathy and helps rule out focal infectious or neoplastic pathologies.

### Muscle biopsy findings: perifascicular necrosis, MHC‑Ia, C5b‑9 deposition
Histopathology remains the gold standard for confirming dermatomyositis. The patient's thigh biopsy exhibited classic features: necrotic, vacuolated, and regenerating myofibres arranged in a perifascicular pattern, with scattered perimysial infiltration by histiocytes. Immunohistochemistry for the membrane attack complex (C5b‑9) demonstrated deposition in perifascicular capillaries—an attribute almost pathognomonic for dermatomyositis, reflecting complement‑mediated microvascular injury. The presence of upregulated MHC‑I on muscle fibers (though not explicitly described in the report, it is commonly seen) further supports an immune‑mediated process.

### Correlating skin manifestations (Holster sign, periungual erythema) with systemic disease
Dermatomyositis is a multisystem disease, and cutaneous signs often herald systemic involvement. In this patient, subtle skin findings, such as the Holster sign (a V‑shaped erythema over the hips) and periungual erythema with capillary changes seen on nailfold capillaroscopy, were pivotal clues. These cutaneous manifestations correlate with underlying muscle inflammation and are essential in tying together the clinical, serologic, and histologic data. Notably, classic features like Gottron’s papules or heliotrope rash were absent, underscoring the variability of skin findings and the need for a high index of suspicion.

---

**Diagnostic pathway summary:**  
1. **Clinical suspicion** triggered by progressive proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, unexplained neck swelling, and elevated inflammatory markers.  
2. **Laboratory assessment** revealing extremely high CK and a positive ANA.  
3. **Autoantibody panel** identifying strong anti‑SAE1 positivity, with normal complement work‑up to exclude angioedema.  
4. **Imaging** (MRI and FDG‑PET) confirming widespread muscle inflammation.  
5. **Confirmatory muscle biopsy** showing perifascicular necrosis and C5b‑9 deposition.  
6. **Skin examination** supporting systemic disease (Holster sign, periungual changes).  

This stepwise integration of multidisciplinary data led to a definitive diagnosis of anti‑SAE1 antibody–positive dermatomyositis, guiding the subsequent aggressive immunosuppressive therapy that resulted in clinical remission.

## 3. Management Strategies and Immunosuppressive Planning

Dermatomyositis that presents with significant muscle inflammation, dysphagia and airway compromise demands a rapid, well‑coordinated approach that balances prompt disease control against the risks of potent immunosuppression in a patient with existing respiratory compromise. The therapeutic plan in this case evolved through a tiered escalation: immediate high‑dose corticosteroids, followed by a combination of steroid‑sparing immunosuppressants (mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab) and adjunctive intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). Each step was chosen to target the underlying autoimmune inflammation while mitigating the potential for drug‑related harm, particularly in a patient with evidence of interstitial lung involvement and ongoing dysphagia.

### High‑dose IV methylprednisolone and taper schedule

The initial 3‑day course of 1 000 mg IV methylprednisolone is the standard induction regimen for severe dermatomyositis with compromised airway function. High‑dose pulses rapidly suppress cytokine production, limit complement‑mediated endothelial injury (as evidenced by C5b‑9 deposition on biopsy), and reduce muscle oedema, thereby improving swallowing mechanics. Following the pulse, a conventional prednisone taper was instituted, starting at 60 mg daily with a 5‑mg reduction every 2–3 weeks. This schedule balances the need for sustained immunosuppression to prevent rebound inflammation against the cumulative glucocorticoid burden that could exacerbate pulmonary function or precipitate steroid‑related complications (e.g., hyperglycaemia, osteoporosis). The taper interval was adapted to the patient’s biochemical response (CK normalization) and clinical improvement (strength, edema resolution).

### Adjunctive immunosuppressants: mycophenolate mofetil, rituximab, IVIG

The complexity of the patient’s disease (moderate–severe disease activity, dysphagia, and pulmonary findings) warranted early introduction of steroid‑sparing agents.  

- **Mycophenolate mofetil (MMF)** was chosen for its oral administration and established efficacy in dermatomyositis; it inhibits lymphocyte proliferation by blocking de novo guanosine nucleotide synthesis. MMF was initiated at 1 g twice daily, the typical therapeutic dose for myositis, and tolerated well. Its safety profile is favourable in interstitial lung disease because it is not associated with fibrosis.

- **Rituximab**, a CD20‑directed monoclonal antibody that depletes B cells, was given in two 1 000 mg IV infusions 2 weeks apart. Rituximab addresses B‑cell–mediated autoantibody production (anti‑SAE1) and has shown benefit in refractory dermatomyositis, particularly when other immunosuppressants are insufficient.

- **IVIG** (2 g/kg over 5 days) was administered early (within the first week of hospitalization) to provide anti‑inflammatory effects through Fc receptor blockade, neutralization of pathogenic antibodies, and modulation of complement activation. The patient received five additional 2 g/kg cycles at 4‑week intervals, a regimen commonly adopted in severe autoimmunity to maintain remission. IVIG has the advantage of a relatively benign adverse event profile, making it suitable for patients with concurrent infections or organ dysfunction.

The combined use of MMF, rituximab, and IVIG allows for lower cumulative steroid exposure, potentially shortening the timeframe for taper and reducing long‑term complications.

### Avoiding methotrexate in context of lung disease

Methotrexate is a conventional immunosuppressant for dermatomyositis but is a known inciting agent for drug‑induced interstitial lung disease (ILD). Given the patient’s pre‑existing restrictive pattern on pulmonary function testing and CT evidence of ground‑glass opacities (likely aspiration pneumonitis rather than drug toxicity), a decision was made to refrain from methotrexate. Alternative agents (MMF, rituximab) provided effective disease control without the risk of exacerbating pulmonary pathology.

### Pneumocystis prophylaxis options and management of haemolytic anaemia

High‑dose glucocorticoids (>20 mg/day prednisone equivalent) for ≥4 weeks combined with other immunosuppressants confer a heightened risk of Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP). The initial strategy employed sulfamethoxazole‑trimethoprim (SMX‑TMP), the standard prophylaxis. However, the patient developed haemolytic anaemia (without G6PD deficiency), prompting discontinuation. The alternative, nebulised pentamidine 300 mg every 4 weeks, was chosen owing to its efficacy in preventing PJP and its negligible systemic absorption, thereby limiting systemic toxicity in a patient with liver enzyme elevations. Prophylaxis was maintained throughout the first year of disease control.

### Monitoring for immunosuppressant toxicity and drug interactions

- **Renal and hepatic function**: MMF and rituximab can induce transaminase elevation; routine monitoring every 2–4 weeks was instituted. Renal function guides MMF dosing and vigilance for nephrotoxicity.

- **Hematologic parameters**: Absolute neutrophil count (ANC), platelets, and hemoglobin were checked monthly to detect cytopenias from MMF or rituximab.

- **Infection surveillance**: Regular assessment for respiratory infections (given pulmonary involvement) and opportunistic infections during PJP prophylaxis.

- **Drug interactions**: MMF is contraindicated with nevirapine and severe hepatic impairment; rituximab may potentiate the neutropenic effect of concomitant agents. Careful review of the patient’s concomitant medications mitigated the risk of additive myelosuppression or hepatotoxicity.

### Feeding and dysphagia management (NG tube, swallow therapy)

Persistent dysphagia for >2 months necessitated enteral nutrition via a nasogastric tube. A multidisciplinary swallow assessment (speech and language therapist) guided safe swallowing techniques once initial inflammation subsided. Swallow therapy focused on:

- **Optimizing oral-motor strength**: Strengthening pharyngeal and laryngeal muscles through exercises.
- **Fluid management**: Use of thin, mildly thick liquids with thickening agents to reduce aspiration risk.
- **Positioning**: Prolonged post‑prandial head‑tilt to encourage airway protection.

Gradual removal of the NG tube was timed with clinical improvement in muscle strength and CK normalization, ensuring adequate caloric intake during the taper of steroids.

### Multidisciplinary coordination (ENT, rheumatology, pulmonology, nutrition)

- **ENT**: Surgical and airway expertise for monitoring deep neck space involvement, performing nasendoscopy, and coordinating swallow therapy.
- **Rheumatology**: Overseeing immunosuppressive regimen, adjusting drug doses, and interpreting laboratory markers (CK, transaminases).
- **Pulmonology**: Monitoring pulmonary function tests, interpreting imaging, and guiding PJP prophylaxis decisions.
- **Nutrition**: Managing enteral feeding, ensuring caloric sufficiency, and collaborating on swallow therapy.

Regular case conferences ensured that therapeutic decisions were aligned across specialties, allowing for timely adjustments (e.g., changing PJP prophylaxis, modifying steroid taper) based on evolving clinical status.

--- 

In summary, the treatment strategy for this SAE1‑positive dermatomyositis combined rapid, high‑dose steroid induction with early, multi‑modal immunosuppression (MMF, rituximab, IVIG), careful avoidance of methotrexate due to lung risk, proactive infection prophylaxis, vigilant safety monitoring, and comprehensive multidisciplinary care. This cohesion facilitated rapid resolution of airway inflammation, normalization of muscle enzymes, and eventual recovery of swallowing function, illustrating a pragmatic model for complex dermatomyositis management.

## 4. Follow‑up, Outcome, and Long‑Term Surveillance

The clinical course of the patient demonstrates a textbook evolution from an acute, life‑threatening presentation of dermatomyositis to sustained remission after aggressive immunosuppression. Serial objective measures allow precise assessment of treatment response and guide subsequent therapy adjustments.

### Short‑term recovery

Within three weeks of high‑dose intravenous methylprednisolone and a single course of intensive IVIG, the patient’s serum creatine kinase (CK) fell from 16 184 U/L to 37 U/L, reflecting complete biochemical remission of myofibre necrosis. Muscle strength, evaluated by the Medical Research Council (MRC) grading system, improved from 4/5 in the hip flexors to full strength (5/5) over the same period. Cervical soft‑tissue swelling, the most striking clinical feature, decreased markedly on physical examination and was corroborated by repeat magnetic‑resonance imaging showing near‑resolution of edema in the strap muscles and retropharyngeal space. These changes occurred in parallel with a substantial drop in acute‑phase reactants (CRP < 5 mg/L).

### Long‑term remission milestones

After four weeks of oral prednisone (starting 60 mg/day) and concurrent mycophenolate mofetil (1 g BID), the patient achieved corticosteroid tapering to 5 mg/day over seven months, at which point prednisone was discontinued. Dysphagia, initially necessitating a nasogastric tube, had resolved sufficiently by three months to allow a return to a soft‑solid diet and eventual tube removal. This functional recovery aligns with the normalization of swallowing biomechanics seen on video‑fluoroscopic studies and comprehensive PET‑CT that revealed no persistent muscular hypermetabolism.

### Pulmonary function and imaging follow‑up

The initial restrictive pattern on spirometry (FEV₁/FVC < 0.8) and CT‑identified ground‑glass opacities were attributed to aspiration pneumonitis secondary to dysphagia. Repeat pulmonary function tests at 12 months showed only a mild residual restrictive defect (FEV₁ 80 % predicted), compatible with a baseline of reduced thoracic compliance in the patient’s age group. Serial chest CT at 11 months demonstrated near‑complete resolution of ground‑glass infiltrates and atelectasis, confirming recovery of the airway‑air‑way interface. No new nodules or fibrotic changes emerged, mitigating concern for interstitial lung disease progression.

### Risk assessment for occult malignancy

Anti‑SAE1 dermatomyositis confers a moderate to high risk for malignancy, particularly in male patients with dysphagia. Applying the IMACS consensus criteria, the patient accrued two high‑risk factors (dermatomyositis diagnosis, dysphagia) and two intermediate‑risk factors (anti‑SAE1 positivity, male sex), yielding a high‑risk classification. Initial malignancy work‑up—including CT scans of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis, comprehensive tumor‑marker panel (CEA, CA19‑9, CA‑125, PSA), and a PET‑CT scan—identified no tumours. Consequently, a vigilant surveillance strategy has been instituted, premised on early detection of malignancy in a disease that typically manifests within 5 years of myositis onset.

### Surveillance plan

1. **Imaging**: Annual low‑dose CT of the chest, with repeat scans every 18 months or sooner if symptoms arise. Given the absence of lymphadenopathy and the patient’s age, abdominal imaging is performed every 24 months.  
2. **Tumor markers**: Quarterly assessment of CEA, CA19‑9, CA‑125, and PSA to detect organ‑specific neoplasia.  
3. **Endoscopy**: Colonoscopy and esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) within 12 months of diagnosis, repeated annually thereafter given persistent dysphagia and the association of dermatomyositis with upper‑GI tract malignancies.  
4. **Physical examination**: Bi‑annual dermatologic review focusing on cutaneous manifestations (heliotrope rash, Gottron’s papules) that may herald relapse or new tumours.  
5. **Functional assessments**: Quarterly MRC strength testing and swallowing evaluations for early relapse detection.

### Role of maintenance IVIG and tapering mycophenolate

Maintenance IVIG (2 g/kg every 4 weeks) was continued for the first 12 months, a regimen supported by retrospective data indicating reduced relapse rates in refractory dermatomyositis. Mycophenolate mofetil was gradually tapered over 6 months, contingent on sustained remission of CK, muscle strength, and absence of active disease on imaging. The decision to phase out this agent was guided by the patient’s favorable tolerability profile and lack of corticosteroid‑related adverse effects.

### Patient education and self‑monitoring

The patient was counselled on recognizing early signs of relapse: new or worsening proximal weakness, facial or neck swelling, unexplained fevers, or increased fatigue. A home symptom diary was instituted, with prompts to record daily muscle strength (self‑rated) and any cutaneous changes. Education included dietary modifications for dysphagia and safe swallowing techniques. Emphasis was placed on the importance of adhering to scheduled follow‑ups, promptly reporting any new symptoms, and maintaining a healthy lifestyle to reduce overall malignancy risk.

In summary, the patient’s trajectory exemplifies a favorable outcome in anti‑SAE1 dermatomyositis when prompt recognition, aggressive early therapy, and rigorous long‑term surveillance are employed. The multidisciplinary approach—encompassing rheumatology, immunology, ENT, pulmonology, and oncology—proved essential in achieving remission and mitigating both systemic disease progression and malignancy risk.

## 5. Clinical Pearls and Lessons Learned  

**Endoscopic appearance of neck edema:**  
Flexible naso‑endoscopy in this case revealed **no intraluminal obstruction or perforation**; the only abnormality noted was increased secretions and mis‑directed coughing.  In true angioedema, the submucosal tissues are markedly edematous, but the airway mucosa itself is typically smooth and the secretions are not excessive.  A careful inspection for **pseudo‑tubular swelling or a “wrinkled” epithelium** can help differentiate a **vascular swelling** from inflammatory myositis of the strap muscles, which may appear “blurry” on imaging but is not visualised on the endoscope.  

**Value of early CK measurement in unexplained soft‑tissue swelling:**  
A serum creatine kinase (CK) level was requested **anticipating a myopathic cause** because the neck swelling was persistent, non‑painful, and **inconsistent with typical angioedema** (no urtication, no known triggers).  The CK was >16 000 U/L—an unmistakable indicator of muscle injury that shifted the diagnostic trajectory away from ENT infection toward an inflammatory myopathy.  Routine CK testing should therefore be part of the work‑up for any patient with **diffuse, facial/neck edema plus proximal weakness**, even when skin signs are subtle or absent.  

**Recognizing subtle cutaneous signs when rash is absent:**  
In this patient, **Gottron’s papules and heliotrope rash were negative**, yet the combination of **periungual telangiectasia, nailfold micro‑haemorrhages, and a faint Holster (shawl) sign** were telling.  Dermatologic examination should extend beyond the typical rashes; palpation of fingers for “frosted glass” periungual changes and inspection of the nasolabial folds for faint erythema can reveal early dermatomyositis manifestations that might otherwise escape notice.  

**Importance of autoantibody profiling in guiding therapy intensity:**  
The presence of **anti‑SAE1 antibodies** provided a diagnostic anchor and a prognostic cue: these antibodies are linked to a **higher likelihood of dysphagia, interstitial lung disease (ILD), and a more aggressive disease course**.  In practice, once SAE1 positivity is confirmed, clinicians should anticipate **intensive immunosuppression** (high‑dose steroids plus early steroid‑sparing agents) and schedule **serial pulmonary function tests** to monitor ILD progression.  Autoantibody panels should therefore be ordered promptly in any suspected dermatomyositis case, rather than reserved for refractory disease.  

**Balancing immunosuppression with organ‑specific considerations (lung disease):**  
Methotrexate was deliberately avoided because of its known pulmonary toxicity and the patient’s pre‑existing restrictive pattern on spirometry.  Instead, **mycophenolate mofetil** (oral) and **rituximab** (IV) were chosen, recognising that these agents have a more favourable respiratory profile.  The decision to use **IVIG** was influenced by the severe dysphagia and need for rapid improvement in swallowing function.  Clinicians should routinely assess baseline organ function before initiating immunosuppression, tailoring drug choice to minimise iatrogenic injury while still achieving disease control.  

**Documentation of multidisciplinary care pathways in complex autoimmune cases:**  
This case underscores the necessity of a **structured, multidisciplinary approach**: ENT for initial assessment, immunology for serology and initial therapy, rheumatology for ongoing management, pulmonary medicine for ILD surveillance, and gastroenterology for malignancy screening.  A formal care pathway that includes **standardised imaging (CT neck, MRI thigh, FDG‑PET), laboratory checkpoints (CK, complement, autoantibody panel)**, and **scheduled follow‑up visits** facilitated early diagnosis and prevented treatment delays.  For residency training, outlining such pathways in the electronic medical record helps ensure consistency and completeness of care.  

---

**Take‑Home Points**

1. **Neck swelling with proximal weakness warrants early CK testing**; a markedly elevated CK should trigger a myopathy work‑up.  
2. **Endoscopic clues are subtle**; lack of mucosal disease favors myositis over classic angioedema.  
3. **Dermatologic examination must look beyond visible rashes**—periungual changes and subtle erythema can be diagnostic.  
4. **Autoantibody profiling informs both diagnosis and treatment intensity**; SAE1 positivity predicts a more aggressive course.  
5. **Immunosuppression must be balanced against organ vulnerability**—avoid toxic agents when baseline organ disease is present.  
6. **Multidisciplinary documentation and care pathways improve outcomes** by preventing diagnostic delays and ensuring systematic monitoring.
