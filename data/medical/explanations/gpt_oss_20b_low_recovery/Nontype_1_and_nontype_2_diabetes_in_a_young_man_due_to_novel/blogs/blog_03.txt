Title: Blog about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

# Glucagon‑Stimulated C‑Peptide Re‑Considered: A Practical Guide for the Emergency Room

## Why this case is a show‑stopper

Our young patient came to the lab for a routine health exam and, instead of a normal glucose curve, we discovered a 10.2 % HbA1c, fasting glucose of 12.4 mmol/L and a 2‑hour OGTT of 19.8 mmol/L. He was sweating, thirsty, had lost weight, but the family history looked suspicious – a classic MODY tease. What set him apart, beyond the sad numbers, was a low‑dose glucagon stimulation test that yielded a 6‑minute C‑peptide of just 0.83 nmol/L – low but not zero. That subtle hint opened the door to a different therapeutic universe: from daily insulin to a GLP‑1 agonist and finally to a metformin‑plus‑DPP‑4 strategy. In an ER setting, where decisions must be rapid, we often rely on quick surrogate markers of β‑cell function, and glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide (GSP) is one of our best bets.

## The glucagon test in a nutshell

| Step | What happens | Why it matters |
|------|---------------|----------------|
| **Baseline** | Collect fasting blood for C‑peptide | Sets the “resting” insulin secretory tone |
| **Glucagon infusion** | 1 mg glucagon IV or SC | Drives counter‑regulatory rise in glucose → insulin secretion |
| **Blood draw at 6 min** | Measure C‑peptide | Captures peak β‑cell response (most people see a peak ≈ 30–45 min, but 6 min is a convenient surrogate for bedside use) |
| **Interpretation** | <0.6 nmol/L: poor reserve; ≥ 0.6 nmol/L: preserved | Threshold derived from large cohort studies; acts as a gatekeeper for sulfonylureas vs insulin vs GLP‑1 |

In the ER we rarely get the luxury of a full 30‑minute OGTT or a stimulated C‑peptide that takes a day to return. A single glucagon dose and a 6‑minute draw give us a rapid, practical snapshot of β‑cell health.

## Interpreting the numbers – what do we see?

| Category | C‑peptide (6 min) | Clinical implication |
|----------|-------------------|-----------------------|
| **Preserved** | ≥ 0.6 nmol/L | Good β‑cell reserve → consider oral agents (sulfonylureas, GLP‑1 agonists) |
| **Intermediate** | 0.3–0.6 nmol/L | Partial reserve → high‑dose sulfonylureas or low‑dose insulin plus oral |
| **Low** | < 0.3 nmol/L | Little to no reserve → insulin therapy likely required |

> **Note:** These thresholds are **guidelines**, not hard rules. A patient’s age, duration of hyperglycaemia, and comorbidities can shift the decision curve.

In our case, the patient’s 0.83 nmol/L fell into the “preserved” bracket, yet his HbA1c was soaring. This discordance raised the question: **Is he a typical type 2 or an underlying monogenic form?** The negative autoantibodies, strong family history, and the glucagon response nudged us toward a MODY work‑up.

## From insulin to oral: the therapeutic domino

1. **Baseline insulin (degludec) + metformin + DPP‑4 inhibitor**  
   *Goal:* Quickly bring down the erratic glucose spikes while we await genetic confirmation.

2. **Genetic confirmation of HNF1A‑MODY**  
   *Effect:* Eliminates the “type 2” label, shifts our therapeutic lens.

3. **Discontinue insulin; start GLP‑1 agonist (dulaglutide)**  
   *Why GLP‑1?*  
   - Stimulates glucose‑dependent insulin release (no hypoglycaemia risk)  
   - Reduces weight (the patient had a BMI of 24.6, but metabolic syndrome was present)  
   - Preserves β‑cell function over time  
   - Avoids sulfonylureas that can cause long‑term hypoglycaemia in MODY

4. **Once HbA1c stabilised (< 6.5 %)**  
   *Mode:* Metformin + DPP‑4 inhibitor (saxagliptin) for maintenance  
   *Outcome:* Sustained glycaemic control, no microalbuminuria or retinopathy after 6 months.

### Practical take‑away for the ER

- **Ask for a glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide when you suspect early‑onset diabetes but lack autoantibodies.**  
- **If the 6‑minute C‑peptide is ≥ 0.6 nmol/L, do not set down insulin immediately.**  
- **Use the glucagon response as a built‑in “MODY test.”**  
- **If family history is strong and C‑peptide is preserved, send a MODY panel (NGS) ASAP.**  
- **Once a monogenic mutation is found, transition from insulin to a GLP‑1 or sulfonylurea early, and avoid SGLT‑2 inhibitors unless carefully monitored.**  

## Why this matters for us

- **Reduced hypoglycaemia** – GLP‑1 agonists don’t trigger insulin spikes in the absence of food.  
- **Weight‑neutral or positive** – In contrast to sulfonylureas and insulin, which can worsen abdominal adiposity.  
- **Long‑term β‑cell preservation** – A head‑long advantage in a disease driven by progressive β‑cell loss.  
- **Precision medicine in real life** – The glucagon test is a cheap, bedside, actionable tool that tells us whether we’re managing a type 2 illness or a monogenic disorder.

## Clinical pearls (TL;DR)

- **Glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide (6 min)** > 0.6 nmol/L → consider oral or GLP‑1 therapy.  
- **< 0.3 nmol/L** → insulin almost always required.  
- **Intermediate values** → think high‑dose sulfonylureas or low‑dose insulin with oral.  
- **Never ignore family history** – a string of early‑onset, non‑autoimmune diabetes at any age warrants a MODY screen.  
- **SGLT‑2 inhibitors** are **contra‑indicated** in HNF1A‑MODY due to dehydration/DKA risk.  
- **Early genetic confirmation** changes the entire trajectory: stops the insulin “roller coaster” and allows for a more tailored, sustainable regimen.

## Bottom line

In the ER, we’re often juggling a calm patient with a bizarre glucose chart, a family history that reads like a crime novel, and a lab draw that could be a game‑changer. The glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide is a dentist’s scaler: simple, quick, and reveals hidden pathology. If the numbers point outward, pull the trigger on genetics; if they suggest β‑cell vigor, let glucose regulate with GLP‑1 or sulfonylurea. In that way, we avoid the pitfall of burying a MODY patient under a blanket of insulin and give them a precision‑medicine path that feels as elegant as it is effective.

**Take‑home points (bullet‑style)**

- ⬅︎ Request glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide for all young patients ≤ 35 yrs with hyperglycaemia and negative autoantibodies.  
- ⬅︎ Threshold ≥ 0.6 nmol/L invites GLP‑1 or sulfonylurea; < 0.3 nmol/L demands insulin.  
- ⬅︎ A strong family history + preserved C‑peptide = high probability of MODY → send NGS panel.  
- ⬅︎ Stop insulin early once a monogenic mutation is confirmed; start GLP‑1.  
- ⬅︎ Avoid SGLT‑2 inhibitors in HNF1A‑MODY due to high DKA risk.  

A glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide is not just a lab test – it’s a clinical compass pointing us toward the right therapeutic vessel. Use it wisely, and watch patients transition from the insulin treadmill to a more sustainable, personalized journey.
