Title: Blog about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

# From Gene Panel to Clinical Practice: How Next‑Generation Sequencing Is Changing Diabetes Care

## Why this case grabbed my attention

A 28‑year‑old man walked into the clinic with a HbA1c of 10.2 % and a family history that could only be described as “diabetes on recursion.” He was lean (BMI 24.6 kg/m²), had no acanthosis nigricans, yet his 75 g OGTT spiked to 19.8 mmol/L and he even had a modest sweet sweat (low‑grade sudomotor dysfunction). At first blush you’d think type 2 diabetes on a pre‑diabetic trajectory—so insulin was started. Six months later, a single VHL‑style panel revealed a novel **HNF1A** mutation (c.809A>G, p.N270S). Suddenly, insulin became redundant and a GLP‑1 agonist, metformin and a DPP‑4 inhibitor took center stage, crushing his HbA1c to 6 % and ending his insulin injections altogether. That story shows the full arc from “mystery hyperglycaemia” to precision medicine.

## The clinical workflow in a nutshell

| Step | What happens | Practical tips |
|------|--------------|----------------|
| **1. Recognise a MODY‑like picture** | Early‑onset diabetes < 25 yrs, family history suggestive of autosomal‑dominant inheritance, negative autoantibodies, normal BMI or mild obesity, preserved C‑peptide. | Use bedside MODY calculators (e.g., Exeter) but remember they were built on White cohorts; don’t let them be the sole gatekeeper. |
| **2. Order an NGS panel** | A focused MODY panel (typically 10‑15 genes) delivered in ~3 weeks. | Ask your lab to provide: 1) the target genes, 2) the sequencing depth, 3) the bioinformatics pipeline and 4) a copy of the ACMG‑based report. |
| **3. Interpret the variant** | Use ACMG/AMP guidelines: population frequency, computational evidence (e.g., AlphaMissense), segregation, functional data, phenotype match. | For a novel variant, gather: 1) family segregation (co‑segregation?), 2) in‑silico pathogenicity scores, 3) literature evidence of the domain, 4) any functional studies if available. |
| **4. Discuss the result with the patient** | Frame it as “this mutation is likely pathogenic—this explains your early glucose elevation and will inform treatment.” | Use plain language: “You’re not “type 2” who needs insulin; you have a form of diabetes that responds to certain drugs.” |
| **5. Adjust therapy** | Switch from insulin to sulfonylureas or GLP‑1 agonists (or combo metformin + DPP‑4). | Start low‑dose sulfonylureas if beta‑cell reserve is good; otherwise GLP‑1. Monitor for hypoglycaemia. |
| **6. Cascade testing** | Offer relatives (parents, siblings) same panel or targeted variant test. | Provide genetic counseling; obtain consent first. |

## ACMG criteria done right (in practice)

When the lab reports a "variant of uncertain significance" (VUS), the typical response is “Wait and see.” In this case, the variant had:

| ACMG criterion | How we met it |
|----------------|---------------|
| **PM2** – rarity in gnomAD | Not found in any population database |
| **PM3** – segregation | Present in 3 affected family members (grandmother, mother, brother) |
| **PM1** | Located in DNA‑binding domain, a hotspot for pathogenic HNF1A changes |
| **PP3** | AlphaMissense score 0.995, predictive of damaging effect |
| **PS4** | Increased prevalence in affected individuals compared to general population |

Putting these under ACMG’s ladder pushes the classification from “VUS” to “likely pathogenic.” This underscores that the *interpretation* phase is as critical as *sequencing* itself.

| **Laboratory variant interpretation** | 81255 (bioinformatics) | Often bundled with panel; ensure CPT inclusion |
| **Genetic counseling visit** | 96140 (genetic counseling) | $150–$250 per 30 min |
| **Physician visit for result disclosure** | 99213/99214 (office visit) | $100–$200 |

Tip: Ask the billing office for a “MODY sequencing bundle” – some payors will co‑pay 30 % if a genetic diagnosis changes management. Also, keep a copy of the ACMG report to submit as evidence for “clinically actionable” ruling.

## From insulin to precision drug therapy

The shift from insulin to a GLP‑1 analogue in this patient illustrates three cardinal advantages:

1. **Avoid hypoglycaemia** – GLP‑1 (dulaglutide) only stimulates insulin when glucose is high, so the risk of nadir lows is minimal.
2. **Preserve beta‑cell mass** – by avoiding the “excessive insulin load” of sulphonylureas and the metabolic stress of insulin.
3. **Improved adherence** – once‑weekly injections are far less burdensome than daily basal‑bolus insulin.

After six months of dulaglutide + metformin + saxagliptin, HbA1c fell from 10.2 % to 6 %, weight remained stable, and no microvascular complications emerged. That’s a textbook example of how a genetic diagnosis can change the therapeutic trajectory entirely.

## Take‑home pearls

- **MODY is under‑diagnosed**. Even in populations where type 2 is common, a fraction of young diabetic adults with a strong family history likely harbor mono‑genic disease.
- **Next‑generation sequencing is fast and affordable** today. Turn‑around time is ~3 weeks, and the cost is comparable to an expensive “screen‑all” approach.
- **Interpretation matters**. Don’t hand off the variant to the lab and call it a day; the ACMG framework is mandatory for turning a VUS into a clinically actionable finding.
- **Therapy changes**. A pathogenic HNF1A mutation almost always renders insulin unnecessary. Start with sulfonylurea or GLP‑1, monitor, and titrate. Reserve insulin for those with very low or absent C‑peptide.
- **Cascade testing** saves a world of future medical costs. Screen relatives; educate them on treatment options; identify those at risk before hyperglycaemia becomes a crisis.

---

**Bottom line:** A single panel can transform a young patient’s life—from “you need insulin, here’s a vial” to “here’s a once‑weekly pill that keeps you in the sweet spot.” Genomics isn’t just a research tool; it’s becoming a front‑line diagnostic. And for us clinicians, it’s a reminder that a deeper dive into the genome can unearth a therapeutic halo that otherwise would stay hidden.
