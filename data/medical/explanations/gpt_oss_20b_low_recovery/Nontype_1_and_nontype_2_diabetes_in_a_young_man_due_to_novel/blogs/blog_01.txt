Title: Blog about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

# When a Young Patient’s Blood Sugar Doesn’t Fit the Ordinary Diabetes Puzzle  

## A story that started in a routine health exam

You’re at the office, the receptionist has the patient’s chart in hand, and the kid in front of you has a diagnosis scribbled on the paper: “Type 2 diabetes, HbA1c 10.2 %.” The lab report shows a fasting glucose of 12.4 mM, a post‑OGTT peak of 19.8 mM, and—curious to mention—it pours glucose into the urine, yet no signs of ketoacidosis. Blood pressure is 112/70 mmHg, BMI is a lean 24.6 kg/m², and there are no hints of acanthosis nigricans. That’s the scene reported by Liu et al. for a 25‑year‑old Chinese man who turned out to have a novel HNF1A mutation that causes maturity‑onset diabetes of the young (MODY).  

What made this case *surprising* wasn’t the high HbA1c or the hyperglycaemia; it was the mismatch between the clinical picture and the usual diabetes archetypes. In the fast‑paced world of endocrinology, a diagnosis of type‑1 or type‑2 diabetes seems almost automatic once a K g/L fails fasting glucose or HbA1c hits a threshold. But that night, as your colleagues debated insulin therapy versus sulfonylureas, a humble list of clues began to point elsewhere: a dramatic, multigenerational family history of early‑onset diabetes, a lack of autoantibodies, a low C‑peptide reserve that was not zero, and a normal BMI.

## The tell‑ta‑tale clues that should trigger a MODY work‑up

| Clue | Why it matters | How it fits the case |
|------|----------------|----------------------|
| **Onset before age 25** | MODY is a monogenic, autosomal‑dominant form of diabetes that appears in childhood or early adulthood. | The patient is 25, first clinical presentation at 24 y. |
| **Unexplained glycosuria with no ketoacidosis** | Certain MODY subtypes (e.g. HNF1A) reduce renal SGLT2 expression, lowering the glucose threshold for reabsorption. | Urinalysis showed glucose excretion while ketones were absent. |
| **Family history of early‑onset diabetes** | Classic autosomal dominant inheritance; multiple generations show the disease by their 20s–30s. | Grandmother (30s), mother (late 20s), younger brother (20). |
| **Non‑obese, non‑metabolic phenotype** | MODY patients often have a normal BMI and minimal dyslipidaemia, unlike typical type‑2 diabetes. | BMI 24.6 kg/m², LDL normal, HDL low‑borderline, TG 4.31 mmol/L but no acanthosis nigricans. |
| **Negative autoantibodies (GADA, IA‑2A, ZnT8)** | Distinguishes non‑autoimmune diabetes from type‑1. | All autoantibodies were negative. |
| **Preserved-but‑low C‑peptide** | Suggests a defect in insulin secretion rather than absolute loss (type‑1) or resistance (type‑2). | Glucagon‑stimulation test: 0.83 nmol/L <0.6 nmol/L threshold for preserved function, but still detectable. |
| **Early‑in‑treatment insulin requirement that can be stopped** | In HNF1A‑MODY, sulfonylureas or GLP‑1 agonists often suffice; insulin is rarely needed long‑term. | After genetic confirmation, insulin was discontinued in favour of dulaglutide and metformin, with HbA1c dropping to 6.0 %. |

Seeing even three of these on the same patient should make a clinician pause. “Why isn’t this a textbook type‑2 case?” the question is all anyone will wonder, but the answer lies in the subtlety of the presentation.

## Using an algorithm to filter the “likely MODY” candidates

The Exeter MODY probability calculator has become the bread‑and‑butter tool in many practices. It asks for age, BMI, family history, autoantibody status, and C‑peptide values. When the case above was entered, the algorithm returned a 62.4 % probability of MODY—a figure that would normally spark a referral for genetic testing.  

A few caveats:

- It was derived from a White European cohort; applying it to Asian or African populations may skew risk estimates, but in practice it still flag higher odds than the default 1‑in‑10 000 prevalence.
- Because the calculator weights family history heavily, a patient with a robust pedigree will get a high score even if other features are mild.
- A low BMI can tip the needle toward MODY; in an era of rising obesity, that may be under‑recognized.

So, the algorithm is a **clinical rapid‑fire test, not a definitive diagnosis**. Once a patient throws up the characteristic flags, the next step is to order a MODY gene panel, typically via a next‑generation sequencing (NGS) panel that covers the 15 known MODY genes (e.g., HNF1A, GCK, HNF1B, HNF4A).

## Why genetics matters – and how it altered therapy

In the anecdote, the patient was initially started on basal insulin, metformin, and a DPP‑4 inhibitor (saxagliptin) because clinicians behaved as a typical type‑2 provider would. After just one month, HbA1c fell to 9.1 %—still high, but a good sign, so they kept insulin. Genetic sequencing revealed a novel heterozygous variant: **c.809A>G (p.N270S) in HNF1A**. This variant was absent from ClinVar and VarSome, but it satisfied ACMG criteria for pathogenicity (novel, in a critical DNA‑binding domain, high in silico deleteriousness, familial segregation).

Why did this change the approach so dramatically?

| Initial Regimen | Post‑MODY Diagnosis | Rationale |
|-----------------|--------------------|-----------|
| Basal insulin + metformin + saxagliptin | GLP‑1 RA (dulaglutide) + metformin | GLP‑1 agonists potentiate glucose‑dependent insulin secretion and provide weight neutrality, reducing hypoglycaemia risk. |
| Continue insulin + add sulfonylurea | Switch to sulfonylurea (e.g., glimepiride) **or** add GLP‑1 | Sulfonylureas have long been the first‑line for HNF1A‑MODY, but can precipitate hypoglycaemia as β‑cell function wanes. GLP‑1 RA offers safer glucose control. |
| None | Add SGLT‑2 inhibitor? | Not recommended; HNF1A‑MODY patients may have impaired renal glucose reabsorption and increased risk of ketoacidosis with SGLT‑2 blockage. |

The patient’s HbA1c eventually dropped to 6.0 %, maintained with metformin + saxagliptin. Crucially, insulin was never re‑introduced, preserving his endogenous β‑cell function and reducing the burden of injections.

## Clinical pearls & take‑home messages

- **High suspicion, low threshold**: Any young adult with hyperglycaemia, a strong family history of early diabetes, and a non‑obese, non‑metabolic phenotype should undergo a MODY probability screen.
- **Autoantibody negativity + preserved C‑peptide**: Negative GADA, IA‑2A, ZnT8 and a glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide above 0.6 nmol/L (or 0.5 ng/mL) are key pointers toward MODY rather than type‑1 or type‑2.
- **Genetic confirmation allows *drug repurposing***: HNF1A‑MODY responds brilliantly to sulfonylureas or GLP‑1 agonists; insulin can often be replaced or avoided.
- **Beware of SGLT‑2 inhibitors**: In HNF1A‑MODY, diminished SGLT2 activity can predispose to dehydration and ketoacidosis; use with extreme caution.
- **Family screening matters**: A confirmed MODY mutation justifies testing at least two relatives per generation; early diagnosis translates to better management and less morbidity.

## Bottom line

The case by Liu et al. is a textbook reminder that diabetes is not a one‑size‑fits‑all diagnosis. When you see a young, lean patient with dramatic hyperglycaemia, a striking family history, and a positive glucose excretion but no ketoacidosis, **think MODY**. A quick reference tool plus a gene panel can change the therapeutic landscape, spare the patient from lifelong insulin, and give your family a head‑start in monitoring. Keep the red flags in mind, and you’ll be doing your patients— and your own clinical practice— a lot of good.
