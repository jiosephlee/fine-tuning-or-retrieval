Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Clinical Pearls] What clinical features should prompt consideration of MODY over type 1 or type 2 diabetes in a young adult with hyperglycaemia?

**Prompt: MODY vs. type 1/type 2 diabetes in a young adult**

**Key clinical clues for MODY**

| Feature | Why it favors MODY | Evidence from case |
|---------|--------------------|--------------------|
| **Early age of onset (< 25 yr)** | MODY is a congenital β‑cell disorder that usually presents before 25 yr. | Patient diagnosed at 25 yr (early adulthood). |
| **Strong autosomal‑dominant family history** | Classic Mendelian inheritance; > 50 % of cases have ≥ 2 affected relatives in successive generations. | Maternal grandmother (30 s), mother (late 20s), younger brother (20 yr). |
| **Non‑obese BMI and absence of insulin resistance** | MODY patients tend to have a lean phenotype without “metabolic syndrome” features, unlike typical T2D. | BMI 24.6 kg/m², no acanthosis nigricans. |
| **Negative islet autoantibodies (GADA, IA‑2A, ZnT8)** | Autoimmune disease is absent in MODY; autoantibodies are a hallmark of type 1 diabetes. | All antibodies negative. |
| **Preserved, but reduced, endogenous C‑peptide** | MODY preserves β‑cell mass longer than T1D; a low stimulated C‑peptide suggests partial β‑cell defect. | Glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide 0.83 nmol/L (> 0.60 nmol/L threshold). |
| **Post‑prandial hyperglycaemia** | MODY often manifests with marked post‑meal glucose spikes before fasting glucose rises. | 2‑hour OGTT 19.8 mmol/L. |
| **Glycosuria in the absence of hyperglycaemia‑driven overflow** | HNF1A mutations reduce renal SGLT2 expression, lowering the renal threshold. | Patient had glycosuria despite no DKA presentation. |
| **Lack of severe obesity or typical T2D features** | MODY patients rarely exhibit the classic “obesity‑associated insulin resistance” phenotype. | No obesity, HDL low but triglycerides high (possible metabolic syndrome, yet BMI low). |

**Clinical pearls**

1. **Early‑onset, lean, family‑history‑positive diabetes** should prompt MODY consideration.  
2. **Negative autoantibodies plus a modestly preserved C‑peptide** essentially exclude type 1 diabetes.  
3. **Marked post‑prandial hyperglycaemia** (≥ 11.1 mmol/L at 2 h OGTT) is a hallmark of HNF1A‑MODY.  
4. **Glycosuria without overt DKA** is a handy red flag for HNF1A mutations.  
5. When these clues coexist, run a **MODY probability calculator** (exeter tool) or, ideally, **NGS panel** for all HNF1A–related genes.  

**Bottom line**

In a young adult with hyperglycaemia, a lean phenotype, positive family history, negative islet antibodies, preserved C‑peptide and prominent post‑prandial glucose spike strongly suggest MODY over type 1 or type 2 diabetes. Early genetic confirmation, as in this case (novel HNF1A p.N270S mutation), guides therapy—shifting from insulin to sulfonylureas, GLP‑1 agonists, or metformin—and improves long‑term outcomes.

### [Pathophysiology] Explain how a glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide test can help differentiate MODY from type 1 diabetes and interpret the values 0.39 nmol/L (baseline) and 0.83 nmol/L (6 min) seen in this patient.

**Glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide: a practical tool for distinguishing MODY from type 1 diabetes**

The glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide test evaluates residual β‑cell function by measuring C‑peptide after a 1‑mg intramuscular glucagon bolus.  Glucagon rapidly raises intracellular cAMP and counter‑acts the first‑phase insulin release, giving a robust, reproducible secretion stimulus. Because C‑peptide is secreted in equimolar amounts with insulin and is not extracted by the liver, it reflects endogenous insulin production.

| Condition | Typical response | Interpretation |
|-----------|------------------|----------------|
| **Type 1 diabetes** (open‑ended autoimmunity) | Baseline C‑peptide <0.1 nmol/L; stimulated <0.1–0.3 nmol/L | Marked β‑cell failure |
| **MODY (especially HNF1A‑MODY)** | Baseline ≥0.2–0.4 nmol/L; stimulated 0.5–1.5 nmol/L | Modestly preserved β‑cell function, often >0.6 nmol/L |
| **Type 2 diabetes** (early disease) | Baseline 0.3–0.7 nmol/L; stimulated >1.0 nmol/L | Preserved or hyper‑secretory β‑cells |

In the reported patient, the baseline C‑peptide was **0.39 nmol/L** and rose to **0.83 nmol/L** after 6 min of glucagon.  The increase (≈ +0.44 nmol/L) falls in the range typical of modest residual insulin secretion.  This value is **above the 0.6 nmol/L cut‑off** that is often used to argue against type 1 diabetes and is markedly higher than what would be expected in newly diagnosed type 1 diabetes, where stimulated levels typically stay <0.4 nmol/L.  The modest but demonstrable response, coupled with negative islet autoantibodies and a strong familial pattern, supports a monogenic β‑cell defect rather than autoimmune destruction.

**Bottom line:** In a young patient with hyperglycaemia, a glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide ≥0.6 nmol/L argues strongly for MODY (or type 2 diabetes) over type 1 diabetes.  The values in this case (0.39 → 0.83 nmol/L) meet that criterion and, together with the negative antibodies and family history, helped clinch the diagnosis of HNF1A‑MODY before genetic confirmation.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Describe the inheritance pattern and the typical age range of onset for HNF1A‑MODY, and explain why a strong family history is a key diagnostic criterion.

**Inheritance and age of onset of HNF1A‑MODY**  
- **Inheritance:** Autosomal dominant.  
  A pathogenic variant in one HNF1A allele is sufficient to produce disease; the mutation is inherited from a parent who is usually an affected family member.  
- **Typical age of onset:** Before 25 years.  
  The disorder is usually diagnosed in childhood or adolescence, although presentation can extend into early adulthood (see the patient in the case who developed diabetes at age 20).

**Why a strong family history is a key criterion**  
Because the condition follows a classic dominant pattern, affected individuals almost always have at least one first‑degree relative with early‑onset diabetes that is not type 1 or type 2. A sequential pedigree of diabetes emerging in successive generations strongly suggests a monogenic etiology and makes HNF1A‑MODY highly probable. In the presented case, the maternal grandmother, mother, and younger brother all had diabetes before 30 years, providing the classic “strong family history” that was essential for prompting genetic testing and confirming the novel HNF1A variant.

### [Pathophysiology] What genetic testing approach was used to confirm the HNF1A mutation in this case, and what ACMG criteria support the classification of the novel p.N270S variant as pathogenic?

**Answer**

The patient’s variant was identified by a **next‑generation sequencing (NGS) MODY panel** that interrogated all exons of the 15 genes most commonly associated with MODY. The panel, applied to the patient’s genomic DNA, revealed a single‑nucleotide change c.809A>G in exon 4 of **HNF1A**, producing the missense change **p.N270S** (Asn→Ser at codon 270). The variant was present in heterozygosity, segregating with diabetes in the mother and maternal grandmother, and was absent in the patient’s unaffected brother.

To classify the novel variant as pathogenic, the authors applied the **American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) 2015 guidelines**. The following criteria were met:

| ACMG Criterion | Evidence in this case | Interpretation |
|----------------|-----------------------|----------------|
| **PVS1 (null variant in a gene where loss of function is a known mechanism)** | Not applicable – missense. |
| **PS1 (same amino‑acid change as a previously established pathogenic variant)** | None – novel residue. |
| **PS2/PS3/PS4 (segregation evidence)** | *PS4*: The variant co‑segregated with early‑onset diabetes in the mother and maternal grandmother, and was absent in an unaffected sibling. |
| **PM1 (located in a mutational hot‑spot or critical domain)** | *PM1*: The variant lies in the DNA‑binding domain of HNF1A, a region with a high density of pathogenic mutations. |
| **PM2 (absent from controls)** | *PM2*: The variant is not present in large population databases (gnomAD, ExAC, 1000 Genomes). |
| **PM3 (detected in trans with a pathogenic variant)** | Not applicable. |
| **PM4 (protein length change)** | Not applicable. |
| **PM5 (novel missense at a residue where another pathogenic missense has been seen)** | *PM5*: The adjacent residue N269 is known to harbor pathogenic mutations; a substitution at the neighboring residue is therefore highly suggestive. |
| **PP1 (co‑segregation with disease)** | *PP1 strong*: Segregation with diabetes in the mother and maternal grandmother. |
| **PP2 (missense in a gene where missense variants are a common mechanism)** | *PP2*: HNF1A pathogenicity is predominantly due to missense changes. |
| **PP3 (computational evidence supporting deleterious effect)** | *PP3*: In silico predictors (e.g., AlphaMissense score 0.995, SIFT, PolyPhen‑2) all predict a damaging effect. |
| **PP4 (patient’s phenotype matches disease)** | *PP4*: Early‑onset diabetes with preserved C‑peptide, glycosuria, negative autoantibodies, and a strong family history—all classic for HNF1A‑MODY. |

Combining **one very strong (PP1) and several moderate/strong criteria** (PM1, PM2, PM5, PP2, PP3, PP4) fulfills the ACMG “Pathogenic” classification (≥1 very strong + ≥2 moderate/strong). Thus, the novel **p.N270S** variant is classified as **pathogenic** for HNF1A‑MODY.

### [Management] Why are sulfonylureas considered first‑line therapy for HNF1A‑MODY, and under what circumstances might a GLP‑1 receptor agonist be preferred over sulfonylureas?

**Why sulfonylureas are first‑line for HNF1A‑MODY**

HNF1A mutations impair β‑cell transcriptional regulation of insulin and GLUT2, leading to a progressive, dose‑dependent loss of insulin secretion. The defect is primarily in the ability of the β‑cell to respond to glucose‑induced ATP production. Sulfonylureas (e.g., glimepiride, glyburide) bind the SUR1 subunit of the K_ATP channel, bypassing the defective glucose‑sensing machinery and driving insulin release in a glucose‑independent manner. Because the residual β‑cell mass is usually preserved in early disease, low‑dose sulfonylureas reliably achieve glycaemic control, are inexpensive, and have a well‑known safety profile. The case report notes that the patient’s glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide (0.83 nmol/L) indicated sufficient β‑cell function for this approach, which is why a sulfonylurea would have been the first step.

**When to favour a GLP‑1 receptor agonist**

1. **Progressive β‑cell decline** – As HNF1A‑MODY advances, insulin secretory reserve wanes. GLP‑1 agonists enhance glucose‑stimulated insulin release, preserve β‑cell mass, and delay progression to insulin dependence.  
2. **Hypoglycaemia risk** – Sulfonylureas can provoke symptomatic or severe hypoglycaemia, especially in patients with intermittent insulin needs. GLP‑1 agonists have intrinsic counter‑regulatory mechanisms that markedly reduce this risk, a point highlighted by the trial cited in the discussion where liraglutide had a lower hypoglycaemia rate than glimepiride.  
3. **Weight‑neutral or loss** – HNF1A‑MODY patients are not typically obese, but the patient in the report had triglyceride‑rich metabolic syndrome. GLP‑1 agonists promote weight loss and improve lipid profiles, fitting the patient’s phenotype.  
4. **Renal considerations** – HNF1A mutations down‑regulate SGLT2; SGLT2 inhibitors can precipitate dehydration or ketoacidosis. GLP‑1 agonists are safe in this context; the report describes their successful use to achieve 6.0 % HbA1c in 3 months.  
5. **Personal preference or adverse effects** – If the patient experiences sulfonylurea‑related side effects (e.g., dizziness, nausea) or prefers once‑weekly injection, a GLP‑1 agonist is suitable.

In summary, sulfonylureas exploit preserved β‑cell mass for quick insulin release and are the textbook first‑line for HNF1A‑MODY, while GLP‑1 receptor agonists become the preferred choice when β‑cell reserve declines, hypoglycaemia risk is high, or metabolic benefits beyond glucose control are desired. The clinical course in the case report—switching from sulfonylureas to dulaglutide after genetic confirmation and achieving durable glycaemic control—illustrates this strategic shift.

### [Morning Report] The patient was initially treated with basal insulin, metformin and a DPP‑4 inhibitor. What clinical or laboratory indicators led to the transition to a GLP‑1 receptor agonist, and what advantages does this have over insulin in MODY?

**Morning Report – Clinical Teaching Answer**

The patient’s initial regimen—basal insulin degludec, metformin and a DPP‑4 inhibitor—was chosen empirically while T2D was being ruled out. However, several clinical and laboratory clues shifted the diagnosis toward MODY and prompted the transition to a GLP‑1 receptor agonist (GLP‑1RA):

| Indicator | What the data show | Why it pushes toward a GLP‑1RA for HNF1A‑MODY |
|-----------|--------------------|----------------------------------------------|
| **Preserved but sub‑optimal β‑cell function** (glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide 0.83 nmol/L) | Insulin secretion is not totally absent; β‑cells are still responsive to secretagogues. | GLP‑1RAs amplify glucose‑stimulated insulin release and improve β‑cell health, matching the residual function better than exogenous insulin, which provides no endogenous regulation. |
| **Strong family history of early‑onset diabetes** | Matrilineal onset in the late 20s‑30s and a younger brother at 20. | Classic MODY pattern; supports genetic etiology over exogenous insulin requirement. |
| **Negative islet autoantibodies** | GADA and IA‑2A were absent. | Rules out autoimmune type 1 diabetes; insulin therapy is unnecessary unless β‑cell function has largely disappeared. |
| **Rapid HbA1c drop with basal insulin** (10.2 % → 9.1 % in one month) | Insulin provided short‑term glycaemic control. | Demonstrates that insulin can lower glucose, but sustained control is achievable with a drug that preserves β‑cell function. |

Once the genetic test revealed the pathogenic HNF1A p.N270S mutation, the treating team discontinued insulin after confirming stable glycaemia (HbA1c 9.1 %). They started dulaglutide 1.5 mg weekly:

1. **Mechanistic fit** – HNF1A‑MODY causes defective insulin‑gene transcription and a gradual loss of β‑cell function. GLP‑1RAs enhance glucose‑dependent insulin secretion, slow β‑cell apoptosis, and improve post‑prandial control – precisely what the patient needed.
2. **Safety profile** – Sulfonylureas, the traditional first‑line therapy in HNF1A‑MODY, carry a hypoglycaemic risk that increases as β‑cell reserve wanes. GLP‑1RAs have a lower hypoglycaemic risk and avoid the need for frequent dosing adjustments.
3. **Efficacy evidence** – A small randomized crossover trial cited in the case showed liraglutide and glimepiride similarly lowered fasting and post‑prandial glucose, but GLP‑1RAs yielded fewer hypoglycaemic events. In this patient, HbA1c fell from 9.1 % to 6.0 % in 6 months, confirming superiority in this context.

In summary, **moderately preserved C‑peptide, a strong familial pattern, negative autoantibodies, and early genetic confirmation of HNF1A mutation** guided the shift from basal insulin to a GLP‑1RA. Compared with insulin, GLP‑1RAs preserve endogenous β‑cell function, offer a lower hypoglycaemic risk, and better align with the pathophysiology of HNF1A‑MODY.

### [Management] Discuss the potential risks of prescribing SGLT2 inhibitors to patients with HNF1A‑MODY and explain the underlying molecular mechanism that increases those risks.

**SGLT2 Inhibitors in HNF1A‑MODY: Why They’re a Red Flag**

In patients with HNF1A‑MODY, the **renal glucose‑transport machinery is already compromised** due to the loss‑of‑function mutation in the HNF1A transcription factor. HNF1A normally drives expression of **SGLT2 (the main renal glucose re‑absorber)**; when it is defective, SGLT2 levels fall, lowering the renal threshold for glucose and causing spontaneous glycosuria. Adding an SGLT2 inhibitor, which blocks the remaining transporter activity, can therefore have disproportionate consequences:

| Risk | Molecular underpinning | Clinical consequence |
|------|-----------------------|-----------------------|
| **Excessive glycosuria → dehydration** | Baseline SGLT2 is already reduced; inhibition pushes urinary glucose loss beyond the kidney’s compensatory capacity. | Rapid fluid loss, orthostatic hypotension, volume collapse. |
| **Euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA)** | SGLT2 inhibition lowers insulin secretion demand (via glucosuria), but the patient’s β‑cell reserve is modest (C‑peptide 0.83 nmol/L). | Accumulating ketones despite near‑normal glucose; often missed because glucose is low. |
| **Genital/mycotic infections** | High urinary glucose encourages fungal growth; patients already have high urine glucose from the disease. | Increased incidence of yeast infections, balanitis. |
| **Renal injury** | Hyperglycemic urine and dehydration can aggravate pre‑existing nephropathy. | Rise in albuminuria, decline in eGFR. |

The case report cites the very same mechanism: “**glycosuria in HNF1A‑MODY is attributed to reduced expression of SGLT2, which is under the transcriptional control of HNF1A**” and warns that “HNF1A‑MODY patients may experience severe dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis due to high inhibition of residual SGLT‑2 activity.” Thus, prescribing an SGLT2 inhibitor in this setting **exacerbates a pre‑existing defect** and pushes the patient toward potentially life‑threatening complications.

**Bottom line:** In HNF1A‑MODY, the kidney already leaks glucose because of low SGLT2 expression. Adding an SGLT2 blocker removes the last line of re‑absorption, leading to profound osmotic diuresis, dehydration, and an increased risk of euglycemic DKA. Avoid this class; instead, use agents that enhance endogenous insulin secretion (sulfonylureas, GLP‑1 agonists) or preserve β‑cell function (DPP‑4 inhibitors).

### [Clinical Pearls] What role does a MODY probability calculator play in the diagnostic workflow, and what are its limitations when applied to non‑White populations?

**Answer**

The MODY probability calculator is a bedside tool designed to triage patients for genetic testing. In practice it integrates key clinical variables—age at diagnosis, BMI, HbA1c, fasting C‑peptide, family history, and autoantibody status—to output a probability of a monogenic glucose‐regulation defect. In the case of the young Taiwanese man described above, inputting his data yielded a 62.4 % estimated risk of MODY, which warranted urgent next‑generation sequencing and ultimately confirmed an HNF1A‑c.809A>G (p.N270S) mutation.

**Key clinical roles**

| Role | Why it matters |
|------|----------------|
| **Screening** | Provides a rapid, data‑driven estimate of MODY likelihood, focusing costly molecular testing on the highest‑risk patients. |
| **Referral decision** | Helps clinicians decide whether to refer to a genetics service or to initiate targeted therapy (e.g., sulfonylurea or GLP‑1 agonist) before genetic confirmation. |
| **Family counselling** | A high probability score signals the need to assess relatives and consider cascade testing. |
| **Educational tool** | Reinforces awareness of MODY among clinicians who may otherwise attribute early‑onset diabetes to type 1 or type 2 disease. |

**Limitations in non‑White populations**

1. **Population derivation** – The calculator was derived from a predominantly White European cohort. Its logistic‑regression coefficients were fitted to that distribution and may not reflect the epidemiology of MODY in Asian, African or other non‑White groups.
2. **Differential prevalence** – Studies (e.g., Chen et al. in Chinese early‑onset T2D patients) report MODY prevalence ranging from 0.7 % to 1.5 %, with varying proportions of each subtype. If baseline prevalence differs, the absolute risk estimates shift.
3. **Variable clinical phenotype** – Certain MODY genes (e.g., HNF1B) can present with renal malformations that are under‑recognized in some ethnicities, potentially altering the relevance of clinical variables included in the model.
4. **Threshold uncertainty** – A “high‑risk” cutoff (often >20 %) may misclassify patients: over‑estimation leads to unnecessary testing, under‑estimation delays diagnosis. In the reported case, the calculator gave >60 %, but this figure may be inflated for a non‑White individual.
5. **Autoantibody and BMI patterns** – Autoantibody frequencies and obesity rates vary by ethnicity; the predictor weights for these variables may not be equally valid across groups.

**Take‑home point**

The MODY calculator is a useful first‑pass screening tool that can streamline genetic testing and early therapeutic decisions. However, clinicians should interpret its output with caution in non‑White patients, recognizing that its predictive accuracy may be reduced. Confirmation with genetic testing remains the gold standard, and individual clinical judgment—especially in the presence of strong family history or atypical features—should guide the decision to pursue sequencing.

### [Pathophysiology] From a pathophysiological standpoint, how does a mutation in the DNA‑binding domain of the HNF1A transcription factor lead to the observed biochemical abnormalities in glucose metabolism?

**Answer**

A heterozygous substitution in the DNA‑binding domain of HNF‑1α (p.N270S) disrupts the transcription factor’s ability to bind promoter elements of key genes that control β‑cell function and glucose handling. HNF‑1α normally activates transcription of insulin, *GLUT2*, and *SLC2A2*, as well as metabolic enzymes such as pyruvate kinase. Loss of binding activity causes:

1. **Reduced insulin gene expression** → inadequate pro‑insulin production and impaired insulin synthesis.
2. **Diminished *GLUT2* expression** in hepatocytes and pancreatic β‑cells → impaired glucose uptake into these cells and altered hepatic glycogen synthesis, contributing to fasting hyperglycaemia.
3. **Decreased *SGLT2* transcription in proximal tubules** → lower renal glucose reabsorption threshold, explaining the glycosuria seen in the patient even at modest hyperglycaemia.

Collectively, these perturbations lead to a progressive decline in endogenous insulin secretion (evidenced by the low stimulated C‑peptide of 0.83 nmol/L) and post‑prandial glucose spikes (2‑hour OGTT 19.8 mmol/L). The pattern of biochemical abnormality—fasting hyperglycaemia, marked post‑meal elevation, and glycosuria without ketoacidosis—is characteristic of HNF‑1α‑MODY and aligns with the patient’s clinical picture.

### [Management] Outline a follow‑up strategy for a patient with confirmed HNF1A‑MODY to monitor for long‑term complications, citing specific parameters and screening intervals.

**Follow‑up Plan for a Patient With Confirmed HNF1A‑MODY**

| Parameter | Target | Screening Frequency | Rationale |
|-----------|--------|---------------------|-----------|
| **Glycemic control** (HbA1c, fasting & post‑prandial glucose) | HbA1c < 6.5 % (≈38 mmol/mol) | Clinic visit **every 3 months**; fasting glucose at each office visit; post‑prandial glucose on a quarterly basis if dose adjustments are needed | HNF1A‑MODY progresses with progressive β‑cell failure; tight control is needed to prevent macro‑ and microvascular disease. |
| **β‑cell reserve** (C‑peptide) | Nadir > 0.6 nmol/L at 6‑min glucagon test in uncomplicated disease | **Every 12 months** (or sooner if HbA1c rises > 1 % or hypoglycaemia develops) | Declining C‑peptide predicts transition from sulfonylurea/GLP‑1 therapy to insulin. |
| **Renal function & microalbuminuria** (serum creatinine, eGFR, urine albumin‑creatinine ratio) | Normal eGFR > 60 mL/min/1.73 m²; ACR < 30 mg/g | **Annually** (or 6 months if ACR 30‑300 mg/g) | HNF1A mutations can impair SGLT2 expression; early nephropathy signals need for tighter control. |
| **Retinal exam** | Normal retinal findings | **Annually** (Dilated dilated fundus exam by an ophthalmologist) | Diabetic retinopathy is the most common complication in HNF1A‑MODY. |
| **Neuropathy** (sudomotor testing, vibration perception) | Normal or mild symptoms | **Every 12 months** (or sooner if symptoms develop) | The case report shows autonomic dysfunction; early detection guides treatment of neuropathic pain. |
| **Blood pressure** | < 130/80 mmHg | **Clinic visit** (same as HbA1c) | Hypertension accelerates nephropathy and retinopathy. |
| **Lipid profile** | LDL < 1.8 mmol/L (70 mg/dL) | **Every 12 months** | Dyslipidemia contributes to ASCVD; aggressive treatment improves long‑term CV outcomes. |
| **Weight/BMI** | Maintain BMI < 25 kg/m² | At each clinic visit | Modest weight gain worsens insulin resistance, even in MODY. |
| **Medication review** | Optimize GLP‑1/DPP‑4/Metformin regimen | **At each 3‑month visit** | Close monitoring prevents hypoglycaemia and preserves β‑cell function. |
| **SGLT‑2 inhibitor safety** | Avoid or use with caution | Not recommended unless compelling evidence; if used, monitor hydration status, ketones, and renal function closely | The case notes potential dehydration and ketoacidosis risk in HNF1A‑MODY due to reduced SGLT2 expression. |

**Key Points for Clinicians**

1. **Frequency**: Every 3 months for glycaemic control and medication adjustment; yearly for microvascular complications (retinopathy, nephropathy, neuropathy).  
2. **Early β‑cell decline**: C‑peptide monitoring informs transition to insulin if reserve falls below 0.6 nmol/L.  
3. **Avoid SGLT2 inhibitors** unless comorbidities justify therapy; if used, initiate with low dose and monitor for dehydration and ketoacidosis.  
4. **Family screening**: Offer first‑degree relatives genetic testing—early diagnosis allows proactive management and prevention of complications.  

This structured schedule, grounded in the patient’s documented course (HbA1c down to 6.0 % with GLP‑1/Metformin/DPP‑4 therapy and absent microalbuminuria/retinopathy to date), will help preserve organ function and mitigate the long‑term risks associated with HNF1A‑MODY.
