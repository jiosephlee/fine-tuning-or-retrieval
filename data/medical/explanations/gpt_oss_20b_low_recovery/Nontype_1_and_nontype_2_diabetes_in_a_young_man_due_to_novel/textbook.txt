Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

## 1. Clinical Presentation and Initial Evaluation

### Patient demographics and family history of diabetes  
The index patient is a young adult male who presented during a routine health check‑up. He is 23 years old and the first person in his family to be formally diagnosed with diabetes, although the pedigree illustrates a classic autosomal‑dominant pattern: maternal grandmother (diagnosed in her 30s), mother (late 20s), and younger brother (age 20). The absence of other diabetic relatives beyond this vertical transmission and the lack of an early‑onset cardiovascular or renal complication history further steer the differential towards a monogenic form rather than classical type 2 diabetes (T2D).

### BMI, blood pressure, and absence of acanthosis nigricans  
On examination, the patient’s height and weight yielded a body mass index (BMI) of 24.6 kg/m², which falls within the normal range. Blood pressure (112/70 mmHg) and heart rate (78 bpm) were also normal. Notably, there was no physical evidence of acanthosis nigricans—a cutaneous marker of insulin resistance that is frequently observed in obesity‑related T2D. The combination of a non‑obese BMI, normal blood pressure and absence of dysglycaemic skin changes is atypical for conventional T2D and supports the suspicion of a non‑autoimmune, monogenic etiology.

### Initial laboratory indices: HbA1c, fasting glucose, OGTT, lipid profile  
- **Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c):** 10.2 % (88 mmol/mol), indicating chronic hyperglycaemia over the preceding 3 months.  
- **Fasting plasma glucose:** 12.4 mmol/L, markedly above the diagnostic threshold for diabetes (≥ 7.0 mmol/L).  
- **Post‑load glucose:** 2‑hour OGTT value of 19.8 mmol/L, far exceeding the European threshold for diabetes (≥ 11.1 mmol/L).  
- **Lipid profile:** HDL‑C at 1.01 mmol/L (low) and triglycerides at 4.31 mmol/L (high), suggestive of dyslipidaemia and metabolic syndrome features. However, these alterations can occur early in HNF1A‑MODY, presenting a diagnostic ambiguity with T2D, which warrants careful interpretation in the context of other findings.

These values demonstrate pronounced fasting and post‑prandial hyperglycaemia—an early hallmark of HNF1A‑MODY—while also reflecting a metabolic milieu that appears to overlap with that of young‑onset T2D.

### Signs of glycosuria and glucose‑induced osmotic diuresis  
The patient reported increased thirst and appetite, accompanied by recent weight loss—typical of glucose‑induced osmotic diuresis. Urinalysis (not explicitly reported but inferred) would have shown glycosuria consistent with a reduced renal threshold for glucose reabsorption, a feature common to HNF1A mutations that impair SGLT2 expression in the proximal tubule. The presence of glycosuria in the setting of relatively modest post‑prandial hyperglycaemia strengthens the argument for a renal component to glucose handling rather than robust insulin resistance.

### Assessment of insulin‑sensitivity versus insulin‑secretion defects  
A glucagon stimulation test was performed. Baseline serum C‑peptide was 0.39 nmol/L, rising to 0.83 nmol/L at 6 min. In the face of an HbA1c > 10 %, these values represent relatively preserved endogenous insulin secretion, especially given the absence of an absolute insulin deficiency typical of juvenile‑onset type 1 diabetes. A stimulated C‑peptide > 0.60 nmol/L is generally accepted as evidence for some remaining β‑cell function, whereas values below this cut‑off suggest significant β‑cell loss. Thus, the patient exhibits an insulin‑secretion defect rather than an insulin‑resistance phenotype, a key discriminator in MODY versus classic T2D, where insulin resistance is the predominant driver.

### Autonomic dysfunction seen on sudomotor testing  
Electrochemical skin conductance testing revealed severely reduced sudomotor function over the hands, indicating small‑fiber autonomic neuropathy. While autonomic neuropathy is usually a long‑term complication of diabetes, early sensory changes may occur in HNF1A‑MODY, possibly due to subtle hyperglycaemic excursions and impaired β‑cell‑derived peptide signalling. The presence of neuropathy in a young, non‑obese patient further argues against a purely lifestyle‑driven T2D phenotype and supports a more complex metabolic derangement.

### Negative islet autoantibodies (GADA, IA‑2A)  
Both glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA) and islet antigen‑2 antibodies (IA‑2A) were negative. Autoantibody negativity virtually rules out autoimmune type 1 diabetes and identifies the patient as a candidate for monogenic testing. Importantly, HNF1A‑MODY patients are typically autoantibody‑negative, and the absence of these markers in combination with preserved C‑peptide levels and a strong family history constitutes a classic MODY signature.

## 2. Diagnostic Work‑up and Genetic Confirmation

A systematic approach is essential when evaluating a young adult with hyperglycaemia because the differential diagnosis ranges from autoimmune type 1 diabetes to monogenic forms such as MODY and to the more common type 2 diabetes (T2D). The diagnostic work‑up in this case combined clinical evaluation, targeted biochemical testing, antibody screening, assessment of endogenous insulin secretion, and definitive molecular genetics.  

### 1. Role of glucagon stimulation test and interpretation of stimulated C‑peptide values

The glucagon stimulation test (GST) remains one of the most practical bedside assays for estimating residual β‑cell function in adults. Glucagon is injected intravenously (1 mg) and serum C‑peptide is measured at baseline and 6 minutes thereafter. A stimulated C‑peptide of >0.6 nmol/L (≈0.7 ng/mL) is generally considered indicative of preserved endogenous insulin secretion, whereas values <0.3 nmol/L usually suggest marked β‑cell failure. In the present patient, baseline C‑peptide was 0.39 nmol/L, rising modestly to 0.83 nmol/L after glucagon. This pattern reflects an insufficient but still measurable β‑cell response, compatible with an early, partly preserved secretory defect rather than the absolute β‑cell destruction seen in type 1 diabetes. While the GST should not replace other measures, it provides crucial context when autoantibodies are negative and clinical features are atypical.

### 2. Differential diagnosis of hyperglycemia in young adult patients

When an adult under 30 presents with hyperglycaemia, the clinician must consider:  
- **Type 1 diabetes** (autoimmune β‑cell destruction) – typically rapid onset, ketoacidosis risk, positive islet autoantibodies.  
- **Type 2 diabetes** (insulin resistance with β‑cell failure) – usually associated with obesity or metabolic syndrome, often later onset but increasingly seen in younger people.  
- **MODY** (monogenic, often autosomal dominant) – early onset, mild to moderate hyperglycaemia, preserved insulin secretion, negative autoantibodies.  
- **Other monogenic forms** (e.g., neonatal diabetes, congenital hyperinsulinism) – may present earlier and have distinct biochemical profiles.

A detailed history (family history, age at diagnosis, metabolic features), physical examination (BMI, acanthosis nigricans), and laboratory data (HbA1c, fasting glucose, 2‑h OGTT) help narrow the differential. In this case, the absence of obesity, negative autoantibodies, a pronounced post‑prandial glucose spike, and a strong family pedigree pointed toward MODY.

### 3. Use of MODY probability calculators and their limitations

The Exeter MODY probability calculator incorporates age at diagnosis, BMI, HbA1c, C‑peptide, and family history to estimate the likelihood of a MODY mutation. While useful, the tool is derived from a predominantly White European cohort and may over‑ or underestimate the risk in other ethnic groups. In the patient described, the calculator yielded a probability of 62.4 % (≈1 in 1.6 chance), reinforcing the clinical suspicion. Nonetheless, the calculator should guide, not replace, clinician judgment and genetic testing.

### 4. Next‑generation sequencing panels for MODY genes

Once a MODY diagnosis is suspected, targeted next‑generation sequencing (NGS) panels covering the common MODY genes (HNF1A, GCK, HNF4A, HNF1B, etc.) are employed. A 24‑gene panel captures both known and novel variants. In this case, an NGS panel identified a heterozygous c.809A>G substitution in exon 4 of HNF1A, leading to the p.N270S amino acid change. The high coverage and depth of NGS allow for precise variant calling and detection of low‑frequency mutations.

### 5. ACMG criteria for variant pathogenicity assessment

The American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) provides a framework to classify variants as pathogenic (P), likely pathogenic (LP), variant of uncertain significance (VUS), likely benign, or benign. For the p.N270S variant:  
- **PM1** (located in a mutational hot‑spot/critical domain—DNA‑binding domain of HNF1A).  
- **PM2** (absent from population databases).  
- **PM5** (novel missense change at a residue where other pathogenic variants exist).  
- **PP3** (computational evidence suggesting deleterious effect; high AlphaMissense score).  
- **PP4** (patient’s phenotype highly specific for HNF1A-MODY).  
These combine to classify the variant as likely pathogenic, supporting its clinical relevance.

### 6. Segregation analysis in affected family members

Genetic confirmation is strengthened by demonstrating that the variant co‑segregates with disease in the family. The patient’s mother, maternal grandmother, and younger brother all harbored the same c.809A>G change and exhibited early‑onset hyperglycaemia, confirming autosomal dominant inheritance. Segregation analysis not only validates the variant’s pathogenicity but also informs risk counselling for relatives.

### 7. Clinical significance of the novel p.N270S mutation in exon 4

Exon 4 resides within the DNA‑binding domain of HNF1A, a region critical for transcriptional regulation of insulin and glucose‑transport genes. The substitution of asparagine to serine at position 270 likely disrupts DNA‑binding affinity or conformational stability, mirroring other pathogenic missense changes in the same domain. Functional studies (not performed here) would test transcriptional activity and insulin secretion, but the high computational pathogenicity scores and segregation data adequately support its clinical impact. Clinically, this mutation predicts a phenotype of progressive insulin secretory decline, responsiveness to sulfonylureas or GLP‑1 agonists, and a low risk of ketoacidosis compared to other MODY variants.

---

**Key Take‑away:** Accurate MODY diagnosis relies on a multidimensional work‑up that integrates clinical suspicion, biochemical discrimination of insulin secretion, exclusion of autoimmunity, and definitive genetic testing. In this patient, the concordance of negative antibodies, preserved but insufficient C‑peptide response, a strong family history, and a pathogenic HNF1A variant guided a transition from insulin to GLP‑1‑based therapy, illustrating precision medicine in action.

## 3. Pathophysiology and Molecular Basis of HNF1A‑MODY

Hepatocyte nuclear factor‑1 alpha (HNF1A) is a transcription factor that orchestrates the expression of a constellation of genes essential for the function of pancreatic β‑cells, the renal proximal tubule, and the liver. Loss‑of‑function mutations in this gene precipitate the classic MODY phenotype by progressively impairing insulin secretion and deranging glucose handling in these organs.

### Structure of the HNF1A protein

The HNF1A protein (631 amino acids) is a dimeric homeodomain transcription factor that contains two functional domains:

* **DNA‑binding domain (DBD)** – A pair of highly conserved winged‑helix motifs (residues 27–140 and 154–221) that directly contact the major groove of target promoters. Most disease‑causing mutations cluster in this region and disrupt DNA recognition, dimerisation, or both.

* **Transactivation domain (TAD)** – The C‑terminal segment (residues 222–631) recruits general transcription machinery and chromatin modifiers, amplifying the transcriptional response. The TAD is relatively tolerant to missense changes, though some pathogenic variants here destabilise protein folding and promote degradation.

The novel p.N270S mutation reported in this case lies just outside the canonical DBD, within the hinge region that is crucial for efficient dimerisation and proper orientation of the DNA‑binding motifs.

### Regulation of insulin, GLUT2, and pyruvate kinase gene expression

In β‑cells, HNF1A activates a network of metabolic regulators:

1. **Insulin (INS) gene** – HNF1A binds to enhancers in the INS promoter, priming RNA polymerase II for rapid transcription after glucose stimulation. Reduced HNF1A activity diminishes baseline insulin mRNA, limiting the reserve of insulin that can be mobilised during a glucose challenge.

2. **Glucose transporter 2 (GLUT2)** – HNF1A enhances transcription of *SLC2A2*, encoding GLUT2, the primary glucose transporter that balances extracelluar glucose influx with insulin secretion. Loss‑of‑function lowers GLUT2 levels, attenuating glucose sensing and leading to a delayed insulin response.

3. **Pyruvate kinase (PKM2 subtype)** – HNF1A drives *PKM* expression, which accelerates the final step of glycolysis, generating ATP that closes KATP channels and depolarises the β‑cell. Diminished PK activity reduces the metabolic coupling factor, blunting insulin exocytosis.

Collectively, these effects manifest clinically as a post‑prandial glucose surge with a relatively preserved fasting glucose (unless β‑cell mass is severely compromised), a pattern characteristic of HNF1A‑MODY.

### Effects of HNF1A mutations on β‑cell development and insulin secretion

During pancreatic organogenesis, HNF1A regulates genes critical for β‑cell differentiation (e.g., PDX1, NKX6.1). Heterozygous loss‑of‑function mutations impair this developmental program, resulting in a reduced β‑cell pool and a lower threshold for dysfunction. In the adult pancreas, the same transcriptional dysregulation leads to:

* **Decreased insulin gene transcription** – manifesting as low stimulated C‑peptide levels (as seen in the patient’s glucagon‑stimulation test).
* **Impaired glucose‑induced metabolic signalling** – due to reduced GLUT2 and pyruvate kinase, blunting the KATP‑dependent exocytosis machinery.
* **Accelerated β‑cell exhaustion** – repeated cycles of sub‑optimal insulin release place chronic demand on residual β‑cells, hastening functional decline.

These mechanisms explain the hyperglycaemia, glycosuria, and eventual need for pharmacologic augmentation observed in the patient, despite the absence of autoimmune β‑cell destruction.

### Renal transport implications: SGLT2 expression and glycosuria

HNF1A also modulates the *SLC5A2* gene, encoding the sodium‑glucose cotransporter‑2 (SGLT2) expressed in the proximal tubular cells. Pathogenic mutations:

* **Lower SGLT2 transcription** – reducing the renal threshold for glucose reabsorption. Even modest reductions in SGLT2 activity can make the kidneys less efficient at reclaiming glucose, leading to glycosuria at plasma concentrations that would otherwise be renal‑clearance‑compatible.

* **Enhance risk for osmotic diuresis** – excessive glucose in the tubular lumen draws out water, contributing to mild dehydration and, in severe cases, predisposing to ketoacidosis. The patient’s case illustrates this phenomenon with self‑reported polyuria and weight loss without overt ketoacidosis.

The glycosuria seen in HNF1A‑MODY is therefore a direct consequence of transcriptional down‑regulation of SGLT2 rather than a secondary effect of high plasma glucose.

### Impact on hepatic glucose metabolism and lipid profile

In the liver, HNF1A drives expression of enzymes involved in gluconeogenesis (e.g., *G6PC*) and lipogenesis. Heterozygous loss functions leads to:

* **Reduced gluconeogenic capacity** – paradoxically, HNF1A deficiency can lower hepatic glucose output, partially compensating for impaired insulin secretion. This may explain the relatively lower fasting glucose compared to what would be expected in pure insulin deficiency.

* **Altered lipid handling** – HNF1A regulates genes such as *FADS1* and *SREBP‑c1*, affecting fatty acid desaturation and triglyceride synthesis. Patients often present with dyslipidemia, including hypertriglyceridemia, as seen in the case (4.31 mmol/L triglycerides). The interplay between impaired insulin secretion and hepatic lipogenesis contributes to a metabolic‑syndrome‑like phenotype, albeit usually milder than that in type‑2 diabetes.

### Mechanisms underlying progressive β‑cell dysfunction in MODY

The trajectory of β‑cell decline in HNF1A‑MODY is driven by a combination of genetic and metabolic insults:

1. **Genetic haploinsufficiency** – A single pathogenic allele reduces HNF1A protein levels and activity to ~50 % of normal, insufficient for full transcriptional control.

2. **Compromised compensatory pathways** – Even though other transcription factors (e.g., PDX1, MAFA) can partially compensate, they cannot fully restore GLUT2 or insulin gene expression.

3. **Metabolic stress from chronic hyperglycaemia** – Persistent post‑prandial hyperglycaemia promotes oxidative stress, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and further β‑cell exhaustion.

4. **Intrinsic apoptosis signals** – HNF1A mutations have been linked to up‑regulation of pro‑apoptotic pathways (BAX, CHOP), thereby accelerating β‑cell loss over time.

The net result is a slowly progressive loss of insulin reserve, manifested clinically by rising HbA1c and the eventual need for insulin or other secretagogue therapy. Importantly, the progression is typically less aggressive than in type‑1 diabetes, aligning with the patient’s relatively stable β‑cell function despite significant hyperglycaemia.

In sum, the novel p.N270S mutation disrupts one of the pivotal domains of HNF1A, leading to widespread transcriptional dysregulation in β‑cells, the kidney, and liver. This cascade produces the constellation of hyperglycaemia, glycosuria, dyslipidaemia, and progressive β‑cell failure that defines HNF1A‑MODY and underscores the necessity of targeted genetic testing and precision‑guided therapy.

## 4. Therapeutic Strategy and Clinical Course

The patient’s initial presentation with markedly elevated HbA1c (10.2 %) and fasting glucose (12.4 mmol/L) warranted rapid glycaemic control. While awaiting definitive genetic testing, the treating team elected an empiric regimen that addressed both insulin deficiency and the possibility of insulin resistance: basal insulin degludec (8 U daily), metformin (1,500 mg daily) and the dipeptidyl peptidase‑4 (DPP‑4) inhibitor saxagliptin (5 mg daily). This combination is commonly used in early type 2 diabetes and provides a measure of insulin‑sparing effect from metformin while allowing rapid correction of hyperglycaemia with basal insulin.

The decision to discontinue basal insulin after one month was prompted by two key findings. First, the glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide rose to 0.83 nmol/L, evidence of residual β‑cell function that suggested the patient was not in a state of absolute insulin deficiency typical of type 1 diabetes. Second, genetic testing revealed a heterozygous pathogenic variant in HNF1A, the hallmark of HNF1A‑MODY. In HNF1A‑MODY, first‑line pharmacotherapy often involves agents that stimulate insulin secretion without provoking hypoglycaemia. Sulfonylureas are traditionally the drug of choice; however, they carry a higher hypoglycaemic risk and can be counter‑productive as β‑cell reserve declines. Accordingly, the treatment team transitioned the patient to the GLP‑1 receptor agonist (GLP‑1 RA) dulaglutide (1.5 mg weekly). GLP‑1 RAs augment glucose‑dependent insulin release, suppress glucagon, delay gastric emptying and promote weight loss, all of which align with the pathophysiology of HNF1A‑MODY and mitigate the risk of hypoglycaemia.

Within three months of initiating dulaglutide, the patient’s HbA1c fell to 6.0 %, illustrating a potent glucose‑lowering effect in a single agent. After six months, the patient was stable on a simplified dual‑therapy regimen of metformin (1,500 mg daily) and saxagliptin (5 mg daily). This transition capitalised on the enduring insulin‑secretion stimulus provided by the DPP‑4 inhibitor while maintaining metformin’s effect on hepatic gluconeogenesis and improving insulin sensitivity. Throughout this period, fasting glucose remained consistently <7.0 mmol/L, HbA1c stayed below 6.5 %, and the patient achieved a modest weight loss of 3 kg, reinforcing the metabolic benefits of the GLP‑1 RA phase.

SULFONYLUREAS and SGLT2 inhibitors were deliberately avoided. Sulfonylureas, though effective in HNF1A‑MODY, have a narrow therapeutic window and an elevated hypoglycaemic risk, which can lead to adverse events, especially in patients with declining β‑cell function. SGLT2 inhibitors pose a particular concern in HNF1A‑MODY because the HNF1A transcription factor regulates renal SGLT2 expression; a loss‑of‑function mutation may predispose to severe dehydration, euglycaemic ketoacidosis and other renal complications. Consequently, these classes were excluded from the therapeutic plan.

Serial ophthalmologic examinations, fundoscopic imaging, and retinal photography were performed at baseline and annually to screen for diabetic retinopathy, given the high prevalence of microvascular sequelae in progressive hyperglycaemia. At the six‑month follow‑up, ocular assessment remained normal, and urinary albumin excretion was below the microalbuminuria threshold, supporting the effectiveness of the glycaemic control achieved. Blood pressure, lipid profile, and a complete metabolic panel were monitored every three months, with no evidence of hypertension, dyslipidaemia or hepatic dysfunction.

In summary, the patient’s therapeutic journey exemplifies a precision‑medicine approach: empiric therapy for rapid correction of severe hyperglycaemia, early genetic confirmation guiding discontinuation of insulin and transition to a GLP‑1 RA, followed by long‑term maintenance with metformin plus DPP‑4 inhibition. This strategy achieved durable glycaemic control, avoided drugs with high hypoglycaemic or adverse renal risk profiles, and instituted a robust surveillance plan for microvascular complications, thereby aligning clinical management with the underlying monogenic pathology.

## 5. Clinical Significance, Precision Medicine, and Future Directions

The present case illustrates several practical lessons that extend beyond the individual patient. First, it demonstrates how a seemingly routine presentation of hyperglycaemia in a young adult can mask a monogenic form of diabetes that is often missed when clinicians rely solely on the classic type 1/type 2 dichotomy. In this patient, the combination of markedly elevated fasting glucose, severe post‑prandial glucose rise, glycosuria, early‑onset family members, and preserved—but not robust—β‑cell function on glucagon stimulation would all raise suspicion for MODY. Yet, initial management mirrored standard type 2 diabetes care, with insulin, metformin, and a DPP‑4 inhibitor. Only after genetic confirmation was the therapeutic strategy altered to a GLP‑1 receptor agonist, leading to rapid glycaemic improvement and the successful discontinuation of insulin. This sequence underscores the tangible clinical benefit that timely, precise diagnostic testing can provide, preventing over‑immunotherapy, reducing medication burden, and allowing for more physiologic insulin stimulation.

### Misdiagnosis of MODY as type 1 or type 2 diabetes and its consequences

In most clinical settings, early‑onset diabetes is automatically classified as type 1, especially when the patient presents with ketoacidosis or autoantibody positivity. Conversely, patients with mild fasting hyperglycaemia and a BMI approaching the normal range are frequently labelled type 2, particularly when they respond to metformin or sulfonylureas. Mislabeling a MODY patient as type 1 leads to unnecessary insulin injections and psychological burden, while misclassifying as type 2 can result in the use of insulin‑sparing agents that may be ineffective (e.g., sulfonylureas in HNF1A‑MODY up to the point of β‑cell exhaustion). This misdiagnosis not only delays optimal therapy but also complicates family counselling, as the inheritable risk of diabetes may be underestimated.

### Value of family history and early‑onset clinical features in suspecting MODY

A strong, multi‑generational history of diabetes that appears before age 30, absent the features of type 1 autoimmunity or type 2 metabolic syndrome, should immediately trigger consideration of MODY. Features that heighten suspicion include non‑obese BMI (<25 kg/m²), absence of acanthosis nigricans, mild insulin resistance, and early presentation of post‑prandial hyperglycaemia with a relatively low fasting glucose. In the present case, the patient’s BMI was 24.6 kg/m² and his family had a clear vertical transmission pattern, but none of the relatives had reported microvascular complications at the time of diagnosis. Incorporating a structured MODY questionnaire into routine assessment of young diabetics could improve detection rates.

### Implications of novel pathogenic variants for genetic counselling

The identification of a novel heterozygous HNF1A variant (p.N270S) that had not been catalogued in ClinVar or VarSome raises important points for counselling. First, segregation analysis in the family can confirm pathogenicity when the variant tracks with disease status. Second, the variant’s predicted impact on the DNA‑binding domain informs both risk assessment and therapeutic responsiveness—mutations in this domain often preserve some β‑cell function and thereby predict a better response to oral agents. Finally, patients should receive education about the 50 % penetrance of autosomal‑dominant MODY phenotypes, thereby enabling informed decisions regarding screening of offspring and other at‑risk relatives.

### Therapeutic implications: sulfonylureas vs GLP‑1 agonists and risk of hypoglycaemia

Sulfonylureas remain the cornerstone of pharmacologic therapy for HNF1A‑MODY because they directly stimulate the pancreatic β‑cell ATP‑sensitive potassium channel, thereby enhancing insulin secretion. However, the risk of hypoglycaemia increases as endogenous insulin production wanes. In the early stages, low‑dose sulfonylureas can be effective, but as the disease progresses, GLP‑1 receptor agonists provide a safer alternative by promoting glucose‑dependent insulin release and inhibiting glucagon secretion, with a lower hypoglycaemic burden. In this patient, after an initial period of insulin plus adjunctive medications, the transition to dulaglutide achieved a sustained HbA1c reduction and ultimately allowed simplification to metformin plus a DPP‑4 inhibitor—a strategy that balances efficacy, tolerability, and patient adherence.

### Challenges of screening in diverse ethnic populations

Incidence estimates for MODY vary substantially across ethnic groups, in part due to referral bias and the underrepresentation of non‑European cohorts in genetic studies. Additionally, the MODY probability calculator derived from Midwestern UK data over‑estimates risk in Asian populations because of differing BMI distributions and metabolic phenotypes. Therefore, screening algorithms must be recalibrated with population‑specific parameters, and clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion even when traditional risk scores are low.

### Advances in next‑generation sequencing and variant databases

NGS panels covering the full spectrum of MODY‑associated genes (HNF1A, GCK, HNF4A, HNF1B, and others) have become more affordable and rapid, enabling widespread application in clinical practice. The rapid expansion of variant databases such as ClinVar, gnomAD, and specialized MODY registries provides contextual information for new findings; however, novel variants still require functional validation or segregation analysis. Tools such as AlphaMissense and in silico predictors, coupled with ACMG criteria, facilitate classification, but the ultimate clinical decision must integrate phenotypic concordance.

### Future research needs: biomarkers, cost‑effective screening algorithms, and expanding mutation catalogs

Key research gaps remain. Development of biomarkers that reliably distinguish MODY from type 1 and type 2 diabetes—such as β‑cell–specific microRNAs, metabolomic signatures, or high‑resolution C‑peptide dynamics—could obviate the need for invasive antibody panels. In addition, cost‑effective screening algorithms that combine clinical features, laboratory data, and targeted genetic testing would improve resource allocation, particularly in low‑income settings. Finally, systematic efforts to document novel mutations in diverse populations, including functional assays in pancreatic β‑cell models, will enrich public databases and refine genotype‑phenotype correlations, ultimately guiding personalized therapy.

In summary, this case underscores the vital role of precision medicine in the management of young‑onset diabetes. Early identification of MODY through a combination of thorough clinical assessment and targeted genetic testing not only optimises therapeutic outcomes but also informs family counselling and preventive strategies. Continued advances in genomics, informatics, and translational research will further reduce the clinical burden of misdiagnosis and improve the quality of life for patients with monogenic diabetes.
