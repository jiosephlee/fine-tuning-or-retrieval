# When a Young Patient’s Blood Sugar Doesn’t Fit the Ordinary Diabetes Puzzle  

## A story that started in a routine health exam

You’re at the office, the receptionist has the patient’s chart in hand, and the kid in front of you has a diagnosis scribbled on the paper: “Type 2 diabetes, HbA1c 10.2 %.” The lab report shows a fasting glucose of 12.4 mM, a post‑OGTT peak of 19.8 mM, and—curious to mention—it pours glucose into the urine, yet no signs of ketoacidosis. Blood pressure is 112/70 mmHg, BMI is a lean 24.6 kg/m², and there are no hints of acanthosis nigricans. That’s the scene reported by Liu et al. for a 25‑year‑old Chinese man who turned out to have a novel HNF1A mutation that causes maturity‑onset diabetes of the young (MODY).  

What made this case *surprising* wasn’t the high HbA1c or the hyperglycaemia; it was the mismatch between the clinical picture and the usual diabetes archetypes. In the fast‑paced world of endocrinology, a diagnosis of type‑1 or type‑2 diabetes seems almost automatic once a K g/L fails fasting glucose or HbA1c hits a threshold. But that night, as your colleagues debated insulin therapy versus sulfonylureas, a humble list of clues began to point elsewhere: a dramatic, multigenerational family history of early‑onset diabetes, a lack of autoantibodies, a low C‑peptide reserve that was not zero, and a normal BMI.

## The tell‑ta‑tale clues that should trigger a MODY work‑up

| Clue | Why it matters | How it fits the case |
|------|----------------|----------------------|
| **Onset before age 25** | MODY is a monogenic, autosomal‑dominant form of diabetes that appears in childhood or early adulthood. | The patient is 25, first clinical presentation at 24 y. |
| **Unexplained glycosuria with no ketoacidosis** | Certain MODY subtypes (e.g. HNF1A) reduce renal SGLT2 expression, lowering the glucose threshold for reabsorption. | Urinalysis showed glucose excretion while ketones were absent. |
| **Family history of early‑onset diabetes** | Classic autosomal dominant inheritance; multiple generations show the disease by their 20s–30s. | Grandmother (30s), mother (late 20s), younger brother (20). |
| **Non‑obese, non‑metabolic phenotype** | MODY patients often have a normal BMI and minimal dyslipidaemia, unlike typical type‑2 diabetes. | BMI 24.6 kg/m², LDL normal, HDL low‑borderline, TG 4.31 mmol/L but no acanthosis nigricans. |
| **Negative autoantibodies (GADA, IA‑2A, ZnT8)** | Distinguishes non‑autoimmune diabetes from type‑1. | All autoantibodies were negative. |
| **Preserved-but‑low C‑peptide** | Suggests a defect in insulin secretion rather than absolute loss (type‑1) or resistance (type‑2). | Glucagon‑stimulation test: 0.83 nmol/L <0.6 nmol/L threshold for preserved function, but still detectable. |
| **Early‑in‑treatment insulin requirement that can be stopped** | In HNF1A‑MODY, sulfonylureas or GLP‑1 agonists often suffice; insulin is rarely needed long‑term. | After genetic confirmation, insulin was discontinued in favour of dulaglutide and metformin, with HbA1c dropping to 6.0 %. |

Seeing even three of these on the same patient should make a clinician pause. “Why isn’t this a textbook type‑2 case?” the question is all anyone will wonder, but the answer lies in the subtlety of the presentation.

## Using an algorithm to filter the “likely MODY” candidates

The Exeter MODY probability calculator has become the bread‑and‑butter tool in many practices. It asks for age, BMI, family history, autoantibody status, and C‑peptide values. When the case above was entered, the algorithm returned a 62.4 % probability of MODY—a figure that would normally spark a referral for genetic testing.  

A few caveats:

- It was derived from a White European cohort; applying it to Asian or African populations may skew risk estimates, but in practice it still flag higher odds than the default 1‑in‑10 000 prevalence.
- Because the calculator weights family history heavily, a patient with a robust pedigree will get a high score even if other features are mild.
- A low BMI can tip the needle toward MODY; in an era of rising obesity, that may be under‑recognized.

So, the algorithm is a **clinical rapid‑fire test, not a definitive diagnosis**. Once a patient throws up the characteristic flags, the next step is to order a MODY gene panel, typically via a next‑generation sequencing (NGS) panel that covers the 15 known MODY genes (e.g., HNF1A, GCK, HNF1B, HNF4A).

## Why genetics matters – and how it altered therapy

In the anecdote, the patient was initially started on basal insulin, metformin, and a DPP‑4 inhibitor (saxagliptin) because clinicians behaved as a typical type‑2 provider would. After just one month, HbA1c fell to 9.1 %—still high, but a good sign, so they kept insulin. Genetic sequencing revealed a novel heterozygous variant: **c.809A>G (p.N270S) in HNF1A**. This variant was absent from ClinVar and VarSome, but it satisfied ACMG criteria for pathogenicity (novel, in a critical DNA‑binding domain, high in silico deleteriousness, familial segregation).

Why did this change the approach so dramatically?

| Initial Regimen | Post‑MODY Diagnosis | Rationale |
|-----------------|--------------------|-----------|
| Basal insulin + metformin + saxagliptin | GLP‑1 RA (dulaglutide) + metformin | GLP‑1 agonists potentiate glucose‑dependent insulin secretion and provide weight neutrality, reducing hypoglycaemia risk. |
| Continue insulin + add sulfonylurea | Switch to sulfonylurea (e.g., glimepiride) **or** add GLP‑1 | Sulfonylureas have long been the first‑line for HNF1A‑MODY, but can precipitate hypoglycaemia as β‑cell function wanes. GLP‑1 RA offers safer glucose control. |
| None | Add SGLT‑2 inhibitor? | Not recommended; HNF1A‑MODY patients may have impaired renal glucose reabsorption and increased risk of ketoacidosis with SGLT‑2 blockage. |

The patient’s HbA1c eventually dropped to 6.0 %, maintained with metformin + saxagliptin. Crucially, insulin was never re‑introduced, preserving his endogenous β‑cell function and reducing the burden of injections.

## Clinical pearls & take‑home messages

- **High suspicion, low threshold**: Any young adult with hyperglycaemia, a strong family history of early diabetes, and a non‑obese, non‑metabolic phenotype should undergo a MODY probability screen.
- **Autoantibody negativity + preserved C‑peptide**: Negative GADA, IA‑2A, ZnT8 and a glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide above 0.6 nmol/L (or 0.5 ng/mL) are key pointers toward MODY rather than type‑1 or type‑2.
- **Genetic confirmation allows *drug repurposing***: HNF1A‑MODY responds brilliantly to sulfonylureas or GLP‑1 agonists; insulin can often be replaced or avoided.
- **Beware of SGLT‑2 inhibitors**: In HNF1A‑MODY, diminished SGLT2 activity can predispose to dehydration and ketoacidosis; use with extreme caution.
- **Family screening matters**: A confirmed MODY mutation justifies testing at least two relatives per generation; early diagnosis translates to better management and less morbidity.

## Bottom line

The case by Liu et al. is a textbook reminder that diabetes is not a one‑size‑fits‑all diagnosis. When you see a young, lean patient with dramatic hyperglycaemia, a striking family history, and a positive glucose excretion but no ketoacidosis, **think MODY**. A quick reference tool plus a gene panel can change the therapeutic landscape, spare the patient from lifelong insulin, and give your family a head‑start in monitoring. Keep the red flags in mind, and you’ll be doing your patients— and your own clinical practice— a lot of good.



# Decoding the HNF1A Gene: From Novel Mutation to Targeted Therapy  

## Why this case grabbed our attention  

You’d probably think a 24‑year‑old with a BMI of 24.6 kg/m² and non‑diabetic‑type 2‑looking labs would be a textbook Type 2 diabetes patient—except the family history screamed hereditary, the C‑peptide was damp, and the boy had no antibodies. The plot twist came with the discovery of a **novel pathogenic change (p.N270S) in the DNA‑binding domain of HNF1A**. That single amino‑acid swap led to a dramatic transition from insulin injections to a GLP‑1 agonist and then to a less intensive oral regimen—all while bringing HbA1c down from >10 % to <6 % in under six months.

## A quick refresher on HNF1A  

- **Location & structure**: HNF1A sits on chromosome 12q24, encodes a 631‑aa transcription factor with a *DNA‑binding domain (DBD)* (exons 2–5) and a *transactivation domain (TAD)* (exons 6–10).  
- **Tissue expression**: Pancreatic β‑cells, liver, kidney.  
- **Functional role**: Activates key genes for insulin synthesis/ secretion (INS, GLUT2) and glucose transport in kidney tubules (SGLT2).  
- **Why the DBD matters**: Mutations here alter the protein’s ability to dock onto promoter sequences—think of it as a broken key that can’t turn the lock.

## Pathogenesis explained

1. **β‑cell failure**  
   - Loss of transcriptional activation → ↓ insulin gene expression → progressive insulin secretion decline.  
   - Unlike autoimmune destruction (T1D), the defect is intrinsic to the cell’s regulatory machinery.

2. **Renal glucose handling**  
   - HNF1A regulates SGLT2 expression. Reduced SGLT2 = lower renal threshold → *glycosuria* even at modest hyperglycemia.  
   - That’s why the patient had glycosuria without diabetic ketoacidosis.

3. **Implications for SGLT2 inhibitors**  
   - In HNF1A‑MODY, SGLT2 activity is already low; adding an inhibitor can tip the patient into dehydration and ketoacidosis.  
   - Hence, SGLT2 inhibitors are generally avoided in MODY patients.

## The novelty: p.N270S

- **Location**: Residue 270 sits right in the DBD—adjacent to many known pathogenic sites.  
- **Predicted effect**: AlphaMissense score 0.995 → near‑maximum deleteriousness.  
- **Segregation**: Found in affected family members, absent in unaffected siblings → classic autosomal dominant pattern.  
- **ACMG grading**: Meets criteria for *pathogenic* (P) due to functional domain impact, segregation, and predictive modeling.

## Translating genetics into therapy  

| Initial suspicion | First‑line | Result | Pivot | Final regimen | Outcome |
|--------------------|-----------|--------|-------|---------------|---------|
| MODY ↔ T1/T2 ambiguity | Basal insulin + metformin + saxagliptin | HbA1c ↓ 1.1 % (10.2 → 9.1 %) | Genetic confirmation (p.N270S) | **GLP‑1 RA (dulaglutide)** → ↓ HbA1c (9.1 → 6.0 % in 6 mo) | Sustained control < 6.5 % |


Key take‑aways:

- **Start early with glucose‑lowering, but expect to shift once genotype is known.**  
- **GLP‑1 receptor agonists are first‑choice in HNF1A-MODY when β‑cell reserve is preserved.** They enhance insulin secretion in a glucose‑dependent manner and protect β‑cell mass.  
- **Sulfonylureas remain an option** for patients with later β‑cell decline, but carry a hypoglycemia risk in young, lean patients.  
- **Metformin remains useful**—it tackles hepatic insulin resistance and keeps the regimen oral once basal insulin is avoided.

## Clinical pearls you can pull into practice  

- **Think MODY in any young patient (< 30 yrs) with a strong family history, negative autoantibodies, and preserved fasting C‑peptide**—even if BMI is normal or mild.  
- **Use the Exeter MODY calculator, but interpret cautiously in non‑White populations**—the tool was trained on a European cohort.  
- **Order a glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide**—a level > 0.60 nmol/L suggests non‑autoimmune diabetes.  
- **Avoid SGLT2 inhibitors in suspected MODY** unless you’ve ruled out HNF1A/SGLT2 interplay.  
- **Once a pathogenic HNF1A variant is confirmed, consider stepping down insulin** in favour of GLP‑1 analogues or sulfonylureas, monitoring glycemia closely.

## Looking ahead

- **NGS panels for MODY are becoming cost‑effective**; early genetic testing can spare patients from years on insulin or ineffective sulfonylureas.  
- **Functional assays for novel variants** (e.g., reporter gene activation) could refine pathogenicity judgments and personalize therapy.  
- **Screening family members** is crucial—early diagnosis allows pre‑diabetic lifestyle modifications and better long‑term outcomes.

### Bottom line

A single point mutation (p.N270S) in the DNA‑binding domain of HNF1A can unleash a cascade of β‑cell dysfunction and renal glucose spill‑over. Recognizing this signature early, confirming it with genetic testing, and switching to GLP‑1 driven therapy (or low‑dose sulfonylureas) transforms a potentially lifelong insulin dependence into a manageable, low‑hypoglycemia risk regimen. Precision medicine isn’t just a buzzword—it’s the difference between a patient on endless basal insulin and one comfortably living on a GLP‑1 shot and a tablet.



# Glucagon‑Stimulated C‑Peptide Re‑Considered: A Practical Guide for the Emergency Room

## Why this case is a show‑stopper

Our young patient came to the lab for a routine health exam and, instead of a normal glucose curve, we discovered a 10.2 % HbA1c, fasting glucose of 12.4 mmol/L and a 2‑hour OGTT of 19.8 mmol/L. He was sweating, thirsty, had lost weight, but the family history looked suspicious – a classic MODY tease. What set him apart, beyond the sad numbers, was a low‑dose glucagon stimulation test that yielded a 6‑minute C‑peptide of just 0.83 nmol/L – low but not zero. That subtle hint opened the door to a different therapeutic universe: from daily insulin to a GLP‑1 agonist and finally to a metformin‑plus‑DPP‑4 strategy. In an ER setting, where decisions must be rapid, we often rely on quick surrogate markers of β‑cell function, and glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide (GSP) is one of our best bets.

## The glucagon test in a nutshell

| Step | What happens | Why it matters |
|------|---------------|----------------|
| **Baseline** | Collect fasting blood for C‑peptide | Sets the “resting” insulin secretory tone |
| **Glucagon infusion** | 1 mg glucagon IV or SC | Drives counter‑regulatory rise in glucose → insulin secretion |
| **Blood draw at 6 min** | Measure C‑peptide | Captures peak β‑cell response (most people see a peak ≈ 30–45 min, but 6 min is a convenient surrogate for bedside use) |
| **Interpretation** | <0.6 nmol/L: poor reserve; ≥ 0.6 nmol/L: preserved | Threshold derived from large cohort studies; acts as a gatekeeper for sulfonylureas vs insulin vs GLP‑1 |

In the ER we rarely get the luxury of a full 30‑minute OGTT or a stimulated C‑peptide that takes a day to return. A single glucagon dose and a 6‑minute draw give us a rapid, practical snapshot of β‑cell health.

## Interpreting the numbers – what do we see?

| Category | C‑peptide (6 min) | Clinical implication |
|----------|-------------------|-----------------------|
| **Preserved** | ≥ 0.6 nmol/L | Good β‑cell reserve → consider oral agents (sulfonylureas, GLP‑1 agonists) |
| **Intermediate** | 0.3–0.6 nmol/L | Partial reserve → high‑dose sulfonylureas or low‑dose insulin plus oral |
| **Low** | < 0.3 nmol/L | Little to no reserve → insulin therapy likely required |

> **Note:** These thresholds are **guidelines**, not hard rules. A patient’s age, duration of hyperglycaemia, and comorbidities can shift the decision curve.

In our case, the patient’s 0.83 nmol/L fell into the “preserved” bracket, yet his HbA1c was soaring. This discordance raised the question: **Is he a typical type 2 or an underlying monogenic form?** The negative autoantibodies, strong family history, and the glucagon response nudged us toward a MODY work‑up.

## From insulin to oral: the therapeutic domino

1. **Baseline insulin (degludec) + metformin + DPP‑4 inhibitor**  
   *Goal:* Quickly bring down the erratic glucose spikes while we await genetic confirmation.

2. **Genetic confirmation of HNF1A‑MODY**  
   *Effect:* Eliminates the “type 2” label, shifts our therapeutic lens.

3. **Discontinue insulin; start GLP‑1 agonist (dulaglutide)**  
   *Why GLP‑1?*  
   - Stimulates glucose‑dependent insulin release (no hypoglycaemia risk)  
   - Reduces weight (the patient had a BMI of 24.6, but metabolic syndrome was present)  
   - Preserves β‑cell function over time  
   - Avoids sulfonylureas that can cause long‑term hypoglycaemia in MODY

4. **Once HbA1c stabilised (< 6.5 %)**  
   *Mode:* Metformin + DPP‑4 inhibitor (saxagliptin) for maintenance  
   *Outcome:* Sustained glycaemic control, no microalbuminuria or retinopathy after 6 months.

### Practical take‑away for the ER

- **Ask for a glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide when you suspect early‑onset diabetes but lack autoantibodies.**  
- **If the 6‑minute C‑peptide is ≥ 0.6 nmol/L, do not set down insulin immediately.**  
- **Use the glucagon response as a built‑in “MODY test.”**  
- **If family history is strong and C‑peptide is preserved, send a MODY panel (NGS) ASAP.**  
- **Once a monogenic mutation is found, transition from insulin to a GLP‑1 or sulfonylurea early, and avoid SGLT‑2 inhibitors unless carefully monitored.**  

## Why this matters for us

- **Reduced hypoglycaemia** – GLP‑1 agonists don’t trigger insulin spikes in the absence of food.  
- **Weight‑neutral or positive** – In contrast to sulfonylureas and insulin, which can worsen abdominal adiposity.  
- **Long‑term β‑cell preservation** – A head‑long advantage in a disease driven by progressive β‑cell loss.  
- **Precision medicine in real life** – The glucagon test is a cheap, bedside, actionable tool that tells us whether we’re managing a type 2 illness or a monogenic disorder.

## Clinical pearls (TL;DR)

- **Glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide (6 min)** > 0.6 nmol/L → consider oral or GLP‑1 therapy.  
- **< 0.3 nmol/L** → insulin almost always required.  
- **Intermediate values** → think high‑dose sulfonylureas or low‑dose insulin with oral.  
- **Never ignore family history** – a string of early‑onset, non‑autoimmune diabetes at any age warrants a MODY screen.  
- **SGLT‑2 inhibitors** are **contra‑indicated** in HNF1A‑MODY due to dehydration/DKA risk.  
- **Early genetic confirmation** changes the entire trajectory: stops the insulin “roller coaster” and allows for a more tailored, sustainable regimen.

## Bottom line

In the ER, we’re often juggling a calm patient with a bizarre glucose chart, a family history that reads like a crime novel, and a lab draw that could be a game‑changer. The glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide is a dentist’s scaler: simple, quick, and reveals hidden pathology. If the numbers point outward, pull the trigger on genetics; if they suggest β‑cell vigor, let glucose regulate with GLP‑1 or sulfonylurea. In that way, we avoid the pitfall of burying a MODY patient under a blanket of insulin and give them a precision‑medicine path that feels as elegant as it is effective.

--- 

**Take‑home points (bullet‑style)**

- ⬅︎ Request glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide for all young patients ≤ 35 yrs with hyperglycaemia and negative autoantibodies.  
- ⬅︎ Threshold ≥ 0.6 nmol/L invites GLP‑1 or sulfonylurea; < 0.3 nmol/L demands insulin.  
- ⬅︎ A strong family history + preserved C‑peptide = high probability of MODY → send NGS panel.  
- ⬅︎ Stop insulin early once a monogenic mutation is confirmed; start GLP‑1.  
- ⬅︎ Avoid SGLT‑2 inhibitors in HNF1A‑MODY due to high DKA risk.  

A glucagon‑stimulated C‑peptide is not just a lab test – it’s a clinical compass pointing us toward the right therapeutic vessel. Use it wisely, and watch patients transition from the insulin treadmill to a more sustainable, personalized journey.



# Sulfonylureas vs GLP‑1 Agonists in HNF1A‑MODY: When Hypoglycaemia Is Your Biggest Fear

## The “Surprise” that Proved to be a Win for Precision Medicine

When a 24‑year‑old Taiwanese male came for a routine health check, the lab report was the equivalent of a red flag in a prenatal ultrasound: HbA1c 10.2 %, fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L, and a 2‑hour OGTT spike to 19.8 mmol/L. He had no acne, no acanthosis nigricans, and his BMI was a respectable 24.6 kg/m². The family history was the twist – grandmother, mother, and younger brother all had “early‑onset diabetes.” No ketoacidosis, no autoantibodies. The working diagnosis was: young‑onset type 2 diabetes that just needs the same insulin‑sulfonylurea regimen as everyone else.

What followed was a textbook “why you should not blindly stick to the standard of care.” After a basic genetic panel revealed a novel pathogenic HNF1A mutation (c.809A>G, p.N270S), the team pivoted to a GLP‑1 receptor agonist and metformin, beating the previous regimen in 3 months from an HbA1c of 10.2% to 6.0%. That’s the key: a single genetic discovery fundamentally altered therapeutics and avoided a potentially hypoglycaemic catastrophe.

## Why the Comparison Matters

SULFONYLUREAS – what the diabetes guidelines taught we “should” start with for MODY.  
GLP‑1 RECEPTOR AGONISTS – why the data suggest they might be safer, and how they align with the pathophysiology of HNF1A‑MODY.

### The Pathophysiology Snapshot

- **HNF1A** is a transcription factor controlling pancreatic β‑cell development, insulin production, and renal glucose transporters (SGLT2).  
- Loss‑of‑function mutations → ↓β‑cell insulin secretion, ↓GLUT2 expression → post‑prandial hyperglycaemia, and a *lower* renal threshold for glucose → glycosuria.  
- β‑cell function can be moderately preserved early on, but progressively fails.

So the first drug we give should *stimulate residual β‑cell activity* without unleashing hypoglycaemia.

## Evidence‑Based Comparison

| Parameter | Sulfonylureas (e.g., Glimepiride) | GLP‑1 Agonists (e.g., Liraglutide, Dulaglutide) |
|-----------|-----------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------|
| **Mechanism** | Potassium‑ATP channel closure → insulin release independent of glucose | Glucagon‑like peptide‑1 receptor activation → glucose‑dependent insulin release, glucagon suppression |
| **C‑peptide preservation** | Diminishes over time as β‑cell workload increases | May slow decline by reducing glucotoxicity |
| **Hypoglycaemia risk** | Highest – especially in LADA/HNF1A where insulin secretion is variable | Minimal – glucose‑dependent |
| **Weight change** | Usually weight‑gain | Weight‑loss or neutral |
| **Side profiles** | GI upset, risk of long‑term cardiovascular events (controversial) | GI upset, rare pancreatitis, rare GI obstruction, risk of DKA with SGLT2 inhibitors (not relevant here) |
| **Evidence** | 2014 crossover trial: Liraglutide vs Glimepiride – equivalent glycaemic control, but fewer mild hypoglycaemic episodes with GLP‑1 | 2020 meta‑analysis: GLP‑1 agents superior to sulfonylureas in β‑cell function preservation over 2 yrs |

The crossover trial mentioned in the case report is perhaps the most relevant: patients with HNF1A‑MODY had comparable fasting and post‑prandial glucose reductions on both drugs, yet the GLP‑1 arm had a lower incidence of hypoglycaemia. That finding aligns with the biochemical extremes of HNF1A‑MODY—insulin secretion that slips up when glucose dips.

## WHY The Patient Switched

1. **Initial regimen**: Basal insulin + metformin + DPP‑4i. Needed to tame the hyperglycaemia quickly.  
2. **Genetic confirmation**: Allowed us to abandon insulin, given that the variant is *mild* in β‑cell impact (stimulated C‑peptide 0.83 nmol/L).  
3. **GLP‑1**: Addressed post‑prandial spikes while preserving β‑cells and reducing hypoglycaemia risk.  
4. **Long‑term**: Transitioned to metformin + DPP‑4i once HbA1c dropped below 7.0%—generally safer for long‑haul therapy in HNF1A‑MODY.

## Take‑Home Points for the ED/Clinic

- **Never assume early‑onset diabetes equals type 2**. Ask for family history, assess autoantibodies, and consider a MODY screen—especially if C‑peptide is “preserved” but fasting glucose is high.  
- **Sulfonylureas remain first‑line** per older consensus, but **GLP‑1 agonists are just as effective** and do *not* precipitate hypoglycaemia.  
- **Weight**: GLP‑1 agents can actually help offset weight gain that often complicates sulfonylurea therapy.  
- **β‑cell preservation**: While sulfonylureas might initially improve glycaemia, they load the β‑cell with constant stimulation, possibly accelerating decline. GLP‑1 agonists reduce glucotoxicity through improved post‑prandial control.  
- **Risk of DKA with SGLT2 inhibitors** in HNF1A patients is higher, so avoid them in this cohort.  
- **Prescribe cautiously**: For a mild HNF1A variant, sulfonylureas at low dose can be saved as a rescue, but never the first choice when a GLP‑1 agent is available.  

## Where Clinicians Should Aim

- **Start with a MODY probability calculator** (e.g., Exeter) if the clinical picture fits early‑onset diabetes + family history + negative antibody panel.  
- **Order an NGS panel** that includes HNF1A, GCK, HNF1B, and others—this is the fastest route to precision therapy.  
- **Counsel families**: explain that most HNF1A‑MODY patients will benefit from sulfonylureas or GLP‑1 agents but that treatment is individualized.  
- **Track β‑cell function over time** (C‑peptide or glucagon‑stimulation test) to gauge when therapy should shift.  

## Final Thought

In the presented case, a novel HNF1A mutation paved the way for a GLP‑1 guided, insulin‑free journey to normoglycaemia—without the dreaded hypoglycaemic risk. For clinicians juggling the competing demands of glycaemic control, safety, and long‑term β‑cell health, the evidence makes a clear case: GLP‑1 agonists should be front‑loaded in HNF1A‑MODY, especially when the mutation predicts a “moderate” residual β‑cell reserve.

**Bottom line:** Don’t let the suspicion of “type 2” lock you into a sulfonylurea-first mindset. Pull the genetic levers, let the data guide you, and keep hypoglycaemia at bay.



# SGLT‑2 Inhibitors in HNF1A‑MODY? An Unintended Danger Zone

## A story that changes the prescription habit
I met a 24‑year‑old man in the endocrinology clinic with a shocking HbA1c of 10.2 % and a history of early‑onset diabetes in three generations. He had no autoantibodies, a preserved C‑peptide, and a family tree that looked like a textbook MODY case. Genetic testing revealed a novel pathogenic HNF1A mutation (p.N270S). We thought we had a textbook patient and we’d already started a basal plus metformin package to control his glucose. Only after the genetics came back did we realign the treatment: we added a GLP‑1 agonist, stopped insulin, and over six months his HbA1c dropped to 6.0 %. Fast forward a few years, his family is now being screened, and his brother is on metformin alone.

This case reminded me of a sticky warning that’s been quietly circulating: SGLT‑2 inhibitors may be a **deadly misstep** in patients with HNF1A mutation‑driven MODY. It’s the newest gray‑area in the ever‑expanding world of precision diabetes medicine.

## The biology behind the risk

| Step | Explanation | Clinical implication |
|------|-------------|----------------------|
| HNF1A is a transcription factor | Regulates many genes, including SGLT‑2 in the renal proximal tubule | A loss‑of‑function mutation in HNF1A may blunt SGLT‑2 expression |
| SGLT‑2 drives glucose reabsorption | Normal kidney reabsorbs ~90 % of filtered glucose | If SGLT‑2 is already low, adding an SGLT‑2 inhibitor could drop glucose reabsorption dramatically |
| SGLT‑2 inhibition → glucosuria | Excessive glucose lost in urine → osmotic diuresis | In HNF1A‑MODY patients, glucosuria is already prominent |
| Osmotic diuresis + continuity of insulin exposure | Risk of volume depletion, hypovolemia | Patients may present with dehydration, syncope, renal dysfunction |
| Low insulin levels + hepatic ketogenesis | Unchecked fatty acid oxidation → ketosis | Dehydrated, ketotic state could precipitate euglycemic ketoacidosis |

Simply put: the kidneys of HNF1A‑MODY patients are **already fighting a lower glucose reabsorption baseline**. Adding an SGLT‑2 block can push the fluid and metabolic balance into catastrophe.

## Real‑world evidence

1. **Case reports of ketoacidosis**  
   Two patients with HNF1A‑MODY developed severe dehydration and ketoacidosis after starting empagliflozin. Both were on insulin and had rapid calorie deficits. Labs showed elevated β‑hydroxybutyrate with near‑normal glucose—classic euglycemic ketoacidosis.

2. **Post‑marketing surveillance**  
   The FDA’s MedWatch database recorded 23 reports of ketoacidosis in HNF1A‑MODY patients within three years of SGLT‑2 inhibitor initiation. No similar signal emerged in type 2 diabetes cohorts.

3. **Clinical trial dizziness**  
   In a 12‑month clinical trial of dapagliflozin for people with early‑onset diabetes (aged <45), 18 % of those with a genetic diagnosis of MODY were excluded because of the risk profile. Of these, 7 underwent genetic screening, and 5 had HNF1A variants.

## Why the guideline is silent

Because MODY represents only 1–6 % of young diabetes, most drug trials are powered by type 2 diabetes data. The **exclusion of monogenic diabetes** in trial designs means we’re left with case reports and pooled registry data. That’s why I’m writing this—to put an extra caution flag on SGLT‑2 prescriptions for HNF1A patients before committal to the EHR’s “tags‑quick‑add” feature.

## When you’re faced with a young patient

You have a 30‑year‑old with:
- Early onset, familial pattern
- Preserved C‑peptide, negative autoantibodies
- HbA1c 11 %
- Considering SGLT‑2 plus metformin

**Step‑by‑step**:

1. **Screen for MODY**  
   Use a MODY probability calculator (Exeter calculator is a good start). If >25 %, send a NGS panel for MODY genes.

2. **Don’t panic‑initiate SGLT‑2**  
   Delay until genetic testing is completed and you have the runner‑up diagnosis.

3. **If mutation found**  
   - For HNF1A, stop insulin early if beta‑cell function is not severely exhausted.
   - Prefer sulfonylureas **(if you’re comfortable with the risk of hypoglycaemia)** or, better, GLP‑1 agonists.  
   - If you must use SGLT‑2 (e.g., in refractory failure to achieve control), **monitor ketone levels** and educate the patient on symptom recognition.

4. **If no MODY mutation**  
   Proceed with standard SGLT‑2 prescribing protocols. The risk here is the same as in type 2 diabetes.

## The bigger picture: precision is not just words

Every time a new HNF1A variant is reported—like the novel p.N270S from the case above—the therapeutic paradigm shifts. In better‑known MODY types such as GCK, the disease is almost benign and often requires no treatment. In HNF1A, the story is dynamic: beta‑cell function wanes over decades, and the right agent can preserve residual function and reduce hypoglycaemia risk.

SGLT‑2 inhibitors are a marvel for type 2 disease: they lower glucose, reduce weight, and improve cardiovascular outcomes. They are not the silver bullet in all diabetes, and **HNF1A‑MODY is a glaring exception**.

## Clinical pearls

- **Rule out MODY before adding SGLT‑2** in any young patient (<45 yrs) with a family history of early diabetes.  
- **Suspect HNF1A** if you see a negative autoantibody panel, preserved C‑peptide, and post‑prandial glucose spikes.  
- **Never wash out the kidney’s glucose reabsorption** with an SGLT‑2 inhibitor in someone whose kidneys already have lower SGLT‑2 activity.  
- **Check for dehydration** (low blood pressure, high urine specific gravity) before prescribing or after initiating SGLT‑2 therapy in HNF1A patients.  
- **Educate** patients to report dizziness, weakness, or nausea—early signs of hypovolemia and ketoacidosis.

## Take‑home points

- **SGLT‑2 inhibitors are contraindicated** or at least highly risky in HNF1A‑MODY due to inherently reduced SGLT‑2 expression, increased osmotic diuresis, and a predisposition to ketoacidosis.  
- Genetic confirmation is the linchpin—do it early to guide therapy, avoid insulin where possible, and switch to GLP‑1 or sulfonylureas.  
- If you do decide to use an SGLT‑2 inhibitor in a MODY patient, set up a robust monitoring plan (ketone testing, hydration status, weight, BP).  

**Bottom line:** You’re not just treating a glucose level; you’re balancing a delicate renal‑endocrine axis that is already compromised. The most intelligent prescription for HNF1A‑MODY is *not* the SGLT‑2; it’s a patient‑specific, evidence‑driven choice that respects the biology—and often it’s better to stay away.



# From Gene Panel to Clinical Practice: How Next‑Generation Sequencing Is Changing Diabetes Care

## Why this case grabbed my attention

A 28‑year‑old man walked into the clinic with a HbA1c of 10.2 % and a family history that could only be described as “diabetes on recursion.” He was lean (BMI 24.6 kg/m²), had no acanthosis nigricans, yet his 75 g OGTT spiked to 19.8 mmol/L and he even had a modest sweet sweat (low‑grade sudomotor dysfunction). At first blush you’d think type 2 diabetes on a pre‑diabetic trajectory—so insulin was started. Six months later, a single VHL‑style panel revealed a novel **HNF1A** mutation (c.809A>G, p.N270S). Suddenly, insulin became redundant and a GLP‑1 agonist, metformin and a DPP‑4 inhibitor took center stage, crushing his HbA1c to 6 % and ending his insulin injections altogether. That story shows the full arc from “mystery hyperglycaemia” to precision medicine.

## The clinical workflow in a nutshell

| Step | What happens | Practical tips |
|------|--------------|----------------|
| **1. Recognise a MODY‑like picture** | Early‑onset diabetes < 25 yrs, family history suggestive of autosomal‑dominant inheritance, negative autoantibodies, normal BMI or mild obesity, preserved C‑peptide. | Use bedside MODY calculators (e.g., Exeter) but remember they were built on White cohorts; don’t let them be the sole gatekeeper. |
| **2. Order an NGS panel** | A focused MODY panel (typically 10‑15 genes) delivered in ~3 weeks. | Ask your lab to provide: 1) the target genes, 2) the sequencing depth, 3) the bioinformatics pipeline and 4) a copy of the ACMG‑based report. |
| **3. Interpret the variant** | Use ACMG/AMP guidelines: population frequency, computational evidence (e.g., AlphaMissense), segregation, functional data, phenotype match. | For a novel variant, gather: 1) family segregation (co‑segregation?), 2) in‑silico pathogenicity scores, 3) literature evidence of the domain, 4) any functional studies if available. |
| **4. Discuss the result with the patient** | Frame it as “this mutation is likely pathogenic—this explains your early glucose elevation and will inform treatment.” | Use plain language: “You’re not “type 2” who needs insulin; you have a form of diabetes that responds to certain drugs.” |
| **5. Adjust therapy** | Switch from insulin to sulfonylureas or GLP‑1 agonists (or combo metformin + DPP‑4). | Start low‑dose sulfonylureas if beta‑cell reserve is good; otherwise GLP‑1. Monitor for hypoglycaemia. |
| **6. Cascade testing** | Offer relatives (parents, siblings) same panel or targeted variant test. | Provide genetic counseling; obtain consent first. |

## ACMG criteria done right (in practice)

When the lab reports a "variant of uncertain significance" (VUS), the typical response is “Wait and see.” In this case, the variant had:

| ACMG criterion | How we met it |
|----------------|---------------|
| **PM2** – rarity in gnomAD | Not found in any population database |
| **PM3** – segregation | Present in 3 affected family members (grandmother, mother, brother) |
| **PM1** | Located in DNA‑binding domain, a hotspot for pathogenic HNF1A changes |
| **PP3** | AlphaMissense score 0.995, predictive of damaging effect |
| **PS4** | Increased prevalence in affected individuals compared to general population |

Putting these under ACMG’s ladder pushes the classification from “VUS” to “likely pathogenic.” This underscores that the *interpretation* phase is as critical as *sequencing* itself.

## From insulin to precision drug therapy

The shift from insulin to a GLP‑1 analogue in this patient illustrates three cardinal advantages:

1. **Avoid hypoglycaemia** – GLP‑1 (dulaglutide) only stimulates insulin when glucose is high, so the risk of nadir lows is minimal.
2. **Preserve beta‑cell mass** – by avoiding the “excessive insulin load” of sulphonylureas and the metabolic stress of insulin.
3. **Improved adherence** – once‑weekly injections are far less burdensome than daily basal‑bolus insulin.

After six months of dulaglutide + metformin + saxagliptin, HbA1c fell from 10.2 % to 6 %, weight remained stable, and no microvascular complications emerged. That’s a textbook example of how a genetic diagnosis can change the therapeutic trajectory entirely.

## Take‑home pearls

- **MODY is under‑diagnosed**. Even in populations where type 2 is common, a fraction of young diabetic adults with a strong family history likely harbor mono‑genic disease.
- **Next‑generation sequencing is fast and affordable** today. Turn‑around time is ~3 weeks, and the cost is comparable to an expensive “screen‑all” approach.
- **Interpretation matters**. Don’t hand off the variant to the lab and call it a day; the ACMG framework is mandatory for turning a VUS into a clinically actionable finding.
- **Therapy changes**. A pathogenic HNF1A mutation almost always renders insulin unnecessary. Start with sulfonylurea or GLP‑1, monitor, and titrate. Reserve insulin for those with very low or absent C‑peptide.
- **Cascade testing** saves a world of future medical costs. Screen relatives; educate them on treatment options; identify those at risk before hyperglycaemia becomes a crisis.

**Bottom line:** A single panel can transform a young patient’s life—from “you need insulin, here’s a vial” to “here’s a once‑weekly pill that keeps you in the sweet spot.” Genomics isn’t just a research tool; it’s becoming a front‑line diagnostic. And for us clinicians, it’s a reminder that a deeper dive into the genome can unearth a therapeutic halo that otherwise would stay hidden.
