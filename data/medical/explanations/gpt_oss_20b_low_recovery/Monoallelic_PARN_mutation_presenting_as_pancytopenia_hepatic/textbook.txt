Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Evaluation

### Patient demographics and symptom chronology
The index patient is a woman in her late twenties who presented with a five‑year history of progressive fatigue and generalised weakness. Over the preceding months, her functional capacity deteriorated to the point that she required assistance with activities of daily living. She also reported exertional dyspnoea, intermittent chest discomfort, weight loss, and anorexia, all of which suggest a systemic process rather than isolated organ failure.

### Physical examination
On examination she was haemodynamically stable but appeared pale and icteric. Herald signs of premature ageing were evident: premature greying of the hair, hyperpigmented knuckles, and a bald tongue with atrophy of the lingual papillae. Musculoskeletal assessment revealed thoracic scoliosis and bilateral thenar atrophy, hinting at chronic neuropathic or musculoskeletal involvement. Abdominal palpation uncovered hepatomegaly (approx. 5 cm below the right costal margin) and an extended liver span (18 cm). Respiratory auscultation demonstrated reduced breath sounds over both infra‑scapular regions, consistent with sub‑pleural pathology. Cardiovascular and other systemic examinations were otherwise unremarkable.

### Initial laboratory panel
A complete blood count showed pancytopenia (the absolute counts for haemoglobin, white cells and platelets were all below normal reference ranges) with a markedly low reticulocyte count (0.39 %). A peripheral smear revealed:
- Dimorphic red cells
- Basophilic stippling
- Cabot rings  
- Hypersegmented neutrophils

These features are classic for ineffective erythropoiesis. Serum chemistry confirmed severe vitamin B12 deficiency and folate deficiency. Iron studies were paradoxically elevated (serum iron and ferritin high), a typical finding when marrow is unable to utilize iron efficiently. Inflammatory markers (CRP, ESR) were raised, although autoantibody panels (ANA, anti‑smooth muscle, anti‑LKM‑1) remained negative.

Bone marrow aspirate and trephine biopsy demonstrated trilineage hematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia and abundant iron stores; no dysplasia or malignant infiltration was observed. The absence of cytogenetic abnormalities further argued against an inherited marrow disorder at first glance.

### Imaging studies
An abdominal ultrasound revealed hepatosplenomegaly. Transient elastography quantified hepatic stiffness at 18.1 kPa (classifying as cirrhosis) and a controlled attenuation parameter of 295 dB/m, indicating grade II steatosis. 

A positron emission tomography–computed tomography (PET‑CT) scan showed:
- Diffuse, inhomogeneous fluorodeoxyglucose uptake in the axial and appendicular bone marrow, suggestive of marrow activation or chronic inflammation  
- Multinodular, sub‑pleural fibrotic changes and ground‑glass opacities bilaterally, consistent with pulmonary fibrosis  
- Ascites and splenomegaly without evidence of malignancy

### Assessment of organ involvement
Beyond hematologic and pulmonary abnormalities, ancillary investigations demonstrated:
- Dual‑energy X‑ray absorptiometry: osteoporosis requiring calcium, vitamin D and bisphosphonate therapy  
- Nerve conduction and electromyography: asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy with chronic neurogenic changes  
- Laboratory evidence of secondary hyperparathyroidism, hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia, likely secondary to hepatic dysfunction and impaired vitamin absorption

Taken together, the clinical picture portrays a multisystem disease involving bone marrow, liver, lungs, skeletal system and peripheral nerves. The combination of pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, osteoporosis and neuropathy in a young adult strongly suggests an inherited telomere biology disorder rather than a single‑organ pathology.

## 2. Differential Diagnosis and Diagnostic Reasoning

The patient’s presentation—a young adult with progressive fatigue, pancytopenia, and signs of premature aging—initially suggested a spectrum of common and less common disorders. A systematic evaluation was required to discriminate between transient nutritional deficiencies, immune-mediated cytopenias, infiltrative marrow processes, and inherited marrow failure syndromes. The ensuing diagnostic pathway illustrates how evidence from laboratory data, ancillary imaging, and clinical features circumscribed the differential and ultimately led to the consideration of a telomere biology disorder (TBD).

### Analysis of Pancytopenia and Ineffective Erythropoiesis Indicators

The complete blood count revealed marked pancytopenia with a low reticulocyte count (0.39 %). The peripheral smear demonstrated dimorphic red cells, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils—all classic morphological hallmarks of ineffective erythropoiesis. Elevated lactate dehydrogenase and indirect hyperbilirubinemia further supported intramedullary hemolysis, while the lack of a robust reticulocyte surge and a low bone‑marrow erythroid hyperplasia fatigued a diagnosis of immune‑mediated hemolysis. The presence of increased iron stores in the marrow (high ferritin, high serum iron) fits the pattern of “iron loading” that characterizes ineffective erythropoiesis rather than iron‑deficiency anemia. Thus, the constellation of laboratory findings prioritized marrow‑intrinsic causes or conditions that intrinsically impair erythropoietic fidelity.

### Evaluation of Vitamin B12 Deficiency and Intrinsic Factor Impairment

Serum analysis uncovered severe vitamin B12 deficiency (the most profound deficiency recorded in the case) and folate deficiency, both of which can explain pancytopenia and ineffective erythropoiesis. The patient’s duodenal biopsy revealed mild chronic gastritis with reduced parietal‑cell mass, suggesting impaired intrinsic factor production and subsequent B12 malabsorption. The high degree of marrow dysfunction relative to the anticipated clinical impact of B12 deficiency raised the question of whether nutritional deficiency alone could account for the full spectrum of findings. Moreover, even after adequate B12 repletion, the patient’s other systemic abnormalities (hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying, osteoporosis) persisted, indicating an additional, underlying defect.

### Consideration of Autoimmune Cytopenias and Connective Tissue Disease

A weakly positive direct antiglobulin test (IgG 2+) could have pointed toward an immune‑mediated hemolysis. However, the absence of significant hemolytic markers (notably, no marked bilirubinemia or sudden drops in hemoglobin) and the absence of widespread autoantibodies (negative ANA, negative hepatitis serologies) diminished the likelihood of systemic lupus erythematosus or other connective‑tissue disorders. Persistently elevated inflammatory markers without an overt cause further suggested that a silent, low‑grade immune process was unlikely the primary driver of pancytopenia.

### Exclusion of Infectious, Metabolic, and Infiltrative Causes

A broad infectious work‑up—including hepatitis panel, HIV, CMV, and tick‑borne pathogens—returned negative or non‑contributory, except for a past EBV infection. Metabolic disturbances (electrolyte imbalance, hypocalcemia, hypokalemia) were managed but did not explain the marrow failure. Imaging (ultrasound, PET‑CT) ruled out infiltrative disorders such as lymphoma or sarcoidosis; there was no evidence of an extramedullary tumor mass or granulomatous inflammation. Absence of malignant cytogenetic abnormalities on marrow karyotyping further excluded myelodysplastic syndromes. Thus, the differential moved beyond common acquired etiologies.

### Identification of Multisystem Features Suggestive of a Telomere Biology Disorder

The presence of early greying, nail and skin changes, hepatosplenomegaly, pulmonary fibrosis, osteoporosis, and neuropathy in a single patient strongly implied a systemic predisposition. These features are hallmarks of telomere biology disorders, which manifest with damage to highly proliferative tissues such as bone marrow, skin, mucosa, liver, and lung. While the classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita (oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, skin pigmentation) was not fully expressed, the patient’s premature aging signs and organ fibrosis fit within the variable expressivity observed in TBDs.

### Construction of a Focused Differential Diagnosis List

1. **Nutritional deficiency–induced marrow failure**  
   - Severe vitamin B12 + folate deficiency with ineffective erythropoiesis  
2. **Immune‑mediated cytopenias**  
   - Autoimmune hemolytic anemia (IgG autoantibodies)  
   - Connective‑tissue malignancies (e.g., lupus, scleroderma)  
3. **Marrow‑intrinsic failure disorders**  
   - Myelodysplastic syndrome (excluded by cytogenetics)  
   - Fanconi anemia or other inherited marrow failure syndromes (considered but lacking congenital anomalies)  
4. **Infiltrative or infectious marrow diseases**  
   - Lymphoma, sarcoidosis, viral infections (ruled out)  
5. **Telomere biology disorder**  
   - Dyskeratosis congenita spectrum, including variants caused by monoallelic PARN, TERT, TERC, or RTEL1 mutations  

The convergence of ineffective erythropoiesis, persistent cytopenia beyond nutritional correction, and a multisystem profile of premature aging and fibrosis redirected the diagnostic pathway toward a telomere biology disorder. Genetic testing confirmed a monoallelic PARN variant, cementing the diagnosis and underscoring the diagnostic reasoning that progressed from common acquired causes to an inherited marrow failure syndrome.

## 3. Genetic Investigation and Interpretation of PARN Pathogenicity

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) arise from inherited defects that interfere with telomere maintenance, leading to premature cellular senescence in highly proliferative tissues. The core molecular machinery comprises telomerase (encoding the protein reverse transcriptase TERT and the RNA template TERC) and accessory proteins such as DKC1 and the RNA‑deadenylating enzyme PARN. Pathogenic variants in any of these genes can shorten telomeres, but the severity and tissue distribution depend on the specific gene, inheritance pattern, and variant type.

### Clinical Exome Sequencing Workflow

The patient’s medical history suggested a syndromic etiology, prompting a clinical exome panel targeting TBD genes. High‑throughput sequencing of ~4,500 genes was performed, with a mean depth exceeding 100× to ensure reliable detection of single‑nucleotide variants (SNVs). Standard bioinformatics pipelines filtered out common polymorphisms (minor allele frequency >1 % in gnomAD) and prioritized loss‑of‑function or missense changes in known TBD loci. Candidate variants were then cross‑referenced against databases such as ClinVar, HGMD, and internal cohorts.

### The PARN c.613 T>C (p.Cys205Arg) Missense Variant

The identified variant is a single‑base transition in exon 8 of PARN, substituting an AAG codon (lysine) to CGG (arginine) at c.613, resulting in the change of cysteine to arginine at amino acid 205. Cys205 lies within the highly conserved R3H RNA‑binding domain (residues 178–245), essential for catalytic activity. Multiple sequence alignments show that cysteine is invariant across vertebrate orthologs, underscoring its functional importance.

In silico prediction tools (SIFT, MutationTaster, LRT, MutationAssessor, and CADD) uniformly predict a deleterious effect, with a CADD score of 28, placing the variant in the top 0.1 % of predicted pathogenic mutations. No benign reports of this allele exist in public databases; the allele frequency in gnomAD is <0.00001, consistent with a rare, potentially disease‑causing variant.

### Application of ACMG/AMP Guidelines

The American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) provides a framework for variant classification based on population data, computational evidence, functional studies, and segregation. For this PARN variant, the following criteria were satisfied:

| Criterion | ACMG/AMP Code | Rationale |
|-----------|---------------|-----------|
| **PM1** (moderate) | Substitution within a critical functional domain with no known benign variants | Located in R3H domain, essential for RNA binding |
| **PM2** (moderate) | Variant absent or extremely rare in population databases | gnomAD MAF < 0.00001 |
| **PP3** (supporting) | Multiple computational predictions suggest damaging effect | SIFT, MutationTaster, LRT, MutationAssessor, CADD |
| **PP2** (supporting) | Gene has low rate of benign missense variants; missense is common disease mechanism | PARN pathogenicity largely due to missense variants |

Combining two moderate criteria (PM1, PM2) and two supporting criteria (PP3, PP2) places the variant in the **Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS)** category per ACMG/AMP guidelines (since pathogenicity requires either higher‑strength evidence or functional confirmation).

### Contextualizing with Other PARN Variants

Monoallelic pathogenic variants in PARN have been implicated in a spectrum ranging from isolated idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) to bone marrow failure and hepatic fibrosis. For example, the p.Lys59Arg variant (ClinVar VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar VCV000180661.1) are classified as likely pathogenic and are associated with pulmonary fibrosis and early‑onset liver disease. Comparatively, the present c.613 T>C variant affects a different domain but similarly disrupts RNA processing. Functional assays that demonstrate impaired deadenylase activity or defective TERC maturation would strengthen evidence toward pathogenicity.

### Clinical Implications of a VUS

A VUS designation does not exclude a causal role but indicates that the evidence is insufficient for definitive clinical action based solely on genetics. In practice, this means:

- **Surveillance**: Continue monitoring for progression of bone marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis, and hepatic dysfunction, as the variant may predispose to late‑onset manifestations.
- **Family Testing**: Cascade testing of relatives can clarify segregation; segregation with disease in multiple affected family members would shift the variant toward likely pathogenic.
- **Functional Studies**: Referral to research laboratories for in‑vitro assays (e.g., deadenylase activity, TERC tail length analysis) could provide decisive evidence.
- **Management**: Treatment remains supportive; the VUS status does not preclude consideration of hematopoietic stem cell transplantation or anti‑fibrotic therapy if clinical progression occurs.

In summary, the genetic workup confirms a rare, deleterious‑predicting PARN missense change within a critical domain, yet the absence of definitive functional data and segregation keeps it classified as a VUS. This highlights the importance of integrating genomic findings with phenotypic depth and iterative evidence to guide patient care in telomere biology disorders.

## 4. Management Strategy and Follow‑Up

The therapeutic plan for this patient must be individualized, recognising that the underlying telomere biology disorder (TBD) predisposes multiple organ systems to progressive failure. The objectives of care are to (1) correct reversible, non‑genetic contributors, (2) stabilise organ function, (3) mitigate complications, and (4) prepare for eventual curative intervention should disease progression occur. Below is a practical, evidence‑based roadmap that integrates the clinical findings described in the case report.

### Vitamin B12 Replacement Schedule and Monitoring

Severe vitamin B12 deficiency was the most readily correctable cause of her anaemia and neurologic symptoms. The standard regimen in an adult with a reticulocyte recovery lag is:

1. **Intravenous Cyanocobalamin 1 mg**  
   - 1000 µg daily for 5 consecutive days (to rapidly saturate stores).  
   - 1000 µg once weekly for 4 weeks (to build a reliable base).  
   - Transition to **1000 µg monthly** for maintenance if serum levels remain > 200 pg/mL.

2. **Monitoring**  
   - Serum methylmalonic acid (MMA) and homocysteine are sensitive functional markers; both should fall within normal limits after 2–4 weeks of therapy.  
   - Hemoglobin and reticulocyte count should rise by > 1 g/dL and > 1 % respectively within 4–6 weeks.  
   - Neurologic follow‑up (clinical exam and, if indicated, repeat nerve conduction studies) at 3 months.

### Transfusion and Supportive Care for Anaemia

While B12 replacement improves endogenous erythropoiesis, the patient’s baseline hemoglobin was below 8 g/dL, warranting red‑cell transfusion:

- **Packed red blood cells** targeting a hemoglobin > 10 g/dL for symptomatic relief; maintenance thresholds may be individualized based on fatigue scores and functional capacity.
- **Iron‑replacement** is not indicated, as iron stores were elevated; high ferritin reflects ineffective erythropoiesis rather than iron overload.
- **Folic acid 1 mg daily** to support erythropoiesis and prevent homocysteine‑mediated vascular complications.

### Osteoporosis Management

Dual‑energy X‑ray absorptiometry (DXA) confirmed osteopenia/osteoporosis. A three‑pronged approach is recommended:

1. **Calcium** – 1000–1200 mg elemental calcium/day, preferably split doses with citrate or phosphate to enhance absorption.  
2. **Vitamin D** – 60 000 IU weekly or 2 000 IU daily, titrated to serum 25‑OH‑vitamin D > 30 ng/mL.  
3. **Bisphosphonate** – Once skeletal retention and safety profile are confirmed, a bisphosphonate (e.g., alendronate 70 mg weekly) may be started, unless contraindicated by renal function or severe hypocalcemia.  
4. **Monitoring** – Repeat DXA at 12 months or sooner if fracture risk escalates; lipid profile and renal function before initiating bisphosphonates.

### Correction of Electrolyte and Endocrine Derangements

The patient exhibited hypocalcemia, hypokalemia, secondary hyperparathyroidism and vitamin D deficiency:

- **Hypocalcemia** – calcium supplementation and adequacy of vitamin D correction; monitor PTH to assess endogenous bone resorption.  
- **Hypokalemia** – oral potassium chloride 20–40 mmol/day, titrated based on serum potassium and ECG.  
- **Secondary hyperparathyroidism** – treat underlying hypocalcemia and vitamin D deficiency; consider calcitriol if PTH remains persistently elevated (> 70 pg/mL) after nutritional optimisation.  
- **Monitoring** – electrolytes, renal function, calcium‑phosphate product every 3–4 weeks initially, then every 3 months.

### Monitoring of Hepatic and Pulmonary Status

The patient has established hepatic fibrosis (cirrhosis on elastography) and pulmonary fibrosis (ground‑glass opacities with subpleural traction).

#### Hepatic Surveillance

| Parameter | Frequency | Rationale |
|-----------|-----------|-----------|
| Liver Function Tests (ALT, AST, ALP, GGT, bilirubin, albumin) | Every 3 months | Detect liver injury, cholestasis, synthetic dysfunction |
| INR, Platelet count | Every 3 months | Evaluate coagulation and portal hypertension |
| FibroScan (liver stiffness) | Every 6–12 months | Track progression or regression of fibrosis |
| Ultrasound ± Doppler | Every 6–12 months | Screen for splenomegaly, portal hypertension, nodules |
| AFP | Every 6–12 months | Baseline for HCC surveillance (if cirrhosis confirmed) |

#### Pulmonary Surveillance

| Test | Frequency | Rationale |
|------|-----------|-----------|
| Pulmonary Function Tests (PFTs) – FVC, DLCO | Every 6 months | Quantify restrictive defect and gas exchange |
| High‑resolution CT (HRCT) | Every 12–18 months | Evaluate fibrosis progression, honeycombing, new nodules |
| Six‑minute walk test/NYHA class | Every 3 months | Functional status and exertional tolerance |
| Oxygen saturation (SpO₂) at rest and activity | At each visit | Detect hypoxemia prompting supplemental O₂ |

All imaging and functional studies should be interpreted in the context of the low‑grade cirrhosis and progressive fibrosis; timely intervention (e.g., antifibrotic agents) may be considered when criteria for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis are met.

### Bone Marrow Monitoring for Progression to Failure

Given the TBD predisposition, periodic marrow assessment is critical:

- **Complete Blood Count (CBC)** with differential every 4–6 weeks initially, then every 3–6 months.  
- **Peripheral smear** when cytopenias are noted or if rapid changes in CBC occur.  
- **Bone Marrow Aspiration/Biopsy** if cytopenias persist > 1 month, or if there is unexplained drop in any line (absolute neutrophil count < 1 × 10⁹/L, platelets < 50 × 10⁹/L).  
- **Telomere Length Analysis** – Flow‑FISH or qPCR on leukocytes every 12 months or when marrow changes arise; values < 10th percentile for age are suggestive of accelerated telomere attrition.  

Early detection of marrow failure permits pre‑emptive discussion of hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT).

### Genetic Counselling and Family Risk Assessment

The identified PARN variant, although classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance, is biologically plausible and likely contributory. Counselling should include:

- **Inheritance pattern** – Monoallelic (autosomal dominant, though incomplete penetrance) with potential for de novo occurrence.  
- **Maternal and paternal carrier testing** – If feasible, to clarify segregation and reduce uncertainty.  
- **Reproductive options** – Discuss pre‑implantation genetic testing (PGT‑M) for future pregnancies, counseling on the risks of germline versus somatic mosaics, and the limited predictive value of current telomere testing in asymptomatic carriers.  
- **Psychosocial support** – Address anxiety over family transmission and explain that many carriers remain asymptomatic into adulthood.

### Potential Curative Therapies

#### Hematopoietic Stem Cell Transplantation (HSCT)

- **Indication** – Progressive bone marrow failure unresponsive to supportive care, or for hematologic malignancy risk in TBD patients.  
- **Considerations** – Patient age (< 35 y is preferable), organ function (pulmonary and hepatic capacity, cardiac function), and viral serostatus.  
- **Risks** – GVHD, graft failure, infection, and transplant‑related mortality; must be balanced against the high risk of progressive multi‑organ failure.  
- **Monitoring** – Post‑transplant telomere length and chimerism; early detection of graft failure or relapse.

#### Telomerase‑Elongating Agents

- **Androgens** – Danazol or nandrolone have shown promise in elongating telomeres and improving cytopenias in some TBDs.  
- **Clinical trials** – Early‑phase data suggest modest hematologic benefit; long‑term safety remains uncertain, especially in patients with pulmonary fibrosis.  
- **Use** – Consider as a bridge to HSCT or in patients unsuitable for transplantation, after thorough risk‑benefit discussion.

#### Emerging Therapies

- **Gene‑editing** – CRISPR/Cas9–mediated correction of germline PARN mutations is experimental; not yet clinically available.  
- **RNA‑based therapeutics** – Skipping or rescuing defective polyadenylation sites may restore TERC maturation; still in preclinical stage.

### Follow‑Up Schedule Summary

| Timepoint | Evaluation |
|-----------|------------|
| Baseline | CBC, CMP, vitamin levels, telomere analysis, PFTs, HRCT, FibroScan, DXA |
| 4–6 wk | CBC, CMP, transplant/androgen candidacy reassessment |
| 3 mo | CBC, CMP, PFTs, HRCT (optional), liver panel |
| 6 mo | CBC, CMP, PFTs, HRCT, liver panel, DXA |
| 12 mo | CBC, CMP, telomere analysis, PFTs, HRCT, FibroScan, DXA |
| Every 12 mo thereafter | Repeat all above, with tighter intervals if any parameter deteriorates |

Patients with such a multisystem TBD require multidisciplinary coordination (hematology, pulmonology, hepatology, endocrinology, genetics, and physiotherapy). The primary goal is to maintain quality of life, prevent irreversible organ damage, and promptly identify cues for curative interventions.

## 5. Clinical Significance, Prognosis and Future Directions

The case of a young woman with pancytopenia, hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis, and accelerated ageing‑related findings expands the clinically recognized spectrum of PARN‑associated telomere biology disorders (TBDs). Traditionally, PARN mutations have been linked mainly to bone‑marrow failure or idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) in adults, whereas the classic dyskeratosis congenita (DC) phenotype is typically associated with biallelic PARN variants that cause an autosomal‑recessive form (DKCB6). This report demonstrates that monoallelic PARN variants can also manifest with multi‑organ fibrosis and premature ageing features, even in the absence of the classic mucocutaneous triad.

### Extension of the clinical spectrum

- **Pulmonary involvement**: The diffuse ground‑glass opacities, subpleural fibrosis and atelectatic changes seen on PET‑CT reflect the fibrotic healing response characteristic of IPF. In TC‑associated cases, the pathology usually shows temporally heterogenous interstitial fibrosis reminiscent of usual interstitial pneumonia. The presence of pulmonary fibrosis in a young adult with a single PARN variant underlines the need for high suspicion in patients presenting with unexplained dyspnea and early‑onset fibrotic lung disease.

- **Hepatic fibrosis**: Transient elastography revealed stage 2 steatosis and cirrhosis (liver stiffness 18 kPa). Liver involvement in TBDs has often been under‑reported, yet recent registry data indicate that over 80 % of patients with pathogenic TERC or TERT mutations have moderate to severe hepatic disease. The patient’s hepatic fibrosis likely reflects accelerated hepatocyte telomere shortening, leading to chronic injury and fibrogenesis.

- **Premature ageing features**: Premature greying of hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation and oral leukoplakia (bald tongue) are classic mucocutaneous manifestations of DC. Although the patient lacked nail dystrophy and reticulate skin pigmentation, the presence of two classic markers suffices to meet a DC‑like diagnosis when combined with bone‑marrow findings. Neuropathy and osteoporosis further support an accelerated senescence phenotype.

- **Neuropathy**: The asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy detected on nerve conduction studies may reflect mitochondrial dysfunction or cumulative DNA damage due to impaired telomere maintenance, a phenomenon increasingly reported in telomeropathies.

### Prognostic indicators

- **Telomere length dynamics**: The most objective marker of disease progression is leukocyte telomere length (LTL). Shorter telomeres at diagnosis are associated with earlier onset of organ failure, higher risk of marrow failure, and increased malignancy risk. Serial measurement can detect accelerated attrition, prompting timely intervention (e.g., hematopoietic stem cell transplantation or lung transplant). In this case, baseline telomere length was not reported; however, future screening should incorporate LTL assessment for prognostication and family counselling.

- **Organ fibrosis progression**: Imaging (high‑resolution CT for lungs, elastography and liver biopsy for the liver), serologic biomarkers (fibro‑inflammatory cytokines), and functional tests (pulmonary function tests, liver stiffness monitoring) help chart disease trajectory. The patient’s CT showed extensive fibrosis but no radiographic evidence of ongoing inflammation, suggesting a smouldering fibrotic process that may progress steadily.

- **Hematologic parameters**: Sustained pancytopenia, especially with low reticulocyte count and ineffective erythropoiesis, portends impending bone‑marrow failure. The persistence of cytopenia after correction of B12 deficiency in the present case cautions that telomere attrition may be compounding marrow failure risk.

### Impact of monoallelic versus biallelic PARN mutations

- **Biallelic (recessive) PARN mutations** produce a classic DC phenotype—premature hair greying, nail dystrophy, skin hyperpigmentation, bone‑marrow failure, and later‑onset pulmonary fibrosis. The clinical course is typically more penetrant and severe, often presenting in childhood or early adulthood.

- **Monoallelic (monoallelic heterozygous) variants** tend to have a more variable expressivity. While some carriers develop isolated IPF or bone‑marrow failure, others remain asymptomatic or exhibit a late‑onset, mild phenotype. The variability is likely influenced by modifier genes, somatic rescue (secondary mutations that restore telomerase activity), and environmental factors (smoking, alcohol, viral infections). 

In the current patient, a heterozygous missense change (c.613T>C, p.Cys205Arg) was detected; functional studies are lacking, but the variant meets multiple ACMG criteria for pathogenicity, hinting at a disease‑causing role. This underscores that even a single allele mutation can have clinically significant consequences in TBDs, necessitating a high index of suspicion.

### Utility of telomere length testing in the work‑up

- **Diagnostic confirmation**: Flow cytometry‑based fluorescence in situ hybridisation (flow‑FISH) or quantitative PCR (qPCR) of peripheral blood leukocytes provides a rapid, cost‑effective screen for markedly short telomeres. Short telomeres (≤10th centile for age and sex) in the presence of multisystem disease should trigger genetic evaluation.

- **Family screening**: Once a pathogenic or likely pathogenic telomere‑related variant is identified, relatives can be offered telomere length testing to assess penetrance and to inform screening intervals for lung or liver disease.

- **Guiding therapy**: Telomere length informs eligibility for hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT). Patients whose telomeres are severely shortened may have poorer transplant outcomes due to impaired engraftment. Conversely, those with longer telomeres may respond better to antiviral or antifibrotic therapies.

### Emerging therapies and clinical trials in TBDs

- **Androgens**: Oral danazol and nandrolone have been used off‑label to stimulate telomerase activity and telomere elongation, demonstrating modest improvement in blood counts and some organ function. Clinical trials are ongoing to better characterize efficacy and toxicity.

- **Gene‑editing**: CRISPR/Cas9–based approaches have shown proof‑of‑concept in vitro for correcting pathogenic telomerase mutations. Early‑phase trials will assess safety and durability in patients with inherited telomeropathies.

- **Antifibrotic agents**: In IPF, drugs such as pirfenidone and nintedanib may slow progression. Their role in TBD‑associated fibrosis remains to be clarified, particularly in patients with systemic disease.

- **Telomerase activators**: Small molecules that enhance TERT expression are in preclinical development; their potential to ameliorate multi‑organ involvement may represent a future targeted strategy.

- **Cell‑based therapies**: Umbilical cord blood or matched unrelated donor HSCT remains the only curative option for bone‑marrow failure. Lung transplantation outcomes in TBD patients are improving with better peri‑operative management and donor matching.

### Importance of multidisciplinary care and patient education

The multi‑system nature of TBDs mandates coordination among hematology, pulmonology, hepatology, dermatology, neurology, genetics, radiology, and psychology. Regular surveillance (LFTs, pulmonary function tests, imaging, bone density scans) must be scheduled according to individual risk profiles.

Patients should receive comprehensive education on lifestyle modifications that may mitigate telomere attrition (cessation of smoking, limiting alcohol, maintaining a healthy diet, regular exercise, and avoidance of hepatotoxic drugs). Genetic counselling should address recurrence risk, options for family planning, and the implications of germline testing for relatives.

In conclusion, this case illustrates that monoallelic PARN variants can masquerade as acquired organ failure syndromes, prompting a paradigm shift toward aggressive evaluation and multidisciplinary management of young patients with unexplained multisystem disease. As the understanding of TBDs expands and therapeutic options evolve, early recognition and systematic surveillance will be pivotal in improving outcomes for these patients.
