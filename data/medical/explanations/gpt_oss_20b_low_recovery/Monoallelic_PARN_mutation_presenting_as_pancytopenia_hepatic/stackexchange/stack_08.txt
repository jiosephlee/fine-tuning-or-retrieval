Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Clinical Pearls] What key findings on bone marrow aspirate would support a diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis secondary to vitamin B12 deficiency as opposed to a primary marrow failure syndrome?

**Key marrow‑aspirate findings that favor ineffective erythropoiesis from vitamin B12 deficiency over a primary marrow‑failure syndrome**

| Finding | Why it points to B12‑deficiency‑driven ineffective erythropoiesis | How it differs in primary marrow‑failure syndromes (e.g., aplastic anemia, myelodysplastic syndrome, telomere‑related bone‑marrow failure) |
|---------|--------------------------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Erythroid hyperplasia with markedly expanded megaloblastoid precursors** | The marrow in B12 deficiency typically shows crowded erythroid series with large, often dysplastic, precursor cells. In the case, the aspirate demonstrated “erythroid hyperplasia” consistent with a compensatory response to anemia. | In aplastic anemia the marrow is hypocellular; in many MDS subtypes the erythroid lineage is under‑represented or shows hypocellularity. |
| **Large, hypochromatic (megakaryocytic or erythroblastic) cells with perinuclear hypogranularity (“megaloblastoid” appearance)** | This is classic for megaloblastic processes; B12‑deficiency produces nuclear‑cytoplasmic asynchrony, giving “megaloblastoid” appearance. | Dysplastic changes may be present, but they tend to be more varied (e.g., ring sideroblasts, pseudo‑clustering) rather than pure megaloblastoid morphology. |
| **Increased iron stores (iron in marrow)** | Severe B12 deficiency leads to ineffective erythropoiesis with recurrent erythroblasts that are iron‑rich but fail to mature, yielding marrow iron overload. The case notes “increased iron stores” and a bone‑marrow aspirate with “increased iron” consistent with this pattern. | In aplastic anemia or early MDS the marrow shows low iron or sideroblasts but not diffuse iron overload. |
| **Lack of dysplasia or malignant infiltration** | The aspirate was reported as “no evidence of malignancy or dysplasia.” B12 deficiency does not produce cytogenetic or morphologic dysplasia beyond the megaloblastoid changes. | Primary marrow‑failure syndromes almost always show cytogenetic abnormalities, morphological dysplasia (e.g., multi‑lineage dysplasia, ring sideroblasts), or clonal proliferation. |
| **Normal cytogenetics** | The case had normal karyotype, which is expected in nutrient‑deficiency marrow. | Aplastic or telomere‑related marrow failure may have cytogenetic abnormalities (e.g., 7q‑).

**Take‑home point**  
In B12 deficiency the marrow is *hypercellular* with a *prominent, dysplastic erythroid series* (megaloblasts) and *diffuse iron overload*, whereas primary bone‑marrow failure presents with *hypocellularity or dysplasia across multiple lineages, without the characteristic megaloblastoid change or iron overload*. The aspirate in this patient displays exactly those B12‑deficiency hallmarks—erythroid hyperplasia, megaloblasts, increased iron stores, and absence of cytogenetic or dysplastic abnormalities—supporting ineffective erythropoiesis secondary to severe vitamin B12 deficiency rather than a telomere‑related marrow‑failure syndrome.
