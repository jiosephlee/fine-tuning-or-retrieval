Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

Chapter 2: ## 2. Differential Diagnosis and Diagnostic Reasoning

The patient’s presentation—a young adult with progressive fatigue, pancytopenia, and signs of premature aging—initially suggested a spectrum of common and less common disorders. A systematic evaluation was required to discriminate between transient nutritional deficiencies, immune-mediated cytopenias, infiltrative marrow processes, and inherited marrow failure syndromes. The ensuing diagnostic pathway illustrates how evidence from laboratory data, ancillary imaging, and clinical features circumscribed the differential and ultimately led to the consideration of a telomere biology disorder (TBD).

### Analysis of Pancytopenia and Ineffective Erythropoiesis Indicators

The complete blood count revealed marked pancytopenia with a low reticulocyte count (0.39 %). The peripheral smear demonstrated dimorphic red cells, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils—all classic morphological hallmarks of ineffective erythropoiesis. Elevated lactate dehydrogenase and indirect hyperbilirubinemia further supported intramedullary hemolysis, while the lack of a robust reticulocyte surge and a low bone‑marrow erythroid hyperplasia fatigued a diagnosis of immune‑mediated hemolysis. The presence of increased iron stores in the marrow (high ferritin, high serum iron) fits the pattern of “iron loading” that characterizes ineffective erythropoiesis rather than iron‑deficiency anemia. Thus, the constellation of laboratory findings prioritized marrow‑intrinsic causes or conditions that intrinsically impair erythropoietic fidelity.

### Evaluation of Vitamin B12 Deficiency and Intrinsic Factor Impairment

Serum analysis uncovered severe vitamin B12 deficiency (the most profound deficiency recorded in the case) and folate deficiency, both of which can explain pancytopenia and ineffective erythropoiesis. The patient’s duodenal biopsy revealed mild chronic gastritis with reduced parietal‑cell mass, suggesting impaired intrinsic factor production and subsequent B12 malabsorption. The high degree of marrow dysfunction relative to the anticipated clinical impact of B12 deficiency raised the question of whether nutritional deficiency alone could account for the full spectrum of findings. Moreover, even after adequate B12 repletion, the patient’s other systemic abnormalities (hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying, osteoporosis) persisted, indicating an additional, underlying defect.

### Consideration of Autoimmune Cytopenias and Connective Tissue Disease

A weakly positive direct antiglobulin test (IgG 2+) could have pointed toward an immune‑mediated hemolysis. However, the absence of significant hemolytic markers (notably, no marked bilirubinemia or sudden drops in hemoglobin) and the absence of widespread autoantibodies (negative ANA, negative hepatitis serologies) diminished the likelihood of systemic lupus erythematosus or other connective‑tissue disorders. Persistently elevated inflammatory markers without an overt cause further suggested that a silent, low‑grade immune process was unlikely the primary driver of pancytopenia.

### Exclusion of Infectious, Metabolic, and Infiltrative Causes

A broad infectious work‑up—including hepatitis panel, HIV, CMV, and tick‑borne pathogens—returned negative or non‑contributory, except for a past EBV infection. Metabolic disturbances (electrolyte imbalance, hypocalcemia, hypokalemia) were managed but did not explain the marrow failure. Imaging (ultrasound, PET‑CT) ruled out infiltrative disorders such as lymphoma or sarcoidosis; there was no evidence of an extramedullary tumor mass or granulomatous inflammation. Absence of malignant cytogenetic abnormalities on marrow karyotyping further excluded myelodysplastic syndromes. Thus, the differential moved beyond common acquired etiologies.

### Identification of Multisystem Features Suggestive of a Telomere Biology Disorder

The presence of early greying, nail and skin changes, hepatosplenomegaly, pulmonary fibrosis, osteoporosis, and neuropathy in a single patient strongly implied a systemic predisposition. These features are hallmarks of telomere biology disorders, which manifest with damage to highly proliferative tissues such as bone marrow, skin, mucosa, liver, and lung. While the classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita (oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, skin pigmentation) was not fully expressed, the patient’s premature aging signs and organ fibrosis fit within the variable expressivity observed in TBDs.

### Construction of a Focused Differential Diagnosis List

1. **Nutritional deficiency–induced marrow failure**  
   - Severe vitamin B12 + folate deficiency with ineffective erythropoiesis  
2. **Immune‑mediated cytopenias**  
   - Autoimmune hemolytic anemia (IgG autoantibodies)  
   - Connective‑tissue malignancies (e.g., lupus, scleroderma)  
3. **Marrow‑intrinsic failure disorders**  
   - Myelodysplastic syndrome (excluded by cytogenetics)  
   - Fanconi anemia or other inherited marrow failure syndromes (considered but lacking congenital anomalies)  
4. **Infiltrative or infectious marrow diseases**  
   - Lymphoma, sarcoidosis, viral infections (ruled out)  
5. **Telomere biology disorder**  
   - Dyskeratosis congenita spectrum, including variants caused by monoallelic PARN, TERT, TERC, or RTEL1 mutations  

The convergence of ineffective erythropoiesis, persistent cytopenia beyond nutritional correction, and a multisystem profile of premature aging and fibrosis redirected the diagnostic pathway toward a telomere biology disorder. Genetic testing confirmed a monoallelic PARN variant, cementing the diagnosis and underscoring the diagnostic reasoning that progressed from common acquired causes to an inherited marrow failure syndrome.
