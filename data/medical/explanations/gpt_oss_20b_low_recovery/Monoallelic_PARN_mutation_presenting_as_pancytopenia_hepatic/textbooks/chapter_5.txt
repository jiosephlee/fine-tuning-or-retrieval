Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

Chapter 5: ## 5. Clinical Significance, Prognosis and Future Directions

The case of a young woman with pancytopenia, hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis, and accelerated ageing‑related findings expands the clinically recognized spectrum of PARN‑associated telomere biology disorders (TBDs). Traditionally, PARN mutations have been linked mainly to bone‑marrow failure or idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) in adults, whereas the classic dyskeratosis congenita (DC) phenotype is typically associated with biallelic PARN variants that cause an autosomal‑recessive form (DKCB6). This report demonstrates that monoallelic PARN variants can also manifest with multi‑organ fibrosis and premature ageing features, even in the absence of the classic mucocutaneous triad.

### Extension of the clinical spectrum

- **Pulmonary involvement**: The diffuse ground‑glass opacities, subpleural fibrosis and atelectatic changes seen on PET‑CT reflect the fibrotic healing response characteristic of IPF. In TC‑associated cases, the pathology usually shows temporally heterogenous interstitial fibrosis reminiscent of usual interstitial pneumonia. The presence of pulmonary fibrosis in a young adult with a single PARN variant underlines the need for high suspicion in patients presenting with unexplained dyspnea and early‑onset fibrotic lung disease.

- **Hepatic fibrosis**: Transient elastography revealed stage 2 steatosis and cirrhosis (liver stiffness 18 kPa). Liver involvement in TBDs has often been under‑reported, yet recent registry data indicate that over 80 % of patients with pathogenic TERC or TERT mutations have moderate to severe hepatic disease. The patient’s hepatic fibrosis likely reflects accelerated hepatocyte telomere shortening, leading to chronic injury and fibrogenesis.

- **Premature ageing features**: Premature greying of hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation and oral leukoplakia (bald tongue) are classic mucocutaneous manifestations of DC. Although the patient lacked nail dystrophy and reticulate skin pigmentation, the presence of two classic markers suffices to meet a DC‑like diagnosis when combined with bone‑marrow findings. Neuropathy and osteoporosis further support an accelerated senescence phenotype.

- **Neuropathy**: The asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy detected on nerve conduction studies may reflect mitochondrial dysfunction or cumulative DNA damage due to impaired telomere maintenance, a phenomenon increasingly reported in telomeropathies.

### Prognostic indicators

- **Telomere length dynamics**: The most objective marker of disease progression is leukocyte telomere length (LTL). Shorter telomeres at diagnosis are associated with earlier onset of organ failure, higher risk of marrow failure, and increased malignancy risk. Serial measurement can detect accelerated attrition, prompting timely intervention (e.g., hematopoietic stem cell transplantation or lung transplant). In this case, baseline telomere length was not reported; however, future screening should incorporate LTL assessment for prognostication and family counselling.

- **Organ fibrosis progression**: Imaging (high‑resolution CT for lungs, elastography and liver biopsy for the liver), serologic biomarkers (fibro‑inflammatory cytokines), and functional tests (pulmonary function tests, liver stiffness monitoring) help chart disease trajectory. The patient’s CT showed extensive fibrosis but no radiographic evidence of ongoing inflammation, suggesting a smouldering fibrotic process that may progress steadily.

- **Hematologic parameters**: Sustained pancytopenia, especially with low reticulocyte count and ineffective erythropoiesis, portends impending bone‑marrow failure. The persistence of cytopenia after correction of B12 deficiency in the present case cautions that telomere attrition may be compounding marrow failure risk.

### Impact of monoallelic versus biallelic PARN mutations

- **Biallelic (recessive) PARN mutations** produce a classic DC phenotype—premature hair greying, nail dystrophy, skin hyperpigmentation, bone‑marrow failure, and later‑onset pulmonary fibrosis. The clinical course is typically more penetrant and severe, often presenting in childhood or early adulthood.

- **Monoallelic (monoallelic heterozygous) variants** tend to have a more variable expressivity. While some carriers develop isolated IPF or bone‑marrow failure, others remain asymptomatic or exhibit a late‑onset, mild phenotype. The variability is likely influenced by modifier genes, somatic rescue (secondary mutations that restore telomerase activity), and environmental factors (smoking, alcohol, viral infections). 

In the current patient, a heterozygous missense change (c.613T>C, p.Cys205Arg) was detected; functional studies are lacking, but the variant meets multiple ACMG criteria for pathogenicity, hinting at a disease‑causing role. This underscores that even a single allele mutation can have clinically significant consequences in TBDs, necessitating a high index of suspicion.

### Utility of telomere length testing in the work‑up

- **Diagnostic confirmation**: Flow cytometry‑based fluorescence in situ hybridisation (flow‑FISH) or quantitative PCR (qPCR) of peripheral blood leukocytes provides a rapid, cost‑effective screen for markedly short telomeres. Short telomeres (≤10th centile for age and sex) in the presence of multisystem disease should trigger genetic evaluation.

- **Family screening**: Once a pathogenic or likely pathogenic telomere‑related variant is identified, relatives can be offered telomere length testing to assess penetrance and to inform screening intervals for lung or liver disease.

- **Guiding therapy**: Telomere length informs eligibility for hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT). Patients whose telomeres are severely shortened may have poorer transplant outcomes due to impaired engraftment. Conversely, those with longer telomeres may respond better to antiviral or antifibrotic therapies.

### Emerging therapies and clinical trials in TBDs

- **Androgens**: Oral danazol and nandrolone have been used off‑label to stimulate telomerase activity and telomere elongation, demonstrating modest improvement in blood counts and some organ function. Clinical trials are ongoing to better characterize efficacy and toxicity.

- **Gene‑editing**: CRISPR/Cas9–based approaches have shown proof‑of‑concept in vitro for correcting pathogenic telomerase mutations. Early‑phase trials will assess safety and durability in patients with inherited telomeropathies.

- **Antifibrotic agents**: In IPF, drugs such as pirfenidone and nintedanib may slow progression. Their role in TBD‑associated fibrosis remains to be clarified, particularly in patients with systemic disease.

- **Telomerase activators**: Small molecules that enhance TERT expression are in preclinical development; their potential to ameliorate multi‑organ involvement may represent a future targeted strategy.

- **Cell‑based therapies**: Umbilical cord blood or matched unrelated donor HSCT remains the only curative option for bone‑marrow failure. Lung transplantation outcomes in TBD patients are improving with better peri‑operative management and donor matching.

### Importance of multidisciplinary care and patient education

The multi‑system nature of TBDs mandates coordination among hematology, pulmonology, hepatology, dermatology, neurology, genetics, radiology, and psychology. Regular surveillance (LFTs, pulmonary function tests, imaging, bone density scans) must be scheduled according to individual risk profiles.

Patients should receive comprehensive education on lifestyle modifications that may mitigate telomere attrition (cessation of smoking, limiting alcohol, maintaining a healthy diet, regular exercise, and avoidance of hepatotoxic drugs). Genetic counselling should address recurrence risk, options for family planning, and the implications of germline testing for relatives.

In conclusion, this case illustrates that monoallelic PARN variants can masquerade as acquired organ failure syndromes, prompting a paradigm shift toward aggressive evaluation and multidisciplinary management of young patients with unexplained multisystem disease. As the understanding of TBDs expands and therapeutic options evolve, early recognition and systematic surveillance will be pivotal in improving outcomes for these patients.
