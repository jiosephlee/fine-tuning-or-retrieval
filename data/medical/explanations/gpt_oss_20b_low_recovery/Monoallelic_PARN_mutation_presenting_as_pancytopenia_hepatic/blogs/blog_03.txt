Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# Pancytopenia and Fibrosis: When a Vitamin B12 Deficiency Isn’t the Whole Story

## Why this case prints in the FAST‑LANE

You can spend two years chasing sickle‑cell rate, autoimmune hemolysis panels and a maze of iron‑status tests, only to finish with a “classic” B12‑deficiency picture: macrocytosis, hypersegmented neutrophils and a diffuse pancytopenia. In most settings that’s the whole of it. Yet, when a young adult with a 5‑year history of fatigue, weight loss, bone pain, and a constellation of organ fibrosis (liver + lungs) finally sees a hematologist, the reality is far less tidy. The culprit behind the macrocytosis may be the “smoking gun” of a nutrient defect, but the underlying “how-come?” question can only be answered with a deeper dive into genetics and telomere biology.

Below is a walk‑through of that diagnostic journey—a reminder that *not every vitamin highway is cul-de-sac*.

## First‑hand patient window

- **Age 28, chronic fatigue, progressive dyspnoea** – more than a feeling of “just not enough energy.”
- **Physical exam**: premature greying, knuckle hyperpigmentation, scalloped tongue, thoracic scoliosis, hepatosplenomegaly, hypokalaemia, hypocalcaemia, osteoporosis.
- **Labs**: pancytopenia, low retic count, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, hypersegmented neutrophils. Severe B12 & folate deficiency. Elevated LDH, indirect bilirubin, halo‑positive DAT (IgG).
- **Imaging**: hepatic steatosis, cirrhosis (liver stiffness 18.1 kPa), diffuse pulmonary fibrosis, bone marrow PET‑uptake.
- **Bone marrow**: trilineage, erythroid hyperplasia, increased iron stores, no dysplasia or malignancy. Normal cytogenetics.

> *The red flag*: persistent organ fibrosis, skeletal changes, and a hypogonadotropic “premature ageing” picture that vitro ex TBB—should we stop at nutritional therapy?

## Core pearls: Decoding a “marrow‑failure‑like” picture

| Question | What to look for | Why it matters |
|--------|-----------------|----------------|
| 1. **Is the anemia truly macrocytic?** | Peripheral smear: dacrocytes, hypersegmented neutrophils, dimorphic erythrocytes | In B12/folate deficiency, expect hypersegmented neutrophils; in ineffective erythropoiesis from marrow failure, may see similar but without macrocytosis. |
| 2. **What does the retics‑to‑WBC ratio say?** | Low retic % with low WBC | Poor marrow response to peripheral loss, points away from pure peripheral destruction. |
| 3. **Is haemolysis the driver?** | LDH, bilirubin, haptoglobin, DAT | DAT+ IgG suggests warm AIHA; however, low LDH relative to retic response suggests ineffective erythropoiesis dominates. |
| 4. **What does the marrow look like?** | Hyperplasia, iron stores, dysplasia | Erythroid hyperplasia + iron overload --> ineffective erythropoiesis; absence of dysplasia → likely inherited dysfunction or marrow suppression. |
| 5. **What about organ fibrosis?** | Imaging, elastography for liver; HRCT for lung | Indeterminate for autoimmune or infectious causes? – Think telomere biology disorders (TBDs). |

### The twist: A VUS hides a telomere clock

- **Gene**: PARN (poly(A)-specific ribonuclease) – involved in TR (telomerase RNA) maturation.
- **Variant**: c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg (mono‑allelic, VUS).
- **Phenotypic Fit**: Pancytopenia, hepatic & pulmonary fibrosis, premature ageing signs, osteoporosis. While B12 deficiency explains the anemia, the residual multi‑organ involvement hints at an underlying genetic problem.

> *Take‑home*: Even after correcting B12, persistent cytopenia + fibrosis → genetic testing advised.

## Systematic diagnostic approach (FOAM‑edition)

1. **Confirm and correct modifiable causes**  
   - Repeat B12/folate once, rule out malabsorption (duodenal biopsy).  
   - Treat B12 with IV cyanocobalamin, monitor retic counts.

2. **Rule out classic autoimmune & infectious triggers**  
   - ANA, SMA, ASMA, LKM, viral hepatitis panel, HIV, EBV IgG/IgM, CMV.  
   - If negative, consider organ‑specific secondary causes (e.g., portal hypertension leading to hypersplenism).

3. **Bone marrow morphology + cytogenetics**  
   - Look for dysplasia (Auer rods, megakaryocytic clustering, erythroid maturation arrest).  
   - Absent dysplasia → less likely MDS, more likely inherited marrow insufficiency.

4. **Measure telomere length**  
   - Flow-FISH or PCR‑based assay, benchmark against age‑matched controls.  
   - Short telomeres (<1st percentile) strongly suggest TBD.

5. **Targeted or whole‑exome sequencing**  
   - Panel for DC/TBD genes (TERT, TERC, RTEL1, PARN, DKC1, TINF2, etc.).  
   - Interpret variants using ACMG criteria; VUS warrants familial segregation studies.

6. **Multidisciplinary surveillance**  
   - Pulmonology for IPF monitoring (PFTs, HRCT).  
   - Hepatology for fibrosis progression (elastography, liver function).  
   - Endocrinology for bone health, electrolytes.  
   - Genetic counseling for family planning.

## What’s the clinical relevance?

1. **Early recognition** | TBDs often masquerade as “ordinary” cytopenias; catching it early gives you the window for surveillance and pre‑emptive interventions (and transplant discussion if needed).  
2. **Therapeutic opacity** | No definitive cure—supportive care, anti‑dengue meds (e.g., danazol) and potential HSCT for marrow failure.  
3. **Future of personalized medicine** | As variant databases grow, what today is a VUS might tomorrow be re‑classified, altering surveillance and treatment.

### Real‑world take‑away

- **In a young woman with pancytopenia, weight loss, liver & lung fibrosis, and signs of premature ageing**, do not stop at B12.  
- A *positive DAT* can be a red herring—look at the marrow and retic response.  
- *Mono‑allelic PARN variants* may cause pulmonary fibrosis and, more rarely, bone marrow failure.  
- **If telomere shortening is suspected**, refer for telomere length assay and consider a TBD panel.  
- **Multidisciplinary follow‑up** (pulm, hep, bone, cardio) is essential.

---

**Bottom line**: Vitamin B12 deficiency can be a loud **symptom** in a sea of inherited telomere dysfunction. Correcting the B12 is necessary but not sufficient—underlying telomere biology disorders can continue to loom over the bone marrow and the organs, demanding a higher tier of investigation. Keep the *telescope open*—the universe of TBDs is wider than our usual differential.
