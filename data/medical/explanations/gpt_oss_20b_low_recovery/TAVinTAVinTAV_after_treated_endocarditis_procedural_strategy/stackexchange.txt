### [Pathophysiology] What are the key anatomical measurements derived from multislice CT that predict the risk of coronary obstruction during a transcatheter valve‑in‑transcatheter valve (TAV‑in‑TAV) procedure?

**Key multislice‑CT measurements that predict coronary obstruction during a TAV‑in‑TAV (or TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV) procedure**

| Measurement | Rationale | Typical “safe” threshold (if available) | How it was used in the reported case |
|-------------|-----------|----------------------------------------|--------------------------------------|
| **Coronary ostial height** (cm from annular plane) | Low ostia are more likely to be covered by the displaced valve frame or neo‑skirt. | ≥ 12 mm is generally considered low risk; < 10 mm is high risk. | CT showed the left and right coronary beds were ≥ 12 mm, which guided the decision to forego coronary protection. |
| **Sinus of Valsalva diameter (anterior‑posterior or maximal) at the level of the coronary ostia** | A small sinus limits the amount of radial space the valve and its skirt can occupy before impinging on the coronary cusp. | > 28 mm is desirable; < 26 mm is concerning. | The patient’s sinus diameter measured ~30 mm, reducing the perceived obstruction risk. |
| **Valve‑to‑coronary distance** (distance from the outer edge of the deployed prosthesis’s leaflet base to the coronary ostium) | Directly represents the clearance left for coronary flow after valve expansion. | ≥ 4 mm is considered safe; < 4 mm increases obstruction risk. | Pre‑procedural CT modeling predicted a valve‑to‑coronary distance of ≥ 5 mm for the planned Acurate Neo2 position, supporting a high‑implant strategy. |
| **Predicted neo‑skirt height** (height of the valve cuff that extends below the annulus when implanted at the planned depth) | An oversized or low‑implanted skirt can protrude into the coronary sinus. | Seat the valve so the skirt does not extend below the coronary annulus; a “neo‑skirt” height < 5 mm is preferable. | Modelling suggested the neo‑skirt would not extend below the coronary ostia when the third valve was positioned above the Evolut leaflets. |
| **Cusp‑to‑cusp (CTCA) relationship / aortic root angulation** | Severe angulation can push the valve frame against the sinuses or directly into the conduit. | No specific numeric threshold; a lateral view should show < 45° angulation of the coronary ramus to the valve plane. | The aortic root had moderate angulation, allowing confident prediction that the third valve would not encroach on the coronary ostia. |

**Clinical take‑away**

1. **Anatomy over anatomy‑growing assumptions** – Even after two transcatheter valves, the critical determinants of coronary protection remain the same: ostial height, sinus size, and valve‑to‑coronary distance.  
2. **High‑implant strategy pays off** – By positioning the third valve above the prior Evolut leaflets, the team increased the valve‑to‑coronary distance and minimized neo‑skirt protrusion, eliminating the need for prophylactic wiring or chimney stenting.  
3. **Pre‑procedural CT is non‑negotiable** – In complex serial–valve cases, dedicated 3‑D CT modeling allows assessment of how the new frame will interact with the previous stent frames, the coronary ostia, and the aortic root geometry.  

These measurements, derived from high‑resolution multislice CT, form the cornerstone of risk stratification for coronary obstruction in multi‑valve‑in‑valve interventions and guided the successful outcome in the presented TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV case.

### [Morning Report] Explain how commissural alignment of a self‑expanding valve can help preserve future coronary access after a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV implantation.

**Answer**

Commissural alignment of a self‑expanding valve in a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV implantation preserves future coronary access by ensuring that the new valve’s struts and skirt lie directly over the preceding valve’s commissures rather than between them. This positioning has two key consequences:

1. **Minimises “neo‑skirt” encroachment on the coronary ostia.**  
   When a self‑expanding valve (e.g., Acurate neo2) is deployed, its lower stent frame becomes the new “neo‑skirt.” If the implant is mis‑aligned, the skirt can extend laterally across the sinus of Valsalva and obstruct the coronary ostium, or create a channeled path that makes subsequent coronary wiring difficult. Aligning the new valve’s commissures with those of the underlying Evolut frame confines the skirt to the native commissural planes, preserving a clear channel to the left and right coronary arteries (Fig. 5, 7 in the report).  

2. **Maintains a vertical “tube” of the aortic root.**  
   The third valve’s supra‑annular leaflets are positioned above the Evolut leaflets, and the commissures line up such that the radial expansion of the new frame does not squeeze the sinus walls inward. This keeps the aortic annulus generously open and prevents the pinch‑off of the coronary arteries that can occur when successive frames overlap off‑center.  

In the reported case, pre‑procedural CT modeling identified the best depth and orientation to avoid coronary obstruction. During deployment, fluoroscopic landmarks were used to align the Acurate neo2’s commissures with those of the Evolut prosthesis. Post‑dilation further expanded the Evolut stent, improving leaflet excursion and reducing transvalvular gradients while the aligned commissures ensured that the final neo‑skirt did not overhang the coronary ostia. Consequently, aortography after the third implantation showed preserved coronary flow and no need for coronary protection.

Thus, meticulous commissural alignment in serial TAV‑in‑TAV procedures is a critical strategy to safeguard future coronary interventions, a consideration that becomes increasingly important as patients require multiple transcatheter re‑interventions.

### [Clinical Pearls] What haemodynamic parameters on transthoracic echocardiography are most sensitive for detecting early valve‑patient mismatch after a third valve implantation?

**Answer**

In a patient who has undergone a *third* transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV), the most sensitive transthoracic echocardiographic variables for early detection of prosthesis‑patient mismatch (PPM) are the **velocity‑derived indices that quantify orifice area restriction**:

| Parameter | What it reflects | Threshold indicating early PPM | Relevance in the case |
|-----------|------------------|--------------------------------|-----------------------|
| **Mean transvalvular pressure gradient** | Global flow resistance across the valve | > 20 mm Hg (≥ 30 mm Hg in follow‑up) | After the third valve the immediate mean gradient was 67 mm Hg, the steepest elevation seen during the series. |
| **Peak aortic jet velocity** | Maximal instantaneous pressure gradient (Jet Vpeak) | > 3.5 m s⁻¹ | Directly proportional to gradient; increments > 0.2 m s⁻¹ flag rising mismatch. |
| **Effective orifice area (AVA) by continuity equation** | Functional area of the prosthesis | < 0.6 cm² (indexed < 0.65 cm² m⁻²) | The first post‑op AVA was 0.9 cm² (index 0.40 cm² m⁻² – below the threshold for severe PPM). |
| **Doppler velocity index (DVI)** = Vpeak / Vbefore | Ratio of downstream to upstream velocity; lower values mean higher obstruction | < 0.25 | The immediate DVI was 0.24, diagnostic of severe PPM. |

**Why these indices are most useful**

1. **Velocity‑based gradients (mean, peak)** are the first signs of rising resistance; they change within hours of valve deployment and are not greatly influenced by loading conditions in the short term.

2. **AVA calculation** incorporates left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) dimensions and is the gold‑standard for quantifying the effective opening. In the reported case, a dramatic rise in mean gradient (67 mm Hg) corresponded with a low AVA (0.9 cm²) and indexed AVA (0.40 m⁻²), immediately classifying the prosthesis as severely mismatched.

3. **DVI** is a load‑independent, easily obtainable ratio. A DVI < 0.25 is highly specific for severe PPM and was seen in the patient right after the third valve.

**Clinical application**

- **Early post‑procedural TTE (within 24–48 h)** should focus on these four values. A sudden increase in gradient or drop in AVA/indexed AVA should prompt further hemodynamic assessment (e.g., pressure tracing, LVOT VTI consistency) and consideration of post‑dilation or, if refractory, alternative strategies.

- In sequential reinterventions, the *relative* change in these parameters compared with the immediately preceding valve is often more informative than absolute numbers, because “baseline” gradients can be already elevated from prior valve frames.

- Finally, if early PPM is identified, prompt optimization (e.g., high‑pressure post‑dilatation, valve‑fit adjustment or annular expansion) can reduce the risk of early heart‑failure symptoms, as illustrated by the patient’s gradient dropping from 67 mm Hg to 27 mm Hg within one month.

**Bottom line** – In TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV cases, the mean pressure gradient, peak velocity, calculated AVA (and its indexed value), and DVI are the most sensitive and immediately actionable TTE parameters for detecting early PPM.

### [Management] Describe the step‑by‑step procedural strategy that was employed to avoid coronary obstruction during the third valve deployment.

**Step‑by‑step strategy to avoid coronary obstruction during the third TAVI**

1. **Pre‑procedural imaging and risk assessment**  
   - **Multislice CT** was used to measure:  
     * Coronary ostial height from the aortic annulus.  
     * Sinus of Valsalva dimensions and valve‑to‑coronary distance.  
     * Interaction between the previous Lotus (27 mm) and Evolut (23 mm) frames and the aortic root.  
   - The CT analysis confirmed that, at the level of the Evolut leaflets, the neo‑skirt would not intrude into the coronary ostia, allowing a safe high‑implantation strategy.

2. **Access and initial hemodynamic assessment**  
   - Left femoral arterial access for the delivery system and a left radial access for continuous coronary imaging (if needed).  
   - Aortography (pigtail) was performed to document severe regurgitation and to verify that the existing frame geometry permits a third valve.

3. **Guidewire positioning and balloon pre‑dilation**  
   - A Safari guidewire was advanced through the femoral sheath into the left ventricle.  
   - A 22 × 40 mm balloon was inflated under rapid ventricular pacing (180 bpm) for pre‑dilation.  
   - Simultaneous aortography confirmed that coronary flow remained unobstructed during this step.

4. **Valve selection and planned high implantation**  
   - A self‑expanding Acurate Neo2 **size S** was chosen because of its:  
     * Supra‑annular leaflet positioning (low frame height).  
     * Ability to be aligned with the Evolut leaflets to minimise PPM.  
   - The deliverable height of the Neo2 was set to **match the Evolut leaflet level**.  This “high” implantation keeps the neo‑skirt above the coronary ostia and reduces the risk of a protruding skirt into the sinuses.

5. **Commissural alignment**  
   - Fluoroscopic landmarks from the pre‑procedural CT were used to align the Neo2 commissures with those of the Evolut valve.  
   - Alignment was adjusted by manipulating the delivery system torque, ensuring that the new valve’s scallops would sit adjacent to the prior leaflets and that the radial expansion would not impinge on the coronary pathways.

6. **Valve deployment**  
   - The Neo2 was advanced to the planned high position and released while the patient was again paced.  
   - The device was **positioned against the Evolut stent frame** rather than the annulus to keep the neo‑skirt from encroaching the coronary ostia.

7. **Post‑dilation to optimize expansion**  
   - A 20 × 40 mm balloon was inflated at full pressure under pacing to expand the Neo2 and to provide a “push” that further dilated the underlying Evolut frame.  
   - This step helped secure valve apposition and prevent any residual obstruction of the coronary ostia.

8. **Final assessment**  
   - Aortography post‑deployment showed:  
     * Correct valve position with no protrusion into the sinuses.  
     * Preserved coronary perfusion (no shading on angiography).  
     * No perivalvular leak and acceptable transvalvular gradients.  

   - TTE immediately afterward confirmed a mobile, well‑functioning valve and a mean gradient of 67 mm Hg, which improved to 27 mm Hg at one month.

**Key elements that prevented coronary obstruction**

- **High implantation** at the level of the Evolut leaflets (keeping the neo‑skirt above the coronaries).  
- **Commissural alignment** guided by CT modeling to avoid overlapping frames over the ostia.  
- **Balloon pre‑ and post‑dilation** to ensure full expansion of the new valve and partial radial expansion of the underlying Evolut frame, thereby keeping the sinus ostia patent.  

This meticulous, imaging‑guided strategy allowed a safe TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV in a triple‑stented anatomy without the need for coronary protection hardware.

### [Pathophysiology] Why was a supra‑annular Acurate neo2 valve chosen over a fully‑in‑annular design in this triple‑stented aortic root?

**Why a supra‑annular Acurate Neo₂ was selected rather than a fully‑in‑annular design**

In a triple‑stented aortic root the principal objective is to **maximize the residual effective orifice area (EOA)** while preserving coronary flow and future access. The two earlier transcatheter valves (Lotus 27 mm and Evolut 23 mm) were positioned at or below the aortic annulus, forming a **layered frame stack** that already occupied radial space and limited the lumen available for subsequent valves.

| Key consideration | What a supra‑annular valve offers | What a fully‑in‑annular valve would add |
|-------------------|---------------------------------|----------------------------------------|
| **Leaflet height & radial expansion** | Leaves the leaflets *above* the annulus, allowing the valve to sit higher without protruding into the ventricular outflow tract or impeding the coronary ostia. | Leaflets are anchored at annular level, forcing the frame to rest against the annulus and thereby pushing the exposed portion of prior frames inward, reducing EOA. |
| **Effective orifice area** | The neo‑oval flow path is created *above* the prior stent frames, minimizing the inward folding of the old leaflets and preserving a larger cross‑sectional area. This is critical in a device already operating with a 13.7 mm minimum lumen (MSCT). | Each additional full‑annular frame adds radial wall, further narrowing the lumen and potentially causing severe prosthesis‑patient mismatch (PPM). The case demonstrated an indexed EOA of 0.4 cm²/m² that would likely deteriorate with a new fully‑annular frame. |
| **Coronary access & protection** | A supra‑annular design allows a **higher implantation** so the neo‑skirt sits *above* the coronary ostia, preserving the valve‑to‑coronary distance measured on MSCT. In this patient, CT confirmed satisfactory clearance for a supra‑annular valve but would have narrowed the margin if the valve were fixed at annular level. | A full‑annular frame positioned too low would leave a neo‑skirt distal to the coronary ostia, increasing the risk of obstruction and complicating future coronary interventions. |
| **Commissural alignment** | The Neo₂ has controlled commissural markers that can be matched to the Evolut leaflets, helping to keep the next valve’s frame low yet aligned, thus keeping the residual lumen open. | Full‑annular valves lack this commissural‑sealing advantage, and misalignment could create “overhanging” leaflets that further reduce flow. |

**In this case** the MSCT‑guided planning revealed a 13.7 mm lumen within the Evolut frame and a limited space for an additionalAnnular prosthesis. By selecting a supra‑annular Acurate Neo₂ (size S), the team achieved:

1. **Higher implantation**—the Neo₂ leaflets were anchored above the Evolut leaflets, preventing additional inward folding of the older frames.
2. **Larger effective orifice area**—post‑dilation improved the EOA from a transiently low 0.9 cm² to 1.1 cm² at 1 month, avoiding the severe PPM seen immediately after deployment (mean gradient 67 mm Hg).
3. **Preservation of coronary flow**—the neo‑skirt sat safely above the coronary ostia, and the procedure was performed without coronary protection.
4. **Future coronary access**—commissural alignment and the reduced frame height of the Neo₂ allowed the possibility of coronary intervention should it become necessary later.

Thus, the supra‑annular design was the anatomically and physiologically optimal choice for a patient with a layered, narrow aortic root and a history of endocarditis, where maximizing flow and preserving access outweigh the benefits of a fully‑annular valve.

### [Clinical Pearls] What role does rapid ventricular pacing play during balloon pre‑dilation and post‑dilation in transcatheter valve procedures?

**Rapid ventricular pacing (RVP) – a procedural “hand‑hold” in balloon‑pre‑ and post‑dilation**

In transcatheter valve procedures, especially in complex valve‑in‑valve scenarios, the aortic annulus and adjacent structures are often sustained by a narrow, highly compliant aortic root that is quickly distended by the balloon. RVP at 170–200 bpm dramatically reduces left‑ventricular ejecta and annular pulse pressure. The resulting transient drop in transvalvular flow gives the balloon a stable, low‑pressure environment, allowing it to dilate the valve ring without over‑stretching the aortic wall or inducing dislodgement of the native or prior valve frames.

*Clinical rationale shown in the case:*

1. **Balloon pre‑dilation (22/40 mm) under RVP** – With ventricular pacing the clinicians achieved a clean, unobstructed aortogram (Fig. 3) and avoided oversizing the first valve in the 13.7 mm Evolut lumen. The pacing also minimized the risk of acute annular rupture in a heavily calcified, stented root.

2. **Post‑dilation (20/40 mm) under RVP** – After deploying the Acurate neo2, a high‑pressure balloon was rapidly paced to force maximal radial expansion. This step is critical in multi‑prosthesis anatomy; the balloon’s pressure pushes the overlapping stent frames, optimising leaflet hinge motion and restoring valvular orifice area (0.9 → 1.1 cm²) while keeping the aortic root from being over‑distended.

*Key points for trainees:*

- **Hemodynamic benefit** – RVP creates a transient, low‑flow state, reducing the risk of balloon migration or leaflet injury.
- **Safety** – The pacing counter‑acts the Doppler velocity spike that would otherwise occur during high‑pressure dilation, preventing sudden increases in transvalvular gradients that could compromise coronary perfusion.
- **Technique** – Love a “gentle‑push” strategy: pace before balloon inflation, maintain pacing for the full inflating period, then stop pacing immediately after balloon deflation.

In summary, RVP during both pre‑ and post‑dilation is a proven method to stabilize the aortic root, protect coronary flow, and achieve optimal valve expansion in multi‑prosthesis transcatheter interventions.

### [Management] After a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV, what long‑term surveillance strategy should be implemented to monitor for structural valve deterioration?

**Long‑term surveillance after a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV**

1. **Baseline assessment (post‑discharge)**  
   * Trans‑Ventricular echocardiography (TTE) obtained within 30 days of the third TAVI – determine valve gradients, effective orifice area (AVA), ventricular size and function, and screen for residual regurgitation.  
   * Multislice computed tomography (MSCT) within 3–6 months to document the final valve‑frame geometry, coronary ostial clearance, neo‑skirt height, and to establish a “template” for future coronary access.  

2. **Early follow‑up (first 12 months)**  
   * Follow‑up TTE at **3 months** (as in the case) and at **6 months**.  
   * Repeat MSCT at **12 months** to assess any frame deformation, leaflet thickening, or early pannus formation—particularly important in a triple‑valve configuration where layered stent frames may progress silently.  
   * If the patient is symptomatic, evaluate sooner.  

3. **Ongoing surveillance (after 12 months)**  
   * TTE at **every 6–12 months** thereafter. The deciding points are:  
     - Gradients > 20 mmHg or AVA < 1.2 cm² (modest stenosis in a fully expanded valve)  
     - Development of new‑onset paravalvular or transvalvular regurgitation > mild  
     - Rising left‑ventricular wall stress or clinical symptoms (NYHA > II).  
   * MSCT or CT aortogram at **years 3 and 5** (or sooner if gradients rise > 10 mmHg from the last study). This imaging is crucial to measure valve‑to‑coronary distance and to assess whether any “neo‑ostial” obstruction may develop with progressive leaflet thickening or calcification.  

4. **Special triggers**  
   * Any episode of fever, bacteremia, or new valvular lesion should prompt a transthoracic, then trans‑oesophageal echo plus CT, and blood cultures.  
   * If the patient requires coronary intervention (e.g., stenting of a native coronary artery), a pre‑procedure coronary CT or left heart catheterization is mandatory to plan for potential difficulty crossing the triple‑stent layers.  

5. **Preventive measures**  
   * Continue guideline‑directed medical therapy for heart failure, atrial fibrillation, and risk factor control.  
   * Immunization against pneumococcus and influenza (to lower endocarditis risk).  
   * Patient education on dental hygiene and early recognition of endocarditis.  

**Rationale**

The case demonstrates successful use of CT‑guided planning and serial echo data to maintain valve function to 1 year. In a triple‑valve setting, structural valve deterioration (SVD) may be accelerated by layered leaflet restriction and frame‑related calcification. Regular Doppler hemodynamics provide the earliest signal of SVD, while periodic CT imaging tracks anatomic changes that echo cannot detect (e.g., neo‑ostial remodeling). This combination aligns with ESC/ACC guideline recommendations for adults undergoing TAVI and with emerging evidence that patients with multiple transcatheter interventions require a more intensive surveillance schedule.

By following the above algorithm—baseline echo and CT, 3‑ and 6‑month echoes, 12‑month CT, then biannual echo with CT at years 3 and 5—we provide a pragmatic, evidence‑based framework for long‑term monitoring that balances diagnostic yield with patient burden.

### [Differential Diagnosis] How does the presence of a prior prosthetic valve endocarditis influence peri‑procedural antibiotic stewardship in a re‑intervention setting?

**Peri‑procedural antibiotic stewardship in a patient with prior prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE)**  

1. **Confirm resolution of infection before intervention**  
   * The decision to re‑intervene hinges on absence of active infection. In the reported case, 6 weeks of targeted intravenous ampicillin plus oral consolidation were completed, with negative blood cultures, repeat transesophageal echocardiography and multidetector CT showing no vegetation or paravalvular leaks. Guideline‑based practice (ESC 2025, IDSA 2023) requires that all cultures be negative for at least 48–72 h and that echocardiographic criteria for “incomplete endocarditis” be absent before transcatheter valve therapies are considered.

2. **Obtain a full pre‑procedural infectious work‑up**  
   * 1) Serial blood cultures (≥2 sets) within 24 h of the planned procedure.  
   * 2) Echocardiographic confirmation of leaflet motion and absence of paravalvular regurgitation.  
   * 3) Imaging (CT/MRI) to rule out abscess or ongoing tissue destruction that could harbor bacteria.  
   In this patient, all three modalities were negative, satisfying the threshold for proceeding.

3. **Short‑term peri‑operative antibiotics**  
   * Even when infection is deemed quiescent, a brief post‑implantation antibiotic course (e.g., 1–2 days of intravenous cefazolin or vancomycin guided by local flora) is reasonable to cover potential peri‑procedural bacteremia, especially when vascular access and cardiopulmonary manipulation could transiently expose non‑sterile blood.  
   * The case report does not indicate any peri‑operative antibiotics beyond completion of the 6‑week course; some centres will continue a 24‑h IV regimen if the patient has high surgical risk or complex vascular access.

4. **Avoid unnecessary long‑term suppressive therapy**  
   * Long‑term suppressive antibiotics (e.g., lifelong rifampin/β‑lactam) are reserved for patients with incomplete eradication, persistent bacteremia, or high risk of relapse (e.g., prosthetic material left in situ).  
   * In the case, no long‑term suppression was started because imaging and cultures confirmed complete cure, and the patient’s PVE episode resolved before the third TAVI.

5. **Incorporate infectious disease consultation into the heart‑team decision**  
   * The multidisciplinary team reviewed infectious disease input, confirming that the endocarditis was fully treated and that operative risk outweighed the risk of reinfection.  
   * This aligns with ESC 2025 recommendations that infectious disease involvement is essential for patients undergoing re‑intervention after PVE.

6. **Post‑procedural surveillance**  
   * Routine follow‑up includes daily vitals, serial lactate/CRP, and repeat blood cultures if fever or hemodynamic instability develops.  
   * In the reported patient, 1‑year follow‑up showed no recurrence, supporting the strategy of aggressive definitive treatment with no suppressive antibiotics.

**Bottom line:** In a patient with a prior treated PVE who meets strict microbiologic, echocardiographic, and imaging criteria for infection quiescence, peri‑procedural antibiotic stewardship should focus on a brief peri‑operative prophylactic regimen and **no long‑term suppression**, provided the cardiac team confirms that the prosthetic material is fully eradiated and that the patient’s surgical risk is prohibitive. This approach, illustrated by the successful TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV in the case report, balances infection control with avoidance of unnecessary antibiotic exposure.

### [Morning Report] What future catheter‑based techniques could be considered if the third valve fails, given the complex layered anatomy in this case?

**Future catheter‑based options if the third valve (Acurate Neo‑2) degrades or fails**

When a TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV prosthesis eventually abandons optimal function, the usual surgical approach becomes prohibitive because of the layered stent frame, adhesions to the native aorta and the patient’s high operative risk (EuroSCORE II ≈ 27%).  The subsequent interventional strategies must therefore balance feasibility against the complex scaffold that now occupies the aortic root.  The following catheter‑based options could be considered, each with a brief discussion of how the ultra‑conformal, multi‑stent anatomy of the presented case would influence feasibility, safety and likely outcome.

| Technique | Rationale | Feasibility in triple‑stent anatomy | Key risks / limitations |
|-----------|-----------|--------------------------------------|--------------------------|
| **Bioprosthetic valve fracture (BVF)** | Expands the stent frame of a previous bioprosthesis by high‑pressure ballooning, thereby enlarging the effective orifice area and reducing PPM. | The Acurate Neo‑2 is supra‑annular and relatively low‑profile; its stent frame can be dissected from the underlying Evolut frame.  In a triple‑stent construct, the outermost valve is the only one that needs to be fractured.  The Evolut and Lotus frames remain intact beneath and will not be affected by the high‑pressure inflation. | Requires a compliant, high‑pressure balloon (e.g., 24–26 mm, 10–12 atm).  Over‑inflation can dislodge a leaf or damage the aortic wall.  Prior evaluation with post‑deployment CT to identify the neo‑annulus and the presence of calcification is mandatory. |

Requires pre‑procedural imaging to confirm that the coronary ostia are ≥10 mm above the Neo‑2 leaflets. |
| **Surgical removal of the outer valve via percutaneous extraction (DAVID or NEAR‑S)** | Mechanical retrieval of the latest valve without full surgical aortic root replacement. | This is an experimental concept; the device tip would have to engage the Acurate Neo‑2 stent struts.  In a duplex‑layered prosthesis, the stent is firmly embedded and adhesion to the native aortic wall is significant.  Early experience suggests high failure rates. | Very high procedural risk, unlikely to be pursued once the third valve is implanted unless a salvage strategy is urgently needed. |
| **Valve‑in‑valve with a lower‑profile valve (e.g., 23 mm Sapien 3 Ultra)** | Shortens the overall implant height, thereby creating more space for the next layer and reducing statistical chance of coronary obstruction. | In a triple‑stented root, the inner lumen is already constrained.  Using a low‑profile balloon‑expandable valve would reduce the radial force and could preserve some coronary clearance.  Post‑deployment, a balloon‑expansion might open the underlying Evolut frame slightly, improving effective orifice area. | Potential for higher transvalvular gradients; the backup frame may limit complete expansion.  Coronary obstruction remains a concern if the Neo‑2 is high‑placed. |
| **Valve‑in‑valve with a supra‑annular, low‑profile valve (e.g., 27 mm Evolut‑T** | Continues the self‑expanding design, preserving commissural alignment and avoiding the need for large‑balloon dilatation. | The triple‑stent environment may still obstruct the valve’s expansion due to the underlying Lotus struts.  Re‑implanting a self‑expanding valve in the same place may not appreciably improve flow unless a controlled fracture of the outer Neo‑2 stent is performed beforehand. | Requires careful sizing; risk of aortic trauma if balloon post‑dilation exceeds the capacity of the outer composite frame. |
| **Surgical aortic root replacement with mechanical or biological valve** | Definitive solution but technically demanding in a patient with heavy calcification and previous endocarditis. | Historically the “gold standard” for valve failure after PVE, but in this case the authors and ESC guidelines already opted against surgery because of prohibitive risk. | Not a catheter option, but remains on the back‑board if all catheter options fail. |

**Practical pathway for the next interventional step**

1. **Re‑imaging** – A contrast–enhanced, high‑resolution CT scan with dedicated valve‑framing analysis should be acquired.  This will guide whether the Neo‑2 stent can be fractured (BVF) or whether BASILICA is required before a further valve‑in‑valve.  The neo‑annulus, valve lumen, and coronary ostia height must be quantified again because the existing frames can alter with time.

2. **Decide on primary strategy** –  
   *If BVF is feasible*, a high‑pressure balloon (e.g., 24 mm E-sheath, 12 atm) can be used to separately expand the Neo‑2’s stent.  Post‑fracture CT and echocardiography confirm increased orifice area and absence of leaflet tear or annular rupture.  
   *If coronary obstruction risk is high*, BASILICA of the Neo‑2 leaflets should be performed prior to a valve‑in‑valve, ideally with a Sapien 3 Ultra or a low‑profile Evolut.  

3. **Redo valve‑in‑valve** – After BVF/BASILICA, a new valve can be implanted with a controlled, low‑height deployment.  Using a self‑expanding design that allows commissural realignment (e.g., Evolut‑F) can preserve future coronary access.

4. **Follow‑up** – Serial TTE and CT at 1 month, 3 months and 1 year to monitor gradients, AVA and left‑ventricular remodeling.  Any rise in gradient beyond 30 mm Hg warrants early re‑intervention planning.

**Conclusion**

In a triple‑stent aortic root, the next catheter‑based step is most likely *bioprosthetic valve fracture* with subsequent valve‑in‑valve, or *BASILICA* if coronary obstruction is predicted.  These techniques have been largely reported in two‐stent environments and are technically adaptable to the layered anatomy described.  Ongoing imaging surveillance and multidisciplinary review remain essential, as future interventions will inevitably be constrained by the cumulative stent burden and may require increasingly novel strategies such as percutaneous valve extraction or the development of evolvable, lower‑profile valves.
