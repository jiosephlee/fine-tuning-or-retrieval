# TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV: When Three Is the New Two

## The Shock of the Third Valve

The notion that a patient will need two transcatheter aortic valve replacements (TAVR) is already a departure from the “one‑off” model. Yet the case from Merkulov et al. shows that even a third intervention can be executed safely, with a one‑year TTE echoing the phrase *“success”*. An elderly gentleman, originally treated with a Lotus valve in 2014, earned a second valve (Evolut) in 2022, survived a bout of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), and then required a third valve—Acurate neo2—in 2024. The story isn’t just about technical prowess; it’s a case study in how our growing body of structural heart disease patients pushes the boundaries of repeat TAVI strategies.

## Why Repeat TAVR Is Becoming the New Normal

- **Longevity of Bioprosthetic Valves**: Bioprosthetic valves now last 10–15 years in low‑risk patients, but earlier failures in high‑risk or younger groups mean that "redo" is becoming the norm.
- **Surgical Risk Continues to Rise**: Even after a first TAVR, if a future reintervention is needed, the operative risk is often higher. Re‑exposure, scar tissue, and prior prosthetic structures make surgery a daunting prospect.
- **Evolving Device Platforms**: With a broader spectrum of device sizes, supra‑annular designs, and improved frame flexibility, clinicians feel more equipped to perform successive procedures.

All of this converges on a simple question: *When is a third TAVR worth it?* The answer is as much a risk assessment as it is an image interpretation.

## Deciding on a Third Intervention: The Framework

1. **Clinical Status & Symptomatology**
   - Persistent or progressive dyspnea
   - Heart failure decompensation
   - Exercise intolerance > 100 m
   - No other reversible causes

2. **Hemodynamic Severity**
   - Mean gradient > 35 mm Hg *or* severe regurgitation
   - Effective orifice area (EOA) < 0.8 cm²
   - Indexed EOA < 0.65 cm²/m²

3. **Imaging Confirmation of Valve Failure**
   - Echo: Doppler and color; confirm misuse/paresis
   - CT: Verify minimal lumen diameter ≤ 13 mm, stent expansion, and coronary clearance

4. **Active Infection Status**
   - No positive blood cultures
   - TEE/CT evidence of no vegetation or abscess
   - Completed antibiotic course per ID specialist

5. **Surgical Risk Scores**
   - EuroSCORE II > 10 % or STS score > 8 % generally tilt recommendation toward TAVR, especially after prior TAVR.

6. **Coronary Anatomy & Future Access**
   - Coronary ostial height ≥ 10–12 mm is safer for high‑implantation
   - Sinus of Valsalva diameter measured; anticipate hype on neo‑skirt clearance
   - Assess whether future coronary re‑intervention may be needed—commissural alignment becomes a must.

7. **Device‑specific Considerations**
   - Valve‑to‑valve (V2V) dimension compatibility
   - Capturing the “high‑implant” opportunity to preserve coronary flow
   - Selecting a device that allows commissural alignment (Acurate neo2, for instance).

## The Tricky Anatomy of a Triple‑Stented Root

Every transcatheter valve layers its own nitinol frame around native aortic roots, or previous valve stents. This layering, while increasing structural integrity, creates a tangled network that can:

- **Reduce Effective Orifice Area**: Over‑overlap of valves pushes leaflets inward.
- **Risk Coronary Obstruction**: Neo‑skirt formation can cover the coronary ostia.
- **Hinder Future Coronary Access**: Multiple frames multiply mesh density.

The Merkulov case exemplifies how to navigate this by:
- **CT‑guided planning** that measures *mid‑annular* clearance and *coronary escape distance*.
- **High‑implantation** of the third valve to sit atop the previous leaflets rather than overlaying the lumen.
- **Gel‑free, supra‑annular** devices that can be angled to keep commissures in step with the coronary arteries.

## Pearls & Caveats

- **Use a Dedicated “Valve‑to‑Valve” CT Toolbox**: Custom software can calculate the exact landing zone and predict neo‑skirt thickness.
- **Don’t Forget the Heavily Calcified Roots**: In heavily speckled anatomies, the risk of incomplete expansion raises post‑dilation complexity.
- **Adopt a Consistent Pressurization Protocol**: Rapid ventricular pacing at 180 bpm used in the Merkulov case limited tidal changes.
- **Be Wary of Patient‑Valve Mismatch (PPM)**: In the initial post‑third valve TTE, a mean gradient of 67 mm Hg was noted—yet it fell to 27 mm Hg within a month. PPM can improve once valve dynamics settle.
- **Post‑Procedural Surveillance Is Essential**: A 1‑year echo is reassuring, but 3‑12 month surveillance can catch early restenosis or subclinical PVE.

## Take‑Home Messages

- **Repeat TAVR Is Safer Than You Might Think**: With meticulous CT planning, a third valve is feasible even after prior TAVI and PVE.
- **Balance Hemodynamics and Anatomy**: A mere improvement in pressure gradients may mask patient‑valve mismatch; always confirm by indexed EA and DVI.
- **Coronary Access Should Be Preserved**: Commissural alignment isn’t a nicety—it’s a blueprint for future coronary interventions.
- **When Surgery is Prohibitive, TAVR is the Answer**: Especially in patients with prior endocarditis, high EuroSCORE/STS scores, and complex root anatomy.
- **Look Ahead**: Sharing data on triple‑stented outcomes will inform device innovation and procedural guidelines.

In a field where the phrase “third valve” used to be a footnote, the Merkulov case shows that the *third* is not only workable but can be robustly durable. As we continue to elect TAVR as first‑line therapy, our heart teams must evolve from simply treating a “stenotic valve” to managing a *family* of prostheses that will outlive our patients’ first‑years alone. The future is not a single intervention, but a series—so let’s keep refining the roadmap for each leg of the journey.
