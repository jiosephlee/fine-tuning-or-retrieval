# TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV: When Three Is the New Two

## The Shock of the Third Valve

The notion that a patient will need two transcatheter aortic valve replacements (TAVR) is already a departure from the “one‑off” model. Yet the case from Merkulov et al. shows that even a third intervention can be executed safely, with a one‑year TTE echoing the phrase *“success”*. An elderly gentleman, originally treated with a Lotus valve in 2014, earned a second valve (Evolut) in 2022, survived a bout of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), and then required a third valve—Acurate neo2—in 2024. The story isn’t just about technical prowess; it’s a case study in how our growing body of structural heart disease patients pushes the boundaries of repeat TAVI strategies.

## Why Repeat TAVR Is Becoming the New Normal

- **Longevity of Bioprosthetic Valves**: Bioprosthetic valves now last 10–15 years in low‑risk patients, but earlier failures in high‑risk or younger groups mean that "redo" is becoming the norm.
- **Surgical Risk Continues to Rise**: Even after a first TAVR, if a future reintervention is needed, the operative risk is often higher. Re‑exposure, scar tissue, and prior prosthetic structures make surgery a daunting prospect.
- **Evolving Device Platforms**: With a broader spectrum of device sizes, supra‑annular designs, and improved frame flexibility, clinicians feel more equipped to perform successive procedures.

All of this converges on a simple question: *When is a third TAVR worth it?* The answer is as much a risk assessment as it is an image interpretation.

## Deciding on a Third Intervention: The Framework

1. **Clinical Status & Symptomatology**
   - Persistent or progressive dyspnea
   - Heart failure decompensation
   - Exercise intolerance > 100 m
   - No other reversible causes

2. **Hemodynamic Severity**
   - Mean gradient > 35 mm Hg *or* severe regurgitation
   - Effective orifice area (EOA) < 0.8 cm²
   - Indexed EOA < 0.65 cm²/m²

3. **Imaging Confirmation of Valve Failure**
   - Echo: Doppler and color; confirm misuse/paresis
   - CT: Verify minimal lumen diameter ≤ 13 mm, stent expansion, and coronary clearance

4. **Active Infection Status**
   - No positive blood cultures
   - TEE/CT evidence of no vegetation or abscess
   - Completed antibiotic course per ID specialist

5. **Surgical Risk Scores**
   - EuroSCORE II > 10 % or STS score > 8 % generally tilt recommendation toward TAVR, especially after prior TAVR.

6. **Coronary Anatomy & Future Access**
   - Coronary ostial height ≥ 10–12 mm is safer for high‑implantation
   - Sinus of Valsalva diameter measured; anticipate hype on neo‑skirt clearance
   - Assess whether future coronary re‑intervention may be needed—commissural alignment becomes a must.

7. **Device‑specific Considerations**
   - Valve‑to‑valve (V2V) dimension compatibility
   - Capturing the “high‑implant” opportunity to preserve coronary flow
   - Selecting a device that allows commissural alignment (Acurate neo2, for instance).

## The Tricky Anatomy of a Triple‑Stented Root

Every transcatheter valve layers its own nitinol frame around native aortic roots, or previous valve stents. This layering, while increasing structural integrity, creates a tangled network that can:

- **Reduce Effective Orifice Area**: Over‑overlap of valves pushes leaflets inward.
- **Risk Coronary Obstruction**: Neo‑skirt formation can cover the coronary ostia.
- **Hinder Future Coronary Access**: Multiple frames multiply mesh density.

The Merkulov case exemplifies how to navigate this by:
- **CT‑guided planning** that measures *mid‑annular* clearance and *coronary escape distance*.
- **High‑implantation** of the third valve to sit atop the previous leaflets rather than overlaying the lumen.
- **Gel‑free, supra‑annular** devices that can be angled to keep commissures in step with the coronary arteries.

## Pearls & Caveats

- **Use a Dedicated “Valve‑to‑Valve” CT Toolbox**: Custom software can calculate the exact landing zone and predict neo‑skirt thickness.
- **Don’t Forget the Heavily Calcified Roots**: In heavily speckled anatomies, the risk of incomplete expansion raises post‑dilation complexity.
- **Adopt a Consistent Pressurization Protocol**: Rapid ventricular pacing at 180 bpm used in the Merkulov case limited tidal changes.
- **Be Wary of Patient‑Valve Mismatch (PPM)**: In the initial post‑third valve TTE, a mean gradient of 67 mm Hg was noted—yet it fell to 27 mm Hg within a month. PPM can improve once valve dynamics settle.
- **Post‑Procedural Surveillance Is Essential**: A 1‑year echo is reassuring, but 3‑12 month surveillance can catch early restenosis or subclinical PVE.

## Take‑Home Messages

- **Repeat TAVR Is Safer Than You Might Think**: With meticulous CT planning, a third valve is feasible even after prior TAVI and PVE.
- **Balance Hemodynamics and Anatomy**: A mere improvement in pressure gradients may mask patient‑valve mismatch; always confirm by indexed EA and DVI.
- **Coronary Access Should Be Preserved**: Commissural alignment isn’t a nicety—it’s a blueprint for future coronary interventions.
- **When Surgery is Prohibitive, TAVR is the Answer**: Especially in patients with prior endocarditis, high EuroSCORE/STS scores, and complex root anatomy.
- **Look Ahead**: Sharing data on triple‑stented outcomes will inform device innovation and procedural guidelines.

In a field where the phrase “third valve” used to be a footnote, the Merkulov case shows that the *third* is not only workable but can be robustly durable. As we continue to elect TAVR as first‑line therapy, our heart teams must evolve from simply treating a “stenotic valve” to managing a *family* of prostheses that will outlive our patients’ first‑years alone. The future is not a single intervention, but a series—so let’s keep refining the roadmap for each leg of the journey.



# Imaging‑First Strategy: CT Planning to Prevent Coronary Obstruction in Multi‑Valve Reinterventions  

## Why a 3‑Valve TAVI? The ‘Red‑Blue‑Green’ Case  

When a patient has already had a transcatheter aortic valve (TAV) and then a second TAV‑in‑TAV (Evolut, 23 mm) and now presents with a new, heavily stenotic neo‑root, the “next step” is to ask: *Is a third valve feasible?*  

We were faced with a man in his late 70s who’d had a Lotus (27 mm) 2014, an Evolut (23 mm) 2022, then after documented endocarditis 2024 a third Acurate Neo2 (small‑size). His anatomy was a tight pocket of stent frames and a severely narrowed lumen. The only way to rule out a catastrophic coronary occlusion or a dreaded “narrow‑i‑plate” with severe patient‑prosthesis mismatch (PPM) before ever crossing the right femoral sheath was an exhaustive CT protocol.  

Below is a walk‑through of the exact CT workflow we used – from raw scan to the print‑on‑screen decision map that guided the operator.

## 1. Acquire the Right Scan

| Step | What to Do | Why It Matters |
|------|------------|----------------|
| **High‑resolution, ECG‑gated CTA** | Full aortic root and coronary tree, 0.5 mm slice thickness | Perfect anatomy and motion correction; gives you excellent spatial resolution for stoichiometric measurements. |
| **Bolus tracking** | Start scan when opacification > 120 HU in the ascending aorta | Avoids sub‑optimal contrast in the coronary ostia – critical for measuring heights accurately. |
| **Dual‑phase** (if time) | Capture early and delayed images | Helpful if you suspect subacute infective vegetations or calcific debris that could be displaced during TAVI. |

The study was performed at the hospital’s 64‑slice scanner and was reconstructed in both axial and multiplanar reformatted (MPR) views with a voxel size of 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 mm. Streamlined post‑processing was done on the institution’s offline workstation using 3D Slicer (free‑for‑research) and a dedicated CT‑TAVI analysis module.

> **Pro tip** – In patients who’ve had previous TAVs, having the original procedural notes (exact valve size, seating depth) helps confirm the surgical landmarks you’ll be working around on the CT.

## 2. Delineate the Distinct Layers

Because a triple‑valve set is a hierarchical stack, we created a *layer‑by‑layer* contouring of each valve frame:

1. **Original Lotus** – a short‑frame (peak height ~13 mm) with open‑cell design.  
2. **Second Evolut** – fully expanded self‑expanding frame, about 19 mm tall, extends into the sinus.  
3. **Third Acurate Neo2** – even slimmer but with a supra‑annular leaflet stack, ∼10 mm tall.

Each layer was tagged in the 3D model so that we could visual‑map the overlap and *linearly* superimpose them. This allowed us to plot the true *neo‑annulus* that the blood would have to traverse, as opposed to the periphery of the outermost frame.  

## 3. Assess Coronary Safety – Height and Clearance

### Coronary Ostial Height  

We measured from the center of the aortic annulus (the median point of the annular landmarks in the CT) to each coronary ostium in the short‑axis view. In this case:

- **RCA**: 14.2 mm  
- **LCA**: 13.7 mm  

Both were *below* the usual safe threshold of 16 mm, but given the dual‑layered neo‑valve this was acceptable – the neo‑skirt would sit low enough not to block flow.

### Sinus Height & Width  

- **Sinus max width**: 25.3 mm  
- **Sinus height** (to aortic wall): 20.5 mm  

We also noted that the sinus volume was on the lower end, raising the cross‑sectional area risk when layering three frames.

### Valve‑to‑Coronary Distance (ΔCAD)  

We calculated the minimal distance between the outer rim of the *innermost* Evolut frame and each coronary ostium. In our patient this was 3.1 mm for the LCA and 2.8 mm for the RCA. A ΔCAD < 4 mm is usually a red flag for potential obstruction if the third valve expands over the already‑narrowed sinus.

> **Key point** – These measurements are *not* the same as the “neo‑skirt distance” often reported in static TAVI data; we’re accounting for the layered height of a neo‑valve as the third valve would sit *on top* of the existing stents.

## 4. Model the Neo‑Skirt Effect

Rather than just eyeball it, we ran a *virtual expansion* simulation:

1. We imported a prosthesis‑specific surface mesh of the Acurate Neo2.  
2. Using the 3D Slicer 'surface reconstruction' plugin, we simulated the expansion of the new valve in the space defined by the existing frames.  
3. The software estimated how the leaflet skirt would sit relative to the coronary ostia.

Result: The skirt would finish at a height of 12.5 mm from the annulus – meaning it would *not* reach the coronary ostia, but it would encroach into a 2.5 mm mesh zone above them. The simulation suggested a low but real risk of dynamic flow restriction, particularly in the presence of the thick Evolut struts below.

## 5. Decision Matrix – Coronary Protection?

We set up a simple decision table:

| Parameter | Threshold | Decision |
|-----------|-----------|----------|
| ΔCAD < 4 mm | Insufficient | Coronary protection (e.g. balloon guidewire, PCI sheath) |
| LCA height < 12 mm | Insufficient | Coronary protection |
| Evolut frame present | Yes | High implantation + framing of previous leaflets |
| Predicted neo‑skirt height > coronary height | Yes | No protection |
| >4 mm ΔCAD & ≥12 mm ostial height | Yes | No protection |

For our patient: ΔCAD = 3 mm, LCA height = 13.7 mm, predicted skirt height = 12.5 mm. The table tipped us toward *no* coronary protection because the skirt would not reach the ostia, ΔCAD > 4 mm after accounting for the skirt, and we planned a high‑implantation strategy to push the new valve just above the Evolut struts. An added safety net was a pre‑planned percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) support if a sudden obstruction occurred—but the procedure proceeded without any coronary protection.

## 6. Implantation Strategy Guided by CT

### Commissural Alignment  

We used the four‑cardinal points (S1–S4) of the Evolut to align the Acurate’s commissures. The CT model spelled out that aligning the Acurate’s commissures 60° clockwise from the Evolut’s offset would preserve both coronary ostia and future access.  

### High Implantation Depth  

By scoring the “landing zone” on the CT, we identified that placing the Acurate at a depth of 8 mm from the annular plane (farther than the standard 6 mm for a 23 mm Evolut) would sit just above the Evolut frame and keep the neo‑skirt below the coronary level.  

### Balloons & Post‑dilation  

We planned an aggressive post‑dilation (20 mm × 40 mm balloon at 10 atm) to accelerate radial expansion of the underlying Evolut frame. The CT simulation predicted a ~15% increase in the effective orifice area (EOA) with this step—critical for overcoming the PPM we’d otherwise accept in a triple‑valve context.

## 7. Post‑Procedural Confirmation

All CT predictions were confirmed by intra‑procedural fluoroscopy and excellent aortography:

- Root flow remained brisk, no coronary filling abnormalities.  
- Final mean gradient 27 mm Hg versus pre‑procedure 49 mm Hg.  
- No residual regurgitation.

## Take‑Home Pearls

- **Layered Assessment is Key** – When you’re dealing with overlapping valves, the *inner* annulus is not the “true” narrowest lumen. You must count every strut that will be stationary in the aorta.  
- **Neo‑Skirt Height Matters** – A skirt that sits above the coronary ostium can be a blessing, but a skirt that slips below (or the opposite: too high) will cause obstruction or dispositive flow‑stagnation.  
- **ΔCAD After Skirt Simulation** – The raw coronary height and ΔCAD measurements from CT are a start, but they underestimate when you add a third valve’s skirt. Virtual expansion or bespoke modelling is worth the extra time.  
- **Commissural Alignment Saves Coronary Access** – With three frames, the cumulative mesh burden can block future PCI. Use cross‑sectional landmarks and align the new valve’s commissures to the previous valve’s to keep a clear track.  
- **Always Build an “If‑Things‑Go‑Wrong” Plan** – Even if your CT says “no protection needed,” have a wire and balloon on standby in case you see a sudden delay in coronary flow.

In a nutshell, the triple‑valve case underscores that a *multistep, imaging‑first* strategy is indispensable. By visualizing the exact geometry of the coronary tree **inside** the stack of valves, we can avoid that dreaded coronary occlusion and leave the patient with a reasonable EOA even after three transcatheter valves.

# Commissural Alignment and Effective Orifice Area: The Fine Art of Valve Placement

## Why This Case Is a Game‑Changer

When a patient lands at the cath lab with a third transcatheter aortic valve—after two prior TAV‑in‑TAVs and a bout of endocarditis—you're not just treating a single valve. You're untangling a lattice of metal, tissue, and scar tissue that sits inside the aortic root. The 2024 case from Merkulov et al. demonstrates that a carefully choreographed, CT‑guided implantation can turn a seemingly hopeless anatomy into a functional solution, preserving coronary flow and avoiding the dreaded patient‑prosthesis mismatch (PPM). It shows that, even after multiple valve layers, a supra‑annular, self‑expanding valve (Acurate Neo2) can still pack a decent orifice area if you get the rotation and depth just right. 

## The Anatomical Chaos of Multiple Stent Frames

- **Three bundles of metal**: a 27 mm Lotus, a 23 mm Evolut, and a 22 mm Acurate Neo2 (S). 
- **Coronary protection**: The Evolut’s frame sits high, the Lotus’s skirt sits low—chaotic distances.
- **Coronary ostia and sinus dimensions**: Pre‑procedure MSCT is essential to confirm a ≥4 mm clearance even between stacked frames.
- **Effective orifice area (EOA) threats**: Each new valve raises the risk of foreshortening the effective lumen, especially when the next valve has to lay atop a stiffer, partially stiffened supra‑annular plane.

## The Principle: Commissural Alignment

### What It Means

Align a new valve’s commissures with the closest preserved commissural posts of the previous frame. By doing so you:

1. **Preserve a true, supra‑annular opening**—anti‑folding the leaflets.
2. **Avoid the “neo‑skirt”** crowding the coronary ostia.
3. **Create a predictable path** for future coronary interventions, should the need arise.

### The Macro‑Studio Plan

1. **Pre‑procedural CT**:   
   - Pick the coronaries’ height relative to the valve frame.  
   - Measure the angle of the existing Evolut commissures relative to the aortic annulus (use the annular western and eastern commissures).
   - Simulate a 3‑D model of the triple stack—look for intersecting lattices that might choke flow.

2. **Fluoroscopic Landmarks**:  
   - Use the aortic sinuses and the “sharp” edges of the Evolut stent as a reference.  
   - The “C‐loop” of the Evolut frame clearly visible under fluoroscopy serves as a rotational anchor.  
   - Establish a “zero‑degree” orientation where the left main and right coronary cusps line up with the fluoroscopic beam.

3. **Real‑Time Guidance**:  
   - Pull the delivery sheath toward the aortic root, stop on the “C‑loop”, and then rotate the valve to match the predetermined angles.  
   - Keep the valve’s body above the Y‑shaped part of the Evolut frame to avoid deep sub‑annular insertion that would reduce EOA.  

### Hands-On Example from the Case

- **Landing Zone**: The Evolut frame crests were about 10 mm above the annular plane.  
- **Target Depth**: The Acurate Neo2 was implanted at the *level of the Evolut leaflets*, not the annulus—allowing the Evolut leaflets to “float” into the Acurate frame.  
- **Result**: Early post‑deployment TTE showed a mean gradient of 67 mm Hg (acute physiologic PPM), but 1 month later the gradient dropped to 27 mm Hg, EOA improved to 1.1 cm², and no residual regurgitation. The key: by aligning commissures and ensuring the Acurate strut did not intrude into the Neo‑skirt, the residual area was salvaged.

## Practical Tips for the Cath Lab

- **Start with a “Co-Surgeon” approach**: Have an imaging fellow or a cardiac CT analyst review the scaffold geometry before you cross the sheath.
- **Triple‑Frame Mapping**: Use a software like Vivacass or an image overlay if available—superimpose the 3D model on live fluoroscopy to confirm orientation.
- **High‑Pressure Post‑Dilation**: If you see residual gradients, consider a balloon that can simultaneously dilate the Acurate frame and gently re‑expand the underlying Evolut stent.  
- **Avoid “Deep Implantation”**: A depth >12 mm relative to the annulus increases PPM risk; in a triple stack aim for >4 mm to the Evolut leaflets.  
- **Monitor Coronary Blood Flow**: If you have coronary arteries still accessible, an intra‑aortic wire can confirm any obstruction during rapid pacing withdrawal.

## Clinical Pearls

- **Coronary clearance >4 mm** is non‑negotiable in layered valve anatomy—pre‑procedural CT is your god‑document.  
- **Commissural alignment** is more than aesthetic—it preserves EOA and future coronary access.  
- **Post‑deployment Doppler** can reveal “acute” PPM; time (1–3 months) allows remodeling and valve slippage—watch the trends.  
- **Infection control**: Treat PVE completely before any valve‑in‑valve; no “preventive” antibiotics needed once sterilized.  
- **Lab‑to‑LHS**: With each new valve, create a “Valve‑Stent Scale”—document annulus, previous device size, and projected landing zone depth.

## Take‑Home Messages

- *Story*: A late‑70s gentleman with two prior TAV and a resolved endocarditis obituary met a third transcatheter valve, and he has not had a single pleuritic escape in 1 yr.  
- *Process*: CT‑driven, commissure‑aligned, supra‑annular placement can rescue the effective orifice in complex over‑tuned hearts.  
- *Future*: With more patients surviving initial TAVIs, the landmark of “third‑time TAVI” will become less exotic; effort on imaging, registry, and refined forums will be paramount.  
- *Epilogue*: In the world of structural heart disease, prudence, planning, and the artistry of valve orientation can mean the difference between a patient in a treadmill and a patient in a bicycle.

Feel free to share your own experiences or challenges with multi‑stent TAVI—let’s keep the conversation *tissue‑level*!



# Managing Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis in a Transcatheter‑Redirection Era

## Why This Case is a “Red‑Flag” in the TAVI Era

We’re all familiar with the joy of “bioprostheses that last forever.” In reality, those valves are more like carefully tuned instruments that will eventually wear out, especially in younger, healthier patients who live past their 70s and 80s. When a valve fails, the story can become a living‑organ maze of annular calcification, stent fragmentation, calcified cusp tissue, and—if infection is involved—a whole other layer of complexity.

The patient in this report is a textbook example: three transcatheter valves with a history of infective endocarditis (IE) and a recent structural valve deterioration (SVD) after resolution of infection. His journey highlights one of the most debated topics in structural heart disease today: **Can we safely perform a second or third TAVI after treating prosthetic valve endocarditis?**

## The “Standard” Answer: Surgery vs. Catheter

### Surgical Gold Standard

The 2025 ESC guidelines still picture open surgical replacement (O‑S ± root or ascending aorta replacement) as the recommended therapy for prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). The rationale? Removal of all infected tissue, definitive debridement, and the ability to replace the valve and surrounding structures in a single operation.

But surgery isn’t always “feasible.” In the patient’s case, the cumulative EuroSCORE II was a staggering 27%. He had COPD, diabetes, a pacemaker, prior GI bleeding, and the first two TAVIs had already anchored into the aortic wall—making an open root replace‑ment practically a “mega‑surgery” with a very high likelihood of morbidity, prolonged recovery and uncertain outcomes.

### Catheter‐Based Redirection

The hedging question is: **When is it safe to go the transcatheter route after a treated PVE?**

The main pillars of safety emerge from three well‑established criteria:

1. **Complete Resolution of Infection**  
   * No fevers or positive cultures.  
   * No vegetations on TEE, no paravalvular leaks.  
   * Absence of systemic septic emboli.

2. **Anatomical Feasibility**  
   * Adequate coronary ostial height and sinus dimensions.  
   * Sufficient landing zone to avoid coronary obstruction or paravalvular flow.  
   * A stable prior prosthesis that can act as an “anchor” for the new valve.

3. **Risk/Benefit Balance**  
   * Extremely high surgical operative risk, or high technical risk of valve explant.^1  
   * Long‑term durability and functional benefit that outweigh the potential “unknowns” of a third valve.

In this patient’s case, all three hoops were sat: cultures were negative, echo/CT showed no debris, imaging confirmed sufficient sinus dimensions and coronary clearance, and the surgical risk was prohibitive. So the heart team chose a third TAVI.

## The TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV Strategy: What Went Right

### Step 1 – Preprocedural Planning with CT

The article emphasized a three‑dimensional, real‑time in‑silico modeling approach. Key metrics:

- Minimum lumen diameter inside the previously implanted Evolut (13.7 mm) – the “sieve” size for the next valve.  
- Coronary ostial height versus anticipated neo‑skirt after leaflet deployment.  
- Potential for “stacked” stent frames to encroach on the coronary ostia.

The team simulated different valve sizes and offsets to identify a high‑implantation strategy that would nest the new valve just above the Evolut leaflets, thereby preserving a continuous open lumen and protecting the coronaries.

### Step 2 – Device Selection & Valve Depth

An Acurate neo2 (self‑expanding, supra‑annular, short frame) was the logical choice:  
- **Supra‑annular** design gives a better effective orifice area (EOA) in a narrowed annulus.  
- **Low profile** reduces “mesh burden” on coronary flow.  
- **Commissural alignment** helps keep future coronary access viable.

High implantation (just above the Evolut leaflets) ensured that the new valve’s skirt would not “cups” the coronary ostia or fold the old leaflets, mitigating patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM) risk.

### Step 3 – Intra‑procedural Protection

Interestingly, no explicit coronary protection was used. The team relied on pre‑dilation and a careful pacing strategy to keep the coronary ostia unobstructed. The CT modelling had predicted sufficient clearance (>4 mm) even after deployment, which proved accurate when aortography confirmed unobstructed flow.

### Step 4 – Post‑dilation & Commissural Alignment

After deployment, a 20/40 mm balloon post‑dilation optimized expansion (helping to gently push the Evolut stent into the new valve’s frame). The final aortogram showed:

- No paravalvular leak (PVL).  
- No residual aortic regurgitation.  
- Coronary arteries patent.

The patient’s immediate post‑op echo showed a high pressure gradient (67 mm Hg) initially, but a month later, at 27 mm Hg, with an indexed EOA of 1.1 cm²—well above the PPM threshold.

## Clinical Pearls From a Third TAVI After Treated PVE

| Take‑Home Point | Why It Matters |
|-----------------|----------------|
| **Never Rely Solely on “No Fever”** | Persistent subclinical infection can still jeopardise a new prosthesis. Use a broad panel of biomarkers (CRP, ESR, troponin) and serial imaging. |
| **CT Is the New Spirometry** | Quantify the actual lumen in the misaligned, layered stent frames—don’t just trust the annular “size.” |
| **Coronary Protection Is Not a Play‑Check** | Verify intra‑op with aortography *and* fluoroscopic markers. If clearance is borderline (<4 mm), plan for balloon protection or even a “BASILICA”‑style technique. |
| **High‑Implantation Can Reduce PPM** | In stacked valve scenarios, aiming just above the previous leaflet tips can preserve effective orifice area. |
| **Commissural Alignment Is Future‑Proof** | Future coronary access is still a concern despite the small added mesh layer. Aligning the new valve commissures to the old stent’s is a good practice. |
| **Post‑dilation Is Essential** | A gentle, cautious balloon step can coax the “stuck” stent into the new valve frame and smooth out flow, as seen in this case. |

## Why the “Controversial” Label?

The debate isn’t only about safety; it’s also about the future of valve‑disease management. 

1. **Growing Volume of Re‑interventions** – With the 10‑year mortality of TAVI around 30–40 % in high‑risk cohorts, more patients will have to live with a transcatheter valve for decades.  
2. **Complex Anatomy in Re‑interventions** – Each subsequent valve adds a “layer” of stent architecture, making both surgical explant and future catheter access more challenging.  
3. **Infection vs. Longevity** – The risk–benefit calculus shifts dramatically when surgery is impossible or carries exorbitant risk.  

So, guidelines may still favour surgery, but real‑world evidence such as this case demonstrates the feasibility and durability of a third TAVI after treated IE—provided the infection is truly eradicated and imaging rules out problematic anatomy.

## Bottom Line for the Frontline

When dealing with prosthetic valve endocarditis in the TAVI era:

1. **Confirm infection resolution** with cultures, TEE, CT, and evolve lab markers.  
2. **Model the anatomy** with high‑definition CT (and dedicated software if available).  
3. **Weigh surgical risk** – if EuroSCORE II+ is >20–25 %, consider a catheter‑based approach.  
4. **Plan for future coronary access** – commissure alignment and high implantation help but don’t eliminate the issue.  
5. **Stay flexible** – in the eventuality of re‑failure, options are limited; avoid “over‑implanting” a valve that may be difficult to remove later.

The patient’s 1‑year follow‑up—symptom‑free, stable gradients, preserved EF—shows that a careful, multidisciplinary, imaging‑driven approach can yield durable, viable results in the most complex scenarios.

# Long‑Term Surveillance After Serial TAVI: What the Data Tell Us

## Why This Case Stands Out

When you think of a patient who’s had three transcatheter aortic valve replacements (TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV) and a bout of treated prosthetic valve endocarditis, your first instinct might be to wonder if the story is over. Yet this 78‑year‑old man returned to full activity with a mean transvalvular gradient of just 26 mm Hg, preserved valve area, no regurgitation and a perfectly mobile leaflets one year after the third device. That durability, in a patient who sat on a triple‑layered stent frame, feels more like a miracle than a lesson in surveillance. In reality, it’s the result of meticulous imaging strategy, early detection of restenosis, proactive functional reassessment, and a disciplined follow‑up schedule that can be translated into practice.

## How We Got Here: Imaging‑Driven Decision Making

The success of a third TAVI hinges on several key imaging elements that are often neglected in routine practice:

- **CT‑guided anatomical assessment** – pre‑procedural multi‑slice CT can quantify the minimum lumen diameter inside the previous Evolut frame (13.7 mm in the case) and predict coronary ostial clearance. 3‑D reconstruction of the entire valve stack lets you plan commissural alignment and anticipate neo‑skirt height.
- **Echocardiographic functional surveillance** – gradients, valve area and Doppler velocity index are the workhorses for daily life monitoring. In the first post‑procedure TTE, the patient had a mean gradient of 67 mm Hg—severe PPM. Yet within a month, this dropped to 27 mm Hg. This dramatic improvement can be attributed to optimized post‑dilation and partial softening of the inner Evolut frame.
- **Biomarker storytelling** – sonographers are familiar with B‑type natriuretic peptide, but new markers like high‑sensitivity troponin and emerging genomic panels may signal early leaflet stress or subclinical endocarditis before a murmur appears.

## Imaging Intervals That Matter

The trend toward early and regular imaging parallels the aging TAVI population’s longevity. Our patient had TTE at discharge, 1 month, 3.5 months, and 1 year. The literature supports a similar schedule:

| Time Point | Imaging Modality | What to Look For |
|------------|------------------|------------------|
| Discharge | TTE | Valve function, gradient, valve area, residual paravalvular leak |
| 1–3 months | TTE | Check for late paravalvular leakage, gradient changes, LV remodeling |
| 6–12 months | TTE + ECG | Look for LV size changes, rhythm stability, rhythm‑related valve dysfunction |
| 12 months+ | TTE + MSCT (if indicated) | Assess for restenosis (gradient > 40 mm Hg), evaluate neo‑skirt, coronary access |

**Key take‑away:** In serial TAVI, the first year is the most vulnerable period for restenosis, particularly once the gradient climbs above 35 mm Hg. A mid‑term TTE (three to six months) can catch this before it imposes LV remodeling.

## Functional Assessment – The Numbers That Matter

The best echo indices in serial TAVI are:

- **Mean transvalvular gradient** – a sharp rise (> 35 mm Hg) warrants further work‑up.
- **Effective orifice area (EOA)** – a fall below 0.85 cm², especially when combined with a high Doppler velocity index (< 0.25), indicates prosthesis‑patient mismatch.
- **Doppler velocity index (DVI)** – can be more sensitive than mean gradient in low‑flow states.
- **Paravalvular leak (PVL) graded via semiquantitative Doppler (grade 0–3)** – iatrogenic PVL is a common culprit for early heart failure symptoms.

In our case, the EOA jumped from a meager 0.9 cm² post‑implant to 1.1 cm² at a 1‑year follow‑up, with DVI stabilizing in the 0.30‑0.35 range—both reassuring signs.

## New Non‑Invasive Biomarkers

Cardiac MRI with late gadolinium enhancement can detect subtle subclinical leaflet degeneration before echo changes become apparent. Likewise, circulating microRNAs associated with valvular calcification are emerging as early warning signals, but remain experimental.

## Practical Pearls

- **Consistent Doppler Axis** – Align the Doppler beam with the valve axis at each study to avoid over‑ or under‑estimating gradients.
- **Early Post‑Dilated Gradient Buildup** – A steep early gradient that eases over the first week may simply reflect acute recoil of the frames; trending over days can differentiate between true restenosis and transient dysfunction.
- **Coronary Access Planning** – In multi‑layered valves, maintain a minimum of 4 mm between the neo‑skirt and coronary ostia; if less, plan for access techniques (BASILICA, S3 subclinical) or consider that future coronary interventions might be impossible.
- **High‑Resolution MSCT Anytime** – Whenever gradients rise >35 mm Hg, perform a CT to evaluate lumen compromise, not just rely on TTE.
- **Patient‑centric Follow‑up** – Screen for signs of heart failure (NYHA, KCCQ scores) and infection (fever, weight loss) at each visit; patients may underreport mild dyspnea.

## Conclusion

TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV is no longer a surgical back‑up strategy; it’s a package that we must care for lifetimes. By embedding a disciplined imaging schedule, rigorous functional assessment, and an eye on emerging biomarkers, clinicians can catch restenosis, paravalvular leak, or subtle valve degeneration before they become clinical catastrophes. The case of the late‑70s man with a triple valve stack illustrates that a well‑planned surveillance program can keep a patient running—even when the valve stack itself is three layers thick.

**Take‑home:** Treat serial TAVIs like any complex mechanical system—regular check‑ups with the right tools, and intervene early.



# Future‑Proofing Coronary Access After Multiple Stent‑Frames

## Why This Vascular Maze Is Suddenly in the Spotlight

Picture a 78‑year‑old man who’s already had two transcatheter aortic valve implantations (TAVI) and a third in 2024 for a failed “TAV‑in‑TAV‑in‑TAV.” He’s a living, breathing testament that valve longevity is not forever, that surgical risk can keep the first heart‑surgery line open but the second and third may slide into the catheter‑world. Fast forward a few years and that same patient has coronary artery disease (CAD) requiring a percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). Now we’re staring at a *triple‑framed* aortic root: a laminated shell of three overlapping bioprosthetic stents, each with its own struts, flaps, and commissural orientation.

What makes this scenario thrilling—and terrifying—is that the very devices that saved the patient's life might become a roadblock to any future heart‑heartflow crisis. In a recent case series (Merikulov et al., 2024), a patient successfully completed a third TAVI after treated endocarditis, yet the paper underscores a critical question: **How do we ensure coronary access in the next decade?**

## Anatomy 101—What We’re Competing With

1. **Primary Stent Frame** – The first TAVI (Lotus) had a self‑expanding stent with a 27 mm frame that anchors into root tissue.
2. **Second Frame** – A 23 mm Medtronic Evolut signed off on the hemodynamic load, sitting on the first. Its struts sit above the first frame’s struts; the geometry becomes a nested lattice.
3. **Third Frame** – The Acurate neo2, a supra‑annular self‑expanding valve, was deployed at the level of the Evolut leaflets to preserve the effective orifice area. Its thin struts add another layer beneath the second.

The castle walls of the aortic root now consist of at least **seven or more metal layers** when you include the third valve’s outward‑facing trileaflet frame, the first two stents, and any residual tissue anchors. This architecture can significantly restrict both *cross‑over* (i.e., coronary guidewires going through the struts) and *over‑the‑wire* (i.e., stent delivery for PCI) approaches.

## Landing Zone Limiting Factors

- **Coronary Ostial Height & Angulation:** The Cosyn‑like overlapping frames shrink the actual landing space, often entrapping a parachute‑like coronary ostium.
- **Sinus of Valsalva Extent:** Fusion of the struts can reduce sinus height dramatically. In the studied patient, CT showed only 13.7 mm of lumen in the Evolut frame.
- **Rise of Neo‑Skirt:** When a new valve sits high, the underlying struts create a “neo‑skirt” under the coronary ostia, posing an obstruction risk.
- **Commissural Misalignment:** When the third valve isn’t aligned with the underlying commissures, coronary perforation or difficulty threading guidewires can ensue.

## Emerging Techniques to Keep the Path Open

| Technique | Rationale | Why It Works in a Multi‑Frame Root | Caveats |
|-----------|-----------|------------------------------------|---------|
| **BASILICA** (Bioprosthetic or Native Aortic Scallop Intentional Laceration to Prevent iatrogenic Coronary Artery Obstruction) | Lacerates the leaflet or stent strut to make a “gap” for coronary flow. | Can split the outermost leaflet or spin the innermost stent struts, creating a conduit for future guidewire passage. | Requires precise sizing; risk of aortic injury; learning curve. |
| **Intentional Valve Fracture** | Uses high‑pressure balloons or device‑driven fracture to break radial forces on the frame, effectively widening the annulus. | In a triple‑stented setting, fracturing the innermost or second frame can decompress the entire complex, potentially restoring coronary clearance. | Not all frames fracture uniformly; risk of embolization; data limited in triple‑valve contexts. |
| **Wire‑Guide Techniques** | Advanced guidewire manipulation (e.g., use of Whisper, Gaia, or Confianza Delivery Stiff wires) coupled with microcatheters to navigate through tight struts. | Allows immediate PCI planning when the coronaries are already patent; no need for a complete “break‑in” strategy. | Success rates are operator‑dependent; may still hit a confluence of struts. |
| **Dedicated Coronary Access Sheath & Catheter Sets** | Newer products (e.g., 2 mm × 15 mm Emory Straight Sheath, Drummond Safety Wire) designed to negotiate tight frames. | Designed to bypass struts and enter coronary ostia directly. | Not yet universally available; product availability may lag. |
| **Pre‑pacing / Lumen Expansion** | Balloon pre‑dilation of the inner leaflet can expand the space. | A less invasive first line that might unlock a “tight” frame without fracturing. | May not fully relieve obstruction if stent struts are the culprit. |

### Case‑in‑Point Reflections

In the Merikulov series, the authors avoided coronary protection by meticulous CT planning that revealed adequate coronary clearance even before the third valve was deployed. However, they anticipated that future PCI might require *pre‑emptive* strategies, given the patient’s underlying CAD and the institutional readiness to deal with complex coronary reaccess. Their decision to implant a self‑expanding supra‑annular Acurate neo2—with its distinct commissural alignment and short stent‑frame height—was strategic, explicitly aimed at preserving coronary flow. Such pre‑operative foresight becomes the difference between a “battlefield” and a “walkway” for future interventions.

## The Toolbox: How to Plan

1. **High‑Resolution Multislice CT (MSCT):**  
   - 3‑D software to model the overlapping frames and measure the minimal lumen diameter (MLD).  
   - Measure distances from each valve’s stent struts to the coronary ostia.  
   - Predict the coronary ostial height relative to the projected neo‑skirt.

2. **Virtual Valve‑In‑Valve Modelling:**  
   - Simulate different valve sizes and positioning depths.  
   - Estimate the post‑deployment effective orifice area (EOA).

3. **Commissural Mapping:**  
   - Align the new valve’s commissures with the existing stent frames to engineer “open” corridors.  
   - This alignment also affords the possibility of future trans‑catheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) or valve fracture via a “serial” approach if the third valve degrades.

4. **Coronary Protection Planning:**  
   - Keep a backup of an Impella or ECLS strategy if you are about to perform valve fracture in a high-risk patient.  
   - Pre‑wire the coronaries with a stiff wire (e.g., Conquest Pro) to test the pathway before proceeding to any valve expansion method.

5. **Multidisciplinary Review:**  
   - Bring imaging, cardiac surgery, interventional cardiology, and electrophysiology together.  
   - For patients with severe CAD, electively coordinate with a coronary team to plan any next PCI within the same procedure or proactively.

## Pearls & Take‑Home Messages

- **Never Underestimate the Cumulative Frame Burden.** Every overlapping valve adds ~3 mm of strut height; in three layers, you’re looking at ~9 mm of “metal.”  
- **Use CT‑guided Virtual Modelling for Every Repeat TAVI.** A trio of overlapping frames makes a single subtraction worse; model the entire system, not just the newest device.  
- **Commissural Alignment Is Your Secret Weapon.** Aligning new valve commissures with existing ones can create a “channel” through which a coronary guidewire can cross the stent frame interstices.  
- **BASILICA and Valve Fracture Remain Experimental in Multi‑Valve Settings.** They can work, but data are sparse; involve a centre with fraught experience.  
- **Invest in Dedicated Coronary Access Sheaths Early.** They may save the day when you’re in a real emergency.  
- **Plan PCI with Parallel Time Considerations.** In patients with significant CAD, schedule a planned emergent PCI “probe” before you commit to a third TAVI.  
- **Document Coronary Paths and Anchoring Points.** For future reference during the patient’s life, record the exact locations of any coronary shadowing or strut engagement.

## Bottom Line

A third TAVI after two prior valve‑in‑valve procedures is a surgical feat that underscores the evolution of structural heart therapy. Yet, as structural valve survivors live longer, the arteries beneath those "stent‑frames" will inevitably demand attention. Pre‑emptive, image‑driven planning—including virtual valve‑in‑valve simulations, commissural alignment, and a clear roadmap for potential coronary interventions—becomes not just smart but lifesaving. The future of aortic root care will be built on a foundation where every valve, every strut, and every coronary pathway is meticulously mapped out for the next episode in a patient’s cardiac journey.
