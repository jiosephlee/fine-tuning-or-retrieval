Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

Chapter 1: ## 1. Clinical Presentation and Initial Evaluation

An Asian man in his early 60s presented to the emergency department with a 3‑day history of fever (maximum 38.1 °C), pounding headache, general fatigue, loss of appetite, persistent hiccups, and arthralgia involving the shoulders and ankles. He also reported oral aphthous ulcers that had appeared in the preceding days. His past medical history revealed untreated type 2 diabetes mellitus, with uncontrolled hyperglycaemia (fasting plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L and HbA1c 8.2 %). He had no significant family history of autoimmune disease and no recent travel, occupational exposures, or infectious contacts.

On examination the patient was alert and oriented but appeared exhausted. Blood pressure was 135/73 mm Hg, heart rate 119 bpm, respiratory rate 19/min, and oxygen saturation 96 % on room air. The cardiovascular, pulmonary, and abdominal examinations were normal. Skin inspection revealed multiple oral aphthous lesions and folliculitis‑like erythematous papules on the face and trunk. No focal neurological deficits, neck stiffness, or meningeal signs were noted, and manual muscle testing of all extremities was normal. There was no peripheral oedema, and the patient was clinically euvolemic.

The initial laboratory panel showed several striking abnormalities (Table 1). Serum sodium was markedly low (124 mmol/L), indicating hyponatraemia. Hyperglycaemia was evident along with an elevated glycated haemoglobin, reflecting poor glycaemic control. Creatine kinase was 1,349 U/L (normal <250 U/L), suggesting muscle injury or rhabdomyolysis, and transaminases (AST 77 U/L, ALT 64 U/L) were mildly elevated, likely reflecting systemic inflammation rather than primary hepatic injury. Inflammatory markers were profoundly raised: C‑reactive protein 180 mg/L (normal <3 mg/L) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate 70 mm/h (normal <15 mm/h). Renal function was preserved (BUN 5.1 mmol/L, creatinine 60 µmol/L). Thyroid function tests (TSH, free T4) and serum cortisol were within normal limits, excluding hypothalamic‑pituitary‑adrenal axis or thyroid disorders as contributors to the hyponatraemia.

Early imaging studies were unremarkable: a chest radiograph and computed tomography of the head, chest, and abdomen showed no focal lesions, infiltrates, or cerebral pathology. The absence of pulmonary infiltrates or mass lesions made pulmonary or malignant causes of SIADH unlikely at this stage.

Given the constellation of fever, headache, mucocutaneous lesions, and hyponatraemia in a euvolemic patient, the bedside differential diagnosis focused on three broad categories:

1. **Central nervous system infection** – viral or bacterial meningitis/encephalitis, given the fever, headache, and hyponatraemia (a common feature of meningitis‑related SIADH).
2. **Systemic inflammatory disease** – autoimmune or autoinflammatory conditions that can present with mucocutaneous lesions (e.g., Behçet’s disease, sarcoidosis, systemic lupus erythematosus) and may trigger SIADH through neuro‑inflammatory pathways.
3. **Endocrine disturbance** – primarily SIADH itself, secondary to an undetected central nervous system process or a paraneoplastic/hypothalamic‑pituitary axis disorder.

The overlapping features of infection (fever, headache) and inflammation (mucocutaneous lesions, elevated CRP/ESR) necessitated an urgent and broad work‑up: cultures, broad‑spectrum antibiotics, empiric antiviral therapy, and a lumbar puncture for cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis were all considered early in the admission course. Meanwhile, endocrine evaluation of serum osmolality, urine osmolality, and urinary sodium confirmed the diagnosis of euvolemic hyponatraemia with inappropriately high ADH secretion—i.e., SIADH—setting the stage for a more focused investigation into the underlying trigger, ultimately leading to the recognition of concurrent acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease.
