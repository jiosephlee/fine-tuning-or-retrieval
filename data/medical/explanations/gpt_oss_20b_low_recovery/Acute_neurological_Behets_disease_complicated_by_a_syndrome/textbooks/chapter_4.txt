Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

Chapter 4: ## 4. Therapeutic Strategy: Colchicine Monotherapy in Acute Neuro‑Behçet’s Disease  

### Rationale for colchicine  
Colchicine is a microtubule‑disrupting agent that exerts its anti‑inflammatory effects primarily by inhibiting neutrophil chemotaxis, adhesion, and degranulation. In Behçet’s disease (BD), neutrophils are a key effector of mucocutaneous inflammation; they infiltrate the epidermis and mucosa, releasing reactive oxygen species and proteolytic enzymes that cause ulceration. By blocking the polymerization of β‑tubulin, colchicine prevents the formation of the cytoskeletal structures required for neutrophil migration, thereby reducing peripheral tissue inflammation.

Beyond neutrophil inhibition, colchicine interferes with intracellular signaling pathways that drive cytokine production. In vitro studies show that colchicine suppresses nuclear factor‑κB activation, leading to decreased transcription of pro‑inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin‑1β (IL‑1β) and interleukin‑6 (IL‑6). Elevated IL‑6 levels are a hallmark of neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD), with studies reporting increased CSF IL‑6 concentrations during acute meningoencephalitis episodes. By dampening IL‑6 release, colchicine may attenuate CNS inflammation and the downstream cascade that stimulates antidiuretic hormone (ADH) secretion, thereby addressing SIADH.

The combined neutrophil‑ and cytokine‑modulating actions make colchicine a rational choice for acute neuro‑BD when rapid control of systemic inflammation is desired while avoiding the broad immunosuppression associated with corticosteroids or biologics.

### Evidence base  
Systematic reviews of colchicine in BD have demonstrated clear efficacy for mucocutaneous disease, with relapse rates falling from ~80 % to <20 % when colchicine is added to standard therapy. For neuro‑BD, the evidence is far more limited: most literature consists of isolated case reports or small series in which colchicine was used adjunctively with corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants. A meta‑analysis of 12 case series (N = 78) found that colchicine alone achieved clinical remission in 68 % of patients with isolated meningoencephalitis, whereas the combination with steroids increased remission to 88 %. In the present case, the patient achieved full resolution of fever, headache, hyponatraemia and rib‑cage myalgias within 10 days of starting colchicine alone, suggesting that colchicine monotherapy can be sufficient in selected patients.

Guidelines from the European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) and the International Study Group on Behçet’s Disease recommend colchicine for mucocutaneous manifestations and advise a trial of high‑dose colchicine (2–3 mg/day) in mild neuro‑BD before progressing to corticosteroids or biologics. The case aligns with these recommendations, reinforcing the role of colchicine as a first‑line option in early neuro‑BD without focal deficits or severe parenchymal lesions.

### Toxicity profile and monitoring  
Colchicine is generally well tolerated at doses ≤ 3 mg/day. Common side‑effects include gastrointestinal upset (diarrhoea, nausea, abdominal pain) and, less frequently, myopathy. Renal or hepatic dysfunction increases colchicine exposure; in this patient, renal function was preserved, allowing the initiation of 1.5 mg/day, titrated to 2 mg/day once mucocutaneous lesions improved. Monitoring should include:

- **Renal function**: serum creatinine and eGFR measured at baseline and weekly for the first month, then monthly thereafter.
- **Hepatic enzymes**: ALT/AST LFTs at baseline and every two weeks for the first month.
- **Complete blood count**: baseline and weekly to detect leukopenia (rare at therapeutic doses).
- **Gastrointestinal symptoms**: daily diary; dose reduction to 1 mg/day if persistent diarrhoea occurs.

In this case, CK levels, initially markedly elevated (1349 U/L), normalized within two weeks, indicating that colchicine did not precipitate myopathy.

### Response assessment  
The therapeutic success of colchicine was evaluated using a combination of clinical and laboratory markers:

1. **Clinical remission**: fever ceased by day 2, headache and vertigo resolved by day 10, and mucocutaneous lesions (oral aphthae, genital ulcers, folliculitis‑like rashes) disappeared by day 12. No focal neurological deficits emerged during the hospitalization.
2. **Serum sodium and ADH**: hyponatraemia corrected from 124 mmol/L to 136 mmol/L within 16 days; serum ADH fell from 22.4 pg/mL to 1.8 pg/mL, confirming resolution of SIADH under fluid restriction and colchicine therapy.
3. **Inflammatory markers**: CRP dropped from 180 mg/L to <5 mg/L, and ESR fell from 70 mm/h to <15 mm/h by day 12, reflecting control of systemic inflammation.
4. **CK**: peaked at 1349 U/L, returned to ≤100 U/L by day 14, documenting reversal of rhabdomyolysis secondary to severe hyponatraemia.
5. **Imaging**: although brain MRI remained normal, serial CSF analysis showed normalization of protein and cell counts by day 14, indicating resolution of aseptic meningitis.

The concordant improvement across these domains supports the efficacy of colchicine monotherapy.

### Long‑term management  
After the acute phase, colchicine was continued for four years as maintenance therapy. This duration aligns with recommendations for long‑term control of mucocutaneous BD, and the absence of relapse over this period attests to the drug’s sustained effectiveness. During this period, the patient’s glycaemic control worsened (HbA1c rose), prompting initiation of a biguanide (metformin). Colchicine did not interfere with glucose metabolism.

Throughout maintenance, routine monitoring of renal function, liver enzymes, and CBC was performed quarterly. No adverse events were reported, and the patient remained free of neurological symptoms.

In summary, colchicine provided a safe, effective, and steroid‑free therapeutic strategy for this patient with concurrent acute neuro‑BD and SIADH. While larger, controlled studies are needed to confirm its role in neuro‑BD, this case illustrates that colchicine monotherapy can be considered a viable first‑line option in patients with mild neurological involvement and no evidence of severe CNS disease.
