Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

Chapter 5: ## 5. Clinical Lessons and Take‑Home Points

The convergence of fever, mucocutaneous lesions characteristic of Behçet’s disease (BD) and hyponatraemia in this patient provides several practical messages for clinicians managing patients with suspected neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD).

### High index of suspicion for neuro‑BD in febrile, hyponatraemic patients with oral/genital ulcers
A febrile episode with diffuse headache and evidence of systemic inflammation should prompt evaluation for a broad spectrum of infections, malignancies and inflammatory disorders. When oral aphthae, genital ulcers or folliculitis‑like rashes are present, BD should be considered early. The International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD) assign points to these mucocutaneous manifestations; a cumulative score ≥ 4 constitutes definite BD. In a patient presenting with hyponatraemia—especially when serum osmolality is low, urine osmolality is inappropriately high and urinary sodium is elevated—the differential must include central nervous system (CNS) inflammation, even if imaging is initially unremarkable.

### When a normal MRI does not exclude neuro‑BD
Conventional brain MRI is the most sensitive imaging modality for parenchymal neuro‑BD, yet up to 40 % of patients with acute neuro‑BD may have a normal scan, particularly in the first week of disease. In this case, MRI of the brain, including the hypothalamic region, revealed no lesions despite the presence of aseptic meningitis on CSF analysis. Clinicians should therefore rely on a combination of clinical features, CSF findings (elevated protein, lymphocytic pleocytosis, negative infectious studies) and the temporal relationship to mucocutaneous BD features rather than dismissing neuro‑BD on the sole basis of a normal brain MRI.

### SIADH as a marker of CNS inflammation
SIADH commonly accompanies CNS inflammatory processes because cytokine‑mediated stimulation of antidiuretic hormone (ADH) release can occur in the context of meningitis or encephalitis. In this patient, profound hyponatraemia (Na⁺ = 124 mmol/L) and a high ADH level (22.4 pg/mL) fulfilled the classic laboratory criteria for SIADH. Importantly, SIADH can be a subtle but clinically significant clue to underlying neuro‑inflammation; its presence in a febrile patient with BD‑typical skin lesions should raise suspicion for neuro‑BD even when imaging is negative.

### Colchicine as a steroid‑sparing option in selected neuro‑BD cases
Corticosteroids remain the cornerstone of treatment for acute neuro‑BD, but they carry substantial side‑effect burdens and are not always desirable—particularly in elderly patients or those with contraindications. Colchicine, a cytoskeleton‑disrupting agent that impairs neutrophil chemotaxis and suppresses IL‑1β and IL‑6 release, was effective in this case both for mucocutaneous manifestations and for resolving neurological symptoms and SIADH. While the literature suggests that colchicine is inadequate as a monotherapy for neuro‑BD, this case demonstrates that, in selected patients presenting with mild or purely meningoencephalitic disease and without focal deficits, colchicine may serve as a reasonable steroid‑sparing strategy. Clinical trials are required to define its precise role, but practitioners should remain open to its use in situations where steroid toxicity is a concern.

### Key warning signs of severe hyponatraemia: hiccups, rhabdomyolysis
Hyponatraemia can manifest with surprisingly vague or unrelated symptoms. In this patient, persistent hiccups, vertigo and a marked rise in creatine kinase (CK = 1349 U/L) reflected severe hyponatraemia‑induced electrolyte disturbance and rhabdomyolysis. Other subtle signs include acute confusion, seizures, muscle cramping and unexplained fatigue. Clinicians should therefore maintain a high index of suspicion for hyponatraemia in any patient with CNS symptoms and monitor serum sodium closely, intervening with fluid restriction and, if necessary, hypertonic saline or vasopressin receptor antagonists.

**Take‑home pearls**

| Clinical scenario | Action | Rationale |
|--------------------|--------|-----------|
| Fever + abdominal or CNS symptoms + oral/genital ulcers | Assess serum sodium, osmolality, urine osmolality | Hyponatraemia may signal SIADH secondary to neuro‑BD |
| Normal brain MRI in a febrile, BD‑positive patient | Obtain CSF early; consider repeat MRI if clinical status worsens | Up to 40 % of acute neuro‑BD cases have a normal MRI initially |
| Severe hyponatraemia with neurological signs | Initiate fluid restriction, closely monitor sodium; consider hypertonic saline only if symptomatic | Rapid correction risks osmotic demyelination |
| Desire to avoid steroids in mild neuro‑BD | Trial colchicine (0.6–1.2 mg daily) with close follow‑up | May provide adequate control in selected cases |
| Persisting hiccups or rising CK in a hyponatraemic patient | Evaluate for rhabdomyolysis; correct electrolytes | Both can be manifestations of profound hyponatraemia |

By integrating these lessons into clinical practice, physicians can improve early recognition, avoid unnecessary investigations, and tailor therapy to achieve optimal outcomes for patients with Behçet’s disease complicated by CNS inflammation and SIADH.
