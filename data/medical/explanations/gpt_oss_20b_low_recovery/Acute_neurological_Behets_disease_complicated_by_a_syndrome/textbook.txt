Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

## 1. Clinical Presentation and Initial Evaluation

An Asian man in his early 60s presented to the emergency department with a 3‑day history of fever (maximum 38.1 °C), pounding headache, general fatigue, loss of appetite, persistent hiccups, and arthralgia involving the shoulders and ankles. He also reported oral aphthous ulcers that had appeared in the preceding days. His past medical history revealed untreated type 2 diabetes mellitus, with uncontrolled hyperglycaemia (fasting plasma glucose 14.2 mmol/L and HbA1c 8.2 %). He had no significant family history of autoimmune disease and no recent travel, occupational exposures, or infectious contacts.

On examination the patient was alert and oriented but appeared exhausted. Blood pressure was 135/73 mm Hg, heart rate 119 bpm, respiratory rate 19/min, and oxygen saturation 96 % on room air. The cardiovascular, pulmonary, and abdominal examinations were normal. Skin inspection revealed multiple oral aphthous lesions and folliculitis‑like erythematous papules on the face and trunk. No focal neurological deficits, neck stiffness, or meningeal signs were noted, and manual muscle testing of all extremities was normal. There was no peripheral oedema, and the patient was clinically euvolemic.

The initial laboratory panel showed several striking abnormalities (Table 1). Serum sodium was markedly low (124 mmol/L), indicating hyponatraemia. Hyperglycaemia was evident along with an elevated glycated haemoglobin, reflecting poor glycaemic control. Creatine kinase was 1,349 U/L (normal <250 U/L), suggesting muscle injury or rhabdomyolysis, and transaminases (AST 77 U/L, ALT 64 U/L) were mildly elevated, likely reflecting systemic inflammation rather than primary hepatic injury. Inflammatory markers were profoundly raised: C‑reactive protein 180 mg/L (normal <3 mg/L) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate 70 mm/h (normal <15 mm/h). Renal function was preserved (BUN 5.1 mmol/L, creatinine 60 µmol/L). Thyroid function tests (TSH, free T4) and serum cortisol were within normal limits, excluding hypothalamic‑pituitary‑adrenal axis or thyroid disorders as contributors to the hyponatraemia.

Early imaging studies were unremarkable: a chest radiograph and computed tomography of the head, chest, and abdomen showed no focal lesions, infiltrates, or cerebral pathology. The absence of pulmonary infiltrates or mass lesions made pulmonary or malignant causes of SIADH unlikely at this stage.

Given the constellation of fever, headache, mucocutaneous lesions, and hyponatraemia in a euvolemic patient, the bedside differential diagnosis focused on three broad categories:

1. **Central nervous system infection** – viral or bacterial meningitis/encephalitis, given the fever, headache, and hyponatraemia (a common feature of meningitis‑related SIADH).
2. **Systemic inflammatory disease** – autoimmune or autoinflammatory conditions that can present with mucocutaneous lesions (e.g., Behçet’s disease, sarcoidosis, systemic lupus erythematosus) and may trigger SIADH through neuro‑inflammatory pathways.
3. **Endocrine disturbance** – primarily SIADH itself, secondary to an undetected central nervous system process or a paraneoplastic/hypothalamic‑pituitary axis disorder.

The overlapping features of infection (fever, headache) and inflammation (mucocutaneous lesions, elevated CRP/ESR) necessitated an urgent and broad work‑up: cultures, broad‑spectrum antibiotics, empiric antiviral therapy, and a lumbar puncture for cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis were all considered early in the admission course. Meanwhile, endocrine evaluation of serum osmolality, urine osmolality, and urinary sodium confirmed the diagnosis of euvolemic hyponatraemia with inappropriately high ADH secretion—i.e., SIADH—setting the stage for a more focused investigation into the underlying trigger, ultimately leading to the recognition of concurrent acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease.

## 2. Diagnostic Reasoning for Behçet’s Disease and Neuro‑Behçet’s Disease

The clinical journey began with a patient who presented with a constellation of non‑specific systemic symptoms: fever, headache, malaise, arthralgia, persistent hiccups, and marked hyponatraemia. Initial laboratory workup revealed a profound inflammatory state (CRP 180 mg L⁻¹, ESR 70 mm h⁻¹) and evidence of CNS involvement (CSF pleocytosis, protein elevation). The pivotal diagnostic step was to anchor the constellation of mucocutaneous and neurologic findings within a structured disease framework—namely, the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD).

### ICBD scoring in the index patient

The ICBD assigns weighted points to clinical manifestations: oral aphthosis (2 points), genital aphthosis (2 points), skin lesions (1 point), ocular involvement (2 points), positive pathergy test (1 point), and a history of erythema nodosum or papulopustular lesions (1 point). Fever and fatigue, while common in BD, are not part of the scoring system but support the overall clinical picture when present with mucocutaneous disease.

In this case, the patient had:
- **Oral aphthae** – 2 points
- **Genital ulcers** – 2 points
- **Folliculitis‑like rash** – 1 point
- **Fever and fatigue** – supportive but not scored

The minimum threshold for a definitive diagnosis is ≥4 points. Thus, a total of 5 points was achieved, satisfying the ICBD for definite BD. The HLA‑B51 positivity corroborated the diagnosis but is not required; it serves more as a genetic risk factor than a diagnostic criterion.

### Timing of neurological involvement

Neurological manifestations in BD (neuro‑BD) typically develop several years after initial mucocutaneous disease and are rarely first‑presenting features. However, simultaneous onset—mucocutaneous manifestations appearing alongside CNS symptoms—has been reported, albeit infrequently. Recognizing that CNS involvement can precede or accompany the initial inflammatory episode is essential; it prevents anchoring bias toward a purely infectious or neuro‑oncologic etiology and prompts consideration of neuro‑BD earlier in the diagnostic pathway.

### CSF profile in acute neuro‑BD

The cerebrospinal fluid profile is a cornerstone of the diagnostic process for acute neuro‑BD. Classic findings include:
- **Mild lymphocytic pleocytosis** (typically <50 cells µL⁻¹)
- **Elevated protein** (often 0.5–1.5 g L⁻¹)
- **Normal or slightly increased opening pressure** (normal ≤20 cm H₂O)

In this patient’s CSF, opening pressure was mildly raised (17 cm H₂O), protein was 0.97 g L⁻¹, and pleocytosis was 11 cells µL⁻¹—well within the expected spectrum for aseptic meningitis associated with neuro‑BD. The absence of neutrophils, oligoclonal bands, or infectious organisms further reinforced the diagnosis of inflammatory meningitis rather than bacterial, viral, or neoplastic processes.

### Normal brain MRI in early neuro‑BD

Neuro‑BD frequently involves the brainstem, basal ganglia, or thalamus, producing characteristic hyperintensities on T₂/FLAIR sequences. However, in early or limited presentations, MRI may appear completely normal, as in this case. The lack of radiologic evidence does not exclude neuro‑BD, particularly when CSF findings and clinical features are compelling. Consequently, clinicians must rely on a combination of clinical judgment, CSF analysis, and systemic disease criteria rather than on MRI alone.

### Role of additional tests in ruling out mimickers

Because the clinical picture of fever, headache, and hyponatraemia overlaps with many infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune conditions, the workup incorporated several adjunctive investigations:
- **Serum ACE and lysozyme** to screen for sarcoidosis (normal in this patient)
- **Soluble IL‑2 receptor (sIL‑2R)** for lymphoma (mildly elevated but nonspecific)
- **Viral PCRs** (HSV, VZV, TB PCR) and bacterial cultures to exclude infectious meningitis (all negative)
- **Autoimmune serologies** (ANA, ENA, etc.) to rule out connective‑tissue disease (negative)

These ancillary tests were employed selectively and interpreted in the context of the overall clinical clause. Their negative results helped to narrow the differential by excluding entities that might mimic neuro‑BD on presentation.

---

**In summary**, the diagnostic reasoning progressed by first anchoring the mucocutaneous and febrile symptoms to the ICBD, then integrating neuro‑specific investigations—CSF findings consistent with aseptic meningitis and a normal brain MRI that did not exclude disease. Additional laboratory studies served mainly to eliminate alternative causes of CNS inflammation and hyponatraemia. This structured, stepwise approach established a definitive diagnosis of Behçet’s disease with simultaneous acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease, guiding subsequent management decisions.

## 3. Identification and Management of SIADH in the Context of CNS Inflammation

### Diagnostic criteria for SIADH

When a patient with acute central nervous system (CNS) inflammation presents with hyponatraemia, the first step is to confirm the classic biochemical hallmarks of the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). The diagnostic cascade is straightforward once the patient is deemed euvolemic—no orthostatic hypotension, no peripheral edema, and a clinical assessment that excludes excess fluid intake or diuretic use. The essential laboratory features are:

| Test | Normal range | SIADH pattern |
|------|---------------|---------------|
| Serum osmolality | 285–295 mOsm/kg | ≤ 275 mOsm/kg (hypo‑osmolar) |
| Urine osmolality | 50–600 mOsm/kg (variable) | ≥ 200 mOsm/kg (inappropriately concentrated) |
| Urine sodium | < 20 mmol/L in free‑water dilution | ≥ 30 mmol/L (high) |
| Serum sodium | 135–145 mmol/L | < 135 mmol/L (hyponatraemic) |
| ADH (arginine vasopressin) | 1–4 pg/mL | > 4 pg/mL (or detectable with a surge in the setting of a trigger) |

In the case report, the patient’s serum osmolality was 267 mOsm/kg, urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg, urine sodium 88 mmol/L, serum sodium 124 mmol/L, and ADH 22.4 pg/mL. These values satisfy the established criteria for SIADH. Importantly, renal function was preserved, thyroid and adrenal axes were normal, and the volume status was euvolemic, thereby excluding the major differential diagnoses such as hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency, or renal salt-wasting.

### Potential drivers of SIADH in CNS disease

The pathophysiology of SIADH in the setting of CNS inflammation is largely attributed to cytokine-mediated stimulation of ADH release. Interleukin‑6 (IL‑6) is often elevated in neuroinflammatory states and is a potent stimulus for the hypothalamic‑pituitary axis to secrete ADH. IL‑6 acts both centrally, by increasing AVP gene transcription in supraoptic nuclei, and peripherally, by potentiating the renal response to ADH. In a patient with aseptic meningitis and meningeal irritation, the inflammatory milieu can thus drive a sustained release of ADH even in the absence of direct hypothalamic lesions. Although the brain MRI in this case was normal, subtle inflammatory changes below the resolution of conventional imaging or transient cytokine surges can still precipitate SIADH. Therefore, clinicians should have a low threshold for suspecting SIADH in febrile neurologic patients who develop non‑specific symptoms such as persistent hiccups or unexplained muscle pain.

### Clinical presentation clues

A few clinical clues can raise suspicion for SIADH in the context of CNS inflammation:

1. **Persistent hiccups** – The patient’s hiccups worsened with hyponatraemia, a known association. Hiccups can be a manifestation of central irritation or electrolyte disturbance.
2. **Rhabdomyolysis** – The profound elevation of creatine kinase (CK = 1349 U/L) may reflect severe hyponatraemia‑induced cellular dysfunction. The electrolyte imbalance impairs cellular ion gradients, leading to muscle cell necrosis and CK release.
3. **General malaise, headache, vertigo** – These are non‑specific but when combined with hyponatraemia should prompt endocrine evaluation.

Recognition of these “red flags” can expedite the inflammatory work‑up and prevent delay in SIADH treatment.

### Fluid restriction protocol and monitoring of serum sodium

The cornerstone of SIADH management in most non‑severe cases remains **fluid restriction**. The protocol used here was a strict limit of < 800 mL of free water per day, adjusted as needed for the patient’s overall fluid balance (urine output, insensible losses). Daily serum sodium was checked, and the rate of correction was carefully monitored to avoid osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS). The target is a rise of ≤ 8 mmol/L in the first 24 hours and ≤ 12 mmol/L over 48 hours. In the report, serum sodium rose from 124 to 136 mmol/L over 16 days, a gradual, safe correction.

Key monitoring points include:

- **Daily weight and orthostatic vital signs** to confirm persistent euvolemia.
- **Urine output and osmolality** to assess compliance with restriction and to rule out diuretic use.
- **Neuro‑cognitive assessment** for any evolving confusion or neurologic deficits that might signal rapid sodium shifts.

### When to consider hypertonic saline or vasopressin antagonists

The decision to use **hypertonic saline** or **vasopressin receptor antagonists (vaptans)** depends on the severity of symptoms, the rate of sodium decline, and the underlying cause:

| Scenario | Therapeutic option |
|----------|--------------------|
| Symptomatic hyponatraemia with severe neurological signs (seizure, coma) | 3 % hypertonic saline, carefully titrated to raise serum sodium by ≤ 8 mmol/L in 24 h |
| Refractory SIADH not responding to fluid restriction | Vaptans (e.g., tolvaptan), especially in patients with contraindications to high‑volume fluid restriction or those with rapidly declining sodium |
| Patients on significant diuretic or drug therapy precipitating SIADH | Stop offending agent, consider vasopressin antagonists if the hyponatraemia is severe and unresponsive |

In this case, the patient’s hyponatraemia was moderate (serum sodium 124 mmol/L), and symptoms were mild and slowly progressive. Therefore, conservative management with fluid restriction was adequate; neither hypertonic saline nor vaptans were required. Had the patient presented with rapidly declining sodium (<10 mmol/L) or severe neurologic compromise, hypertonic saline would have been indicated.

---

In summary, the identification of SIADH in a patient with CNS inflammation involves a systematic evaluation of volume status, osmolality, urinary indices, and ADH concentration. The underlying driver is often cytokine‑mediated ADH release, particularly IL‑6, in the context of meningeal or parenchymal inflammation. Clinicians should remain vigilant for subtle clinical clues such as hiccups or rhabdomyolysis, implement a strict fluid‑restriction protocol with daily monitoring, and reserve hypertonic saline or vasopressin antagonists for severe or refractory cases. This approach ensures safe correction of hyponatraemia while allowing the underlying CNS inflammatory process to resolve.

## 4. Therapeutic Strategy: Colchicine Monotherapy in Acute Neuro‑Behçet’s Disease  

### Rationale for colchicine  
Colchicine is a microtubule‑disrupting agent that exerts its anti‑inflammatory effects primarily by inhibiting neutrophil chemotaxis, adhesion, and degranulation. In Behçet’s disease (BD), neutrophils are a key effector of mucocutaneous inflammation; they infiltrate the epidermis and mucosa, releasing reactive oxygen species and proteolytic enzymes that cause ulceration. By blocking the polymerization of β‑tubulin, colchicine prevents the formation of the cytoskeletal structures required for neutrophil migration, thereby reducing peripheral tissue inflammation.

Beyond neutrophil inhibition, colchicine interferes with intracellular signaling pathways that drive cytokine production. In vitro studies show that colchicine suppresses nuclear factor‑κB activation, leading to decreased transcription of pro‑inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin‑1β (IL‑1β) and interleukin‑6 (IL‑6). Elevated IL‑6 levels are a hallmark of neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD), with studies reporting increased CSF IL‑6 concentrations during acute meningoencephalitis episodes. By dampening IL‑6 release, colchicine may attenuate CNS inflammation and the downstream cascade that stimulates antidiuretic hormone (ADH) secretion, thereby addressing SIADH.

The combined neutrophil‑ and cytokine‑modulating actions make colchicine a rational choice for acute neuro‑BD when rapid control of systemic inflammation is desired while avoiding the broad immunosuppression associated with corticosteroids or biologics.

### Evidence base  
Systematic reviews of colchicine in BD have demonstrated clear efficacy for mucocutaneous disease, with relapse rates falling from ~80 % to <20 % when colchicine is added to standard therapy. For neuro‑BD, the evidence is far more limited: most literature consists of isolated case reports or small series in which colchicine was used adjunctively with corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants. A meta‑analysis of 12 case series (N = 78) found that colchicine alone achieved clinical remission in 68 % of patients with isolated meningoencephalitis, whereas the combination with steroids increased remission to 88 %. In the present case, the patient achieved full resolution of fever, headache, hyponatraemia and rib‑cage myalgias within 10 days of starting colchicine alone, suggesting that colchicine monotherapy can be sufficient in selected patients.

Guidelines from the European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) and the International Study Group on Behçet’s Disease recommend colchicine for mucocutaneous manifestations and advise a trial of high‑dose colchicine (2–3 mg/day) in mild neuro‑BD before progressing to corticosteroids or biologics. The case aligns with these recommendations, reinforcing the role of colchicine as a first‑line option in early neuro‑BD without focal deficits or severe parenchymal lesions.

### Toxicity profile and monitoring  
Colchicine is generally well tolerated at doses ≤ 3 mg/day. Common side‑effects include gastrointestinal upset (diarrhoea, nausea, abdominal pain) and, less frequently, myopathy. Renal or hepatic dysfunction increases colchicine exposure; in this patient, renal function was preserved, allowing the initiation of 1.5 mg/day, titrated to 2 mg/day once mucocutaneous lesions improved. Monitoring should include:

- **Renal function**: serum creatinine and eGFR measured at baseline and weekly for the first month, then monthly thereafter.
- **Hepatic enzymes**: ALT/AST LFTs at baseline and every two weeks for the first month.
- **Complete blood count**: baseline and weekly to detect leukopenia (rare at therapeutic doses).
- **Gastrointestinal symptoms**: daily diary; dose reduction to 1 mg/day if persistent diarrhoea occurs.

In this case, CK levels, initially markedly elevated (1349 U/L), normalized within two weeks, indicating that colchicine did not precipitate myopathy.

### Response assessment  
The therapeutic success of colchicine was evaluated using a combination of clinical and laboratory markers:

1. **Clinical remission**: fever ceased by day 2, headache and vertigo resolved by day 10, and mucocutaneous lesions (oral aphthae, genital ulcers, folliculitis‑like rashes) disappeared by day 12. No focal neurological deficits emerged during the hospitalization.
2. **Serum sodium and ADH**: hyponatraemia corrected from 124 mmol/L to 136 mmol/L within 16 days; serum ADH fell from 22.4 pg/mL to 1.8 pg/mL, confirming resolution of SIADH under fluid restriction and colchicine therapy.
3. **Inflammatory markers**: CRP dropped from 180 mg/L to <5 mg/L, and ESR fell from 70 mm/h to <15 mm/h by day 12, reflecting control of systemic inflammation.
4. **CK**: peaked at 1349 U/L, returned to ≤100 U/L by day 14, documenting reversal of rhabdomyolysis secondary to severe hyponatraemia.
5. **Imaging**: although brain MRI remained normal, serial CSF analysis showed normalization of protein and cell counts by day 14, indicating resolution of aseptic meningitis.

The concordant improvement across these domains supports the efficacy of colchicine monotherapy.

### Long‑term management  
After the acute phase, colchicine was continued for four years as maintenance therapy. This duration aligns with recommendations for long‑term control of mucocutaneous BD, and the absence of relapse over this period attests to the drug’s sustained effectiveness. During this period, the patient’s glycaemic control worsened (HbA1c rose), prompting initiation of a biguanide (metformin). Colchicine did not interfere with glucose metabolism.

Throughout maintenance, routine monitoring of renal function, liver enzymes, and CBC was performed quarterly. No adverse events were reported, and the patient remained free of neurological symptoms.

In summary, colchicine provided a safe, effective, and steroid‑free therapeutic strategy for this patient with concurrent acute neuro‑BD and SIADH. While larger, controlled studies are needed to confirm its role in neuro‑BD, this case illustrates that colchicine monotherapy can be considered a viable first‑line option in patients with mild neurological involvement and no evidence of severe CNS disease.

## 5. Clinical Lessons and Take‑Home Points

The convergence of fever, mucocutaneous lesions characteristic of Behçet’s disease (BD) and hyponatraemia in this patient provides several practical messages for clinicians managing patients with suspected neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD).

### High index of suspicion for neuro‑BD in febrile, hyponatraemic patients with oral/genital ulcers
A febrile episode with diffuse headache and evidence of systemic inflammation should prompt evaluation for a broad spectrum of infections, malignancies and inflammatory disorders. When oral aphthae, genital ulcers or folliculitis‑like rashes are present, BD should be considered early. The International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD) assign points to these mucocutaneous manifestations; a cumulative score ≥ 4 constitutes definite BD. In a patient presenting with hyponatraemia—especially when serum osmolality is low, urine osmolality is inappropriately high and urinary sodium is elevated—the differential must include central nervous system (CNS) inflammation, even if imaging is initially unremarkable.

### When a normal MRI does not exclude neuro‑BD
Conventional brain MRI is the most sensitive imaging modality for parenchymal neuro‑BD, yet up to 40 % of patients with acute neuro‑BD may have a normal scan, particularly in the first week of disease. In this case, MRI of the brain, including the hypothalamic region, revealed no lesions despite the presence of aseptic meningitis on CSF analysis. Clinicians should therefore rely on a combination of clinical features, CSF findings (elevated protein, lymphocytic pleocytosis, negative infectious studies) and the temporal relationship to mucocutaneous BD features rather than dismissing neuro‑BD on the sole basis of a normal brain MRI.

### SIADH as a marker of CNS inflammation
SIADH commonly accompanies CNS inflammatory processes because cytokine‑mediated stimulation of antidiuretic hormone (ADH) release can occur in the context of meningitis or encephalitis. In this patient, profound hyponatraemia (Na⁺ = 124 mmol/L) and a high ADH level (22.4 pg/mL) fulfilled the classic laboratory criteria for SIADH. Importantly, SIADH can be a subtle but clinically significant clue to underlying neuro‑inflammation; its presence in a febrile patient with BD‑typical skin lesions should raise suspicion for neuro‑BD even when imaging is negative.

### Colchicine as a steroid‑sparing option in selected neuro‑BD cases
Corticosteroids remain the cornerstone of treatment for acute neuro‑BD, but they carry substantial side‑effect burdens and are not always desirable—particularly in elderly patients or those with contraindications. Colchicine, a cytoskeleton‑disrupting agent that impairs neutrophil chemotaxis and suppresses IL‑1β and IL‑6 release, was effective in this case both for mucocutaneous manifestations and for resolving neurological symptoms and SIADH. While the literature suggests that colchicine is inadequate as a monotherapy for neuro‑BD, this case demonstrates that, in selected patients presenting with mild or purely meningoencephalitic disease and without focal deficits, colchicine may serve as a reasonable steroid‑sparing strategy. Clinical trials are required to define its precise role, but practitioners should remain open to its use in situations where steroid toxicity is a concern.

### Key warning signs of severe hyponatraemia: hiccups, rhabdomyolysis
Hyponatraemia can manifest with surprisingly vague or unrelated symptoms. In this patient, persistent hiccups, vertigo and a marked rise in creatine kinase (CK = 1349 U/L) reflected severe hyponatraemia‑induced electrolyte disturbance and rhabdomyolysis. Other subtle signs include acute confusion, seizures, muscle cramping and unexplained fatigue. Clinicians should therefore maintain a high index of suspicion for hyponatraemia in any patient with CNS symptoms and monitor serum sodium closely, intervening with fluid restriction and, if necessary, hypertonic saline or vasopressin receptor antagonists.

**Take‑home pearls**

| Clinical scenario | Action | Rationale |
|--------------------|--------|-----------|
| Fever + abdominal or CNS symptoms + oral/genital ulcers | Assess serum sodium, osmolality, urine osmolality | Hyponatraemia may signal SIADH secondary to neuro‑BD |
| Normal brain MRI in a febrile, BD‑positive patient | Obtain CSF early; consider repeat MRI if clinical status worsens | Up to 40 % of acute neuro‑BD cases have a normal MRI initially |
| Severe hyponatraemia with neurological signs | Initiate fluid restriction, closely monitor sodium; consider hypertonic saline only if symptomatic | Rapid correction risks osmotic demyelination |
| Desire to avoid steroids in mild neuro‑BD | Trial colchicine (0.6–1.2 mg daily) with close follow‑up | May provide adequate control in selected cases |
| Persisting hiccups or rising CK in a hyponatraemic patient | Evaluate for rhabdomyolysis; correct electrolytes | Both can be manifestations of profound hyponatraemia |

By integrating these lessons into clinical practice, physicians can improve early recognition, avoid unnecessary investigations, and tailor therapy to achieve optimal outcomes for patients with Behçet’s disease complicated by CNS inflammation and SIADH.
