Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Clinical Pearls] What are the key diagnostic criteria for Behçet’s disease in adults, and how do mucocutaneous findings contribute to a definite diagnosis?

**Key diagnostic criteria for Behçet’s disease in adults**

| Criterion | Points (ICBD 2014) | Clinical relevance |
|---|---|---|
| Recurrent oral aphthosis (≥3/month or ≥2 in previous year) | 2 | Most common and earliest feature; high sensitivity |
| Recurrent genital aphthosis (≥2 in previous year) | 2 | Highly specific; strong indicator of definite BD |
| Ocular lesions (uveitis, retinal vasculitis, etc.) | 2 |  |
| Skin lesions (erythema nodosum, papulopustular lesions, folliculitis‑like rash, thrombophlebitis) | 1 |  |
| Vascular involvement (vein or artery thrombosis) | 1 |  |
| Neurologic involvement (meningoencephalitis, parenchymal lesions) | 1 |  |
| Positive HLA‑B51 | 0.5 | Supports diagnosis but not required |

**Definite adult Behçet’s disease** is diagnosed when the total ICBD score is **≥4**.  
In the presented case, the patient achieved:

* Oral aphthae – 2 pts  
* Genital ulcers – 2 pts  
* Folliculitis‑like rash – 1 pt  

Total = 5 pts → **definite BD** (≥ 4). HLA‑B51 positivity further corroborated the clinical picture, but it is not part of the scoring system.

---

### How mucocutaneous findings contribute to a definitive diagnosis

1. **Specificity of genital ulcers.**  
   The International Criteria assign 2 pts to genital aphthosis, the highest single contribution. In the case, genital ulcers appeared on day 5, tipping the balance toward certainty. Genital lesions are seldom seen in other inflammatory or infectious conditions, so their presence is a powerful discriminator.

2. **Sensitivity of oral aphthous ulcers.**  
   Oral ulcers are very common in the general population, but when they recur ≥3 times/month over a year, they strongly suggest BD. The patient had persistent oral aphthae at admission, already providing a baseline of 2 pts.

3. **Supplementary skin manifestations.**  
   The folliculitis‑like rash (1 pt) extended the diagnostic confidence. Skin lesions are often the first extra‑oral symptoms and, while not specific, add to the cumulative score.

4. **Temporal relationship with systemic symptoms.**  
   The mucocutaneous lesions helped link the fever, headache, and hyponatraemia to an underlying inflammatory process rather than a primary infection or malignancy. Their appearance and evolution paralleled the neurological findings (aseptic meningitis) and resolution with colchicine, reinforcing the BD diagnosis.

Thus, in adults the combination of recurrent oral and genital ulceration plus supportive cutaneous lesions yields a high ICBD score and a robust, clinically actionable diagnosis, as illustrated by this patient’s concurrent neuro‑Behçet’s and SIADH.

### [Morning Report] Describe the typical neurological manifestations of neuro‑Behçet’s disease and explain why a normal brain MRI does not exclude the diagnosis.

**Typical neuro‑Behçet’s manifestations and the rationale for a normal MRI**

1. **Clinical spectrum**  
   • The neurologic involvement of Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) is usually categorized into *parenchymal* and *vasculitic* disease.  
   • **Parenchymal neuro‑BD**—the most frequent type—presents with **meningo‑encephalitis**, fever, headache, vertigo, nausea, and “behavioural” or psychiatric changes.  Focal deficits (e.g., cranial‑nerve palsies, hemiparesis) and seizures are less common but can occur when the brainstem, basal ganglia or thalamus is affected.  
   • **Vasculitic neuro‑BD** (rare) produces stroke‑like focal deficits and hemorrhage when cerebral vessels are inflamed.  
   • In the acute setting, the hallmark is often a **self‑limited meningo‑encephalitis** with CSF pleocytosis and elevated protein, as seen in the present case (11 cells/µL, 0.97 g/L protein).  Neurologic symptoms may be nonspecific but are usually accompanied by systemic mucocutaneous features.

2. **Why a normal brain MRI does **not** exclude neuro‑BD**  
   • The “classical” parenchymal lesions of neuro‑BD are small, often focal, and classically involve the **brainstem** (midbrain, pons).  Even when present, they may be too small to be appreciated on routine imaging, especially early in the disease, or may be missed on standard‑sized scans.  
   • Acute neuro‑BD most often presents as **aseptic meningitis** without a detectable parenchymal lesion, as in our patient whose MRI was normal despite clear CSF inflammation.  The inflammatory process can be confined to the meninges, the subarachnoid space, or very small focal areas that fall below the spatial resolution of conventional MRI.  
   • Serial imaging—preferably with high‑resolution or contrast‑enhanced sequences—may reveal evolving lesions in 20–30 % of patients.  A single negative study therefore should not reassure clinicians; repeat imaging is recommended when symptoms persist or worsen.  
   • In practice, the diagnosis of acute neuro‑BD is one of **exclusion**: negative infectious, malignant, and other autoimmune work‑up, together with characteristic mucocutaneous findings (oral/genital aphthae, skin rash) and a compatible clinical picture, is sufficient to support the diagnosis even with a normal brain MRI.

**Bottom line:** In a patient with Behçet’s disease who develops fever, headache, and CSF evidence of aseptic meningitis, a normal brain MRI should not preclude the diagnosis of acute neuro‑BD.  The disease can be strictly meningeal and may evolve radiologically over time; hence, clinical judgment and repeat imaging are essential.

### [Pathophysiology] What laboratory findings confirm the diagnosis of syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH), and how can hyponatraemia from SIADH present symptomatically?

**Answer**

**Laboratory confirmation of SIADH**  
The diagnostic criteria for SIADH are met when a patient presents with:

1. **Hyponatraemia** (serum Na⁺ < 135 mmol/L) – in this case 124 mmol/L.  
2. **Inappropriately concentrated urine**:  
   - Urine osmolality > 300 mOsm/kg (900 mOsm/kg in the report).  
   - Urine sodium > 20 mmol/L (88 mmol/L here).  
3. **Low plasma osmolality** (≤ 275 mOsm/kg) – 267 mOsm/kg.  
4. **Euvolemia** on clinical exam (no edema, stable vital signs).  
5. **Normal adrenal, thyroid, and renal function** – all within reference ranges.  
6. **Elevated plasma ADH** (or a “non‑dilutional” ADH level) – 22.4 pg/mL here, confirming that the antidiuretic hormone is not suppressed.  

These laboratory findings together satisfy the Seewald–Shwartz–Sims criteria used by the authors to diagnose SIADH.

---

**Symptomatic presentation of hyponatraemia from SIADH**  
The loss of free water relative to sodium causes a gradual shift of extracellular fluid into cells, producing a spectrum of symptoms that correlate poorly with serum sodium concentration.

- **Early, mild symptoms**: nausea, headache, fatigue, mood changes.  
- **Severe, persistent hyponatraemia** (as in this patient, with Na⁺ ≈ 124 mmol/L) can produce:  
  - **Neurologic signs**: confusion, seizures, delirium, focal deficits, and, as noted in the report, *persistent hiccups* – a well‑documented clue to severe hyponatraemia from CNS inflammation.  
  - **Muscle injury**: rhabdomyolysis can develop, evidenced by markedly elevated CK (1349 U/L) and normal renal function, a complication often overlooked but recognized in profound hyponatraemia.  
  - **Other systems**: nausea, vomiting, fatigue, and in extreme cases pulmonary edema from cerebral edema.

Thus, in a patient with neuro‑Behçet’s disease presenting with fever, headache, and mucocutaneous findings, the co‑existent hyponatraemia may manifest subtly (headache, fatigue) or dramatically (hiccups, seizures, rhabdomyolysis). Early recognition of these features, coupled with the laboratory triad above, allows prompt diagnosis and safe management (fluid restriction, careful sodium monitoring) as was successfully employed in this case.

**Conclusion**  
In the setting of fever, headache, mucocutaneous lesions consistent with Behçet’s disease, and euvolemic hyponatraemia fulfilling SIADH criteria, the most parsimonious explanation is acute neuro‑BD with secondary SIADH. The temporal resolution of all symptoms with colchicine monotherapy further supports a self‑limited inflammatory etiology rather than a persistent infectious or neoplastic process.

### [Pathophysiology] Explain the proposed role of interleukin‑6 (IL‑6) in the pathogenesis of SIADH secondary to central nervous system inflammation, and how this may relate to neuro‑Behçet’s disease.

**Interleukin‑6 and the “Central” SIADH seen in neuro‑Behçet’s disease**

1. **CNS inflammation → IL‑6 spill‑over**  
   In acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease the brain is inflamed (aseptic meningitis, CSF protein ↑, pleocytosis). Activated microglia, macrophages and infiltrating lymphocytes produce large amounts of IL‑6. Although this case did not measure IL‑6, the dramatic rise in CRP (180 mg/L) strongly suggests a systemic IL‑6‑driven inflammatory response, because CRP synthesis in the liver is exquisitely IL‑6‑dependent.

2. **IL‑6 stimulates ADH release**  
   Several experimental and clinical studies show that IL‑6 can directly stimulate hypothalamic magnocellular neurons, prompting extra‑physiologic secretion of antidiuretic hormone (ADH). IL‑6 also up‑regulates the pituitary’s release of vasopressin‑releasing hormone (VRH) and can reduce renal water‑excretion by acting on V₂ receptors. Thus, a heightened IL‑6 milieu in CNS inflammation creates a “central” SIADH: euvolemic hyponatraemia with inappropriately concentrated urine and elevated serum ADH.

3. **Why neuro‑Behçet’s is a perfect fit**  
   • **Monocyte hyper‑activity** in BD leads to spontaneous IL‑6 production.  
   • **CSF IL‑6 elevations** have been documented in acute neuro‑Behçet’s and correlate with headache severity and CSF pleocytosis.  
   • **Colchicine** – the therapy that resolved the patient’s hyponatraemia – inhibits downstream signaling of IL‑1β and IL‑6; its success in this case indirectly supports the IL‑6‑SIADH hypothesis.

4. **Clinical pearl**  
   When a patient with Behçet’s disease presents with fever, headache and hyponatraemia, consider SIADH secondary to CNS inflammation. The hallmark is a moderate‑to‑severe rise in inflammatory markers (especially CRP) with no obvious drug or malignancy trigger. Recognizing the IL‑6–ADH axis allows prompt fluid restriction and IL‑6‑targeted therapy (e.g., colchicine, IL‑6 blockers) rather than unnecessary high‑dose steroids.

In summary, IL‑6 produced during acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease can drive inappropriate ADH secretion, producing the SIADH observed in this case. The temporal improvement of hyponatraemia alongside neurological recovery after colchicine monotherapy underscores the clinical relevance of this cytokine pathway.

### [Management] What are the mainstay therapies for acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease, and why might colchicine be considered an unconventional but reasonable monotherapy in selected cases?

**Answer**

**Mainstay therapy for acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD)**  
The classic treatment strategy for an acute neuro‑BD flare is high‑dose intravenous corticosteroids (e.g., methylprednisolone 1 g/day for 3–5 days, followed by a tapering course of oral prednisone). When patients are steroid‑refractory, intolerant, or have a severe systemic relapse, additional immunosuppression—cyclophosphamide, mycophenolate mofetil, or anti‑TNFα agents—is typically added. These agents target the heightened inflammatory milieu seen in neuro‑BD, especially the pro‑inflammatory cytokines IL‑6 and IL‑1β that drive both CNS and systemic manifestations.

**Why colchicine can be a reasonable monotherapy in select cases**  
1. **Targeted anti‑inflammatory action** – Colchicine blocks neutrophil chemotaxis and degranulation, and it suppresses IL‑1β and IL‑6 production. In the case at hand, the CSF was consistent with aseptic meningitis, and the patient’s serum CRP (180 mg/L) implied a cytokine‑driven process. Colchicine’s ability to dampen these cytokines may therefore alleviate both meningeal inflammation and the downstream SIADH (a known consequence of IL‑6‑mediated dysregulated ADH release).  
2. **Efficacy in mucocutaneous BD** – Colchicine is a first‑line agent for oral ulcers, genital aphthae and cutaneous lesions. When the neurologic picture is limited to meningoencephalitis without focal parenchymal lesions—as in this patient—colchicine can address the systemic disease and potentially prevent further neuro‑inflammation.  
3. **Reduced steroid burden** – High‑dose steroids carry significant side‑effects (hyperglycaemia, hypertension, infection risk). In a 60‑year‑old with poorly controlled diabetes, avoiding steroids is attractive. The patient’s rapid resolution of fever, headaches, vertigo, and hyponatraemia after colchicine initiation supports its effectiveness as a standalone agent.  
4. **Evidence of spontaneous remission** – Although most neuro‑BD lesions resolve with steroids, isolated meningoencephalitis has been reported to remit spontaneously. In the absence of focal deficits or MRI abnormalities, colchicine’s anti‑inflammatory power may be sufficient to abort the inflammatory cascade before irreversible CNS injury occurs.  

**Clinical takeaway**  
- **Default**: start IV corticosteroids for acute neuro‑BD.  
- **Consider colchicine monotherapy** when:  
  - Presentation is limited to aseptic meningitis without focal brain lesions,  
  - The patient has contraindications or high risk for steroid toxicity,  
  - Mucocutaneous disease is prominent and responds rapidly to colchicine.  

Monitoring of serum sodium, liver enzymes, and renal function is essential, as in the case report, to ensure that SIADH and systemic inflammation resolve without steroid‑induced complications.

### [Differential Diagnosis] Identify the clinical features that distinguish acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease from other causes of aseptic meningitis, and discuss the utility of CSF analysis in this context.

**Acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) versus other causes of aseptic meningitis**

| Feature | Acute neuro‑BD | Non‑vesicular viral meningitis (HSV, VZV, enterovirus) | Bacterial meningitis | Tuberculous or fungal meningitis | CNS malignancy |
|---------|----------------|--------------------------------------------------------|----------------------|----------------------------------|----------------|
| **Systemic clues** | Oral/genital aphthae, folliculitis‑like rash, HLA‑B51 positivity, elevated CRP/ESR, skin/genital ulcers | Usually absent; may have flu‑like prodrome | Neutrophilic pleocytosis, high protein, low glucose | Low glucose, lymphocytic pleocytosis, often chronic | Often no systemic rash or fever |
| **CSF pattern** | Mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (≤20 cells/µL), moderate protein elevation (≈0.8‑1.0 g/L), normal glucose | Variable lymphocytic pleocytosis, normal or mildly elevated protein, normal glucose | Neutrophile‑predominant pleocytosis (>100 cells/µL), high protein, low glucose | Lymphocyte‑predominant, very high protein, low glucose | Variable; may show atypical cells, high protein |
| **MRI** | Often normal early; later shows brain‑stem or thalamic lesions in ~50% of patients | Normal in most cases; may show “discoid” lesions in HSV encephalitis | Diffuse leptomeningeal enhancement (rare), brain abscess | Mass lesions or tuberculomas (in contrast, often normal early) | Mass effect, abnormal enhancement |
| **Response to steroids** | Rapid improvement with steroids (but spontaneous clearance may occur) | Viral: usually self‑limited; steroids rarely used | Required if bacterial therapy delayed | Steroids may help after antibiotics | Steroids rarely help, unless lymphoma|
| **Other clinical clues** | Frequently associated with psychiatric symptoms, headaches, vertigo, hiccups; may be complicated by SIADH (as in the case) | Often presents with neck stiffness, photophobia | Severe headache, vomiting, lethargy, seizures | Subacute onset, constitutional symptoms, cranial neuropathies | Usually focal deficits, cranial neuropathies |

**Key distinguishing elements in the index patient**

1. **Concurrent mucocutaneous BD phenotype** – oral aphthae, genital ulcer, folliculitis‑like rash, HLA‑B51 positivity, high CRP/ESR satisfied international BD criteria (score ≥ 4).  
2. **CSF findings** – mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL) and moderate protein (0.97 g/L) with negative cultures, PCR for HSV/VZV and cryptococcal antigen. The pattern is most compatible with an inflammatory, non‑infectious process rather than bacterial (neutrophilic), tuberculous or fungal meningitis.  
3. **Normal MRI** – early in the disease, brain‑stem or other characteristic lesions can be absent, as seen here.  
4. **Clinical course** – fever and headache improved with colchicine alone, without corticosteroids, and SIADH resolved with fluid restriction, supporting an inflammatory, BD‑related etiology.

**Utility of CSF analysis**

- **Diagnostic ruling‑out**: Identifies or excludes infectious agents, malignancy, and parasitic causes that would prompt markedly different management (antimicrobials, chemotherapy, etc.).  
- **Pattern recognition**: The lymphocytic pleocytosis with moderate protein elevation is the classic “aseptic” picture; it helps narrow the differential to viral, inflammatory, or neoplastic causes. In neuro‑BD, the lymphocyte count is usually low‑to‑moderate, distinguishing it from bacterial meningitis (high neutrophils) and TB/cryptococcal meningitis (high protein/low glucose).  
- **Monitoring response**: Serial CSF protein and cell counts can track resolution (as in this patient, CSF data were not repeated, but symptom resolution mirrors csf trend).  
- **Guiding therapy**: In neuro‑BD, a normal or moderately abnormal CSF profile together with mucocutaneous disease clinches the diagnosis, allowing steroid‑sparing options such as colchicine to be trialed when appropriate.

**Bottom line**  
In patients with fever, headache, and hyponatraemia, a CSF profile of mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, moderate protein elevation, normal glucose, and a negative infectious and malignant work‑up, combined with classic mucocutaneous BD findings, points toward acute neuro‑BD rather than other causes of aseptic meningitis. CSF remains indispensable for excluding life‑threatening etiologies and for confirming the inflammatory nature of the cerebrospinal fluid.

### [Management] Discuss the management strategy for hyponatraemia due to SIADH in the setting of neuro‑Behçet’s disease, including fluid restriction targets and monitoring parameters.

**Management of SIADH‑related hyponatraemia in acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease**

1. **Initial stabilization**  
   * Treat any symptomatic hyponatraemia (seizures, severe confusion, or coma) with a rapid‑acting 3 % saline bolus (100 cc in 10 min); do **not** administer 0.9 % saline because it can worsen sodium levels.  
   * Avoid hypertonic saline unless the patient is critically symptomatic; if used, limit the rate to ≤ 8 mEq Na⁺ L⁻¹ h⁻¹ (≈ 12 mmol/L daily).

2. **Fluid restriction**  
   * Target **< 800 mL of total fluid intake per day** in the setting of neuro‑BD, as in the case report.  
   * Include all oral fluids, drinks, and enteral tube feeds.  
   * In patients with mild to moderate hyponatraemia without overt symptoms, a 12–18 h intermittent restriction (no fluids for 6 h, then 1 L) can be initially tried before moving to a strict 800 mL/d schedule.

3. **Monitoring parameters**  
   * **Serum sodium**: check every 6–12 h for the first 48 h, then at least daily until ≥ 135 mmol/L.  
   * **Serum osmolality** and **urine osmolality**: assess daily or more frequently if sodium worsens.  
   * **Urine sodium**: monitor for response to fluid restriction; a drop < 20 mmol/L suggests adequate restriction.  
   * **Vital signs** and **mental status**: assess every 4–6 h during the acute phase.  
   * **Renal function and electrolytes**: daily; hyperchloremia or rising creatinine may signal over‑restriction.  
   * **Duration of restriction**: continue until serum sodium stabilizes ≥ 135 mmol/L and the underlying inflammatory trigger (neuro‑BD activity) has resolved.

4. **Adjunctive measures**  
   * **Fludrocortisone** (0.1 mg PO daily) can be added if free water intake is > 1 L/d and the patient remains euvolemic, but its use is limited by mineralocorticoid side‑effects and is rarely required in isolated SIADH.  
   * **Vasopressin receptor antagonists** (tolvaptan) are generally avoided in acute neuro‑BD because of cost, lack of evidence in this specific population, and the pre‑existing inflammatory milieu; they are reserved for refractory cases where fluid restriction fails.

5. **Red flags for rapid correction**  
   * Do **not** allow serum sodium to rise > 6 mmol/L in 24 h or > 10 mmol/L in 48 h to prevent osmotic demyelination.  
   * If sodium rises too quickly, consider administering free water or discontinuing fluid restriction temporarily.

6. **Integration with neuro‑BD therapy**  
   * Treat the underlying CNS inflammation (colchicine, corticosteroids, or other immunosuppressants as appropriate).  
   * As the inflammatory activity declines, the SIADH often resolves concurrently, as observed in the case report (serum Na⁺ 124 → 136 mmol/L, ADH 22.4 → 1.8 pg/mL after colchicine alone).  
   * Continue fluid restriction only as long as serum sodium remains < 135 mmol/L and ADH remains inappropriately high.

**Take‑home point**: In neuro‑Behçet’s disease, SIADH is managed with a conservative fluid restriction of < 800 mL/day, vigilant serial sodium monitoring, and avoidance of rapid correction. The underlying inflammation is the key driver; thus, treating neuro‑BD often leads to SIADH resolution.

### [Pathophysiology] Explain how rhabdomyolysis can arise as a complication of severe hyponatraemia, and describe the clinical clues that should prompt evaluation of serum creatine kinase in patients with CNS inflammation.

**Clinical Teaching Answer**

Severe hyponatraemia, especially when it is chronic or develops rapidly, can precipitate rhabdomyolysis through a combination of osmotic stress, hypovolaemia‑induced muscle ischemia, and impaired cellular ion homeostasis. In the patient described, the markedly low plasma sodium (124 mmol/L) and low serum osmolality (267 mOsm/kg) created an osmotic gradient that drew water into muscle cells, causing cellular swelling and lysis. Concomitant stimulation of the sympathetic nervous system and cytokine release (e.g., IL‑6, CRP 180 mg/L) further taxed muscle metabolism. The resultant release of intracellular creatine kinase (CK = 1349 U/L) and transaminases reflected muscle necrosis rather than hepatic injury. Rapid correction of sodium is avoided because replacement of extracellular fluid volume superimposed on the already hypovolemic state can lead to acute renal failure and acute tubular necrosis.

**Clinical clues that raise suspicion for rhabdomyolysis in patients with CNS inflammation**

| Feature | Reason for concern |
|--------|------------------|
| **Persistent or worsening muscle pain, aches, or fatigue** | Direct muscle injury; often subtle in neuro‑inflammatory states |
| **Red‑brown urine or dark discoloration** | Hemoglobinuria/mieoglobinuria from muscle breakdown |
| **Elevated serum creatine kinase** | Gold‑standard diagnostic marker; should be checked with any unexplained myalgia or fatigue |
| **Elevated transaminases (AST > ALT)** | AST is abundant in skeletal muscle; a disproportionate rise suggests muscle injury |
| **Symptoms of systemic inflammation (fever, CRP, ESR)** | Inflammatory cytokines (IL‑6) can stimulate both ADH release (SIADH) and muscle catabolism |
| **Signs of SIADH (euvolemic hyponatraemia, low serum osmolality, high urine osmolality)** | Hyponatraemia itself is a recognized precipitant of rhabdomyolysis |
| **Neurologic complaints (headache, vertigo, hiccups)** | May be manifestations of both CNS inflammation and severe hyponatraemia; the latter can worsen neurological status and can be a red flag for muscle breakdown |
| **No obvious trauma or statin use** | Exclude other common causes of rhabdomyolysis |

In the present case, the patient’s **headache, persistent hiccups, generalized malaise, and hyponatraemia** prompted evaluation, and the laboratory workup revealed a **markedly elevated CK**. The absence of overt muscle tenderness or focal weakness is typical: muscle injury can be silent in septic or inflammatory states. These findings underscore the importance of serial CK measurements whenever hyponatraemia and CNS inflammation coexist, even when the clinical picture is dominated by neurologic symptoms.

---

**Key Take‑away**

- Severe hyponatraemia can directly cause rhabdomyolysis via osmotic and metabolic derangements; this complication is frequently overlooked in patients with CNS inflammation.
- Persistent hiccups, unexplained fatigue, or mild myalgias in the setting of hyponatraemia should prompt early serum CK assessment.
- Early detection allows prompt supportive management (volume resuscitation, monitoring of renal function, cautious sodium correction) and prevents progression to acute kidney injury.
