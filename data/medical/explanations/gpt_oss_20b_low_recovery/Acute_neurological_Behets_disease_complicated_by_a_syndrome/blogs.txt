Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

## When Brain and Electrolytes Collide: SIADH as a Hidden Complication of Acute Neuro‑Behçet’s

### The “Nice” Surprising Picture

I recently read a case report that had me double‑checking my glances at a patient’s sodium levels. An Asian gentleman in his 60s presented with fever, headache, oral ulcers, and—most strikingly—marked hyponatraemia (124 mmol/L). Brain MRI was clean, CSF showed aseptic meningitis, and the hyponatraemia fulfilled SIADH criteria. The kicker? All of this resolved with **colchicine alone**—no steroids, no plasma exchange, no vasopressin antagonists.

At first glance, a classic three‑step puzzle: fever → headache → hyponatraemia → “brain thing” → Behçet’s disease. But the story underlines a subtle but important lesson: when a neuro‑inflammatory disease presents with electrolyte disturbances, SIADH may be the weirder culprit lurking behind the usual headache.

---

## A Quick Refresher: What Is SIADH?

- **Definition**: Inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone (ADH) in euvolemic patients, leading to water retention and hyponatraemia.
- **Key lab pattern**: low serum osmolality, inappropriately concentrated urine (high osmolality > 100 mOsm/kg), and urine sodium > 20 mmol/L.
- **Common triggers**: CNS insults (infectious meningitis, encephalitis, stroke), pulmonary disease, malignancies, certain drugs (SSRIs, carbamazepine, etc.), and systemic inflammatory states.

In the reported case, all SIADH criteria were met—fluid restriction <800 mL/day and no immunosuppressants fixed the sodium in the upper-120s by day 16.

---

## How CNS Inflammation Gets In the Mix

### 1. Cytokines – the “Bad Actors”

- **IL‑6** is a star player in neuro‑inflammation. Elevated IL‑6 drives nociception, vascular leakage, and—crucially—stimulates the hypothalamic–pituitary axis to secrete ADH.
- In Behçet’s disease, monocytes are hyperactive and release IL‑6 even without overt infection.
- Even if we didn’t measure IL‑6 in this patient, the dramatic CRP spike (180 mg/L) hinted at a high IL‑6 milieu.
  
> *Take home*: Think of IL‑6 as a cytokine that can “turn on” the ADH pump when CNS is inflamed.

### 2. The Timing of Neuro‑BD

- Around 10–20 % of BD patients develop neuro‑BD, usually **several years** after the initial mucocutaneous flare.
- Rare cases like this one happen **concurrently** (systemic BD + acute neuro‑BD). The first NSD symptom is often a “meningo‑encephalitis” picture without focal deficits.
- MRI can be normal early on, especially if the inflammation is confined to the leptomeninges. This normal imaging can lull us into a false sense of security.

---

## Classic (and Not‑So‑Classic) Bedside Clues

| Symptom | Why It Matters |
|---------|----------------|
| **Hiccups** | Can be a red‑flag for severe hyponatraemia. In the setting of meningitis, hiccups may reflect brainstem irritation or even induced metabolic derangement. |
| **Rhabdomyolysis (CK > 1,300 U/L)** | Severe hyponatraemia can damage muscle membranes, leading to CK rise. Hypernatremia or aggressive correction would risk osmotic demyelination, but should not ignore the underlying electrolyte issue. |
| **Normal MRI** | Absence of focal lesions does **not** exclude neuro‑BD. Leptomeningeal inflammation might not be picked up until later or may not produce contrast enhancement. |
| **Sudden onset of mucocutaneous lesions (or newly identified genital ulcers)** | Adds weight to the Behçet’s diagnosis, even if the nervous system is already involved. |

---

## Diagnostic Work‑Up: From “What’s Wrong?” to “It’s Behçet’s”

1. **Initial labs**  
   - Serum sodium, osmolality, urine osmolality, urine sodium.  
   - Renal, liver function, glucose.  
2. **Exclude infections**  
   - Blood cultures, CSF culture, PCRs for HSV, VZV, TB, fungi.  
3. **Imaging**  
   - Baseline CT to rule out mass/hemorrhage, MRI if clinically justified.  
4. **Rule out autoimmunity and malignancy**  
   - ANA, RF, ACE, sIL‑2R, beta‑2 microglobulin (depending on suspicion).  
5. **Behçet’s score**  
   - Oral aphthae (2 pts), genital ulcers (2 pts), folliculitis‑like rash (1 pt). → Total 5 pts = definite BD.  
6. **SIADH confirmation**  
   - Exclude hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency, diuretics, and other cause. The patient was euvolemic, with normal cortisol and TSH.

---

## Treatment: The Unconventional Colchicine Success Story

Traditionally, **high‑dose corticosteroids** are the first line for acute neuro‑BD, with immunosuppressive agents (cyclophosphamide, azathioprine, mycophenolate) added for refractory disease. But in this case:

- **Colchicine** (oral, 0.6 mg three times daily) was  initiated on day 8.  
- No steroids ever.  
- No vasopressin antagonists; fluid restriction alone normalized sodium.  
- Outcomes: 
  - Fever, headache, vertigo resolved within 2 weeks.  
  - CK and CRP trended down.  
  - Sodium normalized by day 16.  
  - No relapse over 4 years of colchicine maintenance.  

Why might colchicine work?  
- Inhibits neutrophil chemotaxis and degranulation.  
- Suppresses IL‑1β and IL‑6 production—critical drivers for both BD inflammation and ADH secretion.  

> *Note*: While colchicine is acknowledged for mucocutaneous BD, its use as monotherapy for neuro‑BD is rare and often reserved for mild cases or when steroids are contraindicated.

---

## What Should You Remember, Clinicians?

- **Keep SIADH high on the differential** even in patients with CNS inflammation who look otherwise euvolemic.  
- **Assess hyponatraemia early**—if sodium < 130 mmol/L, perform the full SIADH panel (serum osmolality, urine osmolality, urine sodium).  
- **Beware incidental clues**: persistent hiccups, unexplained CK rise, and normal MRI can point to neuro‑BD + SIADH.  
- **Consider cytokine-driven ADH** in the context of fever, headache, and high CRP—especially in diseases known for IL‑6 dysregulation (like BD).  
- **Colchicine can be a game‑changer** in selected neuro‑BD cases—especially when steroids may carry high morbidity (e.g., uncontrolled diabetes, infection risk).  

---

### Take‑Home Points

- **SIADH can masquerade as “just a low sodium”** in CNS inflammation; always run the SIADH diagnostic criteria.  
- **Normal MRI does not exclude neuro‑BD**—assess CSF for pleocytosis and elevated protein.  
- **IL‑6 is a common link** between CNS inflammation and inappropriate ADH release.  
- **Colchicine may suffice** for mild neuro‑BD complicated by SIADH, obviating the need for steroids in some patients.  
- **Throughout management, monitor sodium cautiously**; overcorrection can be catastrophic.

In the end, this case reminds us that the “brain” and the “electrolytes” can collide in ways that are subtle, but with a high index of suspicion, we can untangle the mystery—and spare patients from unnecessary treatments.



# Colchicine‑First? Rethinking the Role of Low‑Dose Colchicine in Acute Neuro‑Behçet’s

## The “surprising” pivot from steroids to colchicine

Most of us learned in EM that acute neuro‑Behçet’s (neuro‑BD) screams for high‑dose steroids, sometimes followed by cyclosporine or cyclophosphamide. But a senior case in the literature shows a 60‑year‑old with fever, headache, fever, mucocutaneous lesions, and a bewilderingly low serum sodium that turned out to be SIADH from CNS inflammation, and who improved *entirely* on colchicine. No prednisolone, no pulse steroids, no immunosuppressants. The improvement was rapid—symptoms went away in a week, sodium normalized in 2 weeks, and the patient stayed well for four years on colchicine alone.

That is the hook every EM fellow wants: “Could colchicine be a true first‑line in neuro‑BD?” Let’s unpack the evidence, mechanisms, and where the gray zone lies.

## How do we get a diagnosis that feels like a tightrope walk?

1. **Presentation**: systemic BD looking for skin, oral ulcers, and a “bug‑like” rash.  
2. **Neurologic spark**: fever, persistent headache, vertigo, mild lymphocytic CSF, no parenchymal MRI lesions.  
3. **Hyponatremia puzzle**: SIADH confirmed by low serum osmolality, high urine osmolality, and elevated ADH.  
4. **Rule‑out**: extensive negative infectious work‑up, no malignant cells, normal ACE, mildly high sIL‑2R but nonspecific.

In a real‑world ED, you might keep a differential wide. Viral meningitis, TB, bacterial meningitis, CNS lymphoma, sarcoidosis, connective tissue disease—all get a go‑round. But remember, a normal MRI does *not* exclude neuro‑BD; it can be a purely aseptic meningitic picture.

## Why did this patient get the full neon­detic picture *at* first presentation?

- **Concurrent systemic and neuro‑BD** is rare (≈5 % of new BD pts).  
- **SIADH as a consequence of CNS inflammation**: elevated IL‑6 can spur ADH release.  
- No immunosuppressive drug exposure to explain SIADH (rules out cyclophosphamide‑induced SIADH).  
- The lingering “hiccups” and rhabdomyolysis tell us how subtle hyponatremia could actually go untreated and cause real harm.

In short, the central message: *Cerebral inflammation can tug on ADH pathways even in the absence of radiologic lesions.*

## Colchicine: the under‑the‑radar anti‑inflammatory

### Mechanisms that might make sense for neuro‑BD

| Target | Why it matters in neuro‑BD |
|--------|----------------------------|
| **Neutrophil chemotaxis inhibition** | BD is a neutrophil‑driven vasculitis; blocking migration dampens vascular inflammation. |
| **IL‑1β suppression** | IL‑1β drives fever and cytokine cascade; colchicine reduces its production. |
| **IL‑6 inhibition** | IL‑6 is elevated in neuro‑BD CSF and is implicated in SIADH through ADH secretion. |
| **Microtubule disruption** | Potentially helps reduce cytokine release from activated immune cells. |

These are not magic bullets, but they do hit the theoretical culprits behind both cutaneous and CNS inflammation, plus the side‑effect – SIADH – that may be tied to IL‑6.

### The evidence snapshot

- **Systemic BD**: colchicine’s role is well accepted for oral/genital ulcers, skin disease, and arthralgia.  
- **Neuro‑BD**: most literature says colchicine *needs* steroids or biologics. A few case reports (including our “colchicine‑first” case) show spontaneous resolution or add‑on improvements, but no large series or RCTs.  
- **Safety profile**: moderate dose (0.6 mg BID for adults) is generally well tolerated; GI upset is the main hazard.  
- **Cost and access**: colchicine is cheap, easily stocked, and does not require monitoring like steroids or biologics.

### When is colchicine a sensible first‑choice?

- **Steroid contraindication**: uncontrolled diabetes, active GI ulcers, risk of infection.  
- **Mild neuro‑BD**: isolated aseptic meningitis without focal deficits or brainstem signs.  
- **Patient preference**: those who opt-out of steroids or have difficulty with daily steroid tapering.  
- **Adjunctive therapy**: as a steroid-sparing agent in steroid‑resistant neuro‑BD.  

When the disease is severe (e.g., sensorimotor deficits, cranial nerve palsy, BME on MRI), colchicine alone is unlikely to be enough, and you should still consider steroids.

## Infectious vs inflammatory: the art of bracketing

The case highlight a **“dead‑even”** common pitfall: over‑investigation. A battery of expensive tests (ACE, sIL‑2R, fungal markers) yielded little. The lesson: **treat the suspected diagnosis once reasonable evidence is convincing**; wait for a full evaluation no more than the 48–72 hour window when the disease can still be reversed.

## Take‑home pearls

- **Autoimmune CNS inflammation can precipitate SIADH** – any patient with unexplained hyponatremia and CNS inflammation should be on a SIADH eye‑belt.  
- **Colchicine may be a viable first‑line for mild, non‑focal neuro‑BD**; it is inexpensive, widely available, and avoids steroid side‑effects.  
- **Monitor sodium, serum osmolality, and ADH** early – a rise of 2–3 mmol/L risk increases the likelihood of neurologic deterioration.  
- **Steroid intolerance = colchicine alternative** – but be ready to step up if symptoms worsen.  
- **Always reassess clinically** – if the cholinergic-hypo-osmolar environment improves, the inflammatory drivers may wane.  

## Final thought: Evidence is balancing on a razor’s edge

We’re still a few high‑quality trials away from re-writing the offending neuro‑BD guideline. Until then, consider colchicine a tactical choice rather than a strategic force‑push. When the disease is “simple” and the patient frail or steroid‑averse, give it a shot. If the patient’s neurologic picture is anything but trivial, steroids and possibly biologics remain the standard. In either scenario, the case we dissected offers a compelling roadmap to revisit our own practice patterns.

# Beyond Brain Imaging: When a Normal MRI Fails to Rule Out Neuro‑Behçet’s

## What’s the Big Surprise?

An older‐than‑usual patient with fever, headache, and a sore throat just can’t stay quiet. He has mucocutaneous sores, a rash that looks like folliculitis, and a mild drop in sodium. The first thing your brain (and your EM’s dashboard) goes to: “Brain MRI.” It’s bright, bright, beautiful, but … nothing out of the ordinary. No brainstem lesion, no pituitary flare, no ring‑enhancing mass.

In a nutshell, a **normal MRI doesn’t mean a normal brain in acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD)**. Think of the brain like a weather map; sometimes the storm is brewing in the deep, and you just haven’t caught it yet.

---

## Why Clinicians Trust the MRI

- High sensitivity for space‑occupying lesions (tumors, abscesses).
- Ability to spot classic BD hot‑spots (brainstem, basal ganglia, cerebellum).
- Negates surgical risks in dangerous areas.

But those reasons only hold if you’ve already captured the lid of the storm. With aseptic meningitis‑type neuro‑BD, the **inflammation is more like a whisper than a thunderclap**—it may not leave a visible ripple on that sweet fMRI.

---

## A Case That Told a Different Story

- **Patient**: 60‑ish man, sudden fever, headache, hiccups, arthralgia, oral/genital ulcers, mild rash.
- **Labs**: profound hyponatraemia (124 mmol/L), elevated CRP (180 mg/L), CK 1,349 U/L, hyperglycaemia.
- **Endocrine work‑up**: low serum osmolality, high urine osmolality, elevated ADH → SIADH.
- **Brain MRI**: Completely clean.
- **CSF**: Mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL), protein 0.97 g/L.
- **Diagnosis**: Acute neuro‑BD + systemic BD + SIADH.
- **Treatment**: Colchicine monotherapy (no steroids, no immunosuppressants); fluid restriction.
- **Outcome**: Full neurology + mucocutaneous recovery in 2 weeks; sodium normalized by day 16; colchicine continued for 4 years, no relapse.

> **Take‑away**: The “silent MRI” was a red flag, not a reassuring green.

---

## Why MV: The “Missed‑Variables” of Neuro‑BD Imaging

| Variable | Why It Matters | Typical MRI Finding | When It Can be Missed |
|----------|----------------|---------------------|-----------------------|
| **Timing** | Acute inflammation peaks early | Lesions may be too small or not yet contrast‑enhancing | <48 h after symptom onset |
| **Lesion Location** | Some BD lesions in the *hypothalamus* or *interpeduncular cistern* | Very small signal changes | Partial volume effects |
| **Meningeal Involvement** | Classic presentation is aseptic meningitis | Ignores meninges on routine sequences | When CSF is abnormal but brain parenchyma is spared |
| **Vascular Changes** | Dynamic vasculitis can be transient | Normal T1/T2/T2∗ | Often only seen with high‑resolution vessel wall imaging |
| **"Functional" Perfusion** | Inflammation can alter perfusion without causing structural change | Normal conventional MRI | Perfusion imaging or PET may be needed |

---

## Alternative Diagnostic Strategies

### 1. CSF Analysis (the Real Gold Standard)

- A mild pleocytosis (10–50 cells/µL) with lymphocytic predominance is a hallmark for aseptic meningitis.
- Elevated protein (≥0.8 g/L) suggests BBB disruption.
- Rule out bacterial, viral, mycobacterial, fungal, and malignant causes with cultures, PCRs, cytology.
- In the described case, CSF was the clue that the brain was **inflamed, even though MRI was silent**.

#### Pearls:
- **Opening pressure**: Even mild elevation (≥15 cmH₂O) points toward inflammation.
- **IgG index**: May help distinguish CNS infection from immune‑mediated disease.

### 2. Symptomatology & Physical Exam

- **Hiccups, vertigo, nausea**: Can hint at meningeal irritation.
- **Discordant focal deficits**: In BD, deficits often recur in the same regions; a new deficit warrants imaging but not a “no‑MRI” verdict.
- **Systemic clues**: Oral/genital ulcers, skin lesions, uveitis—all tie BD’s multisystem nature together.

### 3. Serial Imaging

- A follow‑up scan after 48–72 h can capture evolving lesions.
- Contrast‑enhanced MRI with diffusion weighting is sensitive for early inflammatory changes.

### 4. Advanced Imaging (Optional, If Available)

- **High‑resolution vessel wall MRI** – detects cortical vasculitis.
- **18F‑FDG PET** – reveals hypermetabolic inflammatory foci often missed on structural MRI.
- **Susceptibility‑Weighted Imaging (SWI)** – helpful for microhemorrhages triggered by neuro‑BD.

---

## SIADH in Neuro‑BD: A Symptom, a Sign, or a Player?

In the case above, SIADH was part of the puzzle. CNS inflammation (especially interleukin‑6‑mediated pathways) can blunt the blood–brain barrier and spur excess ADH release. Importantly:

- **Persistent hiccups** and **myalgias** may be red‑flags for SIADH‑induced hyponatraemia.
- **Rhabdomyolysis** (CK spike) can be a downstream effect of severe hyponatraemia.
- **Fluid restriction** and sodium monitoring are backbone; no vasopressin antagonists needed in mild or moderate cases.

---

## Colchicine: Repercussion or Revelation?

Traditionally, steroids are the first‑line for acute neuro‑BD, but this patient recovered on colchicine alone. Why consider colchicine?

- **Mechanism**: Neutrophil inhibition, downregulates IL‑1β, IL‑6.
- **Safety**: Less immunosuppression, fewer steroid‑related side effects.
- **Evidence**: Limited but promising in cases *without* severe focal deficits.

⚠️ **Caveat**: Colchicine should not replace steroids in patients with life‑threatening CNS disease (e.g., demyelinating lesions, significant focal deficits). Use it selectively, usually as an adjunct or in mild presentations.

---

## The Bottom Line in Conversation

1. **Never rely solely on MRI** early in acute neuro‑BD—especially if CSF shows inflammation.
2. **Keep the bedside CSF** as a powerful, inexpensive diagnostic tool.
3. **Watch for hyponatraemia** and related symptoms; SIADH may be a clue to CNS inflammation.
4. **Consider colchicine** for mild to moderate cases, but remember steroids remain first‑line for severe disease.
5. **Serial imaging** is the magician’s trick—what you miss today may show up tomorrow.

---

## Take‑Home Blueprint

- **If “headache + fever + mucocutaneous lesions” → check:**  
  - Serum sodium and ADH level.  
  - CSF (cell count, protein, cultures, PCR).  
  - Initial MRI (but know its limits).  

- **If CSF abnormal but MRI clean →**  
  - Diagnose acute neuro‑BD (aseptic meningitis pattern).  
  - Start colchicine + fluid restriction.  

- **If MRI shows focal lesion →**  
  - Add corticosteroids promptly.  

- **Follow up**: Repeat serum sodium, repeat MRI if deficits evolve or no clinical improvement.

End with a gentle reminder: **Your brain may be silent; your body may be loudly calling for help.** When in doubt, lean on the CSF—and the humble watchful delay of a repeat MRI—rather than dismissing the silent scan.



# Hyponatraemia in the Emergency Room: A Case‑Based Guide to SIADH in Infectious and Inflammatory Conditions  

## Why This Case Is a “Wake‑up Call”  

When an older male with a history of untreated diabetes presents with fever, headache, hiccups and a 3‑day course of oral aphthae, the first thing that comes to mind is infection. Yet when the labs show a sodium of **124 mmol/L**, a low plasma osmolality (267 mOsm/kg) and a high urine osmolality (900 mOsm/kg), you’re staring at something entirely different: an endocrine derangement driven by inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH).  

What makes this case memorable is that the SIADH was **not** Drug‑induced, nor did it arise from a classic CNS tumor or pulmonary disease. Instead, the culprit was a fulminant, *concurrent* diagnosis of Behçet’s disease (BD) and acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) – a rare overlap that can stealthily masquerade as meningitis or a viral encephalitis.  

The lesson? In the ER, hyponatraemia with normal volume status should always prompt a “Think CNS inflammation” panel, and the work‑up can be rapid enough to divert from unnecessary antibiotics or chemo‑drugs.



## A Quick Algorithm for Severe Hyponatraemia (≤120 mmol/L)

| Step | What to do | Why it matters |
|------|------------|----------------|
| 1. Confirm labs | Repeat serum Na and osmolality; check urine Na and osmolality | Avoid alarmism from a single lab error |
| 2. Assess volume | Auscultate heart, check cap refill, physical exam, urine output | Distinguish euvolemic SIADH from hypovolemic or hypervolemic states |
| 3. Get the basics | CBC, BMP, LFTs, TSH, cortisol, serum electrolytes | Rule out hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency |
| 4. Think cause | Infection (especially CNS), malignancy, pulmonary disease, drug exposure | Narrow the differential quickly |
| 5. Order imaging | CT head (or MRI if suspicion high) and chest/abdo CT if indicated | Exclude masses, opportunistic infections, and metastasis |
| 6. CSF analysis if CNS suspected | Opening pressure, cell count, protein, gram stain, cultures, PCRs for HSV/VZV/TB | Confirm or rule out meningitis/encephalitis |
| 7. Begin treatment | Fluid restriction (≤800 mL/day in euvolemic SIADH) and monitor daily Na | Prevent over‑correction |

*Time‑sensitive notes* – If Na <110 mmol/L or neurological signs appear (seizures, altered mentation), consider cautious hypertonic saline (2 % 100 ml over 20 min) but aim for <8–10 mmol rise in 24 h.



## Red Flags That Should Pull the Gears

- **Euvolemic status with low plasma osmolality** – classic SIADH pattern.  
- **Elevated urine osm >300 mOsm/kg** with **urine Na >20 mmol/L** – kidneys excreting water but retaining sodium.  
- **Normal thyroid, cortisol, and kidney function** – eliminates endocrine and renal causes.  
- **Concurrent systemic inflammatory signs** – high CRP, ESR, fever; in this case, on top of mucocutaneous BD lesions.  
- **Neurological symptoms without focal deficit** – headache, vertigo, hiccups—often seen in intracranial inflammation but can also be electrolyte‑driven.  

In our patient, the **hiccups** were a subtle but telling neurologic sign that paralleled the degree of hyponatraemia (a known but under‑reported correlation).



## SIADH Secondary to CNS Inflammation: The Behçet’s Connection

Behçet’s disease can present as **acute neuro‑BD** even before classic mucocutaneous lesions appear. In this setting, pleocytosis in CSF, mild protein elevation, and a negative infectious work‑up point toward aseptic meningitis. The brain MRI may be *normal* early on; lesion-free imaging does not rule out CNS inflammation.  

Why SIADH happens here? Inflammatory cytokines—especially **IL‑6**—drive ectopic ADH release from the hypothalamus or posterior pituitary. Although IL‑6 was not measured in the published case, the marked CRP (180 mg/L) hints at a cytokine surge that could have spurred the inappropriate water retention.



## Management Pearls (Take‑Home Bullet Points)

- **Fluid restriction is first‑line** in euvolemic SIADH; aim for ≤800 mL/day and titrate to serum Na rise.  
- **Daily serum Na & urine Na** should be checked every 6–12 h initially, then every 12–24 h as the Na stabilises.  
- **Do not jump to hypertonic saline** unless Na <110 mmol/L or there is neurological decline; if you do, limit to 2 % 100 ml over 20 min and watch closely.  
- **Avoid overly aggressive correction**; the rule of thumb is <8–10 mmol/24 h to prevent osmotic demyelination.  
- **Treat the underlying inflammation**—in this case colchicine monotherapy resolved not only mucocutaneous lesions but also the SIADH and neurologic symptoms.  
- **Consider early rheumatology or neurology consult** when SIADH is accompanied by unexplained febrile illness and normal imaging.  
- **Monitor for rhabdomyolysis** (CK can rise dramatically in profound hyponatraemia); treat with aggressive IV fluids if true rhabdo develops.  
- **Hiccups may be a subtle clue**—if you see persistent hiccups with hyponatraemia, think not just “GERD” but underlying electrolyte disturbance.  


## Post‑Discharge Conundrums

- **Long‑term colchicine**—this patient stayed on colchicine for 4 years with no recurrence, suggesting that anti‑neutrophil agents can sometimes bridge acute neuro‑BD and SIADH without steroids.  
- **Glycaemic control**—his untreated diabetes contributed to a higher glucose load. Recognise that hyperglycaemia can *mask* hyponatraemia, so always correct glucose before interpreting Na.  
- **Re‑exposure risk**—if future steroids or cyclophosphamide are needed, re‑elevated SIADH risk exists, so monitor closely.



## What We’ve Learned

1. **Hyponatraemia in the ED is a red river**: any low Na with low serum osmolality should be suspect for SIADH.  
2. **CNS inflammation can masquerade as infection**; drug‑induced SIADH is not the only culprit.  
3. **Normal imaging does not rule out neuro‑BD**; a high index of suspicion and CSF analysis are often decisive.  
4. **Colchicine may be effective** for acute neuro‑BD in the absence of steroids, but evidence is limited and case‑based.  
5. **Fluid restriction + close monitoring** are the safest way to manage SIADH; avoid the temptation of rapid correction.  

In a busy ER, these pearls can streamline a dangerous situation—prevent ICU transfers, avoid neurological sequelae, and most importantly, pull an otherwise misdiagnosed autoimmune flare back into the realm of rational, evidence‑based care.  

---  

*If you ever see a patient with hyponatraemia that looks “too easy” for simple causes, grab your bedside glucose (and if the patient is a coffee‑drinking mime, catch the hiccup too). It might just be the tip of an underlying inflammatory iceberg.*



# The Silent “Other” Symptoms of SIADH: Hiccups, Rhabdomyolysis, and Mis‑attributed Fatigue

## Why this case feels like a “clinical mystery”

Picture a patient in their 60s who comes in with fever, a pounding headache, and a persistent hiccup that’s cracking open his ribs more than any mucocutaneous rash. His blood tests reveal a wildly low sodium that’s been hidden behind the normal‑looking electrolytes. When you start digging, you run the usual suspects—meningitis, sepsis, cancer—only to hit a wall. The breakthrough arrives when you remember that a silent player, syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH), often rides shotgun with central nervous system (CNS) inflammation. In a recent Neuro‑Behçet’s disease case, the SIADH didn’t just flinch it— it manifested as hiccups, muscle breakdown, and a fog of generalized fatigue that could have fooled anyone into blaming the underlying disease.

## SIADH in a nutshell

- **Definition**: Excess ADH in a euvolemic state → water retention → hyponatraemia.
- **Typical clues**: Euvolemic hyponatraemia, low plasma osmolality, high urine osmolality, urinary sodium > 20 mmol/L, and **no renal, adrenal, or thyroid failure**.
- **Common triggers**: CNS pathology (infectious, inflammatory, neoplastic), pulmonary disease, drugs (SSRIs, anticonvulsants, chemotherapy), and rarely, systemic inflammation.

When the brain is inflamed—as in meningitis, encephalitis, or even an acute flare of Behçet’s disease—ADH can be “oh‑no‑it’s‑the‑brain” stimulated. And yes, it can do so even in the absence of any radiographic evidence.

## The triad of “accidental” symptoms

| Symptom | How SIADH tricks us | Why it shows up in CNS inflammation |
|---------|--------------------|-------------------------------------|
| **Hiccups** | Often dismissed as gastro‑esophageal reflux or medication side‑effects. | Rapid, uncontrolled diaphragmatic contractions are triggered by fluid shifts and electrolyte imbalances in the central pattern‑generator circuits. Acute hyponatraemia makes the neurons hyperexcitable, leading to hiccups. |
| **Rhabdomyolysis** | Usually linked to trauma, drugs, or metabolic myopathies. | Severe hyponatraemia disturbs Na⁺/K⁺‑ATPase function in myocytes, causing intracellular Na⁺ overload, Ca²⁺ influx, and muscle breakdown. CK spikes, IV hydration is essential, and you could easily attribute the rise to “general malaise.” |
| **Fatigue/Dyspnea** | Classic for chronic illness—cardiac failure, anemia, depression. | In SIADH, water retention and cerebral edema can impair oxygen delivery, create a “brain fog,” and cause a sense of global exhaustion. In neuro‑inflammation, cytokine release adds the “inflammatory fatigue” layer. |

### The clinical pearls

- **Hiccups + fever = MENINGITIS?** Don’t overlook the possibility of SIADH, especially if the hiccups are refractory to the usual treatments and the patient is hyponatraemic.
- **CK elevation + hyponatraemia = Rhabdomyolysis?** Work useful when you see a CK > 10 000 U/L with low sodium—simple fluid restriction and cautious saline can arrest the cascade.
- **Persistent fatigue + normal vital signs**: Check serum sodium first. Treating the sodium often unravels the fatigue dramatically.
- **Never ignore “isolated” laboratory abnormalities**: A low serum sodium of 124 mmol/L coupled with a high urine osmolality should flag SIADH, no matter how “minor” the clinical picture might appear.

## How to flag SIADH early (and what to watch for)

1. **Baseline electrolytes** in any patient with CNS symptoms—fever, headache, confusion, or even hiccups.  
2. **Urine osmolality and sodium** if serum sodium < 130 mmol/L.  
3. **Review medications**: SSRIs, antiepileptics, bisphosphonates—any known SIADH triggers?  
4. **Check volume status**: A truly euvolemic picture is a red flag for SIADH versus hypovolemia or heart failure.  
5. **Track trends**: Rapid drops in sodium (<10 mmol/L in 24 h) often accompany acute CNS insults.

## What to do when SIADH is found in neuro‑inflammation

| Management | How it helps |
|------------|--------------|
| **Fluid restriction** (≤800 mL/day) | Directly limits water intake, allowing sodium to rise. Works best when hyponatraemia is a secondary effect. |
| **Salt tablets** | Provides extra sodium without adding water; handy in chronic or refractory cases. |
| **Hypertonic saline (3%)** | Reserved for severe symptoms (seizures, altered mental status). Tight monitoring required to avoid osmotic demyelination. |
| **Vaptans (e.g., tolvaptan)** | If refractory, they block V2 receptors in the collecting duct. Use with caution—rapid correction is dangerous. |
| **Treat the underlying CNS inflammation** | Steroids, immunosuppressants, or disease‑specific therapy (e.g., colchicine, biologics) may dampen cytokine‑driven ADH release. |

## Take‑home points

- **Hiccups, muscle pain, fatigue—** not just CNS complaints; they can be the first hints toward a hidden hyponatraemia.  
- **In any CNS inflammatory syndrome,** quickly rule out SIADH before you start steroids or immunosuppressants—because treating the root of the electrolyte imbalance may blunt the need for aggressive immunosuppression.  
- **Early recognition and conservative fluid management** can reverse SIADH and restore neurological function faster than waiting for the CNS disease to settle on its own.  
- **Colchicine can work** when you’re dealing with Behçet’s disease and neuro‑inflammation, possibly by dampening the IL‑6 drive behind both the CNS flare and SIADH.  
- **Always keep a low threshold** to check serum sodium in patients with any unexplained “generalised malaise” or “persistent hiccups.”

---

When the story starts with a “simple” headache or a quick‑now‑faded hiccup, you might be tempted to think of a self‑limited viral infection. The bigger lesson? A little sodium can have a big voice in the room. Keep your electrolyte panel handy, and don’t let the hiccups or faint muscle aches distract you from the sodium levels that could be the missing piece of the puzzle.



# Avoiding the ‘Lab Lure’: Targeted Investigations in Unexplained CNS Inflammation  

## The Strange Patient Who Fell Through the Diagnostic Net  

When a man in his 60s walked into the ED with fever, headache, hiccups, and a tongue‑dragging rash, the first thought that popped into the attending physicians’ heads was “common pathogens.” Yet as the story unfolded, what should have started as a routine work‑up for meningo‑encephalitis turned into a lesson about how to snake through the diagnostic battlefield without drowning in zeroth‑degree tests.  

What made this case interesting?  
- **Concomitant surface and deep involvement:** mucocutaneous Behçet’s disease (BD) and *acute neuro-*BD presented *at the same time*, a scenario seen in <5 % of BD patients.  
- **SIADH nested in the soup:** hyponatraemia driven by inappropriate ADH secretion, spurred by CNS inflammation, but *without* any of the usual culprits (malignancy, drugs, classic infections).  
- **Colchicine saved the day:** the patient improved on colchicine alone, sidelining steroids and heavy immunosuppression that most neuro‑BD cases need.  

## The Diagnostic Tangle: Where to Stop, Where to Keep Going  

When you have a fever and CSF pleocytosis, the instinct is to cast a wide net: viral panels, bacterial cultures, TB nucleic acid tests, autoimmune antibodies, tumor markers, etc. The case under review shows that an *exclusion‑first*, *symptom‑driven* approach can keep the work‑up lean and smart.

### Why the “Lab Lure” is Dangerous  
- **Cost and patient‑time:** Each panel adds up; some are not reimbursable.  
- **Over‑interpretation:** Mild sIL‑2R elevations or positive ANA can mislead you toward a malignancy or connective‑tissue disease.  
- **Delays in treatment:** A protracted lab list often pushes therapeutic decisions (e.g., steroids) into the waiting room, prolonging illness.

### Step‑by‑Step Focused Work‑up  

| Step | Rationale | What We Did Here | What We Might Skip |  
|------|-----------|------------------|-------------------|  
| **1. Clinical mapping** | Nail down the constellation of symptoms. Fever, headache, focal deficits, mucocutaneous lesions? | Fever, headache, hiccups, oral aphthae, genital ulcers, follicular rash. | None – everything was relevant. |  
| **2. Basic labs and imaging** | Rule out most common bacterial causes quickly. | CBC, CMP, CRP, ESR, blood cultures, chest X‑ray, CT head. | CT head with contrast (if no focal deficit or MRI available). |  
| **3. CSF analysis** | Viral vs. bacterial vs. aseptic? | Opening pressure, WBC count, protein, glucose, cultures, HSV/VZV PCR, cryptococcal antigen. | Routine CSF glucose >50% of serum not always needed. |  
| **4. Exclusion of common infections** | HSV, VZV, TB are the usual suspects. | PCRs, acid‑fast stain, TB PCR. | Meningococcal serology if no epidemiological clue. |  
| **5. Evaluate for malignancy only if red flags** | Lymphoma or carcinoma is a rare trigger for SIADH; you need evidence of mass or smear changes. | The only abnormality was mild sIL‑2R elevation; no lymphadenopathy, no cytology abnormality, normal imaging. | Full tumor panel (CEA, CA‑125, AFP) – overkill. |  
| **6. Autoimmune serology—“when to order”** | Anti‑dsDNA, ANA, ENA, ANCA are helpful if there's a rheumatic picture or organ involvement. | All autoantibodies were negative. | Antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody panel could be omitted without missing a diagnosis in this context. |  
| **7. Endocrine work‑up for hyponatraemia** | SIADH can be secondary to CNS inflammation or other triggers. | Serum osmolality, urine osmolality, sodium, ADH levels, thyroid panel, cortisol. | Hyperglycaemic correction effect on sodium? Performed anyway. |  
| **8. Final diagnosis by exclusion** | If SLE, sarcoidosis, lymphoma, TB, bacterial/viral infections, and cancers are all ruled out, focus on clinical criteria. | HLA‑B51 positive, mucocutaneous findings, CSF pleocytosis → definite BD → acute neuro‑BD. | None. |  

> **Lesson:** Design your labs around *what can change management* rather than infinite rule‑outs.

## Interpreting the “Non‑Specific” Findings  

### sIL‑2R in the Spotlight  
- Normally a marker for lymphoma but can rise in any inflammatory state.  
- In our patient, it was 704 U/mL (slightly >500).  
- Without lymphadenopathy, abnormal cytology, or mass lesions, consider it “nuisance” rather than diagnosis.

### Hyponatraemia & SIADH  
- The CSF protein was mildly high (0.97 g/L) and there was lymphocytic pleocytosis.  
- Core SIADH criteria were met: euvolemia, low serum osmolality, high urine osmolality, high urine sodium, elevated ADH.  
- Importantly, *no pulmonary infiltrates, no malignancy, no drugs* → CNS inflammation most plausible driver.

### The Role of IL‑6 (or lack thereof)  
- The authors speculated that IL‑6 could potentiate ADH release.  
- No IL‑6 measurement in this case; the elevated CRP likely reflects the same inflammatory up‑regulation.  

## The Practical Take‑Home  

- **Focus on the red flags**: fever + CSF pleocytosis + non‑specific rash → think meningitis; only add cultures and PCRs for bacteria/virus if clinically warranted.  
- **Avoid blanket autoimmune panels** unless organ‑specific features emerge (e.g., renal, pulmonary), or if steroid response is uncertain.  
- **Interpret sIL‑2R within context**; a mild elevation alone does not mandate lymphoma work‑up.  
- **Address electrolyte disturbances early**: hyponatraemia can manifest as hiccups or rhabdomyolysis; manage with fluid restriction before jumping to hormone infusions.  
- **Colchicine as a first‑line for BD flare** can be trialed even when neuro‑BD is suspected, especially if steroids are contraindicated or delayed.  
- **Keep the work‑up dynamic**: if the patient improves dramatically with a trial of colchicine and fluid management, reassess the need for further immunosuppression or invasive tests.

## The Bottom Line  

When the lab list grows, the risk of “diagnostic inertia” rises. The case shows that a **systems‑based, exclusion‑first approach**—aligned tightly with the evolving clinical picture—can spare both patients and institutions from unnecessary tests, while still catching the critical diagnoses that dictate treatment.  

If you’re in the neurology or emergency department dealing with CSF pleocytosis and occult hyponatraemia, remember the *lab lure*: order what’s needed, stop what’s not, and always interpret each result in the clinical context.  

**Take‑home bullets:**  

- 🚀 Start with symptom‑driven labs; avoid a one‑size‑fits‑all panel.  
- 💡 Mild sIL‑2R elevations are often “noise” in an inflammatory backdrop.  
- ⚖️ Hyponatraemia management should precede aggressive steroid trials unless septic shock is present.  
- 🔧 Colchicine can be an effective first‑line for acute neuro‑Behçet’s without corticosteroids in selected patients.  
- 🧠 A focused, stepwise exclusion process saves time, money, and most importantly, accurate diagnosis.
