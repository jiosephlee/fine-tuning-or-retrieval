Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Pathophysiology] Explain how rhabdomyolysis can arise as a complication of severe hyponatraemia, and describe the clinical clues that should prompt evaluation of serum creatine kinase in patients with CNS inflammation.

**Clinical Teaching Answer**

Severe hyponatraemia, especially when it is chronic or develops rapidly, can precipitate rhabdomyolysis through a combination of osmotic stress, hypovolaemia‑induced muscle ischemia, and impaired cellular ion homeostasis. In the patient described, the markedly low plasma sodium (124 mmol/L) and low serum osmolality (267 mOsm/kg) created an osmotic gradient that drew water into muscle cells, causing cellular swelling and lysis. Concomitant stimulation of the sympathetic nervous system and cytokine release (e.g., IL‑6, CRP 180 mg/L) further taxed muscle metabolism. The resultant release of intracellular creatine kinase (CK = 1349 U/L) and transaminases reflected muscle necrosis rather than hepatic injury. Rapid correction of sodium is avoided because replacement of extracellular fluid volume superimposed on the already hypovolemic state can lead to acute renal failure and acute tubular necrosis.

**Clinical clues that raise suspicion for rhabdomyolysis in patients with CNS inflammation**

| Feature | Reason for concern |
|--------|------------------|
| **Persistent or worsening muscle pain, aches, or fatigue** | Direct muscle injury; often subtle in neuro‑inflammatory states |
| **Red‑brown urine or dark discoloration** | Hemoglobinuria/mieoglobinuria from muscle breakdown |
| **Elevated serum creatine kinase** | Gold‑standard diagnostic marker; should be checked with any unexplained myalgia or fatigue |
| **Elevated transaminases (AST > ALT)** | AST is abundant in skeletal muscle; a disproportionate rise suggests muscle injury |
| **Symptoms of systemic inflammation (fever, CRP, ESR)** | Inflammatory cytokines (IL‑6) can stimulate both ADH release (SIADH) and muscle catabolism |
| **Signs of SIADH (euvolemic hyponatraemia, low serum osmolality, high urine osmolality)** | Hyponatraemia itself is a recognized precipitant of rhabdomyolysis |
| **Neurologic complaints (headache, vertigo, hiccups)** | May be manifestations of both CNS inflammation and severe hyponatraemia; the latter can worsen neurological status and can be a red flag for muscle breakdown |
| **No obvious trauma or statin use** | Exclude other common causes of rhabdomyolysis |

In the present case, the patient’s **headache, persistent hiccups, generalized malaise, and hyponatraemia** prompted evaluation, and the laboratory workup revealed a **markedly elevated CK**. The absence of overt muscle tenderness or focal weakness is typical: muscle injury can be silent in septic or inflammatory states. These findings underscore the importance of serial CK measurements whenever hyponatraemia and CNS inflammation coexist, even when the clinical picture is dominated by neurologic symptoms.

---

**Key Take‑away**

- Severe hyponatraemia can directly cause rhabdomyolysis via osmotic and metabolic derangements; this complication is frequently overlooked in patients with CNS inflammation.
- Persistent hiccups, unexplained fatigue, or mild myalgias in the setting of hyponatraemia should prompt early serum CK assessment.
- Early detection allows prompt supportive management (volume resuscitation, monitoring of renal function, cautious sodium correction) and prevents progression to acute kidney injury.
