Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Morning Report] Describe the typical neurological manifestations of neuro‑Behçet’s disease and explain why a normal brain MRI does not exclude the diagnosis.

**Typical neuro‑Behçet’s manifestations and the rationale for a normal MRI**

1. **Clinical spectrum**  
   • The neurologic involvement of Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) is usually categorized into *parenchymal* and *vasculitic* disease.  
   • **Parenchymal neuro‑BD**—the most frequent type—presents with **meningo‑encephalitis**, fever, headache, vertigo, nausea, and “behavioural” or psychiatric changes.  Focal deficits (e.g., cranial‑nerve palsies, hemiparesis) and seizures are less common but can occur when the brainstem, basal ganglia or thalamus is affected.  
   • **Vasculitic neuro‑BD** (rare) produces stroke‑like focal deficits and hemorrhage when cerebral vessels are inflamed.  
   • In the acute setting, the hallmark is often a **self‑limited meningo‑encephalitis** with CSF pleocytosis and elevated protein, as seen in the present case (11 cells/µL, 0.97 g/L protein).  Neurologic symptoms may be nonspecific but are usually accompanied by systemic mucocutaneous features.

2. **Why a normal brain MRI does **not** exclude neuro‑BD**  
   • The “classical” parenchymal lesions of neuro‑BD are small, often focal, and classically involve the **brainstem** (midbrain, pons).  Even when present, they may be too small to be appreciated on routine imaging, especially early in the disease, or may be missed on standard‑sized scans.  
   • Acute neuro‑BD most often presents as **aseptic meningitis** without a detectable parenchymal lesion, as in our patient whose MRI was normal despite clear CSF inflammation.  The inflammatory process can be confined to the meninges, the subarachnoid space, or very small focal areas that fall below the spatial resolution of conventional MRI.  
   • Serial imaging—preferably with high‑resolution or contrast‑enhanced sequences—may reveal evolving lesions in 20–30 % of patients.  A single negative study therefore should not reassure clinicians; repeat imaging is recommended when symptoms persist or worsen.  
   • In practice, the diagnosis of acute neuro‑BD is one of **exclusion**: negative infectious, malignant, and other autoimmune work‑up, together with characteristic mucocutaneous findings (oral/genital aphthae, skin rash) and a compatible clinical picture, is sufficient to support the diagnosis even with a normal brain MRI.

**Bottom line:** In a patient with Behçet’s disease who develops fever, headache, and CSF evidence of aseptic meningitis, a normal brain MRI should not preclude the diagnosis of acute neuro‑BD.  The disease can be strictly meningeal and may evolve radiologically over time; hence, clinical judgment and repeat imaging are essential.
