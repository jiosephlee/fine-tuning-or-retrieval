Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Differential Diagnosis] Identify the clinical features that distinguish acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease from other causes of aseptic meningitis, and discuss the utility of CSF analysis in this context.

**Acute neuro‑Behçet’s disease (neuro‑BD) versus other causes of aseptic meningitis**

| Feature | Acute neuro‑BD | Non‑vesicular viral meningitis (HSV, VZV, enterovirus) | Bacterial meningitis | Tuberculous or fungal meningitis | CNS malignancy |
|---------|----------------|--------------------------------------------------------|----------------------|----------------------------------|----------------|
| **Systemic clues** | Oral/genital aphthae, folliculitis‑like rash, HLA‑B51 positivity, elevated CRP/ESR, skin/genital ulcers | Usually absent; may have flu‑like prodrome | Neutrophilic pleocytosis, high protein, low glucose | Low glucose, lymphocytic pleocytosis, often chronic | Often no systemic rash or fever |
| **CSF pattern** | Mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (≤20 cells/µL), moderate protein elevation (≈0.8‑1.0 g/L), normal glucose | Variable lymphocytic pleocytosis, normal or mildly elevated protein, normal glucose | Neutrophile‑predominant pleocytosis (>100 cells/µL), high protein, low glucose | Lymphocyte‑predominant, very high protein, low glucose | Variable; may show atypical cells, high protein |
| **MRI** | Often normal early; later shows brain‑stem or thalamic lesions in ~50% of patients | Normal in most cases; may show “discoid” lesions in HSV encephalitis | Diffuse leptomeningeal enhancement (rare), brain abscess | Mass lesions or tuberculomas (in contrast, often normal early) | Mass effect, abnormal enhancement |
| **Response to steroids** | Rapid improvement with steroids (but spontaneous clearance may occur) | Viral: usually self‑limited; steroids rarely used | Required if bacterial therapy delayed | Steroids may help after antibiotics | Steroids rarely help, unless lymphoma|
| **Other clinical clues** | Frequently associated with psychiatric symptoms, headaches, vertigo, hiccups; may be complicated by SIADH (as in the case) | Often presents with neck stiffness, photophobia | Severe headache, vomiting, lethargy, seizures | Subacute onset, constitutional symptoms, cranial neuropathies | Usually focal deficits, cranial neuropathies |

**Key distinguishing elements in the index patient**

1. **Concurrent mucocutaneous BD phenotype** – oral aphthae, genital ulcer, folliculitis‑like rash, HLA‑B51 positivity, high CRP/ESR satisfied international BD criteria (score ≥ 4).  
2. **CSF findings** – mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL) and moderate protein (0.97 g/L) with negative cultures, PCR for HSV/VZV and cryptococcal antigen. The pattern is most compatible with an inflammatory, non‑infectious process rather than bacterial (neutrophilic), tuberculous or fungal meningitis.  
3. **Normal MRI** – early in the disease, brain‑stem or other characteristic lesions can be absent, as seen here.  
4. **Clinical course** – fever and headache improved with colchicine alone, without corticosteroids, and SIADH resolved with fluid restriction, supporting an inflammatory, BD‑related etiology.

**Utility of CSF analysis**

- **Diagnostic ruling‑out**: Identifies or excludes infectious agents, malignancy, and parasitic causes that would prompt markedly different management (antimicrobials, chemotherapy, etc.).  
- **Pattern recognition**: The lymphocytic pleocytosis with moderate protein elevation is the classic “aseptic” picture; it helps narrow the differential to viral, inflammatory, or neoplastic causes. In neuro‑BD, the lymphocyte count is usually low‑to‑moderate, distinguishing it from bacterial meningitis (high neutrophils) and TB/cryptococcal meningitis (high protein/low glucose).  
- **Monitoring response**: Serial CSF protein and cell counts can track resolution (as in this patient, CSF data were not repeated, but symptom resolution mirrors csf trend).  
- **Guiding therapy**: In neuro‑BD, a normal or moderately abnormal CSF profile together with mucocutaneous disease clinches the diagnosis, allowing steroid‑sparing options such as colchicine to be trialed when appropriate.

**Bottom line**  
In patients with fever, headache, and hyponatraemia, a CSF profile of mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, moderate protein elevation, normal glucose, and a negative infectious and malignant work‑up, combined with classic mucocutaneous BD findings, points toward acute neuro‑BD rather than other causes of aseptic meningitis. CSF remains indispensable for excluding life‑threatening etiologies and for confirming the inflammatory nature of the cerebrospinal fluid.
