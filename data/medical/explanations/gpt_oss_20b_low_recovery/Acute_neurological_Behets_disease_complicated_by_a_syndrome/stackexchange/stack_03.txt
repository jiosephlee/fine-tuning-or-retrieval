Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Pathophysiology] What laboratory findings confirm the diagnosis of syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH), and how can hyponatraemia from SIADH present symptomatically?

**Answer**

**Laboratory confirmation of SIADH**  
The diagnostic criteria for SIADH are met when a patient presents with:

1. **Hyponatraemia** (serum Na⁺ < 135 mmol/L) – in this case 124 mmol/L.  
2. **Inappropriately concentrated urine**:  
   - Urine osmolality > 300 mOsm/kg (900 mOsm/kg in the report).  
   - Urine sodium > 20 mmol/L (88 mmol/L here).  
3. **Low plasma osmolality** (≤ 275 mOsm/kg) – 267 mOsm/kg.  
4. **Euvolemia** on clinical exam (no edema, stable vital signs).  
5. **Normal adrenal, thyroid, and renal function** – all within reference ranges.  
6. **Elevated plasma ADH** (or a “non‑dilutional” ADH level) – 22.4 pg/mL here, confirming that the antidiuretic hormone is not suppressed.  

These laboratory findings together satisfy the Seewald–Shwartz–Sims criteria used by the authors to diagnose SIADH.

---

**Symptomatic presentation of hyponatraemia from SIADH**  
The loss of free water relative to sodium causes a gradual shift of extracellular fluid into cells, producing a spectrum of symptoms that correlate poorly with serum sodium concentration.

- **Early, mild symptoms**: nausea, headache, fatigue, mood changes.  
- **Severe, persistent hyponatraemia** (as in this patient, with Na⁺ ≈ 124 mmol/L) can produce:  
  - **Neurologic signs**: confusion, seizures, delirium, focal deficits, and, as noted in the report, *persistent hiccups* – a well‑documented clue to severe hyponatraemia from CNS inflammation.  
  - **Muscle injury**: rhabdomyolysis can develop, evidenced by markedly elevated CK (1349 U/L) and normal renal function, a complication often overlooked but recognized in profound hyponatraemia.  
  - **Other systems**: nausea, vomiting, fatigue, and in extreme cases pulmonary edema from cerebral edema.

Thus, in a patient with neuro‑Behçet’s disease presenting with fever, headache, and mucocutaneous findings, the co‑existent hyponatraemia may manifest subtly (headache, fatigue) or dramatically (hiccups, seizures, rhabdomyolysis). Early recognition of these features, coupled with the laboratory triad above, allows prompt diagnosis and safe management (fluid restriction, careful sodium monitoring) as was successfully employed in this case.
