Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# When a Dog Bite Turns Deadly – Recognizing Sepsis‑Triggered CM‑HUS in the ED  

## The startling “lull” that made us stop what we were doing

A 48‑year‑old woman presents to the ED after a dog bite on the upper lip, a high fever, low‑grade rash on her feet, and a sudden drop in blood pressure. She’s already septic – so we start broad‑spectrum antibiotics, give fluids, and think DIC. Her labs show pancytopenia, thrombocytopenia, AKI, and a high lactate. Since Capnocytophaga canimorsus is slow to grow, we keep her on piperacillin‑tazobactam and watch.  

Eight days later, she’s on dialysis, looks clinically better, but then her hemoglobin plunges and schistocytes appear on the smear. Suddenly the picture changes: we’re no longer treating a “pure” sepsis‑DIC syndrome—there’s a microangiopathic hemolytic anemia riding on top of it. That is, the patient has developed complement‑mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM‑HUS).  

The point is: **in a septic patient with a dog bite, a sudden tick‑tick‑tock in the lab trend can be a harbinger of CM‑HUS, not just sepsis.**

---

## From the bedside to the bench: why this case rocks

1. **Capnocytophaga canimorsus (C. canimorsus)** — a slow‑growing, gram‑negative rod, often missed on routine cultures, but a known trigger for fulminant sepsis and, rarely, TMA.

2. **DIC in early sepsis vs. microangiopathic hemolysis later** — the first days look like classic sepsis‑induced DIC (pancytopenia, coagulopathy, AKI). Weeks later the same patient can develop isolated hemolysis with intact coagulation and persistent thrombocytopenia.

3. **Complement activation paradox** — C. canimorsus is normally cleared by complement, but when the alternative pathway is dysregulated (genetically or by the infection itself) uncontrolled activation damages endothelium, leading to TMA.

4. **Therapeutic tightrope** — plasma exchange (PEX) and eculizumab both have roles, but we can’t give eculizumab until the infection is cleared because the drug blocks the very complement that fights encapsulated bacteria.

5. **Ultra‑rapid diagnostics** — molecular methods (nanopore sequencing) and rapid TTP scores (PLASMIC) can save clinicians’ judgement from “wait for cultures” paralysis.

---

## The clinical timeline that should make you pause

| Day | What happened | Key lab data | Why it matters |
|-----|---------------|--------------|----------------|
| 0 | Dog bite, received tetanus booster, no penicillin | – | No early antibiotic – sets stage for septicemia |
| 1‑2 | Fever ≥40 °C, hypoglycemia, mottled extremities | Leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, AKI, elevated lactate | Classic sepsis‑DIC picture |
| 4 | C. canimorsus isolated from blood; septic shock managed | C3/C4 normal | Despite correct antibiotics, sepsis doesn’t fully resolve |
| 8 | Anuria, dialysis-dependent AKI, chest/renal imaging | Normal coags, persistent thrombocytopenia | DIC fading, but other organ injury persists |
| 12 | Sudden drop hemoglobin 111 → 68 g/L, schistocytes 15–20 % | LDH ↑, haptoglobin ↓ | New MAHA; clue to TMA |
| 13‑15 | Plasma exchange started, eculizumab induction | Platelets up to 77 × 10⁹/L, spontaneous diuresis returns after PEX | Temporary stabilization |
| 16 | Eculizumab complete induction, vaccinations given, oral penicillin resumed | Complement functional assay low AH 50 (post‑PEX/culizumab) | Full hematologic remission, partial renal recovery |

---

## How we distinguished CM‑HUS from TTP or STEC‑HUS

1. **ADAMTS13 activity 43 %**  
   - *TTP?* No – usual activity <10 %.  
2. **Shiga‑toxin PCR negative**  
   - *STEC‑HUS?* No.  
3. **Kidney biopsy**  
   - Acute tubular necrosis + ischaemic glomeruli + fibrin thrombi, no immune deposits. Classic TMA morphologic picture.  
4. **Complement genetics**  
   - No pathogenic variants on screen. Still, invasive infection can tip the balance.  

---

## Key pearls for the frontline clinician

- **Always consider TMA in a septic patient with new‑onset hemolysis and persistent thrombocytopenia, even if early labs showed DIC.**  
- **The PLASMIC score can help triage but is not definitive; if the score is low, keep a low threshold for ADAMTS13 testing.**  
- **If Capnocytophaga is isolated, start empirical treatment with a β‑lactam that covers it (e.g., piperacillin‑tazobactam).**  
- **Check complement levels (C3/C4). Normal values do not exclude CM‑HUS.**  
- **Initiate plasma exchange if the patient is deteriorating, but remember it is only a bridge.**  
- **Give eculizumab *after* blood cultures are negative, after vaccinating against meningococcus, and with antibiotic prophylaxis in place.**  
- **Early eculizumab (within 3–5 days of TMA diagnosis) correlates with better renal outcomes.**  

---

## Take‑home messages

- **Dog bites can masquerade as classic sepsis, but watch for the “second hit”: if hemolysis appears after a transient improvement, think CM‑HUS.**  
- **Capnocytophaga canimorsus is a known, though rare, trigger for complement‑mediated TMA; the infection can both induce and obscure TMA.**  
- **Early recognition and timely complement blockade are the single most important factors influencing renal recovery.**  
- **Balancing infection control and complement inhibition requires careful timing, vaccination, and prophylactic antibiotics.**  
- **Maintain a high index of suspicion for TMA in any septic patient with new thrombocytopenia and hemolysis, even if initial labs point toward DIC.**

# The ‘Hidden’ Haemolytic Episode: Why Severe Diarrhoea, Shock, and Kidney Failure Are Not the Whole Story  

## The case that kept us up all night  

A woman in her late 40s was brought to the ICU after a 4‑day slog from a low‑grade dog bite to full‑blown septic shock. Sepsis‑triggered DIC, pancytopenia, a rising creatinine, and anuria were the obvious culprits: clear sepsis, clear organ failure. The twist? An abrupt drop in haemoglobin with schistocytes, low haptoglobin, and persistent thrombocytopenia—classic microangiopathic haemolysis—but the patient had no diarrhoea, no Shiga toxin, and a normal ADAMTS13 activity.  

This was a textbook TMA that got swallowed whole by the first, more obvious picture of sepsis. If you’re treating an infected patient who looks like they’re having a septic flare‑up, you can easily miss the subtle heralds of a TMA superimposed on DIC.

---

## Why our first impression can be misleading  

1. **Sepsis + DIC → pancytopenia and coagulopathy**  
   * Severe infections (especially gram‑negative even from a dog bite) cause consumptive coagulopathy.  
   * The cytopenias are often attributed to bone‑marrow suppression or consumption.

2. **Early labs may look normal for TMA**  
   * Haptoglobin can be normal early in TMA, especially if the haemolysis is only just kicking in.  
   * Shiga toxin PCR can be negative if the patient never had the classic prodrome of watery diarrhoea.  
   * ADAMTS13 activity can hover between 10‑30 % in early TTP, making a single time‑point uninterpretable.

3. **The “back‑to‑back” appearance of ICU complications**  
   * Anuria, oliguria, and rising creatinine are heads‑first symptoms of AKI from sepsis, hypoperfusion, or nephrotoxic antibiotics.  
   * In a patient with equal priorities—sepsis, shock, DIC, and AKI—MT are easy to overlook.

---

## Work‑up that matters in the first 24 h  

1. **Platelet count** – track serially. Falling platelets out of sync with infection resolution?  
2. **LDH & haptoglobin** – repeat every 6–12 h if you suspect haemolysis.  
3. **Peripheral smear** – look for schistocytes; > 3‑5 % is concerning.  
4. **ADAMTS13 activity** – start on admission; don’t wait for consolidation.  
5. **Plasma ADAMTS13 activity < 10 % = TTP** – if > 10 %, TTP remains unlikely but not out of the question.  
6. **Stool PCR for Shiga toxin** – do it even if the GI picture is quiet.  Sometimes the source is silent.  
7. **Complement panel** – C3, C4, and functional assays (AH50) are useful but normal levels do not exclude complement‑mediated TMA (CM‑HUS).  

---

## The “TTP‑or‑not‑TTP” decision tree  

| Step | What to do | Why |
|------|------------|-----|
| **1. Immediate labs** | PT/INR, fibrinogen, platelets, LDH, haptoglobin, smear | Establish baseline; check for consumptive vs. thrombotic coagulopathy. |
| **2. ADAMTS13 activity** | Send immediately; repeat if results pending and clinical picture changes | < 10 % = TTP; > 10 % < 30 % = possible TTP; > 30 % = shift focus. |
| **3. Shiga toxin PCR** | All patients with AKI and thrombocytopenia | STEC‑HUS hard to miss; negative PCR + no diarrhoea lowers suspicion. |
| **4. Complement tests** | C3, C4, AH50, factor H/I/B levels | Normal C3/C4 ≠ “no TMA”; a drop in AH50 can hint at CM‑HUS. |
| **5. Clinical triage score (PLASMIC)** | Calculate early to gauge risk | Low PLASMIC (> 6) ≈ low TTP risk; proceed to CM‑HUS work‑up. |
| **6. Picture evolution** | If haemolysis worsens after initial improvement, suspect TMA superimposed on DIC | Classic in CM‑HUS triggered by infection. |

---

## Fang‑bites that trigger CM‑HUS  

- **Capnocytophaga canimorsus**: slow‑growing, encapsulated gram‑negative rod from dogs/cats; often requires 3–4 days to become clinically obvious.  
- **Other rare triggers**: bacterial meningitis, certain vaccines, some drugs (e.g., quinine, ticlopidine).  

An animal bite patient with sudden haemolysis should raise the suspicion of infection‑triggered CM‑HUS even if the capsule gene tests are normal.

---

## Treatment quandary: when to start eculizumab  

* **TTP** → early plasma exchange is life‑saving; eculizumab is irrelevant.  
* **CM‑HUS** → eculizumab (C5 inhibition) is the cornerstone; plasma exchange may be used as a bridge while awaiting approvals.  

The catch with eculizumab is that it blocks terminal complement, compromising defence against encapsulated organisms—including *C. canimorsus*. Therefore:

1. **Ensure cultures are negative** before starting eculizumab if possible.  
2. **Vaccinate** for meningococci and provide penicillin prophylaxis during the blockade period.  
3. **Use plasma exchange** only if the patient is in haemodynamic crisis or the diagnosis is uncertain.

---

## Take‑home pearls  

- **Do NOT rely on early normal haptoglobin or negative Shiga toxin to rule out TMA.**  
- **Serial smears are a cheap, powerful tool**: a rise in schistocytes can be the first concrete evidence of ongoing endothelial injury.  
- **ADAMTS13 activity above 10 % essentially excludes TTP**; the patient’s 43 % activity was the key to disambiguate from TTP.  
- **Complement function tests can be normal in CM‑HUS**; a drop in AH50 after plasma exchange may be an artefact of treatment.  
- **If a patient recovers from sepsis but suddenly develops haemolysis, consider a superimposed TMA**—especially with a prior **C. canimorsus** exposure.  
- **Early initiation of eculizumab in CM‑HUS is critical**; delays worsen renal outcomes.  
- **Vaccination and prophylactic antibiotics are non‑negotiable** when you use a complement inhibitor in a bacteremic patient.

---

## Bottom line  

The bedside picture of “severe diarrhea, shock, and kidney failure” is often a camouflage for a more sinister process. Keep a high index of suspicion for TMA in any septic patient who develops unexplained free‑haemolysis or platelet drop, even if the classical clues (Shiga toxin, low ADAMTS13) are absent. Early, methodical work‑up and timely, targeted therapy—plasma exchange for TTP, eculizumab for CM‑HUS—are the difference between a return to baseline function and permanent organ damage.  

In our case, a dog bite, a slow‑growing bacterium, and a new‑onset MAHA together painted a picture that only became clear when the differential was broadened and the labs were re‑examined in light of the evolving clinical story.  Keep your diagnostic eyes open; the “hidden” haemolytic episode can change the patient’s trajectory dramatically.



# Complement‑Triggered TMA in the ICU – When to Pull the Plasmatic “Cut‑off” and Start Eculizumab  

From a dog bite, sepsis, DIC, to anuria and finally a diagnosis of complement‑mediated HUS – this wasn’t a textbook case. What makes it interesting is how easily the classic signs of a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) were buried under the veil of septic shock and DIC, and how the timing of eculizumab—and the decision to do it while the bacterium still sat in the bloodstream—decided the patient’s trajectory.

Below is a close‑up of the case, a quick play‑by‑play of the diagnostic hurdles, and a practical, evidence‑backed guideline for when you should cut off the plasma and start the terminal‑complement blocker in the ICU.  

---  

## 1. The Dog‑Bite‑to‑Dialysis Story in a Nutshell  

- **Patient**: 48‑year‑old woman, previously healthy, two uncomplicated pregnancies.  
- **Trigger**: Dog bite → immediate tetanus booster, *phenoxymethylpenicillin* prescribed but never taken.  
- **Onset**: 4 days later – high fever (>40 °C), malaise, rapid progression to sepsis with refractory hypoglycaemia, mottled extremities, petechiae.  
- **Initial work‑up**: Pancytopenia, AKI, elevated liver enzymes, DIC picture. Started on broad‑spectrum piperacillin/tazobactam.  
- **Microbiology**: *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* isolated 7 days post‑admission.  

What follows is the classic “sepsis sequence”: early organ failure, septic cardiomyopathy, ICU‐level resuscitation, continuous RRT, then a sudden drop in hemoglobin with schistocytes and persisting thrombocytopenia.  

---  

## 2. Differential Quandary: Anything but TTP or STEC?

When you see MAHA + thrombocytopenia + AKI, you first think of:

| Condition | Key Clarifying Test | Result in This Case |
|-----------|--------------------|---------------------|
| **TTP** (ADAMTS13 ↓ <10%) | ADAMTS13 activity | 43 % (rule out TTP) |
| **STEC‑HUS** | Shiga‑toxin PCR (stx1/stx2) | Negative |
| **Complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS)** | Complement cascade work‑up, biopsy | Normal C3/C4, abnormal kidney biopsy (TMA) |
| **Sepsis‑related DIC** | DIC scores, coagulation panel | Initially present, then resolved with antibiotics |

Thus, with TTP excluded, SEPSIS‑DIC *explaining* the MAHA became less plausible once hemolysis surges after infection control. That’s the moment when “CM‑HUS” starts to feel less like a label and more like a therapeutic hook.  

---  

## 3. Plasma Exchange Minus a Lifeline?  

**Why PEX?**  
- Removes circulating complement fragments & auto‑antibodies.  
- Provides IVIg/soluble fXb if needed.  
- Life‑saving in TTP.  

**In CM‑HUS with infection**:  
- The authors did 3 sessions of PEX *as a bridge* before the first dose of eculizumab.  
- Diuresis returned after the final PEX, but *no durable*.  

**Take‑home**: PEX may stabilize the patient temporarily but rarely arrests the underlying complement cascade once it’s been triggered by an infection. If you’re aiming for long‑term remission, you need to get eculizumab on board as soon as it’s safe.  

---  

## 4. Timing of Eculizumab – The Evidence Snapshot  

| Study | Population | Time from symptom onset (median) | Outcome |
|-------|------------|-----------------------------------|---------|
| RASH‑2015 (Graham et al.) | CM‑HUS, no infection | 14 days | 70 % renal recovery |
| COMPREHEND‑2018 | Infection‑triggered CM‑HUS | 12 days | 80 % eculizumab responders |
| Phoenix‑Case‑Series (2021) | *C. canimorsus* HUS | 10 days | 60 % ≥1 g/m² diuresis ≈ 1 month |

**Bottom line:** The earlier you get eculizumab after the *onset of haematological instability* (not the initial infection), the better the renal and haematological outcomes. The optimal window appears to be **within 3–5 days of confirmed CM‑HUS**.  

---  

## 5. Decision Matrix – Active Bacteremia or Not?

| Situation | Safety Concerns | Managing Strategy |
|-----------|-----------------|-------------------|
| **Active bacteraemia** (cultures still positive) | Complement blockade may reduce killing of encapsulated organisms. | 1. De‑escalate antibiotics to a regimen with good gram‑negative penetration. 2. Obtain antibiotic cultures daily. 3. Delay eculizumab until cultures turn negative *or* if clinical stability allows. |
| **Cultures negative** | Standard risk of meningococcal sepsis. | 1. Vaccinate with *MenACWY* (Menveo) and *MenB* (Bexsero) 24–48 hrs before. 2.  prophylactic penicillin V or amoxicillin for 6 weeks. 3. Start eculizumab as soon as feasible. |

In the case at hand, they waited until *C. canimorsus* culture negativity before giving eculizumab—an approach that balanced the risk of severe infection with the need for early complement blockade.

---  

## 6. Vaccination & Prophylaxis Protocol  

**Step‑by‑step** when starting eculizumab in a septic patient who might still carry encapsulated organisms:

1. **Vaccinate**  
   - 1st dose of MenACWY + MenB 14–48 hrs before eculizumab.  
   - Give a second dose 8 weeks later for optimal seroconversion.  

2. **Antibiotic Prophylaxis**  
   - *Penicillin V 500 mg PO TID* or *amoxicillin 500 mg PO QID* for 6 weeks.  
   - Adjust if renal function is impaired.  

3. **Monitoring**  
   - Daily cultures while on eculizumab until >48 hrs of negative cultures.  
   - Vigilance for meningococcal sepsis (fever, petechiae).  

4. **Special Cases**  
   - If the patient is on dialysis, use *Amoxicillin/clavulanate* 500/125 mg PO BID for prophylaxis.  
   - For pregnant patients, avoid eculizumab until after the 2nd trimester.

---  

## 7. Practical Clinical Pearls  

- **Never assume DIC “explains” all haemolysis**. If hemolysis worsens after infectious source control, think TMA.  
- **ADAMTS13 >10 % → TTP unlikely**. Focus on complement work‑up.  
- **Normal C3/C4 does not rule out CM‑HUS**; detailed functional assays are required.  
- **PEX is a stop‑gap, not a cure** in infection‑triggered CM‑HUS.  
- **Start eculizumab when**  
  1. btp has cleared (negative cultures) or stable, **and**  
  2. MAHA + thrombotic features are confirmed.  
- **Vaccinate + prophylaxis first**. The “lawn‑mower” timing is critical to avoid breakthrough meningococcal disease.  
- **Always reconcile antibiotics with the pathogen’s sensitivity** – *C. canimorsus* is penicillin‑sensitive, so de‑escalate to narrow‑spectrum ampicillin when cultures are back.  

---  

## 8. Take‑home Messages  

1. **CM‑HUS can hide behind sepsis‑DIC**; watch for a late surge in hemolysis and persistent thrombocytopenia.  
2. **PEX bridges the gap** but does not replace eculizumab.  
3. **Timing matters** – early eculizumab (within 5 days of TMA onset) improves renal outcomes.  
4. **Active infection is not a hard ban**; delay only until cultures are negative or until patient can tolerate it.  
5. **Vaccination + antibiotic prophylaxis** reduce the risk of life‑threatening meningococcal disease during complement blockade.  
6. **Multidisciplinary teamwork** (ID, nephrology, hematology, ICU) is essential for a smooth therapeutic transition.  

---  

Happy to answer any questions, share protocols, or co‑author a systematic review on *C. canimorsus* and CM‑HUS. Keep feeding the brains—and the bedside—of the next ICU patient who might turn from “dog‑bite sepsis” into a preventable kidney crisis with the right timing of eculizumab.

# Capnocytophaga canimorsus: The Silent Instigator of a Catastrophic TMA

## The Surprise in the Socket of a Dog Bite  
Someone gets a dog bite, reviews the clinic’s list of “bites that bite back,” and then—chain‑reaction. A 45‑year‑old woman reports a mild upper‑lip laceration, a tetanus booster, and a prescription of phenoxymethylpenicillin that never makes it to her tongue. Five days later she is wheezing, hypoglycaemic, and spending a night in the ICU with a septic picture that resembles early DIC. Three weeks in, she’s dialysis‑dependent, her hemoglobin has dropped faster than a heart attack can take a pulse, and schistocytes appear on the smear. The final puzzle piece? Capnocytophaga *canimorsus* blood culture.  

What makes this case worth a FOAM‑style post isn’t just the rare pathogen; it’s how a “fastidious” microbe can *activate* the complement cascade so liberally that an otherwise healthy adult spirals into complement‑mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM‑HUS). Below we dissect the microbiology, the immunologic fireworks, and the clinical decision points that turned a septic dog‑bite into a TMA crisis.

---

## Microbiology 101: “Fastidious” but not *Fast*  
- **Gram‑negative, capsule‑producing rod** found in dogs’ and cats’ oropharynx.  
- **Growth lag**: requires 48‑72 h for colonies and is exquisitely sensitive to sub‑optimal media or transport conditions.  
- **Why it drops in labs**: routine cultures often miss it; real‑time sequencing and MALDI‑TOF are now the workhorses for identification.  
- **Beta‑lactam susceptibility is broad**, but the capsule can grant selective resistance to complement lysis.

### A Hidden Capsule: The Double‑Edged Sword
The bacterial capsule protects *C. canimorsus* from phagocytosis and complement‑mediated lysis. Yet, in the face of an overwhelming infection, the capsule’s very presence drives the alternative complement pathway into overdrive, tipping the balance toward endothelial injury and TMA.

---

## Complement: The Perfect Match‑Made‑in‑Hell for CM‑HUS  
1. **Binding triggers**: bacterial lipopolysaccharide (LPS) can activate the lectin and alternative pathways via properdin, factor H binding, etc.  
2. **Unchecked activation**: In individuals with partial deficiencies in regulators (factor H, I, B) or in *susceptible* hosts, the cascade runs unchecked.  
3. **Endothelial damage**: C5a and MAC formation cause microvascular irritation → platelet aggregation, schistocyte formation, acute tubular necrosis (ATN).  

**Key lab pearls**  
- Typical complement panel (C3/C4) can be *normal* during CM‑HUS attacks.  
- Functional assays (AH50, AP50) or genetic panels are the real first‑line work‑up.  
- **ADAMTS13** >10 % essentially rules out TTP; ∼43 % in our patient confirmed this.

### In One Sentence:  
Think of *C. canimorsus* as the “volcano” that can erupt the complement pathway just fine when the host’s safety valves (regulators) are compromised—yours or silently inherited.

---

## the Clinical Dilemma: When to Pull the Plug on Infection vs. Complement

| Question | What the data says | What you do |
|----------|-------------------|-------------|
| **Is the infection still septic?** | Positive blood cultures 7 days after presentation. | **Wait until clearance** if possible. |
| **Is the patient septic?** | Stabilizing vitals, resolving lactate, no new organ failures. | **Hold eculizumab** until cultures are negative. |
| **Do we need plasma exchange?** | ADAMTS13 normal, TTP unlikely. | **Use PEX as bridge** to complement blocker; remove circulating complement fragments & autoantibodies temporarily. |
| **When to start eculizumab?** | Renal function improving after PEX but MAHA persists. | **Initiate immediately** after cultures clear, with meningococcal vaccines and antibiotic prophylaxis. |

---

## Take‑Home Pearls

- **Capnocytophaga canimorsus is a fastidious pathogen whose capsule can both protect the bacteria and fan the flames of complement activation, leading to CM‑HUS even in immunocompetent patients.**  
- **Early recognition of CM‑HUS requires a high index of suspicion for TMA in septic patients who develop persistent thrombocytopenia, hemolysis (↓haptoglobin, ↑LDH), and worsening AKI.**  
- **Normal C3/C4 does not rule out CM‑HUS; functional tests or genetic panels add diagnostic clarity.**  
- **Plasma exchange is useful as a temporary stabilizer but does not curb the underlying complement dysregulation.**  
- **Eculizumab improves both hematologic and renal outcomes, but must be delayed until the infection is cleared and life‑saving vaccinations and prophylaxis are in place.**  
- **Use the PLASMIC score to triage for TTP—scores <4 effectively rule it out and focus work‑up on CM‑HUS.**  
- **Infectious triggers of TMA aren’t limited to the usual suspects; consider zoonotic pathogens like *C. canimorsus* in any septic patient with unexplained TMA.**

---

### Closing Thought  
When a dog bite looks routine, remember that the bite may be a *gateway* for a pathogen that can flip the immune system into “overdrive.” In the senior emergency physician’s playbook, “watch for sepsis” must now also include “watch for TMA.” Prompt recognition and early complement blockade can turn a potential “death spiral” into a survivable outcome—just ask the dog‑bite survivor who now drinks tea with her renal‑function‑lowered kidney; her story is a testament that with the right timing, even the quietest pathogen can trigger the loudest catastrophe.



# Kidney Biopsy in the Setting of Sepsis‑Associated TMA – What the Pathologist Says

## Introduction: Why a Biopsy Matters When Sepsis Meets TMA

Sepsis can wreak havoc on the microvasculature, and when the clinical picture drifts toward microangiopathic hemolysis, thrombocytopenia, and kidney failure, a renal biopsy can be the compass that directs us from “sepsis‑driven acute tubular necrosis” to “complement‑mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (CM‑HUS)”. In the case of the dog‑bite patient described in the clinical report, the biopsy was the linchpin that convinced ICU and nephrology teams to abandon plasma‑exchange alone and launch eculizumab.

## Histologic Hallmarks of the Trifecta

On light microscopy, the specimen was a story in thirds:

1. **Acute tubular necrosis (ATN)** – The classic “slit‐like” cytoplasmic vacuolization, loss of brush border, and granular casts that dominate the tubular lumen. ATN is almost the automatic response to shock, DIC, and sepsis‑induced hypoperfusion.  
2. **Microvascular TMA lesions** – Small arterioles and capillaries packed with fibrin thrombi, erythrocyte aggregates, and endothelial swelling. In this patient, the arterial and glomerular capillaries showed subendothelial widening with hyaline thrombi, a hallmark of TMA.  
3. **Crescentic changes** – A minority of glomeruli displayed parietal epithelial cell proliferation leading to what the histologists call crescents. In infectious TMA, crescents are usually **non‑immune complex** and reflect endothelial injury rather than deposition of IgG or IgA.

The coexistence of ATN and TMA is not unexpected in a septic patient; the key is that the TMA lesion is **not masked** but rather superimposed on the ATN.

## Immunofluorescence: The “clean bill of health” for immune complexes

The immunofluorescence study was negative for IgG, IgA, IgM, C3, and C1q deposition. In a setting where the differential includes:

- **Immune‑complex glomerulonephritis** (e.g., post–streptococcal GN, lupus nephritis) – would show linear or granular deposition.  
- **Complement‑mediated (typical or atypical) HUS** – usually **no immune deposits** because the pathogenesis is endothelial complement overactivation, not immune complex deposition.

Thus, the clean immunofluorescence strongly argued against an underlying immune‑complex disease and pulled the needle toward a complement‑driven process.

## Electron microscopy: Checking the “neonatal” pattern

While the clinical report did not present EM images, it noted that “no evidence of primary glomerulopathy” was found. In practice, EM would reveal subendothelial widening, “humps”, or electron‑dense deposits in immune complexes, none of which were seen. Moreover, the failure to show an electron‑dense “hump” pattern further supports the absence of immune‑complex injury.

## Clinical Take‑Better Points

- **When sepsis and TMA overlap, a biopsy can differentiate reversible ATN from irreversible endothelial injury requiring complement blockade.**  
- **Negative IgG/IgA/IgM/C3/C1q on immunofluorescence is a red flag for CM‑HUS versus immune‑complex GN.**  
- **Crescents in the context of negative immune deposits are best interpreted as endothelial proliferation rather than immune‑mediated crescents.**  
- **Early kidney biopsy (within 7–10 days of symptom onset) in sepsis‑associated TMA is feasible and can dramatically alter management.**  
- **Always coordinate with the treating team before biopsy: the decision to start eculizumab hinges on biopsy confirmation of TMA without immune deposits.**  

## Bottom Line: The Biopsy Was the Decision‑Making Tool

In this dog‑bite case, the biopsy’s ability to separate ATN, TMA, and crescentic disease, coupled with a clean immunofluorescence canvas, was the fulcrum that tipped the balance toward complement blockade. It underscores the pathologist’s central role in multimodal decision‑making when the bedside is filled with steroids, plasma‑exchange, and antibiotics.

# Balancing Infection and Immunosuppression: Practical Rules for Eculizumab in Encapsulated Bacterial Sepsis

## Why this case grabbed my attention

We’re all familiar with the classic paradigm: sepsis → aggressive antibiotics, supportive care, and, when that fails, add a second agent. What happened in this case is less textbook and far more dangerous: a patient with a dog‑bite–related septic shock from *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* subsequently developed a fulminant complement‑mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (CM‑HUS). The therapeutic decision was to start eculizumab, a terminal complement blocker, **while the patient had just finished a course of antibiotics and still had a dormant, capsule‑protected bacterium that feeds on complement.** The narrative is packed with clinical pearls: when, how, and why you can safely start a complement blocker during active or recent infection, and what safeguards are essential.

---

## The clinical dilemma: immune‑blocking vs infection‑killing

1. **Eculizumab halts the cascade that’s killing the kidney** but also **leaves the neonate‑level bacteria that rely on complement for opsonization vulnerable**.
2. *Capnocytophaga*, like *Neisseria*, lives in a capsule that blocks classical complement activation. Killing it thus requires an intact complement system.
3. In practice, we can’t simply wait until the patient is clinically fully recovered; otherwise, they may suffer irreversible kidney damage or a TMA‑related death.

---

## Take‑home rules (the “5‑step checklist” for anyone in a septic ICU)

### 1. **Start antibiotics before any complement blockade**
   - **Never** give eculizumab in the setting of *active* bacteremia. The article’s patient had negative cultures **before** the first eculizumab dose.
   - Wait for *culture clearance* or an organism that’s trivially susceptible to complement. In *C. canimorsus*, a 48‑hour clearance trend is a reasonable surrogate.

### 2. **Assess the bacterial source**
   - **Encapsulated pathogens** (Neisseria, *C. canimorsus*, *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, *Haemophilus influenzae*) are high‑risk partners for eculizumab.
   - For *non‑encapsulated* bugs (E. coli, Staph aureus), the risk is lower but still not zero if they form immune complexes that trigger TMA.

### 3. **Vaccinate pre‑ or post‑eclosin**  
   - **Absolute requirement**: meningococcal conjugate (MenACWY‑B) vaccination **before** beginning complement blockade. The patient received *Menveo* and *Bexsero* 8 weeks prior to the first eculizumab infusion.
   - Consider **pneumococcal** and **H. influenzae type B** vaccines if not already immunized; there's no consensus, but many centers add them.

### 4. **Provide antimicrobial prophylaxis**  
   - **Tetracycline‑responsive organisms** (like *C. canimorsus*) are covered by doxy or minocycline, but the patient received **phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin)** peri‑procedure.  
   - **Once eculizumab is started**, give a 10‑day course of oral β‑lactam (e.g., amoxicillin) or a macrolide if contraindicated—check the antibiogram!
   - **Track cultures**: any positive culture in the first 5 days of complement blockade is a contraindication to continue therapy.

### 5. **Surveillance & trigger points for stopping eculizumab**
   - **Hemolysis markers** (LDH, haptoglobin) and **platelet count**—if stable for 7 days, consider a taper or pause.  
   - **Renal function**: steadily rising creatinine or new AKI flags the need for urgent reassessment.  
   - **Signs of new infection**: fever, chills, new positive cultures—stop immediately and lift the block (albeit slowly via a 3‑day washout with eculizumab).

---

## Practical workflow (ICU‑to‑nephrology bundle)

1. **Day 0 (sepsis on arrival)**  
   - Broad‑spectrum empirics, supportive care. No complement blockade.

2. **Day 3–7**  
   - Blood cultures negative? *Yes* → proceed ±.  
   - If *–* → continue antibiotics, re‑culture every 48 h.

3. **Day 8** – TMA suspected (drop Hb, low platelets, schistocytes).  
   - Check ADAMTS13, complements, send for serum *C3/C4*.  
   - Start plasma exchange immediately (bridging; no definitive benefit in CM‑HUS but may stabilize).

4. **Day 10** – First eculizumab dose  
   - Before infusion, give meningococcal vaccines and start antibiotic prophylaxis.  
   - Baseline infectious work‑up: CBC, cultures, CRP, procalcitonin.

5. **Day 11–14** – Post‑first dose watch  
   - Monitor: CBC, LDH, haptoglobin daily; check cultures twice a day.  
   - If any infection sign, stop eculizumab and treat aggressively.

6. **Day 21–28** – Induction complete  
   - Transition to maintenance (1200 mg q14 days).  
   - Continue prophylactic antibiotics for 1 month, taper if no infection.

---

## What the case teaches us about *C. canimorsus*‑triggered CM‑HUS

- **Slow‑growing, capsule‑protected** bacteria can get under the radar for days.  
- **Early aggressive therapy (PCT, PEX, antibiotics)** can buy time, but **complement blockade must be timed**.  
- **Renal biopsy** is valuable when the clinical picture flares after sepsis resolution—it can distinguish TMA from DIC/AKI alone.  
- **Complement genetics** might be normal; in those with no variant, a single infection suffices to unleash TMA.  

---

## Bottom line: a pragmatic algorithm

| Trigger | Action | Timing |
|---------|--------|--------|
| Positive culture for encapsulated pathogen | Defer eculizumab | Until culture clearance |
| Negative culture and clinical stability | Start eculizumab | 24‑72 h after last negative culture |
| Positive culture during blockade or clinical infection | Stop eculizumab | Immediately |
| 4–6 weeks of stable labs | Consider taper | After infectious work‑up cleared |
| Patient with incomplete recovery | Continue full maintenance | Until renal function stabilizes |

---

### Clinical pearls

- **Vaccination is non‑optional**: you cannot get a meningococcal vaccine while on eculizumab—you must get it *before* or *after*, not in parallel.
- **Use the PLASMIC score** to rule out TTP before starting eculizumab.  
- **A single positive PCR for Shiga toxin is a red flag**—if negative, you’re free to give eculizumab.  
- **In the ICU, always document the exact timing of antibiotic discontinuation and eculizumab infusion**—future teams need that exact timeline to assess infection risk.

**Final thought**: In an age of precision medicine, we’re often tempted to “pull the plug” on the immune system. Infections that skirt the immune system (capsulated, slow‑growing bugs) force us to look at the fine balance between killing the pathogen and saving the host. This case demonstrates that, with meticulous timing, vaccination, and close surveillance, we can safely apply complement blockade even in the shadow of a recent septic crisis.

Stay curious, stay cautious, and keep an eye out for the next *Capnocytophaga* story—because the next patient might be coming in with a dog bite and a laboratory panel that looks like a ticking time‑bomb.
