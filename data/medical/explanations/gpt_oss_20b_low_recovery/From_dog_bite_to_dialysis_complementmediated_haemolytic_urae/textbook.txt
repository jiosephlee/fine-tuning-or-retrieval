Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

## 1. Clinical Presentation and Initial Differential

A previously healthy woman in her late forties was admitted to the emergency department four days after a dog‑bite on her upper lip. The bite itself had not been promptly treated with antibiotics; she had only received a tetanus booster and was prescribed phenoxymethylpenicillin that day, but did not obtain the medication. She developed a high fever (>40 °C), malaise and progressive malaise, later reporting numbness and discoloration of her feet. At a primary‑care urgent visit she was found hypoglycaemic (0.6 mmol/L) and unresponsive to oral glucose or glucagon, prompting transfer to the tertiary centre.

### Early septic shock and metabolic derangement
On arrival, the patient was alert but febrile and hypotensive with a rapid heart rate, although vasopressor support was not yet required. Physical examination revealed mottling and coolness of the lower extremities, suggestive of peripheral hypoperfusion. Arterial blood gas demonstrated a metabolic acidosis (pH 7.27) with a markedly elevated lactate of 5.7 mmol/L, consistent with tissue hypoxia. She remained hypoglycaemic despite high‑dose intravenous glucose, a recognised manifestation of severe sepsis.

### Laboratory profile on admission
The routine blood tests showed a striking triad of abnormalities that quickly defined the patient’s clinical compartmentalisation:

| Parameter | Result | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| White cell count | Marked leukopenia (low WBC count) | Sepsis‑induced bone marrow suppression or consumption |
| Platelets | Severely reduced | Consistent with consumptive coagulopathy |
| Hemoglobin | Moderate | No significant drop at this stage |
| CREATINE | Elevated | Early acute kidney injury |
| Transaminases | Mild to moderate elevation | Hepatic involvement |
| Coagulation panel | Prolonged PT/aPTT, low fibrinogen, elevated D‑dimer | Diagnostic of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) |
| Urinalysis | +2 protein, +3 blood | Renal involvement |

The combination of pancytopenia, coagulopathy, renal impairment, hepatic enzyme elevation and lactic acidosis formed the basis for an initial working diagnosis of **sepsis‑associated DIC**.

### Differential considerations for the initial presentation
The clinical picture of hypotension, multi‑organ dysfunction and DIC in a patient immediately after a dog bite raised several possibilities:

1. **Bacterial sepsis from a dog bite organism** – Capnocytophaga canimorsus, Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus species, diphtheroids, and other oral flora.
2. **Infective shock** – septic shock leading to global hypoperfusion, lactic acidosis and hypoglycaemia.
3. **Primary DIC** – consumptive coagulopathy secondary to systemic inflammation.
4. **Other surgical emergencies** – necrotising fasciitis or other soft‑tissue infections that can precipitate hyperinflammatory responses and coagulopathy.

Imaging (plain radiographs and early ultrasound) excluded necrotising soft‑tissue infection or other surgical catastrophes, allowing the team to focus on infectious and inflammatory causes of DIC.

### Early microbiologic strategy
Given the presumed exposure to dog oral flora and the high risk for Capnocytophaga—which is notoriously slow‑growing and under‑diagnosed—the empirical antibiotic regimen was initiated with **piperacillin‑tazobactam**. This broad‑spectrum coverage includes beta‑lactamase–producing gram‑negative rods and gram‑positive cocci, with documented in‑vitro activity against Capnocytophaga species. Blood cultures (both aerobic and anaerobic) were drawn before antibiotic administration.

Thus, the initial clinical and laboratory findings—dog‑bite exposure, delayed antibiotic use, early septic shock signs (fever, hypoglycaemia, lactate acidosis, mottled limbs), pancytopenia, acute kidney injury, hepatic dysfunction, and coagulopathy—led to an early differential that favored sepsis‑induced DIC secondary to a dog‑associated bacteremia, most likely Capnocytophaga canimorsus, while excluding surgical emergencies and other infectious triggers.

## 2. Progression to Thrombotic Microangiopathy – Diagnostic Reasoning

After the initial septic phase had been stabilized, the patient’s clinical picture suddenly changed on day 8 of admission. Renal function deteriorated rapidly from early insult (creatinine 98 µmol/L) to complete anuria, prompting the initiation of continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT). Concomitantly, the patient developed diffuse ecchymoses and distal cyanosis of toes and fingers, despite preserved pulses and sensation—findings that suggested localized microvascular occlusion rather than widespread ischemic shock.  

The laboratory evolution was striking. Hemoglobin fell from 111 to 68 g/L over six days, without evidence of overt bleeding or hemodynamic compromise. This isolated drop raised suspicion of intravascular hemolysis. Peripheral smear revealed 15‑20 % schistocytes, a hallmark of mechanical fragmentation of erythrocytes in a microangiopathic environment. Concomitant laboratory markers confirmed hemolysis: haptoglobin became undetectable, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) rose above 700 U/L, and the reticulocyte index failed to show the appropriate compensatory response, indicating impaired erythropoiesis rather than a straightforward regenerative anemia. 

An initial direct antiglobulin test (DAT) was positive for IgG, prompting consideration of autoimmune hemolytic anemia (AIHA). However, this result was likely a false positive, induced either by penicillin exposure or prior transfusion. Repeat DAT testing was negative, thereby removing AIHA from the differential and reinforcing the interpretation that the anemia was hemolytic rather than immune-mediated.  

With microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA) now established, the differential narrowed to thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), Shiga toxin‑producing Escherichia coli (STEC)–associated HUS, and complement‑mediated HUS (CM‑HUS). The PLASMIC score—an eight‑parameter clinical tool that assigns weight to platelet count, hemolysis indicators, ADAMTS13 activity, and other laboratory values—was calculated as 4/7, placing the patient in the low‑probability category for TTP. This clinical pretest probability was confirmed biochemically: ADAMTS13 activity measured 43.5 %, well above the <10 % threshold diagnostic of TTP.  

The possibility of STEC‑associated HUS was excluded via stool PCR, which returned negative for Shiga toxin. Thus, an infection‑triggered CM‑HUS remained the most plausible explanation. Confirmation of the thrombotic microangiopathy required tissue evidence. A kidney biopsy performed after partial diuresis return demonstrated acute tubular necrosis, fibrin thrombi within glomerular capillaries, and scattered extracapillary crescents—all classic histopathologic features of TMA. Immunofluorescence and electron microscopy did not reveal immune complex deposition or primary glomerular disease, supporting a non‑immune trigger.

In summary, the progression from sepsis‑induced multi‑organ failure to a distinct microangiopathic syndrome was discerned through a sequential evaluation of clinical signs (skin bleeding, cyanosis), laboratory evolution (MAHA, schistocytes, undetectable haptoglobin), and targeted diagnostic tools (DAT repeats, PLASMIC score, ADAMTS13 activity, stool PCR, renal biopsy). This methodical approach allowed the exclusion of TTP and STEC‑HUS and led to the diagnosis of CM‑HUS in the setting of Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteraemia.

## 3. Pathophysiology of Infection‑Triggered Complement‑Mediated HUS

Capnocytophaga canimorsus can act as a “second hit” that unleashes a dormant complement defect or, in some instances, triggers a de novo dysregulated complement response. The sequence of events is best understood by reviewing the anatomy of the alternative complement pathway, the ways in which C. canimorsus engages or evades it, and the relevance of host‐derived regulatory proteins.

### 3.1  Alternative complement pathway and its key regulators

The alternative pathway (AP) is a constitutively active “tick‑over” that amplifies complement activation without requiring antibody binding. In venous plasma, C3 undergoes spontaneous hydrolysis to C3(H₂O), which, in complex with factor B (CFB), is cleaved by factor D into the fluid‑phase C3 convertase (C3(H₂O)Bb). This convertase cleaves further C3 molecules into C3a and C3b, and the deposited C3b binds factor H (CFH) to form a decay‑accelerating complex that limits convertase activity. Crucially, CFH, factor I (CFI), CFB and membrane‑bound CD46 (MCP) provide a feedback loop that prevents excessive amplification on host cells. Loss or functional impairment of any of these proteins—through heterozygous or homozygous mutations, or by autoantibodies—tips the balance toward uncontrolled convertase activity and terminal pore formation (C5b‑9), which injures the renal microvasculature in HUS.

### 3.2  Capnocytophaga‑induced complement activation

C. canimorsus is a gram‑negative rod that displays a polysaccharide capsule and various lipooligosaccharide components. In serum, its surface structures can bind properdin, a positive regulator of the AP, stimulating C3b deposition on the bacterial surface. The capsule, however, shields the bacterium from opsonisation and phagocytosis. In vitro, some C. canimorsus isolates are highly sensitive to complement, whereas capsulated strains show partial resistance, underscoring the tug‑of‑war between bacterial evasion and host defence. When complement activation proceeds unchecked, C5a is generated, recruiting neutrophils and inducing a pro‑inflammatory milieu that further primes endothelial cells for thrombus formation.

### 3.3  Role of the capsule in complement evasion and endothelial activation

The polysaccharide capsule stabilises the bacterium against C3b‑mediated lysis but can also promote binding of complement components to the capsule itself. This local assembly of C3b and subsequent convertase formation may, paradoxically, release soluble fragments (C3a, C5a) into the circulation while sparing the bacteria from killing. The resulting systemic anaphylatoxins activate complement‑deep endothelial cells, upregulating tissue factor and adhesion molecules, which predispose to microthrombi formation. In a host with impaired regulation (e.g., CFH deficiency), the capsule acts as a chronic source of AP activation, overwhelming the limited control available and precipitating TMA.

### 3.4  Genetic predisposition and the value of testing

Approximately 50–70 % of patients who develop CM‑HUS carry pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in complement genes (CFH, CFI, CFB, CD46, CFHR3/CFHR1) or possess autoantibodies (anti‑CFH). Even heterozygous carriers often remain asymptomatic until an external trigger triggers a “second hit” of complement amplification. Genetic testing, therefore, is essential not only for prognostication but also for guiding treatment duration and counselling family members. In the case presented, extended genetic analysis returned no pathogenic alleles, supporting an infection‑induced activation rather than a pre‑existing dysregulation—yet the absence of a detectable mutation does not rule out a functional defect, as non‑canonical variants or epigenetic changes may be involved.

### 3.5  Evidence from the literature

CM‑HUS triggered by C. canimorsus is exceedingly rare, but several case reports now support this connection. One notable report described a patient with a confirmed CFB variant who developed TMA after C. canimorsus bacteraemia; treatment with eculizumab led to rapid recovery. Another case described a young adult with no known genetic predisposition who, following a dog bite, progressed to dialysis‑dependent AKI and microangiopathic haemolysis, with renal biopsy confirming TMA; eculizumab therapy restored renal perfusion and halted haematological deterioration. These reports share a common pathogenetic thread: capsulated C. canimorsus activates the AP, and in susceptible hosts, the activation surpasses the bounds of regulation, resulting in endothelial injury and microthrombi. The scarcity of such reports likely reflects both under‑recognition and the challenge of performing comprehensive complement testing in the acute setting.

### 3.6  Diagnostic caveats: normal C3/C4 levels and functional assays

In active CM‑HUS, serum C3 and C4 concentrations may remain within normal limits because consumption is moderate relative to the large circulating complement pool, or because alternative pathway activation does not drain classical or lectin components. Consequently, relying solely on C3/C4 can miss underlying dysregulation. Functional assays—such as the AH50 (alternative pathway hemolysis assay), C3 convertase stability tests, and measurement of soluble C5b‑9—provide a more sensitive readout of complement activity. In the presented patient, these functional studies showed markedly reduced alternative pathway activity (AH50 6 %), but the result must be interpreted in the context of recent plasma exchanges and eculizumab infusion, both of which can artificially depress complement activity. Thus, serial testing before, during, and after therapy is essential to discern true dysregulation from therapeutic suppression.

---

In sum, infection with C. canimorsus can be the proverbial “pressure cap” that opens a fissure in the complement control system. Whether the fissure is widened by a pre‑existing genetic defect or by a transient functional impairment, the end result is the same: overactivated AP, unchecked complement‑mediated endothelial injury, and the catastrophic microvascular thrombosis that defines CM‑HUS. Understanding this interplay is critical for timely diagnosis, appropriate timing of complement inhibition, and for anticipating the need for prophylactic measures against encapsulated organisms during therapy.

## 4. Therapeutic Strategy – Balancing Infection Control and Complement Blockade

The therapeutic narrative of this case illustrates a classic dilemma in the management of complement‑mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (CM‑HUS): how to halt an unchecked complement cascade while still allowing the host to fight an active, encapsulated bacteremia. The decisions made during the clinical course were dictated by evolving laboratory data, the patient’s haemodynamic stability, and the logistics of biologic therapy. The following discussion dissects the key milestones in that pathway, from antibiotic stewardship through to maintenance eculizumab.

### Antibiotic de‑escalation guided by culture results

Initial empiric therapy comprised intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam, a broad‑spectrum agent that covers most dog‑associated pathogens including *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*. Once standard aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures yielded *C. canimorsus* after seven days, the antibiotic regimen was tailored accordingly. The isolate was susceptible to penicillin G, and the treatment was switched to intravenous ampicillin (or, for practical considerations in this case, later to orally administered phenoxymethylpenicillin). This de‑escalation achieved two objectives: it reduced the potential for antibiotic‑associated nephrotoxicity and bacterial resistance, and it aligned therapy with the organism’s documented susceptibility profile.

Critically, the timing of de‑escalation coincided with the patient’s first signs of septic shock resolution (a drop in lactate, improvement in urine output, and a return of diuresis). This clinical stabilization made it more feasible to pursue a therapeutic strategy devoted to complement inhibition, knowing that the bacterial burden had been sufficiently suppressed.

### Use of plasma exchange as bridging therapy; impact on hemolysis and renal recovery

Once laboratory evidence of microangiopathic haemolysis emerged (undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH, schistocytes on smear) but before confirmatory TTP or CM‑HUS diagnostics could be obtained, plasma exchange (PEX) was initiated. In acute TTP this is life‑saving; in CM‑HUS, PEX can temporarily reduce circulating complement fragments and inflammatory mediators but does not modulate the underlying gain‑of‑function in the complement pathway.

In this patient, three PEX sessions were delivered over two days, each employing Octaplas or similar plasma substitute. The immediate haemodynamic impact was modest: platelet counts rose modestly but remained below 50 × 10⁹/mL, and hemolysis continued to accrue (serial LDH remained high). Renal function, however, improved notedly after the final PEX session, as evidenced by a return of spontaneous diuresis. This suggests that removing circulating microangiopathic mediators can provide a temporary window of organ perfusion, but without complement blockade the core pathophysiology persists.

### Criteria for initiating eculizumab: persistence of TMA, platelets ≤ 50 × 10⁹/L, unresolved hemolysis

The decision to start eculizumab—an anti‑C5 monoclonal antibody—is typically governed by the persistence of active TMA features despite supportive care. In this case, the patient met the following thresholds:
- Platelets < 50 × 10⁹/L sustained over > 48 h after the last PEX session.
- Persistent hemolysis: undetectable haptoglobin, LDH > 2× upper limit, and > 10 % schistocytes on peripheral smear.
- No evidence of alternative TMA drivers (ADAMTS13 activity > 10 %, negative Shiga toxin PCR).
- Clinical deteriorations (e.g., worsening AKI, unexplained anaemia) that could not be attributed to sepsis alone.

Once these criteria were met, eculizumab infusions (900 mg IV weekly for four induction doses) were commenced. Initiation was timed to precede the first dose of meningococcal vaccines, allowing the patient to generate protective antibody titres before complement blockade.

### Vaccination schedule (Menveo, Bexsero) prior to complement inhibition

Because terminal complement blockade eliminates an essential defense against encapsulated organisms, patients must receive meningococcal vaccination at least 10 days before the first eculizumab dose. In this case, the patient received Menveo (covering serogroups A, C, Y, W) and Bexsero (serogroup B) concurrently with the start of eculizumab. A second series of vaccinations was scheduled eight weeks later once the patient’s clinical status had stabilized and eculizumab therapy was established. This two‑dose schedule satisfies current guidelines and maximizes the likelihood of a robust protective humoral response.

### Prophylactic antibiotics during eculizumab therapy

When terminal complement is inhibited, patients are at increased risk of fulminant meningococcal disease and other encapsulated infections such as *C. canimorsus*, even after de‑contamination of the bloodstream. Therefore, prophylactic penicillin (e.g., phenoxymethylpenicillin or amoxicillin) is recommended for the duration of complement blockade. In this case, prophylaxis was started with oral Apocillin on day 15 of hospitalization and maintained throughout the maintenance phase of eculizumab (1200 mg IV every two weeks). Antimicrobial coverage was tailored to the local microbiology and the patient’s prior isolates.

### Monitoring for infectious complications and managing therapeutic windows

Clinical vigilance is paramount. During eculizumab therapy the patient was monitored for fever, new bacteremia, and signs of invasive meningococcal disease. Serial blood cultures, complete blood counts, and inflammatory markers such as CRP and procalcitonin were obtained every 48 h. Because *C. canimorsus* can persist in the host for several weeks, the therapeutic window was defined as the period from the last negative blood culture to the initiation of eculizumab. This approach minimized the risk of exposing the patient to complement blockade while still effectively clearing the infectious trigger.

In the event of any breakthrough infection, eculizumab infusion would be temporarily withheld and the patient re‑evaluated once the infection was controlled. This “stop‑watch” strategy is increasingly used in the clinical setting to balance the risk of active infection against the benefit of early complement inhibition.

### Maintenance therapy duration and criteria for discontinuation

The standard maintenance schedule for CM‑HUS is eculizumab 1200 mg every two weeks, which was continued for four months post‑induction in this patient. The decision to cease therapy hinges on the resolution of TMA features: normalized platelet count (> 150 × 10⁹/L), absence of haemolysis (normal haptoglobin, LDH), improvement in renal function, and the absence of a discernible complement trigger. Because the patient had no identifiable pathogenic complement genetic variant, a gradual taper of eculizumab is warranted once clinical stability is achieved. Close longitudinal follow‑up, including repeat complement functional studies, will inform whether continuation beyond the initial maintenance period offers benefit.

In summary, the therapeutic strategy in this case involved a rational sequence: prompt empiric broad‑spectrum antibiotics, targeted de‑escalation once culture data were available, bridging PEX while awaiting definitive diagnostics, initiation of complement blockade after confirming a persistent, unexplained TMA, and rigorous infection prophylaxis combined with meningococcal vaccination. This balanced approach allowed for both infection control and rapid attenuation of complement‑mediated endothelial injury, culminating in partial renal recovery and haematological remission.

## 5. Outcome, Follow‑up and Clinical Pearls

The clinical trajectory of this patient illustrates how recognition, timely diagnosis and prompt intervention can alter the course of a potentially fatal complement‑mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (CM‑HUS). The following points distill the quantitative milestones, interpretive findings and practical lessons that arise from this case.

### Timeline of hemolysis resolution and renal recovery after eculizumab

Hemolysis began de novo eight days after the onset of septic shock, evidenced by a precipitous fall in hemoglobin, a rising lactate‑dehydrogenase (LDH), and a disappearance of haptoglobin. Initiation of plasma exchange (PEX) was followed by a modest rise in platelet count and a partial reduction of LDH, indicating temporary mitigation of complement‑driven endothelial injury. The first dose of eculizumab (900 mg IV weekly for four weeks) was given on day 20 of hospitalization, after the patient’s cultures had become negative and a full complement of baseline tests had been obtained. Following this initiation, hematologic indices rebounded rapidly: hemoglobin increased to 115 g/L within 48 h, haptoglobin normalized, and schistocytes fell below 5 %. Importantly, spontaneous diuresis returned after the third PEX session, but it was sustained only after the eculizumab induction course. Over the ensuing 12 weeks, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) climbed from a nadir of 12 mL/min/1.73 m² to 45 mL/min/1.73 m², enabling discontinuation of dialysis. The patient has remained dialysis‑independent for nine months at the time of manuscript submission.

### Ongoing renal function: serum creatinine, estimated GFR, dialysis independence

At discharge, serum creatinine was 175 µmol/L, corresponding to an eGFR of ~40 mL/min/1.73 m² (CKD‑EPI equation). The kidney biopsy revealed acute tubular necrosis with focal glomerular ischemia, features that are frequently reversible once complement activation is suppressed. The patient now maintains creatinine 140–160 µmol/L on maintenance eculizumab every two weeks, with mild proteinuria (30 mg/g) and stable blood pressure under dual antihypertensive therapy. The absence of recurrent hematuria and the lack of biopsy evidence of ongoing thrombotic necrosis underscore the therapeutic efficacy of complement blockade.

### Extended complement and genetic testing results, interpretation of negative findings

Extended complement assays performed after the final PEX session and one week after the first eculizumab dose revealed an alternative pathway activity (AH50) of 6 % (normal 30–100 %). This suppression is attributable to therapeutic complement blockade rather than an intrinsic deficiency. Levels of complement components H, I, B, and factor D were within normal ranges, and no anti‑factor H antibodies were detected. Whole‑exome sequencing of the complement regulatory genes (CFH, CFI, MCP/CD46, CFB, and complement factor I) returned no pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants. In the context of a clear infection trigger and the absence of family history or previous episodes, these findings support an *infection‑induced* CM‑HUS rather than a hereditary complement dysregulation syndrome. Consequently, the decision to continue maintenance eculizumab for four months is driven by the severity of organ involvement rather than by a provable genetic defect.

### Prognostic factors: time to eculizumab initiation, initial renal status, TMA severity

Retrospective analyses have shown that the composite score of renal function at presentation (eGFR ≤ 30 mL/min/1.73 m²), the duration of dialysis dependence before therapy (≥ 7 days), and the degree of hemolysis (LDH > 3× upper limit) independently predict progression to end‑stage kidney disease if eculizumab is initiated late. In this patient, eculizumab was started at day 20, well before the ten‑day threshold that has been associated with irreversible nephron loss. The ΔLDH over the first 48 h of PEX and eculizumab correlated with hematologic remission, underscoring the need for early therapeutic escalation in patients who fail to improve with PEX alone.

### Clinical take‑aways: high index of suspicion for TMA in septic patients with persistent thrombocytopenia and hemolysis

Sepsis, by its very nature, can induce disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) and mimic TMA on the lab bench. However, persistent thrombocytopenia beyond the first week of ICU stay, evolving anemia with rising LDH, a falling haptoglobin, and the appearance of schistocytes should prompt evaluation for TMA. In this case, the first hint came from a sudden hemoglobin drop accompanied by an irrefutable rise in LDH and the absence of overt bleeding. Early recognition allowed for the initiation of PEX, and subsequent confirmatory testing (ADAMTS13 > 40 %) ruled out TTP, shifting focus to CM‑HUS. Clinicians should therefore consider a TMA work‑up whenever classic sepsis markers fluctuate independently of inflammatory activity.

### Importance of rapid molecular diagnostics (e.g., real‑time nanopore sequencing) for fastidious organisms

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is notoriously slow growing and often missed on conventional cultures. In this case, the pathogen was identified a week after the initial presentation, which delayed optimal targeted therapy. Emerging rapid sequencing platforms, such as real‑time nanopore sequencing, can provide organism identification within hours, enabling earlier de‑escalation of broad‑spectrum antibiotics and earlier recognition of an infectious trigger that predisposes to CM‑HUS. Incorporating these tools into routine sepsis work‑ups may shorten the window between infection onset and definitive diagnosis, which, as this case shows, can be critical for renal recovery.

### Avoidance of unnecessary invasive procedures (bone marrow biopsy) when clinical picture favors sepsis

The initial pancytopenia raised concerns for leukaemia or haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, both of which would necessitate bone marrow confirmation. Normal flow cytometry and the rapid clinical decline argued strongly for sepsis‑induced cytopenia. A bone marrow biopsy would have been invasive, posed a bleeding risk in a thrombocytopenic patient, and offered little additional therapeutic guidance. The decision to forego the biopsy exemplifies judicious weighing of diagnostic yield against procedural harm, a principle that should guide practice in similar scenarios.

### Future directions: data registries for infection‑triggered CM‑HUS, research into complement‑inhibitor safety profiles

This case underscores the scarcity of data on infection‑triggered CM‑HUS, especially with rare pathogens such as C. canimorsus. Multicenter registries capturing triggers, genetic findings, therapeutic timings, and outcomes would illuminate prognostic factors and refine treatment algorithms. Moreover, long‑term safety data on complement blockade in the setting of infectious triggers remain limited; prospective studies exploring vaccine‑based prophylaxis, antibiotic stewardship, and infection surveillance in patients receiving eculizumab are essential. Finally, the development of next‑generation complement inhibitors with reversible or tissue‑specific actions may reduce the risk of encapsulated‑bacteria infections while maintaining efficacy.

### Bottom line for clinicians

1. **Maintain high vigilance for TMA when septic patients exhibit persistent thrombocytopenia, evolving hemolysis, or new schistocytosis.**  
2. **Initiate ADAMTS13 testing promptly; a > 10 % activity steers away from TTP and towards CM‑HUS or other TMAs.**  
3. **Early eculizumab is a game‑changer; delays beyond 7 days are associated with irreversible renal loss.**  
4. **Use rapid molecular diagnostics to identify fastidious bacteria, both to guide antibiotics and to anticipate complement‑mediated complications.**  
5. **Avoid unnecessary invasive diagnostics when the clinical context strongly supports a sepsis‑driven cytopenia.**  

By integrating these pearls into practice, clinicians can improve recognition, expedite definitive therapy, and ultimately enhance outcomes for patients presenting with rare infection‑triggered complement‑mediated thrombotic microangiopathies.
