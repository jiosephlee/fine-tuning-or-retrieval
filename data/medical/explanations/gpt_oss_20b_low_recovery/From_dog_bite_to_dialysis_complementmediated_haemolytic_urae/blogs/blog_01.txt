Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# When a Dog Bite Turns Deadly – Recognizing Sepsis‑Triggered CM‑HUS in the ED  

## The startling “lull” that made us stop what we were doing

A 48‑year‑old woman presents to the ED after a dog bite on the upper lip, a high fever, low‑grade rash on her feet, and a sudden drop in blood pressure. She’s already septic – so we start broad‑spectrum antibiotics, give fluids, and think DIC. Her labs show pancytopenia, thrombocytopenia, AKI, and a high lactate. Since Capnocytophaga canimorsus is slow to grow, we keep her on piperacillin‑tazobactam and watch.  

Eight days later, she’s on dialysis, looks clinically better, but then her hemoglobin plunges and schistocytes appear on the smear. Suddenly the picture changes: we’re no longer treating a “pure” sepsis‑DIC syndrome—there’s a microangiopathic hemolytic anemia riding on top of it. That is, the patient has developed complement‑mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM‑HUS).  

The point is: **in a septic patient with a dog bite, a sudden tick‑tick‑tock in the lab trend can be a harbinger of CM‑HUS, not just sepsis.**

---

## From the bedside to the bench: why this case rocks

1. **Capnocytophaga canimorsus (C. canimorsus)** — a slow‑growing, gram‑negative rod, often missed on routine cultures, but a known trigger for fulminant sepsis and, rarely, TMA.

2. **DIC in early sepsis vs. microangiopathic hemolysis later** — the first days look like classic sepsis‑induced DIC (pancytopenia, coagulopathy, AKI). Weeks later the same patient can develop isolated hemolysis with intact coagulation and persistent thrombocytopenia.

3. **Complement activation paradox** — C. canimorsus is normally cleared by complement, but when the alternative pathway is dysregulated (genetically or by the infection itself) uncontrolled activation damages endothelium, leading to TMA.

4. **Therapeutic tightrope** — plasma exchange (PEX) and eculizumab both have roles, but we can’t give eculizumab until the infection is cleared because the drug blocks the very complement that fights encapsulated bacteria.

5. **Ultra‑rapid diagnostics** — molecular methods (nanopore sequencing) and rapid TTP scores (PLASMIC) can save clinicians’ judgement from “wait for cultures” paralysis.

## The clinical timeline that should make you pause

| Day | What happened | Key lab data | Why it matters |
|-----|---------------|--------------|----------------|
| 0 | Dog bite, received tetanus booster, no penicillin | – | No early antibiotic – sets stage for septicemia |
| 1‑2 | Fever ≥40 °C, hypoglycemia, mottled extremities | Leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, AKI, elevated lactate | Classic sepsis‑DIC picture |
| 4 | C. canimorsus isolated from blood; septic shock managed | C3/C4 normal | Despite correct antibiotics, sepsis doesn’t fully resolve |
| 8 | Anuria, dialysis-dependent AKI, chest/renal imaging | Normal coags, persistent thrombocytopenia | DIC fading, but other organ injury persists |
| 12 | Sudden drop hemoglobin 111 → 68 g/L, schistocytes 15–20 % | LDH ↑, haptoglobin ↓ | New MAHA; clue to TMA |
| 13‑15 | Plasma exchange started, eculizumab induction | Platelets up to 77 × 10⁹/L, spontaneous diuresis returns after PEX | Temporary stabilization |
| 16 | Eculizumab complete induction, vaccinations given, oral penicillin resumed | Complement functional assay low AH 50 (post‑PEX/culizumab) | Full hematologic remission, partial renal recovery |

## How we distinguished CM‑HUS from TTP or STEC‑HUS

1. **ADAMTS13 activity 43 %**  
   - *TTP?* No – usual activity <10 %.  
2. **Shiga‑toxin PCR negative**  
   - *STEC‑HUS?* No.  
3. **Kidney biopsy**  
   - Acute tubular necrosis + ischaemic glomeruli + fibrin thrombi, no immune deposits. Classic TMA morphologic picture.  
4. **Complement genetics**  
   - No pathogenic variants on screen. Still, invasive infection can tip the balance.  

---

## Key pearls for the frontline clinician

- **Always consider TMA in a septic patient with new‑onset hemolysis and persistent thrombocytopenia, even if early labs showed DIC.**  
- **The PLASMIC score can help triage but is not definitive; if the score is low, keep a low threshold for ADAMTS13 testing.**  
- **If Capnocytophaga is isolated, start empirical treatment with a β‑lactam that covers it (e.g., piperacillin‑tazobactam).**  
- **Check complement levels (C3/C4). Normal values do not exclude CM‑HUS.**  
- **Initiate plasma exchange if the patient is deteriorating, but remember it is only a bridge.**  
- **Give eculizumab *after* blood cultures are negative, after vaccinating against meningococcus, and with antibiotic prophylaxis in place.**  
- **Early eculizumab (within 3–5 days of TMA diagnosis) correlates with better renal outcomes.**  

---

## Take‑home messages

- **Dog bites can masquerade as classic sepsis, but watch for the “second hit”: if hemolysis appears after a transient improvement, think CM‑HUS.**  
- **Capnocytophaga canimorsus is a known, though rare, trigger for complement‑mediated TMA; the infection can both induce and obscure TMA.**  
- **Early recognition and timely complement blockade are the single most important factors influencing renal recovery.**  
- **Balancing infection control and complement inhibition requires careful timing, vaccination, and prophylactic antibiotics.**  
- **Maintain a high index of suspicion for TMA in any septic patient with new thrombocytopenia and hemolysis, even if initial labs point toward DIC.**
