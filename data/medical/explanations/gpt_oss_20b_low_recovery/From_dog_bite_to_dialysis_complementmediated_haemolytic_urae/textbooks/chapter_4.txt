Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

Chapter 4: ## 4. Therapeutic Strategy – Balancing Infection Control and Complement Blockade

The therapeutic narrative of this case illustrates a classic dilemma in the management of complement‑mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (CM‑HUS): how to halt an unchecked complement cascade while still allowing the host to fight an active, encapsulated bacteremia. The decisions made during the clinical course were dictated by evolving laboratory data, the patient’s haemodynamic stability, and the logistics of biologic therapy. The following discussion dissects the key milestones in that pathway, from antibiotic stewardship through to maintenance eculizumab.

### Antibiotic de‑escalation guided by culture results

Initial empiric therapy comprised intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam, a broad‑spectrum agent that covers most dog‑associated pathogens including *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*. Once standard aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures yielded *C. canimorsus* after seven days, the antibiotic regimen was tailored accordingly. The isolate was susceptible to penicillin G, and the treatment was switched to intravenous ampicillin (or, for practical considerations in this case, later to orally administered phenoxymethylpenicillin). This de‑escalation achieved two objectives: it reduced the potential for antibiotic‑associated nephrotoxicity and bacterial resistance, and it aligned therapy with the organism’s documented susceptibility profile.

Critically, the timing of de‑escalation coincided with the patient’s first signs of septic shock resolution (a drop in lactate, improvement in urine output, and a return of diuresis). This clinical stabilization made it more feasible to pursue a therapeutic strategy devoted to complement inhibition, knowing that the bacterial burden had been sufficiently suppressed.

### Use of plasma exchange as bridging therapy; impact on hemolysis and renal recovery

Once laboratory evidence of microangiopathic haemolysis emerged (undetectable haptoglobin, rising LDH, schistocytes on smear) but before confirmatory TTP or CM‑HUS diagnostics could be obtained, plasma exchange (PEX) was initiated. In acute TTP this is life‑saving; in CM‑HUS, PEX can temporarily reduce circulating complement fragments and inflammatory mediators but does not modulate the underlying gain‑of‑function in the complement pathway.

In this patient, three PEX sessions were delivered over two days, each employing Octaplas or similar plasma substitute. The immediate haemodynamic impact was modest: platelet counts rose modestly but remained below 50 × 10⁹/mL, and hemolysis continued to accrue (serial LDH remained high). Renal function, however, improved notedly after the final PEX session, as evidenced by a return of spontaneous diuresis. This suggests that removing circulating microangiopathic mediators can provide a temporary window of organ perfusion, but without complement blockade the core pathophysiology persists.

### Criteria for initiating eculizumab: persistence of TMA, platelets ≤ 50 × 10⁹/L, unresolved hemolysis

The decision to start eculizumab—an anti‑C5 monoclonal antibody—is typically governed by the persistence of active TMA features despite supportive care. In this case, the patient met the following thresholds:
- Platelets < 50 × 10⁹/L sustained over > 48 h after the last PEX session.
- Persistent hemolysis: undetectable haptoglobin, LDH > 2× upper limit, and > 10 % schistocytes on peripheral smear.
- No evidence of alternative TMA drivers (ADAMTS13 activity > 10 %, negative Shiga toxin PCR).
- Clinical deteriorations (e.g., worsening AKI, unexplained anaemia) that could not be attributed to sepsis alone.

Once these criteria were met, eculizumab infusions (900 mg IV weekly for four induction doses) were commenced. Initiation was timed to precede the first dose of meningococcal vaccines, allowing the patient to generate protective antibody titres before complement blockade.

### Vaccination schedule (Menveo, Bexsero) prior to complement inhibition

Because terminal complement blockade eliminates an essential defense against encapsulated organisms, patients must receive meningococcal vaccination at least 10 days before the first eculizumab dose. In this case, the patient received Menveo (covering serogroups A, C, Y, W) and Bexsero (serogroup B) concurrently with the start of eculizumab. A second series of vaccinations was scheduled eight weeks later once the patient’s clinical status had stabilized and eculizumab therapy was established. This two‑dose schedule satisfies current guidelines and maximizes the likelihood of a robust protective humoral response.

### Prophylactic antibiotics during eculizumab therapy

When terminal complement is inhibited, patients are at increased risk of fulminant meningococcal disease and other encapsulated infections such as *C. canimorsus*, even after de‑contamination of the bloodstream. Therefore, prophylactic penicillin (e.g., phenoxymethylpenicillin or amoxicillin) is recommended for the duration of complement blockade. In this case, prophylaxis was started with oral Apocillin on day 15 of hospitalization and maintained throughout the maintenance phase of eculizumab (1200 mg IV every two weeks). Antimicrobial coverage was tailored to the local microbiology and the patient’s prior isolates.

### Monitoring for infectious complications and managing therapeutic windows

Clinical vigilance is paramount. During eculizumab therapy the patient was monitored for fever, new bacteremia, and signs of invasive meningococcal disease. Serial blood cultures, complete blood counts, and inflammatory markers such as CRP and procalcitonin were obtained every 48 h. Because *C. canimorsus* can persist in the host for several weeks, the therapeutic window was defined as the period from the last negative blood culture to the initiation of eculizumab. This approach minimized the risk of exposing the patient to complement blockade while still effectively clearing the infectious trigger.

In the event of any breakthrough infection, eculizumab infusion would be temporarily withheld and the patient re‑evaluated once the infection was controlled. This “stop‑watch” strategy is increasingly used in the clinical setting to balance the risk of active infection against the benefit of early complement inhibition.

### Maintenance therapy duration and criteria for discontinuation

The standard maintenance schedule for CM‑HUS is eculizumab 1200 mg every two weeks, which was continued for four months post‑induction in this patient. The decision to cease therapy hinges on the resolution of TMA features: normalized platelet count (> 150 × 10⁹/L), absence of haemolysis (normal haptoglobin, LDH), improvement in renal function, and the absence of a discernible complement trigger. Because the patient had no identifiable pathogenic complement genetic variant, a gradual taper of eculizumab is warranted once clinical stability is achieved. Close longitudinal follow‑up, including repeat complement functional studies, will inform whether continuation beyond the initial maintenance period offers benefit.

In summary, the therapeutic strategy in this case involved a rational sequence: prompt empiric broad‑spectrum antibiotics, targeted de‑escalation once culture data were available, bridging PEX while awaiting definitive diagnostics, initiation of complement blockade after confirming a persistent, unexplained TMA, and rigorous infection prophylaxis combined with meningococcal vaccination. This balanced approach allowed for both infection control and rapid attenuation of complement‑mediated endothelial injury, culminating in partial renal recovery and haematological remission.
