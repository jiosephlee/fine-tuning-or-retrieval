Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

Chapter 3: ## 3. Pathophysiology of Infection‑Triggered Complement‑Mediated HUS

Capnocytophaga canimorsus can act as a “second hit” that unleashes a dormant complement defect or, in some instances, triggers a de novo dysregulated complement response. The sequence of events is best understood by reviewing the anatomy of the alternative complement pathway, the ways in which C. canimorsus engages or evades it, and the relevance of host‐derived regulatory proteins.

### 3.1  Alternative complement pathway and its key regulators

The alternative pathway (AP) is a constitutively active “tick‑over” that amplifies complement activation without requiring antibody binding. In venous plasma, C3 undergoes spontaneous hydrolysis to C3(H₂O), which, in complex with factor B (CFB), is cleaved by factor D into the fluid‑phase C3 convertase (C3(H₂O)Bb). This convertase cleaves further C3 molecules into C3a and C3b, and the deposited C3b binds factor H (CFH) to form a decay‑accelerating complex that limits convertase activity. Crucially, CFH, factor I (CFI), CFB and membrane‑bound CD46 (MCP) provide a feedback loop that prevents excessive amplification on host cells. Loss or functional impairment of any of these proteins—through heterozygous or homozygous mutations, or by autoantibodies—tips the balance toward uncontrolled convertase activity and terminal pore formation (C5b‑9), which injures the renal microvasculature in HUS.

### 3.2  Capnocytophaga‑induced complement activation

C. canimorsus is a gram‑negative rod that displays a polysaccharide capsule and various lipooligosaccharide components. In serum, its surface structures can bind properdin, a positive regulator of the AP, stimulating C3b deposition on the bacterial surface. The capsule, however, shields the bacterium from opsonisation and phagocytosis. In vitro, some C. canimorsus isolates are highly sensitive to complement, whereas capsulated strains show partial resistance, underscoring the tug‑of‑war between bacterial evasion and host defence. When complement activation proceeds unchecked, C5a is generated, recruiting neutrophils and inducing a pro‑inflammatory milieu that further primes endothelial cells for thrombus formation.

### 3.3  Role of the capsule in complement evasion and endothelial activation

The polysaccharide capsule stabilises the bacterium against C3b‑mediated lysis but can also promote binding of complement components to the capsule itself. This local assembly of C3b and subsequent convertase formation may, paradoxically, release soluble fragments (C3a, C5a) into the circulation while sparing the bacteria from killing. The resulting systemic anaphylatoxins activate complement‑deep endothelial cells, upregulating tissue factor and adhesion molecules, which predispose to microthrombi formation. In a host with impaired regulation (e.g., CFH deficiency), the capsule acts as a chronic source of AP activation, overwhelming the limited control available and precipitating TMA.

### 3.4  Genetic predisposition and the value of testing

Approximately 50–70 % of patients who develop CM‑HUS carry pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in complement genes (CFH, CFI, CFB, CD46, CFHR3/CFHR1) or possess autoantibodies (anti‑CFH). Even heterozygous carriers often remain asymptomatic until an external trigger triggers a “second hit” of complement amplification. Genetic testing, therefore, is essential not only for prognostication but also for guiding treatment duration and counselling family members. In the case presented, extended genetic analysis returned no pathogenic alleles, supporting an infection‑induced activation rather than a pre‑existing dysregulation—yet the absence of a detectable mutation does not rule out a functional defect, as non‑canonical variants or epigenetic changes may be involved.

### 3.5  Evidence from the literature

CM‑HUS triggered by C. canimorsus is exceedingly rare, but several case reports now support this connection. One notable report described a patient with a confirmed CFB variant who developed TMA after C. canimorsus bacteraemia; treatment with eculizumab led to rapid recovery. Another case described a young adult with no known genetic predisposition who, following a dog bite, progressed to dialysis‑dependent AKI and microangiopathic haemolysis, with renal biopsy confirming TMA; eculizumab therapy restored renal perfusion and halted haematological deterioration. These reports share a common pathogenetic thread: capsulated C. canimorsus activates the AP, and in susceptible hosts, the activation surpasses the bounds of regulation, resulting in endothelial injury and microthrombi. The scarcity of such reports likely reflects both under‑recognition and the challenge of performing comprehensive complement testing in the acute setting.

### 3.6  Diagnostic caveats: normal C3/C4 levels and functional assays

In active CM‑HUS, serum C3 and C4 concentrations may remain within normal limits because consumption is moderate relative to the large circulating complement pool, or because alternative pathway activation does not drain classical or lectin components. Consequently, relying solely on C3/C4 can miss underlying dysregulation. Functional assays—such as the AH50 (alternative pathway hemolysis assay), C3 convertase stability tests, and measurement of soluble C5b‑9—provide a more sensitive readout of complement activity. In the presented patient, these functional studies showed markedly reduced alternative pathway activity (AH50 6 %), but the result must be interpreted in the context of recent plasma exchanges and eculizumab infusion, both of which can artificially depress complement activity. Thus, serial testing before, during, and after therapy is essential to discern true dysregulation from therapeutic suppression.

---

In sum, infection with C. canimorsus can be the proverbial “pressure cap” that opens a fissure in the complement control system. Whether the fissure is widened by a pre‑existing genetic defect or by a transient functional impairment, the end result is the same: overactivated AP, unchecked complement‑mediated endothelial injury, and the catastrophic microvascular thrombosis that defines CM‑HUS. Understanding this interplay is critical for timely diagnosis, appropriate timing of complement inhibition, and for anticipating the need for prophylactic measures against encapsulated organisms during therapy.
