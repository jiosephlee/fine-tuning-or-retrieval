Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Clinical Pearls] What clinical features helped distinguish Capnocytophaga canimorsus sepsis from other causes of sepsis in this patient, and why is rapid identification of this organism important for management?

**Clinical Pearls – Capnocytophaga canimorsus Sepsis**

**Key distinguishing features**

| Feature | Why it points to C. canimorsus |
|---------|--------------------------------|
| **Recent dog bite (upper lip)** | The organism is a normal canine oral commensal. A bite is the classic exposure route. |
| **Rapid onset of fever (>40 °C), hypoglycaemia, neutropenia and marked leukopaenia** | C. canimorsus bacteremia frequently produces fulminant sepsis with profound cytopenias, a pattern less common in other septic pathogens. |
| **Severe coagulopathy with DIC‑like picture (prolonged PT/INR, low fibrinogen, high D‑dimer)** | Although DIC can occur with any septicemia, the combination of pancytopenia and coagulopathy shortly after a dog bite strongly suggests this organism. |
| **Early‑onset renal dysfunction (creatinine rise, proteinuria, hematuria) with no prior kidney disease** | C. canimorsus may trigger endothelial injury and complement activation early, predisposing to AKI before other organ failures. |
| **Normal ADAMTS13 activity (~44 %)** | Rules out TTP; the timing of hemolysis improving after antibiotics and before complement blockade favors a post‑septic TMA rather than primary TTP. |
| **Negative Shiga‑toxin PCR and absence of prodromal diarrhoea** | Excludes STEC‑HUS, the most common TMA in adults; the dog‑bite context shifts suspicion to a zoonotic trigger. |

**Why rapid identification matters**

1. **Antibiotic stewardship** – C. canimorsus is intrinsically susceptible to β‑lactams but is slow‑growing; early confirmation allows de‑escalation to narrower therapy (e.g., penicillin‑G) and limits broad‑spectrum use.

2. **Infection‑triggered complement‑mediated TMA (CM‑HUS)** – The organism’s capsule can activate the alternative complement pathway. Prompt detection permits early initiation of plasma exchange and, critically, complement blockade with eculizumab. Delayed therapy is associated with irreversible renal injury.

3. **Infection‑related safety of complement inhibition** – Complement remains essential for defense against encapsulated bacteria. Knowing the etiologic agent and ensuring clearance before eculizumab minimizes the risk of life‑threatening infections (e.g., Neisseria spp. and further C. canimorsus replication).

4. **Prognostication and monitoring** – Identification of the pathogen guides prognosis (30 % mortality in untreated C. canimorsus sepsis) and informs close surveillance for evolving TMA, guiding timing of renal biopsy and complement testing.

**Bottom line:** A history of dog bite, coupled with rapid septic deterioration, pancytopenia, coagulopathy, and early renal impairment, should prompt suspicion of C. canimorsus. Confirming the organism early not only tailors antibiotics but also alerts clinicians to the possibility of infection‑triggered CM‑HUS, where early complement blockade can spare renal function and improve survival.
