Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

Chapter 5: ## 5. Outcome, Follow‑up and Clinical Lessons

### Platelet count recovery and neuro‑functional outcomes

After the switch to therapeutic fondaparinux on postoperative day 14, the patient’s platelet count entered a steady upward trajectory. Recovery was slow, reflecting the profound degree of consumptive thrombopoiesis mediated by the initially aggressive aHIT antibodies. Platelets rose to 54 ×10⁹/L by day 14, 155 ×10⁹/L by day 62, and 202 ×10⁹/L by day 87, comfortably above the published threshold (>50 ×10⁹/L) considered safe for resuming oral anticoagulation. Concomitantly, the patient’s neurologic status improved from delirium and a left‑parietal infarct on imaging to complete resolution of all focal deficits. Neurological examination at 3 months post‑discharge was normal, and there were no further radiologic changes on follow‑up MRI.

### Serial serotonin‑release assay (SRA) profiles

The initial “indeterminate” SRA on day 5 demonstrated robust serotonin release at low heparin concentrations (0.1 and 0.3 U/mL) and, notably, at 0 U/mL, confirming a heparin‑independent component. The partially resistant IV.3 blockade raised the possibility of atypical Fc‐γIIa receptor engagement. Over the ensuing weeks, serial SRAs revealed a gradual shift toward a more classic heparin‑dependent pattern: by day 124 the assay showed minimal release (≤15 %) in the absence of heparin but a full release at 0.3 U/mL. This transition mirrors the clinical course: as the pathological antibodies waned, the remaining reactivity became attributable to low‑affinity, heparin‑dependent IgG typical of “classical” HIT.

Persistently high SRA reactivity even after overt clinical remission underscores the dissociation between laboratory markers and clinical status in aHIT. The patient remained SRA positive for months, reflecting residual low‑level antibody production. Although the clinical significance of this sustained reactivity is unclear, it cautions against premature cessation of non‑heparin anticoagulation solely on the basis of serologic negativity.

### Lessons for early postoperative thrombocytopenia

1. **Early onset is a red flag**: Seventeen days of pre‑operative catheterization delivered 2 000 U of UFH. The platelets fell within the first 5 days after cardiac surgery, a time period normally too early for classic postoperative HIT. A prior exposure to UFH can precipitate aHIT within 48–72 h, so clinicians should keep this possibility in mind when thrombocytopenia occurs unusually early.

2. **Serological nuance matters**: A strong PF4–ELISA accompanied by an atypical SRA – notably serotonin release at 0 U/mL and partial resistance to IV.3 – should trigger a diagnosis of aHIT. Recognizing this pattern avoids misclassifying the patient as “non‑HIT”, preventing the dangerous continuation of heparin or LMWH.

3. **Therapeutic implications**: Because aHIT antibodies may be heparin‑independent, low‑molecular‑weight heparin or direct thrombin inhibitors are not guaranteed to block platelet activation. In this case, the choice of argatroban was driven by the need for rapid, reversible anticoagulation in the setting of recent stroke and severe thrombocytopenia. The eventual switch to fondaparinux, after AT normalization, proved effective and easier to administer, highlighting the importance of individualized anticoagulant selection.

4. **Monitoring strategy**: Routine coagulation panels (PT, aPTT) are inadequate for tracking aHIT activity. Serial SRA or functional platelet activation assays (e.g., heparin‑induced platelet aggregation) should be employed where available, especially in patients with a strong ELISA but ambiguous clinical picture. However, the cost and availability of such assays limit their routine use; clinical judgment remains paramount.

### Recommendations for clinicians

- **Vigilance for pre‑operative heparin exposure**: A detailed medication and procedural history is essential. Even a single intra‑arterial catheterization dose can set the stage for aHIT.
- **Early laboratory work‑up**: For postoperative thrombocytopenia that occurs <5 days after surgery, obtain a PF4–ELISA and, if positive, request a confirmatory functional assay. Wait for day 5 or 6 to repeat the ELISA if the initial result is equivocal, but do not delay empiric therapy if clinical suspicion is high.
- **Avoid heparin derivatives**: Once aHIT is suspected or confirmed, discontinue all heparin products. Direct thrombin inhibitors or factor Xa inhibitors (fondaparinux, rivaroxaban) are preferable unless contraindicated.
- **High‑dose intravenous Ig**: Consider early high‑dose IV Ig (0.5–1 g/kg) to accelerate platelet recovery in severe aHIT, especially when antiplatelet therapy is needed urgently. Monitor platelet trend closely; a delayed response may indicate a more refractory antibody population.
- **Peri‑operative heparin stewardship**: Use the lowest effective UFH dose during cardiac surgery and limit the duration of exposure. Consider alternatives (bivalirudin) in patients with a history of heparin exposure or unusual laboratory findings.

### Implications for peri‑operative heparin management

The case demonstrates that a single dose of UFH during a coronary catheterisation can elicit a robust autoimmune response that manifests weeks later. In the catheterization–surgery continuum, we must:

- **Coordinate anticoagulation plans** across specialties, ensuring that the last heparin exposure is documented and considered in peri‑operative risk assessment.
- **Plan postoperative antithrombotic prophylaxis** with agents that do not depend on heparin epitope recognition if the patient has a history of early post‑operative thrombocytopenia or is otherwise high risk for HIT.
- **Educate teams** on the temporal patterns of HIT, emphasizing that “early” can be misnomer when prior exposure exists.

### Future directions

Current HIT diagnostic panels are tailored to classic heparin‑dependent antibodies. The emergence of aHIT, especially the subset with partial IV.3 inhibition, highlights a gap in detection. Future assays could:

- **Employ dual‑antigen panels** that simultaneously assess heparin‑dependent and -independent platelet activation.
- **Incorporate IC‑5 blocking or anti‑FcγRIIa functional inhibition** to differentiate between classic and autoimmune HIT.
- **Use high‑throughput flow cytometry** to measure platelet activation in response to patient sera across a gradient of heparin concentrations.
- **Develop point‑of‑care tests** allowing rapid assessment of infectious versus immune platelet activation, facilitating timely therapeutic decisions.

In sum, this case illustrates that severe aHIT can arise from even a single pre‑operative heparin dose, present with early thrombocytopenia and stroke, and display an atypical laboratory profile that evolves over time. Recognition of the early commencement of aHIT, the importance of comprehensive serology, and the potential for partial IV.3 inhibition are essential for ensuring prompt, appropriate treatment and safeguarding patient outcomes.
