Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

### [Clinical Pearls] Long‑term anticoagulation was resumed with apixaban once platelet recovery was achieved. Under what circumstances is it safe to transition a patient with a history of HIT to a direct oral anticoagulant, and what monitoring should be performed?

**Clinical Pearls – When and How to Switch a HIT‑pursued Patient to a DOAC**

1. **Safety criteria for transition**  
   * Platelet count recovery to ≥ 150 × 10⁹/L is the minimum threshold; the patient in the report reached 155 × 10⁹/L on POD 62. *  
   * No evidence of active thrombosis or recent (≤ 48 h) overt bleeding. *  
   * Absence of persistent hypercoagulability: d‑dimer and fibrinogen should have stabilized, and any consumptive coagulopathy (schistocytes, falling fibrinogen) must have resolved. *  
   * No recent high‑dose IVIG or other interventions that alter platelet‑autoimmune dynamics, which might mask lingering antibody activity.  

2. **Why a DOAC is acceptable after HIT**  
   * Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) (apixaban, rivaroxaban, dabigatran, edoxaban) act independently of the platelet‑FcγIIa pathway that fuels HIT antibodies.  
   * Several retrospective and registry studies have shown that, once platelet counts recover, switching to a DOAC is well tolerated and confers comparable stroke and venous‑thromboembolism protection to parenteral agents.  

3. **Monitoring after transition**  
   * Platelet count: check daily for the first 3 days, then every 2–3 days until beyond 10 days to confirm no rebound drop.  
   * Coagulation parameters: routine INR/PTT are not useful; however, serial d‑dimer and fibrinogen (weekly) can reassure that the hypercoagulable cascade has ceased.  
   * Clinical surveillance: Watch for new ischemic symptoms (neurologic deficits, chest pain) or signs of bleeding (bruising, hematuria).  
   * If a patient remains at high thrombosis risk or has fluctuating platelet counts, consider a short bridge back to a parenteral Agent (argatroban or fondaparinux) until stability is documented.

In the reported case, the patient met all of the above criteria—platelets > 150 × 10⁹/L, resolution of stroke‑related thrombosis, normalized fibrinogen and d‑dimer, no hemorrhage—and thus safely resumed apixaban. Regular platelet and hemostatic surveillance reinforced this transition.
