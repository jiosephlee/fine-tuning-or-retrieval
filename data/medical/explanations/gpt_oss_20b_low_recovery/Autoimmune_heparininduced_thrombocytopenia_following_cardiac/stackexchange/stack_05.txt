Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

### [Differential Diagnosis] List the differential diagnoses for profound postoperative thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery and explain why each was considered unlikely in this patient.

**Differential diagnoses for profound postoperative thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery and why they were considered unlikely in this patient**

| Differential | Rationale for consideration | Why it was unlikely in this case |
|--------------|-----------------------------|----------------------------------|
| **Heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia (HIT)** | Classic cause of thrombocytopenia and thrombosis after CPB; platelets fall 5–10 days post‑heparin exposure. | The patient’s platelet count began falling on POD 1, **before the usual 5‑day window**, but he had **pre‑operative UFH exposure** (2000 U during coronary angiography). The strong‑positive PF4‑ELISA and atypical serotonin‑release assay (SRA) with heparin‑independent activation confirmed an **autoimmune HIT (aHIT)** subtype. |
| **Sepsis / septic thrombocytopenia** | Infection can cause consumptive thrombocytopenia and DIC. | Cultures were negative; WBC, hemoglobin and coagulation parameters (PTT, INR, fibrinogen) were normal or mildly elevated (acute‑phase). No clinical signs of sepsis or organ dysfunction, making this unlikely. |
| **Post‑CPB consumptive coagulopathy / DIC** | Cardiopulmonary bypass can trigger platelet consumption and DIC, especially with massive transfusion. | No transfusions were given; peripheral smear showed **no schistocytes early**; hemoglobin remained stable, and fibrinogen was high (expected acute‑phase), not low. Coagulation tests were normal. |
| **Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) / Shiga‑toxin TTP** | TTP produces profound thrombocytopenia, microangiopathic hemolysis, neurologic deficits. | Haptoglobin was elevated, **no schistocytes initially**, and neurological lesions were due to an ischemic infarct, not microangiopathic. ADAMTS13 testing was not indicated because features were absent. |
| **Post‑transfusion purpura (PTP)** | Occurs 5‑10 days after transfusion; severe thrombocytopenia with normal platelet antigen screening. | The patient received **no blood products** pre‑ or post‑operatively, making PTP impossible. |
| **Drug‑induced immune thrombocytopenia (DIIT)** | Drugs such as vancomycin, cefazolin, tranexamic acid, furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole can trigger immune thrombocytopenia. | The onset was **too early** to be caused by intra‑operative drugs (given only on the day of surgery) and by post‑operative drugs (started after POD 1). No temporal relationship or prior exposure or peak drug levels were reported. |
| **Consumptive platelet sequestration (splenic sequestration or mechanical platelet destruction)** | Spleen enlargement or mechanical destruction (e.g., from circulating foreign bodies) can consume platelets. | Peripheral smear showed **no platelet clumps or giant platelets**; the patient had no splenomegaly on physical exam or imaging, and there was no evidence of mechanical injury (e.g., from ventricular assist devices). |
| **Autoimmune thrombocytopenia (ITP)** | Primary ITP can cause severe thrombocytopenia. | ITP would typically present without a temporal association to heparin exposure or thrombosis, and the PFA‑100/platelet‑activation tests would be negative. The presence of a heparin‑independent SRA confirmed an immune complex mediated by PF4‑heparin, not classic anti‑GPIIb/IIIa antibodies. |
| **Acute disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) from major surgery** | Major surgeries can precipitate DIC, causing thrombocytopenia. | Coagulation times were largely normal; fibrinogen was elevated (acute‑phase reactant), not consumed; d‑dimer rose but was interpreted as a marker of HIT‑related hypercoagulability rather than DIC. |
| **Bone marrow suppression (e.g., postoperative myelosuppression or leukemia)** | Post‑operative stress or marrow infiltration can cause low platelet production. | Reticulocyte count and bone‑marrow function were not reported, but the **rapid decline** within a few days, coupled with normal hemoglobin and RBC indices, argues against marrow failure. Also, marrow suppression would not explain the thrombotic stroke. |

**Key reasons the classical causes were ruled out**

1. **Temporal pattern** – Platelet drop began on POD 1, too early for postoperative consumptions but consistent with early HIT triggered by pre‑operative UFH.  
2. **Laboratory confirmation** – Strong PF4‑ELISA positivity with a heparin‑independent SRA pattern and only partial inhibition by IV.3 implicate aHIT.  
3. **Clinical context** – No transfusion, no infection, normal coagulation parameters (except for expected acute‑phase elevation), and a typical thrombotic complication (cerebral infarct) are hallmark of HIT rather than other etiologies.  
4. **Exclusion of drug timing** – Drugs known to cause DIIT were administered either intra‑operatively or post‑operatively but did not align temporally with the thrombocytopenia onset.  

Thus, the patient’s profound early postoperative thrombocytopenia is best explained by **autoimmune heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia**, a severe variant characterized by heparin‑independent platelet activation and partial resistance to Fc‑receptor blockade. All other differential diagnoses were systematically excluded by clinical timing, laboratory profiles, and absence of supporting evidence.
