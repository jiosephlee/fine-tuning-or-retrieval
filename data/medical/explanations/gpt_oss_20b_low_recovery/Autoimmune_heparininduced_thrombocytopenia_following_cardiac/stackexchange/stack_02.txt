Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

### [Clinical Pearls] Describe the classical serologic test sequence used to diagnose HIT and explain why a ‘strongly positive’ PF4/polyanion ELISA alone is insufficient to confirm the diagnosis.

**Clinical teaching answer**

**Why a strong‑positive PF4/polyanion ELISA does *not* confirm HIT**

The diagnostic work‑up of suspected heparin‑induced thrombocytopenia follows a two‑step sequence that mirrors the strengths and limits of the available assays:

| Step | Test | What it measures | Typical cut‑off |
|------|------|------------------|-----------------|
| 1 | **Screening** – PF4/polyanion ELISA (or PF4‑ELISA or HIT‐screen IgG ELISA) | IgG antibodies against the PF4–heparin complex | Optical density (OD) > 0.4 (strong positive ≈ OD > 2.0) |
| 2 | **Confirmatory** – Functional assay (serotonin‑release assay [SRA] or heparin‑induced platelet aggregation) | The ability of patient serum to activate donor platelets in the presence of therapeutic‑dose heparin, and suppression of activation at high‑dose heparin | ≥ 20 % serotonin release at 0.1–0.3 U mL⁻¹ UFH; < 20 % at 100 U mL⁻¹ UFH |

The rationale is that the ELISA is highly sensitive (≈ 95 %) but only *moderately* specific (≈ 60–70 %). A strong‑positive OD (often > 2.0) identifies patients whose antibody titre is high enough to be clinically relevant, but it cannot differentiate between:

* **Classic, heparin‑dependent HIT antibodies** – which require PF4‑heparin complexes to activate platelets;
* **Autoimmune HIT (aHIT) antibodies** – which can activate platelets even in the absence of added heparin (as seen in this case); and
* **Non‑pathogenic antibodies** – such as low‑affinity antibodies, anti‑PF4 antibodies in the absence of heparin, or assay‑interfering substances (e.g., lupus anticoagulant, rheumatoid factor).

Because many of these antibodies bind PF4 in vitro but do **not** trigger platelet activation in vivo, a strong‑positive ELISA alone can over‑estimate the probability of true HIT. In the reported patient, the PF4/polyanion ELISA was 2.2 OD, well above the “strong‑positive” threshold, yet without functional evidence of platelet activation the diagnosis would remain equivocal. Only the subsequent serotonin‑release assay—showing both classic heparin‑dependent activation (strong release at 0.1–0.3 U mL⁻¹ UFH, no release at 100 U mL⁻¹) and heparin‑independent activation (96 % release at 0 U mL⁻¹)—confirmed that the antibodies were clinically relevant and that an autoimmune form of HIT was present.

**Key take‑away**

A high OD on PF4/polyanion ELISA is a *screening* marker, not a definitive diagnosis. Definitive diagnosis requires a functional assay that demonstrates platelet activation in the appropriate heparin‑dependent (or –independent, for aHIT) context. This two‑step sequence safeguards against unnecessary discontinuation of heparin and avoids missing a potentially life‑threatening thrombotic disorder, as illustrated by the autoimmune HIT case described.
