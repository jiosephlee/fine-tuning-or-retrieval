Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

### [Management] High‑dose intravenous immunoglobulin was used in this patient. Explain the proposed mechanism by which IVIg improves platelet counts in autoimmune HIT and why the response was blunted in this case.

**Answer**

High‑dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) is used in autoimmune HIT (aHIT) to arrest the catastrophic cycle of platelet consumption and thrombus formation. The mechanism is multifactorial but centers on blockade of FcγRIIa‑mediated platelet activation.  

1. **FcγRIIa blockade** – IVIg provides a large excess of IgG that competes for the platelet FcγRIIa receptors. In aHIT the offending IgG antibodies cross‑link PF4/heparin complexes (or PF4 alone) on platelets, inducing their activation, aggregation and clearance. By saturating FcγRIIa, IVIg prevents further antibody‑mediated platelet activation, curbing both thrombosis and thrombocytopenia.  
2. **Modulation of the immune response** – IVIg can sequester circulating anti‑PF4 antibodies in serum, increase neonatal Fc receptor‑mediated clearance, and dampen inflammatory cytokine release, all of which help restore platelet counts.  
3. **Platelet “reset”** – Reduction of platelet activation allows restored megakaryocyte output and replenishment of a platelet pool that is no longer being consumed.  

In this case the response to IVIg was markedly blunted. The single factor that explains this is the **partial resistance of the HIT antibodies to FcγRIIa blockade**. The serotonin‑release assay showed that even in the presence of the FcγRIIa‑blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3) there remained 76 % of serotype‑induced platelet activation. This atypical serologic phenotype indicates that the aHIT antibodies were unusually potent, perhaps engaging platelets via additional, non‑FcγRIIa–dependent mechanisms (e.g., direct PF4 binding or complement). Consequently, the IVIg dose that would normally saturate FcγRIIa could not fully suppress platelet activation, delaying and diminishing the rise in platelet counts. The prolonged hypercoagulability (low fibrinogen, rising d‑dimer, schistocytosis) and the delayed platelet recovery until anticoagulation was switched to fondaparinux reflect this incomplete blockade and the extreme severity of the aHIT phenotype.
