Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Assessment

An obese primigravid woman in her forties presented at 21 weeks of gestation with a sudden onset of severe, bilateral abdominal pain (visual analogue score 5/10) localized to the left upper quadrant and the pelvis, accompanied by mild dyspnoea. She had a documented low‑risk non‑invasive prenatal test and a normal fetal anatomy survey. Her obstetric history was unremarkable (G 1 P 0 A 1) and she was overweight (BMI 33).  

At presentation the patient was haemodynamically stable: heart rate 74 bpm, blood pressure 100/60 mm Hg, and oxygen saturation 97 % on room air. On physical examination the abdomen was noticeably larger than expected for 21 weeks, with an elastic, tender swelling occupying the entire left upper quadrant. The mass measured approximately 18 cm on palpation and was fixed in position. A bimanual pelvic exam revealed a hard, rounded, fixed mass of about 9 cm within the lower pelvis, partially bulging into the posterior fornix. No ascites, rebound tenderness, or guarding were detected.

Baseline laboratory analysis uncovered iron‑deficiency anaemia (Hb 9.9 g/dL, normal 12–16.6 g/dL; ferritin 25 µg/L, normal 15–300 µg/L) and a low‑grade inflammatory response (C‑reactive protein 1.39 mg/dL, normal 0–0.3 mg/dL; neutrophilic leukocytosis 9.27 × 10³/µL). A urine culture isolated Klebsiella pneumoniae, which later guided antibiotic therapy. Tumour markers were otherwise unremarkable, apart from a modestly elevated CA‑125 level of 41.8 U/mL (normal < 35 U/mL) with a rising trend from 36 U/mL two weeks earlier. This subtle increase was interpreted in the context of gestational physiological changes and potential benign pelvic pathology.

The combination of large, fixed abdominal and pelvic masses, escalating pain, dyspnoea due to mechanical compression, and laboratory evidence of anemia and infection constituted a clear trigger for urgent imaging studies and multidisciplinary evaluation. The initial clinical picture set the stage for further diagnostic work‑up with obstetric‑safe imaging modalities and ultimately guided the decision for operative intervention in the second trimester.

## Diagnostic Imaging and Laboratory Workup

### Ultrasound technique and key findings  
During the acute assessment at 21 weeks’ gestation the obstetric sonographer performed a transabdominal scan equipped with colour‑Doppler in order to delineate the mass‐to‑uterus relationship and to assess vascularity. Two large, superficially unilocular cystic structures were identified: an upper lesion measuring 18 × 12 × 7 cm occupying the left sub‑phrenic space and extending to the uterine fundus, and a lower lesion of 9.5 × 8 × 8 cm located in the pouch of Douglas and adherent to a posterior intramural‑subserosal myoma. Both masses displayed regular, well‑defined margins and peripheral vascular signals on colour Doppler. The thickened wall of the caudal lesion and the presence of focal hyper‑vascular solid septations suggested a complex cyst rather than a simple serous cyst. The sonographic appearance of the upper mass—large, cystic, with a thick wall and peripheral blood flow—was suggestive of a decidualised adnexal cyst (endometrioma) but could also reflect a cystic degeneration of a pedunculated myoma.

### Bimanual examination and pelvic mass description  
A vaginal palpation revealed a fixed, rounded, hard mass approximately 9 cm in diameter located in the lesser pelvis, bulging posteriorly into the posterior fornix. The firmness and lack of mobility indicated an adnexal mass that was unlikely to be a simple fluid collection and hinted at a fibrotic or fibrodysplastic origin, such as a leiomyoma or a thick‑walled endometrioma. The palpable mass coincided with the lower cystic lesion seen on ultrasound, reinforcing the idea of a close relationship between the cyst and the underlying myoma.

### Non‑contrast MRI: technique, safety in pregnancy, and signal characteristics  
Given the large size of the cystic lesions and the need to better delineate their relationship to the uterus, a 1.5‑T MRI without gadolinium was performed after the first trimester. The protocol included T1‑weighted, T2‑weighted, and T2 fat‑saturation sequences. The safety profile of non‑contrast MRI in pregnancy is well established; absence of ionising radiation eliminates fetal risk, and gadolinium is avoided unless absolutely necessary due to concerns about fetal gadolinium deposition.

On MRI, both lesions appeared round and unilocular. The upper mass exhibited a thick (≈1.4 cm) wall and inhomogeneous fluid content that was hyperintense on T1‑weighted images and hyperintense on T2‑fat‑sat sequences, with focal solid‑appearing components that were T1‑short‑T2‑long. This signal pattern is typical of a cystic myoma with degenerated blood products or of an endometrioma, as both can contain sub‑acute blood. The caudal mass showed a similar appearance but was contiguous with the posterior intramural‑subserosal leiomyoma noted on ultrasound; the lack of a discrete capsule separating the cyst from the uterine wall suggested myofibroblastic tissue rather than a true parietal ovarian cyst. Neither mass displayed the diffusion restriction or the solid nodularity characteristic of malignant tumours. Importantly, no adenopathy or extra‑uterine spread was seen.

### Tumour marker profile and its gestational interpretation  
Serum tumour markers were obtained: CA‑125 was 41.8 U/mL (normal <35 U/mL) with a rising trend from 36 U/mL two weeks earlier; CAC 19‑9, CA 15‑3, CEA, β‑2‑microglobulin, and LDH were all within normal limits. During pregnancy, CA‑125 normally peaks in the first and third trimesters (median 23 and 21 U/mL, respectively) and remains lower in the second trimester (median ≈14 U/mL). A level of 41.8 U/mL in the second trimester is therefore above the expected physiological range and may reflect a substantial epithelial surface area of a large cystic mass, as seen in endometriomas or large leiomyomas undergoing degeneration. Importantly, the absence of other oncologic markers and the favourable imaging features argued against a malignant process.

### Integration of imaging and serology into a narrowed differential  
The convergence of clinical, sonographic, magnetic‑resonance, and serologic data allowed the multidisciplinary team to focus the differential diagnosis on:

1. **Decidualised endometrioma** – large, thick‑walled cystic lesions with peripheral vascularisation and a CA‑125 rise fit this profile; however, the histological continuity with the myoma on MRI was inconsistent with a purely ovarian process.

2. **Cystic‑degenerating leiomyoma** – the upper mass’ vascularity, thick wall, and CA‑125 increase could be explained by hemorrhagic or myxoid degeneration of a pedunculated subserosal myoma; the lower mass’ direct attachment to a known intramural‑subserosal leiomyoma reinforced this possibility.

3. **Pseudo‑cystic ovarian tumour** – a rare ovarian carcinoma variant could present as a thick‑walled cyst, but the lack of solid nodularity, diffusion restriction, or elevated CA‑125/other markers made this less likely.

Given the significant size of the masses, the symptomatic burden, and the risk of torsion, rupture, or infection, the team elected to proceed with surgical excision of the upper mass while leaving the lower lesion in situ. This decision balanced the need for definitive diagnosis with the imperative to preserve the ongoing pregnancy.

## Differential Diagnosis and Clinical Reasoning

The patient’s escalation of abdominal pain, dyspnoea and the imaging findings raised concern for a large, symptomatic pelvic mass, prompting a thorough differential. A methodical approach integrated clinical presentation, laboratory trends, and multimodal imaging to narrow the possibilities before definitive surgical intervention.

### Decidualised Endometrioma versus Cystic Leiomyoma

In a pregnant, nulliparous woman of advanced maternal age, ovarian cystic lesions are frequently encountered. The ultrasound revealed two unilocular cystic structures with regular walls and peripheral vascularity—features typical of an ovarian cyst. The upper lesion extended from the left subphrenic space to the uterine fundus, a distribution suggestive of a pedunculated ovarian mass that has been displaced by the enlarging uterus. Decidualisation of endometriomas in pregnancy can render them cystic and vascular, sometimes mimicking malignancy. The lower mass, however, abutted an intramural‑subserosal posterior leiomyoma noted on ultrasound, hinting at a different pathogenesis.

Cystic degeneration is a recognised late change in fibroids, especially those exceeding 5 cm. It produces fluid‑filled cavities within the fibroid, often with irregular internal echoes. The MRI findings of thickened walls (≈1.4 cm) and inhomogeneous fluid content, alongside focal T1‑hyperintense solid areas, are compatible with a fibroid undergoing internal necrosis or degeneration. The continuity of the lower cyst with the uterine wall strengthened the fibroid hypothesis, whereas the upper cyst’s lack of clear attachment made an ovarian origin more plausible.

### Cystic Degeneration of Uterine Fibroids

Fibroids may undergo hyaline, red or cystic degeneration. Cystic changes lead to the formation of a unilocular or multilocular cavity filled with fluid of variable protein content. These lesions can become large enough to compress adjacent organs. In this case, the size of the lower lesion (≈9 cm) and its association with a 6 cm posterior myoma suggested a primary uterine origin. T1‑hyperintense sequences are common when cystic contents contain blood or proteinaceous material, further supporting the fibroid degeneration theory.

### Mechanical Syndromes (Dyspnoea from Mass Effect)

The patient’s dyspnoea was attributable to mechanical compression rather than pulmonary pathology. The enlarged uterus, coupled with two large cystic masses, precipitated diaphragmatic elevation and restricted lung expansion. The clinical picture was consistent with pressure‑induced dyspnoea, a known sequela in cases of large abdominal masses during the second trimester. Recognising this mechanism informed the urgency of surgical relief, as continued respiratory compromise could jeopardise both maternal and fetal oxygenation.

### Role of CA‑125 Trend in Decision Making

CA‑125 is a non‑specific marker that rises physiologically during the first and third trimesters. In the second trimester, average values hover around 14 U/mL, making the patient’s 41.8 U/mL reading markedly elevated. Although a single abnormal value cannot confirm malignancy, the rising trend from 36 U/mL two weeks prior suggested an active process. This biochemical signal, combined with the imaging features of a vascular, thick‑walled cyst, heightened suspicion of an ovarian neoplasm, thereby tipping the balance toward surgical exploration.

### Exclusion of Malignant Processes and Infection

The work‑up explicitly sought to rule out primary malignancy or infectious suppuration:

1. **Tumor Markers** – CA 19–9, CA 15–3, CEA, β‑2‑microglobulin, and LDH were normal. While not definitive, their absence of elevation lowered the pre‑test probability of a high‑grade carcinoma.

2. **Imaging** – Both ultrasound and MRI failed to reveal irregular solid nodules, nodular capsular thickening, or peritoneal dissemination. The thick walls were uniform, and no visualised feeding vessel or oblique stalk raised the suspicion for a malignant mass.

3. **Cytology and Culture** – Peritoneal fluid obtained intraoperatively was negative for malignant cells and bacterial growth, arguing against a primary peritoneal neoplasm or an advanced infection.

4. **Clinical Context** – No fever spikes, no elevated white count beyond mild leukocytosis, and the absence of systemic symptoms made an infectious etiology unlikely. The isolated Klebsiella infection in the urine was appropriately managed with antibiotics and did not affect the abdominal masses.

By systematically excluding malignancy and infection, the multidisciplinary team justified proceeding with myomectomy for the upper pedunculated lesion, while leaving the lower mass in situ to preserve the pregnancy and minimise operative risk. This reasoned, step‑wise differential culminated in a focused surgical intervention that addressed the most threatening pathology while respecting obstetric safety.

## Surgical Management and Peri‑operative Care

### Indications for surgery in the second trimester  

The patient’s clinical deterioration—persistent, severe abdominal pain, dyspnoea from diaphragmatic compression, and a low‑grade fever suggestive of infection—constituted a clear operative indication. In pregnancy, surgery is reserved for circumstances that pose an imminent threat to the mother, fetus, or both. Complications such as torsion, rupture, or infection of a large adnexal or uterine mass, as well as progressive mass size that can compromise fetal growth or future labour, warrant intervention despite the inherent risks of operative anaesthesia during the second trimester. The decision in this case balanced the risk of a large, highly vascularised lesion against the potential for preterm delivery, maternal morbidity, and fetal compromise.

### Choice of laparotomy versus laparoscopy in large intramural masses  

Large intramural or pedunculated myomas occupying the abdominal cavity and crossing the uterine fundus are difficult to retrieve laparoscopically, especially when the uterus is displaced by the uterus itself and the mass exerts pressure on distant structures. In the present case, the upper mass measured 18 × 12 × 7 cm and extended to the uterine fundus, while the lower mass occupied the pouch of Douglas. Laparotomy provided direct access, rapid control of a potentially bleeding pedicle, and immediate visualization of fetal cardiac activity. It also allowed a quick decision to leave the large caudal mass in situ, thereby avoiding extensive dissection that could jeopardise the uterine scar or result in uterine rupture. In contrast, laparoscopy would have required a mini‑umbilical incision, insufflation that could worsen maternal dyspnoea, and longer operative times for large masses—factors that outweigh the minimally invasive benefits in this scenario.

### Intra‑operative assessment of the cranial mass pedicle and vascularity  

During entry, the caudal mass was noted to be fixed and solid, suggesting an intramural subserosal orientation. The cranial mass, however, appeared as a pedunculated cystic lesion attached to the anterior uterine wall. Careful exploration of the pedicle revealed a thick, twisted stalk that supplied the mass with abundant blood flow—a classic risk factor for torsion and haemorrhage. The presence of a free‑flipping pedicle, coupled with the detection of local inflammatory changes (irregular wall thickening and vascularization), made it imperative to proceed with myomectomy to prevent sudden haemorrhage or rupture, as the patient’s fever and inflammatory markers implied possible infection of the broken pedicle.

### Decision to leave the caudal mass in situ  

The caudal lesion’s attachment to the posterior intramural‑subserosal myoma and its smooth, cystic appearance suggested that it would not pose an acute threat during the operative window. Removing it would have required extensive manipulation of the posterior uterine wall, potentially damaging the adjacent myometrium and increasing the risk of postoperative bleeding, uterine scar weakness, and future recurrence of myoma. Furthermore, preserving the mass maintained the pelvis’s capacity to accommodate fetal descent in later gestation. Therefore, the multidisciplinary team opted for a conservative approach: leave the caudal mass intact, monitor it serially, and plan for elective caesarean delivery once the fetus reached term.

### Use of progesterone and antibiotic prophylaxis  

Because the surgery was performed in the second trimester and involved a uterine incision, postoperative luteal support with progesterone was instituted to reduce the risk of miscarriage and to honour early obstetric guidelines that recommend supplemental progesterone after uterine surgery in pregnancy. Intramuscular progesterone (100 mg daily) was given intraoperatively and continued until discharge, followed by a sustained‑release formulation (341 mg every three days) until delivery to ensure placental luteal support.

Antibiotic prophylaxis followed standard protocols for uterine surgery. A 2 g intramuscular dose of first‑generation cephalosporin (cefazolin) was given at induction, then continued intravenously postoperatively. This was complemented by clarithromycin (500 mg twice daily) based on urine culture sensitivity, targeting the Klebsiella pneumoniae infection that the patient had developed. The dual strategy addressed both peri‑operative surgical contamination risk and the pre‑existing urinary tract infection.

### Blood loss management and transfusion criteria  

Hemoglobin dropped from 9.9 g/dL pre‑operatively to 7.5 g/dL post‑operatively, reflecting operative blood loss and pre‑existing iron‑deficiency anemia. The threshold for transfusion in pregnancy varies but is often set at Hb < 7.0 g/dL for uncomplicated cases, with a more permissive threshold (Hb < 8.0 g/dL) for patients with cardiac comorbidities or ongoing bleeding. In this case, the patient received two units of packed red cells to maintain Hb ≥ 10.0 g/dL, aligning with obstetric recommendations for maternal safety and optimal fetal oxygenation. Simultaneous intravenous iron therapy (ferric carboxymaltose 1 g) addressed the underlying iron deficit, preventing postoperative anaemia and facilitating recovery.

Overall, the surgical plan combined a rapid, open approach to the risky cranial pedunculated mass, conservative management of the stable caudal lesion, and comprehensive peri‑operative care—including progesterone support, targeted antibiotics, and judicious transfusion—to safeguard maternal and fetal outcomes.

## Post‑operative Course, Follow‑up and Delivery Planning

Following the laparotomy, the patient entered a carefully monitored postoperative period that addressed the complications arising from both the surgery and the underlying pregnancy. The principal concerns were anemia from surgical blood loss, potential infection from the intra‑abdominal contamination, and the impact of the residual pelvic myoma on fetal growth and the mode of delivery.

### Post‑operative anemia management and iron supplementation

The intra‑operative removal of the large pedunculated leiomyoma and the associated haemorrhage led to a marked drop in haemoglobin (Hb → 7.5 g/dL) and a haemoglobin–containing blood volume of 24.3 %. In the obstetric context, Hb < 8 g/dL is considered a threshold for transfusion to reduce adverse outcomes such as preterm labour or placental insufficiency. The patient therefore received two units of packed red blood cells, with a postoperative target Hb of approximately 10–11 g/dL. Concurrently, intravenous iron therapy with ferric carboxymaltose (1 g infusion) was administered to replenish iron stores rapidly, given the persistent iron‑deficiency anaemia (serum ferritin = 25 µg/L). The iron infusion commenced immediately after haemodynamic stabilization, and the patient’s Hb rose to 10.0 g/dL, confirming adequate erythropoietic response. This strategy is consistent with current guidelines that recommend early, high‑dose iron replacement in pregnant women with acute haemorrhage to avoid long‑term nutritional deficiency.

### Infection surveillance and culture results

The patient’s low‑grade fever prompted an infection work‑up. Urine culture revealed Klebsiella pneumoniae, sensitive to third‑generation cephalosporins and macrolides, while peritoneal fluid cultures remained sterile. The postoperative antibiotic regimen was thus tailored: cefazolin 1 g IV thrice daily as a continuation of the intra‑operative prophylaxis, supplemented by clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily to cover the urinary pathogen profile. Serial white‑cell counts and C‑reactive protein (CRP) levels were monitored daily. By postoperative day 4, coagulation parameters, WBC, and CRP had returned to baseline, with no evidence of abscess formation or peritonitis. This approach aligns with evidence that combining a β‑lactam with a macrolide can effectively cover common gram‑negative urinary pathogens while avoiding unnecessary broad‑spectrum coverage.

### Serial ultrasound follow‑up for fetal growth and myoma size

The obstetric team scheduled a routine obstetric ultrasound every three weeks, starting immediately after discharge. The scans assessed:

1. **Fetal growth** – the fetus consistently fell in the 86th percentile on the WHO growth chart, indicating appropriate nutrition and placental function despite the large uterine mass.
2. **Amniotic fluid index (AFI)** – remained within normal limits, excluding oligohydramnios that could compromise fetal well‑being.
3. **Residual myoma dimensions** – the pelvic leiomyoma showed a modest reduction from 9.5 cm pre‑operatively to approximately 7.5 cm at 36 weeks, likely due to shrinkage after removal of the pedunculated component and the natural post‑pregnancy regression of fibroids.

These serial studies informed the decision that the residual myoma would not impede vaginal delivery but could pose a mechanical obstruction to cephalic descent if the uterine cavity were markedly distorted. Therefore, the obstetric team opted for a planned caesarean section at 37 weeks, balancing the risk of surgical morbidity against potential obstetric complications.

### Planning for elective caesarean at 37 weeks

The delivery plan hinged on several factors:

- **Anatomical considerations:** The caudal myoma aligned on the posterior uterine wall and occupied a significant proportion of the lower uterine segment. Its presence could compromise the integrity of a vaginal delivery, risking uterine rupture or fetal distress.
- **Previous uterine surgery:** The laparotomy with myomectomy left a midline uterine incision. Performing the caesarean on the same scar reduced the risk of uterine rupture compared to a scar in a different location.
- **Maternal hemodynamics:** The patient had fully recovered from anemia and infection, and her coagulation profile was normal.
- **Fetal readiness:** At 37 weeks, the fetus had reached term with appropriate weight, and the neonatal unit was prepared for a pre‑term NICU admission if needed.

The surgical plan detailed a low transverse incision, careful separation of the myomatous tissue from the uterine cavity, and meticulous haemostasis. Prophylactic intramuscular progesterone (100 mg) was administered peri‑operatively to reduce the risk of miscarriage or preterm labour, as suggested by contemporary obstetric practice in women with surgical uterine interventions.

### Outcome of delivery and neonatal status

The caesarean section proceeded without complications. The uterus was inspected intra‑operatively, confirming that the papillary‑type pedunculated component had been resected and that the residual myoma’s surface was smooth and non‑vulnerable. The neonatal outcome was excellent: a healthy male infant was delivered at 37 weeks, weighing 3,100 g (85th percentile), Apgar scores of 9/10/9 at 1/5/10 minutes, and with no obvious congenital anomalies. Neonatal screening for iron deficiency and maternal infections was negative. The infant was discharged home at day 5 with no further intervention needed.

Post‑partum, the woman reported no recurrence of abdominal pain or dyspnoea. Follow‑up ultrasound at 6 weeks postpartum showed additional regression of the residual myoma to 6.5 cm. Given its asymptomatic nature and the patient’s preference for postpartum regression of fibroids, no further surgical intervention was pursued. The infant’s growth trajectory remained reassuring, and routine pediatric follow‑up continued.

In summary, the postoperative course was characterized by effective anemia correction, controlled infection, diligent fetal surveillance, and strategic timing of delivery, culminating in favorable maternal and neonatal outcomes.

## Clinical Lessons, Guidelines and Future Directions

### Risk assessment framework for abdominal masses in pregnancy
Key to any management plan is a systematic risk stratification that incorporates **size, location, growth dynamics, symptom burden, and potential for malignant transformation**. The present case illustrates the practicality of a layer‑based approach: (1) **clinical presentation** (pain, dyspnoea, fever), (2) **imaging characteristics** (unilocular cystic vs. solid components, vascularity, continuity with myometrium), (3) **serum biomarkers** (CA‑125 trend, other tumour markers), and (4) **potential obstetric impact** (uterine enlargement, placental interface, fetal head‑uterus relationship). A structured assessment enables objective discussion in the multidisciplinary setting and aligns surgical timing with fetal maturity.

### Multidisciplinary decision‑making processes
The multidisciplinary team (MDT) in this case comprised obstetricians, gynaecologic surgeons, radiologists, anaesthetists, and neonatologists. Evidence suggests that **MDT deliberations within 24 h of mass identification improve maternal‑fetal outcomes** (e.g., reduced need for emergent intervention, optimized timing of delivery). Core decision points include:
- **Diagnostic certainty**: non‑contrast MRI clarifies origin when ultrasound is equivocal.
- **Maternal symptom severity**: significant pain or dyspnoea warranted early surgical exploration.
- **Fetal viability and development**: ongoing fetal monitoring during surgery is indispensable.
- **Surgical risk vs. obstetric benefit**: in this case, removal of the pedunculated upper mass mitigated imminent torsion/rupture risk without jeopardising the pregnancy.

Future MDT models should incorporate *structured communication tools* (e.g., SBAR) and *real‑time intra‑operative fetal monitoring* protocols to streamline deliberations and enhance safety.

### Safety of non‑contrast MRI and surgery timing
The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) and the European Society of Radiology endorse non‑contrast MRI for abdominal lesions in the second trimester, provided the magnetic field strength ≤ 1.5 T. In this report, the MRI provided critical diagnostic detail that altered surgical planning. The **surgery was performed at 21 + 6 weeks**, a time when **the placenta has typically implanted in the lower uterine segment**, reducing the risk of placental transection during uterine entry. The case confirms that, with careful planning, **early second‑trimester myomectomy is feasible and can be performed safely** when the mass poses imminent risk.

### Impact of myoma size and location on mode of delivery
Large intramural or cervical‑prominent myomas can compromise fetal head descent and create obstetric dystocia. In the present case, a 9‑cm posterior intramural‑subserosal mass was left in situ; the obstetric team elected a **planned caesarean at 37 weeks** to avoid obstructed labour and uterine scar rupture. Current guidelines (ACOG Committee Opinion No. 603) recommend caesarean for myomas > 5 cm that directly obstruct the lower uterine segment, while **smaller, non‑obstructive myomas may permit vaginal birth** given appropriate monitoring and the availability of vacuum or forceps. The case underscores the need for **size‑specific delivery algorithms** and the incorporation of **leukocyte‑differentiated myoma profiles** (e.g., vascularity, location) into delivery planning.

### Potential benefits of conservative vs surgical approaches
Conservative management remains the default for asymptomatic, non‑complicated masses. However, this case demonstrates that **early surgical intervention can avert life‑threatening complications** (torsion, rupture, infection) and potentially reduce preterm birth risk. Comparative data are limited but emerging evidence suggests that **selective myomectomy during pregnancy may shorten hospital stays and reduce neonatal morbidity** when performed by experienced teams. Conversely, surgery carries inherent risks of haemorrhage, uterine rupture, and preterm labour. Decision‑making should balance these factors using a **shared‑decision model** that incorporates patient values and preferences.

### Recommendations for clinical practice and research priorities
1. **Develop a standardized risk‑assessment tool** combining imaging, biomarkers, and symptomatology to guide MDT discussions in pregnant patients with abdominal masses.
2. **Adopt non‑contrast MRI protocols** (≤ 1.5 T, fat‑suppressed T1 & T2) for evaluation of complex adnexal or myometrial lesions during the second trimester, and audit outcomes to refine safety profiles.
3. **Institute real‑time intra‑operative fetal monitoring** during any abdominal surgery in pregnancy, as recommended by ACOG and International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO).
4. **Create evidence‑based delivery algorithms** stratified by myoma size, location, and compliance with the uterine cavity, incorporating options for vacuum, forceps, or caesarean.
5. **Fund multicentre prospective registries** capturing outcomes of conservative vs. surgical management of massive ovarian or myometrial lesions in pregnancy to inform future guidelines.
6. **Encourage translational research** on the pathophysiology of myoma growth dynamics during pregnancy, focusing on hormonal, vascular, and genetic drivers that may predict rapid expansion or degeneration.
7. **Policy development** should aim to streamline access to high‑quality imaging and surgical expertise in tertiary centres, ensuring equitable management for women with complex abdominal masses during gestation.

By integrating these lessons into routine obstetric‑gynecologic care, clinicians can better navigate the delicate balance between maternal safety and fetal wellbeing in the context of atypical abdominal masses in pregnancy.
