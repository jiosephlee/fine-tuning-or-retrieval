Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# Surgery in Second‑Trimester Pregnancy: When and How to Perform Myomectomy Safely  

> A real‑world case of a 40‑year‑old, obese, first‑time mother who presented at 21 weeks gestation with two gigantic abdominal cystic masses that turned out to be leiomyomas. The story shows how a multidisciplinary team balanced obstetric risk, symptomatic relief, and oncologic doubt to perform a selective upper‑myomectomy and preserve the pregnancy.  

---

## Why This Case Stood Out  

- **Pneumonitic dyspnea** from a 18 × 12 × 7 cm uterine mass pressing on the diaphragm – a clinical picture that demanded urgent action, yet had to be weighed against the safety of the fetus.  
- **Two huge cystic lesions** with ambiguous imaging: the upper mass occupied the entire left subphrenic space, the lower one bloated the pouch of Douglas.  
- **Elevated CA‑125** in a second‑trimester patient (41.8 U/mL) raised suspicion for malignancy, but also overlapped with benign endometriosis/leiomyoma.  
- **Obesity (BMI 33) + iron‑deficiency anemia** added surgical complexity, but the patient’s presentation was stable enough for a planned operative approach instead of an emergency intervention.  

*In short:* A growing, symptomatic, mixed‑solid cystic mass in late second trimester, in a patient with comorbidities that amplified pregnancy‑related morbidity. 

---

## Pre‑operative Work‑up: Getting the Picture Right  

| Step | Rationale | Clinical Take‑away |
|------|-----------|--------------------|
| **Thorough obstetric & gynecologic exam** (bimanual, finger‑pelvis) | Assesses mass mobility, attachment, and possible uterine involvement. | Even in pregnancy, a careful TG exam is indispensable for localizing the lesion. |
| **High‑resolution abdominal US** (colour Doppler) | Differentiates cystic vs solid, identifies vascular pedicles or feeding vessels. | Look for thick walls, peripheral flow, and any apparent connection to the uterus. |
| **Non‑contrast MRI (1.5 T)** | Provides superior soft‑tissue characterization; confirms continuity with the uterine myometrium. | In pregnancy, gadolinium is avoided; plain MRI is safe after the first trimester. |
| **Tumor markers (CA‑125, CA19‑9, CEA, LDH, β2‑microglobulin)** | Trend analysis rather than single cutoff: rising CA‑125 warrants closer surveillance. | A modest increase (≈35–42 U/mL) in a second‑trimester patient is concerning but not diagnostic. |
| **Baseline labs (CBC, iron studies, cultures)** | Screen for infection (Klebsiella pneumoniae was found in urine) and anemia severity. | Treat iron deficiency aggressively; pre‑operative planning must include possible transfusion. |

---

## When to Operate: The Decision Matrix  

| Symptom/Feature | Risk Factor | Rationale to Operate |
|-----------------|-------------|----------------------|
| **Severe abdominal pain** (VAS 5 upper and lower) | Pain refractory to analgesics | Uncontrolled pain increases risk of preterm labor & fetal compromise. |
| **Dyspnea** from diaphragmatic compression | > 18 cm mass occupying left subphrenic space | Mechanical ventilation of the uterus is a known trigger for preterm labor. |
| **Potential torsion/necrosis** (twisted pedicle on imaging, low‑grade fever) | Lesion is highly vascularised and pedunculated | Risk of ischemia‑related rupture → emergency surgery. |
| **Rapid growth or increasing CA‑125** | Rising trend (from 36 → 41 U/mL) | Suspects malignant change; earlier intervention favored. |
| **Anemia + infection** | Hb ≈ 10 g/dL pre‑op, Klebsiella bacteriuria | Surgical cure may alleviate anemia via removal of a bleeding source, and antibiotics can be optimally targeted. |

**Bottom line:** If the mass is **symptomatic, has a pedicle or shows signs of impending torsion/necrosis, or is rapidly growing,** proceed with surgical removal—ideally early in the second trimester when uterine size still allows safe access and postoperative recovery.

---

## Operative Planning: How to Do It Safely  

1. **Incision**  
   - *Longitudinal xipho‑pubic cut* was chosen to expose both the upper mass and the uterus, while keeping the uterine incision on the lower segment if needed.  
   - For smaller lesions or those near the lower uterine segment, a *lower segment transverse (LS)** or *Hasson laparoscopy* may be preferable.

2. **Fetal Monitoring**  
   - Continuous intra‑operative fetal heart rate (FHR) monitoring: baseline, every 5 min, and with fetal scalp electrodes if needed.  
   - If FHR shows > 120 bpm or > 10 beats/min deceleration, brace the uterus, stop manipulation, and consider abortive measures.

3. **Gestational Support**  
   - **Intra‑operative progesterone** 100 mg IM (or 400 mg IM for severe preterm risk) immediately upon incision.  
   - Post‑op **progesterone caproate** 341 mg IM q3 days until elective cesarean (to reduce preterm labor risk).  

4. **Antibiotic Prophylaxis**  
   - **Pre‑op**: Cefazolin 2 g IV 30 min before incision.  
   - **Post‑op**: Cefazolin 1 g IV q8 h + Clarithromycin 500 mg PO BID (per urine culture).  
   - Rationale: Treat Klebsiella infection, reduce surgical site infection risk.

5. **Anemia Management**  
   - **Pre‑op**: Iron‑replacement (oral or IV) tailored to Hb.  
   - **Intra‑op**: Directly visualize bleeding points, control vasculature, and use absorbable sutures for hemostasis.  
   - **Post‑op**: Ferric carboxymaltose 1 g IV (single dose) + transfusion if Hb < 7 g/dL, with close monitoring.

6. **Surgical technique** (Upper‑myomectomy)  
   - Identify the pedicle, clamp with a double clamp, then transect with a scalpel or electrocautery, ensuring the uterine cuff is closed with a single absorbable suture to prevent future uterine defect.  
   - Avoid enucleation of the lower cystic mass due to its deep pelvic location and risk of uterine scar rupture.

7. **Post‑op surveillance**  
   - Serial US (every 3 weeks) to track fetal growth & amniotic fluid.  
   - Monitor for residual myoma size (it dropped from 9.5 cm to 7.5 cm by 37 weeks).  

---

## Outcomes & Take‑Home Messages  

- **Maternal**: No intra‑operative demise, anemia resolved with iron & transfusion, infection cleared.  
- **Obstetric**: Fetal growth appropriate (86 % percentile), elective cesarean at 37 weeks on the same surgical scar.  
- **Neonatal**: Excellent outcome, small baby with no complications.  
- **Long‑term**: Residual myoma regressed post‑partum, no further surgery planned.

### Key Pearls

- **Early second‑trimester surgery** is feasible and can avert catastrophic complications if the mass is symptomatic or shows signs of torsion/necrosis.  
- **Non‑contrast MRI** remains the gold standard for anatomical mapping when US is equivocal.  
- **Fetal monitoring** throughout surgery is mandatory; abortive measures (uterine support, tocolysis, emergency cesarean) must be ready.  
- **Progesterone supplementation** (both intra‑ and post‑op) can mitigate the risk of preterm labor triggered by uterine manipulation.  
- **Aggressive anemia management** (IV iron, transfusion as needed) is crucial because blood loss during myomectomy can be significant and may compound pregnancy‑related anemia.  
- **Selective excision** (upper mass only) can preserve the uterus and reduce postoperative scar risk, while leaving a non‑obstructive lower mass in situ.  

---

### Bottom Line

When the picture is clear—symptomatic, at risk for torsion/necrosis, or rapidly enlarging—don’t wait for 30‑proportions of “obstetric safety” to bite. A well‑planned, multidisciplinary, and obstetric‑surgery hybrid approach, as illustrated, can lead to *excellent maternal and neonatal outcomes* even in the second trimester.
