Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# Endometrioma vs. Fibroid: The Role of Bimanual Examination and Differential Diagnosis

## The Hook: Two Huge Abdominal Giants, One Epic Misdiagnosis

We were all paid to read that “clinical intuition is a great guide in obstetrics.” That turned out to be very true when a 26‑week pregnant patient presented with a *large* left‑upper quadrant lump and a *large* lower pelvic mass, both oozing out of the uterus like a bad dream. While ultrasound and non‑contrast MRI tipped us toward decidualised endometrioma, the intra‑operative findings told us otherwise: a pedunculated cystic leiomyoma and a cystic‑degenerating intramural myoma were the real culprits. How did we salvage the situation? By relying on a meticulous bimanual exam to parse texture, mobility, and anatomical relationships before we threw the knife—or, better yet, a surgical plan—into action.

## Why the Physical Exam Still Matters in the Age of High‑Tech Imaging

1. **Speed and safety** – With a pregnant patient, time is (and oxygen is) precious. A quick bimanual exam can help rule out torsion or necrosis that would warrant immediate surgery, even before imaging is ready.
2. **Localization** – Imaging can sometimes mis‑assign a mass to the ovary when it’s actually uterine; the bimanual exam can confirm whether a lump truly moves with uterine contractions or stays fixed relative to the cervix.
3. **Texture and consistency** – A hard, fixed, non‑compressible mass is more suspicious for a fibroid; a soft, doughy, easily compressible one leans toward an ovarian cyst—or a decidualised endometrioma—especially when it’s hugging the ovary.

The lesson: never let a “focused” imaging study dethrone the basics of lay‑hemorrhage examination.

## The Case in Detail: Two Giant Bills in the Abdomen

| Mass | Location | Imaging Findings | Bimanual Findings | Final Diagnosis |
|------|----------|-----------------|-------------------|-----------------|
| **Upper** | Left sub‑phrenic, down to fundus | 18 × 12 × 7 cm, unilocular, regular margins, peripheral vascularisation, moderate enhancement on T2 fat‑sat | Firm, fixed, non‑mobile, not tethered to uterine cavity | Pedunculated, cystic decidual‑fibroid (leiomyoma) |
| **Lower** | Pouch of Douglas, attached to uterine posterior wall | 9.5 × 8 × 8 cm, unilocular, cystic degeneration of an intramural‑subserosal myoma | Soft, doughy, slightly mobile, partially bulging into the posterior fornix | Cystic‑degenerating intramural leiomyoma |

Because CA‑125 was only “slightly” raised (41.8 U/mL), there was no clear biochemical clue. So, we turned to the bimanual exam: the key was the *relationship* of each mass to the uterine cavity and cervix. The upper mass, though suggestive of an ovarian cyst on ultrasound, was tethered to the fundus and moved in synchrony with uterine activity. The lower mass, by contrast, sat beneath the posterior fornix and could be felt to be independent, albeit attached to the myometrium.

## Imaging vs. Physical Examination: When They Agree, When They Conflict

- **Ultrasound** is excellent for internal structure but can be confounded by the gravid uterus pushing organs in unusual positions.
- **Non‑contrast MRI** offers excellent soft‑tissue contrast but still may mis‑identify a cuff‑shaped myoma as an ovarian cyst if the attachment is subtle.
- **Bimanual exam** is the gold standard for determining *fixed vs. mobile* and *compressibility vs. firmness*, attributes that strongly differentiate fibroids (hard, non‑compressible) from cysts (soft, compressible).

## Why the Pelvic Myoma Was Left In Situ

Even though the upper fibroid was removed, the lower one was left untouched. The reasons were multifold:

- **Maternal safety** – The uterine scar from myomectomy increases the risk of rupture, especially if the scar is near the cervix or lower uterine segment.
- **Obstetric planning** – A massive posterior myoma would impede safe vaginal delivery; planning a cesarean on the same scar lower the risk of uterine rupture during labor.
- **Timing** – The patient had an excellent fetal and maternal course; postponing the second surgery allowed for natural regression of the residual myoma postpartum.

## Management Take‑aways

- **Perform a detailed bimanual exam** before any imaging, especially when dealing with large adnexal‑appearing masses in pregnancy.
- **Use imaging to confirm but not replace** the physical exam. Don’t let ultrasound or MRI dictate the surgical plan without clinical correlation.
- **Weigh surgery vs. expectant management** carefully: treat torsion, necrosis, or hemorrhagic complications aggressively; otherwise, observe with serial ultrasounds.
- **Plan delivery based on mass location**—large uterine myomas are best delivered by cesarean, especially if they obstruct the lower uterine segment or are near the cervix.
- **Post‑operative care**: aggressive iron repletion and consider progesterone to mitigate preterm labor risks.

## Clinical Pearls

- A *fixed, hard* mass that moves with uterine contractions is likely a fibroid, even if it sits in the adnexal region on imaging.
- A *mobile, soft* mass that doesn’t tug on the uterine cavity suggests an ovarian cyst or decidualised endometrioma.
- The *degree of vascularity* on Doppler can help differentiate a leiomyoma (often low‑to‑moderate flow) from a highly vascular ovarian cyst (especially endometrioma).

## Take‑home Messages

- **Bimanual examination remains a cornerstone** in the evaluation of abdominal/pelvic masses during pregnancy.
- **Integrate physical findings with imaging** to avoid misdiagnosis and unnecessary surgery.
- **Tailor surgical timing and approach** to balance maternal safety, fetal well‑being, and optimal obstetric outcomes.
- **Pregnancy is not a “do‑not‑treat” period**—when a mass threatens maternal or fetal life, intervene based on the full clinical picture, not just imaging.
