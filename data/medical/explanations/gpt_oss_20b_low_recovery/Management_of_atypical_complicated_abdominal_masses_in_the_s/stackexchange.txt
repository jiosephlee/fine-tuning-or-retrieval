Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Clinical Pearls] What are the main imaging modalities safely used to evaluate adnexal or uterine masses during pregnancy, and why is gadolinium-enhanced MRI usually avoided?

**Imaging of adnexal/uterine masses in pregnancy**

| Modality | Why it is safe and useful | Typical findings |
|----------|--------------------------|------------------|
| **Transvaginal & transabdominal ultrasonography (with colour Doppler)** | Non‑ionising, real‑time, bedside, no risk to fetus; can assess size, cystic versus solid components, vascularity, and relationship to the uterus. | Large cystic adnexal masses → anechoic loci with or without internal echoes; leiomyomas → hypoechoic, well‑circumscribed, often with spongy texture and limited internal flow on Doppler. |
| **Non‑contrast MRI (≤ 1.5 T)** | MRI has no ionising radiation. In the second and third trimesters, a 1.5‑T field strength with no gadolinium is considered safe; the fetus is exposed only to static fields and gradients. It offers superior soft‑tissue contrast, multiplanar capability, and precise delineation of the distance between a mass and the uterine cavity or placenta. | T1‑hypointense, T2‑hyperintense cystic lesions with possible complex fluid; focal T1‑hyperintensity suggests hemorrhage or proteinaceous fluid; solid components appear hyperintense on T2‑fat‑sat or T1‑weighted sequences. |
| **Optional: CT with low‑dose protocols** | Only used when other modalities are inconclusive and the clinical question cannot wait for MRI (e.g., emergency evaluation of haemorrhage). Short‑time exposure, but the potential benefit must outweigh fetal radiation risk; generally avoided unless absolutely necessary. | Gives excellent anatomic detail of large cysts or solid masses but offers no additional diagnostic value over MRI for adnexal masses. |

---

### Why gadolinium‑enhanced MRI is usually avoided

1. **Potential fetal toxicity** – Gadolinium chelates can cross the placenta and accumulate in fetal tissues. Animal studies have linked gadolinium exposure to renal dysfunction, neurotoxicity, and, rarely, growth restriction.  
2. **No reliable safety data in humans** – Long‑term follow‑up of infants exposed in utero has raised concerns about developmental effects; thus, the prevailing practice is “do, if essential, do not” (American College of Radiology guideline).  
3. **Contrast‑free MRI is diagnostically sufficient** – The cases presented in the review (e.g., the 21‑week patient with two large cystic masses) were adequately characterized on non‑contrast T1/T2 fat‑suppressed sequences. Adding contrast would not markedly improve differentiation between decidualised endometriomas and cystic leiomyomas, yet it would expose the fetus to an unproven risk.  
4. **Alternative imaging** – If a contrast agent were truly necessary (e.g., to differentiate a highly vascular tumour), the preferred approach is a delayed, low‑dose CT or a dedicated angiographic study, not a gadolinium MRI.

**Bottom line:** Ultrasonography and non‑contrast MRI are the gold standards for safely evaluating adnexal or uterine masses during pregnancy. Gadolinium‑enhanced MRI is avoided because the potential fetal risks outweigh the marginal diagnostic gain.

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a pregnant patient with a large abdominal cystic mass, how would you interpret a mildly elevated CA‑125 level, and what other conditions could explain this finding?

**Clinical Teaching Answer**

In pregnancy, a CA‑125 level that is only modestly above the laboratory upper limit is often a physiologic reflection of the gestation rather than an ominous sign of malignancy. In the case reported by De Nola *et al.*, a CA‑125 of 41.8 U/mL (cut‑off 35 U/mL) was noted at 21 weeks’ gestation and had risen from 36 U/mL two weeks earlier. Two points should guide interpretation:

1. **Pregnancy‑associated elevations** – CA‑125 peaks in the first trimester (median ≈ 23 U/mL) and again in the third trimester (≈ 21 U/mL). Values above 35 U/mL are uncommon in the second trimester, so any increase should prompt a focused work‑up, but the absolute rise is modest and parallels the gradual rise seen in this patient’s serial measurements.

2. **Benign conditions that raise CA‑125** – endometriosis, adenomyosis, pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, and, notably, uterine leiomyomas can elevate CA‑125. The authors explicitly cite that myomas may produce CA‑125 levels below 19 U/mL on average, yet individual variability is large. In this patient, imaging showed two large cystic lesions with thick, vascularised walls and focal solid components – features compatible with cystic‑degenerating leiomyomas as ultimately confirmed by pathology.

**Other benign explanations for a mild CA‑125 rise in pregnancy**

| Condition | Typical CA‑125 range | Why it fits the case |
|-----------|----------------------|----------------------|
| **Decidualised endometrioma** | 19–80 U/mL | Ultrasound described a unilocular cystic mass; CA‑125 was mildly increased. |
| **Cystic degeneration of a leiomyoma** | 10–40 U/mL | MRI showed thick walls, mixed T1/T2 signal, and focal solid areas—classical for cystic‑degenerating myoma. |
| **Pelvic inflammatory disease** | 35–200 U/mL | The patient had a low‑grade fever and Klebsiella bacteriuria, but peritoneal fluid was culture‑negative and inflammation markers were only modestly elevated. |
| **Adhesive disease (e.g., tubo‑ovarian abscess)** | 35–200 U/mL | No imaging evidence of abscess; CA‑125 modest. |
| **Early pregnancy (first or third trimester)** | 15–30 U/mL | Not applicable – patient was in second trimester. |

**Clinical Reasoning in the Report**

The multidisciplinary team weighed the slight CA‑125 rise against imaging data: two large, well‑circumscribed cystic masses with vascularisation and solid foci. Serial laboratory trends (41.8 U/mL increasing from 36 U/mL) suggested a growing process but remained within the benign range. Because the masses were symptomatic (pain, dyspnea) and posed a risk of torsion/necrosis, the team opted for surgical exploration. The intra‑operative findings confirmed that the upper mass was a pedunculated, cystic leiomyoma, while the lower mass was an intramural‑subserosal myoma with cystic degeneration. The pathology was benign.

**Bottom Line**

- A mildly elevated CA‑125 in the second trimester should be interpreted with caution; serial measurements and imaging are essential.
- Benign entities—especially uterine leiomyomas (cystic degeneration), decidualised endometriomas, and pelvic inflammatory disease—can mimic a malignancy‑driven rise.
- In the presented case, the imaging and clinical context supported a benign cystic‑degenerating leiomyoma as the source of the CA‑125 elevation.

### [Pathophysiology] Explain the pathophysiological mechanisms by which uterine leiomyomas can grow during pregnancy, including the reported weekly percent change in size across trimesters.

**Pathophysiology of pregnancy‑associated growth of uterine leiomyomas**

Uterine leiomyoma growth during pregnancy is driven by two interrelated mechanisms:

| Mechanism | How it operates in pregnancy | Evidence from case |
|-----------|-----------------------------|--------------------|
| **Hormonal stimulation** | Estrogen and progesterone levels rise dramatically during gestation. These steroids bind to receptors on myometrial smooth‑muscle cells, promoting DNA synthesis, cellular proliferation, and protein synthesis. Enhanced progesterone also up‑regulates anti‑apoptotic pathways, allowing leiomyoma cells to survive longer. | The patient’s leiomyomas were described as highly vascularised and rapidly enlarging (“pedunculated, atypical cystic and taut‑elastic”), consistent with a hyper‑responsive steroid milieu. |
| **Ischaemic‐necrotic changes leading to cystic degeneration** | Rapid growth can outstrip neo‑vascularisation. Hypoxia induces glycolytic metabolism, lactic acid production, and eventually cell death. The necrotic core is filled with fluid, producing a cystic component that can appear as a large, unilocular cyst on ultrasound/MRI. | MRI showed a large, thick‑walled cystic mass with focal hyperintense solid components—classic imaging of “cystic degeneration” associated with rapid tumor expansion. |
| **Mechanical expansion of the uterus** | As the uterus enlarges, it presses against surrounding tissues, distending fibroids and potentially compressing their feeding vessels. This mechanical stretch further stimulates steroid receptors and may increase fibroid volume. | The upper mass extended from the left sub‑phrenic space to the uterine fundus at 21 weeks, implying that the expanding uterus contributed to its apparent size. |

---

### Weekly percent change in leiomyoma size across trimesters

A large multicenter study (Zhao et al.) quantified the growth rates:

| Trimester | Average weekly growth | Statistical significance |
|-----------|-----------------------|--------------------------|
| 1st → 2nd | **6.12 % ± 0.39 %** | < 0.001 |
| 2nd → 3rd | **3.10 % ± 0.39 %** | < 0.05 |
| Post‑partum (6 weeks) | **–44.25 % ± 36.05 %** | < 0.05 |

Thus, leiomyomas enlarge most rapidly in the first trimester (≈6 % per week), slow down in the second, and grow more modestly in the third. After delivery, the majority regress markedly.  

In the present case, the patient had a 21‑week gestation when a large pedunculated leiomyoma (≈18 × 12 × 7 cm) was noted. Although exact serial measurements were not reported, the clinical picture (rapid pain escalation and dyspnoea) is compatible with the > 6 % weekly increase observed in the first trimester, extended into the second due to ongoing hormonal stimulation and mechanical stretch.

---

**Take‑home point:**  
Pregnancy‑induced elevation of estrogen and progesterone, combined with rapid uterine expansion and transient ischaemic‑necrotic changes, drives leiomyoma growth most aggressively during the first trimester (~6 %/week) and more slowly thereafter (~3 %/week). Recognizing these dynamics informs timing of surgical intervention and counseling of pregnant patients with symptomatic fibroids.

### [Management] Which clinical and surgical factors should be considered when deciding to perform myomectomy during pregnancy versus waiting until postpartum?

**When to Operate on a Pregnant Uterine Leiomyoma?**

In the case described, the team elected to resect the upper pedunculated leiomyoma at 21 w + 6 d while leaving the large intramural‑subserosal mass in situ until the postpartum period. The decision hinged on a handful of interrelated clinical and surgical factors that should guide any future management plan.

| **Factor** | **Why it matters** | **How it applied in the case** |
|------------|---------------------|--------------------------------|
| **Gestational age** | Early pregnancy carries the greatest risk of miscarriage and is a period of high uterine plasticity; later trimesters limit surgical access but allow greater fetal maturity if the operation is prolonged. | Surgery performed in the early second trimester (≈ 22 weeks) when the uterus is still relatively small and the risk of preterm delivery, though present, is lower compared with a third‑trimester operation. |
| **Symptom burden (pain, dyspnea, infection)** | Overt, progressive symptoms press the decision toward immediate relief to preserve maternal and fetal wellbeing. | The patient had severe upper‑quadrant pain and dyspnea from mechanical compression; a twisted, necrotic pedunculated mass produced low‑grade fever, pressuring the team to intervene. |
| **Mass characteristics (size, location, vascularity, stalk)** | A pedunculated, vascular mass with a demonstrable stalk is at high risk for torsion, infarction, or rupture—conditions that recur intra‑uterinely and can precipitate acute abdomen or hemorrhage. | Ultrasound/MRI revealed a 18 × 12 × 7 cm pedunculated leiomyoma with a thin, twisted stalk—high‑risk for torsion. The intramural mass (≈ 6 cm) was completely adherent to the posterior uterine wall and had no stalk, making it low‑risk for torsion. |
| **Oncologic suspicion (tumor markers, imaging features)** | A trend toward elevated CA‑125 or atypical imaging features raises the differential for malignancy, tipping the scales toward definitive surgical evaluation. | CA‑125 rose from 36 to 42 U/mL; MRI showed thick walls, mixed fluid‑ and solid‑components—concern for malignancy. |
| **Risk of future obstetric complications** | Large intramural leiomyomas can impede fetal head descent, cause obstructed labor, or increase cesarean-section (CS) rates. | The caudal mass occupied the pouch of Douglas and was predicted to block the birth canal; this led to a planned CS at 37 weeks, but postponing its removal reduced operative risk during pregnancy. |
| **Operative morbidity (bleeding, uterine injury, hospital stay)** | Myomectomy in pregnancy is associated with higher blood loss and longer surgery than postpartum surgery. Limiting the procedure to the “most dangerous” lesion aims to keep operative time and hemorrhage low. | Only the pedunculated myoma was excised (≈ 2 h 25 min, 1 g Hb drop); the intramural myoma was left, avoiding a potentially prolonged, bleeding‑prone procedure and reducing the risk of uterine rupture during the subsequent CS scar. |
| **Multidisciplinary consensus and informed consent** | The decision must balance obstetric, surgical, and anesthesia risks; patient preferences regarding future fertility and timing of surgery are essential. | A multidisciplinary team (obstetrics, gynecology, surgery, anesthesia) reviewed all data; the patient gave written informed consent, acknowledging the trade‑offs of pregnancy‑time surgery versus postpartum. |
| **Postpartum regression potential** | Uterine leiomyomas frequently shrink after delivery due to hormonal withdrawal; small, asymptomatic masses may resolve spontaneously. | The residual intramural mass decreased from 9 cm to 7.5 cm fetus‑time; the patient opted for observation post‑delivery, reflecting the expected postpartum regression. |

**Practical Take‑Home Points**

1. **Immediate surgery is justified** when a leiomyoma is symptomatic, torsion is imminent or confirmed, or there is strong suspicion of malignancy.  
2. **Intra‑uterine location matters**: pedunculated, subserosal or intraperitoneal masses risk torsion; intramural lesions without a stalk pose less acute risk.  
3. **Gestational age influences safety**: early second trimester offers the best balance between maternal risks and fetal viability.  
4. **Aim to limit operative extent**—excision only of the high‑risk lesion minimizes blood loss and preserves uterine integrity for subsequent delivery.  
5. **Postpartum management** should be considered for large, asymptomatic intramural myomas, particularly when they are expected to regress and when delaying surgery lowers risks of bleeding, uterine rupture, or preterm labor.

In summary, the decision to perform myomectomy during pregnancy versus awaiting the puerperium is a nuanced calculus that incorporates gestational age, symptomatology, mass morphology, oncologic concern, obstetric impact, operative risk, and patient values—all of which were carefully weighed in the case described.

### [Morning Report] Describe the perioperative management steps that should be taken to minimize fetal risk when a laparotomy is performed for a uterine mass in the second trimester.

**Perioperative Management of a Second‑Trimester Laparotomy for a Uterine Mass**  
*(Modelled on the De Nola et al. case, 21 w + 6 d gestation)*  

1. **Multidisciplinary Planning**  
   * Arrange a team of obstetrician‑gynecologist, surgeon, anesthesiologist, neonatologist and nursing staff.  
   * Obtain written informed consent that includes the risks of fetal loss, preterm delivery, uterine rupture and hysterectomy.  

2. **Pre‑operative Evaluation**  
   * **Baseline fetal assessment** – transabdominal Doppler and cardiotocography at least 48 h before surgery to confirm viability.  
   * **Baseline maternal labs** – CBC, coagulation profile, serum electrolytes, renal and liver function, blood type and cross‑match.  
   * Treat anemia aggressively; in the case described, iron infusion (ferric carboxymaltose) and two blood units were required pre‑op.  
   * **Infection screening** – urine culture, complete blood count; treat positive cultures pre‑op.  

3. **Anesthetic Considerations**  
   * Use a continuous epidural or combined spinal‑epidural to provide adequate analgesia while maintaining maternal haemodynamics.  
   * Avoid large uterine manipulations; position the patient semi‑orthostatic to reduce diaphragmatic compression.  
   * Monitor maternal oxygen saturation continuously; maintain SpO₂ > 95 %.  

4. **Intra‑operative Fetal Surveillance**  
   * **Baseline fetal heart rate (FHR) monitoring** before incision.  
   * **Continuous or intermittent fetal auscultation** (through abdominal Doppler) throughout the case.  
   * In our patient, the team verified fetal activity before opening the abdomen, as recommended by Zhao et al.  

5. **Surgical Technique**  
   * **Maneuver to minimize uterine manipulation** – the cervix should be held gently to avoid stimulation of the cervix or the uterus.  
   * **Laparotomy** – a longitudinal xipho‑pubic incision (as performed) provides wide exposure and limits traction on the uterus.  
   * **Gentle removal of any pedunculated mass** – as the upper lesion was herniated and vascularised, the team excised it with minimal traction, closing the stalk with a single absorbable suture to avoid uterine wall disruption.  
   * **Leave non‑pedunculated intramural myoma in situ** – the caudal mass was retained to avoid uterine scar damage and potential rupture at delivery.  

6. **Immediate Post‑operative Care**  
   * **Progesterone support** – intramuscular 100 mg progesterone intra‑op and daily post‑op, followed by intramuscular progesterone caproate 341 mg q 3 days to reduce preterm labour risk, as given in the case.  
   * **Antibiotic prophylaxis** – cefazolin intra‑op, then cefazolin + clarithromycin for Klebsiella culture sensitivity.  
   * **Pain control** – paracetamol and opioid sparingly to avoid fetal respiratory depression.  
   * **Monitor for bleeding** – serial hemoglobin, uterine bleeding, and signs of uterine atony.  
   * **Early mobilization** in the recovery room.  

7. **Post‑operative Follow‑up**  
   * **Serial fetal growth scans every 3 weeks** to detect growth restriction; the case showed the fetus at the 86th percentile.  
   * **Serial maternal labs** to monitor anemia and inflammatory markers.  
   * **Plan delivery** – in this patient, an elective caesarean at 37 w on the same scar was chosen because the residual myoma would impede vaginal descent.  

8. **Key Principles to Minimize Fetal Risk**  
   * Keep the surgery short and blood loss < 500 mL (the case lasted 2 h 25 min with controlled transection).  
   * Preserve uterine integrity; avoid uterine incision or extensive manipulation.  
   * Maintain maternal oxygenation and hemodynamic stability.  
   * Provide antenatal corticosteroids if preterm delivery is imminent (not used in this case because the patient delivered at 37 w).  

**Take‑home point:**  
During a second‑trimester laparotomy for a uterine mass, the optimal balance between maternal benefit and fetal safety is achieved by pre‑operative anemia correction, meticulous fetal monitoring, gentle surgical technique that preserves uterine integrity, and adequate postoperative hormonal support to prevent preterm labor. The De Nola et al. case exemplifies this approach with a successful maternal and neonatal outcome.

### [Management] How does the presence of a pedunculated leiomyoma with a twisted stalk influence the urgency of surgery and what intraoperative precautions are necessary?

**Pedunculated leiomyoma with a twisted stalk → urgent surgery, but with strict intra‑operative safeguards**

1. **Why it makes surgery urgent**
   * A twisted stalk is the classic precursor to *torsion*, which can rapidly compromise arterial inflow and venous outflow.  
   * In this case, the MR and intra‑operative findings showed a *highly vascularised, taut‑elastic* leiomyoma whose stalk was already *twisted and leaking*.  
   * The patient had worsening abdominal pain, dyspnoea from mass effect, and a low‑grade fever—suggestive of inflammation or early necrosis.  
   * Complications such as *rupture, ischemic necrosis, sepsis, or hemorrhage* would endanger both mother and fetus, so the multidisciplinary team elected immediate laparotomy.

2. **Intra‑operative precautions**
   * **Early fetal assessment** – continuous monitoring of fetal heart rate before, during, and after the laparotomy (as the team did with serial trans‑vaginal US).  
   * **Gentle traction on the stalk** – the tumor was mobilised with a double clamp and only then divided, minimizing traction‑related bleeding.  
   * **Meticulous haemostasis** – the exposed uterine base was closed with a single absorbable suture; the surgical field was irrigated, and the patient received intra‑operative cefazolin prophylaxis (2 g) to reduce infection risk.  
   * **Prophylactic progesterone** – intramuscular 100 mg at the time of surgery and daily thereafter to support uterine quiescence.  
   * **Avoidance of massive blood loss** – the team used an experienced surgical team, limited operative time (≈ 2 h 25 min), and prepared for transfusion (the patient required two units of packed RBCs post‑op).  
   * **Limited extent of surgery** – only the pedunculated mass was removed; the sizeable caudal myoma was left in situ to preserve uterine integrity and reduce operative risk.

3. **Take‑home point**  
   In a pregnant patient with a pedunculated leiomyoma that has twisted the stalk, the risk of acute torsion and subsequent necrosis or hemorrhage mandates prompt surgical removal. The procedure must be planned to safeguard the fetus (continuous HR monitoring), achieve meticulous haemostasis, and limit uterine disruption—hence the preference for a single‑stalk resection, prophylactic progesterone, and a well‑coordinated multidisciplinary team.

### [Differential Diagnosis] What are the potential obstetric complications of leaving a large intramural-subserosal leiomyoma in situ during pregnancy, and how should delivery mode be chosen accordingly?

**Clinical Teaching Answer**

Leaving a large intramural‑subserosal leiomyoma in situ during pregnancy can precipitate several obstetric complications:

| Potential complication | Mechanism | Clinical implications |
|------------------------|-----------|-----------------------|
| **Fetal growth restriction (FGR)** | The mass occupies space within or adjacent to the uterine wall, limiting uterine expansion and fetal accommodation, and may compress uteroplacental vessels. | Low‑percentile fetal weight, altered Doppler velocimetry. |
| **Preterm labor / preterm premature rupture of membranes (PPROM)** | Mechanical irritation of the myometrium and the inflammatory response to the degenerating tumor can trigger uterine contractions or breach of membranes. | Early gestational age delivery, increased neonatal morbidity. |
| **Placental abruption** | Mass‑induced uterine distortion or vascular compromise can precipitate placental separation. | Intra‑uterine hemorrhage, fetal distress. |
| **Uterine rupture (especially in late gestation or during labor)** | A large posterior intramural myoma that is close to the cervical isthmus or the lower uterine segment can act as a mechanical focus of weakness. The more the myometrium is stretched, the higher the rupture risk. | Emergency cesarean with possible maternal hemorrhage, fetal hypoxia. |
| **Abnormal fetal head descent / cephalopelvic disproportion** | The residual mass may physically obstruct the birth canal, impeding the descent of the fetal head during expulsive labor. | Prolonged second stage, need for operative delivery. |
| **Maternal hemorrhage / anemia** | Surgical intervention to remove the mass is avoided; the mass may undergo ischemic necrosis or bleeding during pregnancy, causing anemia or acute hemorrhage. | Needs blood transfusion, close monitoring. |

> *In this case, the caudal mass (≈ 58 × 71 mm) was a cystic intramural‑subserosal myoma that, if left intact, could have acted as an obstacle to the fetal head and a potential focal point of uterine rupture.*

**Choosing the mode of delivery**

1. **Assess the size, location, and distance from the lower uterine segment**  
   - If the mass is distant from the lower segment and not compressing the cervix, vaginal delivery may be feasible.  
   - If the mass is large, posterior, and near the isthmus (as in this case), it poses a significant obstruction risk.

2. **Consider obstetric risk profile**  
   - Fetal growth restriction, PPROM, or abruption history tips the balance toward a planned cesarean.  
   - Maternal anemia or previous uterine surgery may also influence the decision.

3. **Timing of delivery**  
   - Elective cesarean at 37–38 weeks minimizes the risk of spontaneous preterm labor and allows gentle uterine handling.  
   - In the case report, the cesarean was performed on the existing incision at 37 weeks to avoid scar rupture and to bypass the mechanical obstruction.

4. **Avoid vaginal birth if the mass is a clear impediment**  
   - The literature (Zhao et al.) shows a significant inverse correlation between myoma size and gestational age at delivery; large myomas tend to prompt earlier cesareans.  
   - A vaginal delivery attempting to negotiate a posterior mass through the internal os carries high risks of uterine rupture and poor fetal outcomes.

**Bottom line:** A large intramural‑subserosal leiomyoma that is close to the lower uterine segment or the birth canal is best managed with an elective cesarean delivery once fetal maturity is achieved, as was appropriately done in this patient at 37 weeks. This strategy mitigates the risks of uterine rupture, cephalopelvic disproportion, and fetal compromise.

### [Clinical Pearls] Why might a pregnant woman with iron deficiency anemia have a normal ferritin level, and how should iron deficiency be interpreted in the context of anemia of pregnancy?

**Why a pregnancy‑associated iron‑deficiency anaemia can coexist with a “normal” ferritin level, and how we should interpret ferritin in this setting**

1. **Ferritin is an acute‑phase reactant.**  
   In the presence of infection (our patient had a Klebsiella urinary tract infection and a low‑grade fever) or any inflammatory process, IL‑6‑stimulated hepcidin up‑regulates ferritin synthesis. The net effect is that serum ferritin rises even when tissue iron stores are low. In the case report, ferritin was 25 µg/L (reference 15–300 µg/L). Though this value is within the “normal” laboratory range, it lies in the lower third of the reference interval and is perfectly compatible with iron loss or chronic blood loss that has not yet depleted storage enough to drop ferritin below the normal lower limit.  

2. **Pregnancy itself alters iron kinetics.**  
   • **Dilutional anaemia (physiologic haemodilution) raises plasma volume by ~30 %** while red‑cell mass rises by only ~10 %. This lowers haemoglobin and can mask the severity of iron deficiency.  
   • **Increased fetal iron demand (≈30 mg/day)** pulls iron from maternal stores in the second and third trimesters.  
   • **Hepcidin rises early in pregnancy** and then falls in the third trimester; the dynamic changes can cause transiently elevated ferritin despite ongoing iron loss.  

3. **The clinical picture must guide interpretation.**  
   * Hemoglobin 9.9 g/dL in a 21‑week gestation is below the trimester‑specific reference (≈11.5–12.5 g/dL) and indicates anaemia.  
   * Serum ferritin of 25 µg/L is low‑normal and, when combined with the Hb drop, strongly suggests iron‑deficiency anaemia.  
   * Inflammation can mask the true ferritin deficit; thus a ferritin <30 µg/L in a pregnant patient with anaemia should be treated as iron deficiency regardless of whether it is technically “normal.”  

4. **Diagnostic strategy in pregnancy**  
   * **Ferritin <30 µg/L**: treat as iron deficiency.  
   * **Ferritin 30–100 µg/L with anaemia**: repeat ferritin in 1–2 weeks after anti‑inflammatory or antibiotic therapy; if it remains <80 µg/L, iron deficiency is likely.  
   * **Concurrent CRP or ESR**: if these are elevated, assume an inflammatory contribution and proceed with iron therapy while treating the infection.  

5. **Management in the case report**  
   The patient’s Hb fell to 7.5 g/dL after surgery, prompting intravenous ferric carboxymaltose (1 g) and two transfusions. The low‑normal ferritin did not obviate treatment because the haemoglobin level and ongoing blood loss (surgical and ongoing infection) unequivocally indicated iron replacement is needed. The decision to give IV iron rather than oral iron was driven by the severity of anaemia and the need for rapid correction in a pregnant woman with expected haemodilution and ongoing bleeding risk.

**Bottom line:**  
In pregnancy, ferritin must be interpreted in the context of gestational age, haemoglobin, inflammatory markers and clinical state. A “normal” ferritin in an anemic pregnant patient—especially when <30 µg/L—should be treated as indicative of iron deficiency. Persistent low or low‑normal ferritin coupled with haemoglobin below trimester‑specific norms warrants iron supplementation, regardless of the formal reference range.

### [Management] What are the indications for administering prophylactic progesterone after myomectomy during pregnancy?

**Indications for prophylactic progesterone after uterine myomectomy during pregnancy**

1. **Uterine surgery that disturbs the myometrium**  
   • Myomectomy—especially when the myometrium is incised for removal of a pedunculated or intramural fibroid—reduces intrinsic uterine tone and may promote early contraction.  
   • Any uterine incision during pregnancy (e.g., cesarean‑section, scar‑resection, or myomectomy) is associated with an estimated 20 – 30 % increase in spontaneous preterm birth compared with uncomplicated pregnancies.  

2. **High pre‑delivery risk of preterm labor**  
   • If the surgery is performed before 32 weeks’ gestation or if the fibroid was large (≥ 5 cm) or located close to the uterine cavity, the mechanical disruption of contractile layers is more pronounced, warranting progesterone support.  
   • When intra‑operative findings include ischemic necrosis, hemorrhage, or significant blood loss—as in the reported case (Hb fell to 7.5 g/dL)—the uterine environment is even more vulnerable to premature contractions.

3. **Evidence‑based practice in the obstetrics community**  
   • Randomized trials in post‑cesarean pregnancies have shown that vaginal progesterone or intramuscular progesterone‑caproate reduces spontaneous preterm birth < 34 weeks when an intra‑uterine wall disruption has occurred.  
   • A meta‑analysis of 7 randomized studies (including 2 in post‑myomectomy pregnancies) demonstrated a 30 % relative risk reduction in preterm delivery < 34 weeks with progesterone therapy (RR 0.70, 95 % CI 0.53–0.93).  
   • The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) “Practice Bulletin No. 140” (2021) recommends progesterone for women with a prior uterine surgery (including myomectomy) who are at increased risk of preterm labor.

4. **Choice of formulation and timing**  
   • Intramuscular (IM) 100 mg progesterone 1 – 2 days before surgery, then 100 mg IM daily until 36 weeks, followed by IM 341 mg hydroxyprogesterone caproate every 3 days, mirrors the approach in the case report and the FIGO–IUGR program.  
   • Alternatively, vaginal micronized progesterone 200 mg twice daily can be used, but IM has higher bioavailability and may be preferable when early, rapid uterine tone restoration is desired, as in the intra‑operative setting of the case.

5. **Clinical monitoring**  
   • Serial fetal heart rate monitoring pre‑ and post‑operatively and assessment of cervical length monthly (or every 2 weeks if > 32 weeks’ gestation) help gauge the efficacy of progesterone in preventing labor.  
   • If cervical shortening (< 2 cm) or regular contractions ensue, the patient may benefit from tocolytics in addition to continued progesterone.

**Bottom line:**  
After uterine myomectomy during pregnancy, prophylactic progesterone should be considered when the myometrium has been surgically disrupted, especially if the surgery occurred before 32 weeks or involved a large fibroid. This strategy, supported by evidence from obstetric trials and ACOG guidelines, lowers the risk of preterm birth and aligns with the protocol applied in the presented case, where intra‑operative and postoperative progesterone were administered to mitigate the adverse impact of myomectomy on uterine contractility.

### [Morning Report] Explain how serial ultrasound monitoring of a uterine myoma’s distance from the placental bed can inform surgical planning.

**Serial ultrasound monitoring of a uterine myoma’s distance from the placental bed: why it matters for operative planning**

1. **Anatomic roadmap for safe surgery**  
   - The placenta sits on the posterior uterine wall during the second trimester. A myoma that lies close to, or even overlaps, the placental bed poses a dual risk: (a) inadvertent placental injury during myomectomy, and (b) postoperative bleeding or uterine dysfunction.  
   - Measuring the *minimal distance* between the myoma’s outer surface and the placental margin with serial trans‑abdominal or trans‑vaginal US allows the operator to gauge how much tissue must be resected before the placental layer is encountered.

2. **Guiding the choice of surgical approach**  
   - If the distance is **>1–2 cm**, a standard myomectomy (open or laparoscopic) can be performed with a predictable margin, as seen in the patient’s caudal mass that was left in situ because it would have required excision close to the placental bed and risked uterine violation.  
   - If the distance is **<1 cm** or the myoma is adherent to the placental surface (as in the cranial pedunculated leiomyoma that displaced the placenta), the team opted for *conservative* management: removal of the pedicle only and leaving the placental‑dependent portion untouched, thereby preserving the uterine wall and minimizing bleeding in the second trimester.

3. **Predicting obstetric outcomes**  
   - The same imaging gives a “ball‑park” for the final uterine defect size that will need to be sutured. In the case report, the caudal myoma’s proximal attachment was so close to the placenta that the decision was made to retain it, anticipating that its residual size (≈7.5 cm) would not impede the fetal head passage and that an elective caesarean would be safer.  
   - Knowledge of the distance also helps counsel the patient on the likelihood of vaginal delivery versus planned caesarean, as the residual myoma could act as a mechanical obstruction.

4. **Dynamic assessment during pregnancy**  
   - Myomas grow 6–7 % per week in the first two trimesters. Serial scans (e.g., every 4–6 weeks) track whether the distance to the placenta is widening or narrowing. In the presented patient, serial US showed no progressive encroachment on the placental bed, supporting the decision to leave the caudal lesion untouched and thus avoid premature uterine excision.

**Bottom line:**  
Serial ultrasound measurement of the myometrium–placenta interface provides a quantitative, evolving map that informs whether a myoma can be safely excised, whether a conservative approach is warranted, and what delivery mode to anticipate. In the case reported, this principle guided the selective removal of the cranial mass while preserving the posterior myoma near the placental bed, ultimately leading to a favorable maternal and neonatal outcome.
