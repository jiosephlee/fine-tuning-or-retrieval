Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Management] Which clinical and surgical factors should be considered when deciding to perform myomectomy during pregnancy versus waiting until postpartum?

**When to Operate on a Pregnant Uterine Leiomyoma?**

In the case described, the team elected to resect the upper pedunculated leiomyoma at 21 w + 6 d while leaving the large intramural‑subserosal mass in situ until the postpartum period. The decision hinged on a handful of interrelated clinical and surgical factors that should guide any future management plan.

| **Factor** | **Why it matters** | **How it applied in the case** |
|------------|---------------------|--------------------------------|
| **Gestational age** | Early pregnancy carries the greatest risk of miscarriage and is a period of high uterine plasticity; later trimesters limit surgical access but allow greater fetal maturity if the operation is prolonged. | Surgery performed in the early second trimester (≈ 22 weeks) when the uterus is still relatively small and the risk of preterm delivery, though present, is lower compared with a third‑trimester operation. |
| **Symptom burden (pain, dyspnea, infection)** | Overt, progressive symptoms press the decision toward immediate relief to preserve maternal and fetal wellbeing. | The patient had severe upper‑quadrant pain and dyspnea from mechanical compression; a twisted, necrotic pedunculated mass produced low‑grade fever, pressuring the team to intervene. |
| **Mass characteristics (size, location, vascularity, stalk)** | A pedunculated, vascular mass with a demonstrable stalk is at high risk for torsion, infarction, or rupture—conditions that recur intra‑uterinely and can precipitate acute abdomen or hemorrhage. | Ultrasound/MRI revealed a 18 × 12 × 7 cm pedunculated leiomyoma with a thin, twisted stalk—high‑risk for torsion. The intramural mass (≈ 6 cm) was completely adherent to the posterior uterine wall and had no stalk, making it low‑risk for torsion. |
| **Oncologic suspicion (tumor markers, imaging features)** | A trend toward elevated CA‑125 or atypical imaging features raises the differential for malignancy, tipping the scales toward definitive surgical evaluation. | CA‑125 rose from 36 to 42 U/mL; MRI showed thick walls, mixed fluid‑ and solid‑components—concern for malignancy. |
| **Risk of future obstetric complications** | Large intramural leiomyomas can impede fetal head descent, cause obstructed labor, or increase cesarean-section (CS) rates. | The caudal mass occupied the pouch of Douglas and was predicted to block the birth canal; this led to a planned CS at 37 weeks, but postponing its removal reduced operative risk during pregnancy. |
| **Operative morbidity (bleeding, uterine injury, hospital stay)** | Myomectomy in pregnancy is associated with higher blood loss and longer surgery than postpartum surgery. Limiting the procedure to the “most dangerous” lesion aims to keep operative time and hemorrhage low. | Only the pedunculated myoma was excised (≈ 2 h 25 min, 1 g Hb drop); the intramural myoma was left, avoiding a potentially prolonged, bleeding‑prone procedure and reducing the risk of uterine rupture during the subsequent CS scar. |
| **Multidisciplinary consensus and informed consent** | The decision must balance obstetric, surgical, and anesthesia risks; patient preferences regarding future fertility and timing of surgery are essential. | A multidisciplinary team (obstetrics, gynecology, surgery, anesthesia) reviewed all data; the patient gave written informed consent, acknowledging the trade‑offs of pregnancy‑time surgery versus postpartum. |
| **Postpartum regression potential** | Uterine leiomyomas frequently shrink after delivery due to hormonal withdrawal; small, asymptomatic masses may resolve spontaneously. | The residual intramural mass decreased from 9 cm to 7.5 cm fetus‑time; the patient opted for observation post‑delivery, reflecting the expected postpartum regression. |

**Practical Take‑Home Points**

1. **Immediate surgery is justified** when a leiomyoma is symptomatic, torsion is imminent or confirmed, or there is strong suspicion of malignancy.  
2. **Intra‑uterine location matters**: pedunculated, subserosal or intraperitoneal masses risk torsion; intramural lesions without a stalk pose less acute risk.  
3. **Gestational age influences safety**: early second trimester offers the best balance between maternal risks and fetal viability.  
4. **Aim to limit operative extent**—excision only of the high‑risk lesion minimizes blood loss and preserves uterine integrity for subsequent delivery.  
5. **Postpartum management** should be considered for large, asymptomatic intramural myomas, particularly when they are expected to regress and when delaying surgery lowers risks of bleeding, uterine rupture, or preterm labor.

In summary, the decision to perform myomectomy during pregnancy versus awaiting the puerperium is a nuanced calculus that incorporates gestational age, symptomatology, mass morphology, oncologic concern, obstetric impact, operative risk, and patient values—all of which were carefully weighed in the case described.
