Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Pathophysiology] Explain the pathophysiological mechanisms by which uterine leiomyomas can grow during pregnancy, including the reported weekly percent change in size across trimesters.

**Pathophysiology of pregnancy‑associated growth of uterine leiomyomas**

Uterine leiomyoma growth during pregnancy is driven by two interrelated mechanisms:

| Mechanism | How it operates in pregnancy | Evidence from case |
|-----------|-----------------------------|--------------------|
| **Hormonal stimulation** | Estrogen and progesterone levels rise dramatically during gestation. These steroids bind to receptors on myometrial smooth‑muscle cells, promoting DNA synthesis, cellular proliferation, and protein synthesis. Enhanced progesterone also up‑regulates anti‑apoptotic pathways, allowing leiomyoma cells to survive longer. | The patient’s leiomyomas were described as highly vascularised and rapidly enlarging (“pedunculated, atypical cystic and taut‑elastic”), consistent with a hyper‑responsive steroid milieu. |
| **Ischaemic‐necrotic changes leading to cystic degeneration** | Rapid growth can outstrip neo‑vascularisation. Hypoxia induces glycolytic metabolism, lactic acid production, and eventually cell death. The necrotic core is filled with fluid, producing a cystic component that can appear as a large, unilocular cyst on ultrasound/MRI. | MRI showed a large, thick‑walled cystic mass with focal hyperintense solid components—classic imaging of “cystic degeneration” associated with rapid tumor expansion. |
| **Mechanical expansion of the uterus** | As the uterus enlarges, it presses against surrounding tissues, distending fibroids and potentially compressing their feeding vessels. This mechanical stretch further stimulates steroid receptors and may increase fibroid volume. | The upper mass extended from the left sub‑phrenic space to the uterine fundus at 21 weeks, implying that the expanding uterus contributed to its apparent size. |

---

### Weekly percent change in leiomyoma size across trimesters

A large multicenter study (Zhao et al.) quantified the growth rates:

| Trimester | Average weekly growth | Statistical significance |
|-----------|-----------------------|--------------------------|
| 1st → 2nd | **6.12 % ± 0.39 %** | < 0.001 |
| 2nd → 3rd | **3.10 % ± 0.39 %** | < 0.05 |
| Post‑partum (6 weeks) | **–44.25 % ± 36.05 %** | < 0.05 |

Thus, leiomyomas enlarge most rapidly in the first trimester (≈6 % per week), slow down in the second, and grow more modestly in the third. After delivery, the majority regress markedly.  

In the present case, the patient had a 21‑week gestation when a large pedunculated leiomyoma (≈18 × 12 × 7 cm) was noted. Although exact serial measurements were not reported, the clinical picture (rapid pain escalation and dyspnoea) is compatible with the > 6 % weekly increase observed in the first trimester, extended into the second due to ongoing hormonal stimulation and mechanical stretch.

---

**Take‑home point:**  
Pregnancy‑induced elevation of estrogen and progesterone, combined with rapid uterine expansion and transient ischaemic‑necrotic changes, drives leiomyoma growth most aggressively during the first trimester (~6 %/week) and more slowly thereafter (~3 %/week). Recognizing these dynamics informs timing of surgical intervention and counseling of pregnant patients with symptomatic fibroids.
