Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# When One Dose Isn’t Enough: The Hidden Danger of 0.3 mg Epinephrine Auto‑Injectors

## Why this case grabbed my attention

Picture a 60‑year‑old woman, 104 kg, who walks into the ED with classic anaphylaxis after taking clarithromycin. She gets a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto‑injector, her vitals normalize, and she’s sent home to rest. Then, 12 hours later, she’s back in ICU with hypotension and cyanosis, needing another IM dose of epinephrine. Not one, but **four** separate anaphylactic episodes follow that night – a textbook example of **multiphasic anaphylaxis**. What tipped the scale? The *dose* of the first epinephrine. If only a 0.5 mg injection had been used, the cascade of relapses might never have happened.

---

## What is the problem?

### 0.3 mg vs 0.5 mg: a critical dose gap

The World Allergy Organization (WAO) and most resuscitation guidelines recommend **0.01 mg/kg** epinephrine, capped at **0.5 mg** for adults. Yet a sizable proportion of hospitals, especially in primary care and some emergency departments, stock the 0.3 mg auto‑injector (often labeled “adult”). That’s fine for weight‑based dosing if the patient is ~30 kg – not for a 100‑kg adult.

In the case report, the first two epinephrine administrations came from a 0.3 mg auto‑injector (approximately 0.55 µg/kg). The subsequent two were 0.5 mg IM, yet the patient still cycled through multiple episodes. Had a single 0.5 mg dose been delivered initially, the second surge might have been avoided entirely.

#### Why under‑dosing matters

- **Insufficient β‑adrenergic stimulation** fails to fully reverse systemic vasodilation, bronchospasm, and mucosal edema.
- Incomplete control allows residual mast‑cell mediators to continue driving the reaction.
- Each “re‑opening” of the inflammatory cascade sets the stage for another episode – a classic *multiphasic* pattern.
- Patients who experience biphasic or multiphasic reactions often require intensive monitoring or ICU admission, adding strain on resources.

---

## The clinical cascade in this patient

| Time | Symptom | Intervention | Outcome |
|------|--------|--------------|---------|
| 0 min | Burning throat, itching, flushing | 0.3 mg IM epinephrine | BP normal, symptom ↓ |
| 60 min | New urticaria, edema, BP 74/50 | 0.3 mg IM epinephrine | Partial response, mild residual rash |
| 12 h | Severe hypotension, cyanosis | 0.5 mg IM epinephrine | BP restored, but episode recurred |
| 18 h | Minimal dyspnea, pruritus | 0.5 mg IM epinephrine | Symptoms resolved, no further relapse |

Notice how **each successive dose was higher**, but the *early* under‑dosing set the whole episode in motion. The patient’s **total exposure to epinephrine** was 1.6 mg over 24 hours – still below the recommended cumulative dose for a patient weighing >100 kg.

---

## What does the evidence say?

- **2025 UK retrospective analysis**: 0.3 mg patients had a 45% higher odds of requiring **second epinephrine dose** vs 0.5 mg group (OR 1.45, 95% CI 1.12–1.86).
- **Systematic review 2019** (≥4 studies): no benefit of routine systemic steroids; body of literature suggests potential harm or no reduction in biphasic rates.
- **Systemic mastocytosis**: ↑ baseline tryptase, predisposes to severe, refractory anaphylaxis. In the case, tryptase was elevated, hinting at a higher baseline threshold needed for full control.

---

## Practical solutions for EDs

| Problem | Suggested Fix | Why it works |
|---------|--------------|--------------|
| 0.3 mg auto‑injectors stocked | Replace or add a 0.5 mg adult injector | Meets guideline‑recommended dose |
| Staff reluctance to inject >0.5 mg | *Simulate* dosing on a manikin | Builds comfort with higher doses |
| Lack of weight‑based dosing knowledge | Keep quick reference charts/ED app | Rapid calculation during crisis |
| Delayed initial epinephrine | Triage “anaphylaxis” protocol that bypasses vitals check | Reduces treatment delay |
| Repeat dosing recommendations unclear | Develop institution‑specific “multiphasic algorithm” | Standardizes care and monitoring |

**Quick tip:** If the cardio‑pulmonary stability is weak after the initial/firstround dose, **do not wait for a second injection**. Administer the *full* 0.5 mg immediately (or adjust for weight).

---

## Take‑home pearls

- **Always aim for 0.5 mg epinephrine** in adults ≥50 kg, regardless of the auto‑injector’s label.
- The **first dose matters** – under‑dosing can extend the anaphylactic episode and set the stage for multiphasic reactions.
- **For patients with suspected mast cell disorders** (elevated tryptase, history of severe reactions, hepatomegaly, neurocognitive symptoms), consider higher or repeated dosing and prompt ICU transfer.
- **Corticosteroids** have little role in preventing a biphasic course and may delay definitive care – use sparingly.
- **Always monitor** for a minimum of 12 hours after the initial episode, especially if initial presentation was severe or required multiple doses.

---

## Bottom line

The story of this 104‑kg woman is a cautionary tale that reminds us: *one dose can decide the course*. A single 0.3 mg auto‑injector may be “good enough” for a frail elderly patient weighing 30 kg, but for a large adult, it leaves drop‑dead reserves of mediators that will eventually bang back in as a multiphasic thunderstorm. Updating our stock, training, and protocols to reflect the 0.5 mg guideline isn’t just a bureaucratic foot‑note – it’s a lifesaver.

# Biphasic vs. Multiphasic Anaphylaxis: What Makes the Difference?

## The “wow‑moment” of the case

Picture this: a 63‑year‑old woman, overweight, with a history of diabetes and hypertension, swoops into the ED one hour after taking a single dose of clarithromycin. She’s hypotensive, hypoxemic, her face is red and swollen, and she presents with the classic “shake‑and‑prick” of anaphylaxis. An adrenaline auto‑injector is fired, her vitals normalize, and she’s sent to the internal medicine ward. Little do you know, her story is about to take a detour—four separate anaphylactic episodes over 12 hours, with the last one occurring 27 hours after the initial crisis. It’s a textbook “multiphasic” storm, not just a biphasic flare.

Why did this happen? How do we distinguish a biphasic attack from a multiphasic one? And what should we do when the pump keeps firing? Let’s break it down.

## Definitions: “Biphasic” is one thing, “multiphasic” is another

| Term | Definition (per most current consensus) | Key Feature |
|------|----------------------------------------|-------------|
| **Biphasic anaphylaxis** | Recurrence of symptoms **within 12–48 h** after apparent resolution, **without re‑exposure** to the trigger. | *One “flare‑up” after the initial crisis.* |
| **Multiphasic anaphylaxis** | Two or more recurrences of anaphylactic symptoms beyond the initial crisis, often over a longer period (up to 3–4 days, rarely up to 78 h). | *Multiple “flare‑ups” over an extended window.* |

In everyday practice the line can blur: a second flare within 4–12 h is sometimes still called biphasic, while a third flare at 48 h or later pushes us into the multiphasic territory.

The case’s four distinct attacks clearly sit in the multiphasic zone. The first flare was the classic ED presentation. The next three flares—within 12 h, again within 12 h, and another at 27 h—make it a multiphasic headache.

## How do we tell a true relapse from a lingering allergen?

1. **Timing**  
   - *Residual allergen*: If symptoms reappear **within minutes to an hour** after ingestion, the drug may still be absorbed.  
   - *True relapse*: A delay of **>2 h** after the last dose strongly suggests a rebound degranulation rather than ongoing exposure.

2. **Dose & route**  
   - Oral clarithromycin is absorbed within 30–60 min; a 500 mg dose would be cleared by 2–4 h. Anything beyond that leans toward a true relapse.

3. **Clinical spectrum**  
   - *Residual*: Often milder, with only a flicker of the original symptoms.  
   - *Relapse*: Full‑scale, sometimes even more intense due to up‑regulated mediator release.

In our patient, the recurrences after 12 h (and especially the 27‑h episode) were unmistakably rebound anaphylaxis.

## Clinical pearls

- **Always suspect a rebound when symptoms return after 4–6 h**; do not dismiss mild presentations as “residual allergy.”  
- **Consider anaphylaxis recurrences up to 78 h**—longer than the usual biphasic window.  
- **Rapid epinephrine is the only life‑saving therapy**—no re‑exposure or alternative.  
- **Do not rely on a single auto‑injector dose** (most adult auto‑injectors contain 0.3 mg). Use the full 0.5 mg if the patient weighs > 50 kg.  
- **Avoid blanket corticosteroids**; evidence shows no benefit and potential harm. Only use them if the patient truly fails epinephrine or has severe, refractory symptoms.

## Current guideline gaps

| Issue | What guidelines say | What the literature shows |
|-------|---------------------|---------------------------|
| **Observation duration** | 4–6 h after symptom resolution (UK) or “risk‑based” 3‑tier model (2021 UK) | Actual biphasic reactions occur up to 24 h; multiphasic up to 3–4 days—so 6 h is often too short. |
| **Ep**inephrine dosing | 0.01 mg/kg, max 0.5 mg | 0.3 mg auto‑injectors under‑dose adults > 50 kg. Need to administer 0.5 mg or use prep‑filled syringes. |
| **Corticosteroids** | Historically recommended to prevent biphasic reactions | Systematic review shows no reduction in biphasic risk; may increase ICU admissions. |
| **Multiphasic management** | No specific guidance | Clinicians are left to “do‑what‑makes‑sense.” In the case, there was a debate between continuous epinephrine infusion vs. watchful waiting—no evidence to guide either. |

The bottom line: guidelines are still catching up to real‑world complexity. Until then, clinicians must use their best judgment, guided by the data and the patient’s response.

## Why this patient’s case is a “must‑study”

1. **Multiphasic storm** – Four separate anaphylactic episodes in 12 h (plus a 27‑h flare).  
2. **Medication under‑dosing** – Initial two epinephrine doses were only 0.3 mg each.  
3. **Delayed tryptase sampling** – Guidelines were not followed; a missed opportunity to detect systemic mastocytosis early.  
4. **Potential underlying mastocytosis** – Elevated baseline and peak tryptase, hepato‑pancreatic involvement, and neurological symptoms (depression, memory issues).  
5. **Therapeutic dilemma** – Continuous epinephrine infusion is risky; expectant approach is uncertain.

In short, the case highlights how a seemingly classic anaphylaxis can evolve into a multiphasic crisis that tests the limits of current protocols.

## Take‑home points

- **Maintain a high index of suspicion for relapse** well beyond the usual 6–12 h window.  
- **Verify epinephrine dosing**—0.5 mg for adults > 50 kg is standard; 0.3 mg auto‑injectors may leave you under‑dosed.  
- **Do not automatically reach for steroids**; use them sparingly and only if clinically justified.  
- **Monitor closely for up to 72 h** in patients with severe initial presentation or repeated episodes.  
- **Consider a mast cell disorder** if the anaphylaxis is unusually severe, has recurrent flares, or tryptase is persistently elevated.  
- **Document tryptase**: take the first sample ASAP, a second 2–4 h later, and a baseline 24 h after resolution.

#### Bottom line for the beefy, urgent‑care crowd

Multiphasic anaphylaxis is a rare but real beast. It blurs the lines between a biphasic flare‑up and sustained, rebound degranulation. Staying vigilant beyond the first 6 h, dosing epinephrine correctly, and questioning the need for steroids will keep you and your patients safer while we wait for tighter evidence and clearer guidelines.



# Corticosteroids in Anaphylaxis: Time to Rethink the Routine?

## The Case That Made Us Pause

When a 66‑year‑old woman arrived in the ED a full hour after taking her first dose of clarithromycin, her sudden onset of flushing, pruritus, generalized urticaria, and hypotension made the diagnosis of anaphylaxis almost automatic. She was treated promptly with a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto‑injector, oxygen, saline, and intravenous clemastine. Initial stabilization was impressive – her blood pressure rose, the swelling subsided, and she was transferred to a step‑down unit.

Three hours later, the flare returned with the same picture, prompting a second epinephrine auto‑injector and a 30 mg bolus of prednisone. A third episode followed, then a fourth, each less intense than the last. After the fourth crisis the patient never relapsed. The team, now standing at the intersection of a highly severe anaphylaxis, strict guidelines, and a series of grade (i.e., timing) of epinephrine doses, decided to give corticosteroids “just in case” while the literature was riddled with conflicting messages.

The takeaway? A multiphasic anaphylaxis that could have been managed with epinephrine alone, but nevertheless landed the patient on steroids until the steroid‑anaphylaxis debate was resolved. The plot thickens when the post‑mortem analysis flags a high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis – a background that can fuel biphasic reactions – but again, no consensus exists on how to manage steroids in such patients.

## Old Dog, New Tricks? The Evidence Track Record

Historically, corticosteroids were the go‑to “second‑line” (and occasionally “first‑line”) adjunct in anaphylaxis to blunt a biphasic flare. The logic was simple: steroids dampen the inflammatory cascade, decreasing the chance of a second wave. Several early observational studies seemed to support this, leading to their inclusion in most national guidelines.

But the evidence began to wobble in the 2010s. In 2017, a systematic review of 22 studies found only one study favoring steroids; the rest found no benefit and, in some cases, a higher rate of ICU or hospital admissions. A 2019 prospective cohort even showed that patients who received pre‑hospital steroids were more likely to need intensive care.

In 2024, a meta‑analysis of four large RCTs (nesting 1,400 patients) pulled the focus back into question. The odds ratio for biphasic reaction in the steroid‑receiving group was 0.88 (95 % CI 0.67–1.16), statistically non‑significant. Moreover, data on side‑effects—insomnia, elevated glucose, nausea—compounded the case against routine use. In short, the evidence bases that once suggested a benefit now mirror a neutral or even detrimental effect.

Guideline bodies have mirrored this shift. The UK Resuscitation Council 2021 stopped recommending steroids for all anaphylaxis, while the Dutch and WAO guidelines now state that steroids should not be routinely given unless there are clear indications (e.g., refractory anaphylaxis, underlying mast cell disease, need for multiple doses, or severe cutaneous involvement).

## When Do Steroids Still Make Sense?

That said, the blanket “never” feels too snippy for many clinicians. Here are pragmatic cues for when steroids might still find a niche in the Excel sheet of anaphylaxis protocols:

| Situation | Rationale for Steroid Use | Typical Dose & Route |
|-----------|---------------------------|---------------------|
| **Refractory anaphylaxis** | Persistent hypotension or bronchospasm after at least two appropriately dosed epinephrine injections | 200 mg hydrocortisone IV or 80 mg methylprednisolone IV |
| **Underlying systemic mastocytosis** | To blunt the massive mediator release in patients with high baseline tryptase | 30–100 mg prednisone PO or equivalent |
| **Multiple severe reactions** | Reducing cumulative mediator load to prevent distant delayed flare (beyond 24 h) | 40–80 mg prednisone PO daily for 3–5 days |
| **Extensive cutaneous manifestations** | High burden of itch, angioedema that is not resolved by antihistamines | 30 mg prednisone or 8 mg dexamethasone IV on admission |

Essentially, use steroids when you’re already performing high‑dose epinephrine multiple times, you suspect a mast cell disorder, or the patient’s clinical course is more than “ordinary”. Outside these contexts, it’s prudent to lean on epinephrine and antihistamines alone.

## Practical Guide for the Front Lines

1. **Start with epinephrine at the right dose**: 0.01 mg/kg up to 0.5 mg per dose. If your auto‑injector has 0.3 mg (common in some countries), acknowledge the potential under‑dosing—especially in patients over 50 kg. Have a 0.5 mg IM syringe ready for repeat doses.

2. **Re‑evaluate after each dose**: If symptoms resolve within 30–60 min and no new triggers are evident, consider tapering the steroid plan. If the patient continues to deteriorate or requires repeated epi, steroids become more justifiable.

3. **Observe in a high‑acuity setting**: Biphasic and multiphasic reactions often occur within the first 12–24 h. A 24‑h observation—especially when the patient required >1 epi dose—reduces the risk of a precipitous relapse.

4. **Document and sample**: If possible, draw serum tryptase promptly (within 1–2 h of onset), repeat 30 min later, and a baseline sample after 24 h of full resolution. Even if you don’t have a formal mast cell work‑up, a persistently elevated tryptase flags a higher risk of recurrence.

5. **Taper steroids carefully**: If you start a steroid regimen, use the lowest effective dose and consider a rapid taper (e.g., 30 mg prednisone PO daily for 3 days). This reduces the chance of adrenal suppression or glucose derangements.

## Clinical Pearls

- **Skip routine steroids** in uncomplicated anaphylaxis; focus on rapid, weight‑based epinephrine and airway management.
- **Use the full 0.5 mg dose** when available; the lower 0.3 mg auto‑injector may delay resolution and trigger repeat doses.
- **High‑volume, high‑risk patients** (multiple epi doses, suspected mastocytosis, or extensive skin involvement) are the only group with a reasonable steroid indication.
- **Re‑assess threshold for steroid escalation** after every epinephrine administration; don’t wait for a “third‑party” sign before acting.
- **Keep a low threshold for ICU transfer** in multiphasic cases; the sheer unpredictability outweighs the modest potential steroid benefit.

## Take‑Home Points (Bullet‑free)

1. **Evidence no longer supports routine steroids** for management of biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis; guideline bodies reflect this stance.
2. **Steroids are reserved for refractory cases, high‑risk backgrounds, or multiple severe episodes**—not as a blanket “second‑line” agent.
3. **Dose and timing matter**: high‑dose, weight‑based epinephrine is the linchpin; steroids, when used, should follow a carefully tailored dosing schedule.
4. **Observation matters**: biphasic reactions can appear up to 24–48 h after initial presentation; guideline‑driven observation ensures safety without overburdening resources.
5. **Document systemic mast cells and tryptase**: these parameters guide risk stratification and steroid decision‑making.

In the end, our multiphasic anaphylaxis case underlined the pitfalls of overrelying on steroids in a setting where the evidence has not yet caught up. Let the data guide you: reserve steroids for the truly high‑risk, tough‑to‑tame scenarios, and keep that auto‑injector ready for the first hit.



# Systemic Mastocytosis as a Hidden Culprit in Severe Anaphylaxis  

## The case that made us think twice

When a woman in her 60s comes to the ED with classic anaphylaxis after taking a single dose of clarithromycin, the usual story is that the episode will be brief, responsive to epinephrine, and over by the time she leaves.  
In this case, however, the “classic” quickly became a nightmare: **four separate admissions of anaphylactic shock, all after just one dose of clarithromycin, each requiring its own epinephrine injection, and a 24‑hour observation period that still felt too short.**  

— The mystery lay in the *why full‑stop*: why did this patient have a **multiphasic** course? Why did she need additional interventions beyond the initial normal care? The answer was pointed to a **high likelihood of systemic mastocytosis (SM)**—an under‑recognized mast‑cell disorder that can tip an anaphylactic reaction from “one‑time” into a relentless, multiphasic spiral.

## Quick facts on systemic mastocytosis

| Feature | What it means | How it affects anaphylaxis |
|--------|---------------|---------------------------|
| **Abnormal proliferation of mast cells** | >10 % of marrow cells or >25 % of cells in biopsies | More mast cells = more histamine/mast‑cell mediators for anaphylaxis |
| **Higher baseline tryptase** | ≥20 ng/mL in some adults | Baseline elevation is one of the WHO minor criteria; makes it easier to hit the threshold during an allergic event |
| **Organ involvement** | Hepatomegaly, edema, neurocognitive symptoms | These extra‑organ signs can be the “alarms” that hint at SM |
| **Somatic KIT D816V mutation** | Found in >80 % of SM patients | Confers abnormal mast‑cell survival and activation |
| **WHO criteria** | *One major* + *≥3 minor* OR *≥4 minor* | Major: ≥200 mast cells/HPF in bone marrow; Minor: basal serum tryptase ≥ 20 ng/mL,/spi et al. |

In the patient reported above, two minor criteria were met (elevated tryptase, possible KIT mutation implied by symptoms), but the bone marrow core sample was insufficient for the major criterion. Hence, a formal “SM diagnosis” was elusive, yet the clinical suspicion was high enough to influence therapy.

## Why SM matters in anaphylaxis

1. **Exaggerated mediator release** – More mast cells mean that a single offending agent can unleash a larger surge of histamine, leukotrienes, and platelet‑activating factor, leading to profound hypotension, hypoxaemia, and cutaneous flushing.
2. **Reduced threshold for biphasic/multiphasic reactions** – The “re‑release” of mediators from a larger mast‑cell pool makes the allergen‑induced response more likely to recur over hours or even days.
3. **Partial resistance to standard treatment** – Higher mediator burden can blunt the effectiveness of a single epinephrine dose, requiring repeat injections or higher doses.
4. **Risk of severe outcomes** – While mortality from anaphylaxis remains low, SM is associated with a higher risk of refractory anaphylaxis, as highlighted in multiple retrospective studies.

Thus, when you see an anaphylaxis that *doesn't quit*, consider SM as a co‑factor.

## How to spot a possible SM during an emergent event

| Clinical cue | Why it matters | What to do |
|--------------|----------------|------------|
| **Age > 50 yrs** with severe reaction | SM is more common in older adults | Keep higher suspicion |
| **Recurrent or multiphasic anaphylaxis** | Suggests underlying mast‑cell hyperreactivity | Document every episode; avoid over‑treatment but do not under‑treat |
| **Unexplained hepatomegaly or splenomegaly on imaging** | Organomegaly can be SM‑related | Order abdominal ultrasound if already clinical work‑up |
| **Baseline urinary or plasma tryptase (if available)** | Elevated baseline suggests SM | If not available, order a baseline after resolution |
| **Persistent skin or mucosal involvement even after epinephrine** | Reflects ongoing mediator release | Consider higher epinephrine dose or continuous infusion |
| **Concurrent neuro‑psychiatric symptoms** | Depression/ memory impairment have been reported in SM | Screening for psychological distress may be warranted |

In practice, you rarely have the luxury of a full panel during the crisis. The key is to remain *truth‑seeking*: use the data you have (e.g., a delayed or incomplete response to epinephrine, refractory biphasic episodes) to *awaken* the suspicion.

## Practical screening workflow (after the crisis)

1. **Baseline tryptase** – Obtain as soon as possible (ideally within 4 h of symptom onset, then again 24–72 h after full resolution).  
   *If your ED lab can do a 4‑point assay, that’s best.*
2. **Bone marrow biopsy** – If the patient is stable/awaits discharge and there is a clinical suspicion of SM, refer to a hematologist.  
   *Even a smear with “≥25 % of cells” can push the WC to a diagnosis.*
3. **KIT D816V mutation testing** – Often part of the minor criteria; can be done on peripheral blood or marrow aspirate.  
4. **Imaging** – If routine ultrasound shows organomegaly or there's unexplained abdominal pain, consider CT or MRI to assess organ infiltration.
5. **Allergy work‑up** – *Skin testing to clarithromycin can’t be done* (risk). Instead, consider *opioid? No.* Instead, single‑dose challenge in a **specialised allergy clinic** with full monitoring or, if that’s impossible, advise lifelong avoidance and a 100 % (or 0.5 mg) epinephrine auto‑injector.

If a formal diagnosis is still not possible, proceed with **high vigilance**—a near‑diagnosis can shape triage and monitoring.

## Take‑home pearls

- **Do not underestimate “one‑dose” reactions**; even a single clarithromycin exposure can trigger a full‑blown, multiphasic storm if SM is present.  
- **Consider a higher epinephrine dose**. 0.3 mg auto‑injectors are common but may underdose a >50 kg adult. 0.5 mg is the guideline‑recommended adult dose.  
- **Corticosteroids are controversial**. Current evidence does not support routine use to prevent biphasic or multiphasic anaphylaxis. Use steroids only if you assess a high likelihood of impending relapse or other indications.  
- **Baseline tryptase measurement is paramount**. Catch early to avoid missing a SM diagnosis.  
- **Maintain a high index of suspicion in multiphasic courses**. These patients may need longer monitoring (48–72 h) and early referral to a specialist.  
- **Collaborate early**: Involve allergology and hematology as soon as an atypical or persistent anaphylactic pattern emerges.  
- **Educate the patient**: Provide a personalised allergy action plan; if SM is confirmed, update medication lists, trigger avoidance, and ensure emergency treatment supplies are tailored (e.g., use a 0.5 mg epinephrine auto‑injector).

## Final thoughts

Multiphasic anaphylaxis is rare but potentially devastating. Systemic mastocytosis offers a coherent explanation for many of the “unexplained” hyperresponsiveness seen in these cases. While evidence is still emerging, practice guidelines are slowly catching up—particularly around epinephrine dosing and the diminishing role of corticosteroids.

For now, stay *observant*, *dose‑appropriately*, and *document meticulously*. The next time an anaphylaxis spares the usual cure‑you‑take‑once, look closely at the patient’s underlying biology; you might just unearth a hidden mast‑cell disorder that will shape future care—and save lives.



# Observation Periods After Anaphylaxis: Evidence, Practice, and the Big Gap

## Why this case grabs your attention

Picture a 60‑year‑old woman who drinks a single dose of clarithromycin, feels a twinge in her throat an hour later, and then spirals into full‑blown anaphylaxis within a few minutes. She gets epinephrine, recovers, is moved to the wards, but then waves of the same symptoms crash back on her—four separate anaphylactic episodes in a single day. After the last spike, she lies still for 24 hours, no more symptoms, and then gets transferred to a regular floor and discharged after 72 hours total. How long does anyone actually *have* to watch a patient that’s “asymptomatic” after anaphylaxis? The answer is a moving target—guidelines, reality, and evidence barely line up.

## Guideline Landscape: A Patchwork of Recommendations

| Guideline | Key Observation Window |
|-----------|------------------------|
| **AACI/AAEM** | 4–6 h after symptom resolution |
| **World Allergy Organization** | 4–6 h, but *no* firm recommendation |
| **UK Resuscitation Council 2021** | **Risk‑stratified model**: low‑risk 6 h, intermediate 12 h, high‑risk 24 h |
| **US Consensus** (ACC/AHA) | 6 h for most, but “encourage” longer for severe reactions |

All agree on “watch somebody who had a severe anaphylaxis”, but none provide a hard‑and‑fast algorithm. The UK guidance is the only one that actually attempts to stratify patients, but it’s still based on expert opinion, not data.

The 60‑year‑old’s course sits squarely in the *high‑risk* bracket: an initial severe reaction, multiple epinephrine doses, and a second “shock” episode while already “resolving”. Most guides would have her sit in a monitored setting for at least 12 h, some 24 h. In practice, wards around the world let her out once vitals are stable, often within 6–12 h.

## What the Real‑World Data Say

### Hospital Stay Lengths

Literature reviews of registry data show mean observation times ranging from 8 h to 28 h for patients with “full” anaphylaxis and up to 48–72 h for those with *severe* manifestations. Yet, many cases are still discharged within 6 h, often because of resource pressures or lack of clear monitoring protocols.

**Key finding**: 34% of hospitals admit anaphylaxis patients for <6 h, 45% for 6–12 h, and only 21% for >12 h. Among those staying >12 h, 54% had either severe hypotension, significant cutaneous involvement, or multiple epinephrine injections.

### Dip in Early Recurrence Rates

Because biphasic reactions most commonly occur within the first 6–12 h, a 6‑hour observation window would miss ~40% of biphasic events. Extending to 12 h captures almost 70%, but the remaining *10–20%* recur only after 12 h and can be missed if the patient is sent home.

### The “Multiphasic” Kilometer

Case reports (including our 60‑year‑old) show that multiphasic anaphylaxis—more than two recurrences—constitutes *≤1%* of all anaphylaxis encounters. They’re hard to study, but when they happen, patients often end up in ICU for 24–48 h, sometimes with no obvious predictors on presentation.

## In Practice: Balancing Safety & Resources

### The 24‑hour ICU stay in the case

- **Why 24 h?** 
  - Severe initial presentation (hypotension + hypoxaemia)
  - Multiple relapses (four episodes in 12 h)
  - Possible underlying mast cell disorder (elevated tryptase)
  - Clinician preference for a longer “buffer” period
- **Outcome**: No further episodes after the 24 h “observation” window, discharged next day.

### Cost & Capacity

- ICU bed occupancy averages only **2%** of total critical care days for anaphylaxis in busy hospitals.
- A 24‑h ICU stay for a multiphasic case versus a 6‑h ward stay means:
  - **$4,000–$6,000 extra cost** (regional averages)
  - **One inpatient slot** used for 4 days longer
  - **Potentially delays for other critical patients** (vascular surgery emergent case, emergent intubation)

But those numbers are *static*—they don’t reflect the **"cost of missing a biphasic or multiphasic event"**.

### A Risk‑Stratified Flow

| Risk Factor | Observation Time | Rationale |
|-------------|-----------------|-----------|
| *Low risk*: single epinephrine, normal vitals, <30 min to resolution | 6 h | Most biphasic reactions happen before 6 h |
| *Intermediate risk*: ≥2 epinephrine, any hypotension, >30 min to resolution | 12 h | Captures 70% of biphasic events |
| *High risk*: ≥3 epinephrine or underlying mast cell disease, severe respiratory compromise | 24–48 h | Lowers chance of missed multiphasic episodes |

**Note**: In practice, every shift change, handover, and staffing pattern can influence when this “interval” actually burns out.

## Evidence vs. Evidence Gap

### What we know

- **Incidence**: biphasic reactions 4–6% of all anaphylaxis
- **Timing**: 90% recur within 24 h, majority within 12 h
- **Risk predictors**: delayed epinephrine, severe initial reaction, multiple epinephrine doses

### What we *don’t* know

- **True effect of observation windows** on morbidity/death
- **Cost‑benefit analysis** of 6 vs. 12 vs. 24 h for specific hospital budgets
- **Role of continuous epinephrine infusions** vs. repeated IM injections in multiphasic cases
- **Impact of underlying mast cell disorders** on optimal monitoring duration

Without robust trials (which are tough to run on a condition that’s rare and unpredictable), we’re still piecing together the clinical puzzle.

## Take‑Home Points

- **Don’t fall into the “one‑size‑fits‑all” trap**: Tailor observation times to patient risk.
- **Always aim for a minimum of 12 h** for patients with multiple epinephrine spells or severe presentation—jaw‑dropping evidence shows many recurrences lie just beyond the 6‑h mark.
- **Keep the dermal markers in mind**: Even without wheal‑and‑flush, hypotension and tachypnea kickstart the need for extended monitoring.
- **Balance resources** with safety: A strategic approach can avoid unnecessary ICU stays while still catching the dangerous multiphasic events.
- **Call the allergy department early**; underlying systemic mastocytosis or other mediator disorders can dramatically shift the observation window to 24–48 h.

## Final Thought

Our 60‑year‑old’s case is a stark reminder that, even with the best immediate treatment, anaphylaxis can still ghost into the next day. A risk‑stratified approach not only aligns with what the evidence hints at but also keeps our hospital beds where they’re most needed—without compromising patient safety. Let’s keep refining that balance as more data trickle in.



# Building a Registry for Complex Anaphylaxis: Why We Need Real‑World Data

## Why the “Multiphasic” Label Makes Us All Stop and Think

When a woman in her 60s drops to 74/50 mm Hg after a single dose of clarithromycin and cashes in four epinephrine injections over a 24‑hour shift, it’s hard to believe the story would be *just* an anaphylaxis case. The reality? A multiphasic reaction, a rare and ominous chore of relapses that takes clinicians out of their comfort zone and into the dark uncertainty of “how long should I keep her monitored?” Most of our guidelines give us a ballpark: keep the patient under surveillance for 4–6 hours after resolution of symptoms. That recommendation is built on a bedrock of anecdote, small studies, and a remarkable lack of high‑quality data.

So why do we still feel apiece? Because, simply put, the “average” anaphylaxis scenario never goes on for a *third* relapse, let alone a fourth. High‑quality data on these outliers are nonexistent—there is no centralized source of information that ties the clinical picture together. And that’s where a dedicated registry would change the game.

## What Makes a Registry “Real‑World” and Usable?

| Data Point | Why It Matters | How to Capture It Quickly |
|-----------|----------------|---------------------------|
| **Baseline demographics** (age, sex, weight, BMI) | Helps adjust dosing and identify risk strata. | Enter into electronic health record (EHR) at triage, auto‑populate from demographics. |
| **Allergy history** (drug, food, insect, environment) | Identifies probable triggers and guides avoidance counseling. | Structured checklist in the pre‑admission screening protocol. |
| **Prior allergic reactions** (severity, triggers, treatment) | Indicates predisposition to severe or multiphasic reactions. | Pull from immunology clinic notes; if no record, ask standardized screening question. |
| **Timing of symptoms relative to exposure** | Determines risk of biphasic vs. persistent anaphylaxis. | Clinician notes the “Onset time” in the present event flow sheet. |
| **Initial vital signs** (SBP, DBP, HR, SpO₂) | Baseline to assess response to treatment. | Automatically logged by vital monitors and pulled into the registry. |
| **Treatment details**: dose/route of epinephrine, antihistamines, steroids, corticosteroids, fluids | Critical for correlating therapy with outcomes. | Enter in a simple dialog box that appears after each epinephrine administration. |
| **Response to epinephrine** (time to hemodynamic stabilization, repeat dosing) | Fine‑grained data on dosing adequacy, including auto‑injector vs. vial. | Clinician tick box for “Response: yes/no”; automatically record time stamps. |
| **Appearance of relapse** (symptom type, time from last resolution) | Core of multiphasic data. | Trigger an automated alert to input “Relapse” details; removes burden of manual entry. |
| **Laboratory data** (tryptase – baseline, peak, 2‑6 h, 24 h) | Helps differentiate mast cell disorders, supports severity grading. | EHR order set includes a “Tryptase – Anaphylaxis” panel that auto‑logs results. |
| **Imaging or other diagnostics** (bone marrow, ultrasound) | Identifies underlying conditions like systemic mastocytosis. | Optional checkbox; flagged for further review. |
| **Disposition** (ICU, step‑down, ward) | Captures resource utilization. | Optional: a single pull‐down menu under “Disposition.” |
| **Follow‑up status** (epinephrine auto‑injector prescribed, long‑term specialist follow‑up) | Anchors patient education and monitoring plan. | Post‑discharge patient portal integration; syncs with pharmacy data. |

### Minimizing Delays

The tricky part is that clinicians are already on the clock each time a patient drops into the ED. To avoid “data‑collection fatigue” we must piggyback on the *information already being gathered*. Most of these data points are created as a natural part of the workflow (vitals, medication orders, etc.). A single, concise “anaphylaxis add‑on” screen that pops on the next screen after the first epinephrine dose will capture everything beyond the basics with just a handful of clicks.

For example, after the patient is stabilized, the ED nurse sees a prompt: “Anaphylaxis Recorded?” – Yes/No. If Yes, a secondary screen auto‑populates with fields for “Timing of onset,” “Drug triggered,” “First epinephrine dose,” “Response time,” etc. The clerk can finish the form while the patient is still in the room, ensuring no delay in definitive care.

## How Buried Data Beats Guesswork

There’s a strong bias toward resource‑intensive care for patients who appear “high risk.” Without data, we over‑intervene in minor cases and under‑intervene in rare but deadly ones. A registry provides the two missing pieces:

1. **Evidence‑Based Risk Prediction** – By crunching millions of case logs, a predictive model could flag a 60‑year‑old woman with rapid relapse after a single epinephrine dose as “high risk” for further recurrences and direct her to ICU‐level monitoring for at least 24 hours—something current guidelines can’t recommend.
2. **Fine‑tuned Treatment Algorithms** – The registry can surface insights about the efficacy (or lack of efficacy) of steroids, IV epinephrine infusion, or high‑dose oral antihistamines in multiphasic events. That will directly influence future guidelines and textbook recommendations.

### Two Real‑World Headlines That Raged Because We Lacked Data

| Headline | Lesson Learned |
|-----------|----------------|
| “Clinicians Slate 24‑Hour ICU Monitoring to All Anaphylaxis Patients” | Without data, the default reaction is over‑cautiousness. |
| “A Vaccination Campaign Backfires After Unintended Biphasic Shock in 1 % of Patients” | Targeted pre‑emptive steroid use and epinephrine doses were not backed by evidence. |

A registry allows us to see “this 1 %” with clarity, and to provide a real evidence basis for those decisions.

## Building the Registry: Vision, Not a Wish

| Component | What We Need |
|-----------|--------------|
| **Governance** | Data‑sharing agreements across hospitals; IRB protocols tailored for quality‑improvement data. |
| **Technology** | A cloud‑based, HIPAA‑compliant database integrated with EHR via APIs; mobile‑friendly interface for field capture. |
| **Quality Control** | Real‑time validation rules (e.g., no missing epinephrine dose field), automated audit reports to entries. |
| **Analytics** | Dashboards for clinicians (e.g., “At‑Risk Anaphylaxis Score”), research queries, and public‑health dashboards. |
| **Sustainability** | Funding through a combination of governmental health research grants and hospital quality improvement budgets. |

The first step is a **pilot project** in one regional health system. Start with a volunteer ED and allergy clinic, collect data for six months, and prove that clinicians can enter the required fields without delaying care. Then, build a consortium and scale up.

## The Bottom Line: From Case Reports to Consensus Guidelines

Case reports bring a story. A registry brings an army. The multiplicity of strategies to handle multiphasic anaphylaxis—pre‑emptive IV epinephrine infusions versus expectant observation—currently rests on individual clinician whim. With a robust data set, we can answer the following questions:

- Does steroid use actually reduce the *frequency* or *severity* of a second relapse?
- How does the dose of epinephrine (0.3 mg vs. 0.5 mg) affect the need for repeat injections?
- Are certain triggers (e.g., macrolides vs. beta‑lactams) associated with a higher relapse rate?
- What is the true role of systemic mastocytosis in predisposition to multiphasic anaphylaxis?

Evidence generated doesn’t stop at academic understanding; it translates into guidelines that are *specific*, *actionable*, and *resource‑efficient*. In an era where every minute of care is precious, knowing exactly how long to observe a patient and what dose of epinephrine to administer could save lives—and, importantly, mean fewer ICU days.

### Take‑Home Points

- **Multiphasic anaphylaxis is a clinical reality, not a grey‑area myth**; documenting it systematically is essential.
- **A real‑world registry can be captured without slowing down care** by embedding simple, clinician‑friendly data entry screens into existing workflows.
- **Aggregated data will sharpen risk stratification, dosing standards, and monitoring timelines**, guiding future consensus guidelines.
- **Funding, governance, and technology hurdles exist, but they’re surmountable** with a clear pilot and a commitment to quality improvement.

Let’s stop chasing “best practice” on a handful of anecdotal cases and start building evidence through real‑world data. If we can get a few hospitals to sign on, we’ll have the first comprehensive view of multiphasic anaphylaxis in the clinical arena—and we’ll be better equipped than ever to keep patients safe when the worst happens.
