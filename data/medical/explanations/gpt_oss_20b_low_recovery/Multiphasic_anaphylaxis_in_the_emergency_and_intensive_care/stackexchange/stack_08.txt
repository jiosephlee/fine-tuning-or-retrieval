Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Clinical Pearls] Describe the potential clinical consequences of delayed epinephrine administration in anaphylaxis, and analyze how the 1‑hour delay from clarithromycin ingestion to symptom onset might have influenced the severity of the patient’s reaction.

**Clinical Consequence of Delayed Epinephrine and the 1‑Hour Clarithromycin Window**

Prompt epinephrine is the cornerstone of anaphylaxis therapy because it reverses life‑threatening mediator‑driven vasodilation, bronchospasm, and pruritus. Delays in giving the first dose have clear, graded risks:

| Time from symptom onset to first epinephrine | Typical clinical sequelae |
|-------------------------------------------|---------------------------|
| <5 min | Rapid reversal of hypotension, airway edema; minimal morbidity |
| 5–15 min | Higher likelihood of hypotensive shock, progressive hypoxia, need for ICU monitoring |
| >15 min | Increased risk of cardiac arrhythmias, organ hypoperfusion, refractory shock, and higher mortality |

Evidence from observational studies shows that every 10‑minute delay increases the odds of requiring ≥2 epinephrine injections by ~20 % and escalates the need for ICU admission. The underlying pathophysiology is the continued unchecked release of histamine, tryptase, leukotrienes, and platelet‑activating factor; without the vasoconstrictor and bronchodilator effects of epinephrine, the patient’s hemodynamics deteriorate while mucosal edema progresses.

---

### How the 1‑hour delay from clarithromycin ingestion likely amplified severity

1. **Rapid, but incomplete, absorption of clarithromycin**  
   Clarithromycin is orally absorbed within 30–90 min. In this patient, the first symptoms appeared 1 h after ingestion, indicating that systemic levels had risen sufficiently to trigger mast‑cell degranulation. A 1‑h lag between ingestion and symptomatic onset is typical for oral antibiotics in anaphylaxis.

2. **Escalating mediator release during the untreated interval**  
   Without epinephrine, mast cells continued to liberate histamine, tryptase, and other mediators, causing progressive hypotension, hypoxemia, and cutaneous manifestations. The patient’s BP fell to 74/50 mm Hg and SpO₂ to 88 % before EM received the 0.3 mg IM dose, illustrating that the delayed drug allowed the reaction to reach a fulminant stage.

3. **Mitigation of airway edema and bronchoconstriction**  
   Epinephrine’s α‑adrenergic effect constricts blood vessels, limiting angioedema, while β2 agonism dilates airways. The 1‑h delay meant the patient’s airway swelled and bronchoconstriction progressed, leading to the hypoxemia that required temporary oxygen re‑initiation during subsequent relapses.

4. **Propensity for multiphasic courses in high‑tryptase patients**  
   Baseline and peak tryptase levels in this case were markedly elevated, a known risk factor for biphasic/multiphasic anaphylaxis. Delayed first‑dose epinephrine likely allowed a larger mediator burden, predisposing the patient to multiple recurrences as the initial burst waned and a secondary wave emerged.

5. **Dose‑related under‑treatment due to auto‑injector size**  
   The emergency department’s first two injections were 0.3 mg each (less than the guideline‑recommended 0.5 mg for a 104‑kg patient). Even after the initial dose, the sustained hypotension and rash suggested that the dose was insufficient to blunt the ongoing mediator release, necessitating a second and third IM dose over the next 12 h.

---

### Take‑home points for practice

- **Administer epinephrine within the first 5–7 min** of anaphylaxis onset. A 1‑hour delay, as seen here, exposes the patient to a higher risk of refractory shock and multiphasic recurrence.
- **Use the guideline‑recommended dose (0.01 mg/kg, max 0.5 mg)**; auto‑injectors containing only 0.3 mg may under‑dose adults >50 kg, prolonging the reaction and increasing the need for repeat doses.
- **Early epinephrine also reduces downstream complications**: it limits the extent of mucosal edema, preserves hemodynamic stability, and may blunt the secondary mediator surge that feeds multiphasic episodes.
- **In patients with high tryptase or suspected mast‑cell disorders, monitor closely for at least 12 h after initial improvement**, as recurrent episodes are more common.

In our case, a one‑hour lag between clarithromycin ingestion and the first epinephrine injection both allowed the reaction to become severe and set the stage for a multiphasic course. Rapid, appropriately dosed epinephrine remains the single most effective intervention to prevent these consequences.
