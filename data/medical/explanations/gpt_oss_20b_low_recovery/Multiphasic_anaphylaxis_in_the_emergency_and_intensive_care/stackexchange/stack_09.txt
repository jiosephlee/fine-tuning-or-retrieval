Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Morning Report] What alternative therapeutic approaches were considered for managing multiphasic anaphylaxis in this patient, and what are the risks and benefits of continuous intravenous epinephrine versus an expectant approach?

**Clinical Teaching Point – Managing Multiphasic Anaphylaxis**

In the case of the woman in her 60s who experienced four separate anaphylactic attacks after a single dose of clarithromycin, the care team faced a classic dilemma: **continue epinephrine continuously or wait (“expectant”) for a spontaneous resolution?**  The authors explicitly considered both strategies and ultimately observed that the patient did not recur.

---

### 1. Rationale for Continuous Intravenous (IV) Epinephrine

| **Potential Benefit** | **Associated Risk** |
|------------------------|---------------------|
| *Pre‑emptive control* of mast‑cell degranulation, potentially lowering the need for repeated IM injections and preventing hemodynamic collapse | *Alpha‑adrenergic toxicity* – severe hypertension, arrhythmias, precipitating ischemia |
| *Stable hemodynamics* during extended monitoring beds and in ICU settings | *Alpha‑tone leading to peripheral vasoconstriction* – decreased gastric/renal perfusion |
| *Avoidance of repeated IM injections* in patients who may have difficult access or subcutaneous edema | *Blunted response* – if epinephrine exhausted, ischemic risk increases |
| *Potentially shorter total duration of anaphylaxis* by keeping mast‑cell mediators in check | *Over‑administration* – risk of tachycardia, anxiety, tremor |

The **dose** would normally be 0.01 mg/kg IV (≈0.03–0.05 mg/min).  In practice, continuous infusion is rarely used because most guidelines recommend intermittent IM dosing and because monitoring for hypertension and arrhythmia can be resource‑intensive.  In the present case, the authors noted that the patient’s episodes were "decreasing in intensity," which made continuous infusion less attractive.

---

### 2. Rationale for an Expectant Approach

| **Potential Benefit** | **Associated Risk** |
|------------------------|---------------------|
| Avoids **invasive therapy** and its side effects, preserving hemodynamic stability | Possibility of a rapid, severe biphasic or multiphasic relapse |
| Reduces **drug exposure** – especially useful when steroids are withheld (no evidence for benefit) | Unpredictable timing of recurrences (up to 78 h reported) |
| Simplifies monitoring – fewer nursing interventions | Missed early warning signs in a patient with underlying mastocytosis (higher mediator burden) |

The decision to observe without continuous IV epinephrine therefore relies on **clinical stability**, absence of airway compromise, and a trend toward decreasing symptom severity—exactly what the team noted (“symptom intensity lessened with each episode”).

---

### 3. Weighing the Two Options in this Case

1. **Clinical Status** – After the fourth episode, the patient was stable, with no ongoing hypotension or hypoxemia, and the severity of symptoms was attenuating.  
2. **Underlying Risk** – The high baseline and peak tryptase levels and suspected systemic mastocytosis technically raise the risk of a late, potentially severe flare.  
3. **Resource Considerations** – Continuous IV epinephrine requires intensive nursing oversight, frequent BP/HR checks, and the ability to titrate quickly – a significant burden in an ICU that might be better allocated to other patients.  
4. **Evidence Gaps** – No randomized data exist comparing continuous infusion to intermittent IM dosing in multiphasic anaphylaxis. Current practice derives from expert consensus and anecdotal reports.

Given these factors, the care team opted for **expectant monitoring in the ICU**.  They augmented this with **corticosteroids** (though evidence for their efficacy is weak), antihistamines, and fluid resuscitation—standard supportive measures.  Importantly, no invasive complications arose, and the patient never required additional epinephrine after the fourth episode.

---

### 4. Take‑Home Messages for Practice

- **IV continuous epinephrine** can be considered when a patient is at *very high risk* for severe, recurrent anaphylaxis (e.g., known mastocytosis, severe initial presentation, or refractory to IM dosing).  It is *not* routinely required for all multiphasic cases.
- An **expectant approach** is reasonable in patients who are *clinically improving* and when close bedside monitoring is possible.  Ensure rapid access to ICU or high‑dependency care if symptoms recur.
- **Monitor meticulously**: hourly BP/HR, continuous pulse oximetry, and bedside observation of airway and skin signs.  Use a lower‑dose epinephrine auto‑injector judiciously, but be prepared to give a 0.5‑mg IM dose if needed.
- **Corticosteroids** remain controversial; consider them only if the patient has a history of refractory anaphylaxis or if the clinician is concerned about delayed reactions in a mast‑cell disorder.
- **Pre‑emptive epinephrine infusion** should be reserved for cases where the *balance of risk* tilts clearly toward forbidding a severe relapse, rather than as a default in every multiphasic scenario.

In this patient, the expectant approach was safe and effective, illustrating that careful clinical judgment can guide the choice between continuous IV epinephrine and close observation in multiphasic anaphylaxis.
