Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Pathophysiology] Why is the timing of tryptase sampling critical in suspected anaphylaxis, and how might the delays observed in this patient’s case have impacted diagnostic clarity?

**Answer**

Tryptase is the most specific mast‑cell mediator that rises in the first few hours of a systemic anaphylactic reaction. Peak plasma concentrations usually occur 30–60 minutes after symptom onset, and levels fall back toward baseline within 4–6 hours. Consequently, the AAP/FAAN guideline recommends three draws:   

1. **Baseline** as soon as possible (ideally before or immediately after treatment)—defines the patient’s resting mediator level.  
2. **Peak** 1–2 hours after symptom onset—captures the maximal release of mast‑cell content.  
3. **Post‑resolution** ≥24 hours after complete clinical recovery—to confirm that the baseline is stable.

The timing is critical because:

* **Sensitivity is window‑dependent** – a sample taken too early (before exocytosis) or too late (after degranulation has ceased) will underestimate the true maximum, potentially masking a clinically significant reaction or suggesting a milder event than actually occurred.  
* **Differentiation of biphasic/multiphasic disease** – serial peaks help distinguish a true second wave of mediator release from residual medication or sustained mild reaction.  
* **Diagnostic work‑up for mast cell disorders** – a persistently elevated baseline or two separate peaks strongly supports systemic mastocytosis or other mast‑cell activation syndromes, which are risk factors for severe and recurrent anaphylaxis.

---

**Impact of the delays in this patient**

In the presented case, the first tryptase sample was obtained 1 hour after the *third* anaphylactic episode, and the second sample 27 hours after the last recurrence. Consequently:

1. **The initial “peak” sample missed the true maximum** that would have occurred after the first ED presentation (when tryptase would have peaked within 1–2 hours). The measured level therefore underestimated the severity of the mast‑cell degranulation.  
2. **A baseline sample was never obtained** – without a pre‑reaction value, it is impossible to ascertain whether the later “peaks” were truly elevated or simply reflective of an already high baseline.  
3. **Serial comparisons were impossible** – we could not document the rise and fall of tryptase across the multiphasic course, limiting the ability to correlate clinical recurrence with biochemical evidence.  
4. **Risk stratification was impaired** – elevated baseline tryptase is a known marker of increased risk for biphasic or refractory anaphylaxis. In the absence of a baseline, clinicians could not use this parameter to decide on observation duration or escalation of care.  
5. **Diagnostic certainty for systemic mastocytosis remained equivocal** – while the two elevated peaks suggested a mast‑cell disorder, the lack of a confirmed baseline and timely serial sampling made it difficult to meet WHO criteria or to reliably rule out other causes of elevated tryptase (e.g., non‑allergic triggers).

In short, the delayed sampling forfeited the opportunity to:

* Quantify the true magnitude of mast‑cell activation at the first event,  
* Demonstrate dynamic changes that would confirm a multiphasic reaction, and  
* Establish a robust baseline to support a diagnosis of systemic mastocytosis.

Adhering to the guideline‑recommended sampling intervals would have provided clearer biochemical evidence of the severity and recurrence of the reaction, potentially informing a more targeted monitoring strategy and aiding in the early identification of an underlying mast‑cell disorder.
