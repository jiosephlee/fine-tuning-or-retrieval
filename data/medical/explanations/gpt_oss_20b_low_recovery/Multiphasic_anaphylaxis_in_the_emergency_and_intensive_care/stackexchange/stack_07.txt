Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Management] Enumerate at least three monitoring strategies recommended for patients after an initial episode of anaphylaxis, and justify the choice of the ICU admission in this particular case.

**Monitoring strategies for the post‑anaphylaxis period**

1. **Continuous cardiac and respiratory telemetry** – Continuous ECG and pulse‑oximetry allow detection of transient hypotension, bradycardia, or desaturation that may herald a biphasic event. In the case report the patient’s first biphasic attack occurred within two hours of the initial episode; continuous telemetry would have documented the rapid drop in BP and the subsequent response to epinephrine.

2. **Serial vital‑sign assessment at 15‑minute intervals** – Rigidly scheduled checks (blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, and temperature) permit prompt identification of subtle physiological changes that precede clinical deterioration. The patient experienced four distinct relapse episodes over a 12‑hour window; a 15‑minute monitoring schedule would have captured the trajectory of each episode and guided timely intervention.

3. **Serial serum tryptase and other laboratory monitoring** – Guidelines recommend at least three tryptase samples: (i) at symptom onset, (ii) 1–2 h afterwards, and (iii) ≥24 h after resolution, to confirm mast cell degranulation and to distinguish true biphasic from sustained reactions. The index patient had tryptase measured only after the third episode; routine serial sampling would have provided objective evidence of ongoing mast cell activation and potentially guided the decision to intensify therapy or to initiate prophylactic steroids/antihistamines.

**Why ICU admission was appropriate in this case**

- **Frequency and severity of biphasic attacks**: Four anaphylactic episodes within 12 h, each requiring intramuscular epinephrine, marked a highly unstable clinical course. The clinical guidelines and the authors’ own discussion emphasize that multiple recurrences and the need for repeated epinephrine injections are strong predictors of further biphasic events and are indications for intensive monitoring.

- **Underlying mast cell disorder**: The subsequent work‑up suggested systemic mastocytosis (elevated baseline and peak tryptase, hepatomegaly). Patients with mastocytosis have a higher baseline risk of refractory or multiphasic anaphylaxis. ICU care provides immediate access to advanced airway management, vasoactive support, and rapid re‑dosing of epinephrine if needed.

- **Resource and safety considerations**: ICU settings guarantee 24‑hour availability of multidisciplinary staff (intensivists, anesthesiologists, allergists), rapid infusion capability, and the ability to perform continuous telemetry. Given that the patient’s hemodynamics were unstable even after treatment and that traditional inpatient wards do not routinely provide telemetry or immediate critical‑care resources, admission to the ICU met both the risk‑stratified monitoring recommendations and the patient‑specific safety needs highlighted in the case report.

In short, continuous telemetry, frequent vital‑sign checks, and serial laboratory monitoring form the core of post‑anaphylaxis surveillance, and ICU admission was warranted by the patient’s multiphasic, high‑severity course and suspected mast cell disorder.
