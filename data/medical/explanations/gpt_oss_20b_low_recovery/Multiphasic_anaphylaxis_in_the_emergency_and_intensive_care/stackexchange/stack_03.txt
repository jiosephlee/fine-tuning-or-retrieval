Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Differential Diagnosis] Name at least three risk factors that have been suggested to predict a biphasic or multiphasic anaphylactic course, and evaluate how the patient’s presentation met or lacked these indicators.

**Risk factors for a biphasic or multiphasic anaphylactic course**  
(approximated from the literature and highlighted in this case)

| Suggested predictor | Rationale | How the patient’s presentation aligns |
|---------------------|-----------|---------------------------------------|
| **Delayed or inadequate initial epinephrine** (≤15 min to first dose or sub‑therapeutic dose) | Late or insufficient adrenergic reversal allows continued mediator release and predisposes to rebound symptoms. | The patient received epinephrine within 1 hour, but the auto‑injector contained only 0.3 mg—approximately 30 % of the guideline‑recommended 0.5 mg for her 104 kg body weight. This under‑dosing likely contributed to incomplete initial reversal and subsequent recurrences. |
| **Severe initial presentation (hypotension, hypoxaemia, extensive cutaneous involvement)** | Extremes of cardiovascular and respiratory compromise reflect a robust systemic mediator surge, increasing the chance of a late‑phase reaction. | Her first ED episode produced profound hypotension (74/50 mm Hg), hypoxaemia (SaO₂ 88 % on room air), and generalized urticaria/angio‑edema—meeting criteria for a “severe” anaphylaxis. |
| **Requirement for multiple epinephrine doses** | A need for ≥2 epinephrine administrations during the same event signals refractory or prolonged mediator activity, raising the risk of later peaks. | She required four separate epinephrine injections (two auto‑injector doses, two 0.5 mg IM doses) within 12 h, clearly exceeding the “multiple‑dose” threshold. |
| **History of prior biphasic reactions** | Prior recurrence implies a heightened propensity for delayed mediator release. | No documented previous biphasic event; this was her first full‑blown anaphylaxis. |
| **Underlying mast cell disorder (systemic mastocytosis)** | Excess mast cells and heightened tryptase release enhance both severity and recurrence likelihood. | Post‑hoc analysis revealed elevated tryptase during the third episode, hepatomegaly, and other clues suggestive of systemic mastocytosis, supporting this high‑risk component. |
| **Obesity / high body weight** | Larger body mass may dilute drug effect and is associated with altered pharmacokinetics of epinephrine. | At 104 kg, she exceeded the 50 kg weight that underpins the standard 0.5 mg epinephrine dose, and the 0.3 mg auto‑injector was markedly under‑dosed for her weight. |

**Assessment of the patient**

1. **Adequacy of initial therapy** – The timely administration of epinephrine is a strength, but the dose delivered was likely insufficient for her body weight, fulfilling the “delayed or inadequate initial epinephrine” predictor.
2. **Severity** – Her severe hypotensive, hypoxaemic, and cutaneous presentation qualifies as a high‑risk trigger for recurrence.
3. **Multiple doses** – Four doses of epinephrine within 12 h meets the risk factor “need for multiple doses.”
4. **Underlying mastocytosis** – The elevated baseline and peak tryptase and associated findings strongly support a mast cell disorder, the most robust predictor of multiphasic reactions identified in the literature.

Overall, the patient exhibited **three of the key risk factors** (inadequate initial dosing, severe initial presentation, and multiple epinephrine doses) and likely the strongest one (underlying mastocytosis). These factors collectively explain her multiphasic course and underscore the need for extended observation and consideration of higher initial epinephrine doses in similar patients.
