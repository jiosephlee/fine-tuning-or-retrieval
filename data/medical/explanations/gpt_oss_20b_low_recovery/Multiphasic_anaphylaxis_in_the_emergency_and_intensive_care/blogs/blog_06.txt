Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# Building a Registry for Complex Anaphylaxis: Why We Need Real‑World Data

## Why the “Multiphasic” Label Makes Us All Stop and Think

When a woman in her 60s drops to 74/50 mm Hg after a single dose of clarithromycin and cashes in four epinephrine injections over a 24‑hour shift, it’s hard to believe the story would be *just* an anaphylaxis case. The reality? A multiphasic reaction, a rare and ominous chore of relapses that takes clinicians out of their comfort zone and into the dark uncertainty of “how long should I keep her monitored?” Most of our guidelines give us a ballpark: keep the patient under surveillance for 4–6 hours after resolution of symptoms. That recommendation is built on a bedrock of anecdote, small studies, and a remarkable lack of high‑quality data.

So why do we still feel apiece? Because, simply put, the “average” anaphylaxis scenario never goes on for a *third* relapse, let alone a fourth. High‑quality data on these outliers are nonexistent—there is no centralized source of information that ties the clinical picture together. And that’s where a dedicated registry would change the game.

## What Makes a Registry “Real‑World” and Usable?

| Data Point | Why It Matters | How to Capture It Quickly |
|-----------|----------------|---------------------------|
| **Baseline demographics** (age, sex, weight, BMI) | Helps adjust dosing and identify risk strata. | Enter into electronic health record (EHR) at triage, auto‑populate from demographics. |
| **Allergy history** (drug, food, insect, environment) | Identifies probable triggers and guides avoidance counseling. | Structured checklist in the pre‑admission screening protocol. |
| **Prior allergic reactions** (severity, triggers, treatment) | Indicates predisposition to severe or multiphasic reactions. | Pull from immunology clinic notes; if no record, ask standardized screening question. |
| **Timing of symptoms relative to exposure** | Determines risk of biphasic vs. persistent anaphylaxis. | Clinician notes the “Onset time” in the present event flow sheet. |
| **Initial vital signs** (SBP, DBP, HR, SpO₂) | Baseline to assess response to treatment. | Automatically logged by vital monitors and pulled into the registry. |
| **Treatment details**: dose/route of epinephrine, antihistamines, steroids, corticosteroids, fluids | Critical for correlating therapy with outcomes. | Enter in a simple dialog box that appears after each epinephrine administration. |
| **Response to epinephrine** (time to hemodynamic stabilization, repeat dosing) | Fine‑grained data on dosing adequacy, including auto‑injector vs. vial. | Clinician tick box for “Response: yes/no”; automatically record time stamps. |
| **Appearance of relapse** (symptom type, time from last resolution) | Core of multiphasic data. | Trigger an automated alert to input “Relapse” details; removes burden of manual entry. |
| **Laboratory data** (tryptase – baseline, peak, 2‑6 h, 24 h) | Helps differentiate mast cell disorders, supports severity grading. | EHR order set includes a “Tryptase – Anaphylaxis” panel that auto‑logs results. |
| **Imaging or other diagnostics** (bone marrow, ultrasound) | Identifies underlying conditions like systemic mastocytosis. | Optional checkbox; flagged for further review. |
| **Disposition** (ICU, step‑down, ward) | Captures resource utilization. | Optional: a single pull‐down menu under “Disposition.” |
| **Follow‑up status** (epinephrine auto‑injector prescribed, long‑term specialist follow‑up) | Anchors patient education and monitoring plan. | Post‑discharge patient portal integration; syncs with pharmacy data. |

### Minimizing Delays

The tricky part is that clinicians are already on the clock each time a patient drops into the ED. To avoid “data‑collection fatigue” we must piggyback on the *information already being gathered*. Most of these data points are created as a natural part of the workflow (vitals, medication orders, etc.). A single, concise “anaphylaxis add‑on” screen that pops on the next screen after the first epinephrine dose will capture everything beyond the basics with just a handful of clicks.

For example, after the patient is stabilized, the ED nurse sees a prompt: “Anaphylaxis Recorded?” – Yes/No. If Yes, a secondary screen auto‑populates with fields for “Timing of onset,” “Drug triggered,” “First epinephrine dose,” “Response time,” etc. The clerk can finish the form while the patient is still in the room, ensuring no delay in definitive care.

## How Buried Data Beats Guesswork

There’s a strong bias toward resource‑intensive care for patients who appear “high risk.” Without data, we over‑intervene in minor cases and under‑intervene in rare but deadly ones. A registry provides the two missing pieces:

1. **Evidence‑Based Risk Prediction** – By crunching millions of case logs, a predictive model could flag a 60‑year‑old woman with rapid relapse after a single epinephrine dose as “high risk” for further recurrences and direct her to ICU‐level monitoring for at least 24 hours—something current guidelines can’t recommend.
2. **Fine‑tuned Treatment Algorithms** – The registry can surface insights about the efficacy (or lack of efficacy) of steroids, IV epinephrine infusion, or high‑dose oral antihistamines in multiphasic events. That will directly influence future guidelines and textbook recommendations.

### Two Real‑World Headlines That Raged Because We Lacked Data

| Headline | Lesson Learned |
|-----------|----------------|
| “Clinicians Slate 24‑Hour ICU Monitoring to All Anaphylaxis Patients” | Without data, the default reaction is over‑cautiousness. |
| “A Vaccination Campaign Backfires After Unintended Biphasic Shock in 1 % of Patients” | Targeted pre‑emptive steroid use and epinephrine doses were not backed by evidence. |

A registry allows us to see “this 1 %” with clarity, and to provide a real evidence basis for those decisions.

## Building the Registry: Vision, Not a Wish

| Component | What We Need |
|-----------|--------------|
| **Governance** | Data‑sharing agreements across hospitals; IRB protocols tailored for quality‑improvement data. |
| **Technology** | A cloud‑based, HIPAA‑compliant database integrated with EHR via APIs; mobile‑friendly interface for field capture. |
| **Quality Control** | Real‑time validation rules (e.g., no missing epinephrine dose field), automated audit reports to entries. |
| **Analytics** | Dashboards for clinicians (e.g., “At‑Risk Anaphylaxis Score”), research queries, and public‑health dashboards. |
| **Sustainability** | Funding through a combination of governmental health research grants and hospital quality improvement budgets. |

The first step is a **pilot project** in one regional health system. Start with a volunteer ED and allergy clinic, collect data for six months, and prove that clinicians can enter the required fields without delaying care. Then, build a consortium and scale up.

## The Bottom Line: From Case Reports to Consensus Guidelines

Case reports bring a story. A registry brings an army. The multiplicity of strategies to handle multiphasic anaphylaxis—pre‑emptive IV epinephrine infusions versus expectant observation—currently rests on individual clinician whim. With a robust data set, we can answer the following questions:

- Does steroid use actually reduce the *frequency* or *severity* of a second relapse?
- How does the dose of epinephrine (0.3 mg vs. 0.5 mg) affect the need for repeat injections?
- Are certain triggers (e.g., macrolides vs. beta‑lactams) associated with a higher relapse rate?
- What is the true role of systemic mastocytosis in predisposition to multiphasic anaphylaxis?

Evidence generated doesn’t stop at academic understanding; it translates into guidelines that are *specific*, *actionable*, and *resource‑efficient*. In an era where every minute of care is precious, knowing exactly how long to observe a patient and what dose of epinephrine to administer could save lives—and, importantly, mean fewer ICU days.

### Take‑Home Points

- **Multiphasic anaphylaxis is a clinical reality, not a grey‑area myth**; documenting it systematically is essential.
- **A real‑world registry can be captured without slowing down care** by embedding simple, clinician‑friendly data entry screens into existing workflows.
- **Aggregated data will sharpen risk stratification, dosing standards, and monitoring timelines**, guiding future consensus guidelines.
- **Funding, governance, and technology hurdles exist, but they’re surmountable** with a clear pilot and a commitment to quality improvement.

Let’s stop chasing “best practice” on a handful of anecdotal cases and start building evidence through real‑world data. If we can get a few hospitals to sign on, we’ll have the first comprehensive view of multiphasic anaphylaxis in the clinical arena—and we’ll be better equipped than ever to keep patients safe when the worst happens.
