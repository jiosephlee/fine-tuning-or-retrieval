Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Clinical Pearls] What are the key clinical features that differentiate immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) from statin‑induced myopathy and rhabdomyolysis?

**Clinical Pearl – Distinguishing Immune‑mediated Necrotising Myopathy (IMNM) from Statin‑Induced Myopathy and Rhabdomyolysis**

| Feature | IMNM | Statin‑Induced “toxic” Myopathy | Drug‑induced Rhabdomyolysis |
|---------|------|---------------------------------|-----------------------------|
| **Onset & Course** | Gradual, progressive proximal weakness over weeks–months; often after a drug trigger but can be insidious. | Rapid, usually within days of statin initiation or dose escalation; usually resolves when drug is stopped or dose reduced. | Sudden, often within hours–days of exposure; dramatic spike in CK followed by *rapid* improvement after drug cessation. |
| **Creatine Kinase (CK)** | Moderate–severe elevation (1000–10 000 IU/L) that **persists or slowly falls** despite stopping the inciting agent; often > 5× ULN. | Mild–moderate rise (few hundred IU/L); often < 5× ULN; usually normalizes within a week of withdrawal. | Extremely high peak (10 000–100 000 IU/L) that **declines within 48–72 hrs** after withdrawal and adequate hydration. |
| **Muscle Biopsy** | Extensive fibre necrosis + macrophage (“muscle‑phagocytosis”) with *scarce* lymphocytic infiltrate; membranous attack complex deposition on necrotic fibres. | No specific histology; often shows mild myophospholipidosis or interstitial oedema; no necrosis. | No biopsy usually performed; if done, shows necrosis but without inflammatory infiltrate. |
| **Autoantibodies** | Anti‑HMGCR or anti‑SRP (≈ 60 % of cases); seronegative subset exists. | Typically *negative* for these autoantibodies; the condition is considered a toxic myopathy. | No autoantibodies. |
| **Response to Immunosuppression** | **Improvement** with corticosteroids ± IVIG or other immunosuppressants; may require prolonged therapy. | No benefit from steroids; improvement with drug withdrawal alone. | No role for immunosuppression; recovery is driven by drug discontinuation and supportive care. |
| **Other Systemic Signs** | Often mild systemic inflammation (elevated ESR/CRP); may have dysphagia, dysphonia, cardiac involvement (rare). | Usually isolated muscle, no systemic inflammation. | May have myoglobinuria leading to renal failure, but no systemic immune features. |
| **Temporal Relation to Drug** | Can have a delayed/subclinical trigger (e.g., semaglutide in the case report) but CK rises gradually over weeks. | Immediate or within days after initiation or change. | Immediate, often dramatic rise within hours of exposure. |

**Key Take‑home Points from the Semaglutide Case**

1. **Slow Muscle‑CK Trajectory** – CK rose from ~300 U/L at initial hospitalisation to > 4000 U/L over 4 months, whereas a drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis would have peaked and fallen within days.  
2. **Persistent Weakness** – Progressive proximal weakness with dysphagia/dysphonia is classic for IMNM; statin toxicity usually spares distal muscles and does not produce swallowing difficulty.  
3. **Biopsy Confirmation** – The muscle biopsy showed classic necrotising myopathy with macrophage‑mediated myophagocytosis and minimal lymphocytic infiltrates—hallmarks of IMNM not seen in toxic myopathies.  
4. **Immunosuppression Response** – Rapid clinical improvement and CK decline after a short course of methylprednisolone and IVIG is consistent with an autoimmune process; statin toxicity would not benefit from steroids.  

**Bottom Line**

When faced with proximal muscle weakness and a markedly elevated CK, ask:
- Is the CK trajectory **rapid and resolving** (rhabdomyolysis) or **persistent and progressive** (IMNM)?
- Are there **autoantibodies** (HMGCR/SRP) or a **biopsy** showing necrosis with macrophage phagocytosis?
- Does the patient **improve** with steroids/IVIG, or does recovery depend solely on drug withdrawal?

The pattern in the semaglutide‑associated case—gradual CK rise, disabling weakness, biopsy‑proven necrosis, and steroid/IVIG response—points squarely to immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy rather than statin toxicity or classic rhabdomyolysis.

### [Pathophysiology] Which autoantibodies are most frequently associated with IMNM, and how would a seronegative profile influence your diagnostic approach?

**Answer**

The two autoantibodies most strongly linked to immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) are:

| Autoantibody | Typical association in IMNM |
|---------------|----------------------------|
| **Anti‑HMGCR** | Statin‑related IMNM; often presents after a long course of cholesterol‑lowering therapy; high CK, proximal weakness, necrotising histology. |
| **Anti‑SRP** | Idiopathic IMNM; occurs without statins and may coexist with other autoimmune features; also high CK, proximal weakness, necrosis with few lymphocytes. |

**Diagnostic implications of a seronegative profile**

1. **IMNM cannot be ruled out** – Approximately one‑third of patients with classic IMNM have *no* detectable anti‑HMGCR or anti‑SRP antibodies. If the clinical picture (rapidly progressive proximal weakness, markedly elevated CK, muscle biopsy with necrosis and macrophage‑mediated phagocytosis, and low lymphocytic infiltrate) is classic, seronegativity does not exclude the diagnosis.

2. **Broaden the myositis panel** – In a seronegative patient, continue testing for the full spectrum of myositis‑specific antibodies (Jo‑1, Pl‑7, PL‑12, Mi‑2, etc.) because other autoantibodies may be present or may emerge over time. In the case report, the patient’s panel was negative for all tested antibodies, yet the biopsy and clinical course fulfilled the criteria for IMNM.

3. **Focus on tissue morphology** – Muscle biopsy is the gold standard in seronegative IMNM. The study demonstrates classic necrotising fibres with macrophage phagocytosis and occasional T‑cell infiltration, confirming the diagnosis despite negative serology.

4. **Consider drug triggers** – When antibodies are absent, a thorough medication history is critical. The patient’s abrupt and dramatic rise in CK after a single dose of semaglutide, followed by gradual improvement with immunosuppression and drug withdrawal, suggests a drug‑induced IMNM rather than an autoimmune disease driven by unknown antibodies.

5. **Tailor therapy** – Seronegative IMNM may respond rapidly to short courses of high‑dose steroids and IVIG, as in the reported case, because the underlying trigger (here, a GLP‑1 receptor agonist) is removed. Long‑term immunosuppression may be unnecessary when the inciting agent is discontinued and the patient’s weakness resolves.

**Bottom line:** Anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP antibodies are the hallmark serologic markers of IMNM. However, a negative antibody panel does not exclude the disease; confirmatory muscle biopsy and a meticulous review of drug exposures become pivotal in the diagnostic algorithm. In seronegative patients, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for drug‑induced IMNM and prioritize prompt withdrawal of potential culprits alongside immunosuppressive therapy.

### [Differential Diagnosis] What histopathological findings on muscle biopsy are characteristic of IMNM, and why is the paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates significant?

**Histopathology of IMNM**

A muscle biopsy in immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy shows:

| Feature | What you see | Why it matters |
|---------|--------------|----------------|
| **Necrotic fibers** | Numerous acutely necrotic myofibers, frequently with a “ghost” nuclear outline or characteristic cytoplasmic fragmentation | Demonstrates the primary muscle‑cell injury that defines the disease |
| **Regenerating fibers** | Capsulated glycogen‑rich or “bamboo‑shaped” nuclei | Confirms ongoing repair of the necrotic injury |
| **Macrophage‑mediated phagocytosis** | Acid phosphatase‑positive, CD68‑positive macrophages engulfing or “spearing” necrotic cores (myophagocytosis) | The dominant effector cells in IMNM are macrophages rather than lymphocytes |
| **Membrane‑attack‑complex (MAC) deposition** | C5b-9 immunostain on necrotic and surrounding fibers | Indicates complement‑mediated cytotoxicity, a central pathogenetic mechanism |
| **Sparse lymphocytic infiltrate** | Rare CD3⁺ T‑cells and minimal perivascular inflammation | The hallmark of IMNM; the paucity differentiates it from polymyositis/dermatomyositis, which show dense interstitial or perivascular lymphocytic infiltrates and sometimes fibrin strands |

**What the paucity of lymphocytes tells us**

1. **Pathophysiologic distinction** – In IMNM the primary injury is not driven by T‑cell mediated attack on myofibers but by antibody‑dependent complement activation and macrophage‑mediated clearance. Thus, immune cells are largely absent from the interstitium.

2. **Diagnostic clarity** – The absence of a prominent lymphocytic infiltrate helps rule out overlap myositis or dermatomyositis and supports the diagnosis when the clinical picture and CK elevation are consistent.

3. **Therapeutic implications** – Because the main effector arm involves complement and macrophages, treatments that blunt antibody‑mediated injury (corticosteroids, IVIG, rituximab) can be effective even when standard anti‑inflammatory regimens might be less so.

In the case presented, the biopsy of the right vastus lateralis showed “dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibres,” acid phosphatase and CD68 staining confirming macrophage phagocytosis, MAC deposition on fibers, and only *rare* CD3⁺ T‑cells and perivascular inflammation. This classic histologic triad—necrosis, macrophage phagocytosis, and minimal lymphocytic infiltrate—confirms an immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy, supporting the diagnosis that led to prompt immunosuppressive therapy and recovery.

### [Morning Report] In this case, why might the initial creatine kinase (CK) elevation of 337 U/L be considered incongruent with a classic rhabdomyolysis picture?

**Answer**

A CK of 337 U/L is only modestly above the upper limit of normal and does not meet the classic laboratory threshold for rhabdomyolysis (typically >5,000 U/L). In classical drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis, a single exposure to a myotoxic agent produces an abrupt, dramatic surge in CK (often 10‑ to 40‑fold above normal) accompanied by myalgias, dark‑urine pigmenturia, and sometimes acute kidney injury that improves rapidly with hydration and drug discontinuation. In this patient:

* **Magnitude** – The initial CK was only ~1.8 × ULN, far below the >5‑fold rise characteristic of rhabdomyolysis.
* **Symptomatology** – She had no muscle pain, myoglobinuria, or other signs of myofiber necrosis at that time; the only complaints were gastrointestinal and renal in‑patient symptoms.
* **Time course** – Creatinine and metabolic acidosis returned with hydration, but CK rose slowly over months, eventually reaching 3,992 U/L after 4 months—an indolent, progressive pattern more typical of an autoimmune myopathy than an acute, toxin‑driven muscle injury.

Thus, the initial CK elevation is incongruent with a classic rhabdomyolysis picture; it fits better with the early, low‑grade muscle damage that precedes the full‑blown immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy observed later in this case.

### [Management] Explain the rationale for using a short course of high‑dose methylprednisolone followed by oral prednisone and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) in treating IMNM.

**Short‑course high‑dose steroids + oral prednisone + IVIG: the rational strategy for treating IMNM**

Immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is a severe, rapidly progressive myopathy in which necrotic fibres predominate, lymphocytic infiltration is sparse, and serum creatine kinase (CK) is markedly elevated.  Two well‑established therapeutic principles underlie the regimen used in the case:

1. **Rapid control of the acute inflammatory‑immune response**  
   • Pulsed methylprednisolone (1 g IV daily for 3–5 days) delivers a high steroid concentration that quickly suppresses cytokine production, blocks macrophage recruitment, and limits complement‑mediated myofibre injury.  In severe IMNM, patients often have life‑threatening respiratory or bulbar weakness; the pulse dose can reverse these deficits within hours to days, as seen clinically in this patient (improved dysphagia and proximal strength after 5 days).  
   • The high dose also dampens the activity of the complement membrane‑attack complex, which immunohistochemistry in the biopsy showed staining for C5b‑9 on necrotic fibres.

2. **Maintaining immunosuppression until the autoimmune process is silenced**  
   • Reinitiation of oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg/day keeps the immune system suppressed while the patient’s muscles recover.  A gradual taper over 4–6 weeks avoids rebound inflammation and permits endogenous regulatory T‑cell re‑establishment.  In this report the patient was weaned off prednisone over ~2 months, consistent with the expected time needed for muscle regeneration and normalization of CK.

3. **Targeted modulation of pathogenic antibodies and immune complexes**  
   • Because many IMNM patients are seropositive for anti‑HMGCR or anti‑SRP antibodies (the so‑called “autoantibody‑positive” subsets), or have otherwise pathogenic immune complexes, IVIG at 2 g/kg infused over 2–4 days provides several mechanisms:  
     ‑ competition for Fc receptors, thereby reducing macrophage‑mediated myofibre phagocytosis (the acid‑phosphatase‑positive, CD68‑positive macrophages seen on the biopsy).  
     ‑ neutralization of autoantibodies and harmful cytokines.  
     ‑ blockade of the complement cascade, important given the membrane‑attack complex deposition on necrotic fibres.  
   • In drug‑induced cases like the semaglutide‑associated IMNM reported here, the inflammatory trigger may be removed (cessation of the drug), but the immune system can remain “primed.”  IVIG accelerates resolution of this primed state, which explains the rapid drop in CK over 2 weeks noted in the figure.

4. **Practical advantages of a short course**  
   • The combination achieves a swift deceleration of muscle destruction while limiting long‑term steroid exposure, thereby reducing infection risk (the patient later developed a septic shock from a pseudomonal UTI).  
   • IVIG, although costly, is most effective within the early acute phase; continued therapy is unnecessary once CK normalizes and strength improves, as observed.

**Bottom line:** In acute, severe IMNM—especially when a potential drug trigger has been identified—a brief, high‑dose methylprednisolone pulse followed by oral prednisone and a single cycle of IVIG delivers rapid, multi‑pronged immunosuppression, leading to swift CK reduction and functional recovery while permitting quick steroid tapering and avoiding prolonged immunosuppression.

### [Pathophysiology] What is the pathophysiological mechanism by which GLP‑1 receptor agonists could theoretically precipitate a necrotising myopathy?

**Answer**

GLP‑1 receptor agonists (GLP‑1 RAs) can theoretically trigger an immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) by inducing a loss of skeletal‑muscle tissue that becomes a target for a dysregulated immune response.  The following pathophysiologic sequence is plausible and consistent with the case of the 60‑year‑old woman who developed IMNM after a single dose of semaglutide:

1. **Direct myotoxic effect** – GLP‑1 RAs are known to reduce lean body mass and, in rare instances, cause acute rhabdomyolysis (e.g., tirzepatide).  Even a subclinical insult may create fragmentary myofiber necrosis that serves as an antigenic source.

2. **Release of intracellular “danger” signals** – Necrotic myofibers expose intracellular proteins (e.g., myosin heavy chain, enolase) and danger‑associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) such as HMGB1 and ATP.  These molecules activate innate immune pathways (TLR, NLRP3 inflammasome), promoting local macrophage and complement deposition, as seen in the muscle biopsy (CD68⁺ macrophages, membrane‑attack‑complex staining).

3. **Activation of autoreactive lymphocytes** – The inflammatory milieu can lead to epitope spreading and presentation of myogenic peptides by antigen‑processing cells.  T‑cell help and autoantibody production may ensue, although the patient’s serology was negative for classic IMNM antibodies (HMGCR, SRP).  The weak SS‑B positivity suggests a broader autoimmune predisposition that may become manifest once the muscle membrane is breached.

4. **Complement‑mediated myofiber lysis** – Complement activation, evidenced by C5b‑9 (membrane‑attack‑complex) deposition on necrotic and viable fibers, can directly damage muscle cells, perpetuating the cycle of necrosis and inflammation.

5. **Clinical progression** – The gradual rise in creatine kinase (from 337 U/L to >4000 U/L over months) and progressive proximal weakness aligns with a slowly evolving autoimmune process rather than acute, purely toxic myopathy.

Thus, semaglutide may initiate a cascade in which an initial drug‑related myogenic insult unlocks an immune response against muscle antigens, culminating in an immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy that responds to immunosuppression and withdrawal of the GLP‑1 RA.

**Applying the WHO‑Uppsala Monitoring Centre (WHO‑UMC) Causality Assessment to the Semaglutide‑Associated IMNM Case**

The WHO‑UMC system classifies drug‑adverse event relationships into six categories—*Certain, Probable/Likely, Possible, Unlikely, Conditional/Unclassified,* and *Unassessable/Unclassifiable*—based on 10 criteria grouped into four sets (see Table 1).  

| Criterion Set | What it asks | How it was met (case) |
|---------------|--------------|-----------------------|
| **A. Timing** | How closely the event onset follows drug exposure? | IMNM symptoms began 4 months after a *single* semaglutide injection. The rise in CK (from 337 U/L to >3 900 U/L) occurred progressively over that period. The latency is longer than typical for a direct toxic effect (e.g., acute rhabdomyolysis), but within the plausible window for an immune‑mediated reaction. |
| **B. Course on cessation** | Does the reaction improve after drug withdrawal? | Semaglutide was never re‑administered. The patient stopped using semaglutide after the initial dose and experienced *gradual* improvement of symptoms and CK with immunosuppression, without recurrence. |
| **C. Re‑challenge/Re‑exposure** | Does the reaction recur upon drug re‑introduction? | Not applicable (unethical; patient declined). |
| **D. Alternatives** | Are other causes (disease, other drugs, or chance) excluded? | No statin use, no other known IMNM‑triggering supplements, no other autoimmune disease or infection explaining the necrotising myopathy, and the only plausible trigger was semaglutide. | **F. Biological plausibility** | Is there a plausible mechanism? | GLP‑1 receptor agonists have been linked to muscle mass loss and rare rhabdomyolysis in other reports (tirzepatide). Immune‑mediated muscle injury is plausible via drug‑induced autoimmunity or direct immune activation. |
| **G. Prior information** | Is there prior evidence of the drug causing the event? | No prior reports of semaglutide‑induced IMNM, but similar events with other GLP‑1 RA (tirzepatide) have been described, suggesting class effect. |

**Interpretation**

Applying the WHO‑UMC algorithm, all *Positive* criteria (A, B, D, E, F) are fulfilled, and no *Negative* criteria (C, G) are present. The absence of a rechallenge precludes a *Certain* rating, and the relatively long latency and lack of prior reports argue against a *Probable/Likely* assignment. Consequently, the authors’ conclusion of **“probable causality”** is justified under WHO‑UMC: *the event is more likely than not attributable to semaglutide, given the temporal relationship, exclusion of alternatives, and objective evidence*. 

**Clinical Take‑away**

- Use the WHO‑UMC matrix to systematically weigh timing, de‑challenge, exclusion of alternatives, objective findings, and prior data.
- In this case, “probable” is the appropriate rating; “certain” would require a rechallenge or more definitive pre‑existing data.
- Clinicians should remain vigilant for muscle‑related adverse events when initiating GLP‑1 RA therapy, especially in patients with pre‑existing hepatic or metabolic disease.

### [Clinical Pearls] Given the patient's isolated SS‑B positivity without ANA or SS‑A, how might this serology influence your evaluation for overlap connective‑tissue disease?

**Clinical Pearls – Serology and Overlap Connective‑Tissue Disease**

**Question:** *Given the patient’s isolated SS‑B positivity without ANA or SS‑A, how might this serology influence your evaluation for overlap connective‑tissue disease?*

**Answer:**

1. **Interpretation of isolated SS‑B positivity**  
   - Anti‑SS‑B (Ro‑52) can be found in isolation and is not specific for Sjögren’s syndrome.  
   - In patients without anti‑SSA (Ro‑60) or a positive ANA, it usually reflects low‑titres of a nonspecific autoantibody (e.g., in idiopathic inflammatory myopathies, mixed connective‑tissue disease, or even in healthy controls).  
   - The titre in this case was low (2.4, vs < 1.0 normal). Low‑titre, isolated SS‑B is less likely to signify a clinically significant overlap syndrome.

2. **Guiding the evaluation**  
   - **Routine work‑up remains unchanged:** ANA, anti‑SSA/SS‑B, anti‑Ro52, anti‑Jo‑1, anti‑SRP, anti‑HMGCR, and other myositis‑specific antibodies should all be obtained.  
   - An isolated SS‑B does **not** mandate an exhaustive search for a systemic autoimmune disease, but it should lower the threshold for pursuing a detailed musculoskeletal and systemic assessment if clinical features evolve.  
   - In this patient, the lack of sicca symptoms, normal salivary gland ultrasound, and absence of other connective‑tissue disease markers argue against Sjögren’s or mixed connective‑tissue disease.

3. **Overlap disease considerations**  
   - Overlap syndromes (e.g., Polymyositis + systemic sclerosis, or polymyositis + systemic lupus) typically present with multiple autoantibodies, a positive ANA, and clinical clues (e.g., skin changes, Raynaud phenomenon).  
   - Isolated anti‑SS‑B, especially at low titre, is not a diagnostic criterion for overlap and rarely predicts progression to a systemic entity.  
   - Therefore, the serology should not tip the clinician toward classifying this case as “overlap connective‑tissue disease” but rather keep the possibility of a drug‑induced myopathy in mind.

4. **Clinical application in the presented case**  
   - The patient’s IMNM was confirmed on biopsy, autoantibody panel was negative for myositis‑specific antibodies, and no other systemic autoimmune features were present.  
   - The isolated SS‑B positivity was noted but did not correlate with clinical disease activity or overlap syndrome.  
   - The temporal relationship with semaglutide and improvement after drug withdrawal and brief immunosuppression strongly favor a drug‑induced IMNM rather than a primary autoimmune connective‑tissue disease.

5. **Take‑home point**  
   - An isolated, low‑titre SS‑B autoantibody in the absence of ANA or SS‑A should be interpreted as a nonspecific finding. It does not, by itself, push the diagnosis toward overlap connective‑tissue disease. Focus should remain on the clinical picture, histology, and response to therapy.

### [Clinical Pearls] What clinical clues would help you distinguish early myocarditis from stress‑induced cardiomyopathy in a patient presenting with elevated troponin and BNP but normal ejection fraction?

**Early myocarditis vs stress‑induced (takotsubo) cardiomyopathy**  
Both entities can present with an acute rise in troponin and BNP while the left‑ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) is preserved.  Clinical differentiation hinges on the **temporal profile of biomarkers, electrocardiographic changes, pattern of wall‑motion abnormality, and imaging correlates**.

| Feature | Early Myocarditis | Stress‑Induced (Takotsubo) Cardiomyopathy |
|---------|-------------------|-------------------------------------------|
| **Etiology/Trigger** | Viral prodrome, immune‑mediated (e.g., drug‑induced IMNM), autoimmune disease | Emotional/physical stress, catecholamine surge (e.g., recent surgery, anesthesia) |
| **Troponin kinetics** | Rapid rise within hours, often >10 × ULN, plateaus then slowly declines over 1–2 weeks | Moderate rise, typically <5 × ULN, peaks later (1–3 days) |
| **BNP/NT‑proBNP** | Modest elevation proportional to myocardial injury | Often markedly elevated, reflecting transient wall‑stress overload |
| **ECG** | Diffuse ST‑segment elevations or PR depressions, low‑voltage or T‑wave inversions, may mimic ACS | Typically marked ST‑segment elevation in precordial leads, T‑wave inversion, QTc prolongation; often a “tako‑tup” pattern |
| **Wall‑motion abnormality** | Diffuse or focal hypokinesis; usually no characteristic regional pattern | Apical or mid‑ventricular ballooning with basal hypercontractility; classic “apical ballooning” |
| **Echocardiography** | Normal or mildly reduced LVEF; global wall‑motion score often highlights subendocardial involvement | LVEF may drop transiently (35–50 %); clear regional pattern |
| **Cardiac MRI** | Myocardial edema on T2‑weighted images; subepicardial or mid‑myocardial late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) | Diffuse myocardial edema; LGE typically absent or limited to small subepicardial foci; no significant scarring |
| **Coronary angiography** | Often normal; no obstructive lesions | Normal coronaries (usually performed to rule out ACS) |

### Practical clues in the present case

- **Cardiac MRI** showed **myocardial edema** and mild mid‑anteroseptal hypokinesis but **no late gadolinium enhancement**, favoring **early myocarditis** with an inflammatory substrate rather than scar‑forming takotsubo.  
- **Troponin** (HS‑troponin 62 pg/mL) was only slightly above the upper limit, which fits the modest rise seen in myocarditis rather than the higher elevations typical of takotsubo.  
- **BNP** was markedly elevated (4358 pg/mL), consistent with both conditions but usually higher in takotsubo; however, the presence of myocardial inflammation can also drive BNP release.  
- **Wall‑motion** pattern was focal (mid‑anteroseptal) rather than the classic apical ballooning of takotsubo.

### Bottom line for teaching
When a patient with normal LVEF presents with troponin and BNP elevation, consider:

1. **Troponin rise magnitude & timing**  
2. **ECG evolution** (diffuse vs regional changes)  
3. **Wall‑motion pattern on echo** (global vs apical ballooning)  
4. **Cardiac MRI** (edema + LGE vs edema without LGE)

In the discussed case, the combination of focal edema, modest troponin rise, and lack of typical takotsubo wall‑motion abnormality supports **early myocarditis** over stress‑induced cardiomyopathy.
