Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

## Clinical Context and Baseline Pathophysiology

Obesity, type 2 diabetes, and associated dyslipidemia form a tightly coupled metabolic milieu that predisposes individuals to a spectrum of organ dysfunction. In the patient described here, the long‑standing hepatic steatosis, hypertension, and hypothyroidism represent sequelae of this metabolic derangement and set the stage for interventions such as weight‑loss pharmacotherapy. Glucagon‑like peptide‑1 (GLP‑1) receptor agonists occupy an essential niche in the contemporary treatment armamentarium for type 2 diabetes and obesity, partially because their mechanism of action targets the same tissue systems affected by the metabolic syndrome.

### GLP‑1 Receptors, Mechanism of Action, and Therapeutic Uses

GLP‑1 is an incretin hormone secreted by intestinal L‑cells in response to nutrient ingestion. It binds to the GLP‑1 receptor, a G‑protein‑coupled receptor expressed predominantly on pancreatic β‑cells, but also on cardiac myocytes, adipocytes, and skeletal‐muscle tissues. Activation of this receptor stimulates cyclic AMP production, leading to glucose‑dependent insulin secretion, suppression of glucagon release, delays in gastric emptying, and central appetite suppression. Clinically, GLP‑1 receptor agonists such as semaglutide and liraglutide are employed to lower glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), promote weight loss, and reduce cardiovascular risk in patients with diabetes or severe obesity. Their pharmacokinetic profiles—subcutaneous injection with once‑weekly formulations for semaglutide—offer convenient therapeutic options.

### Metabolic and Muscular Adverse Effects of GLP‑1 Receptor Agonists

The most frequently reported adverse effects of GLP‑1 receptor agonists are gastrointestinal, including nausea, vomiting, and anorexia, often transient and dose‑dependent. Less common but increasingly recognized are electrolyte disturbances, pancreatitis, and acute kidney injury related to volume depletion. A growing body of case reports and post‑marketing surveillance suggest that GLP‑1 receptor agonists may also precipitate skeletal‑muscle injury. Evidence includes acute rhabdomyolysis with markedly elevated creatine kinase (CK) levels and, more recently, necrotising myopathy with histologic manifestations of myofiber necrosis and macrophage infiltration. Although the pathogenic mechanism remains incompletely understood, hypotheses include cytokine‑mediated muscle inflammation, direct mitochondrial toxicity, or unmasking of an autoimmune process in susceptible hosts.

### Immune‑Mediated Necrotising Myopathy (IMNM): Clinical Hallmarks

IMNM is a distinct autoimmune myopathy characterized by severe, often rapidly progressive proximal muscle weakness. Pathologically, muscle biopsies reveal widespread myofiber necrosis with active phagocytosis and remarkable paucity of inflammatory infiltrates compared to other idiopathic inflammatory myopathies such as polymyositis or dermatomyositis. Clinicians should recognize that the clinical picture is dominated by a loss of power in the shoulders and hips, difficulty rising from a chair, dysphagia, or dysphonia, with relatively few extra‑muscular manifestations. Imaging typically shows symmetric muscular edema or atrophy, whereas laboratory evaluation shows markedly elevated CK, often in the thousands, accompanied by muscle‐specific enzymes such as aldolase and sometimes mildly abnormal transaminases.

### Serological Markers in IMNM: HMGCR, SRP, and the Seronegative Spectrum

Autoantibody testing forms a cornerstone of IMNM diagnosis because the presence of specific myositis‑associated antibodies strongly directs the clinician toward a certain disease subset. Anti‑HMGCR antibodies are best known for their association with statin‑related myopathies and correlate with a more aggressive disease course and higher propensity for permanent weakness. Anti‑SRP antibodies, on the other hand, are linked to early, severe muscle involvement and a higher likelihood of treatment resistance. However, roughly one third of patients clinically fulfilling criteria for IMNM lack either of these antibodies (seronegative). Seronegativity complicates diagnostic certainty and often necessitates reliance on muscle biopsy and the exclusion of other causes of muscle disease. In the case presented, the patient’s myositis panel was negative for both anti‑HMGCR and anti‑SRP, placing her firmly in the seronegative category.

### Statins and Other Drugs as Triggers of IMNM

Statins have long been implicated as inciting agents for IMNM, particularly in those with anti‑HMGCR antibodies. The mechanism may involve the inhibition of the mevalonate pathway and subsequent down‑regulation of cholesterol synthesis, leading to reduced β‑cell survival signals and increased susceptibility to autoimmunity. Nonetheless, several reports have linked other pharmacologic agents—including certain antibiotics (e.g., minocycline), antimalarials, and even iodinated contrast agents—to the development of IMNM. The dense immune milieu in patients with metabolic syndrome, coupled with low‑grade chronic inflammation, may further lower the threshold for drug‑induced muscle autoimmunity. In the patient at hand, the temporal relationship between the single semaglutide dose and the subsequent rise in CK, along with the absence of other recognized triggers (e.g., statins, nuclear‑statin supplements, or mushrooms), suggests that semaglutide may have acted as a precipitating antigenic stimulus in an otherwise susceptible host.

## 2. Clinical Presentation and Initial Work‑Up

The patient’s clinical course began abruptly after her first subcutaneous injection of semaglutide (0.25 mg). Within hours she developed profuse nausea and vomiting, prompting self‑medication with oral anti‑emetics. Two days later she presented to the emergency department with persistent emesis, worsening dyspnoea, and marked oliguria. Physical examination revealed clear‑to‑audible lungs and a non‑pulsatile tachycardia; there were no peripheral stigmata of volume depletion despite a history of normal oral intake. Initial laboratory testing documented an acute rise in serum creatinine to 2.1 mg/dL (baseline 0.9 mg/dL) and a severe metabolic acidosis (pH 7.15, bicarbonate 11 mmol/L). The only muscular abnormality noted at that moment was a creatine kinase (CK) level of 337 U/L (normal 12–191 U/L). No myalgias, tenderness, or weakness were reported, and the patient denied any “muscle cramps” or “sharp pains.” She was admitted for aggressive intravenous hydration, bicarbonate therapy, and anti‑emetic support. Low‑volume status resolved within 48 h, and renal function returned to baseline without dialysis. At discharge she was afebrile, hemodynamically stable, and without objective neuromuscular findings.

Although the initial episode was largely gastrointestinal and renal in nature, a subtle elevation in CK prompted routine surveillance. However, CK levels remained stable for the next two weeks and then began a gradual upward trajectory. During this period the patient reported beginning to “feel tired” and “exhausted after walking the hallway,” but she denied pain or weakness. Over the next month, she could no longer maintain an upright posture without assistance; she complained of a sour taste, throat tightness, and difficulty eating solid food. By the end of the fourth month after semaglutide initiation, she could not stand or sit unassisted and reported choking on liquids. Physical examination at this point revealed marked proximal weakness in the upper and lower limbs (Medical Research Council grade 3/5 bilaterally in the shoulder abductors, elbow flexors, hip flexors, and knee extensors) and a dysphonic voice with mild dysphagia. Cranial nerve testing was otherwise normal.

The evolution of laboratory values mirrored the clinical decline. CK climbed steadily: 999 U/L at month 2, 1,237 U/L at month 3, and 3,992 U/L at the time of transfer to the tertiary centre. Transaminases remained mildly elevated (AST 191 U/L, ALT 165 U/L) but did not track with CK, suggesting that the liver injury identified years prior (non‑alcoholic fatty liver disease) was the likely source. Thyroid‑stimulating hormone (TSH) remained within normal limits (1.2 mIU/L), effectively excluding thyroid‑associated myopathy. The patient’s anti‑rheumatic serology (ANA, SS‑A, SS‑B, anti‑Smith, anti‑U1 RNP, ANCA, anti‑mitochondrial) was negative; the only positive serology was a low‑titre SS‑B (2.4 U/mL) without accompanying SS‑A or ANA. Inflammatory markers were mildly elevated (ESR 22 mm/h, CRP 22.8 mg/L), and cardiac troponin-T was slightly above the upper limit of normal (62 pg/mL). No evidence of systemic infection or malignancy was found on full imaging work‑up.

The early magnetic‑resonance imaging (MRI) of the bilateral thighs, performed after the onset of dysphagia, revealed symmetric muscle oedema predominantly involving the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps, as well as subcutaneous edema in the lateral thigh. No focal necrosis or fatty infiltration was yet evident. Chest high‑resolution computed tomography (HRCT) was unremarkable for interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. A liver ultrasound with Doppler confirmed the pre‑existing steatosis but showed no portal hypertension or focal lesions.

Differential diagnosis at this juncture included:

1. **Rhabdomyolysis** secondary to an acute drug effect or volume depletion. Classic rhabdomyolysis presents with markedly elevated CK (>10,000 U/L), myalgias, and prodromal symptoms such as dark urine. These features were not observed.
2. **Drug‑induced myopathy** from semaglutide or other medications (e.g., statins, immunosuppressants). The temporal association with semaglutide and the unremarkable medication history weighed in favor of this.
3. **Idiopathic immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM)**, usually associated with anti‑HMGCR or anti‑SRP antibodies. Seronegativity does not exclude IMNM, but early muscle biopsy was not yet performed.

The progressive rise in CK, the pattern of proximal weakness, dysphagia, and imaging evidence of widespread myositis led the treating team to consider a diagnosis of IMNM, prompting the performance of a muscle biopsy. The early laboratory and imaging data were pivotal in shifting suspicion away from a purely gastrointestinal/renal picture toward a systemic myopathic process.

## 3. Diagnostic Reasoning and Confirmatory Testing

Diagnosing immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) hinges on a systematic exclusion of mimicking conditions and the demonstration of characteristic histopathologic findings. In this case, the clinical picture of progressive proximal weakness, marked dysphagia, and a dramatic rise in creatine kinase (CK) prompted a broad laboratory and imaging work‑up that progressively narrowed the differential to IMNM.

### Serologic Work‑up

The patient’s serologic panel was extensive and uniformly negative for the classic autoantibodies that accompany connective‑tissue disorders. Anti‑nuclear antibody (ANA), anti‑SSA (Ro) and anti‑SSB (La) antigens were all negative, except for a low‑titre anti‑SSB (2.4 index, normal < 1.0). Anti‑Ro tends to accompany anti‑La positivity, so isolated anti‑SSB is uncommon and, in this context, did not point to Sjögren’s syndrome or other systemic autoimmune disease. The myositis autoantibody panel, encompassing anti‑HMGCR, anti‑SRP, anti‑Jo‑1, anti‑PL‑7, anti‑PL‑12, anti‑MDA‑5, anti‑TIF1‑γ, anti‑Mi‑2, anti‑NXP‑2, and anti‑cN‑1A, was negative. This seronegative status is consistent with the estimated one third of IMNM cases that lack detectable autoantibodies and therefore rely more heavily on biopsy for diagnosis.

### Inflammatory Markers and Cardiac Biomarkers

High‑sensitivity troponin T was modestly elevated (62 pg/mL, normal ≤ 53 pg/mL), and pro‑BNP was markedly raised (4,358 pg/mL). ESR (22 mm/h) and CRP (22.8 mg/L) were also elevated, reflecting a systemic inflammatory state. While troponin and BNP elevations could suggest myocarditis or stress‑induced cardiomyopathy, the subsequent imaging studies (see below) failed to demonstrate definitive cardiac involvement. Elevated inflammatory markers are nonspecific but reinforce the need to consider inflammatory myopathies.

### MRI of Thigh Muscles

The thigh MRI revealed symmetric, bilateral oedema predominantly affecting proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps muscle groups, along with subcutaneous oedema of the lateral thighs. This pattern is highly suggestive of an inflammatory myopathy with a predilection for proximal musculature. Unlike dermatomyositis, which classically shows subcutaneous fat involvement, or inclusion body myositis, which typically displays focal, irregular, non‑symmetrical oedema, the symmetric oedema here is more compatible with IMNM or other myositis subtypes. Importantly, there were no areas of fatty infiltration or chronic changes that would signal a prior chronic myopathy.

### Cardiac MRI and Echocardiography

Echocardiography demonstrated preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (65–70 %) but a mildly elevated right ventricular systolic pressure (46 mm Hg). Cardiac MRI revealed mild hypokinesis of the mid‑anteroseptal and anterior segments, with associated myocardial oedema but no late gadolinium enhancement or scarring. These findings argue against significant myocardial injury and support the interpretation that the troponin and BNP elevations may represent an early, reversible stress reaction rather than fulminant myocarditis. Excluding active cardiac inflammation was essential because myocarditis can coexist with myositis and would alter management.

### Muscle Biopsy Findings

The definitive evidence for IMNM came from the muscle biopsy of the right vastus lateralis. Histology showed:

- **Necrotising and regenerating myofibres**: Occasional fibre necrosis with loss of cytoplasm, surrounded by macrophage‑mediated phagocytosis.  
- **Macrophage infiltrates**: CD68‑positive cells and acid phosphatase staining highlighted myophagocytosis.  
- **Membrane attack complex (MAC)** deposition on both necrotising and non‑necrotising fibres, indicating complement‑mediated damage.  
- **Sparse T‑cell infiltrates**: Rare CD3‑positive cells and perivascular lymphocytes, confirming the paucity of lymphocytic inflammation that distinguishes IMNM from polymyositis.  
- **Absence of granulomas, vasculitis, or inclusion bodies**, ruling out other inflammatory myopathies.

These microscopic hallmarks—necrosis with macrophage phagocytosis, complement deposition, and very few lymphocytes—are the clinching features that specifically differentiate IMNM from polymyositis (which has abundant CD8⁺ T‑cell infiltrates), dermatomyositis (characterised by perivascular B‑cell and CD4⁺ T‑cell inflammation with perifascicular atrophy), inclusion body myositis (with rimmed vacuoles and amyloid deposits), and sarcoidosis (non‑caseating granulomas).

### Exclusion of Alternative Etiologies

- **Metabolic myopathies** (e.g., glycogen storage disease, mitochondrial disorders) were unlikely because the patient lacked a history of episodic exertional rhabdomyolysis, exercise intolerance, or organomegaly, and the CK elevation did not resolve with dietary restriction or glucose loading.  
- **Toxic myopathies**—including statin‑induced myopathy—were excluded by the absence of prior statin exposure and the normal myositis autoantibody profile (anti‑HMGCR negative).  
- **Other systemic autoimmune diseases**, such as systemic lupus erythematosus or rheumatoid arthritis, were ruled out by the negative ANA, dsDNA, rheumatoid factor, and complement levels, as well as the lack of extra‑muscular manifestations (no rash, arthritis, or serositis).  
- **Infectious myositis** was excluded by normal viral serologies, including hepatitis viruses and mycoplasma, and by the lack of fever or systemic infection during the acute phase.

### Summary of Diagnostic Reasoning

The progression from an acute CK rise after semaglutide exposure, through a systematic serologic and imaging work‑up that ruled out other inflammatory and toxic causes, culminated in a muscle biopsy that fulfilled the histopathologic criteria for IMNM. The integration of clinical presentation, laboratory findings, imaging, and pathology allowed a confident diagnosis of drug‑associated IMNM, distinct from other myopathies that might present similarly.

## 4. Therapeutic Strategy and Outcome

The clinical course of this patient—rapidly rising creatine kinase (CK), marked proximal muscle weakness, and characteristic biopsy findings—fulfilled the diagnostic criteria for immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) of likely drug‑induced etiology. Because IMNM can be life‑threatening when severe, early immunosuppression is advocated. In drug‑associated disease, however, the offending agent is removed and may, in some cases, allow a shorter course of therapy than is typically required for idiopathic IMNM. The management of this patient exemplifies that principle: high‑dose corticosteroids were combined with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) in a single hospital admission, and the patient subsequently achieved lasting remission while on minimal maintenance therapy and without rechallenge of the drug.

### Initial Pulse Methylprednisolone and Transition to Oral Prednisone

High‑dose intravenous methylprednisolone (1 g/day for 3–5 days) is the established first‑line therapy for acute, severe IMNM. The goal is to arrest ongoing inflammation and muscle necrosis within the first week. The steroid bolus also serves to mitigate the potential for catastrophic respiratory muscle failure that can accompany severe myopathy. Once clinically stable, the pulse is transitioned to oral prednisone at a dose of 0.75–1.0 mg/kg/day (≈ 30–40 mg for a 30‑kg patient). This tapering schedule is orientated toward a trial of 4–6 weeks of therapy, with the dose reduced by 5–10 mg every 1–2 weeks, contingent on clinical and biochemical response.

In this case, the patient received 1 g of methylprednisolone for five consecutive days, followed immediately by oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg. The rapid elevation of CK to nearly 4000 U/L at presentation—well above the usual upper limits for healthy adults—justified the high dose, as did the presence of dysphagia and dysphonia, which are surrogates for bulbar involvement and signal possible impending respiratory compromise.

### Indications for IVIG in Severe IMNM: Dosage, Schedule, and Mechanism

IVIG is reserved for patients who fail to improve adequately with steroids alone or who have disease that threatens life‑saving functions (e.g., respiratory weakness, dysphagia, severe dysphonia). In the context of drug‑induced IMNM, the pathogenesis is thought to be a drug‑triggered autoimmune cascade; IVIG can modulate this process by multiple mechanisms:
1. **Neutralization of pathogenic autoantibodies** (via anti‑idiotypic activity).
2. **Fc receptor blockade** on macrophages, limiting myofiber phagocytosis.
3. **Complement inhibition** (the muscle biopsy showed MAC deposition).
4. **Cytokine modulation** (down‑regulation of pro‑inflammatory TNF‑α, IL‑6).

The recommended dosage is 2 g/kg administered over four consecutive days (0.5 g/kg per day); the patient received exactly this regimen (2 g/kg total). Although the evidence base is extrapolated from other myopathies (e.g., Guillain‑Barré, chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy), case series of drug‑induced IMNM have documented robust improvement with this protocol. The patient’s CK fell markedly within two weeks, confirming clinical efficacy.

### Monitoring Response: Serial CK, Strength Testing, Functional Milestones

Objective monitoring encompasses:
- **Serial CK** (every 3–5 days initially, then weekly) to assess ongoing muscle injury.
- **Manual Muscle Testing** (MMT) of key proximal groups (shoulder abductors, hip flexors, quadriceps) to quantify strength gains.
- **Functional milestones** (standing from supine, climbing stairs, safe oral intake).

In this patient, CK dropped from 3992 to 1237 U/L before discharge and normalized within weeks. Strength testing revealed recovery of ≥ 4/5 on the Medical Research Council scale for proximal groups by the third week of therapy, and the patient was able to rise from a chair unaided. Dysphagia resolved after 10 days of combined therapy, indicated by a return to oral intake of liquids.

### Weaning Protocols and Timing of Immunosuppression Cessation

After the initial bolus and a 3‑to‑4‑week period of oral prednisone, the patient’s CK was already within normal limits. A cautious taper—30 mg every 10–14 days—was employed. Because the underlying trigger (semaglutide) had been withdrawn and there were no other potential inciting medications, the risk of flare was reduced. The patient achieved complete weaning of prednisone within six weeks, with no relapse noted over a subsequent 8‑month period. This rapid taper contrasts with the prolonged immunosuppression often necessary in seropositive, idiopathic IMNM, highlighting the importance of prompt drug cessation and limited therapy in the drug‑associated subset.

### Avoidance of GLP‑1 Agonists and Discussion of Risk–Benefit for Future Glucose/Weight Control

Given the temporal association and severity of the reaction, the patient’s medical team unequivocally advised permanent avoidance of GLP‑1 receptor agonists. Alternative therapies for obesity/diabetes were discussed: metformin, SGLT2 inhibitors, or behavioral interventions were considered. The potential risk of recurrence and the lack of alternative long‑term weight‑loss data for GLP‑1 agonists in this individual made avoidance the most prudent choice. The case underscores that clinicians should weigh the benefits of GLP‑1 therapy against the rare but serious risk of immune‑mediated myopathy, especially when a patient displays early signs of muscle injury.

### Rechallenge Considerations and Ethical Issues in Severe Adverse Drug Reaction

Rechallenging a patient with a drug that precipitated a potentially fatal drug‑induced IMNM is generally contraindicated. The American Society of Clinical Oncology consensus panel consider it unethical to re‑expose patients to a drug known to have caused a severe reaction when safer alternatives exist. In this scenario, re‑exposure would have risked repeat or worsening muscle damage, life‑threatening respiratory failure, and prolonged hospitalization. The Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) causality assessment framework classifies this case as “probable,” reinforcing that a rechallenge would be both unethical and unnecessary. Consequently, the patient was not offered GLP‑1 agonists again, and her care plan was adjusted accordingly.

### Summary

The therapeutic strategy—a brief course of high‑dose steroids followed by a single course of IVIG—was effective in halting muscle destruction, normalizing CK, and restoring function. The patient remained symptom‑free off immunosuppression for several months, a hallmark of drug‑induced IMNM. The case illustrates key principles: aggressive early therapy in severe disease, judicious use of IVIG when steroid response is insufficient or the disease threatens vital function, rigorous monitoring of biochemical and functional parameters, and a cautious tapering schedule aligned with drug withdrawal. Finally, it highlights the ethical imperatives and practical considerations surrounding drug rechallenge in the setting of a severe adverse reaction.

## 5. Clinical Significance and Future Directions

The presentation of immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) after a single dose of semaglutide illustrates a potentially serious, under‑recognised adverse event in patients treated with GLP‑1 receptor agonists.  Clinicians should consider this possibility when encountering unexplained proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, or markedly elevated creatine kinase (CK) in patients recently started on, or with prior exposure to, a GLP‑1 agonist.  The following points distil the lessons from the case and outline avenues for further investigation.

### Probable causality under WHO‑UMC criteria

Using the World Health Organization‑Uppsala Monitoring Centre (WHO‑UMC) causality assessment framework, the reaction satisfies the *probable* (previously *reasonable possibility*) category.  The temporal association is clear: symptoms began within hours of a single semaglutide injection and progressed over the subsequent four months.  The disease course—progressive muscle weakness, CK rise, and resolution with drug withdrawal and a brief immunosuppressive regimen—matches the classic pattern of drug‑induced IMNM.  Alternative explanations (e.g., statin toxicity, other myotoxic agents, occult connective‑tissue disease) are excluded by negative relevant serologies and lack of exposure.  Thus, the case meets the WHO‑UMC criteria for a *probable* causal relationship, though re‑challenge would be unethical and thus unavailable.

### Contrast with tirzepatide‑associated rhabdomyolysis and other drug‑induced myopathies

A recent report of tirzepatide (a dual GLP‑1/Glucagon‑like peptide‑1 receptor agonist) implicated in a reversible rhabdomyolysis episode underscores that GLP‑1 receptor agonists can cause significant skeletal‑muscle injury.  However, the clinical trajectory differs markedly.  In the tirzepatide case, CK was markedly elevated (≈57 000 U/L) and declined rapidly with hydration and discontinuation, consistent with acute, direct myotoxicity.  Conversely, the present semaglutide case showed a slower, progressive CK increase (peak ≈4 000 U/L) over months, intense necrosis on biopsy, and a pronounced inflammatory component—hallmarks of an autoimmune rather than purely toxic process.  This distinction is critical, as management of drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis is supportive, whereas IMNM requires immunosuppression and often drug withdrawal.

### Potential predisposing factors

Three patient‑specific characteristics may have increased susceptibility:

1. **Obesity and hepatic steatosis** – Adiposity and NAFLD are known to alter drug pharmacokinetics and amplify inflammatory cascades; fatty infiltration of muscle may also predispose to vulnerability.
2. **Isolated SS‑B positivity** – Although the patient lacked a classic systemic autoimmune profile (negative ANA, SS‑A, and other myositis‑specific autoantibodies), isolated anti‑SS‑B can signal subclinical autoimmunity.  Such pre‑existing immune dysregulation may lower the threshold for drug‑induced autoantibody production or unmask latent myositis.
3. **Single high‑dose exposure** – The acute reaction to the inaugural dose suggests that an idiosyncratic, or possibly hypersensitivity, response can be triggered without cumulative exposure, differing from the cumulative toxicity seen with some other agents.

These hypotheses remain speculative; nonetheless, they highlight the need to identify specific risk markers.

### Imperative for systematic reporting and registries

Current pharmacovigilance relies on spontaneous case reports, which under‑represent the true incidence of rare but serious toxicities.  A dedicated registry for GLP‑1 receptor agonist–associated muscular adverse events would provide several benefits:

- **Quantify incidence** and establish temporal patterns.
- **Collect uniform data** (baseline CK, autoantibody panels, imaging, biopsy findings).
- **Facilitate signal detection** for specific medication classes (e.g., semaglutide vs. tirzepatide).
- **Guide evidence‑based monitoring protocols** and risk‑stratification tools.

Clinicians should, at a minimum, submit all suspected cases to national adverse drug reaction databases, such as EudraVigilance or the FDA’s VAERS, to expedite signal recognition.

### Baseline CK and monitoring recommendations

For patients at elevated risk (obesity, hepatic steatosis, subclinical autoimmunity), a pragmatic strategy would be:

1. **Baseline CK measurement** before initiating any GLP‑1 agonist.
2. **Periodic CK checks** (e.g., every 3–6 months) for the first year, or sooner if symptoms emerge.
3. **Prompt evaluation** of unexplained proximal weakness, exercise intolerance, or dysphagia with CK, EMG, and, if indicated, muscle biopsy.
4. **Avoid re‑exposure** if a significant rise in CK or clinical myopathy occurs.

Such proactive monitoring can enable early detection of subclinical myotoxicity and prevent progression to full‑blown IMNM.

### Future research directions

1. **Mechanistic studies** exploring how GLP‑1 receptor agonists might incite autoimmunity—examining cytokine profiles, antigen‐presenting cell activation, and cross‑reactivity with muscle antigens.
2. **Genetic predisposition analyses** to identify polymorphisms in immune‑regulatory genes or HLA types that correlate with drug‑induced IMNM.
3. **Pharmacokinetic investigations** in patients with hepatic steatosis to determine whether altered drug clearance contributes to increased exposure of muscle tissue.
4. **Comparative safety trials** that explicitly monitor muscle biomarkers in GLP‑1 agonist–treated cohorts versus control groups.

Such investigations will clarify whether the observed association is causal, identify the underlying pathophysiology, and ultimately yield targeted prevention strategies.

---

**Clinical Take‑home:**  
When prescribing GLP‑1 receptor agonists, especially to patients with metabolic comorbidities or subtle autoimmune markers, obtain baseline CK and counsel patients on recognizing myopathic symptoms.  A sudden CK rise or unexplained muscle weakness warrants immediate evaluation for IMNM, and early immunosuppression plus drug discontinuation can lead to complete recovery, as demonstrated in this case.  Systematic reporting and further research will be essential to delineate the true risk and pathogenesis of GLP‑1‑associated muscular toxicity.
