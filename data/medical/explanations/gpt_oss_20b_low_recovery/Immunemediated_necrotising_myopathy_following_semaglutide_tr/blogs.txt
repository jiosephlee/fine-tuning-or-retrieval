# When GLP‑1 Gets Muscular: Is Semaglutide a New Culprit for Immune‑Mediated Necrotising Myopathy?

## The “wow” of the case

A woman in her 60s, healthy enough to be on her own adult routine, dropped the table in a single dose of semaglutide. Within months her creatine kinase (CK) blew up from near‑normal to a four‑fold surge, her muscles frayed, and her tongue and voice lost their power. The fascinating twist? She never tried any other drugs that could trigger a myopathy – no statin, no red‑yeast rice, no “natural” supplements. Her only recent medication change was a weekly subcutaneous GLP‑1 receptor agonist for obesity. That single injection was the last thing she’d access for nine months – patient severe weakness, IVIG, steroids, and a triumphant recovery.  It’s the first documented case of semaglutide‑associated immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM), a new chapter in the drug‑induced myopathy anthology.

## Quick recap: what is IMNM?

- **Clinical picture**: Acute or subacute proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, dysarthria; CK ≥ 1 000 IU/L.
- **Histology**: Neck‑ing or complete necrosis of fibres with little or no lymphocytic infiltrate; macrophage‑driven phagocytosis (CD68+).
- **Serology**: Anti‑HMGCR (~50 % of patients), anti‑SRP (~10 %); ~30 % seronegative, which is our patient.
- **Prognosis**: Without treatment, progressive weakness and variable CK rebound; with steroids and/or IVIG, many recover clinically and in CK.  
- **Triggers**: Statins, pregnancy, certain infections, and increasingly, drug exposures.

## Why GLP‑1 agonists deserve our attention

### Pharmacokinetics & muscle

GLP‑1 receptors are expressed on skeletal muscle cells where they modulate insulin sensitivity, glucose uptake, and potentially protein synthesis. High‑dose or chronic exposure could disrupt muscle energy metabolism, predisposing to a “metabolic myopathy” picture. Two major recent events underline this:

1. **Tirzepatide** (a GLP‑1/GIP dual agonist) – case of massive rhabdomyolysis (CK ~ 57 000 IU/L) that resolved with drug discontinuation and IV fluids.
2. **Semaglutide** – the first evidence of an autoimmune form, where muscle destruction escalated over weeks rather than hours.

### From toxicity to autoimmunity

- **Direct toxicity**: Classic rhabdomyolysis, immediate spike, CK dumping in 1‑2 days, improves with hydration.
- **Autoimmune**: A delayed, progressive rise over weeks, muscle biopsy shows necrosis with macrophage phagocytosis, and improvement requires immunosuppression—not just drug cessation.

Our patient illustrates the autoimmune route: a single dose triggers a cascade that takes months to amplify CK, and only IVIG plus steroids cracked her myopathy.  

## How to spot a GLP‑1‑induced myopathy

| # | Feature | Why it matters |
|---|---------|----------------|
| **1** | **Temporal association** – Symptoms begin within days to weeks after starting a GLP‑1 agonist. | Drug exposure precedes onset; crucial for causality assessment. |
| **2** | **Rapid CK rise** – CK initially modest, then escalates dramatically over weeks/months. | Distinguishes autoimmune from metabolic myopathy: metabolic peaks abruptly, autoimmune climbs slowly. |
| **3** | **Proximal, symmetric weakness** – often with dysphagia/dysarthria. | Classic IMNM symmetric, proximal pattern is hallmark. |
| **4** | **Absence of rash or classic connective tissue disease** | Reduces likelihood of polymyositis/dermatomyositis. |
| **5** | **Seronegative autoantibodies** – Negative anti‑HMGCR, anti‑SRP, ANA, etc. | ~30 % of IMNM patients are seronegative; still pathologic if histology fits. |
| **6** | **Muscle biopsy** – necrosis, macrophage infiltration, few lymphocytes, CD68+, MHC‑I upregulation. | Gold‑standard for diagnosis. |
| **7** | **Response to immunosuppression** – Rapid CK fall, muscle strength improves. | Indicates autoimmunity; distinguishes toxic myopathy. |

## Clinical pearls

- **Keep a high index of suspicion** even if a patient has only one dose of a GLP‑1 agonist. A single exposure may suffices to launch an autoimmune attack.
- **Early laboratory workup**: Check CK, LDH, transaminases at baseline and at 2‑4 weeks if symptoms arise.  
- **Rule out other causes**: Statins, mitochondrial toxins, infections, endocrine disorders.  
- **Biopsy early**: If CK > 1 000 IU/L and muscle weakness outpaces resolution after drug withdrawal, tissue diagnosis is lifesaving.  
- **Treat aggressively**: High‑dose steroids plus a course of IVIG can reverse the disease quickly, preventing permanent disability.  
- **Monitor for recurrence**: Any re‑exposure to GLP‑1 agonists should be avoided; a secondary reaction can be fulminant.

## Take‑home points

- **GLP‑1 agonists, especially semaglutide and tirzepatide, may trigger immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy in susceptible individuals.**  
- **The clinical signature is a delayed, progressive CK rise and proximal weakness that responds to immunosuppression rather than simple drug discontinuation.**  
- **Early muscle biopsy with immunohistochemistry is the linchpin for diagnosis and can guide therapy.**  
- **Prompt initiation of steroids and IVIG can restore function, and many patients recover fully once the offending drug is removed.**  
- **Clinicians prescribing GLP‑1 agents should counsel patients about the rare but real risk of muscle weakness and advise timely evaluation if symptoms appear.**

## Final thought

As the climate of obesity and type 2 diabetes treatment shifts toward biologics and peptide drugs, we’re learning that even “well‑tolerated” agents can unmask hidden autoimmune pathways. The semaglutide‑IMNM case reminds us that in medicine, a single dose can ignite a delayed, immune‑driven storm. Stay observant, test early, and treat fast—your patients’ muscles will thank you.



# Steroid‑Free or Short‑Course?  A Practical Guide to Managing Drug‑Induced IMNM

## The Hook  
Imagine a patient who gets a single shot of semaglutide for weight loss, feels fine for a few hours, then spirals into profound weakness with a CK of nearly 4,000 U/L months later. No statins, no supplements, no family history. A biopsy shows classic necrotizing myopathy. The culprit? The very drug she was given the day before. In this case, stopping the drug + a brief course of steroids and IVIG got her back to normal in under a year. The lesson? Drug‑induced immune‑mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) may need a *different*, *short‑term* approach than its idiopathic cousin.

## Why This Case Stands Out  
- **Temporal relationship**: Symptoms began within hours of the first dose and only resolved after the drug was stopped and immunotherapy started.  
- **Rapid, severe CK rise**: From 300 U/L to almost 4,000 U/L over 4 months—more explosive than classic drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis.  
- **Seronegative autoantibodies**: No HMGCR, no SRP, no myositis panel positivity—yet the muscle biopsy confirmed an IMNM.  
- **Excellent recovery**: Switched off steroids and IVIG after 4 weeks, remained CK‑normal for 8 months, no relapse after avoidance of GLP‑1 RA.

## What is Drug‑Induced IMNM?  
IMNM is a distinct muscle disease featuring:
- Severe proximal weakness  
- Marked CK elevation (often >1,000 U/L)  
- Muscle biopsy: necrotic fibres, macrophage phagocytosis, minimal lymphocytic infiltrate.  

When a drug *trigger* is in the picture—statins, certain antibiotics, even newer GLP‑1 agonists—the disease can resolve with withdrawal and *short‑term* immunosuppression, whereas idiopathic IMNM often requires years of steroids/rituximab.

## The Therapeutic Algorithm – Drug‑Induced First, Idiopathic Second  

| Step | Drug‑Induced IMNM | Idiopathic IMNM |
|------|------------------|-----------------|
| **Immediate** | Stop offending agent | No drug to remove |
| **First 1–2 weeks** | High‑dose pulse methylprednisolone 1 g/day × 3–5 days (or equivalent IV dose) | Same pulse, but often continued >6 months |
| **After pulse** | Oral prednisone 1 mg/kg/day → taper over 8–12 weeks | Taper over 6–12 months; may need >2 years |
| **Adjunct** | IVIG 2 g/kg over 4 days *single round* (if weakness severe or CK >10× ULN) | IVIG only if refractory or steroid‑intolerant; usually >1 g/kg/month for several months |
| **Monitoring** | CK every 1–2 weeks until <2× ULN, then monthly | CK every 2–3 months during taper |
| **Long‑term** | Remission when off steroids/IVIG + drug avoidance | Often chronic disease; may need maintenance immunosuppression (azathioprine, methotrexate, rituximab) |

**Key difference**: Drug‑induced IMNM often *does not* require prolonged steroids; once the inciting agent is removed, the immune cascade is dampened quickly, and the muscle recovers.

## Why IVIG?  
In the reported case, IVIG was given because:
- The patient had **severe dysphagia** and **proximal loss of ambulation**—life‑threatening deficits.
- CK was >12× ULN, indicating aggressive muscle necrosis.
- Evidence (though limited) suggests IVIG can accelerate recovery in strongly inflammatory myopathies by neutralizing autoantibodies and modulating complement activation.

For drug‑induced cases, a **single infusion** is often adequate, compared to the repeated courses typical for idiopathic IMNM.

## What If Steroids Falter?  
If a patient fails to improve after pulse‐steroids + IVIG, consider:
- **Mycophenolate mofetil** or **azathioprine** as second‑line agents (often used in idiopathic disease).
- **Rituximab** (RTX) or **cyclophosphamide** for refractory cases.
- **Consider re‑evaluation for underlying autoimmune disease** (autoantibody panel, ANA, SS‑A/B serology, etc.), given that a subset of idiopathic IMNM is associated with other connective tissue disorders.

In our patient, rapid improvement avoided the need for chronic immunosuppression.

## Practical Take‑Aways for the ED / ICU  

1. **Keep a high index of suspicion** for IMNM in any patient with rapidly rising CK, proximal weakness, and recent medication changes—even if the drug is not a classic “statin” or “antibiotic.”  
2. **Order a muscle biopsy promptly** if CK >5× ULN + weakness; a diagnosis can be made safely with a core needle and saves time.  
3. **Stop the offending agent ASAP**—the drug itself must be removed before even thinking of steroids.  
4. **Initiate pulse methylprednisolone** early; most clinicians will do 500–1,000 mg IV for 3–5 days.  
5. **Add IVIG** if CK >10× ULN or if the patient is disabling (falls, breathing difficulty, dysphagia).  
6. **Wean steroids quickly**—lead‑in for drug‑induced disease is usually 8–12 weeks.  
7. **Follow CK trends closely**—differential volume status (renal function, hydration) can affect readings.  
8. **Avoid rechallenge**—the risk of recurrence/poorer outcomes outweighs any benefit.  

## Bottom Line  

Drug‑induced IMNM is a treatable, reversible disorder if caught early. Unlike idiopathic IMNM, it does not necessitate lifelong immunosuppression. A *short* course of high‑dose steroids, OCEAN’s (or your own) single run of IVIG, and drug removal can restore normal CKs and muscle strength within weeks to months. Be vigilant about a patient’s medication list, use a muscle biopsy to clinch the diagnosis, and remember that steroids can be “pulled” much sooner in drug‑induced cases.  

**Clinical pearls**

- **Pulse steroids + single IVIG** can be enough for drug‑induced IMNM.  
- **Don't wait for autoantibody positivity**—seronegative cases are common.  
- **Early muscle biopsy** arrives at the diagnosis faster and steers therapy appropriately.  
- **Drug withdrawal is the cornerstone**—there’s no point in prolonged steroids if the inciting factor is gone.

In the era of expanding GLP‑1 RA use, let this case remind us that a muscle flare after a single dose could be more than a transient side effect—it could be the tip of an autoimmune iceberg that resolves with a swift, targeted counterattack.



# From Nausea to Dysphagia: When a First‑Dose GI Reaction Signals a Beneath Myopathy  

## The “First‑Dose Pseudo‑Rejection” that Turns Out to be a Myopathy  
A 68‑year‑old woman came to the ED 24 hours after her **first** subcutaneous semaglutide injection. She was racing for the couch after a dose that should have helped her lose weight, but she ended up throwing up, unable to drink, and developed acute kidney injury. On the first shift, the lab people ran a CK and got 337 U/L – mildly elevated, nothing that screams rhabdomyolysis.  

That was the last “big” thing we knew about her for almost 4 months — until she was found unable to sit up or swallow liquids or solids. CK? 3992 U/L. The diagnosis: **immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM)**. The culprit? Semaglutide.  

What makes this case ruby‑red is the paradox: a single, apparently benign dose of a GLP‑1 agonist that set off a cascade of chronic, disabling muscle disease.

## What Clocked the Puzzle?  
| Timeline | Symptom | Lab/Impression |
|----------|---------|----------------|
| -3 yrs | Fatty liver biopsy | NAFLD |
| 0 yr | Initiated semaglutide 0.25 mg | GI upset, nausea, vomiting |
| ~0.5 yr | Hospitalised for renal failure + 337 CK | mild CK, no myalgia |
| 4 mo | Progressive weakness | 3992 CK, dysphagia, dysphonia |

The dramatic jump from 337 to > 3900 CK over 4 months is textbook for a **necrotising** process, not a clean‑cut drug toxicity that resolves within hours or days. The classic “one‑shot” toxicity picture (tirzepatide case) would show a CK over 20 000 that drops within days of stopping the drug. This patient’s CK stayed low for months, climbed, and then fell only after immunosuppression.  

## Key Features that Should Alert the Emergency Physician  

1. **Early GI upset + renal dysfunction** can be a red flag for drug‑induced muscle injury.  
2. **Serial CK**: The first measurement was 337 U/L. Four months later, it was 3992 U/L. Nothing else was explaining the rise — no statin, no supplements, no massive exercise.  
3. **Proximal weakness** with dysphagia/dysphonia. In an older woman, think myopathy before neuro‑plastic changes.  
4. **Negative ANA, negative myositis panel**. Seronegative IMNM is real – especially when you have a plausible drug trigger.  

## What to Do When a ‘Typical’ Adverse Effect Precedes Muscle Failure  

1. **Document baseline CK** when a patient has an acute reaction to a new drug, even if the CK is normal or mildly elevated.  
2. **Repeat CK** as part of the work‑up for any persistent weakness (e.g., every 48–72 hrs in the acute phase, then weekly if the patient is still deteriorating).  
3. **Hold the suspect drug** immediately if a patient shows any CK rise > 10× ULN or develops symptoms of weakness.  
4. **Early rheumatology/neurology consult** if CK keeps climbing or the patient develops dyspnea, dysphagia, or severe proximal weakness.  
5. **Order a muscle MRI** if the clinical picture is unclear – PROFUSE study says MRI can pick up the “diaphragm sign” and guide biopsy.  

## The Diagnostic Work‑up – What We Did, What We Could Learn  

- **Biopsy:** Necrotising fibres, macrophage infiltrate (CD68+), rare T‑cells, membrane attack complex – classic for IMNM.  
- **Autoantibodies:** HMGCR, SRP, and the full myositis panel were negative. Only a low‑titre SS‑B. Seronegative doesn't mean seronegative – could just be under‑powered panel.  
- **Cardiac Work‑up:** Mild myocarditis versus stress cardiomyopathy; resolved after immunosuppression.  
- **Liver:** Persistent steatosis, not a new hepatotoxicity driver.  

The story stresses that **histology remains king** when suspecting IMNM, especially if autoantibodies are negative.

## Treatment Layman's Story

- **High‑dose IV methylprednisolone (1 g ×5 d)** → drop in CK over a week.  
- **Oral prednisone 1 mg/kg** → tapered over 2 months.  
- **IVIG 2 g/kg** over 4 days because the weakness was life‑threatening (swallowing, breathing).  
- **No further IVIG** due to cost and good recovery.  
- **No statins or other immunosuppressants** needed thereafter.

By 10 months, the patient was back to baseline, off steroids, and never restarted on a GLP‑1.

## Pearls to Remember  

- *A single dose of a GLP‑1 agonist can trigger a delayed, progressive IMNM*.  
- **Serial CK is your compass**. Do it pre‑ and post‑drug initiation, especially when GI or renal side‑effects occur.  
- **Weakness with dysphagia in a patient with a recent drug reaction = think myopathy, not stroke**.  
- **Early rheumatology/neurology referral** shortens time to diagnosis and improves outcomes.  
- **IVIG can be lifesaving** in severe IMNM even when steroids alone seem inadequate.  
- **Stop the offending drug immediately**; recancelling it may be life‑saving even if you’re unsure of causality.  

## Take‑home Message  

A seemingly innocuous nausea episode doesn’t always mean the drug is fine. In patients who develop proximal weakness weeks to months later, consider drug‑induced myopathy. Serial CK, prompt imaging, and a low threshold for biopsy and immunotherapy can make the difference between a long, disabling course and a quick, full recovery.

**Bottom line:** If a patient who has just tolerated a first‑dose of semaglutide (or any new agent) later complains of progressive weakness, ask the kitchen question: *Did you feel worse earlier?* If yes — start CK trending a **today**. And if the CK climbs, dial the neuro‑ or rheum consult right away.



# Autoantibody‑Negative Yet Autoimmune: The Challenge of Seronegative IMNM  

## The Unexpected Twist in a Classic Myopathy  

We recently read a case of a woman in her 60s who developed classic *immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy* (IMNM) after a single dose of semaglutide. Her serology was tidy‑blank: negative for HMGCR, SRP, and every other myositis‑specific antibody on a full panel. Yet her biopsy still showed classic necrotising fibres with macrophage faggots and a membrane‑attack‑complex wall. She improved dramatically after a short course of steroids and IVIG, suggesting an autoimmune driver, yet the usual fingerprints were missing.  

This is not an isolated oddball. Roughly one third of all IMNM cases are *seronegative*—no detectable HMGCR or SRP antibodies. The clinical community has long debated whether these patients represent a distinct disease entity, a subgroup of drug‑induced myopathy, or just the limits of our current testing. The semaglutide story offers a fresh lens on this issue.

## Why “Seronegative” Isn’t the Same as “Non‑Autoimmune”

At first glance, a negative autoantibody panel might make you think, “Okay, this isn’t an autoimmune thing; maybe it’s toxic, metabolic, or viral.” But in IMNM, the term *seronegative* should be treated with caution:

- **Autoantibodies may be below the detection threshold**: Some patients possess very low titres or novel autoantibodies that commercial assays miss.  
- **Granular vs. systemic disease**: Even without detectable antibodies, immune complexes and complement activation can still drive the necrosis seen on biopsy.  
- **Drug triggers**: Certain medications (e.g., statins, GLP‑1 agonists, immune checkpoint inhibitors) can precipitate a self‑perpetuating autoimmune loop that is not mediated by classic HMGCR or SRP antibodies.

In the semaglutide case, the patient’s CK climbed 12‑fold over four months, her weakness progressed steadily, and she responded to immune suppression—classic autoimmune hallmarks—yet had no serologic evidence. This suggests that the pathogenesis is still an autoimmune attack, just without our current antibody markers.

## How to Approach a Seronegative IMNM Work‑Up  

1. **History & Drug Review**  
   - Statins, GLP‑1 agonists, methotrexate, anti‑PD‑1/PD‑L1 agents, and other small molecules are top culprits.  
   - Over‑the‑counter supplements (e.g., statin‑containing ergosterols, red yeast rice) often fly under the radar.  

2. **Muscle Biopsy**  
   - The gold standard for diagnosing IMNM.  
   - Look for:  
     - **Necrotising fibres** with minimal lymphocytic infiltrate.  
     - **Macrophage infiltration** (CD68+) and **myophagocytosis** (acid phosphatase+).  
     - **Membrane‑attack‑complex deposition** on necrotic fibres (C5b‑9).  
     - Rare T‑cell (CD3) presence does not exclude IMNM.  

3. **Laboratory & Imaging**  
   - CK often skyrockets (thousands, sometimes >10,000 U/L).  
   - Elevated transaminases may be hepatic in origin; exercise caution before attributing them to myositis.  
   - MRI can demonstrate muscle oedema; it is supportive but not diagnostic.  

4. **Autoantibody Panels**  
   - Even if HMGCR/SRP are negative, run a comprehensive myositis panel (Jo‑1, Mi‑2, MDA‑5, etc.).  
   - Consider custom assays or referral to a reference lab if clinical suspicion remains high.  

5. **Rule‑Out Other Conditions**  
   - Rhabdomyolysis (acute CK elevation with normal inflammatory markers).  
   - Toxic myopathy (no necrosis, high CK, but resolves on drug withdrawal).  
   - Metabolic myopathies (e.g., mitochondrial, glycolytic).  

6. **Follow‑Up**  
   - Serial CK.  
   - Muscle strength grading (MRC 0‑5).  
   - Functional scales (MRC sum score, MFI‑28).  

## Treatment Nuances in Seronegative IMNM  

| Approach | What’s Known | What the Case Tells Us |
|----------|--------------|-----------------------|
| **High‑dose steroids** (methylprednisolone 1 g daily 3–5 days, then prednisone 1 mg/kg) | First‑line; reduces inflammation, improves strength | Patient responded well, CK fell in 2 weeks |
| **IVIG** | 2 g/kg over 4 days has shown benefit, especially in rapidly progressive cases | The semaglutide patient got IVIG and improved dramatically |
| **DMARDs (azathioprine, methotrexate, mycophenolate)** | Long‑term steroid maintenance; varies in efficacy | Not needed here; the patient weaned steroids over 2 months |
| **Plasmapheresis** | Used in some refractory cases | Not used in this case but may be considered if IVIG contraindicated |

**Key take‑away:** Even when autoantibody tests are negative, seronegative IMNM can respond robustly to conventional immunosuppression. The absence of an antibody does not mean the disease is milder or less autoimmune.

## Why Drug‑Induced IMNM May Lie in the Seronegative Zone  

There is a growing literature linking IMNM to medications:

- **Statins** → HMGCR autoantibody positivity in ~60 % of drug‑induced cases; remaining ~40 % seronegative.  
- **GLP‑1 receptor agonists** (semaglutide, tirzepatide) → limited reports of rhabdomyolysis; this case suggests a necrotising immune response without classic autoantibodies.  
- **Immune checkpoint inhibitors** → can trigger a spectrum of myopathies, often seronegative.  

Drug‑induced phenotypes may provoke a **direct cytotoxic** or **immune‑complex mediated** injury that bypasses B‑cell autoreactivity detectable by standard panels.

## Clinical Pearls  

- **Don’t dismiss a severe myopathy as “non‑autoimmune” just because the antibody panel is clean.**  
- **Muscle biopsy remains the cornerstone for diagnosis in seronegative IMNM.**  
- **A short course of steroids plus IVIG can be curative in drug‑triggered cases; long‑term immunosuppression may be unnecessary.**  
- **Always review the patient’s medication list, including over‑the‑counter supplements, when CK spikes dramatically.**  
- **Keep a high index of suspicion for drug‑induced IMNM in patients with a recent medication change and progressive proximal weakness.**  

## Summary  

Seronegative IMNM challenges the conventional narrative that autoimmunity requires a detectable antibody. The semaglutide case demonstrates that a robust autoimmune response—evidenced by necrotising pathology and response to immunosuppression—can occur without HMGCR or SRP seropositivity. Clinicians should treat seronegative IMNM with the same priority as seropositive disease, leveraging muscle biopsy and a thoughtful drug history to guide diagnosis and therapy. As GLP‑1 agonists and other novel agents become mainstream, the importance of recognizing and managing seronegative drug‑induced myopathies will only grow.



# Muscle and Metabolism: Considering Pre‑Existing Liver Disease and Muscle Inflammation

## What Makes This Case Unexpected

A 60‑year‑old woman with a long history of non‑alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) gets a dose of semaglutide for weight loss. In hours she develops nausea, vomiting and acute renal failure—but she does *not* have a dramatic spike in creatine kinase (CK). Over the next four months her proximal muscle weakness worsens, her CK rises an alarming 12‑fold, and she is ultimately diagnosed with immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). The twist? Her liver enzymes (AST/ALT) have been mildly elevated for years, raising the question: are we mistaking a hepatic flare for a muscular injury?

For any clinician who sees a patient with elevated transaminases, the instinct is to first think of liver disease. Yet, the same enzymes can soar during muscle injury, and distinguishing “hepatic AST/ALT” from “muscular AST/ALT” is not just academic—it changes management from watchful waiting to aggressive immunosuppression.

---

## Clinical Story in a Nutshell

| Timeline | What happened | Key labs/Imaging |
|----------|---------------|------------------|
| **3 yrs ago** | Hepatomegaly, NAFLD on biopsy (HAD score 4/8). | Mildly ↑ALT/AST (~2× ULN). |
| **2.5 yrs later** | Started semaglutide (first dose). | CK 337 U/L (normal 12‑191). |
| **+4 mo** | Progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia. | CK 3992 U/L; MRI: symmetric proximal muscle edema. |
| **During work‑up** | Negative myositis autoantibodies (HMGCR, SRP, etc.). | Muscle biopsy: necrotising fibres, macrophage infiltrates. |
| **Treatment** | Methylprednisolone + IVIG 2 g/kg. | CK fell within 2 weeks; patient weaned off steroids. |

The narrative hinges on *two* intertwined mysteries: a first‑dose adverse reaction to semaglutide, and chronic liver enzyme elevation that could mask a muscular injury.

---

## Why NAFLD Can Confound a Myopathy Work‑up

1. **AST/ALT Overlap**  
   - AST is ~70 % mitochondrial (in muscle) and 30 % cytosolic.  
   - ALT is more liver‑specific but still leaks from muscle during intense injury.  
   - In patients with fatty liver, mild transaminase elevations are common and often attributed to hepatic steatosis.

2. **CK vs AST/ALT**  
   - CK rises 10‑fold earlier in rhabdomyolysis/IMNM.  
   - In this case, CK was only 337 U/L at the time of semaglutide exposure—far below the threshold for clinically significant rhabdomyolysis.  
   - Yet, the AST/ALT remained mildly elevated, tempts the clinician to stay in the “liver” box.

3. **Autoimmune Overlap**  
   - Positive SS‑B autoantibody (isolated) could hint at a systemic autoimmune process affecting liver and muscle.  
   - In the absence of ANA or other myositis antibodies, seronegative myopathy remains a possibility.

4. **Mis‑attribution Timing**  
   - A chronic liver enzyme elevation predating the drug exposure creates a “background noise” that can delay recognition of a new muscle injury.

---

## Key Points in the Evaluation of Muscular vs. Hepatic AST/ALT

| Question | Should it be asked? | Why it matters |
|----------|---------------------|----------------|
| **Is the patient on hepatotoxic meds, alcohol, or viral hepatitis?** | Yes | Exclude common hepatic insults |
| **Does the patient have CK levels >5× ULN?** | Yes | Suggestive of muscle injury |
| **Does CK correlate with symptom severity?** | Yes | Lack of correlation may bias toward liver |
| **Is there eosinophilia, rash, or other systemic features?** | Yes | Could indicate drug toxicity vs. autoimmune myopathy |
| **Are transaminases >5× ULN without CK rise?** | No | More liver‑centric; muscle injury unlikely |

- **Rule out** rhabdomyolysis by checking CK early.  
- **Rule in** IMNM if muscle biopsy shows necrosis with macrophage infiltrate and a paucity of lymphocytes.

---

## Clinical Pearls

- **Never trust AST/ALT alone to localize injury**—always ask the same question for CK and LDH.  
- **Initial drug reactions may not reveal the full picture**; the absence of a CK spike after a severe GI reaction does *not* preclude a delayed, progressive myopathy.  
- **Seronegative IMNM is a real diagnostic snafu**—it can manifest with dramatic CK elevations but negative autoantibody panels. Muscle biopsy remains the gold standard.  
- **Drug‑induced IMNM may respond rapidly to steroids + IVIG** once the offending agent is stopped—contrast with idiopathic IMNM that often needs prolonged immunosuppression.  
- **Avoid “drug rechallenge”** if severe muscle weakness has already been documented; the risk outweighs any potential benefit.

---

## Take‑Home Messages

- In patients with chronic NAFLD and mild transaminase elevations, any new or worsening proximal muscle weakness should prompt a full myopathy work‑up (CK, LDH, muscle imaging, and biopsy if indicated).  
- The temporal relationship between semaglutide exposure and subsequent rise in CK/weakness in this case supports a probable causal link, even though the initial event was not a classic rhabdomyolysis presentation.  
- A negative myositis antibody panel does **not** rule out IMNM; consider muscle biopsy if clinical suspicion remains high.  
- If you encounter a similar scenario, obtain a baseline CK before initiating a GLP‑1 receptor agonist if the patient has renal or hepatic comorbidities.  
- Keep a high index of suspicion for drug‑induced myopathy in the setting of weight‑loss agents—several such reports are emerging, and the class effect may extend beyond classic side‑effects.

---

## Final Thought

When the liver and muscle share the same biochemical alphabet, our diagnostic toolkit must be sharpened. In the era of GLP‑1 agonists, clinicians should widen their differential for new-onset weakness to include drug‑induced IMNM, even if initial labs look “mild.” A proactive, multidisciplinary approach (endocrinology, rheumatology, neurology, and pathology) ensures that the right diagnosis is made—and the right treatment is started—before the patient’s function declines irreversibly.



# Pharmacovigilance in the Age of Obesity Drugs: How Case Reports Shape Safe Prescribing  

## The “What’ and the “Why” — A Case That Got Our Attention  

When a 60‑year‑old woman on a single sub‑cutaneous dose of semaglutide develops progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, a soaring creatine kinase (CK) and a biopsy‑confirmed immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM), you’d think “this is a knee‑jerk reaction to a one‑off toxicity.” Instead, the pattern of illness—gradual decline over 4 months, a 12‑fold rise in CK, and a prompt resolution after stopping semaglutide and a brief course of steroids plus IVIG—speaks of a drug‑triggered autoimmune process rather than classic rhabdomyolysis.

What makes this story stand out?

* **Single dose, long‑term consequence** – most drug myopathies resolve quickly once the drug is cleared. Here the injury persisted for weeks after semaglutide was gone.  
* **Biopsy‑confirmed IMNM with a negative myositis panel** – we don’t have the usual HMGCR or SRP antibodies, yet the histology is textbook for an immune‑mediated cycle.  
* **Clear temporal linkage & withdrawal response** – the classic “probable” causal relationship per WHO‑UMC criteria, flagged by a meaningful improvement after stopping the drug, without any other obvious trigger.

It underscores a very real and increasingly common scenario: powerful metabolic drugs, especially GLP‑1 receptor agonists used for obesity and type‑2 diabetes, may unleash rare but serious immune reactions.

## What Can We Learn About “Drug‑Induced” IMNM from this Case?

1. **Not all myopathies are the same** – classical drug‑induced rhabdomyolysis presents with abrupt, massive CK spikes and normalises within days. Drug‑induced IMNM, by contrast, shows a protracted course and requires immunosuppression.  
2. **Autoantibodies are not mandatory** – a third of IMNM patients are seronegative. Relying on autoantibody panels alone can snap the diagnosis.  
3. **Clinical vigilance matters** – even “minor” CK elevations (in the 300‑500 u/L range) after a first drug dose can herald an emerging autoimmune process if accompanied by new weakness.  

## WHO‑UMC Causality Assessment in Practice  

The WHO‑Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) tool scores causality into five categories: certain, probable/likely, possible, unlikely, and unassessable. For a drug‑induced myopathy, the criteria consider:

| Criterion | How the Case Measures Up |
|-----------|--------------------------|
| **Temporal relationship** | Symptoms began within hours of first dose; CK rose over 4 months. |
| **De‑challenge** | Stopping semaglutide and administering steroids/IVIG led to marked improvement. |
| **Re‑challenge** | Not performed (unethical due to severe reaction). |
| **Alternative causes** | No statins, supplements, strenuous activity, or known connective tissue disease. |

Given the above, the case comfortably lands in the *probable* slot—a useful signal to pharmacovigilance databases and to the clinicians who may prescript GLP‑1 agents for their next patient.

## Practical Steps for Clinicians  

1. **Baseline and Ongoing CK Checks**  
   * Consider a baseline CK before starting a GLP‑1 agonist if you have any risk factors (prior liver disease, borderline transaminases, prior non‑diabetic obesity‑related glomerular hypertension).  
   * Re‑check CK monthly for the first 3–6 months, especially if the patient reports new proximal weakness or fatigue.

2. **Early Recognition & Rapid Work‑up**  
   * **Red flag symptoms**: progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia, dysphonia, difficulty rising from a chair or walking > 5 m.  
   * **Initial labs**: CK, AST/ALT (to discern hepatic vs muscle injury), LDH, electrolytes, basic metabolic profile.  
   * If CK rises > 3× ULN or the patient has symptoms, expedite a rheumatology consult.

3. **Biopsy When Indicated**  
   * A muscle biopsy remains the gold standard to differentiate necrotising myopathy from myositis or metabolic myopathy.  
   * Look for necrotic fibers, macrophage infiltrates, and membrane attack complex deposition.

4. **Report – Amplify the Signal**  
   * **Pharmacovigilance systems**: Your national drug‑reaction database (e.g., VigiBase, FAERS). Include detailed narrative: dates, dose, CK trends, symptoms, histology.  
   * **Literature: case reports, letters** – the only other way to alert the world. Use the exact language of the WHO‑UMC criteria to classify your allergy as *probable* or *possible*.

5. **Patient Counseling & Pharmacologic Decision**  
   * Patient education: “If you feel faint, bruised, or unusually weak—call us immediately.”  
   * Consider switching to a different class (e.g., SGLT‑2 inhibitors, DPP‑4 inhibitors) if risk is high.

## Clinical Pearls  

- **Persistently rising CK after drug withdrawal** (not just an acute spike) is a red flag for an immune‑mediated process.  
- **Seronegative IMNM is 33 % of cases** – muscle biopsy and clinical context can override negative autoantibodies.  
- **Early steroid pulse + IVIG** can reverse a drug‑induced IMNM and shorten the duration of immunosuppression drastically.  
- **One dose doesn’t equal “one‑time risk.”** Drug‑induced autoimmunity can peak weeks to months later.  
- **Pharmacovigilance starts with you.** A single unreported event could mean the difference between a health‑span and early disability.

## Take‑Home Points

- **Baseline muscle enzymes, regular follow‑up, and keen symptom reporting can catch drug‑induced IMNM early.**  
- **Use WHO‑UMC criteria to categorize causality and to structure your reporting narrative.**  
- **When a GLP‑1 agonist is implicated, a brief, aggressive trial of steroids and IVIG often suffices.**  
- **Report promptly to national and international databases; every case builds the safety profile.**  
- **Educate patients on symptoms and encourage immediate communication.**  

Glucose and weight management are critical, but as we broaden our therapeutic armamentarium with GLP‑1 drugs, our pharmacovigilance vigilance must keep pace. One case doesn’t prove causality, but it does put a label on the risk—one that can be remedied with early detection, reporting, and thoughtful clinical decision‑making.  

*Keep an eye on the CK, stay curious about the symptoms, and never dismiss a single adverse event—every report potentially protects the next patient.*
