Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# Muscle and Metabolism: Considering Pre‑Existing Liver Disease and Muscle Inflammation

## What Makes This Case Unexpected

A 60‑year‑old woman with a long history of non‑alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) gets a dose of semaglutide for weight loss. In hours she develops nausea, vomiting and acute renal failure—but she does *not* have a dramatic spike in creatine kinase (CK). Over the next four months her proximal muscle weakness worsens, her CK rises an alarming 12‑fold, and she is ultimately diagnosed with immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). The twist? Her liver enzymes (AST/ALT) have been mildly elevated for years, raising the question: are we mistaking a hepatic flare for a muscular injury?

For any clinician who sees a patient with elevated transaminases, the instinct is to first think of liver disease. Yet, the same enzymes can soar during muscle injury, and distinguishing “hepatic AST/ALT” from “muscular AST/ALT” is not just academic—it changes management from watchful waiting to aggressive immunosuppression.

---

## Clinical Story in a Nutshell

| Timeline | What happened | Key labs/Imaging |
|----------|---------------|------------------|
| **3 yrs ago** | Hepatomegaly, NAFLD on biopsy (HAD score 4/8). | Mildly ↑ALT/AST (~2× ULN). |
| **2.5 yrs later** | Started semaglutide (first dose). | CK 337 U/L (normal 12‑191). |
| **+4 mo** | Progressive proximal weakness, dysphagia. | CK 3992 U/L; MRI: symmetric proximal muscle edema. |
| **During work‑up** | Negative myositis autoantibodies (HMGCR, SRP, etc.). | Muscle biopsy: necrotising fibres, macrophage infiltrates. |
| **Treatment** | Methylprednisolone + IVIG 2 g/kg. | CK fell within 2 weeks; patient weaned off steroids. |

The narrative hinges on *two* intertwined mysteries: a first‑dose adverse reaction to semaglutide, and chronic liver enzyme elevation that could mask a muscular injury.

---

## Why NAFLD Can Confound a Myopathy Work‑up

1. **AST/ALT Overlap**  
   - AST is ~70 % mitochondrial (in muscle) and 30 % cytosolic.  
   - ALT is more liver‑specific but still leaks from muscle during intense injury.  
   - In patients with fatty liver, mild transaminase elevations are common and often attributed to hepatic steatosis.

2. **CK vs AST/ALT**  
   - CK rises 10‑fold earlier in rhabdomyolysis/IMNM.  
   - In this case, CK was only 337 U/L at the time of semaglutide exposure—far below the threshold for clinically significant rhabdomyolysis.  
   - Yet, the AST/ALT remained mildly elevated, tempts the clinician to stay in the “liver” box.

3. **Autoimmune Overlap**  
   - Positive SS‑B autoantibody (isolated) could hint at a systemic autoimmune process affecting liver and muscle.  
   - In the absence of ANA or other myositis antibodies, seronegative myopathy remains a possibility.

4. **Mis‑attribution Timing**  
   - A chronic liver enzyme elevation predating the drug exposure creates a “background noise” that can delay recognition of a new muscle injury.

---

## Key Points in the Evaluation of Muscular vs. Hepatic AST/ALT

| Question | Should it be asked? | Why it matters |
|----------|---------------------|----------------|
| **Is the patient on hepatotoxic meds, alcohol, or viral hepatitis?** | Yes | Exclude common hepatic insults |
| **Does the patient have CK levels >5× ULN?** | Yes | Suggestive of muscle injury |
| **Does CK correlate with symptom severity?** | Yes | Lack of correlation may bias toward liver |
| **Is there eosinophilia, rash, or other systemic features?** | Yes | Could indicate drug toxicity vs. autoimmune myopathy |
| **Are transaminases >5× ULN without CK rise?** | No | More liver‑centric; muscle injury unlikely |

- **Rule out** rhabdomyolysis by checking CK early.  
- **Rule in** IMNM if muscle biopsy shows necrosis with macrophage infiltrate and a paucity of lymphocytes.

---

## Clinical Pearls

- **Never trust AST/ALT alone to localize injury**—always ask the same question for CK and LDH.  
- **Initial drug reactions may not reveal the full picture**; the absence of a CK spike after a severe GI reaction does *not* preclude a delayed, progressive myopathy.  
- **Seronegative IMNM is a real diagnostic snafu**—it can manifest with dramatic CK elevations but negative autoantibody panels. Muscle biopsy remains the gold standard.  
- **Drug‑induced IMNM may respond rapidly to steroids + IVIG** once the offending agent is stopped—contrast with idiopathic IMNM that often needs prolonged immunosuppression.  
- **Avoid “drug rechallenge”** if severe muscle weakness has already been documented; the risk outweighs any potential benefit.

---

## Take‑Home Messages

- In patients with chronic NAFLD and mild transaminase elevations, any new or worsening proximal muscle weakness should prompt a full myopathy work‑up (CK, LDH, muscle imaging, and biopsy if indicated).  
- The temporal relationship between semaglutide exposure and subsequent rise in CK/weakness in this case supports a probable causal link, even though the initial event was not a classic rhabdomyolysis presentation.  
- A negative myositis antibody panel does **not** rule out IMNM; consider muscle biopsy if clinical suspicion remains high.  
- If you encounter a similar scenario, obtain a baseline CK before initiating a GLP‑1 receptor agonist if the patient has renal or hepatic comorbidities.  
- Keep a high index of suspicion for drug‑induced myopathy in the setting of weight‑loss agents—several such reports are emerging, and the class effect may extend beyond classic side‑effects.

---

## Final Thought

When the liver and muscle share the same biochemical alphabet, our diagnostic toolkit must be sharpened. In the era of GLP‑1 agonists, clinicians should widen their differential for new-onset weakness to include drug‑induced IMNM, even if initial labs look “mild.” A proactive, multidisciplinary approach (endocrinology, rheumatology, neurology, and pathology) ensures that the right diagnosis is made—and the right treatment is started—before the patient’s function declines irreversibly.
