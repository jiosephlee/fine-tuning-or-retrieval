Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# Autoantibody‑Negative Yet Autoimmune: The Challenge of Seronegative IMNM  

## The Unexpected Twist in a Classic Myopathy  

We recently read a case of a woman in her 60s who developed classic *immune‑mediated necrotising myopathy* (IMNM) after a single dose of semaglutide. Her serology was tidy‑blank: negative for HMGCR, SRP, and every other myositis‑specific antibody on a full panel. Yet her biopsy still showed classic necrotising fibres with macrophage faggots and a membrane‑attack‑complex wall. She improved dramatically after a short course of steroids and IVIG, suggesting an autoimmune driver, yet the usual fingerprints were missing.  

This is not an isolated oddball. Roughly one third of all IMNM cases are *seronegative*—no detectable HMGCR or SRP antibodies. The clinical community has long debated whether these patients represent a distinct disease entity, a subgroup of drug‑induced myopathy, or just the limits of our current testing. The semaglutide story offers a fresh lens on this issue.

## Why “Seronegative” Isn’t the Same as “Non‑Autoimmune”

At first glance, a negative autoantibody panel might make you think, “Okay, this isn’t an autoimmune thing; maybe it’s toxic, metabolic, or viral.” But in IMNM, the term *seronegative* should be treated with caution:

- **Autoantibodies may be below the detection threshold**: Some patients possess very low titres or novel autoantibodies that commercial assays miss.  
- **Granular vs. systemic disease**: Even without detectable antibodies, immune complexes and complement activation can still drive the necrosis seen on biopsy.  
- **Drug triggers**: Certain medications (e.g., statins, GLP‑1 agonists, immune checkpoint inhibitors) can precipitate a self‑perpetuating autoimmune loop that is not mediated by classic HMGCR or SRP antibodies.

In the semaglutide case, the patient’s CK climbed 12‑fold over four months, her weakness progressed steadily, and she responded to immune suppression—classic autoimmune hallmarks—yet had no serologic evidence. This suggests that the pathogenesis is still an autoimmune attack, just without our current antibody markers.

## How to Approach a Seronegative IMNM Work‑Up  

1. **History & Drug Review**  
   - Statins, GLP‑1 agonists, methotrexate, anti‑PD‑1/PD‑L1 agents, and other small molecules are top culprits.  
   - Over‑the‑counter supplements (e.g., statin‑containing ergosterols, red yeast rice) often fly under the radar.  

2. **Muscle Biopsy**  
   - The gold standard for diagnosing IMNM.  
   - Look for:  
     - **Necrotising fibres** with minimal lymphocytic infiltrate.  
     - **Macrophage infiltration** (CD68+) and **myophagocytosis** (acid phosphatase+).  
     - **Membrane‑attack‑complex deposition** on necrotic fibres (C5b‑9).  
     - Rare T‑cell (CD3) presence does not exclude IMNM.  

3. **Laboratory & Imaging**  
   - CK often skyrockets (thousands, sometimes >10,000 U/L).  
   - Elevated transaminases may be hepatic in origin; exercise caution before attributing them to myositis.  
   - MRI can demonstrate muscle oedema; it is supportive but not diagnostic.  

4. **Autoantibody Panels**  
   - Even if HMGCR/SRP are negative, run a comprehensive myositis panel (Jo‑1, Mi‑2, MDA‑5, etc.).  
   - Consider custom assays or referral to a reference lab if clinical suspicion remains high.  

5. **Rule‑Out Other Conditions**  
   - Rhabdomyolysis (acute CK elevation with normal inflammatory markers).  
   - Toxic myopathy (no necrosis, high CK, but resolves on drug withdrawal).  
   - Metabolic myopathies (e.g., mitochondrial, glycolytic).  

6. **Follow‑Up**  
   - Serial CK.  
   - Muscle strength grading (MRC 0‑5).  
   - Functional scales (MRC sum score, MFI‑28).  

## Treatment Nuances in Seronegative IMNM  

| Approach | What’s Known | What the Case Tells Us |
|----------|--------------|-----------------------|
| **High‑dose steroids** (methylprednisolone 1 g daily 3–5 days, then prednisone 1 mg/kg) | First‑line; reduces inflammation, improves strength | Patient responded well, CK fell in 2 weeks |
| **IVIG** | 2 g/kg over 4 days has shown benefit, especially in rapidly progressive cases | The semaglutide patient got IVIG and improved dramatically |
| **DMARDs (azathioprine, methotrexate, mycophenolate)** | Long‑term steroid maintenance; varies in efficacy | Not needed here; the patient weaned steroids over 2 months |
| **Plasmapheresis** | Used in some refractory cases | Not used in this case but may be considered if IVIG contraindicated |

**Key take‑away:** Even when autoantibody tests are negative, seronegative IMNM can respond robustly to conventional immunosuppression. The absence of an antibody does not mean the disease is milder or less autoimmune.

## Why Drug‑Induced IMNM May Lie in the Seronegative Zone  

There is a growing literature linking IMNM to medications:

- **Statins** → HMGCR autoantibody positivity in ~60 % of drug‑induced cases; remaining ~40 % seronegative.  
- **GLP‑1 receptor agonists** (semaglutide, tirzepatide) → limited reports of rhabdomyolysis; this case suggests a necrotising immune response without classic autoantibodies.  
- **Immune checkpoint inhibitors** → can trigger a spectrum of myopathies, often seronegative.  

Drug‑induced phenotypes may provoke a **direct cytotoxic** or **immune‑complex mediated** injury that bypasses B‑cell autoreactivity detectable by standard panels.

## Clinical Pearls  

- **Don’t dismiss a severe myopathy as “non‑autoimmune” just because the antibody panel is clean.**  
- **Muscle biopsy remains the cornerstone for diagnosis in seronegative IMNM.**  
- **A short course of steroids plus IVIG can be curative in drug‑triggered cases; long‑term immunosuppression may be unnecessary.**  
- **Always review the patient’s medication list, including over‑the‑counter supplements, when CK spikes dramatically.**  
- **Keep a high index of suspicion for drug‑induced IMNM in patients with a recent medication change and progressive proximal weakness.**  

## Summary  

Seronegative IMNM challenges the conventional narrative that autoimmunity requires a detectable antibody. The semaglutide case demonstrates that a robust autoimmune response—evidenced by necrotising pathology and response to immunosuppression—can occur without HMGCR or SRP seropositivity. Clinicians should treat seronegative IMNM with the same priority as seropositive disease, leveraging muscle biopsy and a thoughtful drug history to guide diagnosis and therapy. As GLP‑1 agonists and other novel agents become mainstream, the importance of recognizing and managing seronegative drug‑induced myopathies will only grow.
