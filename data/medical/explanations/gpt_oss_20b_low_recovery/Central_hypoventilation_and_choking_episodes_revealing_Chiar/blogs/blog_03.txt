Title: Blog about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

# Lost in the Old CT: The Importance of Re‑reviewing Retrospective Imaging for Hidden Chiari

## Why This Case Keeps Me Up at Night

I see a lot of patients with “mysterious” choking, nocturnal hypoxia, or occipital pain that no one can explain. You order laryngoscopy, GERD work‑up, even a muscle biopsy, and the investigations come back clean. Until the patient pulls a pulse oximeter across a week and reports *frequent* desaturations in the 70–80 % range **without any cough or dyspnea**. At that point, you go back a step and *look at an old head CT that was done years ago for a car accident* – a scan that was reported as normal but that, on re‑review, shows a crowds‑y foramen magnum and a hint of tonsillar herniation. That’s the moment the diagnosis of Chiari I malformation (CMI) suddenly click into place, and the patient’s chronic symptoms suddenly have a unifying explanation.

This isn’t a rare or exotic scenario – it’s a textbook reminder that **structural brain abnormalities can masquerade as functional or “psychiatric” disorders if you ignore the imaging history**.

## The Diagnostic Pitfall: Crowded Foramen Magnum

The classic description of CMI is >5 mm cerebellar tonsillar descent below the foramen magnum on sagittal MRI. Yet, even a **non‑contrast head CT** can reveal the key features:

- **Crowded foramen magnum**: reduced CSF cistern space, loss of normal sagittal midline landmarks.
- **Forward‑shifted tonsils or “bulging” through the foramen**.
- **“Sinking” of the occipital bone** (though subtle on CT).

Radiologists often filter out these subtle findings if the scan is performed for trauma or stroke evaluation, under the assumption that the brainstem is normative. But take a second look, and the imprint of a CMI can be unmistakable.

### Why CTs are Under‑utilized for CMI

- **Anatomical limitation**: CT is less sensitive than MRI for cerebellar tonsil morphology, but its axial sections still reveal compressed CSF spaces.
- **Reporting bias**: Radiology reports are usually guided by the clinical question (“is there a bleed?”); an incidental crowding may be *itinerant*.
- **Historical data**: In the era of electronic health records, a dozen hours after a trauma or accident, the radiologist’s impression focuses on the acute injury, not the skull base.

Our patient’s 14‑year CT was initially read as "normal" because the emergency department radiologist was hunting for hemorrhage, not for micro‑deformities.

## History‑Guided Imaging Review: The First Step

When the patient described **nocturnal choking and sudden hypoxia during meals**, a high index of suspicion for **central hypoventilation** should be the first cue. From there, ask:

1. **Do I have any old imaging?** Even a "routine" head CT can be a goldmine.
2. **Was there a prior brain‑base CT or MRI?** Retrieve it, ttemp for crowding.
3. **Do any prior studies show a reduced CSF space or tonsillar descent?** 
4. **If the CT is old, re‑align sagittal reconstructions; the foramen magnum is best seen in the sagittal plane.**

Often the answer is a compelling “yes.” In this case, the retrospective review identified a crowded foramen magnum, a finding that, if missed, would have kept the patient on a treadmill of endoscopy, acid suppression, and anxiety.

## Clinical Consequences of Missing CMI

- **Respiratory dysfunction**: Acquired central hypoventilation (Ondine’s curse) or central sleep apnea.
- **Dysphagia**: Neurogenic choking that feels mechanical but is actually a reflex failure.
- **Pain syndromes**: Vertex, occipital neuralgia, or burning vertex syndrome tied to CSF flow disruptions.
- **Progressive neurological decline**: Syringomyelia, cerebellar signs, or brainstem compression if left untreated.

Missing the diagnosis can lead to years of unnecessary testing, functional impairment, and a profound loss of quality of life.

## Management: When the CT Gets a Second Look

Once **CMI is identified**, the next steps usually involve:

- **Multidisciplinary consult**: neurosurgery, ENT, pulmonology.
- **Polysomnography** (preferably full type I with EEG, capnography) to quantify central vs obstructive events.
- **Surgical evaluation**: Posterior fossa decompression with duraplasty is the gold standard for symptomatic patients.
- **Symptom monitoring**: Monthly clinical visits, monthly in‑home oximetry, quarterly sleep studies.

In our case, the patient opted for conservative monitoring while weighing surgical options – a prudent approach until the sleep study’s true severity could be quantified.

## What We Can Do as Clinicians

1. **Keep a low threshold for re‑examining old imaging** in patients with unexplained neuro‑symptoms.
2. **Use the CT as a teaching tool**: highlight the foramen magnum and CSF spaces to trainees.
3. **Document “incidental findings” comprehensively** so that future reviewers can see the context.
4. **Advocate for a “clinical image review” protocol** in your institution—when a patient feels they need a scan, deeper analysis can sometimes change the diagnosis.

### Quick Checklist for Reviewing Old Scans

- Examine the **sagittal plane** for tonsillar position.
- Measure **CSF space** at the foramen magnum (normal >3 mm).
- Look for **caudal migration** of cerebellar tissue.
- Note **any aqueductal patency** or cystic changes.
- Correlate with **clinical timeline** (e.g., the onset of headaches, choking).

## Take–Home Points

- A **crowded foramen magnum** on a routine CT can point to CMI, even in the absence of overt brain‑stem injury.
- Re‑analysis of **retrospective imaging** is a low‑risk, high‑yield step when patients report neuro‑symptoms that are not explained by routine work‑ups.
- The classic presentation of **central hypoventilation**—“forgetting to breathe” without dyspnea—is a hallmark of brainstem compression in CMI.
- A **full type I polysomnography** remains the best tool to delineate the severity of central sleep apnea in these patients.
- **Posterior fossa decompression** can reverse respiratory symptoms and improve quality of life; early surgical consideration is key.

In the end, this case reminds us that the answer to a patient’s decade‑long struggle may be hiding in a box behind a triage nurse—an old CT that simply hasn’t been seen through the right eyes. The next time a patient reports choking or unexplained hypoxia, pull up that old study and give it one more look. You might just solve the mystery—and save a life.
