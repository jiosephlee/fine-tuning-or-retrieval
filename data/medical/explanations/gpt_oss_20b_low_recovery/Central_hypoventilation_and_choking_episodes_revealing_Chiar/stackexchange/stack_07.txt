Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

### [Clinical Pearls] Why might a patient with Chiari malformation experience vertex scalp allodynia that improves after hair cutting, and how does this symptom fit into current headache classification systems?

**Why vertex‐scalp allodynia that improves after a haircut occurs in Chiari I malformation, and how is it classified?**

1. **Pathophysiology in Chiari I**  
   * The 11 mm tonsillar herniation seen in this patient compresses the cerebellomedullary cistern and impinges on the dorsal roots of the upper occipital nerves (greater and lesser), as well as the small‐fiber pathways that mediate cutaneous pain and temperature in the vertex.  
   * Compression of these structures leads to ectopic firing and sensitisation, producing cutaneous allodynia: even the light weight of long hair becomes painful.  
   * When the hair is cut short, the mechanical afferent input is reduced, temporarily relieving the allodynia.

2. **Clinical picture**  
   * The patient describes a “coin‑sized” vertex pressure that is allodynic to light touch or hair weight, improving after hair cutting – classic features of *occipital neuralgia* secondary to a structural lesion.  
   * Occipital neuralgia can be part of the “Chiari syndrome” constellation; however, its prevalence is low, and systematic data are sparse, as noted in the case report (“no conclusive data establishing a causative link”).

3. **Headache classification**  
   * **ICHD‑3** classifies occipital neuralgia as *secondary nerve‑sparing occipital pain* when it is precipitated by a structural abnormality (e.g., Chiari I).  
   * The patient’s vertex pain satisfies the ICHD‑3 criteria for *occipital neuralgia* (pain localized to one or more occipital nerves, precipitated by trigger points, and relieved by nerve block).  
   * The ICHD‑3 distinguishes this from *nummular headache* or *burning vertex syndrome* by the presence of objective scalp allodynia and a clear anatomical correlate.

4. **Clinical teaching point**  
   * In patients with long‑standing vertex scalp pain that is allodynic to light touch or hair, especially when unresponsive to conventional headache therapies, a high index of suspicion for a structural cause such as Chiari I should be maintained.  
   * A screening MRI of the cranio‑cervical junction is warranted; in this case, it confirmed the diagnosis and provided the rationale for the patient’s other symptoms (choking, hypoxia, central hypoventilation).

**Bottom line**  
Vertex scalp allodynia relieved by haircut in a Chiari I patient is most parsimoniously explained by occipital neuralgia caused by tonsillar compression of the occipital nerves and small‑fiber pathways. It fits the ICHD‑3 definition of *secondary occipital neuralgia* and underscores the need to consider a neuroanatomical origin in atypical scalp pain.
