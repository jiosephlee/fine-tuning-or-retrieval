Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

### [Differential Diagnosis] What specific features of a videofluoroscopic swallowing study would be expected in a neurogenic dysphagia associated with Chiari malformation versus a mechanical or esophageal cause?

**Videofluoroscopic Swallowing Study: Neurogenic (Chiari I) versus Mechanical/Esophageal Causes**

**Key point:** In neurogenic dysphagia (as exemplified by the patient with Chiari malformation type I in the case report), the videofluoroscopic swallow study (VFSS) reveals *functional* deficits that affect both solids and liquids in a similar fashion. Mechanical or purely esophageal lesions, on the other hand, typically produce a *congruence* between the consistency of the bolus and the site of abnormality.

| Feature | Neurogenic (Chiari I) | Mechanical/Esophageal |
|---------|-----------------------|-----------------------|
| **Bolus consistency** | Both solids **and** liquids equally impaired | Often **solids** are more difficult; liquids may flow normally if the obstacle is in the esophagus |
| **Swallow timing** | Prolonged **delay** before the pharyngeal swallow reflex; poor coordination of oral–pharyngeal phases | Timing is usually normal; delay occurs only when a structural stenosis blocks passage |
| **Pharyngeal contraction** | Weak, uncoordinated pharyngeal constriction; absent or diminished “pharyngeal” response | Pharyngeal contraction is preserved; bolus moves through a narrowed lumen |
| **Tongue base excursion** | Reduced retraction and modulus; decreased contact with vallecula | Tongue function often normal unless the lesion is in the tongue tip |
| **Epiglottic closure** | Incomplete or delayed epiglottic descent, leaving a “vent” for aspiration | Epiglottis usually normal unless the lesion is a laryngeal web or mass |
| **Penetration/aspiration** | Frequent and often with oropharyngeal residue; aspiration occurs with both consistencies | Residue may be seen, but aspiration typically correlates with the bolus size and the location of the obstruction |
| **Residue pattern** | Global oropharyngeal and/or vallecular residue on all consistencies | Residue concentrated at the site of the structural lesion (e.g., anterior cervical fibromatosis, Schatzki ring) |
| **Reaction to effort** | Swallow becomes more efficient **⇒** compensatory effort possible, but reflex remains inadequate | Effort does not significantly alter residue or aspiration if the mechanical barrier remains |
| **Expected response to swallowing maneuvers** | Weak or absent improvement with effortful swallow, Mendelsohn, or supraglottic swallow | Larger bolus can sometimes bypass a fixed mechanical obstacle (e.g., in strictures) |
| **Day‑to‑day variability** | Consistent findings; not dependent on food amount or time | Fluctuating if lesion is dynamic (e.g., hiatal hernia) |

**Pathophysiology in the case**

The patient’s VFSS (not shown in the manuscript but noted in the report) demonstrated *equal dysphagia for solids and liquids*, a hallmark of a neurogenic pattern. This aligns with lower cranial‑nerve involvement secondary to cerebellar tonsillar descent in Chiari I, which can compress the nuclei and tracts responsible for timing the pharyngeal swallow and coordinating tongue base retraction. The normal laryngoscopies and endoscopies in the case further support a neurologic, rather than an anatomic, etiology.

**Take‑home teaching points**

1. **Consistency‑independent impairment** → neurogenic dysphagia.
2. **Global oropharyngeal weakness (tongue base, pharyngeal constriction, epiglottic reflex)** → neurogenic pattern typical of brain‑stem involvement (e.g., Chiari, stroke, multiple sclerosis).
3. **Localized residue correlating with a fixed anatomic obstruction** and preservation of swallow reflex → mechanical/structural esophageal pathology.
4. In patients with unexplained choking at meals and sleep, a VFSS that shows *uniform* dysfunction across consistencies should prompt a neurologic work‑up, as in the presented case of Chiari malformation type I.

Thus, the videofluoroscopic features in Chiari‑related dysphagia differ fundamentally from those of purely mechanical or esophageal causes, providing a clear diagnostic pathway when integrated with the clinical picture.
