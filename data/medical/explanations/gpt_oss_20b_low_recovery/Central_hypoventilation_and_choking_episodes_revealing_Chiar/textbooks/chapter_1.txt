Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

Chapter 1: ## 1. Clinical Presentation and Initial Work‑up

A woman in her early 40 s presented to primary care with an evolving constellation of symptoms that initially suggested a benign or functional disorder but ultimately revealed a neurologic cause. Her clinical course began with a motor‑vehicle collision in middle‑age, followed by chronic occipital headaches and, after several years, recurrent choking episodes that affected both solids and liquids, occurring during meals and at night. The temporal order of these events is critical to understand the pathophysiology that eventually led to the diagnosis of Chiari malformation type I.

### Chronology of Symptom Onset
The patient reported a brief loss of consciousness following a minor motor‑vehicle accident roughly 14 years prior. In the weeks after the trauma, she developed persistent pressure‑like pain at the vertex and occipital region that she described as a coin‑sized, constant headache, unresponsive to typical migraine treatments and not worsened by coughing or Valsalva maneuvers. About seven years after the incident she began experiencing sudden, alarming choking episodes, both during feeding and sleep, occurring 3–4 times weekly. The evolution from trauma‑related headache to full‑blown respiratory‑related dysphagia underscores the progressive nature of the underlying structural abnormality.

### Characterisation of Choking Episodes
These choking events were noted to be abrupt, frightening, and accompanied by violent coughing and gasping. Importantly, they never resulted in complete airway obstruction; the patient could still inspire and expire comfortably during awake intervals. The episodes affected solids and liquids equally, suggesting a neurogenic rather than mechanical dysphagia. The nocturnal frequency (up to four episodes per night) and the absence of awakenings or dyspnea during the episodes hinted at a failure of automatic respiratory control, a hallmark of central hypoventilation.

### Reports of Asymptomatic Hypoxia
During the COVID‑19 pandemic, the patient began self‑monitoring oxygen saturation with a home pulse oximeter. Over several months, she consistently documented unexplained desaturations to 70–80 % while at rest, yet never complained of shortness of breath. A memorable episode at an international airport—where she experienced acute dizziness and nausea and was found to have a saturation of 78 %—provided objective confirmation of significant hypoxia. These findings suggested central control of breathing was impaired even in the absence of conscious dyspnea.

### Baseline Physical Examination and Normal Pulmonary/Cardiac Work‑up
On presentation, vital signs were normal (BP 118/76 mm Hg, HR 72 bpm, RR 14/min, SpO₂ 96 % RA). Physical exam revealed no cervical or cranial nerve deficits, normal respiratory–pulmonary assessment, and no jugular venous distention or heart murmurs. Pulmonary function tests—including FEV₁, FVC, and respiratory muscle strength (MIP/MEP)—were within normal limits. Arterial blood gases on room air were unremarkable (pH 7.41, PaCO₂ 42 mm Hg, PaO₂ 88 mm Hg, SaO₂ 95 %). These normal baseline studies effectively excluded primary pulmonary disease and underscored that the respiratory dysfunction was likely central.

### Impact on Quality of Life and Initial Differential Diagnoses
The choking episodes and persistent headaches had a profound psychosocial impact. The patient avoided social meals and public gatherings, developed significant anxiety, and was reluctant to seek further evaluation. Early intervention focused on gastrointestinal and otolaryngologic causes. Flexible laryngoscopies (three times) and upper endoscopies (twice) were all reported as normal, ruling out structural airway obstruction or gastro‑oesophageal reflux disease. The persistence of symptoms with normal investigations broadened the differential to include neurogenic dysphagia, myasthenia gravis, and central respiratory disorders—each presenting with similar but distinct clinical features. The lack of fatigue or diurnal variation argued against myasthenia, and the normal antibody panel further reduced that likelihood. Ultimately, the combination of nocturnal choking, unrecognized hypoxia, and a chronic occipital headache directed the clinician toward a central nervous system etiology, prompting a neuroradiologic reassessment that would unveil the underlying Chiari malformation.
