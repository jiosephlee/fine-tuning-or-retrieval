Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I"

## 1. Clinical Presentation and Initial Work‑up

A woman in her early 40 s presented to primary care with an evolving constellation of symptoms that initially suggested a benign or functional disorder but ultimately revealed a neurologic cause. Her clinical course began with a motor‑vehicle collision in middle‑age, followed by chronic occipital headaches and, after several years, recurrent choking episodes that affected both solids and liquids, occurring during meals and at night. The temporal order of these events is critical to understand the pathophysiology that eventually led to the diagnosis of Chiari malformation type I.

### Chronology of Symptom Onset
The patient reported a brief loss of consciousness following a minor motor‑vehicle accident roughly 14 years prior. In the weeks after the trauma, she developed persistent pressure‑like pain at the vertex and occipital region that she described as a coin‑sized, constant headache, unresponsive to typical migraine treatments and not worsened by coughing or Valsalva maneuvers. About seven years after the incident she began experiencing sudden, alarming choking episodes, both during feeding and sleep, occurring 3–4 times weekly. The evolution from trauma‑related headache to full‑blown respiratory‑related dysphagia underscores the progressive nature of the underlying structural abnormality.

### Characterisation of Choking Episodes
These choking events were noted to be abrupt, frightening, and accompanied by violent coughing and gasping. Importantly, they never resulted in complete airway obstruction; the patient could still inspire and expire comfortably during awake intervals. The episodes affected solids and liquids equally, suggesting a neurogenic rather than mechanical dysphagia. The nocturnal frequency (up to four episodes per night) and the absence of awakenings or dyspnea during the episodes hinted at a failure of automatic respiratory control, a hallmark of central hypoventilation.

### Reports of Asymptomatic Hypoxia
During the COVID‑19 pandemic, the patient began self‑monitoring oxygen saturation with a home pulse oximeter. Over several months, she consistently documented unexplained desaturations to 70–80 % while at rest, yet never complained of shortness of breath. A memorable episode at an international airport—where she experienced acute dizziness and nausea and was found to have a saturation of 78 %—provided objective confirmation of significant hypoxia. These findings suggested central control of breathing was impaired even in the absence of conscious dyspnea.

### Baseline Physical Examination and Normal Pulmonary/Cardiac Work‑up
On presentation, vital signs were normal (BP 118/76 mm Hg, HR 72 bpm, RR 14/min, SpO₂ 96 % RA). Physical exam revealed no cervical or cranial nerve deficits, normal respiratory–pulmonary assessment, and no jugular venous distention or heart murmurs. Pulmonary function tests—including FEV₁, FVC, and respiratory muscle strength (MIP/MEP)—were within normal limits. Arterial blood gases on room air were unremarkable (pH 7.41, PaCO₂ 42 mm Hg, PaO₂ 88 mm Hg, SaO₂ 95 %). These normal baseline studies effectively excluded primary pulmonary disease and underscored that the respiratory dysfunction was likely central.

### Impact on Quality of Life and Initial Differential Diagnoses
The choking episodes and persistent headaches had a profound psychosocial impact. The patient avoided social meals and public gatherings, developed significant anxiety, and was reluctant to seek further evaluation. Early intervention focused on gastrointestinal and otolaryngologic causes. Flexible laryngoscopies (three times) and upper endoscopies (twice) were all reported as normal, ruling out structural airway obstruction or gastro‑oesophageal reflux disease. The persistence of symptoms with normal investigations broadened the differential to include neurogenic dysphagia, myasthenia gravis, and central respiratory disorders—each presenting with similar but distinct clinical features. The lack of fatigue or diurnal variation argued against myasthenia, and the normal antibody panel further reduced that likelihood. Ultimately, the combination of nocturnal choking, unrecognized hypoxia, and a chronic occipital headache directed the clinician toward a central nervous system etiology, prompting a neuroradiologic reassessment that would unveil the underlying Chiari malformation.

## 2. Pathophysiology of Chiari Malformation Type I and Respiratory Control  

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is defined radiologically by a caudal displacement of the cerebellar tonsils of ≥ 5 mm below the foramen magnum on sagittal MRI.  The clinical relevance of this anatomic abnormality depends on the degree of posterior fossa crowding, the extent of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow obstruction, and the degree of compression or irritation of brain‑stem structures that regulate respiration.  Understanding these three elements is essential to explain why a patient may retain full voluntary control of breathing yet experience profound loss of automatic ventilation, a syndrome analogous to acquired “Ondine’s curse”.

### 2.1 Normal posterior fossa anatomy and CSF dynamics  
The posterior fossa is a confined cranial compartment containing the cerebellum, brainstem, and the fourth ventricle.  It is bounded anteriorly by the clivus, posteriorly by the occipital bone, laterally by the petrous apex, and inferiorly by the foramen magnum.  The cerebellar tonsils occupy the lowest portion of the cerebellum and normally lie within the skull, with a CSF‑filled space surrounding them.  

CSF is produced mainly at the choroid plexus of the lateral and fourth ventricles, circulates down through the foramina of Luschka and Magendie into the subarachnoid space, and is absorbed into the venous system via arachnoid granulations.  At the craniocervical junction, CSF forms a continuous column that allows pulsatile transmission of arterial‑to‑venous pressure gradients during the respiratory cycle.  This pulsatility is critical for maintaining normal intracranial compliance and for ensuring that the medullary respiratory centers receive appropriate afferent input via the descending and ascending tracts.

### 2.2 Definition and spectrum of CMI  
While the 5‑mm descent threshold is the standard diagnostic criterion, a spectrum exists ranging from asymptomatic anatomical variants to clinically significant malformations.  Recent volumetric studies have shown that posterior‑fossa size varies considerably with age, sex, and body habitus, and that many individuals with mild tonsillar descent are asymptomatic.  Therefore, symptom severity does not correlate perfectly with the amount of descent; rather, the key determinants of clinical manifestations are the presence of CSF flow disturbance and the extent of brain‑stem compression.

| Feature | Typical Threshold | Clinical Relevance |
|---------|-------------------|--------------------|
| Tonsillar descent | ≥ 5 mm | Requires imaging confirmation; associated with increased risk of symptoms |
| CSF flow obstruction | Visible by phase‑contrast MRI or tonsillar angulation | Correlates with syringomyelia, headaches, and respiratory disturbance |
| Clinical symptomatology | Variable | Determines need for surgical decompression |

### 2.3 Brain‑stem respiratory centres  
Respiratory rhythm and drive arise from the medulla and pons.  Key structures include:

- **Medullary respiratory complex**: Respiratory centre (central pattern generator) comprising the dorsal and ventral respiratory groups; chemoreceptors (central) in the retrotrapezoid nucleus and peripheral carotid bodies change activity according to CO₂/H⁺ levels.  
- **Dorsolateral pons**: Pontine respiratory group (PRG) modulates the transition between inhalation and exhalation and responds to CO₂ gradients.  
- **Medullary chemoreceptors**: Sensitive to changes in arterial CO₂, they adjust the rhythm generator output.  

These centers are predominantly driven by automatic, unconscious processes that maintain ventilation during wakefulness and sleep.  Voluntary breathing, on the other hand, recruits descending corticospinal pathways from cortical and subcortical areas and bypasses the medullary rhythm generator when the patient is awake and alert.

### 2.4 Mechanisms of disrupted automatic breathing while preserving voluntary ventilation  
In CMI, the herniated tonsils press against the cervicomedullary junction.  Two complementary mechanisms contribute to selective loss of automatic ventilation:

1. **Mechanical compression of respiratory nuclei**  
   - The dorsal surface of the medulla, where the chemoreceptors and the inspiratory/expiratory rhythm generators reside, lies directly beneath the tonsils.  
   - Persistent compression reduces the neuron's ability to respond to hypercapnia or hypoxia.  The result is blunted afferent signaling and impaired drive during periods of reduced conscious control (e.g., sleep or distraction).  
   - The ventral respiratory group and pontine PRG are similarly affected, disrupting the normal phasic pattern of breathing.

2. **CSF outflow obstruction and increased intracranial pressure gradients**  
   - Tonsillar descent interferes with CSF flow at the foramen magnum and within the cisterna magna.  
   - This obstruction alters the pressure dynamics across the brainstem, impairing the transmission of the CO₂/H⁺ signal from peripheral chemoreceptors to the medullary centers.  
   - Consequently, the automatic respiratory drive fails to adjust ventilation appropriately, while the cortex‑driven voluntary system remains intact because it utilizes separate descending pathways that are less affected by CSF pressure disturbances.

By preserving voluntary breathing pathways, patients can consciously control their respirations when awake or engaged, but when automatic control is required—such as during sleep or distraction—they inadvertently “forget” to breathe, leading to episodic hypoxemia and hypercapnia.

### 2.5 Comparison with congenital central hypoventilation syndrome (PHOX2B) and acquired Ondine’s curse  
- **Congenital central hypoventilation syndrome (CCHS)**  
  - Genetic disorder (often PHOX2B mutation) that manifests in infancy with failure of automatic ventilation during sleep.  
  - The primary defect lies in the intrinsic development of the brainstem respiratory centers, leading to lifelong hypoventilation.  
  - Patients can learn volitional breathing, but automatic control is permanently impaired.

- **Acquired Ondine’s curse (central hypoventilation syndrome)**  
  - Result of structural lesions (stroke, tumor, trauma, or, as in CMI, tonsillar herniation) affecting the brainstem.  
  - The disease is acquired, and the underlying neuroanatomy is morphologically identifiable.  
  - Ventilation is preserved voluntarily but fails automatically; the pattern is similar to CCHS, but the etiology is damage rather than a developmental anomaly.

Thus, CMI exemplifies an acquired form of central hypoventilation where mechanical brain‑stem compromise replicates the pathophysiology seen in congenital disorders.  Recognizing this pathogenesis is crucial for diagnosis and for guiding therapeutic interventions such as posterior fossa decompression, which can relieve the compression, normalize CSF dynamics, and potentially restore automatic breathing.

## 3. Diagnostic Reasoning and Key Investigations

The diagnostic journey in this case illustrates the importance of a systematic, hypothesis‑driven approach that integrates clinical history, targeted imaging, functional studies, and a broad differential. The patient’s presentation—recurrent choking during meals and sleep, nocturnal desaturations, chronic vertex pain, and a history of trauma—initially suggested a gastric or upper‑airway etiology. A stepwise re‑examination of the available data, however, revealed a structural brain abnormality that fundamentally altered the diagnostic direction.

### Re‑examination of the 14‑Year‑Old CT

The first pivotal moment was the meticulous review of the non‑contrast head CT obtained post–motor‑vehicle crash 14 years earlier. The original radiology report had focused on the acute traumatic findings and had labeled the scan as “normal.” A fresh appraisal uncovered a crowded foramen magnum: the posterior fossa was relatively small, with the cerebellar tonsils extending to the level of the opisthion and an absence of the typical cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) cleft at the craniocervical junction. These classic features of Chiari malformation type I (CMI) were easily missed in the era of rapid imaging interpretation. Recognizing the abnormal posterior fossa configuration prompted the clinicians to suspect a structural cause of the patient’s symptoms, leading to further definitive imaging.

### MRI Findings: Confirmation and Characterisation

A dedicated magnetic‑resonance imaging (MRI) study of the brain and cervical spine provided the decisive evidence. Sagittal sequences demonstrated an 11 mm descent of the cerebellar tonsils below the foramen magnum—well above the 5 mm threshold that defines CMI. Axial images revealed compression of the medullary tissue, especially the dorsal respiratory centres, but there was no evidence of a syrinx or other posterior fossa mass lesions. The lack of a syringomyelia contributed to a focused interpretation: the tonsillar herniation alone could explain the constellation of symptoms, including cranial neuropathies, headaches, and respiratory dysfunction.

### Polysomnography Interpretation: Type III Limitations and Central Events

A type III home sleep study was performed to assess for sleep‑disordered breathing. Although it recorded airflow, respiratory effort, oxygen saturation, and heart rate, it lacked electroencephalographic monitoring and continuous capnography. The calculated apnea–hypopnea index (AHI) of 6.0 was modest, but analysis of the events revealed that 72 % were central—not obstructive—apneas. In a patient with preserved voluntary breathing, a predominance of central events strongly indicates dysfunction of the brainstem’s automatic respiratory drive. The study’s limitations—particularly the use of total recording time instead of true sleep time—likely yielded an underestimation of the AHI. Furthermore, absent capnography precluded detection of hypoventilation or hypercapnia that may have occurred during sleep.

### Systematic Exclusion of Alternative Causes

The diagnostic reasoning process required rigorous exclusion of other potential etiologies for choking, hypoxia, and headaches:

1. **Gastro‑oesophageal Reflux Disease (GERD) / Laryngopharyngeal Reflux**  
   – pH monitoring and esophagogastroduodenoscopy were normal.  
   – Lack of response to high‑dose proton‑pump inhibitor therapy and absence of typical reflux symptoms made GERD unlikely.

2. **Myasthenia Gravis and Other Muscle‑Weakening Disorders**  
   – Serum acetylcholine‐receptor and anti‑MuSK antibodies were negative.  
   – No fatigable weakness, fluctuating symptom pattern, or ocular involvement was reported, making a neuromuscular junction disorder improbable.

3. **Primary Pulmonary Disease**  
   – Chest radiography and computed tomography showed clear lung fields.  
   – Pulmonary function tests revealed normal spirometry and maximal inspiratory/expiratory pressures, excluding airway or parenchymal lung disease.

4. **Other Central Sleep Apnoea Causes**  
   – No known brainstem tumours, demyelinating disease, or congenital central hypoventilation syndrome (PHOX2B mutations) were identified.  
   – The temporal sequence of symptom onset following trauma and the imaging findings provided a more parsimonious explanation.

By systematically ruling out these differentials, the diagnostic focus narrowed to a brainstem structural lesion.

### Multidisciplinary Team: Integrating Perspectives

The complexity of this case underscores the value of a multidisciplinary approach:

- **Neurology** supplied expertise in interpreting neuroimaging, evaluating brainstem function, and guiding the decision for posterior fossa decompression.
- **Ear, Nose, and Throat (ENT)** specialists examined the patient’s dysphagia and ruled out mechanical causes of choking through flexible laryngoscopy and upper gastrointestinal endoscopy.
- **Sleep Medicine** provided polysomnographic assessment and insight into central versus obstructive events, as well as recommendations for further, more comprehensive sleep testing.
- **Radiology** played a critical role in revisiting old imaging and in producing high‑resolution MRI, confirming the anatomical abnormality.

This collaborative dialogue ensured that the final diagnosis of CMI with acquired central hypoventilation was both evidence‑based and clinically coherent. It also allowed for the development of a nuanced management plan that balanced surgical intervention against conservative monitoring.

---

In summary, the diagnostic reasoning in this case hinged on re‑examining an overlooked CT scan, confirming the findings with MRI, interpreting a limited sleep study that revealed a predominance of central apneas, and systematically excluding other plausible causes for the patient’s symptoms. The multidisciplinary collaboration facilitated a rapid, accurate diagnosis and informed a patient‑centred management strategy.

## 4. Management Options and Treatment Decision‑Making

Management of symptomatic Chiari malformation type I (CMI) rests on two mutually exclusive pathways: surgical intervention, most commonly posterior fossa decompression, and a conservative, symptom‑oriented approach. The choice hinges on a balance between the severity and progression of the patient’s clinical manifestations and the risk profile of surgery. In this case, the patient’s persistent choking episodes, unexplained nocturnal hypoxia, and hypoventilation‑like breathing patterns provide concrete justification for considering decompression, yet her cultural preferences and relatively mild polysomnographic findings have led her to elect close observation.

### Indications for Surgical Decompression

The primary indication for posterior fossa decompression in adult CMI is the presence of debilitating or progressive neurological symptoms that are unlikely to resolve spontaneously. This patient fulfills several high‑stakes criteria:

1. **Respiratory compromise** – Frequent nocturnal hypoxic events (SpO₂ 70–80 %) and a predominance of central apnea (≈70 %) on type‑III study suggest impaired automatic respiratory drive. Respiratory symptoms are the most ominous manifestation, especially when they precipitate acute life‑threatening episodes, as encountered during the airport incident.  
2. **Ongoing dysphagia and choking** – Recurrent aspiration‑like episodes that directly jeopardise airway integrity and diminish quality of life.  
3. **Cerebellar tonsillar descent** – Imaging shows 11 mm of tonsillar herniation below the foramen magnum, exceeding the 5 mm threshold that defines CMI. Volumetric studies indicate that discs >10 mm correlate with a higher risk of symptom severity, including respiratory involvement.  
4. **Failure of medical therapies** – Although the patient has received no specific pharmacologic therapy aimed at the underlying defect, non‑invasive measures (changing diet, otolaryngologic reassurance) have not alleviated her choking episodes.  

Other scenarios that strengthen the case for decompression include progressive neurological deficits, pseudoparesis of lower cranial nerves, or the presence of syringomyelia. Our patient does not have a syrinx, but the high‑risk respiratory features are sufficient.

### Expected Outcomes of Posterior Fossa Decompression

Posterior fossa decompression relieves compression at the craniocervical junction by re‑opening the foramen magnum and allowing restoration of normal CSF dynamics. Clinical evidence from longitudinal cohorts documents:

- **Improvement in headache** – Over 70 % of symptomatic patients experience significant relief of occipital pain.  
- **Resolution or reduction of dysphagia** – Surgical decompression has been associated with a 40–60 % reduction in swallowing dysfunction scores.  
- **Normalization of central sleep apnea** – Pre‑operative apnoea–hypopnoea indices (AHI) often drop from a median of 6‑8 to 1–3 post‑operatively, with a marked decline in central event proportion.  
- **Enhanced automatic ventilation** – In patients resembling the “Ondine’s curse” phenotype, the automatic respiratory drive can recover to levels comparable to healthy controls when the mechanical obstruction is relieved.  

Complication rates remain low (~5 % overall), dominated by wound infection, cerebrospinal fluid leak, and transient postoperative dysphagia. Long‑term follow‑up indicates sustained improvement in most patients, although relapse in CSF flow obstruction can occur if the decompression fails to address a pre‑existing ventral hernia or Chiari‑associated syrinx.

### Conservative Management Strategies

When the clinical picture does not reach the threshold for surgery, a structured conservative program may be suitable. The patient’s plan incorporates several elements:

1. **Symptom‑directed measures** – Dietary modifications (smaller, well‑moistened foods), use of thickening agents for liquids, and avoidance of high‑risk triggers such as certain positions during sleep.  
2. **Behavioral therapy** – Cognitive‑behavioral intervention (CBT) can reduce anxiety associated with choking, improve coping, and potentially mitigate sleep‑related respiratory events through relaxation techniques.  
3. **Respiratory monitoring** – Periodic overnight oximetry (at least quarterly) gauges the persistence or progression of hypoxia.  
4. **Medical support** – While no pharmacologic agent targets the mechanical defect, non‑steroidal anti‑inflammatory drugs can ameliorate associated headache, and neuromuscular‑stimulant drugs (e.g., modafinil) are occasionally trialed for central sleep apnea, though evidence is limited.  

The benefits of surveillance lie in avoiding the surgical risk in a patient who might experience spontaneous improvement or whose symptoms may remain stable. The drawback is the potential for delayed progression to irreversible respiratory compromise.

### Shared Decision‑Making

The decision to proceed with decompression requires thorough alignment of clinical evidence, patient values, and cultural context. Key points discussed with the patient included:

- **Risk–benefit analysis** – A 5 % risk of major surgical complications versus a potential to recover automatic breathing and eliminate choking episodes.  
- **Functional impact** – Evaluation of the patient’s quality of life, social withdrawal, and sleep disturbances.  
- **Cultural considerations** – The patient’s experience with hair cutting and its therapeutic effect on headaches suggests a sensitivity to body‑image and cultural expectations. These aspects may influence postoperative recovery expectations and grooming habits post‑surgery.  
- **Future planning** – A realistic timeline for postoperative rehabilitation and potential need for temporary ventilatory support in the rare event of peri‑operative respiratory failure.  

After detailed discussions, the patient elected a “watch‑and‑wait” approach, preferring to avoid surgery at this time. This decision respects her autonomy while ensuring that a surgical option remains open if her symptoms deteriorate.

### Follow‑Up Schedule

Given the patient’s chosen strategy, the monitoring plan is:

| Timepoint | Evaluation | Modality | Purpose |
|-----------|------------|----------|---------|
| Baseline | Clinical interview, focused neurologic exam | – | Document current symptom burden and baseline vital signs |
| 1‑3 months | Home overnight oximetry (28 h) | Pulse oximeter | Detect new or worsening nocturnal desaturations |
| 3‑12 months | Repeat oximetry, symptom questionnaire | – | Trend changes, assess psychosocial impact |
| 12 months | MRI brain & cervical spine | 3T MRI, sagittal & postoperative view | Monitor tonsillar position and CSF flow |
| 24‑36 months | Sleep study (Type‑I polysomnography) | Full PSG | Objectively quantify sleep architecture and central apnea severity |
| Ongoing | Psychiatric and CBT sessions as needed | – | Address anxiety, coping mechanisms, and potential emergent symptoms |

If any of these assessments reveal a notable uptick in central apnea burden, worsening dysphagia, or new neurological deficits, the surgical curriculum will be revisited with the patient and surgical team.

In summary, posterior fossa decompression offers definitive resolution of obstruction‑related respiratory dysfunction and headache in symptomatic CMI. However, in patients who are stable clinically or who decline surgery, a disciplined outpatient surveillance strategy can effectively track disease evolution and preserve the opportunity for intervention when medically indicated.

## 5. Clinical Lessons, Prognosis, and Research Implications

The experience of a patient with Chiari malformation type I (CMI) who presented primarily with choking episodes, nocturnal hypoxia, and chronic occipital pain underscores several practical points for clinicians who encounter seemingly idiopathic dysphagia or unexplained central hypoventilation.

### Red flags for CMI in dysphagia and hypoxia

1. **Equal difficulty with solids and liquids**  
   Neurogenic dysphagia, as opposed to mechanical obstruction, often manifests this way. When routine endoscopy and radiologic studies of the aerodigestive tract are normal, a neurological etiology should be considered.

2. **Unexplained nocturnal hypoxia or central apnoea**  
   Sudden drops in oxygen saturation without dyspnoea, especially occurring on waking or during quiet periods, are highly suggestive of impaired automatic respiratory control. In the absence of primary pulmonary disease or sleep‑apnoea syndrome, a brainstem‑related etiology should be sought.

3. **Concurrent occipital or vertex pain with allodynia**  
   While not a classic Chiari symptom, scalp allodynia or burning vertex pain may reflect neuralgia of the greater occipital nerve due to tonsillar encroachment. Its presence, particularly when relieved by hair trimming, should prompt imaging of the craniocervical junction.

4. **History of head trauma, prior imaging, or rapid evolution of symptoms**  
   A previous head CT or MRI showing a crowded foramen magnum or mild tonsillar descent that was initially missed can become a clue when followed by progressive symptoms years later.

When these red flags co‑occur, a sagittal MRI of the brain and cervical spine with careful attention to tonsillar descent and posterior fossa volume is indicated.

### Prognostic indicators

| Indicator | Prognostic relevance |
|-----------|----------------------|
| **Tonsillar descent (mm)** | Greater descent (>10 mm) correlates with increased likelihood of respiratory dysfunction and neurological deficits; may predict need for surgical decompression. |
| **Central event percentage on sleep study** | A high proportion (>50 %) of central apnoeas suggests significant medullary involvement; predicts persistence of symptoms despite conservative measures. |
| **Presence of syringomyelia** | Associated with more severe bulbar and respiratory compromise; worsens long‑term functional outlook. |
| **Neurological exam findings** | Cranial nerve deficits or ataxia indicate advanced compressive effects; associated with poorer outcomes if untreated. |

In the present case, 11 mm tonsillar descent and 72 % central events were strongly predictive of an acquired central hypoventilation syndrome phenotype.

### Risks of untreated central hypoventilation

Without timely recognition and intervention, chronic hypoventilation can lead to several serious sequelae:

- **Pulmonary hypertension and cor pulmonale** due to chronic low‑grade hypoxia.  
- **Neurocognitive decline** from intermittent cerebral hypoxia, especially affecting executive function and memory.  
- **Sudden death** during sleep or quiet wakefulness when automatic breathing fails.  
- **Progressive bulbar dysfunction**, including worsening dysphagia, voice changes, and aspiration pneumonia.

Early identification and, when appropriate, posterior fossa decompression can reverse or halt these processes.

### Research gaps

1. **Prevalence and characterization of burning vertex syndrome in CMI**  
   While small‑fibre‑mediated pain at the vertex has been reported, systematic data on its frequency, clinical picture, and response to intervention are lacking.

2. **Standardized assessment of scalp allodynia**  
   No validated instrument currently exists to quantify scalp hypersensitivity in CMI patients. Development of such a tool would facilitate research and improve clinical recognition.

3. **Long‑term outcomes of conservative versus surgical management**  
   Most current literature derives from small series or case reports. Prospective cohort studies comparing quality of life, respiratory parameters, and neurological function over several years are needed.

4. **Optimal polysomnographic protocol**  
   The role of type I versus type III studies in CMI‑related sleep‑disordered breathing remains undefined. Clarifying EEG necessity, end‑tidal CO₂ monitoring, and video analysis would refine diagnostic accuracy.

5. **Impact of posterior fossa decompression on sleep‑disordered breathing**  
   Controlled trials assessing pre‑ and post‑operative polysomnography could determine whether surgical intervention consistently normalizes central apnoea indices.

### Guideline implications

Current neurosurgical and sleep‑medicine guidelines recommend considering imaging of the cranio‑cervical junction in patients with unexplained central apnoea or dysphagia of unclear origin. This case reinforces that recommendation and suggests the following refinements:

- **Routine polysomnography** should be performed in symptomatic CMI patients, regardless of the presence or absence of overt CPAP‑eligible obstructive events, to detect central hypoventilation.  
- **Early neurologic assessment** in patients with persistent, idiopathic dysphagia should include a focused review of cranial nerve function and posterior fossa imaging.  
- **Multidisciplinary care pathways** incorporating neurology, sleep medicine, otolaryngology, and neurosurgery should be established to expedite diagnosis and management.

By remaining vigilant for these red flags, clinicians can reduce diagnostic delay, prevent potentially reversible complications, and coordinate care that aligns with emerging evidence and guideline updates.
