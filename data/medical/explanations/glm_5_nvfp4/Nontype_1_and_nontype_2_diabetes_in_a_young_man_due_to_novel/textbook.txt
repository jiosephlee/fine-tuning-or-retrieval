Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Assessment

The patient, a young adult, presented for evaluation after an annual health examination revealed hyperglycemia and glycosuria. At initial assessment, he reported classic symptoms of hyperglycemia, including polydipsia, polyphagia, and recent weight loss. His glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) was markedly elevated at 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), with a fasting glucose of 12.4 mmol/L and a 2-hour post-challenge glucose of 19.8 mmol/L following a 75-gram oral glucose tolerance test. Despite these significant abnormalities, there was no evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis, an important negative finding that influenced the initial differential diagnosis.

Physical examination revealed a body mass index (BMI) of 24.6 kg/m², which falls within the normal range for Asian populations. Vital signs were unremarkable, with blood pressure at 112/70 mmHg and heart rate at 78 beats per minute. Notably, acanthosis nigricans—a cutaneous marker of insulin resistance—was absent. This finding is clinically significant, as the presence of acanthosis nigricans would strongly suggest type 2 diabetes mellitus with underlying insulin resistance. Its absence, combined with a normal BMI, raised early suspicion for an alternative diabetes etiology. However, the lipid profile complicated this picture, revealing elevated triglycerides (4.31 mmol/L) and low high-density lipoprotein (1.01 mmol/L), findings consistent with possible metabolic syndrome. This mixed presentation illustrates a common diagnostic challenge: the increasing overlap between metabolic syndrome features and monogenic diabetes as obesity rates rise globally.

The family history provided a critical clue to the diagnosis. The patient's maternal grandmother was diagnosed with diabetes in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at age 20. This pattern of early-onset diabetes across three consecutive generations, transmitted through the maternal line, is highly suggestive of autosomal dominant inheritance—a hallmark of maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY). The absence of early-onset cardiovascular disease among affected family members is also noteworthy, as HNF1A-MODY typically carries a lower risk of macrovascular complications compared to type 2 diabetes at similar levels of glycemic control.

The initial differential diagnosis included type 1 diabetes, type 2 diabetes, and monogenic diabetes. Several features argued against typical type 1 diabetes: the patient's age at presentation, the absence of ketoacidosis despite marked hyperglycemia, and the strong multigenerational family history. Against typical type 2 diabetes, the patient's relatively lean BMI and absence of acanthosis nigricans were relevant, though the dyslipidemia introduced some ambiguity. The constellation of findings—early-onset diabetes without features of insulin resistance, preserved endogenous insulin secretion (suggested by the glucagon stimulation test showing a stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L), negative islet autoantibodies (GADA and IA-2A), and an autosomal dominant family history—collectively pointed toward MODY as the leading diagnostic consideration.

This case illustrates that clinical suspicion for MODY should arise when young patients present with diabetes that does not cleanly fit the typical phenotypes of type 1 or type 2 disease. Key clinical clues include diagnosis before age 25, absence of autoantibodies, evidence of preserved beta-cell function, and a multigenerational family history of early-onset diabetes. The presence of glycosuria at relatively modest glucose elevations can also serve as a subtle marker, as HNF1A-MODY is associated with a lowered renal threshold for glucose reabsorption due to reduced expression of sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 in the renal tubules. Recognizing these patterns early is essential, as the definitive diagnosis requires molecular genetic testing, and treatment decisions differ substantially between MODY subtypes and other forms of diabetes.

## Pathophysiology and Genetic Basis of HNF1A-MODY

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) represents a distinct category of diabetes mellitus that differs fundamentally from type 1 and type 2 diabetes in its etiology. MODY encompasses a group of autosomal dominantly inherited, non-autoimmune diabetes disorders characterized by a primary defect in pancreatic β-cell development and function. The diagnostic criteria typically require onset before age 25, though this threshold is somewhat arbitrary and later presentations occur. MODY accounts for approximately 1% to 6% of all young-onset diabetes cases, though this figure likely represents an underestimate due to underdiagnosis. Among the various MODY subtypes, HNF1A-MODY is the most prevalent, constituting 30% to 65% of all genetically confirmed MODY cases. The patient in this case report exemplifies the classic inheritance pattern: his maternal grandmother was diagnosed in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at age 20, demonstrating the autosomal dominant transmission that characterizes this condition.

The HNF1A gene is located on the q24 region of chromosome 12 and spans 23,790 base pairs across 10 exons. It encodes hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF-1α), a transcription factor protein of 631 amino acids that belongs to the homeodomain-containing transcription factor family. HNF-1α is expressed in multiple tissues, including the liver, pancreas, and kidneys—organs central to glucose homeostasis. In pancreatic β-cells, HNF-1α functions as a master regulator of gene expression, controlling the transcription of genes essential for glucose sensing and insulin secretion. The protein contains a dimerization domain, a DNA-binding domain, and a transactivation domain, each critical for its function as a transcription factor. Understanding this structure is essential for interpreting how different mutations produce varying clinical effects.

The molecular mechanisms by which HNF1A mutations impair β-cell function involve multiple downstream targets. HNF-1α directly regulates the expression of genes encoding glucose transporter 2 (GLUT2) and pyruvate kinase, both critical for glucose-stimulated insulin secretion. GLUT2 functions as the primary glucose sensor on the β-cell surface, facilitating glucose uptake in proportion to extracellular concentrations. Reduced GLUT2 expression impairs the β-cell's ability to detect rising blood glucose levels, blunting the insulin secretory response. Similarly, decreased pyruvate kinase activity disrupts glycolytic flux and ATP generation, compromising the signaling cascade that triggers insulin granule exocytosis. The net result is progressive β-cell dysfunction characterized by inadequate insulin secretion in response to glucose, despite preserved β-cell mass. This explains the patient's insufficient insulin response on glucagon stimulation testing, with a stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L—detectable but suboptimal for his degree of hyperglycemia.

A distinctive pathophysiological feature of HNF1A-MODY is glycosuria occurring at relatively lower blood glucose concentrations than in other forms of diabetes. This phenomenon results from HNF-1α's role in regulating renal glucose reabsorption. The sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2), located in the proximal renal tubule, is responsible for reabsorbing the majority of filtered glucose. HNF-1α directly controls SGLT2 gene transcription, and mutations in HNF1A lead to reduced SGLT2 expression. Consequently, patients with HNF1A-MODY have a decreased renal threshold for glucose reabsorption, resulting in glycosuria at blood glucose levels that would not typically cause glucose spillage into urine. The patient presented with glycosuria detected during a routine health examination, which, in the context of his family history, should have raised clinical suspicion for MODY. This renal phenotype has therapeutic implications: SGLT2 inhibitors are relatively contraindicated in HNF1A-MODY because further inhibition of already diminished SGLT2 activity can precipitate severe dehydration, volume depletion, and potentially diabetic ketoacidosis.

Genotype-phenotype correlations in HNF1A-MODY provide insight into disease severity and inform prognosis. Over 1000 distinct HNF1A variants have been reported, with the majority located in the DNA-binding domain. Mutations in this domain tend to produce more severe functional impairment because they directly compromise the protein's ability to bind target gene promoters. The transactivation domain has a lower mutation rate and may be associated with milder phenotypes. The patient in this case carried a novel heterozygous variant, p.N270S (c.809A>G), located in exon 4 within the DNA-binding domain. This mutation substitutes serine for asparagine at residue 270, affecting an amino acid adjacent to known pathogenic residues. The variant was classified as pathogenic based on American College of Medical Genetics criteria, supported by its high AlphaMissense score (0.995) indicating predicted functional disruption, and its segregation with diabetes in the family. This molecular understanding directly influenced treatment decisions: the confirmed HNF1A-MODY diagnosis allowed transition from insulin to oral agents and GLP-1 receptor agonists, medications particularly effective in this genetic subtype due to preserved sulfonylurea sensitivity and favorable β-cell preservation effects.

## Diagnostic Workup and Clinical Reasoning

The diagnostic approach to this patient illustrates a systematic process that clinicians must follow when evaluating early-onset diabetes with atypical features. The workup progressed from initial biochemical characterization through functional assessment of β-cell reserve, exclusion of autoimmune etiology, and ultimately to molecular genetic confirmation.

**Initial Biochemical Evaluation**

The patient presented with marked hyperglycemia, with an HbA1c of 10.2% (88 mmol/mol) and fasting glucose of 12.4 mmol/L. An oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) demonstrated a 2-hour post-challenge glucose of 19.8 mmol/L, revealing significant postprandial glucose excursion. This degree of hyperglycemia indicated established diabetes, but the pattern of glycemic elevation—particularly the pronounced postprandial rise—offered an early clue. In HNF1A-MODY, postprandial hyperglycemia tends to be prominent due to impaired insulin secretion in response to glucose loads, a finding that was clearly present in this case. The lipid profile showed elevated triglycerides (4.31 mmol/L) and low HDL cholesterol (1.01 mmol/L), suggesting concurrent metabolic dyslipidemia, though the absence of acanthosis nigricans argued against severe insulin resistance.

**Assessment of β-Cell Function**

A critical step in distinguishing monogenic diabetes from type 1 diabetes was the glucagon stimulation test. This test evaluates residual β-cell function by measuring C-peptide response to intravenous glucagon. In this patient, fasting C-peptide was 0.39 nmol/L, rising to 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes post-stimulation. A stimulated C-peptide exceeding 0.60 nmol/L generally indicates preserved endogenous insulin secretion, which would be unusual in established type 1 diabetes. While the patient's C-peptide response demonstrated some β-cell reserve, the absolute values were insufficient for normal glycemic control, reflecting the progressive β-cell dysfunction characteristic of HNF1A-MODY. This pattern—preserved but inadequate insulin secretion—distinguished the patient from both type 1 diabetes (where secretion is typically minimal) and type 2 diabetes (where secretion is often preserved or elevated early in disease).

**Autoantibody Testing**

Negative results for glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA) and islet antigen 2 antibodies (IA-2A) provided essential evidence against autoimmune type 1 diabetes. Zinc transporter 8 antibodies (ZnT8) are also standard in this evaluation panel. The absence of all three autoantibodies in a patient with early-onset diabetes significantly reduces the probability of type 1 diabetes, particularly when combined with preserved C-peptide secretion. However, clinicians must recognize that approximately 5–10% of patients with true type 1 diabetes may be antibody-negative, and conversely, rare cases of MODY have been reported with positive autoantibodies. Therefore, autoantibody testing serves as a supportive tool rather than a definitive diagnostic test.

**Integration of Clinical Data**

Several clinical features raised suspicion for MODY in this case: the patient's young age at diagnosis, absence of obesity (BMI 24.6 kg/m²), negative autoantibodies, preserved C-peptide response, and most importantly, the striking family history of early-onset diabetes affecting three generations. The maternal grandmother was diagnosed in her 30s, the mother in her late 20s, and the younger brother at age 20. This autosomal dominant pattern of inheritance is a hallmark of MODY and provided a strong clinical signal before genetic testing was even performed.

**MODY Probability Calculators**

The Exeter group developed a probability calculator to estimate the likelihood of MODY based on clinical parameters. When this patient's data were entered, the calculator returned a 62.4% probability (1 in 1.6 chance) of MODY. While this tool can help identify candidates for genetic testing, clinicians must understand its limitations. The calculator was derived predominantly from White European populations and may overestimate or underestimate MODY probability in other ethnic groups. Additionally, as obesity rates rise globally, traditional clinical discriminators such as BMI and metabolic syndrome lose their differential value, since patients with MODY can also be overweight or obese.

**Genetic Testing and Variant Identification**

Definitive diagnosis required molecular genetic testing. A next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel targeting known MODY genes identified a heterozygous variant in HNF1A: c.809A>G, resulting in the amino acid substitution p.N270S in exon 4. This variant had not been previously reported in ClinVar, the literature, or other variant databases, representing a novel finding. The detection of a novel variant raises the critical question of pathogenicity—how can clinicians determine whether a previously unreported genetic change is disease-causing?

**ACMG Guidelines for Variant Interpretation**

The American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) provides a standardized framework for classifying variants into five categories: pathogenic, likely pathogenic, variant of uncertain significance (VUS), likely benign, and benign. In this case, multiple lines of evidence supported pathogenicity. The variant affects an amino acid residue adjacent to known pathogenic residues in the DNA-binding domain of HNF1A, a region where most disease-causing mutations cluster. The AlphaMissense computational algorithm returned a high pathogenicity score of 0.995. Critically, the variant segregated with diabetes in the family, meaning it was present in affected family members and consistent with the autosomal dominant inheritance pattern. These criteria collectively met ACMG standards for pathogenic classification despite the variant's absence from public databases.

**Diagnostic Synthesis**

The convergence of clinical, biochemical, and genetic evidence established the diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY. This case demonstrates that genetic testing should not be viewed as an isolated test but rather as the final step in a comprehensive diagnostic evaluation. The preceding workup—characterizing glycemic patterns, assessing β-cell function, excluding autoimmune diabetes, and documenting family history—provides the clinical context necessary to interpret genetic results meaningfully and guide subsequent management decisions.

## Treatment Selection and Precision Medicine Approach

The management of this patient illustrates the critical transition from empiric, symptom-based treatment to precision medicine guided by genetic diagnosis. At initial presentation, the patient had markedly elevated glucose levels with an HbA1c of 10.2% and fasting glucose of 12.4 mmol/L, requiring immediate intervention before genetic testing could be completed. The initial regimen of basal insulin (degludec 8 units daily), metformin (1500 mg daily), and the DPP-4 inhibitor saxagliptin (5 mg daily) reflected a practical approach to achieving rapid glycemic control while addressing potential insulin resistance, given the preliminary consideration of type 2 diabetes. This empiric strategy underscores an important clinical reality: treatment decisions often must precede definitive diagnosis, particularly when patients present with significant hyperglycemia requiring urgent intervention.

The genetic confirmation of HNF1A-MODY fundamentally altered the therapeutic approach. HNF1A-MODY is characterized by a primary defect in pancreatic β-cell function rather than insulin resistance, and patients demonstrate particular sensitivity to certain medication classes. First-line therapy for HNF1A-MODY traditionally consists of low-dose sulfonylureas, which enhance insulin secretion by closing ATP-sensitive potassium channels on β-cells, triggering membrane depolarization and calcium influx. Patients with HNF1A mutations exhibit enhanced sulfonylurea sensitivity due to the specific pathophysiology of their β-cell defect, often achieving excellent glycemic control at relatively low doses. However, sulfonylureas carry a risk of hypoglycemia, which becomes increasingly relevant as β-cell function declines over time.

GLP-1 receptor agonists have emerged as a valuable alternative or addition to sulfonylureas in HNF1A-MODY. Clinical trial evidence demonstrates that GLP-1 receptor agonists, such as liraglutide, achieve comparable reductions in fasting and postprandial glucose to glimepiride in HNF1A-MODY patients, but with a significantly lower risk of hypoglycemia. In this case, after one month of initial therapy reduced the HbA1c to 9.1%, insulin was discontinued and replaced with dulaglutide (1.5 mg weekly). This transition reflected a precision medicine approach: once the genetic diagnosis established the primary defect as impaired insulin secretion with preserved insulin sensitivity, the treatment plan could shift from insulin replacement to agents that enhance endogenous insulin secretion while minimizing hypoglycemia risk.

An equally important aspect of precision medicine involves recognizing medications that may pose particular risks in specific genetic forms of diabetes. SGLT-2 inhibitors deserve special consideration in HNF1A-MODY because the HNF1A gene transcriptionally regulates SGLT2 expression in renal proximal tubules. Patients with HNF1A mutations already have a reduced renal threshold for glucose reabsorption, manifesting as glycosuria at relatively lower blood glucose concentrations. Further pharmacologic inhibition of SGLT2 in this context can precipitate severe dehydration, volume depletion, and an elevated risk of euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis. The potential risks of SGLT-2 inhibitors in HNF1A-MODY—including dehydration, vascular collapse, genital infections, and ketoacidosis—generally outweigh the glycemic benefits, and these agents should be avoided or used with extreme caution.

The long-term maintenance regimen for this patient ultimately stabilized on metformin (1500 mg daily) combined with saxagliptin (5 mg daily), with HbA1c consistently maintained below 6.5%. The initial GLP-1 receptor agonist was discontinued after glycemic control was established, demonstrating that treatment intensity can be de-escalated once targets are achieved. The absence of microalbuminuria and diabetic retinopathy at follow-up reflects the benefit of early, effective glycemic control. This case exemplifies how genetic diagnosis enables treatment optimization: the discontinuation of insulin, selection of appropriate oral and injectable agents, and avoidance of potentially harmful medications all flowed directly from identifying the underlying HNF1A mutation. For clinicians encountering young patients with early-onset diabetes and a suggestive family history, maintaining a high index of suspicion for MODY can prevent years of inappropriate treatment and enable truly personalized care.

## Outcomes, Clinical Implications, and Lessons Learned

The patient's response to treatment demonstrates the substantial clinical benefit that can be achieved when therapy is aligned with the underlying molecular diagnosis. Within three months of initiating appropriate therapy, his glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) decreased dramatically from 10.2% to 6.0%, well below the general treatment target of 7.0% for most adults with diabetes. This improvement occurred after transitioning from an initial insulin-based regimen to a combination of a GLP-1 receptor agonist (dulaglutide) and metformin. The initial regimen, which included basal insulin, reflected a preliminary working diagnosis of type 2 diabetes and was appropriately chosen to achieve rapid glycemic control given the presenting severity. However, once genetic testing confirmed HNF1A-MODY, the treatment strategy could be refined to better match the patient's pathophysiology. On long-term follow-up, the patient's HbA1c has remained consistently below 6.5% on a simplified maintenance regimen of metformin and a DPP-4 inhibitor (saxagliptin). This trajectory illustrates that while initial stabilization may require insulin, patients with HNF1A-MODY can often achieve excellent glycemic control with oral agents or injectable therapies that do not require the intensive monitoring burden of insulin.

Long-term surveillance for diabetes complications remains essential, regardless of the monogenic etiology. To date, this patient has shown no evidence of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy, indicating that early detection and treatment likely prevented the development of these microvascular complications during the follow-up period. However, sudomotor testing at presentation revealed severely reduced function in the hands, suggesting early autonomic neuropathy—a finding that may reflect the duration of undiagnosed hyperglycemia before presentation. This highlights an important clinical point: patients with MODY are not immune to diabetes complications, and the degree of glycemic exposure matters. The absence of microalbuminuria and retinopathy on current surveillance does not preclude future development, and ongoing annual screening for nephropathy, retinopathy, and neuropathy should continue according to standard diabetes care guidelines. The presence of autonomic dysfunction at diagnosis in this young patient underscores that even monogenic diabetes can present with complications if hyperglycemia has been longstanding.

The identification of a pathogenic HNF1A variant has implications beyond the individual patient. The patient's family history—maternal grandmother diagnosed in her 30s, mother in her late 20s, and younger brother at age 20—now takes on clear clinical significance. Cascade genetic testing becomes a priority for first-degree relatives, particularly the mother and younger brother who already carry diagnoses of early-onset diabetes. Confirming HNF1A-MODY in affected family members would allow for treatment optimization, potentially liberating them from unnecessary insulin therapy. Equally important, testing unaffected at-risk relatives (such as the patient's siblings or future children) can identify carriers before hyperglycemia develops, enabling anticipatory monitoring and early intervention. Genetic counseling should address the autosomal dominant inheritance pattern, meaning each first-degree relative has a 50% chance of inheriting the pathogenic variant. The psychological and practical implications of predictive genetic testing should be discussed, including considerations for family planning, insurance implications, and the potential for anxiety related to knowing one carries a diabetes-predisposing gene.

This case illustrates several persistent barriers to timely MODY diagnosis. Prior to genetic confirmation, this patient was managed as having type 2 diabetes, reflecting a common pattern of misdiagnosis. Several factors contributed to diagnostic uncertainty: the patient's BMI of 24.6 kg/m² falls within the overweight range, and his lipid profile was suggestive of metabolic syndrome—features more typical of type 2 diabetes than classically described MODY presentations. However, as obesity prevalence increases globally, BMI has become a less reliable distinguishing feature. The strong family history of early-onset diabetes across three generations and the absence of islet autoantibodies were key clues that should have prompted earlier consideration of monogenic diabetes. The MODY probability calculator estimated a 62.4% probability of MODY based on clinical features, yet even with this moderately high score, the diagnosis was only confirmed through molecular testing. Clinical prediction tools, while helpful, have limitations when applied to non-European populations, as they were derived predominantly from White cohorts. The case reinforces that clinicians should maintain a low threshold for ordering genetic testing in young patients with diabetes who have atypical features, negative autoantibodies, or a compelling family history—rather than waiting for a "classic" MODY phenotype that may not exist.

The discovery of a novel HNF1A variant in this case contributes to the broader understanding of the genetic spectrum of MODY. The identified variant, p.N270S (c.809A>G), was previously unreported in ClinVar and other variant databases. It was classified as likely pathogenic based on American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) criteria, supported by multiple lines of evidence: it affects an amino acid adjacent to known pathogenic residues in the DNA-binding domain, it has a high AlphaMissense score (0.995) indicating predicted pathogenicity, and it segregates with diabetes in the family. This finding expands the catalog of known pathogenic HNF1A mutations and will inform future interpretations when this variant is encountered in other patients. Each novel pathogenic variant adds to the reference databases, progressively improving the accuracy of variant interpretation for subsequent patients worldwide.

The principle of precision medicine—matching treatment to the molecular basis of disease—is vividly demonstrated in this case. Patients with HNF1A-MODY exhibit particular sensitivity to sulfonylureas due to the specific role of HNF1A in regulating β-cell ATP-sensitive potassium channels. While sulfonylureas have traditionally been considered first-line therapy, GLP-1 receptor agonists have emerged as an effective and potentially safer alternative, offering similar glycemic efficacy with lower hypoglycemia risk and additional benefits for weight management and cardiovascular risk reduction. Importantly, patients with HNF1A-MODY may be at increased risk of adverse effects from SGLT2 inhibitors due to the role of HNF1A in regulating renal glucose transport; case reports have described severe dehydration and ketoacidosis in HNF1A-MODY patients receiving these agents. Without genetic diagnosis, inappropriate therapy might have been selected. The ability to discontinue insulin and achieve durable glycemic control on non-insulin therapy represents a meaningful improvement in quality of life for this young patient. This case exemplifies how molecular diagnosis transforms clinical care: it guides therapeutic selection, informs prognosis, enables family screening, and ultimately delivers on the promise of precision medicine in diabetes.
