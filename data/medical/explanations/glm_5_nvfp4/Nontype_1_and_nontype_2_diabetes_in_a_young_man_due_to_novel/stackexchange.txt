Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Pathophysiology] How does a mutation in the HNF1A gene lead to both impaired insulin secretion and glycosuria in patients with HNF1A-MODY?

**Clinical Teaching Answer:**

The HNF1A gene encodes a transcription factor expressed in pancreatic β-cells, hepatocytes, and renal proximal tubules. Understanding its role in these tissues explains the dual presentation of impaired insulin secretion and glycosuria seen in this patient.

**In the pancreas**, HNF1A regulates the expression of genes critical for glucose-stimulated insulin secretion, including GLUT2 (the glucose transporter) and pyruvate kinase. GLUT2 allows glucose entry into β-cells, and pyruvate kinase facilitates glucose metabolism through glycolysis, generating ATP that triggers insulin release via closure of ATP-sensitive potassium channels. As noted in the case discussion, mutations in HNF1A lead to "impaired expression of insulin and GLUT2 genes, as well as reduced pyruvate kinase activity," resulting in defective glucose sensing and progressive β-cell dysfunction. This explains why our patient had an insufficient C-peptide response on glucagon stimulation testing (stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L) despite having no evidence of autoimmune destruction.

**In the kidney**, HNF1A regulates the expression of SGLT2 (sodium-glucose cotransporter 2), the primary transporter responsible for glucose reabsorption in the proximal tubule. When HNF1A function is impaired, SGLT2 expression is reduced, lowering the renal threshold for glucose reabsorption. This produces glycosuria at lower blood glucose concentrations than in typical diabetes—a hallmark clinical feature demonstrated in our patient, who presented with "hyperglycaemia and glycosuria" on routine examination.

This dual mechanism is pathognomonic for HNF1A-MODY and explains why patients may present with relatively modest hyperglycemia yet significant glycosuria, an important clinical clue that should prompt consideration of monogenic diabetes.

### [Clinical Pearls] What clinical features should raise suspicion for MODY rather than type 1 or type 2 diabetes in a young patient presenting with hyperglycemia?

### Clinical Features Raising Suspicion for MODY

Several key features in this case should prompt consideration of MODY over type 1 or type 2 diabetes:

**1. Autosomal dominant family history of early-onset diabetes**
This is the most critical clue. The patient had a striking multigenerational pedigree: maternal grandmother diagnosed in her 30s, mother in her late 20s, and younger brother at age 20. This vertical transmission pattern across three generations, with onset before age 25 in multiple affected members, is classic for MODY.

**2. Absence of autoimmune markers**
Negative glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA) and islet antigen-2 antibodies (IA-2A) argued against type 1 diabetes. While type 1 diabetes remains possible in antibody-negative patients, the combination of negative antibodies with preserved β-cell function strongly favors a non-autoimmune etiology.

**3. Preserved C-peptide secretion**
The glucagon stimulation test demonstrated a stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L, exceeding the 0.60 nmol/L threshold that indicates preserved endogenous insulin secretion. This finding, in the context of several months of symptomatic hyperglycemia and weight loss, would be atypical for type 1 diabetes, where β-cell destruction typically leads to rapid loss of insulin production.

**4. Lack of insulin resistance features**
Despite elevated HbA1c (10.2%), the patient had a BMI of 24.6 kg/m² and no acanthosis nigricans. While not definitive—since MODY patients can develop metabolic syndrome features—this phenotype contrasts with typical type 2 diabetes presentations, where obesity and insulin resistance signs are expected.

**5. Absence of diabetic ketoacidosis**
The patient presented with significant hyperglycemia (fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L) and glycosuria without DKA, despite having symptoms of polydipsia, polyphagia, and weight loss. This pattern is consistent with the gradual β-cell dysfunction seen in HNF1A-MODY rather than the acute insulin deficiency of type 1 diabetes.

**Clinical Pearl**
Recognizing MODY has direct therapeutic implications: this patient was transitioned off insulin to a GLP-1 receptor agonist and metformin after genetic confirmation, achieving excellent glycemic control (HbA1c 6.0%) without hypoglycemia risk. Additionally, HNF1A-MODY patients have increased sensitivity to sulfonylureas but should avoid SGLT2 inhibitors due to underlying reduced SGLT2 expression and elevated risk of ketoacidosis.

### [Differential Diagnosis] What laboratory findings help distinguish HNF1A-MODY from type 1 diabetes and type 2 diabetes during the diagnostic evaluation?

### Clinical Teaching Answer

Distinguishing HNF1A-MODY from type 1 and type 2 diabetes requires integrating several key laboratory findings, as illustrated in this case.

**First, islet autoantibodies are essential.** In HNF1A-MODY, autoantibodies—including glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), islet antigen-2 antibodies (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 antibodies (ZnT8)—are typically **negative**, whereas they are positive in approximately 85–90% of patients with type 1 diabetes. In this case, both GADA and IA-2A were negative, effectively ruling out autoimmune type 1 diabetes and prompting consideration of a monogenic form.

**Second, C-peptide levels help differentiate HNF1A-MODY from type 1 diabetes.** Patients with HNF1A-MODY have preserved or moderately reduced β-cell function, with measurable C-peptide levels. A stimulated C-peptide level >0.60 nmol/L generally indicates preserved endogenous insulin secretion. In this patient, the glucagon stimulation test demonstrated a stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L, confirming residual β-cell function inconsistent with classic type 1 diabetes, where C-peptide is typically low or undetectable. However, as noted in the case, C-peptide can be preserved early in type 1 diabetes, so this finding should be interpreted alongside autoantibody status.

**Third, glycosuria at relatively lower glucose thresholds is a distinctive feature of HNF1A-MODY.** HNF1A regulates SGLT2 expression in the renal tubules; mutations reduce the renal threshold for glucose reabsorption, leading to glycosuria even at modest hyperglycemia. This patient presented with glycosuria detected incidentally during a health examination—an important clue.

**Fourth, insulin resistance markers help distinguish HNF1A-MODY from type 2 diabetes.** Patients with HNF1A-MODY typically lack features of insulin resistance. This patient had a BMI of 24.6 kg/m², no acanthosis nigricans, and no clinical evidence of insulin resistance—unlike typical type 2 diabetes presentations, where obesity, acanthosis nigricans, and metabolic syndrome features are common.

**Ultimately, genetic testing is the definitive diagnostic tool.** When clinical and laboratory features suggest HNF1A-MODY—early-onset diabetes, negative autoantibodies, preserved C-peptide, glycosuria, and a strong family history in an autosomal dominant pattern—next-generation sequencing confirms the diagnosis, as it did in this case with identification of a novel pathogenic HNF1A variant.

**In summary:** Negative islet autoantibodies, preserved C-peptide secretion, glycosuria at lower glucose thresholds, and absence of insulin resistance markers together support HNF1A-MODY over type 1 or type 2 diabetes, with genetic testing providing definitive confirmation.

### [Management] What is the first-line pharmacologic treatment for HNF1A-MODY, and what alternative agents have demonstrated efficacy in this population?

**First-line therapy for HNF1A-MODY is low-dose sulfonylureas.**

Sulfonylureas are the pharmacologic mainstay because they directly address the underlying pathophysiology—HNF1A mutations cause progressive β-cell dysfunction with impaired insulin secretion, but the β-cells remain responsive to sulfonylureas, which close ATP-sensitive potassium channels and stimulate insulin release independent of glucose sensing. Patients with HNF1A-MODY often exhibit remarkable sensitivity to sulfonylureas, sometimes allowing transition off insulin entirely.

**Alternative agents with demonstrated efficacy include GLP-1 receptor agonists.** As noted in the discussion, a small randomized crossover trial showed that liraglutide and glimepiride achieved comparable reductions in fasting and postprandial glucose, but GLP-1 receptor agonists carried a lower risk of hypoglycemia. In this case, after genetic confirmation, insulin was discontinued and replaced with dulaglutide, which—combined with metformin—reduced the patient's HbA1c from 10.2% to 6.0% within months. GLP-1 receptor agonists are particularly valuable when β-cell function has declined or when patients are prone to sulfonylurea-induced hypoglycemia.

**SGLT-2 inhibitors should generally be avoided** in HNF1A-MODY. Because HNF1A regulates SGLT2 expression in the renal tubules, these patients already have a reduced renal threshold for glucose reabsorption. Further pharmacologic inhibition of SGLT2 increases the risk of severe dehydration, ketoacidosis, and vascular collapse.

This case exemplifies precision medicine: genetic diagnosis directly informed the decision to discontinue insulin and transition to oral and injectable non-insulin therapies, achieving sustained glycemic control.

### [Clinical Pearls] Why are SGLT2 inhibitors potentially harmful in patients with HNF1A-MODY?

**Clinical Teaching Answer**

SGLT2 inhibitors are potentially harmful in HNF1A-MODY because these patients already have genetically reduced SGLT2 expression and function in the renal proximal tubules, making pharmacological inhibition of the residual transporter activity particularly dangerous.

**Pathophysiology:** HNF1A is a transcription factor that regulates SGLT2 gene expression in the kidneys. In patients with HNF1A-MODY, loss-of-function mutations lead to decreased SGLT2 expression, which results in a lower renal threshold for glucose reabsorption. This explains why glycosuria occurs at relatively lower blood glucose concentrations in these patients—a clinical feature noted in the case presentation, where the patient presented with glycosuria despite the absence of severe hyperglycemia or diabetic ketoacidosis.

**Clinical Consequence of SGLT2 Inhibitor Use:** When an SGLT2 inhibitor is administered to a patient with HNF1A-MODY, the drug inhibits the already diminished pool of functional SGLT2 transporters. This can lead to excessive glucosuria, causing profound volume depletion, dehydration, and potentially vascular collapse. As described in the case report's discussion, HNF1A-MODY patients treated with SGLT2 inhibitors are at increased risk for severe dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis, along with genital infections. The risks of these agents in this specific population generally outweigh their potential glycemic benefits.

**Clinical Implication:** This is a prime example of precision medicine in diabetes management. While SGLT2 inhibitors are first-line agents for many patients with type 2 diabetes due to their cardiovascular and renal benefits, they should be avoided in HNF1A-MODY. Instead, as demonstrated in this case, patients with HNF1A-MODY respond well to low-dose sulfonylureas or GLP-1 receptor agonists, which enhance insulin secretion through mechanisms independent of the HNF1A pathway defect.

### [Morning Report] A 22-year-old man presents with HbA1c of 10.2%, negative diabetes autoantibodies, and a stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L after glucagon administration. His mother was diagnosed with diabetes in her 20s and his maternal grandmother in her 30s. What is the significance of the glucagon stimulation test results, and how do they inform the differential diagnosis?

### Clinical Teaching Answer

The glucagon stimulation test in this patient provides critical information for differentiating diabetes classification. A stimulated C-peptide level of **0.83 nmol/L**—above the 0.60 nmol/L threshold for preserved endogenous insulin secretion—effectively excludes type 1 diabetes as the primary diagnosis and points toward a non-autoimmune etiology.

**Interpretation of the test:**
The glucagon stimulation test assesses residual β-cell function by provoking insulin release. In type 1 diabetes, autoimmune β-cell destruction typically results in a blunted or absent C-peptide response (often <0.2 nmol/L when stimulated). This patient's robust response indicates that his pancreatic β-cells retain functional capacity, despite the elevated HbA1c of 10.2%. Notably, the fasting C-peptide of 0.39 nmol/L rising to 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes demonstrates a dynamic secretory response that would be highly unusual in established type 1 diabetes.

**How this narrows the differential:**
When combined with **negative diabetes autoantibodies** (GADA, IA-2A) and a compelling **family history** of early-onset diabetes across three generations, the preserved C-peptide response strongly favors **maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY)** over type 1 diabetes. In HNF1A-MODY specifically, β-cell function declines progressively but remains detectable for years after diagnosis—distinct from the rapid autoimmune destruction seen in type 1 diabetes.

The test should be interpreted as **supportive rather than definitive**. As the case illustrates, patients with HNF1A-MODY can present with substantial hyperglycemia and symptoms of polydipsia, polyuria, and weight loss that mimic type 1 diabetes clinically. The glucagon stimulation test helped appropriately direct genetic testing, which ultimately confirmed the novel heterozygous *HNF1A* variant.

**Key teaching point:**
In any young patient with diabetes, negative autoantibodies, preserved C-peptide secretion, and an autosomal dominant family history should prompt consideration of MODY—recognizing that accurate classification fundamentally alters management, including the potential to discontinue insulin therapy in favor of oral agents or GLP-1 receptor agonists, as demonstrated in this case.
