Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

Chapter 4: ## Treatment Selection and Precision Medicine Approach

The management of this patient illustrates the critical transition from empiric, symptom-based treatment to precision medicine guided by genetic diagnosis. At initial presentation, the patient had markedly elevated glucose levels with an HbA1c of 10.2% and fasting glucose of 12.4 mmol/L, requiring immediate intervention before genetic testing could be completed. The initial regimen of basal insulin (degludec 8 units daily), metformin (1500 mg daily), and the DPP-4 inhibitor saxagliptin (5 mg daily) reflected a practical approach to achieving rapid glycemic control while addressing potential insulin resistance, given the preliminary consideration of type 2 diabetes. This empiric strategy underscores an important clinical reality: treatment decisions often must precede definitive diagnosis, particularly when patients present with significant hyperglycemia requiring urgent intervention.

The genetic confirmation of HNF1A-MODY fundamentally altered the therapeutic approach. HNF1A-MODY is characterized by a primary defect in pancreatic β-cell function rather than insulin resistance, and patients demonstrate particular sensitivity to certain medication classes. First-line therapy for HNF1A-MODY traditionally consists of low-dose sulfonylureas, which enhance insulin secretion by closing ATP-sensitive potassium channels on β-cells, triggering membrane depolarization and calcium influx. Patients with HNF1A mutations exhibit enhanced sulfonylurea sensitivity due to the specific pathophysiology of their β-cell defect, often achieving excellent glycemic control at relatively low doses. However, sulfonylureas carry a risk of hypoglycemia, which becomes increasingly relevant as β-cell function declines over time.

GLP-1 receptor agonists have emerged as a valuable alternative or addition to sulfonylureas in HNF1A-MODY. Clinical trial evidence demonstrates that GLP-1 receptor agonists, such as liraglutide, achieve comparable reductions in fasting and postprandial glucose to glimepiride in HNF1A-MODY patients, but with a significantly lower risk of hypoglycemia. In this case, after one month of initial therapy reduced the HbA1c to 9.1%, insulin was discontinued and replaced with dulaglutide (1.5 mg weekly). This transition reflected a precision medicine approach: once the genetic diagnosis established the primary defect as impaired insulin secretion with preserved insulin sensitivity, the treatment plan could shift from insulin replacement to agents that enhance endogenous insulin secretion while minimizing hypoglycemia risk.

An equally important aspect of precision medicine involves recognizing medications that may pose particular risks in specific genetic forms of diabetes. SGLT-2 inhibitors deserve special consideration in HNF1A-MODY because the HNF1A gene transcriptionally regulates SGLT2 expression in renal proximal tubules. Patients with HNF1A mutations already have a reduced renal threshold for glucose reabsorption, manifesting as glycosuria at relatively lower blood glucose concentrations. Further pharmacologic inhibition of SGLT2 in this context can precipitate severe dehydration, volume depletion, and an elevated risk of euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis. The potential risks of SGLT-2 inhibitors in HNF1A-MODY—including dehydration, vascular collapse, genital infections, and ketoacidosis—generally outweigh the glycemic benefits, and these agents should be avoided or used with extreme caution.

The long-term maintenance regimen for this patient ultimately stabilized on metformin (1500 mg daily) combined with saxagliptin (5 mg daily), with HbA1c consistently maintained below 6.5%. The initial GLP-1 receptor agonist was discontinued after glycemic control was established, demonstrating that treatment intensity can be de-escalated once targets are achieved. The absence of microalbuminuria and diabetic retinopathy at follow-up reflects the benefit of early, effective glycemic control. This case exemplifies how genetic diagnosis enables treatment optimization: the discontinuation of insulin, selection of appropriate oral and injectable agents, and avoidance of potentially harmful medications all flowed directly from identifying the underlying HNF1A mutation. For clinicians encountering young patients with early-onset diabetes and a suggestive family history, maintaining a high index of suspicion for MODY can prevent years of inappropriate treatment and enable truly personalized care.
