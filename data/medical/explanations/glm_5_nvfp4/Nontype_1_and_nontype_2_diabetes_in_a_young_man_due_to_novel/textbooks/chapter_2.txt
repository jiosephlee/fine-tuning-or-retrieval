Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

Chapter 2: ## Pathophysiology and Genetic Basis of HNF1A-MODY

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) represents a distinct category of diabetes mellitus that differs fundamentally from type 1 and type 2 diabetes in its etiology. MODY encompasses a group of autosomal dominantly inherited, non-autoimmune diabetes disorders characterized by a primary defect in pancreatic β-cell development and function. The diagnostic criteria typically require onset before age 25, though this threshold is somewhat arbitrary and later presentations occur. MODY accounts for approximately 1% to 6% of all young-onset diabetes cases, though this figure likely represents an underestimate due to underdiagnosis. Among the various MODY subtypes, HNF1A-MODY is the most prevalent, constituting 30% to 65% of all genetically confirmed MODY cases. The patient in this case report exemplifies the classic inheritance pattern: his maternal grandmother was diagnosed in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at age 20, demonstrating the autosomal dominant transmission that characterizes this condition.

The HNF1A gene is located on the q24 region of chromosome 12 and spans 23,790 base pairs across 10 exons. It encodes hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF-1α), a transcription factor protein of 631 amino acids that belongs to the homeodomain-containing transcription factor family. HNF-1α is expressed in multiple tissues, including the liver, pancreas, and kidneys—organs central to glucose homeostasis. In pancreatic β-cells, HNF-1α functions as a master regulator of gene expression, controlling the transcription of genes essential for glucose sensing and insulin secretion. The protein contains a dimerization domain, a DNA-binding domain, and a transactivation domain, each critical for its function as a transcription factor. Understanding this structure is essential for interpreting how different mutations produce varying clinical effects.

The molecular mechanisms by which HNF1A mutations impair β-cell function involve multiple downstream targets. HNF-1α directly regulates the expression of genes encoding glucose transporter 2 (GLUT2) and pyruvate kinase, both critical for glucose-stimulated insulin secretion. GLUT2 functions as the primary glucose sensor on the β-cell surface, facilitating glucose uptake in proportion to extracellular concentrations. Reduced GLUT2 expression impairs the β-cell's ability to detect rising blood glucose levels, blunting the insulin secretory response. Similarly, decreased pyruvate kinase activity disrupts glycolytic flux and ATP generation, compromising the signaling cascade that triggers insulin granule exocytosis. The net result is progressive β-cell dysfunction characterized by inadequate insulin secretion in response to glucose, despite preserved β-cell mass. This explains the patient's insufficient insulin response on glucagon stimulation testing, with a stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L—detectable but suboptimal for his degree of hyperglycemia.

A distinctive pathophysiological feature of HNF1A-MODY is glycosuria occurring at relatively lower blood glucose concentrations than in other forms of diabetes. This phenomenon results from HNF-1α's role in regulating renal glucose reabsorption. The sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2), located in the proximal renal tubule, is responsible for reabsorbing the majority of filtered glucose. HNF-1α directly controls SGLT2 gene transcription, and mutations in HNF1A lead to reduced SGLT2 expression. Consequently, patients with HNF1A-MODY have a decreased renal threshold for glucose reabsorption, resulting in glycosuria at blood glucose levels that would not typically cause glucose spillage into urine. The patient presented with glycosuria detected during a routine health examination, which, in the context of his family history, should have raised clinical suspicion for MODY. This renal phenotype has therapeutic implications: SGLT2 inhibitors are relatively contraindicated in HNF1A-MODY because further inhibition of already diminished SGLT2 activity can precipitate severe dehydration, volume depletion, and potentially diabetic ketoacidosis.

Genotype-phenotype correlations in HNF1A-MODY provide insight into disease severity and inform prognosis. Over 1000 distinct HNF1A variants have been reported, with the majority located in the DNA-binding domain. Mutations in this domain tend to produce more severe functional impairment because they directly compromise the protein's ability to bind target gene promoters. The transactivation domain has a lower mutation rate and may be associated with milder phenotypes. The patient in this case carried a novel heterozygous variant, p.N270S (c.809A>G), located in exon 4 within the DNA-binding domain. This mutation substitutes serine for asparagine at residue 270, affecting an amino acid adjacent to known pathogenic residues. The variant was classified as pathogenic based on American College of Medical Genetics criteria, supported by its high AlphaMissense score (0.995) indicating predicted functional disruption, and its segregation with diabetes in the family. This molecular understanding directly influenced treatment decisions: the confirmed HNF1A-MODY diagnosis allowed transition from insulin to oral agents and GLP-1 receptor agonists, medications particularly effective in this genetic subtype due to preserved sulfonylurea sensitivity and favorable β-cell preservation effects.
