Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Workup and Clinical Reasoning

The diagnostic approach to this patient illustrates a systematic process that clinicians must follow when evaluating early-onset diabetes with atypical features. The workup progressed from initial biochemical characterization through functional assessment of β-cell reserve, exclusion of autoimmune etiology, and ultimately to molecular genetic confirmation.

**Initial Biochemical Evaluation**

The patient presented with marked hyperglycemia, with an HbA1c of 10.2% (88 mmol/mol) and fasting glucose of 12.4 mmol/L. An oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) demonstrated a 2-hour post-challenge glucose of 19.8 mmol/L, revealing significant postprandial glucose excursion. This degree of hyperglycemia indicated established diabetes, but the pattern of glycemic elevation—particularly the pronounced postprandial rise—offered an early clue. In HNF1A-MODY, postprandial hyperglycemia tends to be prominent due to impaired insulin secretion in response to glucose loads, a finding that was clearly present in this case. The lipid profile showed elevated triglycerides (4.31 mmol/L) and low HDL cholesterol (1.01 mmol/L), suggesting concurrent metabolic dyslipidemia, though the absence of acanthosis nigricans argued against severe insulin resistance.

**Assessment of β-Cell Function**

A critical step in distinguishing monogenic diabetes from type 1 diabetes was the glucagon stimulation test. This test evaluates residual β-cell function by measuring C-peptide response to intravenous glucagon. In this patient, fasting C-peptide was 0.39 nmol/L, rising to 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes post-stimulation. A stimulated C-peptide exceeding 0.60 nmol/L generally indicates preserved endogenous insulin secretion, which would be unusual in established type 1 diabetes. While the patient's C-peptide response demonstrated some β-cell reserve, the absolute values were insufficient for normal glycemic control, reflecting the progressive β-cell dysfunction characteristic of HNF1A-MODY. This pattern—preserved but inadequate insulin secretion—distinguished the patient from both type 1 diabetes (where secretion is typically minimal) and type 2 diabetes (where secretion is often preserved or elevated early in disease).

**Autoantibody Testing**

Negative results for glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA) and islet antigen 2 antibodies (IA-2A) provided essential evidence against autoimmune type 1 diabetes. Zinc transporter 8 antibodies (ZnT8) are also standard in this evaluation panel. The absence of all three autoantibodies in a patient with early-onset diabetes significantly reduces the probability of type 1 diabetes, particularly when combined with preserved C-peptide secretion. However, clinicians must recognize that approximately 5–10% of patients with true type 1 diabetes may be antibody-negative, and conversely, rare cases of MODY have been reported with positive autoantibodies. Therefore, autoantibody testing serves as a supportive tool rather than a definitive diagnostic test.

**Integration of Clinical Data**

Several clinical features raised suspicion for MODY in this case: the patient's young age at diagnosis, absence of obesity (BMI 24.6 kg/m²), negative autoantibodies, preserved C-peptide response, and most importantly, the striking family history of early-onset diabetes affecting three generations. The maternal grandmother was diagnosed in her 30s, the mother in her late 20s, and the younger brother at age 20. This autosomal dominant pattern of inheritance is a hallmark of MODY and provided a strong clinical signal before genetic testing was even performed.

**MODY Probability Calculators**

The Exeter group developed a probability calculator to estimate the likelihood of MODY based on clinical parameters. When this patient's data were entered, the calculator returned a 62.4% probability (1 in 1.6 chance) of MODY. While this tool can help identify candidates for genetic testing, clinicians must understand its limitations. The calculator was derived predominantly from White European populations and may overestimate or underestimate MODY probability in other ethnic groups. Additionally, as obesity rates rise globally, traditional clinical discriminators such as BMI and metabolic syndrome lose their differential value, since patients with MODY can also be overweight or obese.

**Genetic Testing and Variant Identification**

Definitive diagnosis required molecular genetic testing. A next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel targeting known MODY genes identified a heterozygous variant in HNF1A: c.809A>G, resulting in the amino acid substitution p.N270S in exon 4. This variant had not been previously reported in ClinVar, the literature, or other variant databases, representing a novel finding. The detection of a novel variant raises the critical question of pathogenicity—how can clinicians determine whether a previously unreported genetic change is disease-causing?

**ACMG Guidelines for Variant Interpretation**

The American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) provides a standardized framework for classifying variants into five categories: pathogenic, likely pathogenic, variant of uncertain significance (VUS), likely benign, and benign. In this case, multiple lines of evidence supported pathogenicity. The variant affects an amino acid residue adjacent to known pathogenic residues in the DNA-binding domain of HNF1A, a region where most disease-causing mutations cluster. The AlphaMissense computational algorithm returned a high pathogenicity score of 0.995. Critically, the variant segregated with diabetes in the family, meaning it was present in affected family members and consistent with the autosomal dominant inheritance pattern. These criteria collectively met ACMG standards for pathogenic classification despite the variant's absence from public databases.

**Diagnostic Synthesis**

The convergence of clinical, biochemical, and genetic evidence established the diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY. This case demonstrates that genetic testing should not be viewed as an isolated test but rather as the final step in a comprehensive diagnostic evaluation. The preceding workup—characterizing glycemic patterns, assessing β-cell function, excluding autoimmune diabetes, and documenting family history—provides the clinical context necessary to interpret genetic results meaningfully and guide subsequent management decisions.
