Title: Blog about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

# The MODY Miss: Why We're Overlooking Monogenic Diabetes in Young Adults

Let's start with a number that should make every emergency physician and internist pause: up to 80-90% of Maturity-Onset Diabetes of the Young (MODY) cases are misdiagnosed as either Type 1 or Type 2 diabetes. That means we are likely missing a significant chunk of young diabetic patients who have a completely different disease trajectory, genetic inheritance, and—most importantly—treatment pathway.

The recent case report by Liu et al. highlights this exact diagnostic dilemma. We have a young adult male presenting with hyperglycemia and classic symptoms—polydipsia, polyphagia, weight loss. His HbA1c was sitting at a hefty 10.2%. It would be easy to slap a label of Type 1 diabetes on him given his age and weight loss, or perhaps Type 2 given he wasn't exactly thin (BMI 24.6). But the devil, as always, is in the details.

## The Case That Almost Got Away

This patient was initially started on basal insulin, metformin, and a DPP-4 inhibitor—a reasonable "shotgun" approach when you're trying to knock down a high glucose load in a young person. But the clues were there from the jump. First, he had negative autoantibodies (GADA and IA-2A). Second, his C-peptide was preserved (0.83 nmol/L on stimulation), proving his beta cells weren't under autoimmune attack. Third, and perhaps the biggest red flag, was the family history: maternal grandmother diagnosed in her 30s, mother in her late 20s, and a younger brother at age 20. That is a textbook autosomal dominant inheritance pattern, not the complex polygenic inheritance of Type 2 diabetes.

Genetic testing confirmed the suspicion: a novel heterozygous mutation in *HNF1A*. This is HNF1A-MODY, the most common form of MODY, accounting for 30-65% of cases. Once the diagnosis was confirmed, the treatment changed drastically. Insulin was stopped, and he was transitioned to a GLP-1 receptor agonist and metformin. His HbA1c dropped from 10.2% to 6.0% in just a few months.

## Why We Miss It

The problem is that MODY doesn't always look like the textbooks say it should. We were taught to look for a thin, young patient with a strong family history and no features of insulin resistance. But as this case shows, patients with MODY can have BMIs in the overweight range, and as the global prevalence of obesity rises, the BMI gap between MODY and Type 2 diabetes is narrowing. This patient even had dyslipidemia, suggesting metabolic syndrome, yet he had a monogenic form of diabetes.

We also rely too heavily on clinical intuition without pulling the trigger on genetic testing. The Exeter MODY probability calculator is a fantastic tool, but it has limitations. In this case, the calculator gave the patient a 62.4% probability of having MODY—a "1 in 1.6 chance." That sounds convincing, but the calculator was derived from White European populations. In Asian and other non-White populations, the calculator may misclassify patients, either overestimating or underestimating risk.

## The Treatment Paradigm Shift

This is where the rubber meets the road. Diagnosing MODY isn't just an academic exercise; it fundamentally changes management. Patients with HNF1A-MODY are exquisitely sensitive to sulfonylureas. Historically, low-dose sulfonylureas have been the gold standard, but this case adds to the growing evidence for GLP-1 receptor agonists. The authors successfully used dulaglutide, noting that GLP-1 agonists offer a lower risk of hypoglycemia compared to sulfonylureas while effectively preserving beta-cell function.

Furthermore, and this is critical for the ED or acute care setting: SGLT2 inhibitors can be dangerous in HNF1A-MODY. These patients have a reduced renal threshold for glucose reabsorption due to the *HNF1A* mutation itself; adding an SGLT2 inhibitor can precipitate severe dehydration and euglycemic DKA. If you misdiagnose them as Type 2 diabetes and start them on empagliflozin, you could be setting them up for a disastrous outcome.

## Key Take-Home Points

*   **Think MODY in young diabetics with negative autoantibodies.** If a patient under 25 presents with diabetes and lacks GADA, IA-2A, and ZnT8 antibodies, MODY should be on your differential.
*   **Preserved C-peptide is a clue.** In the context of hyperglycemia, a measurable or preserved C-peptide level suggests non-autoimmune etiology, though be aware that C-peptide can be normal early in Type 1 diabetes.
*   **Family history matters.** Look for the autosomal dominant pattern: multiple generations affected, often diagnosed young (under 25).
*   **Treatment is specific.** HNF1A-MODY patients often respond to low-dose sulfonylureas or GLP-1 agonists and may come off insulin entirely.
*   **Avoid SGLT2 inhibitors in HNF1A-MODY.** Due to the underlying genetic renal tubule dysfunction, these patients are at high risk for dehydration and ketoacidosis with this drug class.
*   **Use screening tools with caution.** The Exeter calculator is helpful, but remember its derivation population and interpret results with caution in diverse ethnic groups.

The bottom line is that monogenic diabetes is a chameleon, hiding in plain sight among our Type 1 and Type 2 diabetic populations. By keeping a low threshold for genetic testing—especially in young patients with negative antibodies and a suggestive family tree—we can transition patients from insulin regimens to oral or injectable non-insulin therapies, dramatically improving their quality of life and preventing iatrogenic harm.



# De-Prescribing Insulin: When Genetic Results Change Everything

## The Case That Makes You Rethink "Just Start Insulin"

A young adult walks into your clinic. HbA1c is 10.2%. Fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L. He's reporting polydipsia, polyphagia, and weight loss. Your instinct? Type 1 diabetes, start insulin. And that's exactly what the initial treating team did—basal insulin degludec plus metformin and a DPP-4 inhibitor.

But then the genetic testing came back. And everything changed.

This case is a masterclass in why we need to maintain a broad differential for young-onset diabetes. The patient had a BMI of 24.6 kg/m², no acanthosis nigricans, and critically—negative GADA and IA-2 antibodies. The family history was the real clue: maternal grandmother diagnosed in her 30s, mother in her late 20s, and a younger brother at age 20. That's an autosomal dominant pattern staring you in the face.

Genetic testing confirmed a novel pathogenic HNF1A variant (p.N270S). Within one month of the genetic diagnosis, insulin was stopped. The patient transitioned to a GLP-1 receptor agonist plus metformin. HbA1c dropped from 10.2% to 6.0% within six months—and has stayed below 6.5% since.

## MODY: The Diagnosis We're Probably Missing

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young accounts for an estimated 1–6% of young-onset diabetes cases. That's not rare. Yet how often are we actively screening for it? The problem is that MODY masquerades as type 1 or type 2 diabetes, and without genetic testing, we're essentially guessing.

HNF1A-MODY is the most common form, representing 30–65% of all MODY cases. These patients have a primary defect in pancreatic β-cell development and function due to mutations in a transcription factor expressed in the liver, kidneys, and pancreas. The clinical presentation overlaps significantly with both type 1 and type 2 diabetes, which makes clinical suspicion and systematic evaluation essential.

What should trigger your MODY radar? Think about it when you see diabetes diagnosed before age 25, a strong multigenerational family history, absent autoantibodies, and preserved C-peptide levels. The Exeter MODY probability calculator can help estimate pre-test probability, though it was derived from White European populations and may perform differently in other ethnic groups.

## The Treatment Paradigm Shift

Here's where this case gets practically useful for your next clinic day.

Historically, low-dose sulfonylureas have been first-line therapy for HNF1A-MODY. They work by stimulating insulin secretion via ATP-sensitive potassium channels. But there's a growing body of evidence supporting GLP-1 receptor agonists as a safer and equally effective alternative—particularly as β-cell function declines over time.

The data is compelling. A small randomized crossover trial demonstrated that liraglutide and glimepiride produced similar reductions in fasting plasma glucose and postprandial glucose fluctuations. The critical difference? GLP-1 receptor agonists carried significantly lower risk of hypoglycemia. For patients with HNF1A-MODY who are already prone to hypoglycemia due to their β-cell defect, this matters enormously.

In this case, the patient was transitioned from insulin to dulaglutide after genetic confirmation. The results speak for themselves: HbA1c dropped from 9.1% to 6.0% over six months, and long-term maintenance was achieved with metformin plus saxagliptin.

One important caution: SGLT2 inhibitors should be used with significant caution in HNF1A-MODY. These patients already have a reduced renal threshold for glucose reabsorption due to decreased SGLT2 expression under HNF1A transcriptional control. Case reports describe severe dehydration and even DKA in HNF1A-MODY patients on SGLT2 inhibitors. The risks likely outweigh the benefits.

## Why This Matters Beyond One Patient

Precision medicine isn't just an academic exercise. Getting the diagnosis right changes treatment, counseling, and family screening.

For this patient, the novel HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S) hadn't been reported in ClinVar or other databases. It was classified as likely pathogenic based on ACMG criteria—the variant affects a residue adjacent to known pathogenic variants, has a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), and segregates with diabetes in the family. This expands the known spectrum of HNF1A mutations and will help future patients with the same variant.

The quality-of-life implications are substantial. Instead of lifelong insulin therapy, this patient now takes oral medications and has excellent glycemic control. No daily injections. No hypoglycemia. And now the family can be screened appropriately.

## Key Take-Home Points

- Maintain a broad differential for young-onset diabetes: MODY accounts for 1–6% of cases and is frequently misdiagnosed as type 1 or type 2 diabetes.

- Clinical features suggesting HNF1A-MODY include early-onset diabetes, autosomal dominant family history, negative autoantibodies, preserved C-peptide, and glycosuria due to reduced renal threshold.

- GLP-1 receptor agonists offer a safe and effective alternative to sulfonylureas in HNF1A-MODY, with lower hypoglycemia risk—particularly valuable given the progressive β-cell dysfunction in these patients.

- Avoid SGLT2 inhibitors in HNF1A-MODY: these patients already have reduced SGLT2 expression and are at increased risk for dehydration and DKA.

- Genetic diagnosis enables treatment de-escalation: this patient went from insulin-dependent to oral maintenance therapy with excellent glycemic control, demonstrating how precision medicine directly improves patient outcomes.

The bottom line? When you see a young patient with diabetes and something feels atypical—negative antibodies, strong family history, modest BMI—think beyond the usual type 1 versus type 2 dichotomy and consider MODY. Genetic testing isn't just about confirming a diagnosis; it's about fundamentally changing management and improving your patient's life.



# HNF1A-MODY: A Therapeutic Minefield — Why SGLT2 Inhibitors Could Be Dangerous

## The Case That Stops You in Your Tracks

A young adult presents with an HbA1c of 10.2% and classic symptoms—polydipsia, polyphagia, weight loss. You're thinking new-onset type 1 diabetes, maybe type 2 in a overweight young adult. But then you notice something: glycosuria on routine testing, negative autoantibodies, and a family history that reads like a multigenerational diabetes pedigree. Maternal grandmother diagnosed in her 30s. Mother in her late 20s. Younger brother at age 20.

This wasn't type 1 or type 2 diabetes. Genetic testing confirmed a novel pathogenic variant in HNF1A—HNF1A-MODY. And here's the kicker: the treatment that would be reflexive for most young patients with diabetes could have been dangerous, even life-threatening.

## Why MODY Matters More Than You Think

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young accounts for an estimated 1–6% of young-onset diabetes cases, yet it remains dramatically underdiagnosed. Most patients are mislabeled as type 1 or type 2 diabetes, leading to inappropriate treatment regimens. HNF1A-MODY specifically represents 30–65% of all MODY cases, making it the most common monogenic diabetes variant.

The clinical hallmarks are subtle but learnable. These patients typically present before age 25 with autosomal dominant inheritance patterns. They lack features of insulin resistance—no acanthosis nigricans—and have negative autoantibodies. A key diagnostic clue: glycosuria at relatively modest glucose elevations. This occurs because HNF1A regulates SGLT2 expression in the renal tubules. When HNF1A is mutated, these patients have an inherently reduced renal threshold for glucose reabsorption. They spill glucose into their urine at lower serum concentrations than the general population.

## The Treatment Landscape: Old Dogma Meets New Evidence

Historically, low-dose sulfonylureas have been first-line therapy for HNF1A-MODY. These agents work beautifully because they bypass the defective glucose-sensing pathway and directly stimulate insulin secretion via ATP-sensitive potassium channels. Patients with HNF1A-MODY are exquisitely sensitive to sulfonylureas—sometimes dramatically so, which brings us to the first therapeutic caution: hypoglycemia risk is real.

Emerging evidence supports GLP-1 receptor agonists as a compelling alternative. A small randomized crossover trial demonstrated that liraglutide achieved equivalent glycemic control to glimepiride in HNF1A-MODY patients, but with significantly lower hypoglycemia risk. In the case presented here, the patient was transitioned from insulin to dulaglutide after genetic confirmation, with metformin as adjunctive therapy. The result? HbA1c dropped from 10.2% to 6.0% within six months—no insulin required.

This therapeutic pivot illustrates the power of precision medicine. Before genetic diagnosis, this patient was on basal insulin, metformin, and a DPP-4 inhibitor. After diagnosis, insulin was discontinued entirely. The patient achieved superior glycemic control on a GLP-1 receptor agonist and metformin.

## The SGLT2 Inhibitor Trap: A Prescription for Disaster

Here's where this case takes a critical turn. SGLT2 inhibitors have become a cornerstone of type 2 diabetes management, with proven cardiovascular and renal benefits. In a young patient with apparent type 2 diabetes, many clinicians would reflexively reach for an SGLT2 inhibitor—especially given the elevated triglycerides and potential metabolic syndrome features.

But in HNF1A-MODY, SGLT2 inhibitors are potentially dangerous.

The mechanism is elegantly sinister. HNF1A functions as a transcription factor controlling SGLT2 expression. Patients with HNF1A mutations already have reduced SGLT2 activity in the renal tubules—that's why they develop glycosuria at presentation. Adding pharmacologic SGLT2 inhibition on top of genetically reduced SGLT2 expression can precipitate severe volume depletion and dehydration. Case reports have documented euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis in HNF1A-MODY patients prescribed SGLT2 inhibitors, driven by the combined effects of impaired glucose reabsorption and increased glucagon secretion.

The glycosuria that served as a diagnostic clue becomes a therapeutic landmine. These patients are walking a tightrope of fluid balance, and SGLT2 inhibitors can push them off.

## When to Suspect MODY: A Practical Framework

This case reinforces several clinical decision points. The MODY probability calculator from the Exeter group can help risk-stratify, though it was derived from White European populations and may overestimate probability in other ethnic groups. Still, in this patient, it estimated a 62.4% probability of MODY—a strong signal.

Key features that should trigger genetic testing include early-onset diabetes with autosomal dominant family history, negative islet autoantibodies, preserved C-peptide secretion, and absence of insulin resistance features. The glucagon stimulation test in this patient showed a stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L—above the 0.60 nmol/L threshold suggesting preserved beta-cell function, though still insufficient for normal glycemic control.

Don't be fooled by BMI. While MODY patients classically have lower BMI than type 2 diabetes counterparts, rising obesity rates mean that overweight and obesity are increasingly common across all diabetes phenotypes. This patient had a BMI of 24.6 kg/m² and evidence of dyslipidemia—features that could easily steer clinicians toward a type 2 diabetes diagnosis.

## Key Take-Home Points

- Consider MODY in any patient with early-onset diabetes, autosomal dominant family history, negative autoantibodies, and preserved C-peptide—regardless of BMI.
- Glycosuria at presentation is a diagnostic clue and reflects genetically reduced SGLT2 expression in HNF1A-MODY.
- GLP-1 receptor agonists are emerging as effective alternatives to sulfonylureas with lower hypoglycemia risk in HNF1A-MODY.
- **SGLT2 inhibitors should be avoided in HNF1A-MODY due to risk of severe dehydration, volume depletion, and euglycemic DKA.**
- Genetic diagnosis transforms management—in this case, allowing discontinuation of insulin and achievement of excellent glycemic control on oral and injectable non-insulin therapy.

The message is clear: in young-onset diabetes, phenotype matters. A thorough family history, antibody testing, and low threshold for genetic screening can prevent therapeutic misadventures and open the door to precision medicine approaches that genuinely improve outcomes.



# Beyond Autoantibodies: The Role of C-Peptide in Diabetes Classification

We've all been there. A young patient walks in with newly diagnosed diabetes, and the clinical picture doesn't neatly fit into our mental framework for Type 1 or Type 2. Autoantibodies come back negative, but what does that really tell us? This case of a novel HNF1A-MODY mutation reminds us that C-peptide testing, particularly stimulated C-peptide, can be the decisive factor in navigating diagnostic uncertainty and ultimately changing management.

## The Case That Challenges Our Algorithm

A young adult presented with hyperglycemia and glycosuria found incidentally on an annual health examination. His HbA1c was 10.2% with a fasting glucose of 12.4 mmol/L, and he reported classic symptoms of polydipsia, polyphagia, and weight loss. Initial presentation suggested Type 1 diabetes, but several features gave pause: his BMI was 24.6 kg/m², he had no signs of insulin resistance like acanthosis nigricans, and perhaps most strikingly, he had a remarkable family history of early-onset diabetes spanning three generations. Autoantibodies (GADA and IA-2A) were negative, which narrowed the differential but didn't seal the diagnosis.

Here's where C-peptide entered the picture. A glucagon stimulation test revealed a fasting C-peptide of 0.39 nmol/L and a stimulated level of 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes. The stimulated value told us something crucial: this patient had preserved, albeit insufficient, beta-cell function. That single data point shifted the diagnostic trajectory away from classic Type 1 diabetes and toward a monogenic form, ultimately confirmed by genetic testing as a novel HNF1A mutation.

## Why C-Peptide Matters More Than We Think

C-peptide is often relegated to an afterthought in diabetes classification, but it's actually a window into pancreatic beta-cell physiology that we underutilize. When proinsulin is cleaved to form insulin, C-peptide is released in equimolar amounts. Unlike insulin, C-peptide isn't extracted by the liver and has a longer half-life, making it a reliable surrogate for endogenous insulin production.

The distinction between fasting and stimulated C-peptide is where clinical nuance lives. Fasting C-peptide tells us about basal insulin secretion, but it can be misleading in early diabetes or in patients with hyperglycemia-induced glucotoxicity. Stimulated C-peptide, whether via glucagon injection or a mixed-meal tolerance test, reveals the secretory reserve of the pancreas under stress. As demonstrated in this case, a fasting C-peptide of 0.39 nmol/L might raise concern for beta-cell failure, but the stimulated value of 0.83 nmol/L demonstrated that this pancreas could still respond, just not adequately.

## The Gray Zones That Keep Us Honest

Here's a clinical pearl that deserves emphasis: preserved C-peptide does not definitively rule out Type 1 diabetes, especially early in the disease course. In the honeymoon phase of Type 1 diabetes, which can last weeks to months or even longer, residual beta-cell function can produce measurable C-peptide. This is why the case report wisely notes that normal C-peptide values may be observed in early Type 1 diabetes, and subsequent monitoring is essential.

The threshold cited in this case deserves attention: a stimulated C-peptide level greater than 0.60 nmol/L generally indicates preserved endogenous insulin secretion. However, this isn't a binary switch. Values between 0.3 and 0.6 nmol/L represent a gray zone where clinical context becomes paramount. In this patient, a stimulated value of 0.83 nmol/L sat comfortably above the threshold, arguing against absolute insulin deficiency and prompting the search for alternative etiologies.

## Integrating C-Peptide with the Clinical Picture

C-peptide doesn't exist in a diagnostic vacuum. Its utility multiplies when interpreted alongside autoantibody status and clinical features. Negative autoantibodies with preserved C-peptide strongly argues against autoimmune Type 1 diabetes, but positive autoantibodies with preserved C-peptide doesn't automatically confirm Type 1, as case reports have described autoantibodies in MODY patients.

The family history in this case was a red flag that couldn't be ignored. Three generations of early-onset diabetes with an autosomal dominant pattern screamed monogenic etiology, and the Exeter MODY probability calculator estimated a 62.4% probability of MODY. But calculators and clinical features alone can't confirm the diagnosis; genetic testing remains the gold standard, and C-peptide helped justify the decision to pursue it.

## When to Order C-Peptide Testing

Practical decision-making about when to order C-peptide testing matters. Consider it in any patient with newly diagnosed diabetes where the classification is uncertain, particularly in young adults with atypical features. Negative autoantibodies should prompt C-peptide assessment. A strong family history of early-onset diabetes, absence of insulin resistance features, or modest insulin requirements in someone diagnosed as Type 1 should all trigger consideration of C-peptide testing.

The glucagon stimulation test used in this case involves measuring C-peptide at baseline and 6 minutes after intravenous glucagon injection. It's relatively quick and provides a snapshot of secretory reserve. Alternatively, mixed-meal tolerance testing offers a more physiological stimulus and is preferred in some centers. Fasting C-peptide alone has limitations but may be the only practical option in some settings.

## Clinical Take-Home Points

* A stimulated C-peptide greater than 0.60 nmol/L generally indicates preserved beta-cell function, but interpret this in clinical context.
* Preserved C-peptide early in the disease course does not exclude Type 1 diabetes; serial monitoring may be needed to clarify the diagnosis.
* Negative autoantibodies combined with preserved C-peptide should prompt consideration of monogenic diabetes, especially with a suggestive family history.
* C-peptide testing is underutilized but can be decisive in guiding genetic testing and ultimately changing management, as seen in this case where insulin was discontinued in favor of GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy after genetic confirmation of HNF1A-MODY.

This case reminds us that diabetes classification is often messier than textbooks suggest. C-peptide testing isn't just a confirmatory test; it's a strategic tool that can redirect the entire diagnostic approach. When autoantibodies come back negative and the clinical picture doesn't fit, let C-peptide be your next step rather than defaulting to a presumptive diagnosis that might be wrong. The right diagnosis, as this patient demonstrates, can mean the difference between lifelong insulin therapy and targeted oral medications that preserve beta-cell function and quality of life.



# Novel Genetic Variants in MODY: A Clinician's Guide to Variant Interpretation

You order a genetic test for suspected MODY, and the report comes back with a variant you've never heard of. It's not in ClinVar. It's not in the literature. Is this significant, or is it just noise? This scenario is increasingly common as next-generation sequencing becomes more accessible, and the case of a young man with a novel HNF1A mutation offers a masterclass in how to think about these findings.

## The Case That Stops You in Your Tracks

A young adult presents with an HbA1c of 10.2%, negative diabetes autoantibodies, and a family history that screams inheritance: maternal grandmother diagnosed in her 30s, mother in her late 20s, and a younger brother at age 20. Initial management followed standard type 2 diabetes protocols, but genetic testing revealed something unexpected: a heterozygous HNF1A variant designated p.N270S (c.809A>G), a mutation never before reported in any database.

Here's where it gets interesting. This variant wasn't just absent from databases—it sat in a critical location, adjacent to known pathogenic residues in the DNA-binding domain. And when the clinical team applied American College of Medical Genetics guidelines, this "unknown" variant was classified as likely pathogenic, fundamentally changing the patient's management trajectory.

## Why Your Patient's Variant Might Not Be in Any Database

The instinct when seeing an unreported variant is to question its relevance. But here's the reality: databases like ClinVar represent only a fraction of human genetic variation, and they carry significant population bias. Most entries come from White European populations. A variant absent from these databases may simply reflect that nobody has looked for it in your patient's demographic.

The HNF1A gene alone has over 1000 distinct variants catalogued in ClinVar, ranging from pathogenic to benign to variants of uncertain significance. That number continues to grow as sequencing becomes routine. The p.N270S variant in this case highlights an essential truth: every pathogenic variant was once "novel."

## The ACMG Framework: What Clinicians Need to Know

The American College of Medical Genetics established a structured approach to variant interpretation that moves beyond gut feelings. For the p.N270S variant, several lines of evidence converged. The variant segregates with diabetes in the family, meaning affected members carry it while unaffected members do not. It affects a highly conserved amino acid residue. The AlphaMissense score came back at 0.995, essentially screaming pathogenicity.

AlphaMissense is a machine learning tool that predicts whether a missense variant is pathogenic based on evolutionary conservation and protein structure. Scores approach 1.0 for likely pathogenic variants and approach 0.0 for likely benign ones. A score of 0.995 carries substantial weight.

The variant's location in the DNA-binding domain adds biological plausibility. HNF1A functions as a transcription factor, and mutations in this region directly impair the protein's ability to regulate downstream genes involved in insulin secretion and glucose transport.

## When to Suspect MODY and Pull the Trigger on Genetic Testing

This case reinforces classic MODY red flags: diagnosis before age 25, autosomal dominant family history, absent autoantibodies, and preserved C-peptide. But it also complicates the picture. The patient had a BMI of 24.6 kg/m² and metabolic syndrome features—characteristics traditionally associated with type 2 diabetes rather than MODY.

The Exeter MODY probability calculator estimated a 62.4% probability of MODY for this patient, but remember that this tool derives from White European data and may perform differently in other populations. Clinical judgment still matters.

The key insight is that MODY diagnostic criteria were met through combined clinical, biochemical, and genetic evidence. No single feature was definitive, but the constellation was compelling enough to justify testing.

## Treatment Implications: Why the Genetic Diagnosis Matters

Before genetic confirmation, this patient was on basal insulin plus metformin and saxagliptin. After confirming HNF1A-MODY, insulin was discontinued in favor of a GLP-1 receptor agonist. Within six months, HbA1c dropped from 10.2% to 6.0%.

HNF1A-MODY patients classically respond to low-dose sulfonylureas, but GLP-1 receptor agonists offer a compelling alternative with lower hypoglycemia risk. Critically, SGLT2 inhibitors should be avoided in HNF1A-MODY due to the already reduced renal threshold for glucose reabsorption—these patients carry inherent glycosuria, and further SGLT2 inhibition risks severe dehydration and euglycemic DKA.

## The Family Screening Cascade

Once a pathogenic variant is identified, first-degree relatives can be tested with targeted sequencing rather than full panels. This is faster, cheaper, and clinically actionable. For this patient's family—mother, brother, maternal grandmother—the p.N270S variant provides a molecular diagnosis that can guide surveillance and treatment before complications develop.

Genetic counseling is essential. Patients need to understand the inheritance pattern, implications for family planning, and that being a carrier doesn't guarantee disease manifestation, though penetrance in HNF1A-MODY exceeds 95% by age 50.

## Key Take-Home Points

- A variant absent from ClinVar or published literature is not automatically benign; it may simply be previously unreported, particularly in underrepresented populations
- ACMG guidelines provide a structured framework for variant interpretation that integrates multiple evidence streams, including segregation data, computational predictions, and functional domains
- AlphaMissense scores approaching 1.0 strongly suggest pathogenicity for missense variants and should factor into clinical decision-making
- HNF1A-MODY has specific treatment implications: sulfonylureas and GLP-1 receptor agonists are effective, while SGLT2 inhibitors should be avoided due to inherent glycosuria and dehydration risk
- Novel variant identification enables cascade family screening, transforming a molecular diagnosis into a family-wide prevention strategy

## The Bottom Line

Genetic testing is only as useful as our ability to interpret results. Novel variants will appear more frequently as sequencing expands, and clinicians need a framework for distinguishing pathogenic findings from incidental polymorphisms. The p.N270S variant in this case passed every test: computational predictions, functional domain location, family segregation, and clinical phenotype alignment. When the genetics align with the clinical picture, trust the data and adjust management accordingly.
