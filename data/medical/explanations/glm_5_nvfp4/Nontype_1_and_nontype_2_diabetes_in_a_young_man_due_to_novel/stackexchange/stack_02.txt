Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Clinical Pearls] What clinical features should raise suspicion for MODY rather than type 1 or type 2 diabetes in a young patient presenting with hyperglycemia?

### Clinical Features Raising Suspicion for MODY

Several key features in this case should prompt consideration of MODY over type 1 or type 2 diabetes:

**1. Autosomal dominant family history of early-onset diabetes**
This is the most critical clue. The patient had a striking multigenerational pedigree: maternal grandmother diagnosed in her 30s, mother in her late 20s, and younger brother at age 20. This vertical transmission pattern across three generations, with onset before age 25 in multiple affected members, is classic for MODY.

**2. Absence of autoimmune markers**
Negative glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA) and islet antigen-2 antibodies (IA-2A) argued against type 1 diabetes. While type 1 diabetes remains possible in antibody-negative patients, the combination of negative antibodies with preserved β-cell function strongly favors a non-autoimmune etiology.

**3. Preserved C-peptide secretion**
The glucagon stimulation test demonstrated a stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L, exceeding the 0.60 nmol/L threshold that indicates preserved endogenous insulin secretion. This finding, in the context of several months of symptomatic hyperglycemia and weight loss, would be atypical for type 1 diabetes, where β-cell destruction typically leads to rapid loss of insulin production.

**4. Lack of insulin resistance features**
Despite elevated HbA1c (10.2%), the patient had a BMI of 24.6 kg/m² and no acanthosis nigricans. While not definitive—since MODY patients can develop metabolic syndrome features—this phenotype contrasts with typical type 2 diabetes presentations, where obesity and insulin resistance signs are expected.

**5. Absence of diabetic ketoacidosis**
The patient presented with significant hyperglycemia (fasting glucose 12.4 mmol/L) and glycosuria without DKA, despite having symptoms of polydipsia, polyphagia, and weight loss. This pattern is consistent with the gradual β-cell dysfunction seen in HNF1A-MODY rather than the acute insulin deficiency of type 1 diabetes.

**Clinical Pearl**
Recognizing MODY has direct therapeutic implications: this patient was transitioned off insulin to a GLP-1 receptor agonist and metformin after genetic confirmation, achieving excellent glycemic control (HbA1c 6.0%) without hypoglycemia risk. Additionally, HNF1A-MODY patients have increased sensitivity to sulfonylureas but should avoid SGLT2 inhibitors due to underlying reduced SGLT2 expression and elevated risk of ketoacidosis.
