Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Morning Report] A 22-year-old man presents with HbA1c of 10.2%, negative diabetes autoantibodies, and a stimulated C-peptide of 0.83 nmol/L after glucagon administration. His mother was diagnosed with diabetes in her 20s and his maternal grandmother in her 30s. What is the significance of the glucagon stimulation test results, and how do they inform the differential diagnosis?

### Clinical Teaching Answer

The glucagon stimulation test in this patient provides critical information for differentiating diabetes classification. A stimulated C-peptide level of **0.83 nmol/L**—above the 0.60 nmol/L threshold for preserved endogenous insulin secretion—effectively excludes type 1 diabetes as the primary diagnosis and points toward a non-autoimmune etiology.

**Interpretation of the test:**
The glucagon stimulation test assesses residual β-cell function by provoking insulin release. In type 1 diabetes, autoimmune β-cell destruction typically results in a blunted or absent C-peptide response (often <0.2 nmol/L when stimulated). This patient's robust response indicates that his pancreatic β-cells retain functional capacity, despite the elevated HbA1c of 10.2%. Notably, the fasting C-peptide of 0.39 nmol/L rising to 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes demonstrates a dynamic secretory response that would be highly unusual in established type 1 diabetes.

**How this narrows the differential:**
When combined with **negative diabetes autoantibodies** (GADA, IA-2A) and a compelling **family history** of early-onset diabetes across three generations, the preserved C-peptide response strongly favors **maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY)** over type 1 diabetes. In HNF1A-MODY specifically, β-cell function declines progressively but remains detectable for years after diagnosis—distinct from the rapid autoimmune destruction seen in type 1 diabetes.

The test should be interpreted as **supportive rather than definitive**. As the case illustrates, patients with HNF1A-MODY can present with substantial hyperglycemia and symptoms of polydipsia, polyuria, and weight loss that mimic type 1 diabetes clinically. The glucagon stimulation test helped appropriately direct genetic testing, which ultimately confirmed the novel heterozygous *HNF1A* variant.

**Key teaching point:**
In any young patient with diabetes, negative autoantibodies, preserved C-peptide secretion, and an autosomal dominant family history should prompt consideration of MODY—recognizing that accurate classification fundamentally alters management, including the potential to discontinue insulin therapy in favor of oral agents or GLP-1 receptor agonists, as demonstrated in this case.
