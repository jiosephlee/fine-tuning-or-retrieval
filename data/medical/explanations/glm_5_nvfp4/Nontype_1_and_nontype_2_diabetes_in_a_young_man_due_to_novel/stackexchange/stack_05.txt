Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A"

### [Clinical Pearls] Why are SGLT2 inhibitors potentially harmful in patients with HNF1A-MODY?

**Clinical Teaching Answer**

SGLT2 inhibitors are potentially harmful in HNF1A-MODY because these patients already have genetically reduced SGLT2 expression and function in the renal proximal tubules, making pharmacological inhibition of the residual transporter activity particularly dangerous.

**Pathophysiology:** HNF1A is a transcription factor that regulates SGLT2 gene expression in the kidneys. In patients with HNF1A-MODY, loss-of-function mutations lead to decreased SGLT2 expression, which results in a lower renal threshold for glucose reabsorption. This explains why glycosuria occurs at relatively lower blood glucose concentrations in these patients—a clinical feature noted in the case presentation, where the patient presented with glycosuria despite the absence of severe hyperglycemia or diabetic ketoacidosis.

**Clinical Consequence of SGLT2 Inhibitor Use:** When an SGLT2 inhibitor is administered to a patient with HNF1A-MODY, the drug inhibits the already diminished pool of functional SGLT2 transporters. This can lead to excessive glucosuria, causing profound volume depletion, dehydration, and potentially vascular collapse. As described in the case report's discussion, HNF1A-MODY patients treated with SGLT2 inhibitors are at increased risk for severe dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis, along with genital infections. The risks of these agents in this specific population generally outweigh their potential glycemic benefits.

**Clinical Implication:** This is a prime example of precision medicine in diabetes management. While SGLT2 inhibitors are first-line agents for many patients with type 2 diabetes due to their cardiovascular and renal benefits, they should be avoided in HNF1A-MODY. Instead, as demonstrated in this case, patients with HNF1A-MODY respond well to low-dose sulfonylureas or GLP-1 receptor agonists, which enhance insulin secretion through mechanisms independent of the HNF1A pathway defect.
