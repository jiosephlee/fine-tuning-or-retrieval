Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Workup and Pre-Procedural Imaging

The evaluation of this patient prior to his third transcatheter aortic valve intervention illustrates the critical importance of systematic, multimodality imaging in complex structural heart disease. Following his treatment for prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) caused by *Enterococcus faecalis*, the patient developed recurrent symptoms and worsening prosthetic valve function, necessitating a comprehensive diagnostic workup to establish candidacy for repeat intervention.

Echocardiography served as the primary tool for initial assessment and ongoing surveillance. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) performed on admission in June 2024 revealed the hallmarks of severe bioprosthetic valve dysfunction: grade 2–3 intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation, a mean transvalvular gradient of 49 mm Hg, and a markedly reduced aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.5 cm². For medical students learning to interpret prosthetic valve function, it is essential to understand that these parameters do not exist in isolation. The AVA was calculated using the continuity equation, which relates flow through the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) to flow across the prosthetic valve. In the setting of a transcatheter valve, this calculation requires careful measurement of the LVOT diameter and Doppler-derived velocity-time integrals. A mean gradient exceeding 40 mm Hg in conjunction with an AVA below 1.0 cm² signifies severe prosthetic stenosis, while the concomitant regurgitation indicated mixed dysfunction—a particularly unfavorable hemodynamic profile.

Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) played a distinct and complementary role. While TTE provided hemodynamic data, TEE was essential for excluding active infective endocarditis—a critical step before proceeding with any re-intervention. In patients with prior PVE, the risk of persistent or recurrent infection must be definitively ruled out, as implanting a new prosthesis in an infected field carries catastrophic consequences. TEE in this patient confirmed the absence of vegetations, abscesses, or paravalvular complications, corroborating the clinical assessment that antimicrobial therapy had successfully eradicated the infection.

The interpretation of hemodynamic parameters in this case requires nuanced understanding. The mean transvalvular gradient of 49 mm Hg represented severe obstruction, but clinicians must recognize that gradients are flow-dependent. The patient's left ventricular ejection fraction had recovered to 66%, indicating adequate flow conditions for reliable gradient measurement. In low-output states, gradients may underestimate stenosis severity, making the calculated AVA and Doppler velocity index (DVI) valuable adjuncts. The DVI, the ratio of LVOT velocity-time integral to aortic velocity-time integral, provides a dimensionless measure of valve function; values below 0.25 indicate significant stenosis. Post-procedurally, this patient's DVI of 0.24 reflected severe prosthesis-patient mismatch—a consequence of the triple-stented anatomy that will be discussed further.

Multislice computed tomography (MSCT) was the cornerstone of procedural planning, transforming the case from a seemingly impossible technical challenge to a feasible intervention. MSCT revealed a minimum lumen diameter of only 13.7 mm within the previously implanted Evolut prosthesis—confirming severe hemodynamic compromise and establishing the anatomical target for the third valve. Beyond measuring the landing zone, CT allowed detailed analysis of the complex triple-stented anatomy. The patient had a 27 mm Lotus valve implanted in 2014, followed by a 23 mm Evolut valve in 2022. Understanding how these two frames interacted—their relative positions, expansion characteristics, and overlap patterns—was essential for determining where a third valve could be safely anchored and how it would expand.

Coronary obstruction risk assessment represented perhaps the most critical aspect of CT planning. In TAV-in-TAV procedures, the leaflets of the failed prosthesis are pushed outward against the frame when a new valve is deployed. This creates a "neoskirt" of tissue that can obstruct coronary ostia if they lie at a similar height. With three overlapping valve frames, this risk is magnified substantially. MSCT enabled measurement of coronary ostial height relative to the valve landing zone, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, and valve-to-coronary distance. These measurements guided the decision to implant the third valve at a relatively high position—aligning it with the Evolut valve leaflets—to minimize neoskirt formation below the coronary ostia. This strategy preserved coronary flow and maintained future coronary access without requiring coronary protection devices during the procedure.

The selection of the Acurate neo2 prosthesis was directly informed by CT findings. This valve's supra-annular leaflet positioning, relatively low frame height, and favorable hemodynamic profile in narrow anatomies made it suitable for this challenging configuration. Additionally, its design facilitated commissural alignment—a consideration for preserving coronary access through the stent cells of all three frames.

Surgical risk stratification completed the pre-procedural evaluation. The patient's EuroSCORE II of 27.36% indicated a very high surgical mortality risk. This score, combined with his multiple comorbidities—COPD, type 2 diabetes mellitus, three-vessel coronary artery disease with prior interventions, third-degree AV block requiring permanent pacemaker, and recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding—rendered open surgery prohibitive. Furthermore, the presence of two well-integrated transcatheter valves meant that surgical explantation would require total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement, dramatically increasing operative complexity. The heart team's multidisciplinary review confirmed that transcatheter intervention, despite its technical challenges, represented the only viable option for this patient.

This case demonstrates that successful outcomes in complex structural valve interventions depend fundamentally on meticulous pre-procedural imaging and planning. For trainees, the lesson is clear: the procedure itself represents only the final step in a comprehensive diagnostic process that defines feasibility, guides strategy, and anticipates complications before they occur.
