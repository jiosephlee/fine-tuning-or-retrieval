Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Management] When planning a valve-in-valve TAVI procedure, what are the key CT-derived measurements used to assess the risk of coronary artery obstruction, and what anatomical features are most concerning?

**Clinical Teaching Answer**

When planning a valve-in-valve TAVI procedure, the key CT-derived measurements for assessing coronary artery obstruction risk include **coronary ostial height**, **valve-to-coronary distance**, and **sinus of Valsalva dimensions**. Additionally, the relationship between the anticipated neo-skirt—the cylinder created by the expanded leaflets of the existing prosthesis pinned against the new valve frame—and the coronary ostia must be carefully evaluated.

Coronary ostial height is measured from the aortic annulus to the coronary ostium. Low-lying ostia, generally defined as less than 10–12 mm above the annulus, are at higher risk because the prosthetic valve's skirt or pinned leaflets may extend above the ostial level, sealing the artery against the aortic wall. Valve-to-coronary distance measures the linear distance from the coronary ostium to the existing prosthetic frame; a value less than 4 mm is considered high-risk, as there is insufficient clearance for flow once the new valve is deployed and the old leaflets are displaced outward. Sinus of Valsalva width and depth are also critical—narrow or shallow sinuses limit the space available for leaflet displacement and increase the likelihood that the pinned leaflets will obstruct the coronaries.

In this patient's case, preprocedural MSCT was used to evaluate coronary ostial height, sinus dimensions, and valve-to-coronary distance, with particular attention to the interaction between the two previously implanted valve frames. The case report highlights that the team assessed "the relationship between the anticipated neo-skirt and coronary ostia" and determined that high implantation of the third valve would preserve coronary flow. Notably, the procedure was performed without coronary protection, indicating that CT measurements confirmed sufficient anatomical clearance.

The most concerning anatomical features are low coronary ostia, narrow sinuses, short valve-to-coronary distances, and—in repeat valve-in-valve cases—the cumulative effect of multiple overlapping stent frames, which can further restrict coronary access. In such complex anatomies, adjunctive techniques such as chimney stenting, Bioprosthetic or Native Aortic Scallop Intentional Laceration to Prevent Iatrogenic Coronary Artery Obstruction (BASILICA), or prophylactic coronary protection with guide wires and balloons may be necessary.
