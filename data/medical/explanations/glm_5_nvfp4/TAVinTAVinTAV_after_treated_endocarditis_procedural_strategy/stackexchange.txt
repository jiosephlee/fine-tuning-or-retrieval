Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

### [Differential Diagnosis] A patient with a prosthetic aortic valve presents with progressive dyspnea and elevated transvalvular gradients on echocardiography. What are the major causes of prosthetic valve dysfunction, and what imaging modalities help differentiate between them?

### Teaching Answer

The major causes of prosthetic valve dysfunction fall into structural and non-structural categories. **Structural valve deterioration (SVD)** represents the most common cause of late bioprosthetic failure, characterized by leaflet calcification, fibrosis, and tears leading to progressive stenosis or regurgitation. **Prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE)** can cause vegetations, leaflet destruction, and paravalvular abscesses, resulting in acute or subacute valve dysfunction. **Thrombosis** and **pannus formation** may restrict leaflet motion, while **patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM)** produces elevated gradients without true valve pathology. Paravalvular leaks, typically from incomplete sealing or dehiscence, cause regurgitation rather than stenosis.

In this case, the patient demonstrated multiple mechanisms of dysfunction sequentially. The initial Lotus valve failure in 2022—with a mean gradient of 83 mm Hg—represented classic structural degeneration after 8 years. The subsequent Evolut prosthesis dysfunction involved both **endocarditis-related injury** ( Enterococcus faecalis PVE at 3 months) and **post-infectious structural deterioration**, ultimately producing combined stenosis (mean gradient 49 mm Hg) and moderate-to-severe regurgitation.

**Imaging modalities for differentiation:**

**Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE)** serves as the initial screening tool, quantifying transvalvular gradients, valve area, and regurgitation severity. However, TTE has limited sensitivity for vegetations and paravalvular complications.

**Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE)** is essential when PVE is suspected, offering superior detection of vegetations, abscesses, and paravalvular leaks. In this patient, TEE confirmed resolution of infection prior to the third intervention.

**Multislice cardiac CT (MSCT)** is indispensable for comprehensive evaluation and procedural planning. CT assesses leaflet mobility, thrombus versus calcification, valve frame expansion, and—critically for reintervention—coronary ostial height, sinus dimensions, and valve-to-coronary distances. In this case, MSCT demonstrated a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm within the Evolut frame, confirmed absence of active infection, and guided commissural alignment strategy for the third valve.

**Fluoroscopy** can differentiate thrombus (mobile, resolves with anticoagulation) from pannus (fixed obstruction), though this was not required in the present case.

The key teaching point: when evaluating prosthetic valve dysfunction, always consider the temporal relationship to implantation, infectious triggers, and use multimodal imaging—TTE for screening, TEE for infectious or paravalvular pathology, and CT for anatomic characterization and procedural planning.

### [Clinical Pearls] What is prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM) following aortic valve replacement, and what echocardiographic parameters are used to define and classify its severity?

**Prosthesis-Patient Mismatch (PPM)**

Prosthesis-patient mismatch occurs when the effective orifice area of a prosthetic valve is too small relative to the patient's body surface area, resulting in abnormally high transvalvular gradients despite normal prosthesis function. This creates a functional stenosis that can limit symptomatic improvement and contribute to adverse long-term outcomes, including persistent left ventricular hypertrophy, heart failure, and reduced survival.

**Echocardiographic Definition and Classification**

PPM is defined and classified using the **indexed aortic valve area (AVAi)**, calculated as the aortic valve area divided by the patient's body surface area:

| Severity | Indexed AVA (cm²/m²) |
|----------|---------------------|
| Moderate PPM | 0.65 – 0.85 |
| Severe PPM | ≤ 0.65 |

A complementary parameter is the **Doppler velocity index (DVI)**, the ratio of the left ventricular outflow tract velocity-time integral to the aortic velocity-time integral. A DVI ≤ 0.25 suggests significant obstruction.

**Application to This Case**

Following the third transcatheter valve implantation, our patient had an indexed AVA of 0.4 cm²/m² and a DVI of 0.24, meeting criteria for **severe PPM**. The initial mean gradient of 67 mm Hg reflected this mismatch. Notably, at 1-month and 1-year follow-up, the mean gradient improved to 26–27 mm Hg with an AVA of 1.1 cm², likely due to continued valve expansion, left ventricular remodeling, and adaptation—a phenomenon sometimes observed as the prosthesis settles.

This case illustrates that PPM is a critical consideration in valve-in-valve procedures, where overlapping stent frames and restricted leaflet mobility can progressively reduce the effective orifice area with each successive intervention.

### [Morning Report] A patient with a transcatheter aortic valve presents with fever and new-onset heart failure. What are the modified Duke criteria for diagnosing prosthetic valve endocarditis, and what additional imaging should be obtained?

**Modified Duke Criteria for Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis**

The modified Duke criteria remain the diagnostic standard for infective endocarditis and are particularly important when evaluating a patient with a prosthetic valve who presents with fever and new heart failure—exactly the scenario described in this case.

**Definite endocarditis** requires either:
- **Pathologic criteria**: Histologic or microbiologic evidence of infection in vegetation or intracardiac abscess
- **Clinical criteria**: 2 major criteria, OR 1 major + 3 minor criteria, OR 5 minor criteria

**Major criteria** include:
1. **Microbiologic**: Positive blood cultures for typical organisms (including *Enterococcus faecalis*, as in this case) from two separate draws, or a single positive culture for *Coxiella burnetii*
2. **Endocardial involvement**: Echocardiographic evidence of vegetation, abscess, new prosthetic valve dehiscence, or new valvular regurgitation. For prosthetic valves, **transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) is essential**—transthoracic echo has significantly lower sensitivity due to acoustic shadowing from the prosthesis

**Minor criteria** include:
- Predisposing cardiac condition (prosthetic valve is a key risk factor)
- Fever ≥38°C
- Vascular phenomena (embolic events, mycotic aneurysm)
- Immunologic phenomena (glomerulonephritis, Osler nodes, Roth spots)
- Microbiologic evidence not meeting major criteria

In this patient, the combination of positive blood cultures for *E. faecalis* (major), fever (minor), and predisposing prosthetic valve (minor) met clinical criteria for definite PVE.

**Essential Additional Imaging**

For suspected prosthetic valve endocarditis, obtain:

1. **Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE)** – Mandatory for all suspected PVE. TEE provides superior visualization of vegetations, abscesses, and paravalvular complications that TTE may miss.

2. **Multislice CT (MSCT)** – Recommended to assess for perivalvular complications including abscesses, pseudoaneurysms, and prosthetic valve dehiscence.

3. **18F-FDG PET/CT or radiolabeled leukocyte SPECT/CT** – Now incorporated into the ESC guidelines as an additional major criterion for prosthetic valve endocarditis. Abnormal metabolic activity around the prosthesis increases diagnostic sensitivity, particularly when echocardiography is equivocal.

In this case, the diagnosis was confirmed with blood cultures and echocardiography, and the patient received appropriate antimicrobial therapy with subsequent imaging (TEE and MSCT) confirming resolution before consideration of re-intervention.

### [Management] What is the recommended antimicrobial treatment regimen for prosthetic valve endocarditis caused by Enterococcus faecalis, including the typical duration and preferred antibiotic agents?

## Clinical Teaching Answer

For prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) caused by *Enterococcus faecalis*, the recommended treatment regimen generally consists of **6 weeks of intravenous ampicillin combined with an aminoglycoside** (typically gentamicin), provided the isolate is susceptible to both agents.

### Key Treatment Principles

**Preferred regimen for penicillin-susceptible strains:**
- **Ampicillin** 2 g IV every 4 hours **plus gentamicin** 1 mg/kg IV every 8 hours (with dose adjustments for renal function)
- Duration: **6 weeks** for prosthetic valve involvement

This combination provides synergistic bactericidal activity, which is essential for enterococcal endocarditis given the inherent resistance of enterococci to cell-wall active agents alone.

**Alternative regimens:**
- If high-level gentamicin resistance is present, **streptomycin** may be substituted if susceptible
- For penicillin-allergic patients or resistant strains, **vancomycin** plus gentamicin is the alternative, though efficacy may be inferior

### Case Application

In this patient, blood cultures grew *E. faecalis*, and he received **6 weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based therapy** followed by oral step-down treatment per infectious disease recommendations. This approach aligns with guideline-directed therapy for PVE. Notably, the case emphasizes that successful completion of antimicrobial therapy with documented resolution of infection (confirmed by repeat blood cultures and transoesophageal echocardiography) was essential before proceeding with the third transcatheter intervention.

### Important Considerations

Enterococcal PVE carries higher relapse rates than streptococcal endocarditis, making the full 6-week parenteral course critical. Monitoring for aminoglycoside toxicity (nephrotoxicity, ototoxicity) is required, and trough levels should be followed. In complex cases involving prosthetic material, infectious disease consultation is mandatory to tailor therapy to susceptibility profiles and guide decisions regarding oral suppressive therapy.

### [Management] When planning a valve-in-valve TAVI procedure, what are the key CT-derived measurements used to assess the risk of coronary artery obstruction, and what anatomical features are most concerning?

**Clinical Teaching Answer**

When planning a valve-in-valve TAVI procedure, the key CT-derived measurements for assessing coronary artery obstruction risk include **coronary ostial height**, **valve-to-coronary distance**, and **sinus of Valsalva dimensions**. Additionally, the relationship between the anticipated neo-skirt—the cylinder created by the expanded leaflets of the existing prosthesis pinned against the new valve frame—and the coronary ostia must be carefully evaluated.

Coronary ostial height is measured from the aortic annulus to the coronary ostium. Low-lying ostia, generally defined as less than 10–12 mm above the annulus, are at higher risk because the prosthetic valve's skirt or pinned leaflets may extend above the ostial level, sealing the artery against the aortic wall. Valve-to-coronary distance measures the linear distance from the coronary ostium to the existing prosthetic frame; a value less than 4 mm is considered high-risk, as there is insufficient clearance for flow once the new valve is deployed and the old leaflets are displaced outward. Sinus of Valsalva width and depth are also critical—narrow or shallow sinuses limit the space available for leaflet displacement and increase the likelihood that the pinned leaflets will obstruct the coronaries.

In this patient's case, preprocedural MSCT was used to evaluate coronary ostial height, sinus dimensions, and valve-to-coronary distance, with particular attention to the interaction between the two previously implanted valve frames. The case report highlights that the team assessed "the relationship between the anticipated neo-skirt and coronary ostia" and determined that high implantation of the third valve would preserve coronary flow. Notably, the procedure was performed without coronary protection, indicating that CT measurements confirmed sufficient anatomical clearance.

The most concerning anatomical features are low coronary ostia, narrow sinuses, short valve-to-coronary distances, and—in repeat valve-in-valve cases—the cumulative effect of multiple overlapping stent frames, which can further restrict coronary access. In such complex anatomies, adjunctive techniques such as chimney stenting, Bioprosthetic or Native Aortic Scallop Intentional Laceration to Prevent Iatrogenic Coronary Artery Obstruction (BASILICA), or prophylactic coronary protection with guide wires and balloons may be necessary.

### [Pathophysiology] What are the primary mechanisms of structural valve deterioration in bioprosthetic heart valves, and how does a history of infective endocarditis accelerate this process?

### Clinical Teaching Answer

Structural valve deterioration (SVD) in bioprosthetic valves results from two interrelated processes: **progressive calcification** and **collagen fiber degradation**.

**Primary Mechanisms of Structural Valve Deterioration:**

1. **Intrinsic calcification** — The dominant mechanism. Glutaraldehyde fixation, while necessary for immunogenicity reduction, leaves residual aldehyde groups that attract calcium phosphate crystals. Mechanical stress at hinge points and leaflet flexion zones accelerates this process, leading to leaflet stiffening, restricted mobility, and ultimately stenosis.

2. **Collagen fatigue and fragmentation** — Repetitive cyclic loading causes progressive disruption of the collagen matrix architecture. This manifests as leaflet thinning, tears, or perforation, resulting in regurgitation.

3. **Pannus ingrowth** — Fibrous tissue proliferation from the host can encroach upon the leaflet hinges and margins, further restricting motion.

The timeline in our patient illustrates this well: his Lotus valve functioned for approximately 7 years before developing severe stenosis (mean gradient 83 mm Hg), consistent with the typical durability curve of bioprosthetic valves in this age group.

**How Infective Endocarditis Accelerates Deterioration:**

Prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) dramatically hastens SVD through several mechanisms:

1. **Direct microbial and inflammatory destruction** — Bacterial adherence to the prosthetic surface triggers intense inflammation, with activated neutrophils and macrophages releasing proteolytic enzymes that digest collagen and extracellular matrix. Enterococcus faecalis, as seen in our patient, is particularly adept at adhering to prosthetic material.

2. **Vegetation-mediated mechanical disruption** — Vegetations cause physical distortion of leaflets, impeding coaptation and accelerating wear.

3. **Post-infectious fibrosis** — Even after successful antimicrobial therapy, the healing response leaves behind dense fibrotic scarring. In this case, the discussion section notes "leaflet fibrosis from prior degeneration and probable mechanical restriction following infection" — a critical insight. The treated PVE left the Evolut leaflets thickened and stiff, contributing to the rapid restenosis observed.

4. **Accelerated dystrophic calcification** — Necrotic tissue and inflammatory debris serve as a nidus for accelerated calcium deposition, fundamentally altering the degeneration trajectory.

In our patient, the temporal relationship is telling: within months of the treated Enterococcus endocarditis, the Evolut prosthesis developed severe dysfunction with a mean gradient of 49 mm Hg and an aortic valve area of only 0.5 cm². This rapid deterioration after PVE exemplifies how infection converts a gradual degenerative process into an acute or subacute failure mode.

**Key Teaching Point:** Following successful treatment of PVE, patients require heightened surveillance for accelerated SVD, as the structural integrity of the valve has been permanently compromised even in the absence of active infection.

### [Clinical Pearls] What is commissural alignment in transcatheter aortic valve implantation, and why is this consideration important for preserving future coronary access?

### Clinical Teaching Answer

**Commissural alignment** in transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) refers to the intentional orientation of the new valve's commissural posts—the structural components where leaflets attach—to align with those of a previously implanted prosthesis or with the native aortic valve commissures. This is achieved through preprocedural CT planning and controlled rotational deployment using fluoroscopic landmarks.

This consideration is critical for preserving future coronary access for several reasons:

**Anatomical rationale.** When a transcatheter valve is deployed, its stent frame creates a cylindrical mesh with commissural posts that project into the sinus of Valsalva. If these posts are misaligned relative to the coronary ostia, they can obstruct or severely complicate catheter engagement of the coronaries—particularly the left coronary artery, given its typical anatomical position.

**Clinical implications in this case.** Our patient had three-vessel coronary disease with multiple prior percutaneous interventions, making preserved coronary access essential for future management. The third valve (Acurate neo2) was deliberately aligned with the Evolut valve leaflets based on CT modelling. This alignment ensured that the commissural posts of all three overlapping valve frames remained oriented away from the coronary ostia, maintaining a clear pathway for coronary catheterization.

**Heightened importance in redo procedures.** As the case illustrates, TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedures create a cumulative "mesh burden" from multiple overlapping stent frames. Misalignment compounds with each intervention—misaligned commissures from a first valve become a fixed obstacle that subsequent valves cannot fully correct. Early attention to commissural alignment at the time of initial valve implantation is therefore a key principle in lifetime management of aortic valve disease.

### [Management] According to current European Society of Cardiology guidelines, what is the recommended management strategy for prosthetic valve endocarditis, and in what circumstances might a transcatheter approach be considered as an alternative to surgical valve replacement?

### Clinical Teaching Answer

According to the 2025 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines for the management of valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis, **surgical valve replacement remains the standard of care for prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE)**, particularly in cases complicated by heart failure, uncontrolled infection, perivalvular extension, or persistent bacteremia. Surgery allows for complete debridement of infected tissue, removal of the infected prosthesis, and definitive reconstruction.

However, the guidelines emphasize that **management must be individualized** based on three key factors: infection control status, surgical risk profile, and anatomical feasibility. A transcatheter approach may be considered as an alternative in select circumstances:

1. **Prohibitive surgical risk** — as in this case, where the patient had a EuroSCORE II of 27.36% along with significant comorbidities including COPD, diabetes, and recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding.

2. **Complex anatomical considerations** — this patient had two previously implanted transcatheter prostheses with deep stent integration into the aortic wall. Surgical explantation would have required total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement, rendering the procedure technically hazardous.

3. **Documented resolution of active infection** — critical prerequisites before any transcatheter intervention. In this case, the patient had completed a full course of targeted antimicrobial therapy, with negative blood cultures, and both transesophageal echocardiography and CT confirming absence of vegetations or paravalvular complications.

4. **CT-confirmed anatomical feasibility** — preprocedural imaging must demonstrate that a transcatheter strategy can achieve adequate valve function while preserving coronary access and minimizing risks such as coronary obstruction.

This case exemplifies the importance of a **multidisciplinary heart team approach** in navigating these complex decisions. The decision to proceed with a third transcatheter intervention (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV) was made only after confirming infection resolution and determining that surgical risk was prohibitive. At 1-year follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic with preserved valve function and no recurrence of endocarditis, supporting the viability of this strategy in carefully selected patients.
