# TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV: Pushing the Technical Envelope of Transcatheter Redo

What happens when you've already implanted two transcatheter aortic valves and your patient comes back with severe prosthetic degeneration—after surviving prosthetic valve endocarditis? You implant a third one. Welcome to the world of TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV.

This case from Merkulov and colleagues is a striking example of how far transcatheter technology has pushed the boundaries of what's technically feasible. A man in his late 70s, initially treated with a Lotus valve in 2014, underwent TAV-in-TAV with an Evolut prosthesis in 2022 for structural valve deterioration. Three months later, he developed Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis. After successful antimicrobial therapy and confirmed resolution of infection, he presented again in 2024 with severe restenosis and moderate-to-severe regurgitation. Mean gradient was 49 mm Hg, aortic valve area had collapsed to 0.5 cm². The heart team's solution: a third transcatheter valve, making him one of the rare documented cases of TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV post-endocarditis.

## The Technical Gauntlet

Let's be clear—this is not your standard redo procedure. With three overlapping bioprosthetic frames, the margin for error shrinks dramatically. The primary concerns stack up quickly: coronary artery obstruction risk, catastrophic prosthesis-patient mismatch, and the nightmare scenario of a neo-skirt forming from the cumulative leaflet burden that could occlude coronary ostia.

The team's pre-procedural CT planning was exhaustive. They measured coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, and critically, the valve-to-coronary distance. The minimum lumen diameter in the Evolut prosthesis was 13.7 mm—confirming severe haemodynamic compromise but also providing a target for sizing the third valve.

## Strategic High Implantation: The Key Move

Here's where this case gets really clever. The team deliberately implanted the Acurate neo2 high—at the level of the Evolut valve leaflets. Why does this matter? By positioning the device to fix the pre-existing leaflets against the stent frame rather than below them, they prevented additional inward leaflet folding that would have further reduced the effective orifice area. This approach also minimised neo-skirt formation below the coronary ostia, preserving both immediate coronary flow and future coronary access.

Valve selection wasn't arbitrary either. The Acurate neo2 was chosen for its supra-annular leaflet positioning, relatively low frame height, and favourable haemodynamic performance in narrow anatomies. Critically, its design supports commissural alignment—a consideration that becomes exponentially more important when you're stacking three valve frames.

## The Gradient Story: Immediate vs. Follow-up

Here's something that should catch your attention: immediately post-procedure, the mean gradient was 67 mm Hg with an aortic valve area of 0.9 cm²—findings consistent with severe prosthesis-patient mismatch. But hold judgment. At one month, the mean gradient had dropped to 27 mm Hg and AVA improved to 1.1 cm². By one year, these parameters held stable at 26 mm Hg and 1.1 cm² respectively.

What explains this early improvement? The authors attribute it to a combination of optimised commissural alignment, effective post-dilation, and partial mechanical expansion of the prior Evolut frame. High-pressure balloon post-dilation likely contributed to further radial expansion of the Evolut, enhancing overall valve function. The left ventricle also remodelled favourably, with reduced chamber volumes documented on follow-up imaging.

## The Endocarditis Question

Let's address the elephant in the room. Current ESC guidelines recommend surgical valve replacement for prosthetic valve endocarditis, particularly with heart failure or uncontrolled infection. But this patient's EuroSCORE II was 27.36%, with COPD, diabetes, prior GI bleeding, and two previously implanted transcatheter prostheses deeply integrated into the aortic wall. Surgical explantation would have required total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement.

The critical distinction here: the endocarditis was successfully treated. Blood cultures were negative, transoesophageal echocardiography showed no vegetations, and CT confirmed no paravalvular complications. The patient had completed antimicrobial therapy with no ongoing suppressive antibiotics needed. Once infection was definitively resolved, the procedural decision-making shifted to standard considerations of anatomical feasibility and surgical risk.

## Where Do We Go From Here?

This case is a harbinger of what's coming. With expanding TAVI indications and improving long-term survival, we're going to see more patients outliving their first—and second—transcatheter valves. Each reintervention compounds the technical complexity: frame layering dynamics, coronary access challenges, and the relentless squeeze on effective orifice area.

The authors make an essential point about lifetime management strategies. These conversations need to happen at the time of the first intervention, especially in younger patients. Valve selection, implantation depth, and commissural alignment aren't just procedural details—they're setting up (or foreclosing) future options.

**Key Take-Home Messages:**

- TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV is technically feasible with meticulous CT-guided planning, even after treated prosthetic valve endocarditis
- High implantation strategy reduces neo-skirt formation and preserves coronary access by fixing pre-existing leaflets against the stent frame
- Post-dilation with high-pressure balloons may improve haemodynamics through partial frame expansion of prior valves
- Immediate post-procedural gradients may not predict follow-up performance; watch for early remodelling and gradient improvement
- Valve selection must account for commissural alignment capability and frame height in triple-stented anatomy
- Prosthetic valve endocarditis that has been definitively treated does not preclude transcatheter reintervention in prohibitive surgical candidates

At one-year follow-up, this patient remained asymptomatic with preserved valve function—proof that even in the most anatomically hostile terrain, careful strategy and imaging-guided execution can push the boundaries of what's possible. Whether this represents sustainable long-term success or simply buying time in an increasingly complex anatomical situation remains to be seen, but this case demonstrates that the ceiling of transcatheter reintervention continues to rise.



# TAVI After Treated Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis: When Guidelines Meet Reality

## The Case That Makes You Pause

Picture this: a man in his late 70s sits in front of you with severe prosthetic valve dysfunction. His mean gradient is 49 mm Hg, his aortic valve area is 0.5 cm², and he can barely walk 200 meters without chest tightness and dyspnea. Standard stuff, right? Except he's already had two transcatheter aortic valves implanted, and in between them, he survived prosthetic valve endocarditis. Now you're considering a third transcatheter valve—TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV—in a patient with a history of treated PVE. The 2025 ESC guidelines are clear: surgical valve replacement is the recommended strategy for PVE. But what happens when surgical risk is prohibitive, infection is definitively resolved, and anatomy is so hostile that surgery would require total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement?

This case forces us to confront an uncomfortable truth about modern structural heart disease: guidelines provide frameworks, not scripts. And as TAVI volumes explode and patients outlive their first prosthesis, we're going to see more of these scenarios whether we're ready or not.

## The Clinical Journey

This patient's timeline reads like a case study in structural valve disease progression. His first TAVI came in 2014—a 27 mm Lotus valve for severe aortic stenosis in the setting of multiple comorbidities, including three-vessel coronary disease, a pacemaker for third-degree AV block, COPD, and diabetes. Seven years later, that prosthesis had structurally deteriorated, with mean gradients climbing to 83 mm Hg and EF dropping to 42%. A TAV-in-TAV with a 23 mm Evolut valve in May 2022 restored valve function and improved EF from 42% to 54%.

But three months later, the other shoe dropped. Fever, worsening heart failure, and blood cultures positive for Enterococcus faecalis. Prosthetic valve endocarditis. He completed six weeks of IV ampicillin-based therapy followed by oral treatment, with documented resolution of infection by clinical assessment, repeat blood cultures, transesophageal echo, and CT. No vegetations, no paravalvular complications, no active infection. By January 2024, however, his valve had deteriorated again—severe stenosis with moderate-to-severe regurgitation, mean gradient 49 mm Hg, AVA 0.5 cm².

## The Decision Point

Here's where the case gets genuinely interesting from a clinical ethics standpoint. The 2025 ESC guidelines recommend surgical valve replacement for PVE, particularly with heart failure or uncontrolled infection. But this patient's EuroSCORE II was 27.36%, he had extensive comorbidities, and two previously implanted transcatheter prostheses were deeply integrated into the aortic wall. Surgical explantation would have meant total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement—an undertaking that might have killed him.

The heart team's decision-making hinged on several critical factors. First, infection control: antimicrobial therapy was complete, blood cultures were negative, and multimodality imaging confirmed no active endocarditis. Second, surgical futility: the anatomical complexity and procedural risk made open surgery untenable. Third, technical feasibility: CT-guided planning demonstrated that a third transcatheter valve could be implanted with preserved coronary flow and acceptable hemodynamics.

This wasn't about ignoring guidelines. It was about applying them in context, with multidisciplinary input, documented infection resolution, and clear-eyed assessment of risk-benefit ratios.

## The Procedural Strategy

If you're going to attempt TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV, your CT planning needs to be meticulous. The team evaluated coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, valve-to-coronary distance, and frame interaction between the two existing prostheses. They calculated a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm in the Evolut prosthesis—severe hemodynamic compromise that demanded intervention.

The procedural strategy prioritized several technical goals. High implantation of the third valve at the level of the Evolut leaflets would fix existing leaflets against the stent frame, preventing further reduction of the effective orifice area from inward leaflet folding. This approach also minimized neo-skirt formation below the coronary ostia, preserving coronary flow and future access. Commissural alignment, guided by preprocedural CT and fluoroscopic landmarks, was essential for maintaining coronary access in a triple-stented anatomy.

The team selected an Acurate neo2 prosthesis for its supra-annular leaflet positioning, relatively low frame height, and favorable hemodynamic performance in narrow anatomies. Balloon pre-dilation with a 22/40 mm catheter under rapid ventricular pacing was followed by valve deployment and post-dilation with a 20/40 mm balloon. Final angiography confirmed correct positioning, preserved coronary perfusion, and no regurgitation.

## What We Can Learn

The immediate post-procedure echo showed something that might have caused panic: a mean gradient of 67 mm Hg with severe prosthesis-patient mismatch. But before you assume the procedure failed, look at the trajectory. At one month, mean gradient dropped to 27 mm Hg, and AVA increased to 1.1 cm². At one year, the patient remained asymptomatic with EF at 65%, mean gradient stable at 26 mm Hg, and no valve-related complications or recurrent endocarditis.

The gradient improvement likely resulted from optimized commissural alignment, effective post-dilation, and partial mechanical expansion of the prior Evolut frame—enhancing leaflet motion and central flow dynamics. This case demonstrates that immediate post-procedure gradients in complex TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV may not predict long-term outcomes, and early remodeling can significantly improve hemodynamics.

But let's be honest about the limitations. Should this prosthesis fail prematurely, options are vanishingly few. Surgical explantation would be even more difficult now. Emerging techniques like bioprosthetic valve fracture or BASILICA might theoretically be considered, but their feasibility in triple-stented configurations is entirely unknown.

## Key Take-Home Points

- TAVI after treated PVE can be considered in select patients when infection is definitively resolved, surgical risk is prohibitive, and CT-guided planning confirms technical feasibility.
- Multimodality imaging to exclude active infection—including TEE and CT—should be completed before any transcatheter reintervention in patients with prior PVE.
- High implantation strategy in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV can preserve coronary flow, minimize neo-skirt formation, and optimize effective orifice area.
- Immediate post-procedure gradients may not predict long-term outcomes in complex valve-in-valve procedures; early remodeling can significantly improve hemodynamics.
- Lifetime management strategies should begin at the first intervention, particularly in younger or high-risk patients—each reintervention exponentially increases anatomical and technical complexity.

This case represents the frontier of structural heart disease: patients surviving long enough to need second and third interventions, creating anatomical scenarios that would have been unimaginable a decade ago. As TAVI indications expand and longevity improves, complex multi-prosthesis interventions will become more frequent, and we need better evidence, better planning tools, and better long-term data to guide these decisions.



# The CT Roadmap: Preprocedural Imaging for Complex Valve-in-Valve Cases

## When Three Valves Stack in a Single Aorta

Picture this: a late-70s gentleman rolls in with dyspnea, dizziness, and a spastic chest sensation walking just 200 meters. His cardiac history reads like a procedural textbook—prior PCIs spanning every major vessel, a pacemaker for complete heart block, and not one but *two* transcatheter aortic valves already sitting in his aortic root. Then add a bout of prosthetic valve endocarditis successfully treated with antibiotics. Now his gradient has climbed back to 49 mmHg with an aortic valve area of 0.5 cm². What do you do?

This case from the National Medical Research Center in Russia pushes the boundaries of what we consider technically feasible. A TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedure—three transcatheter valves stacked like Russian nesting dolls—performed after treated endocarditis. But what makes this case truly instructive isn't just the audacity of the intervention; it's how meticulous CT-guided planning turned a potential anatomical nightmare into a successful, durable outcome.

## The CT Planning That Made It Possible

Here's where this case becomes a masterclass in preprocedural imaging. When you're stacking a third valve inside two existing frames, you're not just thinking about the current implant—you're managing a three-dimensional puzzle where every millimeter matters for coronary access, hemodynamic performance, and future bailout options.

The team performed comprehensive MSCT analysis that went well beyond standard annular measurements. They assessed coronary ostial height relative to the existing valve frames, measured sinus of Valsalva dimensions, and calculated valve-to-coronary distances at multiple levels. The minimum lumen diameter within the Evolut prosthesis measured 13.7 mm—confirming severe hemodynamic compromise but also establishing whether a third valve could even be deployed through the existing orifice.

Critically, they modeled the anticipated "neo-skirt"—that curtain of pinned leaflets that forms when you deploy a new valve inside an old one—and its relationship to the coronary ostia. This analysis revealed that high implantation of the third valve could preserve coronary flow without requiring invasive coronary protection techniques like chimney stents or guidewires in the coronaries during deployment. In a patient with prior three-vessel disease and multiple stents, preserving coronary access wasn't just academic—it was essential.

## Commissural Alignment: Thinking Beyond Today's Procedure

The team didn't just plan for successful deployment; they planned for the patient's lifetime management. Using CT fusion imaging and fluoroscopic landmarks, they aligned the Acurate neo2 commissures with the Evolut valve leaflets. This wasn't random—proper alignment maximizes effective orifice area by allowing the existing leaflets to fold outward against the frame rather than inward where they'd encroach on the flow channel.

They selected the Acurate neo2 specifically for its supra-annular leaflet positioning and relatively low frame height—advantages when you're working in narrow, triple-stented anatomy. The deliberate high implantation strategy—positioning the device at the level of the Evolut leaflets—fixed those pre-existing leaflets against the stent frame and minimized neo-skirt formation below the coronaries.

## When High-Pressure Post-Dilation Does the Work

Here's an interesting hemodynamic nuance. Immediately post-procedure, the mean gradient was 67 mmHg—worse than pre-op. But at one month, it dropped to 27 mmHg with an AVA of 1.1 cm². What happened?

The team performed aggressive post-dilation with a 20 mm balloon under rapid pacing. While intentional frame fracture wasn't attempted, high-pressure inflation likely contributed to radial expansion of the Evolut frame, improving leaflet kinematics and central flow dynamics. The mechanical interplay between three overlapping frames takes time to settle, and early gradients don't necessarily predict long-term performance.

## Key Take-Home Points

- **Comprehensive CT assessment for TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV must include**: coronary ostial height, sinus dimensions, valve-to-coronary distances, minimum lumen diameter through existing valves, and modeling of neo-skirt formation
- **High implantation strategy in valve-in-valve**: pins existing leaflets against the frame outward rather than inward, preserving effective orifice area and coronary access
- **Commissural alignment matters more with each successive valve**: misalignment compounds with multiple frames, making future coronary access progressively difficult
- **Early post-procedure gradients may overestimate final stenosis**: high-pressure post-dilation and frame interaction dynamics evolve over weeks, so don't panic at initial numbers
- **Treated prosthetic valve endocarditis isn't an absolute contraindication to redo-TAVI**: once infection is resolved and confirmed by TEE, CT, and negative cultures, decision-making mirrors non-infected cases
- **Lifetime management starts at valve #1**: each intervention narrows future options, so coronary preservation and commissural alignment should be considered from the first implantation

## The Bottom Line

At one-year follow-up, this patient remains asymptomatic with a stable gradient of 26 mmHg, preserved ejection fraction, and no evidence of recurrent endocarditis—a remarkable outcome in a patient whose EuroSCORE II calculated surgical mortality at 27.36%. But this success wasn't procedural heroics; it was predetermined in the CT suite. As TAVI indications expand to younger patients and durability becomes a career-long management challenge, this case demonstrates that the roadmap for complex valve-in-valve interventions is drawn well before the patient reaches the catheterization laboratory.



# Coronary Access in the Triple-Stented Aorta: Planning for Future Interventions

Here's a case that should make every structural interventionalist pause and think about the long game. A patient in his late 70s presents for his **third** transcatheter aortic valve implantation — a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV — with a critical twist: this patient has three-vessel coronary artery disease and multiple prior PCIs. The case forces us to confront an uncomfortable reality we're increasingly facing in structural heart disease: with each successive valve-in-valve procedure, we're literally stacking the deck against future coronary access.

## The Case That Stops You in Your Tracks

Let's set the stage. This patient had a 27 mm Lotus valve implanted in 2014, followed by a TAV-in-TAV with a 23 mm Evolut in 2022 for structural valve deterioration. Three months later, he developed prosthetic valve endocarditis with *Enterococcus faecalis*, treated successfully with a full course of ampicillin-based therapy. By 2024, severe restenosis and regurgitation necessitated a third intervention — an Acurate neo2 implanted in yet another valve-in-valve-in-valve configuration.

But here's what keeps me up at night: this patient had undergone **four prior coronary interventions** across his RCA, LAD, and circumflex between 2009 and 2014. He's the exact patient who will almost certainly need coronary access again, potentially during an acute coronary syndrome when seconds matter. Every procedural decision made during his TAVI journey directly impacts whether that future coronary access will be straightforward, challenging, or frankly impossible.

## The Cumulative Mesh Burden Problem

The discussion of coronary obstruction risk in TAV-in-TAV is now relatively mature — we worry about the neo-skirt effect, we measure valve-to-coronary distances, and we sometimes deploy coronary protection strategies. But the case report highlights a distinct and arguably underappreciated problem: **cumulative mesh burden**.

When you have three overlapping valve frames, you've created a triple-layered sieve between the aorta and the coronary ostia. Even if each individual valve's struts don't directly obstruct the coronaries, the aggregate effect creates a geometric puzzle that can make selective coronary engagement extraordinarily difficult. The case authors explicitly note that "although commissural alignment was attempted, the cumulative mesh burden may still present challenges for future coronary interventions."

This isn't theoretical. We're talking about a patient who has already required multiple coronary interventions and will almost certainly need them again. If he shows up with an inferior STEMI in five years, can you reliably engage his RCA? If he develops unstable angina, can you interventionally manage his disease? These questions need answers **before** we implant the first valve, let alone the third.

## Commissionural Alignment: More Than Just Haemodynamics

The procedural team in this case made a deliberate choice to prioritize commissural alignment during deployment of the Acurate neo2, guided by preprocedural CT and fluoroscopic landmarks. This wasn't just about optimizing haemodynamics — though that mattered given the triple-stented anatomy and risk of prosthesis-patient mismatch. It was fundamentally about preserving a lifeline.

The Acurate neo2 was selected partly because its design supports commissural alignment, with supra-annular leaflet positioning and relatively low frame height. The team used CT modelling to align the device with the Evolut valve leaflets, maximizing effective orifice area while attempting to keep commissural posts clear of coronary ostia.

But here's the hard truth: perfect commissural alignment with one valve doesn't guarantee alignment when you add a second or third. Each valve has its own geometry, and the interaction between multiple frames is unpredictable. The authors acknowledge this limitation, noting that full three-dimensional expansion analysis using dedicated software wasn't performed.

## High Implantation as a Deliberate Strategy

One technical pearl worth emphasizing from this case: the team deliberately employed a **high implantation strategy** for the third valve. By implanting the Acurate neo2 at the level of the Evolut valve leaflets, they achieved two goals simultaneously. First, they fixed the pre-existing leaflets against the stent frame, preventing further reduction of the effective orifice area from inward leaflet folding. Second, and critically for coronary access, they minimized neo-skirt formation below the coronary ostia.

This approach wasn't improvised. Preprocedural CT assessment explicitly evaluated coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, and the anticipated relationship between the neo-skirt and coronary ostia. The team determined they could proceed without coronary protection — but only because they had done the imaging work to prove it.

## The Lifetime Management Imperative

This case exemplifies why we need to shift our thinking from "procedure-focused" to "lifetime management." The authors correctly note that "with each transcatheter reintervention, considerations such as frame layering, commissural alignment, coronary reaccess and residual effective orifice area become increasingly complex."

The uncomfortable reality is that options become progressively more limited with each intervention. If this patient's current prosthesis fails prematurely, surgical explantation would likely require total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement — an operation of magnitude that borders on prohibitive given his EuroSCORE II of 27.36% and extensive comorbidities. Emerging catheter-based solutions like bioprosthetic valve fracture or BASILICA might be considered, but as the authors note, their feasibility and safety in triple-stented configurations remain uncertain.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Coronary access must be a primary consideration, not an afterthought**, in any TAV-in-TAV case, especially in patients with known coronary artery disease — plan for the STEMI that hasn't happened yet.
- **Cumulative mesh burden from multiple overlapping valve frames creates exponentially increasing complexity for coronary engagement**, even when individual valve positions appear acceptable.
- **Commissionural alignment should be actively pursued at every intervention**, using CT-guided planning and appropriate valve selection — but recognize that alignment becomes harder to achieve and maintain with each successive valve.
- **High implantation strategies can preserve coronary ostia patency** by minimizing neo-skirt formation below coronary ostia, but require meticulous preprocedural CT planning.
- **Lifetime management planning should begin at the first TAVI**, particularly in younger patients or those with coronary disease — every valve choice has downstream consequences.

## The Bottom Line

This patient's successful TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV with preserved coronary flow and 1-year durability is a technical triumph. But it should also serve as a warning shot. As TAVI indications expand and patients live longer with their prostheses, we will see more multi-valve configurations. The patients who need these interventions are often the same ones who will require coronary access during their most vulnerable moments. If we don't prioritize coronary preservation in our procedural planning — starting with that first valve — we may be trading excellent haemodynamics today for an unsurvivable coronary occlusion tomorrow.



# Patient-Prosthesis Mismatch in Serial TAVI: The Hemodynamic Toll of Valve Stacking

Three valves. One aortic root. That's the headline here—and while the procedural success is impressive, the real story is what happened to the gradients afterward. This case forces us to confront an uncomfortable question: in serial transcatheter reinterventions, is patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM) inevitable?

## The Case That Stops You in Your Tracks

A man in his late 70s with a Lot of cardiac history—pun intended. His first TAVI was a 27 mm Lotus valve back in 2014. By 2022, that valve had failed with a mean gradient of 83 mm Hg and EF crashing to 42%. He got a TAV-in-TAV with a 23 mm Evolut prosthesis. Three months later? Prosthetic valve endocarditis with *Enterococcus faecalis*. He survived that too, completed a full course of antimicrobial therapy, but by 2024 his valve was failing again—mean gradient 49 mm Hg, AVA of 0.5 cm².

So they did what increasingly skilled teams can do: they implanted a third valve, an Acurate neo2, using CT-guided planning and careful technique. One-year follow-up shows he's asymptomatic with a stable 26 mm Hg mean gradient.

But here's what grabbed me: immediately post-procedure, his mean gradient was **67 mm Hg**. That's worse than before the intervention. The AVA was 0.9 cm² with an indexed AVA of 0.4 cm²/m²—severe PPM by definition. Yet by one month, the gradient dropped to 27 mm Hg, and it held steady at one year. What happened in between tells us something important about hemodynamics in stacked valves.

## Why This Matters for Your Practice

We're living through the TAVI generation. The patients who got early TAVI valves are now coming back with structural degeneration. Many are too high-risk for surgery, so we're doing TAV-in-TAV. But as this case demonstrates, when you start stacking three valves, you're creating a geometry problem that no amount of technical skill can fully overcome.

The team here used several smart strategies worth understanding:

**High implantation technique**: They deliberately implanted the third valve at the level of the Evolut's leaflets rather than going lower. This pinned the existing leaflets against the stent frame, preventing them from folding inward and further narrowing the orifice. It also reduced the "neo-skirt" below the coronary ostia—critical for preserving coronary access in a patient with three-vessel CAD and multiple prior PCIs.

**Commissural alignment**: Using pre-procedural CT modelling, they aligned the Acurate neo2 with the Evolut valve leaflets to maximise effective orifice area. This wasn't just about haemodynamics—it was about preserving future coronary access in an anatomy that's becoming a procedural nightmare.

**High-pressure balloon post-dilation**: A 20/40 mm balloon under rapid pacing helped expand the Evolut frame further. While intentional frame fracture wasn't performed, the mechanical effect likely contributed to the haemodynamic improvement.

## The Gradient Paradox

Here's what fascinates me: the immediate post-procedure gradient of 67 mm Hg looked like a procedural failure. But by one month, it was 27 mm Hg. What changed?

Part of this is likely adaptive remodelling—the stent frames settling, leaflets endothelialising, and flow dynamics stabilising. But part of it also reflects what happens when you push a system that's already at its limits. The pre-procedural MSCT showed a minimum lumen diameter of only 13.7 mm in that Evolut prosthesis. You cannot cheat physics—you're asking three valve frames to occupy a space that's fundamentally constrained.

The indexed AVA of 0.4 cm²/m² meets criteria for severe PPM, and at one year, the patient remains asymptomatic. This raises a fair question: does PPM matter as much when the alternative is no intervention at all? This patient had prohibitive surgical risk (EuroSCORE II of 27.36%) and two prior transcatheter valves already integrated into his aortic wall. Surgical explant would have required total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement. Sometimes "good enough" haemodynamics is actually a win.

## The Coronary Access Time Bomb

What worries me more than the gradients is what happens when this patient needs his LAD interventioned again. He's had three stents and now three valve frames creating a meshwork between his coronary ostia and his aorta. The team deliberately used high implantation to preserve coronary flow, but as they acknowledge, the cumulative mesh burden makes future coronary access exponentially more difficult.

This is the conversation we need to be having at the *first* TAVI, not the third. In younger patients, commissural alignment and valve selection should account for the possibility—maybe probability—of future reinterventions. Each valve we stack makes the next one harder.

## Take-Home Points

- **Severe PPM may be unavoidable in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV**—the goal shifts from perfect haemodynamics to acceptable function in a patient with no surgical option.

- **Immediate post-procedure gradients can be misleading**—this patient went from 67 mm Hg to 26 mm Hg over a month, likely from stent settling and adaptive remodelling.

- **High implantation strategy serves dual purposes**: it prevents leaflet folding inward (preserving EOA) and reduces neo-skirt formation below coronaries (preserving access).

- **CT-guided planning is non-negotiable** for serial TAVI—valve-to-coronary distances, sinus dimensions, and frame interaction modelling determined valve selection and implantation depth.

- **Post-endocarditis TAVI is feasible** when infection is definitively treated—this patient had completed antimicrobial therapy with negative blood cultures, TEE, and CT prior to intervention.

- **We need to think about the last valve before we implant the first one**—lifetime management strategies should be discussed early, especially in younger or lower-risk TAVI candidates.

## The Bottom Line

This case proves TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV is technically feasible with good one-year outcomes, even after treated prosthetic valve endocarditis. But it also shows the hemodynamic compromises we inherit with each successive intervention. As we see more structural valve deterioration in the TAVI generation, cases like this will become routine rather than remarkable—and we need to be prepared for the anatomical and physiological constraints that come with them.

One month later, this improved dramatically to a mean gradient of 27 mm Hg and AVA of 1.1 cm² after left ventricular remodeling.

This matters because each layer of valve adds obstruction. When you're stacking prostheses, the effective orifice area becomes a major concern. The high implantation strategy helped, as did aggressive post-dilation with a 20 mm balloon. The disappearance of the elevated gradient likely reflects both mechanical expansion of the prior Evolut frame and improved leaflet dynamics from commissural alignment.

## The Elephant in the Room: What Happens When Valve Number Three Fails?

This is the question that keeps me up at night. The authors acknowledge that should this prosthesis fail, options would be "extremely limited." Surgical explantation would require total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement—technically demanding and high-risk given his EuroSCORE II of 27.36%.

Emerging techniques like bioprosthetic valve fracture or BASILICA might have roles, but their feasibility and safety in triple-stented configurations remain uncertain. We simply don't have data.

This case forces us to think about lifetime management from the very first intervention. When we implant a valve in a 70-year-old today, are we considering that they might need two or three more interventions? Are we choosing valves that facilitate future TAV-in-TAV? Are we thinking about coronary access preservation?

## Planning for Failure from Day One

The key lesson here isn't just that TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV is possible—it's that successful outcomes depend on planning that starts with the first valve. Valve selection matters for commissural alignment. Implantation depth matters for coronary access. Frame design matters for how subsequent valves will interact with existing prostheses.

The reality is that as TAVI indications expand to younger patients with longer life expectancies, many will outlive their first—and possibly their second—bioprosthetic valve. We need to be having conversations about lifetime management strategies earlier, not when the patient shows up with their third episode of valve failure.

## Key Take-Home Points

- TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV is technically feasible with meticulous CT-guided planning, particularly for coronary obstruction risk assessment and commissural alignment optimization
- High implantation of subsequent valves can preserve coronary flow and maximize effective orifice area by preventing inward leaflet folding from prior prostheses
- Each transcatheter reintervention increases complexity—frame layering, coronary access challenges, and cumulative obstruction from prosthesis-patient mismatch compound with every procedure
- Post-endocarditis TAVI can succeed when infection is definitively resolved and imaging confirms no active disease, but surgical options remain limited in this population
- Lifetime management planning should begin at the first intervention, especially in younger patients who may require sequential reinterventions
- We have limited data and even more limited options when the third valve fails—conversations about procedural trajectories need to happen earlier in the disease course

This case demonstrates what's possible when everything goes right: excellent imaging, careful valve selection, and deliberate implantation strategy. But it also highlights the diminishing returns of transcatheter options. At some point, our patients run out of road, and we owe it to them to think about that destination before we start the journey.
