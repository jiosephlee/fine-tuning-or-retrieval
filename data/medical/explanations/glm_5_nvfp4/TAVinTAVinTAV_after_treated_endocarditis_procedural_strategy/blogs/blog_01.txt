Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV: Pushing the Technical Envelope of Transcatheter Redo

What happens when you've already implanted two transcatheter aortic valves and your patient comes back with severe prosthetic degeneration—after surviving prosthetic valve endocarditis? You implant a third one. Welcome to the world of TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV.

This case from Merkulov and colleagues is a striking example of how far transcatheter technology has pushed the boundaries of what's technically feasible. A man in his late 70s, initially treated with a Lotus valve in 2014, underwent TAV-in-TAV with an Evolut prosthesis in 2022 for structural valve deterioration. Three months later, he developed Enterococcus faecalis prosthetic valve endocarditis. After successful antimicrobial therapy and confirmed resolution of infection, he presented again in 2024 with severe restenosis and moderate-to-severe regurgitation. Mean gradient was 49 mm Hg, aortic valve area had collapsed to 0.5 cm². The heart team's solution: a third transcatheter valve, making him one of the rare documented cases of TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV post-endocarditis.

## The Technical Gauntlet

Let's be clear—this is not your standard redo procedure. With three overlapping bioprosthetic frames, the margin for error shrinks dramatically. The primary concerns stack up quickly: coronary artery obstruction risk, catastrophic prosthesis-patient mismatch, and the nightmare scenario of a neo-skirt forming from the cumulative leaflet burden that could occlude coronary ostia.

The team's pre-procedural CT planning was exhaustive. They measured coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, and critically, the valve-to-coronary distance. The minimum lumen diameter in the Evolut prosthesis was 13.7 mm—confirming severe haemodynamic compromise but also providing a target for sizing the third valve.

## Strategic High Implantation: The Key Move

Here's where this case gets really clever. The team deliberately implanted the Acurate neo2 high—at the level of the Evolut valve leaflets. Why does this matter? By positioning the device to fix the pre-existing leaflets against the stent frame rather than below them, they prevented additional inward leaflet folding that would have further reduced the effective orifice area. This approach also minimised neo-skirt formation below the coronary ostia, preserving both immediate coronary flow and future coronary access.

Valve selection wasn't arbitrary either. The Acurate neo2 was chosen for its supra-annular leaflet positioning, relatively low frame height, and favourable haemodynamic performance in narrow anatomies. Critically, its design supports commissural alignment—a consideration that becomes exponentially more important when you're stacking three valve frames.

## The Gradient Story: Immediate vs. Follow-up

Here's something that should catch your attention: immediately post-procedure, the mean gradient was 67 mm Hg with an aortic valve area of 0.9 cm²—findings consistent with severe prosthesis-patient mismatch. But hold judgment. At one month, the mean gradient had dropped to 27 mm Hg and AVA improved to 1.1 cm². By one year, these parameters held stable at 26 mm Hg and 1.1 cm² respectively.

What explains this early improvement? The authors attribute it to a combination of optimised commissural alignment, effective post-dilation, and partial mechanical expansion of the prior Evolut frame. High-pressure balloon post-dilation likely contributed to further radial expansion of the Evolut, enhancing overall valve function. The left ventricle also remodelled favourably, with reduced chamber volumes documented on follow-up imaging.

## The Endocarditis Question

Let's address the elephant in the room. Current ESC guidelines recommend surgical valve replacement for prosthetic valve endocarditis, particularly with heart failure or uncontrolled infection. But this patient's EuroSCORE II was 27.36%, with COPD, diabetes, prior GI bleeding, and two previously implanted transcatheter prostheses deeply integrated into the aortic wall. Surgical explantation would have required total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement.

The critical distinction here: the endocarditis was successfully treated. Blood cultures were negative, transoesophageal echocardiography showed no vegetations, and CT confirmed no paravalvular complications. The patient had completed antimicrobial therapy with no ongoing suppressive antibiotics needed. Once infection was definitively resolved, the procedural decision-making shifted to standard considerations of anatomical feasibility and surgical risk.

## Where Do We Go From Here?

This case is a harbinger of what's coming. With expanding TAVI indications and improving long-term survival, we're going to see more patients outliving their first—and second—transcatheter valves. Each reintervention compounds the technical complexity: frame layering dynamics, coronary access challenges, and the relentless squeeze on effective orifice area.

The authors make an essential point about lifetime management strategies. These conversations need to happen at the time of the first intervention, especially in younger patients. Valve selection, implantation depth, and commissural alignment aren't just procedural details—they're setting up (or foreclosing) future options.

**Key Take-Home Messages:**

- TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV is technically feasible with meticulous CT-guided planning, even after treated prosthetic valve endocarditis
- High implantation strategy reduces neo-skirt formation and preserves coronary access by fixing pre-existing leaflets against the stent frame
- Post-dilation with high-pressure balloons may improve haemodynamics through partial frame expansion of prior valves
- Immediate post-procedural gradients may not predict follow-up performance; watch for early remodelling and gradient improvement
- Valve selection must account for commissural alignment capability and frame height in triple-stented anatomy
- Prosthetic valve endocarditis that has been definitively treated does not preclude transcatheter reintervention in prohibitive surgical candidates

At one-year follow-up, this patient remained asymptomatic with preserved valve function—proof that even in the most anatomically hostile terrain, careful strategy and imaging-guided execution can push the boundaries of what's possible. Whether this represents sustainable long-term success or simply buying time in an increasingly complex anatomical situation remains to be seen, but this case demonstrates that the ceiling of transcatheter reintervention continues to rise.
