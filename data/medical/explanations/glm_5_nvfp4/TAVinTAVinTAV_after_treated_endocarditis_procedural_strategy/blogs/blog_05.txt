Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# Patient-Prosthesis Mismatch in Serial TAVI: The Hemodynamic Toll of Valve Stacking

Three valves. One aortic root. That's the headline here—and while the procedural success is impressive, the real story is what happened to the gradients afterward. This case forces us to confront an uncomfortable question: in serial transcatheter reinterventions, is patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM) inevitable?

## The Case That Stops You in Your Tracks

A man in his late 70s with a Lot of cardiac history—pun intended. His first TAVI was a 27 mm Lotus valve back in 2014. By 2022, that valve had failed with a mean gradient of 83 mm Hg and EF crashing to 42%. He got a TAV-in-TAV with a 23 mm Evolut prosthesis. Three months later? Prosthetic valve endocarditis with *Enterococcus faecalis*. He survived that too, completed a full course of antimicrobial therapy, but by 2024 his valve was failing again—mean gradient 49 mm Hg, AVA of 0.5 cm².

So they did what increasingly skilled teams can do: they implanted a third valve, an Acurate neo2, using CT-guided planning and careful technique. One-year follow-up shows he's asymptomatic with a stable 26 mm Hg mean gradient.

But here's what grabbed me: immediately post-procedure, his mean gradient was **67 mm Hg**. That's worse than before the intervention. The AVA was 0.9 cm² with an indexed AVA of 0.4 cm²/m²—severe PPM by definition. Yet by one month, the gradient dropped to 27 mm Hg, and it held steady at one year. What happened in between tells us something important about hemodynamics in stacked valves.

## Why This Matters for Your Practice

We're living through the TAVI generation. The patients who got early TAVI valves are now coming back with structural degeneration. Many are too high-risk for surgery, so we're doing TAV-in-TAV. But as this case demonstrates, when you start stacking three valves, you're creating a geometry problem that no amount of technical skill can fully overcome.

The team here used several smart strategies worth understanding:

**High implantation technique**: They deliberately implanted the third valve at the level of the Evolut's leaflets rather than going lower. This pinned the existing leaflets against the stent frame, preventing them from folding inward and further narrowing the orifice. It also reduced the "neo-skirt" below the coronary ostia—critical for preserving coronary access in a patient with three-vessel CAD and multiple prior PCIs.

**Commissural alignment**: Using pre-procedural CT modelling, they aligned the Acurate neo2 with the Evolut valve leaflets to maximise effective orifice area. This wasn't just about haemodynamics—it was about preserving future coronary access in an anatomy that's becoming a procedural nightmare.

**High-pressure balloon post-dilation**: A 20/40 mm balloon under rapid pacing helped expand the Evolut frame further. While intentional frame fracture wasn't performed, the mechanical effect likely contributed to the haemodynamic improvement.

## The Gradient Paradox

Here's what fascinates me: the immediate post-procedure gradient of 67 mm Hg looked like a procedural failure. But by one month, it was 27 mm Hg. What changed?

Part of this is likely adaptive remodelling—the stent frames settling, leaflets endothelialising, and flow dynamics stabilising. But part of it also reflects what happens when you push a system that's already at its limits. The pre-procedural MSCT showed a minimum lumen diameter of only 13.7 mm in that Evolut prosthesis. You cannot cheat physics—you're asking three valve frames to occupy a space that's fundamentally constrained.

The indexed AVA of 0.4 cm²/m² meets criteria for severe PPM, and at one year, the patient remains asymptomatic. This raises a fair question: does PPM matter as much when the alternative is no intervention at all? This patient had prohibitive surgical risk (EuroSCORE II of 27.36%) and two prior transcatheter valves already integrated into his aortic wall. Surgical explant would have required total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement. Sometimes "good enough" haemodynamics is actually a win.

## The Coronary Access Time Bomb

What worries me more than the gradients is what happens when this patient needs his LAD interventioned again. He's had three stents and now three valve frames creating a meshwork between his coronary ostia and his aorta. The team deliberately used high implantation to preserve coronary flow, but as they acknowledge, the cumulative mesh burden makes future coronary access exponentially more difficult.

This is the conversation we need to be having at the *first* TAVI, not the third. In younger patients, commissural alignment and valve selection should account for the possibility—maybe probability—of future reinterventions. Each valve we stack makes the next one harder.

## Take-Home Points

- **Severe PPM may be unavoidable in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV**—the goal shifts from perfect haemodynamics to acceptable function in a patient with no surgical option.

- **Immediate post-procedure gradients can be misleading**—this patient went from 67 mm Hg to 26 mm Hg over a month, likely from stent settling and adaptive remodelling.

- **High implantation strategy serves dual purposes**: it prevents leaflet folding inward (preserving EOA) and reduces neo-skirt formation below coronaries (preserving access).

- **CT-guided planning is non-negotiable** for serial TAVI—valve-to-coronary distances, sinus dimensions, and frame interaction modelling determined valve selection and implantation depth.

- **Post-endocarditis TAVI is feasible** when infection is definitively treated—this patient had completed antimicrobial therapy with negative blood cultures, TEE, and CT prior to intervention.

- **We need to think about the last valve before we implant the first one**—lifetime management strategies should be discussed early, especially in younger or lower-risk TAVI candidates.

## The Bottom Line

This case proves TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV is technically feasible with good one-year outcomes, even after treated prosthetic valve endocarditis. But it also shows the hemodynamic compromises we inherit with each successive intervention. As we see more structural valve deterioration in the TAVI generation, cases like this will become routine rather than remarkable—and we need to be prepared for the anatomical and physiological constraints that come with them.
