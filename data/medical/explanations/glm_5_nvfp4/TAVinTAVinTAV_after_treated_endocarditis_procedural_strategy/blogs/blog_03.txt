Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# The CT Roadmap: Preprocedural Imaging for Complex Valve-in-Valve Cases

## When Three Valves Stack in a Single Aorta

Picture this: a late-70s gentleman rolls in with dyspnea, dizziness, and a spastic chest sensation walking just 200 meters. His cardiac history reads like a procedural textbook—prior PCIs spanning every major vessel, a pacemaker for complete heart block, and not one but *two* transcatheter aortic valves already sitting in his aortic root. Then add a bout of prosthetic valve endocarditis successfully treated with antibiotics. Now his gradient has climbed back to 49 mmHg with an aortic valve area of 0.5 cm². What do you do?

This case from the National Medical Research Center in Russia pushes the boundaries of what we consider technically feasible. A TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedure—three transcatheter valves stacked like Russian nesting dolls—performed after treated endocarditis. But what makes this case truly instructive isn't just the audacity of the intervention; it's how meticulous CT-guided planning turned a potential anatomical nightmare into a successful, durable outcome.

## The CT Planning That Made It Possible

Here's where this case becomes a masterclass in preprocedural imaging. When you're stacking a third valve inside two existing frames, you're not just thinking about the current implant—you're managing a three-dimensional puzzle where every millimeter matters for coronary access, hemodynamic performance, and future bailout options.

The team performed comprehensive MSCT analysis that went well beyond standard annular measurements. They assessed coronary ostial height relative to the existing valve frames, measured sinus of Valsalva dimensions, and calculated valve-to-coronary distances at multiple levels. The minimum lumen diameter within the Evolut prosthesis measured 13.7 mm—confirming severe hemodynamic compromise but also establishing whether a third valve could even be deployed through the existing orifice.

Critically, they modeled the anticipated "neo-skirt"—that curtain of pinned leaflets that forms when you deploy a new valve inside an old one—and its relationship to the coronary ostia. This analysis revealed that high implantation of the third valve could preserve coronary flow without requiring invasive coronary protection techniques like chimney stents or guidewires in the coronaries during deployment. In a patient with prior three-vessel disease and multiple stents, preserving coronary access wasn't just academic—it was essential.

## Commissural Alignment: Thinking Beyond Today's Procedure

The team didn't just plan for successful deployment; they planned for the patient's lifetime management. Using CT fusion imaging and fluoroscopic landmarks, they aligned the Acurate neo2 commissures with the Evolut valve leaflets. This wasn't random—proper alignment maximizes effective orifice area by allowing the existing leaflets to fold outward against the frame rather than inward where they'd encroach on the flow channel.

They selected the Acurate neo2 specifically for its supra-annular leaflet positioning and relatively low frame height—advantages when you're working in narrow, triple-stented anatomy. The deliberate high implantation strategy—positioning the device at the level of the Evolut leaflets—fixed those pre-existing leaflets against the stent frame and minimized neo-skirt formation below the coronaries.

## When High-Pressure Post-Dilation Does the Work

Here's an interesting hemodynamic nuance. Immediately post-procedure, the mean gradient was 67 mmHg—worse than pre-op. But at one month, it dropped to 27 mmHg with an AVA of 1.1 cm². What happened?

The team performed aggressive post-dilation with a 20 mm balloon under rapid pacing. While intentional frame fracture wasn't attempted, high-pressure inflation likely contributed to radial expansion of the Evolut frame, improving leaflet kinematics and central flow dynamics. The mechanical interplay between three overlapping frames takes time to settle, and early gradients don't necessarily predict long-term performance.

## Key Take-Home Points

- **Comprehensive CT assessment for TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV must include**: coronary ostial height, sinus dimensions, valve-to-coronary distances, minimum lumen diameter through existing valves, and modeling of neo-skirt formation
- **High implantation strategy in valve-in-valve**: pins existing leaflets against the frame outward rather than inward, preserving effective orifice area and coronary access
- **Commissural alignment matters more with each successive valve**: misalignment compounds with multiple frames, making future coronary access progressively difficult
- **Early post-procedure gradients may overestimate final stenosis**: high-pressure post-dilation and frame interaction dynamics evolve over weeks, so don't panic at initial numbers
- **Treated prosthetic valve endocarditis isn't an absolute contraindication to redo-TAVI**: once infection is resolved and confirmed by TEE, CT, and negative cultures, decision-making mirrors non-infected cases
- **Lifetime management starts at valve #1**: each intervention narrows future options, so coronary preservation and commissural alignment should be considered from the first implantation

## The Bottom Line

At one-year follow-up, this patient remains asymptomatic with a stable gradient of 26 mmHg, preserved ejection fraction, and no evidence of recurrent endocarditis—a remarkable outcome in a patient whose EuroSCORE II calculated surgical mortality at 27.36%. But this success wasn't procedural heroics; it was predetermined in the CT suite. As TAVI indications expand to younger patients and durability becomes a career-long management challenge, this case demonstrates that the roadmap for complex valve-in-valve interventions is drawn well before the patient reaches the catheterization laboratory.
