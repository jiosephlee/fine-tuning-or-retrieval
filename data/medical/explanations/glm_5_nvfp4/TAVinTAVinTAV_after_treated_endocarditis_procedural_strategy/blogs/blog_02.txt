# TAVI After Treated Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis: When Guidelines Meet Reality

## The Case That Makes You Pause

Picture this: a man in his late 70s sits in front of you with severe prosthetic valve dysfunction. His mean gradient is 49 mm Hg, his aortic valve area is 0.5 cm², and he can barely walk 200 meters without chest tightness and dyspnea. Standard stuff, right? Except he's already had two transcatheter aortic valves implanted, and in between them, he survived prosthetic valve endocarditis. Now you're considering a third transcatheter valve—TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV—in a patient with a history of treated PVE. The 2025 ESC guidelines are clear: surgical valve replacement is the recommended strategy for PVE. But what happens when surgical risk is prohibitive, infection is definitively resolved, and anatomy is so hostile that surgery would require total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement?

This case forces us to confront an uncomfortable truth about modern structural heart disease: guidelines provide frameworks, not scripts. And as TAVI volumes explode and patients outlive their first prosthesis, we're going to see more of these scenarios whether we're ready or not.

## The Clinical Journey

This patient's timeline reads like a case study in structural valve disease progression. His first TAVI came in 2014—a 27 mm Lotus valve for severe aortic stenosis in the setting of multiple comorbidities, including three-vessel coronary disease, a pacemaker for third-degree AV block, COPD, and diabetes. Seven years later, that prosthesis had structurally deteriorated, with mean gradients climbing to 83 mm Hg and EF dropping to 42%. A TAV-in-TAV with a 23 mm Evolut valve in May 2022 restored valve function and improved EF from 42% to 54%.

But three months later, the other shoe dropped. Fever, worsening heart failure, and blood cultures positive for Enterococcus faecalis. Prosthetic valve endocarditis. He completed six weeks of IV ampicillin-based therapy followed by oral treatment, with documented resolution of infection by clinical assessment, repeat blood cultures, transesophageal echo, and CT. No vegetations, no paravalvular complications, no active infection. By January 2024, however, his valve had deteriorated again—severe stenosis with moderate-to-severe regurgitation, mean gradient 49 mm Hg, AVA 0.5 cm².

## The Decision Point

Here's where the case gets genuinely interesting from a clinical ethics standpoint. The 2025 ESC guidelines recommend surgical valve replacement for PVE, particularly with heart failure or uncontrolled infection. But this patient's EuroSCORE II was 27.36%, he had extensive comorbidities, and two previously implanted transcatheter prostheses were deeply integrated into the aortic wall. Surgical explantation would have meant total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement—an undertaking that might have killed him.

The heart team's decision-making hinged on several critical factors. First, infection control: antimicrobial therapy was complete, blood cultures were negative, and multimodality imaging confirmed no active endocarditis. Second, surgical futility: the anatomical complexity and procedural risk made open surgery untenable. Third, technical feasibility: CT-guided planning demonstrated that a third transcatheter valve could be implanted with preserved coronary flow and acceptable hemodynamics.

This wasn't about ignoring guidelines. It was about applying them in context, with multidisciplinary input, documented infection resolution, and clear-eyed assessment of risk-benefit ratios.

## The Procedural Strategy

If you're going to attempt TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV, your CT planning needs to be meticulous. The team evaluated coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, valve-to-coronary distance, and frame interaction between the two existing prostheses. They calculated a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm in the Evolut prosthesis—severe hemodynamic compromise that demanded intervention.

The procedural strategy prioritized several technical goals. High implantation of the third valve at the level of the Evolut leaflets would fix existing leaflets against the stent frame, preventing further reduction of the effective orifice area from inward leaflet folding. This approach also minimized neo-skirt formation below the coronary ostia, preserving coronary flow and future access. Commissural alignment, guided by preprocedural CT and fluoroscopic landmarks, was essential for maintaining coronary access in a triple-stented anatomy.

The team selected an Acurate neo2 prosthesis for its supra-annular leaflet positioning, relatively low frame height, and favorable hemodynamic performance in narrow anatomies. Balloon pre-dilation with a 22/40 mm catheter under rapid ventricular pacing was followed by valve deployment and post-dilation with a 20/40 mm balloon. Final angiography confirmed correct positioning, preserved coronary perfusion, and no regurgitation.

## What We Can Learn

The immediate post-procedure echo showed something that might have caused panic: a mean gradient of 67 mm Hg with severe prosthesis-patient mismatch. But before you assume the procedure failed, look at the trajectory. At one month, mean gradient dropped to 27 mm Hg, and AVA increased to 1.1 cm². At one year, the patient remained asymptomatic with EF at 65%, mean gradient stable at 26 mm Hg, and no valve-related complications or recurrent endocarditis.

The gradient improvement likely resulted from optimized commissural alignment, effective post-dilation, and partial mechanical expansion of the prior Evolut frame—enhancing leaflet motion and central flow dynamics. This case demonstrates that immediate post-procedure gradients in complex TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV may not predict long-term outcomes, and early remodeling can significantly improve hemodynamics.

But let's be honest about the limitations. Should this prosthesis fail prematurely, options are vanishingly few. Surgical explantation would be even more difficult now. Emerging techniques like bioprosthetic valve fracture or BASILICA might theoretically be considered, but their feasibility in triple-stented configurations is entirely unknown.

## Key Take-Home Points

- TAVI after treated PVE can be considered in select patients when infection is definitively resolved, surgical risk is prohibitive, and CT-guided planning confirms technical feasibility.
- Multimodality imaging to exclude active infection—including TEE and CT—should be completed before any transcatheter reintervention in patients with prior PVE.
- High implantation strategy in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV can preserve coronary flow, minimize neo-skirt formation, and optimize effective orifice area.
- Immediate post-procedure gradients may not predict long-term outcomes in complex valve-in-valve procedures; early remodeling can significantly improve hemodynamics.
- Lifetime management strategies should begin at the first intervention, particularly in younger or high-risk patients—each reintervention exponentially increases anatomical and technical complexity.

This case represents the frontier of structural heart disease: patients surviving long enough to need second and third interventions, creating anatomical scenarios that would have been unimaginable a decade ago. As TAVI indications expand and longevity improves, complex multi-prosthesis interventions will become more frequent, and we need better evidence, better planning tools, and better long-term data to guide these decisions.
