Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# Lifetime Management of Aortic Valve Disease: Lessons from the Third Valve

## When Your Patient Runs Out of Road

Here's a case that made me pause and think about where we're headed as TAVI indications keep expanding. A man in his late 70s presents with severe prosthetic valve dysfunction. Standard stuff, right? Except he's already had two transcatheter aortic valves implanted, and between them, he survived prosthetic valve endocarditis. Now he needs his third transcatheter valve. Welcome to the world of TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV.

This case, published by Merkulov and colleagues, represents something we're going to see more of: patients outliving their valves, multiple times. And it forces us to confront an uncomfortable question that we don't talk about enough: what happens when your patient runs out of transcatheter options?

## The Clinical Journey

Let's walk through this patient's timeline because it illustrates just how complex these trajectories can become. He received his first TAVI in 2014—a 27 mm Lotus valve. By 2022, that valve had degenerated severely with a mean gradient of 83 mm Hg and his EF had dropped to 42%. He underwent TAV-in-TAV with a 23 mm Evolut valve.

Three months later, he developed prosthetic valve endocarditis with Enterococcus faecalis. He completed a full course of antimicrobial therapy, and imaging confirmed resolution of infection. But by early 2024, the double-valve configuration had failed again—mean gradient 49 mm Hg, severe aortic stenosis with moderate-to-severe regurgitation, and an aortic valve area of just 0.5 cm².

The team proceeded with a third valve—an Acurate neo2—implanted with deliberate high positioning to maximize effective orifice area and preserve coronary access. At one-year follow-up, he remains asymptomatic with a mean gradient of 26 mm Hg and an AVA of 1.1 cm².

## The Technical Details Matter Immensely

What makes this case particularly instructive is the procedural planning. This wasn't just "let's put another valve in." The team used extensive CT modeling to address several critical issues.

The coronary obstruction risk in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV is real. With three overlapping stent frames, you're creating a complex mesh that can compromise flow to the coronaries. Preprocedural CT assessed coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, and valve-to-coronary distance. The team deliberately implanted the third valve high—at the level of the Evolut valve leaflets—to minimize the "neo-skirt" effect below the coronary ostia.

Commissural alignment was another key consideration. The Acurate neo2 was selected partly because its design supports alignment, which becomes critical when you're thinking about future coronary access through three valve frames. The cumulative metal burden creates real challenges for coronary engagement, and that's before we even consider the patient's history of three-vessel coronary disease and multiple PCIs.

## The Prosthesis-Patient Mismatch Problem

Here's something that caught my attention: immediately after the third valve implantation, the mean gradient was 67 mm Hg with a calculated AVA of 0.9 cm²—consistent with severe prosthesis-patient mismatch. One month later, this improved dramatically to a mean gradient of 27 mm Hg and AVA of 1.1 cm² after left ventricular remodeling.

This matters because each layer of valve adds obstruction. When you're stacking prostheses, the effective orifice area becomes a major concern. The high implantation strategy helped, as did aggressive post-dilation with a 20 mm balloon. The disappearance of the elevated gradient likely reflects both mechanical expansion of the prior Evolut frame and improved leaflet dynamics from commissural alignment.

## The Elephant in the Room: What Happens When Valve Number Three Fails?

This is the question that keeps me up at night. The authors acknowledge that should this prosthesis fail, options would be "extremely limited." Surgical explantation would require total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement—technically demanding and high-risk given his EuroSCORE II of 27.36%.

Emerging techniques like bioprosthetic valve fracture or BASILICA might have roles, but their feasibility and safety in triple-stented configurations remain uncertain. We simply don't have data.

This case forces us to think about lifetime management from the very first intervention. When we implant a valve in a 70-year-old today, are we considering that they might need two or three more interventions? Are we choosing valves that facilitate future TAV-in-TAV? Are we thinking about coronary access preservation?

## Planning for Failure from Day One

The key lesson here isn't just that TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV is possible—it's that successful outcomes depend on planning that starts with the first valve. Valve selection matters for commissural alignment. Implantation depth matters for coronary access. Frame design matters for how subsequent valves will interact with existing prostheses.

The reality is that as TAVI indications expand to younger patients with longer life expectancies, many will outlive their first—and possibly their second—bioprosthetic valve. We need to be having conversations about lifetime management strategies earlier, not when the patient shows up with their third episode of valve failure.

## Key Take-Home Points

- TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV is technically feasible with meticulous CT-guided planning, particularly for coronary obstruction risk assessment and commissural alignment optimization
- High implantation of subsequent valves can preserve coronary flow and maximize effective orifice area by preventing inward leaflet folding from prior prostheses
- Each transcatheter reintervention increases complexity—frame layering, coronary access challenges, and cumulative obstruction from prosthesis-patient mismatch compound with every procedure
- Post-endocarditis TAVI can succeed when infection is definitively resolved and imaging confirms no active disease, but surgical options remain limited in this population
- Lifetime management planning should begin at the first intervention, especially in younger patients who may require sequential reinterventions
- We have limited data and even more limited options when the third valve fails—conversations about procedural trajectories need to happen earlier in the disease course

This case demonstrates what's possible when everything goes right: excellent imaging, careful valve selection, and deliberate implantation strategy. But it also highlights the diminishing returns of transcatheter options. At some point, our patients run out of road, and we owe it to them to think about that destination before we start the journey.
