Title: Blog about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

# Coronary Access in the Triple-Stented Aorta: Planning for Future Interventions

Here's a case that should make every structural interventionalist pause and think about the long game. A patient in his late 70s presents for his **third** transcatheter aortic valve implantation — a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV — with a critical twist: this patient has three-vessel coronary artery disease and multiple prior PCIs. The case forces us to confront an uncomfortable reality we're increasingly facing in structural heart disease: with each successive valve-in-valve procedure, we're literally stacking the deck against future coronary access.

## The Case That Stops You in Your Tracks

Let's set the stage. This patient had a 27 mm Lotus valve implanted in 2014, followed by a TAV-in-TAV with a 23 mm Evolut in 2022 for structural valve deterioration. Three months later, he developed prosthetic valve endocarditis with *Enterococcus faecalis*, treated successfully with a full course of ampicillin-based therapy. By 2024, severe restenosis and regurgitation necessitated a third intervention — an Acurate neo2 implanted in yet another valve-in-valve-in-valve configuration.

But here's what keeps me up at night: this patient had undergone **four prior coronary interventions** across his RCA, LAD, and circumflex between 2009 and 2014. He's the exact patient who will almost certainly need coronary access again, potentially during an acute coronary syndrome when seconds matter. Every procedural decision made during his TAVI journey directly impacts whether that future coronary access will be straightforward, challenging, or frankly impossible.

## The Cumulative Mesh Burden Problem

The discussion of coronary obstruction risk in TAV-in-TAV is now relatively mature — we worry about the neo-skirt effect, we measure valve-to-coronary distances, and we sometimes deploy coronary protection strategies. But the case report highlights a distinct and arguably underappreciated problem: **cumulative mesh burden**.

When you have three overlapping valve frames, you've created a triple-layered sieve between the aorta and the coronary ostia. Even if each individual valve's struts don't directly obstruct the coronaries, the aggregate effect creates a geometric puzzle that can make selective coronary engagement extraordinarily difficult. The case authors explicitly note that "although commissural alignment was attempted, the cumulative mesh burden may still present challenges for future coronary interventions."

This isn't theoretical. We're talking about a patient who has already required multiple coronary interventions and will almost certainly need them again. If he shows up with an inferior STEMI in five years, can you reliably engage his RCA? If he develops unstable angina, can you interventionally manage his disease? These questions need answers **before** we implant the first valve, let alone the third.

## Commissionural Alignment: More Than Just Haemodynamics

The procedural team in this case made a deliberate choice to prioritize commissural alignment during deployment of the Acurate neo2, guided by preprocedural CT and fluoroscopic landmarks. This wasn't just about optimizing haemodynamics — though that mattered given the triple-stented anatomy and risk of prosthesis-patient mismatch. It was fundamentally about preserving a lifeline.

The Acurate neo2 was selected partly because its design supports commissural alignment, with supra-annular leaflet positioning and relatively low frame height. The team used CT modelling to align the device with the Evolut valve leaflets, maximizing effective orifice area while attempting to keep commissural posts clear of coronary ostia.

But here's the hard truth: perfect commissural alignment with one valve doesn't guarantee alignment when you add a second or third. Each valve has its own geometry, and the interaction between multiple frames is unpredictable. The authors acknowledge this limitation, noting that full three-dimensional expansion analysis using dedicated software wasn't performed.

## High Implantation as a Deliberate Strategy

One technical pearl worth emphasizing from this case: the team deliberately employed a **high implantation strategy** for the third valve. By implanting the Acurate neo2 at the level of the Evolut valve leaflets, they achieved two goals simultaneously. First, they fixed the pre-existing leaflets against the stent frame, preventing further reduction of the effective orifice area from inward leaflet folding. Second, and critically for coronary access, they minimized neo-skirt formation below the coronary ostia.

This approach wasn't improvised. Preprocedural CT assessment explicitly evaluated coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, and the anticipated relationship between the neo-skirt and coronary ostia. The team determined they could proceed without coronary protection — but only because they had done the imaging work to prove it.

## The Lifetime Management Imperative

This case exemplifies why we need to shift our thinking from "procedure-focused" to "lifetime management." The authors correctly note that "with each transcatheter reintervention, considerations such as frame layering, commissural alignment, coronary reaccess and residual effective orifice area become increasingly complex."

The uncomfortable reality is that options become progressively more limited with each intervention. If this patient's current prosthesis fails prematurely, surgical explantation would likely require total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement — an operation of magnitude that borders on prohibitive given his EuroSCORE II of 27.36% and extensive comorbidities. Emerging catheter-based solutions like bioprosthetic valve fracture or BASILICA might be considered, but as the authors note, their feasibility and safety in triple-stented configurations remain uncertain.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Coronary access must be a primary consideration, not an afterthought**, in any TAV-in-TAV case, especially in patients with known coronary artery disease — plan for the STEMI that hasn't happened yet.
- **Cumulative mesh burden from multiple overlapping valve frames creates exponentially increasing complexity for coronary engagement**, even when individual valve positions appear acceptable.
- **Commissionural alignment should be actively pursued at every intervention**, using CT-guided planning and appropriate valve selection — but recognize that alignment becomes harder to achieve and maintain with each successive valve.
- **High implantation strategies can preserve coronary ostia patency** by minimizing neo-skirt formation below coronary ostia, but require meticulous preprocedural CT planning.
- **Lifetime management planning should begin at the first TAVI**, particularly in younger patients or those with coronary disease — every valve choice has downstream consequences.

## The Bottom Line

This patient's successful TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV with preserved coronary flow and 1-year durability is a technical triumph. But it should also serve as a warning shot. As TAVI indications expand and patients live longer with their prostheses, we will see more multi-valve configurations. The patients who need these interventions are often the same ones who will require coronary access during their most vulnerable moments. If we don't prioritize coronary preservation in our procedural planning — starting with that first valve — we may be trading excellent haemodynamics today for an unsurvivable coronary occlusion tomorrow.
