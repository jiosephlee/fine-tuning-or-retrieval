Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome"

## Clinical Presentation and Medical History

The patient, a man in his late 70s, presented to a tertiary referral center with symptoms of progressive exertional dyspnea, a spastic sensation in the left chest during physical activity (limiting walking to 200–300 meters), dizziness, and fatigue. These symptoms are classic manifestations of aortic stenosis, representing the clinical consequence of fixed left ventricular outflow tract obstruction. In a patient with known prosthetic aortic valve history, such symptom onset should immediately prompt evaluation for prosthetic valve dysfunction. The combination of dyspnea and chest discomfort reflects elevated left ventricular filling pressures and increased myocardial oxygen demand, while dizziness suggests reduced cardiac output during exertion. The symptom cluster in this context indicates hemodynamically significant valve obstruction and warrants urgent investigation.

His medical history exemplifies the multi-system comorbidity burden commonly encountered in patients selected for transcatheter aortic valve implantation. Extensive three-vessel coronary artery disease had been managed with multiple percutaneous coronary interventions spanning 2009 to 2014, involving the right coronary artery, left anterior descending artery, and circumflex artery. Third-degree atrioventricular block necessitated permanent pacemaker implantation in 2014, a concurrent finding often seen in severe aortic stenosis due to calcific infiltration of the conduction system. Additional comorbidities included arterial hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding beginning in 2022. This comorbidity profile underscores the fundamental rationale for transcatheter intervention: surgical aortic valve replacement would carry prohibitive risk in such a patient.

The timeline of aortic valve interventions reveals a decade-long evolution of structural valve disease. In 2014, the patient underwent his initial transcatheter aortic valve implantation with a 27 mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific) for severe native aortic stenosis. By December 2021, he had developed symptomatic bioprosthetic valve stenosis, with echocardiography demonstrating severe prosthetic stenosis. By early 2022, the mean transvalvular gradient had risen to 83 mm Hg with left ventricular ejection fraction declining from 54% to 42%—a critical degree of obstruction with secondary myocardial depression. In May 2022, a valve-in-valve procedure was performed using a 23 mm Evolut prosthesis (Medtronic), successfully reducing the gradient and improving ejection fraction to 54%. However, the procedure was complicated by a pulsatile femoral hematoma with pseudoaneurysm formation requiring open vascular surgical repair.

Three months after the second TAVI, in September 2022, the clinical course was further complicated by prosthetic valve endocarditis. The patient presented with persistent fever and worsening heart failure symptoms. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed severe restenosis with a mean gradient of 35 mm Hg, and blood cultures identified Enterococcus faecalis as the causative organism. Prosthetic valve endocarditis carries substantial morbidity and mortality, and management requires prolonged antimicrobial therapy with careful monitoring for complications including abscess formation, paravalvular extension, and septic embolization. The patient completed a full treatment course consisting of six weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based therapy followed by oral antimicrobials. Resolution of infection was confirmed through clinical assessment, repeated blood cultures, transesophageal echocardiography, and computed tomography, all demonstrating no evidence of active endocarditis prior to the third intervention.

Following successful treatment of endocarditis, the patient experienced partial symptom resolution. However, routine follow-up echocardiography in January 2024 revealed deteriorating prosthetic valve function with severe aortic stenosis and moderate-to-severe regurgitation. The mean gradient had increased to 49 mm Hg, with left ventricular ejection fraction recovering to 66%—indicating that myocardial function had normalized but valve dysfunction persisted. This pattern of symptom recurrence after a period of stability following treated endocarditis suggests that infection-related leaflet injury, combined with structural degeneration of multiple overlapping bioprosthetic valves, had resulted in progressive hemodynamic compromise. At the time of admission for the third intervention in June 2024, transthoracic echocardiography confirmed grade 2–3 intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation, a mean gradient of 49 mm Hg, and a severely reduced aortic valve area of 0.5 cm², establishing the indication for reintervention.

## Pathophysiology of Prosthetic Valve Deterioration and Endocarditis

Understanding why this patient developed progressive prosthetic valve dysfunction requires familiarity with two intersecting processes: structural valve deterioration (SVD) and prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). In this case, the patient's Lotus valve, implanted in 2014, demonstrated SVD after approximately 7 years—a timeline consistent with the expected durability of bioprosthetic valves. SVD encompasses the intrinsic degeneration of valve leaflets over time, characterized by collagen fiber disruption, calcification, and progressive leaflet thickening or tearing. These changes lead to either stenosis, from reduced leaflet mobility, or regurgitation, from incomplete coaptation. By late 2021, echocardiography revealed severe bioprosthetic stenosis with a mean gradient of 83 mm Hg, reflecting classic SVD pathophysiology.

The TAV-in-TAV procedure performed in May 2022, implanting a 23 mm Evolut valve within the failed Lotus prosthesis, introduced new anatomical constraints. Valve-in-valve procedures inherently reduce the effective orifice area (EOA) because the new valve must fit within the existing frame. The Evolut valve's skirting and leaflets occupy space within the Lotus frame, potentially creating prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM). In this patient, the rapid development of symptoms and the subsequent diagnosis of PVE only 3 months after the TAV-in-TAV procedure suggests that the hemodynamic burden of the valve-in-valve configuration may have contributed to clinical decompensation, even before infection occurred.

PVE represents a distinct and serious complication of prosthetic valves. Enterococcus faecalis, identified in this patient's blood cultures, is a gram-positive organism that accounts for approximately 5–10% of PVE cases. Enterococcal endocarditis is notable for its indolent presentation, often developing over weeks to months, and its association with healthcare exposures, including gastrointestinal procedures. The pathogenesis of PVE involves bacterial adherence to the prosthetic surface, where biofilm formation protects organisms from both host immunity and antimicrobial penetration. On bioprosthetic valves, infection typically involves the leaflets themselves, causing vegetations, leaflet perforation, or destructive lesions that accelerate structural failure.

In this patient, the interaction between prior infection and mechanical failure exemplifies how PVE compounds SVD. Even after successful antimicrobial therapy—6 weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based treatment followed by oral therapy—residual damage to the Evolut leaflets likely contributed to the severe restenosis observed by January 2024. The infection may have caused leaflet injury, fibrosis, or mechanical restriction that persisted beyond bacteriological cure. By the time of the third intervention, the minimum lumen diameter within the Evolut prosthesis had narrowed to 13.7 mm, resulting in a severely reduced aortic valve area of 0.5 cm² and moderate-to-severe regurgitation.

The choice of valve types in this case carries important implications for durability and reintervention. The Lotus valve, discontinued from the market, featured a mechanically adaptive frame designed to minimize paravalvular regurgitation, but its bulky frame created challenges for future interventions. The Evolut valve, a self-expanding supra-annular device, offers favorable hemodynamics but requires careful positioning to avoid coronary obstruction. The Acurate neo2, selected for the third procedure, features supra-annular leaflets and a relatively low frame height—characteristics that influenced the decision-making process for managing triple-stented anatomy.

The hemodynamic consequences of progressive prosthetic stenosis in this patient followed a predictable physiological sequence. As the effective orifice area decreased, left ventricular afterload increased, initially causing a decline in ejection fraction to 42%. The mean gradient of 83 mm Hg before the second TAVI indicated severe stenosis. After successful TAV-in-TAV, the ejection fraction improved to 54%, demonstrating reversible afterload-mediated dysfunction. However, the subsequent episode of endocarditis and the development of combined stenosis and regurgitation necessitated the third intervention. Post-procedural echocardiography revealed severe PPM with an indexed aortic valve area of 0.4 cm²/m²—highlighting the unavoidable hemodynamic compromises inherent in sequential valve-in-valve procedures.

## Diagnostic Workup and Pre-Procedural Imaging

The evaluation of this patient prior to his third transcatheter aortic valve intervention illustrates the critical importance of systematic, multimodality imaging in complex structural heart disease. Following his treatment for prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) caused by *Enterococcus faecalis*, the patient developed recurrent symptoms and worsening prosthetic valve function, necessitating a comprehensive diagnostic workup to establish candidacy for repeat intervention.

Echocardiography served as the primary tool for initial assessment and ongoing surveillance. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) performed on admission in June 2024 revealed the hallmarks of severe bioprosthetic valve dysfunction: grade 2–3 intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation, a mean transvalvular gradient of 49 mm Hg, and a markedly reduced aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.5 cm². For medical students learning to interpret prosthetic valve function, it is essential to understand that these parameters do not exist in isolation. The AVA was calculated using the continuity equation, which relates flow through the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) to flow across the prosthetic valve. In the setting of a transcatheter valve, this calculation requires careful measurement of the LVOT diameter and Doppler-derived velocity-time integrals. A mean gradient exceeding 40 mm Hg in conjunction with an AVA below 1.0 cm² signifies severe prosthetic stenosis, while the concomitant regurgitation indicated mixed dysfunction—a particularly unfavorable hemodynamic profile.

Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) played a distinct and complementary role. While TTE provided hemodynamic data, TEE was essential for excluding active infective endocarditis—a critical step before proceeding with any re-intervention. In patients with prior PVE, the risk of persistent or recurrent infection must be definitively ruled out, as implanting a new prosthesis in an infected field carries catastrophic consequences. TEE in this patient confirmed the absence of vegetations, abscesses, or paravalvular complications, corroborating the clinical assessment that antimicrobial therapy had successfully eradicated the infection.

The interpretation of hemodynamic parameters in this case requires nuanced understanding. The mean transvalvular gradient of 49 mm Hg represented severe obstruction, but clinicians must recognize that gradients are flow-dependent. The patient's left ventricular ejection fraction had recovered to 66%, indicating adequate flow conditions for reliable gradient measurement. In low-output states, gradients may underestimate stenosis severity, making the calculated AVA and Doppler velocity index (DVI) valuable adjuncts. The DVI, the ratio of LVOT velocity-time integral to aortic velocity-time integral, provides a dimensionless measure of valve function; values below 0.25 indicate significant stenosis. Post-procedurally, this patient's DVI of 0.24 reflected severe prosthesis-patient mismatch—a consequence of the triple-stented anatomy that will be discussed further.

Multislice computed tomography (MSCT) was the cornerstone of procedural planning, transforming the case from a seemingly impossible technical challenge to a feasible intervention. MSCT revealed a minimum lumen diameter of only 13.7 mm within the previously implanted Evolut prosthesis—confirming severe hemodynamic compromise and establishing the anatomical target for the third valve. Beyond measuring the landing zone, CT allowed detailed analysis of the complex triple-stented anatomy. The patient had a 27 mm Lotus valve implanted in 2014, followed by a 23 mm Evolut valve in 2022. Understanding how these two frames interacted—their relative positions, expansion characteristics, and overlap patterns—was essential for determining where a third valve could be safely anchored and how it would expand.

Coronary obstruction risk assessment represented perhaps the most critical aspect of CT planning. In TAV-in-TAV procedures, the leaflets of the failed prosthesis are pushed outward against the frame when a new valve is deployed. This creates a "neoskirt" of tissue that can obstruct coronary ostia if they lie at a similar height. With three overlapping valve frames, this risk is magnified substantially. MSCT enabled measurement of coronary ostial height relative to the valve landing zone, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, and valve-to-coronary distance. These measurements guided the decision to implant the third valve at a relatively high position—aligning it with the Evolut valve leaflets—to minimize neoskirt formation below the coronary ostia. This strategy preserved coronary flow and maintained future coronary access without requiring coronary protection devices during the procedure.

The selection of the Acurate neo2 prosthesis was directly informed by CT findings. This valve's supra-annular leaflet positioning, relatively low frame height, and favorable hemodynamic profile in narrow anatomies made it suitable for this challenging configuration. Additionally, its design facilitated commissural alignment—a consideration for preserving coronary access through the stent cells of all three frames.

Surgical risk stratification completed the pre-procedural evaluation. The patient's EuroSCORE II of 27.36% indicated a very high surgical mortality risk. This score, combined with his multiple comorbidities—COPD, type 2 diabetes mellitus, three-vessel coronary artery disease with prior interventions, third-degree AV block requiring permanent pacemaker, and recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding—rendered open surgery prohibitive. Furthermore, the presence of two well-integrated transcatheter valves meant that surgical explantation would require total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement, dramatically increasing operative complexity. The heart team's multidisciplinary review confirmed that transcatheter intervention, despite its technical challenges, represented the only viable option for this patient.

This case demonstrates that successful outcomes in complex structural valve interventions depend fundamentally on meticulous pre-procedural imaging and planning. For trainees, the lesson is clear: the procedure itself represents only the final step in a comprehensive diagnostic process that defines feasibility, guides strategy, and anticipates complications before they occur.

## Procedural Strategy and Technical Execution

The decision to proceed with a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation in this patient required careful consideration of multiple anatomical and clinical factors. With two previously implanted transcatheter valves—a Lotus valve from 2014 and an Evolut valve from 2022—the patient presented with what is often termed "triple-stented anatomy." This configuration presents unique technical challenges that do not exist in first-time TAVI procedures or even standard TAV-in-TAV interventions. The heart team approach was essential in this case, bringing together interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, imaging specialists, and anesthesiologists to evaluate the risks and benefits of each potential strategy.

The primary decision point was whether to pursue surgical valve replacement or a third transcatheter intervention. Surgical aortic valve replacement after two prior TAVI procedures is technically demanding because the stent frames of transcatheter valves become incorporated into the aortic wall and surrounding tissues over time. In this patient, the EuroSCORE II of 27.36% reflected prohibitive surgical risk, compounded by his comorbidities including COPD, diabetes, and recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding. The heart team determined that surgical explantation would likely require total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement, a procedure associated with substantial morbidity and mortality. Given these considerations, a transcatheter approach was selected as the most viable option.

Valve selection represents a critical step in complex TAV-in-TAV procedures. The Acurate neo2 valve was chosen for several specific design characteristics that addressed the patient's anatomical constraints. First, the supra-annular leaflet positioning of this valve allows the functional leaflets to sit above the plane of the native annulus, which can optimize hemodynamic performance when implanted within a narrow anatomy. Second, the relatively low frame height reduces the amount of additional stent material projecting into the ascending aorta, which is particularly important when multiple overlapping valve frames are already present. Third, the Acurate neo2 design facilitates commissural alignment—a technique where the commissural posts of the new valve are oriented to align with those of the existing valve(s), thereby preserving future coronary access.

The implantation strategy was deliberately planned to optimize the effective orifice area in this triple-valve configuration. The third valve was implanted at the level of the Evolut valve leaflets rather than at a lower position within the Evolut frame. This "high implantation" strategy serves several purposes. When a new valve is deployed, the existing bioprosthetic leaflets are pushed outward against the stent frame of the prior valve. If the new valve is implanted too low, the pinned leaflets of the previous valve can create a "neo-skirt"—an additional layer of tissue that further narrows the effective orifice area. By implanting at the level of the Evolut leaflets, the procedure minimized this neo-skirt formation and reduced the risk of severe prosthesis-patient mismatch.

Preprocedural CT imaging was essential for procedural planning. The CT allowed measurement of the minimum lumen diameter within the Evolut prosthesis (13.7 mm), which confirmed severe hemodynamic compromise and helped guide valve sizing. Equally important was the assessment of coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, and valve-to-coronary distance. These measurements informed whether coronary protection would be necessary during valve deployment. The risk of coronary artery obstruction during TAV-in-TAV procedures is significantly elevated because the leaflets of the existing valve can cover the coronary ostia when pushed aside by the new prosthesis. In this case, CT modeling demonstrated adequate clearance, and the procedure was performed without coronary protection devices—a decision that proved successful.

The procedure was performed under local anesthesia with both left radial and left femoral arterial access. The radial access provided a route for aortography and coronary monitoring, while the femoral access was used for valve delivery. After positioning a Safari guidewire in the left ventricle, balloon pre-dilation was performed using a 22/40 mm catheter under rapid ventricular pacing at 180 beats per minute. This pre-dilation step serves to crack any calcification or fibrosis within the existing valve leaflets and create a more receptive landing zone for the new prosthesis. Simultaneous aortography during pre-dilation confirmed that coronary flow remained unobstructed.

The Acurate neo2 valve was then deployed with careful attention to commissural alignment, using preprocedural CT imaging and fluoroscopic landmarks as guides. After deployment, post-dilation was performed using a 20/40 mm balloon under rapid pacing to optimize valve expansion. This post-dilation step likely contributed to improved hemodynamics by causing further radial expansion of the Evolut frame and enhancing leaflet motion. Final aortography confirmed correct valve positioning, preserved coronary perfusion, and absence of both perivalvular and transvalvular regurgitation.

The immediate post-procedural echocardiogram revealed a mean gradient of 67 mm Hg with an AVA of 0.9 cm², consistent with severe prosthesis-patient mismatch. However, this initial result improved substantially over the following month, with the mean gradient decreasing to 27 mm Hg and AVA increasing to 1.1 cm². This phenomenon of gradient improvement over time has been observed in other TAV-in-TAV cases and may relate to continued frame expansion, resolution of edema, and adaptive remodeling of the stacked valve configuration. The case demonstrates that even in the most complex anatomical circumstances, careful CT-guided planning, strategic valve selection, and meticulous procedural technique can achieve acceptable hemodynamic results in patients who would otherwise have limited treatment options.

## Outcomes, Follow-up, and Clinical Implications

The assessment of procedural success in this case was conducted according to the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 (VARC-3) criteria, which provide standardized definitions for evaluating transcatheter aortic valve interventions. These criteria encompass hemodynamic function, residual regurgitation, and procedure-related complications, allowing for systematic comparison across cases and institutions. Understanding these standardized endpoints is essential for interpreting outcomes in complex valve-in-valve procedures.

Immediately following the third transcatheter valve implantation, transthoracic echocardiography revealed a complex hemodynamic picture. The valve leaflets were mobile with complete opening, and there was no aortic regurgitation—demonstrating successful technical deployment. However, the mean transvalvular gradient was strikingly elevated at 67 mm Hg, with a calculated aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.9 cm² and an indexed AVA of 0.4 cm²/m². The Doppler velocity index (DVI) was 0.24. These parameters are consistent with severe prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM), defined as an indexed AVA less than 0.65 cm²/m². This phenomenon occurs when the effective orifice area of the prosthesis is too small relative to the patient's body surface area, resulting in elevated gradients despite normal valve function.

In valve-in-valve procedures, PPM is a particularly important consideration. The pre-existing prosthetic frames create fixed boundaries that limit the maximum achievable orifice area, as the new valve must fit within the old. In this case, with three overlapping stent frames, the cumulative reduction in effective orifice area was substantial. The early high gradients raised concern about the durability of the result and the potential for persistent outflow obstruction.

Notably, the hemodynamic parameters improved substantially over the subsequent weeks. At one month, the mean gradient had decreased from 67 mm Hg to 27 mm Hg, and the AVA had increased to 1.1 cm². This improvement likely reflects several mechanisms: continued expansion of the self-expanding valve frame, resolution of peri-procedural edema, and left ventricular adaptation to the new hemodynamic load. Additionally, high-pressure balloon post-dilation may have contributed to further radial expansion of the Evolut frame, improving leaflet coaptation and central flow dynamics. Left ventricular remodeling was observed, with reduction in chamber volumes—a favorable sign of reverse remodeling in response to afterload reduction.

By 3.5 months, the patient remained asymptomatic with good exercise tolerance and no signs of heart failure or recurrent infection. At one year, clinical stability was maintained: the mean gradient was 26 mm Hg, AVA was 1.1 cm², and ejection fraction was preserved at 65%. There was no evidence of valve-related complications, regurgitation, or recurrent endocarditis. This trajectory illustrates an important principle in valve-in-valve interventions: early post-procedural gradients may not reflect long-term hemodynamic performance, and a period of observation is warranted before concluding that PPM will be clinically significant.

The distinction between true PPM and transiently elevated gradients is clinically relevant. Severe PPM has been associated with increased mortality and heart failure hospitalization, but these associations are derived primarily from surgical valve replacement data. In transcatheter valve-in-valve procedures, the relationship between indexed AVA and clinical outcomes is less well established, and many patients tolerate moderate elevations in gradient without symptoms. In this case, despite meeting echocardiographic criteria for severe PPM initially, the patient became and remained asymptomatic, suggesting that the hemodynamic burden was physiologically tolerable in the context of his overall clinical picture.

Surveillance strategies for patients with multiple transcatheter valves require careful consideration. Standard follow-up protocols include clinical assessment and transthoracic echocardiography at baseline, 30 days, one year, and annually thereafter. However, patients with complex multi-prosthesis anatomy may benefit from more intensive monitoring, given the potential for late complications including structural deterioration, thrombosis, or infection. Computed tomography can provide additional information about valve positioning, frame integrity, and coronary access, though its routine use in surveillance is not standardized. In this patient, the absence of recurrent endocarditis at one year is particularly reassuring given his prior history of prosthetic valve endocarditis.

This case underscores the growing importance of lifetime management principles in structural valve disease. With expanding indications for transcatheter aortic valve implantation and increasing longevity among treated patients, the population requiring repeat interventions is growing. Each transcatheter reintervention adds complexity: frame layering can impair coronary access, reduce effective orifice area, and increase the risk of coronary artery obstruction. These considerations argue for early discussion of procedural trajectories at the time of initial intervention, particularly in younger patients or those with long life expectancy. Valve selection, implantation depth, and commissural alignment at the first procedure can influence the feasibility and safety of future reinterventions.

If the current prosthesis were to fail prematurely, treatment options would be extremely limited. Surgical explantation would be technically demanding due to the presence of three valve frames integrated into the aortic root, likely necessitating total aortic root and ascending aorta replacement—a high-risk procedure in this patient with prohibitive surgical risk. Emerging catheter-based techniques, such as bioprosthetic valve fracture or BASILICA (bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction), represent potential options, though their feasibility and safety in triple-stented configurations remain uncertain. The limited therapeutic reserve in this case highlights the importance of maximizing the durability of each intervention and underscores the consequences of sequential valve-in-valve procedures on future treatment options.
