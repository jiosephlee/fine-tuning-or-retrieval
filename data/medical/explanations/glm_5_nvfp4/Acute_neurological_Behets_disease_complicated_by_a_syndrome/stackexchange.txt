Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

### [Clinical Pearls] What are the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease, and what score is required to classify a patient as having definite disease?

**Clinical Teaching Answer**

The International Criteria for Behçet's Disease (ICBD), revised in 2013, utilize a point-based scoring system to classify patients with Behçet's disease. The criteria and their assigned point values are as follows:

- **Oral aphthosis:** 2 points
- **Genital aphthosis:** 2 points
- **Ocular lesions** (uveitis or retinal vasculitis): 2 points
- **Skin lesions** (pseudofolliculitis, papulopustular lesions, or erythema nodosum–like lesions): 1 point
- **Neurological involvement:** 1 point
- **Vascular manifestations** (venous thrombosis, arterial thrombosis, or aneurysm): 1 point
- **Positive pathergy test:** 1 point

**A total score of ≥4 points classifies a patient as having definite Behçet's disease.**

In the presented case, the patient met criteria with a score of 5 points: oral aphthae (2 points), genital ulceration (2 points), and folliculitis-like rash (1 point). Notably, he also had HLA-B51 positivity and aseptic meningitis consistent with acute neuro-Behçet's disease, though these findings supported rather than established the diagnosis. The ICBD emphasizes clinical manifestations and does not require HLA-B51 positivity for classification, reflecting its role as a supportive rather than diagnostic marker.

### [Morning Report] This patient presented with hyponatremia (serum sodium 124 mmol/L), low serum osmolality (267 mOsm/kg), elevated urine osmolality (900 mOsm/kg), and elevated urinary sodium (88 mmol/L). What are the essential diagnostic criteria for SIADH, and what conditions must be excluded before establishing this diagnosis?

### Teaching Answer

The diagnosis of SIADH requires meeting essential biochemical criteria while systematically excluding other causes of hyponatremia. In this case, the patient demonstrated the classic diagnostic triad: **hypo-osmolar hyponatremia** (serum sodium 124 mmol/L, serum osmolality 267 mOsm/kg), **inappropriately concentrated urine** (urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg), and **elevated urinary sodium excretion** (88 mmol/L). Additionally, the patient was **clinically euvolemic** on examination.

**Essential Diagnostic Criteria for SIADH (Bartter-Schwartz criteria):**

1. **Hypo-osmolar hyponatremia** — Serum osmolality <275 mOsm/kg with corresponding hyponatremia
2. **Inappropriate urine concentration** — Urine osmolality >100 mOsm/kg (often >300 mOsm/kg) despite low serum osmolality, indicating failure to maximally dilute urine
3. **Elevated urinary sodium** — Typically >30 mmol/L with normal salt and water intake, reflecting the sodium-wasting state that accompanies volume expansion
4. **Clinical euvolemia** — Absence of signs of hypovolemia (orthostasis, tachycardia, dry mucous membranes) or hypervolemia (edema, ascites)

**Conditions That Must Be Excluded:**

Before diagnosing SIADH, you must rule out conditions that can mimic this biochemical pattern:

1. **Hypovolemic hyponatremia** — GI losses, burns, or third-spacing cause volume depletion, triggering appropriate ADH release. Urinary sodium is typically <20 mmol/L unless the patient is on diuretics or has salt-wasting nephropathy.

2. **Hypervolemic hyponatremia** — Heart failure, cirrhosis, and nephrotic syndrome cause effective arterial underfilling with appropriate ADH secretion. These patients demonstrate clinical signs of volume overload.

3. **Adrenal insufficiency** — Cortisol deficiency removes negative feedback on ADH release. This patient's serum cortisol was appropriately elevated at 42 µg/dL, excluding this diagnosis.

4. **Hypothyroidism** — Can cause hyponatremia through reduced cardiac output and GFR. This patient had normal thyroid function (TSH 1.11 mIU/L, free T4 1.25 ng/dL).

5. **Diuretic use** — Particularly thiazides, which impair urinary dilution without affecting concentration. Medication review is essential.

6. **Primary polydipsia** — Excessive water intake can overwhelm normal excretory capacity, though urine osmolality is typically maximally dilute (<100 mOsm/kg).

In this case, the diagnosis of SIADH secondary to acute neuro-Behçet's disease was established only after excluding infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune etiologies of CNS inflammation, confirming that the hyponatremia was a direct consequence of cytokine-mediated (likely IL-6) dysregulation of ADH secretion from CNS inflammation rather than medication effect or endocrine dysfunction.

### [Pathophysiology] The authors propose that IL-6 may mediate the association between acute neuro-Behçet's disease and SIADH. What is the proposed mechanism by which IL-6 contributes to inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion in the setting of CNS inflammation?

### Answer

The proposed mechanism centers on IL-6 as a proinflammatory cytokine that directly stimulates arginine vasopressin (AVP) release from the posterior pituitary and hypothalamus.

In acute neuro-Behçet's disease, the underlying pathophysiology involves aberrant monocyte activation with spontaneous IL-6 production. The authors note that elevated IL-6 levels have been detected in the cerebrospinal fluid of patients with acute neuro-BD, reflecting CNS inflammation. IL-6, in turn, acts as a secretagogue for antidiuretic hormone—several studies have demonstrated an association between elevated IL-6 and increased ADH release in neuroinflammatory states.

The mechanism is thought to involve direct stimulation of the hypothalamic-neurohypophyseal axis by IL-6, bypassing normal osmoregulatory feedback. The result is ADH secretion that persists despite hypo-osmolality, producing the classic SIADH phenotype: concentrated urine (900 mOsm/kg in this case), elevated urinary sodium (88 mmol/L), and hyponatremia with an inappropriately elevated ADH level (22.4 pg/mL).

This case supports this mechanism through several observations. First, the patient had marked systemic inflammation with a CRP of 180 mg/L, which the authors note may reflect underlying IL-6–mediated inflammation. Second, CSF analysis confirmed aseptic meningitis with lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein, consistent with CNS inflammation. Third, and most importantly, both the hyponatremia and the elevated ADH normalized in parallel with resolution of the neuroinflammatory process following colchicine therapy—which suppresses IL-6 production—without any direct SIADH-targeted treatment such as vasopressin receptor antagonists.

The temporal association between CNS inflammation and SIADH in this patient, with concurrent resolution after anti-inflammatory treatment, supports the proposed IL-6–mediated mechanism.

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a patient presenting with fever, headache, and lymphocytic pleocytosis in the CSF with negative bacterial cultures and viral PCR studies, what non-infectious causes should be considered in the differential diagnosis of aseptic meningitis?

**Clinical Teaching Answer: Non-Infectious Causes of Aseptic Meningitis**

When evaluating a patient with fever, headache, and lymphocytic pleocytosis in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) with negative infectious workup, the differential diagnosis must expand beyond infection to include important non-infectious etiologies.

The major non-infectious causes of aseptic meningitis fall into four categories:

**1. Drug-Induced Meningitis**
Common culprits include nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (especially ibuprofen), intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), certain antibiotics (trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, cephalosporins), and monoclonal antibodies. A careful medication history is essential. Drug-induced meningitis typically presents acutely after exposure and resolves with drug discontinuation. CSF shows lymphocytic predominance with negative cultures.

**2. Autoimmune and Inflammatory Conditions**
Several systemic inflammatory disorders can present with aseptic meningitis. These include:
- **Behçet's disease** (as illustrated in this case)
- Systemic lupus erythematosus
- Neurosarcoidosis
- Sjögren's syndrome
- Vogt-Koyanagi-Harada syndrome

In the presented case, the patient had oral aphthae, genital ulcers, and folliculitis-like rashes—mucocutaneous findings that, combined with the neurological presentation, led to the diagnosis of acute neuro-Behçet's disease. Notably, neuro-Behçet's can present as aseptic meningitis even in the absence of parenchymal brain lesions on MRI, as was seen here.

**3. Neoplastic Meningitis (Carcinomatous Meningitis)**
Leptomeningeal involvement by malignancy, including primary CNS lymphoma, leukemia, or metastatic solid tumors, can mimic infectious meningitis. CSF cytology is critical for diagnosis, though sensitivity is limited and may require repeated sampling.

**4. Miscellaneous Causes**
Chemical meningitis following neurosurgical procedures or myelography, as well as paraneoplastic syndromes, should be considered in appropriate clinical contexts.

**Key Clinical Pearls from This Case:**

First, this case demonstrates that a **normal brain MRI does not exclude non-infectious inflammatory meningitis**. The patient had clear clinical and CSF evidence of CNS inflammation despite unremarkable imaging.

Second, **systemic findings are critical diagnostic clues**. The recognition of oral aphthae, genital ulcers, and characteristic skin lesions was essential to establishing the diagnosis of Behçet's disease. Without these mucocutaneous features, the diagnosis would have been considerably more challenging.

Third, **hyponatremia in the setting of meningitis warrants evaluation for SIADH**. This patient's presentation with hyponatremia, low serum osmolality, and elevated urine osmolality and urinary sodium confirmed SIADH secondary to CNS inflammation—a complication that clinicians should actively seek in patients with meningitis, as it may manifest with subtle symptoms such as persistent hiccups.

In summary, when infectious studies are negative in a patient with aseptic meningitis, systematically consider drug exposures, autoimmune/inflammatory conditions (particularly Behçet's disease when mucocutaneous lesions are present), and malignancy. The diagnosis often hinges on careful clinical examination for systemic manifestations, as CSF and imaging findings alone may be non-specific.

### [Management] Corticosteroids are typically considered the mainstay of treatment for acute neuro-Behçet's disease. What is the mechanism of action of colchicine in Behçet's disease, and what factors might influence the decision to use colchicine monotherapy versus corticosteroids?

**Mechanism of Action of Colchicine in Behçet's Disease**

Colchicine exerts its anti-inflammatory effects through two primary mechanisms. First, it inhibits microtubule polymerization, which blocks neutrophil chemotaxis, adhesion, and activation—neutrophils are key mediators of the pathologic inflammation in Behçet's disease. Second, colchicine suppresses the production of proinflammatory cytokines, particularly interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and interleukin-6 (IL-6). This cytokine suppression is relevant because aberrant monocyte activation with spontaneous IL-6 production is a hallmark of Behçet's pathophysiology, and elevated IL-6 levels have been detected in the cerebrospinal fluid of patients with acute neuro-Behçet's disease.

**Factors Influencing Colchicine Monotherapy Versus Corticosteroids**

Corticosteroids remain the standard of care for acute neuro-Behçet's disease, particularly when parenchymal brain involvement is present. However, several factors may favor colchicine monotherapy in select cases:

1. **Disease severity and phenotype**: This patient presented with aseptic meningitis without parenchymal brain lesions on MRI. Acute neuro-Behçet's disease presenting as isolated meningoencephalitis may have a more favorable trajectory than cases with brainstem or basal ganglia involvement. The absence of focal neurologic deficits or radiographic abnormalities can support a more conservative approach.

2. **Corticosteroid contraindications**: The patient had poorly controlled type 2 diabetes mellitus with an HbA1c of 8.2% and hyperglycemia on presentation. Corticosteroids would have predictably worsened glycemic control, increasing the risk of infectious complications and metabolic derangement. In such patients, avoiding systemic corticosteroids is clinically advantageous.

3. **Predominant mucocutaneous disease**: When mucocutaneous manifestations dominate the clinical picture with milder neurological involvement, colchicine—which is highly effective for oral aphthae, genital ulcers, and folliculitis-like lesions—may provide adequate systemic anti-inflammatory effect while addressing the primary disease burden.

4. **Potential for spontaneous resolution**: As noted in this case, spontaneous recovery has been reported in neuro-Behçet's disease presenting with meningoencephalitis-like symptoms alone. This observation, combined with the favorable safety profile of colchicine compared to systemic corticosteroids, supports a trial of colchicine in carefully selected patients with close clinical monitoring.

This case demonstrates that colchicine monotherapy can be effective for acute neuro-Behçet's disease in patients with aseptic meningitis, no parenchymal lesions, and relative contraindications to corticosteroids. However, clinicians should be prepared to escalate to corticosteroids or other immunosuppressants if clinical deterioration occurs, as systematic reviews generally indicate that immunosuppressive agents are required for adequate disease control in neuro-Behçet's disease.

### [Clinical Pearls] This patient presented with persistent hiccups and was found to have rhabdomyolysis (CK 1349 U/L). How can these findings serve as clinical clues to the presence and severity of hyponatremia?

### Clinical Teaching Answer

Persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis are underrecognized but clinically important manifestations of severe hyponatremia, and their presence should prompt immediate evaluation of serum sodium.

**Persistent hiccups** in this context reflect central nervous system irritation from hypoosmolality. Cerebral edema from rapid or profound hyponatremia affects the brainstem and medullary centers, including the respiratory rhythm generators and phrenic nerve pathways. In this patient, hiccups persisted alongside headache and vertigo—symptoms consistent with CNS involvement from hyponatremia secondary to SIADH. Notably, hiccups have been specifically reported as a clinical clue to severe hyponatremia in the setting of CNS inflammation, including meningitis.

**Rhabdomyolysis**, evidenced by the elevated CK of 1349 U/L in this case, is an established but frequently overlooked complication of severe hyponatremia. The mechanism involves osmotically mediated muscle cell injury. Rapid shifts in extracellular osmolality cause cellular swelling, membrane instability, and subsequent leakage of intracellular contents, including CK. Importantly, this patient had no clinical evidence of muscle weakness, seizures, or immobilization—making hyponatremia itself the most likely precipitant of his rhabdomyolysis.

The severity of hyponatremia in this case—124 mmol/L with hypoosmolality (267 mOsm/kg)—correlates with the presence of these findings. Both manifestations serve as clinical indicators that the hyponatremia is not merely a laboratory abnormality but is causing end-organ effects. When hiccups or unexplained rhabdomyolysis are present, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for profound hyponatremia and evaluate promptly, particularly in patients with neuroinflammatory conditions where SIADH may coexist.
