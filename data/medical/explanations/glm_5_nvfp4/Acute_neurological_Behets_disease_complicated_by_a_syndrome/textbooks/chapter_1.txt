Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and Initial Assessment

When a patient presents to the emergency department with non-specific constitutional symptoms, the clinician's initial task is to systematically gather information that narrows a broad differential diagnosis. This case illustrates how careful attention to seemingly disparate findings—fever, headache, mucocutaneous lesions, and electrolyte abnormalities—can ultimately coalesce into a unifying diagnosis.

**Presenting Symptoms and Their Clinical Significance**

The patient, an Asian man in his 60s, presented with a three-day history of fever, headache, general malaise, loss of appetite, persistent hiccups, and arthralgia affecting the shoulders and ankles. He also reported oral aphthae. This constellation of symptoms spans multiple organ systems and initially suggests infectious, inflammatory, or neoplastic etiologies. Fever and headache raise immediate concern for central nervous system infection, particularly meningitis or encephalitis. The presence of persistent hiccups, while often overlooked, can be a valuable clinical clue. In this context, hiccups may reflect irritation of the medullary respiratory center or diaphragmatic stimulation, but they have also been reported as a manifestation of severe hyponatraemia. The arthralgia involving large joints raises the possibility of systemic inflammation, while oral aphthae, though common in the general population, warrant attention when occurring alongside other systemic symptoms.

**Medical History and Social Context**

The patient's medical history included untreated type 2 diabetes mellitus, with poor dietary and physical activity habits. This history is relevant for several reasons. First, diabetes mellitus is a risk factor for certain infections, and immunocompromised status broadens the differential diagnosis to include opportunistic pathogens. Second, the patient's HbA1c of 8.2% and plasma glucose of 14.2 mmol/L confirmed poor glycemic control, which may contribute to delayed wound healing and increased susceptibility to infections. Importantly, hyperglycemia can also cause pseudohyponatraemia, although the measured low serum osmolality in this case confirmed true hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia. His social history was notable for occasional alcohol consumption, no smoking, no recent travel, no animal exposures, and no recent sexual activity—factors that help exclude certain infectious etiologies such as tick-borne illnesses, zoonotic infections, or sexually transmitted infections.

**Physical Examination Findings**

On examination, the patient was alert but fatigued, with a temperature of 38.1°C, tachycardia at 119 beats per minute, and normal blood pressure of 135/73 mmHg. The respiratory rate was 19 breaths per minute with oxygen saturation of 96% on room air. The tachycardia likely reflected the systemic inflammatory response and fever, though thyroid dysfunction was later excluded. Crucially, physical examination revealed oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rashes on the face and trunk. These mucocutaneous findings, when considered together, would later prove essential to the diagnosis. Assessment of volume status is particularly important in the evaluation of hyponatraemia. This patient was clinically euvolemic, with no peripheral edema, no jugular venous distension, and no signs of dehydration. The euvolemic state, combined with hyponatraemia, narrowed the differential for hyponatraemia to conditions such as the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH), adrenal insufficiency, or hypothyroidism.

**Initial Laboratory Abnormalities**

Initial laboratory tests revealed several striking abnormalities. The serum sodium was markedly low at 124 mmol/L, indicating moderate-to-severe hyponatraemia. Inflammatory markers were significantly elevated, with C-reactive protein at 180 mg/L and erythrocyte sedimentation rate at 70 mm/hour, indicating a robust acute-phase response. The creatine kinase was elevated at 1349 U/L, along with mild elevations in aspartate aminotransferase and alanine aminotransferase. The elevated creatine kinase raised concern for rhabdomyolysis, which can occur as a complication of severe hyponatraemia due to osmotic shifts causing muscle cell swelling and injury. Renal function was normal, which helped exclude renal causes of hyponatraemia such as acute kidney injury or nephrotic syndrome. Hyperglycemia and elevated HbA1c reflected his known but untreated diabetes.

**Formulating the Initial Differential Diagnosis**

At this early stage, the differential diagnosis remained broad. The combination of fever, headache, and aseptic meningitis-like symptoms suggested central nervous system infection, including viral meningoencephalitis (herpes simplex virus, varicella zoster virus), bacterial meningitis, or tuberculous meningitis. Systemic inflammatory conditions such as autoimmune vasculitides, sarcoidosis, or Behçet's disease were considered, particularly given the mucocutaneous findings. Malignancy, including primary central nervous system lymphoma or carcinomatous meningitis, remained in the differential, especially given the patient's age and systemic symptoms. The hyponatraemia, later confirmed as SIADH, was initially approached cautiously, recognizing that it could result from CNS infection, malignancy, or inflammation.

This case underscores the importance of systematic clinical assessment when confronting non-specific symptoms. The initial presentation—with fever, headache, mucocutaneous lesions, and hyponatraemia—required the clinician to maintain a broad differential while methodically pursuing diagnostic clues through history, physical examination, and targeted investigations.
