Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

Chapter 2: ## Behçet's Disease and Neuro-Behçet's Disease: Clinical Framework

Behçet's disease (BD) is a chronic, multisystem inflammatory disorder that affects blood vessels of all sizes, making it essentially a systemic vasculitis. The underlying pathophysiology involves an aberrant immune response characterized by neutrophil hyperactivation and dysregulated cytokine production, particularly involving proinflammatory mediators such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and IL-1β. A strong genetic association exists with HLA-B51, which is present in our patient and serves as a supportive finding, though it is neither diagnostic nor required for diagnosis. The disease displays notable geographic predilection, occurring most frequently along the ancient Silk Road, including Turkey, the Middle East, and East Asia—regions where our Asian patient may have heightened genetic susceptibility.

The International Criteria for Behçet's Disease provide a standardized, point-based scoring system that allows clinicians to establish a diagnosis with confidence. A score of 4 or more points indicates definite BD. Oral aphthous ulcers, the hallmark of BD, are assigned 2 points, as are genital ulcers. Skin lesions—including folliculitis-like eruptions, erythema nodosum, or acne-like nodules—contribute 1 point, while ocular involvement such as uveitis adds 2 points. Our patient presented with oral aphthae (2 points), a genital ulcer that appeared on day 5 of hospitalization (2 points), and folliculitis-like rashes (1 point), yielding a total score of 5 points and meeting criteria for definite BD. The emergence of the genital ulcer was diagnostically pivotal, as it confirmed a systemic process rather than isolated oral lesions.

The core clinical manifestations of BD reflect its vasculitic nature. Recurrent oral aphthae are nearly universal and typically present first, often years before other organ involvement. Genital ulcers, while less frequent, are more specific for BD and tend to be painful, deep, and scarring. Cutaneous findings include folliculitis-like lesions, erythema nodosum, and pathergy—a hypersensitivity reaction where minor skin trauma induces pustule formation, though our patient did not exhibit this finding. Ocular disease, particularly uveitis, can cause significant morbidity including blindness and was notably absent in this case. The mucocutaneous triad of oral aphthae, genital ulcers, and skin lesions formed the clinical foundation for diagnosis before neurological symptoms fully declared themselves.

Neurological involvement, termed neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD), complicates approximately 10–20% of BD cases and represents one of the disease's most serious manifestations. Classically, neuro-BD develops several years after the initial BD diagnosis, with major organ involvement typically appearing around 4 years after disease onset. This delayed pattern makes our case unusual: BD and neuro-BD were diagnosed concurrently, a scenario occurring in fewer than 5% of patients. Neuro-BD most commonly manifests as acute meningoencephalitis with parenchymal brain lesions, preferentially affecting the brainstem. Patients present with headache, fever, and altered mental status, sometimes accompanied by focal neurological deficits or behavioral changes. The differential diagnosis is broad, necessitating careful exclusion of infectious, neoplastic, and other autoimmune etiologies.

A critical lesson from this case is that acute neuro-BD may present as isolated aseptic meningitis without demonstrable parenchymal lesions on brain MRI. Our patient's MRI was normal, which initially complicated the diagnostic process. However, cerebrospinal fluid analysis revealed the characteristic profile of aseptic meningitis: mildly elevated opening pressure, increased protein concentration, and lymphocytic pleocytosis. The absence of radiological abnormalities does not exclude neuro-BD, particularly in early disease. Clinicians must recognize that meningoencephalitis-like presentations in the setting of compatible mucocutaneous findings warrant serious consideration of neuro-BD, even when imaging is unrevealing. This understanding proved essential in establishing the correct diagnosis and initiating appropriate therapy.
