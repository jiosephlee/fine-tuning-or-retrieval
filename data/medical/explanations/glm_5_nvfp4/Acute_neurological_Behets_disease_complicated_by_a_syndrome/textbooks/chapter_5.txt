Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

Chapter 5: ## Management Decisions, Treatment Rationale, and Clinical Outcomes

The management of Behçet's disease (BD) varies depending on the extent and severity of organ involvement. For mucocutaneous manifestations, colchicine is considered first-line therapy, while corticosteroids and immunosuppressive agents such as cyclophosphamide, azathioprine, or anti-tumor necrosis factor agents are typically reserved for severe organ involvement, including neurological disease. In neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD), corticosteroids are regarded as the mainstay of treatment for acute episodes, given the potential for significant morbidity from parenchymal brain lesions. However, treatment decisions must be individualized, and in patients presenting with isolated meningeal inflammation without evidence of parenchymal involvement, the necessity of aggressive immunosuppression is less clearly defined. This case presented a unique therapeutic scenario: a patient with newly diagnosed BD and acute neuro-BD complicated by SIADH, in whom colchicine monotherapy was initiated rather than corticosteroids.

Colchicine exerts its anti-inflammatory effects through multiple mechanisms that are particularly relevant to BD pathophysiology. The drug inhibits microtubule polymerization, which in turn blocks neutrophil adhesion, migration, and activation at sites of inflammation. Additionally, colchicine suppresses the production of proinflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and interleukin-6 (IL-6). This cytokine suppression is especially pertinent in neuro-BD, where elevated IL-6 levels in the cerebrospinal fluid have been documented and may contribute to both neuroinflammation and the dysregulated secretion of antidiuretic hormone. In this patient, the decision to use colchicine monotherapy was guided by several factors: the absence of parenchymal brain lesions on MRI, the clinical presentation limited to aseptic meningitis, and the goal of avoiding corticosteroid-related complications, particularly given the patient's poorly controlled diabetes mellitus.

The management of SIADH in this case followed established conservative principles. Fluid restriction to less than 800 mL daily was implemented, with careful daily monitoring of serum sodium concentrations. The avoidance of rapid sodium correction was critical, as overly rapid correction of hyponatraemia can result in osmotic demyelination syndrome, a potentially devastating neurological complication. Hypertonic saline and vasopressin receptor antagonists (vaptans) were not required, as the patient's sodium levels responded adequately to conservative measures. Importantly, the concurrent treatment of the underlying inflammatory process with colchicine likely facilitated the resolution of SIADH by addressing the presumed cytokine-mediated drive for inappropriate ADH secretion. This dual approach—managing the acute electrolyte disturbance while treating the underlying disease—proved effective.

The clinical response to colchicine monotherapy was notably rapid and comprehensive. Within one day of initiating colchicine, the patient's fever resolved and his appetite improved. Over the subsequent two weeks, all manifestations gradually resolved: vertigo, general malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions including oral aphthae and genital ulcers. By day 16, serum sodium had normalized to 136 mmol/L, and ADH levels had decreased from an elevated 22.4 pg/mL to 1.8 pg/mL. The elevated creatine kinase, which had indicated rhabdomyolysis as a complication of severe hyponatraemia, also returned to normal. This temporal relationship between colchicine initiation and the resolution of both neurological symptoms and SIADH supports a possible pathophysiological link between neuro-BD inflammation and inappropriate ADH secretion. It remains possible that spontaneous resolution contributed to the favorable outcome, as acute neuro-BD presenting solely as meningoencephalitis can be self-limiting. Nevertheless, the prompt and sustained response suggests that colchicine was an effective intervention in this case.

Long-term follow-up demonstrated durable remission. Colchicine therapy was continued for approximately four years, during which time the patient experienced no recurrence of BD symptoms or neurological manifestations. After his disease stabilized, treatment for his type 2 diabetes mellitus was initiated with a biguanide (metformin), and good glycaemic control was subsequently maintained. The absence of recurrence over this extended observation period reinforces the effectiveness of the initial therapeutic approach. This case provides clinically relevant lessons: first, colchicine monotherapy may be a viable option for acute neuro-BD presenting as isolated aseptic meningitis, particularly when corticosteroids are undesirable; second, SIADH associated with CNS inflammation may resolve with effective treatment of the underlying inflammatory process; and third, clinicians should recognize that acute neuro-BD and systemic BD can present concurrently, necessitating simultaneous attention to mucocutaneous and neurological manifestations.
