Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

Chapter 3: ## SIADH in the Context of Central Nervous System Inflammation

The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) emerged as a critical complication in this patient, transforming what might have been a straightforward evaluation of fever and headache into a complex diagnostic challenge. Understanding SIADH requires recognizing both its defining laboratory features and the physiological mechanisms that link central nervous system pathology to disordered water homeostasis.

The diagnosis of SIADH rests on fulfilling specific biochemical criteria while excluding other causes of hyponatraemia. This patient presented with hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia, with a serum sodium of 124 mmol/L and serum osmolality of 267 mOsm/kg—both below the lower limits of normal. Despite this hypo-osmolar state, his urine osmolality was inappropriately elevated at 900 mOsm/kg, indicating that the kidneys were continuing to concentrate urine rather than appropriately excreting free water. The urinary sodium concentration of 88 mmol/L further supported the diagnosis, reflecting continued sodium excretion in the absence of volume depletion. Importantly, clinical examination confirmed euvolemia, distinguishing SIADH from hypovolemic or hypervolemic causes of hyponatraemia. The elevated ADH level of 22.4 pg/mL provided direct confirmation of inappropriate hormone secretion.

Before establishing SIADH as the explanation for this patient's hyponatraemia, the treating team needed to exclude other endocrine causes. Adrenal insufficiency and hypothyroidism can both produce hyponatraemia through mechanisms distinct from SIADH. In this case, a normal serum cortisol level (42 µg/dL) effectively ruled out adrenal insufficiency, while normal thyroid-stimulating hormone and free T4 excluded hypothyroidism. These investigations are essential because glucocorticoid deficiency impairs free water excretion, and hypothyroidism reduces the renal response to ADH—both conditions that require specific treatments beyond simple fluid restriction.

The pathophysiology of SIADH in the setting of central nervous system inflammation involves complex neuroendocrine dysregulation. Antidiuretic hormone, also known as vasopressin, is normally synthesized in the hypothalamic supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei and released from the posterior pituitary in response to increased plasma osmolality or decreased effective circulating volume. In SIADH, this tight regulatory control is lost, leading to autonomous ADH secretion. CNS inflammation can trigger this dysregulation through several mechanisms. Inflammatory cytokines, particularly interleukin-6 (IL-6), have been shown to stimulate ADH release independently of osmotic cues. In neuro-Behçet's disease, aberrant monocyte activation leads to spontaneous IL-6 production, and elevated IL-6 levels have been documented in the cerebrospinal fluid of patients during acute neurological episodes. While IL-6 was not directly measured in this patient, his markedly elevated C-reactive protein (180 mg/L) may reflect the underlying IL-6-mediated inflammatory cascade that ultimately drove inappropriate ADH secretion.

The clinical manifestations of severe hyponatraemia in this case provided important diagnostic clues that might otherwise be overlooked. Persistent hiccups, one of this patient's presenting symptoms, have been recognized as a clinical marker of severe hyponatraemia secondary to CNS inflammation, including meningitis. Additionally, the elevated creatine kinase (1349 U/L) indicated rhabdomyolysis, a known but frequently underappreciated complication of profound hyponatraemia. These findings underscore that hyponatraemia can present with symptoms that may not immediately suggest an electrolyte disturbance. Clinicians evaluating patients with neurological symptoms should maintain a low threshold for checking serum sodium, particularly when unusual features such as intractable hiccups are present.

The etiologies of SIADH extend beyond CNS inflammation to include malignancy (particularly small cell lung carcinoma), pulmonary disease, and certain medications. In this patient, the absence of a chest radiograph abnormality, the unremarkable CT scans, and the lack of recent medication exposure that could induce SIADH supported a CNS inflammatory etiology. The temporal relationship between the onset of his neurological symptoms and the development of hyponatraemia, followed by the parallel resolution of both with treatment of his underlying Behçet's disease, strongly suggested that acute neuro-Behçet's disease was the causative factor.

Management of SIADH in this case was conservative, employing fluid restriction of less than 800 mL daily with careful monitoring of serum sodium. This approach avoided the risks of overly rapid sodium correction, which can result in osmotic demyelination syndrome. The normalization of sodium to 136 mmol/L by day 16, concurrent with improvement in his mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms, demonstrated that treating the underlying inflammatory condition can resolve SIADH without requiring vasopressin receptor antagonists or hypertonic saline.
