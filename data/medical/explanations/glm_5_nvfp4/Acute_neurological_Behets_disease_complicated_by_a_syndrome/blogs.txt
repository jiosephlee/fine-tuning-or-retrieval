Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# When Behçet's Hits the Brain First: Recognizing Concurrent Neuro-BD at Presentation

Here's a scenario that should make you pause: a patient presents with fever, headache, and hyponatremia. You find oral ulcers and a rash. Your differential is spinning through meningitis, viral encephalitis, maybe something autoimmune. But what if the answer requires you to connect mucocutaneous findings with neurological symptoms in a way that defies the typical timeline of a rare disease?

This case is a reminder that Behçet's disease doesn't always follow the textbook script. Neurological involvement typically develops years after the initial diagnosis—yet sometimes, it walks through the door at the same time.

## The Case That Breaks the Rules

A man in his 60s presented with three days of fever, headache, malaise, arthralgia, and persistent hiccups. He had oral aphthae on exam. Initial labs showed sodium of 124 mmol/L with a markedly elevated CRP. He was euvolemic. The team appropriately worked up his hyponatremia and found low serum osmolality (267 mOsm/kg), elevated urine osmolality (900 mOsm/kg), and elevated urinary sodium (88 mmol/L)—classic SIADH.

On day three, his headache and vertigo worsened. Brain MRI was normal. But CSF analysis told a different story: elevated opening pressure, increased protein, and lymphocytic pleocytosis with negative infectious workup. Then on day five, a painful genital ulcer appeared. Combined with his oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rash, the picture crystallized. HLA-B51 was positive. Using the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease, he scored 5 points—definite BD.

## Why This Matters

Here's what makes this case remarkable: neuro-BD and systemic BD were diagnosed simultaneously. In fewer than 5% of BD patients, neurological manifestations occur at presentation. Typically, major organ involvement develops around four years after initial diagnosis. This patient had no prior history of BD, yet presented with acute neuro-BD manifesting as aseptic meningitis.

The SIADH adds another layer. While drug-induced SIADH has been reported in BD patients on cyclophosphamide, this case demonstrates SIADH occurring solely from CNS inflammation related to acute neuro-BD. The mechanism likely involves IL-6–mediated inflammation triggering aberrant ADH release—a hypothesis supported by the patient's markedly elevated CRP and the resolution of both neurological symptoms and hyponatremia with treatment.

Two clinical clues in this case deserve mention: persistent hiccups and elevated creatine kinase. Hiccups can signal severe hyponatremia from SIADH secondary to CNS inflammation. The CK elevation (1349 U/L) reflected rhabdomyolysis—a recognized but often overlooked complication of profound hyponatremia. These weren't incidental findings; they were clinical breadcrumbs pointing toward a dangerous electrolyte disturbance.

## The Diagnostic Challenge

The absence of parenchymal brain lesions on MRI did not exclude neuro-BD. Acute neuro-BD can present as aseptic meningitis without radiographic abnormalities, particularly early in the disease course. The CSF findings—lymphocytic pleocytosis with elevated protein in the setting of negative infectious studies—were essential to the diagnosis.

The International Criteria for Behçet's Disease provided the framework: oral aphthae (2 points), genital aphthosis (2 points), and folliculitis-like rash (1 point) yielded a score of 5, meeting the threshold for definite disease. Genital aphthosis is particularly specific for BD and should raise your suspicion when present alongside other mucocutaneous findings.

## Treatment: Surprising Success with Colchicine

Corticosteroids are the mainstay for acute neuro-BD. But this patient received colchicine monotherapy—and improved. His fever resolved the day after starting colchicine. Over two weeks, his vertigo, malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions gradually resolved. His sodium normalized to 136 mmol/L, and ADH levels dropped appropriately. He required no systemic corticosteroids or additional immunosuppressants.

This challenges conventional teaching. Systematic reviews have shown colchicine is ineffective for acute neuro-BD, and immunosuppressive agents are generally required. However, in cases presenting solely with meningoencephalitis-like symptoms, spontaneous resolution has been reported. Whether this patient's improvement reflects colchicine's anti-inflammatory effects—suppressing IL-1β and IL-6—or disease self-resolution remains unclear. What we can say: colchicine monotherapy worked here, and it may be a reasonable option when corticosteroids are contraindicated or undesirable.

## Key Take-Home Points

- Neuro-BD typically develops years after BD diagnosis, but concurrent presentation occurs in rare cases—maintain a low threshold for considering neuro-BD in newly diagnosed BD patients with CNS symptoms.
- A normal brain MRI does not exclude acute neuro-BD; CSF analysis showing aseptic meningitis can establish the diagnosis even without parenchymal lesions.
- SIADH can complicate CNS inflammation from acute neuro-BD; watch for hyponatremia in patients presenting with headache, fever, and neurological symptoms.
- Persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis may be clinical clues to severe hyponatremia secondary to SIADH—don't dismiss them as incidental.
- Colchicine monotherapy led to resolution in this case; while corticosteroids remain standard, colchicine may be considered in select patients, particularly when the presentation is limited to meningoencephalitis-like symptoms.
- Use the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease to systematically evaluate suspected cases; genital aphthosis is a specific finding that strongly supports the diagnosis.

This case underscores the importance of pattern recognition across organ systems. When a patient presents with fever, headache, hyponatremia, and mucocutaneous lesions, connecting the dots early can lead to prompt diagnosis and appropriate management—even when the disease defies its typical timeline.



# Aseptic Meningitis with a Normal MRI: Don't Let Imaging Lull You into Missing Neuro-BD

## The Case That Stops You in Your Tracks

A man in his 60s presents with fever, headache, and oral ulcers. His sodium comes back at 124 mmol/L. You're thinking viral syndrome, maybe meningitis, possibly something worse. Then over the next few days, he develops genital ulcers and folliculitis-like rashes. Brain MRI? Completely normal. But the CSF tells a different story: mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, elevated protein, negative infectious workup. The diagnosis? Acute neuro-Behçet's disease, diagnosed concurrently with systemic BD, complicated by SIADH. All of this resolved with colchicine monotherapy alone.

This case is a masterclass in why we need to look beyond imaging and think about the full clinical picture.

## The Normal MRI Trap

Here's what makes this case so instructive. We've been trained that neuro-Behçet's disease means parenchymal brain lesions, typically in the brainstem. The textbooks describe T2 hyperintensities in the basal ganglia, thalamus, or brainstem. So when the MRI comes back stone-cold normal, it's easy to mentally cross "neuro-BD" off your differential.

But here's the critical pearl: acute neuro-BD can present as isolated aseptic meningitis without any parenchymal involvement on MRI. The absence of radiological findings does not exclude the diagnosis, particularly in the early phase of disease. In this patient, the CSF showed the classic pattern of aseptic meningitis — mildly elevated opening pressure, lymphocytic pleocytosis, and elevated protein — despite a completely unrevealing MRI.

The takeaway is straightforward: if you're waiting for brainstem lesions to show up on imaging before considering neuro-BD, you're going to miss cases.

## Concurrent Diagnosis: Breaking the Rules

Classically, neuro-BD develops several years after the initial BD diagnosis. Major organ involvement typically appears around 4 years out, with neurological manifestations at presentation occurring in fewer than 5% of patients. But this case breaks that rule. Here, the systemic BD and neuro-BD were diagnosed simultaneously.

The clinical constellation mattered here. Oral aphthae, genital ulcers, and folliculitis-like rashes earned five points on the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease, clinching the diagnosis of definite BD. But the key was recognizing that the fever, headache, and worsening vertigo represented CNS involvement — even before the genital ulcer appeared on day 5. When you see mucocutaneous lesions in a patient with neurological symptoms and unexplained hyponatremia, you need to broaden your differential beyond infectious causes.

## The SIADH Connection

This case adds another layer: SIADH as a complication of acute neuro-BD. The patient was euvolemic with hypo-osmolar hyponatremia, high urine osmolality, and elevated urinary sodium — classic SIADH. ADH was inappropriately elevated at 22.4 pg/mL.

Why does this matter? CNS inflammation can trigger aberrant ADH secretion. While SIADH is well-described in other neuroinflammatory conditions, its association with neuro-BD is less recognized. The proposed mechanism involves IL-6, a proinflammatory cytokine elevated in neuro-BD CSF, which stimulates ADH release. In this case, the markedly elevated CRP may reflect this IL-6-mediated inflammatory response.

And here's a clinical pearl that shouldn't be missed: persistent hiccups can be an important clue to severe hyponatremia from SIADH. This patient had them. Additionally, the elevated creatine kinase revealed rhabdomyolysis — a recognized but often overlooked complication of profound hyponatremia. These seemingly unrelated symptoms — hiccups, muscle pain — may be pointing you toward a dangerous electrolyte disturbance.

## The Colchicine Curveball

Here's what will surprise you. This patient improved completely on colchicine monotherapy. No steroids. No immunosuppressants. Just colchicine, fluid restriction, and topical treatments for the mucocutaneous lesions.

By conventional wisdom, corticosteroids are the mainstay for acute neuro-BD. Colchicine is generally considered ineffective for neurological disease and is typically used as adjunctive therapy at best. Yet in this case, the fever resolved the day after starting colchicine, and over the following two weeks, all symptoms — mucocutaneous, neurological, and the hyponatremia — gradually improved. The patient remained recurrence-free for four years on colchicine alone.

The authors appropriately acknowledge that spontaneous resolution cannot be excluded, particularly given that some cases of isolated meningoencephalitis-like presentations have been reported to resolve on their own. But the temporal relationship between colchicine initiation and clinical improvement is compelling. Colchicine inhibits neutrophil migration and suppresses IL-1β and IL-6 production, which could theoretically address both the mucocutaneous and CNS manifestations.

What does this mean for practice? Colchicine may be a reasonable option when corticosteroids are contraindicated or undesirable, though we should interpret this cautiously given the n=1 nature of this report.

## A Practical Approach to Aseptic Meningitis

When your patient has fever, headache, and CSF showing lymphocytic pleocytosis with elevated protein, your differential should be systematic. Viral meningoencephalitis (HSV, VZV) needs to be ruled out early. Bacterial meningitis typically shows neutrophilic predominance, but partially treated cases can muddy the waters. Tuberculous meningitis and fungal meningitis should be considered based on risk factors and CSF characteristics.

Beyond infection, think about autoimmune etiologies, neurosarcoidosis (though ACE has limited sensitivity), and malignancy. The authors checked sIL-2R, which was mildly elevated but proved to be a non-specific marker of inflammation rather than indicative of lymphoma.

The key lesson here is that the diagnostic workup should be guided by clinical context, not indiscriminate testing. When mucocutaneous lesions appear in a patient with aseptic meningitis, BD needs to move up your differential — even if the MRI is normal.

## Key Take-Home Points

- A normal brain MRI does not exclude acute neuro-BD; the disease can present as isolated aseptic meningitis without parenchymal lesions, especially early in the disease course.
- Neuro-BD can be diagnosed concurrently with systemic BD, even though it classically develops years after initial diagnosis.
- Consider SIADH in patients with neuro-BD and hyponatremia; CNS inflammation may drive inappropriate ADH secretion.
- Persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis can be clinical clues to severe hyponatremia from SIADH.
- Colchicine monotherapy may be sufficient for some cases of acute neuro-BD, though this requires further study and steroids remain the standard of care.
- When evaluating aseptic meningitis, let the clinical context — including mucocutaneous findings — guide your differential rather than relying solely on imaging findings.



# SIADH and CNS Inflammation: The IL-6 Connection You Need to Know

## The Case That Makes You Rethink Hyponatremia

Here's a case that should make you pause next time you see hyponatremia in a patient with neurological symptoms. A man in his 60s presents with fever, headache, oral ulcers, and a sodium of 124 mmol/L. You're thinking meningitis, maybe encephalitis. But what if the hyponatremia isn't just a bystander—it's a direct complication of the CNS inflammation itself?

This patient turned out to have concurrent diagnosis of Behçet's disease and acute neuro-Behçet's disease, complicated by SIADH. The SIADH wasn't medication-induced. It wasn't from a malignancy. It appears to have been driven entirely by CNS inflammation, with IL-6 as the likely culprit. And here's the kicker: his sodium normalized with colchicine monotherapy—no steroids, no vaptans, no hypertonic saline needed.

## The IL-6 Connection: Pathophysiology Matters

The proposed mechanism here is elegant and worth understanding. IL-6, a proinflammatory cytokine, has been shown to directly stimulate ADH release. In neuroinflammatory states, elevated IL-6 levels in the CSF and serum may drive dysregulated ADH secretion, leading to SIADH. In Behçet's disease specifically, aberrant monocyte activation leads to spontaneous IL-6 production, and elevated IL-6 has been detected in the CSF of patients with acute neuro-BD.

In this case, the patient's markedly elevated CRP (180 mg/L) likely reflected the underlying IL-6-mediated inflammatory response. While IL-6 levels weren't directly measured, the temporal relationship between the CNS inflammation, SIADH, and response to anti-inflammatory therapy strongly suggests this pathway was at play.

This isn't just academic. Understanding that CNS inflammation can cause SIADH through cytokine-mediated pathways expands your differential when evaluating hyponatremia in neuroinflammatory conditions—whether that's neuro-Behçet's, meningitis, autoimmune encephalitis, or other CNS inflammatory states.

## The Diagnostic Workup: What This Case Teaches Us

This patient presented with classic SIADH physiology: hypo-osmolar hyponatremia (serum osmolality 267 mOsm/kg), inappropriately concentrated urine (urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg), elevated urinary sodium (88 mmol/L), and euvolemic status. His ADH level was elevated at 22.4 pg/mL—direct evidence of non-osmotic ADH release.

The critical point is that SIADH remained unexplained until the underlying CNS inflammation was identified. Thyroid function was normal. Serum cortisol was normal. There was no malignancy on imaging. No offending medications. The CSF profile showed aseptic meningitis—mildly elevated opening pressure, elevated protein, and lymphocytic pleocytosis—but here's the crucial detail: the brain MRI was completely normal.

This brings us to an important clinical pearl: a normal brain MRI does not exclude acute neuro-BD. Acute neuro-BD can present as aseptic meningitis without parenchymal lesions, particularly in early disease. The absence of radiological abnormalities should not dissuade you from the diagnosis if the clinical picture fits.

## Clinical Clues You Might Miss

Two findings in this case deserve attention because they're often overlooked. First, persistent hiccups—this patient had them, and they've been reported as a clinical clue to severe hyponatremia from SIADH secondary to CNS inflammation. When you hear hiccups in a patient with neurological symptoms, think about hyponatremia.

Second, rhabdomyolysis. This patient's CK was 1349 U/L. Rhabdomyolysis is a recognized but often missed complication of severe hyponatremia. It's not routinely investigated, but it should be on your radar. The mechanism involves osmotic shifts causing muscle membrane damage.

## Treatment: When Colchicine Alone Is Enough

Here's where this case challenges conventional wisdom. Corticosteroids are considered the mainstay for acute neuro-BD, and most systematic reviews suggest colchicine is ineffective for neurological involvement. Yet this patient had complete resolution of mucocutaneous symptoms, neurological manifestations, and hyponatremia with colchicine monotherapy.

Colchicine works by inhibiting neutrophil migration and suppressing cytokine production, including IL-1β and IL-6. Theoretically, this anti-inflammatory effect could address the underlying IL-6 drive of SIADH. Could spontaneous resolution explain the outcome? Possibly—spontaneous recovery has been reported in neuro-BD presenting with isolated meningoencephalitis-like symptoms. But the temporal response to colchicine, with improvement starting the day after initiation, suggests a therapeutic effect.

The takeaway isn't that colchicine should replace steroids for neuro-BD. Rather, in select cases where corticosteroids are undesirable or contraindicated, colchicine may be a viable option—and it directly targets the inflammatory cascade driving the SIADH.

## Key Take-Home Points

• CNS inflammation can cause SIADH through IL-6-mediated stimulation of ADH release—consider this mechanism in any patient with neuroinflammatory disease and hyponatremia
• Persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis can be clinical clues to severe hyponatremia and warrant investigation
• A normal brain MRI does not exclude acute neuro-BD, especially in early presentations with aseptic meningitis
• The temporal relationship between treatment and resolution suggests SIADH in neuro-BD may respond to targeting the underlying inflammation rather than just the sodium
• When evaluating hyponatremia in the setting of neurological symptoms, maintain a broad differential that includes CNS inflammation itself as the causative mechanism
• Colchicine monotherapy, while not standard, may be effective in select cases of acute neuro-BD, particularly when the presentation is limited to aseptic meningitis

## The Bottom Line

This case reminds us that hyponatremia in a patient with CNS symptoms isn't always a comorbidity—sometimes it's a direct manifestation of the disease process itself. The IL-6 pathway connecting CNS inflammation to SIADH offers a mechanistic explanation that should change how you approach these patients. When you see SIADH in the setting of neuroinflammation, think beyond the usual suspects and consider whether treating the inflammation might be the key to correcting the sodium.



# Colchicine Monotherapy for Acute Neuro-BD: Challenging the Steroid Dogma

Here's a case that made me pause and reconsider what I thought I knew about treating neuro-Behçet's disease. A man in his 60s walks in with fever, headache, oral ulcers, and a sodium of 124 mmol/L. You're thinking infection, maybe meningitis, maybe something systemic. But then a genital ulcer appears on day five, the puzzle pieces click into place, and you're staring at acute neuro-Behçet's disease complicated by SIADH. The plot twist? He got better with colchicine alone—no steroids, no immunosuppressants—and stayed in remission for four years.

## When the Textbook Gets It Wrong

Let's be honest: if this patient landed on your service, corticosteroids would be automatic. Every review article, every guideline, every expert opinion tells us that steroids are the mainstay for acute neuro-Behçet's. The reasoning is sound—this is a devastating condition when untreated, and we've all seen the brainstem lesions, the parenchymal damage, the long-term disability. But this case forces us to ask an uncomfortable question: are we overtreating some patients?

The patient presented with classic mucocutaneous findings: oral aphthae, folliculitis-like rashes, and eventually a genital ulcer. He scored a definitive 5 points on the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease. But what caught my attention was the neurological presentation—a meningoencephalitis picture with headache, vertigo, and persistent hiccups, yet a completely normal brain MRI. The CSF told the real story: elevated opening pressure, increased protein at 0.97 g/L, and mild lymphocytic pleocytosis at 11 cells/μL. All infectious workup was negative. This was aseptic meningitis secondary to acute neuro-BD.

## The SIADH Connection

Here's where it gets interesting. This patient had profound hyponatremia at 124 mmol/L with all the classic SIADH physiology: low serum osmolality (267 mOsm/kg), elevated urine osmolality (900 mOsm/kg), and high urinary sodium (88 mmol/L). We know IL-6 is elevated in neuro-BD CSF, and IL-6 has been implicated in ADH dysregulation. The markedly elevated CRP in this case likely reflects this same inflammatory cascade.

The persistent hiccups turned out to be a clue—not to the Behçet's itself, but to the severity of the hyponatremia. And the elevated CK at 1349 U/L? Rhabdomyolysis from severe hyponatremia, an underappreciated complication that we should be hunting for in these patients.

## Colchicine: A Legitimate Option?

Now for the controversial part. Colchicine is standard for mucocutaneous BD, but systematic reviews have consistently shown it's ineffective for neuro-BD. Most reported cases use colchicine as an adjunct to steroids or other immunosuppressants. But here's the thing—spontaneous resolution has been documented in cases presenting with isolated meningoencephalitis, and this patient had no parenchymal lesions on MRI.

Colchicine works by inhibiting neutrophil chemotaxis and suppressing IL-1β and IL-6 production. If SIADH in this setting is driven by IL-6 mediated inflammation, colchicine's mechanism actually targets the problem directly. Within 24 hours of starting colchicine, the fever resolved and appetite improved. Over two weeks, vertigo, malaise, hiccups, and all mucocutaneous lesions gradually resolved. Sodium normalized to 136 mmol/L by day 16, and ADH dropped to 1.8 pg/mL. Four years of follow-up with no recurrence.

Could this have been spontaneous recovery? Maybe. But the temporal relationship between colchicine initiation and symptom resolution is compelling, and the sustained remission argues against simple natural history.

## Practical Takeaways

This case offers several important lessons for emergency physicians and internists:

*   **Neuro-BD can present simultaneously with systemic BD**—this is rare but real. Don't wait years for neurological involvement if the clinical picture fits.
*   **A normal brain MRI does not exclude acute neuro-BD**. Parenchymal lesions may be absent, especially in early disease or isolated meningeal involvement.
*   **Think SIADH in neuro-BD patients with hyponatremia**. CNS inflammation from any cause can trigger ADH dysregulation.
*   **Colchicine monotherapy may be reasonable in carefully selected patients**—specifically those with isolated meningeal involvement, no parenchymal lesions, and mild neurological symptoms.
*   **Persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis may signal severe hyponatremia**—don't dismiss these as incidental findings.

## The Bottom Line

I'm not ready to abandon steroids for neuro-Behçet's disease. Parenchymal involvement, brainstem lesions, and severe neurological deficits still demand aggressive immunosuppression. But this case reminds us that disease phenotypes vary, and a one-size-fits-all approach may lead to overtreatment. In a patient with isolated aseptic meningitis, no MRI lesions, and mucocutaneous findings responding to colchicine, watchful waiting with colchicine may be a legitimate strategy—particularly when steroids are contraindicated or the patient refuses them. The key is recognizing which patients fall into this favorable category versus those who need the full armamentarium of immunosuppression.



# Hiccups and Rhabdomyolysis: The Underappreciated Clinical Clues to Severe Hyponatremia

## When Hiccups Aren't Just a Nuisance

Let's talk about a case that serves as a perfect reminder of why we need to think beyond the obvious when evaluating patients with non-specific neurological complaints. A man in his 60s presented with fever, headache, malaise, arthralgia, and — here's the kicker — persistent hiccups. Three days of hiccups. Most of us would file that under "annoying but benign." But in this case, those hiccups were screaming a diagnosis that could have been missed if clinicians hadn't stepped back and looked at the bigger picture.

The patient also had oral aphthae, folliculitis-like rashes on his face and trunk, and on day five of admission, a painful genital ulcer appeared. His initial labs revealed a sodium of 124 mmol/L and a creatine kinase of 1349 U/L. That's not a typo. He had profound hyponatremia with concurrent rhabdomyolysis, and neither finding was on anyone's radar at presentation.

## The Hiccup-Hyponatremia Connection

Persistent hiccups in the setting of severe hyponatremia is one of those clinical associations that, once you learn it, you never forget — but many of us never learned it in the first place. The mechanism isn't fully elucidated, but the prevailing theory involves direct irritation of the diaphragm and phrenic nerve pathways due to cellular edema from hypo-osmolar states. The medullary hiccup reflex arc, which includes the phrenic and vagus nerves plus the reticular formation, appears particularly sensitive to electrolyte disturbances.

Previous case reports have documented hiccups as a presenting symptom of SIADH secondary to CNS inflammation, including meningitis. In this patient, the hiccups weren't an incidental finding — they were a direct clinical manifestation of a sodium of 124. When you see a patient with intractable hiccups in the ED, especially with other neurological symptoms, put hyponatremia on your differential. It might just save you from a diagnostic delay.

## Rhabdomyolysis: The Silent Partner

Here's where the case gets even more interesting. The elevated CK of 1349 U/L wasn't initially connected to the hyponatremia, but rhabdomyolysis is a recognized — though often overlooked — complication of severe hyponatremia. The pathophysiology involves osmotic shifts causing muscle cell swelling and subsequent membrane disruption. As sodium drops, water moves into cells, leading to cellular edema, impaired membrane integrity, and eventual leakage of intracellular contents including CK and myoglobin.

This patient also had arthralgia and general fatigue, which could have been attributed to his underlying inflammatory condition. But the markedly elevated CK tells us something else was happening. In profound hyponatremia, muscle cells are literally bursting under osmotic stress. The lesson here is straightforward: if you're working up a patient with severe hyponatremia, check a CK. And if a patient presents with rhabdomyolysis of unclear etiology, especially with neurological symptoms, think about their sodium.

## The Underlying Diagnosis: Neuro-Behçet's with SIADH

This patient's hyponatremia was ultimately attributed to SIADH in the setting of acute neurological Behçet's disease. His evaluation revealed low serum osmolality (267 mOsm/kg), elevated urine osmolality (900 mOsm/kg), elevated urinary sodium (88 mmol/L), and an ADH level of 22.4 pg/mL — all consistent with inappropriate ADH secretion. He was clinically euvolemic, which helped distinguish SIADH from hypovolemic hyponatremia.

CSF analysis showed aseptic meningitis with mild lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein, but brain MRI was completely normal. This is a crucial point: acute neuro-Behçet's can present as aseptic meningitis without any parenchymal lesions on imaging. A normal brain MRI does not exclude the diagnosis, particularly in early presentation. The combination of mucocutaneous findings (oral aphthae, genital ulcers, folliculitis-like rash) with neurological symptoms and CSF inflammation cinched the diagnosis.

The SIADH likely resulted from CNS inflammation itself. Interleukin-6, a proinflammatory cytokine elevated in neuroinflammatory conditions, has been associated with increased ADH release. In Behçet's disease, aberrant monocyte activation leads to spontaneous IL-6 production, and elevated IL-6 has been documented in the CSF of patients with acute neuro-Behçet's. While IL-6 levels weren't measured in this case, the markedly elevated CRP likely reflected an IL-6-mediated inflammatory response.

## Management: Conservative Approach Wins

Here's what I love about this case: the SIADH was managed entirely conservatively with fluid restriction to less than 800 mL daily. No hypertonic saline, no vasopressin receptor antagonists, no aggressive interventions. By day 16, his sodium had normalized to 136 mmol/L with ADH dropping to 1.8 pg/mL.

The underlying Behçet's disease was treated with colchicine monotherapy — no systemic corticosteroids, no immunosuppressants. His fever resolved the day after starting colchicine, and over two weeks, his symptoms including vertigo, malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions gradually improved. Colchicine's anti-inflammatory effects through inhibition of neutrophil migration and suppression of IL-1β and IL-6 production likely addressed both the systemic inflammation and its downstream endocrine effects.

This conservative approach worked beautifully in this case, but let's be clear: most patients with acute neuro-Behçet's require corticosteroids. Spontaneous resolution has been reported in cases presenting with isolated meningoencephalitis-like symptoms, and colchicine is generally considered ineffective for neuro-Behçet's based on systematic reviews. However, this case demonstrates that when corticosteroids are contraindicated or undesirable, colchicine may be a viable option in select patients with mild presentations.

## Clinical Pearls for the ED Physician

The broader takeaway here is about pattern recognition. When you see a patient with non-specific neurological symptoms — headache, malaise, fever — don't stop at the obvious infectious or inflammatory workup. Look for the subtle clues that point toward metabolic derangements. Persistent hiccups might be annoying, but they can signal profound hyponatremia. Elevated CK in a patient with severe hyponatremia isn't always a red herring — it might be a direct consequence of the electrolyte disturbance itself.

In neuroinflammatory conditions, SIADH should be on your radar as a potential complication. The CNS inflammation that drives the primary disease can also dysregulate ADH secretion through cytokine-mediated pathways. And when you're managing hyponatremia, remember that conservative measures like fluid restriction are often sufficient, particularly when you're simultaneously treating the underlying etiology.

**Key Take-Home Messages:**

- Persistent hiccups can be a clinical manifestation of severe hyponatremia and should prompt immediate electrolyte assessment.
- Rhabdomyolysis is an underrecognized complication of profound hyponatremia due to osmotic muscle cell injury; check CK in patients with severe hyponatremia.
- In patients with neuroinflammatory conditions, maintain a high suspicion for SIADH as a secondary complication.
- Acute neuro-Behçet's disease can present with aseptic meningitis and normal brain MRI; do not exclude the diagnosis based on imaging alone.
- Conservative management with fluid restriction may be sufficient for SIADH when the underlying inflammatory condition is being treated.

The next time a patient presents with "just hiccups" and some vague neurological complaints, remember this case and ask yourself: what's the sodium?
