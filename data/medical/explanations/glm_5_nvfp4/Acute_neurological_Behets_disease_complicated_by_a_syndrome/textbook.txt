Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Assessment

When a patient presents to the emergency department with non-specific constitutional symptoms, the clinician's initial task is to systematically gather information that narrows a broad differential diagnosis. This case illustrates how careful attention to seemingly disparate findings—fever, headache, mucocutaneous lesions, and electrolyte abnormalities—can ultimately coalesce into a unifying diagnosis.

**Presenting Symptoms and Their Clinical Significance**

The patient, an Asian man in his 60s, presented with a three-day history of fever, headache, general malaise, loss of appetite, persistent hiccups, and arthralgia affecting the shoulders and ankles. He also reported oral aphthae. This constellation of symptoms spans multiple organ systems and initially suggests infectious, inflammatory, or neoplastic etiologies. Fever and headache raise immediate concern for central nervous system infection, particularly meningitis or encephalitis. The presence of persistent hiccups, while often overlooked, can be a valuable clinical clue. In this context, hiccups may reflect irritation of the medullary respiratory center or diaphragmatic stimulation, but they have also been reported as a manifestation of severe hyponatraemia. The arthralgia involving large joints raises the possibility of systemic inflammation, while oral aphthae, though common in the general population, warrant attention when occurring alongside other systemic symptoms.

**Medical History and Social Context**

The patient's medical history included untreated type 2 diabetes mellitus, with poor dietary and physical activity habits. This history is relevant for several reasons. First, diabetes mellitus is a risk factor for certain infections, and immunocompromised status broadens the differential diagnosis to include opportunistic pathogens. Second, the patient's HbA1c of 8.2% and plasma glucose of 14.2 mmol/L confirmed poor glycemic control, which may contribute to delayed wound healing and increased susceptibility to infections. Importantly, hyperglycemia can also cause pseudohyponatraemia, although the measured low serum osmolality in this case confirmed true hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia. His social history was notable for occasional alcohol consumption, no smoking, no recent travel, no animal exposures, and no recent sexual activity—factors that help exclude certain infectious etiologies such as tick-borne illnesses, zoonotic infections, or sexually transmitted infections.

**Physical Examination Findings**

On examination, the patient was alert but fatigued, with a temperature of 38.1°C, tachycardia at 119 beats per minute, and normal blood pressure of 135/73 mmHg. The respiratory rate was 19 breaths per minute with oxygen saturation of 96% on room air. The tachycardia likely reflected the systemic inflammatory response and fever, though thyroid dysfunction was later excluded. Crucially, physical examination revealed oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rashes on the face and trunk. These mucocutaneous findings, when considered together, would later prove essential to the diagnosis. Assessment of volume status is particularly important in the evaluation of hyponatraemia. This patient was clinically euvolemic, with no peripheral edema, no jugular venous distension, and no signs of dehydration. The euvolemic state, combined with hyponatraemia, narrowed the differential for hyponatraemia to conditions such as the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH), adrenal insufficiency, or hypothyroidism.

**Initial Laboratory Abnormalities**

Initial laboratory tests revealed several striking abnormalities. The serum sodium was markedly low at 124 mmol/L, indicating moderate-to-severe hyponatraemia. Inflammatory markers were significantly elevated, with C-reactive protein at 180 mg/L and erythrocyte sedimentation rate at 70 mm/hour, indicating a robust acute-phase response. The creatine kinase was elevated at 1349 U/L, along with mild elevations in aspartate aminotransferase and alanine aminotransferase. The elevated creatine kinase raised concern for rhabdomyolysis, which can occur as a complication of severe hyponatraemia due to osmotic shifts causing muscle cell swelling and injury. Renal function was normal, which helped exclude renal causes of hyponatraemia such as acute kidney injury or nephrotic syndrome. Hyperglycemia and elevated HbA1c reflected his known but untreated diabetes.

**Formulating the Initial Differential Diagnosis**

At this early stage, the differential diagnosis remained broad. The combination of fever, headache, and aseptic meningitis-like symptoms suggested central nervous system infection, including viral meningoencephalitis (herpes simplex virus, varicella zoster virus), bacterial meningitis, or tuberculous meningitis. Systemic inflammatory conditions such as autoimmune vasculitides, sarcoidosis, or Behçet's disease were considered, particularly given the mucocutaneous findings. Malignancy, including primary central nervous system lymphoma or carcinomatous meningitis, remained in the differential, especially given the patient's age and systemic symptoms. The hyponatraemia, later confirmed as SIADH, was initially approached cautiously, recognizing that it could result from CNS infection, malignancy, or inflammation.

This case underscores the importance of systematic clinical assessment when confronting non-specific symptoms. The initial presentation—with fever, headache, mucocutaneous lesions, and hyponatraemia—required the clinician to maintain a broad differential while methodically pursuing diagnostic clues through history, physical examination, and targeted investigations.

## Behçet's Disease and Neuro-Behçet's Disease: Clinical Framework

Behçet's disease (BD) is a chronic, multisystem inflammatory disorder that affects blood vessels of all sizes, making it essentially a systemic vasculitis. The underlying pathophysiology involves an aberrant immune response characterized by neutrophil hyperactivation and dysregulated cytokine production, particularly involving proinflammatory mediators such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and IL-1β. A strong genetic association exists with HLA-B51, which is present in our patient and serves as a supportive finding, though it is neither diagnostic nor required for diagnosis. The disease displays notable geographic predilection, occurring most frequently along the ancient Silk Road, including Turkey, the Middle East, and East Asia—regions where our Asian patient may have heightened genetic susceptibility.

The International Criteria for Behçet's Disease provide a standardized, point-based scoring system that allows clinicians to establish a diagnosis with confidence. A score of 4 or more points indicates definite BD. Oral aphthous ulcers, the hallmark of BD, are assigned 2 points, as are genital ulcers. Skin lesions—including folliculitis-like eruptions, erythema nodosum, or acne-like nodules—contribute 1 point, while ocular involvement such as uveitis adds 2 points. Our patient presented with oral aphthae (2 points), a genital ulcer that appeared on day 5 of hospitalization (2 points), and folliculitis-like rashes (1 point), yielding a total score of 5 points and meeting criteria for definite BD. The emergence of the genital ulcer was diagnostically pivotal, as it confirmed a systemic process rather than isolated oral lesions.

The core clinical manifestations of BD reflect its vasculitic nature. Recurrent oral aphthae are nearly universal and typically present first, often years before other organ involvement. Genital ulcers, while less frequent, are more specific for BD and tend to be painful, deep, and scarring. Cutaneous findings include folliculitis-like lesions, erythema nodosum, and pathergy—a hypersensitivity reaction where minor skin trauma induces pustule formation, though our patient did not exhibit this finding. Ocular disease, particularly uveitis, can cause significant morbidity including blindness and was notably absent in this case. The mucocutaneous triad of oral aphthae, genital ulcers, and skin lesions formed the clinical foundation for diagnosis before neurological symptoms fully declared themselves.

Neurological involvement, termed neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD), complicates approximately 10–20% of BD cases and represents one of the disease's most serious manifestations. Classically, neuro-BD develops several years after the initial BD diagnosis, with major organ involvement typically appearing around 4 years after disease onset. This delayed pattern makes our case unusual: BD and neuro-BD were diagnosed concurrently, a scenario occurring in fewer than 5% of patients. Neuro-BD most commonly manifests as acute meningoencephalitis with parenchymal brain lesions, preferentially affecting the brainstem. Patients present with headache, fever, and altered mental status, sometimes accompanied by focal neurological deficits or behavioral changes. The differential diagnosis is broad, necessitating careful exclusion of infectious, neoplastic, and other autoimmune etiologies.

A critical lesson from this case is that acute neuro-BD may present as isolated aseptic meningitis without demonstrable parenchymal lesions on brain MRI. Our patient's MRI was normal, which initially complicated the diagnostic process. However, cerebrospinal fluid analysis revealed the characteristic profile of aseptic meningitis: mildly elevated opening pressure, increased protein concentration, and lymphocytic pleocytosis. The absence of radiological abnormalities does not exclude neuro-BD, particularly in early disease. Clinicians must recognize that meningoencephalitis-like presentations in the setting of compatible mucocutaneous findings warrant serious consideration of neuro-BD, even when imaging is unrevealing. This understanding proved essential in establishing the correct diagnosis and initiating appropriate therapy.

## SIADH in the Context of Central Nervous System Inflammation

The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) emerged as a critical complication in this patient, transforming what might have been a straightforward evaluation of fever and headache into a complex diagnostic challenge. Understanding SIADH requires recognizing both its defining laboratory features and the physiological mechanisms that link central nervous system pathology to disordered water homeostasis.

The diagnosis of SIADH rests on fulfilling specific biochemical criteria while excluding other causes of hyponatraemia. This patient presented with hypo-osmolar hyponatraemia, with a serum sodium of 124 mmol/L and serum osmolality of 267 mOsm/kg—both below the lower limits of normal. Despite this hypo-osmolar state, his urine osmolality was inappropriately elevated at 900 mOsm/kg, indicating that the kidneys were continuing to concentrate urine rather than appropriately excreting free water. The urinary sodium concentration of 88 mmol/L further supported the diagnosis, reflecting continued sodium excretion in the absence of volume depletion. Importantly, clinical examination confirmed euvolemia, distinguishing SIADH from hypovolemic or hypervolemic causes of hyponatraemia. The elevated ADH level of 22.4 pg/mL provided direct confirmation of inappropriate hormone secretion.

Before establishing SIADH as the explanation for this patient's hyponatraemia, the treating team needed to exclude other endocrine causes. Adrenal insufficiency and hypothyroidism can both produce hyponatraemia through mechanisms distinct from SIADH. In this case, a normal serum cortisol level (42 µg/dL) effectively ruled out adrenal insufficiency, while normal thyroid-stimulating hormone and free T4 excluded hypothyroidism. These investigations are essential because glucocorticoid deficiency impairs free water excretion, and hypothyroidism reduces the renal response to ADH—both conditions that require specific treatments beyond simple fluid restriction.

The pathophysiology of SIADH in the setting of central nervous system inflammation involves complex neuroendocrine dysregulation. Antidiuretic hormone, also known as vasopressin, is normally synthesized in the hypothalamic supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei and released from the posterior pituitary in response to increased plasma osmolality or decreased effective circulating volume. In SIADH, this tight regulatory control is lost, leading to autonomous ADH secretion. CNS inflammation can trigger this dysregulation through several mechanisms. Inflammatory cytokines, particularly interleukin-6 (IL-6), have been shown to stimulate ADH release independently of osmotic cues. In neuro-Behçet's disease, aberrant monocyte activation leads to spontaneous IL-6 production, and elevated IL-6 levels have been documented in the cerebrospinal fluid of patients during acute neurological episodes. While IL-6 was not directly measured in this patient, his markedly elevated C-reactive protein (180 mg/L) may reflect the underlying IL-6-mediated inflammatory cascade that ultimately drove inappropriate ADH secretion.

The clinical manifestations of severe hyponatraemia in this case provided important diagnostic clues that might otherwise be overlooked. Persistent hiccups, one of this patient's presenting symptoms, have been recognized as a clinical marker of severe hyponatraemia secondary to CNS inflammation, including meningitis. Additionally, the elevated creatine kinase (1349 U/L) indicated rhabdomyolysis, a known but frequently underappreciated complication of profound hyponatraemia. These findings underscore that hyponatraemia can present with symptoms that may not immediately suggest an electrolyte disturbance. Clinicians evaluating patients with neurological symptoms should maintain a low threshold for checking serum sodium, particularly when unusual features such as intractable hiccups are present.

The etiologies of SIADH extend beyond CNS inflammation to include malignancy (particularly small cell lung carcinoma), pulmonary disease, and certain medications. In this patient, the absence of a chest radiograph abnormality, the unremarkable CT scans, and the lack of recent medication exposure that could induce SIADH supported a CNS inflammatory etiology. The temporal relationship between the onset of his neurological symptoms and the development of hyponatraemia, followed by the parallel resolution of both with treatment of his underlying Behçet's disease, strongly suggested that acute neuro-Behçet's disease was the causative factor.

Management of SIADH in this case was conservative, employing fluid restriction of less than 800 mL daily with careful monitoring of serum sodium. This approach avoided the risks of overly rapid sodium correction, which can result in osmotic demyelination syndrome. The normalization of sodium to 136 mmol/L by day 16, concurrent with improvement in his mucocutaneous and neurological symptoms, demonstrated that treating the underlying inflammatory condition can resolve SIADH without requiring vasopressin receptor antagonists or hypertonic saline.

## Diagnostic Reasoning and Differential Diagnosis

The patient's presentation with fever, headache, and hyponatremia necessitated a systematic diagnostic approach to distinguish between infectious, neoplastic, and autoimmune etiologies. This process illustrates the importance of methodical exclusion when confronting non-specific neurological symptoms in the context of systemic inflammation.

**Differential Diagnosis of Fever, Headache, and Aseptic Meningitis**

The combination of fever, headache, and subsequently confirmed aseptic meningitis generates a broad differential diagnosis. Infectious causes represent the most urgent consideration, particularly viral meningoencephalitis caused by herpes simplex virus (HSV) or varicella zoster virus (VZV), which require prompt antiviral therapy. Bacterial meningitis, though typically presenting with more acute deterioration, must be excluded. Tuberculous meningitis remains an important consideration in patients with subacute presentations, particularly in endemic regions. Beyond infection, autoimmune conditions such as neurosarcoidosis, autoimmune encephalitis, and central nervous system (CNS) vasculitis warrant evaluation. Primary CNS malignancy, including lymphoma, can also present with similar symptoms. The initial absence of mucocutaneous findings characteristic of Behçet's disease (BD) made the diagnostic process particularly challenging in this case.

**CSF Analysis and Interpretation**

Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis is essential in evaluating suspected CNS inflammation. In this patient, CSF examination revealed mildly elevated opening pressure (17 cmH₂O), increased protein (0.97 g/L), and mild lymphocytic pleocytosis (11 cells/µL). This pattern—normal to slightly elevated pressure, elevated protein, and lymphocytic predominance—characterizes aseptic meningitis. Lymphocytic pleocytosis typically indicates viral infection, autoimmune inflammation, or indolent bacterial processes such as tuberculosis, as opposed to neutrophilic predominance, which suggests acute bacterial infection. The elevated protein reflects blood-brain barrier dysfunction, a non-specific finding seen in various inflammatory conditions. Crucially, microbiological studies including bacterial and mycobacterial cultures, HSV and VZV polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing, and cryptococcal antigen were all negative, effectively excluding infectious causes. CSF cytology showed no malignant cells, reducing concern for carcinomatous meningitis or primary CNS lymphoma.

**Exclusion of Specific Infectious Etiologies**

The diagnostic process prioritized exclusion of treatable infections. Viral meningoencephalitis, particularly HSV, was initially suspected given the clinical presentation, and empirical acyclovir was appropriately initiated. However, negative viral PCR results definitively excluded HSV and VZV. Bacterial meningitis was ruled out based on the absence of neutrophilic predominance in CSF and negative cultures. Tuberculous meningitis was considered but excluded after acid-fast staining and tuberculosis DNA testing returned negative. This systematic exclusion of infectious causes was critical before considering non-infectious inflammatory conditions.

**Evaluation for Autoimmune and Neoplastic Conditions**

With infection excluded, attention turned to non-infectious inflammatory and neoplastic conditions. Neurosarcoidosis was considered, as it can present with meningeal involvement and normal early MRI findings. However, normal serum angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) and lysozyme levels made this diagnosis unlikely. CNS lymphoma was evaluated given the neurological symptoms; however, normal brain MRI and absence of malignant cells in CSF argued against this diagnosis. The mildly elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R) level was interpreted as a non-specific marker of inflammation rather than evidence of lymphoma, illustrating the importance of interpreting laboratory findings in clinical context. Autoimmune encephalitis screening with comprehensive autoantibody panels was negative, further narrowing the differential.

**Synthesizing the Diagnosis**

The diagnosis emerged through integration of clinical findings rather than a single definitive test. The appearance of genital ulcers on day 5, combined with pre-existing oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rashes, fulfilled the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease. The scoring system awarded two points each for oral aphthae and genital ulcers, plus one point for folliculitis-like rash, totaling five points—exceeding the threshold of four required for definite BD. The neurological symptoms occurring in this context, with aseptic meningitis confirmed on CSF analysis and all alternative causes excluded, established the diagnosis of acute neuro-BD. The concurrent finding of SIADH, characterized by hypo-osmolar hyponatremia with elevated urine osmolality and urine sodium, added complexity. The temporal association between CNS inflammation and SIADH suggested a pathophysiological link, with CNS inflammation potentially causing dysregulated antidiuretic hormone secretion. This case exemplifies how diagnosis often requires serial clinical observation and integration of multiple data points, particularly when presentation does not follow classic patterns.

## Management Decisions, Treatment Rationale, and Clinical Outcomes

The management of Behçet's disease (BD) varies depending on the extent and severity of organ involvement. For mucocutaneous manifestations, colchicine is considered first-line therapy, while corticosteroids and immunosuppressive agents such as cyclophosphamide, azathioprine, or anti-tumor necrosis factor agents are typically reserved for severe organ involvement, including neurological disease. In neuro-Behçet's disease (neuro-BD), corticosteroids are regarded as the mainstay of treatment for acute episodes, given the potential for significant morbidity from parenchymal brain lesions. However, treatment decisions must be individualized, and in patients presenting with isolated meningeal inflammation without evidence of parenchymal involvement, the necessity of aggressive immunosuppression is less clearly defined. This case presented a unique therapeutic scenario: a patient with newly diagnosed BD and acute neuro-BD complicated by SIADH, in whom colchicine monotherapy was initiated rather than corticosteroids.

Colchicine exerts its anti-inflammatory effects through multiple mechanisms that are particularly relevant to BD pathophysiology. The drug inhibits microtubule polymerization, which in turn blocks neutrophil adhesion, migration, and activation at sites of inflammation. Additionally, colchicine suppresses the production of proinflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and interleukin-6 (IL-6). This cytokine suppression is especially pertinent in neuro-BD, where elevated IL-6 levels in the cerebrospinal fluid have been documented and may contribute to both neuroinflammation and the dysregulated secretion of antidiuretic hormone. In this patient, the decision to use colchicine monotherapy was guided by several factors: the absence of parenchymal brain lesions on MRI, the clinical presentation limited to aseptic meningitis, and the goal of avoiding corticosteroid-related complications, particularly given the patient's poorly controlled diabetes mellitus.

The management of SIADH in this case followed established conservative principles. Fluid restriction to less than 800 mL daily was implemented, with careful daily monitoring of serum sodium concentrations. The avoidance of rapid sodium correction was critical, as overly rapid correction of hyponatraemia can result in osmotic demyelination syndrome, a potentially devastating neurological complication. Hypertonic saline and vasopressin receptor antagonists (vaptans) were not required, as the patient's sodium levels responded adequately to conservative measures. Importantly, the concurrent treatment of the underlying inflammatory process with colchicine likely facilitated the resolution of SIADH by addressing the presumed cytokine-mediated drive for inappropriate ADH secretion. This dual approach—managing the acute electrolyte disturbance while treating the underlying disease—proved effective.

The clinical response to colchicine monotherapy was notably rapid and comprehensive. Within one day of initiating colchicine, the patient's fever resolved and his appetite improved. Over the subsequent two weeks, all manifestations gradually resolved: vertigo, general malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions including oral aphthae and genital ulcers. By day 16, serum sodium had normalized to 136 mmol/L, and ADH levels had decreased from an elevated 22.4 pg/mL to 1.8 pg/mL. The elevated creatine kinase, which had indicated rhabdomyolysis as a complication of severe hyponatraemia, also returned to normal. This temporal relationship between colchicine initiation and the resolution of both neurological symptoms and SIADH supports a possible pathophysiological link between neuro-BD inflammation and inappropriate ADH secretion. It remains possible that spontaneous resolution contributed to the favorable outcome, as acute neuro-BD presenting solely as meningoencephalitis can be self-limiting. Nevertheless, the prompt and sustained response suggests that colchicine was an effective intervention in this case.

Long-term follow-up demonstrated durable remission. Colchicine therapy was continued for approximately four years, during which time the patient experienced no recurrence of BD symptoms or neurological manifestations. After his disease stabilized, treatment for his type 2 diabetes mellitus was initiated with a biguanide (metformin), and good glycaemic control was subsequently maintained. The absence of recurrence over this extended observation period reinforces the effectiveness of the initial therapeutic approach. This case provides clinically relevant lessons: first, colchicine monotherapy may be a viable option for acute neuro-BD presenting as isolated aseptic meningitis, particularly when corticosteroids are undesirable; second, SIADH associated with CNS inflammation may resolve with effective treatment of the underlying inflammatory process; and third, clinicians should recognize that acute neuro-BD and systemic BD can present concurrently, necessitating simultaneous attention to mucocutaneous and neurological manifestations.
