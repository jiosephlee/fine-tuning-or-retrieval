Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# Colchicine Monotherapy for Acute Neuro-BD: Challenging the Steroid Dogma

Here's a case that made me pause and reconsider what I thought I knew about treating neuro-Behçet's disease. A man in his 60s walks in with fever, headache, oral ulcers, and a sodium of 124 mmol/L. You're thinking infection, maybe meningitis, maybe something systemic. But then a genital ulcer appears on day five, the puzzle pieces click into place, and you're staring at acute neuro-Behçet's disease complicated by SIADH. The plot twist? He got better with colchicine alone—no steroids, no immunosuppressants—and stayed in remission for four years.

## When the Textbook Gets It Wrong

Let's be honest: if this patient landed on your service, corticosteroids would be automatic. Every review article, every guideline, every expert opinion tells us that steroids are the mainstay for acute neuro-Behçet's. The reasoning is sound—this is a devastating condition when untreated, and we've all seen the brainstem lesions, the parenchymal damage, the long-term disability. But this case forces us to ask an uncomfortable question: are we overtreating some patients?

The patient presented with classic mucocutaneous findings: oral aphthae, folliculitis-like rashes, and eventually a genital ulcer. He scored a definitive 5 points on the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease. But what caught my attention was the neurological presentation—a meningoencephalitis picture with headache, vertigo, and persistent hiccups, yet a completely normal brain MRI. The CSF told the real story: elevated opening pressure, increased protein at 0.97 g/L, and mild lymphocytic pleocytosis at 11 cells/μL. All infectious workup was negative. This was aseptic meningitis secondary to acute neuro-BD.

## The SIADH Connection

Here's where it gets interesting. This patient had profound hyponatremia at 124 mmol/L with all the classic SIADH physiology: low serum osmolality (267 mOsm/kg), elevated urine osmolality (900 mOsm/kg), and high urinary sodium (88 mmol/L). ADH was frankly elevated at 22.4 pg/mL. The authors propose an IL-6 mediated mechanism, and honestly, this makes mechanistic sense. We know IL-6 is elevated in neuro-BD CSF, and IL-6 has been implicated in ADH dysregulation. The markedly elevated CRP in this case likely reflects this same inflammatory cascade.

The persistent hiccups turned out to be a clue—not to the Behçet's itself, but to the severity of the hyponatremia. And the elevated CK at 1349 U/L? Rhabdomyolysis from severe hyponatremia, an underappreciated complication that we should be hunting for in these patients.

## Colchicine: A Legitimate Option?

Now for the controversial part. Colchicine is standard for mucocutaneous BD, but systematic reviews have consistently shown it's ineffective for neuro-BD. Most reported cases use colchicine as an adjunct to steroids or other immunosuppressants. But here's the thing—spontaneous resolution has been documented in cases presenting with isolated meningoencephalitis, and this patient had no parenchymal lesions on MRI.

Colchicine works by inhibiting neutrophil chemotaxis and suppressing IL-1β and IL-6 production. If SIADH in this setting is driven by IL-6 mediated inflammation, colchicine's mechanism actually targets the problem directly. Within 24 hours of starting colchicine, the fever resolved and appetite improved. Over two weeks, vertigo, malaise, hiccups, and all mucocutaneous lesions gradually resolved. Sodium normalized to 136 mmol/L by day 16, and ADH dropped to 1.8 pg/mL. Four years of follow-up with no recurrence.

Could this have been spontaneous recovery? Maybe. But the temporal relationship between colchicine initiation and symptom resolution is compelling, and the sustained remission argues against simple natural history.

## Practical Takeaways

This case offers several important lessons for emergency physicians and internists:

*   **Neuro-BD can present simultaneously with systemic BD**—this is rare but real. Don't wait years for neurological involvement if the clinical picture fits.
*   **A normal brain MRI does not exclude acute neuro-BD**. Parenchymal lesions may be absent, especially in early disease or isolated meningeal involvement.
*   **Think SIADH in neuro-BD patients with hyponatremia**. CNS inflammation from any cause can trigger ADH dysregulation.
*   **Colchicine monotherapy may be reasonable in carefully selected patients**—specifically those with isolated meningeal involvement, no parenchymal lesions, and mild neurological symptoms.
*   **Persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis may signal severe hyponatremia**—don't dismiss these as incidental findings.

## The Bottom Line

I'm not ready to abandon steroids for neuro-Behçet's disease. Parenchymal involvement, brainstem lesions, and severe neurological deficits still demand aggressive immunosuppression. But this case reminds us that disease phenotypes vary, and a one-size-fits-all approach may lead to overtreatment. In a patient with isolated aseptic meningitis, no MRI lesions, and mucocutaneous findings responding to colchicine, watchful waiting with colchicine may be a legitimate strategy—particularly when steroids are contraindicated or the patient refuses them. The key is recognizing which patients fall into this favorable category versus those who need the full armamentarium of immunosuppression.
