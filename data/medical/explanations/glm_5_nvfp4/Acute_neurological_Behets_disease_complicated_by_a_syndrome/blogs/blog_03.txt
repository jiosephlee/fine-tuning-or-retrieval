Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# SIADH and CNS Inflammation: The IL-6 Connection You Need to Know

## The Case That Makes You Rethink Hyponatremia

Here's a case that should make you pause next time you see hyponatremia in a patient with neurological symptoms. A man in his 60s presents with fever, headache, oral ulcers, and a sodium of 124 mmol/L. You're thinking meningitis, maybe encephalitis. But what if the hyponatremia isn't just a bystander—it's a direct complication of the CNS inflammation itself?

This patient turned out to have concurrent diagnosis of Behçet's disease and acute neuro-Behçet's disease, complicated by SIADH. The SIADH wasn't medication-induced. It wasn't from a malignancy. It appears to have been driven entirely by CNS inflammation, with IL-6 as the likely culprit. And here's the kicker: his sodium normalized with colchicine monotherapy—no steroids, no vaptans, no hypertonic saline needed.

## The IL-6 Connection: Pathophysiology Matters

The proposed mechanism here is elegant and worth understanding. IL-6, a proinflammatory cytokine, has been shown to directly stimulate ADH release. In neuroinflammatory states, elevated IL-6 levels in the CSF and serum may drive dysregulated ADH secretion, leading to SIADH. In Behçet's disease specifically, aberrant monocyte activation leads to spontaneous IL-6 production, and elevated IL-6 has been detected in the CSF of patients with acute neuro-BD.

In this case, the patient's markedly elevated CRP (180 mg/L) likely reflected the underlying IL-6-mediated inflammatory response. While IL-6 levels weren't directly measured, the temporal relationship between the CNS inflammation, SIADH, and response to anti-inflammatory therapy strongly suggests this pathway was at play.

This isn't just academic. Understanding that CNS inflammation can cause SIADH through cytokine-mediated pathways expands your differential when evaluating hyponatremia in neuroinflammatory conditions—whether that's neuro-Behçet's, meningitis, autoimmune encephalitis, or other CNS inflammatory states.

## The Diagnostic Workup: What This Case Teaches Us

This patient presented with classic SIADH physiology: hypo-osmolar hyponatremia (serum osmolality 267 mOsm/kg), inappropriately concentrated urine (urine osmolality 900 mOsm/kg), elevated urinary sodium (88 mmol/L), and euvolemic status. His ADH level was elevated at 22.4 pg/mL—direct evidence of non-osmotic ADH release.

The critical point is that SIADH remained unexplained until the underlying CNS inflammation was identified. Thyroid function was normal. Serum cortisol was normal. There was no malignancy on imaging. No offending medications. The CSF profile showed aseptic meningitis—mildly elevated opening pressure, elevated protein, and lymphocytic pleocytosis—but here's the crucial detail: the brain MRI was completely normal.

This brings us to an important clinical pearl: a normal brain MRI does not exclude acute neuro-BD. Acute neuro-BD can present as aseptic meningitis without parenchymal lesions, particularly in early disease. The absence of radiological abnormalities should not dissuade you from the diagnosis if the clinical picture fits.

## Clinical Clues You Might Miss

Two findings in this case deserve attention because they're often overlooked. First, persistent hiccups—this patient had them, and they've been reported as a clinical clue to severe hyponatremia from SIADH secondary to CNS inflammation. When you hear hiccups in a patient with neurological symptoms, think about hyponatremia.

Second, rhabdomyolysis. This patient's CK was 1349 U/L. Rhabdomyolysis is a recognized but often missed complication of severe hyponatremia. It's not routinely investigated, but it should be on your radar. The mechanism involves osmotic shifts causing muscle membrane damage.

## Treatment: When Colchicine Alone Is Enough

Here's where this case challenges conventional wisdom. Corticosteroids are considered the mainstay for acute neuro-BD, and most systematic reviews suggest colchicine is ineffective for neurological involvement. Yet this patient had complete resolution of mucocutaneous symptoms, neurological manifestations, and hyponatremia with colchicine monotherapy.

Colchicine works by inhibiting neutrophil migration and suppressing cytokine production, including IL-1β and IL-6. Theoretically, this anti-inflammatory effect could address the underlying IL-6 drive of SIADH. Could spontaneous resolution explain the outcome? Possibly—spontaneous recovery has been reported in neuro-BD presenting with isolated meningoencephalitis-like symptoms. But the temporal response to colchicine, with improvement starting the day after initiation, suggests a therapeutic effect.

The takeaway isn't that colchicine should replace steroids for neuro-BD. Rather, in select cases where corticosteroids are undesirable or contraindicated, colchicine may be a viable option—and it directly targets the inflammatory cascade driving the SIADH.

## Key Take-Home Points

• CNS inflammation can cause SIADH through IL-6-mediated stimulation of ADH release—consider this mechanism in any patient with neuroinflammatory disease and hyponatremia
• Persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis can be clinical clues to severe hyponatremia and warrant investigation
• A normal brain MRI does not exclude acute neuro-BD, especially in early presentations with aseptic meningitis
• The temporal relationship between treatment and resolution suggests SIADH in neuro-BD may respond to targeting the underlying inflammation rather than just the sodium
• When evaluating hyponatremia in the setting of neurological symptoms, maintain a broad differential that includes CNS inflammation itself as the causative mechanism
• Colchicine monotherapy, while not standard, may be effective in select cases of acute neuro-BD, particularly when the presentation is limited to aseptic meningitis

## The Bottom Line

This case reminds us that hyponatremia in a patient with CNS symptoms isn't always a comorbidity—sometimes it's a direct manifestation of the disease process itself. The IL-6 pathway connecting CNS inflammation to SIADH offers a mechanistic explanation that should change how you approach these patients. When you see SIADH in the setting of neuroinflammation, think beyond the usual suspects and consider whether treating the inflammation might be the key to correcting the sodium.
