Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# Hiccups and Rhabdomyolysis: The Underappreciated Clinical Clues to Severe Hyponatremia

## When Hiccups Aren't Just a Nuisance

Let's talk about a case that serves as a perfect reminder of why we need to think beyond the obvious when evaluating patients with non-specific neurological complaints. A man in his 60s presented with fever, headache, malaise, arthralgia, and — here's the kicker — persistent hiccups. Three days of hiccups. Most of us would file that under "annoying but benign." But in this case, those hiccups were screaming a diagnosis that could have been missed if clinicians hadn't stepped back and looked at the bigger picture.

The patient also had oral aphthae, folliculitis-like rashes on his face and trunk, and on day five of admission, a painful genital ulcer appeared. His initial labs revealed a sodium of 124 mmol/L and a creatine kinase of 1349 U/L. That's not a typo. He had profound hyponatremia with concurrent rhabdomyolysis, and neither finding was on anyone's radar at presentation.

## The Hiccup-Hyponatremia Connection

Persistent hiccups in the setting of severe hyponatremia is one of those clinical associations that, once you learn it, you never forget — but many of us never learned it in the first place. The mechanism isn't fully elucidated, but the prevailing theory involves direct irritation of the diaphragm and phrenic nerve pathways due to cellular edema from hypo-osmolar states. The medullary hiccup reflex arc, which includes the phrenic and vagus nerves plus the reticular formation, appears particularly sensitive to electrolyte disturbances.

Previous case reports have documented hiccups as a presenting symptom of SIADH secondary to CNS inflammation, including meningitis. In this patient, the hiccups weren't an incidental finding — they were a direct clinical manifestation of a sodium of 124. When you see a patient with intractable hiccups in the ED, especially with other neurological symptoms, put hyponatremia on your differential. It might just save you from a diagnostic delay.

## Rhabdomyolysis: The Silent Partner

Here's where the case gets even more interesting. The elevated CK of 1349 U/L wasn't initially connected to the hyponatremia, but rhabdomyolysis is a recognized — though often overlooked — complication of severe hyponatremia. The pathophysiology involves osmotic shifts causing muscle cell swelling and subsequent membrane disruption. As sodium drops, water moves into cells, leading to cellular edema, impaired membrane integrity, and eventual leakage of intracellular contents including CK and myoglobin.

This patient also had arthralgia and general fatigue, which could have been attributed to his underlying inflammatory condition. But the markedly elevated CK tells us something else was happening. In profound hyponatremia, muscle cells are literally bursting under osmotic stress. The lesson here is straightforward: if you're working up a patient with severe hyponatremia, check a CK. And if a patient presents with rhabdomyolysis of unclear etiology, especially with neurological symptoms, think about their sodium.

## The Underlying Diagnosis: Neuro-Behçet's with SIADH

This patient's hyponatremia was ultimately attributed to SIADH in the setting of acute neurological Behçet's disease. His evaluation revealed low serum osmolality (267 mOsm/kg), elevated urine osmolality (900 mOsm/kg), elevated urinary sodium (88 mmol/L), and an ADH level of 22.4 pg/mL — all consistent with inappropriate ADH secretion. He was clinically euvolemic, which helped distinguish SIADH from hypovolemic hyponatremia.

CSF analysis showed aseptic meningitis with mild lymphocytic pleocytosis and elevated protein, but brain MRI was completely normal. This is a crucial point: acute neuro-Behçet's can present as aseptic meningitis without any parenchymal lesions on imaging. A normal brain MRI does not exclude the diagnosis, particularly in early presentation. The combination of mucocutaneous findings (oral aphthae, genital ulcers, folliculitis-like rash) with neurological symptoms and CSF inflammation cinched the diagnosis.

The SIADH likely resulted from CNS inflammation itself. Interleukin-6, a proinflammatory cytokine elevated in neuroinflammatory conditions, has been associated with increased ADH release. In Behçet's disease, aberrant monocyte activation leads to spontaneous IL-6 production, and elevated IL-6 has been documented in the CSF of patients with acute neuro-Behçet's. While IL-6 levels weren't measured in this case, the markedly elevated CRP likely reflected an IL-6-mediated inflammatory response.

## Management: Conservative Approach Wins

Here's what I love about this case: the SIADH was managed entirely conservatively with fluid restriction to less than 800 mL daily. No hypertonic saline, no vasopressin receptor antagonists, no aggressive interventions. By day 16, his sodium had normalized to 136 mmol/L with ADH dropping to 1.8 pg/mL.

The underlying Behçet's disease was treated with colchicine monotherapy — no systemic corticosteroids, no immunosuppressants. His fever resolved the day after starting colchicine, and over two weeks, his symptoms including vertigo, malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions gradually improved. Colchicine's anti-inflammatory effects through inhibition of neutrophil migration and suppression of IL-1β and IL-6 production likely addressed both the systemic inflammation and its downstream endocrine effects.

This conservative approach worked beautifully in this case, but let's be clear: most patients with acute neuro-Behçet's require corticosteroids. Spontaneous resolution has been reported in cases presenting with isolated meningoencephalitis-like symptoms, and colchicine is generally considered ineffective for neuro-Behçet's based on systematic reviews. However, this case demonstrates that when corticosteroids are contraindicated or undesirable, colchicine may be a viable option in select patients with mild presentations.

## Clinical Pearls for the ED Physician

The broader takeaway here is about pattern recognition. When you see a patient with non-specific neurological symptoms — headache, malaise, fever — don't stop at the obvious infectious or inflammatory workup. Look for the subtle clues that point toward metabolic derangements. Persistent hiccups might be annoying, but they can signal profound hyponatremia. Elevated CK in a patient with severe hyponatremia isn't always a red herring — it might be a direct consequence of the electrolyte disturbance itself.

In neuroinflammatory conditions, SIADH should be on your radar as a potential complication. The CNS inflammation that drives the primary disease can also dysregulate ADH secretion through cytokine-mediated pathways. And when you're managing hyponatremia, remember that conservative measures like fluid restriction are often sufficient, particularly when you're simultaneously treating the underlying etiology.

**Key Take-Home Messages:**

- Persistent hiccups can be a clinical manifestation of severe hyponatremia and should prompt immediate electrolyte assessment.
- Rhabdomyolysis is an underrecognized complication of profound hyponatremia due to osmotic muscle cell injury; check CK in patients with severe hyponatremia.
- In patients with neuroinflammatory conditions, maintain a high suspicion for SIADH as a secondary complication.
- Acute neuro-Behçet's disease can present with aseptic meningitis and normal brain MRI; do not exclude the diagnosis based on imaging alone.
- Conservative management with fluid restriction may be sufficient for SIADH when the underlying inflammatory condition is being treated.

The next time a patient presents with "just hiccups" and some vague neurological complaints, remember this case and ask yourself: what's the sodium?
