Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# Aseptic Meningitis with a Normal MRI: Don't Let Imaging Lull You into Missing Neuro-BD

## The Case That Stops You in Your Tracks

A man in his 60s presents with fever, headache, and oral ulcers. His sodium comes back at 124 mmol/L. You're thinking viral syndrome, maybe meningitis, possibly something worse. Then over the next few days, he develops genital ulcers and folliculitis-like rashes. Brain MRI? Completely normal. But the CSF tells a different story: mild lymphocytic pleocytosis, elevated protein, negative infectious workup. The diagnosis? Acute neuro-Behçet's disease, diagnosed concurrently with systemic BD, complicated by SIADH. All of this resolved with colchicine monotherapy alone.

This case is a masterclass in why we need to look beyond imaging and think about the full clinical picture.

## The Normal MRI Trap

Here's what makes this case so instructive. We've been trained that neuro-Behçet's disease means parenchymal brain lesions, typically in the brainstem. The textbooks describe T2 hyperintensities in the basal ganglia, thalamus, or brainstem. So when the MRI comes back stone-cold normal, it's easy to mentally cross "neuro-BD" off your differential.

But here's the critical pearl: acute neuro-BD can present as isolated aseptic meningitis without any parenchymal involvement on MRI. The absence of radiological findings does not exclude the diagnosis, particularly in the early phase of disease. In this patient, the CSF showed the classic pattern of aseptic meningitis — mildly elevated opening pressure, lymphocytic pleocytosis, and elevated protein — despite a completely unrevealing MRI.

The takeaway is straightforward: if you're waiting for brainstem lesions to show up on imaging before considering neuro-BD, you're going to miss cases.

## Concurrent Diagnosis: Breaking the Rules

Classically, neuro-BD develops several years after the initial BD diagnosis. Major organ involvement typically appears around 4 years out, with neurological manifestations at presentation occurring in fewer than 5% of patients. But this case breaks that rule. Here, the systemic BD and neuro-BD were diagnosed simultaneously.

The clinical constellation mattered here. Oral aphthae, genital ulcers, and folliculitis-like rashes earned five points on the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease, clinching the diagnosis of definite BD. But the key was recognizing that the fever, headache, and worsening vertigo represented CNS involvement — even before the genital ulcer appeared on day 5. When you see mucocutaneous lesions in a patient with neurological symptoms and unexplained hyponatremia, you need to broaden your differential beyond infectious causes.

## The SIADH Connection

This case adds another layer: SIADH as a complication of acute neuro-BD. The patient was euvolemic with hypo-osmolar hyponatremia, high urine osmolality, and elevated urinary sodium — classic SIADH. ADH was inappropriately elevated at 22.4 pg/mL.

Why does this matter? CNS inflammation can trigger aberrant ADH secretion. While SIADH is well-described in other neuroinflammatory conditions, its association with neuro-BD is less recognized. The proposed mechanism involves IL-6, a proinflammatory cytokine elevated in neuro-BD CSF, which stimulates ADH release. In this case, the markedly elevated CRP may reflect this IL-6-mediated inflammatory response.

And here's a clinical pearl that shouldn't be missed: persistent hiccups can be an important clue to severe hyponatremia from SIADH. This patient had them. Additionally, the elevated creatine kinase revealed rhabdomyolysis — a recognized but often overlooked complication of profound hyponatremia. These seemingly unrelated symptoms — hiccups, muscle pain — may be pointing you toward a dangerous electrolyte disturbance.

## The Colchicine Curveball

Here's what will surprise you. This patient improved completely on colchicine monotherapy. No steroids. No immunosuppressants. Just colchicine, fluid restriction, and topical treatments for the mucocutaneous lesions.

By conventional wisdom, corticosteroids are the mainstay for acute neuro-BD. Colchicine is generally considered ineffective for neurological disease and is typically used as adjunctive therapy at best. Yet in this case, the fever resolved the day after starting colchicine, and over the following two weeks, all symptoms — mucocutaneous, neurological, and the hyponatremia — gradually improved. The patient remained recurrence-free for four years on colchicine alone.

The authors appropriately acknowledge that spontaneous resolution cannot be excluded, particularly given that some cases of isolated meningoencephalitis-like presentations have been reported to resolve on their own. But the temporal relationship between colchicine initiation and clinical improvement is compelling. Colchicine inhibits neutrophil migration and suppresses IL-1β and IL-6 production, which could theoretically address both the mucocutaneous and CNS manifestations.

What does this mean for practice? Colchicine may be a reasonable option when corticosteroids are contraindicated or undesirable, though we should interpret this cautiously given the n=1 nature of this report.

## A Practical Approach to Aseptic Meningitis

When your patient has fever, headache, and CSF showing lymphocytic pleocytosis with elevated protein, your differential should be systematic. Viral meningoencephalitis (HSV, VZV) needs to be ruled out early. Bacterial meningitis typically shows neutrophilic predominance, but partially treated cases can muddy the waters. Tuberculous meningitis and fungal meningitis should be considered based on risk factors and CSF characteristics.

Beyond infection, think about autoimmune etiologies, neurosarcoidosis (though ACE has limited sensitivity), and malignancy. The authors checked sIL-2R, which was mildly elevated but proved to be a non-specific marker of inflammation rather than indicative of lymphoma.

The key lesson here is that the diagnostic workup should be guided by clinical context, not indiscriminate testing. When mucocutaneous lesions appear in a patient with aseptic meningitis, BD needs to move up your differential — even if the MRI is normal.

## Key Take-Home Points

- A normal brain MRI does not exclude acute neuro-BD; the disease can present as isolated aseptic meningitis without parenchymal lesions, especially early in the disease course.
- Neuro-BD can be diagnosed concurrently with systemic BD, even though it classically develops years after initial diagnosis.
- Consider SIADH in patients with neuro-BD and hyponatremia; CNS inflammation may drive inappropriate ADH secretion.
- Persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis can be clinical clues to severe hyponatremia from SIADH.
- Colchicine monotherapy may be sufficient for some cases of acute neuro-BD, though this requires further study and steroids remain the standard of care.
- When evaluating aseptic meningitis, let the clinical context — including mucocutaneous findings — guide your differential rather than relying solely on imaging findings.
