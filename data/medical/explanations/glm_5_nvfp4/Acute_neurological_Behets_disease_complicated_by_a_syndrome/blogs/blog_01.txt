Title: Blog about the case report: "Acute neurological Behçet's disease complicated by a syndrome of inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone"

# When Behçet's Hits the Brain First: Recognizing Concurrent Neuro-BD at Presentation

Here's a scenario that should make you pause: a patient presents with fever, headache, and hyponatremia. You find oral ulcers and a rash. Your differential is spinning through meningitis, viral encephalitis, maybe something autoimmune. But what if the answer requires you to connect mucocutaneous findings with neurological symptoms in a way that defies the typical timeline of a rare disease?

This case is a reminder that Behçet's disease doesn't always follow the textbook script. Neurological involvement typically develops years after the initial diagnosis—yet sometimes, it walks through the door at the same time.

## The Case That Breaks the Rules

A man in his 60s presented with three days of fever, headache, malaise, arthralgia, and persistent hiccups. He had oral aphthae on exam. Initial labs showed sodium of 124 mmol/L with a markedly elevated CRP. He was euvolemic. The team appropriately worked up his hyponatremia and found low serum osmolality (267 mOsm/kg), elevated urine osmolality (900 mOsm/kg), and elevated urinary sodium (88 mmol/L)—classic SIADH.

On day three, his headache and vertigo worsened. Brain MRI was normal. But CSF analysis told a different story: elevated opening pressure, increased protein, and lymphocytic pleocytosis with negative infectious workup. Then on day five, a painful genital ulcer appeared. Combined with his oral aphthae and folliculitis-like rash, the picture crystallized. HLA-B51 was positive. Using the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease, he scored 5 points—definite BD.

## Why This Matters

Here's what makes this case remarkable: neuro-BD and systemic BD were diagnosed simultaneously. In fewer than 5% of BD patients, neurological manifestations occur at presentation. Typically, major organ involvement develops around four years after initial diagnosis. This patient had no prior history of BD, yet presented with acute neuro-BD manifesting as aseptic meningitis.

The SIADH adds another layer. While drug-induced SIADH has been reported in BD patients on cyclophosphamide, this case demonstrates SIADH occurring solely from CNS inflammation related to acute neuro-BD. The mechanism likely involves IL-6–mediated inflammation triggering aberrant ADH release—a hypothesis supported by the patient's markedly elevated CRP and the resolution of both neurological symptoms and hyponatremia with treatment.

Two clinical clues in this case deserve mention: persistent hiccups and elevated creatine kinase. Hiccups can signal severe hyponatremia from SIADH secondary to CNS inflammation. The CK elevation (1349 U/L) reflected rhabdomyolysis—a recognized but often overlooked complication of profound hyponatremia. These weren't incidental findings; they were clinical breadcrumbs pointing toward a dangerous electrolyte disturbance.

## The Diagnostic Challenge

The absence of parenchymal brain lesions on MRI did not exclude neuro-BD. Acute neuro-BD can present as aseptic meningitis without radiographic abnormalities, particularly early in the disease course. The CSF findings—lymphocytic pleocytosis with elevated protein in the setting of negative infectious studies—were essential to the diagnosis.

The International Criteria for Behçet's Disease provided the framework: oral aphthae (2 points), genital aphthosis (2 points), and folliculitis-like rash (1 point) yielded a score of 5, meeting the threshold for definite disease. Genital aphthosis is particularly specific for BD and should raise your suspicion when present alongside other mucocutaneous findings.

## Treatment: Surprising Success with Colchicine

Corticosteroids are the mainstay for acute neuro-BD. But this patient received colchicine monotherapy—and improved. His fever resolved the day after starting colchicine. Over two weeks, his vertigo, malaise, hiccups, and mucocutaneous lesions gradually resolved. His sodium normalized to 136 mmol/L, and ADH levels dropped appropriately. He required no systemic corticosteroids or additional immunosuppressants.

This challenges conventional teaching. Systematic reviews have shown colchicine is ineffective for acute neuro-BD, and immunosuppressive agents are generally required. However, in cases presenting solely with meningoencephalitis-like symptoms, spontaneous resolution has been reported. Whether this patient's improvement reflects colchicine's anti-inflammatory effects—suppressing IL-1β and IL-6—or disease self-resolution remains unclear. What we can say: colchicine monotherapy worked here, and it may be a reasonable option when corticosteroids are contraindicated or undesirable.

## Key Take-Home Points

- Neuro-BD typically develops years after BD diagnosis, but concurrent presentation occurs in rare cases—maintain a low threshold for considering neuro-BD in newly diagnosed BD patients with CNS symptoms.
- A normal brain MRI does not exclude acute neuro-BD; CSF analysis showing aseptic meningitis can establish the diagnosis even without parenchymal lesions.
- SIADH can complicate CNS inflammation from acute neuro-BD; watch for hyponatremia in patients presenting with headache, fever, and neurological symptoms.
- Persistent hiccups and rhabdomyolysis may be clinical clues to severe hyponatremia secondary to SIADH—don't dismiss them as incidental.
- Colchicine monotherapy led to resolution in this case; while corticosteroids remain standard, colchicine may be considered in select patients, particularly when the presentation is limited to meningoencephalitis-like symptoms.
- Use the International Criteria for Behçet's Disease to systematically evaluate suspected cases; genital aphthosis is a specific finding that strongly supports the diagnosis.

This case underscores the importance of pattern recognition across organ systems. When a patient presents with fever, headache, hyponatremia, and mucocutaneous lesions, connecting the dots early can lead to prompt diagnosis and appropriate management—even when the disease defies its typical timeline.
