Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

Chapter 1: ## Case Presentation and Initial Clinical Assessment

The case involves a woman in her 60s who presented to the emergency department with an acute anaphylactic reaction. Her medical history provides important context for understanding both her risk factors and the clinical decisions that followed. She had known comorbidities including type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. Her medication regimen included metformin, gliclazide, hydrochlorothiazide, omeprazole, and sertraline. Crucially, she carried a known allergy to beta-lactam antibiotics, based on a mild reaction to amoxicillin in adolescence that had been limited to facial swelling without oropharyngeal involvement, respiratory symptoms, or hypotension. She had also experienced a strong local reaction to a wasp sting, though without systemic features. This history of allergic sensitivity, while not previously severe, is relevant when considering her heightened immunological vulnerability.

The sequence of events leading to her presentation began five days earlier, when she was prescribed clindamycin for erysipelas. After five days of clindamycin therapy, she developed a rash on her abdomen that was interpreted as a delayed allergic reaction. Her general practitioner then switched her antibiotic regimen to clarithromycin, a macrolide antibiotic to which she had no prior exposure. Within one hour of taking the first oral dose of clarithromycin 500 mg, she developed symptoms and sought medical attention one hour later.

Her presenting symptoms were characteristic of a systemic allergic reaction: pruritus, flushing, increased perspiration, agitation, and a sensation of throat swelling. The involvement of multiple organ systems—skin, respiratory, and cardiovascular—signaled a potentially life-threatening process. On initial evaluation in the emergency department, she demonstrated clear hemodynamic and respiratory compromise. Her blood pressure was 74/50 mm Hg, indicating hypotension, and her peripheral oxygen saturation was 88% on room air with a respiratory rate of 22 breaths per minute. This combination of hypotension and hypoxemia reflects the pathophysiology of anaphylaxis, where widespread mast cell degranulation releases histamine and other inflammatory mediators, causing vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, and bronchospasm.

Physical examination revealed facial swelling and new-onset generalized erythema and urticaria distributed across her entire body. Importantly, there was no observable swelling of the tongue or throat, and cardiac and lung auscultation were normal. The absence of stridor or wheezing does not exclude anaphylaxis, as cutaneous and hemodynamic manifestations may predominate. Her blood glucose was elevated at 12.7 mmol/L, likely reflecting the physiological stress response, and her ECG showed normal sinus rhythm.

The clinical diagnosis of anaphylaxis is made when specific criteria are met. According to widely accepted diagnostic criteria, anaphylaxis is highly likely when there is acute onset of illness with involvement of the skin or mucosal tissue (such as urticaria, erythema, or angioedema) combined with either respiratory compromise or hypotension. This patient met these criteria unequivocally: she had urticaria and generalized erythema accompanied by hypotension and hypoxemia. The temporal relationship to clarithromycin ingestion further supported the diagnosis, establishing a clear precipitant. Rapid clinical recognition allowed for immediate initiation of treatment, which is essential given that delayed epinephrine administration is associated with worse outcomes in anaphylaxis.
