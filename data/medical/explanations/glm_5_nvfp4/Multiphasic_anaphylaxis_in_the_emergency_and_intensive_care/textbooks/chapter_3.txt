Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

Chapter 3: ## Acute Management and the Challenge of Recurrent Episodes

The management of anaphylaxis centers on prompt recognition and immediate administration of epinephrine, the only medication proven to reduce mortality. In this case, the patient presented with classic anaphylactic features—hypotension, hypoxemia, urticaria, and facial angioedema—prompting rapid treatment. She received intramuscular epinephrine via auto-injector (0.3 mg), intravenous clemastine (an H1 antihistamine), supplemental oxygen, and intravenous fluid resuscitation. This initial approach follows standard anaphylaxis protocols and resulted in rapid clinical improvement. However, the subsequent clinical course highlighted the complexity of managing multiphasic anaphylaxis, where symptoms recur despite appropriate initial treatment.

A critical pharmacologic consideration in this case involves the epinephrine dosing. Current guidelines recommend 0.01 mg/kg intramuscularly, up to a maximum of 0.5 mg for adults. The auto-injectors used for this patient's first two episodes contained only 0.3 mg—below the recommended dose for an adult weighing 104 kg. While auto-injectors offer practical advantages in time-sensitive settings by reducing treatment delays and hesitancy among personnel unfamiliar with vasoactive medications, they may result in underdosing in larger adults. Recent evidence suggests that patients receiving 0.3 mg initially are more likely to require additional epinephrine injections compared to those receiving the full 0.5 mg dose. In this case, the treating team transitioned to individually prepared 0.5 mg intramuscular doses for the third and fourth episodes, aligning with guideline recommendations.

The patient experienced four distinct anaphylactic episodes over approximately 12 hours. Each episode met clinical criteria for anaphylaxis and required intramuscular epinephrine for resolution. Notably, symptom intensity progressively diminished—from profound hypoxemia with cyanosis, hypotension, and severe dyspnea initially, to milder manifestations of dyspnea, rash, and pruritus during the final episode. This pattern suggests gradual resolution of the hyperallergic state, though the mechanism underlying this attenuation remains unclear. The decreasing severity provided some clinical reassurance, yet each episode still warranted full treatment given the unpredictable nature of anaphylaxis.

Beyond epinephrine, adjunctive therapies were employed. Antihistamines (clemastine) targeted cutaneous symptoms, while intravenous normal saline supported hemodynamic stability. Supplemental oxygen was administered during episodes of hypoxemia and could be discontinued between episodes as the patient's oxygen saturation improved. These adjuncts address specific symptom domains but do not replace epinephrine as first-line therapy.

Corticosteroids were administered after the first relapse, reflecting traditional practice aimed at preventing biphasic reactions. The patient received both prednisone (30 mg intravenously daily for five days) and a single dose of dexamethasone (8 mg intravenously), a discrepancy arising from differing protocols between the general ward and intensive care unit. This duplication underscores the persistence of corticosteroid use in clinical practice despite mounting evidence questioning their efficacy. A 2017 systematic review found no benefit of corticosteroids in preventing biphasic anaphylaxis, and recent studies suggest potential harm, including increased rates of hospital admission. Contemporary guidelines increasingly advise against routine corticosteroid administration, yet clinical inertia and lingering beliefs about their utility continue to influence practice.

Following the first relapse, the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit for enhanced monitoring. This decision reflects appropriate caution, as biphasic reactions can be severe and unpredictable. ICU admission enabled continuous assessment and immediate access to resuscitation resources. The allergology department was consulted early, providing specialized input on management options. Two alternative strategies were considered: continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion to preempt recurrent symptoms, or an expectant approach awaiting spontaneous resolution. Both approaches carry risks—continuous epinephrine infusion requires careful titration and monitoring for adverse effects, while expectant management risks delayed treatment of potentially life-threatening recurrences. The lack of evidence-based guidance for multiphasic anaphylaxis complicated this decision-making process.

Ultimately, neither alternative strategy was necessary, as the patient experienced no further episodes. This outcome reinforces the importance of close observation and readiness to intervene, while highlighting the therapeutic uncertainty that characterizes multiphasic anaphylaxis management.
