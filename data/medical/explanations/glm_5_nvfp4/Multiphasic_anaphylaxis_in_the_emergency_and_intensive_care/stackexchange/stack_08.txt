Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Morning Report] What are the proposed risk factors for biphasic anaphylaxis, and why is reliable prediction of this complication challenging?

**Clinical Teaching Answer:**

Biphasic anaphylaxis refers to recurrence of anaphylactic symptoms after apparent clinical resolution, without re-exposure to the triggering allergen. The reported incidence varies widely—from 0.4% to 23.3%, though most data suggest a rate of 4% to 6%. This variability itself reflects one of the fundamental challenges in studying and predicting this phenomenon.

**Proposed Risk Factors**
Based on available evidence, several risk factors have been suggested:
- **Delayed epinephrine administration** during the initial reaction
- **Severity of the initial presentation** (particularly hypotension or respiratory compromise)
- **Requirement for multiple epinephrine doses** to achieve initial symptom control
- **History of prior biphasic reactions**

In the presented case, our patient demonstrated several of these features: a severe initial presentation with hypotension (74/50 mm Hg), hypoxemia (SpO₂ 88%), and requirement for multiple epinephrine doses across four distinct anaphylactic episodes. Additionally, her evaluation revealed elevated tryptase levels and features suggestive of systemic mastocytosis—a condition known to predispose patients to severe anaphylaxis, though its specific relationship to biphasic reactions remains uncharacterized.

**Why Prediction Remains Challenging**
Despite these proposed associations, no reliable predictive model exists for several reasons:

1. **Inconsistent definitions** across studies complicate the evidence base, making meta-analyses difficult.

2. **Low incidence** limits statistical power to identify true predictors versus coincidental associations.

3. **Confounding by treatment factors**—for example, it remains unclear whether multiple epinephrine doses are a marker of reaction severity or whether underdosing (such as the 0.3 mg auto-injector used initially in this case) contributes to incomplete resolution and subsequent recurrence.

4. **Heterogeneous underlying mechanisms**—as illustrated by this patient's probable mastocytosis—suggest that different pathophysiologic processes may drive biphasic patterns in different patients.

The clinical bottom line is that we cannot currently identify which patients will develop biphasic reactions. This uncertainty underlies guideline recommendations for observation periods after anaphylaxis, typically 4 to 6 hours, though even this lacks strong evidence. Until better predictive tools emerge, clinicians must maintain a low threshold for extended observation in patients with severe presentations or multiple epinephrine requirements, while counseling all patients with anaphylaxis about the possibility of symptom recurrence and the need to seek immediate care should symptoms return.
