Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Clinical Pearls] What is the recommended timing for serum tryptase level measurement in patients presenting with anaphylaxis, and what is the rationale for this specific sequence?

### Clinical Teaching Answer

The recommended approach is to obtain **a minimum of two, ideally three, serum tryptase samples** at specific time points: (1) as soon as feasible after symptom onset without delaying treatment, (2) at 1–2 hours (and within 4 hours) of symptom onset when tryptase typically peaks, and (3) at least 24 hours after full clinical resolution to establish a baseline level.

**Rationale:** Tryptase is a protease released from activated mast cells during anaphylaxis. Serum levels rise rapidly, peak within 1–2 hours of symptom onset, and have a half-life of approximately 2 hours. By capturing both the peak level and a baseline level, clinicians can calculate the delta between acute and resting states. A ratio of acute-to-baseline tryptase exceeding 2 (with the baseline sample obtained ≥24 hours after resolution) strongly supports mast cell activation and helps differentiate true anaphylaxis from other causes of hypotension or shock. Importantly, an elevated baseline tryptase may suggest underlying mast cell disorders such as systemic mastocytosis, which predisposes patients to severe or atypical anaphylaxis.

**In this case**, the recommended protocol was not followed. The first tryptase was drawn 1 hour after the *third* anaphylactic episode rather than during initial presentation, and the second sample was obtained 27 hours after the last recurrence. Despite this, both the acute and baseline levels were elevated, raising clinical suspicion for systemic mastocytosis—a diagnosis that could explain her multiphasic course and severe reaction to clarithromycin. This case underscores how adherence to guideline-recommended tryptase timing improves diagnostic yield and facilitates identification of underlying mast cell disease.
