Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Morning Report] How do you differentiate between biphasic anaphylaxis, sustained anaphylaxis, and refractory anaphylaxis in clinical practice?

**Clinical Teaching Answer**

Differentiating these three entities hinges on the **temporal pattern of symptom recurrence** and the **response to appropriately administered epinephrine**.

**Biphasic anaphylaxis** is characterized by an initial anaphylactic reaction followed by complete symptom resolution—an asymptomatic interval—and subsequent recurrence of symptoms without re-exposure to the trigger. The recurrence typically occurs within 24 hours but has been reported up to 72 hours later. In the presented case, the patient experienced multiphasic anaphylaxis, with four discrete episodes requiring separate epinephrine administrations after initial clinical improvement. The key feature is the *clear asymptomatic interval* between episodes.

**Sustained anaphylaxis** represents ongoing symptoms due to persistent antigen absorption or delayed drug clearance (e.g., slow-release formulations, sustained gastrointestinal exposure). Here, symptoms may transiently improve with epinephrine but recur promptly as the drug effect wanes, without a true asymptomatic period. The clinical clue is *failure to achieve sustained resolution* despite appropriate therapy. This must be differentiated from biphasic reactions, where complete resolution occurs between episodes.

**Refractory anaphylaxis** denotes persistent anaphylaxis that fails to respond adequately to correctly administered epinephrine at appropriate doses and intervals. This is a state of *treatment failure*, not recurrence. Clinically, hypotension or airway compromise persists despite adequate dosing.

In practice, careful documentation of symptom timelines and epinephrine response is essential. The case illustrates this challenge well: after initial stabilization, the patient had discrete relapses requiring additional epinephrine doses, consistent with biphasic (multiphasic) anaphylaxis rather than refractory disease, as each episode responded appropriately to treatment. Underlying mast cell disorders, suspected in this case, may predispose to such protracted courses.
