Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# The 0.3mg Problem: Why Your Epinephrine Auto-Injector May Be Underdosing Adults

## The Case That Stopped Me in My Tracks

A woman in her 60s presents with anaphylaxis after taking her first dose of clarithromycin. You do everything right: prompt intramuscular epinephrine, antihistamines, fluids, and supplemental oxygen. She responds beautifully. Blood pressure normalizes, flushing resolves, and she looks ready for observation admission. But then it happens again. And again. And again. Four separate anaphylactic episodes over 12 hours, each requiring IM epinephrine.

But here's what caught my attention: her first two epinephrine doses came from auto-injectors delivering 0.3mg each. Her second two doses? Manually prepared 0.5mg injections. This wasn't arbitrary; it reflected a practical reality in many EDs. Auto-injectors are fast, familiar, and reduce hesitancy when staff aren't comfortable drawing up epinephrine on the fly. But we may be systematically underdosing our adult patients when we reach for that device.

## The Dosing Gap We Don't Talk About Enough

Current guidelines recommend 0.01 mg/kg IM epinephrine for anaphylaxis, with a maximum of 0.5mg for adults. The math is straightforward: most adults exceed 50kg, making 0.5mg the appropriate dose. Yet many auto-injectors labeled for "adults" contain only 0.3mg. That's a 40% underdose for a 60kg patient, and even worse for larger individuals.

The authors cite a 2025 retrospective study showing patients who initially received 0.3mg epinephrine required escalation of care significantly more often than those receiving the guideline-recommended 0.5mg. This shouldn't surprise us. Epinephrine's half-life is short, roughly 2-3 hours, and underdosing means suboptimal alpha and beta effects precisely when you need robust vasoconstriction and bronchodilation.

## Why Auto-Injectors Persist Despite the Problem

The case authors acknowledge something many of us have felt: in time-sensitive settings, or in hospitals where anaphylaxis isn't a daily occurrence, auto-injectors reduce delays. Staff hesitancy with vasoactive agents is real. Drawing up 0.5mg from a vial, calculating the volume, ensuring proper IM injection technique, all while the patient is crashing? That friction matters.

But convenience shouldn't trump adequate dosing for a life-threatening condition. The solution isn't abandoning auto-injectors entirely; they remain essential for prehospital care and patient self-administration. The solution is recognizing when they're inadequate and being prepared to deliver the proper dose manually.

## Multiphasic Anaphylaxis: When One Episode Isn't Enough

This case also highlights the phenomenon of multiphasic anaphylaxis: recurrent symptoms after apparent resolution, without re-exposure to the trigger. Biphasic reactions are reported in 4-6% of anaphylaxis cases, though estimates vary wildly. Multiphasic presentations with multiple recurrences, like this patient experienced, are rarer still and profoundly challenging to manage.

The authors describe considering two approaches: continuous IV epinephrine infusion to pre-empt recurrences versus watchful waiting given the decreasing symptom severity. Neither option felt comfortable given the lack of evidence. This is the uncomfortable reality of managing rare, severe presentations: we're often operating on expert opinion and institutional preference rather than robust data.

## Steroids: The Sacred Cow That Needs Retiring

Here's what struck me about this case's management: the patient received both prednisone 30mg IV daily for 5 days AND a dose of dexamethasone 8mg IV, because two different hospital protocols conflicted. Yet contemporary evidence increasingly shows steroids don't prevent biphasic anaphylaxis. A 2017 systematic review found 21 of 22 studies showed no benefit. A 2019 study even suggested increased ICU admission rates with prehospital steroid administration.

Old habits die hard, and steroid administration for anaphylaxis remains deeply ingrained. But this case illustrates the problem: patients receive medications that don't help, may potentially harm, and divert attention from what actually matters: epinephrine, early and adequate.

## The Hidden Diagnosis: Systemic Mastocytosis

The atypical course prompted investigation into underlying causes, revealing elevated tryptase levels during and after the acute event, along with hepatomegaly and neuropsychiatric symptoms. The conclusion? A high probability of systemic mastocytosis, even without meeting formal WHO diagnostic criteria.

This matters because systemic mastocytosis predisposes patients to severe, prolonged, and potentially refractory anaphylaxis. Elevated baseline tryptase should prompt consideration of mast cell disorders, particularly in patients with atypical anaphylaxis courses or multiple epinephrine requirements.

One technical note: the authors acknowledge they didn't collect tryptase levels at optimal intervals. Guidelines recommend samples at presentation, 1-2 hours post-symptom onset, and at least 24 hours after full resolution. This patient's levels were drawn later than ideal, which limited diagnostic certainty. When anaphylaxis rolls through your ED, remember to grab those tryptase levels early.

## Key Take-Homes

- Adult epinephrine auto-injectors often deliver 0.3mg when guidelines recommend 0.5mg; this underdosing correlates with increased need for treatment escalation and may contribute to prolonged or recurrent anaphylaxis.
- In hospital settings, prioritize manually drawn 0.5mg IM epinephrine for adults over 50kg rather than defaulting to auto-injectors, and ensure staff are trained and comfortable with this technique.
- Corticosteroids for anaphylaxis lack evidence for preventing biphasic reactions and may cause harm; focus your energy on epinephrine and supportive care.
- Multiphasic anaphylaxis is rare and management strategies are largely consensus-based; consider continuous epinephrine infusion for severe or recurrent cases and involve allergy specialists early.
- Atypical, severe, or prolonged anaphylaxis should prompt consideration of systemic mastocytosis; obtain properly timed tryptase levels to aid diagnosis.

This case reminds us that anaphylaxis management extends beyond the initial resuscitation. Understanding dosing nuances, questioning legacy treatments like steroids, and recognizing when to investigate for underlying mast cell disease can make the difference between a straightforward admission and a prolonged, dangerous ICU course. The next time you reach for that auto-injector in a crashing adult, ask yourself: is 0.3mg really enough?
