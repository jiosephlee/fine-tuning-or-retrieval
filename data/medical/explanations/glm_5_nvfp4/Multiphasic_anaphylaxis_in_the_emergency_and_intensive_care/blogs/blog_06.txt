Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# How Long is Long Enough? The Observation Time Dilemma

## The Case That Made Me Rethink Everything

A woman in her 60s presents with anaphylaxis after her first dose of clarithromycin. She gets epinephrine, antihistamines, fluids, and oxygen. She looks great. Symptoms resolve. Blood pressure normalizes. She's admitted to a general ward for observation. And then it happens again. And again. And again.

Four separate episodes of anaphylaxis. Each requiring intramuscular epinephrine. Each occurring without any re-exposure to the trigger. This case of multiphasic anaphylaxis, published by Schneider and colleagues, is the kind of case that keeps emergency physicians up at night, not because it's common, but because it exposes just how little we actually know about observation periods after anaphylaxis.

## The Biphasic Problem

Here's the uncomfortable reality: biphasic anaphylaxis is real, but the data surrounding it is a mess. Reported incidence ranges from 0.4% to 23.3%, though most credible estimates land somewhere between 4% and 6%. That's a wide enough range to be clinically useless for any individual patient. Symptoms can recur anywhere from hours after initial presentation up to 78 hours later, though most happen within the first 24 hours.

The Dutch guidelines and World Allergy Organisation both note that approximately half of biphasic reactions occur within 6 to 12 hours of the initial event, but neither commits to a specific monitoring recommendation. Traditional teaching suggests 4 to 6 hours of observation after symptom resolution, but if half of biphasic reactions happen after 6 hours, are we really observing our patients long enough?

## The UK Three-Tier Approach: A Step Forward?

The UK 2021 guideline attempts to bring some rational structure to this problem with a three-tier risk-stratified approach. Rather than a one-size-fits-all observation period, it proposes stratifying patients based on risk factors and severity. This makes intuitive sense. The patient who needed one dose of epinephrine and responded immediately is different from the patient who required multiple doses, had delayed presentation, or showed severe hypotension and hypoxia.

But here's the problem: we don't actually have reliable predictors for biphasic reactions. Suggested risk factors include delayed epinephrine administration, severe initial presentation, multiple epinephrine doses, and prior biphasic reactions, but none of these are sufficiently sensitive or specific to guide decision-making with confidence. In the case presented, the patient had multiple epinephrine doses, severe hypotension, and hypoxemia. She was exactly the kind of patient who should have been observed closely, and she was. She was in the ICU. And she still had recurrent episodes.

## The Steroid Myth Needs to Die

This case also highlights something we need to confront head-on: corticosteroids do not prevent biphasic anaphylaxis. This patient received both prednisone and dexamethasone per conflicting hospital protocols, and she still had recurrent episodes. The 2017 systematic review examined 22 studies, and only one showed any benefit. Twenty-one studies showed no evidence that corticosteroids reduce the risk of biphasic reaction.

A 2019 study even demonstrated increased likelihood of ICU or hospital ward admission after prehospital corticosteroid treatment. The side effect profile matters. Yet clinical practice persists in prescribing steroids routinely, perhaps out of habit, perhaps out of a need to feel like we're doing something. It is time to stop.

## A Practical Framework

So what do we do with a patient who has fully resolved anaphylaxis in the ED? Here is a practical approach that balances safety with resource utilization.

For low-risk patients, those who had mild reactions, received prompt treatment with rapid response, and required only a single dose of epinephrine, observation for 4 to 6 hours after symptom resolution is reasonable. These patients can likely be safely discharged with clear return precautions and an epinephrine auto-injector prescription.

For moderate-risk patients, those who required multiple epinephrine doses, had delayed presentation, or experienced hypotension or hypoxemia, extended observation of 12 to 24 hours is warranted. This captures most biphasic reactions while avoiding unnecessary prolonged admissions.

For high-risk patients, those with severe presentations requiring ICU-level care, refractory anaphylaxis, or suspected underlying conditions like mastocytosis, admission and prolonged monitoring is appropriate. This patient required ICU observation, and appropriately so.

## Think About Mastocytosis

This case also serves as an important reminder to consider systemic mastocytosis in patients with unusual anaphylaxis presentations. The patient had elevated tryptase levels, hepatomegaly, and neuropsychiatric symptoms including depression and memory problems. She likely had systemic mastocytosis even though she did not meet formal WHO diagnostic criteria.

Patients with mast cell disorders are predisposed to severe, recurrent, and atypical anaphylaxis. If the clinical course seems bizarre, if symptoms keep coming back, or if there is no clear trigger, think about mast cell disease and check tryptase levels. Just remember to draw them correctly: one sample as soon as feasible, another 1 to 2 hours after symptom onset, and a baseline sample at least 24 hours after full resolution.

## Key Take-Home Points

- Biphasic anaphylaxis occurs in approximately 4 to 6 percent of cases, with symptom recurrence possible up to 72 hours after initial presentation.
- There are no reliable predictors for which patients will develop biphasic reactions; risk stratification remains imperfect.
- Corticosteroids do not prevent biphasic anaphylaxis and should not be routinely prescribed; the evidence is clear on this.
- Consider extended observation of 12 to 24 hours for patients who required multiple epinephrine doses or had severe presentations with hypotension or hypoxemia.
- In atypical, severe, or recurrent anaphylaxis, consider underlying mast cell disorders and obtain appropriate tryptase levels.
- Use proper epinephrine dosing: 0.5 mg intramuscularly for adults, recognizing that many auto-injectors contain only 0.3 mg and may represent underdosing.

## The Bottom Line

We still do not have great answers for how long to observe patients after anaphylaxis, but we have enough data to move beyond dogmatic 4-hour observation periods or reflexive steroid prescriptions. This case, with its four separate episodes of recurrent anaphylaxis, reminds us that biphasic and multiphasic reactions are real, that current guidelines are based on limited evidence, and that clinical judgment remains essential in navigating the observation time dilemma.
