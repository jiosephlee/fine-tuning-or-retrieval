Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# The Tryptase Tango: Getting Your Timing Right

We've all been there. A patient rolls in with anaphylaxis, you nail the airway, give the epinephrine, stabilize the hemodynamics, and get them admitted. Job well done. But here's the uncomfortable truth from a fascinating case report out of the Netherlands: if you're not thinking about tryptase sampling from the moment that patient hits the resuscitation bay, you're missing a critical diagnostic window that could change their life.

The case involves a woman in her 60s who presented with anaphylaxis to clarithromycin. What makes this case remarkable isn't just the initial presentation; it's that she went on to have four separate anaphylactic episodes over 12 hours despite appropriate treatment each time. This is multiphasic anaphylaxis, and it's the kind of presentation that should make you step back and wonder whether there's something more going on with your patient's mast cells.

Here's where the tryptase timing really matters. In this case, the first tryptase sample wasn't drawn until after the third anaphylactic episode. The allergology team was consulted, but by then the optimal sampling window had already slipped away. The guidelines are actually quite clear on this: you need a minimum of two samples, ideally three, at specific intervals. The first sample should be drawn as soon as feasible without delaying treatment. The second sample needs to be collected 1 to 2 hours after symptom onset, ideally within 4 hours, when tryptase levels typically peak. Then a third sample at least 24 hours after full clinical resolution establishes the patient's baseline.

Why does this matter so much? Elevated acute tryptase with a normal baseline confirms anaphylaxis. But an elevated baseline tryptase is a red flag for systemic mastocytosis, a condition that predisposes patients to severe and potentially multiphasic anaphylaxis. In this patient, both acute and baseline tryptase levels were elevated, and subsequent workup revealed hepatomegaly and neuropsychiatric symptoms, all pointing toward systemic mastocytosis even though she didn't meet formal WHO diagnostic criteria on bone marrow biopsy.

The diagnostic yield drops dramatically when you miss those sampling windows. Tryptase has a relatively short half-life of about 2 hours, so if you wait until the patient is settled on the floor or until after multiple recurrent episodes, you've lost the ability to see the true peak. And without that baseline sample after resolution, you can't determine whether an elevated tryptase represents an acute mast cell activation event or simply reflects the patient's steady state.

This case also serves as a reminder that multiphasic anaphylaxis, while rare, should prompt you to think beyond the immediate trigger. When a patient requires multiple epinephrine doses over hours without re-exposure to the allergen, consider whether underlying mast cell disease might be contributing. These patients may need longer observation periods and should have expedited referral to allergology for comprehensive evaluation.

The practical reality is that in a busy emergency department, tryptase sampling often falls by the wayside. The patient is sick, you're managing multiple critical tasks, and drawing tubes for what feels like a diagnostic afterthought doesn't feel urgent. But building this into your initial workflow, perhaps even having a standing order set that includes tryptase timing instructions, ensures you don't miss this window. Your future self and the allergist will thank you.

- Draw the first tryptase sample as early as possible without delaying resuscitation
- Draw the second sample 1-2 hours after symptom onset, within 4 hours maximum, to capture peak levels
- Draw the third sample at least 24 hours after full symptom resolution to establish baseline
- In multiphasic or severe anaphylaxis, maintain a high index of suspicion for systemic mastocytosis
- Elevated baseline tryptase warrants outpatient allergology referral for further mast cell disease evaluation

Getting tryptase timing right transforms what could be a missed diagnostic opportunity into actionable information that helps identify patients at risk for recurrent, severe anaphylaxis and connects them with the long-term management they need.
