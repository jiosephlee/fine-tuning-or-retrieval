Title: Blog about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

# When Anaphylaxis Keeps Coming Back: Making Sense of Multiphasic Reactions

You've treated the anaphylaxis. The epinephrine worked. Your patient looks great, symptoms resolved, and you're mentally calculating observation time before discharge. Then the rash returns. The blood pressure drops. You're reaching for epinephrine again. Welcome to the unsettling world of biphasic and multiphasic anaphylaxis.

## The Case That Refused to Stay Resolved

A woman in her 60s presented to the emergency department with anaphylaxis one hour after taking her first dose of clarithromycin. She had a history of a mild allergic reaction to amoxicillin decades prior and a strong local reaction to a wasp sting, but this was her first episode of true anaphylaxis. Her initial presentation was textbook: pruritus, flushing, facial swelling, hypotension (74/50 mm Hg), and hypoxemia (88% on room air). She received intramuscular epinephrine, antihistamines, oxygen, and fluids, with good initial response.

Here's where things get interesting. Despite an apparent full recovery, she developed recurrent anaphylactic shock requiring additional epinephrine doses. In total, she experienced four discrete anaphylactic episodes over approximately 12 hours. Each episode required intramuscular epinephrine to resolve, though interestingly, the severity progressively decreased with each recurrence, from cyanosis and severe hypotension initially to just rash and pruritus by the final episode.

## The Biphasic Problem: What We Actually Know

Biphasic anaphylaxis refers to symptom recurrence after apparent resolution, without re-exposure to the trigger. It's the clinical equivalent of a horror movie where the villain jumps up one last time just when you think it's over. The reported incidence varies wildly, from 0.4% to 23.3%, though most data suggests a real incidence around 4-6%. Multiphasic reactions, with multiple discrete recurrences, are even rarer and essentially unstudied.

The timing is all over the map. Most recurrences happen within 24 hours, but case reports document relapses up to 78 hours later. This creates a genuine clinical dilemma: how long do you observe, and who actually needs prolonged monitoring?

## Prediction: A Fool's Errand

Here's the uncomfortable truth: we don't have reliable predictors for biphasic reactions. Proposed risk factors include delayed epinephrine administration, severe initial presentation, multiple epinephrine doses required, and prior biphasic reactions. But none of these are sufficiently predictive to meaningfully risk-stratify patients. The Dutch and World Allergy Organisation guidelines note that roughly half of biphasic reactions occur within 6-12 hours, but stop short of recommending specific observation durations. The UK guideline attempts a three-tier risk-stratified approach, but the evidence base remains thin.

## The Steroid Myth

This case highlights a persistent myth in anaphylaxis management: the idea that corticosteroids prevent biphasic reactions. The patient in this case received both prednisone and dexamethasone from different hospital protocols, yet she still developed recurrent anaphylaxis. This shouldn't surprise us. A 2017 systematic review examined 22 studies and found that 21 showed no evidence that corticosteroids reduce biphasic reaction risk. A 2019 study even demonstrated increased likelihood of hospital admission after prehospital steroid treatment. Contemporary guidelines increasingly advise against routine corticosteroid administration in anaphylaxis, yet clinical practice lags behind, and hospital protocols often still include them.

## The Dosing Problem You Might Not Know About

Here's a detail that should make you pause. The recommended adult epinephrine dose is 0.5 mg intramuscularly. Yet many auto-injectors available contain only 0.3 mg. In this case, the first two doses were via auto-injector (0.3 mg each), while the subsequent two were manually prepared 0.5 mg doses. A 2025 retrospective study showed that patients who initially received 0.3 mg epinephrine required escalation of care significantly more often than those receiving the recommended 0.5 mg. If you're reaching for an auto-injector in the ED for an adult over 50 kg, you may be systematically underdosing.

## Think Mastocytosis When the Course Is Bizarre

The atypical, prolonged course in this case prompted further investigation. Elevated tryptase levels both during anaphylaxis and at baseline, combined with hepatomegaly and neuropsychiatric symptoms, suggested systemic mastocytosis. She didn't meet formal WHO diagnostic criteria, but the likelihood was high. Systemic mastocytosis predisposes patients to severe and potentially unusual anaphylaxis courses. In any patient with an atypical presentation, prolonged symptoms, or multiphasic course, think about mast cell disorders and check tryptase levels. Just make sure you do it properly: guidelines recommend three samples at specific intervals, something this case unfortunately didn't achieve.

## Managing Multiphasic Anaphylaxis: Flying Blind

When the allergology team was consulted in this case, they considered two theoretical approaches: continuous epinephrine infusion to pre-emptively treat recurrences, or an expectant approach given the decreasing severity. Neither was ultimately needed, but the deliberation highlights an uncomfortable reality. We have essentially no evidence-based guidance for managing multiphasic anaphylaxis. Treatment mirrors standard anaphylaxis management: intramuscular epinephrine, supportive care, repeat as needed. For refractory cases, intravenous epinephrine infusions may be necessary, but the evidence base is vanishingly thin.

## Key Take-Home Points

- Biphasic anaphylaxis occurs in approximately 4-6% of cases; multiphasic reactions are even rarer but reported.
- There are no reliable predictors for who will develop biphasic reactions; observation protocols remain controversial and largely expert-based.
- Corticosteroids do not prevent biphasic anaphylaxis and may cause harm; stop routinely prescribing them for anaphylaxis.
- Many epinephrine auto-injectors contain only 0.3 mg, underdosing most adults; the recommended adult dose is 0.5 mg intramuscularly.
- Consider systemic mastocytosis in patients with atypical, severe, or multiphasic anaphylaxis courses, and obtain proper serial tryptase levels.
- Evidence-based guidance for managing multiphasic anaphylaxis is virtually nonexistent; treatment remains supportive and reactive.

The patient in this case ultimately did well, discharged after three days with an epinephrine auto-injector and antihistamine. But her case serves as a reminder that anaphylaxis can be far more dynamic and unpredictable than our guidelines acknowledge, and in those rare cases where symptoms keep returning, we're largely making it up as we go.



# The 0.3mg Problem: Why Your Epinephrine Auto-Injector May Be Underdosing Adults

## The Case That Stopped Me in My Tracks

A woman in her 60s presents with anaphylaxis after taking her first dose of clarithromycin. You do everything right: prompt intramuscular epinephrine, antihistamines, fluids, and supplemental oxygen. She responds beautifully. Blood pressure normalizes, flushing resolves, and she looks ready for observation admission. But then it happens again. And again. And again. Four separate anaphylactic episodes over 12 hours, each requiring IM epinephrine.

But here's what caught my attention: her first two epinephrine doses came from auto-injectors delivering 0.3mg each. Her second two doses? Manually prepared 0.5mg injections. This wasn't arbitrary; it reflected a practical reality in many EDs. Auto-injectors are fast, familiar, and reduce hesitancy when staff aren't comfortable drawing up epinephrine on the fly. But we may be systematically underdosing our adult patients when we reach for that device.

## The Dosing Gap We Don't Talk About Enough

Current guidelines recommend 0.01 mg/kg IM epinephrine for anaphylaxis, with a maximum of 0.5mg for adults. The math is straightforward: most adults exceed 50kg, making 0.5mg the appropriate dose. Yet many auto-injectors labeled for "adults" contain only 0.3mg. That's a 40% underdose for a 60kg patient, and even worse for larger individuals.

The authors cite a 2025 retrospective study showing patients who initially received 0.3mg epinephrine required escalation of care significantly more often than those receiving the guideline-recommended 0.5mg. This shouldn't surprise us. Epinephrine's half-life is short, roughly 2-3 hours, and underdosing means suboptimal alpha and beta effects precisely when you need robust vasoconstriction and bronchodilation.

## Why Auto-Injectors Persist Despite the Problem

The case authors acknowledge something many of us have felt: in time-sensitive settings, or in hospitals where anaphylaxis isn't a daily occurrence, auto-injectors reduce delays. Staff hesitancy with vasoactive agents is real. Drawing up 0.5mg from a vial, calculating the volume, ensuring proper IM injection technique, all while the patient is crashing? That friction matters.

But convenience shouldn't trump adequate dosing for a life-threatening condition. The solution isn't abandoning auto-injectors entirely; they remain essential for prehospital care and patient self-administration. The solution is recognizing when they're inadequate and being prepared to deliver the proper dose manually.

## Multiphasic Anaphylaxis: When One Episode Isn't Enough

This case also highlights the phenomenon of multiphasic anaphylaxis: recurrent symptoms after apparent resolution, without re-exposure to the trigger. Biphasic reactions are reported in 4-6% of anaphylaxis cases, though estimates vary wildly. Multiphasic presentations with multiple recurrences, like this patient experienced, are rarer still and profoundly challenging to manage.

The authors describe considering two approaches: continuous IV epinephrine infusion to pre-empt recurrences versus watchful waiting given the decreasing symptom severity. Neither option felt comfortable given the lack of evidence. This is the uncomfortable reality of managing rare, severe presentations: we're often operating on expert opinion and institutional preference rather than robust data.

## Steroids: The Sacred Cow That Needs Retiring

Here's what struck me about this case's management: the patient received both prednisone 30mg IV daily for 5 days AND a dose of dexamethasone 8mg IV, because two different hospital protocols conflicted. Yet contemporary evidence increasingly shows steroids don't prevent biphasic anaphylaxis. A 2017 systematic review found 21 of 22 studies showed no benefit. A 2019 study even suggested increased ICU admission rates with prehospital steroid administration.

Old habits die hard, and steroid administration for anaphylaxis remains deeply ingrained. But this case illustrates the problem: patients receive medications that don't help, may potentially harm, and divert attention from what actually matters: epinephrine, early and adequate.

## The Hidden Diagnosis: Systemic Mastocytosis

The atypical course prompted investigation into underlying causes, revealing elevated tryptase levels during and after the acute event, along with hepatomegaly and neuropsychiatric symptoms. The conclusion? A high probability of systemic mastocytosis, even without meeting formal WHO diagnostic criteria.

This matters because systemic mastocytosis predisposes patients to severe, prolonged, and potentially refractory anaphylaxis. Elevated baseline tryptase should prompt consideration of mast cell disorders, particularly in patients with atypical anaphylaxis courses or multiple epinephrine requirements.

One technical note: the authors acknowledge they didn't collect tryptase levels at optimal intervals. Guidelines recommend samples at presentation, 1-2 hours post-symptom onset, and at least 24 hours after full resolution. This patient's levels were drawn later than ideal, which limited diagnostic certainty. When anaphylaxis rolls through your ED, remember to grab those tryptase levels early.

## Key Take-Homes

- Adult epinephrine auto-injectors often deliver 0.3mg when guidelines recommend 0.5mg; this underdosing correlates with increased need for treatment escalation and may contribute to prolonged or recurrent anaphylaxis.
- In hospital settings, prioritize manually drawn 0.5mg IM epinephrine for adults over 50kg rather than defaulting to auto-injectors, and ensure staff are trained and comfortable with this technique.
- Corticosteroids for anaphylaxis lack evidence for preventing biphasic reactions and may cause harm; focus your energy on epinephrine and supportive care.
- Multiphasic anaphylaxis is rare and management strategies are largely consensus-based; consider continuous epinephrine infusion for severe or recurrent cases and involve allergy specialists early.
- Atypical, severe, or prolonged anaphylaxis should prompt consideration of systemic mastocytosis; obtain properly timed tryptase levels to aid diagnosis.

This case reminds us that anaphylaxis management extends beyond the initial resuscitation. Understanding dosing nuances, questioning legacy treatments like steroids, and recognizing when to investigate for underlying mast cell disease can make the difference between a straightforward admission and a prolonged, dangerous ICU course. The next time you reach for that auto-injector in a crashing adult, ask yourself: is 0.3mg really enough?



# Steroids in Anaphylaxis: Killing the Sacred Cow

## The Case That Made Me Question Everything

A woman in her 60s presents with severe anaphylaxis to clarithromycin—hypotension, hypoxemia, urticaria, facial swelling. Standard treatment with IM epinephrine works, but then she relapses. Four separate episodes over 12 hours, each requiring IM epinephrine. By hospital day 3, she's discharged on antihistamines and an EpiPen. Case closed.

But here's what grabbed my attention: despite receiving both prednisone *and* dexamethasone—dual corticosteroid therapy per her hospital's protocols—she still had multiple recurrences. And that's the uncomfortable truth about steroids in anaphylaxis that we need to talk about.

## The Sacred Cow We Keep Milking

For decades, corticosteroids have been automatic additions to anaphylaxis protocols. You give epinephrine, you give fluids, and you give steroids—"to prevent biphasic reactions." It's written in protocols, taught in medical school, and reinforced by specialists. But the evidence base? It's essentially nonexistent.

This case illustrates the problem beautifully. The patient received 30 mg IV prednisone daily for 5 days plus a one-time dose of 8 mg IV dexamethasone. That's aggressive steroid therapy by any standard. Yet she still developed three additional anaphylactic episodes after her initial presentation.

What's particularly telling is that the steroid protocols were inconsistent—general ward guidelines called for prednisone while ICU guidelines called for dexamethasone, so she got both. This haphazard approach reflects the underlying reality: we've been prescribing steroids more out of habit than evidence.

## What the Data Actually Shows

The case report cites a 2017 systematic review that examined 22 studies on corticosteroids for biphasic anaphylaxis. Only one study suggested any benefit. Twenty-one showed no evidence that steroids reduce biphasic reaction risk. That's not a subtle trend—it's a finding screaming for attention.

Even more concerning, a 2019 study demonstrated increased likelihood of ICU or hospital ward admission after prehospital corticosteroid treatment. Not neutral—potentially harmful. We may be keeping patients in hospital longer, exposing them to additional risks, without providing any meaningful benefit.

The mechanism makes intuitive sense: steroids take hours to exert anti-inflammatory effects, while anaphylaxis is a rapidly progressive, life-threatening cascade. By the time steroids could theoretically work, the acute phase has either resolved or the patient has bigger problems. We're throwing a slow-acting medication at a lightning-fast disease process.

## Why Practice Hasn't Caught Up

If the evidence is this clear, why do steroids remain standard practice? Several factors are at play.

First, there's therapeutic inertia. "That's how we've always done it" is powerful medicine, even when the medicine doesn't work. Changing hospital protocols requires navigating committees, updating order sets, and re-educating staff across departments.

Second, there's medicolegal anxiety. In a high-stakes condition like anaphylaxis, clinicians fear being the one who "didn't do everything possible." Adding steroids feels like additional insurance, even if that insurance policy is worthless.

Third, biphasic reactions are genuinely frightening. They're unpredictable, occur in an estimated 4-6% of anaphylaxis cases, and can present up to 78 hours after initial symptom resolution. We want a magic bullet, and steroids have been cast in that role for generations.

## What We Should Do Instead

The case report highlights what actually matters: prompt epinephrine administration and appropriate observation periods. The patient received four doses of IM epinephrine—initially via auto-injector, then via individually prepared doses. The first two doses were 0.3 mg, which the authors note is potentially underdosing for adults over 50 kg. Current guidelines recommend 0.5 mg IM for adults, and a 2025 retrospective study showed that patients receiving 0.3 mg required escalation significantly more often.

This detail matters more than steroid dosing. Proper epinephrine dosing, given promptly, is the intervention that saves lives. Steroids are distraction.

For patients with recurrent or refractory anaphylaxis, the case raises interesting questions about alternative strategies. The treating team considered continuous IV epinephrine infusion versus an expectant approach, but literature guidance was lacking. In this patient, symptoms gradually lessened with each episode and resolved without needing escalation to infusion.

## A Note on Mast Cell Disorders

The patient's workup revealed probable systemic mastocytosis—elevated tryptase levels that remained elevated at follow-up, hepatomegaly, and neuropsychiatric symptoms. While she didn't meet formal WHO diagnostic criteria, this underlying condition likely predisposed her to both severe and multiphasic anaphylaxis.

The authors note that tryptase levels weren't obtained at optimal intervals—a missed opportunity. Current guidelines recommend samples at symptom onset, 1-2 hours later, and at least 24 hours after resolution. This three-point approach helps distinguish acute mast cell activation from baseline elevations seen in mast cell disorders.

While systemic mastocytosis is rare, it's worth considering in patients with severe, recurrent, or atypical anaphylaxis presentations. These patients may benefit from allergology referral and longer observation periods.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- Corticosteroids do not prevent biphasic anaphylaxis; systematic review evidence shows no benefit, and some data suggests potential harm including increased hospital admissions.

- Epinephrine is the only first-line medication that matters for acute anaphylaxis—ensure proper dosing at 0.5 mg IM for adults, as underdosing with 0.3 mg auto-injectors is associated with need for escalation.

- Current guidelines increasingly recommend against routine steroid administration, but practice change lags behind evidence; be willing to challenge institutional protocols.

- For patients with severe or multiphasic anaphylaxis, consider underlying mast cell disorders and obtain properly timed tryptase levels to aid diagnosis.

- Observation periods remain important given biphasic risk, but the duration should be based on clinical judgment and risk stratification rather than arbitrary time windows.

## The Bottom Line

This case of multiphasic anaphylaxis didn't need more steroids—it needed better recognition of what works and what doesn't. The patient recovered because she received repeated doses of the one medication that actually treats anaphylaxis: epinephrine. The corticosteroids were therapeutic theater, and it's time we stop performing it.



# Beyond the Allergen: When to Suspect Systemic Mastocytosis

## The Case That Stops You in Your Tracks

A woman in her 60s presents with anaphylaxis. You do everything right: intramuscular epinephrine, antihistamines, fluids, supplemental oxygen. She responds beautifully. Blood pressure normalizes. Rash fades. You admit her for observation, feeling pretty good about your management.

And then it happens again. And again. And again.

Four separate episodes of anaphylactic shock over 12 hours, each requiring intramuscular epinephrine to resolve. No re-exposure to the trigger. No missed diagnosis. Just wave after wave of life-threatening symptoms despite textbook-perfect initial management. This is multiphasic anaphylaxis, and it forces us to look beyond the obvious allergen to ask a deeper question: is there something wrong with this patient's mast cells?

## When Anaphylaxis Refuses to Stay "Biphasic"

We talk a lot about biphasic anaphylaxis in emergency medicine — that dreaded relapse of symptoms hours after apparent resolution. The incidence sits somewhere around 4-6%, though reported rates vary wildly from less than 1% to over 20% depending on the study. Multiphasic anaphylaxis, with multiple discrete recurrences, is a different beast entirely and one for which our guidelines offer essentially zero specific guidance.

In this case, the patient's symptoms actually decreased in intensity with each episode. Her first relapse brought cyanosis, deep hypoxemia, and hypotension. By her fourth episode, she had dyspnea, rash, and pruritus — serious, but not the same cardiovascular collapse she initially presented with. The natural history of multiphasic anaphylaxis remains poorly characterized, but this pattern of gradually diminishing severity offered at least some reassurance in an otherwise terrifying clinical situation.

The consulting allergology team considered two theoretical approaches: continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion to pre-empt further episodes, or an expectant "wait and see" strategy. Neither option had evidence to support it. The team ultimately continued with episodic intramuscular epinephrine as needed, and fortunately, the patient stabilized after those four episodes without further recurrence.

## The Mast Cell Connection

Here's where this case gets really interesting. The patient's clinical course was sufficiently bizarre that the team went looking for an underlying explanation. What they found should change how you think about severe, atypical, or multiphasic anaphylaxis.

Her tryptase levels were elevated during her third anaphylactic episode and remained elevated at baseline during follow-up. Abdominal ultrasound revealed hepatomegaly. She had a history of depression and memory problems. These findings — elevated baseline tryptase, organomegaly, and neuropsychiatric symptoms — all point toward systemic mastocytosis, a disorder characterized by abnormal mast cell proliferation and excessive mediator release.

Patients with systemic mastocytosis are predisposed to severe anaphylaxis, though the rarity of both conditions means we have virtually no research specifically addressing their intersection with biphasic or multiphasic reactions. In this case, the patient met two WHO minor criteria for systemic mastocytosis, but the major criterion couldn't be assessed due to limited bone marrow biopsy material. She likely has the condition, but formally falls into diagnostic limbo.

## Tryptase: Are You Ordering It Correctly?

If you're not ordering tryptase in anaphylaxis, or if you're ordering it as a single random lab, you're missing a critical diagnostic opportunity. Guidelines recommend a minimum of two, ideally three samples at specific intervals:

First, draw a sample as soon as feasible without delaying treatment. Second, draw another sample 1-2 hours after symptom onset, when tryptase typically peaks. Third, draw a baseline sample at least 24 hours after full clinical resolution.

Comparing acute to baseline tryptase helps establish whether anaphylaxis occurred and whether the patient may have an underlying mast cell disorder. An elevated baseline tryptase should prompt consideration of systemic mastocytosis, especially in patients with severe or atypical reactions.

In this case, tryptase wasn't drawn during the initial presentation — the first sample came an hour after the third episode. The missed opportunity highlights how unfamiliar many clinicians remain with proper tryptase sampling protocols.

## Some Hard Truths About Anaphylaxis Management

This case also exposes some uncomfortable truths about how we treat anaphylaxis.

The patient received two doses from a 0.3 mg epinephrine auto-injector before the team switched to manually prepared 0.5 mg intramuscular doses. Current guidelines recommend 0.5 mg intramuscular epinephrine for adults, with dosing of 0.01 mg/kg and a maximum of 0.5 mg. Yet many adult auto-injectors contain only 0.3 mg — a potentially critical underdosing for patients over 50 kg. A 2025 retrospective study showed that patients who initially received 0.3 mg were significantly more likely to require escalation of care compared to those who got the full 0.5 mg.

Auto-injectors absolutely have a role in the prehospital setting and can reduce treatment delays and clinician hesitancy. But we need to acknowledge that they may underdose many adults, and be prepared to supplement with appropriately dosed epinephrine.

The other uncomfortable truth: corticosteroids. This patient received both prednisone and dexamethasone per different hospital protocols. But a 2017 systematic review of 22 studies found no evidence that corticosteroids reduce the risk of biphasic anaphylaxis, and a 2019 study actually showed increased likelihood of hospital admission after prehospital steroid administration. Contemporary guidelines increasingly advise against routine corticosteroid use, yet our protocols and practice patterns haven't caught up.

## Clinical Pearls

- Severe, atypical, or multiphasic anaphylaxis should prompt evaluation for underlying systemic mastocytosis, especially with elevated baseline tryptase, organomegaly, or unexplained neuropsychiatric symptoms.
- Order tryptase as a three-sample series: immediately, 1-2 hours post-symptoms, and at least 24 hours after resolution to capture peak and baseline levels.
- The standard adult epinephrine dose is 0.5 mg intramuscular; 0.3 mg auto-injectors may underdose adults over 50 kg and risk treatment failure.
- Corticosteroids have no proven benefit in preventing biphasic anaphylaxis and may cause harm; they should not be routinely administered.
- Biphasic reactions occur in roughly 4-6% of anaphylaxis cases, but reliable predictors for which patients will relapse remain elusive.

## The Bottom Line

Not all anaphylaxis is straightforward IgE-mediated allergy gone wrong. Some patients harbor underlying mast cell pathology that predisposes them to severe, prolonged, or multiphasic courses. When you encounter anaphylaxis that doesn't follow the script — multiple recurrences, unusual severity, or incomplete response to standard treatment — broaden your differential. Check tryptase properly. Look for hepatomegaly. Ask about unexplained neuropsychiatric symptoms. And remember that the patient in front of you may have more going on than a simple drug allergy.



# The Tryptase Tango: Getting Your Timing Right

We've all been there. A patient rolls in with anaphylaxis, you nail the airway, give the epinephrine, stabilize the hemodynamics, and get them admitted. Job well done. But here's the uncomfortable truth from a fascinating case report out of the Netherlands: if you're not thinking about tryptase sampling from the moment that patient hits the resuscitation bay, you're missing a critical diagnostic window that could change their life.

The case involves a woman in her 60s who presented with anaphylaxis to clarithromycin. What makes this case remarkable isn't just the initial presentation; it's that she went on to have four separate anaphylactic episodes over 12 hours despite appropriate treatment each time. This is multiphasic anaphylaxis, and it's the kind of presentation that should make you step back and wonder whether there's something more going on with your patient's mast cells.

Here's where the tryptase timing really matters. In this case, the first tryptase sample wasn't drawn until after the third anaphylactic episode. The allergology team was consulted, but by then the optimal sampling window had already slipped away. The guidelines are actually quite clear on this: you need a minimum of two samples, ideally three, at specific intervals. The first sample should be drawn as soon as feasible without delaying treatment. The second sample needs to be collected 1 to 2 hours after symptom onset, ideally within 4 hours, when tryptase levels typically peak. Then a third sample at least 24 hours after full clinical resolution establishes the patient's baseline.

Why does this matter so much? Elevated acute tryptase with a normal baseline confirms anaphylaxis. But an elevated baseline tryptase is a red flag for systemic mastocytosis, a condition that predisposes patients to severe and potentially multiphasic anaphylaxis. In this patient, both acute and baseline tryptase levels were elevated, and subsequent workup revealed hepatomegaly and neuropsychiatric symptoms, all pointing toward systemic mastocytosis even though she didn't meet formal WHO diagnostic criteria on bone marrow biopsy.

The diagnostic yield drops dramatically when you miss those sampling windows. Tryptase has a relatively short half-life of about 2 hours, so if you wait until the patient is settled on the floor or until after multiple recurrent episodes, you've lost the ability to see the true peak. And without that baseline sample after resolution, you can't determine whether an elevated tryptase represents an acute mast cell activation event or simply reflects the patient's steady state.

This case also serves as a reminder that multiphasic anaphylaxis, while rare, should prompt you to think beyond the immediate trigger. When a patient requires multiple epinephrine doses over hours without re-exposure to the allergen, consider whether underlying mast cell disease might be contributing. These patients may need longer observation periods and should have expedited referral to allergology for comprehensive evaluation.

The practical reality is that in a busy emergency department, tryptase sampling often falls by the wayside. The patient is sick, you're managing multiple critical tasks, and drawing tubes for what feels like a diagnostic afterthought doesn't feel urgent. But building this into your initial workflow, perhaps even having a standing order set that includes tryptase timing instructions, ensures you don't miss this window. Your future self and the allergist will thank you.

- Draw the first tryptase sample as early as possible without delaying resuscitation
- Draw the second sample 1-2 hours after symptom onset, within 4 hours maximum, to capture peak levels
- Draw the third sample at least 24 hours after full symptom resolution to establish baseline
- In multiphasic or severe anaphylaxis, maintain a high index of suspicion for systemic mastocytosis
- Elevated baseline tryptase warrants outpatient allergology referral for further mast cell disease evaluation

Getting tryptase timing right transforms what could be a missed diagnostic opportunity into actionable information that helps identify patients at risk for recurrent, severe anaphylaxis and connects them with the long-term management they need.



# How Long is Long Enough? The Observation Time Dilemma

## The Case That Made Me Rethink Everything

A woman in her 60s presents with anaphylaxis after her first dose of clarithromycin. She gets epinephrine, antihistamines, fluids, and oxygen. She looks great. Symptoms resolve. Blood pressure normalizes. She's admitted to a general ward for observation. And then it happens again. And again. And again.

Four separate episodes of anaphylaxis. Each requiring intramuscular epinephrine. Each occurring without any re-exposure to the trigger. This case of multiphasic anaphylaxis, published by Schneider and colleagues, is the kind of case that keeps emergency physicians up at night, not because it's common, but because it exposes just how little we actually know about observation periods after anaphylaxis.

## The Biphasic Problem

Here's the uncomfortable reality: biphasic anaphylaxis is real, but the data surrounding it is a mess. Reported incidence ranges from 0.4% to 23.3%, though most credible estimates land somewhere between 4% and 6%. That's a wide enough range to be clinically useless for any individual patient. Symptoms can recur anywhere from hours after initial presentation up to 78 hours later, though most happen within the first 24 hours.

The Dutch guidelines and World Allergy Organisation both note that approximately half of biphasic reactions occur within 6 to 12 hours of the initial event, but neither commits to a specific monitoring recommendation. Traditional teaching suggests 4 to 6 hours of observation after symptom resolution, but if half of biphasic reactions happen after 6 hours, are we really observing our patients long enough?

## The UK Three-Tier Approach: A Step Forward?

The UK 2021 guideline attempts to bring some rational structure to this problem with a three-tier risk-stratified approach. Rather than a one-size-fits-all observation period, it proposes stratifying patients based on risk factors and severity. This makes intuitive sense. The patient who needed one dose of epinephrine and responded immediately is different from the patient who required multiple doses, had delayed presentation, or showed severe hypotension and hypoxia.

But here's the problem: we don't actually have reliable predictors for biphasic reactions. Suggested risk factors include delayed epinephrine administration, severe initial presentation, multiple epinephrine doses, and prior biphasic reactions, but none of these are sufficiently sensitive or specific to guide decision-making with confidence. In the case presented, the patient had multiple epinephrine doses, severe hypotension, and hypoxemia. She was exactly the kind of patient who should have been observed closely, and she was. She was in the ICU. And she still had recurrent episodes.

## The Steroid Myth Needs to Die

This case also highlights something we need to confront head-on: corticosteroids do not prevent biphasic anaphylaxis. This patient received both prednisone and dexamethasone per conflicting hospital protocols, and she still had recurrent episodes. The 2017 systematic review examined 22 studies, and only one showed any benefit. Twenty-one studies showed no evidence that corticosteroids reduce the risk of biphasic reaction.

A 2019 study even demonstrated increased likelihood of ICU or hospital ward admission after prehospital corticosteroid treatment. The side effect profile matters. Yet clinical practice persists in prescribing steroids routinely, perhaps out of habit, perhaps out of a need to feel like we're doing something. It is time to stop.

## A Practical Framework

So what do we do with a patient who has fully resolved anaphylaxis in the ED? Here is a practical approach that balances safety with resource utilization.

For low-risk patients, those who had mild reactions, received prompt treatment with rapid response, and required only a single dose of epinephrine, observation for 4 to 6 hours after symptom resolution is reasonable. These patients can likely be safely discharged with clear return precautions and an epinephrine auto-injector prescription.

For moderate-risk patients, those who required multiple epinephrine doses, had delayed presentation, or experienced hypotension or hypoxemia, extended observation of 12 to 24 hours is warranted. This captures most biphasic reactions while avoiding unnecessary prolonged admissions.

For high-risk patients, those with severe presentations requiring ICU-level care, refractory anaphylaxis, or suspected underlying conditions like mastocytosis, admission and prolonged monitoring is appropriate. This patient required ICU observation, and appropriately so.

## Think About Mastocytosis

This case also serves as an important reminder to consider systemic mastocytosis in patients with unusual anaphylaxis presentations. The patient had elevated tryptase levels, hepatomegaly, and neuropsychiatric symptoms including depression and memory problems. She likely had systemic mastocytosis even though she did not meet formal WHO diagnostic criteria.

Patients with mast cell disorders are predisposed to severe, recurrent, and atypical anaphylaxis. If the clinical course seems bizarre, if symptoms keep coming back, or if there is no clear trigger, think about mast cell disease and check tryptase levels. Just remember to draw them correctly: one sample as soon as feasible, another 1 to 2 hours after symptom onset, and a baseline sample at least 24 hours after full resolution.

## Key Take-Home Points

- Biphasic anaphylaxis occurs in approximately 4 to 6 percent of cases, with symptom recurrence possible up to 72 hours after initial presentation.
- There are no reliable predictors for which patients will develop biphasic reactions; risk stratification remains imperfect.
- Corticosteroids do not prevent biphasic anaphylaxis and should not be routinely prescribed; the evidence is clear on this.
- Consider extended observation of 12 to 24 hours for patients who required multiple epinephrine doses or had severe presentations with hypotension or hypoxemia.
- In atypical, severe, or recurrent anaphylaxis, consider underlying mast cell disorders and obtain appropriate tryptase levels.
- Use proper epinephrine dosing: 0.5 mg intramuscularly for adults, recognizing that many auto-injectors contain only 0.3 mg and may represent underdosing.

## The Bottom Line

We still do not have great answers for how long to observe patients after anaphylaxis, but we have enough data to move beyond dogmatic 4-hour observation periods or reflexive steroid prescriptions. This case, with its four separate episodes of recurrent anaphylaxis, reminds us that biphasic and multiphasic reactions are real, that current guidelines are based on limited evidence, and that clinical judgment remains essential in navigating the observation time dilemma.
