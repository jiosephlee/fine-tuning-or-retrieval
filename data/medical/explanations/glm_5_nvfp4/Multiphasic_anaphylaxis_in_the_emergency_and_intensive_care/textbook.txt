Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

## Case Presentation and Initial Clinical Assessment

The case involves a woman in her 60s who presented to the emergency department with an acute anaphylactic reaction. Her medical history provides important context for understanding both her risk factors and the clinical decisions that followed. She had known comorbidities including type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, psoriasis, and depression. Her medication regimen included metformin, gliclazide, hydrochlorothiazide, omeprazole, and sertraline. Crucially, she carried a known allergy to beta-lactam antibiotics, based on a mild reaction to amoxicillin in adolescence that had been limited to facial swelling without oropharyngeal involvement, respiratory symptoms, or hypotension. She had also experienced a strong local reaction to a wasp sting, though without systemic features. This history of allergic sensitivity, while not previously severe, is relevant when considering her heightened immunological vulnerability.

The sequence of events leading to her presentation began five days earlier, when she was prescribed clindamycin for erysipelas. After five days of clindamycin therapy, she developed a rash on her abdomen that was interpreted as a delayed allergic reaction. Her general practitioner then switched her antibiotic regimen to clarithromycin, a macrolide antibiotic to which she had no prior exposure. Within one hour of taking the first oral dose of clarithromycin 500 mg, she developed symptoms and sought medical attention one hour later.

Her presenting symptoms were characteristic of a systemic allergic reaction: pruritus, flushing, increased perspiration, agitation, and a sensation of throat swelling. The involvement of multiple organ systems—skin, respiratory, and cardiovascular—signaled a potentially life-threatening process. On initial evaluation in the emergency department, she demonstrated clear hemodynamic and respiratory compromise. Her blood pressure was 74/50 mm Hg, indicating hypotension, and her peripheral oxygen saturation was 88% on room air with a respiratory rate of 22 breaths per minute. This combination of hypotension and hypoxemia reflects the pathophysiology of anaphylaxis, where widespread mast cell degranulation releases histamine and other inflammatory mediators, causing vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, and bronchospasm.

Physical examination revealed facial swelling and new-onset generalized erythema and urticaria distributed across her entire body. Importantly, there was no observable swelling of the tongue or throat, and cardiac and lung auscultation were normal. The absence of stridor or wheezing does not exclude anaphylaxis, as cutaneous and hemodynamic manifestations may predominate. Her blood glucose was elevated at 12.7 mmol/L, likely reflecting the physiological stress response, and her ECG showed normal sinus rhythm.

The clinical diagnosis of anaphylaxis is made when specific criteria are met. According to widely accepted diagnostic criteria, anaphylaxis is highly likely when there is acute onset of illness with involvement of the skin or mucosal tissue (such as urticaria, erythema, or angioedema) combined with either respiratory compromise or hypotension. This patient met these criteria unequivocally: she had urticaria and generalized erythema accompanied by hypotension and hypoxemia. The temporal relationship to clarithromycin ingestion further supported the diagnosis, establishing a clear precipitant. Rapid clinical recognition allowed for immediate initiation of treatment, which is essential given that delayed epinephrine administration is associated with worse outcomes in anaphylaxis.

## Pathophysiology of Anaphylaxis and Definitions of Complex Courses

Anaphylaxis represents the most severe manifestation of an allergic reaction, characterized by rapid onset and systemic involvement that can rapidly progress to life-threatening shock. Understanding the underlying pathophysiology is essential for recognizing why this patient's presentation was so unusual and why her symptoms recurred despite appropriate initial treatment.

At its core, anaphylaxis is driven by mast cell and basophil activation. In IgE-mediated anaphylaxis—the mechanism most relevant to this case—prior exposure to an allergen sensitizes the immune system, leading to the production of allergen-specific IgE antibodies that bind to high-affinity receptors on mast cells and basophils. Upon re-exposure, the allergen cross-links these surface-bound IgE molecules, triggering rapid cellular degranulation. This process releases a cascade of preformed inflammatory mediators, including histamine, tryptase, chymase, and heparin, along with newly synthesized lipid mediators such as prostaglandins and leukotrienes. Histamine is the primary mediator responsible for the classic cutaneous findings (urticaria, flushing, angioedema), while also causing vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, and bronchoconstriction. In this patient, the temporal relationship between clarithromycin ingestion and symptom onset within one hour strongly suggests an IgE-mediated mechanism, though interestingly, this was her first known exposure to this macrolide antibiotic—raising questions about cross-reactivity or underlying mast cell dysregulation.

The clinical diagnosis of anaphylaxis rests on recognized criteria involving multiple organ systems. Contemporary diagnostic criteria require either skin or mucosal involvement plus respiratory compromise, hypotension, or gastrointestinal symptoms; or rapid onset after known allergen exposure with hypotension alone. This patient met multiple criteria: she presented with urticaria and generalized erythema (cutaneous involvement), hypoxia and dyspnea (respiratory compromise), and hypotension (hemodynamic instability). Her initial blood pressure of 74/50 mm Hg and oxygen saturation of 88% on room air confirmed severe systemic involvement. The presence of pruritus, throat swelling sensation, and agitation further supported the diagnosis. These findings fulfill the clinical definition of anaphylaxis even before identifying a specific trigger.

What distinguishes this case from typical anaphylaxis is the subsequent clinical course. Biphasic anaphylaxis refers to the recurrence of anaphylactic symptoms after an asymptomatic interval, without re-exposure to the triggering allergen. The initial reaction resolves—often with treatment—and the patient appears to have fully recovered, only to experience a second episode hours later. This differs fundamentally from refractory anaphylaxis, where symptoms persist without meaningful response to appropriately administered epinephrine. In refractory cases, the reaction never truly resolves; the patient remains in anaphylaxis despite treatment. In biphasic reactions, by contrast, there is a clear period of symptom resolution between episodes. This patient exhibited an even more complex pattern: multiple recurrences following initial resolution, constituting multiphasic anaphylaxis. After each epinephrine administration, she demonstrated clinical improvement with normalization of blood pressure and decreased cutaneous findings, only to develop recurrent symptoms hours later. This pattern of multiple discrete episodes distinguishes multiphasic from both simple biphasic anaphylaxis (one recurrence) and refractory anaphylaxis (no response).

The pathophysiology of biphasic reactions remains incompletely understood. Proposed mechanisms include delayed recruitment of inflammatory cells, ongoing release of mediators from mast cells not immediately depleted during the initial episode, and late-phase inflammatory responses. The timing of recurrent symptoms is variable, though most occur within the first 24 hours after initial allergen exposure. Reports document recurrences up to 78 hours later, highlighting the challenge of determining appropriate observation periods. In this case, the patient experienced four distinct anaphylactic episodes within the first 12 hours of admission, each requiring epinephrine administration. The decreasing intensity of symptoms with each subsequent episode—progressing from severe hypoxemia with cyanosis and hypotension to milder symptoms of dyspnea and rash—suggests a gradual exhaustion of inflammatory mediators or progressive stabilization of mast cell activity.

Reported incidence of biphasic anaphylaxis varies widely in the literature, ranging from 0.4% to 23.3%, though most data converge on an incidence between 4% and 6%. This substantial variation reflects inconsistencies in definition, study methodology, and case identification. Multiphasic presentations with multiple recurrences, as seen in this patient, are considerably rarer and poorly characterized in the literature. The absence of reliable predictors for which patients will develop biphasic or multiphasic courses complicates clinical decision-making. Suggested risk factors include delayed epinephrine administration, severe initial presentation requiring multiple epinephrine doses, and prior history of biphasic reactions, though none have been validated prospectively.

A critical clinical challenge lies in differentiating true biphasic reactions from other explanations for symptom recurrence. Sustained anaphylaxis with transient response to epinephrine may appear biphasic if symptoms return as epinephrine's effects wane. Epinephrine has a short half-life of approximately 3 to 5 minutes, and its clinical effects typically last 10 to 20 minutes. If the inciting allergen remains present—such as ongoing absorption from the gastrointestinal tract after food ingestion or from a slow-release medication formulation—symptoms may recur not because of a biphasic response, but because the trigger persists. In this case, clarithromycin was administered as a single oral dose and discontinued immediately upon presentation, making ongoing absorption an unlikely explanation for her multiple recurrences. Furthermore, her response to each epinephrine dose was genuine and complete, with documented normalization of vital signs before subsequent relapse, supporting true biphasic rather than sustained anaphylaxis.

The association between systemic mastocytosis and severe, recurrent anaphylaxis adds another layer of complexity to this case. Systemic mastocytosis involves abnormal mast cell proliferation and excessive mediator release, predisposing affected individuals to severe anaphylactic reactions. While this patient did not meet formal World Health Organization diagnostic criteria, her elevated tryptase levels, hepatomegaly, and neuropsychiatric symptoms suggest a high probability of underlying mast cell disorder. This background of mast cell dysregulation may explain why she developed such an atypical multiphasic course following what should have been a straightforward allergic reaction to a single medication exposure.

## Acute Management and the Challenge of Recurrent Episodes

The management of anaphylaxis centers on prompt recognition and immediate administration of epinephrine, the only medication proven to reduce mortality. In this case, the patient presented with classic anaphylactic features—hypotension, hypoxemia, urticaria, and facial angioedema—prompting rapid treatment. She received intramuscular epinephrine via auto-injector (0.3 mg), intravenous clemastine (an H1 antihistamine), supplemental oxygen, and intravenous fluid resuscitation. This initial approach follows standard anaphylaxis protocols and resulted in rapid clinical improvement. However, the subsequent clinical course highlighted the complexity of managing multiphasic anaphylaxis, where symptoms recur despite appropriate initial treatment.

A critical pharmacologic consideration in this case involves the epinephrine dosing. Current guidelines recommend 0.01 mg/kg intramuscularly, up to a maximum of 0.5 mg for adults. The auto-injectors used for this patient's first two episodes contained only 0.3 mg—below the recommended dose for an adult weighing 104 kg. While auto-injectors offer practical advantages in time-sensitive settings by reducing treatment delays and hesitancy among personnel unfamiliar with vasoactive medications, they may result in underdosing in larger adults. Recent evidence suggests that patients receiving 0.3 mg initially are more likely to require additional epinephrine injections compared to those receiving the full 0.5 mg dose. In this case, the treating team transitioned to individually prepared 0.5 mg intramuscular doses for the third and fourth episodes, aligning with guideline recommendations.

The patient experienced four distinct anaphylactic episodes over approximately 12 hours. Each episode met clinical criteria for anaphylaxis and required intramuscular epinephrine for resolution. Notably, symptom intensity progressively diminished—from profound hypoxemia with cyanosis, hypotension, and severe dyspnea initially, to milder manifestations of dyspnea, rash, and pruritus during the final episode. This pattern suggests gradual resolution of the hyperallergic state, though the mechanism underlying this attenuation remains unclear. The decreasing severity provided some clinical reassurance, yet each episode still warranted full treatment given the unpredictable nature of anaphylaxis.

Beyond epinephrine, adjunctive therapies were employed. Antihistamines (clemastine) targeted cutaneous symptoms, while intravenous normal saline supported hemodynamic stability. Supplemental oxygen was administered during episodes of hypoxemia and could be discontinued between episodes as the patient's oxygen saturation improved. These adjuncts address specific symptom domains but do not replace epinephrine as first-line therapy.

Corticosteroids were administered after the first relapse, reflecting traditional practice aimed at preventing biphasic reactions. The patient received both prednisone (30 mg intravenously daily for five days) and a single dose of dexamethasone (8 mg intravenously), a discrepancy arising from differing protocols between the general ward and intensive care unit. This duplication underscores the persistence of corticosteroid use in clinical practice despite mounting evidence questioning their efficacy. A 2017 systematic review found no benefit of corticosteroids in preventing biphasic anaphylaxis, and recent studies suggest potential harm, including increased rates of hospital admission. Contemporary guidelines increasingly advise against routine corticosteroid administration, yet clinical inertia and lingering beliefs about their utility continue to influence practice.

Following the first relapse, the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit for enhanced monitoring. This decision reflects appropriate caution, as biphasic reactions can be severe and unpredictable. ICU admission enabled continuous assessment and immediate access to resuscitation resources. The allergology department was consulted early, providing specialized input on management options. Two alternative strategies were considered: continuous intravenous epinephrine infusion to preempt recurrent symptoms, or an expectant approach awaiting spontaneous resolution. Both approaches carry risks—continuous epinephrine infusion requires careful titration and monitoring for adverse effects, while expectant management risks delayed treatment of potentially life-threatening recurrences. The lack of evidence-based guidance for multiphasic anaphylaxis complicated this decision-making process.

Ultimately, neither alternative strategy was necessary, as the patient experienced no further episodes. This outcome reinforces the importance of close observation and readiness to intervene, while highlighting the therapeutic uncertainty that characterizes multiphasic anaphylaxis management.

## Diagnostic Evaluation for Underlying Mast Cell Disorders

Given the unusual multiphasic course of anaphylaxis in this patient, the treating team appropriately initiated an evaluation for underlying mast cell disease. Patients with systemic mastocytosis carry a heightened risk of severe, sometimes refractory, anaphylaxis due to their increased total body burden of mast cells and enhanced releasability of inflammatory mediators. Recognizing this association is critical for the consulting physician, as an underlying mast cell disorder may explain atypical presentations, prolonged symptom courses, or unexpectedly severe reactions to seemingly minor triggers.

Central to this diagnostic evaluation is the measurement of serum tryptase. Tryptase is the most abundant mast cell protease and serves as a specific marker of mast cell activation. During an acute anaphylactic event, mast cell degranulation releases tryptase into the circulation, where levels typically peak within 1 to 2 hours after symptom onset. To properly interpret tryptase results and distinguish between acute mast cell activation and elevated baseline mast cell burden, guidelines recommend obtaining serial measurements at standardized intervals: an initial sample as soon as feasible without delaying treatment, a second sample 1 to 2 hours after symptom onset when levels are expected to peak, and a third sample at least 24 hours after complete clinical resolution to establish the patient's baseline. Comparing the peak acute level to the baseline allows clinicians to determine whether the elevation represents a normal mast cell response to a trigger or suggests an expanded mast cell population.

In this case, the recommended tryptase sampling protocol was not followed due to the clinical exigencies of managing recurrent anaphylaxis. The first sample was obtained one hour after the third anaphylactic episode, and the second sample was drawn 27 hours after the last recurrence. Despite these limitations, the results proved informative: tryptase levels were elevated during the acute event and remained elevated at baseline during subsequent follow-up with the allergology department. This pattern of persistently elevated baseline tryptase is a red flag for underlying systemic mastocytosis and prompted further investigation.

The World Health Organization (WHO) has established diagnostic criteria for systemic mastocytosis. A definitive diagnosis requires the presence of the major criterion—multifocal, dense infiltrates of at least 15 mast cells in bone marrow or other extracutaneous tissues—plus at least one minor criterion. The minor criteria include: (1) greater than 25% of mast cells in bone marrow aspirate smears or tissue sections showing atypical morphology, (2) detection of a KIT D816V mutation in bone marrow, peripheral blood, or other extracutaneous tissue, (3) expression of CD2 and/or CD25 on mast cells, and (4) serum tryptase persistently greater than 20 ng/mL. In the absence of the major criterion, a diagnosis can still be established if at least three minor criteria are present.

For this patient, the diagnostic evaluation yielded findings consistent with systemic mastocytosis but fell short of formal diagnostic criteria. The elevated baseline tryptase, hepatomegaly detected on abdominal sonography, and history of neuropsychiatric symptoms including depression and memory problems all aligned with this diagnosis. These extra-cutaneous manifestations reflect the multiorgan nature of systemic mastocytosis, where mast cell infiltration and mediator release can affect the liver, nervous system, and other tissues. However, the bone marrow biopsy yielded limited material, rendering the major criterion technically not assessable. With only two confirmed minor criteria, the patient did not meet WHO requirements for a formal diagnosis, though the clinical suspicion remained high.

This outcome highlights a practical limitation in diagnosing systemic mastocytosis in the acute care setting. Bone marrow biopsy remains the gold standard for establishing the major criterion, but adequate tissue sampling requires technical expertise and may be compromised by procedural factors or patient factors such as obesity. In this case, the patient's weight of 104 kg may have contributed to the limited specimen obtained. When the major criterion cannot be assessed, clinicians must rely on clinical judgment informed by the pattern of minor criteria, recognizing that a "probable" diagnosis still carries important implications for patient counseling, long-term monitoring, and management of future anaphylactic episodes.

## Clinical Outcome, Discharge Planning, and Long-term Management

The patient's clinical course demonstrates the unpredictable nature of multiphasic anaphylaxis and the challenges of determining safe observation periods. After four distinct anaphylactic episodes requiring intramuscular epinephrine, the patient experienced no further symptom recurrence. Following approximately 24 hours without renewed anaphylaxis, she was transferred from the intensive care unit back to a standard care ward. This 24-hour asymptomatic observation period represents a clinically reasonable threshold in this complex case, though it exceeds traditional observation recommendations of 4 to 6 hours. The patient was ultimately discharged after a total hospital admission of 3 days, with no further episodes of anaphylaxis reported after discharge.

Discharge planning for patients following severe anaphylaxis must address both immediate prevention of recurrence and long-term risk mitigation. This patient was prescribed levocetirizine, a second-generation antihistamine, as a new standing medication. Antihistamines provide ongoing blockade of histamine H1 receptors, which may help reduce mast cell mediator effects and provide symptomatic relief from cutaneous manifestations. More critically, she was provided with an epinephrine auto-injector (0.3 mg) and received education on its proper use. Patient education in this context should cover recognition of anaphylaxis symptoms—including airway compromise, breathing difficulty, and circulatory instability—as well as the importance of early epinephrine administration at the first signs of a systemic reaction. The patient must understand that epinephrine is the first-line treatment for anaphylaxis and that antihistamines and corticosteroids are adjunctive therapies only.

A notable aspect of this case was the decision against performing confirmatory allergy testing. Skin testing or controlled challenge with clarithromycin could theoretically confirm the causative allergen; however, such testing carries the risk of provoking renewed serious anaphylaxis. The consulting allergology team determined that this risk was not proportional to the potential benefit, particularly given the relative ease with which future clarithromycin exposure can be avoided. This clinical reasoning reflects a risk-benefit analysis that prioritizes patient safety when the index event provides sufficiently compelling evidence of causality—in this case, the clear temporal relationship between the first dose of clarithromycin and symptom onset.

The patient's atypical and prolonged clinical course prompted investigation into underlying conditions that might predispose her to severe or recurrent anaphylaxis. This evaluation revealed findings highly suggestive of systemic mastocytosis, a disorder characterized by abnormal mast cell proliferation and excessive mediator release. Although the patient did not meet formal World Health Organization diagnostic criteria—only two minor criteria were documented, and the major criterion could not be assessed due to limited bone marrow biopsy material—the elevated baseline tryptase levels and associated findings (hepatomegaly, neuropsychiatric symptoms) supported a high probability of systemic mastocytosis. Tryptase levels remained elevated during follow-up with the allergologist, reinforcing the need for ongoing monitoring. Patients with suspected or confirmed mast cell disorders require long-term allergology follow-up, as they carry elevated risk for severe anaphylactic reactions and may benefit from personalized management strategies including mast cell-stabilizing medications and comprehensive avoidance counseling.

## Evidence-Based Considerations and Clinical Lessons

This case of multiphasic anaphylaxis offers several critical lessons that reflect broader shifts in evidence-based management while simultaneously exposing significant knowledge gaps. Understanding these considerations is essential for any clinician managing complex allergic emergencies.

The administration of corticosteroids in this case illustrates a common tension between traditional practice and emerging evidence. The patient received both prednisone (30 mg intravenously daily for 5 days) and dexamethasone (8 mg intravenously once), a regimen that emerged from two different hospital protocols—the general ward and ICU anaphylaxis pathways. This redundancy reflects how deeply entrenched corticosteroid use remains in clinical practice. However, current evidence does not support this approach. A 2017 systematic review examining 22 studies found that only one study supported corticosteroid use for preventing biphasic reactions, while 21 studies showed no benefit. More concerning, a 2019 study demonstrated an increased likelihood of ICU or hospital admission following prehospital corticosteroid treatment. Contemporary guidelines now advise against routine corticosteroid administration in anaphylaxis, yet clinical practice has been slow to adapt. For the medical learner, this serves as an instructive example of how established practices can persist despite mounting contrary evidence, and highlights the importance of critically evaluating inherited treatment protocols.

Epinephrine dosing represents another crucial learning point from this case. The patient, weighing 104 kg, initially received epinephrine via auto-injector containing only 0.3 mg. Current guidelines recommend 0.01 mg/kg body weight with a maximum of 0.5 mg for adults—in this patient, the appropriate dose was 0.5 mg. The 0.3 mg auto-injector, while convenient and potentially reducing treatment hesitancy among staff unfamiliar with epinephrine administration, resulted in clinically significant underdosing. A 2025 retrospective study demonstrated that patients initially receiving 0.3 mg epinephrine required escalation of care significantly more often than those receiving the guideline-recommended 0.5 mg. In this case, subsequent doses were correctly administered as individually prepared 0.5 mg intramuscular injections. The case underscores that while auto-injectors offer practical advantages in time-sensitive settings, clinicians must recognize their limitations and be prepared to administer appropriate weight-based dosing.

The patient's clinical course—four separate anaphylactic episodes over approximately 24 hours—illuminates the challenge of determining appropriate observation periods. Traditional observation periods of 4 to 6 hours after symptom resolution would have proven inadequate in this case. Approximately half of biphasic reactions occur within 6 to 12 hours after the initial event, but reports include recurrences up to 78 hours later. The UK guideline 2021 proposes a three-tier risk-stratified approach to monitoring, though consensus strategies remain lacking. The absence of reliable predictors complicates decision-making: while suggested risk factors include delayed epinephrine administration, severe initial presentation, multiple epinephrine doses, and prior biphasic reactions, none reliably identifies patients who will develop biphasic or multiphasic courses. This patient received timely treatment yet still developed a multiphasic course, illustrating the limitations of current predictive models and emphasizing the need for individualized clinical judgment.

The subsequent investigation revealing a high probability of systemic mastocytosis provides essential context for understanding this patient's severe presentation. Systemic mastocytosis, characterized by abnormal mast cell proliferation and excessive mediator release, predisposes patients to severe anaphylaxis. The patient exhibited findings consistent with this diagnosis: elevated tryptase levels during and after the acute event, hepatomegaly on abdominal sonography, and possible neuropsychiatric symptoms including depression and memory problems. However, formal diagnostic criteria were not met—only two WHO minor criteria were fulfilled, with major criteria not assessable due to limited bone marrow biopsy material. For the clinician, this case reinforces that underlying mast cell disorders should be considered in patients presenting with severe, prolonged, or atypical anaphylactic reactions, and that elevated baseline tryptase levels warrant further investigation.

The case also highlights the importance of proper tryptase sampling. Guidelines recommend a minimum of two, ideally three samples: one as soon as feasible without delaying treatment, a second 1 to 2 hours after symptom onset (within 4 hours), and a third at least 24 hours after full resolution to establish baseline. In this case, the first tryptase sample was drawn only after the third anaphylactic episode, limiting the diagnostic utility of acute tryptase measurements. Proper serial tryptase measurement not only confirms anaphylaxis but can also unmask underlying mast cell disorders when baseline levels remain elevated.

Finally, this case exposes a significant knowledge gap in managing multiphasic anaphylaxis. When the allergology consultants considered continuous epinephrine infusion versus expectant management, both approaches carried substantial risks unsupported by available literature. The rarity of biphasic and especially multiphasic anaphylaxis creates substantial barriers to developing evidence-based strategies. The authors appropriately call for a registry of such cases to generate data for expert-based recommendations. Until such infrastructure exists, clinicians must rely on clinical judgment informed by limited evidence and case reports—making each documented case a valuable contribution to collective clinical knowledge.
