Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Multiphasic anaphylaxis in the emergency and intensive care setting"

### [Clinical Pearls] What is the recommended intramuscular epinephrine dose for anaphylaxis in adults, and why might the commonly available 0.3 mg auto-injector lead to suboptimal treatment?

**Clinical Answer:**

The recommended intramuscular epinephrine dose for anaphylaxis in adults is **0.01 mg/kg, up to a maximum of 0.5 mg per dose**. This weight-based approach is often simplified to a fixed 0.5 mg dose for most adults, given that the majority weigh more than 50 kg.

The commonly available 0.3 mg auto-injector can lead to **suboptimal treatment due to systematic underdosing**. This case illustrates the problem clearly: the patient weighed 104 kg, meaning her weight-based dose would have been approximately 1 mg, capped at the 0.5 mg maximum. The 0.3 mg auto-injector delivered only 60% of the recommended maximum dose. As noted in the discussion, patients receiving 0.3 mg rather than 0.5 mg epinephrine require escalation of care—including additional epinephrine injections—significantly more often.

The case demonstrates this principle: the first two episodes were treated with 0.3 mg via auto-injector, while the subsequent two episodes required individually prepared 0.5 mg doses. While auto-injectors offer practical advantages in time-sensitive settings and reduce treatment hesitancy among staff unfamiliar with vasoactive medications, clinicians must recognize that the 0.3 mg dose represents potential underdosing for adults over 50 kg. In severe anaphylaxis, particularly with hypotension and hypoxemia as seen in this case, adequate initial epinephrine dosing is critical to achieving timely resolution and may reduce the risk of refractory or biphasic courses.

### [Morning Report] How do you differentiate between biphasic anaphylaxis, sustained anaphylaxis, and refractory anaphylaxis in clinical practice?

**Clinical Teaching Answer**

Differentiating these three entities hinges on the **temporal pattern of symptom recurrence** and the **response to appropriately administered epinephrine**.

**Biphasic anaphylaxis** is characterized by an initial anaphylactic reaction followed by complete symptom resolution—an asymptomatic interval—and subsequent recurrence of symptoms without re-exposure to the trigger. The recurrence typically occurs within 24 hours but has been reported up to 72 hours later. In the presented case, the patient experienced multiphasic anaphylaxis, with four discrete episodes requiring separate epinephrine administrations after initial clinical improvement. The key feature is the *clear asymptomatic interval* between episodes.

**Sustained anaphylaxis** represents ongoing symptoms due to persistent antigen absorption or delayed drug clearance (e.g., slow-release formulations, sustained gastrointestinal exposure). Here, symptoms may transiently improve with epinephrine but recur promptly as the drug effect wanes, without a true asymptomatic period. The clinical clue is *failure to achieve sustained resolution* despite appropriate therapy. This must be differentiated from biphasic reactions, where complete resolution occurs between episodes.

**Refractory anaphylaxis** denotes persistent anaphylaxis that fails to respond adequately to correctly administered epinephrine at appropriate doses and intervals. This is a state of *treatment failure*, not recurrence. Clinically, hypotension or airway compromise persists despite adequate dosing.

In practice, careful documentation of symptom timelines and epinephrine response is essential. The case illustrates this challenge well: after initial stabilization, the patient had discrete relapses requiring additional epinephrine doses, consistent with biphasic (multiphasic) anaphylaxis rather than refractory disease, as each episode responded appropriately to treatment. Underlying mast cell disorders, suspected in this case, may predispose to such protracted courses.

### [Management] What is the current evidence regarding the use of corticosteroids for preventing biphasic anaphylaxis, and how has this influenced recent guideline recommendations?

### Clinical Teaching Answer

The current evidence does **not support the routine use of corticosteroids for preventing biphasic anaphylaxis**, and this has led to a major paradigm shift in contemporary guideline recommendations.

**Historical Context and Evolving Evidence:**
Traditionally, corticosteroids were administered as part of anaphylaxis management with the rationale that their anti-inflammatory effects might prevent or attenuate delayed symptom recurrence. However, this practice was based on extrapolation from other allergic conditions rather than robust evidence specific to anaphylaxis.

A pivotal 2017 systematic review examined 22 studies addressing this question and found that **21 studies showed no evidence that corticosteroids reduce the risk of biphasic anaphylaxis**, with only a single study suggesting benefit. Furthermore, evidence of potential harm has emerged—a 2019 study demonstrated that prehospital corticosteroid administration was associated with an *increased* likelihood of ICU or hospital ward admission, likely reflecting treatment delays or side effects rather than true therapeutic benefit.

**Current Guideline Recommendations:**
In response to this accumulating evidence, most contemporary guidelines now **advise against routine corticosteroid administration** in anaphylaxis. The mechanism makes biological sense when we consider that corticosteroids have a delayed onset of action (typically 4–6 hours), making them poorly suited to influence the early inflammatory cascade that drives biphasic reactions, which typically occur within the first 12–24 hours.

**Application to This Case:**
The presented case illustrates this evidence gap in real-world practice. Despite receiving both prednisone (30 mg IV daily for 5 days) and dexamethasone (8 mg IV once) after her first recurrence, our patient experienced **two additional anaphylactic episodes** requiring further epinephrine administration. This clinical course is consistent with the systematic review findings—corticosteroids neither prevented nor reduced the severity of her biphasic course.

**Clinical Bottom Line:**
Epinephrine remains the only first-line therapy proven effective for anaphylaxis and its recurrences. The focus should be on prompt intramuscular epinephrine administration at appropriate doses (0.5 mg for adults), close observation for symptom recurrence, and readiness to repeat epinephrine dosing if biphasic reactions occur—rather than relying on corticosteroids for prevention.

### [Clinical Pearls] What is the recommended timing for serum tryptase level measurement in patients presenting with anaphylaxis, and what is the rationale for this specific sequence?

### Clinical Teaching Answer

The recommended approach is to obtain **a minimum of two, ideally three, serum tryptase samples** at specific time points: (1) as soon as feasible after symptom onset without delaying treatment, (2) at 1–2 hours (and within 4 hours) of symptom onset when tryptase typically peaks, and (3) at least 24 hours after full clinical resolution to establish a baseline level.

**Rationale:** Tryptase is a protease released from activated mast cells during anaphylaxis. Serum levels rise rapidly, peak within 1–2 hours of symptom onset, and have a half-life of approximately 2 hours. By capturing both the peak level and a baseline level, clinicians can calculate the delta between acute and resting states. A ratio of acute-to-baseline tryptase exceeding 2 (with the baseline sample obtained ≥24 hours after resolution) strongly supports mast cell activation and helps differentiate true anaphylaxis from other causes of hypotension or shock. Importantly, an elevated baseline tryptase may suggest underlying mast cell disorders such as systemic mastocytosis, which predisposes patients to severe or atypical anaphylaxis.

**In this case**, the recommended protocol was not followed. The first tryptase was drawn 1 hour after the *third* anaphylactic episode rather than during initial presentation, and the second sample was obtained 27 hours after the last recurrence. Despite this, both the acute and baseline levels were elevated, raising clinical suspicion for systemic mastocytosis—a diagnosis that could explain her multiphasic course and severe reaction to clarithromycin. This case underscores how adherence to guideline-recommended tryptase timing improves diagnostic yield and facilitates identification of underlying mast cell disease.

### [Differential Diagnosis] What underlying condition should be suspected in patients with severe, protracted, or multiphasic anaphylaxis, and what clinical and laboratory findings support this diagnosis?

**Systemic mastocytosis should be suspected in patients presenting with severe, protracted, or multiphasic anaphylaxis.**

This underlying condition is characterized by an abnormal clonal proliferation of mast cells, leading to excessive mediator release and predisposition to severe, recurrent anaphylactic reactions. As illustrated in the present case, the patient experienced four distinct anaphylactic episodes over approximately 12 hours despite appropriate epinephrine administration and clinical resolution between events—a classic multiphasic course that prompted evaluation for an underlying mast cell disorder.

**Clinical findings suggestive of systemic mastocytosis include:**
- Severe or refractory anaphylaxis, particularly with hypotension as a prominent feature
- Multiphasic or biphasic anaphylaxis course
- Anaphylaxis triggered by insect stings (the patient had a prior strong local reaction to a wasp sting)
- Hepatomegaly (found on abdominal sonography in this case)
- Neuropsychiatric symptoms such as depression and cognitive difficulties ("brain fog"), which were also noted in this patient

**Laboratory findings supporting the diagnosis:**
- **Elevated baseline serum tryptase**—this is the cornerstone screening test. In this case, tryptase levels were elevated during the anaphylactic episode and remained elevated at follow-up, indicating an abnormally high baseline rather than just acute mast cell activation.
- The World Health Organization (WHO) diagnostic criteria for systemic mastocytosis require either the **major criterion** (dense mast cell aggregates on bone marrow biopsy) plus one minor criterion, or three **minor criteria** (including elevated baseline tryptase >20 ng/mL, abnormal mast cell morphology, presence of KIT D816V mutation, or abnormal CD25/CD2 expression on mast cells).

In this patient, bone marrow biopsy material was limited, precluding assessment of the major criterion; however, the combination of elevated baseline tryptase and clinical findings yielded a "high probability" of systemic mastocytosis even without meeting formal diagnostic criteria.

**The key teaching point:** when you encounter anaphylaxis that is unusually severe, requires multiple epinephrine doses, follows a biphasic or multiphasic course, or occurs without an obvious trigger, send a serum tryptase level—ideally at presentation, 1–2 hours post-event, and at baseline after resolution. An elevated baseline tryptase should prompt referral to allergology or hematology for further evaluation of systemic mastocytosis.

### [Pathophysiology] Why does systemic mastocytosis predispose patients to severe anaphylactic reactions, and what is the pathophysiologic mechanism involved?

### Clinical Teaching Answer

Systemic mastocytosis predisposes patients to severe anaphylaxis through a fundamental pathophysiologic principle: **an expanded mast cell burden creates a larger reservoir of inflammatory mediators available for release upon activation.**

In healthy individuals, mast cell activation—whether IgE-mediated, complement-mediated, or through direct mast cell degranulation—triggers release of pre-formed mediators including histamine, tryptase, chymase, and newly synthesized lipid mediators such as prostaglandin D₂ and leukotriene C₄. In systemic mastocytosis, clonal proliferation of mast cells leads to pathologically increased mast cell numbers in bone marrow, skin, and other organs. This creates two critical problems:

**First**, the sheer quantity of releasable mediators is dramatically increased. When mast cell activation occurs, the scale of mediator release is proportionally greater, producing more profound vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, bronchoconstriction, and cardiovascular collapse.

**Second**, the baseline activation threshold may be lowered. Patients with mastocytosis can experience anaphylaxis with triggers that would cause minimal symptoms in unaffected individuals, or even spontaneously without an identifiable trigger.

This case illustrates the clinical consequence beautifully. Our patient developed severe anaphylaxis—hypotension to 74/50 mm Hg, hypoxemia to 88% saturation, and diffuse urticaria—after a *single* dose of clarithromycin, a drug not classically associated with high anaphylaxis risk. Her clinical course was notably multiphasic, requiring four separate epinephrine administrations across 12 hours. This prolonged, recurrent pattern reflects the expanded mast cell population continuing to generate and release mediators beyond what would be expected from typical IgE-mediated degranulation.

Her elevated baseline tryptase, hepatomegaly on imaging, and neuropsychiatric symptoms all support underlying systemic mastocytosis as the predisposing condition. The elevated baseline tryptase itself is a marker of increased mast cell burden and serves as an independent risk factor for severe anaphylaxis.

The practical implication: Any patient presenting with severe, refractory, or multiphasic anaphylaxis—particularly with elevated tryptase—should prompt evaluation for systemic mastocytosis.

### [Management] What observation period is generally recommended after resolution of anaphylaxis symptoms, and what patient factors might warrant extended monitoring?

### Clinical Teaching Answer

The standard observation period after anaphylaxis resolution is typically **4 to 6 hours**, though this should be individualized based on risk stratification. The rationale for observation stems from the phenomenon of biphasic anaphylaxis—a recurrence of symptoms after apparent resolution without re-exposure to the trigger—which occurs in approximately 4–6% of cases, with approximately half of these recurrences manifesting within 6 to 12 hours of the initial event.

**Patient factors warranting extended observation (12–24 hours or admission) include:**

1. **Severe initial presentation** – Patients presenting with hypotension, hypoxia, or airway compromise are at higher risk for biphasic reactions. Our patient demonstrated both hypotension (74/50 mm Hg) and hypoxemia (SpO₂ 88%) at presentation.

2. **Multiple epinephrine doses required** – Need for more than one dose of epinephrine to control initial symptoms is a well-established risk factor. This patient required four separate epinephrine injections across four distinct anaphylactic episodes.

3. **Delayed epinephrine administration** – Delayed treatment of the initial reaction increases biphasic risk.

4. **Prior history of biphasic reaction** – A previous biphasic episode predicts recurrence.

5. **Underlying mast cell disorders** – The patient's elevated baseline tryptase and probable systemic mastocytosis likely contributed to her multiphasic course, as mast cell disorders predispose patients to severe and recurrent anaphylaxis.

This case illustrates the challenge vividly: despite initial symptom resolution, our patient developed recurrent anaphylactic shock within the first 12 hours, requiring ICU admission and multiple epinephrine doses. She was transferred from the ICU only after approximately 24 hours without symptom relapse.

The UK 2021 guideline offers a practical three-tier risk-stratified approach. For high-risk patients—such as those with severe presentations, multiple epinephrine doses, or underlying mast cell disease—extended observation of 24 hours or admission is prudent. The absence of reliable predictors makes clinical judgment essential, but the consequences of premature discharge can be life-threatening.

### [Morning Report] What are the proposed risk factors for biphasic anaphylaxis, and why is reliable prediction of this complication challenging?

**Clinical Teaching Answer:**

Biphasic anaphylaxis refers to recurrence of anaphylactic symptoms after apparent clinical resolution, without re-exposure to the triggering allergen. The reported incidence varies widely—from 0.4% to 23.3%, though most data suggest a rate of 4% to 6%. This variability itself reflects one of the fundamental challenges in studying and predicting this phenomenon.

**Proposed Risk Factors**
Based on available evidence, several risk factors have been suggested:
- **Delayed epinephrine administration** during the initial reaction
- **Severity of the initial presentation** (particularly hypotension or respiratory compromise)
- **Requirement for multiple epinephrine doses** to achieve initial symptom control
- **History of prior biphasic reactions**

In the presented case, our patient demonstrated several of these features: a severe initial presentation with hypotension (74/50 mm Hg), hypoxemia (SpO₂ 88%), and requirement for multiple epinephrine doses across four distinct anaphylactic episodes. Additionally, her evaluation revealed elevated tryptase levels and features suggestive of systemic mastocytosis—a condition known to predispose patients to severe anaphylaxis, though its specific relationship to biphasic reactions remains uncharacterized.

**Why Prediction Remains Challenging**
Despite these proposed associations, no reliable predictive model exists for several reasons:

1. **Inconsistent definitions** across studies complicate the evidence base, making meta-analyses difficult.

2. **Low incidence** limits statistical power to identify true predictors versus coincidental associations.

3. **Confounding by treatment factors**—for example, it remains unclear whether multiple epinephrine doses are a marker of reaction severity or whether underdosing (such as the 0.3 mg auto-injector used initially in this case) contributes to incomplete resolution and subsequent recurrence.

4. **Heterogeneous underlying mechanisms**—as illustrated by this patient's probable mastocytosis—suggest that different pathophysiologic processes may drive biphasic patterns in different patients.

The clinical bottom line is that we cannot currently identify which patients will develop biphasic reactions. This uncertainty underlies guideline recommendations for observation periods after anaphylaxis, typically 4 to 6 hours, though even this lacks strong evidence. Until better predictive tools emerge, clinicians must maintain a low threshold for extended observation in patients with severe presentations or multiple epinephrine requirements, while counseling all patients with anaphylaxis about the possibility of symptom recurrence and the need to seek immediate care should symptoms return.
