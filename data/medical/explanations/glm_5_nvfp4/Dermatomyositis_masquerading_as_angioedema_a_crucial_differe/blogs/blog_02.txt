Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# Anti-SAE1: The DM Antibody You Can't Afford to Miss

## When "Angioedema" Isn't Angioedema

Picture this: a guy in his 40s rolls into your ED with three weeks of progressive neck swelling. The ENT surgeons have already scoped him and ruled out Ludwig's angina. CT neck shows diffuse soft tissue edema extending into the retropharyngeal space. No fever, no airway compromise. The working diagnosis? Idiopathic angioedema.

But something doesn't sit right.

He's got no history of ACE inhibitor use. No family history of angioedema. No triggers. And when you actually examine him—really examine him—you notice subtle nailfold changes, some facial erythema crossing the nasolabial folds, and a rash on his lateral thigh. His hip flexor strength is maybe 4/5, though he's not really complaining about weakness.

So someone orders a CK.

It comes back at **16,184 U/L**.

That's not angioedema. That's dermatomyositis.

## The Anti-SAE1 Phenotype: What Makes It Distinct

Small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme 1 (SAE1) autoantibodies define a specific subset of dermatomyositis that every emergency physician and internist should have on their radar. This isn't just academic—these patients have a distinct clinical phenotype that demands aggressive immunosuppression and systematic malignancy surveillance.

Let's talk numbers from the largest literature review to date covering 215 patients with anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis:

- **Dysphagia: 46%** — nearly half will have swallowing difficulties
- **Interstitial lung disease: 43%** — get the PFTs and CT chest
- **Malignancy association: 18%** — this is the one that keeps you up at night

The classic cutaneous triad is still there: heliotrope rash, Gottron's papules, the shawl sign and V-neck sign. But here's the thing—our patient didn't have the heliotrope or Gottron's. He had periungual erythema, a positive "Holster sign" (rash on lateral thighs), and facial erythema. The skin findings can be subtle, and you need to look for them.

## The Pseudoangioedema Trap

Subcutaneous edema as a presenting feature of dermatomyositis is rare—only about 6.6% in one cohort of 121 patients. But when it happens, it's a diagnostic landmine.

The neck swelling in dermatomyositis isn't histamine-mediated. It isn't bradykinin-mediated. It's **muscle inflammation** causing tissue edema. That means your standard angioedema treatments—antihistamines, epinephrine, C1 esterase inhibitors—won't touch it.

This patient's CT showed inflammation of the strap muscles extending into the retropharyngeal space. His FDG-PET demonstrated diffuse abnormal uptake in head and neck muscles, bilateral deltoids, forearms, chest wall—this was systemic myositis masquerading as isolated neck swelling.

The lesson? When "angioedema" doesn't fit the clinical picture—no triggers, no medication history, no family history, atypical distribution—think beyond the algorithm. Do a thorough skin exam. Check nailfolds. And for the love of Hippocrates, check a CK.

## The Malignancy Question

Anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis carries intermediate malignancy risk. Our patient had multiple risk factors stacked: dermatomyositis diagnosis, presence of dysphagia (high-risk feature), SAE1 antibody positivity (intermediate risk), and male sex (intermediate risk). That puts him in the high-risk category per International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group guidelines.

He needed the full workup: CT chest, abdomen, pelvis, FDG-PET, tumor markers. And he needs ongoing surveillance—serial imaging, scheduled endoscopy, close clinical follow-up. The malignancy may not be present at diagnosis; it can emerge later. These patients require long-term vigilance.

## Treatment Intensity Matters

This wasn't a "start some prednisone and follow up with rheumatology" situation. The intensity of immunosuppression in anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis reflects the severity of disease involvement.

Our patient received:
- IV methylprednisolone 1000 mg daily x 3 days
- Prednisone taper starting at 60 mg daily
- IVIG 2 g/kg loading, then monthly cycles
- Mycophenolate mofetil 1 g BID
- Rituximab 1000 mg x 2 doses, two weeks apart

Methotrexate was intentionally avoided due to his pulmonary involvement—remember, MTX carries a risk of interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, which you don't want to stack on top of existing ILD concerns.

The dysphagia was severe enough to require nasogastric feeding for two months. But by three weeks post-discharge, his CK had normalized to 37 U/L, and proximal muscle strength had fully recovered. That's the power of aggressive, early treatment.

## When to Order the Myositis Panel

Let's be practical. You're not ordering a 16-antigen myositis antibody panel on every patient with vague complaints. But consider it when you have:

- Elevated CK without clear alternative explanation
- Proximal muscle weakness with or without myalgia
- Characteristic skin findings (even subtle ones)
- Dysphagia with no structural cause
- Interstitial lung disease of unclear etiology
- Unexplained inflammatory edema

The antibody panel matters because it informs prognosis and guides surveillance intensity. Anti-SAE1 tells you to watch the lungs, watch the swallow, and watch for cancer. Anti-Mi-2 has a better prognosis. Anti-MDA5 screams ILD risk. Anti-TIF1-γ screams malignancy risk. These aren't just academic distinctions—they change your clinical approach.

## Key Take-Home Points

- **Atypical "angioedema" warrants a broader differential**: When the clinical picture doesn't fit histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema, examine for cutaneous signs of dermatomyositis and check a CK level

- **Anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis is high-stakes**: Nearly half have dysphagia, nearly half have ILD, and malignancy association approaches 20%

- **Skin findings can be subtle**: This patient had no heliotrope rash or Gottron's papules—look for periungual erythema, nailfold microhaemorrhages, facial erythema, and rashes in atypical locations like the lateral thighs

- **Malignancy surveillance is mandatory**: SAE1 positivity with dysphagia puts patients in a high-risk category requiring comprehensive baseline screening and ongoing monitoring

- **Treatment should be aggressive early**: These patients often require combination immunosuppression including steroids, steroid-sparing agents, IVIG, and sometimes rituximab

## The Bottom Line

Dermatomyositis can walk through your door wearing an angioedema costume. The key to unmasking it lies in the history, the physical exam, and a low threshold to check a CK. Anti-SAE1 positivity carries specific implications for prognosis, malignancy risk, and treatment intensity. Know the phenotype, order the right tests, and get these patients to the right specialists early—their outcomes depend on it.
