Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# Dysphagia in Myositis: The Airway Complication That Defines Prognosis

## The Case That Stops You in Your Tracks

A man in his 40s walks into your ED with three weeks of progressive neck swelling. The ENT surgeons get called first — this looks like Ludwig's angina, right? The CT shows diffuse edema involving the strap muscles and inflammation extending to the retropharyngeal space. But there's no fever, no trismus, no dental infection to explain it. Flexible nasendoscopy is essentially normal. So what are we missing?

The clue was there all along: proximal muscle weakness in his hip flexors. When someone finally thought to check a creatine kinase, it came back at **16,184 U/L**. This wasn't angioedema. This wasn't a deep neck space infection. This was dermatomyositis presenting with pseudoangioedema — subcutaneous edema from intense muscle inflammation — and the dysphagia that followed would define his hospital course and his prognosis.

## Why Dysphagia Matters More Than You Think

Here's the uncomfortable truth about inflammatory myositis: dysphagia isn't just a bothersome symptom. It's a prognostic siren. In dermatomyositis, dysphagia correlates with more severe disease, increased risk of aspiration pneumonitis, prolonged hospital stays, and — critically — higher malignancy risk. In the SAE1 antibody subtype specifically, dysphagia occurs in roughly 46% of patients, reflecting the severe muscle involvement typical of this entity.

Our patient had the classic mechanism at play: pharyngeal muscle inflammation leading to severe dysphagia. His video fluoroscopic swallow study showed severe pyriform fossae residue and post-swallow penetration of fluids to the vocal cords. He wasn't just having trouble swallowing — he was aspirating. His CT chest showed patchy peribronchial consolidation, likely aspiration pneumonitis. He needed a nasogastric tube for two months. This is not unusual. This is the natural history of untreated myositis affecting the swallowing apparatus.

## The Mechanism: More Than Just "Weak Muscles"

The dysphagia in dermatomyositis stems from inflammation affecting the striated muscles of the pharynx and upper esophagus. The cricopharyngeal muscle, in particular, can become dysmotile, leading to incomplete relaxation and functional obstruction. But the problem extends beyond mechanical weakness. The muscle biopsy from our patient showed necrotic, vacuolated, degenerate fibers in a perifascicular distribution with C5b-9 deposition in endomysial capillaries — classic dermatomyositis pathology. This is complement-mediated microangiopathy affecting muscle fibers.

When you combine pharyngeal weakness with impaired cricopharyngeal relaxation, you get the perfect storm: stasis in the pyriform sinuses, overflow into the airway, and recurrent micro-aspiration. The patient may not even cough — the protective reflexes themselves can be blunted. Silent aspiration is the real danger.

## When to Suspect Myositis-Induced Dysphagia

The textbook presentations — heliotrope rash, Gottron's papules, the classic shawl sign — they're helpful when present. But our patient had none of these. He had subtle nailfold changes, periungual erythema, a rash on his lateral thigh (a "Holster sign"), and facial erythema. The neck swelling was the red herring that sent him down the wrong diagnostic path initially.

What should have triggered earlier suspicion? The combination of subacute proximal weakness with any degree of swallowing difficulty should prompt a CK level. Period. The presence of dysphagia with elevated inflammatory markers and no clear infectious source should make you think myositis, especially if there are any cutaneous changes — even subtle ones. Nailfold capillaroscopy showing microhemorrhages and avascular areas is highly specific for dermatomyositis and was present in this case.

## Aggressive Immunosuppression: Why We Don't Wait

This patient received the full arsenal: three days of IV methylprednisolone at 1000 mg daily, followed by oral prednisone taper, IVIG at 2 g/kg loading then monthly cycles, mycophenolate mofetil, and two doses of rituximab. That's quadruple therapy. And it worked — his CK normalized within three weeks, proximal strength recovered, and dysphagia resolved sufficiently to remove the NG tube after two months.

Why such aggressive upfront treatment? Because dysphagia in dermatomyositis responds poorly to inadequate immunosuppression, and the complications of undertreatment — aspiration, malnutrition, prolonged hospitalization, even death — are devastating. IVIG has particular utility in myositis-associated dysphagia, with evidence supporting its role in accelerating recovery of swallowing function. Methotrexate was avoided in this case due to concerns about pulmonary toxicity — an important consideration when baseline lung function may already be compromised by interstitial lung disease or aspiration.

## Key Take-Home Points

- **Dysphagia in dermatomyositis is a prognostic marker, not just a symptom** — it correlates with severe disease, aspiration risk, and malignancy association
- **The mechanism involves pharyngeal muscle inflammation and cricopharyngeal dysfunction**, leading to pyriform sinus stasis and aspiration
- **Silent aspiration is common** — don't assume lack of coughing means safe swallowing; formal swallow studies are essential
- **Aggressive multi-agent immunosuppression is warranted** — these patients often need high-dose steroids, IVIG, and steroid-sparing agents like mycophenolate or rituximab
- **Always check a CK in unexplained neck swelling with any weakness or skin changes** — pseudoangioedema from myositis is rare but treatable, and the diagnosis is often delayed
- **Malignancy screening is mandatory in dermatomyositis with dysphagia** — this combination places patients in a high-risk category requiring comprehensive workup and surveillance

## The Bottom Line

When dysphagia complicates myositis, it transforms the clinical trajectory. These patients need early speech pathology involvement, aggressive immunosuppression, careful airway protection, and patience — recovery of swallowing function can take months. But as this case demonstrates, with appropriate treatment, even severe dysphagia can resolve. The key is recognizing it early and treating it seriously. Because in dermatomyositis, the ability to swallow isn't just a quality-of-life issue — it's a survival issue.
