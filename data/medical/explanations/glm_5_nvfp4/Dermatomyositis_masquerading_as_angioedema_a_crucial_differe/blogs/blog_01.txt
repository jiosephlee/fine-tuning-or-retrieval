Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# "Idiopathic" Angioedema? Think Again.

## The Case That Stops You in Your Tracks

A man in his 40s walks into your ED with three weeks of progressive neck swelling. Your surgical colleagues rule out Ludwig's angina. The immunology team is consulted for suspected idiopathic angioedema. But something doesn't sit right. The swelling has been progressing over weeks, not hours. No ACE inhibitors, no NSAIDs, no family history. And then someone notices subtle nailfold changes and a rash on his thigh.

A creatine kinase level comes back at 16,184 U/L.

This wasn't angioedema at all. It was dermatomyositis presenting with pseudoangioedema—a rare but critical differential that emergency physicians, ENT surgeons, and immunologists cannot afford to miss.

## When "Idiopathic" Means "Look Harder"

Let's be honest: "idiopathic angioedema" is often a diagnosis of exclusion that can become a cognitive trap. In this case, the patient's presentation had several features that should have prompted earlier skepticism about the angioedema diagnosis.

True angioedema—whether histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated—typically has a tempo measured in hours, not weeks. This patient's swelling had been progressive for three weeks. That timeline alone should make you pause. Additionally, the distribution was unusual: diffuse neck edema involving strap muscles with inflammation extending to the retropharyngeal space on CT, but without the typical facial or oropharyngeal involvement you'd expect with angioedema.

The real breakthrough came when someone took a thorough look at his hands and skin. Nailfold microhaemorrhages and avascular areas on capillaroscopy. Periungual erythema. A rash on the lateral thigh. Proximal muscle weakness rated at 4/5 in the hip flexors. These weren't incidental findings—they were the diagnostic keys hiding in plain sight.

## The CK That Changed Everything

Here's the clinical pearl that cannot be overstated: when neck swelling doesn't fit the angioedema paradigm, particularly with any suggestion of muscle involvement or cutaneous changes, check a creatine kinase level.

This patient's CK of 16,184 U/L was staggering. For context, we often think of rhabdomyolysis at CK levels above 1,000 U/L, and this was sixteen times that threshold. But here's what's fascinating—his presentation was dominated by neck swelling and dysphagia, not by the classic proximal muscle weakness we're taught to associate with inflammatory myopathies. The myalgia was described as "mild." The hip flexor weakness was documented, but it wasn't the chief complaint.

This is where diagnostic momentum becomes dangerous. The patient entered the system through an ENT pathway, was labeled as possible Ludwig's angina, then redirected to immunology for angioedema. Each handoff reinforced an assumption. It took an immunologist who recognized that the clinical picture didn't fit, combined with astute physical examination, to break that momentum.

## Anti-SAE1: A Subtype Worth Knowing

This patient tested strongly positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies—a specific myositis antibody with a distinct clinical phenotype. Anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis is characterized by prominent cutaneous features like heliotrope rash and Gottron's papules, but this case reminds us that presentations can be atypical.

What's particularly relevant for emergency physicians and hospitalists is the association data from the literature: in anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis, dysphagia occurs in 46% of cases, interstitial lung disease in 43%, and malignancy in 18%. This patient had severe pharyngeal dysphagia on video fluoroscopic swallow study with aspiration risk. He required nasogastric feeding for two months. The dysphagia wasn't a minor feature—it was a manifestation of severe disease requiring aggressive immunosuppression.

## Pseudoangioedema: How Rare Is It?

In a cohort study of 121 dermatomyositis patients, only 8 (6.6%) presented with angioedema-like swelling. That's rare enough that you might go your entire career without seeing it—but when you do, the consequences of missing it are substantial. This patient required a 21-day hospitalization with IV methylprednisolone, IVIG, rituximab, and mycophenolate mofetil. He needed nasogastric feeding and intensive rehabilitation.

Delaying immunosuppression in dermatomyositis can lead to progressive muscle involvement, respiratory failure from respiratory muscle weakness, and complications from dysphagia including aspiration pneumonitis—which this patient had evidence of on CT chest.

## Practical Takeaways for the ED and Beyond

When you're evaluating unexplained neck or facial swelling, especially when the tempo is subacute rather than acute, widen your differential. Ask about muscle weakness, even if it's not the presenting complaint. Look at the hands—nailfold changes, periungual erythema, Gottron's papules. Examine the skin for heliotrope rash, shawl sign, V-neck sign, or more subtle findings like this patient's lateral thigh rash.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- Not all neck swelling is angioedema. Subacute progression over days to weeks should prompt consideration of inflammatory mimics
- When the clinical picture doesn't fit histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema, step back and reconsider before labeling it "idiopathic"
- A creatine kinase level is inexpensive, rapidly available, and can be diagnostic—check it when you see atypical swelling with any suggestion of muscle involvement
- Cutaneous exam matters: nailfold changes, periungual erythema, and subtle rashes can be the diagnostic clues that unlock the case
- Dysphagia in dermatomyositis indicates severe disease and higher malignancy risk—these patients need aggressive immunosuppression and malignancy screening
- Anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis has high rates of dysphagia (46%), ILD (43%), and malignancy (18%)—know the phenotype
- Diagnostic momentum is real. The label this patient carried shifted from "Ludwig's angina" to "angioedema" without anyone pausing to question whether either fit. Be the clinician who pauses

## The Bottom Line

This case is a masterclass in clinical reasoning and the danger of premature diagnostic closure. A patient with three weeks of neck swelling, mild myalgia, and subtle skin findings turned out to have massive muscle inflammation requiring multi-agent immunosuppression. The diagnosis hinged on recognizing that "idiopathic angioedema" was a label that didn't fit—and having the curiosity to look for what did.

Next time you're tempted to sign off on idiopathic angioedema, remember this case. Look at the hands. Check a CK. And never let a label become a blind spot.
