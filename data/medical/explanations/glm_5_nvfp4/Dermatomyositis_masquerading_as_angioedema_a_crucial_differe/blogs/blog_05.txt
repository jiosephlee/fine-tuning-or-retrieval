Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# Dermatomyositis and Cancer: Who Needs What Screening?

We all know the textbook fact: dermatomyositis carries an association with malignancy. But here's what makes this case particularly instructive—when you actually sit down and risk-stratify a patient using current guidelines, the workup becomes much more targeted and defensible. This case of anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis presenting with dramatic neck swelling gives us a perfect framework to walk through the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) cancer screening recommendations.

## The Case That Stops You in Your Tracks

A previously well man in his 40s presents with three weeks of progressive neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness. Initial concern was Ludwig's angina—reasonable given the clinical picture. CT neck showed diffuse neck edema involving strap muscles with inflammation extending to the retropharyngeal space. But here's the kicker: no fever, normal white count, and a CRP of 55.6 mg/L that didn't fit a fulminant infection. ENT found no abscess. The immunology consult for "idiopathic angioedema" noticed subtle but critical findings—periungual erythema, nailfold microhaemorrhages on capillaroscopy, a rash on the lateral thigh, and 4/5 hip flexor power.

The creatine kinase came back at 16,184 U/L. This wasn't angioedema. This was dermatomyositis with significant myositis causing pseudoangioedema of the neck—a rare presentation seen in only about 6-7% of dermatomyositis patients.

## Risk Stratification: The IMACS Framework

Here's where the teaching value really shines. The IMACS guidelines give us a structured approach to malignancy risk stratification in idiopathic inflammatory myopathy, and this patient hit multiple concerning boxes.

He had two high-risk factors: dermatomyositis diagnosis itself, and dysphagia. Dysphagia in dermatomyositis isn't just a morbidity issue—it's a red flag for malignancy risk. He also had two intermediate risk factors: male sex and SAE1 antibody positivity. That's high risk, full stop.

The antibody profile matters considerably. Anti-TIF1-γ antibodies carry the strongest malignancy association, particularly in juvenile dermatomyositis and adult patients. Anti-NXP2 and anti-SAE1 antibodies also carry elevated malignancy risk, though data for SAE1 specifically shows malignancy in about 18% of cases. Anti-Mi-2 antibodies, interestingly, are associated with better cancer risk profiles and generally better outcomes. Anti-MDA5 antibodies are more associated with rapidly progressive interstitial lung disease and less so with malignancy.

## What Does High-Risk Screening Actually Look Like?

For a high-risk patient like this, the baseline workup should be comprehensive. He underwent CT chest, abdomen, and pelvis, which showed pulmonary nodules and peribronchial consolidation consistent with aspiration but no malignancy. Tumor markers including CEA, CA19-9, CA-125, and PSA were normal. A whole-body FDG-PET scan demonstrated diffuse muscle inflammation but no metabolically active lesions suggesting occult malignancy.

But imaging alone isn't sufficient. The plan includes gastroscopy and colonoscopy to exclude occult gastrointestinal malignancy—particularly important given the dysphagia, which raises concern for upper GI pathology. In female patients, you'd add mammography and pelvic ultrasound; in males, testicular examination and PSA are essential.

The question that often comes up: is PET-CT sufficient, or do you still need organ-specific imaging? Current evidence suggests PET-CT is sensitive but not perfect, and in high-risk patients, complementing with dedicated CT imaging and endoscopy provides the most thorough evaluation. The IMACS approach favors comprehensive baseline screening with interval surveillance based on clinical trajectory.

## Duration of Surveillance: How Long Is Long Enough?

This is where guidelines get a bit murkier, and clinical judgment matters. The highest malignancy risk is in the first year after diagnosis, particularly the first 3-6 months. But surveillance shouldn't stop there. For high-risk patients, ongoing monitoring with serial imaging, regular clinical review, and age-appropriate cancer screening is recommended for at least 3-5 years. Some experts argue for indefinite surveillance given case reports of malignancy presenting years after dermatomyositis diagnosis.

In this case, the plan is symptom-guided monitoring with regular blood tests and serial CT chest imaging. That's reasonable—ongoing vigilance without blanket investigations that could lead to false positives and unnecessary interventions. The key is maintaining a high index of suspicion and acting promptly on any new concerning symptoms.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Risk stratify using IMACS criteria**: Dermatomyositis and dysphagia are high-risk factors; male sex and certain antibodies (SAE1, TIF1-γ, NXP2) are intermediate risk. Multiple risk factors = comprehensive workup.
- **Antibody type guides malignancy risk**: Anti-TIF1-γ carries the highest risk; anti-Mi-2 carries lower risk. Know your myositis antibody panels.
- **Baseline high-risk screening**: CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, tumor markers, age-appropriate cancer screening, PET-CT in select cases, and endoscopy for patients with dysphagia or GI symptoms.
- **Don't forget the basics**: Physical exam findings matter. This case was solved because someone looked at the nails, the skin, and tested a CK level when the angioedema story didn't fit.
- **Surveillance is ongoing**: Highest risk is year one, but maintain vigilance for at least 3-5 years in high-risk patients.

Dermatomyositis presenting as angioedema is rare, but the malignancy association is not. Having a structured approach to cancer screening ensures you don't miss the boat on this critical aspect of care.
