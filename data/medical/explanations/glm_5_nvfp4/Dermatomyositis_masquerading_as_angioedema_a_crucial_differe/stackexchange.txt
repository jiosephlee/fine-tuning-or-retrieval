Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Clinical Pearls] A patient presents with neck swelling initially suspected to be angioedema. What clinical and laboratory features should prompt consideration of dermatomyositis as an alternative diagnosis?

**Clinical Teaching Answer**

When evaluating neck swelling suspected to be angioedema, several clinical and laboratory features should prompt consideration of dermatomyositis as an alternative diagnosis.

**Clinical Features:**

First, assess whether the swelling is truly consistent with angioedema. In this case, the "atypical nature of neck oedema" was an important clue—in true angioedema, swelling is typically transient, often affects the face, lips, tongue, and oropharynx, and resolves within 24–72 hours. Persistent, non-pitting swelling extending into deeper tissues should raise suspicion for an alternative diagnosis.

Second, perform a thorough skin and nail examination. In this patient, subtle findings—including generalized facial erythema involving the nasolabial folds, periungual erythema, nailfold microhemorrhages with avascular areas on capillaroscopy, and a rash on the lateral thigh (consistent with a Holster sign)—provided critical diagnostic clues. Classic features such as Gottron's papules or heliotrope eruption may be absent, as they were in this case.

Third, evaluate for muscle involvement. This patient had proximal muscle weakness (hip flexor power 4/5) with mild myalgia, and dysphagia was evident on flexible nasendoscopy with increased secretions and coughing. Dysphagia can be an early or prominent feature in dermatomyositis and may precede overt weakness.

Fourth, take a detailed medication and family history. The absence of ACE inhibitors, DPP-4 inhibitors, NSAIDs, or a personal/family history of angioedema made hereditary or acquired angioedema less likely.

**Laboratory Features:**

A creatine kinase level is essential and should be obtained early when the clinical picture is atypical. In this case, the CK was markedly elevated at 16,184 U/L, confirming significant muscle injury. Elevated transaminases (ALT 143 U/L, AST 396 U/L) and LDH (692 U/L) further supported muscle inflammation, as these enzymes are released from damaged myofibers.

Complement studies (C3, C4, and C1 esterase function) were normal, effectively excluding C1 esterase inhibitor deficiency—the hallmark of hereditary and acquired angioedema. A positive ANA (1:1280, speckled pattern) and confirmatory anti-SAE1 antibody (a myositis-specific antibody) solidified the diagnosis.

**Key Teaching Point:**

When neck swelling is persistent, atypical in distribution, or accompanied by cutaneous changes, muscle weakness, dysphagia, or markedly elevated CK, dermatomyositis should be strongly considered. Prompt recognition is critical, as patients require intensive immunosuppression to prevent disease progression and complications, including aspiration pneumonitis and malignancy-associated risk.

### [Pathophysiology] How does the mechanism of subcutaneous edema ('pseudoangioedema') in dermatomyositis differ from classic histaminergic and bradykinin-mediated angioedema?

**Clinical Teaching Answer:**

The subcutaneous edema in dermatomyositis—termed "pseudoangioedema"—arises from a fundamentally different mechanism than classic angioedema and represents inflammatory edema rather than a primary vascular permeability disorder mediated by vasoactive substances.

**Classic angioedema mechanisms:**

- **Histaminergic angioedema** results from mast cell and basophil degranulation with histamine release, typically triggered by IgE-mediated allergic reactions. Histamine acts on H1 and H2 receptors, causing transient vasodilation and increased capillary permeability. This form responds to antihistamines, epinephrine, and corticosteroids, and is often accompanied by urticaria and pruritus.

- **Bradykinin-mediated angioedema** involves dysregulation of the kallikrein-kinin cascade, leading to excessive bradykinin production or impaired degradation. This occurs in hereditary angioedema (C1 esterase inhibitor deficiency), acquired angioedema, or ACE inhibitor–associated angioedema. Bradykinin causes sustained vascular leakage without urticaria or pruritus, and crucially, it does not respond to antihistamines or epinephrine.

**Dermatomyositis pseudoangioedema mechanism:**

In contrast, the subcutaneous edema in dermatomyositis reflects **inflammatory infiltration of muscle and connective tissue with associated microvascular injury**. The pathophysiology involves complement activation with deposition of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9) on endomysial capillaries, as demonstrated in this patient's muscle biopsy. This complement-mediated microangiopathy leads to perifascicular muscle fiber damage, inflammation, and tissue edema.

As the authors note, the patient's "subcutaneous oedema arises from muscle inflammation in the setting of myositis, rather than histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema." This is supported by normal C3, C4, and C1 esterase inhibitor levels in this case, which effectively exclude bradykinin-mediated etiologies.

**Key distinguishing features from the case:**

The atypical distribution of swelling—involving the strap muscles and extending to the retropharyngeal space on CT—was inconsistent with classic angioedema, which typically involves the deep dermis and subcutaneous tissues without inflammatory muscle infiltration. Additionally, the associated proximal muscle weakness (4/5 hip flexor power), elevated creatine kinase (16,184 U/L), and subtle cutaneous findings (periungual erythema, nailfold microhemorrhages, Holster sign) provided critical clues to the underlying inflammatory myopathy.

Importantly, pseudoangioedema requires **immunosuppression targeting the autoimmune process** rather than antihistamines or bradykinin pathway inhibitors. This patient's marked improvement with high-dose corticosteroids, IVIG, mycophenolate mofetil, and rituximab confirms the inflammatory etiology.

### [Morning Report] What are the characteristic histopathological findings on muscle biopsy that distinguish dermatomyositis from other inflammatory myopathies such as polymyositis and immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy?

### Clinical Teaching Answer

The muscle biopsy in this case demonstrates several classic histopathological features that distinguish dermatomyositis from polymyositis and immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM).

**Perifascicular distribution of pathology** is the hallmark of dermatomyositis. As seen in this patient, necrotic, vacuolated, and regenerative myofibers were distributed in a perifascicular pattern—meaning the abnormalities concentrate at the edges of muscle fascicles rather than diffusely throughout. This contrasts with polymyositis, where inflammation and fiber invasion occur in an **endomysial** pattern, with CD8+ T cells attacking individual myofibers throughout the fascicle.

**Perimysial inflammation** rather than endomysial inflammation is characteristic of dermatomyositis. This patient showed scattered perimysial inflammation with dispersed histiocytes, reflecting the humorally-mediated pathology of dermatomyositis, as opposed to the direct T-cell mediated cytotoxicity seen in polymyositis.

**C5b-9 (membrane attack complex) deposition** in perifascicular capillaries is virtually pathognomonic for dermatomyositis. This patient's immunohistochemistry demonstrated exactly this finding, reflecting the underlying complement-mediated microangiopathy that characterizes dermatomyositis. This capillary deposition is absent in polymyositis and IMNM.

In contrast, **immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy** shows prominent fiber necrosis and regeneration but with **minimal or absent inflammatory infiltrates**—lacking both the perimysial inflammation of dermatomyositis and the endomysial CD8+ infiltrates of polymyositis. IMNM also lacks the perifascicular capillary C5b-9 deposition seen here.

In summary, the triad of perifascicular pathology, perimysial inflammation, and C5b-9 capillary deposition—as demonstrated in this patient's biopsy—reliably distinguishes dermatomyositis from other inflammatory myopathies.

### [Clinical Pearls] What clinical features and complications are associated with anti-SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis, and how does this myositis-specific antibody compare to others such as anti-Jo-1 or anti-Mi-2 in terms of clinical phenotype?

**Clinical Answer: Anti-SAE1 Antibody–Positive Dermatomyositis**

Anti-SAE1 (small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme) antibodies define a distinct dermatomyositis subtype with characteristic clinical features that differ meaningfully from other myositis-specific antibodies.

**Clinical Features of Anti-SAE1 Dermatomyositis:**

Anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis typically presents with classic cutaneous manifestations—heliotrope rash, Gottron's papules, V-neck sign, and shawl sign—often preceding clinically significant myopathy. A systematic literature review of 215 patients identified dysphagia in approximately 46% and interstitial lung disease (ILD) in 43%, both representing substantial morbidity. The malignancy association is moderate, reported in approximately 18% of cases.

This case illustrates several hallmark features: profound proximal muscle weakness with markedly elevated creatine kinase (16,184 U/L), dysphagia severe enough to require nasogastric feeding, and subtle cutaneous findings including periungual erythema and a positive "Holster sign" (erythema over the lateral thighs). Notably, the patient presented with cervical soft tissue edema masquerading as angioedema—a rare but recognized manifestation occurring in fewer than 7% of dermatomyositis cases. The edema in anti-SAE1 disease likely reflects intense inflammatory muscle involvement rather than histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema.

**Comparison with Other Myositis-Specific Antibodies:**

| Antibody | Key Clinical Features | ILD Risk | Malignancy Risk | Prognosis |
|----------|----------------------|----------|-----------------|-----------|
| **Anti-Jo-1** | Antisynthetase syndrome: myositis, mechanic's hands, arthritis, Raynaud's, fever | High (majority) | Low-Moderate | Moderate; driven by ILD severity |
| **Anti-Mi-2** | Classic dermatomyositis with prominent skin disease; myositis often milder | Low | Low | Generally favorable |
| **Anti-SAE1** | Classic cutaneous features; dysphagia common; skin may precede myopathy | Moderate (~40%) | Moderate (~18%) | Generally favorable with treatment |
| **Anti-MDA-5** | Clinically amyopathic DM; cutaneous ulcerations; palmar papules | Very high (rapidly progressive ILD) | Low-Moderate | Poor if RP-ILD develops |
| **Anti-TIF1-γ** | Extensive cutaneous disease; dysphagia | Low | High (especially adults >40) | Variable; malignancy screening critical |

Anti-Jo-1 defines antisynthetase syndrome, where ILD and arthritis dominate the clinical picture. Anti-Mi-2 identifies patients with "classic" dermatomyositis—prominent cutaneous disease with relatively mild myositis and favorable outcomes. In contrast, anti-SAE1 occupies an intermediate position: patients develop robust myositis with meaningful dysphagia and ILD risk, yet generally respond well to immunosuppression when recognized early.

**Clinical Pearl:**

The key to diagnosing anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis lies in recognizing that cutaneous manifestations may precede overt myopathy by weeks to months. In this case, subtle nailfold changes and elevated transaminases—along with the atypical distribution of neck edema—prompted creatine kinase testing, which established the diagnosis and enabled timely, aggressive immunosuppression with steroids, mycophenolate mofetil, IVIG, and rituximab.

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a patient presenting with proximal muscle weakness and a creatine kinase level exceeding 15,000 U/L, what features on history, examination, and laboratory testing help differentiate dermatomyositis from statin-induced myopathy and immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy?

**Clinical Answer**

In a patient with proximal muscle weakness and a CK exceeding 15,000 U/L, differentiating dermatomyositis from statin-induced myopathy and immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) requires careful attention to medication history, cutaneous examination, autoantibody profiling, and muscle biopsy findings.

**Medication history** is the critical first branch point. This patient had not been taking an HMG-CoA reductase inhibitor, effectively excluding self-limited statin-induced myopathy. However, IMNM can occur either with or without statin exposure—anti-HMGCR antibodies may persist and drive disease even after statin discontinuation.

**Cutaneous findings** distinguish dermatomyositis from the other entities. While classic features like Gottron's papules and heliotrope rash are well-known, their absence does not exclude dermatomyositis. This patient demonstrated subtle but diagnostic findings: periungual erythema with nailfold microhaemorrhages, avascular areas on capillaroscopy, facial erythema, and a Holster sign (rash on the lateral thigh). Neither statin-induced myopathy nor IMNM produces these cutaneous manifestations—IMNM is defined by the absence of dermatomyositis skin findings despite otherwise similar clinical presentation.

**Autoantibody testing** provides essential discrimination. This patient's positive anti-SAE1 antibody is specific for dermatomyositis and was confirmed on a comprehensive myositis antibody panel. For IMNM, anti-HMGCR antibodies (statin-associated or idiopathic) or anti-SRP antibodies are characteristic. The absence of these antibodies, coupled with a strongly positive ANA and anti-SAE1, confirmed dermatomyositis.

**Muscle biopsy** offers definitive distinction. This patient's biopsy showed necrotic and regenerative myofibers in a **perifascicular distribution** with perimysial inflammation and C5b-9 (membrane attack complex) deposition in perifascicular capillaries—a pattern pathognomonic for dermatomyositis. In contrast, IMNM biopsy demonstrates prominent myofiber necrosis with **minimal or absent inflammation**. Statin-induced myopathy (non-immune) shows non-specific myopathic changes without the inflammatory infiltrate or complement deposition seen in autoimmune myositis.

In summary, while all three conditions can produce profound CK elevations, the presence of cutaneous findings, specific myositis antibodies, and biopsy evidence of perifascicular inflammation with complement-mediated microangiopathy confirmed dermatomyositis in this case—and would have been absent in either statin-induced myopathy or IMNM.

### [Management] What factors should guide the selection of immunosuppressive agents in dermatomyositis, particularly when choosing between methotrexate and mycophenolate mofetil in patients with dysphagia or potential pulmonary involvement?

**Clinical Teaching Answer**

The selection between methotrexate and mycophenolate mofetil in dermatomyositis should be guided primarily by the presence or risk of interstitial lung disease (ILD), as well as dysphagia severity and patient-specific factors.

**Pulmonary involvement favors mycophenolate mofetil.** Methotrexate carries a well-established risk of drug-induced pneumonitis and pulmonary fibrosis, which can be difficult to distinguish from underlying ILD—a common and serious manifestation of dermatomyositis. In this case, the patient had abnormal pulmonary function tests with a restrictive pattern and CT evidence of peribronchial consolidation from aspiration, necessitating an agent with a favorable pulmonary safety profile. Mycophenolate mofetil has demonstrated efficacy in treating dermatomyositis-associated ILD and lacks the pulmonary toxicity associated with methotrexate. For this reason, the treating team appropriately avoided methotrexate, explicitly citing its association with interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis.

**Dysphagia influences route and tolerability considerations.** While both agents are available orally, methotrexate can cause mucositis and gastrointestinal upset, which may compound the nutritional challenges in patients with significant dysphagia. This patient had severe pharyngeal dysphagia requiring nasogastric tube feeding, making gastrointestinal tolerability particularly relevant. Mycophenolate was selected in part due to its tolerability profile.

**Anti-SAE1 antibody status adds prognostic context.** In SAE1-positive dermatomyositis, dysphagia and ILD occur in approximately 46% and 43% of patients, respectively. This high prevalence of pulmonary involvement should prompt clinicians to favor mycophenolate as a first-line steroid-sparing agent in this antibody subtype.

**Practical bottom line:** In dermatomyositis patients with any evidence of ILD, abnormal pulmonary function tests, or antibodies associated with pulmonary involvement (including anti-SAE1), mycophenolate mofetil is preferred over methotrexate. Methotrexate remains a reasonable option in patients without pulmonary concerns, particularly when cutaneous disease predominates.

### [Management] How should malignancy screening be approached in a patient newly diagnosed with dermatomyositis, and what clinical and serological factors are associated with higher malignancy risk?

**Clinical Teaching Answer**

In any adult patient newly diagnosed with dermatomyositis, malignancy screening is mandatory because the association between dermatomyositis and malignancy is well established, with an estimated 15–25% of adult patients having an underlying malignancy at diagnosis or developing one within 3–5 years. Screening should be comprehensive and guided by the patient's individual risk profile.

**Approach to Malignancy Screening:**
The International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) recommends age-appropriate screening combined with risk-stratified additional investigations. At minimum, this includes:
- Thorough history and physical examination
- Basic laboratory investigations (complete blood count, comprehensive metabolic panel)
- Age-appropriate cancer screening (colonoscopy for patients ≥45 years, prostate-specific antigen for males, mammography and cervical screening for females)
- Cross-sectional imaging of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis (CT or MRI)
- Consideration of positron emission tomography (PET)-CT, particularly in high-risk patients, as it can detect occult malignancies not visualized on conventional imaging

In this case, the patient underwent appropriately comprehensive screening, including CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis, a PET scan, and tumor markers, with plans for gastroscopy and colonoscopy. The PET scan was particularly valuable in excluding metabolically active disease elsewhere, given his high-risk status.

**Clinical Factors Associated with Higher Malignancy Risk:**
Several clinical features portend increased malignancy risk:
- **Older age at onset** (particularly >45 years)
- **Male sex** (as demonstrated in this case, where male sex was considered an intermediate risk factor)
- **Dysphagia** (designated a high-risk factor in this case)
- **Rapid disease onset** or severe cutaneous manifestations

**Serological Factors Associated with Higher Malignancy Risk:**
Myositis-specific autoantibodies significantly stratify malignancy risk:
- **Anti-TIF1-γ (anti-p155/140)**: Strongly associated with malignancy, particularly in adults; the risk can exceed 50%
- **Anti-NXP2 (anti-p140)**: Associated with malignancy in adults
- **Anti-SAE1**: Associated with intermediate malignancy risk; the case notes an 18% malignancy rate in literature review of SAE1-positive patients

Conversely, **anti-Mi-2** and **anti-Jo-1** antibodies are associated with lower malignancy risk.

**Risk Stratification in This Case:**
This patient was appropriately classified as high risk for malignancy based on two high-risk factors (dermatomyositis diagnosis itself and dysphagia) and two intermediate-risk factors (SAE1 antibody positivity and male sex). This risk stratification appropriately guided the intensity of his malignancy screening and the plan for ongoing surveillance.

**Ongoing Surveillance:**
Malignancy risk persists for 3–5 years following dermatomyositis diagnosis, necessitating continued vigilance. As noted in the case, the patient will continue to be monitored based on symptoms, clinical examination, regular blood tests, and relevant imaging. Repeat screening at regular intervals is warranted even if initial evaluation is negative, particularly in high-risk patients.

### [Clinical Pearls] What is the significance of elevated transaminases (ALT and AST) in a patient presenting with muscle weakness, and how can these values be distinguished from primary hepatic pathology?

### Clinical Teaching Answer

This is an excellent question that highlights a common diagnostic pitfall. In any patient presenting with weakness and elevated transaminases, **clinicians must consider a muscular source before attributing these findings to primary hepatic pathology.**

**The Pathophysiology**
Both AST and ALT are not liver-specific. ALT is present in relatively high concentration in the liver, but AST is found abundantly in skeletal muscle, cardiac muscle, kidney, brain, and red blood cells in addition to the liver. When muscle membranes are disrupted—whether from inflammatory myopathy, trauma, or strenuous exercise—these enzymes leak into the circulation.

**Key Distinguishing Features from the Case**

1. **Markedly Elevated Creatine Kinase (CK)**: This is the most critical discriminant. In this patient, the CK was strikingly elevated at 16,184 U/L, immediately signaling significant muscle injury. In primary liver disease, CK is typically normal or only minimally elevated. A disproportionately high CK relative to transaminases should prompt evaluation for myopathy.

2. **AST Predominance Over ALT**: In muscle injury, AST is typically more elevated than ALT because skeletal muscle contains proportionally more AST. This patient had an AST of 396 U/L compared to an ALT of 143 U/L—an AST:ALT ratio of approximately 2.8:1. While this ratio can occur in alcoholic hepatitis or cirrhosis, the concurrent marked CK elevation clarifies the source.

3. **Clinical Context**: This patient presented with proximal muscle weakness, myalgias, and skin findings. The transaminase elevations must be interpreted within this clinical picture. Notably, his hepatitis serologies were negative, and there was no clinical or imaging evidence of primary liver pathology.

4. **Response to Treatment**: Both the CK and transaminases normalized concurrently after immunosuppressive therapy for dermatomyositis (CK normalized to 37 U/L at 3 weeks), confirming that all elevated enzymes shared a common muscular origin.

**Clinical Bottom Line**
When evaluating elevated transaminases in the setting of muscle weakness, **always check a CK level**. An elevated CK with an AST:ALT ratio >1 strongly suggests a muscular etiology, as demonstrated in this case of dermatomyositis.
