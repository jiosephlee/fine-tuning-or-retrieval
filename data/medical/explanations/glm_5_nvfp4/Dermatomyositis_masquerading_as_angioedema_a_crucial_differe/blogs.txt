Title: Blog about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

# "Idiopathic" Angioedema? Think Again.

## The Case That Stops You in Your Tracks

A man in his 40s walks into your ED with three weeks of progressive neck swelling. Your surgical colleagues rule out Ludwig's angina. The immunology team is consulted for suspected idiopathic angioedema. But something doesn't sit right. The swelling has been progressing over weeks, not hours. No ACE inhibitors, no NSAIDs, no family history. And then someone notices subtle nailfold changes and a rash on his thigh.

A creatine kinase level comes back at 16,184 U/L.

This wasn't angioedema at all. It was dermatomyositis presenting with pseudoangioedema—a rare but critical differential that emergency physicians, ENT surgeons, and immunologists cannot afford to miss.

## When "Idiopathic" Means "Look Harder"

Let's be honest: "idiopathic angioedema" is often a diagnosis of exclusion that can become a cognitive trap. In this case, the patient's presentation had several features that should have prompted earlier skepticism about the angioedema diagnosis.

True angioedema—whether histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated—typically has a tempo measured in hours, not weeks. This patient's swelling had been progressive for three weeks. That timeline alone should make you pause. Additionally, the distribution was unusual: diffuse neck edema involving strap muscles with inflammation extending to the retropharyngeal space on CT, but without the typical facial or oropharyngeal involvement you'd expect with angioedema.

The real breakthrough came when someone took a thorough look at his hands and skin. Nailfold microhaemorrhages and avascular areas on capillaroscopy. Periungual erythema. A rash on the lateral thigh. Proximal muscle weakness rated at 4/5 in the hip flexors. These weren't incidental findings—they were the diagnostic keys hiding in plain sight.

## The CK That Changed Everything

Here's the clinical pearl that cannot be overstated: when neck swelling doesn't fit the angioedema paradigm, particularly with any suggestion of muscle involvement or cutaneous changes, check a creatine kinase level.

This patient's CK of 16,184 U/L was staggering. For context, we often think of rhabdomyolysis at CK levels above 1,000 U/L, and this was sixteen times that threshold. But here's what's fascinating—his presentation was dominated by neck swelling and dysphagia, not by the classic proximal muscle weakness we're taught to associate with inflammatory myopathies. The myalgia was described as "mild." The hip flexor weakness was documented, but it wasn't the chief complaint.

This is where diagnostic momentum becomes dangerous. The patient entered the system through an ENT pathway, was labeled as possible Ludwig's angina, then redirected to immunology for angioedema. Each handoff reinforced an assumption. It took an immunologist who recognized that the clinical picture didn't fit, combined with astute physical examination, to break that momentum.

## Anti-SAE1: A Subtype Worth Knowing

This patient tested strongly positive for anti-SAE1 antibodies—a specific myositis antibody with a distinct clinical phenotype. Anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis is characterized by prominent cutaneous features like heliotrope rash and Gottron's papules, but this case reminds us that presentations can be atypical.

What's particularly relevant for emergency physicians and hospitalists is the association data from the literature: in anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis, dysphagia occurs in 46% of cases, interstitial lung disease in 43%, and malignancy in 18%. This patient had severe pharyngeal dysphagia on video fluoroscopic swallow study with aspiration risk. He required nasogastric feeding for two months. The dysphagia wasn't a minor feature—it was a manifestation of severe disease requiring aggressive immunosuppression.

## Pseudoangioedema: How Rare Is It?

In a cohort study of 121 dermatomyositis patients, only 8 (6.6%) presented with angioedema-like swelling. That's rare enough that you might go your entire career without seeing it—but when you do, the consequences of missing it are substantial. This patient required a 21-day hospitalization with IV methylprednisolone, IVIG, rituximab, and mycophenolate mofetil. He needed nasogastric feeding and intensive rehabilitation.

Delaying immunosuppression in dermatomyositis can lead to progressive muscle involvement, respiratory failure from respiratory muscle weakness, and complications from dysphagia including aspiration pneumonitis—which this patient had evidence of on CT chest.

## Practical Takeaways for the ED and Beyond

When you're evaluating unexplained neck or facial swelling, especially when the tempo is subacute rather than acute, widen your differential. Ask about muscle weakness, even if it's not the presenting complaint. Look at the hands—nailfold changes, periungual erythema, Gottron's papules. Examine the skin for heliotrope rash, shawl sign, V-neck sign, or more subtle findings like this patient's lateral thigh rash.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- Not all neck swelling is angioedema. Subacute progression over days to weeks should prompt consideration of inflammatory mimics
- When the clinical picture doesn't fit histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema, step back and reconsider before labeling it "idiopathic"
- A creatine kinase level is inexpensive, rapidly available, and can be diagnostic—check it when you see atypical swelling with any suggestion of muscle involvement
- Cutaneous exam matters: nailfold changes, periungual erythema, and subtle rashes can be the diagnostic clues that unlock the case
- Dysphagia in dermatomyositis indicates severe disease and higher malignancy risk—these patients need aggressive immunosuppression and malignancy screening
- Anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis has high rates of dysphagia (46%), ILD (43%), and malignancy (18%)—know the phenotype
- Diagnostic momentum is real. The label this patient carried shifted from "Ludwig's angina" to "angioedema" without anyone pausing to question whether either fit. Be the clinician who pauses

## The Bottom Line

This case is a masterclass in clinical reasoning and the danger of premature diagnostic closure. A patient with three weeks of neck swelling, mild myalgia, and subtle skin findings turned out to have massive muscle inflammation requiring multi-agent immunosuppression. The diagnosis hinged on recognizing that "idiopathic angioedema" was a label that didn't fit—and having the curiosity to look for what did.

Next time you're tempted to sign off on idiopathic angioedema, remember this case. Look at the hands. Check a CK. And never let a label become a blind spot.



# Anti-SAE1: The DM Antibody You Can't Afford to Miss

## When "Angioedema" Isn't Angioedema

Picture this: a guy in his 40s rolls into your ED with three weeks of progressive neck swelling. The ENT surgeons have already scoped him and ruled out Ludwig's angina. CT neck shows diffuse soft tissue edema extending into the retropharyngeal space. No fever, no airway compromise. The working diagnosis? Idiopathic angioedema.

But something doesn't sit right.

He's got no history of ACE inhibitor use. No family history of angioedema. No triggers. And when you actually examine him—really examine him—you notice subtle nailfold changes, some facial erythema crossing the nasolabial folds, and a rash on his lateral thigh. His hip flexor strength is maybe 4/5, though he's not really complaining about weakness.

So someone orders a CK.

It comes back at **16,184 U/L**.

That's not angioedema. That's dermatomyositis.

## The Anti-SAE1 Phenotype: What Makes It Distinct

Small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme 1 (SAE1) autoantibodies define a specific subset of dermatomyositis that every emergency physician and internist should have on their radar. This isn't just academic—these patients have a distinct clinical phenotype that demands aggressive immunosuppression and systematic malignancy surveillance.

Let's talk numbers from the largest literature review to date covering 215 patients with anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis:

- **Dysphagia: 46%** — nearly half will have swallowing difficulties
- **Interstitial lung disease: 43%** — get the PFTs and CT chest
- **Malignancy association: 18%** — this is the one that keeps you up at night

The classic cutaneous triad is still there: heliotrope rash, Gottron's papules, the shawl sign and V-neck sign. But here's the thing—our patient didn't have the heliotrope or Gottron's. He had periungual erythema, a positive "Holster sign" (rash on lateral thighs), and facial erythema. The skin findings can be subtle, and you need to look for them.

## The Pseudoangioedema Trap

Subcutaneous edema as a presenting feature of dermatomyositis is rare—only about 6.6% in one cohort of 121 patients. But when it happens, it's a diagnostic landmine.

The neck swelling in dermatomyositis isn't histamine-mediated. It isn't bradykinin-mediated. It's **muscle inflammation** causing tissue edema. That means your standard angioedema treatments—antihistamines, epinephrine, C1 esterase inhibitors—won't touch it.

This patient's CT showed inflammation of the strap muscles extending into the retropharyngeal space. His FDG-PET demonstrated diffuse abnormal uptake in head and neck muscles, bilateral deltoids, forearms, chest wall—this was systemic myositis masquerading as isolated neck swelling.

The lesson? When "angioedema" doesn't fit the clinical picture—no triggers, no medication history, no family history, atypical distribution—think beyond the algorithm. Do a thorough skin exam. Check nailfolds. And for the love of Hippocrates, check a CK.

## The Malignancy Question

Anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis carries intermediate malignancy risk. Our patient had multiple risk factors stacked: dermatomyositis diagnosis, presence of dysphagia (high-risk feature), SAE1 antibody positivity (intermediate risk), and male sex (intermediate risk). That puts him in the high-risk category per International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group guidelines.

He needed the full workup: CT chest, abdomen, pelvis, FDG-PET, tumor markers. And he needs ongoing surveillance—serial imaging, scheduled endoscopy, close clinical follow-up. The malignancy may not be present at diagnosis; it can emerge later. These patients require long-term vigilance.

## Treatment Intensity Matters

This wasn't a "start some prednisone and follow up with rheumatology" situation. The intensity of immunosuppression in anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis reflects the severity of disease involvement.

Our patient received:
- IV methylprednisolone 1000 mg daily x 3 days
- Prednisone taper starting at 60 mg daily
- IVIG 2 g/kg loading, then monthly cycles
- Mycophenolate mofetil 1 g BID
- Rituximab 1000 mg x 2 doses, two weeks apart

Methotrexate was intentionally avoided due to his pulmonary involvement—remember, MTX carries a risk of interstitial lung disease and pulmonary fibrosis, which you don't want to stack on top of existing ILD concerns.

The dysphagia was severe enough to require nasogastric feeding for two months. But by three weeks post-discharge, his CK had normalized to 37 U/L, and proximal muscle strength had fully recovered. That's the power of aggressive, early treatment.

## When to Order the Myositis Panel

Let's be practical. You're not ordering a 16-antigen myositis antibody panel on every patient with vague complaints. But consider it when you have:

- Elevated CK without clear alternative explanation
- Proximal muscle weakness with or without myalgia
- Characteristic skin findings (even subtle ones)
- Dysphagia with no structural cause
- Interstitial lung disease of unclear etiology
- Unexplained inflammatory edema

The antibody panel matters because it informs prognosis and guides surveillance intensity. Anti-SAE1 tells you to watch the lungs, watch the swallow, and watch for cancer. Anti-Mi-2 has a better prognosis. Anti-MDA5 screams ILD risk. Anti-TIF1-γ screams malignancy risk. These aren't just academic distinctions—they change your clinical approach.

## Key Take-Home Points

- **Atypical "angioedema" warrants a broader differential**: When the clinical picture doesn't fit histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema, examine for cutaneous signs of dermatomyositis and check a CK level

- **Anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis is high-stakes**: Nearly half have dysphagia, nearly half have ILD, and malignancy association approaches 20%

- **Skin findings can be subtle**: This patient had no heliotrope rash or Gottron's papules—look for periungual erythema, nailfold microhaemorrhages, facial erythema, and rashes in atypical locations like the lateral thighs

- **Malignancy surveillance is mandatory**: SAE1 positivity with dysphagia puts patients in a high-risk category requiring comprehensive baseline screening and ongoing monitoring

- **Treatment should be aggressive early**: These patients often require combination immunosuppression including steroids, steroid-sparing agents, IVIG, and sometimes rituximab

## The Bottom Line

Dermatomyositis can walk through your door wearing an angioedema costume. The key to unmasking it lies in the history, the physical exam, and a low threshold to check a CK. Anti-SAE1 positivity carries specific implications for prognosis, malignancy risk, and treatment intensity. Know the phenotype, order the right tests, and get these patients to the right specialists early—their outcomes depend on it.



# The CK Test: Diagnostic Gold in Atypical Presentations

## The Case That Stopped Me in My Tracks

A man in his 40s walks into the ED with three weeks of progressive neck swelling. The ENT surgeons get involved first — this looks like Ludwig's angina or a deep neck space infection. CT neck shows diffuse oedema involving strap muscles and inflammation extending to the retropharyngeal space. But here's the thing: he's afebrile, not tachypnoeic, saturating well, and his white cell count is normal. The CRP is 55.6 mg/L — elevated, but not the septic inflammatory response you'd expect from a serious deep neck infection.

So then the working diagnosis shifts: maybe this is angioedema? But something doesn't fit. The swelling has been progressing over three weeks — that's not typical for either histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema. No ACE inhibitors, no family history, no previous episodes. A careful physical exam reveals subtle but crucial clues: periungual erythema, nailfold microhaemorrhages on capillaroscopy, a rash on the lateral thigh, and mildly reduced hip flexor power (4/5).

Then someone does something simple but transformative: they order a creatine kinase level.

The result comes back at **16,184 U/L**.

That single number reframes the entire case. This isn't angioedema. This isn't a deep neck space infection. This is dermatomyositis with severe muscle involvement, presenting with pseudoangioedema of the neck.

## When CK Changes Everything

Here's what makes this case so instructive: the diagnosis was hiding in plain sight until someone thought to check a CK. The patient had proximal muscle weakness, but it was mild enough that it wasn't the dominant complaint. The neck swelling was the dramatic, visible problem that captured clinical attention. Without the CK, this patient could have undergone extensive and unnecessary workup for idiopathic angioedema — or worse, received treatments that would have done nothing for his underlying disease.

The CK level in this case was astronomical. Normal CK is typically less than 200 U/L depending on the lab and patient factors. A CK of 16,184 U/L represents massive muscle breakdown. For context, we often see CK elevations in the thousands with rhabdomyolysis from crush injuries, severe exertion, or drug toxicity. Seeing a CK this high in a patient who walked in complaining mainly of neck swelling should immediately redirect your differential toward inflammatory myopathy.

## The "Liver Function Test" Trap

Here's a pearl that every emergency physician and internist should internalise: AST and ALT aren't liver-specific. Both enzymes are found in skeletal muscle, and in the setting of significant myositis or rhabdomyolysis, they can be markedly elevated from muscle breakdown alone.

In this case, the patient had an ALT of 143 U/L and an AST of 396 U/L. The AST:ALT ratio is notably >1, which is exactly what you'd expect when the source is muscular rather than hepatic. LDH was also elevated at 692 U/L. In a patient with unexplained transaminase elevation, especially with an AST predominance and particularly when there are any musculoskeletal symptoms, checking a CK should be reflexive.

The term "liver function tests" is a misnomer that can mislead clinicians. These are biomarkers of cellular injury that happen to be abundant in hepatocytes — but they're abundant in myocytes too. Don't assume transaminase elevation equals liver disease until you've considered muscle.

## When to Pull the Trigger on a CK in the ED

The question becomes: when should you order a CK in the acute setting? You can't order it on everyone with vague complaints. But this case suggests several clinical scenarios where a CK should be on your differential diagnosis checklist:

Any patient with unexplained swelling, particularly if it's not following the typical pattern of angioedema. Angioedema is usually rapid in onset, episodic, and affects the face, lips, tongue, and oropharynx. Swelling that is progressive over weeks, confined to unusual locations like the neck alone, or associated with any weakness should raise suspicion.

Patients with weakness, even if it seems mild or is attributed to deconditioning. This patient had 4/5 hip flexor strength — not profound weakness, but real and detectable. Proximal weakness is the classic pattern in inflammatory myopathies, and patients may compensate or minimise symptoms until directly tested.

Unexplained transaminase elevation, particularly with AST greater than ALT. If you're working up elevated LFTs and the patient has any musculoskeletal complaints, checking a CK is essential. You might diagnose a myopathy days or weeks earlier.

The presence of any cutaneous findings suggestive of dermatomyositis, even if subtle. This patient didn't have the classic heliotrope rash or Gottron's papules, but he did have periungual erythema, nailfold changes, and a rash on his thigh. Cutaneous findings in dermatomyositis can be heterogeneous, and the classic textbook findings aren't always present.

## What Constitutes a Concerning CK Elevation

Not every elevated CK represents inflammatory myopathy. CK can be mildly elevated (up to perhaps 500-1000 U/L) after strenuous exercise, particularly in patients who are untrained. African American men may have baseline CK levels higher than the general population. Hypothyroidism, certain medications (particularly statins), and trauma can all elevate CK.

But a CK in the thousands or tens of thousands in a patient who hasn't had major trauma or extreme exertion is pathological until proven otherwise. Inflammatory myopathies like dermatomyositis and polymyositis typically present with CK elevations in the range of 1000-10,000+ U/L, though the level doesn't perfectly correlate with disease severity. What matters is the clinical context: if the CK is significantly elevated and there are any accompanying symptoms of weakness, skin changes, dysphagia, or unexplained swelling, you need to think about myositis.

## The Bigger Picture: Pseudoangioedema in Dermatomyositis

This case highlights a phenomenon that's rare but crucial to recognise: pseudoangioedema as a presentation of dermatomyositis. The literature suggests that only about 6-7% of dermatomyositis patients present with angioedema-like swelling. It's caused by subcutaneous tissue oedema in the setting of muscle inflammation — not by histamine or bradykinin, which means antihistamines, epinephrine, and C1 esterase inhibitors won't treat it.

For emergency physicians, this is essential knowledge. If you're seeing a patient with angioedema that doesn't respond to standard treatments, isn't episodic, has been progressing over days to weeks, or has atypical distribution, broaden your differential. Ask about weakness. Examine the nails and skin. Check a CK.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- A single CK level can transform a diagnostic trajectory. In this case, a CK of 16,184 U/L reframed the diagnosis from idiopathic angioedema to dermatomyositis.
- AST and ALT are not liver-specific. In myositis, elevated transaminases often come from muscle, typically with AST > ALT.
- Pseudoangioedema is a rare but important presentation of dermatomyositis. Swelling that is progressive, atypical in distribution, or unresponsive to standard angioedema treatments should prompt consideration of inflammatory myopathy.
- When in doubt, check a CK. It's inexpensive, rapidly available, and can prevent diagnostic delays in patients with atypical presentations of myositis.



# Dysphagia in Myositis: The Airway Complication That Defines Prognosis

## The Case That Stops You in Your Tracks

A man in his 40s walks into your ED with three weeks of progressive neck swelling. The ENT surgeons get called first — this looks like Ludwig's angina, right? The CT shows diffuse edema involving the strap muscles and inflammation extending to the retropharyngeal space. But there's no fever, no trismus, no dental infection to explain it. Flexible nasendoscopy is essentially normal. So what are we missing?

The clue was there all along: proximal muscle weakness in his hip flexors. When someone finally thought to check a creatine kinase, it came back at **16,184 U/L**. This wasn't angioedema. This wasn't a deep neck space infection. This was dermatomyositis presenting with pseudoangioedema — subcutaneous edema from intense muscle inflammation — and the dysphagia that followed would define his hospital course and his prognosis.

## Why Dysphagia Matters More Than You Think

Here's the uncomfortable truth about inflammatory myositis: dysphagia isn't just a bothersome symptom. It's a prognostic siren. In dermatomyositis, dysphagia correlates with more severe disease, increased risk of aspiration pneumonitis, prolonged hospital stays, and — critically — higher malignancy risk. In the SAE1 antibody subtype specifically, dysphagia occurs in roughly 46% of patients, reflecting the severe muscle involvement typical of this entity.

Our patient had the classic mechanism at play: pharyngeal muscle inflammation leading to severe dysphagia. His video fluoroscopic swallow study showed severe pyriform fossae residue and post-swallow penetration of fluids to the vocal cords. He wasn't just having trouble swallowing — he was aspirating. His CT chest showed patchy peribronchial consolidation, likely aspiration pneumonitis. He needed a nasogastric tube for two months. This is not unusual. This is the natural history of untreated myositis affecting the swallowing apparatus.

## The Mechanism: More Than Just "Weak Muscles"

The dysphagia in dermatomyositis stems from inflammation affecting the striated muscles of the pharynx and upper esophagus. The cricopharyngeal muscle, in particular, can become dysmotile, leading to incomplete relaxation and functional obstruction. But the problem extends beyond mechanical weakness. The muscle biopsy from our patient showed necrotic, vacuolated, degenerate fibers in a perifascicular distribution with C5b-9 deposition in endomysial capillaries — classic dermatomyositis pathology. This is complement-mediated microangiopathy affecting muscle fibers.

When you combine pharyngeal weakness with impaired cricopharyngeal relaxation, you get the perfect storm: stasis in the pyriform sinuses, overflow into the airway, and recurrent micro-aspiration. The patient may not even cough — the protective reflexes themselves can be blunted. Silent aspiration is the real danger.

## When to Suspect Myositis-Induced Dysphagia

The textbook presentations — heliotrope rash, Gottron's papules, the classic shawl sign — they're helpful when present. But our patient had none of these. He had subtle nailfold changes, periungual erythema, a rash on his lateral thigh (a "Holster sign"), and facial erythema. The neck swelling was the red herring that sent him down the wrong diagnostic path initially.

What should have triggered earlier suspicion? The combination of subacute proximal weakness with any degree of swallowing difficulty should prompt a CK level. Period. The presence of dysphagia with elevated inflammatory markers and no clear infectious source should make you think myositis, especially if there are any cutaneous changes — even subtle ones. Nailfold capillaroscopy showing microhemorrhages and avascular areas is highly specific for dermatomyositis and was present in this case.

## Aggressive Immunosuppression: Why We Don't Wait

This patient received the full arsenal: three days of IV methylprednisolone at 1000 mg daily, followed by oral prednisone taper, IVIG at 2 g/kg loading then monthly cycles, mycophenolate mofetil, and two doses of rituximab. That's quadruple therapy. And it worked — his CK normalized within three weeks, proximal strength recovered, and dysphagia resolved sufficiently to remove the NG tube after two months.

Why such aggressive upfront treatment? Because dysphagia in dermatomyositis responds poorly to inadequate immunosuppression, and the complications of undertreatment — aspiration, malnutrition, prolonged hospitalization, even death — are devastating. IVIG has particular utility in myositis-associated dysphagia, with evidence supporting its role in accelerating recovery of swallowing function. Methotrexate was avoided in this case due to concerns about pulmonary toxicity — an important consideration when baseline lung function may already be compromised by interstitial lung disease or aspiration.

## Key Take-Home Points

- **Dysphagia in dermatomyositis is a prognostic marker, not just a symptom** — it correlates with severe disease, aspiration risk, and malignancy association
- **The mechanism involves pharyngeal muscle inflammation and cricopharyngeal dysfunction**, leading to pyriform sinus stasis and aspiration
- **Silent aspiration is common** — don't assume lack of coughing means safe swallowing; formal swallow studies are essential
- **Aggressive multi-agent immunosuppression is warranted** — these patients often need high-dose steroids, IVIG, and steroid-sparing agents like mycophenolate or rituximab
- **Always check a CK in unexplained neck swelling with any weakness or skin changes** — pseudoangioedema from myositis is rare but treatable, and the diagnosis is often delayed
- **Malignancy screening is mandatory in dermatomyositis with dysphagia** — this combination places patients in a high-risk category requiring comprehensive workup and surveillance

## The Bottom Line

When dysphagia complicates myositis, it transforms the clinical trajectory. These patients need early speech pathology involvement, aggressive immunosuppression, careful airway protection, and patience — recovery of swallowing function can take months. But as this case demonstrates, with appropriate treatment, even severe dysphagia can resolve. The key is recognizing it early and treating it seriously. Because in dermatomyositis, the ability to swallow isn't just a quality-of-life issue — it's a survival issue.



# Dermatomyositis and Cancer: Who Needs What Screening?

We all know the textbook fact: dermatomyositis carries an association with malignancy. But here's what makes this case particularly instructive—when you actually sit down and risk-stratify a patient using current guidelines, the workup becomes much more targeted and defensible. This case of anti-SAE1 dermatomyositis presenting with dramatic neck swelling gives us a perfect framework to walk through the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) cancer screening recommendations.

## The Case That Stops You in Your Tracks

A previously well man in his 40s presents with three weeks of progressive neck swelling and proximal muscle weakness. Initial concern was Ludwig's angina—reasonable given the clinical picture. CT neck showed diffuse neck edema involving strap muscles with inflammation extending to the retropharyngeal space. But here's the kicker: no fever, normal white count, and a CRP of 55.6 mg/L that didn't fit a fulminant infection. ENT found no abscess. The immunology consult for "idiopathic angioedema" noticed subtle but critical findings—periungual erythema, nailfold microhaemorrhages on capillaroscopy, a rash on the lateral thigh, and 4/5 hip flexor power.

The creatine kinase came back at 16,184 U/L. This wasn't angioedema. This was dermatomyositis with significant myositis causing pseudoangioedema of the neck—a rare presentation seen in only about 6-7% of dermatomyositis patients.

## Risk Stratification: The IMACS Framework

Here's where the teaching value really shines. The IMACS guidelines give us a structured approach to malignancy risk stratification in idiopathic inflammatory myopathy, and this patient hit multiple concerning boxes.

He had two high-risk factors: dermatomyositis diagnosis itself, and dysphagia. Dysphagia in dermatomyositis isn't just a morbidity issue—it's a red flag for malignancy risk. He also had two intermediate risk factors: male sex and SAE1 antibody positivity. That's high risk, full stop.

The antibody profile matters considerably. Anti-TIF1-γ antibodies carry the strongest malignancy association, particularly in juvenile dermatomyositis and adult patients. Anti-NXP2 and anti-SAE1 antibodies also carry elevated malignancy risk, though data for SAE1 specifically shows malignancy in about 18% of cases. Anti-Mi-2 antibodies, interestingly, are associated with better cancer risk profiles and generally better outcomes. Anti-MDA5 antibodies are more associated with rapidly progressive interstitial lung disease and less so with malignancy.

## What Does High-Risk Screening Actually Look Like?

For a high-risk patient like this, the baseline workup should be comprehensive. He underwent CT chest, abdomen, and pelvis, which showed pulmonary nodules and peribronchial consolidation consistent with aspiration but no malignancy. Tumor markers including CEA, CA19-9, CA-125, and PSA were normal. A whole-body FDG-PET scan demonstrated diffuse muscle inflammation but no metabolically active lesions suggesting occult malignancy.

But imaging alone isn't sufficient. The plan includes gastroscopy and colonoscopy to exclude occult gastrointestinal malignancy—particularly important given the dysphagia, which raises concern for upper GI pathology. In female patients, you'd add mammography and pelvic ultrasound; in males, testicular examination and PSA are essential.

The question that often comes up: is PET-CT sufficient, or do you still need organ-specific imaging? Current evidence suggests PET-CT is sensitive but not perfect, and in high-risk patients, complementing with dedicated CT imaging and endoscopy provides the most thorough evaluation. The IMACS approach favors comprehensive baseline screening with interval surveillance based on clinical trajectory.

## Duration of Surveillance: How Long Is Long Enough?

This is where guidelines get a bit murkier, and clinical judgment matters. The highest malignancy risk is in the first year after diagnosis, particularly the first 3-6 months. But surveillance shouldn't stop there. For high-risk patients, ongoing monitoring with serial imaging, regular clinical review, and age-appropriate cancer screening is recommended for at least 3-5 years. Some experts argue for indefinite surveillance given case reports of malignancy presenting years after dermatomyositis diagnosis.

In this case, the plan is symptom-guided monitoring with regular blood tests and serial CT chest imaging. That's reasonable—ongoing vigilance without blanket investigations that could lead to false positives and unnecessary interventions. The key is maintaining a high index of suspicion and acting promptly on any new concerning symptoms.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Risk stratify using IMACS criteria**: Dermatomyositis and dysphagia are high-risk factors; male sex and certain antibodies (SAE1, TIF1-γ, NXP2) are intermediate risk. Multiple risk factors = comprehensive workup.
- **Antibody type guides malignancy risk**: Anti-TIF1-γ carries the highest risk; anti-Mi-2 carries lower risk. Know your myositis antibody panels.
- **Baseline high-risk screening**: CT chest/abdomen/pelvis, tumor markers, age-appropriate cancer screening, PET-CT in select cases, and endoscopy for patients with dysphagia or GI symptoms.
- **Don't forget the basics**: Physical exam findings matter. This case was solved because someone looked at the nails, the skin, and tested a CK level when the angioedema story didn't fit.
- **Surveillance is ongoing**: Highest risk is year one, but maintain vigilance for at least 3-5 years in high-risk patients.

Dermatomyositis presenting as angioedema is rare, but the malignancy association is not. Having a structured approach to cancer screening ensures you don't miss the boat on this critical aspect of care.
