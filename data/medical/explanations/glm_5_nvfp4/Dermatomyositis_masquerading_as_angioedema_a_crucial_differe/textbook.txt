Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Assessment

The patient, a previously well male in his 40s, presented to the emergency department with a three-week history of progressive neck swelling accompanied by proximal muscle weakness in his hip flexors and mild myalgia. He had no significant past medical history and was a lifelong non-smoker. Notably, he had not been taking a 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitor (statin), which is relevant as statins are a well-recognized cause of drug-induced myopathy and would have complicated the diagnostic picture. The subacute nature of his symptoms, developing over weeks rather than hours to days, was an early clue that the underlying etiology might differ from typical infectious or allergic causes.

The initial clinical impression centered on Ludwig's angina, a rapidly progressing, potentially life-threatening cellulitis of the submandibular space. This diagnosis was considered because neck swelling, particularly when accompanied by systemic inflammatory markers, often raises concern for deep neck space infections. His C-reactive protein was elevated at 55.6 mg/L, though his white cell count was normal at 9.1×10⁹/L and he was afebrile. A computed tomography (CT) scan of the neck demonstrated diffuse oedema involving the strap muscles with inflammation extending to the retropharyngeal space. However, no abscess or fluid collection was identified to account for an infectious process.

After transfer to a quaternary center for ear, nose, and throat (ENT) surgery evaluation, flexible nasendoscopy was performed. This examination revealed no significant anatomical abnormalities of the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid space, effectively excluding upper airway obstruction. However, increased secretions and coughing during the examination were noted—subtle findings that in retrospect suggested pharyngeal dysfunction and dysphagia, which are recognized complications of dermatomyositis affecting the striated muscles of the pharynx.

With structural and infectious causes effectively excluded, the diagnostic focus shifted toward angioedema. The immunology consultation was prompted by the unexplained neck swelling without a clear structural or infective cause. Angioedema is characterized by episodic, self-limited swelling of the deeper dermis and subcutaneous tissues, most commonly mediated by either histamine (as in allergic reactions) or bradykinin (as in hereditary or acquired angioedema). A careful history was obtained: the patient denied use of angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs—all medications associated with drug-induced angioedema. He also reported no personal or family history of angioedema. These negative findings made hereditary or acquired angioedema unlikely, as did the clinical observation that his swelling was persistent rather than episodic.

The consulting immunologist noted generalized erythema of the face, including involvement of the nasolabial folds, and an erythematous, swollen, non-tender neck. Closer inspection revealed subtle nailfold changes suggestive of periungual erythema, which were further evaluated by nailfold capillaroscopy demonstrating microhaemorrhages and avascular areas. These capillaroscopic findings are highly characteristic of dermatomyositis and other connective tissue diseases, reflecting the microvascular pathology central to these conditions. Additionally, a rash was present on the right lateral thigh (later identified as a positive Holster sign), and neurological examination revealed reduced hip flexor power graded at 4/5 on the Medical Research Council scale.

The combination of cutaneous findings, proximal muscle weakness, and the atypical nature of the neck swelling prompted measurement of creatine kinase (CK), a sensitive marker for muscle injury. The CK level returned markedly elevated at 16,184 U/L (normal range typically <200 U/L), providing objective evidence of significant muscle inflammation and effectively redirecting the diagnosis toward an inflammatory myopathy. Concurrent transaminases were elevated (alanine aminotransferase 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase 396 U/L), along with lactate dehydrogenase at 692 U/L. In the context of myositis, elevated transaminases often reflect muscle rather than hepatic injury, as these enzymes are released from damaged myofibers. The absence of classic dermatomyositis skin findings such as Gottron's papules and heliotrope eruption did not exclude the diagnosis, as cutaneous manifestations can be subtle or develop later in the disease course.

## Differential Diagnosis of Neck Swelling

Neck swelling presents a diagnostic challenge that requires systematic consideration of infectious, inflammatory, vascular, and allergic etiologies. In this case, the patient's initial presentation with subacute neck swelling and an elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) of 55.6 mg/L prompted evaluation for several diagnostic categories, with Ludwig's angina and angioedema emerging as the leading initial considerations.

**Infectious causes** represent the most urgent diagnostic category for neck swelling. Ludwig's angina, a rapidly progressive cellulitis of the submandibular space, was the initial working diagnosis given the neck swelling and elevated inflammatory markers. Deep neck space infections can spread along fascial planes to involve the retropharyngeal and parapharyngeal spaces, potentially compromising the airway. Cellulitis and erysipelas of the neck are typically accompanied by fever, warmth, and tenderness. Bacterial sialadenitis, involving the submandibular or parotid glands, would produce swelling localized to the gland with associated pain and purulent discharge from the salivary duct. In this patient, the absence of fever, the afebrile presentation, and the lack of focal tenderness argued against an infectious etiology. The CT scan demonstrated diffuse neck edema involving the strap muscles with inflammation extending to the retropharyngeal space but no abscess formation, and flexible nasendoscopy revealed no anatomical abnormalities. These findings effectively excluded an infectious process.

**Angioedema** was the next primary consideration after infectious causes were excluded. Angioedema is classified into three main categories based on pathophysiology: histaminergic (allergic or IgE-mediated), bradykinin-mediated, and non-mediator-driven angioedema. Histaminergic angioedema, the most common form, results from mast cell degranulation and histamine release, typically occurring within minutes to hours after exposure to an allergen such as foods, medications, or insect stings. It is often accompanied by urticaria, pruritus, and other features of allergic reactions. Bradykinin-mediated angioedema includes hereditary angioedema (HAE) and acquired angioedema (AAE). Hereditary angioedema, caused by mutations in the C1 esterase inhibitor gene (type I) or by dysfunctional C1 esterase inhibitor (type II), typically presents with recurrent episodes of swelling affecting the face, extremities, gastrointestinal tract, and larynx, often without urticaria. Acquired angioedema results from consumption or destruction of C1 esterase inhibitor, often associated with lymphoproliferative disorders or autoimmune conditions.

The laboratory workup for angioedema focuses on the complement cascade. C4 levels are typically reduced during and between attacks in both hereditary and acquired angioedema. C1 esterase inhibitor functional assay and quantitative levels distinguish between HAE types I and II, while C1q levels are reduced in acquired angioedema but normal in hereditary forms. In this patient, C3 and C4 levels were normal, and C1 esterase function and quantification were within normal limits, effectively excluding bradykinin-mediated angioedema.

Several features made this patient's presentation atypical for angioedema of any etiology. He had no personal or family history of angioedema, no exposure to medications associated with angioedema (including angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs), and no accompanying urticaria or pruritus. The subacute progression over three weeks was inconsistent with the episodic nature typical of angioedema. Furthermore, the distribution of swelling—involving the strap muscles and extending to the retropharyngeal space on imaging—suggested tissue infiltration rather than the superficial dermal and subcutaneous swelling characteristic of angioedema.

**Other diagnostic considerations** included superior vena cava syndrome, which produces neck and facial swelling due to venous obstruction, typically accompanied by dilated collateral veins and signs of venous congestion. Orofacial granulomatosis, a condition characterized by persistent swelling of the lips and face, was considered but would not explain the systemic features observed. Drug reactions, including drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS), were unlikely given no medication exposure. Acute contact dermatitis would produce more localized, pruritic changes rather than the diffuse, non-tender swelling seen here.

**Red flags** in this case prompted expansion of the differential beyond angioedema and infection. The elevated CRP indicated an inflammatory process, while the presence of skin changes—including generalized erythema of the face, periungual erythema, and a rash on the right lateral thigh—suggested a systemic condition. Nailfold microhaemorrhages and avascular areas on capillaroscopy indicated microvascular pathology. Perhaps most importantly, the identification of reduced hip flexor power (4/5) pointed toward muscle involvement.

The **creatine kinase level** became the pivotal investigation that redirected the diagnostic approach. Ordered due to the atypical nature of the neck edema and the presence of skin, nail, and muscle findings, the result of 16,184 U/L was strikingly elevated, confirming significant muscle injury. This finding, combined with the elevated transaminases (ALT 143 U/L, AST 396 U/L) and LDH (692 U/L), indicated active myositis. Inflammatory myopathies, including dermatomyositis, immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy, and inclusion body myositis, were now the primary diagnostic considerations. The subsequent positive anti-SAE1 antibody and characteristic muscle biopsy confirmed dermatomyositis as the diagnosis.

This case illustrates the phenomenon of **pseudoangioedema**—angioedema-like swelling caused by mechanisms other than histamine or bradykinin release. In dermatomyositis, subcutaneous tissue edema arises from underlying muscle inflammation rather than vascular permeability changes mediated by allergic or bradykinin pathways. Recognition of this entity is critical, as treatment with antihistamines or bradykinin pathway inhibitors is ineffective, and delay in immunosuppressive therapy risks disease progression and multiorgan damage.

## Diagnostic Investigations and Confirmation

The diagnostic workup for this patient unfolded in a stepwise fashion, beginning with tests to exclude more common conditions and culminating in a definitive diagnosis of anti-SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis. Understanding how each investigation contributed to the diagnosis provides a framework for evaluating patients with suspected inflammatory myopathy.

Laboratory investigations played a pivotal role early in the diagnostic process. The creatine kinase (CK) level was markedly elevated at 16,184 U/L—a finding that immediately shifted the differential diagnosis away from angioedema and toward a primary muscle disorder. In dermatomyositis and other inflammatory myopathies, CK is released from damaged muscle fibers, and levels often correlate with disease activity. A CK elevation of this magnitude indicates extensive active muscle injury. Importantly, the patient's transaminases were also elevated, with an alanine aminotransferase (ALT) of 143 U/L and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) of 396 U/L. In patients without liver disease, elevated transaminases frequently originate from muscle rather than hepatic tissue, as both enzymes are present in skeletal muscle. The AST elevation exceeding the ALT elevation is consistent with a muscular source, given that AST is more abundant in muscle than ALT. Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) was elevated at 692 U/L, another non-specific marker of tissue injury that, in this context, reflected ongoing muscle inflammation and necrosis.

Serological testing provided the next critical piece of diagnostic evidence. The anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) was positive at a titer of 1:1280 with a speckled pattern, indicating the presence of autoantibodies against nuclear or cytoplasmic antigens. While ANA is sensitive for autoimmune disease, it lacks specificity. The definitive serological finding came from a comprehensive myositis antibody panel, which revealed strongly positive anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies with a signal intensity of 173 (normal range <35). Anti-SAE1 antibodies are a myositis-specific antibody associated with dermatomyositis, particularly in adults. Patients with anti-SAE1 antibodies typically present with characteristic cutaneous manifestations and may develop dysphagia and interstitial lung disease. The presence of this antibody, combined with clinical features, established the specific subtype of dermatomyositis. Notably, antibodies against other myositis antigens, including anti-Jo-1, anti-Mi-2, and anti-TIF1-γ, were negative, narrowing the diagnostic focus.

Complement studies were performed to exclude hereditary or acquired angioedema, which had been the initial clinical suspicion. C3 and C4 levels were normal, and C1 esterase inhibitor function and quantification were within normal ranges. These findings effectively ruled out angioedema due to C1 esterase inhibitor deficiency or dysfunction, redirecting attention toward an inflammatory myopathy.

Imaging studies provided objective evidence of muscle involvement and assessed disease extent. The initial CT of the neck, performed to evaluate suspected deep neck space infection, revealed diffuse edema involving the strap muscles with inflammation extending to the retropharyngeal space. This finding was instrumental in recognizing that the neck swelling represented muscle inflammation rather than subcutaneous angioedema. MRI of the thighs demonstrated changes consistent with active myositis, including involvement of the obturator internus and externus muscles, hip adductors, gracilis muscles, and distal iliopsoas muscles. MRI is highly sensitive for detecting muscle inflammation, showing edema on T2-weighted images with fat suppression. The FDG-PET scan provided a whole-body assessment, demonstrating diffuse abnormal FDG accumulation within multiple muscle groups—most prominently in the head and neck, bilateral deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdomen, and pelvis. This imaging modality also served to exclude occult malignancy, a critical consideration given the association between dermatomyositis and malignancy.

CT of the chest revealed multiple non-cavitating pulmonary nodules and patchy areas of peribronchial consolidation, likely representing aspiration pneumonitis secondary to dysphagia. No radiological evidence of pulmonary fibrosis was present. Pulmonary function tests demonstrated an extrathoracic restrictive pattern, consistent with upper airway or pharyngeal involvement rather than intrinsic lung disease.

The muscle biopsy of the thigh provided definitive histopathological confirmation. The biopsy showed foci of necrotic, vacuolated, degenerate, and regenerative myofibers in a perifascicular distribution, along with scattered perimysial inflammation. The perifascicular distribution of muscle fiber injury is characteristic of dermatomyositis and distinguishes it from other inflammatory myopathies such as polymyositis, which typically demonstrates endomysial inflammation. Immunohistochemistry revealed deposition of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9) in perifascicular endomysial capillaries, a hallmark finding in dermatomyositis. This pattern of complement deposition reflects the pathogenic role of humoral immunity and complement-mediated microvascular injury in dermatomyositis, distinguishing it from the T-cell-mediated muscle fiber damage seen in polymyositis and inclusion body myositis.

Functional assessments quantified the clinical impact of the disease. A video fluoroscopic swallow study demonstrated severe pharyngeal dysphagia with pyriform fossae residue and post-swallow penetration of fluids to the vocal cords, confirming significant involvement of the swallowing musculature. This finding had immediate clinical implications, necessitating nasogastric tube feeding to prevent aspiration.

Malignancy screening was undertaken given the established association between dermatomyositis and malignancy. The patient had multiple risk factors: a diagnosis of dermatomyositis itself, the presence of dysphagia, anti-SAE1 antibody positivity, and male sex. CT of the abdomen and pelvis showed no evidence of malignancy, and tumor markers including CEA, CA19-9, CA-125, and PSA were normal. The FDG-PET scan demonstrated no metabolically active disease outside the muscles. Plans were made for gastroscopy and colonoscopy as part of comprehensive malignancy surveillance, in accordance with guideline recommendations for patients at high risk.

## Understanding Dermatomyositis and Anti-SAE1 Antibodies

Dermatomyositis is an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy characterized by immune-mediated damage to skin and skeletal muscle, though it may also involve the gastrointestinal tract, joints, lungs, and heart. The estimated incidence is approximately 10 cases per million population, with a bimodal age distribution: one peak occurring in children aged 5–14 years and another in adults aged 45–64 years. This case involves an adult male in his 40s, fitting the second peak. The disease typically presents with symmetric proximal muscle weakness, elevated muscle enzymes, and characteristic cutaneous findings. However, as this case illustrates, the presentation can be atypical, leading to diagnostic delays.

The classic cutaneous manifestations of dermatomyositis are critical diagnostic clues that the astute clinician must actively seek. Gottron's papules—violaceous, scaly papules overlying the dorsal metacarpophalangeal and interphalangeal joints—are considered pathognomonic. The heliotrope eruption is a distinctive violaceous discoloration of the periorbital skin, often with associated edema. Other characteristic findings include the shawl sign (erythema over the shoulders, upper back, and posterior neck) and the V-neck sign (erythema in a V-shaped distribution over the anterior chest). Periungual telangiectasias and nailfold microhaemorrhages, as detected by capillaroscopy in this patient, reflect microvascular involvement. Notably, this patient lacked Gottron's papules and heliotrope eruption, demonstrating that their absence does not exclude the diagnosis. The examining physician instead identified periungual erythema, a rash on the lateral thigh (Holster sign), and nailfold microhaemorrhages—subtle findings that proved diagnostically pivotal.

The identification of anti-SAE1 antibodies was central to confirming the diagnosis in this case. These autoantibodies target the small ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) activating enzyme, a protein involved in post-translational modification through a process termed "sumoylation." This pathway regulates numerous cellular processes, including transcription, DNA repair, and stress responses. The exact mechanism by which antibodies against SAE1 cause disease remains under investigation, but the clinical phenotype is increasingly well-characterized. SAE1-positive dermatomyositis typically presents with prominent cutaneous features—heliotrope rash, Gottron's papules, V-neck sign, and shawl sign—along with significant systemic involvement. Large cohort studies report dysphagia in approximately 46% of cases and interstitial lung disease in approximately 43%, making these complications common rather than exceptional. Malignancy association occurs in approximately 18% of patients, necessitating vigilant cancer screening.

This case highlights an uncommon but clinically important phenomenon: pseudoangioedema in dermatomyositis. True angioedema results from either histaminergic mechanisms (as in allergic reactions) or bradykinin-mediated pathways (as in hereditary or acquired C1 esterase inhibitor deficiency). In dermatomyositis, subcutaneous edema arises through a fundamentally different mechanism—inflammation-driven increased vascular permeability from underlying myositis and microvascular injury. In a cohort study of 121 dermatomyositis patients, only 8 (6.61%) exhibited angioedema-like swelling, confirming its rarity. When present, facial or neck swelling may mimic primary otolaryngologic disorders or idiopathic angioedema, as initially suspected in this patient. The "angioedema" in dermatomyositis does not respond to antihistamines, antihistamine blockade, or C1 esterase inhibitor replacement—knowledge essential for the immunologist or emergency physician evaluating unexplained neck swelling.

The patient's profile warranted classification as high risk for malignancy according to the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group (IMACS) guidelines. He possessed two high-risk factors: a dermatomyositis diagnosis itself and the presence of dysphagia. Additionally, he had two intermediate-risk factors: SAE1 antibody positivity and male sex. This risk stratification guided comprehensive malignancy screening, including CT imaging, PET scan, tumor markers, and planned endoscopic evaluation. Understanding this risk stratification framework is essential for managing dermatomyositis patients, as cancer screening recommendations vary substantially based on individual risk profiles.

## Treatment Strategy and Management Decisions

The management of dermatomyositis requires prompt and aggressive immunosuppression to halt disease progression and prevent irreversible organ damage. This patient's presentation warranted particularly urgent intervention given the significant involvement of cervical and airway musculature, which posed an immediate threat to airway integrity. The treatment strategy was multi-layered, incorporating acute induction therapy, long-term maintenance immunosuppression, supportive care, and vigilant prophylaxis against treatment-related complications.

**Acute Induction Therapy**

High-dose intravenous methylprednisolone at 1000 mg daily for three days was initiated as pulse therapy, a standard approach for severe idiopathic inflammatory myopathies with critical organ involvement. The rationale for this aggressive induction stems from the need to rapidly suppress the intense inflammatory response driving both the muscle necrosis and the subcutaneous edema. In this case, the degree of muscle involvement was substantial, evidenced by the markedly elevated creatine kinase of 16,184 U/L and the diffuse inflammatory changes on imaging. Intravenous administration ensures rapid bioavailability and may provide more consistent immunosuppression than oral preparations in patients with dysphagia and potential gastrointestinal involvement.

Following pulse therapy, the patient was transitioned to oral prednisone at 60 mg daily with a structured tapering regimen, reducing by 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. This tapering approach balances the need for continued immunosuppression during the vulnerable early treatment period against the well-documented complications of prolonged high-dose corticosteroid therapy, including osteoporosis, hyperglycemia, and adrenal suppression.

**Selection of Steroid-Sparing Agents**

Mycophenolate mofetil was selected as the primary steroid-sparing agent at a dose of 1 gram twice daily. This decision was informed by multiple factors. Mycophenolate mofetil inhibits inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase, selectively blocking lymphocyte proliferation and offering a favorable side effect profile compared to alternative agents. Importantly, the patient had already demonstrated abnormal pulmonary function tests with an extrathoracic restrictive pattern, which influenced medication selection. Methotrexate, a commonly used alternative, was specifically avoided due to its association with drug-induced pneumonitis and potential to exacerbate or accelerate pulmonary fibrosis—a critical consideration given the patient's respiratory involvement from aspiration and the inherent risk of interstitial lung disease in dermatomyositis. Azathioprine and tacrolimus were not pursued as mycophenolate was well-tolerated, avoiding unnecessary medication changes in an already complex regimen.

**Adjunctive Immunomodulatory Therapy**

Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered as both induction and maintenance therapy. The loading dose of 2 g/kg (165 g total) was divided over five days, followed by maintenance cycles of 2 g/kg every four weeks for five additional cycles. IVIG is thought to work through multiple mechanisms in dermatomyositis, including neutralization of pathogenic autoantibodies, modulation of Fc receptor function, and inhibition of complement deposition. Its role is particularly valuable in refractory cases or when rapid disease control is essential, as it does not increase infection risk—a significant advantage in a patient requiring multiple immunosuppressive agents.

Rituximab, a B-cell depleting anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody, was administered as two 1000 mg intravenous doses separated by two weeks. This agent targets the autoimmune process upstream by eliminating autoreactive B cells, and has demonstrated efficacy in treatment-refractory inflammatory myopathies. The combination of multiple immunosuppressive agents reflects both the severity of this patient's disease and the limited high-quality evidence guiding optimal pharmacotherapy for anti-SAE1 positive dermatomyositis.

**Infection Prophylaxis and Pre-Treatment Screening**

Before initiating immunosuppression, comprehensive screening for latent infections was performed. This included hepatitis B surface antigen, hepatitis B core antibody, hepatitis B e antibody, hepatitis C antibody, HIV, and varicella zoster virus IgG. The patient was found to be VZV IgG negative, indicating susceptibility to primary varicella infection—a significant concern given the potential for disseminated disease under immunosuppression. Cytomegalovirus serology was negative, and tuberculosis screening with QuantiFERON-TB Gold was indeterminate, prompting three sputum samples for acid-fast bacilli, which were negative.

Prophylaxis against *Pneumocystis jirovecii* pneumonia was initially accomplished with sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim. However, this agent was discontinued when the patient developed hemolytic anemia in the absence of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency. This adverse reaction prompted a switch to nebulized pentamidine 300 mg every four weeks, an alternative prophylactic regimen suitable for patients unable to tolerate sulfa-based medications.

**Supportive Care**

Dysphagia significantly complicated this patient's clinical course and management. The video fluoroscopic swallow study demonstrated severe pharyngeal dysphagia with pyriform fossae residue and penetration to the vocal cords, explaining the aspiration pneumonitis seen on imaging. Nasogastric tube feeding was required during the inpatient stay and continued for two months post-discharge, ensuring adequate nutritional support while protecting the airway from further aspiration events. This prolonged requirement for enteral feeding underscores the severity of bulbar involvement in this case and the importance of early swallowing assessment in patients with dermatomyositis.

## Clinical Outcome, Follow-up, and Key Lessons

Following initiation of aggressive immunosuppressive therapy, the patient demonstrated a gradual but substantial recovery over the ensuing months. His 21-day hospitalization reflected the severity of his presentation, particularly the significant oropharyngeal muscle involvement that resulted in persistent dysphagia. At the time of discharge, he remained dependent on nasogastric tube feeding, a testament to the profound impact of myositis on his swallowing mechanics. The cervical soft tissue swelling, while markedly improved, had not fully resolved—a reminder that disease control in dermatomyositis often requires sustained immunosuppression rather than rapid reversal. The persistence of dysphagia requiring enteral feeding for two additional months post-discharge underscores that bulbar involvement in dermatomyositis can be among the most refractory manifestations.

The recovery timeline offers important clinical insights into the expected response to treatment in severe dermatomyositis. Muscle enzyme normalization often precedes functional recovery. In this case, creatine kinase normalized to 37 U/L by three weeks post-discharge, correlating with full recovery of proximal muscle strength. However, the disconnect between biochemical improvement and clinical resolution of dysphagia is instructive—swallowing dysfunction can lag behind other parameters of disease activity. Cervical soft tissue swelling resolved completely by three months, and dysphagia had fully resolved by seven months, allowing successful discontinuation of prednisone. This temporal pattern emphasizes the need for clinicians to maintain realistic expectations when counseling patients and to ensure adequate nutritional support during the recovery phase.

Long-term management of dermatomyositis requires both maintenance immunosuppression and vigilant malignancy surveillance. The patient's regimen included continuation of mycophenolate mofetil with a planned taper over six months, depending on clinical progress, along with monthly intravenous immunoglobulin infusions. This maintenance strategy reflects the need for sustained immunosuppression to prevent disease flares while allowing gradual reduction of glucocorticoid burden. Malignancy surveillance is particularly critical given the patient's high-risk profile. According to the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group guidelines, he possessed two high-risk factors (dermatomyositis diagnosis and dysphagia) and two intermediate-risk factors (SAE1 antibody positivity and male sex), warranting comprehensive screening. Serial CT chest imaging, regular blood tests, and planned gastroscopy and colonoscopy constitute appropriate surveillance for occult malignancy, particularly given the association between dermatomyositis and cancer within the first years following diagnosis.

Several clinical lessons emerge from this case that warrant emphasis for trainees. First, the importance of obtaining a creatine kinase level in patients with unexplained neck swelling cannot be overstated. In this case, the markedly elevated CK of 16,184 U/L was the critical finding that redirected the diagnostic approach away from angioedema toward an inflammatory myopathy. Second, a thorough skin and nail examination yielded essential diagnostic clues that were initially subtle—periungual erythema, nailfold microhaemorrhages on capillaroscopy, and a rash on the lateral thigh (Holster sign). These findings, while less classic than Gottron's papules or a heliotrope rash, were sufficient to raise suspicion for dermatomyositis when combined with the clinical context. Third, clinicians should recognize that connective tissue diseases can present with "pseudoangioedema"—subcutaneous edema from inflammatory processes that mimics but does not share the pathophysiology of histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema.

The diagnostic journey in this case illustrates the value of a broad differential diagnosis and multidisciplinary collaboration. The initial presentations to emergency medicine and ENT surgery appropriately addressed the most urgent concerns—deep neck space infection and airway compromise. When these were excluded, referral to clinical immunology for suspected angioedema was logical. However, the ultimate diagnosis required integrating findings across specialties: the CT demonstration of muscle inflammation, the nailfold capillaroscopy abnormalities, the elevated muscle enzymes, and the confirmatory autoantibody testing and muscle biopsy. This case reinforces that dermatomyositis should be considered in the differential diagnosis of unexplained neck swelling, particularly when clinical features are atypical for angioedema—such as the absence of episodic swelling, lack of response to antihistamines or bradykinin-targeted therapies, or the presence of accompanying muscle weakness or cutaneous findings. Maintaining diagnostic humility and systematically revisiting the differential diagnosis when the clinical picture does not fit a working diagnosis remains a cornerstone of clinical reasoning.
