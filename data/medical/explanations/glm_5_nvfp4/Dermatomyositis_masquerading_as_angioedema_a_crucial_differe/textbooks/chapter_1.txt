Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation and Initial Assessment

The patient, a previously well male in his 40s, presented to the emergency department with a three-week history of progressive neck swelling accompanied by proximal muscle weakness in his hip flexors and mild myalgia. He had no significant past medical history and was a lifelong non-smoker. Notably, he had not been taking a 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitor (statin), which is relevant as statins are a well-recognized cause of drug-induced myopathy and would have complicated the diagnostic picture. The subacute nature of his symptoms, developing over weeks rather than hours to days, was an early clue that the underlying etiology might differ from typical infectious or allergic causes.

The initial clinical impression centered on Ludwig's angina, a rapidly progressing, potentially life-threatening cellulitis of the submandibular space. This diagnosis was considered because neck swelling, particularly when accompanied by systemic inflammatory markers, often raises concern for deep neck space infections. His C-reactive protein was elevated at 55.6 mg/L, though his white cell count was normal at 9.1×10⁹/L and he was afebrile. A computed tomography (CT) scan of the neck demonstrated diffuse oedema involving the strap muscles with inflammation extending to the retropharyngeal space. However, no abscess or fluid collection was identified to account for an infectious process.

After transfer to a quaternary center for ear, nose, and throat (ENT) surgery evaluation, flexible nasendoscopy was performed. This examination revealed no significant anatomical abnormalities of the soft palate, vallecula, larynx, or post-cricoid space, effectively excluding upper airway obstruction. However, increased secretions and coughing during the examination were noted—subtle findings that in retrospect suggested pharyngeal dysfunction and dysphagia, which are recognized complications of dermatomyositis affecting the striated muscles of the pharynx.

With structural and infectious causes effectively excluded, the diagnostic focus shifted toward angioedema. The immunology consultation was prompted by the unexplained neck swelling without a clear structural or infective cause. Angioedema is characterized by episodic, self-limited swelling of the deeper dermis and subcutaneous tissues, most commonly mediated by either histamine (as in allergic reactions) or bradykinin (as in hereditary or acquired angioedema). A careful history was obtained: the patient denied use of angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs—all medications associated with drug-induced angioedema. He also reported no personal or family history of angioedema. These negative findings made hereditary or acquired angioedema unlikely, as did the clinical observation that his swelling was persistent rather than episodic.

The consulting immunologist noted generalized erythema of the face, including involvement of the nasolabial folds, and an erythematous, swollen, non-tender neck. Closer inspection revealed subtle nailfold changes suggestive of periungual erythema, which were further evaluated by nailfold capillaroscopy demonstrating microhaemorrhages and avascular areas. These capillaroscopic findings are highly characteristic of dermatomyositis and other connective tissue diseases, reflecting the microvascular pathology central to these conditions. Additionally, a rash was present on the right lateral thigh (later identified as a positive Holster sign), and neurological examination revealed reduced hip flexor power graded at 4/5 on the Medical Research Council scale.

The combination of cutaneous findings, proximal muscle weakness, and the atypical nature of the neck swelling prompted measurement of creatine kinase (CK), a sensitive marker for muscle injury. The CK level returned markedly elevated at 16,184 U/L (normal range typically <200 U/L), providing objective evidence of significant muscle inflammation and effectively redirecting the diagnosis toward an inflammatory myopathy. Concurrent transaminases were elevated (alanine aminotransferase 143 U/L, aspartate aminotransferase 396 U/L), along with lactate dehydrogenase at 692 U/L. In the context of myositis, elevated transaminases often reflect muscle rather than hepatic injury, as these enzymes are released from damaged myofibers. The absence of classic dermatomyositis skin findings such as Gottron's papules and heliotrope eruption did not exclude the diagnosis, as cutaneous manifestations can be subtle or develop later in the disease course.
