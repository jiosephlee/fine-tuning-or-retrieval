Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Investigations and Confirmation

The diagnostic workup for this patient unfolded in a stepwise fashion, beginning with tests to exclude more common conditions and culminating in a definitive diagnosis of anti-SAE1 antibody-positive dermatomyositis. Understanding how each investigation contributed to the diagnosis provides a framework for evaluating patients with suspected inflammatory myopathy.

Laboratory investigations played a pivotal role early in the diagnostic process. The creatine kinase (CK) level was markedly elevated at 16,184 U/L—a finding that immediately shifted the differential diagnosis away from angioedema and toward a primary muscle disorder. In dermatomyositis and other inflammatory myopathies, CK is released from damaged muscle fibers, and levels often correlate with disease activity. A CK elevation of this magnitude indicates extensive active muscle injury. Importantly, the patient's transaminases were also elevated, with an alanine aminotransferase (ALT) of 143 U/L and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) of 396 U/L. In patients without liver disease, elevated transaminases frequently originate from muscle rather than hepatic tissue, as both enzymes are present in skeletal muscle. The AST elevation exceeding the ALT elevation is consistent with a muscular source, given that AST is more abundant in muscle than ALT. Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) was elevated at 692 U/L, another non-specific marker of tissue injury that, in this context, reflected ongoing muscle inflammation and necrosis.

Serological testing provided the next critical piece of diagnostic evidence. The anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) was positive at a titer of 1:1280 with a speckled pattern, indicating the presence of autoantibodies against nuclear or cytoplasmic antigens. While ANA is sensitive for autoimmune disease, it lacks specificity. The definitive serological finding came from a comprehensive myositis antibody panel, which revealed strongly positive anti-small ubiquitin-like modifier activating enzyme subunit 1 (SAE1) antibodies with a signal intensity of 173 (normal range <35). Anti-SAE1 antibodies are a myositis-specific antibody associated with dermatomyositis, particularly in adults. Patients with anti-SAE1 antibodies typically present with characteristic cutaneous manifestations and may develop dysphagia and interstitial lung disease. The presence of this antibody, combined with clinical features, established the specific subtype of dermatomyositis. Notably, antibodies against other myositis antigens, including anti-Jo-1, anti-Mi-2, and anti-TIF1-γ, were negative, narrowing the diagnostic focus.

Complement studies were performed to exclude hereditary or acquired angioedema, which had been the initial clinical suspicion. C3 and C4 levels were normal, and C1 esterase inhibitor function and quantification were within normal ranges. These findings effectively ruled out angioedema due to C1 esterase inhibitor deficiency or dysfunction, redirecting attention toward an inflammatory myopathy.

Imaging studies provided objective evidence of muscle involvement and assessed disease extent. The initial CT of the neck, performed to evaluate suspected deep neck space infection, revealed diffuse edema involving the strap muscles with inflammation extending to the retropharyngeal space. This finding was instrumental in recognizing that the neck swelling represented muscle inflammation rather than subcutaneous angioedema. MRI of the thighs demonstrated changes consistent with active myositis, including involvement of the obturator internus and externus muscles, hip adductors, gracilis muscles, and distal iliopsoas muscles. MRI is highly sensitive for detecting muscle inflammation, showing edema on T2-weighted images with fat suppression. The FDG-PET scan provided a whole-body assessment, demonstrating diffuse abnormal FDG accumulation within multiple muscle groups—most prominently in the head and neck, bilateral deltoids, forearms, chest wall, abdomen, and pelvis. This imaging modality also served to exclude occult malignancy, a critical consideration given the association between dermatomyositis and malignancy.

CT of the chest revealed multiple non-cavitating pulmonary nodules and patchy areas of peribronchial consolidation, likely representing aspiration pneumonitis secondary to dysphagia. No radiological evidence of pulmonary fibrosis was present. Pulmonary function tests demonstrated an extrathoracic restrictive pattern, consistent with upper airway or pharyngeal involvement rather than intrinsic lung disease.

The muscle biopsy of the thigh provided definitive histopathological confirmation. The biopsy showed foci of necrotic, vacuolated, degenerate, and regenerative myofibers in a perifascicular distribution, along with scattered perimysial inflammation. The perifascicular distribution of muscle fiber injury is characteristic of dermatomyositis and distinguishes it from other inflammatory myopathies such as polymyositis, which typically demonstrates endomysial inflammation. Immunohistochemistry revealed deposition of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9) in perifascicular endomysial capillaries, a hallmark finding in dermatomyositis. This pattern of complement deposition reflects the pathogenic role of humoral immunity and complement-mediated microvascular injury in dermatomyositis, distinguishing it from the T-cell-mediated muscle fiber damage seen in polymyositis and inclusion body myositis.

Functional assessments quantified the clinical impact of the disease. A video fluoroscopic swallow study demonstrated severe pharyngeal dysphagia with pyriform fossae residue and post-swallow penetration of fluids to the vocal cords, confirming significant involvement of the swallowing musculature. This finding had immediate clinical implications, necessitating nasogastric tube feeding to prevent aspiration.

Malignancy screening was undertaken given the established association between dermatomyositis and malignancy. The patient had multiple risk factors: a diagnosis of dermatomyositis itself, the presence of dysphagia, anti-SAE1 antibody positivity, and male sex. CT of the abdomen and pelvis showed no evidence of malignancy, and tumor markers including CEA, CA19-9, CA-125, and PSA were normal. The FDG-PET scan demonstrated no metabolically active disease outside the muscles. Plans were made for gastroscopy and colonoscopy as part of comprehensive malignancy surveillance, in accordance with guideline recommendations for patients at high risk.
