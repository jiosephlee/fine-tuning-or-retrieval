Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

Chapter 6: ## Clinical Outcome, Follow-up, and Key Lessons

Following initiation of aggressive immunosuppressive therapy, the patient demonstrated a gradual but substantial recovery over the ensuing months. His 21-day hospitalization reflected the severity of his presentation, particularly the significant oropharyngeal muscle involvement that resulted in persistent dysphagia. At the time of discharge, he remained dependent on nasogastric tube feeding, a testament to the profound impact of myositis on his swallowing mechanics. The cervical soft tissue swelling, while markedly improved, had not fully resolved—a reminder that disease control in dermatomyositis often requires sustained immunosuppression rather than rapid reversal. The persistence of dysphagia requiring enteral feeding for two additional months post-discharge underscores that bulbar involvement in dermatomyositis can be among the most refractory manifestations.

The recovery timeline offers important clinical insights into the expected response to treatment in severe dermatomyositis. Muscle enzyme normalization often precedes functional recovery. In this case, creatine kinase normalized to 37 U/L by three weeks post-discharge, correlating with full recovery of proximal muscle strength. However, the disconnect between biochemical improvement and clinical resolution of dysphagia is instructive—swallowing dysfunction can lag behind other parameters of disease activity. Cervical soft tissue swelling resolved completely by three months, and dysphagia had fully resolved by seven months, allowing successful discontinuation of prednisone. This temporal pattern emphasizes the need for clinicians to maintain realistic expectations when counseling patients and to ensure adequate nutritional support during the recovery phase.

Long-term management of dermatomyositis requires both maintenance immunosuppression and vigilant malignancy surveillance. The patient's regimen included continuation of mycophenolate mofetil with a planned taper over six months, depending on clinical progress, along with monthly intravenous immunoglobulin infusions. This maintenance strategy reflects the need for sustained immunosuppression to prevent disease flares while allowing gradual reduction of glucocorticoid burden. Malignancy surveillance is particularly critical given the patient's high-risk profile. According to the International Myositis Assessment and Clinical Studies Group guidelines, he possessed two high-risk factors (dermatomyositis diagnosis and dysphagia) and two intermediate-risk factors (SAE1 antibody positivity and male sex), warranting comprehensive screening. Serial CT chest imaging, regular blood tests, and planned gastroscopy and colonoscopy constitute appropriate surveillance for occult malignancy, particularly given the association between dermatomyositis and cancer within the first years following diagnosis.

Several clinical lessons emerge from this case that warrant emphasis for trainees. First, the importance of obtaining a creatine kinase level in patients with unexplained neck swelling cannot be overstated. In this case, the markedly elevated CK of 16,184 U/L was the critical finding that redirected the diagnostic approach away from angioedema toward an inflammatory myopathy. Second, a thorough skin and nail examination yielded essential diagnostic clues that were initially subtle—periungual erythema, nailfold microhaemorrhages on capillaroscopy, and a rash on the lateral thigh (Holster sign). These findings, while less classic than Gottron's papules or a heliotrope rash, were sufficient to raise suspicion for dermatomyositis when combined with the clinical context. Third, clinicians should recognize that connective tissue diseases can present with "pseudoangioedema"—subcutaneous edema from inflammatory processes that mimics but does not share the pathophysiology of histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema.

The diagnostic journey in this case illustrates the value of a broad differential diagnosis and multidisciplinary collaboration. The initial presentations to emergency medicine and ENT surgery appropriately addressed the most urgent concerns—deep neck space infection and airway compromise. When these were excluded, referral to clinical immunology for suspected angioedema was logical. However, the ultimate diagnosis required integrating findings across specialties: the CT demonstration of muscle inflammation, the nailfold capillaroscopy abnormalities, the elevated muscle enzymes, and the confirmatory autoantibody testing and muscle biopsy. This case reinforces that dermatomyositis should be considered in the differential diagnosis of unexplained neck swelling, particularly when clinical features are atypical for angioedema—such as the absence of episodic swelling, lack of response to antihistamines or bradykinin-targeted therapies, or the presence of accompanying muscle weakness or cutaneous findings. Maintaining diagnostic humility and systematically revisiting the differential diagnosis when the clinical picture does not fit a working diagnosis remains a cornerstone of clinical reasoning.
