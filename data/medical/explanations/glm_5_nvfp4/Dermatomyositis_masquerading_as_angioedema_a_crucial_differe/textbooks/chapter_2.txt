Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

Chapter 2: ## Differential Diagnosis of Neck Swelling

Neck swelling presents a diagnostic challenge that requires systematic consideration of infectious, inflammatory, vascular, and allergic etiologies. In this case, the patient's initial presentation with subacute neck swelling and an elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) of 55.6 mg/L prompted evaluation for several diagnostic categories, with Ludwig's angina and angioedema emerging as the leading initial considerations.

**Infectious causes** represent the most urgent diagnostic category for neck swelling. Ludwig's angina, a rapidly progressive cellulitis of the submandibular space, was the initial working diagnosis given the neck swelling and elevated inflammatory markers. Deep neck space infections can spread along fascial planes to involve the retropharyngeal and parapharyngeal spaces, potentially compromising the airway. Cellulitis and erysipelas of the neck are typically accompanied by fever, warmth, and tenderness. Bacterial sialadenitis, involving the submandibular or parotid glands, would produce swelling localized to the gland with associated pain and purulent discharge from the salivary duct. In this patient, the absence of fever, the afebrile presentation, and the lack of focal tenderness argued against an infectious etiology. The CT scan demonstrated diffuse neck edema involving the strap muscles with inflammation extending to the retropharyngeal space but no abscess formation, and flexible nasendoscopy revealed no anatomical abnormalities. These findings effectively excluded an infectious process.

**Angioedema** was the next primary consideration after infectious causes were excluded. Angioedema is classified into three main categories based on pathophysiology: histaminergic (allergic or IgE-mediated), bradykinin-mediated, and non-mediator-driven angioedema. Histaminergic angioedema, the most common form, results from mast cell degranulation and histamine release, typically occurring within minutes to hours after exposure to an allergen such as foods, medications, or insect stings. It is often accompanied by urticaria, pruritus, and other features of allergic reactions. Bradykinin-mediated angioedema includes hereditary angioedema (HAE) and acquired angioedema (AAE). Hereditary angioedema, caused by mutations in the C1 esterase inhibitor gene (type I) or by dysfunctional C1 esterase inhibitor (type II), typically presents with recurrent episodes of swelling affecting the face, extremities, gastrointestinal tract, and larynx, often without urticaria. Acquired angioedema results from consumption or destruction of C1 esterase inhibitor, often associated with lymphoproliferative disorders or autoimmune conditions.

The laboratory workup for angioedema focuses on the complement cascade. C4 levels are typically reduced during and between attacks in both hereditary and acquired angioedema. C1 esterase inhibitor functional assay and quantitative levels distinguish between HAE types I and II, while C1q levels are reduced in acquired angioedema but normal in hereditary forms. In this patient, C3 and C4 levels were normal, and C1 esterase function and quantification were within normal limits, effectively excluding bradykinin-mediated angioedema.

Several features made this patient's presentation atypical for angioedema of any etiology. He had no personal or family history of angioedema, no exposure to medications associated with angioedema (including angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs), and no accompanying urticaria or pruritus. The subacute progression over three weeks was inconsistent with the episodic nature typical of angioedema. Furthermore, the distribution of swelling—involving the strap muscles and extending to the retropharyngeal space on imaging—suggested tissue infiltration rather than the superficial dermal and subcutaneous swelling characteristic of angioedema.

**Other diagnostic considerations** included superior vena cava syndrome, which produces neck and facial swelling due to venous obstruction, typically accompanied by dilated collateral veins and signs of venous congestion. Orofacial granulomatosis, a condition characterized by persistent swelling of the lips and face, was considered but would not explain the systemic features observed. Drug reactions, including drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS), were unlikely given no medication exposure. Acute contact dermatitis would produce more localized, pruritic changes rather than the diffuse, non-tender swelling seen here.

**Red flags** in this case prompted expansion of the differential beyond angioedema and infection. The elevated CRP indicated an inflammatory process, while the presence of skin changes—including generalized erythema of the face, periungual erythema, and a rash on the right lateral thigh—suggested a systemic condition. Nailfold microhaemorrhages and avascular areas on capillaroscopy indicated microvascular pathology. Perhaps most importantly, the identification of reduced hip flexor power (4/5) pointed toward muscle involvement.

The **creatine kinase level** became the pivotal investigation that redirected the diagnostic approach. Ordered due to the atypical nature of the neck edema and the presence of skin, nail, and muscle findings, the result of 16,184 U/L was strikingly elevated, confirming significant muscle injury. This finding, combined with the elevated transaminases (ALT 143 U/L, AST 396 U/L) and LDH (692 U/L), indicated active myositis. Inflammatory myopathies, including dermatomyositis, immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy, and inclusion body myositis, were now the primary diagnostic considerations. The subsequent positive anti-SAE1 antibody and characteristic muscle biopsy confirmed dermatomyositis as the diagnosis.

This case illustrates the phenomenon of **pseudoangioedema**—angioedema-like swelling caused by mechanisms other than histamine or bradykinin release. In dermatomyositis, subcutaneous tissue edema arises from underlying muscle inflammation rather than vascular permeability changes mediated by allergic or bradykinin pathways. Recognition of this entity is critical, as treatment with antihistamines or bradykinin pathway inhibitors is ineffective, and delay in immunosuppressive therapy risks disease progression and multiorgan damage.
