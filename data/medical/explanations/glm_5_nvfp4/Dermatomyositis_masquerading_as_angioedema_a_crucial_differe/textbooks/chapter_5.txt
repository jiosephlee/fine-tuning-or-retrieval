Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

Chapter 5: ## Treatment Strategy and Management Decisions

The management of dermatomyositis requires prompt and aggressive immunosuppression to halt disease progression and prevent irreversible organ damage. This patient's presentation warranted particularly urgent intervention given the significant involvement of cervical and airway musculature, which posed an immediate threat to airway integrity. The treatment strategy was multi-layered, incorporating acute induction therapy, long-term maintenance immunosuppression, supportive care, and vigilant prophylaxis against treatment-related complications.

**Acute Induction Therapy**

High-dose intravenous methylprednisolone at 1000 mg daily for three days was initiated as pulse therapy, a standard approach for severe idiopathic inflammatory myopathies with critical organ involvement. The rationale for this aggressive induction stems from the need to rapidly suppress the intense inflammatory response driving both the muscle necrosis and the subcutaneous edema. In this case, the degree of muscle involvement was substantial, evidenced by the markedly elevated creatine kinase of 16,184 U/L and the diffuse inflammatory changes on imaging. Intravenous administration ensures rapid bioavailability and may provide more consistent immunosuppression than oral preparations in patients with dysphagia and potential gastrointestinal involvement.

Following pulse therapy, the patient was transitioned to oral prednisone at 60 mg daily with a structured tapering regimen, reducing by 5 mg every 2 to 3 weeks. This tapering approach balances the need for continued immunosuppression during the vulnerable early treatment period against the well-documented complications of prolonged high-dose corticosteroid therapy, including osteoporosis, hyperglycemia, and adrenal suppression.

**Selection of Steroid-Sparing Agents**

Mycophenolate mofetil was selected as the primary steroid-sparing agent at a dose of 1 gram twice daily. This decision was informed by multiple factors. Mycophenolate mofetil inhibits inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase, selectively blocking lymphocyte proliferation and offering a favorable side effect profile compared to alternative agents. Importantly, the patient had already demonstrated abnormal pulmonary function tests with an extrathoracic restrictive pattern, which influenced medication selection. Methotrexate, a commonly used alternative, was specifically avoided due to its association with drug-induced pneumonitis and potential to exacerbate or accelerate pulmonary fibrosis—a critical consideration given the patient's respiratory involvement from aspiration and the inherent risk of interstitial lung disease in dermatomyositis. Azathioprine and tacrolimus were not pursued as mycophenolate was well-tolerated, avoiding unnecessary medication changes in an already complex regimen.

**Adjunctive Immunomodulatory Therapy**

Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered as both induction and maintenance therapy. The loading dose of 2 g/kg (165 g total) was divided over five days, followed by maintenance cycles of 2 g/kg every four weeks for five additional cycles. IVIG is thought to work through multiple mechanisms in dermatomyositis, including neutralization of pathogenic autoantibodies, modulation of Fc receptor function, and inhibition of complement deposition. Its role is particularly valuable in refractory cases or when rapid disease control is essential, as it does not increase infection risk—a significant advantage in a patient requiring multiple immunosuppressive agents.

Rituximab, a B-cell depleting anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody, was administered as two 1000 mg intravenous doses separated by two weeks. This agent targets the autoimmune process upstream by eliminating autoreactive B cells, and has demonstrated efficacy in treatment-refractory inflammatory myopathies. The combination of multiple immunosuppressive agents reflects both the severity of this patient's disease and the limited high-quality evidence guiding optimal pharmacotherapy for anti-SAE1 positive dermatomyositis.

**Infection Prophylaxis and Pre-Treatment Screening**

Before initiating immunosuppression, comprehensive screening for latent infections was performed. This included hepatitis B surface antigen, hepatitis B core antibody, hepatitis B e antibody, hepatitis C antibody, HIV, and varicella zoster virus IgG. The patient was found to be VZV IgG negative, indicating susceptibility to primary varicella infection—a significant concern given the potential for disseminated disease under immunosuppression. Cytomegalovirus serology was negative, and tuberculosis screening with QuantiFERON-TB Gold was indeterminate, prompting three sputum samples for acid-fast bacilli, which were negative.

Prophylaxis against *Pneumocystis jirovecii* pneumonia was initially accomplished with sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim. However, this agent was discontinued when the patient developed hemolytic anemia in the absence of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency. This adverse reaction prompted a switch to nebulized pentamidine 300 mg every four weeks, an alternative prophylactic regimen suitable for patients unable to tolerate sulfa-based medications.

**Supportive Care**

Dysphagia significantly complicated this patient's clinical course and management. The video fluoroscopic swallow study demonstrated severe pharyngeal dysphagia with pyriform fossae residue and penetration to the vocal cords, explaining the aspiration pneumonitis seen on imaging. Nasogastric tube feeding was required during the inpatient stay and continued for two months post-discharge, ensuring adequate nutritional support while protecting the airway from further aspiration events. This prolonged requirement for enteral feeding underscores the severity of bulbar involvement in this case and the importance of early swallowing assessment in patients with dermatomyositis.
