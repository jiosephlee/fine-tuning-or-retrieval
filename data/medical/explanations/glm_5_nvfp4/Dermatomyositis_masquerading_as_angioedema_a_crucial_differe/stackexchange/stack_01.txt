Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Clinical Pearls] A patient presents with neck swelling initially suspected to be angioedema. What clinical and laboratory features should prompt consideration of dermatomyositis as an alternative diagnosis?

**Clinical Teaching Answer**

When evaluating neck swelling suspected to be angioedema, several clinical and laboratory features should prompt consideration of dermatomyositis as an alternative diagnosis.

**Clinical Features:**

First, assess whether the swelling is truly consistent with angioedema. In this case, the "atypical nature of neck oedema" was an important clue—in true angioedema, swelling is typically transient, often affects the face, lips, tongue, and oropharynx, and resolves within 24–72 hours. Persistent, non-pitting swelling extending into deeper tissues should raise suspicion for an alternative diagnosis.

Second, perform a thorough skin and nail examination. In this patient, subtle findings—including generalized facial erythema involving the nasolabial folds, periungual erythema, nailfold microhemorrhages with avascular areas on capillaroscopy, and a rash on the lateral thigh (consistent with a Holster sign)—provided critical diagnostic clues. Classic features such as Gottron's papules or heliotrope eruption may be absent, as they were in this case.

Third, evaluate for muscle involvement. This patient had proximal muscle weakness (hip flexor power 4/5) with mild myalgia, and dysphagia was evident on flexible nasendoscopy with increased secretions and coughing. Dysphagia can be an early or prominent feature in dermatomyositis and may precede overt weakness.

Fourth, take a detailed medication and family history. The absence of ACE inhibitors, DPP-4 inhibitors, NSAIDs, or a personal/family history of angioedema made hereditary or acquired angioedema less likely.

**Laboratory Features:**

A creatine kinase level is essential and should be obtained early when the clinical picture is atypical. In this case, the CK was markedly elevated at 16,184 U/L, confirming significant muscle injury. Elevated transaminases (ALT 143 U/L, AST 396 U/L) and LDH (692 U/L) further supported muscle inflammation, as these enzymes are released from damaged myofibers.

Complement studies (C3, C4, and C1 esterase function) were normal, effectively excluding C1 esterase inhibitor deficiency—the hallmark of hereditary and acquired angioedema. A positive ANA (1:1280, speckled pattern) and confirmatory anti-SAE1 antibody (a myositis-specific antibody) solidified the diagnosis.

**Key Teaching Point:**

When neck swelling is persistent, atypical in distribution, or accompanied by cutaneous changes, muscle weakness, dysphagia, or markedly elevated CK, dermatomyositis should be strongly considered. Prompt recognition is critical, as patients require intensive immunosuppression to prevent disease progression and complications, including aspiration pneumonitis and malignancy-associated risk.
