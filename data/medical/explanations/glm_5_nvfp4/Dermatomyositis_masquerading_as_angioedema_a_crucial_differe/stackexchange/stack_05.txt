Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a patient presenting with proximal muscle weakness and a creatine kinase level exceeding 15,000 U/L, what features on history, examination, and laboratory testing help differentiate dermatomyositis from statin-induced myopathy and immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy?

**Clinical Answer**

In a patient with proximal muscle weakness and a CK exceeding 15,000 U/L, differentiating dermatomyositis from statin-induced myopathy and immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) requires careful attention to medication history, cutaneous examination, autoantibody profiling, and muscle biopsy findings.

**Medication history** is the critical first branch point. This patient had not been taking an HMG-CoA reductase inhibitor, effectively excluding self-limited statin-induced myopathy. However, IMNM can occur either with or without statin exposure—anti-HMGCR antibodies may persist and drive disease even after statin discontinuation.

**Cutaneous findings** distinguish dermatomyositis from the other entities. While classic features like Gottron's papules and heliotrope rash are well-known, their absence does not exclude dermatomyositis. This patient demonstrated subtle but diagnostic findings: periungual erythema with nailfold microhaemorrhages, avascular areas on capillaroscopy, facial erythema, and a Holster sign (rash on the lateral thigh). Neither statin-induced myopathy nor IMNM produces these cutaneous manifestations—IMNM is defined by the absence of dermatomyositis skin findings despite otherwise similar clinical presentation.

**Autoantibody testing** provides essential discrimination. This patient's positive anti-SAE1 antibody is specific for dermatomyositis and was confirmed on a comprehensive myositis antibody panel. For IMNM, anti-HMGCR antibodies (statin-associated or idiopathic) or anti-SRP antibodies are characteristic. The absence of these antibodies, coupled with a strongly positive ANA and anti-SAE1, confirmed dermatomyositis.

**Muscle biopsy** offers definitive distinction. This patient's biopsy showed necrotic and regenerative myofibers in a **perifascicular distribution** with perimysial inflammation and C5b-9 (membrane attack complex) deposition in perifascicular capillaries—a pattern pathognomonic for dermatomyositis. In contrast, IMNM biopsy demonstrates prominent myofiber necrosis with **minimal or absent inflammation**. Statin-induced myopathy (non-immune) shows non-specific myopathic changes without the inflammatory infiltrate or complement deposition seen in autoimmune myositis.

In summary, while all three conditions can produce profound CK elevations, the presence of cutaneous findings, specific myositis antibodies, and biopsy evidence of perifascicular inflammation with complement-mediated microangiopathy confirmed dermatomyositis in this case—and would have been absent in either statin-induced myopathy or IMNM.
