Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Pathophysiology] How does the mechanism of subcutaneous edema ('pseudoangioedema') in dermatomyositis differ from classic histaminergic and bradykinin-mediated angioedema?

**Clinical Teaching Answer:**

The subcutaneous edema in dermatomyositis—termed "pseudoangioedema"—arises from a fundamentally different mechanism than classic angioedema and represents inflammatory edema rather than a primary vascular permeability disorder mediated by vasoactive substances.

**Classic angioedema mechanisms:**

- **Histaminergic angioedema** results from mast cell and basophil degranulation with histamine release, typically triggered by IgE-mediated allergic reactions. Histamine acts on H1 and H2 receptors, causing transient vasodilation and increased capillary permeability. This form responds to antihistamines, epinephrine, and corticosteroids, and is often accompanied by urticaria and pruritus.

- **Bradykinin-mediated angioedema** involves dysregulation of the kallikrein-kinin cascade, leading to excessive bradykinin production or impaired degradation. This occurs in hereditary angioedema (C1 esterase inhibitor deficiency), acquired angioedema, or ACE inhibitor–associated angioedema. Bradykinin causes sustained vascular leakage without urticaria or pruritus, and crucially, it does not respond to antihistamines or epinephrine.

**Dermatomyositis pseudoangioedema mechanism:**

In contrast, the subcutaneous edema in dermatomyositis reflects **inflammatory infiltration of muscle and connective tissue with associated microvascular injury**. The pathophysiology involves complement activation with deposition of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9) on endomysial capillaries, as demonstrated in this patient's muscle biopsy. This complement-mediated microangiopathy leads to perifascicular muscle fiber damage, inflammation, and tissue edema.

As the authors note, the patient's "subcutaneous oedema arises from muscle inflammation in the setting of myositis, rather than histaminergic or bradykinin-mediated angioedema." This is supported by normal C3, C4, and C1 esterase inhibitor levels in this case, which effectively exclude bradykinin-mediated etiologies.

**Key distinguishing features from the case:**

The atypical distribution of swelling—involving the strap muscles and extending to the retropharyngeal space on CT—was inconsistent with classic angioedema, which typically involves the deep dermis and subcutaneous tissues without inflammatory muscle infiltration. Additionally, the associated proximal muscle weakness (4/5 hip flexor power), elevated creatine kinase (16,184 U/L), and subtle cutaneous findings (periungual erythema, nailfold microhemorrhages, Holster sign) provided critical clues to the underlying inflammatory myopathy.

Importantly, pseudoangioedema requires **immunosuppression targeting the autoimmune process** rather than antihistamines or bradykinin pathway inhibitors. This patient's marked improvement with high-dose corticosteroids, IVIG, mycophenolate mofetil, and rituximab confirms the inflammatory etiology.
