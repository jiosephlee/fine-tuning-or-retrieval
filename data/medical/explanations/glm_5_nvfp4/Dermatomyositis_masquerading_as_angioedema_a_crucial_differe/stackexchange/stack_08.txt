Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Dermatomyositis masquerading as angioedema: a crucial differential not to overlook"

### [Clinical Pearls] What is the significance of elevated transaminases (ALT and AST) in a patient presenting with muscle weakness, and how can these values be distinguished from primary hepatic pathology?

### Clinical Teaching Answer

This is an excellent question that highlights a common diagnostic pitfall. In any patient presenting with weakness and elevated transaminases, **clinicians must consider a muscular source before attributing these findings to primary hepatic pathology.**

**The Pathophysiology**
Both AST and ALT are not liver-specific. ALT is present in relatively high concentration in the liver, but AST is found abundantly in skeletal muscle, cardiac muscle, kidney, brain, and red blood cells in addition to the liver. When muscle membranes are disrupted—whether from inflammatory myopathy, trauma, or strenuous exercise—these enzymes leak into the circulation.

**Key Distinguishing Features from the Case**

1. **Markedly Elevated Creatine Kinase (CK)**: This is the most critical discriminant. In this patient, the CK was strikingly elevated at 16,184 U/L, immediately signaling significant muscle injury. In primary liver disease, CK is typically normal or only minimally elevated. A disproportionately high CK relative to transaminases should prompt evaluation for myopathy.

2. **AST Predominance Over ALT**: In muscle injury, AST is typically more elevated than ALT because skeletal muscle contains proportionally more AST. This patient had an AST of 396 U/L compared to an ALT of 143 U/L—an AST:ALT ratio of approximately 2.8:1. While this ratio can occur in alcoholic hepatitis or cirrhosis, the concurrent marked CK elevation clarifies the source.

3. **Clinical Context**: This patient presented with proximal muscle weakness, myalgias, and skin findings. The transaminase elevations must be interpreted within this clinical picture. Notably, his hepatitis serologies were negative, and there was no clinical or imaging evidence of primary liver pathology.

4. **Response to Treatment**: Both the CK and transaminases normalized concurrently after immunosuppressive therapy for dermatomyositis (CK normalized to 37 U/L at 3 weeks), confirming that all elevated enzymes shared a common muscular origin.

**Clinical Bottom Line**
When evaluating elevated transaminases in the setting of muscle weakness, **always check a CK level**. An elevated CK with an AST:ALT ratio >1 strongly suggests a muscular etiology, as demonstrated in this case of dermatomyositis.
