Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Differential Diagnosis] What clinical and imaging features help differentiate between a decidualized endometrioma and a cystic degenerating leiomyoma in a pregnant patient?

### Clinical Teaching Answer

Differentiating between a decidualized endometrioma and a cystic degenerating leiomyoma in pregnancy is challenging, as both can present as complex adnexal masses with mixed solid-cystic features. However, several imaging and clinical characteristics can help distinguish them.

**Anatomic Relationship to the Uterus**
The most reliable distinguishing feature is demonstrating continuity with the uterus. A degenerating leiomyoma will maintain a clear connection to the uterine myometrium, often with a discernible stalk or broad-based attachment. In this case, the lower mass appeared "adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma" on both ultrasound and MRI, which correctly suggested a myomatous origin. Conversely, an endometrioma arises from the ovary and should demonstrate a separable relationship from the uterine corpus. However, as illustrated here, a pedunculated leiomyoma can mimic an adnexal mass when the connecting stalk is obscured by the gravid uterus, leading to diagnostic uncertainty.

**Ultrasound Features**
On ultrasound, decidualized endometriomas classically display homogeneous low-level internal echoes—a "ground-glass" appearance—with possible solid-appearing papillary projections representing decidualized stromal tissue. Degenerating leiomyomas typically show more heterogeneous internal architecture with anechoic or hypoechoic cystic spaces from necrosis and liquefaction. In this patient, both masses appeared as "unilocular cystic masses with regular margins and peripheral vascularisation," highlighting the substantial overlap in sonographic appearance.

**MRI Characteristics**
Non-contrast MRI provides superior soft tissue characterization. Endometriomas typically demonstrate hyperintense signal on T1-weighted sequences due to blood products, often with "shading" on T2 from iron deposition. Cystic degenerating leiomyomas show variable T1 signal but characteristically display well-defined cystic areas with hypointense T2 signal in the solid components. In this case, MRI revealed "thick walls, T1 and T2 fat-sat weighted inhomogeneous fluid content and focal hyperintense T1-T2 weighted solid components"—findings that were ultimately consistent with leiomyomas undergoing cystic change.

**Tumor Markers**
CA-125 has limited discriminative value, as elevation occurs in both conditions. This patient had a slightly elevated CA-125 (41.8 U/mL), which initially favored endometrioma but proved nonspecific.

**Surgical Pathology as the Definitive Standard**
This case underscores that preoperative imaging, while invaluable, cannot always definitively differentiate these entities. At laparotomy, both masses proved to be cystic leiomyomas—one pedunculated and twisted, explaining her fever and acute pain, and one intramural-subserosal. When imaging is equivocal and clinical circumstances warrant surgical intervention, operative evaluation remains the diagnostic gold standard.
