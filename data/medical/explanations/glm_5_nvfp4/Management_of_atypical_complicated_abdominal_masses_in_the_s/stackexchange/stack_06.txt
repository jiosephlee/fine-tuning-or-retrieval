Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Clinical Pearls] What complications can arise from uterine leiomyomas during pregnancy, and how might they present clinically?

### Clinical Teaching Answer

Uterine leiomyomas in pregnancy can lead to several important complications, which are well-illustrated by this case.

**Red (carneous) degeneration** is the most common complication, occurring when rapid myoma growth outstrips its blood supply. This presents with acute, severe abdominal pain, localized tenderness, and low-grade fever—exactly as seen in this patient. The pain typically localizes over the myoma and may be accompanied by leukocytosis and elevated inflammatory markers, which can mimic other acute abdominal emergencies.

**Torsion** occurs specifically with pedunculated subserosal myomas. In this case, the superior 18 cm mass was found intraoperatively to be a pedunculated leiomyoma that had undergone torsion, with pathological confirmation of ischemic necrosis near the twisted pedicle. Torsion should be suspected when acute pain is accompanied by systemic symptoms such as fever, and when imaging shows a cystic appearance due to subsequent necrosis.

**Mechanical compression** from large myomas can cause secondary effects. This patient developed dyspnea from diaphragmatic compression by the massive upper abdominal lesion, which improved with positional changes. Large pelvic myomas, like the 9 cm caudal mass in this case, can obstruct the birth canal and necessitate cesarean delivery.

**Additional complications** include spontaneous rupture (rare but life-threatening), infection of a necrotic myoma, and obstruction of urinary or gastrointestinal tracts depending on location. Anemia may also be exacerbated, as seen here with the patient's hemoglobin dropping to 7.5 g/L postoperatively.

Management is typically conservative with analgesics and observation unless there is concern for torsion, rupture, or malignancy—surgical intervention, as in this case, is reserved for refractory symptoms or diagnostic uncertainty.
