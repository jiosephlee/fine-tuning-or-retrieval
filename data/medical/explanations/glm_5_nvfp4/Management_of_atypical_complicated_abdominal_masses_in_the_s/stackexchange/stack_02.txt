Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Morning Report] What imaging modalities are considered safe and appropriate for evaluating abdominal masses during pregnancy, and what are their respective diagnostic advantages?

### Clinical Teaching Answer

When evaluating abdominal masses during pregnancy, we are limited to two primary imaging modalities: **ultrasound** and **non-contrast MRI**. Both are considered safe, while CT and contrast-enhanced MRI should be avoided.

**Ultrasound** is the first-line modality. It uses no ionizing radiation and is widely available. As demonstrated in this case, ultrasound effectively identified two large cystic masses, characterized their size and vascularity via color Doppler, and established the relationship between the caudal mass and a posterior uterine leiomyoma. Ultrasound is particularly valuable for anterior, submucosal, or pedunculated leiomyomas. However, its limitations include operator dependence and reduced visualization when masses are large, posterior, or deep within the pelvis—especially given the "steric bulk" of the gravid uterus noted in this patient.

**Non-contrast MRI** is the second-line modality when ultrasound is inconclusive or when greater anatomic precision is required. It offers superior soft tissue characterization and can precisely determine the relationship of masses to the uterine cavity—critical for surgical planning. In this case, MRI refined the differential diagnosis by revealing thick walls, inhomogeneous fluid content, and solid components that were not fully appreciated on ultrasound.

Regarding safety parameters, MRI should be performed without gadolinium contrast, as gadolinium crosses the placenta and has been associated with rheumatological and inflammatory conditions in the fetus, as well as potential risks of stillbirth. The field strength should not exceed 1.5 Tesla, and imaging is considered reasonably safe after the first trimester.

The key clinical principle is that these modalities are complementary: ultrasound for initial assessment, and non-contrast MRI when diagnostic uncertainty persists or when surgical planning requires precise anatomic delineation.
