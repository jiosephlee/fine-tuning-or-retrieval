Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Management] What are the indications for performing myomectomy during pregnancy, and what surgical principles should be followed to minimize obstetric risk?

**Indications for Myomectomy During Pregnancy**

Myomectomy during pregnancy is an exceptional procedure that should only be considered when conservative management has failed or when specific complications arise. The general principle is that the benefits of surgical intervention must clearly outweigh the substantial risks of pregnancy loss, preterm labor, hemorrhage, and uterine rupture.

The primary indications include:
- **Torsion of a pedunculated myoma** — this represents a surgical emergency
- **Severe refractory symptoms** — persistent pain unresponsive to analgesics and conservative measures
- **Mass effect causing significant clinical compromise** — such as the restrictive lung disease and dyspnea described in this case
- **Diagnostic uncertainty with concern for malignancy** — when imaging cannot definitively exclude an ovarian or other neoplasm

In the presented case, the decision to operate was driven by multiple factors: the acute clinical deterioration with severe pain and mechanical dyspnea, the inability to exclude malignancy given the imaging characteristics and elevated CA-125, and the significant risk of complications including the torsion that was ultimately discovered intraoperatively.

**Surgical Principles to Minimize Obstetric Risk**

Several critical principles guided the surgical management in this case and should guide any myomectomy during pregnancy:

**Patient selection and surgical restraint.** Only myomas that absolutely require removal should be addressed. The surgical team demonstrated this principle by resecting only the pedunculated, torsed superior myoma while leaving the caudal intramural myoma in situ. Resection of intramural myomas carries substantially higher risks of hemorrhage, uterine rupture, and pregnancy loss.

**Gentle tissue handling.** The pedunculated myoma was carefully excised from its uterine implant using a double clamp technique with a single absorbable suture to close the base. Minimizing uterine manipulation and avoiding excessive traction reduces the risk of uterine irritability and preterm contractions.

**Fetal monitoring.** Continuous assessment of fetal heart rate before, during, and after the procedure is mandatory. The case describes serial transvaginal ultrasound monitoring throughout the operation.

**Tocolytic prophylaxis.** Progesterone supplementation was administered intraoperatively and continued postoperatively. While evidence varies, progesterone support is commonly used to reduce uterine contractility.

**Operative efficiency.** The surgery lasted approximately two and a half hours. Prolonged operative time increases fetal risk, and the procedure should be as brief as technically feasible.

**Delivery planning.** The presence of a uterine scar necessitates careful delivery planning. In this case, an elective cesarean at 37 weeks was chosen to minimize the risk of uterine rupture during labor, given both the myomectomy site and the obstructing pelvic myoma.

The excellent maternal and neonatal outcome in this case underscores that with careful patient selection, adherence to surgical principles, and multidisciplinary coordination, myomectomy during pregnancy can be performed safely when truly indicated.
