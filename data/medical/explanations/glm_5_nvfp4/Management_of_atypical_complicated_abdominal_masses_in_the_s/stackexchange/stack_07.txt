Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Management] In a pregnant patient with a pelvic mass that could obstruct labor, what factors influence the decision between planned cesarean delivery versus trial of labor?

### Clinical Teaching Answer

The decision between planned cesarean delivery and trial of labor in a pregnant patient with a pelvic mass centers on three critical factors: **anatomic location relative to the birth canal, size of the mass, and potential for obstruction during labor.**

**Anatomic location is paramount.** Masses located in the lower uterine segment, cervix, or pouch of Douglas—as in this case, where the caudal mass filled the pouch of Douglas and was adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal leiomyoma—are most likely to obstruct fetal descent. The authors specifically noted that this mass "would have represented a huge obstacle for the fetal head progression during the expulsive phase of labour." In contrast, fundal or anterior wall myomas are less likely to interfere with vaginal delivery.

**Size matters.** While no absolute cutoff exists, masses exceeding 5–7 cm in the lower pelvis warrant careful evaluation. This patient's caudal mass measured approximately 9–10 cm and remained fixed, creating a mechanical barrier that would prevent fetal head engagement and progression.

**Mobility and fixity** influence decision-making. A mobile adnexal cyst may be pushed aside during labor, whereas a fixed mass—like the firm, fixed pelvic mass identified on bimanual examination in this case—cannot be displaced and creates a fixed obstruction.

Additional considerations include:
- **Associated surgical history**—this patient already had a laparotomy with myomectomy at 21 weeks, creating a uterine scar that increased the risk of rupture with labor
- **Number of masses**—multiple large masses complicate both the delivery plan and potential surgical approach
- **Gestational timing of diagnosis**—allowing planning for elective delivery rather than emergent intervention

In this case, the multidisciplinary team appropriately planned an elective cesarean at 37 weeks via the existing surgical incision. The decision was straightforward: a fixed, large pelvic mass obstructing the birth canal combined with a prior uterine scar made trial of labor inadvisable. The key clinical principle is that **cesarean delivery is indicated when a pelvic mass is fixed, located in the lower uterine segment or pouch of Douglas, and of sufficient size to prevent fetal descent.**
