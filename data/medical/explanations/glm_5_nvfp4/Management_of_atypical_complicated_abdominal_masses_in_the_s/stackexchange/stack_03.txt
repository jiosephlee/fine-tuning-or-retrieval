Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Pathophysiology] How do uterine leiomyomas typically change in size during pregnancy and the postpartum period, and what factors influence these changes?

### Clinical Teaching Answer

Uterine leiomyomas demonstrate a dynamic and non-linear pattern of size change throughout pregnancy and the puerperium, driven primarily by hormonal influences and vascular adaptations.

**Growth During Pregnancy:**
Leiomyomas typically increase in size during pregnancy, with the greatest growth rate occurring in the first trimester. As cited in this case, leiomyomas grow by approximately 6.12% between the first and second trimesters, slowing to 3.10% between the second and third trimesters. This early rapid growth corresponds to peak estrogen and progesterone levels, which stimulate smooth muscle hypertrophy and cellular proliferation. However, not all myomas enlarge—some remain stable or even regress, depending on their hormonal receptor density and vascular supply.

**Postpartum Regression:**
Following delivery, leiomyomas typically involute alongside the uterus. The case notes a mean reduction of approximately 44% by six weeks postpartum. In our patient, the residual pelvic myoma decreased from approximately 9.5 cm to 7.5 cm after delivery, demonstrating this expected partial regression. This involution occurs as hormonal stimulation withdraws and the uterus undergoes ischemic remodeling.

**Factors Influencing Size Changes:**
- **Hormonal responsiveness:** Myomas with higher estrogen and progesterone receptor expression tend to grow more during pregnancy.
- **Size and location:** Larger myomas and those with a tenuous vascular supply (particularly pedunculated lesions, as in this case) are more susceptible to outgrowing their blood supply.
- **Degeneration:** Rapid growth can exceed vascular capacity, leading to ischemic changes. Our patient presented with a twisted, pedunculated myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration and ischaemic necrosis—complications that may manifest clinically as acute abdominal pain and low-grade fever.

These dynamics underscore why conservative management is generally preferred during pregnancy, reserving surgical intervention for complications such as torsion, severe pain refractory to medical therapy, or diagnostic uncertainty—as illustrated by this case.
