Title: Blog about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

# The Great Mimicker: When Leiomyomas Masquerade as Adnexal Masses in Pregnancy

## The Setup That Fooled Everyone

Here's a case that will make you humble about your ultrasound skills. A woman in her 40s, 21 weeks pregnant, presents with severe abdominal pain and dyspnea. Ultrasound shows two enormous cystic masses—an 18 cm lesion pushing up against her diaphragm and a 9.5 cm mass in the pouch of Douglas. The imaging team calls them as decidualized endometriomas. CA-125 is elevated and trending upward. The multidisciplinary team gears up for what they think are ovarian masses requiring surgical intervention.

Then comes the laparotomy. Surprise: both masses are uterine leiomyomas. The upper one is a pedunculated myoma that has twisted on its stalk and started leaking—explaining her low-grade fever. What looked like classic adnexal pathology on imaging was actually fibroids undergoing cystic degeneration.

This case is a perfect reminder that in pregnancy, fibroids don't just grow—they transform, degenerate, and occasionally become nearly unrecognizable on imaging.

## Why Pregnancy Makes This So Hard

The gravid uterus creates what the authors call "steric bulk"—essentially crowding the abdomen and pushing everything into unusual positions. That 18 cm mass wasn't just sitting in the left upper quadrant for fun; it had been pushed there by the expanding uterus. When you're scanning a pregnant patient with a large fibroid, the mass can end up in locations that make zero anatomical sense for a uterine lesion.

Adding to the confusion, leiomyomas in pregnancy undergo cystic degeneration. The increased vascularity and hormonal stimulation turn solid fibroids into complex cystic structures with thick walls, inhomogeneous fluid content, and even solid components. On ultrasound, this can look almost identical to an endometrioma or a complex ovarian cyst.

And here's the kicker: the pedunculated nature of some myomas means they can detach entirely from the uterine wall in terms of visible connection. Without a clear stalk or feeding vessel on imaging, you're left guessing about the origin.

## The CA-125 Trap

This case highlights something every emergency physician and obstetrician should know: CA-125 is nearly useless as a standalone marker in pregnancy.

During the first trimester, CA-125 peaks at a median of 23 U/mL. It drops in the second trimester to around 14 U/mL, then rises again near term to about 21 U/mL. This patient had a level of 41.8 U/mL at 21 weeks—clearly elevated for her gestational age, but here's the problem: elevated CA-125 in pregnancy can mean endometriosis, adenomyosis, myomas, or just normal physiological changes.

Serial measurements showing an upward trend (as in this case, from 36 to 41.8 U/mL over two weeks) can provide useful information, but they don't clinch a diagnosis. An isolated elevated CA-125 in a pregnant woman should not push you toward surgery on its own.

## MRI: Worth the Trip

When ultrasound leaves you scratching your head, non-contrast MRI is your friend. It's considered safe after the first trimester (stick to 1.5-T scanners), and it excels at determining the relationship between a mass and the uterine wall—precisely what ultrasound struggles with when the gravid uterus is in the way.

In this case, MRI correctly identified that the caudal mass was continuous with a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma. But even MRI got fooled on the cranial mass, which lacked a visible stalk due to its enormous size and position. The lesson: MRI improves your diagnostic accuracy, but it doesn't guarantee you'll get the diagnosis right preoperatively.

## When to Operate During Pregnancy

This team made a reasonable call to operate. The indications were solid: worsening pain, dyspnea from diaphragmatic compression, risk of torsion or rupture, and uncertainty about the nature of the masses. They also recognized that the uterus would only keep growing, potentially making things worse.

The surgical approach was appropriately conservative. They removed only the pedunculated, twisted myoma—the one causing acute complications. The fixed, intramural caudal myoma was left in situ to minimize obstetric risk. They used intraoperative fetal monitoring, gave progesterone prophylaxis, and kept the surgery under two and a half hours.

This is the model for pregnancy surgery: intervene for complications or diagnostic uncertainty, but preserve as much as you can.

## Key Takeaways

- **Fibroids are the great mimicker in pregnancy.** Cystic degeneration can make them look like ovarian pathology, and pedunculated lesions can migrate far from the uterus.
- **CA-125 is not reliable in pregnancy.** It rises physiologically in the first and third trimesters and can be elevated in benign conditions like fibroids and endometriosis.
- **Non-contrast MRI after the first trimester is your best second-line imaging.** It helps determine the relationship between masses and the uterine wall when ultrasound windows are limited.
- **Surgery during pregnancy should be reserved for complications or oncologic concern.** When you operate, remove only what's necessary and leave the rest for postpartum management.
- **Even excellent imaging can be wrong.** Counsel patients that preoperative diagnosis carries uncertainty, and surgical planning may need to adapt based on intraoperative findings.

## The Bottom Line

This case is a humbling reminder that pregnancy changes everything—physiology, imaging windows, tumor marker interpretation, and surgical risk calculations. A 40-year-old nulliparous woman with abdominal masses and elevated CA-125 looks like ovarian cancer on paper, but in pregnancy, fibroids with cystic degeneration can create the exact same picture. Comprehensive evaluation with bimanual examination, ultrasound, and non-contrast MRI is essential, but even then, you may not know the answer until you're in the operating room. The goal is always to preserve the pregnancy while addressing complications, which means operating when you need to but leaving as much as possible alone.



# CA-125 in Pregnancy: Navigating the False Positives and False Reassurance

## The Setup

Picture this: a nulliparous woman in her 40s, 21 weeks pregnant, presenting with severe abdominal pain and dyspnea. Ultrasound reveals two large abdominal masses—one 18 cm lesion occupying the left subphrenic space and another 9 cm mass in the pouch of Douglas. Your mind immediately jumps to the "dreaded differential": ovarian malignancy? Decidualized endometriomas? Degenerating leiomyomas? You order tumor markers, and CA-125 comes back elevated at 41.8 U/mL, up from 36 U/mL just two weeks prior. Now what?

Here's the twist: despite the concerning imaging and mildly elevated tumor marker, both masses turned out to be benign uterine leiomyomas—one of which had undergone torsion and ischemic necrosis, explaining her fever and pain. The patient underwent successful myomectomy at 21+6 weeks, carried the pregnancy to term, and delivered a healthy baby via elective cesarean. This case perfectly illustrates why CA-125 in pregnancy can be a minefield of misleading information.

## The Physiology Nobody Taught Us

Here's the uncomfortable truth about CA-125 in pregnancy: it physiologically peaks not once, but twice. The first trimester median is around 23 U/mL, dipping to approximately 14 U/mL in the second trimester, then climbing again to a median of 21 U/mL in the third trimester. These values come from large population studies and represent normal physiological variation driven by the decidua, peritoneal irritation, and uterine expansion.

The standard oncological cutoff of ≥35 U/mL for ovarian cancer screening was never validated in pregnant patients, yet we continue to apply it. In this case, the patient's CA-125 of 41.8 U/mL exceeded that arbitrary threshold, but the actual pathology was a twisted pedunculated leiomyoma with necrotic changes—not malignancy. The marker was doing exactly what inflammatory markers do in the setting of tissue ischemia and irritation: it was elevated for a completely benign reason.

## The Benign Mimics

The differential for elevated CA-125 in pregnancy reads like a who's who of benign gynecologic conditions. Endometriosis and adenomyosis regularly produce values above 19 U/mL. Pelvic inflammatory disease, menstruation, and even risk of miscarriage can drive elevations. Uterine leiomyomas themselves are associated with elevated CA-125, though typically below 19 U/mL threshold mentioned in literature. But here's the rub: when a leiomyoma undergoes cystic degeneration, torsion, or necrosis—as happened in this case—the surrounding inflammatory response can push CA-125 significantly higher.

The case authors wisely noted that isolated measurements are essentially useless. A single elevated CA-125 tells you only that there is a process causing peritoneal or decidual irritation. Serial trending, on the other hand, can provide useful information about trajectory. In this patient, the CA-125 rose from 36 to 41.8 U/mL over two weeks—not a dramatic surge suggestive of aggressive malignancy, but also not reassuringly stable. The trend existed in a gray zone that demanded imaging and clinical correlation rather than reflexive surgery.

## Imaging Correlation Is Everything

This case underscores why ultrasound and non-contrast MRI remain the cornerstones of mass characterization in pregnancy. The initial ultrasound suggested two decidualized endometriomas, with the caudal mass appearing adherent to an intramural-subserosal leiomyoma. MRI without gadolinium provided superior anatomical detail, demonstrating thick walls, inhomogeneous fluid content, and focal solid components—features that raised concern but ultimately reflected benign degenerative changes.

The imaging team correctly avoided gadolinium-enhanced MRI, which carries risks of rheumatological and inflammatory skin conditions as well as potential fetal harm. Non-contrast 1.5-T MRI performed after the first trimester is considered reasonably safe and provided sufficient information to guide surgical planning. The key was integrating imaging findings with clinical presentation rather than allowing an isolated tumor marker to dictate management.

## When to Operate

The decision to proceed with myomectomy at 21+6 weeks was not driven by the CA-125 elevation alone. The multidisciplinary team identified multiple compelling indications: worsening clinical symptoms despite analgesia, mechanical dyspnea from mass effect, the substantial size of the masses with risk of further growth, and concern for complications including rupture, torsion, and infection. The low-grade fever and documented Klebsiella pneumoniae urinary infection added complexity, though the fever ultimately reflected torsion of the pedunculated myoma.

The surgical approach was deliberately conservative. The cranial mass—the pedunculated, twisted leiomyoma—was excised. The caudal mass, arising from the uterine torus, was left in situ to minimize obstetric risk. Progesterone prophylaxis was administered intraoperatively and continued postoperatively, with continuous fetal monitoring throughout the procedure. This selective approach preserved the pregnancy while addressing the symptomatic lesion.

## Clinical Pearls

The authors make several evidence-based points worth committing to memory. First, leiomyomas in pregnancy grow approximately 6.90% per week on average, with growth velocity highest in the first trimester. However, they often regress significantly postpartum—by a mean of 44% at six weeks after delivery. This means that expectant management is reasonable for asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic lesions.

Second, myomectomy during pregnancy should be reserved for specific circumstances: torsion of pedunculated myomas, persistent severe symptoms unresponsive to conservative management, or diagnostic uncertainty with concerning imaging features. The risks of severe bleeding, pregnancy loss, preterm labor, and uterine rupture are real and must be weighed against the anticipated benefits.

Third, the mode of delivery should account for the size, number, and location of uterine masses. In this case, the caudal myoma would have obstructed fetal descent, making elective cesarean at term the appropriate choice.

## Key Take-Home Points

- CA-125 physiologically peaks in the first and third trimesters of pregnancy (medians ~23 and ~21 U/mL respectively), making isolated elevations nearly impossible to interpret without clinical context.
- Benign conditions including endometriosis, adenomyosis, leiomyomas, pelvic inflammatory disease, and even normal pregnancy can elevate CA-125 above the standard oncologic cutoff of 35 U/mL.
- Serial trending of CA-125 provides more useful information than a single measurement, though even trends must be interpreted alongside imaging and clinical findings.
- Non-contrast 1.5-T MRI after the first trimester is the imaging modality of choice for complex pelvic masses in pregnancy when ultrasound is insufficient.
- Surgical intervention for uterine or adnexal masses during pregnancy should be reserved for cases with oncologic suspicion, acute complications such as torsion or rupture, or refractory symptoms despite maximal conservative management.
- Postpartum regression of leiomyomas is common and substantial, supporting expectant management when clinically appropriate.

This case serves as a humbling reminder that tumor markers in pregnancy demand interpretation through a completely different lens than in the non-gravid population. The elevated CA-125 was real, the masses were real, and the clinical presentation was concerning—but the pathology was entirely benign. By integrating serial marker trends with careful imaging and multidisciplinary assessment, the team achieved an excellent outcome for both mother and baby without overtreatment or unnecessary delay.



# Cutting During Pregnancy: When Surgery Beats Expectant Management for Abdominal Masses

## The Surprise in the Operating Room

Picture this: you're in the OR for a 21-week pregnant patient with two massive abdominal cystic masses. Your preoperative imaging—ultrasound and MRI—suggested bilateral decidualized endometriomas. You've counseled the patient about the risks, obtained consent, and you open the abdomen. What you find isn't endometriomas at all. The cranial mass is a pedunculated, twisted, leaking leiomyoma that likely explains her recent fever. This case is a masterclass in how imaging can mislead, how surgical indications shift during pregnancy, and why a disciplined, stepwise approach to abdominal masses in the gravid patient matters.

## The Clinical Picture

This nulliparous woman in her 40s presented at 21 weeks gestation with acute, severe abdominal pain (VAS 5/10) in both the upper left quadrant and pelvis, plus mild dyspnea. Her abdomen was more distended than expected for gestational age, with a tender 18-cm mass filling the upper left quadrant. Bimanual exam revealed a fixed, hard 9-cm pelvic mass bulging at the posterior fornix.

She developed low-grade fever during admission. Urine culture grew Klebsiella pneumoniae. Labs showed iron deficiency anemia (Hb 9.9 g/L) and leukocytosis consistent with pregnancy-related changes.

Imaging revealed two large unilocular cystic masses. The upper lesion measured 18×12×7 cm, extending from the left subphrenic region to the uterine fundus. The lower lesion was 9.5×8×8 cm, adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma. Non-contrast MRI showed thick walls, inhomogeneous fluid content, and focal solid components. CA-125 was slightly elevated at 41.8 U/mL with an increasing trend, but all other tumor markers were normal.

## The Decision Matrix: When to Cut

Here's where clinical judgment gets tested. The default approach for myomas during pregnancy is conservative management—surgery carries risks of bleeding, pregnancy loss, preterm labor, and uterine rupture. But this case pushed past the threshold for expectant management.

The team identified several surgical indications. First, the clinical trajectory was worsening—persistent pain and dyspnea from mechanical compression that only partially responded to analgesics and positioning. Second, mass characteristics raised red flags—huge, vascularized masses with mixed solid-cystic components and elevated CA-125 trend, which heightened concern for malignancy despite the limitations of tumor markers in pregnancy. Third, complication risk was real—torsion, rupture, and infection were all on the table, and indeed the operative findings confirmed a twisted pedicle. Fourth, uterine real estate was limited—as the pregnancy advanced, the growing uterus would compete with these masses for space, increasing hypoxia, necrosis, and degeneration risk.

The timing matters too. Second trimester surgery, after organogenesis is complete and before the uterus becomes too large, represents the safest window. This patient went to the OR at 21+6 weeks.

## What Surgery Revealed

The cranial mass was a highly vascularized pedunculated leiomyoma, twisted and leaking—likely the source of her fever. No wonder the imaging was confusing. The team excised it from its uterine implantation using a double clamp technique, with careful closure of the stalk. The caudal mass, a large cystic intramural-subserosal leiomyoma, was left in situ to minimize obstetric risk. Total operative time: 2 hours 25 minutes.

Intraoperative progesterone prophylaxis (100 mg IM) was continued postoperatively, then switched to hydroxyprogesterone caproate every 3 days until delivery. Fetal heart rate was monitored throughout.

Postoperative anemia required intravenous iron and two blood transfusions, raising Hb from 7.5 to 10.0 g/L. Pathology confirmed benign leiomyomas with areas of ischemic necrosis near the twisted pedicle.

## The CA-125 Trap

This case reinforces a critical point: CA-125 is notoriously unreliable during pregnancy. Physiological peaks occur in the first and third trimesters (medians 23 and 21 U/mL respectively), with a second-trimester median around 14 U/mL. This patient's value of 41.8 U/mL was above expected, but isolated measurements are essentially meaningless. Serial trends might help, but even then, CA-125 can be elevated in endometriosis, adenomyosis, myomas, pelvic inflammatory disease, and normal pregnancy. Don't let a single tumor marker value drive your surgical decision-making in a pregnant patient.

## Delivery Planning

The caudal myoma remained as a pelvic obstacle, which would have blocked fetal head descent. An elective cesarean at 37 weeks through the same surgical scar delivered excellent maternal and neonatal outcomes. The residual myoma has since regressed slightly to 7.5 cm, and the patient remains asymptomatic.

## Key Take-Home Points

- **Default to conservative management for uterine myomas in pregnancy**, but recognize surgical indications: worsening symptoms, large size limiting uterine growth, signs of torsion or degeneration, and concern for malignancy based on imaging characteristics and tumor marker trends.
- **Second trimester is the optimal surgical window**—organogenesis complete, uterus manageable, preterm labor risk lowest relative to third trimester.
- **Non-contrast MRI is your imaging workhorse**—avoid gadolinium unless absolutely necessary, and keep field strength ≤1.5 T after the first trimester.
- **CA-125 values in pregnancy are a minefield**—they rise physiologically and in benign conditions; never make surgical decisions based on isolated values.
- **Multidisciplinary planning is non-negotiable**—involve obstetrics, gynecologic oncology, anesthesia, and neonatology.
- **Intraoperative fetal monitoring and progesterone prophylaxis** support pregnancy preservation during myomectomy.
- **Delivery mode depends on mass characteristics**—obstructing myomas warrant cesarean; location, size, and number all factor into the plan.

When the stakes are this high—one patient, two lives—there's no substitute for systematic evaluation, honest risk-benefit conversations, and surgical precision timed to give both mother and baby the best shot at an excellent outcome.



# Myomectomy in Pregnancy: Technical Pearls and Perioperative Prophylaxis

What makes this case from De Nola and colleagues so instructive is the intraoperative surprise: what preoperative imaging had convincingly suggested were two large adnexal masses—possibly decidualized endometriomas—turned out to be something entirely different. The 18-centimeter cranial mass was actually a pedunculated, cystic leiomyoma that had twisted and was actively leaking, likely explaining the patient's low-grade fever. This diagnostic curveball highlights just how challenging it can be to characterize pelvic masses during pregnancy, and more importantly, it provides a masterclass in how to safely proceed when surgery becomes necessary.

The key technical lesson here is straightforward: **pedunculated myomas are the most surgically amenable lesions during pregnancy.** The surgical team was able to gently excise the mass from its uterine implant using a double clamp technique, carefully ligating the stalk and closing the exposed root with a single absorbable suture. This approach minimizes intraoperative bleeding and preserves the integrity of the uterine wall—both critical considerations when you're operating on a gravid uterus. The caudal mass, originating from the uterine torus and fixed in the pelvis, was wisely left in situ to minimize obstetric risk. The lesson is clear: if you're going to operate, be selective.

## Intraoperative Fetal Monitoring and Progesterone Prophylaxis

The team employed several critical safety measures that deserve attention. Fetal heart rate was monitored before, during, and after the procedure—serial and intermittent transvaginal ultrasound assessments documented fetal viability throughout. This isn't just good practice; it's essential. As Zhao and colleagues demonstrated in their large multicenter study, documenting fetal heart rate at all three time points is mandatory.

Progesterone prophylaxis was initiated intraoperatively and continued through discharge, with intramuscular hydroxyprogesterone caproate administered every three days until delivery. While the evidence base for progesterone as tocolysis in this specific context remains limited, the logic is sound: any uterine manipulation carries theoretical risk of irritation and contractility, and progesterone is reasonably safe in pregnancy. The clinical outcome here was excellent, though we should acknowledge this represents a single case rather than definitive evidence.

## Approach Selection: Laparotomy vs. Laparoscopy

The decision to perform laparotomy rather than laparoscopy in this case deserves examination, particularly given the increasing acceptance of minimally invasive approaches. Several factors favored the open approach here: the massive size of the masses (18 cm and 9 cm), the patient's obesity with BMI of 33, the uncertain diagnosis with potential oncological concern, and the gestational age of nearly 22 weeks. The surgical literature suggests that laparoscopy with an open Hasson technique is preferable in earlier gestational ages with smaller volumes and favorable myoma locations—not intramural, preferably pedunculated. This case represents the kind of selection bias that naturally pushes complex presentations toward laparotomy, and that's not necessarily wrong.

## Postoperative Considerations

The postoperative course illustrates the expected challenges. The patient developed worsening anemia with hemoglobin dropping to 7.5 g/L, requiring two units of blood and intravenous iron supplementation. She also had a concurrent Klebsiella pneumoniae urinary tract infection that guided postoperative antibiotic selection. These aren't complications of the surgery itself so much as expected challenges when you're operating on someone with significant comorbidities in a physiologically demanding state. Anticipate blood loss, have crossmatched blood available, and don't forget to address concurrent infections.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Pedunculated myomas are the safest targets for myomectomy during pregnancy**—they can be clamped, ligated, and removed without violating the uterine wall.
- **Document fetal heart rate before, during, and after surgery**—this is non-negotiable standard of care.
- **Progesterone prophylaxis is reasonable but not definitively evidence-based**—consider it as part of your tocolytic strategy when uterine manipulation is significant.
- **Approach selection should be individualized**—laparoscopy may be appropriate for smaller, pedunculated lesions in early pregnancy; laparotomy remains reasonable for massive, complex, or uncertain masses.
- **Anticipate postoperative anemia and have transfusion protocols ready**, particularly when operating on patients with baseline iron deficiency.
- **Leave posterior, intramural, or isthmic lesions in situ** unless absolutely necessary—they're technically challenging and carry unacceptable obstetric risk.
- **Non-contrast MRI after the first trimester is your friend** when ultrasound is inconclusive about mass origin or characteristics.

The authors' decision to perform elective cesarean delivery at 37 weeks was also strategically sound—the caudal myoma, left in situ, would have obstructed fetal head descent during vaginal labor. Operating through the same surgical scar minimized additional tissue trauma. The patient is now asymptomatic, breastfeeding, and the residual myoma has partially regressed postpartum, reminding us that conservative management of non-obstructing myomas is often the right call. Sometimes the best operation is the one you don't perform.



# Delivery Dilemmas: Planning Childbirth Around Residual Pelvic Masses

## The Case That Makes You Rethink Your Delivery Plan

Picture this: a woman in her 40s, primigravida, second trimester. She's got two massive abdominal masses—one pushing up against her diaphragm causing dyspnea, another filling the pouch of Douglas. You take the cranial one out at 22 weeks because it's twisted and leaking. The caudal one? You leave it in situ. Now what? How do you plan delivery when there's a 9-centimeter mass sitting at the posterior isthmus of the uterus?

This case from De Nola and colleagues presents a fascinating surgical and obstetric puzzle. The cranial mass turned out to be a pedunculated leiomyoma that had undergone torsion—explaining the patient's low-grade fever during admission. But the real decision-making challenge came later: what to do about the residual pelvic mass and how to deliver safely.

## Location, Location, Location

Here's the critical anatomy lesson. This caudal myoma wasn't just any fibroid—it was fixed, originating from the uterine torus in a posterior isthmic location. That's a problem. A mass of that size (initially 9 cm, still 7.5 cm postpartum) positioned at the lower uterine segment would physically block fetal head descent during labor. This wasn't a theoretical concern; it was a mechanical impossibility.

The authors made the right call planning an elective cesarean. But the timing mattered too. They delivered at exactly 37 weeks, deliberately using the same surgical scar from the myomectomy. This approach minimizes the risk of uterine rupture by avoiding a second weak point in the uterine wall. When you've already got one scar from a second-trimester myomectomy, you work with what you have.

## The Natural History of Fibroids in Pregnancy

One of the most reassuring clinical pearls from this case is what happened postpartum. The residual myoma decreased from 9 cm to 7.5 cm, and the patient remains asymptomatic. This isn't anecdotal—research shows that myoma size decreases by a mean of 44% by 6 weeks postpartum. The growth pattern during pregnancy is non-linear: fibroids grow faster in the first trimester (about 6% per week) compared to later gestation, and then they regress significantly after delivery.

This matters because it supports expectant management of residual disease. The patient in this case opted against further surgery, citing symptom absence and "fulfillment of motherhood." That's a reasonable choice. Why re-operate on an asymptomatic fibroid that's already shrinking?

## Surgical Considerations During Pregnancy

The decision to operate at 22 weeks wasn't taken lightly. The indications were clear: worsening pain, restrictive dyspnea from mass effect, and concern for complications like torsion, rupture, or infection. The surgical team chose a longitudinal xipho-pubic laparotomy, which makes sense for masses of this magnitude—18 cm cranial and 9 cm caudal. Laparoscopy has its place for smaller, pedunculated fibroids earlier in pregnancy, but this case involved bulk and complexity that favored open surgery.

Intraoperative fetal monitoring and progesterone prophylaxis (100 mg IM intraoperatively, then continued) are key technical points. The authors also continued hydroxyprogesterone caproate every 3 days until delivery—a strategy to reduce preterm labor risk, though the evidence base varies by institution.

## Key Take-Home Points

- **Posterior isthmic myomas can obstruct labor**: Location matters as much as size when planning delivery. A fixed mass at the lower uterine segment is a mechanical barrier to vaginal birth.
- **Timing matters after myomectomy**: Elective cesarean at 37 weeks balances fetal maturity against uterine rupture risk. Using the existing surgical scar avoids creating additional weak points.
- **Expect postpartum regression**: Fibroids shrink significantly after delivery—about 44% on average. Asymptomatic residual myomas may not require additional intervention.
- **CA-125 interpretation during pregnancy is tricky**: This marker rises physiologically in the first and third trimesters (median 21-23 U/mL). A value of 41.8 U/mL in the second trimester raised suspicion, but CA-125 can be elevated in endometriosis, adenomyosis, and even fibroids—not just malignancy.
- **Multidisciplinary decision-making is essential**: This case involved surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians weighing oncologic concern, obstetric risk, and symptom burden together.

The bottom line is that delivery planning after second-trimester myomectomy requires you to think several moves ahead. You're not just removing a mass; you're creating a uterine scar, managing residual disease, and planning a safe delivery route—all while the pregnancy continues to evolve. This case demonstrates that with careful timing, deliberate surgical technique, and realistic expectations about postpartum fibroid behavior, excellent maternal and neonatal outcomes are achievable.
