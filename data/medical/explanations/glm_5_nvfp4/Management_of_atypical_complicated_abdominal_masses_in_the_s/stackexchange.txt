Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

### [Clinical Pearls] How does serum CA-125 normally change throughout pregnancy, and what benign and malignant conditions can cause elevated CA-125 beyond pregnancy-related changes?

### Clinical Answer

**Normal physiologic changes in CA-125 during pregnancy:**

Serum CA-125 follows a biphasic pattern during normal pregnancy. Levels peak during the first trimester (median approximately 23 U/mL) due to shedding of decidual cells from the uterine lining. Values then decline to their nadir in the second trimester (median approximately 14 U/mL), before rising again to a second, smaller peak in the third trimester (median approximately 21 U/mL), likely reflecting mesothelial irritation from uterine enlargement and increased peritoneal surface area.

**Benign conditions causing elevated CA-125:**

Beyond physiologic pregnancy changes, numerous benign gynecologic and non-gynecologic conditions can elevate CA-125:

- **Endometriosis and adenomyosis** (typically >19 U/mL)
- **Pelvic inflammatory disease**
- **Menstruation** (transient elevation)
- **Uterine leiomyomas** (though typically lower, cut-off <19 U/mL)
- **Threatened miscarriage or pregnancy loss**
- **Benign ovarian cysts**, including corpus luteum cysts
- **Peritoneal irritation** from any cause (e.g., ascites, pancreatitis, heart failure)

**Malignant conditions causing elevated CA-125:**

- **Epithelial ovarian cancer** (most common indication; standard cut-off ≥35 U/mL)
- **Endometrial carcinoma**
- **Fallopian tube carcinoma**
- **Primary peritoneal carcinoma**
- **Some non-gynecologic malignancies** (pancreatic, breast, lung, colorectal)

**Clinical application from this case:**

In our patient presenting at 21 weeks gestation, CA-125 was 41.8 U/mL—substantially elevated compared to the expected second-trimester median of 14 U/mL. The differential diagnosis included decidualized endometriomas versus cystic degenerating leiomyomas. The slight CA-125 elevation initially favored endometrioma, though final pathology confirmed benign uterine leiomyomas.

The key teaching point is that isolated CA-125 measurements during pregnancy are of limited diagnostic value given the wide physiologic variation. Serial measurements demonstrating rising trends may be more informative, as seen in this case (increase from 36 to 41.8 U/mL over two weeks). Clinical decision-making should prioritize imaging (ultrasound and non-contrast MRI) and symptom assessment over tumor marker interpretation alone.

### [Morning Report] What imaging modalities are considered safe and appropriate for evaluating abdominal masses during pregnancy, and what are their respective diagnostic advantages?

### Clinical Teaching Answer

When evaluating abdominal masses during pregnancy, we are limited to two primary imaging modalities: **ultrasound** and **non-contrast MRI**. Both are considered safe, while CT and contrast-enhanced MRI should be avoided.

**Ultrasound** is the first-line modality. It uses no ionizing radiation and is widely available. As demonstrated in this case, ultrasound effectively identified two large cystic masses, characterized their size and vascularity via color Doppler, and established the relationship between the caudal mass and a posterior uterine leiomyoma. Ultrasound is particularly valuable for anterior, submucosal, or pedunculated leiomyomas. However, its limitations include operator dependence and reduced visualization when masses are large, posterior, or deep within the pelvis—especially given the "steric bulk" of the gravid uterus noted in this patient.

**Non-contrast MRI** is the second-line modality when ultrasound is inconclusive or when greater anatomic precision is required. It offers superior soft tissue characterization and can precisely determine the relationship of masses to the uterine cavity—critical for surgical planning. In this case, MRI refined the differential diagnosis by revealing thick walls, inhomogeneous fluid content, and solid components that were not fully appreciated on ultrasound.

Regarding safety parameters, MRI should be performed without gadolinium contrast, as gadolinium crosses the placenta and has been associated with rheumatological and inflammatory conditions in the fetus, as well as potential risks of stillbirth. The field strength should not exceed 1.5 Tesla, and imaging is considered reasonably safe after the first trimester.

The key clinical principle is that these modalities are complementary: ultrasound for initial assessment, and non-contrast MRI when diagnostic uncertainty persists or when surgical planning requires precise anatomic delineation.

### [Pathophysiology] How do uterine leiomyomas typically change in size during pregnancy and the postpartum period, and what factors influence these changes?

### Clinical Teaching Answer

Uterine leiomyomas demonstrate a dynamic and non-linear pattern of size change throughout pregnancy and the puerperium, driven primarily by hormonal influences and vascular adaptations.

**Growth During Pregnancy:**
Leiomyomas typically increase in size during pregnancy, with the greatest growth rate occurring in the first trimester. As cited in this case, leiomyomas grow by approximately 6.12% between the first and second trimesters, slowing to 3.10% between the second and third trimesters. This early rapid growth corresponds to peak estrogen and progesterone levels, which stimulate smooth muscle hypertrophy and cellular proliferation. However, not all myomas enlarge—some remain stable or even regress, depending on their hormonal receptor density and vascular supply.

**Postpartum Regression:**
Following delivery, leiomyomas typically involute alongside the uterus. The case notes a mean reduction of approximately 44% by six weeks postpartum. In our patient, the residual pelvic myoma decreased from approximately 9.5 cm to 7.5 cm after delivery, demonstrating this expected partial regression. This involution occurs as hormonal stimulation withdraws and the uterus undergoes ischemic remodeling.

**Factors Influencing Size Changes:**
- **Hormonal responsiveness:** Myomas with higher estrogen and progesterone receptor expression tend to grow more during pregnancy.
- **Size and location:** Larger myomas and those with a tenuous vascular supply (particularly pedunculated lesions, as in this case) are more susceptible to outgrowing their blood supply.
- **Degeneration:** Rapid growth can exceed vascular capacity, leading to ischemic changes. Our patient presented with a twisted, pedunculated myoma exhibiting cystic degeneration and ischaemic necrosis—complications that may manifest clinically as acute abdominal pain and low-grade fever.

These dynamics underscore why conservative management is generally preferred during pregnancy, reserving surgical intervention for complications such as torsion, severe pain refractory to medical therapy, or diagnostic uncertainty—as illustrated by this case.

### [Differential Diagnosis] What clinical and imaging features help differentiate between a decidualized endometrioma and a cystic degenerating leiomyoma in a pregnant patient?

### Clinical Teaching Answer

Differentiating between a decidualized endometrioma and a cystic degenerating leiomyoma in pregnancy is challenging, as both can present as complex adnexal masses with mixed solid-cystic features. However, several imaging and clinical characteristics can help distinguish them.

**Anatomic Relationship to the Uterus**
The most reliable distinguishing feature is demonstrating continuity with the uterus. A degenerating leiomyoma will maintain a clear connection to the uterine myometrium, often with a discernible stalk or broad-based attachment. In this case, the lower mass appeared "adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma" on both ultrasound and MRI, which correctly suggested a myomatous origin. Conversely, an endometrioma arises from the ovary and should demonstrate a separable relationship from the uterine corpus. However, as illustrated here, a pedunculated leiomyoma can mimic an adnexal mass when the connecting stalk is obscured by the gravid uterus, leading to diagnostic uncertainty.

**Ultrasound Features**
On ultrasound, decidualized endometriomas classically display homogeneous low-level internal echoes—a "ground-glass" appearance—with possible solid-appearing papillary projections representing decidualized stromal tissue. Degenerating leiomyomas typically show more heterogeneous internal architecture with anechoic or hypoechoic cystic spaces from necrosis and liquefaction. In this patient, both masses appeared as "unilocular cystic masses with regular margins and peripheral vascularisation," highlighting the substantial overlap in sonographic appearance.

**MRI Characteristics**
Non-contrast MRI provides superior soft tissue characterization. Endometriomas typically demonstrate hyperintense signal on T1-weighted sequences due to blood products, often with "shading" on T2 from iron deposition. Cystic degenerating leiomyomas show variable T1 signal but characteristically display well-defined cystic areas with hypointense T2 signal in the solid components. In this case, MRI revealed "thick walls, T1 and T2 fat-sat weighted inhomogeneous fluid content and focal hyperintense T1-T2 weighted solid components"—findings that were ultimately consistent with leiomyomas undergoing cystic change.

**Tumor Markers**
CA-125 has limited discriminative value, as elevation occurs in both conditions. This patient had a slightly elevated CA-125 (41.8 U/mL), which initially favored endometrioma but proved nonspecific.

**Surgical Pathology as the Definitive Standard**
This case underscores that preoperative imaging, while invaluable, cannot always definitively differentiate these entities. At laparotomy, both masses proved to be cystic leiomyomas—one pedunculated and twisted, explaining her fever and acute pain, and one intramural-subserosal. When imaging is equivocal and clinical circumstances warrant surgical intervention, operative evaluation remains the diagnostic gold standard.

### [Management] What are the indications for performing myomectomy during pregnancy, and what surgical principles should be followed to minimize obstetric risk?

**Indications for Myomectomy During Pregnancy**

Myomectomy during pregnancy is an exceptional procedure that should only be considered when conservative management has failed or when specific complications arise. The general principle is that the benefits of surgical intervention must clearly outweigh the substantial risks of pregnancy loss, preterm labor, hemorrhage, and uterine rupture.

The primary indications include:
- **Torsion of a pedunculated myoma** — this represents a surgical emergency
- **Severe refractory symptoms** — persistent pain unresponsive to analgesics and conservative measures
- **Mass effect causing significant clinical compromise** — such as the restrictive lung disease and dyspnea described in this case
- **Diagnostic uncertainty with concern for malignancy** — when imaging cannot definitively exclude an ovarian or other neoplasm

In the presented case, the decision to operate was driven by multiple factors: the acute clinical deterioration with severe pain and mechanical dyspnea, the inability to exclude malignancy given the imaging characteristics and elevated CA-125, and the significant risk of complications including the torsion that was ultimately discovered intraoperatively.

**Surgical Principles to Minimize Obstetric Risk**

Several critical principles guided the surgical management in this case and should guide any myomectomy during pregnancy:

**Patient selection and surgical restraint.** Only myomas that absolutely require removal should be addressed. The surgical team demonstrated this principle by resecting only the pedunculated, torsed superior myoma while leaving the caudal intramural myoma in situ. Resection of intramural myomas carries substantially higher risks of hemorrhage, uterine rupture, and pregnancy loss.

**Gentle tissue handling.** The pedunculated myoma was carefully excised from its uterine implant using a double clamp technique with a single absorbable suture to close the base. Minimizing uterine manipulation and avoiding excessive traction reduces the risk of uterine irritability and preterm contractions.

**Fetal monitoring.** Continuous assessment of fetal heart rate before, during, and after the procedure is mandatory. The case describes serial transvaginal ultrasound monitoring throughout the operation.

**Tocolytic prophylaxis.** Progesterone supplementation was administered intraoperatively and continued postoperatively. While evidence varies, progesterone support is commonly used to reduce uterine contractility.

**Operative efficiency.** The surgery lasted approximately two and a half hours. Prolonged operative time increases fetal risk, and the procedure should be as brief as technically feasible.

**Delivery planning.** The presence of a uterine scar necessitates careful delivery planning. In this case, an elective cesarean at 37 weeks was chosen to minimize the risk of uterine rupture during labor, given both the myomectomy site and the obstructing pelvic myoma.

The excellent maternal and neonatal outcome in this case underscores that with careful patient selection, adherence to surgical principles, and multidisciplinary coordination, myomectomy during pregnancy can be performed safely when truly indicated.

### [Clinical Pearls] What complications can arise from uterine leiomyomas during pregnancy, and how might they present clinically?

### Clinical Teaching Answer

Uterine leiomyomas in pregnancy can lead to several important complications, which are well-illustrated by this case.

**Red (carneous) degeneration** is the most common complication, occurring when rapid myoma growth outstrips its blood supply. This presents with acute, severe abdominal pain, localized tenderness, and low-grade fever—exactly as seen in this patient. The pain typically localizes over the myoma and may be accompanied by leukocytosis and elevated inflammatory markers, which can mimic other acute abdominal emergencies.

**Torsion** occurs specifically with pedunculated subserosal myomas. In this case, the superior 18 cm mass was found intraoperatively to be a pedunculated leiomyoma that had undergone torsion, with pathological confirmation of ischemic necrosis near the twisted pedicle. Torsion should be suspected when acute pain is accompanied by systemic symptoms such as fever, and when imaging shows a cystic appearance due to subsequent necrosis.

**Mechanical compression** from large myomas can cause secondary effects. This patient developed dyspnea from diaphragmatic compression by the massive upper abdominal lesion, which improved with positional changes. Large pelvic myomas, like the 9 cm caudal mass in this case, can obstruct the birth canal and necessitate cesarean delivery.

**Additional complications** include spontaneous rupture (rare but life-threatening), infection of a necrotic myoma, and obstruction of urinary or gastrointestinal tracts depending on location. Anemia may also be exacerbated, as seen here with the patient's hemoglobin dropping to 7.5 g/L postoperatively.

Management is typically conservative with analgesics and observation unless there is concern for torsion, rupture, or malignancy—surgical intervention, as in this case, is reserved for refractory symptoms or diagnostic uncertainty.

### [Management] In a pregnant patient with a pelvic mass that could obstruct labor, what factors influence the decision between planned cesarean delivery versus trial of labor?

### Clinical Teaching Answer

The decision between planned cesarean delivery and trial of labor in a pregnant patient with a pelvic mass centers on three critical factors: **anatomic location relative to the birth canal, size of the mass, and potential for obstruction during labor.**

**Anatomic location is paramount.** Masses located in the lower uterine segment, cervix, or pouch of Douglas—as in this case, where the caudal mass filled the pouch of Douglas and was adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal leiomyoma—are most likely to obstruct fetal descent. The authors specifically noted that this mass "would have represented a huge obstacle for the fetal head progression during the expulsive phase of labour." In contrast, fundal or anterior wall myomas are less likely to interfere with vaginal delivery.

**Size matters.** While no absolute cutoff exists, masses exceeding 5–7 cm in the lower pelvis warrant careful evaluation. This patient's caudal mass measured approximately 9–10 cm and remained fixed, creating a mechanical barrier that would prevent fetal head engagement and progression.

**Mobility and fixity** influence decision-making. A mobile adnexal cyst may be pushed aside during labor, whereas a fixed mass—like the firm, fixed pelvic mass identified on bimanual examination in this case—cannot be displaced and creates a fixed obstruction.

Additional considerations include:
- **Associated surgical history**—this patient already had a laparotomy with myomectomy at 21 weeks, creating a uterine scar that increased the risk of rupture with labor
- **Number of masses**—multiple large masses complicate both the delivery plan and potential surgical approach
- **Gestational timing of diagnosis**—allowing planning for elective delivery rather than emergent intervention

In this case, the multidisciplinary team appropriately planned an elective cesarean at 37 weeks via the existing surgical incision. The decision was straightforward: a fixed, large pelvic mass obstructing the birth canal combined with a prior uterine scar made trial of labor inadvisable. The key clinical principle is that **cesarean delivery is indicated when a pelvic mass is fixed, located in the lower uterine segment or pouch of Douglas, and of sufficient size to prevent fetal descent.**

### [Management] What is the role of tocolytic therapy such as progesterone prophylaxis during intraabdominal surgery in pregnancy, and when should it be administered?

**Clinical Teaching Answer**

Prophylactic tocolytic therapy, particularly progesterone, plays an important role in reducing uterine irritability and preventing preterm contractions during intraabdominal surgery in pregnancy. The rationale is straightforward: surgical stress, uterine manipulation, and the inflammatory response can trigger prostaglandin release and uterine contractility, placing the pregnancy at risk for preterm labor or pregnancy loss.

In this case, the patient underwent laparotomy at 21 weeks and 6 days gestation for excision of a large, twisted pedunculated leiomyoma. Progesterone prophylaxis (100 mg intramuscularly) was initiated **intraoperatively** and continued daily until discharge, followed by intramuscular hydroxyprogesterone caproate (341 mg every 3 days) until elective delivery. This approach reflects the principle that tocolytic prophylaxis should be administered **before or during the surgical procedure** to establish uterine quiescence preemptively, rather than waiting for contractions to begin.

The mechanism of progesterone in this setting involves smooth muscle relaxation and suppression of the inflammatory cascade that can precipitate contractions. Unlike acute tocolytics such as indomethacin or nifedipine, which are used reactively when preterm labor occurs, progesterone provides sustained prophylactic coverage during the perioperative period.

Key practical points:
- **Timing:** Administer at the onset of surgery or immediately preoperatively; continuation postoperatively depends on clinical circumstances
- **Monitoring:** As demonstrated in this case, serial fetal heart rate monitoring before, during, and after surgery remains essential
- **Indications:** Prophylaxis is most strongly indicated when there is direct uterine manipulation, as in myomectomy, or when the procedure carries significant risk of uterine irritability

While evidence for prophylactic tocolysis remains heterogeneous, progesterone offers a favorable safety profile and is a reasonable adjunct when preserving pregnancy is paramount, as in this successful outcome at 37 weeks.
