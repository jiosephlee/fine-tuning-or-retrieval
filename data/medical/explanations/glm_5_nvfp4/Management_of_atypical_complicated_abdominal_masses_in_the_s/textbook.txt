Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Assessment

The evaluation of acute abdominal pain in pregnancy requires a systematic approach that accounts for the physiological changes of gestation and the potential overlap between normal pregnancy-related symptoms and serious pathology. This case illustrates how presenting symptoms, physical examination, and initial laboratory findings guide the diagnostic workup for a pregnant patient with complex abdominal masses.

### Patient Demographics and Obstetric History

The patient was a nulliparous woman in her 40s presenting at 21 weeks of gestation. Her obstetric history was notable for G1P0A1—one prior pregnancy ending in abortion with no live births. Advanced maternal age, defined as age 35 years or older at delivery, is an important demographic factor associated with increased risks of chromosomal abnormalities, gestational diabetes, preeclampsia, and cesarean delivery. Additionally, this patient had a body mass index of 33, placing her in the obese category. Obesity in pregnancy compounds diagnostic challenges, as physical examination becomes more difficult and the incidence of uterine leiomyomas is increased.

Her prenatal screening had been reassuring: first and second trimester aneuploidy screening was low risk, confirmed by non-invasive prenatal testing, and fetal morphology appeared normal. This baseline information provided context that her acute presentation was unlikely related to a primary fetal or chromosomal abnormality.

### Presenting Symptoms

The patient reported severe acute abdominal pain localized to two distinct regions: the left upper quadrant and the pelvis. Pain severity was quantified using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) at 5 out of 10 for both sites, indicating moderate to severe discomfort. She also reported mild dyspnea. At 21 weeks of gestation, the uterus typically extends to approximately 1 cm above the umbilicus, making the simultaneous presence of upper abdominal and pelvic pain an unusual distribution that immediately suggested pathology beyond typical pregnancy-related discomfort.

Dyspnea during pregnancy is relatively common due to increased oxygen consumption and decreased functional residual capacity from upward diaphragmatic displacement. However, dyspnea severe enough to prompt medical evaluation, particularly when associated with significant abdominal pain, warrants investigation for mechanical compression from abdominal masses, pulmonary embolism, or cardiac pathology.

### Physical Examination Findings

Physical examination revealed two key findings that shaped the clinical picture. Abdominal examination demonstrated a fundal height larger than expected for gestational age, with a tender, taut-elastic mass measuring approximately 18 cm occupying the entire left upper quadrant. The absence of clinical ascites was noted. The discrepancy between expected and actual abdominal size, coupled with a palpable mass, immediately signaled the presence of a space-occupying lesion rather than simple pregnancy-related enlargement.

Bimanual vaginal examination revealed a second, distinct mass: a fixed, rounded, hard mass approximately 9 cm in diameter located in the lesser pelvis, partially bulging into the posterior vaginal fornix. The "fixed" quality of this mass suggested adhesions or attachment to surrounding structures, while the "hard" consistency raised concern for a solid neoplasm. Bimanual examination remains essential in evaluating pelvic masses during pregnancy, as it can distinguish between adnexal masses, uterine leiomyomas, and other pelvic pathology based on mobility, consistency, and relationship to the uterus.

### Initial Cardiopulmonary Assessment and Stabilization

Given the presenting dyspnea, the clinical team prioritized cardiopulmonary assessment. Oxygen saturation was normal at 97%, heart rate was 74 beats per minute, and blood pressure was 100/60 mm Hg—values within acceptable ranges for pregnancy. Electrocardiogram was normal. This assessment confirmed that the dyspnea was not due to primary cardiac or pulmonary dysfunction, supporting the hypothesis that respiratory symptoms resulted from mechanical diaphragmatic compression from the large abdominal masses.

Initial stabilization measures included intravenous paracetamol (1000 mg twice daily) for analgesia and positioning in a semi-orthostatic position. This positioning theoretically reduced diaphragmatic compression by allowing the gravid uterus and masses to shift caudally with gravity. The intervention reduced pain from VAS 5 to VAS 1 without requiring supplemental oxygen, demonstrating effective symptom control while investigations proceeded.

### Laboratory Evaluation

Initial laboratory workup revealed findings consistent with both pregnancy-related physiological changes and intercurrent pathology. Hemoglobin was 9.9 g/L (reference range 12.0–16.6 g/L) with serum ferritin of 25 mcg/L, indicating iron deficiency anemia. Anemia is common in pregnancy due to plasma volume expansion exceeding red blood cell mass increase, but iron deficiency requires supplementation and may contribute to fatigue and dyspnea.

Inflammatory markers showed a modest elevation in C-reactive protein at 1.39 mg/dL (reference range 0–0.3 mg/dL) and leukocytosis with white blood cells at 11.29 × 10³/µL and neutrophils at 9.27 × 10³/µL. While leukocytosis is a normal physiological adaptation in pregnancy, the elevated CRP indicated an inflammatory process. During hospitalization, the patient developed low-grade fever, prompting infectious workup. SARS-CoV-2 testing was negative, but urine culture identified Klebsiella pneumoniae, a gram-negative organism that can cause urinary tract infections and, in pregnant patients, is associated with increased risk of pyelonephritis and preterm labor.

### Multidisciplinary Approach

The complexity of this presentation—large symptomatic abdominal masses in a pregnant patient with multiple risk factors—necessitated a multidisciplinary team approach. The patient was initially evaluated in the emergency department with input from internal medicine, then transferred to the Obstetrical and Gynecological Division for specialized care. This collaborative model reflects the standard of care for complex obstetric cases, ensuring that surgical, obstetric, anesthetic, and neonatal perspectives inform diagnostic and therapeutic decisions.

The initial assessment established the clinical foundation for subsequent imaging and diagnostic reasoning. The presence of two distinct masses—one in the left upper quadrant and one in the pelvis—with associated pain, dyspnea, and inflammatory findings created a clinical picture that demanded further investigation to characterize the lesions, determine their origin, and guide management decisions that would balance maternal safety with pregnancy preservation.

## Diagnostic Imaging and Tumor Markers in Pregnancy

The evaluation of abdominal masses during pregnancy requires a careful balance between diagnostic accuracy and fetal safety. In this case, the diagnostic workup began with ultrasound, which revealed two large unilocular cystic masses with regular margins and peripheral vascularization. The upper mass measured 18 × 12 × 7 cm, extending from the left subphrenic region to the uterine fundus, while the lower mass measured 9.5 × 8 × 8 cm and appeared adherent to a posterior intramural-subserosal myoma with cystic degeneration. Ultrasound is the first-line imaging modality in pregnancy because it is widely available, cost-effective, and lacks ionizing radiation. It is particularly valuable for identifying uterine leiomyomas located along the anterior wall of the gravid uterus and for assessing vascularity using color Doppler.

However, ultrasound has limitations in pregnancy, particularly when masses are large, multiple, or located posteriorly. The "steric bulk" of the gravid uterus can obscure visualization and make it difficult to identify connecting stalks or feeding vessels. In this case, ultrasound could not definitively determine the origin of the upper mass, as no connective stalk or tumor-feeding vessel was identified. The imaging findings initially suggested two huge decidualized endometriomas, which represent a common mimicker of cystic masses in pregnancy due to the hormonal effects on endometriotic cysts.

When ultrasound is inconclusive, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) without contrast is the preferred second-line modality. MRI offers superior soft tissue contrast and can precisely delineate the relationship between masses and uterine structures. In this patient, MRI without gadolinium demonstrated thick walls (1.4 cm), T1 and T2 fat-sat weighted inhomogeneous fluid content, and focal hyperintense T1-T2 weighted solid components. These features raised concern for potential malignancy, as thick walls and solid components can indicate neoplastic processes. Importantly, the MRI confirmed that the caudal mass was in continuity with a posterior uterine leiomyoma. Gadolinium-based contrast agents are avoided during pregnancy due to concerns about fetal exposure and potential association with rheumatological, inflammatory, or infiltrative skin conditions, as well as increased risk of stillbirth or neonatal death. Additionally, MRI should be performed at 1.5 Tesla or lower after the first trimester to minimize potential fetal risks.

Tumor marker interpretation during pregnancy presents unique challenges. CA-125, commonly used for ovarian cancer screening, physiologically rises during the first trimester (median 23 U/mL) and again near term (median 21 U/mL), with lower values in the second trimester (median 14 U/mL). In this case, CA-125 was elevated at 41.8 U/mL during the second trimester, exceeding both the standard cutoff of 35 U/mL and the expected physiological range for this gestational period. The marker showed an increasing trend from 36 U/mL two weeks earlier. This elevation initially favored endometrioma as the differential diagnosis, as CA-125 can be elevated in benign conditions including endometriosis, adenomyosis, pelvic inflammatory disease, and uterine leiomyomas.

The key lesson from this case is that isolated tumor marker measurements are not diagnostic during pregnancy. Serial evaluations can provide useful trends, but clinical correlation with imaging findings is essential. All other tumor markers in this patient (CA 19-9, CA 15-3, CEA, β2-microglobulin, and LDH) were within normal limits. The combination of imaging characteristics and CA-125 elevation created diagnostic uncertainty between decidualized endometriomas and cystic degenerating leiomyomas, ultimately requiring surgical exploration for definitive diagnosis. The pathology confirmed benign uterine leiomyomas with areas of ischemic necrosis near the twisted pedicle, demonstrating that imaging interpretation in pregnancy must account for both the limitations of available modalities and the physiological changes that alter baseline expectations.

## Differential Diagnosis and Clinical Decision-Making

The differential diagnosis of large abdominal masses during pregnancy requires careful integration of clinical examination, imaging findings, and laboratory data, particularly when masses demonstrate atypical features. In this case, the initial ultrasound evaluation revealed two large, unilocular cystic masses with regular margins and peripheral vascularization—an appearance that raised immediate diagnostic consideration of decidualized endometriomas. Endometriomas are ovarian cysts filled with old blood products that, under the influence of high progesterone levels during pregnancy, undergo decidualization, transforming their typical "chocolate cyst" appearance into more complex, heterogeneous lesions that can mimic malignancy. This physiological change accounts for why endometriomas in pregnant patients often appear as cystic masses with internal echoes and thickened walls, closely resembling the sonographic features observed in this patient.

However, the imaging findings also supported alternative diagnoses. The caudal mass demonstrated apparent continuity with a posterior intramural-subserosal leiomyoma measuring 58 × 71 mm, suggesting a uterine origin with cystic degeneration. Leiomyomas are benign smooth muscle tumors that affect approximately 2–4% of pregnancies and can undergo various degenerative changes—including cystic degeneration, hemorrhagic degeneration, or red degeneration—particularly during the second trimester when rapid uterine growth outpaces the blood supply to the fibroid. On ultrasound, cystic degeneration appears as a heterogeneous mass with fluid-filled cavities, while MRI typically shows thick walls and inhomogeneous fluid content on T2-weighted sequences. The presence of a solid component adherent to the uterine wall strongly favored a diagnosis of degenerating leiomyoma for the caudal mass.

Diagnostic uncertainty persisted despite comprehensive imaging. The cranial mass presented a particular challenge: its location extending from the left subphrenic region to the uterine fundus suggested an ovarian origin, and the absence of a visible connective stalk or tumor-feeding vessel—attributable to the "steric bulk" of the gravid uterus obscuring anatomical relationships—precluded definitive determination of organ of origin. The elevated CA-125 level (41.8 U/mL) further complicated the diagnostic picture. While CA-125 is widely recognized as a tumor marker for epithelial ovarian cancer, its interpretation during pregnancy requires considerable caution. Serum CA-125 physiologically peaks during the first trimester (median 23 U/mL) and again near term (median 21 U/mL), with lower median values during the second trimester (14.0 U/mL). This patient's CA-125 exceeded the expected second-trimester range and showed an increasing trend, raising concern for possible malignancy. However, CA-125 elevation also occurs in numerous benign conditions, including endometriosis, adenomyosis, pelvic inflammatory disease, and uterine leiomyomas. Consequently, while the CA-125 elevation heightened clinical vigilance, it could not independently distinguish between benign and malignant etiologies.

Risk stratification in this case centered on three domains: oncological suspicion, mechanical complications, and symptom progression. The combination of large cystic masses with solid components, vascular hypertrophy, and a rising tumor marker created sufficient concern for ovarian malignancy that definitive histopathological diagnosis was warranted. Beyond oncological considerations, the masses posed substantial risks of acute complications. The cranial mass, measuring 18 cm, carried inherent risks of rupture due to its size and potential torsion if pedunculated—concerns that ultimately proved prescient given the intraoperative finding of a twisted, leaking pedicle. The patient's clinical deterioration, manifested by severe abdominal pain (VAS score of 5) and progressive dyspnea due to diaphragmatic compression, further supported the need for intervention. Mechanical dyspnea resulting from mass effect represented a significant quality-of-life burden and threatened to worsen as uterine enlargement progressed throughout the pregnancy.

The decision to proceed with surgical intervention during the second trimester balanced multiple competing considerations. Elective surgery during pregnancy is generally avoided when possible due to risks of pregnancy loss, preterm labor, and maternal morbidity. However, the second trimester (between 12 and 24 weeks of gestation) is considered the optimal window when surgery is unavoidable, as organogenesis is complete and the risk of spontaneous abortion is lower than in the first trimester, while uterine size remains manageable and preterm labor risk is lower than in the third trimester. In this case, the multidisciplinary team determined that the combination of symptomatic deterioration, diagnostic uncertainty with oncological concern, and risk of acute complications justified surgical exploration. Importantly, the surgical plan was designed to achieve diagnosis while preserving the pregnancy—removing only the mass deemed necessary, leaving the caudal mass in situ to minimize obstetric risk.

Informed consent for surgery during pregnancy requires explicit discussion of maternal and fetal risks. The patient received counseling regarding the possibility of pregnancy loss, preterm labor, hemorrhage, and the potential need for emergent cesarean delivery. The intraoperative plan included progesterone prophylaxis (administered intraoperatively and continued postoperatively) to reduce uterine contractility and maintain pregnancy, along with continuous fetal monitoring. This case illustrates that when surgery is indicated during pregnancy, a conservative surgical approach—limited to what is diagnostically or therapeutically necessary, combined with tocolytic prophylaxis and vigilant fetal monitoring—can achieve excellent maternal and neonatal outcomes.

## Surgical Management and Intraoperative Decision-Making

The decision to proceed with surgical intervention during pregnancy requires careful weighing of maternal and fetal risks against the potential consequences of conservative management. In this case, the multidisciplinary team opted for surgical exploration at 21 weeks and 6 days of gestation due to several compelling indications: worsening abdominal pain and restrictive dyspnea from mass effect, the uncertain diagnosis with concern for malignancy given elevated CA-125, and the risk of acute complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection. The timing was critical—delaying surgery would allow continued uterine enlargement, potentially making surgical access more difficult and increasing the risk of mass-related complications as the pregnancy progressed.

A longitudinal xiphopubic laparotomy was selected as the surgical approach. This incision provided optimal exposure for the anticipated complex procedure involving large masses in a gravid uterus. While laparoscopy can be considered for myomectomy during pregnancy, particularly in the first or early second trimester with smaller lesions, this case presented features favoring laparotomy: the enormous size of the masses (18 cm and 9.5 cm), the patient's obesity (BMI 33), the uncertain anatomical relationships, and the need for definitive diagnosis and potential extensive dissection. The open approach minimized operative time—a critical factor in preserving pregnancy—and allowed thorough exploration of the abdominal cavity.

Intraoperative findings fundamentally altered the preoperative diagnosis. The cranial mass, preoperatively thought to be a decidualized endometrioma based on imaging and CA-125 elevation, was revealed to be a pedunculated, cystic leiomyoma originating from the uterus. This mass was twisted and leaking, providing a likely explanation for the low-grade fever the patient developed during her hospital stay. The torsion had caused ischemic necrosis near the pedicle, a finding confirmed on pathology. This intraoperative discovery illustrates a key diagnostic challenge: uterine leiomyomas can closely mimic ovarian pathology on imaging, particularly when they undergo cystic degeneration, a phenomenon more common during pregnancy due to hormonal changes and rapid growth outpacing blood supply.

The surgical decision-making required real-time adaptation. The upper pedunculated myoma was excised by gently clamping and dividing its stalk, with careful closure of the uterine implantation site using absorbable suture. In contrast, the caudal mass—arising from the uterine torus and appearing as a fixed, soft, cystic intramural-subserosal myoma—was left in situ. This conservative approach was deliberate: resecting an intramural myoma during pregnancy carries substantial risks of hemorrhage, uterine dehiscence, and pregnancy loss. The principle guiding this decision was to address only the lesion causing acute symptoms while preserving the integrity of the gravid uterus.

Fetal safety was maintained through vigilant intraoperative monitoring. Fetal cardiac activity was documented before and after the procedure, with serial transvaginal ultrasound assessments throughout the operation. This practice aligns with established recommendations for documenting fetal heart rate before, during, and after any surgical procedure in pregnancy. The surgery lasted 2 hours and 25 minutes, reflecting the complexity of the case while prioritizing efficiency to minimize fetal exposure to anesthesia and maternal surgical stress.

Progesterone prophylaxis was administered to reduce the risk of preterm labor—a recognized complication of intrauterine surgery during pregnancy. The patient received 100 mg of progesterone intramuscularly intraoperatively, continued daily until discharge, followed by hydroxyprogesterone caproate 341 mg intramuscularly every 3 days until delivery. Progesterone supports uterine quiescence by modulating inflammatory pathways and reducing myometrial contractility. While evidence for tocolytic prophylaxis in myomectomy during pregnancy is limited, this approach represents a reasonable precaution given the risk of uterine irritation following surgery.

Antibiotic management addressed both prophylaxis and the patient's active Klebsiella pneumoniae urinary tract infection. Intraoperative prophylaxis with cefazolin 2 g intravenously was followed by postoperative treatment tailored to the urine culture antibiogram: cefazolin 1 g intravenously three times daily plus clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily. This combined approach ensured coverage for potential surgical site infection while treating the documented infection, which may have contributed to the patient's systemic inflammatory response.

## Postoperative Care, Delivery Planning, and Outcomes

The postoperative management of this patient illustrates the critical importance of anticipating and addressing complications following abdominal surgery during pregnancy, while simultaneously planning for safe delivery in the context of residual uterine pathology.

**Postoperative Anemia Management**

Following the laparoscopic myomectomy, the patient developed significant postoperative anemia, with hemoglobin falling to 7.5 g/L and hematocrit to 24.3%. This degree of anemia is a recognized complication of myomectomy, even in the non-pregnant state, due to the vascular nature of leiomyomas. During pregnancy, the stakes are higher: severe anemia compromises oxygen delivery to both mother and fetus and increases the risk of preterm labor. The management in this case followed standard principles—intravenous iron supplementation with ferric carboxymaltose (1 g) was administered to replete iron stores, and two units of packed red blood cells were transfused to rapidly restore hemoglobin to a safer level (10.0 g/L). This approach reflects the balance between conservative management and the urgent correction of anemia in a pregnant patient approaching the third trimester.

**Progesterone Prophylaxis and Tocolysis**

A notable aspect of this case was the use of prophylactic progesterone to mitigate the risk of uterine contractions and preterm labor. The patient received 100 mg of intramuscular progesterone intraoperatively and daily until discharge, followed by intramuscular hydroxyprogesterone caproate (341 mg every 3 days) until delivery. Hydroxyprogesterone caproate is a synthetic progestin approved for reducing the risk of recurrent preterm birth, and its use in this context reflects concern that surgical manipulation of the gravid uterus could trigger contractile activity. While evidence supporting routine tocolytic prophylaxis after myomectomy in pregnancy is limited, this approach represents a reasonable precautionary strategy in high-risk cases, particularly when the surgery involves significant uterine manipulation or entry into the uterine wall.

**Obstetric Surveillance and Fetal Monitoring**

After discharge, the patient entered a structured obstetric follow-up program with visits every 3 weeks. The primary objectives were to monitor fetal growth and amniotic fluid volume, both of which can be affected by maternal anemia, uterine scarring, or placental insufficiency. Fetal growth tracked at the 86th percentile according to WHO standards, indicating robust growth without evidence of restriction. Regular surveillance also allowed for early detection of any signs of preterm labor, uterine dehiscence, or deterioration of the residual pelvic myoma.

**Pathological Findings and Clinical Correlation**

Pathological examination confirmed benign uterine leiomyomas with areas of ischemic necrosis adjacent to the twisted pedicle. This finding corroborates the clinical suspicion that the patient's low-grade fever during the preoperative hospital stay was likely secondary to necrotic degeneration from torsion or ischemia within the pedunculated myoma. Red (or carneous) degeneration, a form of hemorrhagic necrosis, is the most common type of degeneration in pregnancy-related leiomyomas and can present with pain, fever, and leukocytosis—symptoms that overlap with infection or torsion. The absence of malignant features on pathology was reassuring and validated the intraoperative decision to limit resection to the pedunculated mass while preserving the pregnancy.

**Delivery Planning and the Role of Residual Myomas**

The presence of a residual 7.5 cm pelvic myoma fundamentally influenced delivery planning. This lesion, located in the pouch of Douglas and fixed to the posterior uterine wall, would have mechanically obstructed fetal descent during vaginal delivery. In such cases, elective cesarean delivery is the preferred approach to avoid the risks of obstructed labor, which can lead to uterine rupture, fetal distress, or traumatic injury. The timing of delivery at 37 weeks was deliberate—waiting until term minimized the risks of neonatal prematurity while reducing the risk of uterine scar rupture that increases with advancing gestational age and spontaneous labor. Performing the cesarean through the same surgical scar from the myomectomy minimized additional uterine trauma and facilitated efficient surgical management.

**Postpartum Course and Myoma Behavior**

Postpartum, the patient experienced partial regression of the residual myoma (from approximately 9 cm preoperatively to 7.5 cm postpartum), which aligns with known patterns of myoma involution after delivery. Leiomyomas are hormone-dependent tumors, and the rapid decline in estrogen and progesterone following delivery often leads to reduction in size. Many women experience symptom resolution postpartum, as occurred in this case, allowing for conservative management without immediate need for additional surgery. The patient's decision to defer further intervention reflects an acceptable approach when residual disease is asymptomatic and reproductive goals have been met.

This case demonstrates that successful outcomes in complicated pregnancies with large uterine masses require meticulous postoperative care, judicious use of tocolytic prophylaxis, vigilant fetal surveillance, and individualized delivery planning based on the anatomy of residual pathology.

## Evidence-Based Discussion and Clinical Lessons

This case illustrates the diagnostic complexity and management dilemmas posed by large abdominal masses during pregnancy, particularly when imaging findings suggest an alternative diagnosis. The patient presented with two large cystic masses initially interpreted as decidualized endometriomas, yet surgical exploration revealed pedunculated and intramural-subserosal leiomyomas with cystic degeneration. This diagnostic discrepancy underscores several important clinical principles that guide the evaluation and management of uterine myomas in pregnancy.

Uterine leiomyomas are encountered in 0.3–10.1% of pregnancies, while adnexal masses occur in 1–6% of gestations. The overlapping incidence rates contribute to diagnostic uncertainty, particularly when myomas undergo cystic degeneration and mimic adnexal pathology. During pregnancy, leiomyomas exhibit a non-linear growth pattern: they increase by approximately 6.12% between the first and second trimesters and 3.10% between the second and third trimesters, with an overall weekly growth rate of 6.90%. Importantly, this growth trend reverses postpartum, with a mean size reduction of 44.25% by six weeks after delivery. In this case, the residual pelvic myoma demonstrated partial postpartum regression to 7.5 cm, consistent with established growth kinetics.

The diagnostic challenge in this patient arose from several factors. First, the cystic appearance of both masses on ultrasound mimicked decidualized endometriomas, a benign condition that can also present as bilateral adnexal cysts during pregnancy. Second, the slight elevation of CA-125 to 41.8 U/mL—above the second-trimester median of 14.0 U/mL—initially suggested endometriosis, as CA-125 can be elevated in this condition. However, the interpreting physicians recognized that CA-125 lacks specificity during pregnancy; it physiologically peaks in the first trimester (median 23 U/mL) and again in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL), and can be elevated in numerous benign conditions including myomas, pregnancy-related changes, and pelvic inflammatory disease. The absence of a demonstrable stalk or feeding vessel for the upper mass on imaging—attributable to the spatial constraints imposed by the gravid uterus and the lesion's size—further complicated localization. These limitations highlight the importance of relying on clinical context rather than isolated imaging findings or tumor marker values.

The management approach in this case reflects the principle that conservative treatment is preferred for uterine myomas diagnosed during pregnancy, given the risks of bleeding, pregnancy loss, preterm labor, and uterine rupture. Surgical intervention should be reserved for specific indications: torsion of a pedunculated myoma, persistent symptoms unresponsive to conservative measures, oncological suspicion, or complications such as rupture or infection. This patient met several surgical criteria: worsening pain and dyspnea due to mass effect, the risk of complications from large vascularized masses, and the need for a definitive diagnosis given the uncertain origin of the lesions. The multidisciplinary team elected for laparotomy with a longitudinal xiphopubic incision, a choice influenced by the size and location of the masses, the gestational age of 21 weeks and 6 days, and the need to minimize operative time to preserve the pregnancy. While laparoscopic myomectomy can be considered for smaller, pedunculated myomas in early pregnancy—preferably using an open Hasson technique—laparotomy remains appropriate for larger, more complex lesions.

Several technical and perioperative measures contributed to the successful outcome in this case. Intraoperative monitoring of fetal heart rate before, during, and after surgery is mandatory, as is documented in this patient. Progesterone prophylaxis, administered as 100 mg intramuscularly daily during hospitalization followed by hydroxyprogesterone caproate 341 mg every three days until delivery, was used for tocolysis. Antibiotic prophylaxis and treatment were tailored to the patient's Klebsiella pneumoniae urinary tract infection. The decision to excise only the pedunculated upper myoma while leaving the caudal mass in situ exemplifies the principle of limiting surgical intervention to what is strictly necessary, thereby reducing obstetric risk.

Mode of delivery must be individualized based on myoma characteristics. In this case, the caudal myoma originating from the uterine torus would have obstructed fetal descent during the expulsive phase of labor. Consequently, an elective cesarean delivery was performed at 37 weeks of gestation, utilizing the same surgical scar to minimize the risk of uterine rupture. Evidence suggests that cesarean myomectomy does not significantly increase blood transfusion rates compared to cesarean section alone, though it does extend operative time and hospitalization. However, the decision to proceed with myomectomy at the time of cesarean should be guided by myoma size, location, and the surgeon's judgment.

This case yields several key clinical lessons. First, a multidisciplinary approach involving surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians is essential for balancing oncological concerns, symptom management, and pregnancy preservation. Second, imaging has inherent limitations: the "steric bulk" of the gravid uterus can obscure critical anatomical relationships, and tumor markers lack specificity in pregnancy. Third, overtreatment should be avoided; conservative management remains appropriate for asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic myomas, with the understanding that postpartum regression is expected. Finally, individualized care—guided by symptom severity, mass characteristics, and gestational age—should supersede algorithmic approaches in these complex clinical scenarios.
