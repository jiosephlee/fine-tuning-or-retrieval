Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

Chapter 3: ## Differential Diagnosis and Clinical Decision-Making

The differential diagnosis of large abdominal masses during pregnancy requires careful integration of clinical examination, imaging findings, and laboratory data, particularly when masses demonstrate atypical features. In this case, the initial ultrasound evaluation revealed two large, unilocular cystic masses with regular margins and peripheral vascularization—an appearance that raised immediate diagnostic consideration of decidualized endometriomas. Endometriomas are ovarian cysts filled with old blood products that, under the influence of high progesterone levels during pregnancy, undergo decidualization, transforming their typical "chocolate cyst" appearance into more complex, heterogeneous lesions that can mimic malignancy. This physiological change accounts for why endometriomas in pregnant patients often appear as cystic masses with internal echoes and thickened walls, closely resembling the sonographic features observed in this patient.

However, the imaging findings also supported alternative diagnoses. The caudal mass demonstrated apparent continuity with a posterior intramural-subserosal leiomyoma measuring 58 × 71 mm, suggesting a uterine origin with cystic degeneration. Leiomyomas are benign smooth muscle tumors that affect approximately 2–4% of pregnancies and can undergo various degenerative changes—including cystic degeneration, hemorrhagic degeneration, or red degeneration—particularly during the second trimester when rapid uterine growth outpaces the blood supply to the fibroid. On ultrasound, cystic degeneration appears as a heterogeneous mass with fluid-filled cavities, while MRI typically shows thick walls and inhomogeneous fluid content on T2-weighted sequences. The presence of a solid component adherent to the uterine wall strongly favored a diagnosis of degenerating leiomyoma for the caudal mass.

Diagnostic uncertainty persisted despite comprehensive imaging. The cranial mass presented a particular challenge: its location extending from the left subphrenic region to the uterine fundus suggested an ovarian origin, and the absence of a visible connective stalk or tumor-feeding vessel—attributable to the "steric bulk" of the gravid uterus obscuring anatomical relationships—precluded definitive determination of organ of origin. The elevated CA-125 level (41.8 U/mL) further complicated the diagnostic picture. While CA-125 is widely recognized as a tumor marker for epithelial ovarian cancer, its interpretation during pregnancy requires considerable caution. Serum CA-125 physiologically peaks during the first trimester (median 23 U/mL) and again near term (median 21 U/mL), with lower median values during the second trimester (14.0 U/mL). This patient's CA-125 exceeded the expected second-trimester range and showed an increasing trend, raising concern for possible malignancy. However, CA-125 elevation also occurs in numerous benign conditions, including endometriosis, adenomyosis, pelvic inflammatory disease, and uterine leiomyomas. Consequently, while the CA-125 elevation heightened clinical vigilance, it could not independently distinguish between benign and malignant etiologies.

Risk stratification in this case centered on three domains: oncological suspicion, mechanical complications, and symptom progression. The combination of large cystic masses with solid components, vascular hypertrophy, and a rising tumor marker created sufficient concern for ovarian malignancy that definitive histopathological diagnosis was warranted. Beyond oncological considerations, the masses posed substantial risks of acute complications. The cranial mass, measuring 18 cm, carried inherent risks of rupture due to its size and potential torsion if pedunculated—concerns that ultimately proved prescient given the intraoperative finding of a twisted, leaking pedicle. The patient's clinical deterioration, manifested by severe abdominal pain (VAS score of 5) and progressive dyspnea due to diaphragmatic compression, further supported the need for intervention. Mechanical dyspnea resulting from mass effect represented a significant quality-of-life burden and threatened to worsen as uterine enlargement progressed throughout the pregnancy.

The decision to proceed with surgical intervention during the second trimester balanced multiple competing considerations. Elective surgery during pregnancy is generally avoided when possible due to risks of pregnancy loss, preterm labor, and maternal morbidity. However, the second trimester (between 12 and 24 weeks of gestation) is considered the optimal window when surgery is unavoidable, as organogenesis is complete and the risk of spontaneous abortion is lower than in the first trimester, while uterine size remains manageable and preterm labor risk is lower than in the third trimester. In this case, the multidisciplinary team determined that the combination of symptomatic deterioration, diagnostic uncertainty with oncological concern, and risk of acute complications justified surgical exploration. Importantly, the surgical plan was designed to achieve diagnosis while preserving the pregnancy—removing only the mass deemed necessary, leaving the caudal mass in situ to minimize obstetric risk.

Informed consent for surgery during pregnancy requires explicit discussion of maternal and fetal risks. The patient received counseling regarding the possibility of pregnancy loss, preterm labor, hemorrhage, and the potential need for emergent cesarean delivery. The intraoperative plan included progesterone prophylaxis (administered intraoperatively and continued postoperatively) to reduce uterine contractility and maintain pregnancy, along with continuous fetal monitoring. This case illustrates that when surgery is indicated during pregnancy, a conservative surgical approach—limited to what is diagnostically or therapeutically necessary, combined with tocolytic prophylaxis and vigilant fetal monitoring—can achieve excellent maternal and neonatal outcomes.
