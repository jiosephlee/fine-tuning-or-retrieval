Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

Chapter 4: ## Surgical Management and Intraoperative Decision-Making

The decision to proceed with surgical intervention during pregnancy requires careful weighing of maternal and fetal risks against the potential consequences of conservative management. In this case, the multidisciplinary team opted for surgical exploration at 21 weeks and 6 days of gestation due to several compelling indications: worsening abdominal pain and restrictive dyspnea from mass effect, the uncertain diagnosis with concern for malignancy given elevated CA-125, and the risk of acute complications such as rupture, torsion, or infection. The timing was critical—delaying surgery would allow continued uterine enlargement, potentially making surgical access more difficult and increasing the risk of mass-related complications as the pregnancy progressed.

A longitudinal xiphopubic laparotomy was selected as the surgical approach. This incision provided optimal exposure for the anticipated complex procedure involving large masses in a gravid uterus. While laparoscopy can be considered for myomectomy during pregnancy, particularly in the first or early second trimester with smaller lesions, this case presented features favoring laparotomy: the enormous size of the masses (18 cm and 9.5 cm), the patient's obesity (BMI 33), the uncertain anatomical relationships, and the need for definitive diagnosis and potential extensive dissection. The open approach minimized operative time—a critical factor in preserving pregnancy—and allowed thorough exploration of the abdominal cavity.

Intraoperative findings fundamentally altered the preoperative diagnosis. The cranial mass, preoperatively thought to be a decidualized endometrioma based on imaging and CA-125 elevation, was revealed to be a pedunculated, cystic leiomyoma originating from the uterus. This mass was twisted and leaking, providing a likely explanation for the low-grade fever the patient developed during her hospital stay. The torsion had caused ischemic necrosis near the pedicle, a finding confirmed on pathology. This intraoperative discovery illustrates a key diagnostic challenge: uterine leiomyomas can closely mimic ovarian pathology on imaging, particularly when they undergo cystic degeneration, a phenomenon more common during pregnancy due to hormonal changes and rapid growth outpacing blood supply.

The surgical decision-making required real-time adaptation. The upper pedunculated myoma was excised by gently clamping and dividing its stalk, with careful closure of the uterine implantation site using absorbable suture. In contrast, the caudal mass—arising from the uterine torus and appearing as a fixed, soft, cystic intramural-subserosal myoma—was left in situ. This conservative approach was deliberate: resecting an intramural myoma during pregnancy carries substantial risks of hemorrhage, uterine dehiscence, and pregnancy loss. The principle guiding this decision was to address only the lesion causing acute symptoms while preserving the integrity of the gravid uterus.

Fetal safety was maintained through vigilant intraoperative monitoring. Fetal cardiac activity was documented before and after the procedure, with serial transvaginal ultrasound assessments throughout the operation. This practice aligns with established recommendations for documenting fetal heart rate before, during, and after any surgical procedure in pregnancy. The surgery lasted 2 hours and 25 minutes, reflecting the complexity of the case while prioritizing efficiency to minimize fetal exposure to anesthesia and maternal surgical stress.

Progesterone prophylaxis was administered to reduce the risk of preterm labor—a recognized complication of intrauterine surgery during pregnancy. The patient received 100 mg of progesterone intramuscularly intraoperatively, continued daily until discharge, followed by hydroxyprogesterone caproate 341 mg intramuscularly every 3 days until delivery. Progesterone supports uterine quiescence by modulating inflammatory pathways and reducing myometrial contractility. While evidence for tocolytic prophylaxis in myomectomy during pregnancy is limited, this approach represents a reasonable precaution given the risk of uterine irritation following surgery.

Antibiotic management addressed both prophylaxis and the patient's active Klebsiella pneumoniae urinary tract infection. Intraoperative prophylaxis with cefazolin 2 g intravenously was followed by postoperative treatment tailored to the urine culture antibiogram: cefazolin 1 g intravenously three times daily plus clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily. This combined approach ensured coverage for potential surgical site infection while treating the documented infection, which may have contributed to the patient's systemic inflammatory response.
