Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Management of atypical complicated abdominal masses in the second trimester of pregnancy"

Chapter 6: ## Evidence-Based Discussion and Clinical Lessons

This case illustrates the diagnostic complexity and management dilemmas posed by large abdominal masses during pregnancy, particularly when imaging findings suggest an alternative diagnosis. The patient presented with two large cystic masses initially interpreted as decidualized endometriomas, yet surgical exploration revealed pedunculated and intramural-subserosal leiomyomas with cystic degeneration. This diagnostic discrepancy underscores several important clinical principles that guide the evaluation and management of uterine myomas in pregnancy.

Uterine leiomyomas are encountered in 0.3–10.1% of pregnancies, while adnexal masses occur in 1–6% of gestations. The overlapping incidence rates contribute to diagnostic uncertainty, particularly when myomas undergo cystic degeneration and mimic adnexal pathology. During pregnancy, leiomyomas exhibit a non-linear growth pattern: they increase by approximately 6.12% between the first and second trimesters and 3.10% between the second and third trimesters, with an overall weekly growth rate of 6.90%. Importantly, this growth trend reverses postpartum, with a mean size reduction of 44.25% by six weeks after delivery. In this case, the residual pelvic myoma demonstrated partial postpartum regression to 7.5 cm, consistent with established growth kinetics.

The diagnostic challenge in this patient arose from several factors. First, the cystic appearance of both masses on ultrasound mimicked decidualized endometriomas, a benign condition that can also present as bilateral adnexal cysts during pregnancy. Second, the slight elevation of CA-125 to 41.8 U/mL—above the second-trimester median of 14.0 U/mL—initially suggested endometriosis, as CA-125 can be elevated in this condition. However, the interpreting physicians recognized that CA-125 lacks specificity during pregnancy; it physiologically peaks in the first trimester (median 23 U/mL) and again in the third trimester (median 21 U/mL), and can be elevated in numerous benign conditions including myomas, pregnancy-related changes, and pelvic inflammatory disease. The absence of a demonstrable stalk or feeding vessel for the upper mass on imaging—attributable to the spatial constraints imposed by the gravid uterus and the lesion's size—further complicated localization. These limitations highlight the importance of relying on clinical context rather than isolated imaging findings or tumor marker values.

The management approach in this case reflects the principle that conservative treatment is preferred for uterine myomas diagnosed during pregnancy, given the risks of bleeding, pregnancy loss, preterm labor, and uterine rupture. Surgical intervention should be reserved for specific indications: torsion of a pedunculated myoma, persistent symptoms unresponsive to conservative measures, oncological suspicion, or complications such as rupture or infection. This patient met several surgical criteria: worsening pain and dyspnea due to mass effect, the risk of complications from large vascularized masses, and the need for a definitive diagnosis given the uncertain origin of the lesions. The multidisciplinary team elected for laparotomy with a longitudinal xiphopubic incision, a choice influenced by the size and location of the masses, the gestational age of 21 weeks and 6 days, and the need to minimize operative time to preserve the pregnancy. While laparoscopic myomectomy can be considered for smaller, pedunculated myomas in early pregnancy—preferably using an open Hasson technique—laparotomy remains appropriate for larger, more complex lesions.

Several technical and perioperative measures contributed to the successful outcome in this case. Intraoperative monitoring of fetal heart rate before, during, and after surgery is mandatory, as is documented in this patient. Progesterone prophylaxis, administered as 100 mg intramuscularly daily during hospitalization followed by hydroxyprogesterone caproate 341 mg every three days until delivery, was used for tocolysis. Antibiotic prophylaxis and treatment were tailored to the patient's Klebsiella pneumoniae urinary tract infection. The decision to excise only the pedunculated upper myoma while leaving the caudal mass in situ exemplifies the principle of limiting surgical intervention to what is strictly necessary, thereby reducing obstetric risk.

Mode of delivery must be individualized based on myoma characteristics. In this case, the caudal myoma originating from the uterine torus would have obstructed fetal descent during the expulsive phase of labor. Consequently, an elective cesarean delivery was performed at 37 weeks of gestation, utilizing the same surgical scar to minimize the risk of uterine rupture. Evidence suggests that cesarean myomectomy does not significantly increase blood transfusion rates compared to cesarean section alone, though it does extend operative time and hospitalization. However, the decision to proceed with myomectomy at the time of cesarean should be guided by myoma size, location, and the surgeon's judgment.

This case yields several key clinical lessons. First, a multidisciplinary approach involving surgeons, gynecologists, and obstetricians is essential for balancing oncological concerns, symptom management, and pregnancy preservation. Second, imaging has inherent limitations: the "steric bulk" of the gravid uterus can obscure critical anatomical relationships, and tumor markers lack specificity in pregnancy. Third, overtreatment should be avoided; conservative management remains appropriate for asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic myomas, with the understanding that postpartum regression is expected. Finally, individualized care—guided by symptom severity, mass characteristics, and gestational age—should supersede algorithmic approaches in these complex clinical scenarios.
