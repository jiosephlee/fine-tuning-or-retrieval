Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

### [Morning Report] Why is the diagnosis of pancreatopleural fistula frequently delayed, and what historical clue should prompt clinicians to consider this diagnosis in a child with recurrent pleural effusion?

**Clinical Teaching Answer:**

The diagnosis of pancreatopleural fistula is frequently delayed because the disease presents with a "pulmonary mask"—patients typically manifest with respiratory symptoms such as dyspnea, chest pain, and recurrent pleural effusion, while the underlying pathology resides in the pancreas. In this case, the child presented three times over approximately two weeks with right-sided chest pain and respiratory distress, and the clinical focus appropriately centered on thoracic etiologies. He was initially managed for presumed tuberculosis based on a family contact history and mildly elevated pleural fluid ADA, and was even started on empiric antitubercular therapy before the correct diagnosis was established.

This diagnostic redirection occurs for several reasons. First, PPF is exceedingly rare in children, with only 47 pediatric cases reported in the literature. Second, respiratory symptoms predominate in approximately two-thirds of cases, while abdominal symptoms such as epigastric pain, vomiting, or weight loss are present in fewer than 20% of patients. Third, the pleural effusion is often massive and blood-tinged, as was seen here with dark red fluid aspirated repeatedly, which naturally prompts investigation for more common pediatric etiologies like tuberculosis, empyema, or malignancy.

The critical historical clue that should prompt consideration of PPF is a history of **blunt abdominal trauma**. In this case, the child ultimately reported suffering a bicycle handlebar injury to the abdomen one month prior to presentation. This history only emerged after repeated interviews during his third admission. Blunt abdominal trauma accounts for approximately 32% of pediatric PPF cases, as the force disrupts the pancreatic duct or creates a pseudocyst that tracks retroperitoneally through diaphragmatic hiatuses—most commonly the aortic or esophageal hiatus—into the pleural cavity.

When faced with a recurrent, exudative pleural effusion that is bloody or dark red, particularly with improvement after drainage but rapid reaccumulation, clinicians should specifically inquire about antecedent abdominal trauma. This simple historical detail can redirect the diagnostic workup toward pancreatic imaging and pleural fluid amylase and lipase measurement, potentially avoiding unnecessary procedures, empiric therapies, and diagnostic delays.
