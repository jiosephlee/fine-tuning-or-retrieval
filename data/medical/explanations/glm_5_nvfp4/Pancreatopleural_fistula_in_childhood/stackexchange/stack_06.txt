Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

### [Clinical Pearls] What are the primary etiologies of pancreatopleural fistula in pediatric patients compared to adults, and what genetic mutations should be considered in recurrent pediatric pancreatitis?

**Clinical Teaching Answer**

The etiologies of pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) differ substantially between pediatric and adult populations, reflecting the distinct risk factor profiles for pancreatitis in each group.

**In adults**, chronic alcoholic pancreatitis is the dominant cause, accounting for over 99% of cases in large series. The pathophysiology involves chronic ductal disruption and pseudocyst formation from recurrent inflammatory injury, which then tracks into the pleural space.

**In children**, three primary etiologies predominate:
1. **Genetic/hereditary pancreatitis** — the leading cause in pediatric PPF
2. **Blunt abdominal trauma** — comprising approximately 32% of pediatric cases
3. **Idiopathic causes** — often representing unrecognized genetic or ductal variants

This distinction is well-illustrated in our case: the child had no history of alcohol exposure (obviously), and the PPF resulted from blunt abdominal trauma—a bicycle handle injury—that disrupted the pancreatic duct posteriorly, allowing pancreatic secretions to track retroperitoneally through the esophageal hiatus into the pleural cavity.

**Regarding genetic mutations in recurrent pediatric pancreatitis**, the following should be considered:

| Gene | Clinical Significance |
|------|----------------------|
| **PRSS1** (cationic trypsinogen) | Most common; R122H and N29I mutations are classic. Causes hereditary pancreatitis through trypsin autoactivation. |
| **SPINK1** (serine peptidase inhibitor) | Acts as a trypsin inhibitor; mutations reduce protective capacity. Accounts for up to 30% of pediatric chronic pancreatitis when combined with PRSS1. |
| **CFTR** (cystic fibrosis transmembrane regulator) | Associated with idiopathic chronic pancreatitis; some patients have minimal or no pulmonary symptoms. |
| **CTRC** (chymotrypsin C) | Less common; impairs trypsin degradation pathway. |

The key clinical takeaway: When evaluating a child with recurrent pancreatitis or unexplained pleural effusion, a detailed history of antecedent trauma is essential, and if pancreatitis is recurrent or chronic, genetic testing for PRSS1, SPINK1, and CFTR mutations should be pursued. As demonstrated in this case, PPF can present with a "pulmonary mask"—massive recurrent pleural effusion with minimal abdominal symptoms—leading to diagnostic delays if the pancreatic origin is not considered.
