Title: Blog about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

# When the Chest Hides the Belly: Pancreatopleural Fistula and the Pulmonary Mask

A child presents with recurrent right-sided pleural effusion. You drain it, he gets better. He comes back a week later with the same effusion. You drain it again. He comes back a third time. You're investigating for tuberculosis, malignancy, empyema — all the usual thoracic suspects. But what if the problem isn't in the chest at all?

This case stopped me in my tracks because it perfectly illustrates a diagnostic trap we all risk falling into: **the pulmonary mask**. The pathology was in the pancreas. The symptoms were in the chest. And the history that unlocked it all came only on the third admission.

## The Case That Walked in Three Times

A boy in late childhood presented with progressive dyspnea and right-sided chest pain over a week. His exam was classic for a large right pleural effusion: stony dullness to percussion, diminished breath sounds, reduced vocal fremitus. Oxygen saturation sat in the high 80s. Chest X-ray confirmed what the ultrasound already showed — about a liter of pleural fluid.

They drained it. The fluid was dark red — bloody appearing. His oxygen sat corrected, his symptoms resolved, and he went home.

Six days later, he was back. Same story. They drained another liter of dark red fluid. Given a family history of tuberculosis contact and a mildly elevated pleural fluid ADA, they started empiric anti-tubercular therapy.

Five days after that, he returned again. Same complaints. Same findings. It was only on this third admission, after direct and repeated questioning, that the boy finally mentioned what no one had asked about before: **he'd suffered blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handlebar one month earlier**.

That single sentence changed everything.

## The Anatomy of Misdirection

Here's where the case gets fascinating from an anatomical perspective. Pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) is a rare entity — only 47 pediatric cases have been documented in the literature, compared to over 300 in adults. But the mechanism is beautifully logical once you see it.

When the pancreas is disrupted posteriorly — whether from trauma, pancreatitis, or a pseudocyst — pancreatic fluid doesn't just sit there. It follows the path of least resistance. The retroperitoneum communicates with the posterior mediastinum through natural diaphragmatic openings: primarily the aortic and esophageal hiatuses. Pancreatic enzymes track superiorly, enter the mediastinum, and rupture through into the pleural cavity.

It's essentially a leaky pipe running from the pancreas straight into the chest. And the presentation reflects that: over two-thirds of reported cases present with predominant respiratory symptoms — dyspnea, cough, massive recurrent pleural effusion. Less than 20% have abdominal symptoms at presentation.

The chest is screaming. The belly is silent. And if you don't think to ask about the abdomen, you'll miss it.

## The Diagnostic Keys

This case highlights several critical diagnostic pearls. First, **the character of the fluid matters**. Bloody or dark red pleural fluid that recurs should raise eyebrows. The bloody appearance comes from the digestive action of pancreatic enzymes on tissue — not from primary hemorrhage.

Second, **pleural fluid amylase**. A level ≥1000 IU/L is essentially pathognomonic for PPF. In this case, pleural fluid amylase was 6118 U/L and lipase was 7211 U/L. That's not subtle. But if you don't send it, you don't find it.

Third, **imaging**. CT can show the tract in maybe a third to half of cases. MRCP offers 70-80% sensitivity for visualizing the fistula and pancreatic duct anatomy. ERCP remains the gold standard for both diagnosis and therapeutic planning, though in children it's often reserved for cases where intervention is needed.

And fourth — perhaps most importantly — **the history**. This child concealed his trauma history initially. It took multiple admissions and repeated questioning before the bicycle handlebar injury came to light. In pediatric patients especially, the history may not be volunteered. You have to ask, and ask again.

## How They Treated It

Once the diagnosis was established, management followed a conservative approach: nothing by mouth, total parenteral nutrition, octreotide infusion at 30 mcg/kg/day to suppress exocrine pancreatic secretion, pancreatic enzyme replacement, and chest tube drainage.

Over ten days, the output gradually decreased and eventually stopped. The chest tube came out. The child went home and has remained effusion-free on follow-up.

This represents the conservative treatment ladder that should be attempted first in patients without obvious ductal anomalies: bowel rest to stop pancreatic stimulation, somatostatin analogues to reduce secretion, and drainage of the pleural collection. In pediatric cases, the success rate of conservative management alone is reported at only around 12% — so be prepared to escalate.

If conservative measures fail over 2-3 weeks, endoscopic approaches with ERCP and transpapillary stenting are the next step, with success rates approaching 100% in pediatric case series. Surgery — distal pancreatectomy or pancreaticojejunostomy — remains the fallback for refractory cases or when anatomy precludes endoscopic intervention.

## Why We Miss It

The authors of this case put it perfectly: this disease has a "pulmonary mask." The patient presents with thoracic complaints. We investigate the thorax. We find a pleural effusion. We treat the effusion. And we never think to look south of the diaphragm.

In this case, the team initially pursued tuberculosis empirically based on family contact history and a mildly elevated ADA. They were doing thorough workup for hemorrhagic pleural effusion. But they were looking in the wrong cavity.

The lesson here isn't that PPF is common — it isn't. The lesson is about **recurrent effusions that don't behave as expected**. When a pleural effusion keeps coming back after adequate drainage, and especially when the fluid is bloody or serosanguinous, you need to broaden your differential beyond infection and malignancy. Think about fistulous connections. Think about the abdomen. And specifically, think about pancreatic amylase.

## The Take-Home Points

- **Pancreatopleural fistula presents with thoracic symptoms** — dyspnea, cough, massive recurrent pleural effusion — but the pathology is abdominal. Over 68% of cases have predominant respiratory symptoms; less than 20% have abdominal complaints.
- **Pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L is pathognomonic for PPF**. If you see a recurrent pleural effusion with bloody or serosanguinous fluid, send amylase and lipase on the pleural fluid.
- **The anatomical pathway** is through the retroperitoneum and diaphragmatic hiatuses (aortic and esophageal) into the mediastinum and pleural space. Pancreatic fluid follows the path of least resistance — and in posterior disruptions, that path goes up.
- **History is critical and may require persistence**. This child concealed his bicycle handlebar trauma. It came out only on the third admission after repeated questioning. Pediatric patients in particular may not volunteer relevant history.
- **Treatment ladder**: Conservative first (NPO, TPN, octreotide, chest drainage) for 2-3 weeks → Endoscopic stenting via ERCP if conservative fails or if ductal anatomy warrants → Surgery for refractory cases.
- **In children, trauma is a major cause** — blunt abdominal injury accounts for about 32% of pediatric PPF cases, compared to alcohol-related chronic pancreatitis in adults.

When the chest keeps filling up and your thoracic workup is coming up empty, remember: sometimes the belly is whispering while the chest is screaming. You just have to remember to listen.



# Pleural Fluid Amylase: The Single Test That Changed Everything

## The Case That Stopped Me in My Tracks

A child presents with recurrent, massive pleural effusions. You drain a liter of dark red fluid. It comes back. You drain it again. It comes back a third time. You've started empiric antitubercular therapy because there's a family history of TB contact and the ADA is mildly elevated. But something doesn't fit.

Then you finally send one test that changes the entire trajectory: **pleural fluid amylase**.

It comes back at 6118 U/L.

That single result cracked the case wide open. This wasn't tuberculosis. This wasn't malignancy. This was a pancreatopleural fistula — an internal pancreatic leak tracking superiorly through the esophageal hiatus into the chest cavity. The pathology wasn't in the lungs at all. It was in the pancreas, and the clue was sitting in that pleural fluid all along.

## When the Answer Is Hiding in Plain Sight

Let's be honest — how many of us routinely send pleural fluid amylase? It's not part of the standard panel for most effusions. We think about LDH, protein, glucose, cell count, Gram stain, culture. Maybe ADA in endemic areas. Amylase often gets forgotten.

But in this case, that forgotten test was the difference between weeks of ineffective antitubercular therapy and definitive management. The child had a history of blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle injury a month prior — a detail that only emerged after the third admission and repeated history-taking. The pancreatic duct had been disrupted, and pancreatic secretions were following the path of least resistance: retroperitoneally upward through the diaphragmatic hiatuses and into the pleural space.

The effusion kept recurring because the source was still leaking. Every thoracentesis was essentially draining a pancreatic pseudocyst that happened to empty into the chest.

## The Magic Number: ≥1000 IU/L

Here's the clinical pearl that should be burned into memory: **pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L is pathognomonic for pancreatopleural fistula.**

Normal pleural fluid amylase is essentially negligible. Even in other conditions that elevate amylase, levels rarely approach four figures. This child's amylase was over 6000 U/L — there was no ambiguity once the test was sent.

But before we get too comfortable with that cutoff, we need to remember that elevated pleural fluid amylase isn't unique to pancreatic pathology. The differential matters.

## Not All Amylase Is Created Equal

The differential for amylase-rich pleural effusions includes three main categories:

Pancreatopleural fistula is the headliner, but we also need to consider esophageal perforation — Boerhaave's syndrome — where saliva rich in amylase leaks into the pleural space. These patients typically have a clear history of retching or vomiting followed by severe chest pain, and they're usually pretty obviously unwell.

Malignancy is the other major consideration. Lung cancer, leukemia, lymphoma, and metastatic disease can all produce amylase-rich effusions, typically from tumor production of salivary-type amylase.

This is where amylase typing becomes relevant. Pancreatopleural fistulas produce **pancreatic-type amylase** in the pleural fluid. Malignancies and esophageal ruptures tend to produce **salivary-type amylase**. If you're dealing with a confusing case and have the ability to type the amylase, it can help narrow the differential.

In this child's case, the distinction was clinically straightforward. No history of vomiting or retching, normal bowel function, and no signs of malignancy on imaging or peripheral smear. The extremely high amylase level combined with the trauma history made PPF the clear diagnosis.

## A Disease That Wears a Pulmonary Mask

The literature on pancreatopleural fistula is striking: approximately 68% of cases present with predominant respiratory symptoms. Massive, recurrent pleural effusions. Dyspnea. Cough. Less than 20% have abdominal symptoms at presentation. This is a pancreas problem masquerading as a lung problem.

The mechanism explains the clinical presentation. When the pancreatic duct disruption occurs posteriorly, fluid tracks retroperitoneally rather than into the peritoneal cavity. The retroperitoneum communicates with the posterior mediastinum through the aortic and esophageal hiatuses. Pancreatic enzymes then dissect superiorly, eventually rupturing into the pleural space. The result is a recurrent pleural effusion that will never resolve with thoracentesis alone because the faucet is still running.

In adults, chronic alcohol-related pancreatitis dominates as the etiology. In children, think trauma and genetic causes — PRSS1, SPINK1, and CFTR mutations account for a significant portion of pediatric chronic pancreatitis. This child's trigger was blunt abdominal trauma, but the presentation was indistinguishable from genetic cases until the history was teased out.

## The Take-Home Points

- **Send pleural fluid amylase on recurrent or undifferentiated effusions** — it's a low-cost test with high-yield diagnostic potential
- **≥1000 IU/L pleural fluid amylase is pathognomonic for pancreatopleural fistula** — levels this high are essentially diagnostic
- **Differential for amylase-rich effusions includes PPF, esophageal perforation, and malignancy** — clinical context and amylase typing can help distinguish
- **Pancreatic-type vs. salivary-type amylase matters** — PPF produces pancreatic-type; malignancy and esophageal rupture typically produce salivary-type
- **Recurrent pleural effusions may have an abdominal source** — the pathology isn't always in the chest

## Final Thought

This case is a reminder that sometimes the most powerful diagnostic tool isn't advanced imaging or complex algorithms. Sometimes it's a single test that we just need to remember to order. The child in this case underwent three admissions, two thoracenteses, empiric antitubercular therapy, and extensive pulmonary workup before someone thought to send amylase on the pleural fluid.

Once that result came back, everything changed. NPO status, total parenteral nutrition, octreotide infusion, and chest drainage led to resolution within 10 days. No surgery required.

The test was available from day one. We just needed to think of it.



# Bicycle Handlebar Trauma: A Classic Mechanism for Pediatric Pancreatic Injury

## The Case That Teaches Us to Look Below the Diaphragm

A child presents with recurrent, massive, blood-tinged pleural effusions. You drain a liter of dark red fluid, he gets better, and then he bounces right back — not once, not twice, but three times. You're thinking tuberculosis, empyema, maybe even malignancy. But here's the twist: the pathology isn't in the chest at all. It's in the pancreas.

This case of pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) in a child is a masterclass in how pediatric pancreatic trauma can masquerade as respiratory disease — and why that seemingly innocuous bicycle handlebar injury your patient forgot to mention might be the key to everything.

## Why the Handlebar Matters

Bicycle handlebar trauma is one of the most classic — and classically underappreciated — mechanisms of blunt pancreatic injury in children. The physics are elegant in their brutality: a narrow, rigid object impacts the epigastrium with concentrated force, driving the retroperitoneal pancreas directly against the vertebral column. There's no give, no protection, and often no external evidence of injury.

In this case, the child initially concealed the history of handlebar trauma, and it took multiple admissions before the team extracted this crucial detail. This is the rule, not the exception. Kids don't volunteer trauma histories reliably, and when symptoms are delayed by weeks, the connection isn't obvious to anyone.

## The Anatomy of the Leak

Here's where the anatomy gets fascinating. When a pancreatic duct disruption occurs posteriorly, pancreatic secretions don't pool in the abdomen. Instead, they follow the path of least resistance — tracking superiorly through the retroperitoneum, slipping through the aortic or esophageal hiatus, and entering the pleural cavity.

The result? A pleural effusion that keeps coming back because you're draining the wrong end of the pipe. The pleural fluid in PPF isn't just any effusion — it's enzyme-rich pancreatic juice. In this case, pleural fluid amylase was 6118 U/L and lipase 7211 U/L. Pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L should immediately raise your suspicion for a pancreatic source.

## Why Pancreatic Injury Goes Unrecognized Initially

Pediatric pancreatic trauma is notorious for delayed diagnosis for several reasons. First, the retroperitoneal location means peritoneal signs are often absent or minimal early on. Second, initial serum amylase and lipase may be normal — they often rise 24 to 48 hours after injury, so a single set drawn in the ED can be falsely reassuring. Third, children may not localize pain well, and the clinical picture can be dominated by seemingly unrelated symptoms — like, in this case, respiratory distress from a pleural effusion.

The literature notes that fewer than 20% of pediatric PPF cases present with abdominal symptoms. Over two-thirds present with respiratory complaints: dyspnea, cough, chest pain. The disease literally wears a pulmonary mask.

## The Clinical Clues That Should Stop You in Your Tracks

Several features in this case should have triggered earlier consideration of a pancreatic etiology, and they're worth committing to memory for the next time you see a child with a recurrent effusion. The fluid was dark red and hemorrhagic in appearance — not the typical straw-colored transudate or frank pus of more common etiologies. The effusion was massive (a liter each time) and unilateral. It recurred rapidly after drainage. And importantly, the child had risk factors for metabolic syndrome (BMI of 35) and the initial mechanism was obscured by the child's reluctance to disclose it.

When an effusion keeps coming back, think about what's continuously feeding it. A fistula is essentially a leaky pipe — and in PPF, the pancreas is continuously leaking into the chest.

## Getting the Diagnosis Right

If you suspect PPF, imaging is critical but must be targeted appropriately. Chest X-ray and thoracic ultrasound will show the effusion but not the source. CT can identify pancreatic pseudocysts, ductal dilation, and the tracking collection, but visualizes the actual fistula in only about one-third of cases. MRCP is the preferred modality, offering 70-80% sensitivity for visualizing the fistula tract and pancreatic duct anatomy. ERCP remains the gold standard for definitive delineation and therapeutic planning.

In this case, the CT showed a multiloculated collection tracking from the right paraesophageal region to the pancreatic tail — a critical finding that prompted MRCP, which confirmed the diagnosis and guided management.

## Treatment: Rest the Pancreas, Drain the Chest

For pediatric PPF without pancreatic ductal anomalies, conservative management is the first line: bowel rest, total parenteral nutrition, somatostatin analogues like octreotide to suppress exocrine secretion, and thoracic drainage. The pancreas needs to stop secreting long enough for the fistulous tract to close.

In this child, the team placed an intercostal drain, initiated NPO status with TPN, started octreotide at 30 µg/kg/day, and added pancreatic enzyme replacement. Drainage decreased over 10 days and ultimately stopped. He's been symptom-free on follow-up.

However, the literature tells us that conservative management succeeds in only about 12% of pediatric PPF cases. When it fails, or when there's significant ductal disruption, endoscopic stenting or surgical intervention (distal pancreatectomy, Puestow procedure) may be necessary. The key is early recognition so you're not wasting time on ineffective pleural interventions while the leak continues.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Bicycle handlebar trauma is a classic mechanism for pediatric pancreatic injury** — ask specifically about it, even weeks after the event, and recognize that children may not volunteer this history.
- **Pancreatic trauma can present with purely respiratory symptoms** — over two-thirds of pediatric PPF cases present with chest complaints, not abdominal pain.
- **Recurrent, massive, hemorrhagic pleural effusion is a red flag** — especially when it reaccumulates rapidly after drainage.
- **Check pleural fluid amylase and lipase** — pleural fluid amylase ≥1000 IU/L is pathognomonic for PPF; levels in the thousands should immediately redirect your differential.
- **Initial serum pancreatic enzymes may be normal** — they often rise 24-48 hours post-injury, so don't let a single normal value rule out pancreatic injury.
- **MRCP is the preferred imaging modality** for visualizing the fistula tract and planning treatment; CT alone misses the fistula in over half of cases.
- **Treatment success requires resting the pancreas** — octreotide, bowel rest, and TPN give the fistula time to close; thoracic drainage alone won't solve the problem.

## The Bottom Line

This case is a reminder that in pediatric trauma, the mechanism matters more than the timeline. A handlebar to the belly a month ago might be the reason your patient keeps coming back with a bloody pleural effusion today. When the chest keeps filling up, look down — the leak might be coming from the pancreas.



# Third Time's the Charm: When to Re-take the History

A child walks into your ED with dyspnea, right-sided chest pain, and a pleural effusion. You tap it, drain it, and send him home. He comes back a week later with the same thing. You drain it again. He comes back a third time. Same story.

But here's the twist: on that third admission, after yet another round of history-taking, a crucial detail finally emerges—one that changes everything. This wasn't tuberculosis. The pleural effusion? Dark red, bloody-appearing fluid. A liter of it. Each time.

This case is a masterclass in diagnostic momentum and premature closure. The child had a grandfather treated for pulmonary tuberculosis. The pleural fluid ADA was mildly elevated. So naturally, the team started antitubercular therapy. That's a reasonable Bayesian move—until it isn't.

But let's talk about what should have screamed "stop looking at the chest." The pleural fluid was **dark red** and **recurrent**—over a liter each time. That's not your typical transudative effusion. ## Why the History Took Three Admissions to Emerge

Here's where this case gets deeply human. The child didn't mention the bicycle handle trauma initially. He was being bullied at school and deliberately concealed what had happened. It wasn't until the third admission—when the team sat down and took the history again, perhaps with more patience and a different approach—that the trauma history surfaced.

This is not a failure. This is medicine. Patients withhold information for all kinds of reasons: embarrassment, fear, not understanding the relevance, or—in this case—bullying and shame. Our job isn't just to ask once and move on. It's to create enough psychological safety that the truth can emerge, and to revisit our assumptions when the clinical picture doesn't quite fit.

Pancreatopleural fistula is vanishingly rare in children—only about 47 cases reported in the literature. In adults, alcohol-related chronic pancreatitis is the dominant cause. In kids, think blunt trauma and genetic pancreatitis (PRSS1, SPINK1, CFTR mutations).

It's a leaky pipe with a long underground route.

The literature shows about 68% of PPF cases present with respiratory symptoms—dyspnea, cough, massive recurrent pleural effusion. Less than 20% have abdominal symptoms. The abdomen is silent. The chest is screaming. And if you don't think to look below the diaphragm, you'll miss it entirely.

Once the diagnosis clicked, management was straightforward: NPO status, total parenteral nutrition, octreotide to shut down pancreatic exocrine secretion, and a chest drain. The output tapered over ten days, the drain came out, and the kid went home. No surgery needed. He's been symptom-free since.

## Key Take-Home Points

- **Re-take the history.** When a patient keeps bouncing back with the same problem, your initial story is probably incomplete. Sit down again. Ask differently. Build rapport. The truth often emerges on the second or third pass.
- **Bloody recurrent pleural effusions demand amylase.** If you're draining liters of dark fluid and it keeps coming back, send pleural fluid amylase and lipase. It's a cheap test that rules in or out a diagnosis that's easy to miss.
- **The pancreas can present as a lung problem.** Pancreatopleural fistula is the ultimate "pulmonary mask." In kids, think trauma and genetic causes. In adults, think alcohol.
- **Premature closure is seductive.** The grandfather's TB history and mildly elevated ADA were red herrings. The team latched onto a plausible diagnosis and stopped looking. It took a third admission to break that cognitive loop.
- **Patients hide things.** Sometimes intentionally, sometimes not. Our job is to keep asking, keep listening, and resist the urge to close the case before the full picture emerges.

This case reminded me that the most important diagnostic tool we have isn't the CT scanner or the lab panel—it's the repeated, thoughtful, non-judgmental conversation at the bedside. Sometimes the history doesn't surface until you've earned it.



# PPF Management: From NPO to Surgery – A Practical Algorithm

## The Case That Stops You in Your Tracks

A child presents with recurrent, massive, dark-red pleural effusions. You drain a liter, he gets better. Six days later, he's back. Same thing. Five days after that, he returns again. You've started empiric anti-tubercular therapy based on a positive family contact history and mildly elevated ADA. You're chasing a thoracic diagnosis hard.

And then, on the third admission, a seemingly small detail emerges: **blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle one month prior**.

That's the moment everything changes. A contrast-enhanced CT shows a multiloculated collection tracking from the right pleural space, through the retroperitoneum, all the way to the tail of the pancreas. Pleural fluid amylase? **6118 U/L**. Lipase? **7211 U/L**.

This isn't tuberculosis. This is a pancreatopleural fistula – an internal pancreatic leak acting like a drain pipe, diverting enzyme-rich secretions directly into the chest.

## A Rare Entity That Demands a High Index of Suspicion

Let's be honest: PPF in the pediatric population is vanishingly rare. Only 47 cases reported in children, compared to over 300 in adults. But when you see it, you'll never forget it. The classic presentation is massive, recurrent, unilateral or bilateral pleural effusions with minimal to no abdominal symptoms. In the literature, 68% of patients present with respiratory complaints. Less than 20% have abdominal symptoms. The pathology is in the pancreas, but the patient is drowning in their own pancreatic fluid.

The mechanism matters here. When a pancreatic duct disruption occurs posteriorly, fluid tracks retroperitoneally, follows the path of least resistance through the aortic or esophageal hiatus, and breaks into the pleural cavity. Anterior ruptures cause ascites. Posterior ruptures cause effusions. In children, think blunt abdominal trauma (32% in one cohort) and hereditary pancreatitis (PRSS1, SPINK1, CFTR mutations) as your primary etiologies.

## The Diagnostic Key: Send the Amylase

If you take nothing else from this case, let it be this: **in any recurrent, unexplained pleural effusion, send pleural fluid amylase and lipase**. A level ≥1000 IU/L is essentially pathognomonic for PPF. Standard pleural fluid analysis won't catch this. The fluid in this case was dark red and hemorrhagic appearing – and it would have been easy to stop at "empyema versus malignancy versus TB."

The diagnostic workup should include MRCP, which offers 70-80% sensitivity for visualizing the fistula tract and ductal anatomy. CT is useful for showing pseudocysts and pancreatic inflammation, but only visualizes the actual fistula in 33-47% of cases. ERCP remains the gold standard for therapeutic planning.

## The Management Algorithm: What Actually Works

Here's where things get tricky. There are no standardized pediatric guidelines for PPF. You're operating on case reports and small series. But a practical algorithm emerges from the available evidence.

Start with conservative management. This means strict NPO status, total parenteral nutrition to rest the gut and reduce pancreatic stimulation, somatostatin analogues (octreotide at 10-40 µg/kg/day), and adequate thoracic drainage with a chest tube. Add pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy. The goal is simple: reduce pancreatic secretions, decompress the pleural space, and give the fistula time to close.

But here's the uncomfortable truth – **the success rate of conservative management in pediatric PPF is only about 12%**. That's not great. In our case, the child responded to conservative therapy over 10 days, but that puts him in the minority.

If conservative measures fail after 2-3 weeks, escalate to endoscopic intervention. ERCP with trans-papillary stenting reduces intraductal pressure and allows the leak to seal. In pediatric series, ERCP has shown success rates approaching 100%, with long-term success exceeding 90% in mixed-age cohorts. Stents typically remain in place for 4 weeks to 6 months.

Surgery becomes necessary when endoscopic stenting isn't technically feasible – severe ductal obstruction, distally located fistulas, or failed medical management beyond 3-4 weeks. Options include distal pancreatectomy, Puestow procedure (pancreatojejunostomy), or cystojejunostomy. The surgical success rate in children is approximately 93%, with operative mortality under 5% and recurrence around 11%.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- In any recurrent, unexplained pleural effusion – especially hemorrhagic or massive – send pleural fluid amylase and lipase. A level ≥1000 U/L is pathognomonic for PPF.
- PPF wears a "pulmonary mask." The patient presents with respiratory distress, but the pathology is abdominal. Ask specifically about recent abdominal trauma.
- Conservative management (NPO, TPN, octreotide, chest tube drainage) has only a 12% success rate in children. Don't wait too long to escalate.
- ERCP with stenting is highly effective in pediatric PPF and should be considered early if conservative management isn't working.
- Surgery (distal pancreatectomy, Puestow procedure) is definitive and has excellent success rates when endoscopic options fail or aren't feasible.

## The Bottom Line

Pancreatopleural fistula is a diagnosis that requires deliberate clinical suspicion. The child in this case went through three admissions and empiric anti-tubercular therapy before the right questions were asked and the right tests were sent. When you see recurrent pleural effusion with atypical features, pause. Think beyond the chest. Ask about trauma. Send the amylase. The answer might be hiding in the abdomen.
