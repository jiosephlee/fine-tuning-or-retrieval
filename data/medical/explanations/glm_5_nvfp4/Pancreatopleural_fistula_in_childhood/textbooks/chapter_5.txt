Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

Chapter 5: ## Management, Outcomes, and Clinical Lessons

The management of pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) follows a stepwise approach, progressing from conservative medical measures to endoscopic intervention, and finally to surgical options if needed. Due to the rarity of PPF in the pediatric population—with only 47 cases reported—no standardized treatment guidelines exist. Management decisions are therefore based on evidence from case reports and extrapolation from adult literature, with the choice of therapy dependent on ductal anatomy and response to initial interventions.

Conservative management serves as the first-line approach and was the strategy employed in this case. The cornerstone of medical therapy is bowel rest with nil per oral (NPO) status, which minimizes pancreatic exocrine stimulation and reduces the volume of pancreatic secretions. Total parenteral nutrition (TPN) provides caloric support while maintaining bowel rest. Somatostatin analogues, specifically Octreotide, are administered to suppress pancreatic exocrine secretion through inhibition of hormonal pathways that stimulate the pancreas. The mechanism involves somatostatin receptors on pancreatic acinar cells; when activated, Octreotide reduces the production and secretion of digestive enzymes. In this patient, Octreotide was given at 30 µg/kg/day, within the recommended range of 10–40 µg/kg/day. Pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT) was also initiated to support digestion while the fistula healed, although its primary role is to replace enzymes that cannot reach the duodenum due to the leak.

Thoracic drainage via intercostal chest tube is essential for managing the pleural effusion and preventing respiratory compromise. The drain allows evacuation of the amylase-rich fluid and creates conditions favorable for fistula closure. In this case, an intercostal drainage (ICD) tube was placed on the third admission, after imaging confirmed the diagnosis.

The success rates for each treatment tier inform clinical decision-making. Conservative therapy alone has a success rate of approximately 12% in pediatric cases, which is notably lower than in adults. This limited efficacy reflects the reality that many pediatric PPFs involve significant ductal disruption that requires intervention. Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) with transpapillary stenting is the preferred intervention when conservative measures fail or when ductal obstruction is identified. Stent placement bridges the disrupted duct segment, reduces intraductal pressure, and allows pancreatic secretions to flow physiologically into the duodenum rather than through the fistulous tract. Endoscopic intervention achieves fistula closure in 48% of patients within 2–3 weeks, with success rates approaching 100% in pediatric case series. Stents typically remain in place for 4 weeks to 6 months. Surgical options are reserved for cases where endoscopic approaches fail or are technically not feasible due to severe ductal obstruction or distal fistula location. Procedures include distal pancreatectomy (resection of the pancreatic segment containing the fistula), the Puestow procedure (pancreatojejunostomy to decompress the pancreatic duct), or cystojejunostomy (drainage of an associated pseudocyst into the jejunum). Surgery demonstrates a 93% success rate in pediatric patients with operative mortality below 5% and recurrence rates around 11%.

In this patient, the treatment course demonstrated a successful outcome with conservative management alone. After MRCP confirmed the diagnosis, the child was placed on NPO status with TPN, Octreotide infusion, PERT, and continued thoracic drainage via the ICD. Over 10 days, the volume of fluid draining from the chest tube progressively decreased and eventually stopped. This clinical response indicated successful fistula closure, allowing removal of the ICD and discharge from the hospital. The decision to attempt conservative therapy first was appropriate given that MRCP showed the fistula communicated with the distal pancreatic duct without complete ductal discontinuity.

Follow-up care focused on preventing recurrence and addressing modifiable risk factors. The child's BMI of 35 indicated obesity, which increases the risk of pancreatic inflammation. Lifestyle modifications were recommended, including a balanced diet with reduced processed foods and regular exercise. At monthly follow-up visits, the patient remained symptom-free without recurrent pleural effusions, returned to school, and could perform strenuous physical activities without difficulty.

This case offers several important clinical lessons. First, PPF exemplifies how abdominal pathology can present with predominantly thoracic symptoms—a phenomenon the authors describe as a "pulmonary mask." The initial focus on finding a pulmonary cause led to empiric antitubercular therapy based on a positive family contact history and mildly elevated pleural fluid ADA, delaying the correct diagnosis. Second, obtaining a thorough history is critical. The key diagnostic clue—blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle—emerged only after repeated questioning on the third admission. This history, combined with the characteristic dark red pleural fluid and elevated amylase levels, ultimately directed the workup toward a pancreatic etiology. Third, early diagnosis prevents unnecessary interventions and potential complications. Had the history been obtained initially, the child would have avoided multiple thoracenteses, empiric antitubercular therapy, and repeated hospitalizations. Finally, elevated pleural fluid amylase (≥1000 IU/L) should prompt consideration of PPF, and MRCP should be obtained to visualize the fistula and ductal anatomy when this diagnosis is suspected.
