Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Pancreatopleural fistula in childhood"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Assessment

The patient initially presented with a one-week history of progressive dyspnea and right-sided chest pain. On examination, he demonstrated mild tachypnea with a respiratory rate of 20 breaths per minute and hypoxemia, with oxygen saturation of 88% to 89% on room air. The physical examination revealed classic findings of a large right-sided pleural effusion: diminished chest wall movement on the right, stony dullness to percussion over the right subscapular, infra-axillary, and axillary regions, reduced vocal fremitus, and decreased breath sounds in the same distribution. The abdominal examination was unremarkable, with no tenderness, organomegaly, or palpable masses. These findings localized the pathology definitively to the right hemithorax but offered no immediate clue to its underlying cause.

The initial diagnostic workup confirmed a massive pleural effusion. Chest radiography demonstrated right-sided pleural effusion with blunting of the right costophrenic angle. Bedside lung ultrasound quantified the effusion at approximately one liter. Thoracocentesis yielded one liter of dark red pleural fluid, which provided immediate symptomatic relief and improved oxygen saturation to 95% to 98% on room air. At this stage, the clinical picture appeared consistent with an uncomplicated parapneumonic effusion or early empyema, and empirical broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy with piperacillin-tazobactam was initiated after obtaining blood cultures.

The clinical course became complicated when the patient returned six days after discharge with recurrent symptoms. A second thoracocentesis again yielded approximately one liter of dark red pleural fluid. Given the history of household tuberculosis contact and a mildly elevated pleural fluid adenosine deaminase level, empirical antitubercular therapy was initiated. When the effusion recurred a third time within five days, the clinical team recognized that the working diagnosis was incorrect and systematically revisited the history.

A critical aspect of pediatric medicine is the challenge of obtaining accurate histories, particularly when children may withhold information due to embarrassment, fear, or peer-related concerns. In this case, repeated sessions of rapport-building and careful questioning eventually revealed a key historical detail that had been previously concealed: the child had sustained blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handlebar injury one month before symptom onset. This injury had not been reported initially, likely because of social factors mentioned in the case report, including bullying at school. The history of direct abdominal trauma fundamentally reframed the differential diagnosis, redirecting attention from primary pulmonary pathology to potential pancreatic injury.

The initial clinical reasoning appropriately focused on thoracic causes, as the patient presented exclusively with respiratory symptoms and had no abdominal complaints. This phenomenon, wherein an abdominal pathology manifests predominantly with thoracic symptoms, is referred to in the case report as a "pulmonary mask." The concept highlights how anatomical connections between the abdomen and thorax, particularly through the aortic and esophageal hiatuses, can allow disease processes to traverse compartments and present at sites distant from their origin. In pancreatopleural fistula, pancreatic secretions track superiorly through the retroperitoneum into the mediastinum and pleural cavity, producing massive pleural effusion while the pancreas itself remains clinically silent. This case demonstrates that when a pleural effusion recurs without an obvious thoracic explanation, clinicians must broaden the diagnostic search to include extrapulmonary causes, even in the absence of abdominal symptoms.

## Differential Diagnosis of Recurrent Hemorrhagic Pleural Effusion

The approach to recurrent pleural effusion requires a systematic framework that narrows the differential diagnosis through careful analysis of pleural fluid characteristics and clinical context. In this case, the combination of recurrent, hemorrhagic pleural effusion in a child initially led the treating team toward common pediatric etiologies, particularly tuberculosis given the endemic context and family contact history. However, the eventual diagnosis of pancreatopleural fistula emerged only after repeated presentations and a more detailed history revealed the causative abdominal trauma.

The first step in analyzing any pleural effusion is determining whether it is a transudate or exudate using Light's criteria. An exudative effusion is defined by meeting any one of the following: pleural fluid protein divided by serum protein greater than 0.5, pleural fluid lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) divided by serum LDH greater than 0.6, or pleural fluid LDH greater than two-thirds the upper limit of normal for serum LDH. This classification immediately narrows the differential: transudates typically result from systemic factors such as heart failure, nephrotic syndrome, or hepatic cirrhosis, while exudates indicate local pathology affecting the pleura itself, including infection, malignancy, pulmonary embolism, or inflammatory conditions. The dark red, hemorrhagic appearance of the pleural fluid in this case strongly suggested an exudative process, as bloody effusions are virtually always exudative and signal significant pleural or pulmonary pathology.

Hemorrhagic pleural effusion presents a distinct differential that includes malignancy, pulmonary embolism, trauma, tuberculosis, pancreatopleural fistula, and occasionally benign conditions such as post-cardiac injury syndrome. In pediatric patients, the most common causes differ from adults, with infection (particularly tuberculosis in endemic regions) and malignancy representing leading considerations. The recurrent nature of the effusion in this case was a crucial clue, as it suggested ongoing communication with a persistent source rather than a self-limited process.

A pivotal finding that redirected the diagnostic approach was the markedly elevated pleural fluid amylase level of 6118 U/L. Elevated pleural fluid amylase is a signature feature of pancreatopleural fistula but can also occur in other conditions. Malignancy, particularly adenocarcinoma of the lung or pancreas, may produce amylase-rich effusions, though levels are typically much lower than those seen in pancreatic fistulas. Esophageal perforation (Boerhaave's syndrome) can also elevate pleural fluid amylase due to leakage of salivary amylase into the pleural space. Distinguishing between these causes requires amylase isoenzyme analysis: pancreatic-type amylase indicates pancreatopleural fistula or pancreatic malignancy, while salivary-type amylase suggests esophageal perforation or lung cancer. In this case, the amylase level was dramatically elevated, and subsequent lipase measurement (7211 U/L) confirmed the pancreatic origin, as lipase is specific to pancreatic tissue.

The initial diagnostic workup appropriately focused on tuberculosis given the endemic setting and definite household contact history. Tuberculous pleural effusion typically presents as a unilateral, lymphocyte-predominant exudate with elevated adenosine deaminase (ADA) levels. In this patient, ADA was only mildly elevated, which was equivocal and contributed to the initial empirical decision to start antitubercular therapy. However, the absence of constitutional symptoms such as fever, weight loss, and chronic cough should have lowered pretest probability. The definitive negative GeneXpert result ultimately excluded tuberculosis, demonstrating that even in endemic areas with documented contact, alternative diagnoses must be actively considered when clinical features are atypical.

Malignancy was systematically excluded through multiple lines of evidence. Clinically, the child had no history of weight loss, fever, or other constitutional symptoms concerning for malignancy. Laboratory evaluation revealed a normal complete blood count and peripheral smear without blasts or abnormal cells. Pleural fluid cytology showed no malignant cells. While malignancy can produce hemorrhagic, amylase-rich effusions, the combination of absent constitutional symptoms, normal hematologic parameters, and negative cytology made this diagnosis highly unlikely.

Empyema and parapneumonic effusion were considered but deemed unlikely given the absence of fever and lack of neutrophilic predominance in the pleural fluid. Empyema typically presents with acute febrile illness, pleuritic chest pain, and purulent pleural fluid with positive Gram stain or culture. The recurrent, afebrile presentation with consistently dark red fluid without neutrophilia argued strongly against an infectious etiology.

Boerhaave's syndrome, though rare in children, deserved consideration given the elevated amylase. This condition results from esophageal perforation, typically following severe vomiting or retching, and presents with severe chest pain, often with pneumomediastinum on imaging. The absence of any history of vomiting or retching, normal bowel function, and the CT finding of a retroperitoneal collection tracking from the pancreas rather than a mediastinal process excluded this diagnosis.

Mechanical causes were also considered. Acquired diaphragmatic hernia following trauma could potentially allow abdominal contents to enter the pleural space, producing hemorrhagic effusion. However, CT imaging clearly showed no herniation of abdominal organs into the thorax. Hepatic hydrothorax was excluded by normal liver function tests and imaging without evidence of cirrhosis or portal hypertension. Similarly, renal causes of pleural effusion, including nephrotic syndrome or hydronephrosis, were ruled out by normal renal function tests and imaging.

The systematic exclusion of these alternative diagnoses, combined with the recognition of dramatically elevated pleural fluid amylase and lipase, ultimately crystallized the diagnosis. The key clinical lesson from this case is that when pleural effusion recurs despite appropriate drainage, the clinician must broaden the differential beyond primary thoracic pathology and consider disease processes originating outside the chest. The delayed recognition of the abdominal trauma history—revealed only after three hospitalizations—underscores the importance of repeated, thorough history-taking, particularly in pediatric patients who may not spontaneously report antecedent events or may conceal them due to embarrassment or fear.

## Pathophysiology of Pancreatopleural Fistula

Understanding how a pancreatic injury manifests as respiratory distress requires appreciating the unique anatomical relationship between the pancreas and the pleural cavity. The pancreas resides in the retroperitoneum, positioned anterior to the vertebral column and posterior to the stomach. Unlike the anterior abdominal cavity, which is a potential space readily accessed by pancreatic fluid from anterior ductal disruptions, the posterior retroperitoneum offers a different path of least resistance—one that can lead unexpectedly upward toward the chest.

The critical anatomical feature enabling pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) formation is the continuity between the retroperitoneal space and the mediastinum through natural diaphragmatic openings. The aortic and esophageal hiatuses serve as the primary conduits. When pancreatic fluid leaks posteriorly, it dissects through the loose areolar tissue of the retroperitoneum, following the path of least resistance superiorly through these hiatuses into the posterior mediastinum. From there, the fluid can rupture through the mediastinal pleura into the pleural cavity, creating the fistulous connection. This anatomical pathway explains why patients with PPF present with thoracic symptoms despite having an abdominal pathology.

In this case, the mechanism becomes clear when examining the imaging findings. The MRCP revealed a tubular cystic collection originating from the distal body and tail of the pancreas, extending posteriorly through the retroperitoneum, then tracking superiorly through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity. The blunt abdominal trauma from the bicycle handlebar injury likely disrupted the pancreatic duct or parenchyma posteriorly, initiating this cascade. Notably, a narrow communication with the left pleural cavity was also identified, demonstrating that bilateral fistulas can occur when sufficient fluid accumulates and dissects through tissue planes.

The concept of an internal pancreatic fistula can be understood as a "leaky pipe"—the disrupted pancreatic duct diverts secretions away from their normal duodenal destination. Pancreatic fluid, rich in digestive enzymes, creates a inflammatory reaction as it tracks through tissues. The fluid's enzymatic activity may actually facilitate its passage by digesting surrounding tissue, effectively creating its own fistulous tract. This explains the characteristic dark red appearance of the pleural fluid in this case, as blood mixes with pancreatic secretions and inflammatory exudate.

PPF remains exceptionally rare in the pediatric population, with only 47 documented cases compared to over 300 in adults. This epidemiological difference reflects the divergent etiologies between age groups. In adults, chronic alcoholic pancreatitis predominates, accounting for nearly all cases in some series. Children, however, lack this exposure, and their PPF cases arise from different mechanisms. Blunt abdominal trauma constitutes approximately 32% of pediatric PPF cases, as illustrated by this patient's bicycle handlebar injury. Acute and chronic pancreatitis from other causes account for the remainder.

Genetic factors play a significant role in pediatric pancreatitis and consequently in PPF formation. Mutations in PRSS1 (cationic trypsinogen gene), SPINK1 (serine peptidase inhibitor), CFTR (cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator), and CTRC (chymotrypsin C) are well-established causes of hereditary pancreatitis in children. The PRSS1 mutations, particularly R122H and N29I, result in premature trypsinogen activation within the pancreas, leading to autodigestion and recurrent inflammation. SPINK1 mutations impair the natural inhibition of trypsin activity. These genetic predispositions can lead to chronic pancreatic inflammation and ductal changes that predispose to fistula formation, even in the absence of obvious trauma. In this case, while blunt trauma was the identified trigger, the child's obesity (BMI 35) may have contributed to pancreatic vulnerability, though no genetic testing was reported.

The rarity and non-specific presentation of PPF often delays diagnosis. As demonstrated in this case, the child underwent multiple thoracenteses and was even empirically treated for tuberculosis before the pancreatic origin was recognized. The pulmonary symptoms—dyspnea, chest pain, and pleural effusion—mask the underlying abdominal pathology. This diagnostic challenge underscores why clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for PPF when evaluating recurrent pleural effusions, particularly when the fluid is dark red or hemorrhagic in appearance.

## Diagnostic Approach to Pancreatopleural Fistula

The diagnosis of pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) requires a high index of suspicion, as the condition presents with thoracic symptoms that overshadow the underlying pancreatic pathology. In this case, the child presented three times over two weeks with recurrent right-sided pleural effusion before the correct diagnosis was established. This pattern exemplifies the central diagnostic challenge of PPF: the "pulmonary mask" that delays recognition of an abdominal source. Understanding the systematic approach to diagnosis—integrating clinical history, pleural fluid analysis, and imaging—is essential for avoiding unnecessary investigations and interventions.

Pleural fluid analysis provides the critical first clue. In this case, the fluid was consistently dark red, described as hemorrhagic in appearance. While hemorrhagic pleural effusion typically prompts evaluation for malignancy, tuberculosis, or pulmonary embolism, in the context of PPF it reflects the enzymatic digestion of tissue and inflammatory response induced by pancreatic secretions. The diagnostic hallmark, however, is biochemical rather than gross: a pleural fluid amylase level of 1000 IU/L or greater is considered pathognomonic for PPF. In this patient, the pleural fluid amylase was remarkably elevated at 6118 U/L, with lipase similarly elevated at 7211 U/L. These values immediately distinguish PPF from other causes of pleural effusion. Serum amylase and lipase should be obtained concurrently; elevated serum levels support the diagnosis but are neither necessary nor sufficient alone, as systemic levels may normalize while the fistula persists.

When PPF is suspected based on pleural fluid analysis, imaging must delineate both the pancreatic pathology and the fistulous tract. Contrast-enhanced CT of the chest and abdomen is typically the initial study, as it was in this case. CT demonstrated key findings: a multiloculated hypodense collection tracking from the right paraesophageal region to the pancreatic tail, along with right-sided hydropneumothorax and patchy consolidation. These findings suggested a communication between the pleural space and retroperitoneum. However, CT has limitations in visualizing the actual fistula tract, with reported sensitivity of only 33% to 47%. CT excels at identifying associated features—pancreatic calcifications, pseudocysts, ductal dilation—but often fails to demonstrate the fistula itself.

Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) is the preferred modality for confirming PPF and characterizing ductal anatomy. MRCP offers 70% to 80% sensitivity for visualizing the fistula tract and pancreatic duct structure without ionizing radiation—a particular advantage in pediatric patients. In this case, MRCP confirmed a tubular cystic collection extending from the pancreatic tail through the esophageal hiatus into the right pleural cavity, with communication to the distal pancreatic duct and proximal ductal narrowing. These findings definitively established the diagnosis and guided management planning.

Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) remains the gold standard for visualizing ductal anatomy and offers therapeutic capability. However, ERCP is invasive and carries risks of pancreatitis and sedation complications. In pediatric practice, ERCP is typically reserved for cases requiring therapeutic intervention—such as stent placement—when conservative management fails or when ductal anatomy must be precisely defined before surgery. This patient responded to conservative therapy, so ERCP was not required.

For atypical presentations, amylase isoenzyme typing can provide additional diagnostic specificity. PPF produces pancreatic-type amylase in the pleural fluid, while other conditions associated with elevated pleural fluid amylase—such as esophageal perforation, malignancy, and some cases of tuberculosis—produce salivary-type amylase. In this case, the clinical context (history of abdominal trauma) and imaging findings made isoenzyme typing unnecessary, but it remains a useful adjunct when the diagnosis is uncertain.

The diagnostic algorithm for suspected PPF begins with recognition of recurrent pleural effusion that is hemorrhagic, exudative, or recurrent despite drainage. Pleural fluid should be sent for amylase and lipase in addition to standard analysis. Values exceeding 1000 IU/L for amylase strongly suggest PPF. Clinical history must be reviewed for pancreatitis risk factors, including trauma, hereditary pancreatitis, or chronic pancreatic disease. Cross-sectional imaging with CT identifies associated pancreatic pathology, followed by MRCP to confirm the fistula and characterize ductal anatomy. ERCP is reserved for therapeutic intervention or when other imaging is nondiagnostic. In this case, the integration of clinical history (revealed after persistent questioning), characteristic pleural fluid analysis, and confirmatory MRCP established the diagnosis and allowed targeted therapy to begin.

## Management, Outcomes, and Clinical Lessons

The management of pancreatopleural fistula (PPF) follows a stepwise approach, progressing from conservative medical measures to endoscopic intervention, and finally to surgical options if needed. Due to the rarity of PPF in the pediatric population—with only 47 cases reported—no standardized treatment guidelines exist. Management decisions are therefore based on evidence from case reports and extrapolation from adult literature, with the choice of therapy dependent on ductal anatomy and response to initial interventions.

Conservative management serves as the first-line approach and was the strategy employed in this case. The cornerstone of medical therapy is bowel rest with nil per oral (NPO) status, which minimizes pancreatic exocrine stimulation and reduces the volume of pancreatic secretions. Total parenteral nutrition (TPN) provides caloric support while maintaining bowel rest. Somatostatin analogues, specifically Octreotide, are administered to suppress pancreatic exocrine secretion through inhibition of hormonal pathways that stimulate the pancreas. The mechanism involves somatostatin receptors on pancreatic acinar cells; when activated, Octreotide reduces the production and secretion of digestive enzymes. In this patient, Octreotide was given at 30 µg/kg/day, within the recommended range of 10–40 µg/kg/day. Pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT) was also initiated to support digestion while the fistula healed, although its primary role is to replace enzymes that cannot reach the duodenum due to the leak.

Thoracic drainage via intercostal chest tube is essential for managing the pleural effusion and preventing respiratory compromise. The drain allows evacuation of the amylase-rich fluid and creates conditions favorable for fistula closure. In this case, an intercostal drainage (ICD) tube was placed on the third admission, after imaging confirmed the diagnosis.

The success rates for each treatment tier inform clinical decision-making. Conservative therapy alone has a success rate of approximately 12% in pediatric cases, which is notably lower than in adults. This limited efficacy reflects the reality that many pediatric PPFs involve significant ductal disruption that requires intervention. Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) with transpapillary stenting is the preferred intervention when conservative measures fail or when ductal obstruction is identified. Stent placement bridges the disrupted duct segment, reduces intraductal pressure, and allows pancreatic secretions to flow physiologically into the duodenum rather than through the fistulous tract. Endoscopic intervention achieves fistula closure in 48% of patients within 2–3 weeks, with success rates approaching 100% in pediatric case series. Stents typically remain in place for 4 weeks to 6 months. Surgical options are reserved for cases where endoscopic approaches fail or are technically not feasible due to severe ductal obstruction or distal fistula location. Procedures include distal pancreatectomy (resection of the pancreatic segment containing the fistula), the Puestow procedure (pancreatojejunostomy to decompress the pancreatic duct), or cystojejunostomy (drainage of an associated pseudocyst into the jejunum). Surgery demonstrates a 93% success rate in pediatric patients with operative mortality below 5% and recurrence rates around 11%.

In this patient, the treatment course demonstrated a successful outcome with conservative management alone. After MRCP confirmed the diagnosis, the child was placed on NPO status with TPN, Octreotide infusion, PERT, and continued thoracic drainage via the ICD. Over 10 days, the volume of fluid draining from the chest tube progressively decreased and eventually stopped. This clinical response indicated successful fistula closure, allowing removal of the ICD and discharge from the hospital. The decision to attempt conservative therapy first was appropriate given that MRCP showed the fistula communicated with the distal pancreatic duct without complete ductal discontinuity.

Follow-up care focused on preventing recurrence and addressing modifiable risk factors. The child's BMI of 35 indicated obesity, which increases the risk of pancreatic inflammation. Lifestyle modifications were recommended, including a balanced diet with reduced processed foods and regular exercise. At monthly follow-up visits, the patient remained symptom-free without recurrent pleural effusions, returned to school, and could perform strenuous physical activities without difficulty.

This case offers several important clinical lessons. First, PPF exemplifies how abdominal pathology can present with predominantly thoracic symptoms—a phenomenon the authors describe as a "pulmonary mask." The initial focus on finding a pulmonary cause led to empiric antitubercular therapy based on a positive family contact history and mildly elevated pleural fluid ADA, delaying the correct diagnosis. Second, obtaining a thorough history is critical. The key diagnostic clue—blunt abdominal trauma from a bicycle handle—emerged only after repeated questioning on the third admission. This history, combined with the characteristic dark red pleural fluid and elevated amylase levels, ultimately directed the workup toward a pancreatic etiology. Third, early diagnosis prevents unnecessary interventions and potential complications. Had the history been obtained initially, the child would have avoided multiple thoracenteses, empiric antitubercular therapy, and repeated hospitalizations. Finally, elevated pleural fluid amylase (≥1000 IU/L) should prompt consideration of PPF, and MRCP should be obtained to visualize the fistula and ductal anatomy when this diagnosis is suspected.
