Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

Chapter 2: ## Background: Immune-Mediated Necrotising Myopathy and GLP-1 Receptor Agonists

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is a distinct subtype of inflammatory myopathy characterized by aggressive, symmetric proximal muscle weakness and markedly elevated creatine kinase (CK) levels. Unlike polymyositis or dermatomyositis, IMNM is defined histologically by prominent necrosis of muscle fibers with minimal lymphocytic infiltration. This histopathologic signature is central to diagnosis. The European Neuromuscular Centre (ENMC) diagnostic criteria incorporate clinical presentation, laboratory findings, and muscle biopsy characteristics to distinguish IMNM from other myositis subtypes.

The pathophysiology of IMNM involves complement-mediated muscle injury. Deposition of the membrane attack complex (MAC) on myofiber surfaces leads to direct cell lysis and necrosis, even without prominent inflammatory cell infiltrates. Muscle biopsy typically reveals scattered necrotic and regenerating fibers, with macrophages recruited to phagocytose necrotic debris—a process termed myophagocytosis. In our patient's case, the biopsy demonstrated dispersed necrotic fibers, CD68-positive macrophages with myophagocytosis, and MAC deposition on both necrotic and non-necrotic fibers, findings consistent with this complement-mediated mechanism.

Two autoantibodies are strongly associated with IMNM: anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (anti-HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (anti-SRP). Anti-HMGCR antibodies are particularly relevant to drug-induced IMNM, as they were first identified in patients who developed autoimmune myopathy following statin exposure. Anti-SRP antibodies identify a subset of patients with typically severe disease. However, approximately one-third of patients with IMNM are seronegative for both antibodies, suggesting that additional autoantibodies and triggers remain to be discovered. Our patient's comprehensive myositis panel was negative, placing her in this seronegative subset and underscoring the importance of muscle biopsy when clinical suspicion is high.

The statin experience provides a paradigm for understanding drug-induced IMNM. Before anti-HMGCR antibodies were discovered, patients who developed myopathy on statins were often classified as having toxic myopathy. A critical observation emerged: many patients continued to experience progressive weakness and CK elevation even after statin discontinuation. This persistence, coupled with response to immunosuppression, indicated an autoimmune process rather than direct drug toxicity. This clinical pattern—progression despite drug withdrawal and requirement for immunosuppression—distinguishes drug-induced IMNM from direct toxic myopathy, which typically improves rapidly after drug cessation. This distinction is essential for understanding our patient's clinical course.

Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have become widely used for type 2 diabetes and obesity. Semaglutide, the agent implicated in our patient's case, binds GLP-1 receptors to enhance glucose-dependent insulin secretion, suppress glucagon, and promote satiety through central nervous system effects. Common adverse effects are predominantly gastrointestinal, including nausea and vomiting. Emerging data suggest these agents may also cause loss of skeletal muscle mass during weight loss. A recent case report described rhabdomyolysis associated with tirzepatide, another GLP-1 receptor agonist. In that case, the patient developed CK levels in the tens of thousands while on treatment, with rapid normalization after drug discontinuation—consistent with direct toxic injury. This contrasts sharply with our patient's presentation: a progressive rise in CK over months and worsening weakness despite drug cessation, features more aligned with an immune-mediated process than direct toxicity.
