Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

Chapter 1: ## Case Presentation and Clinical Timeline

Understanding the clinical presentation of this case requires careful attention to the temporal relationship between drug exposure and symptom development, as this sequence forms the foundation for the causal inference between semaglutide and immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). The patient, a woman in her 60s, presented with a medical history notable for obesity, hepatosteatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a remote history of venous thromboembolism. Importantly, she had been fully independent in her activities of daily living and in good functional health prior to her acute deterioration—a baseline that makes her subsequent decline particularly striking.

Three years before her neuromuscular presentation, she had undergone evaluation for persistently elevated transaminases. At that time, her aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were approximately twice the upper limit of normal, with ALT exceeding AST. A comprehensive work-up excluded viral hepatitis, autoimmune hepatitis, and other primary liver diseases. Liver biopsy confirmed non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) with an activity score of 4/8. This pre-existing hepatosteatosis is clinically relevant for two reasons: first, it provided the indication for semaglutide initiation, and second, it complicates the later interpretation of transaminase elevations, which can originate from either hepatic or muscular sources.

The patient was initiated on semaglutide for persistent fatty liver and obesity—appropriate indications given the medication's efficacy for weight loss and metabolic improvement. However, within hours of receiving her first and only dose (0.25 mg subcutaneously), she developed severe nausea and vomiting. Two days later, she was hospitalised with dehydration, acute renal failure, and severe metabolic acidosis requiring intravenous hydration, bicarbonate therapy, and brief non-invasive ventilation. During this admission, her creatine kinase (CK) was modestly elevated at 337 U/L—not yet in the range typical for rhabdomyolysis, but notable as a potential early marker of muscle injury. She was discharged after five days with resolved gastrointestinal symptoms but residual deconditioning.

What distinguishes this case from straightforward drug-induced rhabdomyolysis is the trajectory that followed. Over the subsequent four months, the patient experienced progressive proximal muscle weakness, ultimately rendering her unable to stand or sit without assistance. She also developed dysphagia (difficulty swallowing) and dysphonia (voice changes), reflecting involvement of bulbar and pharyngeal musculature. This clinical constellation—progressive proximal weakness with bulbar involvement and a rising CK that peaked at 3992 U/L—is characteristic of inflammatory myopathy rather than direct toxic injury. In drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, CK typically rises acutely and improves rapidly after drug withdrawal; here, the CK increased twelve-fold over four months *after* drug discontinuation, suggesting an ongoing autoimmune process triggered by the initial exposure.

Physical examination at the tertiary care facility revealed profound weakness in proximal muscle groups, including the shoulder abductors, elbow flexors, hip flexors, and knee extensors. The distribution is diagnostically important: proximal greater than distal weakness is the hallmark of myopathy, as opposed to neuropathy, which typically affects distal muscles first. Notably, she had no rash, skin changes, or other features of dermatomyositis, no synovitis or sicca symptoms to suggest an underlying connective tissue disease, and no other neurological deficits to suggest an alternative diagnosis. The focused pattern of weakness with sparing of other neurological functions narrows the differential toward primary muscle disease.

The timeline from drug exposure to definitive presentation spans approximately four months, with the initial dose of semaglutide serving as the sentinel event. This latency period is consistent with immune-mediated pathology, where an inciting trigger (in this case, possibly the drug exposure) initiates an autoimmune response that then becomes self-sustaining. Understanding this temporal sequence is essential for appreciating why the authors classified the causality as "probable" rather than "certain"—the clinical course strongly suggests a drug-triggered autoimmune process, but the single exposure and lack of pre-exposure CK measurement introduce inherent uncertainty. Nonetheless, the clinical picture of progressive weakness, bulbar involvement, and rising CK in temporal proximity to a new medication should prompt clinicians to consider drug-induced IMNM in the differential diagnosis.
