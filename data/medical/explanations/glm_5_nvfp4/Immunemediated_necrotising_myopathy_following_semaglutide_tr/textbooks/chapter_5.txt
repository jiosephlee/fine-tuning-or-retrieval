Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

Chapter 5: ## Clinical Outcomes, Lessons, and Pharmacovigilance Implications

The patient's clinical outcome provides important diagnostic clues about the underlying mechanism of her myopathy. Following treatment with pulse methylprednisolone, oral prednisone, and a single course of IVIG, she demonstrated a marked and sustained improvement. Her CK levels normalized within two weeks and remained normal throughout follow-up. She was successfully weaned off prednisone rapidly over approximately two months—a notably shorter treatment course than typically required for idiopathic immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). At her last outpatient visit ten months after initial presentation, she had regained her baseline functional status and had been off all immunosuppression for seven to eight months without disease recurrence. This clinical trajectory stands in contrast to idiopathic IMNM, which characteristically requires prolonged and often intensive immunosuppressive therapy to prevent relapse. The ability to discontinue immunosuppression relatively quickly while maintaining remission supports the classification of this case as a drug-induced IMNM rather than an idiopathic autoimmune process.

Cardiac involvement, which can accompany inflammatory myopathies, also resolved with treatment. The initial cardiac MRI demonstrated mild hypokinesis of the mid-anteroseptal and anterior wall with myocardial edema, raising concern for possible myocarditis or stress-induced cardiomyopathy. However, follow-up echocardiogram and cardiac MRI after treatment showed normalization of these abnormalities, and she experienced no recurrence of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation. The parallel recovery of cardiac findings alongside muscle disease further supports an inflammatory process that responded appropriately to immunosuppression.

The authors applied the World Health Organization-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (WHO-UMC) Causality Assessment System to evaluate the relationship between semaglutide and IMNM in this case. This standardized framework evaluates drug-induced adverse events based on temporal relationship, alternative explanations, and response to drug withdrawal. The assessment classified the association as "probable," based on several key factors: a reasonable temporal relationship between drug exposure and symptom onset, the absence of other identifiable causes (negative myositis panel, no statin use, no other implicated medications), and a clinically appropriate response following drug discontinuation and immunosuppression. Rechallenge was appropriately deemed unethical given the severity of the initial reaction. While causality cannot be definitively established from a single case report, this structured assessment provides a systematic approach that clinicians can apply when evaluating potential drug-induced autoimmune conditions.

A critical clinical lesson from this case is the importance of distinguishing between direct toxic effects and immune-mediated mechanisms when evaluating drug-associated muscle injury. Direct drug-induced rhabdomyolysis typically presents with an acute, marked CK elevation that normalizes within days to weeks after drug withdrawal, without requiring immunosuppression. In contrast, this patient's CK continued to rise progressively over four months after a single dose of semaglutide, and her clinical improvement required immunosuppressive therapy. This pattern mirrors the historical recognition of statin-induced IMNM, where patients initially classified as having toxic myopathy were later recognized to have an autoimmune process that persisted despite drug discontinuation and responded to immunosuppression. Clinicians should consider immune-mediated mechanisms when muscle injury persists or progresses after withdrawal of the suspected offending agent.

The pharmacovigilance implications of this case are substantial given the exponential increase in GLP-1 receptor agonist prescriptions for obesity and type 2 diabetes. While this represents the first reported case of IMNM associated with this medication class, the widespread use of these agents necessitates heightened clinical awareness. Clinicians should include GLP-1 receptor agonists in the differential diagnosis of drug-induced myopathy, particularly when evaluating patients with proximal muscle weakness and elevated CK. Future case reports and pharmacovigilance data will be essential to clarify whether this association is causal or coincidental and to identify potential predisposing factors that may confer increased risk. Until then, clinicians should maintain a careful medication history in patients presenting with inflammatory myopathy and consider drug discontinuation as part of the management strategy when temporal relationships suggest an association.
