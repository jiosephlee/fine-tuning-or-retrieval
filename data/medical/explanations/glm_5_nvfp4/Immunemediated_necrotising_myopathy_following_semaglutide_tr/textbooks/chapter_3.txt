Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Workup and Differential Diagnosis

The diagnostic approach to a patient presenting with progressive muscle weakness requires a systematic evaluation that integrates laboratory findings, imaging, and ultimately tissue pathology. In this case, the patient's presentation with proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia occurring months after semaglutide exposure prompted a comprehensive myositis evaluation designed to distinguish between toxic myopathy, immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM), and other inflammatory myopathies.

**Laboratory Evaluation and the Significance of Seronegative Status**

Creatine kinase (CK) serves as the primary laboratory marker for muscle injury, and its elevation pattern provides important diagnostic clues. This patient's CK trajectory was particularly informative: it was modestly elevated at 337 U/L during her initial hospitalization after semaglutide administration, then rose dramatically to 3992 U/L over the subsequent four months. This progressive rise contrasted sharply with the expected pattern in acute toxic rhabdomyolysis, where CK typically peaks rapidly and declines within days to weeks after removal of the offending agent. The twelve-fold CK increase over four months signaled an ongoing pathologic process rather than a single acute injury event.

Transaminase interpretation required careful consideration in this patient. While aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) can originate from either liver or skeletal muscle, the AST elevation exceeding ALT at 191 U/L versus 165 U/L respectively, in the context of markedly elevated CK, suggested a muscular contribution. However, this patient also had biopsy-proven non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) with transaminase elevation predating her semaglutide exposure by three years. The persistence of mild transaminase elevation after CK normalization confirmed that her hepatic disease remained an independent process.

The myositis-specific autoantibody panel represents a cornerstone of IMNM diagnosis. Anti-HMGCR (3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase) antibodies are strongly associated with statin-exposed IMNM, while anti-SRP (signal recognition particle) antibodies identify a statin-independent subset. This patient tested negative for both antibodies, as well as for all other myositis-specific antibodies including Jo-1, Mi-2, MDA-5, and NXP-2. This seronegative status occurs in approximately one-third of IMNM patients and poses diagnostic challenges, as it requires clinicians to rely more heavily on histopathology and clinical exclusion criteria. The isolated low-titer anti-SS-B antibody without accompanying anti-SS-A or ANA positivity was an incidental finding without clear relevance to her myopathy, as isolated SS-B positivity has not been associated with IMNM in the literature.

**Imaging Studies and the Role of MRI in Myositis**

Muscle MRI has emerged as a valuable tool for characterizing the distribution and activity of myositis. In this patient, MRI of the bilateral femurs revealed symmetric muscular edema predominantly affecting the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps muscles. This pattern of proximal lower extremity involvement is characteristic of IMNM and other inflammatory myopathies, which typically spare distal muscles early in the disease course. The subcutaneous edema noted along the lateral thighs represented an associated inflammatory response. MRI findings can guide muscle biopsy site selection by targeting areas of active inflammation while avoiding end-stage fibrotic or fatty infiltrated muscle, thereby improving diagnostic yield.

**Cardiac Evaluation in Myositis**

Cardiac involvement in inflammatory myopathies ranges from subclinical to life-threatening and warrants systematic assessment. This patient's elevated high-sensitivity troponin (62 pg/mL) and markedly elevated pro-BNP (4358 pg/mL) raised concern for myocarditis. Echocardiography revealed normal left ventricular ejection fraction (65%–70%) with borderline right ventricular systolic pressure elevation (46 mm Hg). Cardiac MRI demonstrated mild hypokinesis of the mid-anteroseptal and anterior wall with myocardial edema but without late gadolinium enhancement, suggesting either early myocarditis or stress-induced cardiomyopathy. The absence of significant scarring or infarction provided reassurance, and follow-up cardiac imaging after treatment demonstrated resolution of these abnormalities. This case illustrates that cardiac findings in IMNM may represent genuine myocardial involvement, stress-related changes, or incidental comorbidities, requiring careful integration with the clinical context.

**Muscle Biopsy: The Definitive Diagnostic Tool**

Muscle biopsy remains the gold standard for diagnosing IMNM and distinguishing it from other myopathies. The biopsy from this patient's right vastus lateralis demonstrated the characteristic histopathologic features of IMNM: dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers with a paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates. This "necrotising myopathy" pattern—with prominent myofiber necrosis but minimal endomysial inflammation—distinguishes IMNM from polymyositis, which features dense T-cell infiltrates, and from dermatomyositis, which shows perifascicular atrophy and perivascular inflammation.

Immunohistochemical staining provided additional diagnostic clarity. CD68 positivity identified macrophages actively engulfing necrotic muscle fibers (myophagocytosis), confirming an active inflammatory process. The membrane attack complex (MAC) deposition on necrotic and non-necrotic myofibres indicated complement-mediated injury, a hallmark of antibody-driven myopathy even in seronegative cases. The presence of rare CD3-positive T-cells and perivascular inflammatory cells was consistent with IMNM's limited inflammatory infiltrate pattern. These findings collectively established the diagnosis of IMNM.

**Differential Diagnosis: Distinguishing IMNM from Other Myopathies**

The differential diagnosis for this presentation included toxic myopathy (rhabdomyolysis), drug-induced IMNM, idiopathic autoimmune IMNM, and other inflammatory myopathies. Several features distinguished IMNM from acute toxic rhabdomyolysis. First, the temporal course was inconsistent: rhabdomyolysis typically presents with acute CK elevation (often exceeding 10,000 U/L) that normalizes within days to weeks after drug discontinuation, whereas this patient's CK rose progressively over four months despite semaglutide cessation. Second, rhabdomyolysis does not respond to immunosuppression, while this patient improved dramatically with corticosteroids and IVIG. Third, the muscle biopsy pattern of IMNM—with complement deposition, necrosis, and macrophage-predominant infiltrates—differs from the straightforward myofiber injury pattern seen in toxic myopathy.

The distinction between drug-induced IMNM and idiopathic IMNM carries important prognostic implications. Drug-induced IMNM, exemplified by statin-associated HMGCR-positive myopathy, may respond to a finite course of immunosuppression following drug withdrawal, while idiopathic IMNM typically requires prolonged, often intensive immunosuppression. This patient's clinical course—rapid improvement and sustained remission off all immunosuppression after only two months of prednisone and a single course of IVIG—aligned more closely with drug-induced IMNM. The close temporal relationship between her single semaglutide dose and subsequent symptom onset, combined with her recovery without ongoing immunosuppression, supported semaglutide as the probable trigger according to WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre causality criteria.

However, diagnostic uncertainty remains inherent in such cases. The absence of a pre-semaglutide CK measurement prevents definitive exclusion of pre-existing smoldering autoimmune myopathy. The seronegative status also raises the possibility of an as-yet-unidentified autoantibody or trigger. Nevertheless, the weight of evidence—including the temporal association, biopsy findings, response to short-course immunosuppression, and sustained remission with GLP-1 receptor agonist avoidance—supports a probable association between semaglutide and this episode of IMNM.
