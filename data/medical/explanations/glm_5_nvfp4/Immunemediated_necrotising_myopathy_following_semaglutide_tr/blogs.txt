# From Rhabdo to Rheumatology: Differentiating Toxic Myopathy from Immune-Mediated Necrotising Myopathy

## The Case That Stops You in Your Tracks

A woman in her 60s receives her first — and only — dose of semaglutide. Within hours, she's vomiting. Within days, she's hospitalized with acute renal failure and metabolic acidosis. But here's where it gets weird: four months later, she can barely stand, can barely swallow, and her CK has climbed to nearly 4000 U/L. She's been off the drug the entire time.

This case, published by Lam and colleagues, is the first reported case of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) probably associated with GLP-1 receptor agonist use. It also serves as a masterclass in distinguishing drug-induced rhabdomyolysis from immune-mediated myopathy, a distinction that should be on every clinician's radar as GLP-1 agonists become ubiquitous.

## Two Patients, Two Pathologies, One Drug Class

Here's what makes this case so instructive: there was already a case report of rhabdomyolysis associated with tirzepatide (another GLP-1 receptor agonist). That patient presented with a CK of 57,014 U/L — a truly massive elevation. The CK normalized within days of drug discontinuation and hydration. Standard drug-induced rhabdo. No immunosuppression needed.

Now compare that to our semaglutide patient. Her CK was 337 U/L on initial hospitalization. Four months later — while off the drug entirely — it had climbed to 3992 U/L. Her weakness was progressive. She developed dysphagia and dysphonia. She couldn't stand without assistance. And critically, her CK trajectory continued upward long after the drug was gone.

The CK trajectory tells the story. In toxic myopathy, CK elevates acutely and normalizes within days to weeks after drug withdrawal. In IMNM, the immune cascade has been triggered — stopping the drug doesn't stop the disease.

## The Statin Parallel: A Lesson from History

This pattern should sound familiar to anyone who watched the evolution of statin-associated myopathy. Initially, patients on statins who developed myopathy were classified as having "toxic myopathy" or "polymyositis." But then clinicians noticed something: many patients had persistent myopathy and CK elevation despite statin cessation. These patients improved with immunosuppression. That observation led to the discovery of anti-HMGCR autoantibodies and the recognition of statin-associated IMNM as a distinct entity.

The parallel is striking. In this case, the patient had no exposure to statins, red yeast rice, or other known IMNM triggers. She was negative for anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP antibodies — the two classic IMNM antibodies. But remember: approximately one-third of IMNM patients are seronegative. The absence of a known autoantibody doesn't rule out the diagnosis. The muscle biopsy showed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibres with macrophage-mediated myophagocytosis and membrane attack complex deposition — the histologic hallmarks of IMNM.

## The Clinical Features That Should Make You Think IMNM

Progressive proximal muscle weakness is the hallmark. This patient couldn't stand or sit upright without assistance. Dysphagia and dysphonia developed — reflecting involvement of bulbar musculature. The weakness was aggressively progressive over months.

The timeline matters enormously. The 12-fold rise in CK over four months, occurring after just a single dose of semaglutide and continuing despite its discontinuation, suggests an immune-mediated process that was triggered by the drug but became self-sustaining. This is fundamentally different from direct drug toxicity, where the insult ends when the drug is cleared.

Her cardiac findings deserve mention: elevated troponin, markedly elevated proBNP, and cardiac MRI showing myocardial edema with mild hypokinesis. Whether this represents subclinical myocarditis associated with the same immune process remains unclear, but it normalized on follow-up imaging after immunosuppression.

## Why This Distinction Changes Management

If you misclassify IMNM as simple rhabdomyolysis, you treat with hydration and supportive care. The patient doesn't improve. The immune attack continues. Permanent muscle damage accumulates.

This patient received pulse methylprednisolone (1000 mg daily for 5 days), followed by oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg daily, plus IVIG at 2 g/kg. Her CK normalized. She regained her baseline function. She was weaned off immunosuppression entirely and remained in remission at 10-month follow-up.

Notably, drug-induced IMNM often requires less prolonged immunosuppression than idiopathic IMNM, which frequently requires intensive, ongoing therapy. The key is recognition, treatment, and avoidance of re-exposure.

## Key Take-Home Points

- **CK trajectory is diagnostic**: In toxic rhabdomyolysis, CK rises acutely and normalizes within days to weeks of drug withdrawal. In IMNM, CK continues to rise or remains elevated despite drug cessation.

- **Timeline matters**: Progressive weakness and CK elevation over months, as seen in this case, is incompatible with simple drug toxicity and should prompt evaluation for immune-mediated disease.

- **Seronegativity doesn't rule out IMNM**: One-third of IMNM patients lack detectable autoantibodies. Muscle biopsy showing necrosis with minimal inflammatory infiltrate, macrophage-mediated myophagocytosis, and membrane attack complex deposition supports the diagnosis.

- **GLP-1 agonists may trigger IMNM**: This is the first reported case, but given the explosive use of these medications, pharmacovigilance is essential. Consider IMNM in patients on GLP-1 agonists who develop progressive myopathy that doesn't resolve with drug discontinuation.

- **Treatment differs fundamentally**: Rhabdomyolysis requires supportive care and drug withdrawal. IMNM requires immunosuppression — typically corticosteroids and IVIG, with steroid-sparing agents for refractory disease.

- **The statin analogy is instructive**: The recognition of drug-induced IMNM often begins with case reports. Staying alert to atypical patterns of drug-associated myopathy can identify new triggers and improve patient outcomes.

As GLP-1 agonists continue their meteoric rise, clinicians need to expand their differential for muscle-related complaints beyond simple rhabdomyolysis. When the CK doesn't come down and the weakness keeps progressing after drug cessation, think immune-mediated — and call rheumatology.



# Ozempic's Hidden Muscle Risk: The Emerging Spectrum of GLP-1 Agonist Myotoxicity

## The Case That Stopped Me In My Tracks

A single dose. That's all it took.

A woman in her 60s received her first and only 0.25 mg injection of semaglutide. Within hours, she developed nausea and vomiting. Two days later, she was in the ICU with metabolic acidosis and acute renal failure. She survived that admission, but her nightmare was just beginning. Over the next four months, she progressively lost the ability to stand, to sit upright, to swallow. Her CK, initially 337 U/L on admission, climbed to nearly 4000 U/L. She had become profoundly weak from an immune-mediated necrotising myopathy, and the temporal relationship to that single semaglutide dose raises some seriously uncomfortable questions.

This is the first reported case of IMNM probably associated with a GLP-1 receptor agonist, and it demands our attention not because it definitively proves causation, but because it illustrates exactly the kind of pharmacovigilance challenge we face as these medications explode in popularity.

## What Makes This Different From "Routine" Rhabdomyolysis?

Here's where this case gets clinically fascinating, and where the authors make a crucial distinction that every emergency physician and hospitalist needs to understand.

There's already a published case report of rhabdomyolysis associated with tirzepatide, another GLP-1 agonist. But that case followed the classic drug-induced rhabdomyolysis pattern: CK in the tens of thousands (57,014 U/L), rapid improvement within days of drug withdrawal, no immunosuppression needed. Direct toxic effect, drug stops, muscle heals.

This case? Completely different trajectory. The patient's CK was only modestly elevated initially and continued climbing over four months despite being off the drug. She required IV methylprednisolone and IVIG to recover. Her muscle biopsy showed necrotic fibers with macrophage-mediated myophagocytosis and membrane attack complex deposition, the histologic hallmarks of immune-mediated destruction rather than direct toxicity. And critically, she improved with immunosuppression and has stayed well off all medications for over 8 months now, provided she avoids GLP-1 agonists.

That's not rhabdomyolysis. That's an autoimmune myopathy triggered by a drug exposure, and it should remind us of another medication class we've learned to respect.

## Echoes of Statin-Associated IMNM

The authors draw a compelling parallel to statins, and it's worth understanding this history.

Before anti-HMGCR antibodies were discovered, patients on statins who developed muscle weakness with elevated CK were often classified as having "toxic myopathy" or "polymyositis." But clinicians noticed something odd: many patients didn't get better when the statin stopped. They required immunosuppression, and their disease course looked autoimmune, not toxic. That observation eventually led to identifying HMGCR autoantibodies as a marker of a distinct disease entity, immune-mediated necrotising myopathy.

Statin-associated IMNM taught us that drugs can trigger autoimmune muscle destruction that persists independently after the drug is withdrawn. This case raises the question: could GLP-1 agonists do the same thing in susceptible individuals?

Our patient was seronegative, negative for anti-HMGCR, anti-SRP, and the full myositis antibody panel. About one-third of IMNM patients are seronegative, suggesting we haven't identified all the relevant autoantibodies or triggers yet. This case might represent a new entity entirely.

## Why This Matters Right Now

Let's be real about the scope. GLP-1 receptor agonists like semaglutide and tirzepatide have transformed diabetes and obesity management, and their use has grown exponentially. We know about the GI side effects, the potential for pancreatitis, the rare but serious warnings about thyroid C-cell tumors. We're now learning about muscle mass loss as a class effect. But immune-mediated myopathy? That's not on anyone's radar.

The authors used WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre causality criteria and classified this as "probable" causality. That's meaningful. It's not "certain," and rechallenge would be unethical given the severity of her reaction. But the temporal relationship, the absence of other explanations, the histologic findings, and the clinical response to drug withdrawal plus immunosuppression all point in one direction.

## Clinical Approach: What Should We Watch For?

This case offers a practical framework for clinicians evaluating patients on GLP-1 agonists who present with muscle complaints.

First, distinguish the timeline. Acute CK elevation in the thousands occurring during active drug use, with rapid resolution after stopping, suggests direct drug-induced rhabdomyolysis. Progressive weakness and CK elevation that continues to worsen after drug discontinuation suggests immune-mediated pathology.

Second, don't be reassured by a negative myositis panel. This patient had no detectable myositis antibodies, yet her biopsy was unequivocally consistent with IMNM. Seronegative disease is common, and a negative antibody panel doesn't rule out autoimmune myopathy.

Third, consider the full differential. Our patient had no statin exposure, no red yeast rice or mushroom supplements, no family history of myopathy, no excessive exercise. The authors systematically excluded alternative explanations. That workup matters.

Fourth, these patients often need both rheumatology and neurology involvement. The differential includes inflammatory myopathies, muscular dystrophies, metabolic myopathies, and toxic myopathies. The muscle biopsy was essential to confirming the diagnosis in this case.

## The Cardiac Connection

One additional pearl from this case: the patient had cardiac findings that raised concern for myocarditis. Her high-sensitivity troponin was mildly elevated, her proBNP was dramatically elevated at 4358 pg/mL, and her cardiac MRI showed myocardial edema with mild hypokinesis. These findings resolved completely on follow-up.

IMNM can involve cardiac muscle, and this case reminds us to evaluate cardiac involvement in patients with severe necrotising myopathy. The good news: her cardiac function normalized as her myopathy improved.

## Key Take-Home Points

- A single dose of semaglutide preceded this patient's immune-mediated necrotising myopathy, representing the first reported case of probable GLP-1 agonist-associated IMNM
- Distinguish drug-induced rhabdomyolysis (acute, resolves with drug withdrawal) from immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (progressive, requires immunosuppression, can persist after drug cessation)
- One-third of IMNM patients are seronegative, so a negative myositis antibody panel doesn't exclude autoimmune myopathy
- GLP-1 agonist use is growing exponentially, making pharmacovigilance critical for identifying rare adverse effects
- Muscle biopsy remains essential for diagnosing IMNM, particularly in seronegative cases

## The Bottom Line

We don't know yet whether this case represents a true causal association or a temporal coincidence. The authors appropriately acknowledge that uncertainty. But given the explosive growth of GLP-1 agonist prescriptions, even a rare adverse effect could affect substantial numbers of patients. This case report serves as an early warning signal, and it behooves us to keep immune-mediated myopathy on our differential when evaluating muscle weakness in patients on these medications.

The patient returned to her baseline functional status, off immunosuppression, and has stayed well. That outcome required recognition of an unusual diagnosis, appropriate specialty involvement, and targeted immunosuppressive therapy. For clinicians on the front lines seeing patients with new muscle weakness, knowing when to escalate and how to think beyond "rhabdomyolysis" might make all the difference.



# The Antibody-Negative Necrotising Myopathy: Diagnostic Pitfalls and Lessons from Statin-Associated IMNM

## When the Panel Comes Back Negative, Don't Stop Working

Here's a scenario that should give any clinician pause: a patient presents with progressive proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and a CK climbing into the thousands. You order a myositis panel, and it comes back completely negative. What now?

A fascinating case report recently published describes what appears to be the first documented case of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) associated with GLP-1 receptor agonist use. What makes this case particularly instructive isn't just the potential drug association, but that it perfectly illustrates the diagnostic challenge of seronegative IMNM. This patient was negative for anti-HMGCR, anti-SRP, and every other autoantibody on an extensive myositis panel. Yet her muscle biopsy told a completely different story, and more importantly, she responded beautifully to immunosuppression.

The case is a timely reminder that approximately one-third of patients with IMNM are seronegative. That's a substantial chunk of patients who would be misdiagnosed if we rely solely on antibody testing.

## The Statin Parallel: A Cautionary Tale

Before anti-HMGCR antibodies were discovered in 2010, patients who developed myopathy on statins were often labeled as having "toxic myopathy" or "polymyositis." The prevailing logic was straightforward: drug exposure plus muscle injury equals direct toxicity. Stop the drug, problem solved.

But clinicians noticed something peculiar. Many patients continued to deteriorate even after statin cessation. Their CK levels remained elevated, their weakness progressed, and they didn't get better with supportive care alone. What eventually became clear was that these patients had an autoimmune process masquerading as drug toxicity. The statin had triggered an immune-mediated attack that became self-sustaining. The discovery of anti-HMGCR antibodies fundamentally changed how we classify and treat these patients.

This historical lesson is worth keeping in mind as we encounter new potential drug triggers. The case report describes a patient who received a single dose of semaglutide and, within hours, developed severe gastrointestinal symptoms and metabolic derangement requiring ICU care. Her initial CK was only modestly elevated at 337 U/L. But over the subsequent four months, while completely off the drug, her CK climbed to nearly 4000 U/L. She developed profound proximal weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia. This temporal pattern, worsening after drug discontinuation, argues strongly against direct toxicity and points toward an immune-mediated process.

## Making the Diagnosis When Antibodies Are Absent

So how do you diagnose IMNM in a seronegative patient? The answer, quite simply, is muscle biopsy. But not just any biopsy interpretation. The histological features that define IMNM are distinct from other inflammatory myopathies.

In IMNM, you expect to see necrotic muscle fibers with minimal lymphocytic infiltration. This is notably different from polymyositis, which features prominent endomysial inflammatory infiltrates, or dermatomyositis, with its characteristic perifascicular atrophy and perivascular inflammation. In this patient's biopsy, pathologists identified dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers, myophagocytosis with CD68-positive macrophages, and membrane attack complex deposition on both necrotic and non-necrotic fibers. The paucity of CD3-positive T-cells was classic for IMNM.

The membrane attack complex staining is particularly valuable. Its presence on non-necrotic fibers suggests ongoing complement-mediated injury, supporting an immune-mediated pathogenesis rather than simple drug toxicity or physical trauma.

## Distinguishing Drug-Induced from Idiopathic IMNM

Here's where clinical pattern recognition becomes crucial. Drug-induced IMNM and idiopathic IMNM may look identical histologically, but their clinical trajectories diverge in telling ways.

Direct toxic myopathies, like classic drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, follow a predictable course. CK rises sharply, symptoms correlate with drug exposure, and everything normalizes within days to weeks after drug discontinuation. The previously reported case of tirzepatide-associated rhabdomyolysis followed exactly this pattern, with a CK of 57,014 that rapidly normalized with hydration alone.

In contrast, drug-triggered autoimmune myopathy behaves differently. The patient in this case report showed continued deterioration for months after her single semaglutide dose. Her CK increased 12-fold while completely off the offending agent. And critically, she improved only after receiving immunosuppression.

Another telling feature is the treatment response. Idiopathic IMNM typically requires prolonged, often intensive immunosuppression. Patients may need extended courses of steroids, IVIG, and steroid-sparing agents. This patient, however, was weaned off prednisone within two months and received only a single course of IVIG. She remained in remission at 10-month follow-up, off all immunosuppression. This abbreviated treatment course aligns more closely with drug-induced autoimmune myopathy, where removing the trigger plus short-term immunosuppression can induce durable remission.

## When to Pull the Trigger on Biopsy

The practical question for emergency physicians and hospitalists is when to escalate care and pursue muscle biopsy. Several red flags should increase your suspicion for IMNM and prompt rheumatology or neurology consultation.

First, the weakness pattern matters. IMNM typically presents with symmetric proximal weakness. Patients struggle to rise from chairs, climb stairs, or lift objects overhead. Dysphagia and dysphonia, as seen in this patient, indicate more severe disease and carry prognostic significance.

Second, the CK magnitude. While there's no absolute threshold, CK elevations into the thousands, particularly when progressive, should raise concern. This patient's CK peaked at nearly 4000 U/L, well beyond what you'd expect from simple disuse or mild myopathy.

Third, and perhaps most importantly, the temporal pattern. Progressive weakness and rising CK despite removal of potential triggers should immediately prompt consideration of immune-mediated disease. The patient who gets worse after you stop the suspected offending agent needs further evaluation, not reassurance that time will heal.

Fourth, extramuscular manifestations. While IMNM primarily affects skeletal muscle, cardiac involvement can occur. This patient had an elevated troponin and proBNP, with cardiac MRI showing myocardial edema potentially consistent with myocarditis. Whether this represented true cardiac involvement or stress-induced cardiomyopathy remains unclear, but the possibility of cardiac IMNM warrants consideration and appropriate workup.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- Approximately one-third of IMNM patients are seronegative for anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP antibodies. A negative myositis panel does not exclude the diagnosis.
- Muscle biopsy remains the gold standard for diagnosing seronegative IMNM. Key histological features include necrotic fibers with minimal lymphocytic infiltrates, macrophage-predominant inflammation, and membrane attack complex deposition.
- The clinical course distinguishes drug-induced autoimmune myopathy from direct toxicity. Worsening after drug discontinuation suggests immune-mediated disease; rapid improvement suggests toxicity.
- Drug-induced IMNM may respond to shorter immunosuppression courses after drug withdrawal, unlike idiopathic IMNM, which often requires prolonged treatment.
- As GLP-1 receptor agonists become ubiquitous, clinicians should remain vigilant for unusual adverse effects and report potential associations to enhance pharmacovigilance.

## The Bottom Line

The history of statin-associated IMNM teaches us that drug triggers of autoimmune disease can fly under the radar for years before the underlying mechanism is elucidated. This case raises the question of whether GLP-1 receptor agonists might join the list of potential IMNM triggers. The causality assessment using WHO-UMC criteria classified this as "probable," but more reports will be needed to establish whether this association is causal or coincidental.

Regardless, the clinical lessons transcend this specific drug association. When a patient presents with progressive myopathy and a negative antibody panel, don't stop your workup. Muscle biopsy can reveal an immune-mediated process that demands immunosuppression, not just drug cessation. And sometimes, the most important diagnostic tool is simply recognizing that the clinical picture doesn't fit a simple toxic explanation.

The patient in this case went from fully independent to unable to stand or sit upright without assistance. With appropriate treatment, she returned to baseline and remained in remission. That's a outcome worth fighting for, but it requires clinicians to push beyond the easy answer of "negative panel, probably nothing."



# Beyond the Muscle: Cardiac Involvement in Inflammatory Myopathies

## When the Heart Joins the Attack

Here's what makes this case of semaglutide-associated immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) particularly compelling: the patient's CK was climbing, her proximal muscles were failing, and she couldn't swallow—but buried in her labs were a troponin of 62 pg/mL and a proBNP of 4358 pg/mL. That's not just a bystander effect. That's the heart getting pulled into the autoimmune crossfire.

We talk a lot about the muscle in IMNM—the necrosis, the weakness, the anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP antibodies. But cardiac involvement in inflammatory myopathies is one of those complications that's easy to miss until it's catastrophic. This case is a reminder that when you're working up a myopathy, the heart deserves its own workup.

## The Spectrum of Cardiac Involvement

Inflammatory myopathies don't respect the boundary between skeletal and cardiac muscle. The literature describes a range of manifestations, from subclinical troponin elevations to overt myocarditis, arrhythmias, conduction abnormalities, and even heart failure. In polymyositis and dermatomyositis, cardiac involvement is a significant contributor to mortality—some studies suggest it accounts for up to 10-20% of deaths in these patients.

IMNM specifically? We're still learning. The condition is newer on the diagnostic radar, and cardiac manifestations are less systematically characterized. But there's no reason to think the heart is spared. The same immune-mediated attack that's chewing through skeletal muscle fibers can target myocardium, and the histopathology of necrotising myopathy—membrane attack complex deposition, macrophage-mediated myophagocytosis—can show up in cardiac tissue too.

In this patient, the cardiac MRI showed myocardial edema in the anteroseptal and anterior walls with mild hypokinesis. No late gadolinium enhancement yet—which may have spared her from more permanent damage—but the pattern was consistent with early myocarditis. Her elevated proBNP added another data point: this wasn't just troponin leakage from skeletal muscle breakdown. The heart was under stress.

## Myocarditis or Stress Cardiomyopathy? The Diagnostic Dilemma

Here's where clinical judgment gets tested. This patient was critically ill—severe muscle weakness, dysphagia, a prolonged hospitalization, and a subsequent ICU stay for septic shock. Stress-induced cardiomyopathy (takotsubo or its variants) would be reasonable to consider, especially given the stressor of critical illness.

But several features tipped the scales toward myocarditis in this case. First, the troponin elevation was modest and didn't follow a classic ischemic or stress cardiomyopathy pattern of rapid rise and fall. Second, the cardiac MRI showed regional involvement (anteroseptal and anterior walls) rather than the apical ballooning typical of takotsubo. Third, and perhaps most importantly, her cardiac function normalized in parallel with her muscle disease responding to immunosuppression. That temporal relationship suggests a shared inflammatory mechanism rather than a discrete stress event.

The authors acknowledge the ambiguity, and they should—cardiac involvement in myositis can masquerade as other conditions, and overlap is possible. But the takeaway for the clinician is this: in a patient with active inflammatory myopathy, don't reflexively attribute cardiac findings to stress or critical illness. Consider myocarditis on the differential, and let the treatment response inform your certainty.

## A Practical Approach to Cardiac Screening

So how do you approach cardiac evaluation in a patient presenting with inflammatory myopathy? There's no universally agreed-upon protocol, but here's a reasonable framework based on what we know.

Start with baseline labs. Every patient with suspected myositis should get an ECG and a troponin—preferably a high-sensitivity assay. BNP or proBNP is reasonable given this patient's presentation, as it may signal early cardiac strain even when systolic function is preserved. Echocardiography should be part of the initial workup for anyone with clinical myositis, looking specifically for wall motion abnormalities, diastolic dysfunction, and pulmonary hypertension.

In patients with any abnormality on screening—troponin elevation, ECG changes, wall motion abnormalities on echo—cardiac MRI is the next step. It's the gold standard for non-invasive myocarditis diagnosis, capable of detecting edema (on T2-weighted imaging) and fibrosis (late gadolinium enhancement). Early in the disease course, you may see edema without fibrosis, as in this case. That's potentially reversible with timely immunosuppression. Once fibrosis sets in, you're looking at permanent myocardial damage.

For patients with normal baseline studies but ongoing active disease, consider serial reassessment, especially if they develop new cardiac symptoms or if their myopathy proves refractory to treatment. The heart can become involved later in the disease course.

## What Happened to This Patient?

The encouraging news: this patient's cardiac findings normalized. A follow-up echocardiogram and cardiac MRI showed resolution of the earlier abnormalities, and she hasn't had recurrent arrhythmias. She regained baseline function off immunosuppression for months. The parallel recovery of her skeletal muscle and cardiac involvement supports the notion that both were driven by the same inflammatory process—and that prompt treatment broke that cycle.

That's the hopeful message. Cardiac involvement in inflammatory myopathy isn't always a sentence to permanent dysfunction. But catching it early requires maintaining a low threshold for suspicion and a systematic approach to evaluation.

## Key Take-Home Points

- Cardiac involvement in inflammatory myopathies is underrecognized and contributes significantly to mortality; don't assume it's stress or critical illness related without investigation.
- Screening with ECG, troponin, and echocardiography should be standard in initial myositis workup; proBNP adds value for detecting early cardiac strain.
- Cardiac MRI is the gold standard for myocarditis diagnosis and can identify edema before permanent fibrosis develops—treatment at this stage may be reversible.
- In this case, cardiac function normalized with immunosuppression, suggesting myocarditis from the same immune-mediated process affecting skeletal muscle.
- When an inflammatory myopathy patient has cardiac findings, consider them connected until proven otherwise—and treat accordingly.



# One Dose, One Diagnosis: Assessing Causality in Suspected Drug-Induced Autoimmune Disease

## The Case That Stops You in Your Tracks

A woman in her 60s receives a single dose of semaglutide—just 0.25 mg subcutaneously, the starting dose for weight management. Within hours, she's vomiting. Days later, she's in the ICU with metabolic acidosis and acute kidney injury. But here's where it gets strange: four months later, she can't stand without assistance, she's having trouble swallowing, and her CK has climbed to nearly 4000 U/L. She's never taken a statin in her life. Her myositis panel is entirely negative. And after immunosuppression with steroids and IVIG—plus strict avoidance of GLP-1 receptor agonists—she returns to baseline and stays there.

One dose. That's all it took to potentially trigger an immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM).

This case, published by Lam and colleagues, raises a question that keeps pharmacovigilance experts up at night: How do we determine if a drug *actually caused* an autoimmune condition? And what do we do when rechallenge is off the table?

## The WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre Framework

The authors used the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) causality criteria to assess this case. This is a structured approach that moves beyond gut feelings and into systematic evaluation. The categories range from "Certain" to "Unassessable," with "Probable" sitting in that sweet spot where the evidence is compelling but not airtight.

This case landed on "Probable" for several reasons. First, there's a reasonable temporal relationship—the symptoms began after semaglutide exposure. Second, no other obvious culprit exists; the patient denied statin use, red yeast rice, or any other known triggers. Third, she improved with drug withdrawal and immunosuppression, and—crucially—she remained stable off all therapy while avoiding GLP-1 receptor agonists.

But here's the catch: rechallenge would be unethical. No clinician is going to give semaglutide again to a patient who nearly ended up on a ventilator and developed profound proximal weakness. This limitation is built into the UMC framework, and it's one we have to live with in drug-induced autoimmune disease.

## Triggering Versus Causing: A Critical Distinction

This is where the conversation gets philosophically interesting—and clinically relevant. Did semaglutide *cause* this woman's IMNM, or did it *trigger* an underlying predisposition?

The authors acknowledge this uncertainty. Their patient had a low-titer positive SS-B antibody with a negative ANA and SS-A—an unusual and poorly understood finding. Could she have had smoldering autoimmune disease before that fateful injection? They can't definitively exclude it, though the dramatic 12-fold rise in CK after semaglutide and the lack of prior muscle symptoms argue against it.

This distinction matters because it shapes how we think about drug-induced autoimmunity more broadly. Think about statins and HMGCR antibodies. We now know that statins can unmask an autoimmune myopathy in genetically susceptible individuals, and that disease can persist even after the drug is stopped. The drug isn't directly toxic to muscle—it's flipping a switch in someone whose immune system was already primed.

GLP-1 receptor agonists might be similar. We already know these medications cause skeletal muscle mass loss. There's one published case of rhabdomyolysis with tirzepatide that looked like direct toxicity: CK over 57,000, rapid improvement with drug withdrawal and hydration, no immunosuppression needed. That's a different beast entirely from what happened here. This patient's progressive weakness over months, dramatic response to immunosuppression, and sustained remission after a short treatment course all point toward an immune-mediated process—not direct drug toxicity.

## When Rechallenge Is Unethical: The Pharmacovigilance Dilemma

In classical drug adverse effect assessment, rechallenge is the gold standard. Did the rash come back when you restarted the antibiotic? Did the liver enzymes jump again with the second exposure? That's how we move from "Probable" to "Certain."

But with autoimmune disease, rechallenge is often impossible to justify ethically. The consequences are too severe, the recovery too uncertain. This patient spent nearly four weeks in the hospital, required IVIG, and had cardiac involvement on imaging that raised concerns for myocarditis. Exposing her to semaglutide again would be reckless.

This means we rely on other evidence: the temporal relationship, the exclusion of alternative explanations, the response to drug withdrawal, and—critically—whether similar cases start appearing as drug use expands.

## Clinical Pearls for the Frontlines

What should emergency physicians and hospitalists take from this case?

First, GLP-1 receptor agonists are everywhere now. Semaglutide, tirzepatide, liraglutide—these medications have transformed diabetes and obesity management, and their use is exploding. We need to keep autoimmune complications on our radar, even though they appear rare.

Second, distinguish between drug-induced rhabdomyolysis and drug-induced autoimmune myopathy. Rhabdomyolysis is acute, dramatic, and improves rapidly with withdrawal and supportive care. Autoimmune myopathy progresses over weeks to months, requires immunosuppression, and may leave patients with lingering weakness if not recognized and treated promptly.

Third, seronegativity doesn't rule out IMNM. About one-third of patients with immune-mediated necrotising myopathy are seronegative for HMGCR and SRP antibodies. This patient's entire myositis panel was negative, yet her biopsy told the story.

Fourth, report your cases. The authors used established causality frameworks and published this case specifically to raise awareness and contribute to pharmacovigilance. If you encounter something similar, document it thoroughly and report it through your institution's adverse event reporting system and to regulatory authorities.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- A single dose of semaglutide may have triggered immune-mediated necrotising myopathy in this case, assessed as "Probable" using WHO-UMC causality criteria
- Drug-induced autoimmune disease differs from direct drug toxicity: it progresses over time, requires immunosuppression, and may improve with drug withdrawal alone in some cases
- Rechallenge is often unethical in suspected drug-induced autoimmune conditions, making causality assessment inherently limited
- Seronegative IMNM is common—approximately one-third of cases lack identifiable autoantibodies
- As GLP-1 receptor agonist use expands, clinicians should maintain awareness of potential autoimmune complications and report suspected cases through established pharmacovigilance channels

## The Bottom Line

This case is a reminder that new medications can have unexpected consequences, and those consequences don't always fit neatly into our existing diagnostic categories. The patient's remarkable response to immunosuppression and withdrawal from GLP-1 receptor agonists strongly suggests semaglutide played a role—but we can't prove it definitively.

What we can do is stay vigilant, think systematically about causality, and contribute to the collective knowledge that makes medicine safer for everyone.
