Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a patient who develops myopathy while taking a potentially causative medication, what clinical and laboratory features would suggest an immune-mediated process rather than direct drug toxicity?

**Clinical Teaching Answer**

When evaluating a patient who develops myopathy while taking a potentially causative medication, several key clinical and laboratory features distinguish an immune-mediated process from direct drug toxicity.

**First, the temporal course of creatine kinase (CK) elevation is critical.** In direct drug toxicity, CK elevation typically normalizes within days to weeks after drug discontinuation, as the offending agent is cleared and muscle injury resolves. In contrast, immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is characterized by persistent or worsening CK elevation despite drug cessation. In this case, the patient's CK increased 12-fold over 4 months *after* receiving only a single dose of semaglutide—progressing from 337 U/L at initial presentation to 3,992 U/L months later while off the medication entirely. This pattern strongly argues against direct toxicity.

**Second, the response to drug withdrawal versus immunosuppression is informative.** Direct toxic myopathy resolves with supportive care and drug discontinuation alone. Immune-mediated myopathy, however, requires immunosuppression and demonstrates clinical improvement in response to it. This patient showed marked improvement in CK and functional status only after receiving intravenous methylprednisolone and IVIG, and she sustained her remission off immunosuppression while avoiding GLP-1 receptor agonists.

**Third, muscle biopsy findings are essential.** Direct drug toxicity may show necrosis but typically lacks significant inflammation. IMNM is characterized by necrotic myofibers with a paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates, but with macrophage infiltration, myophagocytosis, and complement deposition. This patient's biopsy demonstrated necrotic and regenerating fibers, CD68+ macrophages with myophagocytosis, membrane attack complex deposition, and perivascular inflammatory cells—findings consistent with an immune-mediated process.

**Fourth, the presence of myositis-specific autoantibodies supports an immune mechanism**, though approximately one-third of IMNM patients are seronegative. Anti-HMGCR antibodies, for example, are strongly associated with statin-triggered IMNM. This patient was seronegative, but the histologic and clinical features remained consistent with IMNM.

**Finally, the clinical phenotype matters.** IMNM typically presents with aggressive, progressive proximal muscle weakness and may include dysphagia, as seen in this patient. The persistent, progressive nature of symptoms despite drug discontinuation should prompt evaluation for an immune-mediated etiology.

In summary, persistent or rising CK after drug cessation, need for immunosuppression, necrotizing myopathy with inflammatory features on biopsy, and autoantibody positivity all suggest an immune-mediated process rather than direct drug toxicity.
