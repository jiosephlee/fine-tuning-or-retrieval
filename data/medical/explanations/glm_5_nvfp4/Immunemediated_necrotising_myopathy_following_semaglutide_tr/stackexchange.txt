Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

### [Clinical Pearls] What are the two autoantibodies most strongly associated with immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM), and which medication class is classically associated with one of these autoantibodies?

### Clinical Teaching Answer

The two autoantibodies most strongly associated with immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) are **anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (anti-HMGCR)** and **anti-signal recognition particle (anti-SRP)**. **Statins** are the medication class classically associated with anti-HMGCR antibody positivity.

As described in this case, IMNM is characterized by aggressive proximal muscle weakness, marked creatine kinase elevation, and histology demonstrating necrotising myofibers with a paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates. The association between statins and anti-HMGCR antibodies was recognized when patients previously classified as having statin-induced toxic myopathy or polymyositis failed to improve after statin discontinuation and instead required immunosuppression—revealing an autoimmune mechanism rather than direct drug toxicity. This distinction is clinically important: unlike toxic myopathies that resolve with drug withdrawal, anti-HMGCR-positive IMNM represents a true autoimmune process requiring immunosuppressive therapy.

Notably, approximately one-third of patients with IMNM are seronegative for both anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP, as was this patient. Her negative myositis panel and the temporal relationship between semaglutide exposure and symptom onset raised the possibility of a drug-induced IMNM, underscoring that seronegative disease does not exclude the diagnosis and that additional autoantibodies and triggers likely remain to be discovered.

### [Pathophysiology] What is the characteristic histopathological finding on muscle biopsy that distinguishes immune-mediated necrotising myopathy from polymyositis and other inflammatory myopathies?

**Answer:**

The characteristic histopathological finding that distinguishes immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) from polymyositis and other inflammatory myopathies is **prominent myofiber necrosis with a paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates**.

In IMNM, the muscle biopsy demonstrates scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers with minimal or absent endomysial inflammation—specifically, a notable absence of the CD8+ T-cell infiltrates that characterize polymyositis. This is clearly illustrated in our patient's biopsy, which showed dispersed necrotic and regenerating fibers with only rare CD3+ T cells present. The predominant inflammatory cells observed were macrophages (CD68+), reflecting myophagocytosis of necrotic debris rather than primary autoimmune attack.

Additional supporting features in IMNM include deposition of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9) on the sarcolemma of both necrotic and non-necrotic fibers, which was demonstrated in this case. This finding indicates complement-mediated injury as a key pathogenic mechanism.

By contrast, polymyositis is characterized by endomysial inflammatory infiltrates with CD8+ T cells that actively invade non-necrotic, MHC class I-expressing muscle fibers. Dermatomyositis shows perimysial inflammation, perifascicular atrophy, and complement deposition within the microvasculature. The relative absence of prominent lymphocytic infiltration in IMNM is therefore the critical distinguishing histological feature, guiding both diagnosis and our understanding of the distinct immunopathogenic mechanisms at play.

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a patient who develops myopathy while taking a potentially causative medication, what clinical and laboratory features would suggest an immune-mediated process rather than direct drug toxicity?

**Clinical Teaching Answer**

When evaluating a patient who develops myopathy while taking a potentially causative medication, several key clinical and laboratory features distinguish an immune-mediated process from direct drug toxicity.

**First, the temporal course of creatine kinase (CK) elevation is critical.** In direct drug toxicity, CK elevation typically normalizes within days to weeks after drug discontinuation, as the offending agent is cleared and muscle injury resolves. In contrast, immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is characterized by persistent or worsening CK elevation despite drug cessation. In this case, the patient's CK increased 12-fold over 4 months *after* receiving only a single dose of semaglutide—progressing from 337 U/L at initial presentation to 3,992 U/L months later while off the medication entirely. This pattern strongly argues against direct toxicity.

**Second, the response to drug withdrawal versus immunosuppression is informative.** Direct toxic myopathy resolves with supportive care and drug discontinuation alone. Immune-mediated myopathy, however, requires immunosuppression and demonstrates clinical improvement in response to it. This patient showed marked improvement in CK and functional status only after receiving intravenous methylprednisolone and IVIG, and she sustained her remission off immunosuppression while avoiding GLP-1 receptor agonists.

**Third, muscle biopsy findings are essential.** Direct drug toxicity may show necrosis but typically lacks significant inflammation. IMNM is characterized by necrotic myofibers with a paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates, but with macrophage infiltration, myophagocytosis, and complement deposition. This patient's biopsy demonstrated necrotic and regenerating fibers, CD68+ macrophages with myophagocytosis, membrane attack complex deposition, and perivascular inflammatory cells—findings consistent with an immune-mediated process.

**Fourth, the presence of myositis-specific autoantibodies supports an immune mechanism**, though approximately one-third of IMNM patients are seronegative. Anti-HMGCR antibodies, for example, are strongly associated with statin-triggered IMNM. This patient was seronegative, but the histologic and clinical features remained consistent with IMNM.

**Finally, the clinical phenotype matters.** IMNM typically presents with aggressive, progressive proximal muscle weakness and may include dysphagia, as seen in this patient. The persistent, progressive nature of symptoms despite drug discontinuation should prompt evaluation for an immune-mediated etiology.

In summary, persistent or rising CK after drug cessation, need for immunosuppression, necrotizing myopathy with inflammatory features on biopsy, and autoantibody positivity all suggest an immune-mediated process rather than direct drug toxicity.

### [Morning Report] A patient on a GLP-1 receptor agonist presents with proximal muscle weakness and markedly elevated CK. What temporal pattern of CK changes and symptom progression would favor immune-mediated necrotising myopathy over direct drug-induced rhabdomyolysis?

**Clinical Answer:**

The key temporal distinction lies in what happens after the offending drug is discontinued.

In **direct drug-induced rhabdomyolysis**, CK elevation occurs while the patient is actively taking the medication, and CK normalizes within days to weeks after drug withdrawal—typically without requiring immunosuppression. As illustrated in the referenced tirzepatide case, the patient presented with an acute CK of 57,014 U/L that improved rapidly with drug cessation and hydration alone.

In contrast, **immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM)** is characterized by **persistent or progressive CK elevation and worsening weakness despite drug discontinuation**. In this case, the patient received only a single dose of semaglutide, yet her CK rose 12-fold over the ensuing 4 months—from 337 U/L to nearly 4,000 U/L—while off the medication entirely. Her proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia progressively worsened during this same period. This temporal pattern of continued deterioration after drug exposure is highly atypical for direct toxic injury and instead suggests an immune-mediated process that becomes self-sustaining.

The clinical course post-treatment further supports this distinction: she responded to corticosteroids and IVIG with normalization of CK and sustained improvement after immunosuppression was weaned, and she has remained stable with continued avoidance of GLP-1 agonists. This is analogous to statin-associated IMNM due to anti-HMGCR antibodies, where patients often have persistent myopathy despite statin cessation and require immunosuppression for resolution.

**Bottom line:** If CK and symptoms improve promptly after stopping the drug, think direct toxicity. If CK rises or weakness progresses after drug discontinuation, think immune-mediated necrotising myopathy—and pursue muscle biopsy and myositis antibody testing while initiating immunosuppression.

### [Management] What is the typical first-line immunosuppressive treatment approach for a patient presenting with severe immune-mediated necrotising myopathy, including those with dysphagia and significant functional impairment?

**First-Line Immunoupppressive Treatment for Severe IMNM**

For patients presenting with severe immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM)—particularly those with dysphagia, profound proximal weakness, or significantly elevated creatine kinase (CK)—the standard first-line approach is **combination therapy with high-dose glucocorticoids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG)**.

This patient demonstrated classic severe features: progressive proximal muscle weakness rendering her unable to stand or sit without assistance, dysphagia, dysphonia, and a CK of nearly 4000 U/L. Her treatment exemplifies the appropriate initial strategy: **pulse-dose intravenous methylprednisolone (1000 mg daily for 3–5 days)** followed by **oral prednisone at approximately 1 mg/kg daily**, combined with **IVIG at 2 g/kg administered in divided doses over 2–5 days**.

The rationale for this aggressive combination approach is threefold. First, IMNM—especially when associated with anti-HMGCR or anti-SRP antibodies, or in severe presentations—often has a refractory course with poor response to glucocorticoids alone. Second, patients with dysphagia are at high risk for aspiration and nutritional compromise, necessitating rapid disease control. Third, IVIG has demonstrated efficacy in necrotizing myopathies and can provide more immediate benefit while waiting for glucocorticoid-sparing agents to take effect.

In this case, the patient showed marked improvement in CK within 2 weeks and ultimately regained baseline function. Notably, because her IMNM was drug-induced (temporally associated with semaglutide), she was able to discontinue immunosuppression relatively quickly after drug withdrawal—a course that differs from idiopathic IMNM, which typically requires prolonged immunosuppression with steroid-sparing agents such as methotrexate, azathioprine, or mycophenolate mofetil for maintenance.

### [Clinical Pearls] Approximately what proportion of patients with immune-mediated necrotising myopathy are seronegative for known autoantibodies, and what does this suggest about our current understanding of this disease?

**Clinical Answer:**

Approximately one-third of patients with immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) are seronegative for known autoantibodies. This figure, cited in the case report, carries important implications for both clinical practice and our understanding of disease pathogenesis.

From a clinical standpoint, seronegativity presents diagnostic challenges—without a confirmatory autoantibody, clinicians must rely more heavily on histopathology, which classically demonstrates necrotic myofibers with minimal lymphocytic infiltration, as seen in this patient's muscle biopsy. The diagnosis in such cases requires a high index of suspicion and exclusion of other causes of myopathy.

From a pathophysiologic perspective, this substantial seronegative subset suggests that our current antibody panels are incomplete. The history of IMNM is instructive: before anti-HMGCR antibodies were identified in 2010, many statin-associated cases were misclassified as toxic myopathy or polymyositis. The discovery of this antibody transformed our understanding and enabled targeted serologic diagnosis. The authors argue persuasively that similar discoveries likely await us—both novel autoantibodies and novel triggers.

This case exemplifies that principle. The patient was seronegative for all known myositis-specific antibodies, including anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP, yet demonstrated classic biopsy features and a clear treatment response to immunosuppression. The temporal association with semaglutide exposure, coupled with sustained remission after drug discontinuation and a brief immunosuppressive course, raises the possibility that GLP-1 receptor agonists may represent a previously unrecognized trigger for IMNM—much as statins were before the HMGCR antibody was discovered. Whether an associated autoantibody will eventually be identified in semaglutide-associated cases remains to be seen, but this case underscores the principle that seronegativity reflects the limits of our current diagnostic tools, not the absence of an autoimmune process.

### [Differential Diagnosis] When evaluating a patient with suspected immune-mediated necrotising myopathy, what medication and supplement exposures should be specifically elicited in the history beyond statins?

**Clinical Teaching Answer**

When evaluating a patient with suspected immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM), beyond statins, you should specifically elicit exposure to **red yeast rice** and **mushroom supplements**, as well as newer agents including **GLP-1 receptor agonists**.

**Red yeast rice** contains monacolin K, which is chemically identical to lovastatin, and can therefore trigger the same anti-HMGCR antibody-mediated process as statins. Patients may not volunteer this information unless specifically asked, as they may not consider supplements to be "medications."

**Mushroom supplements** have been associated with IMNM in prior reports, though the pathogenic mechanism remains less well characterized than for statins.

This case highlights the importance of inquiring about **GLP-1 receptor agonists** (such as semaglutide, tirzepatide). The temporal relationship between this patient's single dose of semaglutide and the subsequent development of progressive proximal weakness, CK elevation to nearly 4000 U/L, and biopsy-proven necrotising myopathy—with resolution after drug discontinuation and a short course of immunosuppression—suggests these agents may be a newly recognized trigger. This is biologically plausible given that drug-induced IMNM, unlike direct toxic myopathy, involves an immune-mediated process that can persist and progress even after the offending agent is stopped, as occurred in this case.

The key clinical pearl: In IMNM, the medication or supplement exposure may precede symptom onset by weeks to months, and the myopathy can progress despite discontinuation—features that distinguish immune-mediated necrotising myopathy from simple drug-induced toxic myopathy and should prompt early immunosuppressive therapy.
