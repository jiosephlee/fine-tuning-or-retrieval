Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

## Case Presentation and Clinical Timeline

Understanding the clinical presentation of this case requires careful attention to the temporal relationship between drug exposure and symptom development, as this sequence forms the foundation for the causal inference between semaglutide and immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). The patient, a woman in her 60s, presented with a medical history notable for obesity, hepatosteatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a remote history of venous thromboembolism. Importantly, she had been fully independent in her activities of daily living and in good functional health prior to her acute deterioration—a baseline that makes her subsequent decline particularly striking.

Three years before her neuromuscular presentation, she had undergone evaluation for persistently elevated transaminases. At that time, her aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were approximately twice the upper limit of normal, with ALT exceeding AST. A comprehensive work-up excluded viral hepatitis, autoimmune hepatitis, and other primary liver diseases. Liver biopsy confirmed non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) with an activity score of 4/8. This pre-existing hepatosteatosis is clinically relevant for two reasons: first, it provided the indication for semaglutide initiation, and second, it complicates the later interpretation of transaminase elevations, which can originate from either hepatic or muscular sources.

The patient was initiated on semaglutide for persistent fatty liver and obesity—appropriate indications given the medication's efficacy for weight loss and metabolic improvement. However, within hours of receiving her first and only dose (0.25 mg subcutaneously), she developed severe nausea and vomiting. Two days later, she was hospitalised with dehydration, acute renal failure, and severe metabolic acidosis requiring intravenous hydration, bicarbonate therapy, and brief non-invasive ventilation. During this admission, her creatine kinase (CK) was modestly elevated at 337 U/L—not yet in the range typical for rhabdomyolysis, but notable as a potential early marker of muscle injury. She was discharged after five days with resolved gastrointestinal symptoms but residual deconditioning.

What distinguishes this case from straightforward drug-induced rhabdomyolysis is the trajectory that followed. Over the subsequent four months, the patient experienced progressive proximal muscle weakness, ultimately rendering her unable to stand or sit without assistance. She also developed dysphagia (difficulty swallowing) and dysphonia (voice changes), reflecting involvement of bulbar and pharyngeal musculature. This clinical constellation—progressive proximal weakness with bulbar involvement and a rising CK that peaked at 3992 U/L—is characteristic of inflammatory myopathy rather than direct toxic injury. In drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, CK typically rises acutely and improves rapidly after drug withdrawal; here, the CK increased twelve-fold over four months *after* drug discontinuation, suggesting an ongoing autoimmune process triggered by the initial exposure.

Physical examination at the tertiary care facility revealed profound weakness in proximal muscle groups, including the shoulder abductors, elbow flexors, hip flexors, and knee extensors. The distribution is diagnostically important: proximal greater than distal weakness is the hallmark of myopathy, as opposed to neuropathy, which typically affects distal muscles first. Notably, she had no rash, skin changes, or other features of dermatomyositis, no synovitis or sicca symptoms to suggest an underlying connective tissue disease, and no other neurological deficits to suggest an alternative diagnosis. The focused pattern of weakness with sparing of other neurological functions narrows the differential toward primary muscle disease.

The timeline from drug exposure to definitive presentation spans approximately four months, with the initial dose of semaglutide serving as the sentinel event. This latency period is consistent with immune-mediated pathology, where an inciting trigger (in this case, possibly the drug exposure) initiates an autoimmune response that then becomes self-sustaining. Understanding this temporal sequence is essential for appreciating why the authors classified the causality as "probable" rather than "certain"—the clinical course strongly suggests a drug-triggered autoimmune process, but the single exposure and lack of pre-exposure CK measurement introduce inherent uncertainty. Nonetheless, the clinical picture of progressive weakness, bulbar involvement, and rising CK in temporal proximity to a new medication should prompt clinicians to consider drug-induced IMNM in the differential diagnosis.

## Background: Immune-Mediated Necrotising Myopathy and GLP-1 Receptor Agonists

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is a distinct subtype of inflammatory myopathy characterized by aggressive, symmetric proximal muscle weakness and markedly elevated creatine kinase (CK) levels. Unlike polymyositis or dermatomyositis, IMNM is defined histologically by prominent necrosis of muscle fibers with minimal lymphocytic infiltration. This histopathologic signature is central to diagnosis. The European Neuromuscular Centre (ENMC) diagnostic criteria incorporate clinical presentation, laboratory findings, and muscle biopsy characteristics to distinguish IMNM from other myositis subtypes.

The pathophysiology of IMNM involves complement-mediated muscle injury. Deposition of the membrane attack complex (MAC) on myofiber surfaces leads to direct cell lysis and necrosis, even without prominent inflammatory cell infiltrates. Muscle biopsy typically reveals scattered necrotic and regenerating fibers, with macrophages recruited to phagocytose necrotic debris—a process termed myophagocytosis. In our patient's case, the biopsy demonstrated dispersed necrotic fibers, CD68-positive macrophages with myophagocytosis, and MAC deposition on both necrotic and non-necrotic fibers, findings consistent with this complement-mediated mechanism.

Two autoantibodies are strongly associated with IMNM: anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (anti-HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (anti-SRP). Anti-HMGCR antibodies are particularly relevant to drug-induced IMNM, as they were first identified in patients who developed autoimmune myopathy following statin exposure. Anti-SRP antibodies identify a subset of patients with typically severe disease. However, approximately one-third of patients with IMNM are seronegative for both antibodies, suggesting that additional autoantibodies and triggers remain to be discovered. Our patient's comprehensive myositis panel was negative, placing her in this seronegative subset and underscoring the importance of muscle biopsy when clinical suspicion is high.

The statin experience provides a paradigm for understanding drug-induced IMNM. Before anti-HMGCR antibodies were discovered, patients who developed myopathy on statins were often classified as having toxic myopathy. A critical observation emerged: many patients continued to experience progressive weakness and CK elevation even after statin discontinuation. This persistence, coupled with response to immunosuppression, indicated an autoimmune process rather than direct drug toxicity. This clinical pattern—progression despite drug withdrawal and requirement for immunosuppression—distinguishes drug-induced IMNM from direct toxic myopathy, which typically improves rapidly after drug cessation. This distinction is essential for understanding our patient's clinical course.

Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have become widely used for type 2 diabetes and obesity. Semaglutide, the agent implicated in our patient's case, binds GLP-1 receptors to enhance glucose-dependent insulin secretion, suppress glucagon, and promote satiety through central nervous system effects. Common adverse effects are predominantly gastrointestinal, including nausea and vomiting. Emerging data suggest these agents may also cause loss of skeletal muscle mass during weight loss. A recent case report described rhabdomyolysis associated with tirzepatide, another GLP-1 receptor agonist. In that case, the patient developed CK levels in the tens of thousands while on treatment, with rapid normalization after drug discontinuation—consistent with direct toxic injury. This contrasts sharply with our patient's presentation: a progressive rise in CK over months and worsening weakness despite drug cessation, features more aligned with an immune-mediated process than direct toxicity.

## Diagnostic Workup and Differential Diagnosis

The diagnostic approach to a patient presenting with progressive muscle weakness requires a systematic evaluation that integrates laboratory findings, imaging, and ultimately tissue pathology. In this case, the patient's presentation with proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia occurring months after semaglutide exposure prompted a comprehensive myositis evaluation designed to distinguish between toxic myopathy, immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM), and other inflammatory myopathies.

**Laboratory Evaluation and the Significance of Seronegative Status**

Creatine kinase (CK) serves as the primary laboratory marker for muscle injury, and its elevation pattern provides important diagnostic clues. This patient's CK trajectory was particularly informative: it was modestly elevated at 337 U/L during her initial hospitalization after semaglutide administration, then rose dramatically to 3992 U/L over the subsequent four months. This progressive rise contrasted sharply with the expected pattern in acute toxic rhabdomyolysis, where CK typically peaks rapidly and declines within days to weeks after removal of the offending agent. The twelve-fold CK increase over four months signaled an ongoing pathologic process rather than a single acute injury event.

Transaminase interpretation required careful consideration in this patient. While aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) can originate from either liver or skeletal muscle, the AST elevation exceeding ALT at 191 U/L versus 165 U/L respectively, in the context of markedly elevated CK, suggested a muscular contribution. However, this patient also had biopsy-proven non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) with transaminase elevation predating her semaglutide exposure by three years. The persistence of mild transaminase elevation after CK normalization confirmed that her hepatic disease remained an independent process.

The myositis-specific autoantibody panel represents a cornerstone of IMNM diagnosis. Anti-HMGCR (3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase) antibodies are strongly associated with statin-exposed IMNM, while anti-SRP (signal recognition particle) antibodies identify a statin-independent subset. This patient tested negative for both antibodies, as well as for all other myositis-specific antibodies including Jo-1, Mi-2, MDA-5, and NXP-2. This seronegative status occurs in approximately one-third of IMNM patients and poses diagnostic challenges, as it requires clinicians to rely more heavily on histopathology and clinical exclusion criteria. The isolated low-titer anti-SS-B antibody without accompanying anti-SS-A or ANA positivity was an incidental finding without clear relevance to her myopathy, as isolated SS-B positivity has not been associated with IMNM in the literature.

**Imaging Studies and the Role of MRI in Myositis**

Muscle MRI has emerged as a valuable tool for characterizing the distribution and activity of myositis. In this patient, MRI of the bilateral femurs revealed symmetric muscular edema predominantly affecting the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps muscles. This pattern of proximal lower extremity involvement is characteristic of IMNM and other inflammatory myopathies, which typically spare distal muscles early in the disease course. The subcutaneous edema noted along the lateral thighs represented an associated inflammatory response. MRI findings can guide muscle biopsy site selection by targeting areas of active inflammation while avoiding end-stage fibrotic or fatty infiltrated muscle, thereby improving diagnostic yield.

**Cardiac Evaluation in Myositis**

Cardiac involvement in inflammatory myopathies ranges from subclinical to life-threatening and warrants systematic assessment. This patient's elevated high-sensitivity troponin (62 pg/mL) and markedly elevated pro-BNP (4358 pg/mL) raised concern for myocarditis. Echocardiography revealed normal left ventricular ejection fraction (65%–70%) with borderline right ventricular systolic pressure elevation (46 mm Hg). Cardiac MRI demonstrated mild hypokinesis of the mid-anteroseptal and anterior wall with myocardial edema but without late gadolinium enhancement, suggesting either early myocarditis or stress-induced cardiomyopathy. The absence of significant scarring or infarction provided reassurance, and follow-up cardiac imaging after treatment demonstrated resolution of these abnormalities. This case illustrates that cardiac findings in IMNM may represent genuine myocardial involvement, stress-related changes, or incidental comorbidities, requiring careful integration with the clinical context.

**Muscle Biopsy: The Definitive Diagnostic Tool**

Muscle biopsy remains the gold standard for diagnosing IMNM and distinguishing it from other myopathies. The biopsy from this patient's right vastus lateralis demonstrated the characteristic histopathologic features of IMNM: dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers with a paucity of lymphocytic infiltrates. This "necrotising myopathy" pattern—with prominent myofiber necrosis but minimal endomysial inflammation—distinguishes IMNM from polymyositis, which features dense T-cell infiltrates, and from dermatomyositis, which shows perifascicular atrophy and perivascular inflammation.

Immunohistochemical staining provided additional diagnostic clarity. CD68 positivity identified macrophages actively engulfing necrotic muscle fibers (myophagocytosis), confirming an active inflammatory process. The membrane attack complex (MAC) deposition on necrotic and non-necrotic myofibres indicated complement-mediated injury, a hallmark of antibody-driven myopathy even in seronegative cases. The presence of rare CD3-positive T-cells and perivascular inflammatory cells was consistent with IMNM's limited inflammatory infiltrate pattern. These findings collectively established the diagnosis of IMNM.

**Differential Diagnosis: Distinguishing IMNM from Other Myopathies**

The differential diagnosis for this presentation included toxic myopathy (rhabdomyolysis), drug-induced IMNM, idiopathic autoimmune IMNM, and other inflammatory myopathies. Several features distinguished IMNM from acute toxic rhabdomyolysis. First, the temporal course was inconsistent: rhabdomyolysis typically presents with acute CK elevation (often exceeding 10,000 U/L) that normalizes within days to weeks after drug discontinuation, whereas this patient's CK rose progressively over four months despite semaglutide cessation. Second, rhabdomyolysis does not respond to immunosuppression, while this patient improved dramatically with corticosteroids and IVIG. Third, the muscle biopsy pattern of IMNM—with complement deposition, necrosis, and macrophage-predominant infiltrates—differs from the straightforward myofiber injury pattern seen in toxic myopathy.

The distinction between drug-induced IMNM and idiopathic IMNM carries important prognostic implications. Drug-induced IMNM, exemplified by statin-associated HMGCR-positive myopathy, may respond to a finite course of immunosuppression following drug withdrawal, while idiopathic IMNM typically requires prolonged, often intensive immunosuppression. This patient's clinical course—rapid improvement and sustained remission off all immunosuppression after only two months of prednisone and a single course of IVIG—aligned more closely with drug-induced IMNM. The close temporal relationship between her single semaglutide dose and subsequent symptom onset, combined with her recovery without ongoing immunosuppression, supported semaglutide as the probable trigger according to WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre causality criteria.

However, diagnostic uncertainty remains inherent in such cases. The absence of a pre-semaglutide CK measurement prevents definitive exclusion of pre-existing smoldering autoimmune myopathy. The seronegative status also raises the possibility of an as-yet-unidentified autoantibody or trigger. Nevertheless, the weight of evidence—including the temporal association, biopsy findings, response to short-course immunosuppression, and sustained remission with GLP-1 receptor agonist avoidance—supports a probable association between semaglutide and this episode of IMNM.

## Treatment Strategy and Clinical Management

The management of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) requires prompt recognition and aggressive immunosuppressive therapy to prevent irreversible muscle damage and life-threatening complications such as dysphagia and respiratory weakness. In this case, the severity of the patient's presentation—marked by profound proximal muscle weakness rendering her unable to stand or sit without assistance, dysphagia, and a creatine kinase (CK) level peaking at 3992 U/L—necessitated an intensive treatment approach. The therapeutic strategy centered on two pillars: rapid induction of immunosuppression to halt ongoing muscle injury, and complete avoidance of the presumed triggering agent.

The treatment began with intravenous pulse methylprednisolone at 1000 mg daily for 5 days. Pulse corticosteroid therapy achieves rapid immunosuppression by delivering high plasma concentrations that more effectively suppress inflammatory cascades and reduce autoantibody-mediated damage than oral corticosteroids alone. This approach is particularly important in severe myositis with dysphagia, as the risk of aspiration and respiratory compromise is substantial. The 5-day duration of pulse therapy in this case reflects standard practice for inducing remission in severe inflammatory myopathies before transitioning to maintenance immunosuppression.

Following pulse therapy, the patient was transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg daily. This high-dose oral regimen serves as a bridge to maintain immunosuppression while longer-acting steroid-sparing agents take effect, though in this case, the patient's rapid improvement allowed for an abbreviated course of corticosteroid therapy. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at 2 g/kg divided over 4 days, a standard dosing protocol for inflammatory myopathies. IVIG is particularly valuable in IMNM because it modulates the immune response through multiple mechanisms, including neutralization of autoantibodies, inhibition of complement activation, and suppression of Fc receptor-mediated processes. In this patient, IVIG was selected due to persistent weakness and the severity of her clinical features despite pulse corticosteroid therapy. The combination of corticosteroids and IVIG represents a multi-pronged immunomodulatory approach that addresses both the cellular and humoral components of the autoimmune process.

Monitoring treatment response in IMNM relies on both biochemical markers and functional assessment. CK levels serve as a useful surrogate for ongoing muscle injury, though the correlation between CK and clinical improvement is imperfect. In this patient, CK levels demonstrated a marked decline over 2 weeks following initiation of therapy, falling from 1237 U/L at presentation to normal levels. More importantly, her functional status improved progressively, allowing her to regain independence in activities of daily living. The normalization of CK levels and functional recovery guided the decision to wean prednisone rapidly over approximately 2 months—a shorter course than typically required for idiopathic IMNM, which often necessitates prolonged immunosuppression. This abbreviated taper was appropriate given the suspected drug-induced etiology and the patient's sustained improvement after discontinuation of the triggering agent.

The importance of drug withdrawal cannot be overstated in cases of drug-associated IMNM. In this patient, complete avoidance of semaglutide and other glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists was a critical component of the management strategy. Unlike idiopathic IMNM, which requires ongoing immunosuppression, drug-induced immune myopathies may resolve with removal of the offending agent and a limited course of immunosuppression. The patient's sustained remission off all immunosuppression for 8 months, with continued avoidance of GLP-1 receptor agonists, supports the classification of this as a drug-induced phenomenon rather than a de novo autoimmune condition.

Immunosuppressive therapy carries inherent risks that require vigilance. This patient developed septic shock from a pseudomonal urinary tract infection approximately 12 days after discharge, highlighting the infectious complications associated with high-dose corticosteroids and IVIG. Despite this serious adverse event, her CK remained normal during the readmission, indicating that the myopathy remained controlled. The septic episode did not prompt escalation of immunosuppression, and she ultimately achieved full recovery without recurrence of her myopathy. This outcome reinforces the importance of balancing aggressive immunosuppression in severe disease with appropriate infection prophylaxis and close monitoring for complications.

## Clinical Outcomes, Lessons, and Pharmacovigilance Implications

The patient's clinical outcome provides important diagnostic clues about the underlying mechanism of her myopathy. Following treatment with pulse methylprednisolone, oral prednisone, and a single course of IVIG, she demonstrated a marked and sustained improvement. Her CK levels normalized within two weeks and remained normal throughout follow-up. She was successfully weaned off prednisone rapidly over approximately two months—a notably shorter treatment course than typically required for idiopathic immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM). At her last outpatient visit ten months after initial presentation, she had regained her baseline functional status and had been off all immunosuppression for seven to eight months without disease recurrence. This clinical trajectory stands in contrast to idiopathic IMNM, which characteristically requires prolonged and often intensive immunosuppressive therapy to prevent relapse. The ability to discontinue immunosuppression relatively quickly while maintaining remission supports the classification of this case as a drug-induced IMNM rather than an idiopathic autoimmune process.

Cardiac involvement, which can accompany inflammatory myopathies, also resolved with treatment. The initial cardiac MRI demonstrated mild hypokinesis of the mid-anteroseptal and anterior wall with myocardial edema, raising concern for possible myocarditis or stress-induced cardiomyopathy. However, follow-up echocardiogram and cardiac MRI after treatment showed normalization of these abnormalities, and she experienced no recurrence of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation. The parallel recovery of cardiac findings alongside muscle disease further supports an inflammatory process that responded appropriately to immunosuppression.

The authors applied the World Health Organization-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (WHO-UMC) Causality Assessment System to evaluate the relationship between semaglutide and IMNM in this case. This standardized framework evaluates drug-induced adverse events based on temporal relationship, alternative explanations, and response to drug withdrawal. The assessment classified the association as "probable," based on several key factors: a reasonable temporal relationship between drug exposure and symptom onset, the absence of other identifiable causes (negative myositis panel, no statin use, no other implicated medications), and a clinically appropriate response following drug discontinuation and immunosuppression. Rechallenge was appropriately deemed unethical given the severity of the initial reaction. While causality cannot be definitively established from a single case report, this structured assessment provides a systematic approach that clinicians can apply when evaluating potential drug-induced autoimmune conditions.

A critical clinical lesson from this case is the importance of distinguishing between direct toxic effects and immune-mediated mechanisms when evaluating drug-associated muscle injury. Direct drug-induced rhabdomyolysis typically presents with an acute, marked CK elevation that normalizes within days to weeks after drug withdrawal, without requiring immunosuppression. In contrast, this patient's CK continued to rise progressively over four months after a single dose of semaglutide, and her clinical improvement required immunosuppressive therapy. This pattern mirrors the historical recognition of statin-induced IMNM, where patients initially classified as having toxic myopathy were later recognized to have an autoimmune process that persisted despite drug discontinuation and responded to immunosuppression. Clinicians should consider immune-mediated mechanisms when muscle injury persists or progresses after withdrawal of the suspected offending agent.

The pharmacovigilance implications of this case are substantial given the exponential increase in GLP-1 receptor agonist prescriptions for obesity and type 2 diabetes. While this represents the first reported case of IMNM associated with this medication class, the widespread use of these agents necessitates heightened clinical awareness. Clinicians should include GLP-1 receptor agonists in the differential diagnosis of drug-induced myopathy, particularly when evaluating patients with proximal muscle weakness and elevated CK. Future case reports and pharmacovigilance data will be essential to clarify whether this association is causal or coincidental and to identify potential predisposing factors that may confer increased risk. Until then, clinicians should maintain a careful medication history in patients presenting with inflammatory myopathy and consider drug discontinuation as part of the management strategy when temporal relationships suggest an association.
