Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# One Dose, One Diagnosis: Assessing Causality in Suspected Drug-Induced Autoimmune Disease

## The Case That Stops You in Your Tracks

A woman in her 60s receives a single dose of semaglutide—just 0.25 mg subcutaneously, the starting dose for weight management. Within hours, she's vomiting. Days later, she's in the ICU with metabolic acidosis and acute kidney injury. But here's where it gets strange: four months later, she can't stand without assistance, she's having trouble swallowing, and her CK has climbed to nearly 4000 U/L. She's never taken a statin in her life. Her myositis panel is entirely negative. And after immunosuppression with steroids and IVIG—plus strict avoidance of GLP-1 receptor agonists—she returns to baseline and stays there.

One dose. That's all it took to potentially trigger an immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM).

This case, published by Lam and colleagues, raises a question that keeps pharmacovigilance experts up at night: How do we determine if a drug *actually caused* an autoimmune condition? And what do we do when rechallenge is off the table?

## The WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre Framework

The authors used the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) causality criteria to assess this case. This is a structured approach that moves beyond gut feelings and into systematic evaluation. The categories range from "Certain" to "Unassessable," with "Probable" sitting in that sweet spot where the evidence is compelling but not airtight.

This case landed on "Probable" for several reasons. First, there's a reasonable temporal relationship—the symptoms began after semaglutide exposure. Second, no other obvious culprit exists; the patient denied statin use, red yeast rice, or any other known triggers. Third, she improved with drug withdrawal and immunosuppression, and—crucially—she remained stable off all therapy while avoiding GLP-1 receptor agonists.

But here's the catch: rechallenge would be unethical. No clinician is going to give semaglutide again to a patient who nearly ended up on a ventilator and developed profound proximal weakness. This limitation is built into the UMC framework, and it's one we have to live with in drug-induced autoimmune disease.

## Triggering Versus Causing: A Critical Distinction

This is where the conversation gets philosophically interesting—and clinically relevant. Did semaglutide *cause* this woman's IMNM, or did it *trigger* an underlying predisposition?

The authors acknowledge this uncertainty. Their patient had a low-titer positive SS-B antibody with a negative ANA and SS-A—an unusual and poorly understood finding. Could she have had smoldering autoimmune disease before that fateful injection? They can't definitively exclude it, though the dramatic 12-fold rise in CK after semaglutide and the lack of prior muscle symptoms argue against it.

This distinction matters because it shapes how we think about drug-induced autoimmunity more broadly. Think about statins and HMGCR antibodies. We now know that statins can unmask an autoimmune myopathy in genetically susceptible individuals, and that disease can persist even after the drug is stopped. The drug isn't directly toxic to muscle—it's flipping a switch in someone whose immune system was already primed.

GLP-1 receptor agonists might be similar. We already know these medications cause skeletal muscle mass loss. There's one published case of rhabdomyolysis with tirzepatide that looked like direct toxicity: CK over 57,000, rapid improvement with drug withdrawal and hydration, no immunosuppression needed. That's a different beast entirely from what happened here. This patient's progressive weakness over months, dramatic response to immunosuppression, and sustained remission after a short treatment course all point toward an immune-mediated process—not direct drug toxicity.

## When Rechallenge Is Unethical: The Pharmacovigilance Dilemma

In classical drug adverse effect assessment, rechallenge is the gold standard. Did the rash come back when you restarted the antibiotic? Did the liver enzymes jump again with the second exposure? That's how we move from "Probable" to "Certain."

But with autoimmune disease, rechallenge is often impossible to justify ethically. The consequences are too severe, the recovery too uncertain. This patient spent nearly four weeks in the hospital, required IVIG, and had cardiac involvement on imaging that raised concerns for myocarditis. Exposing her to semaglutide again would be reckless.

This means we rely on other evidence: the temporal relationship, the exclusion of alternative explanations, the response to drug withdrawal, and—critically—whether similar cases start appearing as drug use expands.

## Clinical Pearls for the Frontlines

What should emergency physicians and hospitalists take from this case?

First, GLP-1 receptor agonists are everywhere now. Semaglutide, tirzepatide, liraglutide—these medications have transformed diabetes and obesity management, and their use is exploding. We need to keep autoimmune complications on our radar, even though they appear rare.

Second, distinguish between drug-induced rhabdomyolysis and drug-induced autoimmune myopathy. Rhabdomyolysis is acute, dramatic, and improves rapidly with withdrawal and supportive care. Autoimmune myopathy progresses over weeks to months, requires immunosuppression, and may leave patients with lingering weakness if not recognized and treated promptly.

Third, seronegativity doesn't rule out IMNM. About one-third of patients with immune-mediated necrotising myopathy are seronegative for HMGCR and SRP antibodies. This patient's entire myositis panel was negative, yet her biopsy told the story.

Fourth, report your cases. The authors used established causality frameworks and published this case specifically to raise awareness and contribute to pharmacovigilance. If you encounter something similar, document it thoroughly and report it through your institution's adverse event reporting system and to regulatory authorities.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- A single dose of semaglutide may have triggered immune-mediated necrotising myopathy in this case, assessed as "Probable" using WHO-UMC causality criteria
- Drug-induced autoimmune disease differs from direct drug toxicity: it progresses over time, requires immunosuppression, and may improve with drug withdrawal alone in some cases
- Rechallenge is often unethical in suspected drug-induced autoimmune conditions, making causality assessment inherently limited
- Seronegative IMNM is common—approximately one-third of cases lack identifiable autoantibodies
- As GLP-1 receptor agonist use expands, clinicians should maintain awareness of potential autoimmune complications and report suspected cases through established pharmacovigilance channels

## The Bottom Line

This case is a reminder that new medications can have unexpected consequences, and those consequences don't always fit neatly into our existing diagnostic categories. The patient's remarkable response to immunosuppression and withdrawal from GLP-1 receptor agonists strongly suggests semaglutide played a role—but we can't prove it definitively.

What we can do is stay vigilant, think systematically about causality, and contribute to the collective knowledge that makes medicine safer for everyone.
