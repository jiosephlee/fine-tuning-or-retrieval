Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# The Antibody-Negative Necrotising Myopathy: Diagnostic Pitfalls and Lessons from Statin-Associated IMNM

## When the Panel Comes Back Negative, Don't Stop Working

Here's a scenario that should give any clinician pause: a patient presents with progressive proximal muscle weakness, dysphagia, and a CK climbing into the thousands. You order a myositis panel, and it comes back completely negative. What now?

A fascinating case report recently published describes what appears to be the first documented case of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) associated with GLP-1 receptor agonist use. What makes this case particularly instructive isn't just the potential drug association, but that it perfectly illustrates the diagnostic challenge of seronegative IMNM. This patient was negative for anti-HMGCR, anti-SRP, and every other autoantibody on an extensive myositis panel. Yet her muscle biopsy told a completely different story, and more importantly, she responded beautifully to immunosuppression.

The case is a timely reminder that approximately one-third of patients with IMNM are seronegative. That's a substantial chunk of patients who would be misdiagnosed if we rely solely on antibody testing.

## The Statin Parallel: A Cautionary Tale

Before anti-HMGCR antibodies were discovered in 2010, patients who developed myopathy on statins were often labeled as having "toxic myopathy" or "polymyositis." The prevailing logic was straightforward: drug exposure plus muscle injury equals direct toxicity. Stop the drug, problem solved.

But clinicians noticed something peculiar. Many patients continued to deteriorate even after statin cessation. Their CK levels remained elevated, their weakness progressed, and they didn't get better with supportive care alone. What eventually became clear was that these patients had an autoimmune process masquerading as drug toxicity. The statin had triggered an immune-mediated attack that became self-sustaining. The discovery of anti-HMGCR antibodies fundamentally changed how we classify and treat these patients.

This historical lesson is worth keeping in mind as we encounter new potential drug triggers. The case report describes a patient who received a single dose of semaglutide and, within hours, developed severe gastrointestinal symptoms and metabolic derangement requiring ICU care. Her initial CK was only modestly elevated at 337 U/L. But over the subsequent four months, while completely off the drug, her CK climbed to nearly 4000 U/L. She developed profound proximal weakness, dysphagia, and dysphonia. This temporal pattern, worsening after drug discontinuation, argues strongly against direct toxicity and points toward an immune-mediated process.

## Making the Diagnosis When Antibodies Are Absent

So how do you diagnose IMNM in a seronegative patient? The answer, quite simply, is muscle biopsy. But not just any biopsy interpretation. The histological features that define IMNM are distinct from other inflammatory myopathies.

In IMNM, you expect to see necrotic muscle fibers with minimal lymphocytic infiltration. This is notably different from polymyositis, which features prominent endomysial inflammatory infiltrates, or dermatomyositis, with its characteristic perifascicular atrophy and perivascular inflammation. In this patient's biopsy, pathologists identified dispersed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers, myophagocytosis with CD68-positive macrophages, and membrane attack complex deposition on both necrotic and non-necrotic fibers. The paucity of CD3-positive T-cells was classic for IMNM.

The membrane attack complex staining is particularly valuable. Its presence on non-necrotic fibers suggests ongoing complement-mediated injury, supporting an immune-mediated pathogenesis rather than simple drug toxicity or physical trauma.

## Distinguishing Drug-Induced from Idiopathic IMNM

Here's where clinical pattern recognition becomes crucial. Drug-induced IMNM and idiopathic IMNM may look identical histologically, but their clinical trajectories diverge in telling ways.

Direct toxic myopathies, like classic drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, follow a predictable course. CK rises sharply, symptoms correlate with drug exposure, and everything normalizes within days to weeks after drug discontinuation. The previously reported case of tirzepatide-associated rhabdomyolysis followed exactly this pattern, with a CK of 57,014 that rapidly normalized with hydration alone.

In contrast, drug-triggered autoimmune myopathy behaves differently. The patient in this case report showed continued deterioration for months after her single semaglutide dose. Her CK increased 12-fold while completely off the offending agent. And critically, she improved only after receiving immunosuppression.

Another telling feature is the treatment response. Idiopathic IMNM typically requires prolonged, often intensive immunosuppression. Patients may need extended courses of steroids, IVIG, and steroid-sparing agents. This patient, however, was weaned off prednisone within two months and received only a single course of IVIG. She remained in remission at 10-month follow-up, off all immunosuppression. This abbreviated treatment course aligns more closely with drug-induced autoimmune myopathy, where removing the trigger plus short-term immunosuppression can induce durable remission.

## When to Pull the Trigger on Biopsy

The practical question for emergency physicians and hospitalists is when to escalate care and pursue muscle biopsy. Several red flags should increase your suspicion for IMNM and prompt rheumatology or neurology consultation.

First, the weakness pattern matters. IMNM typically presents with symmetric proximal weakness. Patients struggle to rise from chairs, climb stairs, or lift objects overhead. Dysphagia and dysphonia, as seen in this patient, indicate more severe disease and carry prognostic significance.

Second, the CK magnitude. While there's no absolute threshold, CK elevations into the thousands, particularly when progressive, should raise concern. This patient's CK peaked at nearly 4000 U/L, well beyond what you'd expect from simple disuse or mild myopathy.

Third, and perhaps most importantly, the temporal pattern. Progressive weakness and rising CK despite removal of potential triggers should immediately prompt consideration of immune-mediated disease. The patient who gets worse after you stop the suspected offending agent needs further evaluation, not reassurance that time will heal.

Fourth, extramuscular manifestations. While IMNM primarily affects skeletal muscle, cardiac involvement can occur. This patient had an elevated troponin and proBNP, with cardiac MRI showing myocardial edema potentially consistent with myocarditis. Whether this represented true cardiac involvement or stress-induced cardiomyopathy remains unclear, but the possibility of cardiac IMNM warrants consideration and appropriate workup.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- Approximately one-third of IMNM patients are seronegative for anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP antibodies. A negative myositis panel does not exclude the diagnosis.
- Muscle biopsy remains the gold standard for diagnosing seronegative IMNM. Key histological features include necrotic fibers with minimal lymphocytic infiltrates, macrophage-predominant inflammation, and membrane attack complex deposition.
- The clinical course distinguishes drug-induced autoimmune myopathy from direct toxicity. Worsening after drug discontinuation suggests immune-mediated disease; rapid improvement suggests toxicity.
- Drug-induced IMNM may respond to shorter immunosuppression courses after drug withdrawal, unlike idiopathic IMNM, which often requires prolonged treatment.
- As GLP-1 receptor agonists become ubiquitous, clinicians should remain vigilant for unusual adverse effects and report potential associations to enhance pharmacovigilance.

## The Bottom Line

The history of statin-associated IMNM teaches us that drug triggers of autoimmune disease can fly under the radar for years before the underlying mechanism is elucidated. This case raises the question of whether GLP-1 receptor agonists might join the list of potential IMNM triggers. The causality assessment using WHO-UMC criteria classified this as "probable," but more reports will be needed to establish whether this association is causal or coincidental.

Regardless, the clinical lessons transcend this specific drug association. When a patient presents with progressive myopathy and a negative antibody panel, don't stop your workup. Muscle biopsy can reveal an immune-mediated process that demands immunosuppression, not just drug cessation. And sometimes, the most important diagnostic tool is simply recognizing that the clinical picture doesn't fit a simple toxic explanation.

The patient in this case went from fully independent to unable to stand or sit upright without assistance. With appropriate treatment, she returned to baseline and remained in remission. That's a outcome worth fighting for, but it requires clinicians to push beyond the easy answer of "negative panel, probably nothing."
