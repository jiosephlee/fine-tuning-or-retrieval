Title: Blog about the case report: "Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?"

# From Rhabdo to Rheumatology: Differentiating Toxic Myopathy from Immune-Mediated Necrotising Myopathy

## The Case That Stops You in Your Tracks

A woman in her 60s receives her first — and only — dose of semaglutide. Within hours, she's vomiting. Within days, she's hospitalized with acute renal failure and metabolic acidosis. But here's where it gets weird: four months later, she can barely stand, can barely swallow, and her CK has climbed to nearly 4000 U/L. She's been off the drug the entire time.

This case, published by Lam and colleagues, is the first reported case of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) probably associated with GLP-1 receptor agonist use. It also serves as a masterclass in distinguishing drug-induced rhabdomyolysis from immune-mediated myopathy, a distinction that should be on every clinician's radar as GLP-1 agonists become ubiquitous.

## Two Patients, Two Pathologies, One Drug Class

Here's what makes this case so instructive: there was already a case report of rhabdomyolysis associated with tirzepatide (another GLP-1 receptor agonist). That patient presented with a CK of 57,014 U/L — a truly massive elevation. The CK normalized within days of drug discontinuation and hydration. Standard drug-induced rhabdo. No immunosuppression needed.

Now compare that to our semaglutide patient. Her CK was 337 U/L on initial hospitalization. Four months later — while off the drug entirely — it had climbed to 3992 U/L. Her weakness was progressive. She developed dysphagia and dysphonia. She couldn't stand without assistance. And critically, her CK trajectory continued upward long after the drug was gone.

The CK trajectory tells the story. In toxic myopathy, CK elevates acutely and normalizes within days to weeks after drug withdrawal. In IMNM, the immune cascade has been triggered — stopping the drug doesn't stop the disease.

## The Statin Parallel: A Lesson from History

This pattern should sound familiar to anyone who watched the evolution of statin-associated myopathy. Initially, patients on statins who developed myopathy were classified as having "toxic myopathy" or "polymyositis." But then clinicians noticed something: many patients had persistent myopathy and CK elevation despite statin cessation. These patients improved with immunosuppression. That observation led to the discovery of anti-HMGCR autoantibodies and the recognition of statin-associated IMNM as a distinct entity.

The parallel is striking. In this case, the patient had no exposure to statins, red yeast rice, or other known IMNM triggers. She was negative for anti-HMGCR and anti-SRP antibodies — the two classic IMNM antibodies. But remember: approximately one-third of IMNM patients are seronegative. The absence of a known autoantibody doesn't rule out the diagnosis. The muscle biopsy showed necrotic and regenerating muscle fibres with macrophage-mediated myophagocytosis and membrane attack complex deposition — the histologic hallmarks of IMNM.

## The Clinical Features That Should Make You Think IMNM

Progressive proximal muscle weakness is the hallmark. This patient couldn't stand or sit upright without assistance. Dysphagia and dysphonia developed — reflecting involvement of bulbar musculature. The weakness was aggressively progressive over months.

The timeline matters enormously. The 12-fold rise in CK over four months, occurring after just a single dose of semaglutide and continuing despite its discontinuation, suggests an immune-mediated process that was triggered by the drug but became self-sustaining. This is fundamentally different from direct drug toxicity, where the insult ends when the drug is cleared.

Her cardiac findings deserve mention: elevated troponin, markedly elevated proBNP, and cardiac MRI showing myocardial edema with mild hypokinesis. Whether this represents subclinical myocarditis associated with the same immune process remains unclear, but it normalized on follow-up imaging after immunosuppression.

## Why This Distinction Changes Management

If you misclassify IMNM as simple rhabdomyolysis, you treat with hydration and supportive care. The patient doesn't improve. The immune attack continues. Permanent muscle damage accumulates.

This patient received pulse methylprednisolone (1000 mg daily for 5 days), followed by oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg daily, plus IVIG at 2 g/kg. Her CK normalized. She regained her baseline function. She was weaned off immunosuppression entirely and remained in remission at 10-month follow-up.

Notably, drug-induced IMNM often requires less prolonged immunosuppression than idiopathic IMNM, which frequently requires intensive, ongoing therapy. The key is recognition, treatment, and avoidance of re-exposure.

## Key Take-Home Points

- **CK trajectory is diagnostic**: In toxic rhabdomyolysis, CK rises acutely and normalizes within days to weeks of drug withdrawal. In IMNM, CK continues to rise or remains elevated despite drug cessation.

- **Timeline matters**: Progressive weakness and CK elevation over months, as seen in this case, is incompatible with simple drug toxicity and should prompt evaluation for immune-mediated disease.

- **Seronegativity doesn't rule out IMNM**: One-third of IMNM patients lack detectable autoantibodies. Muscle biopsy showing necrosis with minimal inflammatory infiltrate, macrophage-mediated myophagocytosis, and membrane attack complex deposition supports the diagnosis.

- **GLP-1 agonists may trigger IMNM**: This is the first reported case, but given the explosive use of these medications, pharmacovigilance is essential. Consider IMNM in patients on GLP-1 agonists who develop progressive myopathy that doesn't resolve with drug discontinuation.

- **Treatment differs fundamentally**: Rhabdomyolysis requires supportive care and drug withdrawal. IMNM requires immunosuppression — typically corticosteroids and IVIG, with steroid-sparing agents for refractory disease.

- **The statin analogy is instructive**: The recognition of drug-induced IMNM often begins with case reports. Staying alert to atypical patterns of drug-associated myopathy can identify new triggers and improve patient outcomes.

As GLP-1 agonists continue their meteoric rise, clinicians need to expand their differential for muscle-related complaints beyond simple rhabdomyolysis. When the CK doesn't come down and the weakness keeps progressing after drug cessation, think immune-mediated — and call rheumatology.
