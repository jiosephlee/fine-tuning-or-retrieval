Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# Variants of Uncertain Significance: When Clinical Correlation Trumps Genetic Classification

A woman in her late 20s walks into your clinic with five years of progressive fatigue, pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying, osteoporosis, and neuropathy. You run a clinical exome panel and get back a result that would make most clinicians shrug: a Variant of Uncertain Significance. But here's the thing—in this case, that VUS was the key to unlocking a unifying diagnosis that explained every bizarre manifestation in a patient decades too young for half her problems.

This case is a masterclass in why VUS doesn't mean "ignore this result," and why clinical correlation remains the most powerful tool in genomic medicine.

## The Case That Didn't Fit

The patient presented with what initially looked like severe nutritional deficiency—cobalamin and folate depletion, megaloblastic features, the works. But even after aggressive repletion, she didn't get better in any meaningful way. She had cirrhosis with no viral, autoimmune, or alcoholic etiology. She had pulmonary fibrosis in her twenties. She had premature greying, osteoporosis with fractures, and knuckle hyperpigmentation that looked almost like B12 deficiency but didn't quite fit.

The constellation was weird enough that the treating team stepped back and asked: is there one diagnosis that explains pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, and premature aging in a young adult? That question led them to telomere biology disorders, and specifically to PARN.

## When VUS Becomes Actionable

Here's where it gets interesting. The genetic testing came back with a monoallelic PARN missense variant: c.613T>C, p.Cys205Arg. Official classification? Variant of Uncertain Significance. In many clinical contexts, that's where the story ends—VUS gets filed away, and the workup continues down a different path.

But this team recognized something crucial: the variant sat in the R3H domain, a highly conserved functional region where benign variation is essentially unheard of. Four of five in silico prediction tools called it damaging. The variant was absent from population databases, consistent with a rare disease allele. And critically, other PARN missense variants at different positions have been classified as likely pathogenic in ClinVar.

The ACMG criteria tell us that PM1 (located in a critical functional domain), PM2 (absent from population databases), PP3 (computational evidence of deleterious effect), and PP2 (missense variants are a common disease mechanism in this gene) all point toward pathogenicity. That's not enough to upgrade from VUS to likely pathogenic on genetics alone—but paired with a phenotype that matches PARN-associated disease perfectly, it becomes clinically actionable.

## The Phenotype-Gene Match That Matters

PARN mutations occupy a fascinating niche in telomere biology disorders. Biallelic variants cause severe early-onset presentations like Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome, while monoallelic variants typically present in adulthood with pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure. What made this case unusual was the hepatic involvement—cirrhosis is more classically associated with TERT and TERC mutations, though emerging data suggests PARN's downstream effects on telomerase may produce similar hepatic phenotypes.

The clinical picture in this patient—premature greying, cytopenia, pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, osteoporosis—maps precisely onto what we now understand about telomere biology disorders. These conditions hit rapidly dividing tissues hardest: bone marrow, liver, lung, hair follicles. The classical mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita (oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, reticular pigmentation) was absent, but that's increasingly recognized as the tip of the clinical iceberg.

## Practical Approach to VUS Interpretation

So how do you avoid both over-interpreting uncertain findings and falling into therapeutic nihilism when a VUS might actually matter?

First, look at the gene-disease relationship. PARN has strong biological plausibility for telomere disorders—mechanistic studies show it's required for proper TERC maturation. It's among the 20% of genes least tolerant to rare disruptive variants, meaning missense changes are more likely to be pathogenic than in your average gene.

Second, evaluate the variant location and conservation. A variant affecting a highly conserved residue in a critical functional domain carries more weight than one in a poorly conserved region with no known function. The p.Cys205Arg variant sits in the R3H domain where no benign variants have been documented.

Third, assess phenotypic concordance. This is where clinical judgment matters most. A VUS in PARN in a patient with isolated anemia might warrant a cautious approach, but the same VUS in a patient with multi-organ fibrosis, premature aging features, and cytopenia should prompt serious consideration of a telomere biology disorder. The phenotype here was textbook for TBD, even though the patient lacked the classical features that might have made the diagnosis obvious earlier.

Fourth, partner with clinical geneticists who can navigate the ACMG classification framework and help contextualize uncertain findings. The classification system is designed to be conservative, and appropriately so—but it doesn't capture the full picture of clinical likelihood.

## What Changed Management

Making the TBD diagnosis fundamentally altered this patient's care trajectory. It meant proper genetic counseling for her family. It meant ongoing surveillance for malignant transformation, progressive marrow failure, and organ fibrosis. It meant that if she deteriorates hematologically, she could be considered for hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. It meant understanding that androgen therapies like danazol have some evidence for telomere elongation, though long-term data remains limited.

Perhaps most importantly, it provided an answer. After years of symptoms and investigations, the patient and her family understood that her constellation of problems wasn't bad luck or poor compliance—it was a recognized genetic syndrome with predictable complications and established management pathways.

## The Bottom Line

Variants of uncertain significance are uncertain because we lack sufficient genetic evidence to classify them definitively—not because they're clinically irrelevant. When the phenotype matches the gene's disease association, when the variant location and conservation suggest functional impact, and when alternative diagnoses have been exhaustively excluded, a VUS can and should inform clinical decision-making.

The key take-home points for clinicians facing uncertain genetic findings:

- VUS classification reflects insufficient genetic evidence, not necessarily clinical insignificance
- Evaluate the variant's location, conservation, and in silico predictions within the context of your patient's phenotype
- PARN monoallelic variants can present in adulthood with pulmonary fibrosis, bone marrow involvement, and increasingly recognized hepatic fibrosis
- Telomere biology disorders should be on your differential for young adults with unexplained multi-organ fibrosis, premature aging features, and cytopenias—even without the classical mucocutaneous triad
- Partner with clinical geneticists to interpret uncertain findings, but remember that clinical correlation remains essential to making these results actionable

This case reminds us that genetic testing is a tool that serves clinical judgment, not the other way around. When the clinical picture strongly suggests a diagnosis and the genetic result is plausible but not definitive, the phenotype should drive management while maintaining appropriate uncertainty about the variant's precise classification.
