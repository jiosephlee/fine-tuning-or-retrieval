Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# Dyskeratosis Congenita Without the Triad: Adult Presentations of Telomere Biology Disorders

## The Case That Stopped Me in My Tracks

A woman in her late 20s walks in with five years of progressive fatigue. She's got pancytopenia, liver cirrhosis, pulmonary fibrosis, osteoporosis, premature greying, and a polyneuropathy. You correct her severe B12 deficiency, but the clinical picture doesn't resolve. The constellation is bizarre, the patient is too young, and nothing ties it together until genetic testing reveals a monoallelic PARN mutation. This is a telomere biology disorder (TBD) flying completely under the radar, without a single classic mucocutaneous feature of dyskeratosis congenita (DC).

## When the Textbook Description Fails You

We're taught to recognize dyskeratosis congenita by its mucocutaneous triad: oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation. But here's the problem. That classic presentation represents the pediatric severe end of the spectrum. Adult presentations of TBDs are far more insidious and frequently miss the triad entirely. This patient had none of those hallmark features. What she had instead was a collection of "idiopathic" organ failures that clinicians often evaluate in isolation: pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic cirrhosis, and bone marrow dysfunction.

The premature greying and osteoporosis in a young adult were subtle clues that something was accelerating her cellular aging. These features are easy to dismiss or attribute to other causes, but in context, they pointed toward a fundamental problem with telomere maintenance.

## Why Telomere Biology Disorders Matter

Telomeres cap our chromosomes and shorten with each cell division. When telomerase, the enzyme complex that maintains telomere length, malfunctions due to genetic mutations, cells hit senescence prematurely. The organs that take the hit are those with high cellular turnover: bone marrow, liver, lung epithelium, skin, and hair follicles.

This patient's PARN mutation is particularly instructive. PARN encodes an exoribonuclease involved in processing TERC, the RNA component of telomerase. Monoallelic PARN variants typically present as pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure in adults, while biallelic variants cause more severe childhood-onset dyskeratosis congenita. The fact that she had both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis with a monoallelic variant expands what we know about the phenotypic spectrum.

## The Diagnostic Challenge

Here's what makes these cases so tricky. The patient initially presented with severe B12 deficiency, which explained some of her cytopenias and neurologic symptoms. But even after aggressive repletion, her clinical picture remained disproportionate. Her liver disease had no viral, autoimmune, or metabolic explanation. Her pulmonary fibrosis appeared "idiopathic." The positive direct antiglobulin test was a red herring that briefly suggested immune-mediated disease.

This is exactly how TBDs get missed. They masquerade as common acquired conditions, and when organ systems are evaluated in silos, the pattern recognition fails. It took seeing the whole picture, the multi-organ fibrosis, the premature aging stigmata, and the refractory cytopenias in a young patient, to trigger genetic testing.

## What Should Trigger Suspicion?

Consider a TBD in any young adult (under 50 or 60) presenting with what appears to be idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, cirrhosis without clear etiology, or bone marrow failure. The diagnosis becomes more compelling when you see combinations: pulmonary fibrosis plus liver disease, or cytopenias alongside organ fibrosis. Premature greying, early osteoporosis, and unexplained cytopenias in the same patient should make your ears perk up.

Family history may be absent because these can be de novo mutations, and penetrance is variable. This patient had no reported family history, which initially steered clinicians away from hereditary conditions.

## Making the Diagnosis

The gold standard combines genetic testing through next-generation sequencing with telomere length measurement via flow-FISH. Clinical exome sequencing identified the PARN variant in this case, classified initially as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. But the clinical-genetic correlation, her phenotype matched the known disease spectrum, allowed the team to make the diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder.

Notably, telomere length testing wasn't performed here, and the variant's pathogenicity remains formally uncertain. This highlights a real-world challenge: sometimes the clinical picture is compelling enough to proceed even when genetic results don't give you a slam-dunk answer.

## Management and Implications

There's no cure for TBDs. Management is supportive and organ-specific: correcting nutritional deficiencies, surveillance for progression, and considering hematopoietic stem cell transplantation for severe bone marrow involvement. Androgens like danazol have shown some promise in telomere elongation, though long-term data is limited.

What matters most is recognizing these disorders early. It changes the conversation with patients and families regarding prognosis, genetic counseling, and the potential for future organ involvement. It also affects decisions about transplantation; patients with TBDs have higher transplant-related mortality and require modified conditioning regimens.

## Key Take-Home Points

- The classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita is often absent in adult presentations of telomere biology disorders.
- Consider TBD in any young adult with unexplained pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic cirrhosis, or bone marrow failure, especially when multiple organs are involved.
- Premature greying, early-onset osteoporosis, and cytopenias in a young patient are subtle clues to accelerated cellular aging.
- PARN mutations have a distinct phenotype: monoallelic variants typically cause pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure, while biallelic variants cause more severe childhood-onset disease.
- Family history may be absent due to de novo mutations and variable penetrance.
- Genetic testing plus telomere length measurement (flow-FISH) is the diagnostic standard, but clinical correlation may be necessary when variants are of uncertain significance.
- Early recognition enables appropriate genetic counseling, surveillance, and informed decisions about transplantation.

## The Bottom Line

Telomere biology disorders represent a hidden iceberg in adult medicine. What we see in clinical practice are the tips: the young patient with "idiopathic" pulmonary fibrosis, the thirty-year-old with cryptogenic cirrhosis, the young adult with unexplained pancytopenia. Beneath the surface lies a unifying genetic explanation that connects these seemingly disparate presentations. The next time you're scratching your head over multi-organ dysfunction in a young patient who looks older than their years, think about their telomeres.
