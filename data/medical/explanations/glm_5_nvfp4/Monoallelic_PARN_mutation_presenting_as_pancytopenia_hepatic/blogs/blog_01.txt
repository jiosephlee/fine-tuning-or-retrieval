Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# When B12 Deficiency Doesn't Tell the Whole Story: Recognizing Diagnostic Tension in Multisystem Disease

A woman in her late 20s walks into your clinic with five years of progressive fatigue. You work her up and find severe vitamin B12 deficiency. Classic case, right? You replete her, send her home, and pat yourself on the back for a job well done. But here's the problem—her liver shows cirrhosis on elastography, her lungs have fibrotic changes, her hair went grey prematurely, and she's got osteoporosis with a history of fractures. Suddenly, that B12 deficiency feels like it's only telling you part of the story. That uncomfortable feeling? That's diagnostic tension, and learning to recognize it might save you from missing something critical.

## The Case: When the Pieces Don't Quite Fit

This patient presented to a tertiary care center with a five-year history of progressive generalized weakness and early fatigability that had worsened to the point of requiring assistance for self-care. She also reported exertional dyspnea, intermittent chest discomfort, weight loss, and anorexia. On exam, she had pallor, icterus, premature greying of hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation, and a bald tongue with atrophic lingual papillae—all findings that would make any clinician think nutritional deficiency. She also had thoracic scoliosis, bilateral thenar atrophy, hepatomegaly, and reduced breath sounds bilaterally in the infra-scapular regions.

Her labs showed pancytopenia with a low reticulocyte count, dimorphic anemia, and a peripheral smear with basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils. Severe B12 and folate deficiency were confirmed, and she had elevated iron stores consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis. Bone marrow examination showed trilineage hematopoiesis without malignancy or dysplasia. So far, so good—this all fits together nicely with severe nutritional deficiency causing pancytopenia.

But then things got weird. Her liver elastography showed cirrhosis with a stiffness of 18.1 kPa. Her CT showed diffuse ground-glass haziness with fibrotic changes in both lungs. She had osteoporosis confirmed on DEXA, secondary hyperparathyroidism, and electromyography revealed asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy. She was in her late twenties, and her liver, lungs, bones, bone marrow, and nerves were all showing signs of dysfunction that you'd more typically see in someone decades older.

## Diagnostic Tension: The Red Flag You Can't Ignore

Here's where clinical reasoning really matters. The B12 deficiency was real, and it explained a lot—her pancytopenia, the megaloblastic changes on smear, even some of her neuropathy. But it didn't explain the hepatic fibrosis. It didn't explain the pulmonary fibrosis. It didn't explain the premature greying or the osteoporosis or the cirrhosis. Even after correcting her nutritional deficiencies, something was still wrong.

This is what we call diagnostic tension—that nagging sense that your working diagnosis, while accurate as far as it goes, doesn't fully account for the clinical picture. It's the mental discomfort you feel when you've checked the boxes but something still feels off. In academic terms, it's the mismatch between your proposed diagnosis and the totality of clinical findings. The temptation is to attribute everything to the diagnosis you've already made, or to chalk the rest up to coincidental comorbidities. But when a young patient presents with multi-organ dysfunction that looks like accelerated aging, that's exactly when you need to step back and look for a unifying explanation.

## The Diagnosis: A Telomere Biology Disorder

The clinical team suspected a genetic disorder and sent clinical exome sequencing. The result came back with a monoallelic missense variant in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg). This variant affects a highly conserved residue in the R3H domain and is predicted to be damaging by multiple in silico tools. While classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance, the clinical-genetic correlation supported a diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder.

Telomere biology disorders are rare premature-aging syndromes caused by defects in telomere-related genes. Telomeres cap chromosome ends, and with each cell division, they shorten until reaching a critical length that triggers senescence. In TBDs, this process is accelerated. Actively dividing tissues—bone marrow, skin, mucosa, hair, hepatocytes—are preferentially affected. The prototype is dyskeratosis congenita, classically defined by the mucocutaneous triad of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation. But here's the key point for emergency physicians and hospitalists: TBDs can present in adulthood without the classic triad, manifesting instead as apparently isolated bone marrow failure, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, or cryptogenic cirrhosis.

PARN variants demonstrate a distinct phenotype. Biallelic variants cause autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita, while monoallelic variants more often underlie telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure. This patient had a monoallelic variant, yet she developed both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis along with cytopenias—an expansion of what we traditionally associate with PARN mutations. This case adds to growing evidence that hepatic fibrosis may be more prevalent in PARN-associated TBD than previously recognized.

## Why This Matters for Your Practice

The temptation in emergency medicine and acute care is to focus on the immediate problem—correct the B12, transfuse if needed, and move on. And for the acute presentation, that's entirely appropriate. But we also need to recognize when we're looking at something that requires further investigation. This patient's constellation of findings—cytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying, and osteoporosis in a young adult—represented a recognizable pattern once you stepped back and looked at the whole picture.

The B12 deficiency in this case may have been a red herring of sorts, or more accurately, an epiphenomenon that brought her to medical attention. Interestingly, the authors note that telomere shortening may have rendered her bone marrow more susceptible to hematopoietic stress from the B12 deficiency. The cytopenia likely had multiple contributors, but the underlying genetic vulnerability meant she needed ongoing surveillance for evolving marrow failure.

## Clinical Pearls for Recognizing Telomere Biology Disorders

First, think TBD when you see unexplained multi-organ fibrosis in young patients. Pulmonary fibrosis and hepatic fibrosis together in someone under 50 should make you pause, especially if there's concurrent cytopenia or bone marrow involvement. Second, premature greying—defined as onset before age 20 in Caucasians or before 30 in African Americans—can be a clue, particularly when paired with other findings suggesting accelerated aging. Third, the absence of the classic mucocutaneous triad doesn't rule out TBD. Adult presentations often lack the textbook features we learn for dyskeratosis congenita. Fourth, TBDs can mimic acquired conditions. Patients may carry diagnoses of aplastic anemia, myelodysplastic syndrome, or cryptogenic cirrhosis for years before the underlying genetic cause is identified. Finally, diagnosis requires genetic testing through next-generation sequencing; telomere length measurement via flow-FISH can also support the diagnosis.

## Take-Home Points

- **Respect diagnostic tension**—if your diagnosis doesn't explain all the findings, don't dismiss the outliers; investigate them.
- **Think premature aging syndromes** when young patients present with multi-organ fibrosis, cytopenias, and features like premature greying or early-onset osteoporosis.
- **TBDs can present without the classic mucocutaneous triad**—adult presentations may show only marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis, or hepatic fibrosis.
- **PARN mutations expand the phenotype**—monoallelic variants, traditionally associated with pulmonary fibrosis, can also cause hepatic involvement and cytopenia.
- **Early recognition matters**—diagnosis guides genetic counseling, surveillance strategies, and consideration of bone marrow transplantation in progressive cases.

This case reminds us that rare diseases don't announce themselves with flashing neon signs. They present with subtle patterns that require us to step back, synthesize the full clinical picture, and resist premature closure on diagnoses that feel satisfying but incomplete. When the pieces don't fit together, that's not a problem to ignore—that's exactly where the real diagnosis might be hiding.
