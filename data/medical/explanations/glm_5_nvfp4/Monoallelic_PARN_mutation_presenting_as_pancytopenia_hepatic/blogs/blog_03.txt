Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# PARN Mutations: Expanding the Phenotype Beyond Pulmonary Fibrosis

What happens when a young adult walks in with what looks like severe B12 deficiency, but the clinical picture just doesn't quite add up? You correct the deficiency, and still—the pancytopenia lingers, the organ fibrosis progresses, and the premature graying stares back at you. This case stopped us in our tracks because it reminds us that telomere biology disorders (TBDs) don't always announce themselves with the textbook mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita.

## The Case That Made Us Look Twice

A woman in her late 20s presented with five years of progressive fatigue and weakness that had recently deteriorated to the point of needing assistance with self-care. On exam, she had pallor, icterus, premature graying of hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation, and a bald tongue—the kind of findings that make you think nutritional deficiency right away. But she also had hepatosplenomegaly, thoracic scoliosis, bilateral thenar atrophy, and reduced breath sounds bilaterally.

Her labs showed pancytopenia with a dimorphic picture and severe B12 deficiency. Bone marrow was hyperplastic but not dysplastic or malignant. She got B12 repletion, and some things improved. But here's the catch—she also had hepatic fibrosis (liver stiffness 18.1 kPa on elastography), pulmonary fibrosis on imaging, osteoporosis with recurrent fractures, and a sensorimotor polyneuropathy. The nutritional deficiency alone couldn't explain this constellation.

Clinical exome sequencing hit pay dirt: a monoallelic PARN missense variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg). This variant affects a highly conserved residue in the R3H domain and is predicted damaging by multiple in silico tools. While classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance, the clinical-genetic correlation strongly supported a telomere biology disorder.

## PARN: More Than Just a Pulmonary Fibrosis Gene

Here's where the biology gets fascinating. PARN encodes a poly(A)-specific ribonuclease—an enzyme that trims the poly(A) tails off mRNAs to regulate their stability and degradation. But PARN has a critical side gig in telomere maintenance. It's required for proper 3'-end maturation of TERC (the RNA component of telomerase) by removing post-transcriptionally acquired oligo(A) tails that would otherwise target nuclear RNAs for degradation. Without functional PARN, telomerase can't do its job properly.

PARN is among the 20% of genes least tolerant to rare disruptive variants—this is a gene where haploinsufficiency matters. The inheritance patterns tell an interesting story. Biallelic variants cause autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita type 6 (DKCB6) and severe phenotypes like Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome. Monoallelic variants, on the other hand, are more commonly associated with telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4). The initial association was discovered through exome sequencing of IPF cohorts, and now over 65 cases have been documented in the literature.

## The Hepatic Connection: Underrecognized but Important

What makes this case stand out is the hepatic fibrosis. We typically think of TERT and TERC variants when we consider liver involvement in TBDs, but data from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry showed that 88.8% of adults with pathogenic TERC or TERT mutations had at least moderate hepatic fibrosis, and 33.3% had cirrhosis. Now we're seeing that PARN may belong on that list too.

The literature describes a comparable case of a patient with pancytopenia and hepatic fibrosis who carried monoallelic variants in both PARN and TERT. Our patient had only a single PARN variant yet developed both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. This expands what we thought we knew about PARN's phenotypic reach and suggests that hepatic surveillance should be part of the monitoring protocol for these patients, not just pulmonary and hematologic monitoring.

## When the Classical Features Are Missing

Here's the clinical pearl that bears repeating: dyskeratosis congenita is classically defined by the mucocutaneous triad of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation. But TBDs can present in adulthood without any of these findings. Our patient had none of the classical mucocutaneous features. What she did have was premature graying, organ fibrosis, cytopenia, and osteoporosis in her twenties—features of accelerated aging across multiple organ systems.

The absence of a family history doesn't rule out a hereditary syndrome either. De novo mutations happen, and variable penetrance means affected family members may have gone undiagnosed or died before diagnosis.

## Clinical Takeaways

- Consider telomere biology disorders in young adults with unexplained multisystem disease involving bone marrow, liver, and lungs—even when classical mucocutaneous features of dyskeratosis congenita are absent.

- PARN variants aren't just about pulmonary fibrosis. Hepatic fibrosis and hematologic vulnerability are part of the expanding phenotype, and monoallelic variants can present in adulthood with variable penetrance.

- Biallelic PARN variants cause severe childhood-onset phenotypes (DKCB6, Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson), while monoallelic variants more often present as adult-onset pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure.

- Hepatic involvement in TBDs is likely under-recognized. Transient elastography may be a useful screening tool in patients with known or suspected TBDs.

- Diagnosis requires genetic testing and ideally telomere length measurement by flow-FISH. Treatment is primarily supportive, with hematopoietic stem cell transplantation reserved for progressive marrow failure.

This case reminds us that rare genetic disorders don't always look rare when they walk through the door. Sometimes they look like B12 deficiency that doesn't fully respond, or cirrhosis without a clear cause, or IPF in a young woman. The key is recognizing when the pieces don't fit together—and then having the intellectual curiosity to start looking for a unifying genetic diagnosis.
