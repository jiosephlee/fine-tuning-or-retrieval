Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# The Liver in Telomere Biology Disorders: An Underrecognized Target Organ

Here's a case that stopped me in my tracks. A woman in her late 20s presents with five years of progressive fatigue, and by the time she reaches tertiary care, she's got pancytopenia, cirrhosis, pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying, osteoporosis, and neuropathy. You could be forgiven for initially chasing nutritional deficiencies—she had severe B12 and folate deficiency with classic megaloblastic changes on her peripheral smear. But here's the kicker: even after correcting those deficiencies, her multisystem dysfunction persisted, and the clinical picture didn't add up. What ultimately unified her constellation of findings was a monoallelic PARN mutation, revealing a telomere biology disorder (TBD) that had been flying under the radar for years.

## Pulmonary Fibrosis Gets All the Attention

When we think about TBDs presenting in adulthood, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is the poster child. Monoallelic variants in TERT, TERC, RTEL1, and PARN are established causes of familial IPF, and most pulmonologists are now savvy about sending genetic testing in younger patients with unexplained fibrotic lung disease. But here's what we're missing: the liver.

Hepatocytes are rapidly dividing cells, making them prime targets for telomere-mediated dysfunction. Yet hepatic fibrosis in TBDs has been dramatically underrecognized—often attributed to other causes or simply overlooked. The data emerging from telomere biology registries should change our approach.

## The Numbers Are Striking

A retrospective analysis from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry used transient elastography to systematically evaluate liver involvement in patients with pathogenic TERC or TERT mutations. The results: **88.8% of adult patients had at least moderate hepatic fibrosis, and 33.3% had cirrhosis**. That's one in three patients walking around with established cirrhosis, many of whom had never had their liver evaluated.

The patient in this case had transient elastography showing liver stiffness of 18.1 kPa—consistent with cirrhosis—along with hepatosplenomegaly and ascites on imaging. She also had ground-glass opacities and fibrotic changes in her lungs. But here's what's fascinating: she carried a monoallelic PARN variant, not TERC or TERT.

## PARN Expands the Hepatic Spectrum

PARN is a poly(A)-specific ribonuclease that plays a critical role in TERC maturation. Biallelic PARN mutations cause severe childhood-onset dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6) and Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome. Monoallelic variants are more classically associated with IPF and bone marrow failure—the acronym PFBMFT4 literally encodes this phenotype.

But this case adds to growing evidence that hepatic fibrosis belongs in the PARN spectrum too. The patient had cirrhosis without any alternative explanation—viral hepatitis, autoimmune disease, alcohol, and metabolic causes were all ruled out. A prior case report described a patient with pancytopenia and hepatic fibrosis carrying monoallelic variants in both PARN and TERT. This patient had only a single PARN variant and still developed both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis, suggesting that monoallelic PARN's downstream effects on telomerase function may mirror TERC/TERT mutations in their hepatic impact.

## Why the Liver Gets Missed

Part of the problem is that TBDs often present without the classical mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita—nail dystrophy, oral leukoplakia, and reticular skin hyperpigmentation. In adults, the presentation can be cryptic: isolated cytopenias, unexplained liver disease, or IPF. When a patient presents with cirrhosis, we run the standard workup. When that's negative, we might call it "cryptogenic" and move on.

Additionally, TBDs demonstrate variable penetrance and expressivity. A patient might have significant pulmonary involvement with minimal hematologic disease, or—critically—predominant hepatic disease. Without a systematic approach to screening, these organ systems get evaluated in silos.

## The Premature Ageing Clue

This patient had several red flags for a premature ageing syndrome: premature greying of hair, early-onset osteoporosis with recurrent fractures, and multisystem fibrosis. These features should prompt consideration of a telomere biology disorder, even in the absence of classic mucocutaneous findings. The neuropathy and musculoskeletal involvement (thoracic scoliosis, thenar atrophy) further supported a unifying genetic diagnosis.

Her severe B12 deficiency was a red herring—or perhaps more accurately, a second hit. The authors hypothesize that telomere shortening may have rendered her bone marrow more susceptible to hematopoietic stress, such as nutritional deficiency, and indeed she had a blunted reticulocyte response despite severe anemia.

## Clinical Implications

If you're evaluating a patient with unexplained IPF, bone marrow failure, or liver disease—especially in younger adults—think about TBDs. But more importantly, if you diagnose a TBD, don't stop at the presenting organ. Screen for involvement elsewhere.

Consider transient elastography in TBD patients even if liver enzymes are normal. Liver stiffness measurements are non-invasive and can detect fibrosis before synthetic dysfunction develops. Similarly, patients presenting with cryptogenic cirrhosis, especially with concurrent cytopenias or pulmonary fibrosis, warrant evaluation for telomere gene mutations.

## Key Take-Home Points

- **Hepatic fibrosis is common in TBDs**: Up to one-third of patients with TERC/TERT mutations may have cirrhosis; this case suggests PARN mutation carriers may carry similar risk.

- **Screen the liver in TBD patients**: Transient elastography should be considered even with normal liver enzymes—fibrosis can be silent until decompensation.

- **Consider TBDs in cryptogenic cirrhosis**: Especially in younger patients with concurrent pulmonary fibrosis, cytopenias, or premature ageing features.

- **Classical mucocutaneous features may be absent**: Adults with TBDs often lack the nail dystrophy, oral leukoplakia, and skin pigmentation seen in classic dyskeratosis congenita.

- **Premature ageing signs are clues**: Early greying, osteoporosis, and multi-organ fibrosis in a young adult should raise suspicion for accelerated cellular senescence.

The liver has been hiding in plain sight in telomere biology disorders. It's time we started looking for it.
