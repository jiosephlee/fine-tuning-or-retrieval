Title: Blog about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

# When B12 Deficiency Doesn't Tell the Whole Story: Recognizing Diagnostic Tension in Multisystem Disease

A woman in her late 20s walks into your clinic with five years of progressive fatigue. You work her up and find severe vitamin B12 deficiency. Classic case, right? You replete her, send her home, and pat yourself on the back for a job well done. But here's the problem—her liver shows cirrhosis on elastography, her lungs have fibrotic changes, her hair went grey prematurely, and she's got osteoporosis with a history of fractures. Suddenly, that B12 deficiency feels like it's only telling you part of the story. That uncomfortable feeling? That's diagnostic tension, and learning to recognize it might save you from missing something critical.

## The Case: When the Pieces Don't Quite Fit

This patient presented to a tertiary care center with a five-year history of progressive generalized weakness and early fatigability that had worsened to the point of requiring assistance for self-care. She also reported exertional dyspnea, intermittent chest discomfort, weight loss, and anorexia. On exam, she had pallor, icterus, premature greying of hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation, and a bald tongue with atrophic lingual papillae—all findings that would make any clinician think nutritional deficiency. She also had thoracic scoliosis, bilateral thenar atrophy, hepatomegaly, and reduced breath sounds bilaterally in the infra-scapular regions.

Her labs showed pancytopenia with a low reticulocyte count, dimorphic anemia, and a peripheral smear with basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils. Severe B12 and folate deficiency were confirmed, and she had elevated iron stores consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis. Bone marrow examination showed trilineage hematopoiesis without malignancy or dysplasia. So far, so good—this all fits together nicely with severe nutritional deficiency causing pancytopenia.

But then things got weird. Her liver elastography showed cirrhosis with a stiffness of 18.1 kPa. Her CT showed diffuse ground-glass haziness with fibrotic changes in both lungs. She had osteoporosis confirmed on DEXA, secondary hyperparathyroidism, and electromyography revealed asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy. She was in her late twenties, and her liver, lungs, bones, bone marrow, and nerves were all showing signs of dysfunction that you'd more typically see in someone decades older.

## Diagnostic Tension: The Red Flag You Can't Ignore

Here's where clinical reasoning really matters. The B12 deficiency was real, and it explained a lot—her pancytopenia, the megaloblastic changes on smear, even some of her neuropathy. But it didn't explain the hepatic fibrosis. It didn't explain the pulmonary fibrosis. It didn't explain the premature greying or the osteoporosis or the cirrhosis. Even after correcting her nutritional deficiencies, something was still wrong.

This is what we call diagnostic tension—that nagging sense that your working diagnosis, while accurate as far as it goes, doesn't fully account for the clinical picture. It's the mental discomfort you feel when you've checked the boxes but something still feels off. In academic terms, it's the mismatch between your proposed diagnosis and the totality of clinical findings. The temptation is to attribute everything to the diagnosis you've already made, or to chalk the rest up to coincidental comorbidities. But when a young patient presents with multi-organ dysfunction that looks like accelerated aging, that's exactly when you need to step back and look for a unifying explanation.

## The Diagnosis: A Telomere Biology Disorder

The clinical team suspected a genetic disorder and sent clinical exome sequencing. The result came back with a monoallelic missense variant in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg). This variant affects a highly conserved residue in the R3H domain and is predicted to be damaging by multiple in silico tools. While classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance, the clinical-genetic correlation supported a diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder.

Telomere biology disorders are rare premature-aging syndromes caused by defects in telomere-related genes. Telomeres cap chromosome ends, and with each cell division, they shorten until reaching a critical length that triggers senescence. In TBDs, this process is accelerated. Actively dividing tissues—bone marrow, skin, mucosa, hair, hepatocytes—are preferentially affected. The prototype is dyskeratosis congenita, classically defined by the mucocutaneous triad of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation. But here's the key point for emergency physicians and hospitalists: TBDs can present in adulthood without the classic triad, manifesting instead as apparently isolated bone marrow failure, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, or cryptogenic cirrhosis.

PARN variants demonstrate a distinct phenotype. Biallelic variants cause autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita, while monoallelic variants more often underlie telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure. This patient had a monoallelic variant, yet she developed both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis along with cytopenias—an expansion of what we traditionally associate with PARN mutations. This case adds to growing evidence that hepatic fibrosis may be more prevalent in PARN-associated TBD than previously recognized.

## Why This Matters for Your Practice

The temptation in emergency medicine and acute care is to focus on the immediate problem—correct the B12, transfuse if needed, and move on. And for the acute presentation, that's entirely appropriate. But we also need to recognize when we're looking at something that requires further investigation. This patient's constellation of findings—cytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying, and osteoporosis in a young adult—represented a recognizable pattern once you stepped back and looked at the whole picture.

The B12 deficiency in this case may have been a red herring of sorts, or more accurately, an epiphenomenon that brought her to medical attention. Interestingly, the authors note that telomere shortening may have rendered her bone marrow more susceptible to hematopoietic stress from the B12 deficiency. The cytopenia likely had multiple contributors, but the underlying genetic vulnerability meant she needed ongoing surveillance for evolving marrow failure.

## Clinical Pearls for Recognizing Telomere Biology Disorders

First, think TBD when you see unexplained multi-organ fibrosis in young patients. Pulmonary fibrosis and hepatic fibrosis together in someone under 50 should make you pause, especially if there's concurrent cytopenia or bone marrow involvement. Second, premature greying—defined as onset before age 20 in Caucasians or before 30 in African Americans—can be a clue, particularly when paired with other findings suggesting accelerated aging. Third, the absence of the classic mucocutaneous triad doesn't rule out TBD. Adult presentations often lack the textbook features we learn for dyskeratosis congenita. Fourth, TBDs can mimic acquired conditions. Patients may carry diagnoses of aplastic anemia, myelodysplastic syndrome, or cryptogenic cirrhosis for years before the underlying genetic cause is identified. Finally, diagnosis requires genetic testing through next-generation sequencing; telomere length measurement via flow-FISH can also support the diagnosis.

## Take-Home Points

- **Respect diagnostic tension**—if your diagnosis doesn't explain all the findings, don't dismiss the outliers; investigate them.
- **Think premature aging syndromes** when young patients present with multi-organ fibrosis, cytopenias, and features like premature greying or early-onset osteoporosis.
- **TBDs can present without the classic mucocutaneous triad**—adult presentations may show only marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis, or hepatic fibrosis.
- **PARN mutations expand the phenotype**—monoallelic variants, traditionally associated with pulmonary fibrosis, can also cause hepatic involvement and cytopenia.
- **Early recognition matters**—diagnosis guides genetic counseling, surveillance strategies, and consideration of bone marrow transplantation in progressive cases.

This case reminds us that rare diseases don't announce themselves with flashing neon signs. They present with subtle patterns that require us to step back, synthesize the full clinical picture, and resist premature closure on diagnoses that feel satisfying but incomplete. When the pieces don't fit together, that's not a problem to ignore—that's exactly where the real diagnosis might be hiding.



# Dyskeratosis Congenita Without the Triad: Adult Presentations of Telomere Biology Disorders

## The Case That Stopped Me in My Tracks

A woman in her late 20s walks in with five years of progressive fatigue. She's got pancytopenia, liver cirrhosis, pulmonary fibrosis, osteoporosis, premature greying, and a polyneuropathy. You correct her severe B12 deficiency, but the clinical picture doesn't resolve. The constellation is bizarre, the patient is too young, and nothing ties it together until genetic testing reveals a monoallelic PARN mutation. This is a telomere biology disorder (TBD) flying completely under the radar, without a single classic mucocutaneous feature of dyskeratosis congenita (DC).

## When the Textbook Description Fails You

We're taught to recognize dyskeratosis congenita by its mucocutaneous triad: oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation. But here's the problem. That classic presentation represents the pediatric severe end of the spectrum. Adult presentations of TBDs are far more insidious and frequently miss the triad entirely. This patient had none of those hallmark features. What she had instead was a collection of "idiopathic" organ failures that clinicians often evaluate in isolation: pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic cirrhosis, and bone marrow dysfunction.

The premature greying and osteoporosis in a young adult were subtle clues that something was accelerating her cellular aging. These features are easy to dismiss or attribute to other causes, but in context, they pointed toward a fundamental problem with telomere maintenance.

## Why Telomere Biology Disorders Matter

Telomeres cap our chromosomes and shorten with each cell division. When telomerase, the enzyme complex that maintains telomere length, malfunctions due to genetic mutations, cells hit senescence prematurely. The organs that take the hit are those with high cellular turnover: bone marrow, liver, lung epithelium, skin, and hair follicles.

This patient's PARN mutation is particularly instructive. PARN encodes an exoribonuclease involved in processing TERC, the RNA component of telomerase. Monoallelic PARN variants typically present as pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure in adults, while biallelic variants cause more severe childhood-onset dyskeratosis congenita. The fact that she had both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis with a monoallelic variant expands what we know about the phenotypic spectrum.

## The Diagnostic Challenge

Here's what makes these cases so tricky. The patient initially presented with severe B12 deficiency, which explained some of her cytopenias and neurologic symptoms. But even after aggressive repletion, her clinical picture remained disproportionate. Her liver disease had no viral, autoimmune, or metabolic explanation. Her pulmonary fibrosis appeared "idiopathic." The positive direct antiglobulin test was a red herring that briefly suggested immune-mediated disease.

This is exactly how TBDs get missed. They masquerade as common acquired conditions, and when organ systems are evaluated in silos, the pattern recognition fails. It took seeing the whole picture, the multi-organ fibrosis, the premature aging stigmata, and the refractory cytopenias in a young patient, to trigger genetic testing.

## What Should Trigger Suspicion?

Consider a TBD in any young adult (under 50 or 60) presenting with what appears to be idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, cirrhosis without clear etiology, or bone marrow failure. The diagnosis becomes more compelling when you see combinations: pulmonary fibrosis plus liver disease, or cytopenias alongside organ fibrosis. Premature greying, early osteoporosis, and unexplained cytopenias in the same patient should make your ears perk up.

Family history may be absent because these can be de novo mutations, and penetrance is variable. This patient had no reported family history, which initially steered clinicians away from hereditary conditions.

## Making the Diagnosis

The gold standard combines genetic testing through next-generation sequencing with telomere length measurement via flow-FISH. Clinical exome sequencing identified the PARN variant in this case, classified initially as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. But the clinical-genetic correlation, her phenotype matched the known disease spectrum, allowed the team to make the diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder.

Notably, telomere length testing wasn't performed here, and the variant's pathogenicity remains formally uncertain. This highlights a real-world challenge: sometimes the clinical picture is compelling enough to proceed even when genetic results don't give you a slam-dunk answer.

## Management and Implications

There's no cure for TBDs. Management is supportive and organ-specific: correcting nutritional deficiencies, surveillance for progression, and considering hematopoietic stem cell transplantation for severe bone marrow involvement. Androgens like danazol have shown some promise in telomere elongation, though long-term data is limited.

What matters most is recognizing these disorders early. It changes the conversation with patients and families regarding prognosis, genetic counseling, and the potential for future organ involvement. It also affects decisions about transplantation; patients with TBDs have higher transplant-related mortality and require modified conditioning regimens.

## Key Take-Home Points

- The classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita is often absent in adult presentations of telomere biology disorders.
- Consider TBD in any young adult with unexplained pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic cirrhosis, or bone marrow failure, especially when multiple organs are involved.
- Premature greying, early-onset osteoporosis, and cytopenias in a young patient are subtle clues to accelerated cellular aging.
- PARN mutations have a distinct phenotype: monoallelic variants typically cause pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure, while biallelic variants cause more severe childhood-onset disease.
- Family history may be absent due to de novo mutations and variable penetrance.
- Genetic testing plus telomere length measurement (flow-FISH) is the diagnostic standard, but clinical correlation may be necessary when variants are of uncertain significance.
- Early recognition enables appropriate genetic counseling, surveillance, and informed decisions about transplantation.

## The Bottom Line

Telomere biology disorders represent a hidden iceberg in adult medicine. What we see in clinical practice are the tips: the young patient with "idiopathic" pulmonary fibrosis, the thirty-year-old with cryptogenic cirrhosis, the young adult with unexplained pancytopenia. Beneath the surface lies a unifying genetic explanation that connects these seemingly disparate presentations. The next time you're scratching your head over multi-organ dysfunction in a young patient who looks older than their years, think about their telomeres.



# PARN Mutations: Expanding the Phenotype Beyond Pulmonary Fibrosis

What happens when a young adult walks in with what looks like severe B12 deficiency, but the clinical picture just doesn't quite add up? You correct the deficiency, and still—the pancytopenia lingers, the organ fibrosis progresses, and the premature graying stares back at you. This case stopped us in our tracks because it reminds us that telomere biology disorders (TBDs) don't always announce themselves with the textbook mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita.

## The Case That Made Us Look Twice

A woman in her late 20s presented with five years of progressive fatigue and weakness that had recently deteriorated to the point of needing assistance with self-care. On exam, she had pallor, icterus, premature graying of hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation, and a bald tongue—the kind of findings that make you think nutritional deficiency right away. But she also had hepatosplenomegaly, thoracic scoliosis, bilateral thenar atrophy, and reduced breath sounds bilaterally.

Her labs showed pancytopenia with a dimorphic picture and severe B12 deficiency. Bone marrow was hyperplastic but not dysplastic or malignant. She got B12 repletion, and some things improved. But here's the catch—she also had hepatic fibrosis (liver stiffness 18.1 kPa on elastography), pulmonary fibrosis on imaging, osteoporosis with recurrent fractures, and a sensorimotor polyneuropathy. The nutritional deficiency alone couldn't explain this constellation.

Clinical exome sequencing hit pay dirt: a monoallelic PARN missense variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg). This variant affects a highly conserved residue in the R3H domain and is predicted damaging by multiple in silico tools. While classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance, the clinical-genetic correlation strongly supported a telomere biology disorder.

## PARN: More Than Just a Pulmonary Fibrosis Gene

Here's where the biology gets fascinating. PARN encodes a poly(A)-specific ribonuclease—an enzyme that trims the poly(A) tails off mRNAs to regulate their stability and degradation. But PARN has a critical side gig in telomere maintenance. It's required for proper 3'-end maturation of TERC (the RNA component of telomerase) by removing post-transcriptionally acquired oligo(A) tails that would otherwise target nuclear RNAs for degradation. Without functional PARN, telomerase can't do its job properly.

PARN is among the 20% of genes least tolerant to rare disruptive variants—this is a gene where haploinsufficiency matters. The inheritance patterns tell an interesting story. Biallelic variants cause autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita type 6 (DKCB6) and severe phenotypes like Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome. Monoallelic variants, on the other hand, are more commonly associated with telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4). The initial association was discovered through exome sequencing of IPF cohorts, and now over 65 cases have been documented in the literature.

## The Hepatic Connection: Underrecognized but Important

What makes this case stand out is the hepatic fibrosis. We typically think of TERT and TERC variants when we consider liver involvement in TBDs, but data from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry showed that 88.8% of adults with pathogenic TERC or TERT mutations had at least moderate hepatic fibrosis, and 33.3% had cirrhosis. Now we're seeing that PARN may belong on that list too.

The literature describes a comparable case of a patient with pancytopenia and hepatic fibrosis who carried monoallelic variants in both PARN and TERT. Our patient had only a single PARN variant yet developed both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. This expands what we thought we knew about PARN's phenotypic reach and suggests that hepatic surveillance should be part of the monitoring protocol for these patients, not just pulmonary and hematologic monitoring.

## When the Classical Features Are Missing

Here's the clinical pearl that bears repeating: dyskeratosis congenita is classically defined by the mucocutaneous triad of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation. But TBDs can present in adulthood without any of these findings. Our patient had none of the classical mucocutaneous features. What she did have was premature graying, organ fibrosis, cytopenia, and osteoporosis in her twenties—features of accelerated aging across multiple organ systems.

The absence of a family history doesn't rule out a hereditary syndrome either. De novo mutations happen, and variable penetrance means affected family members may have gone undiagnosed or died before diagnosis.

## Clinical Takeaways

- Consider telomere biology disorders in young adults with unexplained multisystem disease involving bone marrow, liver, and lungs—even when classical mucocutaneous features of dyskeratosis congenita are absent.

- PARN variants aren't just about pulmonary fibrosis. Hepatic fibrosis and hematologic vulnerability are part of the expanding phenotype, and monoallelic variants can present in adulthood with variable penetrance.

- Biallelic PARN variants cause severe childhood-onset phenotypes (DKCB6, Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson), while monoallelic variants more often present as adult-onset pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure.

- Hepatic involvement in TBDs is likely under-recognized. Transient elastography may be a useful screening tool in patients with known or suspected TBDs.

- Diagnosis requires genetic testing and ideally telomere length measurement by flow-FISH. Treatment is primarily supportive, with hematopoietic stem cell transplantation reserved for progressive marrow failure.

This case reminds us that rare genetic disorders don't always look rare when they walk through the door. Sometimes they look like B12 deficiency that doesn't fully respond, or cirrhosis without a clear cause, or IPF in a young woman. The key is recognizing when the pieces don't fit together—and then having the intellectual curiosity to start looking for a unifying genetic diagnosis.



# The Liver in Telomere Biology Disorders: An Underrecognized Target Organ

Here's a case that stopped me in my tracks. A woman in her late 20s presents with five years of progressive fatigue, and by the time she reaches tertiary care, she's got pancytopenia, cirrhosis, pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying, osteoporosis, and neuropathy. You could be forgiven for initially chasing nutritional deficiencies—she had severe B12 and folate deficiency with classic megaloblastic changes on her peripheral smear. But here's the kicker: even after correcting those deficiencies, her multisystem dysfunction persisted, and the clinical picture didn't add up. What ultimately unified her constellation of findings was a monoallelic PARN mutation, revealing a telomere biology disorder (TBD) that had been flying under the radar for years.

## Pulmonary Fibrosis Gets All the Attention

When we think about TBDs presenting in adulthood, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is the poster child. Monoallelic variants in TERT, TERC, RTEL1, and PARN are established causes of familial IPF, and most pulmonologists are now savvy about sending genetic testing in younger patients with unexplained fibrotic lung disease. But here's what we're missing: the liver.

Hepatocytes are rapidly dividing cells, making them prime targets for telomere-mediated dysfunction. Yet hepatic fibrosis in TBDs has been dramatically underrecognized—often attributed to other causes or simply overlooked. The data emerging from telomere biology registries should change our approach.

## The Numbers Are Striking

A retrospective analysis from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry used transient elastography to systematically evaluate liver involvement in patients with pathogenic TERC or TERT mutations. The results: **88.8% of adult patients had at least moderate hepatic fibrosis, and 33.3% had cirrhosis**. That's one in three patients walking around with established cirrhosis, many of whom had never had their liver evaluated.

The patient in this case had transient elastography showing liver stiffness of 18.1 kPa—consistent with cirrhosis—along with hepatosplenomegaly and ascites on imaging. She also had ground-glass opacities and fibrotic changes in her lungs. But here's what's fascinating: she carried a monoallelic PARN variant, not TERC or TERT.

## PARN Expands the Hepatic Spectrum

PARN is a poly(A)-specific ribonuclease that plays a critical role in TERC maturation. Biallelic PARN mutations cause severe childhood-onset dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6) and Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome. Monoallelic variants are more classically associated with IPF and bone marrow failure—the acronym PFBMFT4 literally encodes this phenotype.

But this case adds to growing evidence that hepatic fibrosis belongs in the PARN spectrum too. The patient had cirrhosis without any alternative explanation—viral hepatitis, autoimmune disease, alcohol, and metabolic causes were all ruled out. A prior case report described a patient with pancytopenia and hepatic fibrosis carrying monoallelic variants in both PARN and TERT. This patient had only a single PARN variant and still developed both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis, suggesting that monoallelic PARN's downstream effects on telomerase function may mirror TERC/TERT mutations in their hepatic impact.

## Why the Liver Gets Missed

Part of the problem is that TBDs often present without the classical mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita—nail dystrophy, oral leukoplakia, and reticular skin hyperpigmentation. In adults, the presentation can be cryptic: isolated cytopenias, unexplained liver disease, or IPF. When a patient presents with cirrhosis, we run the standard workup. When that's negative, we might call it "cryptogenic" and move on.

Additionally, TBDs demonstrate variable penetrance and expressivity. A patient might have significant pulmonary involvement with minimal hematologic disease, or—critically—predominant hepatic disease. Without a systematic approach to screening, these organ systems get evaluated in silos.

## The Premature Ageing Clue

This patient had several red flags for a premature ageing syndrome: premature greying of hair, early-onset osteoporosis with recurrent fractures, and multisystem fibrosis. These features should prompt consideration of a telomere biology disorder, even in the absence of classic mucocutaneous findings. The neuropathy and musculoskeletal involvement (thoracic scoliosis, thenar atrophy) further supported a unifying genetic diagnosis.

Her severe B12 deficiency was a red herring—or perhaps more accurately, a second hit. The authors hypothesize that telomere shortening may have rendered her bone marrow more susceptible to hematopoietic stress, such as nutritional deficiency, and indeed she had a blunted reticulocyte response despite severe anemia.

## Clinical Implications

If you're evaluating a patient with unexplained IPF, bone marrow failure, or liver disease—especially in younger adults—think about TBDs. But more importantly, if you diagnose a TBD, don't stop at the presenting organ. Screen for involvement elsewhere.

Consider transient elastography in TBD patients even if liver enzymes are normal. Liver stiffness measurements are non-invasive and can detect fibrosis before synthetic dysfunction develops. Similarly, patients presenting with cryptogenic cirrhosis, especially with concurrent cytopenias or pulmonary fibrosis, warrant evaluation for telomere gene mutations.

## Key Take-Home Points

- **Hepatic fibrosis is common in TBDs**: Up to one-third of patients with TERC/TERT mutations may have cirrhosis; this case suggests PARN mutation carriers may carry similar risk.

- **Screen the liver in TBD patients**: Transient elastography should be considered even with normal liver enzymes—fibrosis can be silent until decompensation.

- **Consider TBDs in cryptogenic cirrhosis**: Especially in younger patients with concurrent pulmonary fibrosis, cytopenias, or premature ageing features.

- **Classical mucocutaneous features may be absent**: Adults with TBDs often lack the nail dystrophy, oral leukoplakia, and skin pigmentation seen in classic dyskeratosis congenita.

- **Premature ageing signs are clues**: Early greying, osteoporosis, and multi-organ fibrosis in a young adult should raise suspicion for accelerated cellular senescence.

The liver has been hiding in plain sight in telomere biology disorders. It's time we started looking for it.



# Variants of Uncertain Significance: When Clinical Correlation Trumps Genetic Classification

A woman in her late 20s walks into your clinic with five years of progressive fatigue, pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying, osteoporosis, and neuropathy. You run a clinical exome panel and get back a result that would make most clinicians shrug: a Variant of Uncertain Significance. But here's the thing—in this case, that VUS was the key to unlocking a unifying diagnosis that explained every bizarre manifestation in a patient decades too young for half her problems.

This case is a masterclass in why VUS doesn't mean "ignore this result," and why clinical correlation remains the most powerful tool in genomic medicine.

## The Case That Didn't Fit

The patient presented with what initially looked like severe nutritional deficiency—cobalamin and folate depletion, megaloblastic features, the works. But even after aggressive repletion, she didn't get better in any meaningful way. She had cirrhosis with no viral, autoimmune, or alcoholic etiology. She had pulmonary fibrosis in her twenties. She had premature greying, osteoporosis with fractures, and knuckle hyperpigmentation that looked almost like B12 deficiency but didn't quite fit.

The constellation was weird enough that the treating team stepped back and asked: is there one diagnosis that explains pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, and premature aging in a young adult? That question led them to telomere biology disorders, and specifically to PARN.

## When VUS Becomes Actionable

Here's where it gets interesting. The genetic testing came back with a monoallelic PARN missense variant: c.613T>C, p.Cys205Arg. Official classification? Variant of Uncertain Significance. In many clinical contexts, that's where the story ends—VUS gets filed away, and the workup continues down a different path.

But this team recognized something crucial: the variant sat in the R3H domain, a highly conserved functional region where benign variation is essentially unheard of. Four of five in silico prediction tools called it damaging. The variant was absent from population databases, consistent with a rare disease allele. And critically, other PARN missense variants at different positions have been classified as likely pathogenic in ClinVar.

The ACMG criteria tell us that PM1 (located in a critical functional domain), PM2 (absent from population databases), PP3 (computational evidence of deleterious effect), and PP2 (missense variants are a common disease mechanism in this gene) all point toward pathogenicity. That's not enough to upgrade from VUS to likely pathogenic on genetics alone—but paired with a phenotype that matches PARN-associated disease perfectly, it becomes clinically actionable.

## The Phenotype-Gene Match That Matters

PARN mutations occupy a fascinating niche in telomere biology disorders. Biallelic variants cause severe early-onset presentations like Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome, while monoallelic variants typically present in adulthood with pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure. What made this case unusual was the hepatic involvement—cirrhosis is more classically associated with TERT and TERC mutations, though emerging data suggests PARN's downstream effects on telomerase may produce similar hepatic phenotypes.

The clinical picture in this patient—premature greying, cytopenia, pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, osteoporosis—maps precisely onto what we now understand about telomere biology disorders. These conditions hit rapidly dividing tissues hardest: bone marrow, liver, lung, hair follicles. The classical mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita (oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, reticular pigmentation) was absent, but that's increasingly recognized as the tip of the clinical iceberg.

## Practical Approach to VUS Interpretation

So how do you avoid both over-interpreting uncertain findings and falling into therapeutic nihilism when a VUS might actually matter?

First, look at the gene-disease relationship. PARN has strong biological plausibility for telomere disorders—mechanistic studies show it's required for proper TERC maturation. It's among the 20% of genes least tolerant to rare disruptive variants, meaning missense changes are more likely to be pathogenic than in your average gene.

Second, evaluate the variant location and conservation. A variant affecting a highly conserved residue in a critical functional domain carries more weight than one in a poorly conserved region with no known function. The p.Cys205Arg variant sits in the R3H domain where no benign variants have been documented.

Third, assess phenotypic concordance. This is where clinical judgment matters most. A VUS in PARN in a patient with isolated anemia might warrant a cautious approach, but the same VUS in a patient with multi-organ fibrosis, premature aging features, and cytopenia should prompt serious consideration of a telomere biology disorder. The phenotype here was textbook for TBD, even though the patient lacked the classical features that might have made the diagnosis obvious earlier.

Fourth, partner with clinical geneticists who can navigate the ACMG classification framework and help contextualize uncertain findings. The classification system is designed to be conservative, and appropriately so—but it doesn't capture the full picture of clinical likelihood.

## What Changed Management

Making the TBD diagnosis fundamentally altered this patient's care trajectory. It meant proper genetic counseling for her family. It meant ongoing surveillance for malignant transformation, progressive marrow failure, and organ fibrosis. It meant that if she deteriorates hematologically, she could be considered for hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. It meant understanding that androgen therapies like danazol have some evidence for telomere elongation, though long-term data remains limited.

Perhaps most importantly, it provided an answer. After years of symptoms and investigations, the patient and her family understood that her constellation of problems wasn't bad luck or poor compliance—it was a recognized genetic syndrome with predictable complications and established management pathways.

## The Bottom Line

Variants of uncertain significance are uncertain because we lack sufficient genetic evidence to classify them definitively—not because they're clinically irrelevant. When the phenotype matches the gene's disease association, when the variant location and conservation suggest functional impact, and when alternative diagnoses have been exhaustively excluded, a VUS can and should inform clinical decision-making.

The key take-home points for clinicians facing uncertain genetic findings:

- VUS classification reflects insufficient genetic evidence, not necessarily clinical insignificance
- Evaluate the variant's location, conservation, and in silico predictions within the context of your patient's phenotype
- PARN monoallelic variants can present in adulthood with pulmonary fibrosis, bone marrow involvement, and increasingly recognized hepatic fibrosis
- Telomere biology disorders should be on your differential for young adults with unexplained multi-organ fibrosis, premature aging features, and cytopenias—even without the classical mucocutaneous triad
- Partner with clinical geneticists to interpret uncertain findings, but remember that clinical correlation remains essential to making these results actionable

This case reminds us that genetic testing is a tool that serves clinical judgment, not the other way around. When the clinical picture strongly suggests a diagnosis and the genetic result is plausible but not definitive, the phenotype should drive management while maintaining appropriate uncertainty about the variant's precise classification.
