Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

## Clinical Presentation: Recognizing a Complex Multisystem Disorder

The patient, a woman in her late 20s, presented with a 5-year history of progressively worsening generalized weakness and early fatigability. This extended timeline of symptom progression is an important clinical clue; the gradual onset and slow evolution of symptoms over years, rather than weeks or months, suggests a chronic, underlying systemic process rather than an acute illness. By the time of presentation, her functional capacity had declined significantly, impairing her ability to perform activities of daily living and requiring assistance with self-care. She also reported exertional dyspnea, intermittent chest discomfort, weight loss, and anorexia. For clinicians evaluating young adults with unexplained fatigue, the duration and progressive nature of symptoms should prompt consideration of disorders beyond common nutritional or situational causes.

Physical examination revealed multiple findings pointing toward a multisystem disorder. Signs suggestive of premature aging were prominent: premature greying of hair in a patient under 30 is unusual and raises suspicion for accelerated cellular senescence. Mucocutaneous findings included pallor and icterus, indicating anemia with associated hemolysis or ineffective erythropoiesis; knuckle hyperpigmentation, which can be seen in vitamin B12 deficiency; and a bald tongue with atrophy of lingual papillae, suggesting chronic nutritional deficiency or mucosal involvement. Musculoskeletal examination revealed thoracic scoliosis and bilateral thenar atrophy, the latter indicating peripheral neuropathy or chronic nerve damage. Abdominal examination demonstrated hepatomegaly (5 cm below the right costal margin with a span of 18 cm), later confirmed as hepatosplenomegaly on imaging. Respiratory examination revealed reduced breath sounds in the bilateral infrascapular regions, hinting at underlying pulmonary pathology. The combination of mucocutaneous, musculoskeletal, hepatic, and pulmonary findings across multiple organ systems should prompt clinicians to consider a unifying systemic diagnosis rather than attributing each abnormality to separate unrelated conditions.

Initial laboratory evaluation demonstrated pancytopenia with a notably low reticulocyte count (0.39%), indicating an inadequate bone marrow response to anemia. The peripheral smear showed dimorphic erythrocytes (two distinct populations of red blood cells), basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils—morphologic features strongly suggestive of megaloblastic anemia due to vitamin B12 or folate deficiency. Serum studies confirmed severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency. Iron studies revealed elevated serum iron and ferritin, consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis where iron is taken up by the marrow but not properly utilized for red blood cell production. Markers of hemolysis, including elevated lactate dehydrogenase and indirect hyperbilirubinemia, were present, and the direct antiglobulin test was positive for IgG. However, the low reticulocyte response argued against peripheral immune-mediated hemolysis and instead supported ineffective erythropoiesis as the primary process.

Bone marrow examination is essential in evaluating unexplained cytopenias. In this case, it revealed trilineage hematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia and increased iron stores, findings consistent with the peripheral smear interpretation. Importantly, there was no evidence of malignancy, dysplasia, or infiltrative disease, and cytogenetic analysis was normal. These findings helped exclude acute leukemia, myelodysplastic syndromes, and other primary bone marrow disorders, narrowing the differential diagnosis toward secondary causes of marrow dysfunction.

Imaging studies further characterized the extent of organ involvement. Abdominal ultrasound confirmed hepatosplenomegaly, while transient elastography revealed grade II hepatic steatosis and cirrhosis (liver stiffness 18.1 kPa)—a striking finding in a young adult with no clear history of alcohol use, viral hepatitis, or autoimmune liver disease. Positron emission tomography-CT demonstrated diffuse ground-glass haziness with fibrotic changes in both lung fields, consistent with pulmonary fibrosis, along with inhomogeneous bone marrow uptake. The presence of both hepatic cirrhosis and pulmonary fibrosis in a young patient without typical risk factors should raise clinical suspicion for genetic or inherited disorders affecting tissue integrity and repair mechanisms.

Taken together, the constellation of premature aging features, pancytopenia with ineffective erythropoiesis, hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, and peripheral neuropathy in a young adult pointed toward an underlying syndromic or genetic etiology. This case illustrates how careful attention to patterns across multiple organ systems—rather than focusing on isolated abnormalities—can guide clinicians toward the correct diagnostic framework.

## Diagnostic Reasoning: From Common Conditions to Rare Genetic Disorders

The diagnostic journey in this case illustrates a fundamental principle of clinical reasoning: when initial diagnoses fail to explain the complete clinical picture, clinicians must broaden their differential to include rare disorders. The patient presented with fatigue, pancytopenia, and multiorgan involvement—a constellation that initially warranted consideration of common acquired conditions before the recognition of an underlying genetic disorder.

**Initial Diagnostic Considerations**

The patient's presenting symptoms of progressive fatigue, weakness, and pancytopenia in a young adult generated a differential diagnosis encompassing nutritional deficiencies, hematologic malignancies, and autoimmune disorders. The markedly low reticulocyte count (0.39%) provided an important early clue, indicating inadequate bone marrow response—a finding more consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis or marrow failure than with peripheral destruction. Peripheral smear examination revealed dimorphic erythrocytes, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils, morphological features highly suggestive of megaloblastic anemia. This prompted focused evaluation for vitamin B12 and folate deficiency, which was subsequently confirmed on serum studies.

**Evaluation of Nutritional Deficiency as a Unifying Diagnosis**

Severe vitamin B12 deficiency was initially an attractive unifying diagnosis. It explained the patient's pancytopenia, the megaloblastic changes on peripheral smear and bone marrow, and several examination findings including the bald tongue with atrophic lingual papillae and knuckle hyperpigmentation. Neurological manifestations of B12 deficiency could also account for her neuropathy. Duodenal biopsy revealed chronic gastritis with reduced parietal cells, providing a mechanistic explanation for B12 malabsorption through impaired intrinsic factor production.

However, several features were disproportionate to the severity of nutritional deficiency alone. The patient had hepatic fibrosis with cirrhosis on transient elastography (liver stiffness 18.1 kPa), findings not explained by B12 deficiency. Pulmonary fibrosis was identified on imaging. Premature greying of hair in a woman in her late 20s, osteoporosis with recurrent fractures, and the overall clinical trajectory of progressive multisystem dysfunction over five years exceeded what nutritional deficiencies would typically produce. These discrepancies signaled that B12 deficiency, while real and requiring treatment, might represent a secondary phenomenon or an epiphenomenon of a broader underlying disorder.

**Systematic Exclusion of Acquired Causes**

The evaluation of hepatic fibrosis exemplified systematic exclusion of common etiologies. Autoimmune hepatitis was considered given the patient's age and sex, but autoimmune serologies including antinuclear antibodies and autoimmune hepatitis antibodies were negative. Infectious causes were thoroughly investigated: viral hepatitis serologies, HIV, cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus testing were unremarkable aside from Epstein-Barr virus IgG positivity, indicative of past infection rather than acute disease. The positron emission tomography-CT scan was performed to evaluate for occult malignancy or granulomatous disease such as lymphoma or sarcoidosis, but revealed no evidence of overt malignancy, granulomatous disease, or infiltrative pathology.

The positive direct antiglobulin test (IgG 2+) briefly raised consideration of immune-mediated hemolysis. However, the clinical picture was inconsistent with autoimmune cytopenia: the low reticulocyte count, absence of spherocytes on peripheral smear, and bone marrow findings of ineffective erythropoiesis rather than compensatory erythroid hyperplasia argued against significant peripheral hemolysis. The elevated lactate dehydrogenase and indirect hyperbilirubinemia reflected ineffective erythropoiesis intramedullary destruction of abnormal erythroid precursors rather than peripheral immune-mediated destruction.

**Recognition of Discrepant Findings**

The recognition that common diagnoses were insufficient required synthesizing multiple clinical clues that individually might be overlooked. Premature greying in a young adult, though sometimes familial, represented an early sign of accelerated ageing when considered alongside osteoporosis, multiorgan fibrosis, and bone marrow dysfunction. The patient's thoracic scoliosis and bilateral thenar atrophy suggested broader connective tissue and neurological involvement. Hepatosplenomegaly, ascites, and cirrhosis in a young woman without alcohol use, viral hepatitis, or autoimmune disease demanded an alternative explanation.

The pulmonary findings—diffuse ground-glass haziness with fibrotic and subpleural changes on imaging, along with exertional dyspnoea—were particularly significant. Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) typically presents in older adults, with a median age at diagnosis of 66 years. Its occurrence in a patient in her late 20s strongly suggested an underlying genetic predisposition rather than an idiopathic process.

**Clinical Suspicion for a Telomere Biology Disorder**

The constellation of features—pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying, and osteoporosis in a young adult—coalesced into a recognizable pattern pointing toward a telomere biology disorder. TBDs share the common mechanism of premature cellular senescence due to impaired telomere maintenance, predominantly affecting tissues with high cellular turnover including bone marrow, liver, lung, skin, and hair follicles. Although the classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita (oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation) was absent, TBDs increasingly are recognized to present with variable penetrance and organ-specific manifestations that may lack classical features.

This case demonstrates the importance of maintaining a low threshold for genetic testing when clinical features suggest premature ageing or when multiorgan fibrosis occurs in a young patient without clear acquired etiology. Clinical exome sequencing ultimately identified the monoallelic PARN variant, providing a unifying molecular diagnosis. The diagnostic reasoning process underscores that rare genetic disorders may masquerade as common acquired conditions, and recognition requires attention to clinical features that are atypical in severity, timing, or constellation for the presumed diagnosis.

## Telomere Biology Disorders and PARN: Foundational Science

Telomeres are repetitive DNA sequences (TTAGGG in humans) that cap the ends of chromosomes, serving as protective buffers that preserve genomic integrity during cell division. Without telomeres, chromosome ends would be recognized as DNA breaks, triggering inappropriate repair mechanisms that could lead to chromosomal fusions, rearrangements, and genomic instability. With each cell division, DNA polymerase cannot fully replicate the extreme 3' end of the lagging strand—a phenomenon known as the "end replication problem"—resulting in progressive telomere shortening. When telomeres reach a critically short length, they trigger cellular senescence or apoptosis, preventing further division of damaged cells.

The telomerase complex counteracts this natural shortening in highly proliferative cell populations. This ribonucleoprotein enzyme consists of two essential components: telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT), the catalytic protein subunit, and telomerase RNA component (TERC), which serves as the template for telomere elongation. Telomerase adds TTAGGG repeats to chromosome ends, maintaining telomere length in stem cells, hematopoietic progenitors, and other rapidly dividing tissues. Pathogenic variants in genes encoding telomerase components or regulators impair this maintenance mechanism, leading to premature and accelerated telomere attrition.

Tissues with high cellular turnover are disproportionately affected when telomere maintenance fails. Bone marrow hematopoietic stem cells, airway epithelial cells, hepatic stellate cells and hepatocytes, epidermal keratinocytes, and gastrointestinal mucosal cells all require robust telomere maintenance to sustain their replicative capacity. When telomeres become critically short in these populations, stem cell exhaustion occurs, manifesting as bone marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, or mucocutaneous abnormalities. This tissue vulnerability explains why the patient presented with pancytopenia, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, and hepatic cirrhosis—each representing failure of a different proliferative compartment.

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) encompass a spectrum of inherited premature aging syndromes caused by pathogenic variants in telomere-related genes. Dyskeratosis congenita (DC) represents the prototype, classically defined by the mucocutaneous triad of nail dystrophy, oral leukoplakia, and reticular skin hyperpigmentation. However, the phenotypic spectrum extends well beyond this classic presentation. More severe childhood variants include Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome (characterized by cerebellar hypoplasia, immunodeficiency, and early-onset bone marrow failure), Revesz syndrome (with retinopathy and cerebellar hypoplasia), and Coats plus syndrome (featuring retinal telangiectasias and intracranial calcifications). Critically for this case, TBDs may present in adulthood without the classic mucocutaneous triad, manifesting instead as apparently isolated bone marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis, or hepatic disease—often leading to delayed or missed diagnosis when the underlying genetic basis is not suspected.

The PARN gene encodes poly(A)-specific ribonuclease, a highly conserved exoribonuclease that regulates gene expression through deadenylation—shortening the poly(A) tails of messenger RNAs. Beyond its general role in mRNA turnover, PARN has a specific and critical function in telomere biology: the maturation of TERC. The TERC RNA molecule acquires post-transcriptional oligo(A) tails that target it for degradation. PARN removes these oligo(A) tails, enabling proper 3'-end maturation and stabilization of TERC. When PARN function is impaired, TERC becomes unstable, telomerase activity decreases, and telomere maintenance fails. This mechanistic link explains how PARN mutations produce a TBD phenotype despite PARN not being a direct component of the telomerase complex.

Genotype-phenotype correlations in PARN-associated disease demonstrate an important inheritance pattern distinction. Biallelic PARN variants (autosomal recessive) cause dyskeratosis congenita type 6 (DKCB6), typically presenting in childhood with severe manifestations including Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome features. Monoallelic variants, by contrast, more commonly underlie telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4), often presenting in adulthood. This patient's monoallelic PARN variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) aligns with this adult-onset pattern, presenting in her late 20s with pulmonary fibrosis as a prominent feature. However, her case expands the recognized spectrum by demonstrating concurrent hepatic fibrosis and cytopenia with a single heterozygous variant.

PARN is among the genes least tolerant to rare disruptive variants, supporting haploinsufficiency as a disease mechanism—where a single functional allele proves insufficient to maintain normal cellular function. This concept explains how monoallelic variants can produce disease. Variable penetrance further complicates the clinical picture: not all carriers develop the same manifestations or severity. Environmental stressors, additional genetic modifiers, and the inherent stochastic nature of telomere shortening all influence phenotype expression. In this patient, severe vitamin B12 deficiency may have acted as a "second hit," stressing an already vulnerable bone marrow and unmasking a predisposition to hematologic dysfunction. The absence of classical DC features in this patient—no nail dystrophy or oral leukoplakia—illustrates how variable penetrance can delay diagnosis, emphasizing the need to consider TBDs in young adults with unexplained multiorgan fibrosis or cytopenia, regardless of mucocutaneous findings.

## Genetic Diagnosis: Testing, Interpretation, and Confirmation

Establishing a genetic diagnosis in this case required careful integration of clinical suspicion, appropriate test selection, and meticulous interpretation of genetic findings. The patient's presentation with pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying, and osteoporosis in her late 20s represented a constellation that could not be explained by nutritional deficiency or acquired disease alone. This multisystem involvement, particularly the combination of bone marrow dysfunction and organ fibrosis in a young adult, raised clinical suspicion for a telomere biology disorder (TBD). After excluding infectious, autoimmune, and malignant etiologies through extensive investigation, clinical exome sequencing was pursued as the definitive diagnostic modality.

Clinical exome sequencing focuses on the protein-coding regions of the genome, where the majority of disease-causing variants reside. This approach offers advantages over targeted gene panels when the differential diagnosis spans multiple disease categories, as was the case here where the phenotype overlapped with bone marrow failure syndromes, inherited fibrotic disorders, and premature aging conditions. In this patient, clinical exome sequencing identified a monoallelic missense variant in the *PARN* gene: c.613T>C, resulting in an amino acid substitution from cysteine to arginine at position 205 (p.Cys205Arg). This variant occurs in exon 8 and affects a highly conserved residue within the R3H domain (residues 178–245), a region critical for the protein's RNA-binding function.

Interpreting newly identified variants requires both computational prediction and systematic classification frameworks. In silico prediction tools analyze variants for their likelihood of pathogenicity based on evolutionary conservation, protein structure, and functional impact. For this variant, four of five prediction tools (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT) predicted a damaging effect on protein function. Conservation analysis confirmed that cysteine at position 205 is highly conserved across species, suggesting that this residue plays an essential role in protein function.

The American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) and the Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP) have established standards for variant classification that provide a structured framework for interpreting genetic findings. This variant met several ACMG/AMP criteria supporting pathogenicity. PM1 applies because the substitution occurs within the R3H domain, a well-established functional domain with no known benign variation. PM2 reflects that the variant is absent or extremely rare in population databases such as gnomAD, indicating it is not a common polymorphism. PP2 acknowledges that *PARN* has a low rate of benign missense variation and that missense variants are a known mechanism of disease in this gene. PP3 captures the multiple computational predictions of damaging effect. Despite meeting these criteria, the variant was classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS), highlighting the inherent conservatism in genetic interpretation and the requirement for additional evidence to definitively classify variants as pathogenic.

The VUS designation presents a clinical challenge, as it indicates insufficient evidence to determine whether the variant causes disease. However, a VUS does not preclude clinical action. The key principle is that genetic findings must be interpreted in the context of the clinical phenotype. In this case, the identification of a monoallelic *PARN* variant in a patient with features characteristic of TBD—bone marrow dysfunction, pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, and premature aging features—provided strong clinical-genetic correlation. The gene's established role in TBDs, coupled with the patient's phenotype matching the known spectrum of *PARN*-associated disease, supported the diagnosis despite the formal VUS classification.

The gold standard for diagnosing TBDs combines genetic testing with telomere length measurement, typically performed using flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH). Telomere length measurement provides functional confirmation of the diagnosis, as patients with TBDs demonstrate telomeres below the first percentile for age. While this testing was not reported in this case, the clinical and genetic evidence was sufficient to establish the diagnosis. It is important to note that telomere length can vary among different cell types and may be influenced by age, making genetic testing the primary diagnostic tool when clinical suspicion is high.

The integration of genetic findings with clinical phenotype exemplifies the modern approach to diagnosing rare inherited disorders. The *PARN* gene encodes a poly(A)-specific ribonuclease involved in mRNA deadenylation and is essential for proper maturation of the telomerase RNA component (TERC). Pathogenic variants in *PARN* cause TBDs through impaired telomerase function, leading to accelerated telomere shortening. Biallelic variants cause autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita type 6 (DKCB6), while monoallelic variants are associated with telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4 (PFBMFT4). This patient's monoallelic variant expands the recognized phenotype to include hepatic fibrosis, consistent with emerging evidence that liver involvement is underrecognized in TBDs.

For the clinician, this case illustrates that genetic diagnosis is not simply a binary result but requires thoughtful integration of multiple data points: the pre-test probability based on clinical presentation, the technical limitations and yield of the chosen testing method, computational predictions of variant impact, systematic classification criteria, and ultimately the fit between the genetic finding and the patient's phenotype. When these elements align, as they did in this case, a diagnosis can be established even in the presence of a VUS, enabling appropriate genetic counseling, surveillance, and management.

## Management, Outcomes, and Clinical Lessons

The management of telomere biology disorders (TBDs) presents unique challenges because no therapy currently exists to correct the underlying defect in telomere maintenance. Treatment therefore focuses on addressing reversible contributors, providing organ-specific supportive care, and implementing vigilant surveillance for disease progression. In this case, the initial priority was correcting severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency, which contributed significantly to the patient's pancytopenia. She received intravenous cyanocobalamin at a dose of 1000 µg daily for 5 days, followed by weekly administration for 4 weeks and then monthly maintenance. Oral folic acid (1 mg daily) was initiated concurrently. This aggressive repletion regimen is standard for severe megaloblastic anemia, as it rapidly corrects the hematologic abnormalities attributable to nutritional deficiency and allows clinicians to assess whether cytopenias persist despite adequate replacement—a critical distinction when evaluating for an underlying bone marrow failure syndrome.

Supportive care measures were tailored to the patient's multisystem involvement. Symptomatic anemia necessitated whole blood transfusions. Electrolyte disturbances, including hypocalcemia and hypokalemia, were corrected with standard supplementation. Osteoporosis, confirmed on dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry and likely related to premature cellular senescence affecting bone remodeling, was managed with high-dose vitamin D (60,000 IU weekly) and elemental calcium (1000 mg daily). Physiotherapy was initiated to address functional decline from neuropathy and musculoskeletal involvement. This multidisciplinary approach—incorporating hematology, hepatology, pulmonology, and rehabilitation—reflects the reality that TBDs require coordinated care across multiple organ systems.

An important clinical lesson from this case is that correction of nutritional deficiencies does not resolve the underlying TBD. Although the patient showed symptomatic improvement at 2-week follow-up with stable blood counts, the fundamental pathophysiology remains. This underscores a key limitation in TBD management: the absence of disease-modifying therapies. While preclinical and small clinical studies have suggested that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone may promote telomere elongation, their long-term efficacy and safety remain uncertain, and they are not universally accepted as standard treatment. Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) represents a potentially curative option for patients with progressive bone marrow failure, but it carries substantial risks in TBD patients, who often have compromised pulmonary and hepatic function that increases transplant-related morbidity and mortality. Transplant candidates require careful selection and specialized conditioning regimens that account for their increased susceptibility to organ toxicity.

Genetic counseling is essential once a TBD diagnosis is established. In this case, the patient's family received counseling regarding the inheritance patterns and reproductive implications. PARN-related TBDs can manifest through both autosomal recessive (biallelic variants causing dyskeratosis congenita type 6) and autosomal dominant (monoallelic variants causing pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure) patterns. The identification of a monoallelic PARN variant raises important considerations for family screening, as first-degree relatives may carry the same variant with variable penetrance. Reproductive counseling should address the 50% transmission risk for monoallelic variants and discuss options such as preimplantation genetic testing. The absence of a known family history in this patient, as is common in TBDs, highlights that de novo mutations and variable expressivity can obscure inheritance patterns.

Surveillance is a cornerstone of long-term management. This patient requires ongoing monitoring of hepatic and pulmonary function, given her established cirrhosis and pulmonary fibrosis. Serial complete blood counts are necessary to detect progression toward bone marrow failure, which may evolve even after nutritional deficiencies are corrected. Patients with TBDs also face an elevated risk of both hematologic malignancies (particularly myelodysplastic syndrome and acute myeloid leukemia) and solid tumors, necessitating age-appropriate cancer screening with heightened vigilance. Periodic assessment of telomere length through flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridization, though not universally available, can provide objective measures of disease severity and help stratify risk.

This case offers several clinical pearls for the practicing clinician. First, TBDs should be considered in young adults presenting with unexplained multisystem disease, even when the classical mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita—oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation—is absent. Second, the combination of bone marrow failure, organ fibrosis, and features of premature aging (such as early graying, osteoporosis, or hepatic fibrosis in a young patient) should prompt evaluation for a telomere biology disorder. Third, hepatic fibrosis, though less frequently recognized than pulmonary involvement in TBDs, may be an underappreciated manifestation of PARN mutations. This case expands the phenotypic spectrum associated with monoallelic PARN variants, demonstrating that hepatic cirrhosis can coexist with pulmonary fibrosis and cytopenia even in the absence of biallelic mutations.

Ultimately, early recognition of TBDs enables appropriate genetic counseling, guides surveillance strategies, and allows timely consideration of HSCT in patients with progressive marrow failure. For this patient, the diagnosis provided an explanation for years of unexplained symptoms and established a framework for long-term monitoring, even though curative options remain limited. The case reinforces that TBDs represent a diagnostic challenge requiring a high index of suspicion, particularly when young patients present with seemingly disparate organ systems failing in concert.
