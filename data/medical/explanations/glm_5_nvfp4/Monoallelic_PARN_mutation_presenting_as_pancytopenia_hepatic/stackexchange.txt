Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Clinical Pearls] What is the classic mucocutaneous triad that defines dyskeratosis congenita, and what percentage of patients with telomere biology disorders lack this triad at presentation?

**Answer:**

The classic mucocutaneous triad that defines dyskeratosis congenita consists of **oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation**. The diagnosis is classically made when all three features are present, or when two of these features occur in the setting of bone marrow failure syndrome.

Importantly, **approximately 30–40% of patients with telomere biology disorders lack this classic triad at presentation**. This case powerfully illustrates that principle—our patient presented in her late 20s with pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, and features of premature aging (including premature graying), yet had none of the classic mucocutaneous findings. Her diagnosis was ultimately secured through clinical exome sequencing identifying a monoallelic *PARN* variant.

This teaching point carries critical clinical implications: the absence of the mucocutaneous triad should **never** exclude a telomere biology disorder from the differential diagnosis in patients presenting with unexplained bone marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, or combinations thereof—particularly in young adults. Relying solely on the presence of the classic triad for diagnostic consideration will lead to missed or delayed diagnoses in a substantial proportion of patients. As emphasized in this case, maintaining a low threshold for genetic testing when the clinical constellation suggests accelerated cellular senescence or multiorgan fibrosis is essential for timely recognition and appropriate management.

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a young adult presenting with unexplained pancytopenia combined with hepatic fibrosis and pulmonary fibrosis, what specific inherited disorder should be considered, and what underlying mechanism connects these seemingly disparate organ systems?

**Clinical Answer:**

In a young adult presenting with this specific triad—unexplained pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis—you should consider a **telomere biology disorder (TBD)**, particularly a mutation in the **PARN gene**.

**The Unifying Mechanism: Premature Telomere Attrition**

The mechanism connecting these seemingly disparate organ systems is fundamentally one of **accelerated cellular senescence due to defective telomere maintenance**.

Telomeres are repetitive nucleotide sequences that cap and protect chromosome ends. With each cell division, telomeres physiologically shorten until a critical length triggers cellular senescence or apoptosis. The enzyme telomerase, composed of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC), counteracts this shortening in highly proliferative tissues.

The **PARN gene** encodes a poly(A)-specific ribonuclease critical for the 3′-end maturation of TERC. When PARN is dysfunctional, TERC processing is impaired, telomerase activity diminishes, and telomeres shorten prematurely. This creates a shared vulnerability across all organs dependent on continuous cellular turnover:

- **Bone marrow:** Hematopoietic stem cells cannot sustain adequate replication, leading to marrow failure and pancytopenia.
- **Liver:** Hepatocytes have limited regenerative capacity, resulting in hepatic fibrosis and cirrhosis.
- **Lungs:** Alveolar epithelial cell dysfunction triggers aberrant repair mechanisms and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis.

**Key Teaching Point from This Case**

As illustrated by our patient—a woman in her late 20s—TBDs frequently present **without the classical mucocutaneous triad** of dyskeratosis congenita (oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation). The presence of "premature aging" features such as early graying, osteoporosis, and unexplained multiorgan fibrosis in a young patient should prompt early consideration of a TBD, even before classic stigmata appear. This recognition is essential because it directs appropriate genetic counseling, surveillance for malignancy, and consideration of hematopoietic stem cell transplantation in progressive marrow failure.

### [Pathophysiology] How does the PARN gene contribute to telomere maintenance, and what is the molecular consequence of PARN deficiency on TERC maturation?

### Clinical Teaching Answer

**PARN (Poly(A)-Specific Ribonuclease)** is a highly conserved exoribonuclease that plays a critical role in telomere maintenance through its function in RNA maturation. While PARN's broad cellular role involves deadenylation—shortening poly(A) tails of mRNAs to regulate gene expression—its specific contribution to telomere biology centers on **TERC (telomerase RNA component) maturation**.

At the molecular level, PARN is responsible for removing post-transcriptionally acquired oligo(A) tails from the 3' end of TERC. These oligo(A) tails are added by noncanonical poly(A) polymerases and serve as signals that target nuclear RNAs for degradation by the nuclear RNA exosome. **When PARN functions normally, it trims these oligo(A) tails, allowing proper 3'-end maturation of TERC and stabilizing the telomerase RNA component.**

In **PARN deficiency**, this deadenylation step fails. The consequence is twofold: (1) TERC retains its oligo(A) tail, marking it for degradation by the nuclear exosome, and (2) mature, functional TERC levels decline. Since TERC is an essential component of the telomerase ribonucleoprotein complex, its depletion impairs telomerase activity, leading to **accelerated telomere attrition** with each cell division.

This molecular defect explains the clinical phenotype observed in our patient. Tissues with high cellular turnover—bone marrow hematopoietic stem cells, pulmonary epithelium, and hepatocytes—are disproportionately affected because they exhaust their replicative capacity more rapidly when telomere maintenance is compromised. This manifests as pancytopenia, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, and hepatic fibrosis, as seen in this case. The finding that PARN is among the genes least tolerant to rare disruptive variants supports the concept of **haploinsufficiency**, explaining why even monoallelic variants, as identified in our patient (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), can produce a clinically significant telomere biology disorder.

### [Clinical Pearls] What are the key phenotypic differences between biallelic and monoallelic PARN variants in terms of clinical presentation and inheritance pattern?

**Clinical Teaching Answer:**

PARN variants demonstrate a genotype-phenotype correlation that follows an inheritance-based pattern, with important implications for disease severity and organ involvement.

**Biallelic PARN variants** cause autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita type 6 (DKCB6), representing the more severe end of the clinical spectrum. These patients typically present in childhood with the classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita—oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation—often accompanied by early-onset bone marrow failure. The most severe phenotype, Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome, has also been reported with biallelic variants and is characterized by cerebellar hypoplasia, immunodeficiency, and catastrophic marrow failure in early childhood.

**Monoallelic PARN variants**, by contrast, follow an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern with variable penetrance and typically present in adulthood. As illustrated in our case, these variants more commonly underlie telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4). The initial association between monoallelic *PARN* mutations and disease came from cohorts of patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), and over 65 cases have now been documented in the literature. Our patient—presenting in her late 20s with pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, and cytopenia—exemplifies how monoallelic variants can manifest without the classical mucocutaneous features, making recognition challenging.

A key principle here is that **monoallelic variants tend toward organ-specific fibrosis** (particularly pulmonary), while **biallelic variants produce the full dyskeratosis congenita phenotype with early multisystem involvement**. However, as our case demonstrates, the spectrum is expanding—this patient developed both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis with a monoallelic variant, suggesting that *PARN*-associated telomere biology disorders represent a continuum rather than discrete entities. This has practical implications: when evaluating young adults with unexplained pulmonary or hepatic fibrosis, particularly with concurrent cytopenias or premature aging features, *PARN* variants should be considered regardless of whether classic mucocutaneous stigmata are present.

### [Morning Report] A 28-year-old woman presents with pancytopenia and is found to have hepatic fibrosis on elastography and pulmonary fibrosis on CT. After correcting nutritional deficiencies, her cytopenias persist. What diagnostic tests should be ordered to evaluate for a telomere biology disorder?

**Diagnostic Evaluation for Telomere Biology Disorders**

When evaluating a young adult with the constellation of unexplained pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis—as in this case—the diagnostic approach should proceed in two parallel tracks: **telomere length measurement** and **genetic testing**.

**Telomere Length Testing**
The preferred method is **flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridization (flow-FISH)**, which measures telomere length in leukocyte subsets (particularly lymphocytes and granulocytes). This is considered the gold standard for clinical assessment. Patients with TBDs typically have telomere lengths below the first percentile for age-matched controls. Alternative methods include quantitative PCR and Southern blotting, though these are less precise and not preferred for clinical diagnosis.

**Genetic Testing**
**Next-generation sequencing**—either through a targeted panel of telomere-related genes or clinical exome sequencing—should be obtained. The TBD-associated genes include *DKC1*, *TERT*, *TERC*, *PARN*, *RTEL1*, *TINF2*, *WRAP53*, and others. In this patient, clinical exome sequencing identified a monoallelic *PARN* variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), which provided the molecular diagnosis.

**Clinical Context Supporting Testing**
The decision to pursue TBD testing was driven by features that could not be explained by nutritional deficiency alone: the triad of bone marrow failure, pulmonary fibrosis, and hepatic fibrosis—along with premature graying and osteoporosis in a young adult. Notably, this patient lacked the classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita (nail dystrophy, oral leukoplakia, and reticular skin pigmentation), underscoring that TBDs frequently present in adulthood without these hallmark features.

**Bottom Line**
Order flow-FISH for telomere length and NGS-based genetic testing when TBD is suspected. Early diagnosis is essential for surveillance, family counseling, and consideration of hematopoietic stem cell transplantation in progressive marrow failure.

### [Management] What is the rationale for using androgens such as danazol in patients with telomere biology disorders, and what is the proposed mechanism of action on telomere length?

### Clinical Teaching Answer

**Androgens such as danazol are used in telomere biology disorders (TBDs) to increase telomerase activity and potentially lengthen critically short telomeres, thereby improving hematopoietic stem cell reserve.**

The rationale stems from the pathophysiology demonstrated in this case. Our patient's monoallelic *PARN* mutation exemplifies how defects in telomere maintenance genes lead to accelerated telomere attrition. Under normal conditions, telomerase—composed of TERT (telomerase reverse transcriptase) and TERC (telomerase RNA component)—maintains telomere length. In TBDs, this complex is impaired, causing premature cellular senescence in highly proliferative tissues: bone marrow, liver, and lung, as seen in our patient's pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis.

**Mechanism of Action:**

The *TERC* gene promoter contains androgen-responsive elements. Androgens like danazol directly upregulate *TERC* transcription, increasing telomerase RNA component availability. Additionally, androgens appear to enhance *TERT* expression. This pharmacologic upregulation of the telomerase complex can elongate telomeres in hematopoietic stem cells, potentially restoring replicative capacity.

**Clinical Evidence:**

Small clinical studies have demonstrated that danazol treatment can produce measurable telomere elongation in leukocytes over 24 months, with corresponding hematologic improvements in some patients with TBD-associated bone marrow failure. However, as noted in this case report, the long-term effects and impact on non-hematologic manifestations—such as the hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis present in our patient—remain uncertain.

**Practical Considerations:**

It is important to recognize that androgen therapy addresses a molecular defect rather than providing definitive cure. For patients like ours with progressive marrow failure, it may serve as a bridge to hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, which remains potentially curative for hematologic manifestations. However, careful patient selection and monitoring for androgen-related toxicities are essential.

### [Pathophysiology] Why are tissues with high cellular turnover—such as bone marrow, skin, mucosa, and liver—preferentially affected in telomere biology disorders?

**Clinical Teaching Answer**

Tissues with high cellular turnover are preferentially affected in telomere biology disorders (TBDs) because these tissues depend on continuous cell division to maintain homeostasis—and telomere shortening occurs with each cell division.

Telomeres are repetitive DNA sequences that cap chromosome ends and preserve genomic integrity. Under normal conditions, telomeres shorten incrementally with each round of cell division, eventually reaching a critical length that triggers cellular senescence or apoptosis. The enzyme telomerase, composed of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and telomerase RNA component (TERC), counteracts this attrition by elongating telomeres. In TBDs such as dyskeratosis congenita, pathogenic variants in telomere-related genes—including *PARN*, as in this case—impair telomerase function, leading to abnormally short or dysfunctional telomeres.

Tissues with high cellular turnover—bone marrow, skin, mucosa, and liver—are uniquely vulnerable because they require constant stem and progenitor cell proliferation. When telomere maintenance is compromised, these rapidly dividing cells exhaust their replicative capacity prematurely. This results in premature stem cell senescence or apoptosis, manifesting as organ dysfunction. In this patient, the consequences included pancytopenia from bone marrow failure, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis—all organs dependent on cellular renewal. Additionally, premature greying and mucosal atrophy reflected compromised turnover in hair follicles and lingual epithelium.

The pathophysiology explains why TBDs present as "premature aging" syndromes: the cumulative cellular stress that typically manifests over decades in normal individuals becomes pathologically accelerated when telomere maintenance fails, disproportionately impacting tissues that cannot afford replicative failure.

### [Morning Report] When a genetic test reveals a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS) in a gene associated with a rare disorder, what clinical and laboratory factors can help determine whether the variant is likely pathogenic in that specific patient?

### Clinical Teaching Answer

When evaluating a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS), the central question is whether the patient's phenotype is sufficiently concordant with the gene's known disease spectrum to support clinical relevance. The answer lies in integrating genetic data with clinical context—neither alone is sufficient.

In this case, the patient harbored a monoallelic *PARN* missense variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), initially classified as a VUS. Several factors converged to support its pathogenicity.

**First, the clinical phenotype must match the gene's disease spectrum.** *PARN* variants are established causes of telomere biology disorders (TBDs)—specifically, biallelic variants cause autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita, while monoallelic variants are associated with pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure. This patient presented with unexplained pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis—a constellation that fits the TBD phenotype. She also exhibited premature greying and osteoporosis, features of accelerated aging seen in TBDs. Crucially, extensive evaluation excluded nutritional, autoimmune, infectious, and malignant causes, leaving a genetic etiology as the most plausible unifying diagnosis.

**Second, in silico prediction tools and variant location matter.** This variant alters cysteine at position 205—a highly conserved residue within the R3H domain, a critical functional region. Four of five computational tools (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, SIFT) predicted a damaging effect. Under ACMG/AMP guidelines, this variant met multiple supportive criteria: PM1 (located in a critical functional domain), PM2 (absent from population databases), PP3 (computational evidence of pathogenicity), and PP2 (missense variants are a common disease mechanism in this gene). Furthermore, analogous *PARN* missense variants, such as p.Lys59Arg and p.Ala383Val, are classified as "likely pathogenic" in ClinVar—providing precedent that missense changes in this gene can be disease-causing.

**Third, gene-specific tolerance to variation provides context.** *PARN* is among the genes least tolerant to rare disruptive variants, supporting haploinsufficiency as a disease mechanism. This makes identifying any rare variant more clinically significant than in a gene with high benign variation.

**Fourth, functional validation—when available—can be decisive.** The gold standard for diagnosing TBDs includes telomere length measurement via flow-FISH. Markedly short telomeres in leukocytes would provide functional confirmation that the variant disrupts telomere biology.

The key teaching point is that a VUS does not mean "no answer"—it signals incomplete evidence. When the phenotype aligns with the gene's disease spectrum, computational predictions favor pathogenicity, and alternative diagnoses have been excluded, clinicians can reasonably treat the variant as clinically relevant while awaiting functional validation or reclassification over time.
