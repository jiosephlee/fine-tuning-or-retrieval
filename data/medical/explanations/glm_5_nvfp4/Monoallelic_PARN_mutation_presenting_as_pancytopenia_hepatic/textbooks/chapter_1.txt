Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

Chapter 1: ## Clinical Presentation: Recognizing a Complex Multisystem Disorder

The patient, a woman in her late 20s, presented with a 5-year history of progressively worsening generalized weakness and early fatigability. This extended timeline of symptom progression is an important clinical clue; the gradual onset and slow evolution of symptoms over years, rather than weeks or months, suggests a chronic, underlying systemic process rather than an acute illness. By the time of presentation, her functional capacity had declined significantly, impairing her ability to perform activities of daily living and requiring assistance with self-care. She also reported exertional dyspnea, intermittent chest discomfort, weight loss, and anorexia. For clinicians evaluating young adults with unexplained fatigue, the duration and progressive nature of symptoms should prompt consideration of disorders beyond common nutritional or situational causes.

Physical examination revealed multiple findings pointing toward a multisystem disorder. Signs suggestive of premature aging were prominent: premature greying of hair in a patient under 30 is unusual and raises suspicion for accelerated cellular senescence. Mucocutaneous findings included pallor and icterus, indicating anemia with associated hemolysis or ineffective erythropoiesis; knuckle hyperpigmentation, which can be seen in vitamin B12 deficiency; and a bald tongue with atrophy of lingual papillae, suggesting chronic nutritional deficiency or mucosal involvement. Musculoskeletal examination revealed thoracic scoliosis and bilateral thenar atrophy, the latter indicating peripheral neuropathy or chronic nerve damage. Abdominal examination demonstrated hepatomegaly (5 cm below the right costal margin with a span of 18 cm), later confirmed as hepatosplenomegaly on imaging. Respiratory examination revealed reduced breath sounds in the bilateral infrascapular regions, hinting at underlying pulmonary pathology. The combination of mucocutaneous, musculoskeletal, hepatic, and pulmonary findings across multiple organ systems should prompt clinicians to consider a unifying systemic diagnosis rather than attributing each abnormality to separate unrelated conditions.

Initial laboratory evaluation demonstrated pancytopenia with a notably low reticulocyte count (0.39%), indicating an inadequate bone marrow response to anemia. The peripheral smear showed dimorphic erythrocytes (two distinct populations of red blood cells), basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils—morphologic features strongly suggestive of megaloblastic anemia due to vitamin B12 or folate deficiency. Serum studies confirmed severe vitamin B12 and folate deficiency. Iron studies revealed elevated serum iron and ferritin, consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis where iron is taken up by the marrow but not properly utilized for red blood cell production. Markers of hemolysis, including elevated lactate dehydrogenase and indirect hyperbilirubinemia, were present, and the direct antiglobulin test was positive for IgG. However, the low reticulocyte response argued against peripheral immune-mediated hemolysis and instead supported ineffective erythropoiesis as the primary process.

Bone marrow examination is essential in evaluating unexplained cytopenias. In this case, it revealed trilineage hematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia and increased iron stores, findings consistent with the peripheral smear interpretation. Importantly, there was no evidence of malignancy, dysplasia, or infiltrative disease, and cytogenetic analysis was normal. These findings helped exclude acute leukemia, myelodysplastic syndromes, and other primary bone marrow disorders, narrowing the differential diagnosis toward secondary causes of marrow dysfunction.

Imaging studies further characterized the extent of organ involvement. Abdominal ultrasound confirmed hepatosplenomegaly, while transient elastography revealed grade II hepatic steatosis and cirrhosis (liver stiffness 18.1 kPa)—a striking finding in a young adult with no clear history of alcohol use, viral hepatitis, or autoimmune liver disease. Positron emission tomography-CT demonstrated diffuse ground-glass haziness with fibrotic changes in both lung fields, consistent with pulmonary fibrosis, along with inhomogeneous bone marrow uptake. The presence of both hepatic cirrhosis and pulmonary fibrosis in a young patient without typical risk factors should raise clinical suspicion for genetic or inherited disorders affecting tissue integrity and repair mechanisms.

Taken together, the constellation of premature aging features, pancytopenia with ineffective erythropoiesis, hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, and peripheral neuropathy in a young adult pointed toward an underlying syndromic or genetic etiology. This case illustrates how careful attention to patterns across multiple organ systems—rather than focusing on isolated abnormalities—can guide clinicians toward the correct diagnostic framework.
