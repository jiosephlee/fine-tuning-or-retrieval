Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

Chapter 4: ## Genetic Diagnosis: Testing, Interpretation, and Confirmation

Establishing a genetic diagnosis in this case required careful integration of clinical suspicion, appropriate test selection, and meticulous interpretation of genetic findings. The patient's presentation with pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, premature greying, and osteoporosis in her late 20s represented a constellation that could not be explained by nutritional deficiency or acquired disease alone. This multisystem involvement, particularly the combination of bone marrow dysfunction and organ fibrosis in a young adult, raised clinical suspicion for a telomere biology disorder (TBD). After excluding infectious, autoimmune, and malignant etiologies through extensive investigation, clinical exome sequencing was pursued as the definitive diagnostic modality.

Clinical exome sequencing focuses on the protein-coding regions of the genome, where the majority of disease-causing variants reside. This approach offers advantages over targeted gene panels when the differential diagnosis spans multiple disease categories, as was the case here where the phenotype overlapped with bone marrow failure syndromes, inherited fibrotic disorders, and premature aging conditions. In this patient, clinical exome sequencing identified a monoallelic missense variant in the *PARN* gene: c.613T>C, resulting in an amino acid substitution from cysteine to arginine at position 205 (p.Cys205Arg). This variant occurs in exon 8 and affects a highly conserved residue within the R3H domain (residues 178–245), a region critical for the protein's RNA-binding function.

Interpreting newly identified variants requires both computational prediction and systematic classification frameworks. In silico prediction tools analyze variants for their likelihood of pathogenicity based on evolutionary conservation, protein structure, and functional impact. For this variant, four of five prediction tools (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT) predicted a damaging effect on protein function. Conservation analysis confirmed that cysteine at position 205 is highly conserved across species, suggesting that this residue plays an essential role in protein function.

The American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) and the Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP) have established standards for variant classification that provide a structured framework for interpreting genetic findings. This variant met several ACMG/AMP criteria supporting pathogenicity. PM1 applies because the substitution occurs within the R3H domain, a well-established functional domain with no known benign variation. PM2 reflects that the variant is absent or extremely rare in population databases such as gnomAD, indicating it is not a common polymorphism. PP2 acknowledges that *PARN* has a low rate of benign missense variation and that missense variants are a known mechanism of disease in this gene. PP3 captures the multiple computational predictions of damaging effect. Despite meeting these criteria, the variant was classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS), highlighting the inherent conservatism in genetic interpretation and the requirement for additional evidence to definitively classify variants as pathogenic.

The VUS designation presents a clinical challenge, as it indicates insufficient evidence to determine whether the variant causes disease. However, a VUS does not preclude clinical action. The key principle is that genetic findings must be interpreted in the context of the clinical phenotype. In this case, the identification of a monoallelic *PARN* variant in a patient with features characteristic of TBD—bone marrow dysfunction, pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, and premature aging features—provided strong clinical-genetic correlation. The gene's established role in TBDs, coupled with the patient's phenotype matching the known spectrum of *PARN*-associated disease, supported the diagnosis despite the formal VUS classification.

The gold standard for diagnosing TBDs combines genetic testing with telomere length measurement, typically performed using flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH). Telomere length measurement provides functional confirmation of the diagnosis, as patients with TBDs demonstrate telomeres below the first percentile for age. While this testing was not reported in this case, the clinical and genetic evidence was sufficient to establish the diagnosis. It is important to note that telomere length can vary among different cell types and may be influenced by age, making genetic testing the primary diagnostic tool when clinical suspicion is high.

The integration of genetic findings with clinical phenotype exemplifies the modern approach to diagnosing rare inherited disorders. The *PARN* gene encodes a poly(A)-specific ribonuclease involved in mRNA deadenylation and is essential for proper maturation of the telomerase RNA component (TERC). Pathogenic variants in *PARN* cause TBDs through impaired telomerase function, leading to accelerated telomere shortening. Biallelic variants cause autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita type 6 (DKCB6), while monoallelic variants are associated with telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4 (PFBMFT4). This patient's monoallelic variant expands the recognized phenotype to include hepatic fibrosis, consistent with emerging evidence that liver involvement is underrecognized in TBDs.

For the clinician, this case illustrates that genetic diagnosis is not simply a binary result but requires thoughtful integration of multiple data points: the pre-test probability based on clinical presentation, the technical limitations and yield of the chosen testing method, computational predictions of variant impact, systematic classification criteria, and ultimately the fit between the genetic finding and the patient's phenotype. When these elements align, as they did in this case, a diagnosis can be established even in the presence of a VUS, enabling appropriate genetic counseling, surveillance, and management.
