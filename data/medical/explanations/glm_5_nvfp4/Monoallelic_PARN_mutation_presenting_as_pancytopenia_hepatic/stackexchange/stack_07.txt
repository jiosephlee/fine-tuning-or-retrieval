Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Pathophysiology] Why are tissues with high cellular turnover—such as bone marrow, skin, mucosa, and liver—preferentially affected in telomere biology disorders?

**Clinical Teaching Answer**

Tissues with high cellular turnover are preferentially affected in telomere biology disorders (TBDs) because these tissues depend on continuous cell division to maintain homeostasis—and telomere shortening occurs with each cell division.

Telomeres are repetitive DNA sequences that cap chromosome ends and preserve genomic integrity. Under normal conditions, telomeres shorten incrementally with each round of cell division, eventually reaching a critical length that triggers cellular senescence or apoptosis. The enzyme telomerase, composed of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and telomerase RNA component (TERC), counteracts this attrition by elongating telomeres. In TBDs such as dyskeratosis congenita, pathogenic variants in telomere-related genes—including *PARN*, as in this case—impair telomerase function, leading to abnormally short or dysfunctional telomeres.

Tissues with high cellular turnover—bone marrow, skin, mucosa, and liver—are uniquely vulnerable because they require constant stem and progenitor cell proliferation. When telomere maintenance is compromised, these rapidly dividing cells exhaust their replicative capacity prematurely. This results in premature stem cell senescence or apoptosis, manifesting as organ dysfunction. In this patient, the consequences included pancytopenia from bone marrow failure, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis—all organs dependent on cellular renewal. Additionally, premature greying and mucosal atrophy reflected compromised turnover in hair follicles and lingual epithelium.

The pathophysiology explains why TBDs present as "premature aging" syndromes: the cumulative cellular stress that typically manifests over decades in normal individuals becomes pathologically accelerated when telomere maintenance fails, disproportionately impacting tissues that cannot afford replicative failure.
