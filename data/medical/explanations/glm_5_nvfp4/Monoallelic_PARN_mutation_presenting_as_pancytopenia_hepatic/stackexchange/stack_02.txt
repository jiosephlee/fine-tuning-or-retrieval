Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis"

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a young adult presenting with unexplained pancytopenia combined with hepatic fibrosis and pulmonary fibrosis, what specific inherited disorder should be considered, and what underlying mechanism connects these seemingly disparate organ systems?

**Clinical Answer:**

In a young adult presenting with this specific triad—unexplained pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis—you should consider a **telomere biology disorder (TBD)**, particularly a mutation in the **PARN gene**.

**The Unifying Mechanism: Premature Telomere Attrition**

The mechanism connecting these seemingly disparate organ systems is fundamentally one of **accelerated cellular senescence due to defective telomere maintenance**.

Telomeres are repetitive nucleotide sequences that cap and protect chromosome ends. With each cell division, telomeres physiologically shorten until a critical length triggers cellular senescence or apoptosis. The enzyme telomerase, composed of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC), counteracts this shortening in highly proliferative tissues.

The **PARN gene** encodes a poly(A)-specific ribonuclease critical for the 3′-end maturation of TERC. When PARN is dysfunctional, TERC processing is impaired, telomerase activity diminishes, and telomeres shorten prematurely. This creates a shared vulnerability across all organs dependent on continuous cellular turnover:

- **Bone marrow:** Hematopoietic stem cells cannot sustain adequate replication, leading to marrow failure and pancytopenia.
- **Liver:** Hepatocytes have limited regenerative capacity, resulting in hepatic fibrosis and cirrhosis.
- **Lungs:** Alveolar epithelial cell dysfunction triggers aberrant repair mechanisms and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis.

**Key Teaching Point from This Case**

As illustrated by our patient—a woman in her late 20s—TBDs frequently present **without the classical mucocutaneous triad** of dyskeratosis congenita (oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation). The presence of "premature aging" features such as early graying, osteoporosis, and unexplained multiorgan fibrosis in a young patient should prompt early consideration of a TBD, even before classic stigmata appear. This recognition is essential because it directs appropriate genetic counseling, surveillance for malignancy, and consideration of hematopoietic stem cell transplantation in progressive marrow failure.
