Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

### [Management] What is the rationale for using high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) in the treatment of autoimmune HIT, and what adjunctive anticoagulation strategies are appropriate for this condition?

**Clinical Teaching Answer**

In autoimmune HIT (aHIT), the pathogenic antibodies have evolved to activate platelets even in the absence of heparin, creating a self-sustaining cycle of platelet activation and thrombosis. This fundamentally differs from classic HIT, where stopping heparin effectively removes the antigenic trigger.

**Rationale for High-Dose IVIG**

The rationale for IVIG in aHIT centers on interrupting FcγRIIa receptor-mediated platelet activation. In classic HIT, PF4/heparin/IgG immune complexes cluster platelet Fc receptors, triggering activation. In aHIT, antibodies recognize PF4 in a conformation that allows platelet activation without heparin—essentially functioning as autoantibodies. High-dose IVIG (typically 1–2 g/kg total, often 100 g/day for 2 consecutive days as in this case) competitively saturates platelet FcγRIIa receptors, preventing pathogenic antibody binding and interrupting the activation cascade.

This patient's laboratory profile was particularly instructive: the serotonin-release assay showed strong platelet activation at 0 U/mL heparin (96% release), and remarkably, only partial inhibition by the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3. This partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade—previously unreported—indicates an unusually severe antibody phenotype with high-affinity Fc receptor binding. This serological finding correlates with the patient's clinical course: his platelet count nadir of 13×10⁹/L was profound, and he required a third 100 g IVIG dose when the expected brisk platelet count recovery did not occur after the initial two doses.

**Adjunctive Anticoagulation Strategies**

Anticoagulation remains essential because IVIG addresses platelet activation but not thrombin generation. Non-heparin anticoagulants are required:

1. **Argatroban** was appropriately selected as initial therapy in this case given the recent stroke, severe thrombocytopenia, and concern for potential bleeding—its short half-life and hepatic clearance allowed rapid dose adjustment. The team monitored fibrinogen and D-dimer trends as indirect markers of anticoagulant efficacy.

2. **Fondaparinux** served as the transition agent. This synthetic pentasaccharide has minimal cross-reactivity with HIT antibodies and offers practical advantages—once-daily subcutaneous dosing without PTT monitoring. Importantly, the team recognized that fondaparinux's anticoagulant effect depends on antithrombin; given the patient's mild antithrombin deficiency (0.70 U/mL) in the setting of severe consumptive coagulopathy, antithrombin concentrate (1000 U) was appropriately administered prior to fondaparinux initiation.

The switch to fondaparinux was prompted by clinical and laboratory evidence suggesting inadequate control of HIT-associated hypercoagulability during argatroban therapy—falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimer, and newly appearing schistocytes. This case underscores that in aHIT, both immunomodulation (IVIG) and effective anticoagulation are often necessary, and monitoring for biochemical markers of ongoing consumption (fibrinogen, D-dimer, schistocytes) can guide therapeutic decisions.
