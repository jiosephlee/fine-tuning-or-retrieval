Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

### [Differential Diagnosis] In a patient with severe thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery, what clinical and laboratory features help distinguish HIT from other causes of postoperative thrombocytopenia such as post-transfusion purpura, post-surgical thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura, or drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia?

### Clinical Teaching Answer

Distinguishing HIT from other causes of postoperative thrombocytopenia requires careful attention to the **temporal relationship to heparin exposure**, the **presence or absence of thrombosis**, and key **laboratory parameters**.

**Timing is critical.** Classic HIT typically manifests with platelet count decline beginning 5–10 days after heparin exposure. However, as illustrated in this case, early onset (before postoperative day 5) can occur when there has been **recent preoperative heparin exposure**—this patient received UFH during cardiac catheterization one week prior to surgery, which explained his unusually early platelet count fall. In contrast, **drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia (DITP)** from newly introduced postoperative medications requires 5–7 days for initial sensitization, making this diagnosis unlikely when thrombocytopenia begins earlier.

**The presence of thrombosis strongly favors HIT.** HIT is notable for its paradoxical association with both thrombocytopenia and thrombosis—the relative risk for thrombosis is approximately 15-fold. This patient presented with an acute cerebral infarct, a hallmark of HIT-associated hypercoagulability. In contrast, DITP and **post-transfusion purpura (PTP)** typically present with isolated severe thrombocytopenia and bleeding, not thrombosis. Notably, PTP was excluded in this case by the absence of any blood transfusions.

**Laboratory markers of hemolysis and hemostatic activation aid differentiation.** **Post-surgical thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP)** characteristically presents with microangiopathic hemolytic anemia—evidenced by schistocytes on peripheral smear, elevated LDH, and low haptoglobin. This patient had normal haptoglobin and initial absence of schistocytes, arguing against TTP. The subsequent appearance of schistocytes later in his course reflected HIT-associated consumptive coagulopathy rather than primary TTP.

**Serological testing confirms the diagnosis.** A strong-positive PF4/polyanion ELISA in the appropriate clinical context raises suspicion for HIT, while functional platelet activation assays (such as the serotonin-release assay) provide specificity. In autoimmune HIT, as seen in this patient, the SRA demonstrates **heparin-independent platelet activation**—strong serotonin release even in the absence of heparin—distinguishing it from classic HIT.

In summary, distinguishing HIT hinges on: (1) temporal relationship to heparin, (2) presence of thrombosis, (3) absence of hemolytic features (ruling out TTP), (4) transfusion history (ruling out PTP), and (5) confirmatory serological testing with functional assays.
