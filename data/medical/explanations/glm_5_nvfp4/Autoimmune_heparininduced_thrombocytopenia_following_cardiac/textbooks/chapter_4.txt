Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

Chapter 4: ## Management Strategy and Treatment Evolution

The management of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) requires prompt intervention to mitigate the risk of life-threatening thrombosis while avoiding bleeding complications. In this case, the therapeutic strategy evolved through several phases as the clinical team responded to the patient's unusually severe and persistent autoimmune HIT (aHIT) phenotype.

The cornerstone of HIT management is immediate discontinuation of all heparin products. In this patient, low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) thromboprophylaxis with dalteparin was stopped on postoperative day 5 (POD5) when the platelet count fell to 50×10⁹/L, raising clinical suspicion for HIT. Importantly, LMWH is contraindicated in HIT because it can cross-react with HIT antibodies and perpetuate platelet activation. The initial substitution with prophylactic-dose fondaparinux (2.5 mg) was a reasonable interim measure, as fondaparinux has low cross-reactivity with HIT antibodies. However, once the diagnosis of HIT was confirmed by strong-positive ELISA and atypical serotonin-release assay (SRA) results, more aggressive therapeutic intervention became necessary.

Argatroban was selected as the initial therapeutic anticoagulant. As a direct thrombin inhibitor, argatroban acts independently of antithrombin to directly inhibit both clot-bound and free thrombin. This mechanism is advantageous in HIT, where thrombin generation is markedly amplified. Argatroban is administered as a continuous intravenous infusion with a short half-life of approximately 40 to 50 minutes, allowing rapid titration and reversal if bleeding occurs. In this patient, dosing started at 0.75 mcg/kg/min with a target partial thromboplastin time (PTT) of 55 to 85 seconds. The selection of argatroban over therapeutic-dose fondaparinux was deliberate: given the patient's recent stroke and profound thrombocytopenia (nadir 13×10⁹/L), the shorter half-life of argatroban provided a safety margin in case hemorrhagic complications arose.

High-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered to address the autoimmune nature of this patient's HIT. In aHIT, antibodies possess heparin-independent platelet-activating properties, meaning they can trigger platelet activation even in the absence of heparin exposure. IVIG is thought to work by blocking platelet FcγIIa receptors, thereby preventing pathogenic antibody binding and subsequent platelet activation. The patient received 100 g on two consecutive days, with a third 100-g dose given due to lack of initial platelet count response. Notably, the platelet count rose only modestly from 13×10⁹/L on POD7 to an interim peak of 54×10⁹/L by POD14—a much more attenuated response than typically expected with IVIG in aHIT. This blunted response signaled the severity of the autoimmune process.

Serial monitoring of fibrinogen, D-dimer, and peripheral blood smears provided critical insight into the adequacy of anticoagulation. HIT can trigger a consumptive coagulopathy characterized by platelet-driven thrombin generation, fibrinogen depletion, and microangiopathic hemolysis. In this patient, fibrinogen levels fell from 6.59 g/L on POD5 to a nadir of 2.49 g/L by POD17, while D-dimer levels peaked around POD12. Schistocytes appeared on peripheral blood films from POD9 to POD16. Together, these findings indicated ongoing, severe HIT-associated hypercoagulability despite argatroban therapy.

The constellation of falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimer, and persistent schistocytes signaled that argatroban was not adequately controlling the coagulopathy. On POD14, the patient was transitioned from argatroban to therapeutic-dose fondaparinux (10 mg once daily subcutaneously). Fondaparinux is a synthetic pentasaccharide that indirectly inhibits factor Xa through antithrombin-dependent mechanisms. Its once-daily subcutaneous administration offered practical advantages over continuous intravenous argatroban infusion. Because the patient's antithrombin level was mildly reduced at 0.70 U/mL (reference 0.80–1.40 U/mL), supplemental antithrombin concentrate (1000 U) was administered prior to initiating fondaparinux to ensure therapeutic efficacy.

The transition to fondaparinux proved effective. Over the subsequent weeks, the platelet count gradually recovered to 155×10⁹/L by POD62, with complete neurological recovery. Fondaparinux was continued until POD65, at which point the patient resumed apixaban 5 mg twice daily for long-term anticoagulation of his atrial fibrillation. The decision to restart a direct oral anticoagulant was made after sustained platelet count recovery and resolution of the hypercoagulable state. This case illustrates the importance of vigilant monitoring and flexible treatment strategies in managing aHIT, where standard HIT therapies may require augmentation or substitution based on dynamic clinical and laboratory parameters.
