Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

Chapter 2: ## Pathophysiology: Classic HIT versus Autoimmune HIT

Understanding why this patient developed such severe thrombocytopenia with thrombotic complications requires differentiating between classic heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) and its more recently recognized variant, autoimmune HIT (aHIT). The pathophysiological distinctions explain both the typical features of HIT and the unusual laboratory and clinical findings observed in this case.

In classic HIT, the initiating event is the formation of immune complexes between heparin and platelet factor 4 (PF4). PF4 is a cationic protein stored in platelet alpha granules and released upon platelet activation. When heparin is administered, this polyanion binds to PF4, forming multimolecular PF4–heparin complexes that expose neoantigens. In susceptible patients, these complexes trigger an immune response, with immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies forming against the PF4–heparin complex. These IgG antibodies bind to the complexes via their Fab regions, while their Fc portions engage FcγRIIa receptors on adjacent platelets. This cross-linking causes Fc receptor clustering, triggering potent platelet activation. Activated platelets release additional PF4, amplifyifying the cycle, and generate procoagulant microparticles. Concurrently, HIT antibodies activate endothelial cells and monocytes, promoting tissue factor expression. The net result is a profound prothrombotic state; HIT carries an approximate 15-fold increased relative risk of thrombosis, making it one of the most thrombogenic acquired conditions in clinical medicine.

For patients undergoing cardiac surgery, HIT typically manifests between postoperative days 5 and 10. This temporal window reflects the time required for a primary immune response to generate detectable antibody levels following heparin exposure during cardiopulmonary bypass. However, approximately 10% of post-cardiac surgery HIT cases exhibit an earlier platelet count decline, occurring before POD5. This phenomenon is linked to recent preoperative heparin exposure, which primes a rapid anamnestic immune response. In this case, the patient received unfractionated heparin during diagnostic cardiac catheterization just one week before surgery. This preoperative exposure explains the unusually early onset of HIT-related thrombocytopenia, which was already apparent by POD5 and reached profound levels by POD6.

Autoimmune HIT represents a distinct pathophysiological subset. While classic HIT antibodies require heparin to form the antigenic complex and trigger platelet activation, aHIT antibodies possess heparin-independent platelet-activating properties. These antibodies can recognize PF4 bound to endogenous polyanions—such as cell-surface glycosaminoglycans—or may recognize PF4 multimers that form spontaneously under certain conditions. Consequently, platelet activation persists even after heparin is discontinued. This case demonstrated a striking example of this phenomenon: the serotonin-release assay (SRA) showed 96% serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin, indicating robust platelet activation in the complete absence of exogenous heparin.

A particularly novel aspect of this case was the partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade. In classic HIT, adding the monoclonal antibody IV.3—which blocks the FcγRIIa receptor—should abolish serotonin release by preventing Fc-dependent platelet activation. In this patient, IV.3 only partially inhibited activation, with 76% serotonin release persisting despite Fc receptor blockade. This finding indicates that the patient's aHIT antibodies had unusual functional properties, perhaps reflecting exceptionally high affinity for PF4 complexes or activation through complementary pathways. To the authors' knowledge, this atypical SRA profile has not been previously reported. The initial designation of an "indeterminate" SRA result reflected laboratory uncertainty about this pattern, though it ultimately confirmed aHIT with unusually pathogenic antibodies.

The pathophysiology of aHIT explains the clinical severity observed in this case. The heparin-independent nature of platelet activation means that thrombocytopenia and hypercoagulability can persist or worsen despite stopping all heparin exposure. This patient's platelet count fell to a nadir of 13×10⁹/L—far lower than typically seen in classic HIT—and was accompanied by falling fibrinogen levels, rising D-dimer concentrations, and schistocyte formation. These laboratory abnormalities reflected a severe consumptive coagulopathy driven by ongoing platelet activation and thrombin generation. The cerebral infarction demonstrated the clinical consequence of this persistent hypercoagulability. Furthermore, the partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade may explain why high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin—which works in part by blocking Fc receptors—produced only a modest platelet count response, in contrast to the typically brisk recovery seen in most aHIT patients.

The evolution of antibody characteristics over time provides additional insight into aHIT pathophysiology. Serial SRA testing showed that the antibody profile gradually shifted: later samples exhibited more characteristic aHIT patterns, and by POD124, the reactivity profile resembled classic heparin-dependent HIT, with minimal activation in the absence of heparin. This waning of heparin-independent properties demonstrates that aHIT antibodies are not a static entity but evolve as the immune response matures and eventually resolves.
