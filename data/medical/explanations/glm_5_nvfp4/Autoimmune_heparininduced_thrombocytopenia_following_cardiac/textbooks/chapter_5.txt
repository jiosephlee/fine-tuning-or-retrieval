Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

Chapter 5: ## Clinical Outcome and Key Lessons

The patient achieved a favorable outcome despite the severity of his clinical presentation. Following the transition to fondaparinux anticoagulation on postoperative day 14, the platelet count gradually recovered, reaching 155×10⁹/L by POD62 and normalizing at 202×10⁹/L by POD87. This prolonged recovery timeline—extending over approximately two months—reflects the persistent nature of autoimmune HIT (aHIT) antibodies compared to classic HIT, where platelet counts typically recover within 1 to 2 weeks after heparin discontinuation. Importantly, the patient made a complete neurological recovery, with resolution of the left parietal lobe infarct deficits and no residual neurological abnormalities. Fondaparinux was continued until POD65, at which point the patient resumed his prior anticoagulation regimen of apixaban for long-term management of atrial fibrillation.

Serial serotonin-release assay (SRA) testing provided valuable insight into the natural history of aHIT antibodies. Initial testing on POD5 demonstrated the characteristic aHIT pattern: strong serotonin release in the absence of heparin (96% release), indicating heparin-independent platelet-activating properties. Over subsequent months, the antibody profile evolved. Several samples showed SRA reactivity patterns more characteristic of aHIT, and notably, the final sample tested at POD124—after platelet count recovery—showed a reversion to a classic heparin-dependent HIT pattern, with only 15% serotonin release in the absence of heparin but 100% release at therapeutic heparin concentrations. This evolution from heparin-independent to heparin-dependent antibody activity mirrors the natural waning of pathogenic antibodies and provides retrospective confirmation of the aHIT diagnosis.

This case presents three novel aspects that expand the known spectrum of aHIT. First, the onset of HIT-related thrombocytopenia began before POD5, which is atypical given that classic HIT typically manifests between POD5 and POD10 following intraoperative heparin exposure. This early onset was attributable to preoperative heparin administration during cardiac catheterization—one week prior to surgery—underscoring that antecedent heparin exposure can accelerate the temporal presentation of HIT. Second, the platelet count nadir of 13×10⁹/L was profoundly low, far exceeding the typical HIT threshold of approximately 50% platelet count decline from postoperative peak. This degree of thrombocytopenia, accompanied by falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimer, and transient schistocytosis, reflected a severe consumptive coagulopathy pathognomonic of aHIT. Third, and most uniquely, the patient's serum demonstrated only partial inhibition by the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3, a serological profile not previously reported in aHIT. This partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade indicates the presence of unusually reactive antibodies capable of platelet activation through pathways that may extend beyond classical FcγRIIa receptor clustering.

Several clinical pearls emerge from this case. First, preoperative heparin exposure—whether from recent cardiac catheterization, hemodialysis, or other indications—can precipitate early-onset HIT or aHIT, with thrombocytopenia manifesting before the conventional POD5 threshold. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for HIT in any postoperative patient with unexplained thrombocytopenia, regardless of timing, if there has been antecedent heparin exposure within the preceding 100 days. Second, atypical or "indeterminate" SRA results should raise immediate suspicion for aHIT rather than prompting dismissal of the HIT diagnosis. An "indeterminate" pattern—particularly strong serotonin release in the absence of heparin or incomplete inhibition by IV.3—may represent pathogenic aHIT antibodies rather than a false-positive result. Third, partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade signals the presence of highly reactive aHIT antibodies, which often correlate with severe clinical manifestations including profound thrombocytopenia, delayed platelet recovery, and increased thrombotic risk. Such patients may require more aggressive immunomodulatory therapy, including high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin, and close monitoring for consumptive coagulopathy.

The case reinforces the importance of considering aHIT in the differential diagnosis of severe, persistent thrombocytopenia with thrombosis following heparin exposure, particularly after cardiac surgery. While classic HIT remains far more common, aHIT represents an important diagnostic entity that carries substantial morbidity if unrecognized. The combination of strong-positive PF4/polyanion ELISA with an atypical SRA profile, especially in the setting of severe thrombocytopenia and thrombosis, should prompt early initiation of alternative anticoagulation and consideration of immunomodulatory therapy, even before confirmatory testing is complete. Early recognition and appropriate management, as demonstrated in this case, can lead to excellent outcomes despite the severity of the initial presentation.
