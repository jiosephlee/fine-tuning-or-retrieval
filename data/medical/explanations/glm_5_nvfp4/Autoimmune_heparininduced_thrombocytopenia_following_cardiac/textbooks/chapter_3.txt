Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

Chapter 3: ## Diagnostic Workup and Interpretation of Atypical Laboratory Findings

The diagnostic evaluation of thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery requires a systematic approach, as the differential diagnosis encompasses several distinct entities with overlapping features. In this case, the combination of severe thrombocytopenia, thrombotic stroke, and atypical laboratory findings ultimately led to the diagnosis of autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT), but arriving at this conclusion required careful interpretation of both standard and specialized tests.

**Differential Diagnosis of Post-Cardiac Surgery Thrombocytopenia**

Thrombocytopenia is common after cardiac surgery, and the initial step is to distinguish between common, expected causes and those suggesting a pathologic immune process. Cardiogenic shock and critical illness can cause thrombocytopenia through consumption and marrow suppression, but this patient maintained normal hemodynamics, making this unlikely. Infection-related thrombocytopenia was ruled out by negative blood and urine cultures. Rare causes were also considered and excluded: post-transfusion purpura was impossible since no transfusions were given; postoperative thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) was unlikely given normal haptoglobin and initial absence of schistocytes; and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia from newly administered medications was temporally inconsistent, as the platelet count decline began too early relative to the drugs administered perioperatively.

A key diagnostic clue emerged when the clinical team recognized that the patient had received unfractionated heparin (UFH) during preoperative cardiac catheterization, one week before surgery. Although HIT classically begins 5–10 days after heparin exposure, this patient's platelet count decline began before postoperative day 5—a timeline that initially seemed inconsistent with HIT from intraoperative heparin. However, preoperative heparin exposure provided a plausible explanation for early-onset HIT, as the immune response can be triggered by prior exposure, leading to a more rapid platelet count decline upon re-exposure.

**Initial Laboratory Evaluation**

Basic laboratory studies provided important negative findings that narrowed the differential. The hemoglobin remained stable following expected surgical blood loss, arguing against ongoing hemorrhage. The white blood cell count and differential were normal, inconsistent with severe infection. Haptoglobin was elevated, as expected in the postoperative acute phase response, but importantly showed no evidence of hemolysis. Coagulation studies revealed normal prothrombin time and partial thromboplastin time, with elevated fibrinogen—an acute phase reactant typically markedly elevated after cardiac surgery. This constellation of findings argued against disseminated intravascular coagulation and other consumptive coagulopathies at this early stage.

**HIT Antibody Testing: Screening Immunoassay**

HIT testing begins with a screening immunoassay. In this case, the PF4/polyanion enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) yielded a strongly positive result at 2.2 optical density (OD) units, well above the reference range of less than 0.4 OD units. The magnitude of positivity correlates with the probability of HIT; strong-positive results (typically defined as OD >1.0–1.5) substantially increase the likelihood that thrombocytopenia is due to HIT rather than other causes. However, ELISA detects both pathogenic and non-pathogenic antibodies, and a positive result alone does not confirm HIT. Functional platelet activation assays are required to identify antibodies with pathologic clinical significance.

**Serotonin-Release Assay: Typical and Atypical Patterns**

The serotonin-release assay (SRA) is a functional assay that detects HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets. The assay measures the release of radioactive serotonin from donor platelets after incubation with patient serum at various heparin concentrations. In classic HIT, patient serum causes strong serotonin release at therapeutic heparin concentrations (0.1–0.3 U/mL), with minimal release in the absence of heparin and at supratherapeutic concentrations (100 U/mL). The suppression of activation at very high heparin concentrations reflects the disruption of PF4-heparin immune complexes and is a hallmark of HIT.

In this patient, the SRA initially yielded an "indeterminate" result that required careful reinterpretation. The serum caused strong serotonin release at therapeutic heparin concentrations (97% and 99% at 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL, respectively) and appropriately minimal release at 100 U/mL—findings consistent with HIT. However, two atypical features were present that pointed toward autoimmune HIT rather than classic HIT.

First, the patient's serum caused strong serotonin release in the absence of heparin (96% at 0 U/mL). This heparin-independent platelet activation is characteristic of aHIT and indicates that the antibodies can activate platelets without requiring exogenous heparin. These pathologic antibodies recognize PF4 in a conformation that mimics the PF4-heparin complex, effectively functioning as autoantibodies.

Second, the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3), which normally inhibits platelet activation by preventing HIT antibodies from binding to platelet FcγIIa receptors, produced only partial inhibition. In classic HIT, IV.3 typically reduces serotonin release to less than 20%. In this case, 76% serotonin release persisted despite IV.3 blockade. This partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade represents a novel serological profile indicating unusually pathogenic antibodies with enhanced affinity or avidity for platelet activation. To our knowledge, this finding has not been previously reported in aHIT.

**Diagnostic Criteria for Autoimmune HIT**

The diagnosis of aHIT was ultimately established through clinical, laboratory, and serological criteria. The patient exhibited hallmark clinical features: severe thrombocytopenia (nadir 13×10⁹/L), thrombosis (parietal stroke), and delayed platelet count recovery despite anticoagulation. The laboratory profile—heparin-independent platelet activation on SRA—confirmed the autoimmune nature of the HIT antibodies. The atypical partial resistance to IV.3 inhibition further characterized this as an unusually severe serological phenotype.

Follow-up serological testing supported the diagnosis. Subsequent samples obtained during the patient's prolonged recovery showed SRA profiles more characteristic of aHIT, and the final sample at day 124 demonstrated a classic heparin-dependent pattern, reflecting waning of the autoimmune antibodies. This evolution from heparin-independent to heparin-dependent reactivity provides additional confirmation that the initial diagnosis was aHIT.

The initial designation of an "indeterminate" SRA result illustrates a critical diagnostic pitfall: laboratory reports may not recognize atypical aHIT patterns, and clinicians must interpret results in context. Awareness that partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade can occur in severe aHIT may prevent missed diagnoses in future cases.
