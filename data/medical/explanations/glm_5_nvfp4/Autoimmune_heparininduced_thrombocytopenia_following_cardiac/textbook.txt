Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery"

## Clinical Presentation and Initial Postoperative Course

The patient was a man in his 70s with a two-year history of exertional dyspnea and longstanding atrial fibrillation, for which he was anticoagulated with apixaban 5 mg twice daily. During evaluation of a newly detected systolic murmur, he underwent diagnostic cardiac catheterization. An important detail emerged: the patient had not held his apixaban prior to the procedure, which influenced the cardiologist's approach. Rather than delay the procedure or reverse the anticoagulation, the cardiologist proceeded via a transradial artery approach—a route associated with lower bleeding risk than the femoral approach—and administered a reduced dose of unfractionated heparin (UFH), only 2000 units. This decision proved clinically appropriate for the immediate situation but had significant implications for the patient's subsequent course. The angiogram revealed 60% stenosis of the obtuse marginal coronary artery, moderate aortic stenosis, and a dilated ascending aorta, leading to a recommendation for surgical intervention one week later.

The cardiac surgery was performed using cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), with intraoperative anticoagulation achieved through administration of 45,000 units of UFH. UFH remains the standard anticoagulant for cardiac surgery with CPB due to several favorable properties: it inhibits thrombus formation on artificial surfaces, has non-renal and non-hepatic clearance (making it suitable for patients with organ dysfunction), allows for rapid intraoperative monitoring via activated clotting time, and is inexpensive. The surgical procedures included aortic valve replacement with a bioprosthetic valve, replacement of the ascending aorta with a Dacron graft, a single coronary artery bypass graft using a saphenous vein to the obtuse marginal artery, and left atrial appendage excision. The operation proceeded uneventfully without major blood loss or need for transfusion, and the patient was weaned from CPB with excellent hemodynamics.

Understanding normal postoperative platelet kinetics after cardiac surgery is essential for recognizing pathological thrombocytopenia in this setting. Platelet counts typically drop by approximately 30–50% within the first 24–48 hours after CPB due to hemodilution, platelet consumption at surgical sites, and transient platelet activation by the bypass circuit. This early decline usually reaches a nadir around postoperative day 2 or 3, followed by a gradual rebound with reactive thrombocytosis peaking around postoperative day 14. In this patient, the immediate preoperative platelet count was 210×10⁹/L, and on postoperative day 1 (POD1), it was 155×10⁹/L—a relatively modest decline consistent with expected early postoperative changes. However, what followed was distinctly abnormal: rather than stabilizing or recovering, the platelet count progressively fell to 50×10⁹/L by POD5.

Routine venous thromboembolism prophylaxis with low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) was initiated on POD1, with 5000 units of dalteparin administered subcutaneously, increased to 7500 units on POD2 through POD4. The patient appeared clinically stable apart from postoperative delirium, a common occurrence after cardiac surgery in elderly patients. However, the combination of progressive thrombocytopenia and worsening delirium raised clinical concern. When the platelet count dropped further to 26×10⁹/L on POD6, and given the lack of focal neurological findings but the presence of altered mental status, computed tomography of the brain was obtained. This imaging revealed an acute cerebral infarct in the left parietal lobe, measuring 3.2 cm × 1.8 cm with extension through the cerebral cortex. The discovery of stroke in the setting of unexplained progressive thrombocytopenia dramatically narrowed the differential diagnosis and pointed toward a thrombotic microangiopathy—most notably heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT).

A crucial temporal detail must be emphasized: classic HIT typically manifests 5–10 days after heparin exposure, corresponding to the time required for formation of pathogenic antibodies. In this patient, the platelet count decline began before POD5, which would be considered too early if the intraoperative heparin on the day of surgery were the only exposure. However, the preoperative cardiac catheterization one week earlier, during which 2000 units of UFH were administered, provided the necessary antecedent sensitization. This timing illustrates an important clinical principle: prior heparin exposure, even in relatively small doses and even 7–100 days earlier, can prime the immune system such that re-exposure triggers a rapid anamnestic response with accelerated onset of HIT. The clinical team's recognition of this preoperative exposure was essential for correctly interpreting the unusually early presentation of thrombocytopenia and thrombosis, ultimately leading to appropriate diagnostic testing and management.

## Pathophysiology: Classic HIT versus Autoimmune HIT

Understanding why this patient developed such severe thrombocytopenia with thrombotic complications requires differentiating between classic heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) and its more recently recognized variant, autoimmune HIT (aHIT). The pathophysiological distinctions explain both the typical features of HIT and the unusual laboratory and clinical findings observed in this case.

In classic HIT, the initiating event is the formation of immune complexes between heparin and platelet factor 4 (PF4). PF4 is a cationic protein stored in platelet alpha granules and released upon platelet activation. When heparin is administered, this polyanion binds to PF4, forming multimolecular PF4–heparin complexes that expose neoantigens. In susceptible patients, these complexes trigger an immune response, with immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies forming against the PF4–heparin complex. These IgG antibodies bind to the complexes via their Fab regions, while their Fc portions engage FcγRIIa receptors on adjacent platelets. This cross-linking causes Fc receptor clustering, triggering potent platelet activation. Activated platelets release additional PF4, amplifyifying the cycle, and generate procoagulant microparticles. Concurrently, HIT antibodies activate endothelial cells and monocytes, promoting tissue factor expression. The net result is a profound prothrombotic state; HIT carries an approximate 15-fold increased relative risk of thrombosis, making it one of the most thrombogenic acquired conditions in clinical medicine.

For patients undergoing cardiac surgery, HIT typically manifests between postoperative days 5 and 10. This temporal window reflects the time required for a primary immune response to generate detectable antibody levels following heparin exposure during cardiopulmonary bypass. However, approximately 10% of post-cardiac surgery HIT cases exhibit an earlier platelet count decline, occurring before POD5. This phenomenon is linked to recent preoperative heparin exposure, which primes a rapid anamnestic immune response. In this case, the patient received unfractionated heparin during diagnostic cardiac catheterization just one week before surgery. This preoperative exposure explains the unusually early onset of HIT-related thrombocytopenia, which was already apparent by POD5 and reached profound levels by POD6.

Autoimmune HIT represents a distinct pathophysiological subset. While classic HIT antibodies require heparin to form the antigenic complex and trigger platelet activation, aHIT antibodies possess heparin-independent platelet-activating properties. These antibodies can recognize PF4 bound to endogenous polyanions—such as cell-surface glycosaminoglycans—or may recognize PF4 multimers that form spontaneously under certain conditions. Consequently, platelet activation persists even after heparin is discontinued. This case demonstrated a striking example of this phenomenon: the serotonin-release assay (SRA) showed 96% serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin, indicating robust platelet activation in the complete absence of exogenous heparin.

A particularly novel aspect of this case was the partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade. In classic HIT, adding the monoclonal antibody IV.3—which blocks the FcγRIIa receptor—should abolish serotonin release by preventing Fc-dependent platelet activation. In this patient, IV.3 only partially inhibited activation, with 76% serotonin release persisting despite Fc receptor blockade. This finding indicates that the patient's aHIT antibodies had unusual functional properties, perhaps reflecting exceptionally high affinity for PF4 complexes or activation through complementary pathways. To the authors' knowledge, this atypical SRA profile has not been previously reported. The initial designation of an "indeterminate" SRA result reflected laboratory uncertainty about this pattern, though it ultimately confirmed aHIT with unusually pathogenic antibodies.

The pathophysiology of aHIT explains the clinical severity observed in this case. The heparin-independent nature of platelet activation means that thrombocytopenia and hypercoagulability can persist or worsen despite stopping all heparin exposure. This patient's platelet count fell to a nadir of 13×10⁹/L—far lower than typically seen in classic HIT—and was accompanied by falling fibrinogen levels, rising D-dimer concentrations, and schistocyte formation. These laboratory abnormalities reflected a severe consumptive coagulopathy driven by ongoing platelet activation and thrombin generation. The cerebral infarction demonstrated the clinical consequence of this persistent hypercoagulability. Furthermore, the partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade may explain why high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin—which works in part by blocking Fc receptors—produced only a modest platelet count response, in contrast to the typically brisk recovery seen in most aHIT patients.

The evolution of antibody characteristics over time provides additional insight into aHIT pathophysiology. Serial SRA testing showed that the antibody profile gradually shifted: later samples exhibited more characteristic aHIT patterns, and by POD124, the reactivity profile resembled classic heparin-dependent HIT, with minimal activation in the absence of heparin. This waning of heparin-independent properties demonstrates that aHIT antibodies are not a static entity but evolve as the immune response matures and eventually resolves.

## Diagnostic Workup and Interpretation of Atypical Laboratory Findings

The diagnostic evaluation of thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery requires a systematic approach, as the differential diagnosis encompasses several distinct entities with overlapping features. In this case, the combination of severe thrombocytopenia, thrombotic stroke, and atypical laboratory findings ultimately led to the diagnosis of autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT), but arriving at this conclusion required careful interpretation of both standard and specialized tests.

**Differential Diagnosis of Post-Cardiac Surgery Thrombocytopenia**

Thrombocytopenia is common after cardiac surgery, and the initial step is to distinguish between common, expected causes and those suggesting a pathologic immune process. Cardiogenic shock and critical illness can cause thrombocytopenia through consumption and marrow suppression, but this patient maintained normal hemodynamics, making this unlikely. Infection-related thrombocytopenia was ruled out by negative blood and urine cultures. Rare causes were also considered and excluded: post-transfusion purpura was impossible since no transfusions were given; postoperative thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) was unlikely given normal haptoglobin and initial absence of schistocytes; and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia from newly administered medications was temporally inconsistent, as the platelet count decline began too early relative to the drugs administered perioperatively.

A key diagnostic clue emerged when the clinical team recognized that the patient had received unfractionated heparin (UFH) during preoperative cardiac catheterization, one week before surgery. Although HIT classically begins 5–10 days after heparin exposure, this patient's platelet count decline began before postoperative day 5—a timeline that initially seemed inconsistent with HIT from intraoperative heparin. However, preoperative heparin exposure provided a plausible explanation for early-onset HIT, as the immune response can be triggered by prior exposure, leading to a more rapid platelet count decline upon re-exposure.

**Initial Laboratory Evaluation**

Basic laboratory studies provided important negative findings that narrowed the differential. The hemoglobin remained stable following expected surgical blood loss, arguing against ongoing hemorrhage. The white blood cell count and differential were normal, inconsistent with severe infection. Haptoglobin was elevated, as expected in the postoperative acute phase response, but importantly showed no evidence of hemolysis. Coagulation studies revealed normal prothrombin time and partial thromboplastin time, with elevated fibrinogen—an acute phase reactant typically markedly elevated after cardiac surgery. This constellation of findings argued against disseminated intravascular coagulation and other consumptive coagulopathies at this early stage.

**HIT Antibody Testing: Screening Immunoassay**

HIT testing begins with a screening immunoassay. In this case, the PF4/polyanion enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) yielded a strongly positive result at 2.2 optical density (OD) units, well above the reference range of less than 0.4 OD units. The magnitude of positivity correlates with the probability of HIT; strong-positive results (typically defined as OD >1.0–1.5) substantially increase the likelihood that thrombocytopenia is due to HIT rather than other causes. However, ELISA detects both pathogenic and non-pathogenic antibodies, and a positive result alone does not confirm HIT. Functional platelet activation assays are required to identify antibodies with pathologic clinical significance.

**Serotonin-Release Assay: Typical and Atypical Patterns**

The serotonin-release assay (SRA) is a functional assay that detects HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets. The assay measures the release of radioactive serotonin from donor platelets after incubation with patient serum at various heparin concentrations. In classic HIT, patient serum causes strong serotonin release at therapeutic heparin concentrations (0.1–0.3 U/mL), with minimal release in the absence of heparin and at supratherapeutic concentrations (100 U/mL). The suppression of activation at very high heparin concentrations reflects the disruption of PF4-heparin immune complexes and is a hallmark of HIT.

In this patient, the SRA initially yielded an "indeterminate" result that required careful reinterpretation. The serum caused strong serotonin release at therapeutic heparin concentrations (97% and 99% at 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL, respectively) and appropriately minimal release at 100 U/mL—findings consistent with HIT. However, two atypical features were present that pointed toward autoimmune HIT rather than classic HIT.

First, the patient's serum caused strong serotonin release in the absence of heparin (96% at 0 U/mL). This heparin-independent platelet activation is characteristic of aHIT and indicates that the antibodies can activate platelets without requiring exogenous heparin. These pathologic antibodies recognize PF4 in a conformation that mimics the PF4-heparin complex, effectively functioning as autoantibodies.

Second, the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3), which normally inhibits platelet activation by preventing HIT antibodies from binding to platelet FcγIIa receptors, produced only partial inhibition. In classic HIT, IV.3 typically reduces serotonin release to less than 20%. In this case, 76% serotonin release persisted despite IV.3 blockade. This partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade represents a novel serological profile indicating unusually pathogenic antibodies with enhanced affinity or avidity for platelet activation. To our knowledge, this finding has not been previously reported in aHIT.

**Diagnostic Criteria for Autoimmune HIT**

The diagnosis of aHIT was ultimately established through clinical, laboratory, and serological criteria. The patient exhibited hallmark clinical features: severe thrombocytopenia (nadir 13×10⁹/L), thrombosis (parietal stroke), and delayed platelet count recovery despite anticoagulation. The laboratory profile—heparin-independent platelet activation on SRA—confirmed the autoimmune nature of the HIT antibodies. The atypical partial resistance to IV.3 inhibition further characterized this as an unusually severe serological phenotype.

Follow-up serological testing supported the diagnosis. Subsequent samples obtained during the patient's prolonged recovery showed SRA profiles more characteristic of aHIT, and the final sample at day 124 demonstrated a classic heparin-dependent pattern, reflecting waning of the autoimmune antibodies. This evolution from heparin-independent to heparin-dependent reactivity provides additional confirmation that the initial diagnosis was aHIT.

The initial designation of an "indeterminate" SRA result illustrates a critical diagnostic pitfall: laboratory reports may not recognize atypical aHIT patterns, and clinicians must interpret results in context. Awareness that partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade can occur in severe aHIT may prevent missed diagnoses in future cases.

## Management Strategy and Treatment Evolution

The management of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) requires prompt intervention to mitigate the risk of life-threatening thrombosis while avoiding bleeding complications. In this case, the therapeutic strategy evolved through several phases as the clinical team responded to the patient's unusually severe and persistent autoimmune HIT (aHIT) phenotype.

The cornerstone of HIT management is immediate discontinuation of all heparin products. In this patient, low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) thromboprophylaxis with dalteparin was stopped on postoperative day 5 (POD5) when the platelet count fell to 50×10⁹/L, raising clinical suspicion for HIT. Importantly, LMWH is contraindicated in HIT because it can cross-react with HIT antibodies and perpetuate platelet activation. The initial substitution with prophylactic-dose fondaparinux (2.5 mg) was a reasonable interim measure, as fondaparinux has low cross-reactivity with HIT antibodies. However, once the diagnosis of HIT was confirmed by strong-positive ELISA and atypical serotonin-release assay (SRA) results, more aggressive therapeutic intervention became necessary.

Argatroban was selected as the initial therapeutic anticoagulant. As a direct thrombin inhibitor, argatroban acts independently of antithrombin to directly inhibit both clot-bound and free thrombin. This mechanism is advantageous in HIT, where thrombin generation is markedly amplified. Argatroban is administered as a continuous intravenous infusion with a short half-life of approximately 40 to 50 minutes, allowing rapid titration and reversal if bleeding occurs. In this patient, dosing started at 0.75 mcg/kg/min with a target partial thromboplastin time (PTT) of 55 to 85 seconds. The selection of argatroban over therapeutic-dose fondaparinux was deliberate: given the patient's recent stroke and profound thrombocytopenia (nadir 13×10⁹/L), the shorter half-life of argatroban provided a safety margin in case hemorrhagic complications arose.

High-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered to address the autoimmune nature of this patient's HIT. In aHIT, antibodies possess heparin-independent platelet-activating properties, meaning they can trigger platelet activation even in the absence of heparin exposure. IVIG is thought to work by blocking platelet FcγIIa receptors, thereby preventing pathogenic antibody binding and subsequent platelet activation. The patient received 100 g on two consecutive days, with a third 100-g dose given due to lack of initial platelet count response. Notably, the platelet count rose only modestly from 13×10⁹/L on POD7 to an interim peak of 54×10⁹/L by POD14—a much more attenuated response than typically expected with IVIG in aHIT. This blunted response signaled the severity of the autoimmune process.

Serial monitoring of fibrinogen, D-dimer, and peripheral blood smears provided critical insight into the adequacy of anticoagulation. HIT can trigger a consumptive coagulopathy characterized by platelet-driven thrombin generation, fibrinogen depletion, and microangiopathic hemolysis. In this patient, fibrinogen levels fell from 6.59 g/L on POD5 to a nadir of 2.49 g/L by POD17, while D-dimer levels peaked around POD12. Schistocytes appeared on peripheral blood films from POD9 to POD16. Together, these findings indicated ongoing, severe HIT-associated hypercoagulability despite argatroban therapy.

The constellation of falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimer, and persistent schistocytes signaled that argatroban was not adequately controlling the coagulopathy. On POD14, the patient was transitioned from argatroban to therapeutic-dose fondaparinux (10 mg once daily subcutaneously). Fondaparinux is a synthetic pentasaccharide that indirectly inhibits factor Xa through antithrombin-dependent mechanisms. Its once-daily subcutaneous administration offered practical advantages over continuous intravenous argatroban infusion. Because the patient's antithrombin level was mildly reduced at 0.70 U/mL (reference 0.80–1.40 U/mL), supplemental antithrombin concentrate (1000 U) was administered prior to initiating fondaparinux to ensure therapeutic efficacy.

The transition to fondaparinux proved effective. Over the subsequent weeks, the platelet count gradually recovered to 155×10⁹/L by POD62, with complete neurological recovery. Fondaparinux was continued until POD65, at which point the patient resumed apixaban 5 mg twice daily for long-term anticoagulation of his atrial fibrillation. The decision to restart a direct oral anticoagulant was made after sustained platelet count recovery and resolution of the hypercoagulable state. This case illustrates the importance of vigilant monitoring and flexible treatment strategies in managing aHIT, where standard HIT therapies may require augmentation or substitution based on dynamic clinical and laboratory parameters.

## Clinical Outcome and Key Lessons

The patient achieved a favorable outcome despite the severity of his clinical presentation. Following the transition to fondaparinux anticoagulation on postoperative day 14, the platelet count gradually recovered, reaching 155×10⁹/L by POD62 and normalizing at 202×10⁹/L by POD87. This prolonged recovery timeline—extending over approximately two months—reflects the persistent nature of autoimmune HIT (aHIT) antibodies compared to classic HIT, where platelet counts typically recover within 1 to 2 weeks after heparin discontinuation. Importantly, the patient made a complete neurological recovery, with resolution of the left parietal lobe infarct deficits and no residual neurological abnormalities. Fondaparinux was continued until POD65, at which point the patient resumed his prior anticoagulation regimen of apixaban for long-term management of atrial fibrillation.

Serial serotonin-release assay (SRA) testing provided valuable insight into the natural history of aHIT antibodies. Initial testing on POD5 demonstrated the characteristic aHIT pattern: strong serotonin release in the absence of heparin (96% release), indicating heparin-independent platelet-activating properties. Over subsequent months, the antibody profile evolved. Several samples showed SRA reactivity patterns more characteristic of aHIT, and notably, the final sample tested at POD124—after platelet count recovery—showed a reversion to a classic heparin-dependent HIT pattern, with only 15% serotonin release in the absence of heparin but 100% release at therapeutic heparin concentrations. This evolution from heparin-independent to heparin-dependent antibody activity mirrors the natural waning of pathogenic antibodies and provides retrospective confirmation of the aHIT diagnosis.

This case presents three novel aspects that expand the known spectrum of aHIT. First, the onset of HIT-related thrombocytopenia began before POD5, which is atypical given that classic HIT typically manifests between POD5 and POD10 following intraoperative heparin exposure. This early onset was attributable to preoperative heparin administration during cardiac catheterization—one week prior to surgery—underscoring that antecedent heparin exposure can accelerate the temporal presentation of HIT. Second, the platelet count nadir of 13×10⁹/L was profoundly low, far exceeding the typical HIT threshold of approximately 50% platelet count decline from postoperative peak. This degree of thrombocytopenia, accompanied by falling fibrinogen, rising D-dimer, and transient schistocytosis, reflected a severe consumptive coagulopathy pathognomonic of aHIT. Third, and most uniquely, the patient's serum demonstrated only partial inhibition by the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3, a serological profile not previously reported in aHIT. This partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade indicates the presence of unusually reactive antibodies capable of platelet activation through pathways that may extend beyond classical FcγRIIa receptor clustering.

Several clinical pearls emerge from this case. First, preoperative heparin exposure—whether from recent cardiac catheterization, hemodialysis, or other indications—can precipitate early-onset HIT or aHIT, with thrombocytopenia manifesting before the conventional POD5 threshold. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for HIT in any postoperative patient with unexplained thrombocytopenia, regardless of timing, if there has been antecedent heparin exposure within the preceding 100 days. Second, atypical or "indeterminate" SRA results should raise immediate suspicion for aHIT rather than prompting dismissal of the HIT diagnosis. An "indeterminate" pattern—particularly strong serotonin release in the absence of heparin or incomplete inhibition by IV.3—may represent pathogenic aHIT antibodies rather than a false-positive result. Third, partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade signals the presence of highly reactive aHIT antibodies, which often correlate with severe clinical manifestations including profound thrombocytopenia, delayed platelet recovery, and increased thrombotic risk. Such patients may require more aggressive immunomodulatory therapy, including high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin, and close monitoring for consumptive coagulopathy.

The case reinforces the importance of considering aHIT in the differential diagnosis of severe, persistent thrombocytopenia with thrombosis following heparin exposure, particularly after cardiac surgery. While classic HIT remains far more common, aHIT represents an important diagnostic entity that carries substantial morbidity if unrecognized. The combination of strong-positive PF4/polyanion ELISA with an atypical SRA profile, especially in the setting of severe thrombocytopenia and thrombosis, should prompt early initiation of alternative anticoagulation and consideration of immunomodulatory therapy, even before confirmatory testing is complete. Early recognition and appropriate management, as demonstrated in this case, can lead to excellent outcomes despite the severity of the initial presentation.
