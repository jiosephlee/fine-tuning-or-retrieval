Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# Eculizumab at the Crossroads: Balancing Complement Blockade and Infection Risk

## When the Cure Could Kill: A Post-Infectious Dilemma

Let's set the scene: you've got a patient who just survived fulminant sepsis from *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*—that slow-growing, fastidious gram-negative rod that lives in dog mouths and has a mortality rate approaching 30%. She's been through DIC, pancytopenia, complete anuria, and dialysis dependence. She's finally turning a corner. Blood cultures are negative. And then... she develops sudden hemolysis, thrombocytopenia, and you realize this isn't just recovering sepsis anymore. She's now got complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS).

Here's your therapeutic tightrope: the standard of care is eculizumab—terminal complement blockade. But terminal complement is critical for host defense against encapsulated organisms, including *Neisseria* and, ironically, *Capnocytophaga*. So do you pull the trigger on complement inhibition in someone who just survived a serious encapsulated organism infection? That's the crossroads.

## The Infection That Triggers the Storm

This case is remarkable because it illustrates how an infection can unmask complement dysregulation even without an identifiable genetic predisposition. Our patient had no pathogenic complement gene variants on extended testing, yet she developed classic CM-HUS triggered by *C. canimorsus*.

The pathophysiology is fascinating: *C. canimorsus* activates complement pathways, and while complement helps clear the pathogen, it can simultaneously trigger uncontrolled endothelial damage in susceptible individuals. This creates a clinical paradox—you need complement to fight the infection, but complement is now destroying the kidneys.

## Timing Is Everything: When to Pull the Trigger

The evidence is clear: early initiation of eculizumab is the single most important predictor of renal recovery in CM-HUS. Delayed initiation correlates with incomplete renal recovery and progression to end-stage kidney disease.

In this case, eculizumab was initiated **after blood cultures became negative**—a pragmatic approach that minimized the risk of uncontrolled infection while still allowing for timely complement blockade. The patient received induction dosing (900 mg IV weekly for 4 weeks) and achieved hematological remission with partial renal recovery (creatinine stabilized at 175 μmol/L).

The key lesson: don't wait for perfect clarity. Once you've excluded TTP (ADAMTS13 >10%) and STEC-HUS, and you have evidence of TMA with organ dysfunction, the clock is ticking on renal recovery.

## The Vaccination Mandate: Non-Negotiable

Before eculizumab touches the infusion bag, meningococcal vaccination is mandatory. This patient received both Menveo (serogroups A, C, Y, W) and Bexsero (serogroup B)—covering the major pathogenic serogroups.

But here's the catch: ideally, vaccines should be administered at least 2 weeks before complement blockade to allow adequate immune response. In this case, prophylactic phenoxymethylpenicillin was given because that 2-week window wasn't feasible given the urgency of her clinical deterioration.

The practical approach: vaccinate immediately, start prophylactic antibiotics (penicillin is the standard), and proceed with eculizumab if the clinical situation demands it.

## Plasma Exchange: Bridge, Not Destination

This case also highlights the limited role of PEX in CM-HUS. Our patient received three sessions of plasma exchange, and diuresis returned after the final session—**before** eculizumab was administered. But this was transient stabilization, not sustained recovery.

PEX removes circulating complement fragments and inflammatory mediators, but it doesn't correct the underlying complement dysregulation. It's a bridge to definitive therapy, not the therapy itself. In contrast, TTP requires PEX as lifesaving treatment—another reason why ADAMTS13 testing is critical early in any TMA workup.

## How Long Do We Treat? The Reassessment Question

The duration of complement blockade remains an ongoing clinical question. This patient continues maintenance eculizumab (1200 mg IV every 2 weeks) with planned reassessment. The absence of identified pathogenic complement gene variants doesn't mean she can stop—infection-triggered CM-HUS can still warrant extended therapy given disease severity.

The current approach: continue complement blockade until renal function stabilizes, then reassess for discontinuation with close monitoring for relapse. Patients with identified pathogenic variants typically require longer-term therapy.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Normal C3/C4 does not exclude CM-HUS**—complement levels can remain within reference ranges during active disease. The diagnosis is clinicopathological and one of exclusion.
- **ADAMTS13 activity >10% effectively rules out TTP**—get this early. A PLASMIC score can help triage, but don't rely on it alone.
- **Early eculizumab = better renal outcomes**—delays in initiation correlate with progression to ESRD.
- **Timing matters for infection risk**—initiate complement blockade after bacteremia clears when possible, but don't delay unnecessarily.
- **Vaccinate, prophylax, proceed**—meningococcal vaccines (both quadrivalent and serogroup B) plus antibiotic prophylaxis if the 2-week window isn't feasible.
- **PEX is a bridge, not definitive therapy**—it buys time but doesn't address the underlying complement dysregulation.
- **Infection-triggered CM-HUS can occur without genetic predisposition**—don't wait for genetic results to start treatment if the clinical picture fits.

## The Bottom Line

CM-HUS following *C. canimorsus* infection is rare, but it illustrates a broader principle: any TMA that isn't TTP or STEC-HUS should prompt consideration of complement-mediated disease. The therapeutic window for renal recovery is finite, and the risks of complement blockade can be mitigated with proper vaccination, prophylaxis, and timing. When you're standing at that crossroads, remember—the infection may have triggered the storm, but the storm won't wait for perfect conditions to treat.
