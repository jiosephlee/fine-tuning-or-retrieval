Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# The TMA Triad: A Systematic Approach to MAHA, Thrombocytopenia, and AKI

## When Sepsis Masks Something Sinister

A woman in her late 40s presents with fulminant sepsis four days after a dog bite. She's hypotensive, mottled, hypoglycemic to 0.6 mmol/L, and has the kind of laboratory derangement that makes you do a double-take—pancytopenia, acute kidney injury, liver enzyme elevation, and frank DIC. You intubate, start broad-spectrum antibiotics, and chase the obvious diagnosis: *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteremia. Blood cultures confirm it. She gets better. Then she gets worse again—but differently.

Here's what makes this case absolutely worth your time: after her sepsis resolved and her DIC cleared, she developed **isolated hemolysis** with a new drop in hemoglobin from 111 to 68 g/L over six days. Her platelets never fully recovered. Her LDH climbed. And when someone finally looked at a peripheral smear, they found 15-20% schistocytes. She had a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA)—but it wasn't DIC, and it wasn't the initial presentation. It was complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS) triggered by the infection itself.

This case is a masterclass in why we need a systematic approach to TMA—and why empiric plasma exchange without a diagnostic framework can delay definitive care.

## The TMA Framework: What Are We Actually Looking At?

When you see the classic triad of **microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury**, you're in TMA territory. But TMA isn't a diagnosis—it's a syndrome with multiple distinct etiologies that require fundamentally different treatments. The three critical differentials in adults are:

**Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP)** – A medical emergency requiring immediate plasma exchange. Defined by severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10% activity). Miss this and mortality approaches 90%.

**Shiga toxin-producing E. coli HUS (STEC-HUS)** – The classic "bloody diarrhea preceded the renal failure by a week" presentation. Confirm with stool PCR.

**Complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS)** – Formerly called "atypical HUS," this is where complement regulation goes haywire. It's a diagnosis of exclusion, but early recognition matters because complement blockade with eculizumab dramatically improves outcomes.

The problem? These conditions can overlap clinically, and in septic patients, DIC muddies the waters further. That's why a systematic approach matters.

## Step 1: Rule Out TTP First—Because It Kills Fastest

In any adult with MAHA and thrombocytopenia, TTP is your first exclusion. The PLASMIC score helps risk-stratify while you're waiting for ADAMTS13 activity results:

**PLASMIC Score Components:**
- Platelets <30 × 10⁹/L
- Hemolysis (reticulocytes >2.5%, undetectable haptoglobin, or indirect bilirubin >34 μmol/L)
- No active cancer
- No history of solid organ or stem cell transplant
- MCV <90 fL
- INR <1.5
- Creatinine <2.0 mg/dL (176 μmol/L)

Score ≥6 carries high probability of TTP; score ≤4 makes it unlikely. Our patient scored 4/7—a low pretest probability. Her ADAMTS13 activity came back at 43.5%, effectively ruling out TTP.

Here's the clinical pearl: **do not wait for ADAMTS13 to initiate plasma exchange if clinical suspicion for TTP is high.** But if your PLASMIC score is low and you have a plausible alternative explanation for TMA (like severe sepsis with DIC), you can hold PEX while pursuing other diagnostics.

## Step 2: Ask About the Prodrome

STEC-HUS classically presents with bloody diarrhea 5-10 days before the onset of MAHA and AKI. Our patient had no gastrointestinal prodrome, and stool PCR for Shiga toxin was negative. That made STEC-HUS unlikely.

But here's where clinical nuance matters: not all STEC-HUS presents with classic bloody diarrhea, and not all TMAs with GI symptoms are STEC-HUS. If the story fits, test. If it doesn't fit, don't assume it's negative—still test.

## Step 3: Consider Complement-Mediated HUS—The Diagnosis of Exclusion

Once TTP and STEC-HUS are ruled out, CM-HUS becomes the working diagnosis. But here's what trips people up: **normal complement levels (C3, C4) do not exclude CM-HUS.** In our patient, standard complement tests were entirely normal. It was only specialized functional testing showing markedly reduced alternative pathway activity (AH50 of 6%) that supported the diagnosis.

The kidney biopsy in this case showed severe acute tubular necrosis, ischemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi, and scattered crescent formation—consistent with TMA. But biopsy isn't always necessary if the clinical picture is clear.

The key insight from this case: CM-HUS can be **triggered by infection** in patients without identifiable genetic complement variants. *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is rare, but it's one of several pathogens that can precipitate complement dysregulation in susceptible hosts.

## The Treatment Dilemma: PEX vs. Eculizumab

Plasma exchange is lifesaving in TTP. In CM-HUS, it's a bridge at best—removing circulating complement fragments and inflammatory mediators without correcting the underlying dysregulation. Our patient received three PEX sessions with return of spontaneous diuresis, but sustained hematological and renal recovery occurred only after eculizumab.

Here's the catch: **eculizumab blocks terminal complement, which is critical for host defense against encapsulated organisms.** Starting it during active bacteremia could be disastrous. Our patient received eculizumab only after blood cultures cleared, with meningococcal vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis in place.

The algorithm is straightforward but requires coordination:
1. Rule out TTP immediately (PLASMIC score, ADAMTS13)
2. Test for STEC (stool PCR)
3. If neither fits, consider CM-HUS and initiate workup
4. Use PEX as bridge if needed while awaiting definitive diagnosis
5. Start eculizumab once infection is controlled—earlier initiation predicts better renal recovery

## Key Take-Home Points

- **TMA is a syndrome, not a diagnosis.** Your job is to systematically rule out TTP, STEC-HUS, and then consider CM-HUS.
- **Use the PLASMIC score** to risk-stratify for TTP while awaiting ADAMTS13 activity. Score ≥6 = high probability; initiate PEX empirically.
- **ADAMTS13 <10% confirms TTP.** Activity >10%—as in this case (43.5%)—rules it out.
- **Normal C3/C4 does not exclude CM-HUS.** Specialized complement testing may be needed, and diagnosis is often clinicopathological.
- **Infections can trigger CM-HUS** even without identifiable genetic variants. Don't assume a patient needs a "second hit" if they have no prior history.
- **Eculizumab is the definitive treatment for CM-HUS**, not PEX. But hold it until bacteremia clears, and always vaccinate against meningococcus first.
- **Timing matters.** Earlier initiation of complement blockade correlates with better renal recovery. A delayed diagnosis means delayed definitive therapy.

This case reminds us that the most dangerous diagnostic trap isn't missing the rare diagnosis—it's stopping at the obvious one. Our patient had sepsis, DIC, and bacteremia. She also had CM-HUS triggered by that same infection. Recognizing the shift from "septic DIC" to "persistent TMA" required stepping back, looking at the smear, and running the algorithm.
