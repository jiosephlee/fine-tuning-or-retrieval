Title: Clinical Q&A about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

### [Morning Report] What are the key epidemiological features and clinical presentations of Capnocytophaga canimorsus infection, and which patient populations are at highest risk for severe disease?

**Epidemiology and Clinical Presentation of *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* Infection**

*Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is a slow-growing, encapsulated, gram-negative rod that resides as normal oropharyngeal flora in dogs and cats. Human infections are rare but important to recognize given their potential severity. Transmission occurs through bites, scratches, or even close animal contact—our patient developed fulminant illness after a dog bite to her upper lip.

The incubation period typically ranges from 1 to 8 days, though this case illustrates a classic presentation at 4 days post-exposure. Clinical manifestations span a wide spectrum: localized wound infections, cellulitis, and isolated meningitis at the milder end, to fulminant sepsis with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), multiorgan failure, and thrombotic microangiopathy at the most severe. Notably, the mortality rate approaches 30% despite broad antibiotic susceptibility.

Our patient's presentation exemplifies the severe end of this spectrum—she arrived with septic shock, profound pancytopenia, DIC, acute kidney injury, hypoglycemia, and lactic acidosis. The subsequent development of complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS) represents a rare but well-described complication of this infection.

**Populations at Highest Risk for Severe Disease**

While our patient was previously healthy and immunocompetent, several populations carry substantially elevated risk for severe *C. canimorsus* infection:

1. **Asplenic or hyposplenic patients** — This is the most significant risk factor. The spleen is critical for clearing encapsulated organisms, and asplenic patients account for the majority of fatal cases.

2. **Immunocompromised hosts** — Including those with HIV, malignancy, or receiving immunosuppressive therapy.

3. **Chronic alcohol abuse** — A well-established risk factor in the literature.

4. **Older adults** — Age-related immune senescence contributes to increased vulnerability.

In immunocompetent patients like ours, severe disease can still occur, particularly with delayed antibiotic prophylaxis—she was unable to fill her prescription for phenoxymethylpenicillin at the time of injury. The delay in initiating appropriate antimicrobial coverage likely contributed to the severity of her clinical course.

**Diagnostic Considerations**

Diagnosis is frequently delayed due to the organism's fastidious nature and slow growth in culture—blood cultures in this case required 7 days for confirmation. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion in any patient presenting with sepsis following canine or feline exposure, and empiric antibiotic coverage for *Capnocytophaga* species should be initiated promptly while awaiting culture results.

### [Differential Diagnosis] A patient presents with microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury. What are the major diagnostic entities in the differential diagnosis of thrombotic microangiopathy, and what key laboratory tests help distinguish between them?

**Clinical Teaching Answer**

The classic triad of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury defines thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). In adults, the three major diagnostic entities to consider are **thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP)**, **Shiga toxin–producing *E. coli* hemolytic uremic syndrome (STEC-HUS)**, and **complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS)**. Distinguishing between them rapidly is essential, as TTP requires urgent plasma exchange, while CM-HUS responds to complement inhibition with eculizumab.

**TTP** is defined by severe acquired or inherited deficiency of ADAMTS13 (activity <10%), leading to unchecked von Willebrand factor–mediated platelet aggregation. The **ADAMTS13 activity assay** is the critical diagnostic test. Clinical prediction scores like PLASMIC can help stratify risk while awaiting results. In this case, the patient's PLASMIC score was 4/7 (low pretest probability), and ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%, effectively excluding TTP.

**STEC-HUS** typically follows a prodromal diarrheal illness. Diagnosis relies on **stool PCR or culture for Shiga toxin–producing *E. coli***. Our patient had no preceding enteric symptoms and tested negative for Shiga toxin by PCR, ruling out STEC-HUS.

**CM-HUS** (formerly "atypical HUS") results from dysregulated alternative complement pathway activation, often triggered by infection, pregnancy, or other stressors in genetically predisposed individuals. It remains a **diagnosis of exclusion**. Importantly, standard complement levels (C3, C4) may be normal during active disease and do not exclude CM-HUS. Extended complement testing—including alternative pathway activity (AH50), genetic panels, and autoantibodies against complement factors—can support the diagnosis, though results may be confounded by plasma exchange or complement blockade. In this patient, kidney biopsy demonstrating ischemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi confirmed TMA, and the clinical response to eculizumab further supported CM-HUS.

The case also illustrates that **disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)** can coexist with or precede TMA, particularly in sepsis. DIC typically presents with elevated D-dimer, prolonged PT/aPTT, and low fibrinogen—features distinct from primary TMA. In this patient, initial coagulopathy and pancytopenia reflected sepsis-associated DIC, but persistent hemolysis and thrombocytopenia after DIC resolution signaled a superimposed TMA process.

In summary, the essential diagnostic workup for TMA includes:
- **ADAMTS13 activity** to exclude TTP
- **Shiga toxin testing** to exclude STEC-HUS
- **Peripheral blood smear** for schistocytes (confirming MAHA)
- **Complement studies**, understanding their limitations
- **Kidney biopsy** when diagnosis remains uncertain

Prompt recognition and targeted therapy—plasma exchange for TTP, eculizumab for CM-HUS—are the key determinants of outcome.

### [Clinical Pearls] In the workup of thrombotic microangiopathy, what ADAMTS13 activity level is diagnostic for thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), and how does a level of 43.5%—as seen in this patient—guide clinical management?

**Clinical Teaching Answer**

In the diagnostic workup of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), an ADAMTS13 activity level **below 10%** is diagnostic for thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP). This severe deficiency reflects the underlying pathophysiology of TTP—insufficient von Willebrand factor cleaving activity leading to uncontrolled platelet microthrombi formation.

A level of **43.5%**, as seen in this patient, effectively **excludes TTP** and redirects the diagnostic approach. ADAMTS13 activity in the normal or near-normal range indicates that alternative TMA etiologies must be pursued. In this case, the ADAMTS13 result—combined with a PLASMIC score of 4/7 (low pretest probability), negative Shiga toxin PCR, and kidney biopsy findings—supported the diagnosis of complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS).

The practical implication is critical: **plasma exchange (PEX) is lifesaving in TTP** but provides only temporary benefit in CM-HUS by removing circulating complement fragments without correcting the underlying complement dysregulation. In this patient, PEX was appropriately initiated as bridging therapy while complement testing was pending, but sustained hematological and renal recovery occurred only after eculizumab was started. This case illustrates how the ADAMTS13 level serves as a pivotal branching point in TMA evaluation—distinguishing a plasma exchange–responsive emergency from complement-mediated disease requiring targeted inhibition.

### [Pathophysiology] What is the pathophysiologic mechanism of complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome, and how does dysregulation of the alternative complement pathway lead to thrombotic microangiopathy?

### Pathophysiologic Mechanism of Complement-Mediated HUS

Complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS) arises from uncontrolled activation of the alternative complement pathway, leading to endothelial injury and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Understanding this mechanism requires reviewing how the alternative pathway normally functions and what happens when regulation fails.

**Normal Alternative Pathway Regulation**

The alternative complement pathway is constitutively active at a low level, generating C3 convertase (C3bBb) through a process called "tickover." This creates a constant state of surveillance that requires tight regulation to prevent tissue damage. Key regulatory proteins—including complement factor H, factor I, and membrane cofactor protein (CD46)—act as brakes on this system by accelerating decay of C3 convertase or acting as cofactors for proteolytic inactivation of C3b.

**From Dysregulation to TMA**

When regulatory control is lost, the alternative pathway amplifies uncontrollably. C3 convertase formation proceeds unchecked, leading to massive C3 activation and assembly of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9) on host cell surfaces. Endothelial cells are particularly vulnerable. The deposition of C5b-9 causes direct endothelial injury, leading to swelling, detachment, and exposure of the subendothelial matrix. This prothrombotic surface triggers platelet adhesion and aggregation, fibrin deposition, and microvascular thrombosis—the hallmark features of TMA.

The mechanical shearing of red blood cells through narrowed, fibrin-laden microvasculature produces microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, while platelet consumption causes thrombocytopenia. The kidneys are preferentially affected due to their dense glomerular capillary network, explaining why acute kidney injury is a defining feature.

**Genetic Predisposition and Triggering Events**

Between 50% and 70% of patients with CM-HUS harbor pathogenic variants in complement regulatory genes. However, penetrance is incomplete—many individuals with these variants never develop disease. Clinical manifestation typically requires a "second hit," most commonly an infectious trigger, pregnancy, or autoimmune disease. As demonstrated in our patient, *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteremia served as the triggering event. Despite extensive genetic testing revealing no pathogenic complement variants, her presentation supports infection-triggered CM-HUS, suggesting that even transient complement dysregulation during severe infection can precipitate TMA in susceptible hosts.

**A Key Diagnostic Pitfall**

An important teaching point from this case is that normal C3 and C4 levels do not exclude CM-HUS. Complement levels may remain within reference ranges during active disease, as consumption may be localized to endothelial surfaces rather than reflected in systemic consumption. In our patient, standard complement tests (C3/C4) were normal, yet functional testing revealed markedly reduced alternative pathway activity (AH50 of 6%), consistent with the underlying pathophysiology.

**Therapeutic Implications**

The pathophysiology directly informs treatment. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody against C5, blocks formation of the membrane attack complex, interrupting the final common pathway of endothelial injury. This explains why our patient achieved sustained hematological remission and partial renal recovery only after complement blockade was initiated, while plasma exchange provided merely transient stabilization by removing circulating complement fragments without correcting the underlying dysregulation.

### [Management] What infection prevention measures must be implemented before initiating eculizumab therapy, and what is the rationale for these precautions?

### Infection Prevention Before Eculizumab Therapy

Before initiating eculizumab, two critical infection prevention measures are required: **meningococcal vaccination** and **antimicrobial prophylaxis** when adequate immunization cannot be achieved prior to therapy.

**Meningococcal Vaccination**

Patients must receive vaccines covering all major meningococcal serogroups. In this case, the patient received Menveo (covering serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (covering serogroup B) before eculizumab initiation. Ideally, vaccines should be administered at least **2 weeks before** starting complement blockade to allow for adequate immune response.

**Antimicrobial Prophylaxis**

When the 2-week window cannot be met, antibiotic prophylaxis is mandatory. This patient received prophylactic phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) because vaccination occurred immediately before eculizumab initiation, precluding full immunologic protection.

**Rationale**

Eculizumab is a monoclonal antibody that inhibits cleavage of complement component C5, thereby preventing formation of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9). While highly effective for complement-mediated HUS, this blockade creates a functional deficiency of terminal complement activity, which is essential for defense against encapsulated bacteria—particularly *Neisseria meningitidis*. Patients on eculizumab carry a **1000- to 2000-fold increased risk** of invasive meningococcal disease compared to the general population.

As highlighted in this case, terminal complement components are also important for host defense against *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*. This underscores the broader principle: complement blockade during active bacteremia requires careful risk-benefit assessment. Here, eculizumab was appropriately withheld until blood cultures were negative, minimizing infection risk while still enabling timely treatment of the underlying complement-mediated TMA.

**Key Teaching Point**

Vaccination and prophylaxis remain essential even after complement blockade is discontinued, as vaccine responses may be suboptimal and infection risk persists.

### [Differential Diagnosis] How can disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) be distinguished clinically and by laboratory findings, and why might these conditions coexist or be confused in a septic patient?

**Distinguishing DIC from Thrombotic Microangiopathy: A Clinical Framework**

This is an excellent question that directly illustrates a common diagnostic challenge in critically ill patients. Let me walk you through the key distinctions and then explain why these conditions overlapped in this case.

**Laboratory Differentiation**

The fundamental distinction lies in what is being consumed. In DIC, there is global activation of the coagulation cascade, leading to consumption of *both* platelets and clotting factors. This produces the classic laboratory signature: prolonged PT and aPTT, decreased fibrinogen, and elevated D-dimer or fibrin degradation products. Think of DIC as a systemic coagulopathy affecting the entire cascade.

In contrast, TMA is characterized by platelet consumption *without* significant consumption of clotting factors. The PT and aPTT typically remain normal, fibrinogen is preserved or may even be elevated as an acute phase reactant, and D-dimer is normal or only minimally elevated. The hallmark of TMA is microangiopathic hemolytic anemia—schistocytes on peripheral smear, elevated LDH, and undetectable haptoglobin—resulting from mechanical destruction of erythrocytes as they traverse thrombus-laden microvasculature.

**Clinical Differentiation**

Clinically, DIC presents with a combination of thrombotic and bleeding manifestations simultaneously—patients may have digital ischemia from microthrombi while also developing diffuse oozing or ecchymoses from depleted clotting factors. TMA, by comparison, tends to cause organ-specific injury: acute kidney injury predominates in HUS, while neurologic dysfunction is more prominent in TTP. Bleeding in TMA is primarily due to thrombocytopenia rather than a true coagulopathy.

**The ADAMTS13 Distinction**

A critical laboratory in this differentiation is ADAMTS13 activity. In TTP, activity falls below 10% due to autoantibody inhibition or congenital deficiency. In our patient, ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%, effectively excluding TTP. This result, combined with her clinical presentation, directed attention toward HUS syndromes.

**Why These Conditions Coexisted in This Patient**

This case beautifully illustrates that DIC and TMA are not mutually exclusive. Our patient initially presented with clear DIC—coagulopathy with prolonged clotting times, hypofibrinogenemia, and clinical evidence of both thrombosis and bleeding consistent with sepsis-induced DIC from *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteremia. The initial evaluation noted normal haptoglobin and non-compensatory reticulocytes, arguing against TMA at presentation.

However, as her DIC resolved with antibiotics and supportive care—coagulation parameters normalized and platelets initially recovered—a new pattern emerged. She developed sudden isolated hemolysis with hemoglobin dropping from 111 to 68 g/L, undetectable haptoglobin, elevated LDH, and 15–20% schistocytes on peripheral smear. Crucially, this occurred *without* corresponding abnormalities in PT, aPTT, or fibrinogen, signaling a shift from DIC to a pure TMA picture.

**Pathophysiologic Overlap in Sepsis**

Sepsis can trigger both conditions through different mechanisms. The systemic inflammatory response activates tissue factor expression and cytokine release, driving DIC through thrombin generation and fibrin deposition. Simultaneously, endothelial injury from infection can trigger complement activation—particularly the alternative pathway—leading to TMA. In this case, *C. canimorsus* triggered complement-mediated HUS, confirmed by kidney biopsy showing ischemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi and by her clinical response to eculizumab.

**The Diagnostic Pearls**

Three key observations should raise suspicion that TMA is developing alongside or following DIC:

First, persistent thrombocytopenia and hemolysis after coagulation parameters have normalized—this pattern emerged clearly in our patient.

Second, severe AKI out of proportion to the degree of coagulopathy—her progression to complete anuria and dialysis dependence prompted the TMA evaluation.

Third, schistocytosis exceeding what would be expected from DIC alone—while DIC can cause some fragmentation, counts exceeding 1–2% schistocytes should prompt evaluation for TMA.

The lesson here is straightforward: in any septic patient with thrombocytopenia and AKI, maintain a broad differential. When thrombocytopenia and hemolysis persist or worsen after DIC has resolved, or when kidney injury is disproportionately severe, think beyond sepsis-related DIC and evaluate for TMA. Early recognition of CM-HUS enabled targeted therapy with eculizumab, which ultimately led to hematologic remission and partial renal recovery in this patient.

### [Clinical Pearls] What are the classic laboratory findings that define microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, and what is the significance of schistocytes on peripheral blood smear?

**Clinical Teaching Answer:**

Microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA) is defined by a characteristic constellation of laboratory abnormalities reflecting mechanical destruction of red blood cells within the microvasculature. The classic findings include:

1. **Schistocytes on peripheral blood smear** (≥1% of RBCs, though often higher in active disease)
2. **Elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH)** from intracellular enzyme release during hemolysis
3. **Undetectable or markedly decreased haptoglobin**, which is consumed binding free hemoglobin
4. **Unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia** from hemoglobin catabolism
5. **A negative direct antiglobulin test (Coombs test)**, excluding immune-mediated hemolysis

In this case, the patient developed a classic MAHA picture after initial improvement from sepsis: hemoglobin dropped from 111 to 68 g/L over 6 days, haptoglobin became undetectable, LDH rose, and the peripheral smear demonstrated 15%–20% schistocytes—well above the threshold for significance. The initial positive DAT was likely a false positive from recent transfusions or penicillin exposure, as repeat testing was negative.

**The significance of schistocytes** lies in their pathophysiologic origin: they represent mechanical fragmentation of erythrocytes as they traverse vessels obstructed by fibrin strands, platelet thrombi, or damaged endothelium. Their presence distinguishes MAHA from other hemolytic processes (such as autoimmune hemolytic anemia, where spherocytes predominate instead). In the appropriate clinical context—MAHA plus thrombocytopenia plus organ dysfunction—schistocytes signal an underlying thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), urgently narrowing the differential to TTP, HUS, or secondary TMAs. Prompt recognition is essential, as these conditions require distinct and time-sensitive interventions; in this patient, identifying schistocytes prompted plasma exchange as a bridge while ADAMTS13 testing was pending, followed by eculizumab for complement-mediated HUS once TTP was excluded.

### [Pathophysiology] Why is plasma exchange considered life-saving therapy for thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) but provides only transient benefit in complement-mediated HUS, and what does this difference reveal about the underlying disease mechanisms?

**Clinical Teaching Answer**

The fundamental difference lies in whether plasma exchange addresses the root cause of the disease or merely provides temporary removal of circulating mediators.

**In TTP**, the pathophysiology centers on severe ADAMTS13 deficiency—typically less than 10% activity—most commonly due to autoantibodies against this von Willebrand factor-cleaving protease. Without functional ADAMTS13, ultra-large von Willebrand factor multimers accumulate on endothelial surfaces, triggering uncontrolled platelet adhesion and microvascular thrombosis. Plasma exchange is life-saving because it simultaneously removes the pathogenic autoantibodies and replaces the deficient enzyme. In essence, plasma exchange directly corrects the underlying defect.

**In complement-mediated HUS**, the problem is dysregulation of the alternative complement pathway—often due to genetic variants in regulatory proteins such as factor H, factor I, or membrane cofactor protein. This results in uncontrolled terminal complement activation, causing endothelial injury and thrombotic microangiopathy. Plasma exchange can transiently remove circulating complement fragments, inflammatory mediators, and potentially pathogenic autoantibodies, which may explain the temporary clinical improvement observed in our patient. However, because the underlying complement regulatory dysfunction persists, the benefit is fleeting.

This distinction is clearly illustrated in the case: after plasma exchange sessions, the patient's diuresis returned, suggesting transient hematological stabilization. Yet sustained remission and meaningful renal recovery occurred only after eculizumab was initiated. As the authors note, plasma exchange provided "only temporary benefit... without correcting the underlying complement system dysfunction."

**The key teaching point**: In TTP, plasma exchange is disease-modifying; in CM-HUS, it is merely a bridge to definitive complement blockade. Recognizing this distinction is critical because delays in initiating eculizumab for CM-HUS are associated with incomplete renal recovery and progression to end-stage kidney disease. The case also underscores why ADAMTS13 testing is essential before committing to prolonged plasma exchange—a normal activity level, as seen here at 43.5%, should prompt early consideration of alternative diagnoses and targeted therapies.
