Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

Chapter 2: ## Disease Evolution and Recognition of Thrombotic Microangiopathy

The patient's clinical course illustrates how thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) can emerge insidiously during recovery from an initial septic insult, creating a diagnostic challenge when classic features are masked by coexisting illness. Following admission to the intensive care unit, her condition deteriorated rapidly despite appropriate antibiotic therapy. She became completely anuric, with acute kidney injury progressing to dialysis dependence. Aggressive fluid resuscitation and loop diuretics failed to restore urine output, and she developed septic cardiomyopathy with volume overload, necessitating continuous renal replacement therapy. By day three in the ICU, she exhibited diffuse skin bleeding, ecchymoses, and distal cyanotic changes affecting multiple digits—findings initially attributed to microthrombotic complications of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) in the setting of severe sepsis.

A crucial inflection point occurred as her septic parameters began to improve. Following transfer to the nephrology ward on day eight, inflammatory markers normalized, leukocyte counts rebounded to reactive levels, and coagulation parameters stabilized, indicating resolving coagulopathy. However, this apparent recovery was disrupted by a sudden, isolated drop in hemoglobin from 111 to 68 g/L over six days, occurring without clinical evidence of bleeding or hemodynamic instability. This temporal pattern—hemolysis emerging as sepsis resolved—provided an essential clue that the process was not simply a continuation of the initial presentation.

Laboratory evaluation revealed findings characteristic of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA): undetectable haptoglobin, elevated lactate dehydrogenase, and a peripheral blood smear demonstrating 15%–20% schistocytes. Schistocytes are fragmented red blood cells formed when erythrocytes are mechanically sheared as they traverse microvasculature obstructed by platelet-rich thrombi, and their presence is a hallmark of TMA. Importantly, the initial direct antiglobulin test (DAT) was positive for IgG, prompting concern for autoimmune hemolytic anemia and initiation of high-dose corticosteroids. However, the hemolytic process failed to respond to steroid therapy, and repeat DAT was negative, suggesting the initial result was a false positive likely caused by penicillin exposure or prior transfusions. This diagnostic detour underscores the importance of recognizing that a positive DAT does not exclude TMA, particularly when the clinical picture is inconsistent with immune-mediated hemolysis.

Differentiating TMA from ongoing DIC requires careful attention to timing and laboratory features. In this case, the hemolysis appeared after coagulation parameters had normalized, arguing against DIC as the primary driver. Furthermore, the combination of MAHA with persistent thrombocytopenia, out of proportion to the recovering clinical picture, signaled a distinct pathological process. This recognition prompted definitive diagnostic studies: ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%, ruling out thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura, and a kidney biopsy confirmed TMA with acute tubular necrosis, ischemic glomeruli containing fibrin thrombi, and crescent formation.

This evolution demonstrates a critical clinical principle: TMA should be suspected when unexplained hemolysis and thrombocytopenia persist or worsen despite improvement in other organ systems. The "second hit" phenomenon—where recovery from one insult unmasks an underlying vulnerability—highlights how complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome may remain occult until an appropriate trigger reveals the dysregulated complement pathway. For clinicians managing complex sepsis, vigilance for new or isolated laboratory abnormalities during the recovery phase is essential, as early recognition of superimposed TMA directly impacts therapeutic decisions and outcomes.
