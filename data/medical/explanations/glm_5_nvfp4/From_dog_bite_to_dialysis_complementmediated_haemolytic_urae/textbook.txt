Title: Textbook chapter about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

## Case Presentation and Initial Clinical Assessment

The patient was a previously healthy woman in her late 40s who presented to the emergency department with rapidly progressive illness following a dog bite. Four days before admission, she had been bitten on the upper lip by her dog. She received appropriate tetanus prophylaxis and was prescribed phenoxymethylpenicillin for antibiotic coverage; however, she did not initiate treatment as she was unable to collect her prescription. This gap in antimicrobial prophylaxis proved critical, as the subsequent days were marked by escalating fever exceeding 40°C, malaise, and reduced oral intake. She eventually sought medical attention when numbness and discoloration of her feet developed, prompting evaluation at a primary-care emergency facility where profound hypoglycemia was detected. Despite administration of oral glucose and glucagon, her blood glucose remained refractory, reaching a nadir of 0.6 mmol/L, and she was urgently transferred to the emergency department.

On arrival, she appeared septic with mottled and cool lower extremities, though she remained alert and responsive. Hypoglycemia in the setting of sepsis is an ominous sign, typically reflecting glycogen depletion and impaired gluconeogenesis in the face of overwhelming metabolic demand. The clinical picture of fever, hypoglycemia refractory to resuscitation, and cutaneous manifestations of poor perfusion suggested severe systemic illness. Initial laboratory evaluation revealed extensive multiorgan dysfunction: metabolic acidosis with pH 7.27, elevated lactate at 5.7 mmol/L, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury (AKI), transaminitis, and coagulopathy consistent with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Urinalysis demonstrated proteinuria and hematuria. The constellation of findings met clinical criteria for sepsis, with organ dysfunction spanning hematologic, hepatic, renal, and metabolic systems.

The association between dog bites and Capnocytophaga canimorsus infection was a key consideration from the outset. C. canimorsus is a slow-growing, encapsulated gram-negative rod that colonizes the oropharynx of dogs and cats. Human infections, while rare, carry significant morbidity and mortality approaching 30%. Transmission occurs through bites, scratches, or close animal contact. The pathogen is notable for its fastidious growth requirements, which can delay diagnosis; cultures typically require extended incubation, and molecular methods may be needed for rapid identification. Clinical manifestations range from localized cellulitis or meningitis to fulminant sepsis with DIC and multiorgan failure. Given the temporal relationship between the dog bite and symptom onset, empiric broad-spectrum intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam was initiated to provide coverage for dog-associated pathogens, including Capnocytophaga species.

The initial differential diagnosis required careful consideration of alternative etiologies. The combination of fever, pancytopenia, and coagulopathy raised concern for acute leukemia, prompting hematology consultation and plans for bone marrow biopsy. Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) was also considered given the cytopenias and systemic inflammation. Necrotizing fasciitis was ruled out after clinical and radiological assessment of the mottled extremities. While hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS) was mentioned in early discussions, the presence of leukopenia, elevated inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin, and lack of reticulocytosis made this diagnosis seem less likely at this stage. Flow cytometry showed no increase in blasts or abnormal phenotypes, and the bone marrow biopsy was ultimately cancelled as the clinical picture became consistent with sepsis. C. canimorsus bacteremia was confirmed by both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures after seven days of incubation.

This initial presentation illustrates several important clinical principles. First, animal bites warrant prompt antimicrobial prophylaxis, as demonstrated by the rapid progression from a seemingly minor lip wound to life-threatening sepsis. Second, the early recognition of DIC and multiorgan dysfunction in the setting of suspected zoonotic infection guides both antibiotic selection and intensive supportive care. Third, the overlapping features of sepsis-associated organ failure with other conditions, including hematologic malignancies and thrombotic microangiopathies, necessitates systematic exclusion of alternative diagnoses. At this stage, the dominant clinical syndrome was septic shock with DIC, and the subsequent development of thrombotic microangiopathy would emerge only after initial clinical stabilization, highlighting how complement-mediated HUS can be obscured during the acute septic phase.

## Disease Evolution and Recognition of Thrombotic Microangiopathy

The patient's clinical course illustrates how thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) can emerge insidiously during recovery from an initial septic insult, creating a diagnostic challenge when classic features are masked by coexisting illness. Following admission to the intensive care unit, her condition deteriorated rapidly despite appropriate antibiotic therapy. She became completely anuric, with acute kidney injury progressing to dialysis dependence. Aggressive fluid resuscitation and loop diuretics failed to restore urine output, and she developed septic cardiomyopathy with volume overload, necessitating continuous renal replacement therapy. By day three in the ICU, she exhibited diffuse skin bleeding, ecchymoses, and distal cyanotic changes affecting multiple digits—findings initially attributed to microthrombotic complications of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) in the setting of severe sepsis.

A crucial inflection point occurred as her septic parameters began to improve. Following transfer to the nephrology ward on day eight, inflammatory markers normalized, leukocyte counts rebounded to reactive levels, and coagulation parameters stabilized, indicating resolving coagulopathy. However, this apparent recovery was disrupted by a sudden, isolated drop in hemoglobin from 111 to 68 g/L over six days, occurring without clinical evidence of bleeding or hemodynamic instability. This temporal pattern—hemolysis emerging as sepsis resolved—provided an essential clue that the process was not simply a continuation of the initial presentation.

Laboratory evaluation revealed findings characteristic of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA): undetectable haptoglobin, elevated lactate dehydrogenase, and a peripheral blood smear demonstrating 15%–20% schistocytes. Schistocytes are fragmented red blood cells formed when erythrocytes are mechanically sheared as they traverse microvasculature obstructed by platelet-rich thrombi, and their presence is a hallmark of TMA. Importantly, the initial direct antiglobulin test (DAT) was positive for IgG, prompting concern for autoimmune hemolytic anemia and initiation of high-dose corticosteroids. However, the hemolytic process failed to respond to steroid therapy, and repeat DAT was negative, suggesting the initial result was a false positive likely caused by penicillin exposure or prior transfusions. This diagnostic detour underscores the importance of recognizing that a positive DAT does not exclude TMA, particularly when the clinical picture is inconsistent with immune-mediated hemolysis.

Differentiating TMA from ongoing DIC requires careful attention to timing and laboratory features. In this case, the hemolysis appeared after coagulation parameters had normalized, arguing against DIC as the primary driver. Furthermore, the combination of MAHA with persistent thrombocytopenia, out of proportion to the recovering clinical picture, signaled a distinct pathological process. This recognition prompted definitive diagnostic studies: ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%, ruling out thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura, and a kidney biopsy confirmed TMA with acute tubular necrosis, ischemic glomeruli containing fibrin thrombi, and crescent formation.

This evolution demonstrates a critical clinical principle: TMA should be suspected when unexplained hemolysis and thrombocytopenia persist or worsen despite improvement in other organ systems. The "second hit" phenomenon—where recovery from one insult unmasks an underlying vulnerability—highlights how complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome may remain occult until an appropriate trigger reveals the dysregulated complement pathway. For clinicians managing complex sepsis, vigilance for new or isolated laboratory abnormalities during the recovery phase is essential, as early recognition of superimposed TMA directly impacts therapeutic decisions and outcomes.

## Differential Diagnosis and Diagnostic Workup for Thrombotic Microangiopathy

Thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) is a pathological process characterized by microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and organ injury—most commonly acute kidney injury. The term TMA describes a clinicopathological syndrome rather than a single disease, and several distinct entities can produce this constellation of findings. The three major diagnostic categories in adults are thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), Shiga toxin-producing Escherichia coli-associated hemolytic uremic syndrome (STEC-HUS), and complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS). These conditions share overlapping clinical features, yet their management differs substantially, making systematic differentiation essential.

In this case, the patient initially presented with findings consistent with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) and sepsis. However, the subsequent development of isolated hemolysis, persistent thrombocytopenia, and worsening kidney function after the sepsis had begun to improve prompted reconsideration of the diagnosis. The peripheral blood smear demonstrating 15%–20% schistocytes confirmed MAHA and established TMA as the unifying explanation for her ongoing hematological and renal abnormalities.

Excluding TTP is the first priority in any adult presenting with TMA, as untreated TTP carries a mortality rate exceeding 90%. TTP results from severe deficiency of ADAMTS13, a metalloprotease that cleaves ultralarge von Willebrand factor multimers. When ADAMTS13 activity falls below 10%, unchecked platelet adhesion produces microthrombi throughout the microvasculature. The PLASMIC scoring system helps stratify pretest probability while awaiting definitive testing. The score incorporates seven variables: platelet count <30×10⁹/L, presence of hemolysis, absence of active cancer, absence of solid organ or stem cell transplant history, presence of mean corpuscular volume <90 fL, international normalized ratio <1.5, and creatinine <2.0 mg/dL. A score of 0–4 indicates low risk, 5 indicates intermediate risk, and 6–7 indicates high risk for severe ADAMTS13 deficiency. In this patient, the PLASMIC score was 4 of 7, suggesting low pretest probability. The ADAMTS13 activity level of 43.5% definitively excluded TTP, as values above 10% are inconsistent with the diagnosis.

STEC-HUS represents the most common form of HUS globally and typically follows a prodrome of bloody diarrhea. Shiga toxin, produced by certain E. coli strains (particularly O157:H7), binds to endothelial cells and triggers a cascade leading to endothelial injury and TMA. The absence of antecedent gastrointestinal illness in this patient made STEC-HUS clinically unlikely, and a negative stool PCR for Shiga toxin confirmed its exclusion.

CM-HUS, historically termed "atypical HUS," arises from dysregulation of the alternative complement pathway. The diagnosis is one of exclusion, established after ruling out TTP and STEC-HUS in a patient with clinical and pathological evidence of TMA. Importantly, serum complement levels (C3 and C4) may be normal during active disease and do not exclude CM-HUS. In this case, standard complement tests (C3, C4) and functional assays of the classical, alternative, and lectin pathways were all within normal limits. Extended complement testing performed after plasma exchange and eculizumab initiation showed markedly reduced alternative pathway activity (AH50 of 6%), which most likely reflected treatment effects rather than intrinsic complement consumption.

Kidney biopsy provided pathological confirmation of TMA. Light microscopy revealed severe acute tubular necrosis, ischemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi, and scattered crescent formation. These findings—particularly the fibrin thrombi within glomerular capillaries—are characteristic of TMA and represent the anatomical correlate of the microthrombi producing hemolysis and thrombocytopenia. Immunofluorescence showed no immune complex deposition, and electron microscopy excluded primary glomerular pathology, supporting a diagnosis of TMA secondary to complement dysregulation rather than an immune-complex-mediated glomerulonephritis.

The stepwise diagnostic approach in this case illustrates a practical algorithm for TMA evaluation: first confirm MAHA with peripheral blood smear, then urgently exclude TTP with ADAMTS13 activity, rule out STEC-HUS with stool testing if clinically indicated, and consider CM-HUS in the appropriate clinical context with pathological confirmation via kidney biopsy when feasible. The diagnostic complexity is heightened when TMA develops in the setting of sepsis and DIC, as initial clinical features may obscure the underlying TMA until the sepsis begins to resolve and isolated hematological abnormalities persist.

## Pathophysiology of Complement-Mediated HUS

Complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS) arises from uncontrolled activation of the alternative complement pathway, leading to endothelial injury and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Understanding this pathophysiology is essential for interpreting this patient's presentation, where a *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* infection triggered a complement-mediated TMA in a previously healthy adult.

The complement system serves as a critical arm of innate immunity, providing defense against pathogens through three activation pathways: classical, lectin, and alternative. The alternative pathway is unique because it is constitutively active at a low level through a process called "tickover," where spontaneous hydrolysis of C3 generates C3(H2O). This low-level activation allows rapid response to pathogens but requires tight regulation to prevent host tissue damage. Under normal conditions, complement regulatory proteins ensure that activation remains controlled and self-limited. When this regulation fails, the alternative pathway amplifies uncontrollably, generating large amounts of C3b, which deposits on host cells, and ultimately activating the terminal pathway to form the membrane attack complex (C5b-9). This terminal activation is central to the pathogenesis of CM-HUS and represents the specific therapeutic target of eculizumab.

Key regulatory proteins maintain control over the alternative pathway. Factor H, a plasma glycoprotein, acts as the primary regulator by binding to C3b and accelerating its decay from the C3 convertase while serving as a cofactor for Factor I-mediated cleavage. Factor I is a serine protease that inactivates C3b when in the presence of cofactors such as Factor H or membrane cofactor protein (CD46). CD46 is a membrane-bound regulator expressed on host cells that provides local protection against complement deposition. Factor B, unlike the regulatory proteins, participates in activation—it binds to C3b to form the alternative pathway C3 convertase (C3bBb). Genetic variants affecting these proteins can shift the balance toward excessive activation. Loss-of-function variants in complement regulators (Factors H and I, CD46) or gain-of-function variants in activators (Factor B, C3) predispose individuals to CM-HUS by reducing the threshold for pathologic activation.

Genetic predisposition alone is insufficient to cause disease. Between 50% and 70% of patients with CM-HUS harbor identifiable genetic variants, yet penetrance is incomplete—many carriers never develop clinical manifestations. This observation led to the "second hit" hypothesis, which proposes that individuals with genetic susceptibility require an environmental trigger to precipitate disease. Common triggers include infections, pregnancy, autoimmune conditions, and certain medications. In this patient, the absence of identifiable pathogenic variants suggests that the *C. canimorsus* infection itself may have provided sufficient trigger without underlying genetic predisposition, or that undetected variants in genes not routinely tested may be present.

Infectious triggers play a dual role in complement activation. Pathogens directly activate complement through pathogen-associated molecular patterns, and the resulting inflammatory response further amplifies complement consumption. In CM-HUS, this physiologic activation becomes pathologic when regulatory mechanisms are overwhelmed or deficient. The consequence is widespread complement deposition on endothelial cells, particularly in the renal microvasculature. C5a generation recruits inflammatory cells, while C5b-9 insertion into endothelial membranes causes sublytic injury that triggers a prothrombotic phenotype. Endothelial cells lose their anticoagulant properties, expose procoagulant surfaces, and release von Willebrand factor multimers. Platelets adhere and aggregate within damaged microvessels, forming the characteristic microthrombi that define TMA. These thrombi shear erythrocytes as they pass through narrowed lumina, producing schistocytes—the hallmark of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia.

The relationship between *C. canimorsus* and complement is particularly relevant to this case. This organism displays variable susceptibility to complement-mediated killing. While many isolates are complement-sensitive and rapidly cleared by serum, capsule-expressing strains demonstrate partial resistance. This variability creates a clinical paradox: complement activity is essential for clearing the infection, yet the same complement activation may trigger TMA in susceptible individuals. In this patient, the infection likely initiated complement activation through bacterial surface components, and the subsequent dysregulated response resulted in endothelial injury. The temporal sequence observed here—hemolysis appeared only after initial improvement from sepsis—supports infection-triggered complement dysregulation rather than direct bacterial invasion of the vasculature.

The finding that this patient had no pathogenic complement gene variants requires careful interpretation. CM-HUS remains a clinical diagnosis, and normal genetic testing does not exclude the diagnosis. The complement system is complex, and current testing panels may not capture all pathogenic variants. Furthermore, this case demonstrates that CM-HUS can occur in the absence of identifiable genetic abnormalities when a sufficiently potent trigger overwhelms normal regulatory capacity. The patient's rapid hematological response to eculizumab—with sustained improvement after complement blockade—provides therapeutic confirmation of complement-mediated pathogenesis, regardless of genetic findings.

This pathophysiologic framework directly informed the diagnostic approach and treatment decisions in this case. Recognition that complement dysregulation was driving the TMA prompted measurement of complement function and ultimately led to targeted therapy with eculizumab, demonstrating how understanding disease mechanisms translates into clinical outcomes.

## Management Strategy and Clinical Outcomes

The management of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) requires distinguishing it from other thrombotic microangiopathies (TMAs) and initiating targeted therapy promptly. In this case, the therapeutic approach evolved from supportive care and plasma exchange to definitive complement inhibition with eculizumab, illustrating both the rationale for and limitations of each intervention.

Plasma exchange (PEX) served as bridging therapy while the diagnostic workup was ongoing. In thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), PEX is lifesaving because it removes autoantibodies against ADAMTS13 and replaces the deficient enzyme. However, in CM-HUS, PEX provides only transient benefit by removing circulating complement fragments and inflammatory mediators without correcting the underlying complement dysregulation. The patient received three sessions of PEX with Octaplas before the first dose of eculizumab. Spontaneous diuresis returned after the second and third PEX sessions, reflecting temporary stabilisation, but sustained haematological and renal recovery only occurred after complement blockade was established. This clinical pattern underscores that PEX alone is insufficient for CM-HUS and should not delay initiation of targeted therapy once the diagnosis is reasonably established.

Eculizumab, a humanised monoclonal antibody targeting complement component C5, is the standard of care for CM-HUS. By binding to C5, eculizumab prevents its cleavage into C5a and C5b, thereby blocking formation of the membrane attack complex (C5b-9) and halting complement-mediated endothelial injury. The induction regimen in this case followed established protocols: 900 mg intravenously weekly for four weeks. Maintenance therapy continued at 1200 mg every two weeks. The patient achieved haematological remission, and kidney function partially recovered to a serum creatinine of 175 µmol/L—consistent with evidence that early initiation predicts better outcomes, while delays correlate with incomplete renal recovery and progression to end-stage kidney disease.

A critical therapeutic dilemma arises when considering complement blockade in the setting of recent bacteraemia. Terminal complement components are essential for host defence against encapsulated organisms, including *Neisseria meningitidis* and *Capnocytophaga* species. Eculizumab increases susceptibility to invasive meningococcal disease by approximately 1,000- to 2,000-fold. In this case, eculizumab was initiated only after blood cultures had become negative, minimising the risk of uncontrolled infection while the patient remained on appropriate antibiotic coverage. This timing reflects the balance between the urgency of treating active TMA and the infection risk posed by complement inhibition.

Vaccination and antimicrobial prophylaxis are mandatory when using complement inhibitors. The patient received meningococcal vaccinations against serogroups A, C, Y, and W (Menveo) and serogroup B (Bexsero) before eculizumab induction, with second doses planned for the outpatient setting. Because the vaccines were not administered the recommended two weeks before starting complement blockade, prophylactic phenoxymethylpenicillin was given to cover the interim period. These measures remain essential for any patient receiving terminal complement inhibition.

The clinical response in this case demonstrates the prognostic importance of timely intervention. Following eculizumab initiation, haematological remission was achieved, and partial renal recovery allowed independence from dialysis. Extended genetic complement testing did not identify pathogenic variants, suggesting infection-triggered CM-HUS without demonstrable inherited predisposition. Nonetheless, complement blockade is being continued given the severity of the initial presentation, with plans to reassess discontinuation once renal function stabilises. This approach reflects current uncertainty regarding optimal treatment duration, particularly in patients without identified genetic variants, and underscores the need for ongoing monitoring for relapse.
