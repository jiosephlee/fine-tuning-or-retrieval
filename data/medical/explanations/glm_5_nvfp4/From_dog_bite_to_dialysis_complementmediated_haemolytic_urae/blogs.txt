Title: Blog about the case report: "From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome"

# Man's Best Friend? Capnocytophaga canimorsus and the Zoonotic Threat You Can't Afford to Miss

## The Case That Stops You in Your Tracks

A woman in her late 40s, previously healthy, walks into your ED with fever >40°C, mottled extremities, and hypoglycemia refractory to glucose—nadir 0.6 mmol/L. Four days earlier, her dog bit her on the upper lip. She was prescribed phenoxymethylpenicillin but never filled it. By the time you're seeing her, she's in fulminant septic shock with DIC, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury. She progresses to complete anuria and becomes dialysis-dependent. Blood cultures eventually grow *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*—seven days later.

But here's where it gets weird. After initial stabilization with antibiotics and supportive care, she develops sudden isolated hemolysis with persistent thrombocytopenia. This isn't just sepsis anymore. This is complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS) triggered by a dog bite.

## Why This Matters

*C. canimorsus* isn't on everyone's radar, and that's precisely the problem. It's a slow-growing, fastidious gram-negative rod found in the normal oropharyngeal flora of dogs and cats. Human infections are rare but devastating—mortality approaches 30% despite broad antibiotic susceptibility. The pathogen has been linked to everything from isolated meningitis to fulminant sepsis with DIC, multiorgan failure, and now, as this case beautifully illustrates, complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy.

The real killer is diagnostic delay. This organism grows slowly. In an era where we demand rapid diagnostics, *C. canimorsus* reminds us that some pathogens require patience. Increasing use of molecular methods like real-time nanopore sequencing may help, but for now, you need a high index of suspicion to empirically cover this pathogen while waiting for cultures to mature.

## The Clinical Spectrum: From "Just a Scratch" to Multiorgan Failure

*C. canimorsus* infections can present anywhere along a spectrum that should terrify you. On the mild end: localized cellulitis, wound infection, or isolated meningitis. On the severe end: fulminant sepsis with DIC, gangrene, multiorgan failure, and death. The presentation in this case—septic shock, severe leukopenia, coagulopathy, and hypoglycemia—is classic for severe *C. canimorsus* infection.

What's particularly insidious is that transmission doesn't even require a significant bite. Scratches, licks on broken skin, or close contact with a dog or cat can be enough. In this patient, a bite to the upper lip—with its rich vascular supply and proximity to the central nervous system—set the stage for rapid dissemination.

## The Antibiotic Question: Empiric Coverage Matters

This patient was prescribed phenoxymethylpenicillin at the time of the bite but never took it. That's a tragedy. *C. canimorsus* is broadly susceptible to penicillins, which remain first-line agents. But here's the nuance: for severe presentations, you need to think broader.

The treating team appropriately started piperacillin/tazobactam to cover dog-associated pathogens, including *Capnocytophaga* species. That was the right call. For outpatient prophylaxis after dog bites, amoxicillin-clavulanate covers the relevant pathogens including *Pasteurella multocida* and *Capnocytophaga*. For penicillin-allergic patients, doxycycline or clindamycin plus a fluoroquinolone are reasonable alternatives.

## The HUS Curveball: When Sepsis Isn't the Whole Story

Here's where this case becomes truly instructive. The patient initially improved with antibiotics and supportive care. But then—after the sepsis began resolving—she developed sudden isolated hemoglobin drop (111 → 68 g/L over 6 days), persistent thrombocytopenia, undetectable haptoglobin, and elevated LDH. Peripheral smear showed 15-20% schistocytes.

This wasn't DIC anymore. This was thrombotic microangiopathy. The kidney biopsy confirmed it: ischemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi, severe acute tubular necrosis, scattered crescents. The diagnosis was complement-mediated HUS.

The teaching point here is critical: CM-HUS can be obscured during the early sepsis and DIC phase. The hemolysis in this case only appeared *after* the sepsis began improving. That temporal pattern—infection-triggered complement dysregulation manifesting as the acute phase resolves—should prompt consideration of CM-HUS in any patient with persistent MAHA and thrombocytopenia after an infectious insult.

## The Diagnostic Workup: Ruling Out the Mimics

When you're facing MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, your differential is TTP, STEC-HUS, and CM-HUS. The stakes are high, and the treatments are different.

ADAMTS13 activity is your first stop. In this case, it was 43.5%—effectively ruling out TTP, which requires activity below 10%. Her PLASMIC score was 4/7, already suggesting low pretest probability for TTP.

STEC-HUS was ruled out with negative stool PCR for Shiga toxin and absence of prodromal diarrheal illness.

Here's the pearl that might surprise you: complement levels (C3/C4) can be *normal* in CM-HUS. In this patient, they were within normal limits. The diagnosis is clinicopathological and one of exclusion. Extended complement testing—when available—can support the diagnosis, but you shouldn't wait for it to start treatment.

## The Treatment Dilemma: Eculizumab in Active Infection

This case highlights a genuine therapeutic tightrope. Eculizumab, the terminal complement inhibitor, is standard of care for CM-HUS and dramatically improves outcomes when initiated early. But terminal complement is critical for host defense against encapsulated organisms—including *Neisseria* and, ironically, *Capnocytophaga*.

The solution? Eculizumab was initiated after blood cultures became negative, with appropriate meningococcal vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis. The patient received Menveo and Bexsero, with prophylactic penicillin until vaccine response was adequate.

Plasma exchange served as a bridge—providing temporary stabilization but not addressing the underlying complement dysregulation. Sustained recovery only occurred after complement blockade.

## The Take-Home Messages

- **Animal exposure history is non-negotiable** in any septic workup. Ask about bites, scratches, and close contact with dogs and cats. It changes your empiric coverage.
- **Prescription compliance is critical**. This patient was prescribed appropriate prophylaxis but never filled it. When discharging patients after animal bites, ensure they understand the stakes and have antibiotics in hand.
- **C. canimorsus grows slowly**. Don't let culture delay stop you from covering it empirically in severe presentations. Use piperacillin/tazobactam or carbapenems for septic patients with dog/cat exposure.
- **CM-HUS can be triggered by infection**, even in immunocompetent adults without identifiable genetic predisposition. The temporal pattern—hemolysis emerging as sepsis resolves—should raise suspicion.
- **Normal C3/C4 does not rule out CM-HUS**. The diagnosis is clinicopathological and requires exclusion of TTP and STEC-HUS.
- **Early eculizumab improves outcomes**, but requires vaccination, prophylaxis, and careful timing relative to active infection.
- **Mortality approaches 30%** for severe C. canimorsus infections. This pathogen deserves your respect and your empiric coverage.

## Final Thoughts

This case is a reminder that zoonotic infections can present in ways that confound even experienced clinicians. *C. canimorsus* isn't just about dog bites and cellulitis—it's a pathogen capable of triggering complement-mediated TMA in previously healthy adults. The dog on your patient's lap might just be the key to unlocking their diagnosis. All you have to do is ask.



# Beyond the DIC Score: Unmasking Thrombotic Microangiopathy in Sepsis

A woman in her late 40s presents with septic shock, DIC, and acute kidney injury following a dog bite. Blood cultures grow *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*. Standard story, right? Broad-spectrum antibiotics, ICU care, supportive transfusions. Except that's not where this story ends—it's where it gets interesting.

Because a week later, as her DIC resolved and her white count normalized, something strange happened: her hemoglobin crashed from 111 to 68 g/L over six days. Her platelets stayed stuck at 30-40 × 10⁹/L. Her LDH climbed. And the peripheral smear showed 15-20% schistocytes.

This wasn't lingering DIC. This was complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS) hiding in plain sight.

## When DIC Isn't the Whole Story

Here's the clinical trap: when a patient presents with sepsis, thrombocytopenia, AKI, and coagulopathy, we correctly reach for the DIC diagnosis. But what happens when the DIC gets better and the hematologic abnormalities don't?

In this case, the patient's coagulation parameters normalized. Her inflammatory markers improved. She was clinically stabilizing. Yet her platelets remained low, and she developed new-onset hemolytic anemia with schistocytes on the smear. That disconnect—the improving coagulopathy alongside persistent or worsening microangiopathic hemolysis—should make you pause.

## The TMA Diagnostic Framework

When thrombotic microangiopathy enters the differential, the algorithmic approach matters. First, rule out TTP, because missing it is fatal. The PLASMIC score can help stratify risk while you wait for ADAMTS13 activity. This patient scored 4/7—low pretest probability—and her ADAMTS13 came back at 43.5%, effectively excluding TTP (which requires activity below 10%).

Next, exclude Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* (STEC-HUS). No prodromal diarrheal illness and a negative stool PCR ruled that out.

That leaves CM-HUS as the diagnosis—and importantly, it's a diagnosis of exclusion. You won't always find a genetic smoking gun. Up to 50% of patients have no identifiable complement gene variant. In this case, extended genetic testing came back negative. The trigger was likely the infection itself.

## The Normal Complement Levels Trap

Here's a pearl worth committing to memory: normal C3 and C4 levels do not exclude CM-HUS. In this patient, standard complement tests were within normal limits. The alternative pathway activity was markedly reduced at 6%, but even that was obtained after plasma exchange and eculizumab—so interpreting it required clinical context.

CM-HUS is a clinicopathological diagnosis. You make it based on the constellation of MAHA, thrombocytopenia, end-organ damage (classically renal), and exclusion of other causes. The kidney biopsy in this case showed ischemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi—consistent with TMA—and sealed the diagnosis.

## Why This Matters: Timing Is Everything

The therapeutic imperative here is real. Eculizumab, the C5 inhibitor, dramatically improves outcomes in CM-HUS—but early initiation matters. Delayed treatment correlates with incomplete renal recovery and progression to end-stage kidney disease.

The tricky part is that eculizumab increases susceptibility to encapsulated organisms, including *Neisseria* and yes, *Capnocytophaga*. In this case, the team waited until blood cultures were negative, ensured meningococcal vaccination, and started prophylactic penicillin before initiating complement blockade.

Plasma exchange was used as a bridge—three sessions—which can temporarily remove circulating complement fragments and inflammatory mediators. But it doesn't correct the underlying dysregulation. Sustained remission required eculizumab.

## Take-Home Points

- **Persistent thrombocytopenia with new-onset hemolysis despite resolving DIC should trigger evaluation for superimposed TMA**
- **Normal C3/C4 levels do not rule out CM-HUS**—the diagnosis is clinicopathological, not purely laboratory-based
- **ADAMTS13 activity >10% excludes TTP**; PLASMIC scoring helps while you wait
- **CM-HUS can be triggered by infection**—including rare organisms like *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*—even without an underlying genetic complement variant
- **Early eculizumab initiation predicts better renal outcomes**, but requires vaccination, prophylactic antibiotics, and ideally negative blood cultures

This case is a reminder that rare diseases don't announce themselves with flashing neon signs. Sometimes they show up as "atypical DIC" or "weirdly persistent thrombocytopenia." The key is recognizing when the clinical picture doesn't quite fit—and having the diagnostic framework to investigate further.



# The TMA Triad: A Systematic Approach to MAHA, Thrombocytopenia, and AKI

## When Sepsis Masks Something Sinister

A woman in her late 40s presents with fulminant sepsis four days after a dog bite. She's hypotensive, mottled, hypoglycemic to 0.6 mmol/L, and has the kind of laboratory derangement that makes you do a double-take—pancytopenia, acute kidney injury, liver enzyme elevation, and frank DIC. You intubate, start broad-spectrum antibiotics, and chase the obvious diagnosis: *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* bacteremia. Blood cultures confirm it. She gets better. Then she gets worse again—but differently.

Here's what makes this case absolutely worth your time: after her sepsis resolved and her DIC cleared, she developed **isolated hemolysis** with a new drop in hemoglobin from 111 to 68 g/L over six days. Her platelets never fully recovered. Her LDH climbed. And when someone finally looked at a peripheral smear, they found 15-20% schistocytes. She had a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA)—but it wasn't DIC, and it wasn't the initial presentation. It was complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS) triggered by the infection itself.

This case is a masterclass in why we need a systematic approach to TMA—and why empiric plasma exchange without a diagnostic framework can delay definitive care.

## The TMA Framework: What Are We Actually Looking At?

When you see the classic triad of **microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury**, you're in TMA territory. But TMA isn't a diagnosis—it's a syndrome with multiple distinct etiologies that require fundamentally different treatments. The three critical differentials in adults are:

**Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP)** – A medical emergency requiring immediate plasma exchange. Defined by severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10% activity). Miss this and mortality approaches 90%.

**Shiga toxin-producing E. coli HUS (STEC-HUS)** – The classic "bloody diarrhea preceded the renal failure by a week" presentation. Confirm with stool PCR.

**Complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS)** – Formerly called "atypical HUS," this is where complement regulation goes haywire. It's a diagnosis of exclusion, but early recognition matters because complement blockade with eculizumab dramatically improves outcomes.

The problem? These conditions can overlap clinically, and in septic patients, DIC muddies the waters further. That's why a systematic approach matters.

## Step 1: Rule Out TTP First—Because It Kills Fastest

In any adult with MAHA and thrombocytopenia, TTP is your first exclusion. The PLASMIC score helps risk-stratify while you're waiting for ADAMTS13 activity results:

**PLASMIC Score Components:**
- Platelets <30 × 10⁹/L
- Hemolysis (reticulocytes >2.5%, undetectable haptoglobin, or indirect bilirubin >34 μmol/L)
- No active cancer
- No history of solid organ or stem cell transplant
- MCV <90 fL
- INR <1.5
- Creatinine <2.0 mg/dL (176 μmol/L)

Score ≥6 carries high probability of TTP; score ≤4 makes it unlikely. Our patient scored 4/7—a low pretest probability. Her ADAMTS13 activity came back at 43.5%, effectively ruling out TTP.

Here's the clinical pearl: **do not wait for ADAMTS13 to initiate plasma exchange if clinical suspicion for TTP is high.** But if your PLASMIC score is low and you have a plausible alternative explanation for TMA (like severe sepsis with DIC), you can hold PEX while pursuing other diagnostics.

## Step 2: Ask About the Prodrome

STEC-HUS classically presents with bloody diarrhea 5-10 days before the onset of MAHA and AKI. Our patient had no gastrointestinal prodrome, and stool PCR for Shiga toxin was negative. That made STEC-HUS unlikely.

But here's where clinical nuance matters: not all STEC-HUS presents with classic bloody diarrhea, and not all TMAs with GI symptoms are STEC-HUS. If the story fits, test. If it doesn't fit, don't assume it's negative—still test.

## Step 3: Consider Complement-Mediated HUS—The Diagnosis of Exclusion

Once TTP and STEC-HUS are ruled out, CM-HUS becomes the working diagnosis. But here's what trips people up: **normal complement levels (C3, C4) do not exclude CM-HUS.** In our patient, standard complement tests were entirely normal. It was only specialized functional testing showing markedly reduced alternative pathway activity (AH50 of 6%) that supported the diagnosis.

The kidney biopsy in this case showed severe acute tubular necrosis, ischemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi, and scattered crescent formation—consistent with TMA. But biopsy isn't always necessary if the clinical picture is clear.

The key insight from this case: CM-HUS can be **triggered by infection** in patients without identifiable genetic complement variants. *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* is rare, but it's one of several pathogens that can precipitate complement dysregulation in susceptible hosts.

## The Treatment Dilemma: PEX vs. Eculizumab

Plasma exchange is lifesaving in TTP. In CM-HUS, it's a bridge at best—removing circulating complement fragments and inflammatory mediators without correcting the underlying dysregulation. Our patient received three PEX sessions with return of spontaneous diuresis, but sustained hematological and renal recovery occurred only after eculizumab.

Here's the catch: **eculizumab blocks terminal complement, which is critical for host defense against encapsulated organisms.** Starting it during active bacteremia could be disastrous. Our patient received eculizumab only after blood cultures cleared, with meningococcal vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis in place.

The algorithm is straightforward but requires coordination:
1. Rule out TTP immediately (PLASMIC score, ADAMTS13)
2. Test for STEC (stool PCR)
3. If neither fits, consider CM-HUS and initiate workup
4. Use PEX as bridge if needed while awaiting definitive diagnosis
5. Start eculizumab once infection is controlled—earlier initiation predicts better renal recovery

## Key Take-Home Points

- **TMA is a syndrome, not a diagnosis.** Your job is to systematically rule out TTP, STEC-HUS, and then consider CM-HUS.
- **Use the PLASMIC score** to risk-stratify for TTP while awaiting ADAMTS13 activity. Score ≥6 = high probability; initiate PEX empirically.
- **ADAMTS13 <10% confirms TTP.** Activity >10%—as in this case (43.5%)—rules it out.
- **Normal C3/C4 does not exclude CM-HUS.** Specialized complement testing may be needed, and diagnosis is often clinicopathological.
- **Infections can trigger CM-HUS** even without identifiable genetic variants. Don't assume a patient needs a "second hit" if they have no prior history.
- **Eculizumab is the definitive treatment for CM-HUS**, not PEX. But hold it until bacteremia clears, and always vaccinate against meningococcus first.
- **Timing matters.** Earlier initiation of complement blockade correlates with better renal recovery. A delayed diagnosis means delayed definitive therapy.

This case reminds us that the most dangerous diagnostic trap isn't missing the rare diagnosis—it's stopping at the obvious one. Our patient had sepsis, DIC, and bacteremia. She also had CM-HUS triggered by that same infection. Recognizing the shift from "septic DIC" to "persistent TMA" required stepping back, looking at the smear, and running the algorithm.



# Normal C3/C4 Doesn't Rule It Out: The Diagnostic Pitfalls of Complement-Mediated HUS

## The Dog Bite That Didn't Bite Back—Until It Did

A woman in her late 40s, previously healthy, gets bitten by her dog on the upper lip. Four days later, she's in fulminant septic shock with DIC, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury. *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* grows in her blood cultures. She survives the sepsis, gets through the ICU, and then—just when things seem to be improving—her hemoglobin crashes from 111 to 68 g/L over six days. Platelets remain stuck at 30-40 × 10⁹/L. LDH climbs. Haptoglobin disappears.

Here's where it gets tricky: her C3 and C4 levels? Completely normal. Her ADAMTS13 activity? 43.5%—nowhere near the <10% threshold for TTP. The peripheral smear shows 15-20% schistocytes. And the clinical picture had shifted from classic DIC to something else entirely.

This case is a masterclass in why complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS) remains one of the most diagnostically treacherous entities in acute medicine—and why waiting for "abnormal complement levels" before acting can cost your patient their kidneys.

## The C3/C4 Trap

Let's be blunt: normal C3 and C4 levels do not exclude CM-HUS. This patient had normal complement levels at multiple time points during her acute illness. The case report explicitly states that "normal complement levels (C3/C4) do not exclude CM-HUS, as levels may remain within reference ranges during the disease course."

This isn't an outlier—it's a recognized pattern. C3 and C4 are crude markers of complement activation. They're acute phase reactants that can actually be elevated by inflammation even as complement consumption occurs elsewhere. In CM-HUS, the problem isn't always systemic depletion; it's dysregulated activation at the endothelial surface, where the alternative complement pathway runs unchecked in the microvasculature.

In this case, extended complement testing ultimately revealed an alternative pathway activity (AH50) of just 6%—markedly reduced—but this was obtained after plasma exchange and eculizumab had already been initiated. The functional assay told a story that standard C3/C4 never could.

## Genetic Testing: Helpful, But Often Non-Diagnostic

Here's another sobering reality: between 50-70% of CM-HUS patients have identifiable genetic variants or autoantibodies affecting complement regulation. That leaves 30-50% where the genetic workup comes back empty—and this patient fell into that group.

Extended genetic testing found no pathogenic variants in complement factors H, I, B, or membrane cofactor protein. No antifactor H antibodies. Yet she still developed a full-blown TMA that responded beautifully to complement blockade.

The lesson? CM-HUS can be purely infection-triggered in individuals without any demonstrable genetic predisposition. *C. canimorsus* itself can activate complement pathways, and in some patients, that activation spirals into uncontrolled endothelial damage. The absence of a genetic hit doesn't mean you don't have a diagnosis—it means you're treating an infection-triggered phenotype.

## Kidney Biopsy: The Tie-Breaker

When the clinical picture is murky—and it often is in post-septic TMA—kidney biopsy can be decisive. This patient's biopsy showed severe acute tubular necrosis, ischemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi, and scattered crescents. That's TMA on a slide. Immunofluorescence was negative for immune complexes, and electron microscopy showed no primary glomerulopathy.

Biopsy confirmed what the labs suggested: this wasn't residual DIC. This wasn't sepsis-associated AKI alone. This was a thrombotic microangiopathy with a specific pathophysiology requiring a specific treatment.

In an anuric, dialysis-dependent patient with persistent hemolysis and thrombocytopenia, biopsy shifted management from supportive care to targeted complement blockade.

## The PLASMIC Score: Useful, But Not Enough

This patient's PLASMIC score was 4/7—low pretest probability for TTP. Her ADAMTS13 activity confirmed it at 43.5%. But "not TTP" is not a diagnosis. The PLASMIC score helps triage who needs urgent plasma exchange while ADAMTS13 is pending. It doesn't tell you what they *do* have.

The differential for MAHA with thrombocytopenia and AKI includes TTP, STEC-HUS, CM-HUS, DIC, and systemic vasculitis. Shiga toxin PCR was negative. ANA and ANCA were negative. The constellation—persistent hemolysis after DIC resolved, schistocytosis, dialysis-dependent AKI, biopsy-proven TMA—pointed to CM-HUS.

## When to Pull the Trigger on Empiric Therapy

Here's the reality: ADAMTS13 results often take days. Extended complement genetic testing takes weeks. This patient's team didn't wait for everything to come back before acting.

They initiated plasma exchange as bridging therapy—knowing it's lifesaving in TTP and provides temporary benefit in CM-HUS by clearing circulating complement fragments. They vaccinated her against meningococcus (Menveo and Bexsero). And once blood cultures were negative, they started eculizumab.

The results spoke for themselves: hematological remission, return of spontaneous diuresis after the final PEX sessions, and partial renal recovery. Her creatinine stabilized at 175 μmol/L—not normal, but dialysis-free.

The team's approach acknowledges a critical principle: in a patient with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, AKI, and biopsy-proven TMA with normal ADAMTS13 and negative Shiga toxin, empiric complement blockade is reasonable while awaiting full complement workup. The risk of delaying treatment—incomplete renal recovery, progression to ESRD—outweighs the theoretical risk of overtreatment.

## The Infection Paradox

There's a particular irony here: *C. canimorsus* is variably serum-sensitive, meaning complement activity is actually part of host defense against it. Some encapsulated strains show partial resistance. Complement activation helps clear the pathogen—but in susceptible individuals, that same activation triggers endothelial catastrophe.

This creates a genuine clinical dilemma. Eculizumab blocks terminal complement, which impairs defense against encapsulated organisms including *Neisseria* and *Capnocytophaga*. The team wisely waited until blood cultures were negative before initiating complement blockade, and they ensured vaccination and prophylactic antibiotics were in place.

## Key Take-Home Points

- **Normal C3/C4 does not rule out CM-HUS.** These are insensitive markers. Functional assays like AH50 may be more informative, but are rarely available in real-time.
- **Genetic testing is negative in 30-50% of CM-HUS cases.** Absence of pathogenic variants doesn't exclude the diagnosis, especially in infection-triggered presentations.
- **Kidney biopsy can confirm TMA when the clinical picture is ambiguous.** In this case, it showed fibrin thrombi in glomeruli—pathognomonic for TMA.
- **CM-HUS is a diagnosis of exclusion.** Rule out TTP (ADAMTS13 <10%), STEC-HUS (Shiga toxin PCR), and other causes of MAHA/AKI.
- **Don't wait for all results to initiate treatment.** Plasma exchange buys time. Eculizumab is disease-modifying. Early initiation predicts better renal recovery.
- **Vaccinate and prophylax before complement blockade.** Meningococcal vaccines and antibiotic prophylaxis are non-negotiable when initiating eculizumab.
- **Infection-triggered CM-HUS exists.** *C. canimorsus* is a rare but documented trigger. Consider it in TMA following dog or cat bites.

## The Bottom Line

This case illustrates why CM-HUS remains a diagnostic minefield. The patient had normal C3/C4, negative genetic testing, and an infection that could explain everything—yet she still had a complement-mediated TMA that responded to targeted therapy.

When you see MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI that doesn't fit neatly into DIC or sepsis, keep CM-HUS on your differential. Order ADAMTS13 early. Send Shiga toxin PCR. Consider kidney biopsy. And remember: normal complement levels don't give you permission to stop thinking.



# Eculizumab at the Crossroads: Balancing Complement Blockade and Infection Risk

## When the Cure Could Kill: A Post-Infectious Dilemma

Let's set the scene: you've got a patient who just survived fulminant sepsis from *Capnocytophaga canimorsus*—that slow-growing, fastidious gram-negative rod that lives in dog mouths and has a mortality rate approaching 30%. She's been through DIC, pancytopenia, complete anuria, and dialysis dependence. She's finally turning a corner. Blood cultures are negative. And then... she develops sudden hemolysis, thrombocytopenia, and you realize this isn't just recovering sepsis anymore. She's now got complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS).

Here's your therapeutic tightrope: the standard of care is eculizumab—terminal complement blockade. But terminal complement is critical for host defense against encapsulated organisms, including *Neisseria* and, ironically, *Capnocytophaga*. So do you pull the trigger on complement inhibition in someone who just survived a serious encapsulated organism infection? That's the crossroads.

## The Infection That Triggers the Storm

This case is remarkable because it illustrates how an infection can unmask complement dysregulation even without an identifiable genetic predisposition. Our patient had no pathogenic complement gene variants on extended testing, yet she developed classic CM-HUS triggered by *C. canimorsus*.

The pathophysiology is fascinating: *C. canimorsus* activates complement pathways, and while complement helps clear the pathogen, it can simultaneously trigger uncontrolled endothelial damage in susceptible individuals. This creates a clinical paradox—you need complement to fight the infection, but complement is now destroying the kidneys.

## Timing Is Everything: When to Pull the Trigger

The evidence is clear: early initiation of eculizumab is the single most important predictor of renal recovery in CM-HUS. Delayed initiation correlates with incomplete renal recovery and progression to end-stage kidney disease.

In this case, eculizumab was initiated **after blood cultures became negative**—a pragmatic approach that minimized the risk of uncontrolled infection while still allowing for timely complement blockade. The patient received induction dosing (900 mg IV weekly for 4 weeks) and achieved hematological remission with partial renal recovery (creatinine stabilized at 175 μmol/L).

The key lesson: don't wait for perfect clarity. Once you've excluded TTP (ADAMTS13 >10%) and STEC-HUS, and you have evidence of TMA with organ dysfunction, the clock is ticking on renal recovery.

## The Vaccination Mandate: Non-Negotiable

Before eculizumab touches the infusion bag, meningococcal vaccination is mandatory. This patient received both Menveo (serogroups A, C, Y, W) and Bexsero (serogroup B)—covering the major pathogenic serogroups.

But here's the catch: ideally, vaccines should be administered at least 2 weeks before complement blockade to allow adequate immune response. In this case, prophylactic phenoxymethylpenicillin was given because that 2-week window wasn't feasible given the urgency of her clinical deterioration.

The practical approach: vaccinate immediately, start prophylactic antibiotics (penicillin is the standard), and proceed with eculizumab if the clinical situation demands it.

## Plasma Exchange: Bridge, Not Destination

This case also highlights the limited role of PEX in CM-HUS. Our patient received three sessions of plasma exchange, and diuresis returned after the final session—**before** eculizumab was administered. But this was transient stabilization, not sustained recovery.

PEX removes circulating complement fragments and inflammatory mediators, but it doesn't correct the underlying complement dysregulation. It's a bridge to definitive therapy, not the therapy itself. In contrast, TTP requires PEX as lifesaving treatment—another reason why ADAMTS13 testing is critical early in any TMA workup.

## How Long Do We Treat? The Reassessment Question

The duration of complement blockade remains an ongoing clinical question. This patient continues maintenance eculizumab (1200 mg IV every 2 weeks) with planned reassessment. The absence of identified pathogenic complement gene variants doesn't mean she can stop—infection-triggered CM-HUS can still warrant extended therapy given disease severity.

The current approach: continue complement blockade until renal function stabilizes, then reassess for discontinuation with close monitoring for relapse. Patients with identified pathogenic variants typically require longer-term therapy.

## Key Take-Home Messages

- **Normal C3/C4 does not exclude CM-HUS**—complement levels can remain within reference ranges during active disease. The diagnosis is clinicopathological and one of exclusion.
- **ADAMTS13 activity >10% effectively rules out TTP**—get this early. A PLASMIC score can help triage, but don't rely on it alone.
- **Early eculizumab = better renal outcomes**—delays in initiation correlate with progression to ESRD.
- **Timing matters for infection risk**—initiate complement blockade after bacteremia clears when possible, but don't delay unnecessarily.
- **Vaccinate, prophylax, proceed**—meningococcal vaccines (both quadrivalent and serogroup B) plus antibiotic prophylaxis if the 2-week window isn't feasible.
- **PEX is a bridge, not definitive therapy**—it buys time but doesn't address the underlying complement dysregulation.
- **Infection-triggered CM-HUS can occur without genetic predisposition**—don't wait for genetic results to start treatment if the clinical picture fits.

## The Bottom Line

CM-HUS following *C. canimorsus* infection is rare, but it illustrates a broader principle: any TMA that isn't TTP or STEC-HUS should prompt consideration of complement-mediated disease. The therapeutic window for renal recovery is finite, and the risks of complement blockade can be mitigated with proper vaccination, prophylaxis, and timing. When you're standing at that crossroads, remember—the infection may have triggered the storm, but the storm won't wait for perfect conditions to treat.
